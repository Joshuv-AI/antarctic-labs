Yolov7 neural network for pelvic X-rays after total hip arthroplasty: automatically measuring component position parameters- a retrospective feasibility study
Authors: Jiawei Li, Liangjia Ding, Kai Zhang, Yunfeng Zhang, Shang Gao, Feng Jin, Qingyu Ji, Qingwei Chen, Zhiyong Guo, Wenjie Lan, Haiyan Wang, Lichun Zhang, Xiaohe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter total hip arthroplasty (THA), regular follow-up X-rays are required to evaluate the position of the prosthesis. However, measuring the parameters of prosthesis position consumes a significant amount of time, and there may be variations in the results obtained by different doctors.PurposeTo validate the feasibility of an artificial intelligence model for the automated measurement of component position parameters on anteroposterior X-rays after THA.Materials and methodsPost-THA anteroposterior X-rays were collected from five hospitals in Inner Mongolia, China. They were divided into training, validation and test sets. The model was divided into left, right, and bilateral THA modules for training. Statistical analysis was performed using Python 3.8 and SPSS 25.0. The accuracy of the model in keypoint detection and measurement were assessed. In addition, we compared the time difference between the model and manual measurements.ResultsA total of 1050 X-rays were included in the training and validation sets (350 X-rays for each module), and 105 in the test set (35 X-rays for each module). Overall, 80.00–100.00% of the model-predicted keypoints fell within 3 mm of the standard keypoints. The model-predicted values were highly consistent with the standard reference values (intra-class correlation coefficient = 0.89–0.99, r = 0.83–0.99, root mean square error = 0.96–3.43, mean absolute error = 0.78–2.87, mean difference = -1.29–1.17). The model-predicted values were highly consistent with the measurements of senior attending radiologists. The model took an average of 3.2 s to measure an X-ray image, which is significantly shorter than the 751.76 s in manual measurement.ConclusionsThis study validates the feasibility of the model for rapid and accurate keypoint identification and component position parameter measurement on anteroposterior X-rays. The model holds promise as a diagnostic aid to alleviate clinicians’ workload and facilitate the monitoring of prosthetic positioning changes following THA.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Yolov7, Total hip arthroplasty, Keypoint identification, Anteroposterior pelvic X-ray

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is an effective treatment that reduces symptoms and significantly improves quality of life in patients with end-stage hip diseases, such as femoral head necrosis, femoral neck fracture, and hip degeneration [1]. There were approximately 370,000 patients undergoing THA in the United States in 2014 [2], 37,000 in Canada [3] and 97,000 in the UK in 2017 [4], and more than 400,000 in China every year [5]. As the incidence of diseases, such as osteoarthritis, continues to increase due to the aging population, the number of patients undergoing THA will continue to increase.

Owing to evolving medical technology, approximately 95% of hip prostheses can stand for more than 10 years, with some even lasting as long as 30 years [6, 7]. Although the life of the prosthesis has been significantly prolonged, dislocation occurs in 0.2% to 10% of patients after THA [8], and loosening in 1.8% [9]. Regular follow-up after THA helps detect abnormal position of prosthesis early and facilitates revision surgery. At present, X-ray is preferred for evaluating component position after THA. Many parameters, such as acetabular component abduction and anteversion angles, vertical and horizontal center of rotation (VCR and HCR), femoral offset (FO), and leg length discrepancy (LLD), are used in X-ray evaluation. Moreover, to help early detection of prosthetic abnormalities, regular follow-up should be carried out based on immediate postoperative X-rays every 3 months within one year after THA, and every six months to one year thereafter [10–12]. However, it takes a long time to accurately measure these parameters. Moreover, different geographical location, education levels, and experiences of clinicians may introduce subjective differences in measurement. Clinicians are eager to reduce their workload using current computer technology. As artificial intelligence (AI) and algorithms have continued to develop in recent years, AI has been increasingly used in the medical field, including management of joints, especially the hip. For example, deep learning was used to measure the acetabular index on plain X-ray to screen children for developmental dysplasia of the hip [13], and to assess the risk of prosthetic dislocation based on post-THA plain X-ray [14]. The success of these studies demonstrates to some extent the feasibility of applying AI in hip management.

Despite the increasing application of AI in THA, few studies have been performed on AI models for automatic measurement and monitoring of component position. Tseng et al. [15] included post-THA anteroposterior X-rays to train an AI model for measuring component position parameters on plain X-ray (BKNet). In contrast to the model by Tseng et al., which remains relatively simplistic without distinct categorization, our proposed model provides a more granular framework by incorporating three specialized modules. Pouria et al. [14] established a convolutional neural network model (U-Net) to measure the acetabular component abduction and anteversion angles. However, the model of Pouria et al. has a limited number of measurable parameters and cannot comprehensively assess post-THA component position. In contrast, our model incorporates six distinct parameters, offering a more robust and comprehensive evaluation. The model is divided into three modules (i.e., left, right, and bilateral THA modules), incorporating six key parameters to provide a more comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. This model has been validated as a promising tool to facilitate rapid and precise clinical assessment of component placement.

Materials and methods

Data sources

A retrospective, multi-center study was conducted using post-THA anteroposterior pelvic X-ray images from five hospitals (Site1, Site2, Site3, Site4, and Site5) in Inner Mongolia. Specifically, 1050 post-THA pelvic anteroposterior X-rays (including 350 each for left, right and bilateral THA) were collected from Site1, Site2, Site3, and Site4 between January 1, 2021 and February 28, 2023 for model training. Additionally, 105 post-THA anteroposterior X-ray images (including 35 images each for left, right and bilateral THA) were collected from Site5 from May 1 to June 30, 2023 for independent external verification (Fig. 1).

Multi-center data composition diagram

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: patients undergoing unilateral or bilateral THA with bioprostheses.

Exclusion criteria: (1) anatomical keypoints (including bilateral teardrops, ischial tuberosities, and lesser trochanters) not clearly visible on X-ray images; (2) asymmetrical pelvic structure; and (3) patients with developmental hip dysplasia, and those with conditions affecting hip radiographic measurements, including local bone defects, bone tumors, and severe degenerative changes of the unaffected joint.

X-ray acquisition parameters

The digital X-ray systems of SIEMENS (Multix Fusion, Germany) and PHILIPS (Digital Diagnost, Netherlands) were used. The patient was placed in a relaxed supine position, with eyes looking straight ahead, upper limbs along the trunk, and lower limbs straight at 15° internal rotation. Images were taken with one-time exposure at distance of 100 cm in the anteroposterior position. The X-ray beam was centered on the upper edge of the pubic symphysis and perpendicular to the patient [16].

Definition of measurement parameters and keypoints

Definition of measurement parameters

Acetabular component abduction angle [10] is defined as the angle between the line connecting the outermost and innermost points of the acetabular component and the line connecting the inferior points of ischial tuberosities.

Acetabular component anteversion angle [12] is calculated according to the formula by Lewinnek, acetabular component anteversion = arcsin(r/R), where r is twice the vertical distance from the inferior point of the short axis of the cup-shaped opening of the acetabular component to the line connecting the outermost and innermost points of the acetabular component, and R is the distance between the outermost and innermost points of the acetabular component.

VCR [17] is defined as the vertical distance from the center of the femoral head (or the center of the femoral head component) to the line connecting the inferior points of the teardrops.

HCR [17] is defined as the distance from the projection of the center of the femoral head (or the center of the femoral head component) on the line connecting the inferior points of the teardrops to the inferior point of the ipsilateral teardrop.

FO is defined as the vertical distance from the center of the femoral head to the axis of the proximal femur. (The axis of the proximal femoral is defined as the line connecting two reference points: the midpoint of medullary canal at the level of the inferior point of the lesser trochanter, and the midpoint of the medullary canal at the level 5 cm below the inferior point of the lesser trochanter.)

LLD [18] is the distance between the lines parallel to the line connecting the inferior points of the teardrops through the most prominent points in the middle of the lesser trochanters.

Data labeling

The training and validation sets were used to train the model. They were labeled by a senior attending radiologist with at least 20 years of experience in bone and joint X-ray diagnosis (Radiologist 1 from Site3) using the software LabelMe. First, a target frame, which must include the relevant keypoints, was drawn on the upper femurs and acetabula. Keypoints were then labeled within the set target frame. The trained model should be able to automatically predict unlabeled keypoints. The test set was used to evaluate the prediction performance of the model. It was labeled by the model, as well as three senior attending radiologists, Radiologists 1, 2, and 3, independently of each other to ensure objectivity. As there was no reference standard for the coordinate data of keypoints, the average of the keypoint coordinate values obtained by the three radiologists by labeling the test set was used as the reference standard to compare with those predicted by the model [19, 20] (Fig. 2).

Data processing

Dataset was processed using Python 3.8. First, to improve generalization and alleviate overfitting, we employed several data augmentation techniques—including horizontal flipping, and color space perturbations (Hue, Saturation, and Value). Implemented via the standard YOLOv7 pipeline, these methods enhance data distribution diversity and ensure model robustness against the variability of complex clinical images. Subsequently, the resolution of all images was scaled to 640 × 640 pixels for machine learning. After prediction by the model, the test set images were resampled as original pixels and used for comparison with the labeling results obtained by the radiologists on the original images. The parameters of the post-THA anteroposterior pelvic X-ray were a resolution of 2801 × 2753 and an original pixel size of 0.143 mm.

Model construction

The model was divided into three modules for left, right, and bilateral THA for training. The model used in this study was constructed based on the convolutional neural network Yolov7 (version yolov7-w6-pose, released on July 7, 2022). In this study, the network architecture was divided into two parts, i.e., Backbone and Head [21]. The model architecture utilizes a Backbone for multi-scale feature extraction, capturing deep semantic information through successive downsampling and channel expansion. The Head integrates SPPCSP and E-ELAN-based PAN modules to optimize gradient paths and enhance feature fusion, thereby improving the network’s overall learning efficiency.

The workflow consists of three stages: target localization, which automatically defines the Region of Interest (ROI) covering the proximal femur and acetabulum; landmark detection, performed precisely within the localized ROI; and post-processing, where identified keypoints are used to automatically calculate clinical parameters, including acetabular abduction and anteversion angles (Figs. 3 and 4).

The model construction process diagram

The experiments were run on CentOS 7.9.2009 with NVIDIA Tesla GPU. The experimental environment is configured with NVIDIA-Tesla-A100-80GB GPU, 8-core 64GB CPU, PyTorch-1.4.0 dependency library, and Jupyter Lab Notebook. Model hyperparameters were as follows: batch-size 16, 500 epochs, img-size 640*640, and 8 workers. The model was optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with an initial learning rate of 0.01, a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 5*10− 4. To enhance training stability and facilitate model convergence, a cosine annealing strategy was employed for dynamic adjustment of the learning rate. Loss Function Design: The model was trained using a multi-task joint optimization strategy. The total loss function comprises four components: bounding box regression loss, objectness loss, classification loss, and keypoint regression loss. Specifically, the keypoint regression component employs an L1 loss to penalize the spatial deviations between the predicted keypoints and the ground-truth annotations. To prevent the model from overfitting on small-sample data, this study employs several regularization and control strategies. First, weight decay is applied as a formal regularization technique to constrain model complexity. Second, diverse data augmentation is introduced to broaden the training distribution. Third, the best model selection is conducted based on performance metrics from the validation set. Furthermore, the trend of validation loss is monitored throughout the training process; early stopping is implemented when performance gains plateau, ensuring that the optimal weights are preserved for subsequent evaluation.

The model was obtained after 5 h and 36 min of training. In all experiments, the same backbone, Yolov7, was used. To construct the keypoint detection network, pre-training was performed using the ImageNet dataset [22] to improve the performance of small datasets to meet the requirements of deep learning for large data volumes. The Yolov7 keypoint detection network used optimizes ImageNet by initializing weights to achieve transfer learning. It prevents overfitting of the neural network with a limited number of training images. Yolov7 maintains high-resolution representations throughout the entire process and is suitable for landmark detection on pelvic X-ray images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python 3.8 and SPSS 25.0. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The distribution of patient age and gender in the training, validation, and test sets was investigated by descriptive statistics.

Consistency of image labeling

The Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) of keypoint labeling was assessed by calculating the distance from labeled point to labeled point between two observers and the percentages of distance differences of 1, 2, 3, and 4 mm [20, 23].

Accuracy of keypoint detection by the model

The accuracy of the model in predicting keypoints was measured by PCK [20, 23]. The pixel coordinates for calculating PCK are original coordinates of the keypoints. Due to the lack of an objective standard, the average coordinate value of the three radiologists was used as the reference standard.

Accuracy of model measurements

Due to the lack of an objective standard, the average measurement of the three radiologists was used as the reference standard for comparison with model predictions. The performance of the deep learning model was evaluated by the Bland-Altman plot, intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), pearson correlation analysis, root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and paired t test.

Comparison of model and manual measurement time

Model and manual measurement time was compared by independent samples t test.

Discussion

Comparison with previous studies

Post-THA X-rays help clinicians understand the position of the implanted prosthesis and monitor the wear and stability of the prosthesis and osteolysis. As the number of patients undergoing THA continues to increase due to the aging population, the workload in postoperative follow-up will inevitably increase significantly. It will definitely consume a lot of time of clinicians. Moreover, there may be subjective differences in the interpretation of imaging data between clinicians with different experiences. In order to address this issue, many researchers have tried to use AI to assess post-THA X-rays [24]. Pouria et al. [14] developed a convolutional neural network model based on ResNet18 and included the 5-year follow-up X-ray data of patients undergoing THA, including 1,490 patients with dislocation and 91,094 without dislocation. This model was demonstrated to provide high sensitivity and negative predictive ability, enable quickly assessing the risk of dislocation after THA based on clinical risk factors. In addition, some researchers have developed an AI model for automatic measurement of component position parameters on X-rays after THA. For example, Tseng et al. [15] included 3,072 post-THA anteroposterior X-rays to train an AI model for measuring component position parameters on plain X-ray (BKNet), which was proven to be able to quickly and accurately measure component position parameters. Pouria et al. [14] developed a convolutional neural network model (U-Net) to measure the abduction and anteversion of the acetabular component, which included 600 anteroposterior and 600 cross-table post-THA X-rays. This model was found to be no different from clinicians in measurement accuracy, and is expected to be applied in clinical practice and scientific research. The AI model developed in this study identifies keypoints on post-THA X-ray and automatically measures angles and distances through the identified keypoints. It is similar to the keypoint detection model developed by Tseng et al. [15]. However, our model includes three modules, i.e., left, right, and bilateral THA modules, which were trained separately. Moreover, a target frame was detected first to increase the accuracy of subsequent keypoint detection. The two features make the present model different from the model developed by Tseng et al. [15]. Regarding the keypoint identification capability at a 5 mm threshold, the BKNET model developed by Tseng et al. achieved a PCK of at least 75%, whereas our model demonstrated superior performance with a PCK of at least 91% under the same threshold, which demonstrate that our model outperformed the model developed by Tseng et al. The model of Pouria et al. [14] was established by segmentation, whereas the present model is a keypoint detection model. Due to the fundamental differences in the methodologies of these two models, their performance cannot be compared directly. In addition, the present model measures six post-THA component position parameters, whereas that of Pouria et al. [14] only measures the abduction and anteversion of the acetabular component. The present AI model was demonstrated to accurately identify keypoints on post-THA anteroposterior pelvic X-rays, with a PCK of 80–100% within a 3-mm distance and 91.42–100.00% within a 4-mm distance. It accurately measured acetabular component abduction and anteversion, bilateral FO, VCR, and HCR differences, and LLD, representing an ICC of 0.89–0.98 with the reference standard. The error of this model was comparable to that of senior attending radiologists.

Performance of YOLOv7 relative to other neural networks

Yolo, short for “you only look once”, was first proposed by Redmon et al. It is an object detection algorithm based on regression, which can detect objects in real time, but is difficult to detect small objects [25]. Yolo has undergone multiple updates. Yolov7 is the new version released on July 7, 2022. It was developed by Wang et al. [21]. Compared to U-Net, which is prone to interference from metallic artifacts during pixel-wise segmentation, and BKNet, which lacks the capacity to capture complex orthopedic features, YOLOv7-W6-Pose offers superior robustness. Its w6 architecture allows for high-resolution processing of X-ray images, ensuring precise localization of key anatomical landmarks and prosthesis centers, which is critical for accurate postoperative geometric measurements. Yolov7 has been rarely used in medicine. The present model was developed based on Yolov7. The results show that Yolov7 is very useful in keypoint detection on plain pelvic X-rays.

Analysis of measurement accuracy and model performance

The ability to accurately identify keypoints is the fundamental prerequisite for reliable parameter measurement. Any excessive discrepancy between the model-identified landmarks and the ground truth would inevitably lead to variations in measurements, potentially altering clinical outcomes. However, inherent variability in landmark detection is unavoidable due to anatomical complexity. Regarding the acceptable degree of discrepancy, several established benchmarks provide guidance. Chen et al. [26] suggested that an average distance difference of keypoints between groups within 2.98 mm was considered satisfactory in clinical analysis. In this study, 92–96% of the keypoints labeled by different radiologists fell within a distance of 3 mm, which indicates that independent labeling between radiologists was reliable. The AI model developed in this study yielded a PCK of 80–100% within 3 mm. This performance demonstrates that our model’s precision aligns with established clinical tolerances. Despite its good performance, there were deviations in the predictions of a few keypoints. In particular, the PCK within 4–5 mm was low for the midpoint of medullary canal at the level 5 cm below the inferior point of the lesser trochanter (L_P15 and L_P17 in left THA, R_P15 and R_P17 in right THA, and D_110 and D_210 in bilateral THA). This may be because the reference anatomical landmarks necessary for localization are often located at a significant distance from the target points, complicating the spatial localization process. The midpoint of medullary canal at the level of the inferior point of the lesser trochanter (L_P14 and L_P16 in left THA, R_P14 and R_P16 in right THA, and D_109 and D_209 in bilateral THA) also has an unclear anatomical landmark. However, the model prediction of this point is based on the prediction of the inferior point of the lesser trochanter, which has a clear anatomical landmark. Therefore, the model provided relatively accurate prediction for the midpoint of medullary canal at the level of the inferior point of the lesser trochanter.

In the left, right, and bilateral THA modules, the model-predicted values were all highly consistent with the standard reference values, with ICC of 0.89–0.99 and r of 0.83–0.99. Furthermore, the model-predicted values were highly correlated with the measurements of senior attending radiologists, with r ranging from 0.77 to 0.98. In all the three modules, the model-predicted values only had a slightly higher MAE for a few parameters than measurements of a single radiologist. The MAE of the model-predicted values of most parameters was not significantly different from or was smaller than that of the measurements of senior attending radiologists. Comparison shows that the error of the model is comparable to that of senior attending radiologists.

Clinical application

Correct prosthesis positioning (including acetabular component anteversion and abduction angles) and restoration of hip biomechanics (including reconstruction of hip rotation center and leg length and offset) are the keys to successful THA. They are also the basis for the long-term stability of the implanted prosthesis. The positioning of the prosthesis can be quantitatively evaluated by measuring the component position parameters on the first plain pelvic X-ray after THA. The position changes of the prosthesis can also be quantitatively monitored by subsequent regular follow-up X-rays. The THA component position parameters selected in this study are internationally recognized indicators. Repeating measurements of these parameters waste the valuable time of clinicians. Moreover, the same patient may not necessarily have all X-rays performed at the same hospital over time. In this case, it is unknown whether the same accurate measurement can be made by radiologists in different regions, with different education levels and experiences. The model developed this study provided comparable predictions of THA component position parameters to the measurements of senior attending radiologists. It can eliminate the differences between radiologists that may be caused by subjective factors to some extent. Moreover, model measurement takes significantly less time than manual measurement. Therefore, this model is expected to be used for measurement in postoperative reexaminations and early detection of changes in component position. When a large change in the component position is found, the patient should be advised to undergo a comprehensive and in-depth examination to prevent further displacement of the prosthesis.

The clinical utility of this work lies in providing a validated foundation for future integration into hospital infrastructures, such as Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). By offering transparent visual overlays of keypoints, the model facilitates a transition where clinicians shift from performing manual measurements to verifying AI-generated results. While currently in the validation phase, this approach has the potential to significantly reduce the manual workload of orthopedic surgeons and radiologists, ultimately enhancing the efficiency and standardization of post-operative follow-up.

As a clinical decision support tool, our model provides intuitive visual overlays of keypoints. The core of its reliability lies in the precise localization of anatomical and prosthetic landmarks. Since the measurement calculations are mathematically derived from these points, training focuses on enabling clinicians to visually verify the accuracy of keypoint identification against the radiographic anatomy. In cases where landmarks may be misaligned due to severe bone loss or image noise, clinicians can immediately identify the discrepancy and perform manual corrections.

Study limitations

Limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a feasibility validation, this study focused on standard anatomical conditions; thus, the model’s reliability in complex or extreme cases (e.g., severe deformities or pelvic obliquity) requires further validation. Second, this pilot study did not extensively analyze the impact of patient demographics; BMI, potential variations in bone density and soft-tissue interference across different populations may influence identification precision. Third, predictive deviations persisted at specific landmarks, notably the medullary canal midpoints located 5 cm below the lesser trochanter (L_P15/17, R_P15/17, D_110/210). Admittedly, these limitations also provide clear directions for our future research.

Future research

Addressing these limitations will be the primary focus of our future research. Building upon our current findings, we intend to integrate large-scale, multi-center datasets—encompassing diverse demographics and complex pathologies like early loosening—to enhance model robustness and generalization. Crucially, we will refine our algorithmic frameworks to specifically improve the identification efficacy for landmarks with indistinct anatomical features. Furthermore, we plan to explore multimodal data fusion to improve the estimation precision of parameters such as the anteversion angle. Ultimately, we aim to refine this model into a reliable clinical decision-support system for standardized longitudinal tracking and early intervention after THA.

Conclusions

This study validated the feasibility of an automated model for measuring component position parameters on post-THA pelvic radiographs. With performance in keypoint identification and parameter prediction comparable to that of senior radiologists, the model demonstrates significant potential as a clinical decision-support tool. Upon further refinement, it is expected to alleviate the diagnostic workload of clinicians and provide robust support for the precise monitoring of post-operative component position changes during follow-up.
