Technical progress

Accomplishments
In the three funded years of research (and one additional year of no-cost extension), we focused
on several areas of interest towards the goal of reducing the content of PGMs in emission control
catalysts. Overall, our research activity and accomplishments can be divided into the following
areas:
- fundamental understanding of the stability of encapsulated catalysts
- understanding of the synthesis conditions in the preparation of encapsulated catalysts
- focus on increasing the performance (activity, stability) of the catalysts by addition of promoters
(ceria) and by geometric modifications of the catalysts by ALD
- expansion of the materials library to new polymer templating materials and new oxide coatings
by ALD, with the opportunity to increase the control over the catalyst parameters (composition,
metal loading, particle density)
- in-depth characterization of the materials to extract information on the chemical and
morphological nature of the improved performance of encapsulated catalysts

Fundamental understanding of stability
We used ALD techniques to prepare catalysts that could help us understand the fundamental
reasons for the observed stability. We developed two alumina ALD deposition strategies, namely
Short & Long Exposure, to synthesize model encapsulated systems with a low and high ALD
alumina content, respectively. The two recipes only differ in the precursors (trimethylaluminum
and water) pulse time, which is kept very low (30ms) for the Short Exposure deposition. The most
important and surprising observation is that only trace amounts of alumina are needed to stabilize
the underlying Pt metal. A starting Pt/ncAl2O3 catalyst is obtained via impregnation of colloidally
synthesized Pt nanoparticles on an alumina supported obtained via nanocasting. The catalyst is
then modified via ALD (50 cycles for both recipes) and the obtained samples are characterized by
means of X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to probe for bulk compositional changes, X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) to observe surface chemical changes, and high angle annular dark field
scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) as well as bright field TEM (BF-
TEM) to characterize morphology and particle size distribution (PSD) of the catalytic materials.

The homogeneous chemical nature of the ALD overlayer and the starting support (both Al2O3)
made it difficult to highlight any specific areas where we can clearly distinguish the added
encapsulating layer form the original catalyst via TEM. A particle size distribution (PSD) analysis
shows how the particles remain unchanged in size after both Short and Long Exposure ALD,
maintaining an average size of about 5 nm, as depicted in Fig.1.

We explored how different metal oxide coatings impact the performance and stability of Al2O3-
supported Pt catalysts. We synthesized colloidal platinum nanoparticles and deposited them onto
a high-surface-area alumina support (Pt/Al2O3). To evaluate how different oxide coatings influence
catalyst durability and Pt accessibility, we then applied 50 cycles of alumina, zirconia, or titania
atomic layer deposition (ALD) to form conformal overlayers around the Pt nanoparticles. Building
on our prior work demonstrating that Al2O3 ALD can suppress Pt sintering, this study extends the
approach to ZrO2 and TiO2 to systematically compare how overlayer chemistry affects catalytic
performance. The resulting materials (Al2O3/Pt/Al2O3, ZrO2/Pt/Al2O3, and TiO2/Pt/Al2O3) were
evaluated alongside the uncoated Pt/Al2O3 catalyst for hydrothermal stability using propene
oxidation as a representative reaction for hydrocarbon combustion. Catalysts were tested in both
their initial (“fresh”) state and after exposure to harsh hydrothermal aging (“aged”) to assess
structural resilience and activity retention.

High-resolution electron microscopy confirmed that ALD coatings of Al2O3, ZrO2, and TiO2
formed uniform overlayers on Pt/Al2O3 without significantly altering the initial Pt particle size
distributions (Fig. 4). All three ALD films were of comparable thickness, and the overall structural
similarity among samples ensures that differences in catalytic behavior arise primarily from the
chemistry of the ALD layers rather than variations in Pt size.

Catalytic testing revealed a strong contrast between the fresh and aged materials. As shown in the
Arrhenius plot, the fresh ALD-coated catalysts exhibited severely diminished activity due to the
thick overlayers blocking access to Pt (Fig. 5). After high-temperature aging, however, all ALD-
modified catalysts became significantly more active, while the uncoated Pt/Al2O3 control
deactivated due to sintering. Among the aged materials, Al2O3- and ZrO2-coated catalysts
performed the best, whereas TiO2-coated samples remained comparatively less active.

Nitrogen physisorption revealed that the thick ALD overlayers substantially reduced surface area
and pore accessibility in the fresh catalysts (Table 1). After high-temperature aging, all the ALD-
modified catalysts showed significant increases in surface area, indicating that the oxide coatings
restructured to create a more open, porous morphology. These results are further confirmed by
high resolution XPS of the Al 2p and Pt 4f core levels (Figure 6). In the fresh ZrO2- and TiO2-ALD
catalysts, signals from the underlying alumina support are fully obscured, but after aging, the Al
2p becomes detectable, indicating that the oxide films restructured under high-temperature aging.
The weaker Al 2p signal in the aged ZrO2-ALD sample is consistent with its lower post-aging

surface area, suggesting less extensive restructuring than in the TiO2 case. As expected, the Al2O3-
coated catalyst shows strong Al signals both before and after aging due to the shared composition
of the overlayer and support. Pt 4f signals disappear in all aged ALD-coated catalysts and weaken
in the aged uncoated sample, consistent with reduced surface Pt concentration and Pt sintering.
Collectively, the XPS results corroborate physisorption and catalytic data, showing that aging
induces partial opening of the ALD films, enabling improved Pt accessibility and higher catalytic

Although the fresh ZrO2-coated catalyst has a similarly low surface area to the Al2O3-coated
sample, it shows noticeably higher activity than both the fresh Al2O3 and TiO2 catalysts. A control
experiment using ZrO2 grown directly on the support confirmed that zirconia itself is not
catalytically active, indicating that the enhanced performance does not arise from promotional
effects. Instead, the ZrO2 ALD layer likely forms a more particle-like, less dense film that leaves
more Pt sites partially accessible compared to the more conformal Al2O3 and TiO2 coatings,
explaining its higher initial activity (Figure 7).

CO DRIFTS measurements highlight how the ALD overlayers influence Pt site accessibility before
and after aging (Fig. 9). Fresh ALD-coated catalysts show no detectable CO–Pt signals, confirming
that the thick oxide layers effectively block CO adsorption. After hydrothermal aging, CO bands
reappear, indicating that restructuring of the overlayers partially reopens access to Pt, though the
overall intensities remain much lower than for uncoated fresh Pt/Al2O3 due to remaining coverage
and sintering. Differences among the aged samples reveal how each oxide influences the types of
Pt sites exposed: Al2O3 coatings maintain some undercoordinated Pt sites associated with small
clusters, while TiO2 produces a distinct higher-frequency band, suggesting altered electronic or
structural environments. These trends help explain the catalytic results: Al2O3-coated catalysts
benefit from retaining small, active Pt clusters, whereas ZrO2-coated catalysts perform better than
TiO2 versions largely because their more porous initial morphology allowed greater Pt accessibility
from the outset.

In parallel to experimental work, we also used theoretical tools to understand the deposition of
alumina layers on the Pt particles. Experimental observation shows that the ALD by AlMe3 in the
presence of water on the 5nm Pt catalyst can prevent sintering under the operating conditions. To
explain this our basic understanding tells that the more highly unsaturated atoms tend to detach
from the surface very easily compared to the saturated atoms (Fig. 10).

Therefore, if step sites in a particular nanoparticle are stabilized by atomic layer deposition (ALD),
it is expected that the nanoparticle will be resistant to sintering. To investigate the unique properties
of both terrace and step sites on Pt nanoparticles, we have analyzed the surfaces of 4-layer 3×3
Pt(111) and 8-layer 3×3 Pt(211), respectively, as depicted in Figure 4b-4e.

We conducted first principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations using the Vienna Ab
initio Simulation Package (VASP) within the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE). To
represent exchange-correlation effects, we employed the BEEF-vdW functional, which has been
benchmarked for the calculation of adsorbate binding energies on transition metal surfaces. We
set the cutoff energy of the plane wave basis sets to 500 eV and optimized all structures until total
energies converged to at least 10-5 eV and forces became less than 0.05 eV/Å. For the Pt(111)
surface, we fixed the bottom two layers while allowing the top layers to relax during system
optimization. For the Pt(211) surface, we fixed the bottom four layers while allowing the top layers
to relax during optimization. We included over 15 Å of vacuum between slabs to avoid any
periodic image interactions and used the Monkhorst−Pack method to generate k-point grids in the
Brillouin zone. We solved the DFT-based Kohn-Sham equations using a 4×4×1 k-point grid for
the reciprocal space of the surfaces and applied a dipole correction to avoid artificial periodic
interactions between slabs. We optimized the lattice parameter and found a Pt lattice constant value
of 3.98 A. We optimized the energies of gas phase molecules in a 21×22×23 Å cell using a 1×1×1
k-point density. We also calculated the binding energies of metal atoms using the following

To gain further insights into the activity trends of the aged catalysts, computational studies were
conducted for the energetics of diffusion and anchoring of Pt atoms within the ALD overlayers.
CO DRIFTS measurements reveal the formation of highly undercoordinated (UC) sites, i.e., small
Pt nanoclusters, after the aging process for Al2O3/Pt/Al2O3 which may result from the diffusion of
Pt atoms from Pt nanoparticles into defect sites within the oxide bulk. Our recently developed
coordination-based α-scheme model, along with DFT-calculated binding energies, shows that the
Pt binding energy in different coordination environments on a nanoparticle range from -4.17 eV to
-6.46 eV. The calculated binding energies of Pt at single defect sites of oxides are higher compared
to the Pt binding energies at various coordination environment as well as bulk. Within an Al2O3
defect the Pt binding energy is –8.27 eV, while for ZrO2 and TiO2, the binding energies are –
8.62 eV and –11.45 eV respectively (Fig. 15).

and TiO2 bulks and (b) vacancy sites with a neighboring site available for diffusion. Corresponding

The diffusion of Pt cannot be driven by Pt interactions with only one defect site as one vacancy
within the oxide layers can only accommodate Pt without providing any additional pathways for
its diffusion. Hence, we also considered Pt binding energies at defect sites with a neighboring site
available for diffusion. In this case, the calculated Pt binding energies were –9.59 eV for Al2O3, –
12.36 eV for ZrO2, and –13.31 eV for TiO2. These energies suggest that Pt binds very strongly
within ZrO2 and TiO2 compared to Al2O3. Although the calculated binding energies for ZrO2 and
TiO2 are comparable, the two overlayers differ markedly in morphology over Pt/Al2O3. As
discussed earlier, the ZrO2 ALD film forms a less dense film over the catalyst than TiO2. This
morphological difference likely enables greater Pt accessibility and diffusion in ZrO2, consistent
with its higher activity in the fresh catalyst. These results correlate with the lack of a signal for
highly UC Pt sites in the CO DRIFTS for the aged ZrO2 and TiO2 ALD catalyst. Thus, while
strong Pt oxide binding compared to metal binding energy in the Pt nanoparticle facilitates
diffusion, excessively high binding energies hinder the formation of accessible Pt sites. An optimal
binding strength is required allowing for diffusion without completely immobilizing the Pt. Among
the ALD catalysts, the Al2O3 overlayer demonstrates the balanced Pt binding behavior, offering
an optimal environment for forming Pt nanoclusters compared to the ZrO2 and TiO2 overlayers.

Our experimental findings indicate that samples characterized by the presence of less coordinated
Pt0 were not prone to sintering, as previously anticipated. This could be explained by the
introduction of an increased resistance to Pt transport due to the addition of Al2O3 ALD overlayers
on Pt nanoparticle. Pt species could potentially diffuse through both the vapor phase and the solid
phase. As our experimental measurements highlighted the inhibition of Pt vapor-phase transport,
we also want to investigate the possibility of Pt solid-state diffusion. To elucidate this
phenomenon, we investigated the energetics of diffusion and anchoring of Pt atoms through the
ALD layers. We assessed metal binding energies using our recently developed coordination-based
α-scheme model on various sites. The predicted metal binding energies were found to be favorable
for the binding of Pt to the ALD overlayer in defective Al vacancy sites. The calculated binding
energies of Pt within Al defect sites within the most preferable intermediate, i.e., Al2(OH)6 and the
final compound Al2O3, were −5.29 and −7.89 eV, respectively, whereas Pt binding energy in Pt
bulk was −6.01 eV (Fig. 16). Interestingly, it is found that Pt will bind very strongly to Al
vacancies, with energetics that are either comparable (0.72 Δ eV for Al2O3 vs Pt) or much more
favorable [−1.88 ΔeV for Al2(OH)6 vs Pt] compared to the Pt bulk. Taken together, the results
showed that Pt atoms can favorably bind to the encapsulating overlayer, especially within defective
sites, indicating that Pt is likely to remain within the encapsulating oxide rather than escaping in
the vapor phase. Overall, this observation suggests a feasible pathway for Pt moieties to diffuse
and find a new anchoring site within the ALD layers, preventing its normal ripening behavior, and
thus inhibiting sintering. This diffusion process might also have contributed to the observed
formation of small nanoclusters following the aging of the LE ALD sample.

To explain the Pt diffusion hypothesis, we calculated diffusion barriers using the climbing-image
nudged elastic band method. In this context, we considered six images within the reactants and
products. We considered Al2(OH)2 vacancies within bulk Al2(OH)6 and the calculated diffusion
barriers for Pt migration within the Al2(OH)2 defect were 0.86 and 1.16 eV, which could be easily
overcome during the aging process (Fig. 17). It is noteworthy to mention that Pt migration likely
depends on defects in various ALD-based intermediates, temperature, and other reaction
conditions, making the study more complex. However, the thermodynamic calculations and
preliminary diffusion barriers of Pt within Al2(OH)2 vacancies in bulk Al2(OH)6 support our
hypothesis that Pt can diffuse through ALD layers and form small nanoclusters during the aging
process.

Microscopy characterization, in combination with theory, was also used to study the fundamental
stability of encapsulated catalysts. Using in situ atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron
microscopy (STEM) combined with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we revealed a
two-step sintering mechanism of Pt nanoparticles on amorphous alumina under realistic oxidizing
and humid conditions across 200-800 °C. At moderate temperatures (~500 °C), oxygen adsorption,
strongly amplified by water vapor, induces facet-dependent, “caterpillar-like” nanoparticle
migration driven by atomic-plane sliding and PtO2 desorption, leading to clustering without
coalescence (Fig. 18). Coalescence initiates only at elevated temperatures (~700 °C), following
surface de-passivation and crystallographic alignment with neighboring particles. First-principles
results show that migration is governed by oxygen adsorption and modulated by surface
hydroxylation. By examining the catalyst in vacuum, oxygen, water vapor, and combined O2-H2O
environments, we revealed that water vapor and oxygen act synergistically to accelerate sintering
through a previously unobserved mechanism involving the dissociation of platinum adatom chains
from nanoparticles. These adatoms migrate across the alumina surface, promoting atomic ripening
and particle coalescence. The presence of both gases eliminates the slower, stepwise material loss
seen under single-gas conditions, instead causing rapid disassociation and structural

The shape of Pt nanoparticle changes in the presence of O2 and H2O. While the initial shape
changes in Pt nanoparticles upon gas exposure can be attributed to variations in surface energy,
the transient nature of the observed morphologies points to more complex thermodynamic
processes. Oxygen and water vapor adsorption are known to modulate the surface energy of Pt,
promoting anisotropic geometries. However, the spontaneous retraction of nanoparticles into
isotropic shapes following migration suggests an additional mechanism at play. We hypothesize
that this transition is mediated by the desorption of volatile PtO2 species, a phenomenon previously
implicated in catalyst restructuring. Previous work in the literature has demonstrated that the
emission of material from catalytic nanoparticles can drive rapid changes in their morphology. We
rely upon a DFT study of the energy landscape of Pt nanoparticles exposed to oxygen and consider
the effect of PtO2 emission to determine if this could explain the particle migration. While other

 21

factors such as water vapor effects may also contribute to particle migration, we propose that the
combined effects of oxygen adsorption and the interaction between the nanoparticle and the oxide
support are sufficient to simulate the dynamic behavior of nanoparticles.

To test this, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on Pt nanoparticles
interacting with oxygen, focusing on the (111) facet, as suggested by experimental observations.
The calculated free energy changes (Ωdiff) for varying oxygen coverages on Pt(111) at temperatures
between 127 to 927 °C and linearly extended to 1000 °C. At 500 °C, a coverage of 1/3 monolayer
(ML) is most stable, with each O adsorption contributing –0.24 eV in energy (Fig. 19). This
stabilizes elongated morphologies by increasing available surface adsorption sites.

Fundamental understanding of deactivation of Rh catalysts
We used ALD techniques to prepare catalysts that could help us understand the factors that
influence deactivation in Rh catalysts supported on alumina via Rh aluminate formation. We
followed two distinct synthetic strategies to investigate the role of Rh mobility and separately the
role of Rh-alumina interface. In one case we support preformed Rh nanoparticles on either alumina
or zirconia and subsequently encapsulate the Rh nanoparticles via alumina or zirconia ALD to
limit mobility of Rh. Successful encapsulation after 50 ALD cycles is confirmed by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). We find in this case that reducing Rh mobility does not
improve Rh stability, as the encapsulated catalysts show worse performance compared to their
supported counterparts after accelerated aging. In a second set of samples, we first deposit
increasing amounts of alumina via ALD on a zirconia support. The modified supports that are
obtained share the same interface with the supported phase, which will always be the deposited
alumina, while differing only in amount of alumina deposited, which is confirmed via both XPS
and X-ray fluorescence (XRF). We subsequently deposit preformed Rh nanoparticles on these

 31

modified supports and perform accelerated aging tests and characterize the spent samples. We
conclude from this study that the amount of Rh-aluminate formed is correlated with the amount of
alumina deposited, indicating that the underlying mechanism for Rh-aluminate formation is not
interface limited. We posit that after reaction at the Rh-alumina interface, Rh can diffuse through
the alumina, freeing up the interface and allowing more Rh to react with alumina. We verify with
density functional theory (DFT) calculations that Rh can indeed find its way into a defective
alumina lattice, more favorably so if in its Rh (IV) state, which is experimentally detected via XPS.
Lastly, mitigating Rh-alumina interactions by modifying an alumina support via ZrO2 deposition
in trace amounts (< 1wt.%) can reduce Rh-aluminate formation and enhance catalyst activity after
aging by four times.
A particle size distribution analysis shows how the particles remain unchanged in size after
samples were encapsulated, maintaining an average size of about 4 nm, as depicted in Fig. 29a-d.
XPS analysis highlights relevant regions that confirm the successful encapsulation of the catalysts,
shown in Fig. 29e-p.

Thermal Analysis of Synthesis Components and Precursors

The synthesis of Pt@Al2O3 (POF) generates a complex mixture of species that are all calcined to
600 °C. To unravel the individual processes that could be occurring during the calcination to give
the final material, such as polymer oxidation and metal precursor decomposition, thermal
gravimetric analysis-differential scanning calorimetry (TGA-DSC) measurements were performed
and correlated with offline mass spectrometry (Fig. 36). Comparison of the individual components
and the mixtures shows that the major POF combustion events occur at lower temperatures than
the pure POF. Additionally, the large O2 consumption and dehydration that occur in Al(NO3)3
9H2O are not apparent in POF-Al(NO3)3 and Pt@POF-Al(NO3)3, indicating that the alumina
formed from the pure precursor is distinct from that formed in the nanocasting process.
Additionally, these two nanocasted samples show a high temperature mass loss that is correlated
with COx evolution from the sample.

Ceria incorporation in sinter-resistant platinum-based catalysts for emission control

We hypothesized that the nanocasting platform could generate a wide range of mixed metal-oxide
supports by tuning the precursors added during the synthesis. First, we synthesized Pt@POF
following the identical protocol we published previously. By infiltrating the Pt@POF with
mixtures of Al(NO3)3*9H2O and Ce(NO3)3*6H2O and subsequently calcining the dried product at
600°C for 5 h, we generated encapsulated Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 catalysts. X-ray diffraction showed the
alumina is amorphous and the ceria forms small crystallites (3.8nm by Sherrer equation). STEM-
EDS mapping showed that the Pt is encapsulated in the support and the ceria crystallites are
distributed throughout the alumina. While any ratio of Al2O3-CeO2 is possible, we utilized 3:1
Al2O3:CeO2 for this study.

 38

First, the stability of the Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 catalyst was evaluated by aging at 800°C for 4 h in a
mixture of 4% O2, 0.5% CO, 0.15% C3H6, 0.15% C3H8, and 4.2% H2O. A supported catalyst was
synthesized by depositing identical Pt nanoparticles on the surface a similarly prepared nanocasted
Al2O3-CeO2 support. Fig. 37 summarizes the TEM characterization of the aged supported and
encapsulated Pt- Al2O3-CeO2 after the aging treatment. The supported catalyst sintered
significantly, showing Pt particles >100 nm. The encapsulated (Pt@Al2O3-CeO2) catalyst
maintained smaller Pt particles (8.8 +/- 7.2 nm). Both catalysts maintained dispersed CeO2
throughout the support (Fig 37c and k). The CeO2 maintained small crystallites as determined by
TEM (Fig 37q) and XRD sherrer equation (5.8 nm).

CeO2 is known to enhance the activity of Pt for oxidation reactions via reverse oxygen spillover at
the Pt-CeO2 interface. This effect is known to be sensitive to both the interfacial area and the ceria

oxidation state. As such, we tested the activity of our Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 catalyst for CO oxidation
activity after various thermal treatments (Fig. 38). Ar and 4.2% H2O at 800C for 4h demonstrated
similar enhancement of CO oxidation activity. The optimized 4 h steam treatment was then tested
on the full set of catalysts (Pt@Al2O3, Pt@Al2O3-CeO2, Pt@CeO2, and Pt/Al2O3-CeO2) and
reaction mixture (CO (0.5%), propene (0.15%), propane (0.15%), oxygen (4%) and steam (4.2%)).
Ceria-containing encapsulated catalysts (Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 and Pt@CeO2) demonstrated enhanced
CO oxidation after steam treatment (Fig. 39a, d), with ΔT50s of -83 and -119°C, respectively.
Steam treatment had a more modest effect on CO oxidation activity of Pt@Al2O3 or Pt/Al2O3-
CeO2, with ΔT50 of -16 and -19°C, respectively. Steam treatment had an opposite effect on C3H8
combustion activity (Fig. 39c, f). T50 for ceria containing catalysts (Pt@ Al2O3-CeO2, Pt@CeO2,
and Pt/Al2O3-CeO2) increased (25, 70, and 38°C, respectively) and T50 for Pt@Al2O3 decreased
by -66°C. The propane combustion activity after steam treatment was similar across all catalysts,
indicating differing mechanisms for activating CO and propane in the ceria containing catalysts.

If the steam-treated catalyst was exposed to oxidative conditions at 800°C, the activity decreased
to nearly the activity of the fresh catalyst (Fig. 40a). This experiment, along with the similar
activation seen with 1 h steam and 1 h H2 treatments, supports the hypothesis that steam treatment
results in a change in ceria oxidation state. We performed temperature programmed reduction
experiments on catalysts at each stage of the experiment shown in Figure 13a (Fig. 40b). Each
TPR experiment was performed on a fresh bed of 100 mg of identical catalyst, and the steam
treatment or steam followed by oxygen treatment were performed in-situ immediately prior to the
TPR. The large decrease in hydrogen consumption between as-synthesized and 4h steam treated
catalyst indicates that the ceria was reduced during the steam treatment. Also, the shift of the low
temperature peak from 310 °C to 120 °C indicates that the surface of the ceria was more easily
oxidized after steam treatment, perhaps due to increased Pt-Ceria interfacial area. The appearance
of the small peak at 190 °C after steam followed by O2 treatment indicates that the surface of ceria
has been oxidized by the high temperature O2 treatment. This is correlated with a decrease in the
activity of the catalyst (evidenced by increased temperature for the light-off curve in panel a).
Cerium 3d X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of a steam treated (800°C, 4 h, 3% H2O/Ar)
and an aged sample (800°C, 4 h, 5% O2, 3% H2O, balance Ar) corroborated the TPR results, with
the steam treated sample resulting in 24.4% Ce3+ versus 21.5% Ce3+ in the aged sample.

To summarize the results of this part of the study, we demonstrated the synthesis of a nanocasted
Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 sample, in which 5nm platinum particles are encapsulated throughout a metal
oxide support consisting of amorphous alumina and evenly distributed crystallites of ceria. The
catalyst stabilized both platinum and ceria crystallites from sintering during aging at 800°C for 4
hours in oxidative, hydrothermal conditions, maintaining the porosity of the support as well as the
activity of the catalyst for simultaneous CO, C3H6 and C3H8 combustion reactions. The as-
synthesized Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 showed similar CO and C3H6 combustion activity as Pt@Al2O3, and
enhanced C3H8 combustion activity. The initially high C3H8 combustion activity is likely due to
the presence of acidic cerium sulfate on the catalyst that forms during the calcination step from
sulfur impurities in the POF. After a 4h 800°C steam treatment, the CO oxidation activity of
Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 catalyst improved over the Pt@Al2O3 while the initially high C3H8 combustion
activity of Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 decreased. Two main factors were considered for the hydrothermally
mediated enhancement of CO oxidation activity in Pt@Al2O3-CeO2: proximity of Pt and CeO2 and
oxidation state of CeO2. STEM-EDS analysis revealed that Pt and CeO2 are always in close
proximity in the nanocasted Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 catalysts. There is some segregation of CeO2 after
long thermal treatments and increased co-location of Pt and CeO2, although this alone does not
explain the trends in CO oxidation activity. TPR experiments and XPS analysis revealed that the
ceria surface is reduced slightly by the steam treatment, in comparison with steam and oxygen
treatments that did not improve CO oxidation activity. Taken together, it is likely the combination
of co-location of Pt and CeO2 and surface reduction of CeO2 enhance the CO oxidation activity of
Pt@Al2O3-CeO2 during steam treatment.

Expansion of the materials library

We built a library of bimetallic Pt-base metal@Al2O3 catalysts, including PtCu@Al2O3,
PtNi@Al2O3, PtCo@Al2O3. The bimetallic alloys were synthesized colloidally, using a seed
mediated approach from an identical batch of 5nm Pt particles. The base metal composition in
each catalyst is between 5-10%. The nanoparticles were then encapsulated in polymer using a
newly developed procedure. Pt-M@POP was synthesized by mixing the particles directly with
divinylbenzene and radical initiator in toluene solvent. The poly-divinylbenzene polymer
incorporates the nanoparticles directly into the matrix by cross-linking with the ligands.
Furthermore, the synthesis of POP avoids the use of sulfur (necessary in the POF synthesis which
uses DMSO solvent), enabling the utilization of base-metals that would otherwise deactivate in
the presence of sulfur. Currently, we have synthesized 3 catalysts, shown in Fig. 41, and the
particle size of the Pt and Pt-M alloys was maintained throughout the encapsulation and
calcination.

Our ongoing work involves completing the catalyst library with the synthesis of the PtCo@Al2O3,
performing a more thorough stability study to understand the high-temperature stability limits of
Pt-M@Al2O3 catalysts, and performing more detailed elemental characterization to confirm the
preservation of homogeneous Pt-M alloy particles during nanocasting and aging. We hypothesize
a trend in both C3H6 combustion activity and particle stability as a function of Pt-M composition,
with PtCu being the most active and stable, followed by PtNi, PtCo, and finally Pt, as predicted by
the d-band theory.

Synthesizing a catalyst library for understanding key variables for catalyst stability
To understand the sinter-resistance of Pt@Al2O3 catalysts and optimize the synthetic protocols,
we have designed a study to systematically probe the influence of three key variables: Pt particle
dispersion, Pt weight loading, and alumina porosity. We can leverage the versatility of the
nanocasting platform to prepare a library of catalysts that isolates the influence of each variable.

 44

Pt dispersion is a function of the Pt concentration in the polymer (Pt@POP), the weight loading is
a function of the Pt@POP concentration and the Pt@POP/Aluminum nitrate ratio, and the porosity
is a function of the Pt@POP/Aluminum nitrate ratio.

6 nanometer colloidally prepared platinum particles are synthesized with porous organic polymer
(POP) at various weight loading conditions: .1%, .5%, and 2% weight of platinum encapsulated in
POP support. Each of the Pt@POP intermediates are then infiltrated onto their final alumina
supports at various weight loading conditions: .1%, .5%, and 2% weight of platinum encapsulated
in alumina support, creating a total of 9 Pt@Al O samples (Fig. 43).
 2 3

Particle dispersion can be characterized via TEM imaging of particles in POP or alumina support
and weight loading can be confirmed via XRF measurements. Initial results show promising
dispersion of Pt@POP as a clearly increasing trend in dispersion (σ) with increased platinum
weight loading.

Support porosity will be determined via physisorption to understand how porosity of the final
Pt@Al O catalyst is changed by the Pt@POP precursors’ weight loadings.
 2 3

Catalyst stability will be resolved by testing catalysts for their resistivity to high temperature (800
℃) sintering. Post-aged particle sizes can be resolved via TEM imaging and XRD. DRIFTS will
assist in characterizing the metal surface for catalyst activity. Catalysts showing promising stability
can then be further probed using propene combustion to demonstrate catalyst activity.
Using this study, a more complete understanding of how catalyst weight loading affects catalytic
performance will be gained.

Novel ALD coatings: TiO2 ALD/Pt/ncAl2O3 and CeO2 ALD/Pt/ncAl2O3

In extension to the encapsulated system, we explored other metal oxides as potential candidates
for preventing Pt sintering and for improving catalytic activity. We chose titania for its precedence
in heterogenous catalysis and ceria for its known ability to act as an oxygen promoter for metal
catalysts. Titania can be easily grown on high surface materials such as catalysts using ALD while
ceria ALD on catalysts has not been well studied in literature. The titania ALD was done using a
previously published process and the ceria ALD process was developed using a novel heterolytic
cerium precursor. The starting Pt/ncAl2O3 catalyst is prepared using the same method mentioned
in section 1. The Pt/ncAl2O3 catalyst is then modified with TiO2 ALD at 200 ºC or CeO2 ALD at
250 ºC deposition temperature.

The surface composition of the ALD-modified Pt/ncAl2O3 catalysts was analyzed using XPS. As
shown by the atomic percentages obtained through high resolution scans, Ti content increases
while Pt and Al content decreases with increasing ALD layers (Fig. 46a). This is an indication that
TiO2 is depositing across the sample. The same trends are observed with the CeO2 catalysts (Fig.
46d). Comparing the Pt4d and Ti2p signals between the 120cyc-TiO2/Pt/ncAl2O3 sample and the
control Pt/ncAl2O3 (denoted as “0 cyc”), there is a clear drop in Pt4d signal and appearance of the
Ti2p signal with the ALD sample. The differences in the Ce3d peaks with increasing ALD cycle
number suggest that there are likely differences in Ce3+ and Ce4+ content between the catalysts.
Peak fitting is required to reveal these differences. Future work will include obtaining higher
quality spectra for fitting.

The Pt-Pt CN for the first shell (CN1) shows that Pt@Al2O3 and Pt/ncAl2O3 experience a small
degree of sintering, while the Pt/γAl2O3 shows a much higher degree of sintering, with the aged
Pt/γAl2O3 having a CN near that of bulk Pt (CN of 12 for fcc Pt). Additionally, by fitting the 3rd
coordination sphere (CN3) and taking the ratio of CN3:CN1, we get some information on the
particle shape. As seen in Table 1, the samples containing ncAl2O3 show only a small difference in
particle size and shape, while the Pt/γAl2O3 experiences a large change in both particle size and
shape.
These EXAFS results indicate that the ncAl2O3 stabilizes the Pt particle to a greater extent than
γAl2O3 does. However, we did not observe significant differences between the supported and
encapsulated samples, suggesting that we need additional analysis to understand these materials.
Difference XANES (ΔXANES) during the first light-off curve (LOC) were generated to gain
insight into more subtle surface behavior of Pt@Al2O3 and Pt/ncAl2O3 and are shown in Fig. 51.
For Pt/ncAl2O3, the white line position shifts to lower energy and the intensity increases steadily
over the course of the LOC. Pt@Al2O3 shows more variable behavior, with the white line position
and intensity oscillating throughout the LOC. These behaviors suggest that the surfaces of these
materials are interacting differently with the reaction gases. Determination of the exact interaction
requires further experiments.
