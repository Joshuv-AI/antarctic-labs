Modification of Fractured Rock Permeability Field Using
 Polymer Foam to Improve Geothermal System Efficiency
 Report for the duration before the project was moved from DRI to UNR in the last quarter of 2023

1. Project Description
The project aims at developing novel methods for blocking high-permeability pathways in
geothermal reservoirs to improve heat exchange efficiency. While the project was at DRI, and now
when it has been transferred to UNR, the goal of blocking high-permeability pathways is achieved
through targeted injection of heat-sensitive polymer foams, particularly foamed epoxy resins,
whose activation and curing are triggered by the ambient thermal field.
An interdisciplinary experimental and computational approach is being developed to understand
the rheology and transport behavior of polymer foams in simple fractures and networks. The
project explicitly accounts for thermal gradients and conditions needed for effective foam
mobilization and curing in target regions, with tailoring the foam properties for optimal
improvement in reservoir heat exchange efficiency.
Two-dimensional laboratory fracture models constructed from transparent glass and aluminum
plate are being used for direct visualization, testing of various polymer formulations, injection
strategies, and curing behaviors. Data from these experiments will support model development
and serve as a proof of concept at the network scale. The specific objectives are to:
a) Develop heat-resistant epoxy foams and characterize their rheology, curing dynamics, and
 foaming behavior.
b) Derive mathematical formulations for foam transport is simple parallel-plate fracture models.
c) Use numerical simulations to identify strategies for foam placement and curing in the fractures.
d) Experimentally study flow pathways, pressure distributions, and permeability evolution in 2D
 fracture models.
2. Project accomplishments

In the first year of the project (for which this report is being submitted) and in continuing work,
we are developing a method for selectively blocking high-permeability fractures by injecting
swarms of thermally activated resin droplets. These droplets are carried passively by the injected
carrier fluid and are designed to activate in situ (that is, foam, expand, and cure) once they reach a
prescribed target temperature. The activation process relies on the thermal release of CO2 from a
blocked resin hardener at around 125–150°C, triggering simultaneous foaming, volume expansion,
and epoxy crosslinking. This process results in rapid solidification of epoxy foam that can reduce
local fracture apertures and, consequently, fracture permeability.

The epoxy foam formulation is specifically engineered to ensure thermal stability during injection
and transport, followed by controlled expansion and curing at the desired activation temperature.
We are using commercially available epoxy resin (Epon 813, viscosity 0.8 Pa.s) combined with a
CO2-blocked diamine curing agent. Under ambient conditions, the curing agent remains inert, but

upon heating to the critical temperature (~125°C), CO2 release causes resin expansion that initiates
cross-linking, producing a robust epoxy foam.

Laboratory experiments were performed using heated 2D fracture models with fixed apertures
(0.75–1.5 mm). The system circulated silicone oil as the carrier fluid at a constant rate (~40 g/min),
while the fracture temperature was gradually increased to 150°C to trigger droplet activation. The
models, constructed from aluminum and heat-resistant glass, allowed direct visualization of the
process. Resin droplets were injected using a syringe pump to achieve droplet concentrations
below 1% of the total fluid volume. Experimental results showed that even with minimal resin
volume (<1%), the upstream pressure increased substantially causing a measurable reduction in
fracture permeability. Aperture reduction occurred preferentially along dominant flow paths,
confirming the method’s potential for targeted flow control and enhanced heat exchange efficiency
in geothermal systems. The outcomes were found to be highly sensitive to thermal gradients, foam
expansion characteristics, and droplet adhesion probabilities.

We developed and are continually refining a numerical model for resin droplet flow in fracture to
investigate the transport and their activation behavior. A stochastic framework describes droplet
transport and adhesion processes, accounting for local fracture geometry, droplet size, and
temperature. The model, based on a modified Stokes equation, simulates droplet advection,
thermal activation, stochastic sticking, and demonstrates how droplet expansion modifies local
apertures, affects upstream pressure, and alters overall permeability.

3. Project Impact
This project introduces a novel approach for mitigating undesirable high-permeability pathways
in Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) using heat-activated resin droplets that expand and
adhere at target locations defined by thermal conditions. The synthesized thermally triggered
epoxy resins can be tailored to achieve desired viscosities and activation temperatures, enabling
precise control on their transport within fractures. Preliminary laboratory experiments using
idealized fracture models confirmed the feasibility of this concept, demonstrating up to an order-
of-magnitude reduction in permeability under practical injection conditions.
