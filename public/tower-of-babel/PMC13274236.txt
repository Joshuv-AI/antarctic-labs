Barriers and facilitators to integrating non-communicable disease care within primary healthcare systems in low-resource countries: a systematic review
Authors: Melkamu Dugassa Kassa, Jeanne Martin Grace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a leading cause of mortality worldwide, disproportionately affecting low-resource countries. Integrating NCD care into primary healthcare (PHC) systems through a systems approach is critical for achieving universal health coverage. However, the barriers and facilitators to this integration in low-resource countries remain inadequately understood.ObjectivesThis systematic review explores the barriers and facilitators to integrating NCD care within PHC systems in low-resource countries, providing actionable insights to strengthen health systems and improve NCD care delivery.MethodsA systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines was conducted searching six databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, EBSCOhost, and Cochrane Library, complemented by grey literature searches. Studies published from 2016 onward that examined the integration of NCD care into PHC systems using a systems approach were included. Data were synthesized thematically, and the quality of studies was assessed using established appraisal tools.ResultsA total of 23 studies met the inclusion criteria. Key barriers included insufficient healthcare workforce capacity, inadequate infrastructure, limited access to essential medicines, and fragmented governance. Patient-related challenges, such as low awareness and cultural barriers, further impeded integration. Facilitators included task shifting to community health workers, strong policy frameworks, financial incentives, health information systems and community engagement.ConclusionsIntegrating NCD services into PHC systems in low-resource settings necessitates a comprehensive systems approach that addresses structural and contextual barriers while leveraging existing facilitators. Policy and programmatic efforts should focus on workforce training, infrastructure development, governance strengthening, and community-centered strategies. These findings provide a roadmap for advancing health system strengthening and achieving equitable NCD care delivery in low-resource settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13332-1.Keywords: Chronic diseases, Integration, Low-resource settings, Primary care, Systems approach

Introduction

The global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory conditions, and cancers, continues to rise, posing significant challenges to healthcare systems worldwide [1]. This is particularly critical in low-resource countries, where health systems are often overburdened and under-resourced [2, 3]. NCDs account for 41 million deaths annually, equivalent to 71% of all deaths globally, with a disproportionate impact on low- and middle-income countries [4].

Primary healthcare (PHC) systems are pivotal in managing NCDs by offering accessible, cost-effective, and patient-centred care [5]. Integrating NCD services into PHC systems aligns with the vision of achieving universal health coverage (UHC) and addressing the broader determinants of health [6]. A systems approach to integration involves leveraging health system components such as the workforce, governance, financing, and information systems toward comprehensive and continuous care delivery [7]. Despite the potential benefits, implementing integrated NCD care in low-resource settings remains a formidable challenge, necessitating a deeper understanding of the barriers and facilitators.

Integrating NCD services into PHC systems in low-resource countries is critical for addressing the rising burden of NCDs and ensuring equitable access to care [7, 8]. However, health systems in these settings often face significant challenges, including insufficient funding, inadequate infrastructure, shortages of trained healthcare personnel, and fragmented service delivery [9, 10]. These systemic gaps hinder the provision of continuous and coordinated care, exacerbating health disparities and undermining progress toward UHC [11].

Conversely, some facilitators can support integration, such as task shifting to community health workers, adopting digital health technologies, and strengthening governance structures [12–14]. However, these facilitators are often underutilized due to systemic barriers. Although there is growing advocacy for incorporating NCD care into primary healthcare systems, existing research points to a lack of understanding regarding the integration processes and how they can be effectively executed. For instance, Kane et al. [15] revealed unclear implementation processes in balancing different health system building blocks. Murphy et al. [16] stated recurrent obstacles like an insufficient workforce and inadequate supply chains without concrete solutions. Moreover, much of the current literature is still focused on specific diseases, frequently hypertension and diabetes, rather than on wider systemic approaches. Kruk et al. [17] have identified that the health systems in low-resource settings lack the versatility to deal with NCDs more effectively. A lack of comprehensive evidence on how to address these challenges and optimize enablers limits the ability of policymakers and health system planners to implement effective integration strategies. This systematic review addresses these gaps by synthesizing cross-cutting barriers and facilitators to several NCDs and contextual integration variations and mapping the outcomes within a health systems framework toward an operational model. The findings aim to inform policy and practice, contributing to more effective and sustainable health system designs.

Objectives

This review aims 1) to identify the key challenges that hinder the integration of non-communicable disease (NCD) services into public heathcare systems, 2) to highlight facilitators and enabling factors that support successful integration, and 3) to synthesize evidence in providing recommendations for policymakers, healthcare providers, and stakeholders to strengthen the integration of NCD services into primary healthcare.

Methods

Study design

A systematic review approach was followed in this qualitative study. The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and checklist to ensure systematic reporting, reproducible evidence synthesis, and transparency.

Search strategy

The review used a combination of search terms and Boolean operators to identify relevant studies on integrating non-communicable disease (NCD) services into primary healthcare (PHC) systems using systems approaches. Keywords used in the search were “non-communicable diseases” OR “ncds”, OR “non-communicable disease” or “ncd” AND “primary healthcare” OR “PHC”, OR “primary care” OR “public health care” OR “community care”, “Systems approach” OR “health systems”, “barriers” AND “facilitators”, AND low-resource countries, OR “low resourced settings”. These terms were adapted to the syntax of each database and combined using Boolean operators to maximize retrieval. The search strategy can be viewed in Supplementary File 1.

Databases searched

Six electronic databases were searched: PubMed, EBSCOHost, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library. Additionally, the review included searches in grey literature repositories such as WHO publications and government health policy documents to ensure comprehensiveness.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria encompassed studies examining the integration of non-communicable disease (NCD) care within primary healthcare (PHC) systems; research investigating barriers and facilitators to such integration; studies conducted in low-middle-income Countries (LMICs), articles adopting a systems approach or analyzing specific health system components; publications in English; peer-reviewed articles, qualitative studies, mixed-methods studies; and studies published from 2016 onward to ensure the inclusion of recent evidence. The exclusion criteria involved articles focusing exclusively on specific diseases without broader system integration insights, studies not related to PHC settings, studies conducted in high-income countries (HIC), non English language (Fench, and Spanish), editorials, opinion pieces, systematic reviews, scoping reviews, quantitative studies or commentaries without empirical data, and articles not available in the full-text were excluded.

Study selection

The study selection process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Titles, abstracts, and full-text articles were screened systematically against the predefined eligibility criteria to ensure the inclusion of relevant studies.

Data extraction

The two authors, MDK and JMG, independently screened the studies for eligibility, and then disagreements were resolved through meetings and discussions. Data extraction from the included studies was made separately, and cross-comparisons were performed to identify discrepancies. Different tools and software were applied to extract data to ensure a comprehensive and systematic approach to data extraction and analysis, each selected for its specific strengths in handling different types of data typically encountered in systematic reviews. Software such as NVivo (for qualitative synthesis), MAXQDA (to support the verification of coding consistency, enabling inter-coder reliability checks and comparison of coding schemes across the included studies), and Microsoft Excel (for managing structured data, including study characteristics, methodological quality assessments, frequency counts, and tabulated summary findings) was employed. Data was extracted step by step by detecting data source databases and articles to be included and setting criteria for selecting data for extraction, including relevance, themes, and keywords. Then, qualitative data was collected based on themes, patterns, demographics, and study settings.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using appropriate tools based on the study design. The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was employed for qualitative studies [15–17]. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) appraisal tools (10 items) were used for qualitative studies [18]. For mixed-methods studies, the JBI 10-item mixed-methods appraisal Tool (MMAT) was utilised [19–22]. The two reviewers (JMG and MDK) independently assessed the quality of the studies, with disagreements resolved through discussion and involving a third reviewer. A total of 23 articles were assessed for methodological quality using a 10-point score tool developed by JBI for qualitative and mixed methods studies. The outcome of the quality appraisal ranged from moderate to high methodological quality in which six studies [23–28] scored 10 points, eleven studies [29–39] scored 9 points. The remaining six studies [40–45] scored 8 points (Supplementary File 2).

Data analysis and synthesis

The extracted data sets were analyzed employing thematic analysis and coding frameworks. The extracted data was validated, and quality was controlled by double-checking the extracted data by both reviewers, inter-coder reliability assessments for thematic coding, and cross-referencing with original data sources for validation. An inductive framework and the WHO Building Blocks were used for the thematic analysis.

Results

Search results

Figure 1 illustrates the search process and how the studies were included in the review using the PRISMA flowchart guidelines. An initial search of databases brought 744 records. After removing duplicates and ineligible records, 205 records were screened for inclusion. Consequently, 182 records were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Among the 13 studies excluded for language reasons, the most common non-English language was French, which accounted for 8 of the excluded studies, and the remaining five were Spanish. Twenty-three (23) studies were included in the final review because they primarily identified barriers and facilitators to integrating NCD prevention into the primary healthcare system in low-resource settings. Of the 23 articles meeting the inclusion criteria (n = 16) studies were qualitative, and the remaining (n = 7) were mixed-method studies (Fig. 1).

Description of included studies

The included 23 studies were analyzed for their setting, objectives, study design, number of participants, and key findings related to barriers and facilitators of integrating non-communicable disease (NCD) care into primary healthcare (PHC) systems in low-resource countries. The 23 studies included in this review involves a diverse set of low- and middle-income countries across Africa (Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, Tanzania Uganda,), South and Southeast Asia (Bangladesh, Cambodia, Nepal, Malaysia, Pakistan, Sri Lanka), and Latin America (Mexico). The NCDs explored most frequently were hypertension and type 2 diabetes, followed by cardiovascular diseases, depression, and breast cancer, with several studies also exploring co-morbid NCD-HIV integration. Most studies focused on integrating NCD prevention, screening, and management within first-line PHC settings, such as community health posts, rural health centers, and outpatient clinics. A few studies extended the analysis to include district-level coordination and referral pathways to higher-level facilities, highlighting the continuum of care. The studies captured a range of perspectives ‒ from patients and caregivers to healthcare providers, managers, and policymakers ‒ providing a comprehensive view of integration efforts across different health system levels. To enhance the analytical structure of our findings, we adopted the WHO Health Systems Framework, which categorizes health system components into six building blocks: 1) service delivery, 2) health workforce, 3) health information systems, 4) access to essential medicines, 5) financing, and 6) leadership/governance. Barriers and enablers identified in the reviewed studies were mapped into these categories, allowing for a structured synthesis and more straightforward interpretation of patterns across the literature (Supplementary File 3).

Thematic synthesis of barriers and facilitators of included studies

Barriers

From the 23 studies reviewed, the most frequently reported barriers to integrating non-communicable disease (NCD) care within primary healthcare (PHC) systems included resource constraints, human resource limitations, fragmented services, and cultural and societal barriers.

Resource constraints

The lack of essential medicines, diagnostic tools, and trained personnel emerged as the most common barrier, reported by 19 out of 23 studies (83%). This was particularly prevalent in low-resource settings across Sub-Saharan Africa (e.g., Malawi, Ethiopia, Tanzania) and South East Asia (e.g., Bangladesh, Nepal), where financial constraints and supply chain inefficiencies hindered service delivery.

Human resource limitations

A shortage of trained healthcare workers and high turnover were mentioned in 15 studies (65%), impacting service quality and consistency. This barrier was especially prominent in remote or rural areas where access to specialized training and support is limited, notably in Ghana, Kenya, and Cambodia.

Fragmented services

The lack of integration across services, with vertical health programs and poorly coordinated referral systems, was noted in 13 studies (57%), especially in countries where HIV-NCD integration was attempted (e.g., Uganda, Malawi, Ethiopia). This fragmentation led to inefficiencies, delays, and gaps in care.

Cultural and societal barriers

Cultural beliefs, gender norms, and stigma were identified in 7 studies (30%) as significant barriers, particularly when integrating hypertension and diabetes care. Countries like Mexico, Nepal, and Pakistan highlighted that misconceptions about illness and treatment adherence, particularly in rural and marginalized communities, affected both healthcare-seeking behavior and care continuity.

The prominence of certain barriers and facilitators appeared to be influenced by the type of NCD being integrated. For example, studies addressing hypertension and diabetes (the most common NCDs in 16 studies) frequently emphasized issues of medication stockouts and diagnostic equipment shortages. In contrast, studies focusing on mental health and depression (e.g., Malawi, Kenya) more often reported limited provider training and a lack of dedicated space for counselling. Similarly, studies on integrated NCD-HIV care (e.g., Uganda, Ethiopia, Malawi) highlighted unique challenges around vertical programming, data integration, and stigma but also noted facilitators like one-stop service models and compatibility with existing HIV infrastructure.

In addition to disease-specific factors, the country context (e.g., donor involvement, policy maturity, or decentralized governance structures) and health system maturity (e.g., availability of community health platforms or digital infrastructure) appeared to shape challenges and opportunities for integration. Facilitators

The most commonly identified facilitators for successful NCD integration were task shifting and community health workers (CHWs), supportive leadership and policy framework, training and capacity building, and community engagement.

Task shifting and community health workers (CHWs)

Task shifting, including delegation of responsibilities to community health workers, was highlighted as a major facilitator in 12 studies (52%), particularly in low-resource settings like Ghana, Kenya, Bangladesh, and Malawi. This approach helped mitigate human resource shortages and improve service delivery in communities with limited formal healthcare infrastructure.

Supportive leadership and policy frameworks

Supportive leadership and the presence of a national policy framework for NCD integration were noted in 11 studies (48%), with countries such as Ghana, Uganda, and Nepal benefiting from strong governance that promoted NCD initiatives and ensured funding, planning, and cross-sector collaboration.

Training and capacity Building

The training of healthcare providers and capacity-building initiatives were central to overcoming barriers related to staff knowledge gaps. Ten studies (43%) emphasized the importance of ongoing education and specialized training in improving quality care and provider confidence, with Kenya and Cambodia being strong examples of successful training interventions.

Community engagement

Community support and engagement were identified as key facilitators in 9 studies (39%), particularly where community-led health programs were implemented. Countries like Uganda and Nigeria successfully leveraged community structures, such as village health committees and local leaders, to drive service uptake and reduce stigma.

Comparison of regional trends

A comparative analysis of these barriers and facilitators across geographical regions reveals several important trends.

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)

Resource constraints and staff shortages were the most commonly cited barriers in the SSA region, reflecting fragile health systems with insufficient funding and infrastructure. The presence of supportive leadership and community health worker models (e.g., Ghana, Kenya, Malawi) appeared to mitigate these challenges, offering promising strategies for sustainable NCD care.

South and Southeast Asia

In countries like Nepal, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, cultural beliefs and gender norms were more pronounced barriers, influencing health-seeking behaviors and adherence to treatment. In Mexico and Pakistan, these barriers were partially addressed through community engagement and educational campaigns designed to overcome misconceptions around NCDs.

Latin America

In Mexico, health system fragmentation and social stigma emerged as prominent barriers, but strong policy frameworks and community-based support systems offset these. Integrating services, particularly breast cancer screening and early diagnosis, highlighted how regional health networks could overcome geographical and societal barriers.

Transferability of findings across diverse Sub-Saharan African contexts

Across Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the integration of non-communicable disease (NCD) services into primary health care (PHC) is shaped by a complex interplay of governance, economic, and sociocultural factors. Governance-related barriers to integrating NCD services into PHC are consistently observed across SSA countries, underscoring systemic challenges such as fragmented leadership and weak coordination. However, the facilitators, such as strong policy commitment and leadership engagement, tend to be highly context-specific. Countries with robust decentralization frameworks and vibrant civil society engagement, such as Ghana and Kenya, demonstrate a greater capacity to adopt and adapt successful integration strategies from other settings.

In contrast, economic barriers and facilitators show higher transferability across the region. The widespread nature of resource limitations, including shortages of medicines, equipment, and trained personnel, means that strategies like task shifting and decentralized service delivery are broadly applicable. Still, effective implementation depends on the specific financing structure and readiness of each country’s PHC system, which may require tailored adaptations.

Sociocultural factors, by their nature, are more variable and context-dependent. Differences in beliefs, stigma, health literacy, and community norms across and within countries can significantly influence health-seeking behaviour and service uptake. As a result, direct transfer of sociocultural interventions is limited. Nevertheless, certain principles—such as fostering community engagement, working through trusted cultural structures, and involving local actors—can be successfully applied across settings, provided they are adapted to the local cultural context.

Bias and reflexivity

In conducting this systematic review, we acknowledge that our perspectives and potential biases may influence various aspects of the analysis, including the selection, interpretation, and synthesis of evidence. These biases may stem from our academic backgrounds, professional experiences, and pre-existing topic knowledge. While we have taken steps to minimize bias through rigorous methodology, such as predefined inclusion criteria and independent review processes, complete objectivity is challenging. Recognizing this, we have made efforts to critically evaluate our assumptions and transparently report our methods to ensure the credibility and reproducibility of our findings.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence on the barriers and facilitators to integrating non-communicable disease (NCD) care within primary healthcare (PHC) systems in low-resource settings, drawing on studies from multiple low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The findings reveal a complex interplay of health system, workforce, community, and policy factors that influence the success of integration efforts.

The principal findings of this systematic review are that we identified four barriers to integrating NCD care into the PHC sector in low-resource countries, including 1) resource constraints, 2) sociocultural aspects, 3) health system shortcomings and 4) coordination and policy gaps. Despite these challenges, we identified four facilitators demonstrating the potential for successfully integrating NCD care into the PHC sector: 1) capacity development, 2) task shifting and community involvement, 3) integrated service delivery models, and 4) policy and leadership support. Concerning the identified barriers, in support of our results, resource constraints like lack of funding in eastern Uganda [46] and competing health priorities in Kenya [47] exacerbate these challenges in integrating NCD care into the PHC sector in many low-resource settings, leaving PHC systems ill-equipped to handle the growing burden of NCDs. Sociocultural barriers like preference for traditional or religious healers and low awareness of improving integrated healthcare for NCDs in Ethiopia were identified as barriers, supporting our findings [48]. Considering that these sociocultural factors shape health-seeking behaviour and hinder timely access to formal healthcare services, it is concerning. Health system shortcomings constitute a significant worry, considering that they undermine the effectiveness, equity, and sustainability of healthcare provision, corroborated by Tesema et al., 2021 [48], who showed that monitoring patients’ conditions is unreliable due to weak healthcare systems. Furthermore, in a scoping review, researchers substantiated our findings by identifying a lack of political will and the absence of relevant national health policies as barriers to strengthening healthcare systems [49]. In addition to the identified four barriers, researchers emphasized that these issues are compounded by weak governance structures and fragmented service delivery, which hinder coordinated and continuous care [50–52].

Regarding facilitators, researchers corroborated our findings where communities were mobilized by capacitating them to engage with individuals utilizing the services offered also by becoming partners in spreading the message about NCD prevention and control, aligning with the concept of Communities of Practice (CoPs), where empowered community members collaboratively engage, learn, and take collective action to promote NCD prevention and control as active partners in health promotion [53]. Task shifting is a skilful facilitator as it addresses human resource shortages and improves access to care. In this regard, researchers supported our results by highlighting that task shifting, particularly to community health workers, has successfully addressed workforce shortages in LMICs while maintaining service quality [54]. Also confirming our findings in terms of integrated service delivery models, researchers developed a programme based on the synergy between primary care-led NCD management, palliative care and community health, which provided a promising model for integrated NCD prevention and control in a low-income context [53]. This is encouraging considering that it enhances efficiency, accessibility, and continuity of care by coordinating services across different levels and sectors of the health system. Strong policy frameworks and leadership were repeatedly stressed as critical enablers in, for example, Nepal, in a rapid review of qualitative evidence on implementation considerations for non-communicable disease-related integration in primary health care [55, 56].

Influence of context on findings

The findings across the 23 studies demonstrate that context plays a pivotal role in shaping both the nature of barriers to NCD integration and the effectiveness of facilitators. For example, resource-constrained settings like Malawi, Ethiopia, and Bangladesh repeatedly cited shortages of trained personnel, medicines, and diagnostic equipment, which were compounded by poor infrastructure and fragile supply chains. In contrast, countries like Mexico and Malaysia highlighted sociocultural barriers such as gender norms, stigma, and mistrust of health providers, underscoring how societal context influences care-seeking behaviors and the success of health interventions.

Settings with a stronger policy environment and donor engagement, such as Ghana or Uganda, were more likely to report facilitators like supportive leadership, integration frameworks, and external technical assistance, which in turn shaped the implementation and outcomes of PHC-based NCD care. These contextual influences suggest that integration strategies must be tailored, not one-size-fits-all.

Effects of barriers on primary health care

The barriers identified have significant implications for primary healthcare’s functioning, quality, and equity. Common challenges such as inadequate human resources, lack of diagnostic tools, and poor referral linkages led to fragmented services, limited continuity of care, and delayed diagnosis and treatment. This increases the burden on patients, particularly in underserved areas, and undermines public trust in PHC systems, leading some patients to bypass PHC entirely or disengage from care.

Additionally, interprofessional role conflict (e.g., in Nigeria) and vertical programming (e.g., HIV-NCD integration in Malawi and Uganda) were found to compromise coordination and service delivery, creating bottlenecks and inefficiencies that further stress already fragile PHC systems.

How barriers have been addressed

Several studies reported adaptive solutions that attempted to address these barriers. For instance, task shifting and using community health workers (CHWs) were widely adopted (e.g., Ghana, Kenya, and Bangladesh) to compensate for human resource shortages. Training programs and continuous medical education helped bridge competency gaps (e.g., Kenya, Cambodia). Sometimes, donor-supported integration pilots (e.g., ComHIP in Ghana, PEN in Nepal) supplied resources and helped standardized protocols. Where possible, leveraging existing HIV infrastructure provided a platform for NCD service integration (e.g., Uganda, Ethiopia). However, these strategies were often project-based or unsustainably funded, raising concerns about long-term viability.

What has worked?

Successful interventions tended to share a few common features: 1) Multisectoral engagement: Community leaders, civil society, and local government support (e.g., Nigeria, Uganda) helped reinforce acceptability and alignment with local norms. 2) Policy alignment and leadership: Studies that reported supportive governance (e.g., Nepal, Sri Lanka) saw smoother implementation and stronger service uptake. 3) Integrated training and protocols: Structured implementation tools and transparent task allocation improved service delivery where they were adopted (e.g., Ghana’s ComHIP, Kenya’s NCD clinics).

Leveraging facilitators through engagement

Several strategies emerged to build on the existing facilitators: Scale up task shifting models with appropriate training and supervision, particularly for CHWs. Embed NCD integration into national health strategies with sustained domestic and donor financing. Invest in digital health and interoperable systems to strengthen referrals, health records, and patient follow-up, especially in resource-limited settings. Community engagement should be seen not only as a delivery mechanism but as a co-design partner to ensure culturally appropriate and acceptable models of care. Decentralization and supportive supervision could empower frontline providers to effectively deliver integrated care.

Limitations of the review

This review identifies important barriers and facilitators to integrating NCD care in PHC in LMICs but is subject to several limitations. Extensive diversity remained in study design, quality and context, which limits generalizability and the ability to meta-analyze. As a result, the synthesis remains descriptive and thematic, potentially overlooking the nuanced characteristics of specific local contexts. Language and publication bias excluded non-English and grey literature, which might have resulted in missing relevant data from underrepresented regions (particularly Francophone, Lusophone and conflict-affected countries) where valuable insights into NCD integration may have been missed. Further, discrepancies in reporting how an intervention is implemented and what impact outcomes were measured hinder efforts to assess effectiveness across settings and compare findings or draw conclusions about the most effective approaches. The preponderance of studies from countries such as Uganda, Kenya and South Africa could have distorted the findings; the overrepresentation of their experiences may have overshadowed those from less-studied settings, limiting the diversity of perspectives captured in the review. These restrictions indicate that caution is necessary regarding generalizability of findings, particularly their applicability to other LMIC contexts. Future studies should be context-specific, current in their analysis, and include varied linguistic sources and grey literature to better understand NCD integration in LMICs.

Conclusion

Integrating non-communicable disease (NCD) services into primary healthcare (PHC) systems is a critical strategy for addressing the escalating global burden of NCDs, particularly in low-resource countries. This systematic review highlighted the multifaceted barriers and facilitators that influence the success of integration efforts. While many of the barriers and facilitators identified align with existing knowledge, this review contributes added value by synthesizing findings across diverse geographic and health system contexts, thereby revealing context-specific nuances—such as the critical role of community health workers in rural integration efforts, or the influence of donor-driven vertical programs on primary care readiness. This review also strategically chose 2016 as the start date to capture the evolution of NCD integration within PHC in the wake of significant policy shifts and health system developments. This ensures that the review provides timely, policy-relevant insights for stakeholders working to strengthen primary healthcare systems in low-resource settings. Moreover, by mapping findings guided by the WHO Health Systems Framework, this review identifies key leverage points within each system component that can guide tailored policy interventions. These insights can inform implementation strategies sensitive to low-resource settings’ infrastructural and human resource constraints, moving beyond generic recommendations to more targeted, system-aware approaches. These findings contribute to strengthening the health system and achieving the broader goals of universal health coverage and equitable health outcomes in low-resource settings.
