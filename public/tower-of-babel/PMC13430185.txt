The Vero cell-adapted infectious bronchitis virus strain rHV80 exhibits high immunogenicity and broad tissue tropism
Authors: Yi Jiang, Xiumei Zhao, Mei Xue, Yan Yu, Di Zhang, Xu Cheng, Mingyan Gao, Xiang Chen, Sheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) is a coronavirus that naturally infects chickens and causes damage to multiple tissues and organs, including the respiratory tract. Vaccination remains the primary method for preventing and controlling infectious bronchitis (IB). Progress toward cell-based, attenuated live vaccines has been hindered by IBV’s poor replication in vitro. In previous work, we utilized the rH120 strain as the parental strain and domesticated it to derive the IBV strain HV80, which replicates efficiently in Vero cells. Using reverse genetics, we then constructed its infectious clone, rHV80. To assess its potential as a cell-adapted attenuated live vaccine, we evaluated the immunogenicity of the rHV80 strain in 1-day-old SPF chicks. We found that the strain elicits a robust immune response after immunization or infection. However, serial passage broadened the strain’s tissue tropism. Compared with the parental strain, the rHV80 strain infects additional tissues, including the pectoralis, heart, and testis. It produces brain lesions that lead to neurological signs and late-stage mortality in chicks, compromising its safety as a live attenuated vaccine. This study shows that using in vitro cell–adapted strains of IBV as seed material for live attenuated vaccines can pose safety risks. Meanwhile, the results offer a theoretical foundation for the rational development of live attenuated IBV vaccines.

Introduction

The avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) belongs to the genus gamma-coronavirus within the family Coronaviridae. IBV primarily infects poultry and has currently evolved into various pathogenic types. These types can lead to infections and lesions in multiple tissues and organs, including the respiratory tract, proventriculus, muscles, kidneys, bursa of Fabricius, and reproductive system (Cosgrove, 1962; Gough et al., 2008; Liu and Kong, 2004; Naqi et al., 2003; Toro et al., 1996; Van Roekel et al., 1951). The outbreak and prevalence of avian infectious bronchitis (IB) have resulted in significant economic losses within the poultry industry.

Control of IBV depends primarily on vaccination. Common vaccine types include live attenuated vaccines, inactivated vaccines, vector vaccines, and novel adjuvants or delivery systems. Live attenuated vaccines are produced by artificially reducing viral pathogenicity while preserving replication competence, which elicits a host immune response that resembles natural infection. Compared with inactivated or subunit vaccines, live attenuated coronavirus vaccines offer advantages in immunogenicity, breadth of protection, and routes of administration. Widely used live attenuated IB vaccines include H120, 4/91, D274, and QXL87 (Sjaak de Wit et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2021). Attenuation is traditionally achieved by serial passage in systems such as chicken embryos or chicken embryo kidney (CEK) cells, which lowers pathogenicity in target organs like the kidneys and oviducts. Notably, the QX-like strain SZ130 was attenuated after 130 consecutive passages in embryos and did not produce significant disease in safety tests (Yan et al., 2018); Sczy3 was passaged 100 times in CEK cells to obtain SczyC100, which showed good safety and protective efficacy (Xia et al., 2018).

Embryo-derived vaccine production depends on a steady supply of SPF chicken embryos, which is constrained by egg availability and vulnerable to livestock epidemics such as avian influenza. The embryo-based process is also labor-intensive and time-consuming (Rajaram et al., 2020). By contrast, cell-based vaccines offer greater efficiency and scalability. Large-scale cell culture in bioreactors enables automated production, shortens the manufacturing cycle, meets demands for mass vaccination, and allows rapid capacity expansion during sudden pandemics (Whitford et al., 2022). From a safety perspective, cell-based systems reduce the risk of exogenous contamination through rigorous screening and testing of cell banks, thereby avoiding the potential introduction of avian viruses present in chicken embryos. Vero cells are among the most widely used substrates for vaccine manufacture; they lack certain interferon- and chemokine-related functions, which increases their susceptibility to viral infection, and they can be adapted to serum-free culture (Emeny and Morgan, 1979; Merten et al., 1994). Nevertheless, IBV exhibits strict cell tropism: aside from the Beaudette strain, IBV strains generally do not replicate effectively in Vero cells, and the Beaudette strain has lost immunogenicity after extensive passage (Geilhausen et al., 1973). Previously, we generated a cell-adapted IBV strain, HV80, derived from the attenuated vaccine strain H120, which can efficiently infect Vero cells (Jiang et al., 2023). We also constructed an infectious clone of HV80, designated rHV80 strain, and investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the adaptation of the virus to Vero cells (Jiang et al., 2024). In this study, we monitored neutralizing antibody levels in chicks after immunization with the rHV80 strain, demonstrating that immunogenicity did not change significantly.

However, long-term passage can accumulate numerous genetic variations. The SARS-CoV-2 VAS5 vaccine strain was derived by continuous passage and screening in Vero cells. In this strain, a seven–amino-acid deletion at the S1/S2 cleavage site of the S protein markedly reduced viral replication in human cells and in mice, while still permitting efficient proliferation in Vero cells (Li et al., 2022). Continuous passage of the IBV SZ strain to the 250th generation produced 23 genomic mutations and a concomitant decline in tissue infectivity (Yan et al., 2020). However, when the S protein mutates during attenuated vaccine development, the vaccine strain may gain the capacity to infect non-target cells, such as neurons and cardiomyocytes, posing serious safety risks for vaccines and for the livestock industry. In vitro studies, we found that the cell tropism of HV80 was expanded, as it could not only infect Vero cells but also replicate effectively in fibroblast cell lines such as DF-1 and BHK-21(Jiang et al., 2023). To further assess whether the tissue tropism of the virus in its natural host, chickens, had been altered, we infected 1-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chicks with the rHV80 strain and conducted a series of experiments to evaluate its pathogenicity in this natural host.

Materials and methods

Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicken embryos were obtained from Beijing Merial Vital Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and hatched in the Lab of Chicken Infectious Disease Protection and Control, Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Science (Yangzhou, China). One-day-old SPF chickens were incubated at 37°C with 55–65% relative humidity. The chickens were housed in isolators with ad libitum access to food and water under negative pressure conditions.

Viruses and cells

Vaccine H120 strain is a widely used vaccine strain in the market at present, which is a 120-generation strain of the Holland strain isolated in 1956. The rH120 strain is an infectious clone strain of the H120 strain constructed in the laboratory (Zhou et al., 2010). The HV80 strain is a Vero cell-adapted variant obtained through serial passage and domestication of the rH120 strain in our laboratory (Jiang et al., 2023). Correspondingly, the rHV80 strain represents an infectious clone of the HV80 strain (Jiang et al., 2024). Vero cells utilized in this study were procured from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Immunogenicity assay

Immunization.Virus preparations from different culture substrates were diluted in PBS, including rH120-allantoic fluid virus (104EID50), rH120-CK cell virus (104TCID50), rHV80-allantoic fluid virus (104EID50), rHV80-CK cell virus (104TCID50), and rHV80–Vero cell virus (104TCID50). One-day-old SPF chicks were inoculated by intranasal and ocular routes, with 8 chicks per group. A PBS-immunized group served as the negative control. Blood (1 mL per chick) was drawn from the jugular vein at 14, 21, and 28 days post-vaccination (dpv). After a 30 min incubation at 37°C, samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C for subsequent assessment of how culture substrate influenced rHV80-induced humoral neutralizing antibodies.

Tenfold serial dilutions of rHV80-containing cell supernatant were prepared in PBS to yield viral doses of rHV80-103TCID50/chick, rHV80-104TCID50/chick, rHV80-105TCID50/chick, and rHV80-106TCID50/chick. The rH120 strain allantoic fluid virus immunized group was used as the positive control, with an immunization dose of 104 EID50/chick and 8 chickens per group. A PBS-immunized cohort served as the negative control. Blood (1 mL per chick) was drawn from the jugular vein at 14, 21, and 28 dpv, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The serum supernatants were stored at −20°C for subsequent assessment of humoral neutralizing antibody responses elicited by the different rHV80 strain doses.

ELISA.An indirect ELISA was performed on the sera as described above to determine the neutralizing antibody levels in the blood of chicks from different immunized groups. The indirect ELISA adopted the test method previously established independently in our laboratory (Jiang et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2022). The coating antigen was the virus supernatant collected and purified in rHV80-infected Vero cells. Individual chicken sera collected from different groups, diluted to 1:200, were loaded onto plates and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Use Anti-Chicken IgY (IgG) (whole molecule)-Peroxidase antibody produced in rabbit (Sigma Aldrich, Riedel-de Haen, Germany) diluted at 1:10,000 as the secondary antibody. After incubating with 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Beytime, Shanghai, China) for 15 min and terminating with 2 mol/L H2SO4 solution, the absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Model 680 microplate reader (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Microneutralization (MN) assays.The micro-neutralization test was performed on the serum samples collected as described above. This test method was also independently established by our laboratory in the early stage (Zhou et al., 2022). Different serum samples were added to 96-well cell plates, with 4 replicate wells for each sample. DMEM high-glucose medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) was used as the diluent for a 1:2 serial dilution. After dilution, an equal volume of rHV80 virus solution at a concentration of 200 TCID50/0.1 mL/well was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated in a 37°C 5% CO2 incubator for 1 h. Then, the digested and neutralized Vero cell suspension was added at 50 μL/well, mixed well, and cultured in a 37°C 5% CO2 incubator. The cytopathic effect was observed at 72 h, and the serum neutralization titer was calculated.

Pathogenicity tests

Animal experimental design.One-day-old SPF chicks were inoculated intranasally and ocularly with rH120 or rHV80 strains at a dose of 105 EID50/chick. The rHV80 strain preparation was a cell-supernatant virus given at 105.25 TCID50/chick, which corresponds to 105 EID50 after conversion. PBS-inoculated chicks served as the negative control. Ten chickens were included in each group, and clinical signs were continuously monitored within 28 days after virus inoculation. Sneezing was quantified for each group at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 days post-infection (dpi) by performing three 2-min counts per group per day.

At 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 dpi, four chicks per group were anesthetized and their tracheal tissues collected for the tracheal ring cilia motility inhibition assay. At 3, 5, and 7 dpi, 0.5 mL of blood was drawn from each chick. After anesthesia, five chicks per group per day were euthanized by exsanguination from the neck, and the trachea, thymus, pectoralis, lungs, heart, proventriculus, liver, spleen, pancreas, testes/ovaries, kidneys, bursa of Fabricius, and brain were dissected and collected. Each tissue was divided into two portions: one for in vivo viral RNA quantification and the other for histological sectioning.

Clinical lesion and inhibition of ciliary activity tracheal.At 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 dpi, five chicks per group were anesthetized and euthanized by jugular exsanguination. The entire trachea was excised, with connective tissue removed, and tracheal rings were collected by location (upper: 3; middle: 4; lower: 3). Rings were placed in 96-well plates with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and ciliary motility was assessed by light microscopy. Virus-induced inhibition of ciliary activity was scored according to the European Pharmacopoeia (Council of Europe, 2021): 4 = 100% inhibition, 3 = 75% inhibition, 2 = 50% inhibition, 1 = 25% inhibition, and 0 = 0% inhibition.

Viral loads for tissue distribution.At 3, 5, and 7 dpi, five chicks per group per time point were selected (two chicks in the control group), and 0.5 ml of blood was collected from each chick. The chicks were then anesthetized and euthanized by jugular exsanguination. Thymus, trachea, lungs, liver, spleen, heart, proventriculus, pectoralis, pancreas, kidneys, testes/ovaries, bursa of Fabricius, and brain were harvested and placed in 1 mL of PBS. After vertexing and homogenization, samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. Frozen tissue samples were thawed and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and 200 μL of the supernatant was applied to the loading well of the Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit 2.0 (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). RNA was extracted with a fully automated nucleic acid extraction instrument (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). After reverse transcription, qPCR was performed to quantify viral RNA copy numbers in different tissues.

Histopathology analysis and Immunohistochemistry.The trachea, lungs, pectoralis, heart, pancreas, testes, bursa of Fabricius, and brain tissue from sacrificed chicks collected at the time points described above were placed in formalin for preservation. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in paraffin to prepare tissue sections. After HE staining, the pathological changes of different tissues were observed under a microscope.

Perform immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining on tissue paraffin sections. Use the laboratory-prepared IBV hyperimmune serum diluted at 1:100 or Monoclonal mouse anti-infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) diluted at 1:2000 as the primary antibody (Hytest, Turku, Finland), and Anti-Chicken IgY (IgG) (whole molecule)-Peroxidase antibody produced in rabbit diluted at 1:10,000 (Sigma Aldrich, Riedel-de Haen, Germany) or Anti-Mouse IgG (whole molecule)–Peroxidase antibody produced in rabbit diluted at 1:80,000 (Sigma Aldrich, Riedel-de Haen, Germany) as the secondary antibody. After staining, take photos and read the slides under a microscope.

RT-qPCR.The viral RNA was quantified by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). RNA from tissue culture supernatant collected at different time points was extracted using the TRIzol method, with 200 μL per sample. Reverse transcription was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time) (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). The viral copies were measured by the absolute quantitative method of Real-time PCR, which was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) on an ABI StepOneTM PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR product from the conserved region of the IBV genome was used as the standard, and a quantitative standard curve was plotted after serial dilution. The forward primer for qPCR was DL2S: 5′-CCGTTGCTTGGGCTACCTAGT-3′, and the reverse primer was DL2A: 5′-CGCCTACCGCTAGATGAACC-3′.

Viral Loads.At 7, 14, 18, 22, and 28 dpi, throat and cloacal swabs were collected from each chick group, submerged in 1 mL PBS, and stored at −80°C. After one freeze-thaw cycle and thorough squeezing of the cotton swabs, samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. 200 μL of the resulting supernatant was loaded into the Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit 2.0 (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), and RNA was isolated with an automatic nucleic acid extractor (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time) (TaKaRa, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). Viral RNA copy numbers in the different swab samples were then determined by the same qPCR absolute quantification method as described above.

Statistics analysis.GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA) was used for all data analysis. Experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P values: *p < 0.05,⁎⁎p < 0.01.

The level of humoral immune response induced by the rHV80 strain in chicks

Effects of different culture substrates on humoral immunity induction by the rHV80 strain.To examine whether the cell culture substrate influences the humoral response induced by the virus, 1-day-old SPF chicks were immunized intranasally and ocularly with virus preparations grown in different substrates. Neutralizing antibody levels in serum were measured by multiple serological assays to assess how the culture substrate affects the humoral response induced by the rHV80 strain (Fig. 1A). ELISA detected serum antibodies beginning at 14 dpv. Neutralizing titers rose thereafter, peaking at 28 dpv. No significant differences in antibody levels were observed among groups at 14 or 21 dpv. At 28 dpv, however, antibody levels in the rHV80 (Vero cells) and rHV80 (CK cells) groups were significantly higher than those in the rH120 group. Clinical symptoms of chicks infected with the rHV80 strain

Clinical signs were monitored for 10 chicks per infected group for 28 days. From 1 to 7 dpi, 40% chicks infected with the rH120 strain exhibited mild coughing with clear vocalizations. Only one chick in the rHV80 group showed minimal respiratory signs, but 70-100% of chicks appeared listless, had elevated body temperatures, and displayed rough, disordered plumage. Clinical symptoms of chicks in different infected groups.

Between 8 and 28 dpi, 20–30% chicks infected with the rH120 and rHV80 strains displayed mild coughing and then gradually recovered. Began on 12 dpi, 60–70% chicks infected with the rHV80 strain developed neurological signs, including listlessness, drooping of the head and neck, circling and ataxia; 40% chicks progressed to twitching and death. None of the chicks in the rH120 group or the PBS control group exhibited these neurological symptoms (Table 1). Compared with the rH120 strain, the rHV80 strain produced clear neurological symptoms in both early and late stages of infection and caused deaths during the late stage.

Discussion

We previously domesticated a Vero cell-adapted strain, rHV80, using H120 as the parental strain. To assess the rHV80 strain as a potential live attenuated vaccine, we conducted in vivo studies of pathogenicity and immunogenicity in accordance with the European Pharmacopeia (Council of Europe, 2021). First, we ruled out the effects of different culture substrates on the immunogenicity of the rHV80 strain. Next, using a range of vaccine doses, we demonstrated that the rHV80 strain retained its ability to elicit neutralizing antibodies; doses of 104-106 TCID50 induced robust humoral responses. In safety testing, we inoculated 1-day-old SPF chicks by the intraocular-nasal route and found that, compared with the rH120 strain, the rHV80 strain displayed markedly expanded tissue tropism. The strain not only infected the respiratory tract but also replicated efficiently in muscle, brain, and testis tissues, producing lesions of varying severity. Virus shedding patterns in chicks were not notably altered by the rHV80 strain.

Vero cells exhibit stable genetic and growth characteristics compared with chicken embryos and some other passaged cell lines. Since they do not produce interferons and are suitable for the replication of various viruses, they are recommended by the WHO as the preferred cell line to produce viral vaccines (Desmyter et al., 1968). Chicken embryos and cultured cells constitute distinct virus propagation systems, which yield different post-translational modifications of viral proteins. Such differences in protein modification can alter viral immunogenicity (Zhang, 2020). To determine whether the adaptation of the rHV80 strain in Vero cells altered its immunogenicity, 1-day-old SPF chicks were immunized with rHV80 viruses propagated in the different substrates, and neutralizing antibody levels were measured in each group after vaccination. The results showed that humoral immunity induced by the rHV80 strain grown in Vero cells was not diminished, supporting the feasibility of using Vero cells as the substrate for future IBV cell-based vaccines. Significant inter-individual variability exists in cellular and humoral immune responses to coronavirus vaccines (Kruttgen et al., 2022; Tychala et al., 2021). In our study, neutralizing antibody titers in chicks vaccinated with rH120 and rHV80 strains likewise exhibited marked inter-individual variation. Moreover, when determining the optimal rHV80 strain dose, the group receiving 106TCID50 showed a relatively low mean neutralizing antibody level at 28 dpi, which may be attributable to deaths of some chicks at that dose.

Although the rHV80 strain elicited relatively strong humoral responses, certain immunization doses produced neurological signs in chicks, including neck droop, convulsions, and occasional deaths. These observations indicated an altered pathogenic profile for the rHV80 strain. Mutations at specific amino acid sites in the coronavirus S protein can alter viral tissue tropism (Ballesteros et al., 1997; Li et al., 2015). The insertion of an S2’ furin cleavage site in the IBV YN strain likewise broadened its tissue tropism and was associated with neurological lesions (Cheng et al., 2021). Because nine amino acids were substituted accumulated in the HV80 S gene during domestication (Jiang et al., 2023), we suspect that the tissue tropism of this strain has changed during the in vitro cell adaptation process. Further challenge experiments showed that, unlike the rH120 strain, rHV80 broke through tissue barriers and efficiently infected the lungs, muscle, testes, and brain of chicks. Viral genome and antigen of the rHV80 strain were detectable in both skeletal muscle and heart tissue, yet no corresponding clinical signs had been observed. Testes exhibit marked lesions, indicating that rHV80 can inflict reproductive damage in infected chicks. However, because the experimental chickens did not reach sexual maturity, we cannot determine whether overt clinical manifestations would emerge or how reproductive capacity might be affected. In brain tissue, viral RNA copies rose markedly during the early phase of infection and could cause neuronal necrosis. Neurological signs appeared later, which suggested that extensive loss of infected neurons underlies clinical features such as ataxia and, ultimately, death. Taking together, these findings demonstrate that the rHV80 strain possesses measurable neurotoxicity. The broadening of tissue tropism of the rHV80 strain may be closely related to the amino acid mutations in its S protein, especially the emergence of neurotoxicity. In our previous work, we showed that the HV80 strain evolved a highly efficient furin recognition motif in the S2’ cleavage site (Jiang et al., 2023). The acquisition of this cleavage site may be a critical determinant of viral neurotoxicity, a finding that aligns with earlier reports on the IBV rYN strain (Cheng et al., 2021). Nonetheless, this hypothesis and the underlying pathogenic mechanism require additional investigation and experimental confirmation.

Interestingly, the pathogenicity of the rHV80 strain to tracheal ring cilia was reduced. Compared with the rH120 strain, the early respiratory symptoms of chicks infected with the rHV80 strain were significantly alleviated, and its ability to inhibit the ciliary activity of tracheal rings was also significantly reduced. However, the infection ability of this strain to the lungs was significantly stronger than that of the parental rH120 strain. There are differences in the receptor or protease environment between the tracheal ring epithelial tissue and the lung tissue of chicks. We speculate that the enhanced pathogenicity of the rHV80 strain to lung tissue may also be related to the mutations at certain amino acid sites on its S protein.

In addition, we also detected viral RNA at low levels in multiple chick organs during the early stage of rH120 strain infection, but by day 7, those levels had fallen to or near the detection threshold. This pattern likely reflects a transient, short-term dissemination of viral genome via the bloodstream and is unlikely to cause significant tissue damage. However, rH120 showed higher infectivity in the bursa of Fabricius than rHV80. Both strains also infected pancreatic tissue efficiently and caused severe pathological damage, a finding rarely reported previously. Due to the wide distribution of proteases in the pancreas, including trypsin (Feinstein et al., 1974), cathepsin (Chen et al., 2022), and elastase (Guyonnet et al., 1999), etc. This pancreatic tropism may be related to the affinity of IBV for these proteases.

Viral shedding duration and quantity are key criteria for assessing live attenuated vaccine safety. In this study, throat and cloacal swabs were collected from chicks at multiple time points after infection. Compared with the rH120 strain, the rHV80 strain showed reduced early-stage viral shedding in throat swabs, while shedding levels at other time points did not change significantly.

In summary, although the rHV80 strain was highly immunogenic and showed an acceptable level of viral shedding, its expanded tissue tropism affected its safety as a live attenuated vaccine. In the future, we need to modify the viral S gene to pinpoint the mutations that underlie this broadened tropism and then assess whether the rHV80 strain can function as a genomic backbone for genetically engineered vaccines.
