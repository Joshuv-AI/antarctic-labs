Long-term outcomes of sutured and sutureless (Yamane) scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation for lens subluxation in Marfan syndrome: a retrospective case series
Authors: Suzan Doğruya, Fatma Dilara Önal Artan, Mustafa Erdoğan, Ömer Can Kayıkçıoğlu, Özcan Rasim Kayıkçıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe scleral-fixation surgical approaches in patients with Marfan syndrome and lens subluxation and to evaluate postoperative visual outcomes and complications.DesignRetrospective case series.MethodsMedical records of 9 patients (15 eyes) with Marfan syndrome who underwent surgery for lens subluxation between January 2015 and March 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Surgical techniques included sutured scleral fixation, sutureless intrascleral fixation (Yamane technique), and vitrectomy-assisted procedures when indicated. Preoperative and postoperative best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), postoperative complications, secondary interventions, and intraocular lens (IOL) position were analyzed. Outcomes according to fixation technique were assessed descriptively due to small and unbalanced subgroup sizes.ResultsSutured scleral fixation was performed in 13 eyes, while sutureless intrascleral fixation using the Yamane technique was applied in 2 eyes. The mean patient age was 15.46 ± 15.88 years (range, 5–70 years), and the mean follow-up duration was 30.66 months (range, 7–120 months). BCVA improved significantly from 1.28 ± 0.76 logMAR preoperatively to 0.69 ± 0.80 logMAR postoperatively (p = 0.01). Postoperative complications occurred in 4 eyes (26.7%), including retinal detachment in one eye (6.7%) and intraocular lens subluxation or pupillary capture in three eyes (20%). All complications were successfully managed with secondary surgical interventions. Visual improvement was limited in two eyes due to pre-existing amblyopia.ConclusionScleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation is an effective option for visual rehabilitation in patients with Marfan syndrome and lens subluxation. However, observed complication patterns highlight the importance of individualized surgical planning and long-term postoperative surveillance. Comparisons between fixation techniques should be interpreted as descriptive.Keywords: Marfan Syndrome, Scleral fixation lens, Visual acuity

Introduction

Marfan syndrome is a genetic connective tissue disorder that is autosomal dominant in two-thirds of cases and develops due to a defect in the fibrillin-1 gene. The population incidence is 2–3 per 10,000. [1] This syndrome affects connective tissue, bones, and muscles. Another distinctive feature of this genetic disorder is the presence of abnormalities in the eyes. Genetically determined connective tissue weakness can affect the zonules and, thus, the stability of the lens within the eye, which can cause lens subluxations and vision problems. Lens subluxation in patients with Marfan syndrome can usually be noticed in the first years of life and may require surgical intervention in the future. [2] This can lead to eye problems such as myopia, astigmatism, and amblyopia. [3, 4].

Although lens subluxation is usually bilateral and symmetrical, unilateral cases may occur. [5] The incidence of ectopia lentis in Marfan syndrome reaches up to 72% and occurs in the fourth and fifth decades of life. [6, 7].

In people with Marfan syndrome, the crystalline lens may subluxate due to zonular dehiscence, accompanying high intraocular pressure and retinal detachment. [7, 8].

Although scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation has been widely reported as an effective option for managing lens subluxation in patients with Marfan syndrome, most published studies focus on short- to mid-term visual outcomes or single surgical techniques. Long-term, real-world data addressing postoperative complications, secondary interventions, and durability of different scleral fixation strategies in this high-risk population remain limited.

In addition, the heterogeneity of surgical approaches and the lack of standardized decision-making algorithms further complicate interpretation of existing literature. The present study aims to address this gap by providing a long-term, technique-inclusive observational analysis of surgical outcomes, complications, and postoperative course in patients with Marfan syndrome undergoing scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation. Given the younger age of patients with Marfan syndrome and their susceptibility to late structural complications, identifying potential long-term complication patterns associated with different scleral fixation strategies is of particular clinical importance.

Material and method

The records of 9 patients diagnosed with Marfan syndrome who applied to our clinic between January 2015 and March 2023 were retrospectively examined. Ethical approval was granted by the Celal Bayar University research ethics committee (31.01.2024/20.478.486/2245). All procedures were performed according to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki for human participants, and written informed consent was obtained before surgical procedures. The diagnosis of Marfan syndrome was based on established clinical diagnostic criteria and multidisciplinary clinical assessment, as genetic testing was not available.

A total of 9 patients were included in the study, comprising 15 operated eyes. Of these, 6 patients underwent bilateral surgery and 3 patients underwent unilateral surgery. According to the IOL fixation technique, 13 eyes received sutured fixation, while 2 eyes underwent sutureless intrascleral fixation using the Yamane technique.

Clinical data, including age, gender, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and complications, were analyzed. Basic medical history and systemic comorbidities were questioned, and all patients underwent a complete ophthalmological examination, including cardiac evaluation. The best-corrected visual acuity was examined using decimal visual acuity, and the data was converted to logMAR. The anterior segment was evaluated with a slit lamp biomicroscopy. Posterior segment examination was performed with a + 90-Diopter and Goldmann 3-mirror lens. Ocular ultrasonography was used if necessary.

The surgical method applied to the patients, preoperative and postoperative visual acuities, complications and the need for a second surgery, and postoperative lens positions were recorded.

Inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed clinically with Marfan syndrome who had unilateral or bilateral lens subluxation resulting in visual impairment and who underwent surgical intervention during the study period. Exclusion criteria included patients with Marfan syndrome and lens subluxation who achieved satisfactory visual acuity with conservative management, patients who declined surgical treatment, and those with insufficient clinical or follow-up data.

Surgical techniques

All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon (ÖK). The choice of surgical technique was determined individually based on the degree of lens subluxation, zonular support, patient age, and associated ocular conditions.

All operated eyes lacked adequate capsular support for in-the-bag intraocular lens implantation; therefore, scleral fixation was required in all cases. Sutured scleral fixation using 10–0 polypropylene sutures was the primary technique applied during the study period in accordance with established clinical practice. In selected cases, sutureless intrascleral fixation was performed using the Yamane double-needle technique, in which the haptics were externalized through scleral tunnels to achieve stable fixation.

For sutured scleral fixation, a posterior chamber intraocular lens was fixated to the sclera using a transscleral ab externo technique. After conjunctival dissection, partial-thickness scleral flaps or scleral tunnels were created approximately 2.0–2.5 mm posterior to the limbus in opposing quadrants. A 10–0 polypropylene suture was passed through the eyelet or haptic of the intraocular lens and externalized through the sclera using a long needle. The sutures were securely tied beneath the scleral flaps or within scleral tunnels to minimize suture exposure, and the flaps were closed with absorbable sutures. The intraocular lenses used for sutured scleral fixation were three-piece posterior chamber intraocular lenses suitable for sulcus placement and transscleral fixation. These lenses were selected according to availability at the time of surgery and featured polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) or polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) haptics, which are considered appropriate for long-term scleral fixation.

Sutureless intrascleral intraocular lens fixation was performed using the Yamane double-needle technique. Two thin-walled 27 gauge needles were inserted transconjunctivally through the sclera at approximately 2.0 mm posterior to the limbus in diametrically opposite positions. The haptics of a three-piece posterior chamber intraocular lens were grasped and externalized through the lumen of each needle. After externalization, the tips of the haptics were cauterized to create a flange, which was then tucked back into the scleral tunnel to achieve stable intrascleral fixation without the use of sutures.

In cases requiring pars plana vitrectomy, a standard three-port vitrectomy was performed prior to intraocular lens fixation to remove vitreous traction and ensure a stable posterior segment. Additional anterior or core vitrectomy was performed when vitreous prolapse into the anterior chamber was present. Pars plana vitrectomy was performed in conjunction with scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation in cases with significant vitreous prolapse, vitreoretinal traction, or inadequate capsular support. In these cases, vitrectomy was completed prior to intraocular lens fixation to ensure a stable posterior segment and to prevent postoperative vitreoretinal complications. The decision to combine pars plana vitrectomy with scleral fixation was made intraoperatively based on vitreous status and lens stability rather than as a routine component of the procedure.

The size and configuration of the corneal incision were determined on a case-by-case basis. Selective enlargement of the corneal incision was performed only when required to facilitate safe intraocular lens explantation or intraocular manipulation, while standard-sized corneal incisions were preferred whenever surgical conditions allowed.

The choice of surgical technique was individualized and based on intraoperative assessment, including the extent of zonular insufficiency, lens stability, patient age, and associated ocular conditions. The primary objective was to achieve stable intraocular lens fixation and visual rehabilitation rather than to directly compare different surgical techniques.

Lens removal was performed using either anterior lensectomy or pars plana–assisted lensectomy depending on the degree of lens subluxation and intraoperative vitreous involvement. In cases with significant vitreous prolapse or posterior segment traction, pars plana vitrectomy was performed prior to or in conjunction with lens removal. In all operated eyes, adequate capsular support was absent; therefore, preservation of a functional capsular bag for in-the-bag intraocular lens implantation was not feasible. Residual capsular remnants, when present, were not used for intraocular lens fixation.

Intraocular lens power calculation was performed using standard biometric measurements available at the time of surgery. In pediatric patients, age, axial length, and refractive status were taken into consideration; however, due to the retrospective design and variability in patient age, a standardized pediatric-specific IOL calculation formula could not be uniformly applied across all cases.

Technique selection criteria

The choice between sutured scleral fixation and sutureless intrascleral fixation (Yamane technique) was based on structured intraoperative assessment rather than random allocation. Sutured scleral fixation was generally preferred in eyes with significant vitreous prolapse, complex anterior segment anatomy, or when controlled and adjustable intraocular lens centration was considered advantageous, particularly in younger patients requiring long-term stability.

Sutureless intrascleral fixation was selectively performed in eyes with extensive zonular compromise but adequate scleral integrity, favorable anterior segment configuration, and minimal vitreous traction, allowing secure haptic externalization.

The final decision was individualized according to anatomical findings, intraoperative stability, and surgeon judgment, with the primary goal of achieving durable intraocular lens fixation and minimizing long-term structural complications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 22.0 software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations and ranges. Preoperative and postoperative best-corrected visual acuity and intraocular pressure were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the small, uneven subgroup sizes, formal statistical comparisons between intraocular lens fixation techniques were not conducted. Outcomes according to fixation technique are presented descriptively, and statistical testing was limited to overall pre- and postoperative changes.

Results

Lens subluxation was detected in 11 of 12 patients who applied with the diagnosis of Marfan syndrome. (Fig. 1) Of the eleven patients with lens subluxation, three were unilateral, and eight were bilateral. Among these patients, two patients with bilateral lens subluxation did not undergo any surgery because they did not accept the operation. IOL implantation with scleral fixation was performed in 15 eyes of 9 patients (6 male and 3 females) with Marfan syndrome due to lens subluxation. One case ( case 7)was a patient with Marfan syndrome who had undergone lens extraction surgery before, but we operated because diplopia developed due to IOL subluxation. The mean age was 15.46 ± 15.88 years (range 5–70). The average follow-up period was 30.66 months( range 7-120). Preoperative IOP was 14.64 ± 2.64 mmHg, postoperative IOP was 14.21 ± 2.25 mmHg(P > 0.05), preoperative visual acuity was 1.28 ± 0.76 logMAR, postoperative visual acuity was 0.69 ± 0.80 logMAR (p = 0.01). None of the patients had cardiac or systemic problems.

Discussion

This study reports long-term outcomes of scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation in patients with Marfan syndrome and explores evolving fixation strategies in a complex and high-risk population. Beyond documenting visual improvement, our findings highlight patterns of postoperative complications and the importance of individualized surgical planning in achieving durable outcomes.

In this real-world clinical series, we observed significant visual improvement following scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation, with complication patterns varying according to fixation strategy and anatomical considerations.

Intraoperative and postoperative vitreous loss, retinal detachment, and intraocular lens position abnormalities cause poor visual outcomes in lens dislocation surgery with Marfan syndrome. [9] Developments in microsurgical instruments and operating techniques have increased interest in surgery in individuals with Marfan syndrome. [10] In patients with lens dislocation secondary to Marfan syndrome, anterior chamber lens implantation causes more complications than posterior chamber lens implantation. Therefore, the posterior chamber lens implantation approach is a more common practice. [11] Marfan syndrome patients require surgery due to anterior or posterior displacement of the lens and secondary glaucoma and cataracts. Loss of capsule support and vitreous and endothelial cell damage may occur during the removal of the subluxated lens in these patients. [12].

When in-bag IOL implantation is not possible in patients with Marfan syndrome, a sutured scleral fixation lens offers superior visual and refractive outcomes. Good visual results were obtained with the sutured scleral fixation intraocular lens applied to pediatric ectopic lens eyes with Marfan syndrome, but complications may occur in some cases. After surgery, 92% of patients reported stable or improved vision. [13] Burcu et al. reported positive short-term visual outcomes with sutured scleral lens, but cautioned about potential problems. [14] The rate of IOL dislocation/subluxation at postoperative follow-up was never reported by Kim et al. [15], 6.8% by Sen et al. [13], 29.6% by Luebke et al. [16] and 48.7% by Zheng et al. [17]. These different results may be due to differences in the postoperative follow-up time of the patients or differences in the material used for suturing. Marfan syndrome patients who had scleral suturing IOL implantation experienced retinal detachment (0-14.8%), pupillary capture (0–24%), vitreous hemorrhage (0-1.3%), and elevated intraocular pressure (0-26.6%) [13, 15–17].

In a study applying posterior chamber intraocular lenses with transscleral fixation in children, the complication rate was found to be high in long-term follow-up. [18] In their study, Zetterstrom et al. implanted the IOL into the sulcus and did not report secondary glaucoma or retinal complications. [19] In their study, Vadala et al. applied scleral fixation (five eyes) and intracapsular lens (one eye) implantation to six eyes with lens dislocation and reported that the scleral fixation technique reduced decentration of the intraocular lens. [20].

In our study, we performed sutured scleral lens implantation in 13 eyes and found a statistically significant improvement in best-corrected visual acuity compared with preoperative values (p = 0.01). In two of these patients, pars plana vitrectomy was performed prior to sutured scleral fixation due to associated vitreous involvement; however, all eyes included in the study were diagnosed with lens subluxation rather than complete lens dislocation. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that individualized surgical planning is essential in managing lens subluxation in patients with Marfan syndrome. Previous studies have reported favorable visual outcomes following scleral fixation techniques, although the risk of postoperative complications remains higher in this patient population due to inherent connective tissue weakness.

Sutured scleral intraocular lens fixation and sutureless scleral fixation techniques represent two main surgical approaches for the management of lens subluxation, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Sutured scleral fixation provides reliable early postoperative intraocular lens stability; however, late complications such as intraocular lens dislocation related to suture degradation have been reported during long-term follow-up. In contrast, sutureless scleral fixation techniques eliminate suture-related complications but may be associated with technical challenges and learning-curve–related issues. Pars plana vitrectomy, when required, serves as an adjunctive procedure to address vitreous prolapse or vitreoretinal pathology and should not be considered a fixation technique itself. Therefore, the choice of surgical approach should be individualized based on patient characteristics, ocular anatomy, and surgeon experience.

Sutureless intrascleral IOL fixation (Yamane) was developed to eliminate complications such as suture-induced inflammation, infection or suture breakage. A scleral tunnel or scleral flap is created for scleral fixation of the intraocular lens. It is aimed to provide IOL stability with scar tissue around the haptics. This technique also allows sutureless fixation of haptics in cases of iris trauma and reduces the risk of postoperative astigmatism [21, 22] Tao et al. applied the yamane technique in 8 eyes with Marfan syndrome and found a significant increase in visual acuity. Except for iris capture in 6 eyes, they did not report any other complications [23]. In our current study, the yamane technique was applied to two patients (cases 5, 7) and a significant increase in preoperative and postoperative best visual acuity was obtained. No complications developed in any of these patients. In addition to the Yamane technique, the Carlevale intraocular lens represents another sutureless scleral fixation approach that has gained increasing attention. The Carlevale lens is specifically designed for scleral fixation and features flexible anchoring haptics that allow stable intrascleral positioning without the need for sutures or haptic externalization. Previous studies have demonstrated favorable stability and visual outcomes with acceptable complication rates, suggesting the Carlevale lens as a viable alternative technique in eyes with insufficient capsular support. [24] However, the Carlevale lens was not used in the present study, and therefore direct comparison with our findings is not possible.

Omulecki et al. performed pars plana vitrectomy and scleral fixation intraocular lens implantation in 19 eyes of 13 patients with Marfan syndrome. Although the operation time was long, they reported good visual results and fewer complications. [25].

Hubbard et al. reported that pars-plana vitreolensectomy was a safe and effective method in 40 patients with Marfan syndrome. They recommended that intraoperative laser treatment be applied only to the area of lattice degeneration to limit the incidence of retinal detachment. [26].

In our series, vitrectomy was required selectively and was performed as an adjunctive procedure rather than a routine component of scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation. The need for anterior or limited core vitrectomy reflected intraoperative vitreous prolapse or vitreoretinal traction rather than complete lens dislocation. This individualized approach is consistent with previous reports emphasizing tailored surgical planning in patients with Marfan syndrome to minimize vitreoretinal complications while preserving posterior segment stability.

Laser photocoagulation was not performed prophylactically at the time of lens extraction or intraocular lens implantation in any patient. However, laser photocoagulation was applied as part of retinal detachment surgery in one eye that developed postoperative retinal detachment.

Within-the-bag lensectomy performed by Anteby et al. in subluxated eyes with Marfan syndrome was reported to be safe. [10] There are many studies showing retinal detachment complications after the removal of subluxated lenses by lens extraction in individuals with Marfan syndrome. [27–29].

Short-term results of pars plana lensectomy and anterior chamber intraocular lens placement showed that visual acuity was good and there were no complications. However, the greater anterior chamber depth of individuals with Marfan syndrome may cause difficulties in intraocular implantation. Mobile intraocular lenses may result in pigment discharge, glaucoma, and corneal endothelial loss. [30].

Postoperative complications observed in our study included intraocular lens subluxation, pupillary capture, and retinal detachment, which are well-documented risks in patients with Marfan syndrome undergoing surgery for lens subluxation. These complications can be attributed to inherent connective tissue weakness, zonular insufficiency, and vitreoretinal alterations characteristic of the syndrome. In particular, late intraocular IOL dislocation associated with suture degradation reflects a known limitation of sutured scleral fixation techniques during long-term follow-up.

In one eye that underwent sutured scleral fixation, retinal detachment developed two years after surgery and was treated with retinal reattachment surgery combined with laser photocoagulation. Additionally, postoperative IOL dislocation occurred in two eyes, and pupillary capture developed in one eye at different time points during follow-up; these cases were managed with intraocular lens repositioning alone. Importantly, all complications were successfully treated with appropriate secondary interventions, and no irreversible vision loss was observed.

Although a numerically higher rate of IOL malpositioning was observed in eyes undergoing sutured IOL fixation compared with the Yamane technique, this difference did not reach statistical significance, most likely due to the small and unbalanced subgroup sizes. Nevertheless, this observation may suggest a potential trend toward improved IOL stability with sutureless intrascleral fixation techniques, as previously reported in the literature. These findings should be interpreted with caution and do not imply superiority of one fixation technique, as the study was not designed or statistically powered to directly compare surgical approaches. Rather than providing a direct comparison, the present study aims to identify clinically relevant trends in long-term complications and postoperative course, which may inform surgical decision-making and future prospective investigations in this vulnerable patient population.

Retinal detachment occurred in one eye in our series, an outcome that warrants discussion in the context of the ongoing debate regarding prophylactic vitrectomy. Omulecki and colleagues emphasized that prophylactic vitrectomy may reduce the risk of subsequent retinal detachment in selected high-risk eyes by allowing early identification and treatment of peripheral retinal pathology. Similarly, Hubbard highlighted the potential protective role of vitrectomy through removal of vitreoretinal traction, particularly in eyes predisposed to peripheral retinal degeneration.

In our case, retinal detachment developed despite prior surgical intervention, suggesting that although prophylactic vitrectomy may lower the overall risk, it does not completely eliminate the possibility of this complication. Differences in baseline retinal status, extent of peripheral examination, and timing of vitrectomy may partly explain discrepancies between reported series. Our finding supports the view that prophylactic vitrectomy should be considered on an individualized basis rather than as a routine strategy, with careful patient selection and close postoperative surveillance remaining essential.

Clinical implications of fixation strategy

In the present series, intraocular lens malpositions were observed only in eyes that underwent sutured scleral fixation; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of eyes treated with sutureless techniques.

Late suture degradation and potential suture breakage represent recognized long-term risks associated with sutured scleral fixation, particularly in younger patients with connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome. In contrast, sutureless intrascleral fixation techniques aim to eliminate suture-related complications and may theoretically provide improved long-term structural stability.

However, sutureless techniques are not without challenges. Surgical expertise, learning curve considerations, precise haptic externalization, and adequate scleral integrity are critical factors influencing outcomes. Anatomical variability and intraoperative findings remain central to fixation strategy selection.

Taken together, these observations underscore the importance of individualized surgical planning rather than rigid preference for a single technique.

Importantly, the variability in surgical approaches, anatomical findings, and follow-up duration observed in this series reflects real-world clinical practice in the management of lens subluxation in patients with Marfan syndrome. Rather than representing solely a methodological limitation, this heterogeneity underscores the complexity of individualized surgical decision-making and enhances the clinical applicability of the present findings.

This study has several limitations that should be explicitly acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and relatively small sample size—particularly the limited number of eyes treated with sutureless intrascleral fixation—restrict the generalizability of the findings and preclude robust statistical comparisons between fixation techniques. As a result, technique-related observations should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Second, the choice of surgical technique was individualized based on intraoperative anatomical findings and surgeon judgment. While this approach reflects real-world clinical practice, anatomical variability and case-specific factors may have influenced complication patterns and postoperative outcomes, limiting the ability to isolate technique-related effects.

Third, although the mean follow-up duration exceeded 2.5 years and was sufficient to evaluate early and mid-term outcomes, longer surveillance is required to fully assess late-onset complications, particularly suture degradation or breakage associated with 10–0 polypropylene sutures in younger patients with Marfan syndrome.

Additional limitations include the absence of genetic confirmation of Marfan syndrome in all patients, reliance on established clinical diagnostic criteria, and the lack of a standardized pediatric intraocular lens power calculation protocol. In pediatric eyes, functional outcomes may also have been influenced by pre-existing amblyopia and the severity of lens subluxation. Furthermore, the absence of detailed biometric and refractive outcome data limits assessment of optical performance and refractive predictability.

Despite these limitations, the heterogeneity of surgical approaches and patient characteristics reflects the inherent complexity of managing lens subluxation in patients with Marfan syndrome and enhances the real-world relevance of the present findings. Future prospective studies with standardized methodologies, larger cohorts, and longer follow-up are warranted to further clarify technique-specific outcomes.

In conclusion, scleral-fixated intraocular lens implantation represents an effective and anatomically stable solution for visual rehabilitation in patients with Marfan syndrome who lack adequate capsular support when performed with appropriate surgical technique. Both sutured and sutureless intrascleral fixation methods were associated with meaningful visual improvement; however, the observation of intraocular lens malposition exclusively in sutured cases underscores that each approach carries distinct risk profiles that must be carefully considered during surgical planning.

Importantly, surgeons should remain vigilant for late-onset complications such as intraocular lens malposition, iris-related events, and retinal detachment, even after initially uneventful postoperative courses. Careful patient selection, meticulous surgical execution, and long-term follow-up are essential to optimize outcomes and ensure durable success in this high-risk patient population.

By providing long-term, real-world data and highlighting potential complication patterns across different scleral fixation strategies rather than asserting technique superiority, this study offers hypothesis-generating insights that may guide individualized surgical planning and inform future prospective investigations in patients with Marfan syndrome.
