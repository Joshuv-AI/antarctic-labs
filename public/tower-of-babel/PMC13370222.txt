Deciphering the genetic mechanism of perenniality in rice potentiates sustainable agricultural production
Authors: Zheng Li, Ruichen Ma, Ole Pedersen
Published: 2026

Main text

Rice domestication stands as one of the most significant technological shifts in human history, transforming a wild, shattering grass into a primary caloric foundation for nearly half the global population. A key change during rice domestication was the transition from a perennial to an annual life history. This transition was driven by our ancestors’ obvious preference then for high-yielding rice, as annual varieties channel a vast proportion of their assimilated photosynthetic energy into seed production during the growing season, whereas wild perennial species need to allocate resources between sexual reproduction and perennating structure development. Despite the significant contribution of this annualization process to human civilization, such a singular pursuit of high yield is increasingly being questioned through the lens of sustainable agriculture. It has been increasingly recognized that annual cropping practices resulting from this transition create several long-term environmental and economic challenges, including soil erosion, land degradation, and high resource requirements. Consequently, crop perennialization, the process of converting annual crops into perennial forms, has been put forward as a strategy to address these sustainability issues (Glover et al., 2010).

A better genetic understanding of perenniality is essential for effective crop perennialization; however, much of our existing knowledge has been gained from studies of model perennial species in the Brassicaceae family (Li et al., 2022; Zhao and Wang, 2024). In these plants, floral transition is controlled by the integration of cues derived from both age and vernalization. As juvenile plants age, a gradual decline in microRNA156 (miR156) levels confers developmental competence required for flowering (Bergonzi and Albani, 2011). In adult plants, the restriction of flowering, which underlies their polycarpic perennial flowering behavior, is largely achieved through a “reactivation” mechanism of floral repressor genes after vernalization (Wang et al., 2009; Zhai et al., 2024). Although these findings have clarified the evolution of plant life-history strategies and have even enabled the conversion of annual Brassicaceae plants into perennial ones, they offer limited utility for perennial rice breeding. This is because, unlike many cruciferous species, wild perennial rice does not require vernalization for flowering and, more critically, its perenniality is attained to a large extent through a distinct characteristic: clonality.

The recent study by Dai et al. (2026) represents a pioneering leap toward rice perennialization through genetic engineering. To elucidate the genetic basis underlying the perennial-to-annual transition in rice, they utilized Oryza rufipogon (the perennial ancestor of Asian cultivated rice [O. sativa]) for genetic mapping. This species propagates through both sexual and asexual means. Its horizontal stems function as clonal growth organs capable of forming new individuals (ramets), each consisting of nascent shoots and adventitious roots. Because of their aboveground origin, they are sometimes referred to as stolons in the literature; however, they can also exhibit characteristics of epigeogenous rhizomes, as they frequently become deeply submerged or buried in sediments (Xie et al., 2001). Similar to many weeds, the invasiveness of O. rufipogon is further enhanced by its continual and extensive branching growth.

By phenotyping these grass-like perennial growth traits in a substitution line population derived from a cross between O. rufipogon and O. sativa, Dai et al. identified the Endless Branches and Tiller 1 (EBT1) locus, which harbors two tandem miR156 genes (MIR156BC), as a major determinant of the perennial growth habit in wild rice (Figure 1). Substitution lines carrying this locus in the O. sativa background produce a large number of secondary tillers after seed ripening, exhibiting sustained vegetative growth. Although these tillers do not grow horizontally, they can develop into a cluster of plants when isolated from the primary individual. Mechanistically, O. rufipogon employs a similar “reactivation” system, but here it reverses the juvenile-to-adult transition rather than the floral transition. Parallel to the regulatory mechanisms uncovered in crucifers, epigenetic reprogramming appears to be a key tactic underlying this process; a reduction in repressive epigenetic modifications at the regulatory region of MIR156BC triggers a rebound in its expression within tiller buds following seed set, thereby promoting continual branching and, additionally, the capacity for clonal reproduction. Interestingly, such co-existence of juvenile and adult phases also occurs in O. longistaminata, whose rhizomes remain in a juvenile state regardless of the aerial shoot’s developmental phase (Yoshida et al., 2016). Given their primary function as clonal growth organs, it is not surprising that these erect secondary tillers in the substitution lines exhibit abnormal floral development, a significant deficiency from a perennial rice breeding perspective. Accordingly, the authors sought to recapitulate the perennial flowering behavior, and thus perennial grain production, of O. rufipogon by fully restoring the clonal growth habit. Introducing PROSTRATE GROWTH 1 (PROG1) and TILLER INCLINED GROWTH 1 (TIG1), two genes responsible for the prostrate growth habit of wild rice, into EBT1-containing backcross inbred lines (BILs) gave rise to horizontal and rooted secondary tillers. Remarkably, in certain BIL-derived lineages, the ramets produced from these tillers have the capacity to flower normally and set seeds. Thus, these lineages, termed wild-like rice, provide a robust foundation for perennial rice breeding.

The potential of perennial rice to enhance sustainable agriculture has been demonstrated previously by Zhang et al. (2023). However, the perennial rice lines in that study were created using different parental material; the rhizome-mediated perenniality of O. longistaminata was introgressed into O. sativa through wide hybridization, and the spreading clonal habit was intentionally restricted through iterative selection (Figure 1). This resulted in a perennial system in which new shoots regrow from the crown. By contrast, the wild-like rice lines developed by Dai et al. (2026) derived their clonality primarily from the combined effects of EBT1, PROG1, and TIG1. Despite sharing a perennial habit, these two systems may differ across agricultural and ecological dimensions. The perennial rice model described by Zhang et al. (2023) reflects repeated cycles of the traditional annual cropping system, offering an advantage within current agricultural frameworks. In comparison, the wild-like lines described by Dai et al. (2026) more closely resemble the ancestral state, potentially providing greater resilience for cultivation in disturbed environments (Klimešová et al., 2021). Further investigation into the perennial strategies of these two wild rice species, coupled with deeper insights into the relationship between perenniality and sustainability, will support the transformation toward sustainable agriculture.
