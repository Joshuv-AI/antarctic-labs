Genetic diversity and evolutionary perspectives of Pfcrt in Plasmodium falciparum: insights from Indian and global isolates
Authors: Geetika Narang, Joseph Hawadak, Jahnvi Jakhan, Vineeta Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChloroquine resistance in Plasmodium falciparum (Pf) driven by mutations in the Pf chloroquine resistance transporter (Pfcrt) gene continues to pose a challenge to malaria control in endemic regions like India. Understanding the genetic diversity and evolutionary patterns of Pfcrt is important for guiding malaria surveillance and future national drug policy.MethodsThis study analysed 979 Indian (exon 2) and 719 global full-length Pfcrt sequences using molecular tools to assess genetic diversity, selection pressure and population structure. Comparative and temporal analyses were conducted across Indian states and global regions to explore evolutionary trends.ResultsIndian sequence analysis revealed regional and temporal variations in haplotype diversity with both widespread and region-specific variants. Haplotype networks identified three major haplotypes namely CVIET, SVMNT and CVMNK, with CVIET predominating, particularly in the North–East, while SVMNT and CVMNK showed variable prevalence across regions and time periods. The greatest diversity was recorded between 2010–2019 despite reduced malaria incidence, suggesting ongoing selection pressure. Mizoram exhibited the highest Tajima’s D value (D = 3.310, p < 0.01) and Pfcrt sequences from North–East region showed a unique genetic structure reflecting a likely role of selection pressures from the Greater Mekong Subregion. Globally, 65 haplotypes were detected with highest diversity in Cambodia with strong genetic differentiation observed across countries. Distinct genetic clusters and limited haplotype sharing across countries suggest that region-specific evolutionary pressures have shaped Pfcrt diversity. Positive selection at 6 codons and linkage disequilibrium between key SNPs indicate functional adaptation under drug pressure.ConclusionThis study highlights the variation in Pfcrt shaped by national drug policies and regional malaria transmission patterns. Continued molecular surveillance is essential to monitor emerging resistant lineages and guide effective malaria treatment strategies. Background

Malaria elimination is a significant objective for the World Health Organization (WHO) but it remains a formidable challenge due to a confluence of complex factors [1]. The disease continues to exert a heavy toll particularly in tropical and subtropical regions including India, with an estimated 263 million cases reported across 83 endemic countries in 2023, a concerning increase of 11 million cases from previous year [2]. Insufficient financial resources hinder the scaling and sustainability of preventive measures, diagnostic tools and treatment interventions, especially in resource-limited regions where the disease burden is highest [1]. Climate change further amplifies these challenges by reshaping malaria epidemiology, potentially broadening its geographic reach and seasonal prevalence [1]. Additionally, the emergence of drug-resistant malaria parasites and insecticide-resistant mosquito vectors worsen the difficulty of controlling transmission, threatening the efficacy of existing tools and strategies [3].

In this context, reassessing the use of past drugs and identifying treatment practices that best preserve drug efficacy is essential, particularly across different malaria-endemic settings. National policy changes often leading to shifts in treatment practices and increased drug pressure have significantly impacted the genetic composition of parasite populations, driving the evolution of resistance [4, 5]. Evidence suggests that the emergence of resistant variants driven by drug pressure is linked to increased parasite fitness and consequently the spread of these variants [6]. To address this growing threat malaria-endemic countries have strengthened surveillance systems to monitor drug efficacy and resistance to ensure the most effective treatments are selected for national policies [2, 7]. While many studies focus on the prevalence of drug resistance mutations, fewer investigate the evolutionary patterns within resistance genes. Understanding the molecular epidemiology and evolution of these genes is crucial for identifying the spread routes of resistant parasites and tailoring effective interventions [8].

Drug resistance and evolutionary adaptations of Plasmodium falciparum (Pf), one of the two predominant malaria species in India remain central to persistence of malaria [2, 9]. The widespread use of antimalarials has exerted tremendous selective pressure on Pf accelerating the spread of drug-resistant strains and increasing morbidity and mortality [9]. Historically, chloroquine (CQ) was introduced in India in 1961 and served as the primary antimalarial for several decades. With the emergence and spread of CQ-resistant Pf, it was gradually phased out and replaced by artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) in 2005. Currently, artesunate+sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (AS + SP) is the recommended first-line treatment for uncomplicated Pf infections across most Indian states, except for North–Eastern states where artemether–lumefantrine (AL) is recommended [10, 11]. Central to this resistance is the Pf chloroquine resistance transporter (Pfcrt) gene which encodes the Pf chloroquine resistance transporter (PfCRT) protein [12]. Key mutations in Pfcrt gene, particularly at positions 72–76 confer CQ resistance and enhance parasite fitness [13]. The prevalence of these mutations, resulting in different haplotypes varies geographically, reflecting the dynamic nature of malaria resistance [11, 14, 15]. Although CQ has been replaced by ACTs as the standard treatment, monitoring CQ resistance patterns remain essential as resistant haplotypes can persist long after drug withdrawal and reduced drug pressure could allow for the re-emergence of CQ sensitivity [16–19].

In this study, we document the high nucleotide and haplotype diversity in Pfcrt gene and examine the global spread of its resistance mutations with a particular focus on the Indian subcontinent. By studying the temporal and spatial distribution of these mutations, we aim to provide insights into about spread of resistance mutations across different regions of India, tracking their evolution over time to shed light on regional dynamics of CQ resistance. On a global scale, we assess the nucleotide and haplotype diversity of the Pfcrt gene, exploring how these mutations are evolving and dispersed across various endemic regions. This comprehensive analysis of genetic diversity and evolutionary patterns will provide insights into the spatial distribution of CQ-resistant parasites, particularly in endemic areas. This study will enhance our understanding of the evolutionary drivers of Pfcrt resistance and offer valuable information for better tracking and mitigating the spread of Pfcrt resistant malaria strains.

Methods

Data source for analysis of Indian and global Pfcrt sequences

The sequence datasets used in this study were sourced from publicly available database PubMed. All published sequences of both Indian and global isolates of Pfcrt gene were included. The available Indian data mainly comprised partial sequences limited to exon 2, which contains the key codons for CQ resistance. Therefore, all Indian sequences were analysed for exon 2 region, covering codons 72–76 to ensure uniform comparison across Indian isolates. Also, complete Indian gene sequences were analysed alongside sequences from other countries to identify possible mutational occurrences across gene length and partial Indian sequences were excluded.

The Pfcrt sequence dataset analysed in the present study was previously used in our earlier publication describing the mutational profiles and haplotype distribution of both Indian and global isolates. The current study extends this work by focusing on the evolutionary patterns, genetic diversity and population structure of Pfcrt. Detailed methodology regarding sequence selection and analysis has been described in the earlier study [20]. However, relevant methodological details and selected results on mutational profiles have also been included here.

Bioinformatics tools were subsequently employed to analyse the gene sequences and provide insights into genetic diversity, evolutionary relationships and population structure. This approach facilitated an in-depth examination of both regional variations and global genetic patterns within Pfcrt gene. Indian data was analysed state-wise, covering 18 states and period-wise across four time intervals: 1993–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019 and 2020–2023. Additionally, the Indian sequences were also grouped into six regions according to the Indian Ministry of Home Affairs (https://www.mha.gov.in/en/page/zonal-council) and analysed region-wise (Fig. 1). For global data, the analysis was conducted on a country-by-country basis, as the sequences were sourced from four countries namely Cambodia, Myanmar, Uganda and India. Since all global sequences belonged to the same time period, a period-wise analysis was not conducted. Furthermore, each of these countries belongs to a different WHO region, making it inappropriate to represent the data by WHO region and therefore, a region-wise analysis was also not performed. The accession numbers of both Indian and global sequences included in the study are provided in Additional file 1.

Sample collection sites and region distribution. On the map, the states in the corresponding regions are: Northern region–Delhi, Rajasthan; Southern region–Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Puducherry; Western region–Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra; Eastern region–Odisha; North–Eastern region–Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Tripura; Central region– Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh

Sequence alignment

Sequence alignment was performed using MUSCLE program of MEGA 11 for the comparison of Pfcrt gene sequences from Indian and global isolates, identifying conserved regions as well as mutations that may have functional implications [21].

Estimates of genetic diversity and neutrality test

To assess genetic variability within the Pfcrt gene across different populations, various measures of genetic diversity were calculated, including the number of segregating sites (S), number of haplotypes (H), nucleotide diversity (π, ϴω), haplotype diversity (Hd) and average nucleotide difference (k). Genetic diversity within the Pfcrt gene was evaluated using DnaSP version 6 software [22]. This tool was utilized to calculate nucleotide and haplotype diversity for both Indian and global isolates, providing detailed metrics of genetic variation within each group. To examine deviation from the neutral model of natural selection, Tajima’s D test (D) was performed. Sliding window analyses with a 90 bp window size and 3 bp step size were performed for both nucleotide diversity (π) and Tajima’s D.

Haplotype network and structure analyses

Haplotype networks were constructed to visualize the relationships between different genetic variants at the population level. The minimal spanning network algorithm in PopART software (Version 1.7) was used to construct haplotype networks, facilitating the visualization of genetic relationships among different Pfcrt variants [23]. Additionally, STRUCTURE v2.3.4 software was employed to infer population structure and identify potential subpopulations based on the Pfcrt gene sequences [24, 25]. The output results were interpreted using the Structure Harvester online tool (https://lmme.ac.cn/StructureSelector/).

Molecular evolutionary analysis

Molecular evolutionary analysis was performed using Datamonkey, a web-based tool designed to identify selective pressures acting on genes [26]. To identify sites that have undergone diversifying or purifying selection, four different algorithms were employed: Single Likelihood Ancestor Counting (SLAC), Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL), Fast Unconstrained Bayesian Approximation (FUBAR), and the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME) [27–29]. Sites with significance level p < 0.05 were considered as significant. Only codons identified by at least 3 different algorithms were considered genuine.

Linkage disequilibrium

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was carried out to examine any genetic associations of alleles at different loci within the Pfcrt gene. This helped assess whether certain mutations co-occurred more frequently than would be expected by chance, potentially indicating functional significance. LD analysis was conducted using SRplot, an online platform for data visualization [30].

Results

Out of all Indian Pfcrt sequences downloaded, 979 high-quality exon 2 sequences were included in the study and the remaining sequences were discarded. Partial Pfcrt sequences were excluded from the global dataset and a total of 719 full-length, high-quality sequences were only included in the analysis.

Indian Pfcrt data

Mutation and haplotype profiles

Among the 976 Pfcrt sequences analysed, K76T mutation was the most predominant, observed in 80.12% of isolates, followed by M74I and N75E/K observed in 49.80% of isolates each and C72S/R in 29.92% of isolates. No mutation was observed at V73K position, while H97L mutation was rare (1.95%), identified only in isolates from Odisha (Eastern region) and Puducherry (Southern region) (Additional file 2).

Analysis of Pfcrt mutations revealed K76T as the predominant mutation, consistently observed at high frequencies across all states, regions and time periods. Regionally, North–Eastern states showed higher frequencies of M74I and N75E/K mutations, whereas the Northern, Western and Southern regions were predominantly characterized by C72S/R and K76T. State-wise, Gujarat, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu exhibited 100% prevalence of K76T, while Mizoram and Tripura showed comparatively lower K76T frequencies but higher prevalence of M74I and N75E/K mutations, highlighting distinct regional evolutionary patterns (Additional file 2). Temporally, K76T remained consistently high across all study periods. However, C72S/R declined markedly from 78.46% during 1993–1999 to 13.21% in 2010–2019, whereas M74I and N75E/K increased substantially, peaking at 60.70% during 2010–2019. However, in the most recent period 2020–2023, C72S/R predominated (79.06%) with a concurrent decline in M74I and N75E/K (Additional file 2).

Six distinct haplotypes were observed for Pfcrt 72–76 codons. Overall, the triple mutant CVIET was the predominant haplotype (49.69%), followed by double mutant SVMNT (29.82%) and wild-type CVMNK (19.88%). Minor haplotypes, including CVMNT, RVMNT and CVIKT, were rare and observed in < 1% of isolates (Additional file 2).

State-wise, CVIET haplotype predominated in most North–Eastern states, with the highest frequencies observed in Tripura (87.01%), Arunachal Pradesh (82.22%), Nagaland (76.32%) and Assam (76.19%). In contrast, the SVMNT haplotype predominated in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu (100% each), followed by Uttar Pradesh (95.65%) and Karnataka (94.44%). The CVMNT haplotype was detected only in Madhya Pradesh (16.67%) and Maharashtra (20.00%), while the rare RVMNT haplotype was observed exclusively in Delhi (1.75%). Similarly, the CVIKT haplotype was identified only in Puducherry (6.67%) (Additional file 2). Regionally, SVMNT predominated in the Northern (88.35%), Southern (85.37%) and Western regions (83.08%) with Gujarat, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu exhibiting 100% SVMNT prevalence, while the Eastern (57.82%) and North–Eastern regions (68.21%) were dominated by CVIET. The Central region showed a mixed pattern, with SVMNT (54.39%) and CVMNK (35.09%) co-circulating alongside low frequencies of CVIET (Additional file 2). Temporal analysis of 775 isolates showed that SVMNT predominated during 1993–1999 (78.46%) and 2000–2009 (71.01%), with low CVIET frequencies. In 2010–2019, CVIET became predominant (60.70%), alongside CVMNK (25.75%) and a sharp decline in SVMNT (13.21%) was observed. However, in 2021–2023, SVMNT re-emerged as the predominant haplotype (76.74%), with a reduction in CVIET frequency (2.33%) (Additional file 2).

Genetic diversity and neutrality test

Analysis of 979 Indian Pfcrt exon 2 sequences revealed six distinct haplotypes (H), with an overall haplotype diversity (Hd) of 0.626 ± 0.008 and genetic diversity (π) of 0.036. Tajima’s D analysis revealed a significant positive value for the Indian samples overall (D = 3.043, p < 0.01).

Temporally, haplotype and nucleotide diversity peaked in samples from 2010–2019, followed by samples from 2000–2009, with the lowest diversity observed in 1993–1999. Tajima’s D values were negative in 1993–1999 and 2020–2023, and positive during 2000–2019. However, these temporal differences were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Haplotype network and structure analysis

Haplotype networks constructed for Pfcrt exon 2 sequences along with 3D7 reference strain, identified six haplotypes circulating within Indian populations. Among these, two haplotypes were singletons. Regionally, Hap_2 corresponding to CVIET haplotype, was the most dominant haplotype, found across all regions, with a majority of samples originating from North–East as shown in Fig. 2a. This was followed by Hap_4 and Hap_1, corresponding to SVMNT and CVMNK haplotypes respectively, both of which were prevalent across all six Indian regions. Samples from Hap_1 clustered with 3D7 reference strain. Singleton haplotypes Hap_5 (RVMNT) and Hap_6 (CVIKT) were exclusively observed in Northern (Delhi) and Southern India (Puducherry), respectively. Hap_3 (CVMNT) was restricted to Central (Madhya Pradesh) and Western India (Rajasthan). Structure analysis exhibited significant genetic differentiation in the Pfcrt gene across different regions in India. The Pfcrt gene showed distinct genetic groups in some areas while there is a mix of multiple genetic groups in others. Northern, Eastern and Western regions exhibit a quasi-homogeneous genetic cluster. In contrast, North–East, Central and Southern regions display a heterogenous genetic structure constituting a mix of multiple subpopulations (Fig. 2b).

Linkage disequilibrium

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was conducted to evaluate the correlation between SNPs across exon 2 of Pfcrt gene in Indian Pf isolates. The analysis revealed a strong LD between mutations at codon positions 72 and 74, forming a distinct block. Additionally, a slightly weaker but still significant LD was observed between mutations at codon positions 74 and 75 (Additional file 3, Figure S1).

Global Pfcrt data

Mutation profiles

Overall, K76T was the most prevalent mutation, detected in 75.10% of isolates, followed by N326S (59.53%), Q271E (57.02%), A220S (55.22%), R371I (54.52%) and I356T (33.24%). M74I and N75D/E mutations, were detected in 31.57% of isolates each. In contrast, mutations such as D24Y, R34G, N88K, Y89F, T93S, A144F, L148I, I194T, A195V, T333S, I337M and M343L were detected at very low frequencies (< 1%) globally (Additional file 4).

In Cambodia, K76T showed the highest prevalence and was detected in 98.45% of isolates. High frequencies of Q271E (80.50%), N326S (78.33%), A220S (77.09%) and R371I (74.92%) were also observed. Additionally, I356T (55.42%), I218F (19.50%), G353V (15.79%), F145I (15.17%) and H97Y/L (14.86%) were also observed, while the remaining mutations occurred at low frequencies (Additional file 4).

Isolates from Myanmar showed high prevalence of M74I, N75D/E and K76T mutations, each occurring in 82.67% of isolates. Similarly, A220S and Q271E were detected in 84.00% of isolates, while R371I and I356T were observed in 85.33% and 80.00% of isolates, respectively. N326S showed a comparatively lower prevalence of 26.67% (Additional file 4).

In Uganda, most mutations were detected at low frequencies. K76T, M74I, N75D/E and A220S were each present in 8.93% of isolates, while Q271E and R371I were observed in 12.50% and 10.71% of isolates, respectively. D24Y and R34G mutations were detected exclusively in Ugandan isolates at low frequencies (Additional file 4).

Indian isolates predominantly showed the M74I, N75D/E, K76T and N326S mutations, each detected in 58.49% of isolates, whereas A220S, Q271E and R371I were observed at a lower frequency of 30.19% (Additional file 4).

Genetic diversity

Analysis of 719 global Pfcrt sequences revealed 65 haplotypes with an overall Hd of 0.897 ± 0.006 and π of 0.004. Cambodia exhibited the highest haplotype and nucleotide diversity, followed by India and Myanmar. Myanmar and India showed positive Tajima’s D values, while Cambodia and Uganda displayed negative values. Among these, only India exhibited a statistically significant Tajima’s D value (D = 3.946, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

In light of the challenges faced in eliminating malaria, re-assessing past drug regimens is essential to optimize current treatments for better efficacy [1]. Assessing current status of CQ resistance both globally and in India is important, as studies suggest a potential return of CQ sensitivity in certain regions [16, 17, 31, 32]. This study examined the genetic diversity of Pfcrt in Indian and global isolates, with a focus on exon 2. While exon 2 has been extensively studied, the genetic diversity of the full Pfcrt gene has received less attention. This study is the first to analyse both exon 2 and the complete Pfcrt gene using isolates from multiple states in India as well as global datasets, providing a comprehensive understanding of genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics.

The genetic analysis of Pfcrt exon 2 in Pf populations across India reveals high haplotype and nucleotide diversity, along with a significantly positive Tajima’s D value, suggesting deviation from neutral evolution, potentially due to population structure across regions or the co-circulation of different haplotypes [33]. Additionally, India’s high malaria burden and endemicity likely contribute to the observed genetic diversity in Pf [34, 35]. Significant spatial and temporal variability was also observed in Pfcrt exon 2. Haplotype diversity and overall genetic diversity varied markedly among states and across time periods, with Tajima’s D values suggesting shifts in selective pressures both geographically and temporally.

Genetic diversity peaked during 2010–2019, followed by 2000–2009 despite a reduction of malaria incidence during these years [2, 36–39]. This is supported by positive Tajima’s D values observed during both periods. Observed pattern can likely be explained by the coexistence of long-standing SP and CQ-resistant lineages with newly emerging variants, whose frequencies have been shaped by changes in treatment policies over time. In contrast, purifying selection was observed during 1993–1999, when malaria incidence was comparatively higher, and again in 2020–2023, a period of low transmission, possibly reflecting clonal expansion in high-burden settings and effective drug pressure removing less-fit variants in recent years [37]. Temporal variation observed in genetic diversity reflects the dynamic nature of evolutionary forces acting on Pfcrt, potentially driven by drug-driven selection pressures and changes in treatment policies. The elevated genetic diversity in Central, Eastern and North–Eastern India likely reflects higher malaria burden in these areas [40]. The higher endemicity in Central India, often associated with forested regions and tribal areas, and in Eastern and North–Eastern India associated with malaria burden from the Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS) region, contributes to increased malaria transmission and greater genetic diversity in these areas [40–43]. Positive and significant Tajima’s D values observed in regions such as Meghalaya, Mizoram and Odisha suggest diversifying selection, likely driven by local factors like host immune responses and drug pressure [10]. This is partly influenced by the GMS, a key source of drug resistance burden for India [41–43]. In contrast, negative Tajima’s D values in regions such as Karnataka could indicate purifying selection, consistent with the significant decline in malaria cases over the past decade [38, 44]. The unique structural Pfcrt profile observed in North–Eastern India could be explained by complex migration of population and gene flow from the GMS [41, 43]. Thus, while regional and spatial factors influence Pfcrt diversity, transmission intensity and drug pressure also have a pronounced impact. Haplotype network analysis identified three major haplotypes in India, including one wild-type haplotype, suggesting a re-emergence of wild-type alleles following withdrawal of drug pressure. However, it remains the third most prevalent haplotype, indicating that while progress is being made, more time is needed for a significant shift in haplotype dominance.

Globally, high haplotype diversity can be attributed to diverse origins of samples across distinct WHO regions, each with unique malaria profile. The observed high haplotype diversity in Cambodia, part of the Western Pacific region, may result from high malaria burden in the region, compounded by the large number of sequences analysed [2]. The combination of high haplotype diversity and low nucleotide diversity in the Cambodian samples likely reflects a population bottleneck followed by rapid expansion, during which new mutations accumulate and generate many closely related haplotypes with minimal sequence divergence [45]. Similarly, India and Myanmar are among the largest contributors to malaria cases in Southeast Asia [2]. This explains the high nucleotide and haplotype diversities documented in the region. Additionally, ongoing social and political instability in Myanmar often leaves vulnerable populations more exposed to malaria and diminishes the focus on prevention efforts [2, 46]. In Indian samples, a statistically significant Tajima’s D value (D = 3.946, p < 0.001) likely reflects the coexistence of diverse Pfcrt haplotypes in the population. The haplotype network revealed 65 haplotypes including several singletons with distinct country-specific clusters. Haplotype sharing was observed only between two countries, Myanmar and Uganda, in small proportions. This was corroborated by STRUCTURE analyses, which revealed substantial genetic differentiation indicating that natural selection has played a significant role in shaping the genetic diversity of Pfcrt. Six codons (97, 145, 220, 326, 356 and 371) were identified to be under positive selection and two codons (263 and 350) under purifying selection. Some of these sites are associated with CQ resistance, while others are linked to piperaquine resistance, highlighting the role of varying national drug policies in exerting selective pressures on the gene and shaping its global genetic diversity [6, 47]. Furthermore, the SNPs in strong LD are likely co-inherited, indicating potential functional mutations that enhance the parasite's adaptive capacity. The strong LD observed between codons 72 and 74 in India and globally between codons 74 and 75 likely reflects the close physical proximity of these SNPs within the gene.

The findings of this study have important implications for malaria surveillance and treatment policy. The persistence of chloroquine resistance despite its withdrawal highlights the need for continued molecular surveillance to monitor Pfcrt diversity and resistance trends, particularly in regions such as North–East India prone to cross-border transmission. The presence of both resistant and wild-type haplotypes could indicate a possible re-emergence of CQ sensitivity in certain areas in the future. However, any reconsideration of CQ use must be guided by region-specific genetic data and aligned with national malaria control strategies.

Limitations of the study

The Indian isolates span a wide temporal and geographical range and the number of samples available for each state and period was inconsistent. Period-wise analyses were conducted using the available samples for each time period, but the sample sets within periods were not identical across states or regions. This heterogeneity may influence the observed genetic patterns and limit our ability to draw precise conclusions about specific periods or states. Therefore, our interpretations emphasise broad nationwide evolutionary trends in Pfcrt diversity rather than strict temporal or spatial equivalence. Similarly, the global dataset consisted of full length Pfcrt sequences available from only four countries. Due to the lack of uniform temporal representation and unequal sample sizes across countries, a detailed period-wise analysis could not be performed for the global dataset. This heterogeneity may have influenced the observed mutation patterns.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive insights into the genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics of Pfcrt gene in Pf across both Indian and global contexts. Analysis of Indian isolates, revealed distinct spatial and temporal distribution patterns within exon 2. Among the identified haplotypes, CVIET and SVMNT emerged as well-established chloroquine-resistant variants. The co-circulation of these prominent haplotypes, alongside less frequent (CVMNT, RVMNT and CVIKT) region-specific variants, drives the complex genetic diversity observed across Indian populations. Globally, full-length Pfcrt sequences revealed high haplotype diversity, with limited haplotype sharing between countries and strong genetic differentiation. Codon-based selection analysis also identified six sites under diversifying selection and two under purifying selection. The observed spatial and temporal variation, coupled with signatures of selection and strong population structure, highlight the role of drug policies, transmission dynamics and host-parasite interactions in shaping the genetic landscape of Pf in India and globally.
