Toward trustworthy water quality monitoring under sensor unreliability
Authors: Jin Li, Kleanthis Malialis, Demetrios G. Eliades, Marios M. Polycarpou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water Distribution Networks (WDNs) are vital for public health and economic activity, and early contamination detection and source-region prioritization are essential for timely response. Although Internet-of-things sensors and machine learning enable online monitoring, water quality sensors are costly and prone to faults, which can distort measurements and lead to false alarms or missed contamination events. Existing contamination localization methods typically assume reliable, fault-free measurements, an assumption often violated in real-world deployments. Here, we propose a unified data-driven framework that explicitly accounts for sensor unreliability while supporting contamination source-region prioritization. In this study, we focus on the monitoring of chlorine-reactive pollutants such as arsenite as a primary application scenario to evaluate the framework’s performance. The framework jointly integrates sensor fault detection, localization, isolation, faulty signal reconstruction, and contamination source-region prioritization within a single monitoring pipeline. A convolutional autoencoder detects and reconstructs corrupted measurements to recover the underlying clean signal, while a CNN–LSTM model performs fault isolation and supports downstream contamination analysis. An explicit fault–contamination differentiation mechanism is further introduced to distinguish sensor-induced deviations from genuine contamination signatures. Comprehensive evaluations are conducted on three realistic WDNs under tested network layouts, synthetic chlorine-reactive contamination scenarios, and evaluated fault models. The results demonstrate that while pinpointing the exact single node is challenging under sparse sensor coverage, the framework can narrow down likely candidate source regions, achieving up to approximately 80% Top-10 source-region prioritization accuracy. These findings highlight that jointly modeling sensor faults and contamination processes is useful for fault-aware contamination source-region prioritization under degraded sensing conditions.

Introduction

Water Distribution Networks (WDNs) are vital for public health and economic development by providing clean water to customers and industry. Early detection of contamination and prioritization of likely source regions are crucial, as illustrated by major incidents: the Milwaukee Cryptosporidium outbreak affected over 400,000 residents1; in November 2007, a human error in Nokia, Finland led to the injection of 450 m3 of wastewater into the drinking water distribution system, causing a large gastroenteritis outbreak. Thousands were infected, hundreds hospitalized, and a 12-week total water-use ban was imposed2; and the 2007 Taihu Cyanobacteria Bloom3 in Jiangsu Province, China, resulted from severe eutrophication, contaminating Wuxi’s tap water and triggering widespread water shortages and public panic. In such cases, while contamination detection in WDNs is essential, narrowing down likely contamination source regions is critical to support timely and effective mitigation.

Ensuring fault-aware water quality monitoring in WDNs is particularly challenging due to the vulnerability of sensor measurements to faults. Sensors may experience communication failures, hardware degradation, calibration drift, or environmental interference4,5. Such faults can distort measurements, leading to inaccurate contamination detection, false alarms, or even missed contamination events. If sensor unreliability is not properly addressed, monitoring systems may produce misleading decisions, undermining public safety and operational stability. To mitigate these risks, effective mechanisms for handling sensor faults are essential. Dealing with sensor faults typically involves three key steps. First, the presence of a fault must be detected (Fault detection). Second, the specific faulty sensor should be identified (Fault localization). Third, the underlying cause of the fault should be determined (Fault isolation), enabling targeted corrective actions such as recalibration or maintenance. However, because maintenance intervals can span several months, monitoring systems must remain operational despite missing or corrupted inputs. Online monitoring of WDNs relies heavily on accurate sensor measurements; however, sensor faults and data corruption can compromise monitoring performance6. In this work, we propose a novel method for faulty signal reconstruction which helps recover the underlying clean signal, thus preserving data continuity and supporting fault-aware downstream monitoring.

Existing research has addressed sensor fault diagnosis and contamination localization largely as separate problems. On one hand, a wide range of approaches have been explored for monitoring sensing reliability in WDNs, including model-based, knowledge-based, and data-driven methods7. While model-based techniques depend on accurate hydraulic models or expert knowledge, such requirements are often difficult to satisfy in real-world deployments, where models may be inaccurate, and network topology is only partially known. Under these conditions, data-driven approaches offer a more flexible and scalable alternative by learning system behavior directly from sensor observations. Earlier work has applied tools such as Hidden Markov Models8, Gaussian processes9, local PCA10, and regression-based techniques11 for fault detection and localization. However, many of these approaches operate offline and cannot isolate fault types. In addition, broader research on sensing systems has explored aspects such as sensor deployment and fault-tolerant architectures to enhance monitoring reliability. For example,12 investigates learning-based strategies for optimizing mobile sensor placement, while13 proposes a fault-tolerant UAV-assisted sensing framework. These studies focus on system-level design rather than data-driven fault diagnosis.

On the other hand, a parallel body of work investigates contamination detection and localization. Model-based strategies incorporate hydraulic behavior, water quality dynamics, and reaction mechanisms14–16, but their reliance on detailed system models limits their applicability. Data-driven methods can alleviate these challenges by exploiting patterns available in sensor observations, thereby reducing the reliance on detailed physical models, particularly when meaningful correlations exist among the monitored variables; an updated overview is provided in ref. 17. Recent advances include methods that integrate anomaly detection, classification, and probabilistic modeling. For instance, Li et al.18 integrated individual and aggregated features with adaptive thresholds for alarm generation; however, their approach is restricted to contamination detection and lacks localization capabilities. Similarly, while Grbvcic et al.19 employed neural networks and random forests alongside Monte Carlo simulations to estimate source characteristics, their method requires extensive contamination scenarios for offline training. Bayesian formulations with mobile sensing platforms enable online intrusion probability updates20, and hydraulic-structure-based methods trace contamination via early detections and flow directions21. More recently, an online decentralized framework22 leverages an LSTM-based variational autoencoder with drift-aware thresholds for online detection. Despite these advances, most approaches assume reliable sensing and do not account for the possibility that faulty measurements may disguise or mimic contamination, leaving contamination localization vulnerable under real-world operational uncertainties.

As a result, current WDN monitoring frameworks exhibit a fragmentation gap: they either manage sensor unreliability or perform contamination localization, but rarely consider their interaction. This separation can limit the reliability of online monitoring decisions in realistic operational settings where sensor faults and contamination may coexist.

In response to these limitations, we propose a unified and data-driven machine-learning framework for comprehensive WDN monitoring. Since sparse sensor coverage makes it difficult to uniquely identify the true source node, in this study, we perform contamination source-region prioritization rather than exact contamination source localization. Therefore, the objective is to rank or narrow down likely candidate source regions to support subsequent investigation and response. Results

Before presenting the experimental results, we briefly summarize the problem setting, framework workflow, and evaluation context to guide interpretation. We consider WDN monitoring under two interacting anomalies: sensor faults and water contamination. Specifically, sensor fault detection determines whether a sensor fault occurs, fault localization identifies which sensor is faulty, fault isolation classifies the fault type, and faulty signal reconstruction recovers the underlying clean measurement. Contamination source-region prioritization then aims to rank or narrow down likely candidate source regions, rather than necessarily identifying a unique source node, especially under sparse sensor coverage. To address these tasks, the proposed framework integrates CAE-based sensor fault detection, fault localization, and faulty signal reconstruction; CNN–LSTM-based fault isolation; and rule-based fault–contamination differentiation for candidate contamination source-region ranking. The framework is evaluated on three realistic WDNs of different scales, including Hanoi, ZJ, and L-Town, under four representative sensor faults: Missing, Bias, Spiky, and Drift. Here, Missing represents data loss, Bias represents systematic measurement offset, Spiky represents transient abnormal peaks, and Drift represents gradual sensor degradation. Full methodological and experimental details, including the formal problem formulation, network descriptions, fault generation, evaluation metrics, and algorithmic procedure, are provided in the Methods section.

Finally, we evaluate the end-to-end contamination source-region prioritization performance against existing methods and ablated variants. While previous experiments evaluated individual modules, this section assesses the end-to-end candidate contamination source-region ranking performance of the proposed framework. We benchmark against state-of-the-art methods21,22 and perform an ablation study by removing the Signal Reconstruction and Differentiation Rules components to quantify their contributions under sensor fault interference. The evaluation is conducted on 20 randomized scenarios with varying arsenite injection locations and random single-sensor faults (injected in two batches) across the Hanoi and ZJ networks, under sparse sensor coverage (19% and 9%, respectively).

As shown in Table 3, the Full Pipeline consistently achieves the best Top-k candidate-ranking performance and the lowest false alarm rate. The ablation study highlights two key findings: (1) Signal Reconstruction is essential for preserving data integrity, as faulty measurements otherwise propagate through the latent space and degrade candidate source ranking; (2) Differentiation Rules act as a critical filter to prevent sensor faults from being misclassified as contamination, and their removal leads to a marked increase in false alarms. In comparison,21 relies on hydraulic backtracking without accounting for sensor faults, limiting its ability to handle fault-contamination coexistence, while22 adopts a decentralized scheme but similarly lacks fault-awareness, resulting in excessive false alarms and degraded candidate ranking under overlapping events. Despite these constraints, the proposed framework maintains useful Top-k source-region narrowing performance, including 80% Top-10 accuracy on the sparsely monitored ZJ network. Sensor fault severity sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the operational boundaries of the proposed framework, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on fault severity across the four representative fault types using the Hanoi network as the experimental benchmark. This analysis aims to quantify the impact of fault magnitude on detection sensitivity, isolation accuracy, and localization performance. Fault magnitudes were categorized into three decreasing intensity levels: Obvious (baseline), Moderate (1/2 of baseline), and Subtle (1/4 of baseline). Except for the Missing fault, the parameters of all other fault types were scaled accordingly. For Bias, the offset ranges were set to [0.05, 0.1] (Obvious), [0.025, 0.05] (Moderate), and [0.0125, 0.025] (Subtle). For Spiky readings, the noise mean and standard deviation were reduced from (0.1, 0.05) to (0.05, 0.025) and (0.025, 0.0125). For Drift, the incremental rates per step were defined as [0.0005, 0.001], [0.00025, 0.0005], and [0.000125, 0.00025], respectively.

Table 5 summarizes the impact of fault severity on detection, isolation, and localization. Results are averaged over 20 independent scenarios and reported as the mean with standard deviation for statistical reliability. As severity decreases from Obvious to Subtle, fault isolation accuracy degrades, particularly for Bias and Spiky faults, whose subtle deviations are more easily confused with Drift. This also leads to increased FNR in some cases (e.g., Spiky), reflecting reduced detectability under low signal-to-noise conditions. In contrast, detection and fault-localization metrics remain relatively consistent with consistently high HitRate@P% and generally low FNR and FPR across most settings. These experiments assess whether the proposed framework can maintain consistent fault localization, isolation, and reconstruction performance when multiple sensors are faulty simultaneously.

First, we consider concurrent sensor faults of the same type. Discussion

Water Distribution Networks are fundamental to public health and wellbeing, as well as economic stability, yet their safe operation is increasingly challenged by the concurrent presence of contamination events and unreliable sensor measurements. In real-world deployments, sensor degradation can obscure contamination signals, thereby undermining monitoring decisions. Achieving fault-aware water quality monitoring, therefore, requires integrated solutions that explicitly account for sensor faults while preserving contamination detection and source-region prioritization capability. In this study, we introduce a unified two-phase deep learning framework that jointly addresses sensor fault diagnosis and contamination source candidate ranking within a single unified architecture. By coupling sensor fault detection, localization, isolation, and faulty signal reconstruction with contamination source-region narrowing, the framework prevents corrupted sensor measurements from propagating errors into downstream contamination analysis. Validation across three realistic WDNs demonstrates that the proposed approach maintains high diagnostic accuracy and improves Top-k candidate source-region narrowing performance under the tested synthetic chlorine-reactive contamination scenarios, network layouts, and sensor fault models.

Intuitively, the improved monitoring performance stems from explicitly accounting for sensor unreliability within the monitoring pipeline. Unlike conventional contamination-localization workflows that implicitly assume fault-free measurements, the proposed framework first performs sensor fault diagnosis (detection, localization, and isolation) and then differentiates sensor faults from contamination events using dedicated decision rules. This design prevents fault-induced deviations from being misinterpreted as contamination signatures, thereby reducing false alarms and improving contamination source candidate ranking when faults and contamination coexist. As a result, contamination detection and source-region narrowing remain useful for decision support under the tested degraded sensing conditions.

Despite these contributions, this study identifies specific limitations that open avenues for future research. From an algorithmic perspective, while the framework maintains high accuracy across most tested sensor fault diagnosis scenarios, its isolation performance exhibits a noticeable decline under extreme cases such as four concurrent Drift faults. Furthermore, the current differentiation and localization mechanisms are established based on chlorine reactivity, with arsenite serving as a representative pollutant; therefore, the results may not be directly generalizable to non-reactive contaminants or systems without chlorine residuals. Future work will therefore focus on developing more robust diagnostic algorithms specifically designed for extreme sensing uncertainties, such as exploring attention-based mechanisms or disentangled representation learning to enhance isolation sensitivity under high-cardinality concurrent faults. Additionally, we aim to extend the framework to incorporate a broader range of water quality signals and diverse contamination types, thereby enhancing the generalization and fault-aware decision-support capability of the monitoring system across more complex, real-world scenarios.

The proposed framework offers several key advantages for fault-aware water-quality monitoring. First, it establishes a unified monitoring paradigm that explicitly accounts for sensor unreliability, eliminating the common assumption of fault-free measurements in contamination analysis. By jointly integrating sensor fault detection, localization, isolation, faulty signal reconstruction, and contamination source candidate ranking within a single pipeline, the framework enhances diagnostic coverage and reduces fault-induced interference under concurrent fault–contamination scenarios. Second, the framework improves Top-k candidate source-region narrowing even when sensor faults and contamination events overlap, demonstrating its potential for decision support under degraded sensing conditions. This capability is particularly important in operational WDNs, where sensor degradation is inevitable. Third, by combining a lightweight CAE with a CNN–LSTM classifier, the framework enables localized, online anomaly interpretation at low computational cost. Our computational analysis (see “Methods”) confirms that the approach remains efficient even in resource-constrained deployments. Finally, the centralized deployment design simplifies real-world implementation. By hosting computation at a single processing unit, the framework reduces communication overhead and synchronization complexity, while avoiding the need for sensor-level processing hardware. This architecture facilitates scalability and cost-effective maintenance in practical WDN settings.

However, the proposed framework has a few limitations. First, the differentiation between contamination events and sensor faults relies on two predefined hydraulic-temporal rules, which are sensitive to network scale and sensing density. In sparse sensing configurations, Rule 1 may encounter identification challenges. This is because the absence of a downstream response, which serves as a key indicator of a local sensor fault, could simply result from insufficient sensor coverage rather than a lack of physical propagation. Furthermore, Rule 2 may be constrained in small-scale networks where the hydraulic transit time between nodes is shorter than the sensor sampling interval. Under these conditions, the sequential propagation pattern along the flow direction becomes difficult to distinguish, as the contaminant can traverse multiple monitoring points within a single sampling step. Consequently, the method is most effective when the sensing frequency is aligned with the network’s hydraulic dynamics and the WDN maintains reasonable sensing coverage.

By improving fault-aware contamination monitoring under sensor degradation, the proposed framework contributes to the resilience and sustainability of water distribution systems. Differentiating sensor faults from genuine contamination events reduces false alarms and unnecessary chemical interventions, while enabling timely and targeted mitigation strategies. In this way, the framework supports proactive water quality management and strengthens infrastructure preparedness against operational uncertainty and environmental stressors.

These findings underscore the necessity of moving beyond fault-free sensing assumptions in WDN monitoring. Designing monitoring systems that explicitly account for sensor unreliability is essential for achieving fault-aware online decision support. This paradigm is particularly relevant for critical infrastructures, where sensing imperfections are inevitable, and system resilience depends on integrated data-driven strategies.

From an implementation perspective, many critical infrastructure (CI) operators have already deployed IoT sensors for routine monitoring. Consequently, adopting the proposed framework primarily involves integrating the proposed intelligent analytics layer into existing SCADA/IoT infrastructures rather than investing in additional hardware. The computational analysis confirms its feasibility. Such integration aligns with ongoing CI digitalization efforts.

Problem formulation

WDNs are modeled as graphs, where nodes represent junctions and undirected edges represent pipes26. A graph G = (V, E) consists of node set V and edge set E, with each edge ei,j = (vi, vj) ∈ E, i ≠ j. The sensors are installed on the nodes; as full sensor coverage is impractical, only k ≪ ∣V∣ sensors are installed. The input signals from all sensorsS = {s1,…,sk} are represented as X ∈ Rk×T = {x1,…,xk}, where xj ∈ RT corresponds to the measurements from sensor sj and j = {1, …, k}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{i}\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{k\times d}=\{{x}_{1}^{i},\ldots ,{x}_{k}^{i}\}$$\end{document}bi∈Rk×d={x1i,…,xki} represents the i-th batch of signals collected from all k sensors. T and d denote the total signal length and batch length, respectively. In this study, contamination is detected through chlorine measurements, as chlorine is a widely used disinfectant and sensitive indicator of water quality due to its reaction with many contaminants27,28. However, chlorine sensors themselves are prone to faults. Therefore, we jointly consider sensor fault diagnosis for chlorine sensors and contamination monitoring.

We consider two main types of anomalies in WDNs: sensor faults and water contamination. This study focuses on four representative sensor fault types commonly encountered in sensor networks: Missing (M), Bias (B), Spiky (S), and Drift (D). These fault types capture distinct and fundamental failure mechanisms, including data loss (Missing), systematic offset (Bias), transient anomalies (Spiky), and gradual temporal degradation (Drift), and are widely adopted in the literature4,33. Their generation in our experiments follows the approach in ref. 34. While this set does not exhaust all possible fault behaviors, it provides a principled basis for evaluating fault diagnosis performance, as more complex or application-specific faults can often be interpreted as variations or combinations of these basic patterns. Accordingly, we use these four types to assess the effectiveness of the proposed framework, while noting that the methodology is not inherently restricted to this specific set. Data generation

In this study, scenario data are generated using EPyT-Flow37 based on realistic seasonal water demand patterns derived from historical consumption data reported by water utilities38 at 30-minute resolution. Hydraulic and water-quality dynamics are simulated using EPANET and EPANET-MSX, which model contaminant transport and reaction processes within the network39. Gaussian noise N(0, 0.01) is added to all sensors, and chlorine is injected at 0.7 mg/L at the reservoir. The offline dataset comprises six months of normal operation and one month of synthetic fault data for each fault type, while the online dataset contains six months of mixed scenarios with injected faults and contamination events. Fault simulations follow four common types33: missing data (value set to zero), bias (offset of 0.05-0.1), spiky readings (noise with mean 0.1, std 0.05), and drift (increase of 0.0005–0.001 per step). Contamination scenarios are generated by injecting arsenite (0.8–1 mg/L) at a random node, specifically N5, N33, and N739 in Hanoi, ZJ, and L-Town, respectively.

We propose a two-phase data-driven framework designed for integrated sensor fault detection, localization, isolation, faulty signal reconstruction, and contamination source-region inference. Step 4: Contamination detection and candidate source-region inference. While multiple sensors may fail simultaneously, it is crucial to distinguish sensor faults from contamination events. This distinction is grounded in the hydraulic and reactive transport behavior of contaminants in WDNs. Under advective-dispersive transport, contaminant concentrations evolve gradually along the flow direction, resulting in spatially correlated anomalies that typically weaken downstream due to dilution and reaction kinetics. In contrast, common sensor faults (e.g., bias, drift, or missing readings) introduce localized measurement deviations without affecting the physical water quality state, and therefore do not exhibit downstream propagation patterns.

Based on these principles, we apply two differentiation rules:Rule 1: A sudden and localized deviation in chlorine concentration at a node, without a corresponding downstream response, suggests a local sensor fault.Rule 2: Anomalies that exhibit a downstream propagation pattern—i.e., consistent temporal onset along the flow direction and non-increasing magnitude until reaching a physical floor (e.g., zero chlorine)—indicate a contamination event.

Computational analysis

External memory requirements: The proposed framework is lightweight in memory usage. At each online inference step, only a single batch of data needs to be stored, with dimensionality k × Tcae, where k is the number of sensors and Tcae is the CAE input length.

Model parameters: The total number of learnable parameters for our CAE and CNN-LSTM models is 193k + 111,040 and 35,940, respectively, where k is the sensor count. In the L-Town scenario, the CAE model occupies approximately 1 MB assuming 8 bytes per parameter; with INT8 quantization (1 byte per parameter), the size reduces to about 120 KB.

Training stage: During offline training, ∣Dn∣ historical normal signals are used to train the CAE, while ∣Df∣ historical faulty signals are used to train the CNN-LSTM. The total historical dataset is D = {Dn, Df}, with size k × Toff. Training is performed entirely offline.

Prediction stage: The system first performs a single forward propagation step of the batch with size k × Tcae through the CAE for reconstruction. Sensors with reconstruction losses exceeding their thresholds (i.e., fewer than k dimensions) are flagged as potentially faulty. Each flagged dimension then undergoes a single forward propagation step through the CNN-LSTM module to isolate the fault type. Finally, Rule 1 and Rule 2 are applied to distinguish sensor faults from contamination events. Suppose the average number of downstream sensors for each sensor is h. Then, the complexity of both rules is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{O}}(k\times h)$$\end{document}O(k×h), where k is the number of sensors to check. Depending on the network topology, h is typically smaller than k and can be significantly smaller. If an event is identified as contamination, the system performs a single forward propagation step using the k prediction results in Eq. (4) to infer the candidate contamination source region. This design reduces computational overhead by applying the CNN-LSTM only to a small subset of dimensions, ensuring efficient online processing.

Evaluation metrics

Fault detection: False Positive Rate (FPR) and False Negative Rate (FNR) are used to quantify detection reliability. FPR represents the probability that the system triggers a false alarm during normal operation, while FNR measures the risk of missing a genuine fault. Additionally, Detection Latency (Det_Latency) is used to quantify the temporal responsiveness of the framework, which is critical in practice for timely intervention and mitigation of potential water quality risks.

Fault isolation & Contamination source-region prioritization: To evaluate fault isolation and contamination source-region prioritization, we adopt a Top-K Accuracy framework. For fault isolation, we report Top-1 Accuracy, reflecting the model’s ability to correctly identify the dominant fault type. For contamination source-region prioritization, we report Top-1, Top-3, and Top-10 Accuracy. While Top-1 represents exact source identification, Top-3 and Top-10 provide a more practical assessment in water distribution networks, where the inverse source identification problem is often ill-posed due to sparse sensor placement and complex hydraulic interactions. These metrics reflect the model’s ability to narrow down a set of likely candidate locations for further investigation. Importantly, Top-10 Accuracy should not be interpreted as exact source localization; instead, it measures whether the true source is included within a prioritized candidate set.

Faulty signal reconstruction: The Mean Squared Error (MSE)35 is used to quantify the accuracy of the reconstructed signals. By measuring the deviation between reconstructed outputs and ground-truth normal patterns, MSE provides an indication of how effectively the model removes fault-induced distortions while preserving the underlying system dynamics.

Fault Localization: We utilize HitRate@P%36 to evaluate anomaly localization in multivariate time series as it provides a more reliable assessment for high-dimensional sensor data compared to fixed-threshold precision metrics. Unlike traditional point-wise evaluations, this ranking-based metric accounts for the relative magnitude of reconstruction errors across all dimensions, which is particularly suitable for identifying faulty sensors when the absolute error magnitude may vary due to different sensor types.
