Grammar of the Wave: Towards Explainable Multivariate Time Series Event Detection via Neuro-Symbolic VLM Agents
Sky Chenwei Wan, Tianjun Hou, Yifei Wang, Xiqing Chang, Aymeric Jan

Abstract
Time Series Event Detection (TSED) has long been an important task with critical applications across many high-stakes domains. Unlike statistical anomalies, events are defined by semantics with complex internal structures, which are difficult to learn inductively from scarce labeled data in real-world settings. In light of this, we introduce Knowledge-Guided TSED, a new setting where a model is given a natural-language event description and must ground it to intervals in multivariate signals with little or no training data. To tackle this challenge, we introduce Event Logic Tree (ELT), a novel knowledge representation framework to bridge linguistic descriptions and physical time series data via modeling the intrinsic temporal-logic structures of events. Based on ELT, we present a neuro-symbolic VLM agent framework that iteratively instantiates primitives from signal visualizations and composes them under ELT constraints, producing both detected intervals and faithful explanations in the form of instantiated trees. To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we release a benchmark based on real-world time series data with expert knowledge and annotations. Experiments and human evaluation demonstrate the superiority of our method compared to supervised fine-tuning baselines and existing zero-shot time series reasoning frameworks based on LLMs/VLMs. We also show that ELT is critical in mitigating VLMs’ inherent hallucination in matching signal morphology with event semantics.

Introduction

Time Series Event Detection (TSED)
aims to locate segments in time series data where specific events happen
. It has long been an important task across many high-stakes domains, including health [
21
;
16
], and energy production
(
7
)
. Compared to other tasks in time series analysis, TSED is especially challenging. Unlike classification, which aims to assign single labels to time series sequences
(
14
)
, TSED requires fine-grained discrimination to be made on small timeframes or timestamps. Compared to anomaly detection, which identifies statistical drifts
(
24
)
, events
usually carry
specific semantic meanings (e.g.,
“a sharp rise in A followed by a plateau in B”
) with internal temporal-logic structures across multiple physical channels.

The prevalent paradigm for TSED task is inductive pattern recognition. Models are trained to learn patterns from a large number of labeled examples. However, in certain domains, acquiring annotated data is time and resource consuming, making the effective training of such models difficult.

Explainability is another shortcoming of inductive learning. Although gradients
(
20
)
or intermediate activations
(
19
)
of neural models can provide transparency, humans do not trust the results without logically organized clues. Such AI systems are difficult to be adopted in the domains where trust between AI and human experts is indispensable.

In this study, we mitigate the issues by looking at TSED from a cognitive point of view: describing the patterns of events on time series with natural language is generally easier than collecting a large amount of data and annotations. This is in line with the cognitive process when human experts interpret time series data: read unstructured linguistic descriptions, comprehend the internal compositional structure, and deduce the occurrence of events from the time series measurements. Therefore, it naturally provides better explainability.

We formalize the problem as

T

S

E

etection (K-TSED)
. As no training data and annotations are needed, we switch the task setting from inductive pattern recognition to deductive knowledge grounding. However, how to accurately and logically ground the knowledge to time series is still a problem. The example below demonstrates an event description in the energy domain:

During the
Buildup
event, the

should remain stable across the entire phase. Simultaneously, the

When humans describe such events, atomic morphological patterns (e.g.
“

,
“

) are defined, along with temporal-logic relations (e.g.
“simultaneously”

. Therefore, an explainable solution should not only map the global event to the time series interval correctly, but also map sub-patterns to their instances accurately, as evidence supporting the global decision that could easily be verified by humans.

To achieve the joint goal of accuracy and explainability, we introduce Event Logic Tree (ELT), a novel knowledge representation framework that translates unstructured event descriptions to tree structures. The tree structures faithfully model the temporal-logic relationships between atomic patterns, clearly demonstrating the hierarchy that constitutes the global event. Based on ELT, we create
SELA
, a neuro-symbolic VLM agent system for zero-shot K-TSED, comprising two types of agents:
Logic Analyst agents
construct ELT schema from the unstructured event description, and
Signal Inspector agents
incrementally locate and refine time series intervals guided by the ELT schema. In
SELA
, ELT plays the critical role in the system as the structured intermediate representation of the target event. It not only guides the agents’ reasoning and behavior, but also provides well-organized clues that can easily be verified by human experts.

To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we create the first K-TSED benchmark based on real-world time series data collected from the energy domain. The event descriptions and ground truth labels are created and verified by human experts. We demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms the previous inductive pattern recognition methods in low resource settings, and the existing deductive knowledge grounding solutions without ELT, approaching human-level performance. Through an ablation study, we also demonstrate the critical role of ELT in mitigating VLM’s inherent hallucination problem.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows: (1) A new problem setting K-TSED. (2) Our solution: A knowledge representation framework with an implementation based on neural-symbolic VLM agents. (3) A new benchmark built from real-world time series data, with experiments showing the superiority of our method.

3
Related Work

3.1
Time Series Symbolic Representation

To achieve the goal of accurate event detection with explainability, the internal structures of events need to be explicitly modeled. Based on the example introduced in Section
1
, we set the following desiderata for designing the logic structure:

D1. Hierarchical Representation:

the ability to capture hierarchical structures of events: the way atomic patterns recursively form sub-patterns, and finally the global pattern.

D2. Semantic Quantification:

the ability to quantify the coherence between the signal morphology and the semantics.

D3. Topological Elasticity:

the ability to define events based on the internal temporal-logic structure, agnostic to actual temporal durations: the representation must support “time warping” (elastic stretching or compressing of time) while maintaining logical validity.

We analyze existing symbolic time series representation frameworks based on our desiderata (summarized in Table
1
).

(1)
SAX [
9
;
13
] and ABBA
(
4
)
map time series into string sequences using unsupervised learning. Each character represents a specific shape or amplitude. However, the unsupervised learning is performed on the dataset level, making the symbolic representation dataset-dependent. Moreover, the repeated sub-strings carry semantics but do not quantify coherence (partially satisfying D2), and do not allow hierarchical structure (failing D1) or complex topological relationships (failing D3).

(2)
Logical-Shapelets
(
15
)
represents entire time series with a series of frequently appearing sub-sequences with fixed length, i.e., shapelets (failing D3). Z-Time
(
8
)
improves the representation by making the length variable (satisfying D3). Both the shapelets and discrete representations of Z-Time carry no semantic abstraction (failing D2). Regarding structural modeling, Logical-Shapelets only considers boolean relations, and while Z-Time uses Allen’s algebra
(
1
)
, only limited hierarchy structures can be represented by stacking temporal relation pairs. Therefore, both approaches partially satisfy D1.

(3)
Chronicle System
(
3
)
applies graph structure to represent temporal events, where each sub-event is considered as a node in the graph, and STL
(
12
)
represents events as recursive logical formulas over signals intervals, both satisfying D1. However, both systems model semantic coherence as binary values (true/false) (partially satisfying D2), and their reliance on actual interval durations does not meet D3.

In summary, existing symbolic systems can only make structured representations from time series but not language, thus not satisfying the requirements of K-TSED. In contrast, our proposed ELT framework employs tree structures based on Allen’s algebra and boolean operators to support D1. The definition of tree schema and instantiation on actual time series data are separated to satisfy D3. ELT supports quantifying semantic coherence with neural models over any combination of basic time series signal attributes, satisfying D2.

3.2
Multimodal LLMs for Time Series Reasoning

Though foundation models pre-trained on large-scale time series data have shown promising performance in low-resource settings, fine-tuning is still necessary, and cross-modal reasoning is restricted [
2
;
11
;
6
]. Recently, LLMs’ potential to serve as few/zero-shot time series reasoners have been widely explored. ChatTS
(
23
)
designed an encoder with synthetic data to align time series with language for QA tasks. However, the domain gap between synthetic and real-world data is difficult to bridge, and embedding time series can still introduce the risk of hallucination. VL-Time
(
10
)
visualizes time series data as figures to directly adopt vision-language reasoning capabilities without extra TS-language alignment. While it has shown advantages in classification, visualization inevitably loses precision, and hallucination is still a risk. Our approach employs active visualization tools to overcome precision loss and effectively mitigates hallucination with ELT representations.

Event Logic Tree

4.1
ELT Schema

The Event Logic Tree schema is parsed from textual descriptions only, independent of actual time series data. One event corresponds to one tree.

Definition 1

(Node Schema)
.

The Event Logic Tree Schema is denoted as
\mathcal{S}
. Any node in the schema
n\in\mathcal{S}
belongs to two categories: primitives and composites.

1. Primitive (Leaf Node)

: The atomic signal patterns over single physical channels, denoted as
n_{p}=\langle\tau,c\rangle
, where:

•

\tau
: The
semantic predicate
that describes signal morphology, which can be a compound of basic time series attributes, e.g.,
“a steep linear increase with high-frequency noise”
.

•

c
: The
physical channel
index where this atomic pattern would occur.

2. Composite (Internal Node).

The temporal-logic relations that form the global event hierarchically with the primitives, denoted as
n_{\phi}=\langle\mathcal{N},op\rangle
, where:

•

\mathcal{N}=[n_{1},\dots,n_{k}]
: An ordered list of child nodes, either primitives or lower-level composites.

•

op\in\{\texttt{SEQ},\texttt{SYNC},\texttt{GUARD},\texttt{OR}\}
: The
temporal-logic operator
defining the relationship between child nodes.

4.2
Constitutive Axioms

Based on the definitions above, we establish three axioms to serve as preconditions for valid schema construction, and properly control the search space during instantiation.

Axiom 1

(Constructive Composition)
.

A composite node must add structural meaning. Formally, for any composite
n_{\phi}
,
|\mathcal{N}|\geq 2
.

Remark.

Axiom 1 excludes bad structures with self-nesting, and any logical
NOT
as composite with a single child. We require all primitives to be defined
positively
.

Axiom 2

(Temporal Compactness)
.

The semantics of any node must cover its full temporal span. Any undefined gap inside the temporal spans of composites must be bounded by a hyperparameter.

Remark.

Primitives are considered to be compact by definition. For any composite, assuming the compactness tolerance is 1, a composite whose children are assigned disjoint intervals like
[1,2]
and
[4,6]
will be prohibited by Axiom 2.

Axiom 3

(Physical Exclusivity)
.

A physical channel
c
can only support one active primitive state at any time point
t
.

SELA
Multi-Agent System

We introduce
SELA
, the Time
S

A
gents system, which
realizes
our Event Logic Tree theory.
SELA
empowers
large vision-language models
to act as time series analysis experts, enabling them to perform explainable event detection with supporting clues organized as instantiated event logic trees. Figure
1
shows the overall architecture. We introduce its major components as follows.

Central Logic Engine.

The central logic engine serves as the
“backend”
of
SELA
with a three-layer architecture. The
Definition Layer
is at the top to store the schema of the event logic tree, including definitions of primitives (semantic description and physical channel designation) and the structural relationships between them defined by nesting operators in Section
4
The
Instantiation Layer
stores the intervals and confidence scores of instantiated primitives, and the
Data Layer
stores the multivariate time series data. The engine is responsible for computing the confidence of the whole event by recursively propagating the confidence scores from primitives to the root node via fuzzy logic operators.

Unified Interface.

The interface connects the agents and the backend. It provides a
joint visual-symbolic visualization
that displays the time series data side-by-side with the state of the logic tree. It parses the function calls from agents, executing actions, and updating the view accordingly: if the action submits an artifact (e.g., a schema definition or primitive instance), the interface passes it to the logic engine, updates the logic tree status based on the execution results (e.g., confidence score on each node). If the action calls active visualization tools, the interface queries the backend and processes the selected signal section. Agents can dynamically
zoom in
on specific time windows augmented with view-dependent normalization to reveal local signal morphologies, and place
reference markers
as anchors to align temporal boundaries across channels.

Agent Reasoning.

The agent reasoning can be formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). At step
k
, the state
S_{k}
is defined by the task prompt
\mathcal{P}_{\text{task}}
and the cumulative interaction history
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
. Specifically, the history consists of a sequence where each step comprises the agent’s thoughts
r_{i}
, the action
a_{i}
, and the multimodal observation
o_{i}
(e.g., execution logs, signal plots, or error feedback) returned from the environment. The process is formalized as follows:

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\pi(a\mid\mathcal{P}_{\text{task}},\mathcal{H}_{k-1})

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\oplus\langle r_{k},a_{k},o_{k}\rangle

The action space of the agents is
standardized JSON function calls
.

Inter-Agent Collaboration.

The complex grounding task is decomposed into two jobs, performed by two specialized agents. The agents are provided with different tool sets, and collaborate via
the shared environment
.
Agents submit their jobs as
artifacts
, which are job-specific data structures with pre-defined schema
(
17
)
. Artifacts are parsed from agents’ function calls, and strict schema checks are enforced to prevent error propagation in the system.

(i)
Logic Analyst
is responsible for the semantic translation of the unstructured textual event description into an Event Logic Tree schema. Its primary duties include identifying atomic primitives, deducing hierarchical temporal-logical relationships (e.g.,
SEQ
,
SYNC
), and organizing these as a

(ii)
Signal Inspector
instantiates the ELT schema by examining the actual time series data. By utilizing visualization tools, the Inspector analyzes both global and local morphologies of the signal. It submits candidate detections as
Instantiation Artifacts
(primitive intervals with confidence scores) and refines these boundaries through cross-referencing the signal view with the updated logic tree status. It maximizes the confidence score at the root node, which represents the global event.

6.1
Data Collection

We use multivariate time series data from
pressure test jobs
, a critical well exploration operation in the oil and gas industry.
The objective of pressure test is to acquire the underground pressure at different depth to characterize the reservoir.
During pressure test jobs, sensors are installed at certain depths in the drilling well for remote measurements. We collected a dataset of 41 real underground pressure test jobs from drilling wells in the North Sea.

In one pressure test job, the pressure acquisitions can be successful (i.e.,
valid test
) or unsuccessful (
lost seal
). A successful test contains two successive sub-stages:
drawdown
and
buildup
. Two channels are essential for event detection:
pressure (psi)
and
volume (cc)
.

6.2
Data Annotation

The collected data was annotated in two stages. In the first stage, two field engineers with over 20 years of experience manually
inspected
every sample in the dataset
using a professional software Insitu Pro
(
5
)
to identify the classes and boundaries of all the events. Their results were then cross-verified to ensure
100% agreement
.

In the second stage, the field engineers documented textual guidance for event detection assisted by an experienced AI engineer. This collaboration ensures a well-structured description and accurate term choice for describing time-series patterns.

Evaluation Metrics.

Following previous work on time series event detection [
18
;
7
], we apply the IoU-based F1 scores as metrics to measure performance. We set two IoU thresholds: 0.5 and 0.9 to better discriminate models by their abilities in precise event localization.

Implementation Details.

For foundation models, we added linear heads for timestamp-wise classification and fine-tuned the entire models. Considering the small scale of datasets in use, all the supervised models were tested with 5-fold cross-validation, and average F1 scores were reported. Regarding few/zero-shot LLM methods, strong instruction following was the major criterion of model selection, and smaller open-source LLMs currently struggle in even basic settings like Numeric and VL-Time. Based on that, we chose to compare a standard model (GPT-4.1) with a reasoning model (GPT-5).

To understand the actual contribution of ELT, we performed an ablation study by letting the LLMs predict solely with the active visualization tools. When it is necessary to decide the type of event, the LLMs are requested to report event-level confidence scores for comparison, instead of using aggregated confidence scores from ELTs. In the easy setting, where the model does not need to discriminate the types, and the temporal-logic compositions are simpler, the models can perform detection mostly by zooming and observing local/global patterns. The contribution of ELT is mainly to marginally improve the detection quality. However, when it comes to mutually exclusive events with deeper composition hierarchies, the active visualization tools become detrimental, causing the entire system to collapse: the VLMs are either trapped in local morphological features or become overly confident, making no discrimination by assigning 0.9 or 1 to all categories. Through the guidance of the logic tree structure, LLMs can make local inspection while keeping the global event structure in mind, and confidence score propagation effectively prevents hallucinated direct prediction.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we define the task of knowledge-guided time series event detection with the first benchmark based on real industry data. To effectively tackle this low-resource challenge, we present the event logic tree knowledge representation system for multivariate time series events, and build
SELA
, a zero-shot neural-symbolic agentic system combining large vision-language models with ELT. Experiments show promising results with
SELA
compared to supervised models and previous methods utilizing LLMs/VLMs in time series analysis. Through an ablation study, we show that ELT is indispensable for discriminative detection on complex time series events with deep compositional hierarchies.
