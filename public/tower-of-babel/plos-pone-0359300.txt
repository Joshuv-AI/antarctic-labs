Compact target-aware stability-based feature selection for cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring

Sarra Ayouni, Maha Sliti, Abeer Abdulaziz Alghanem, Fatimah Alhayan, Mohamed Maddeh

ABSTRACT
Wearable activity-recognition models may lose reliability when transferred across participants, sensor placements, sampling rates, acquisition protocols, and activity definitions. This study evaluates a compact unsupervised target-aware feature-selection strategy for cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring under a pre-specified MHEALTH→PAMAP2 transfer protocol. Five-second wearable-signal windows were represented using 270 handcrafted inertial and magnetic descriptors. Source-domain mutual-information relevance was then combined with unlabeled source–target descriptor stability to select a compact set of 48 features, after which lightweight classifiers were trained using MHEALTH labels only. The original activities were harmonized into three coarse states: sedentary, active, and vigorous, while harmonized PAMAP2 target labels were withheld from model development and used only for final evaluation. Under leave-one-subject-out evaluation on MHEALTH, the Random Forest classifier achieved 0.9985 accuracy and 0.9984 macro-F1. In the primary cross-dataset experiment, the pre-specified stability-aware configuration achieved 0.9042 accuracy and 0.8753 macro-F1 on PAMAP2, compared with 0.8888 accuracy and 0.8436 macro-F1 for the full 270-feature representation and 0.8734 accuracy and 0.7940 macro-F1 for source-mutual-information-only selection. With all operating values frozen, reverse PAMAP2→MHEALTH transfer also ranked Stable-48 highest (accuracy 0.5811; macro-F1 0.4400), ahead of Source-MI-48 (0.5720; 0.4320) and Full-270 (0.5445; 0.4257), although Holm-adjusted participant-level contrasts were not significant. The compact configuration also achieved higher complete-window accuracy and macro-F1 than the tested shallow target-feature transformations and feature-level MLP adaptation baselines within the same handcrafted descriptor space. These findings indicate that jointly considering source-domain relevance and unlabeled cross-domain descriptor stability can improve compact cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring. The conclusions remain specific to the evaluated protocols and activity taxonomies and do not establish general superiority over raw-signal deep-learning methods or clinical readiness.

1 Introduction Wearable sensing is widely used in mobile-health (mHealth) systems to monitor daily activity, functional behavior, rehabilitation progress, and health-related patterns outside laboratory settings [ 1 – 5 ]. Unlike episodic clinical or laboratory measurements, wearable devices can provide repeated observations during daily life and may support rehabilitation follow-up, chronic-condition monitoring, and smart-healthcare services [ 1 , 6 – 8 ]. For these applications, the objective is not only to recognize activity within a single dataset, but also to produce activity-related indicators that remain useful across users, devices, sensor placements, acquisition settings, and operating conditions. Machine-learning methods are commonly used to convert wearable sensor streams into activity labels, functional-state indicators, or behavioral summaries [ 9 , 10 ]. Deep-learning models can perform well when large labeled datasets are available. However, lightweight feature-based models remain relevant for mHealth applications because they offer a practical balance between predictive performance, interpretability, computational cost, and deployment feasibility. These properties are particularly important when data are processed locally on smartphones, wearable gateways, or edge devices before compact descriptors or summarized outputs are transmitted to a remote platform. A major limitation of wearable activity modeling is that strong performance on a single benchmark does not necessarily translate into reliable behavior in another sensing context. A model trained on one dataset may degrade when tested on data collected from different subjects, devices, sampling rates, body locations, activity protocols, or label definitions. This issue is central to wearable mHealth analytics because source-domain performance may overestimate real-world transferability. Cross-dataset evaluation is therefore needed to assess whether a model remains useful beyond the dataset on which it was trained. This degradation is commonly associated with domain shift. In wearable sensing, domain shift may arise from inter-subject variability, hardware mismatch, sensor-placement differences, sampling-rate mismatch, acquisition-context changes, and inconsistent activity taxonomies. As a result, features that are discriminative in the source dataset may not preserve the same statistical behavior or decision relevance in the target dataset. Despite progress in wearable analytics, many studies still emphasize intra-dataset evaluation, while cross-dataset degradation, descriptor stability, class-wise reliability, calibration behavior, operating-point behavior, and subject-level variability remain less systematically examined [ 9 , 11 ]. Moreover, several domain-adaptation approaches rely on complex models or adaptation procedures that may be difficult to integrate into compact and interpretable mHealth pipelines [ 12 , 13 ]. Compact representation is also important for wearable mHealth systems. Transmitting raw multichannel windows can increase bandwidth use, storage demand, and cloud-side processing cost. A compact feature representation can reduce this burden if it preserves useful activity-monitoring performance under heterogeneous sensing conditions. This motivates approaches that jointly consider cross-dataset transfer, feature compactness, interpretability, and efficient inference, rather than optimizing source-domain accuracy alone. In this study, we examine whether a compact subset of handcrafted wearable descriptors can support cross-dataset activity monitoring from MHEALTH to PAMAP2 . The proposed pipeline includes preprocessing, window-based segmentation, handcrafted motion descriptors, source-domain relevance analysis, unlabeled target-domain descriptor-stability estimation, compact feature selection, and lightweight classification. The feature-selection step is target-aware because it uses unlabeled target-domain feature distributions to estimate descriptor stability. This setting assumes that an unlabeled batch of target-domain wearable windows is available before deployment or during a calibration phase. It is therefore transductive with respect to target-feature distributions, but remains unsupervised with respect to target labels. Target labels are reserved only for final evaluation and are not used for feature selection, model training, hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, or operating-point selection. The resulting outputs are interpreted as wearable-derived activity indicators rather than diagnostic decisions, clinical assessments, or autonomous alarms. The same pipeline is also evaluated with PAMAP2 as the labeled source dataset and MHEALTH as the unlabeled target dataset, using the operating values fixed for the primary analysis. The primary evaluation uses MHEALTH as the source dataset and PAMAP2 as the external target dataset. To improve comparability, the original activity labels are mapped into three coarse states: sedentary , active , and vigorous . These states provide a low-complexity operational abstraction for distinguishing posture-dominated behavior, routine movement, and more dynamic movement; they are not clinical diagnoses or validated metabolic-intensity bands. A complementary six-activity evaluation uses lying, sitting, standing, walking, stair climbing, and running to examine performance when biomechanically similar activities are separated. UCI HAR is included only as an exploratory extension and is not used to define the primary transfer protocol, select the main configuration, or support the principal performance claims. The evaluation covers source-domain performance, external transfer behavior, reverse-direction transfer, six-activity classification, feature-selection effects, sensitivity to stability-aware parameters, class-wise reliability, calibration-oriented metrics, operating-point behavior, subject-level variability, compactness, model-inference cost, software-side feature-extraction latency, and communication-payload estimates. Because target windows are grouped within subjects and are not statistically independent, window-level metrics are interpreted together with subject-level summaries. Section 7 concludes the paper. 2 Related work 2.1 Wearable activity monitoring and machine learning Wearable sensing enables continuous observation of physical activity, functional behavior, and rehabilitation-related patterns outside controlled laboratory environments [ 1 – 4 ]. Such measurements support activity recognition, rehabilitation follow-up, behavioral monitoring, and patient-centered health analytics [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. Machine-learning methods transform multichannel sensor streams into activity classes or broader behavioral indicators [ 9 , 10 ]. Although deep architectures can learn representations directly from raw signals, compact feature-based models remain relevant when interpretability, computational efficiency, and local execution are important [ 11 ]. Handcrafted window-level representations combined with lightweight classifiers therefore remain practical for resource-constrained wearable monitoring. Recent work has also reinforced the value of interpretable signal-derived features. Msokar et al. [ 14 ] developed a multi-domain photoplethysmography feature-engineering framework for multi-class blood-pressure staging. Their study illustrates how structured descriptors can preserve feature traceability while supporting conventional machine-learning models. Although their modality and clinical task differ from activity recognition, the same feature-centric motivation underlies the present use of interpretable inertial and magnetic descriptors. 2.2 Cross-dataset reliability and domain shift Strong performance within a single dataset does not necessarily imply reliable behavior in an external sensing environment. Cross-dataset degradation is commonly associated with domain shift [ 12 , 15 ], which in wearable sensing may arise from differences in participants, hardware, body placement, sampling frequency, acquisition protocol, activity execution, and label taxonomy [ 13 , 16 ]. Consequently, descriptors that are discriminative in the source dataset may change in scale, distribution, or predictive relevance after transfer. External evaluation should therefore consider more than aggregate accuracy. Class-specific errors, calibration behavior, and inter-subject variability may remain hidden when multiple windows are pooled across participants. Cross-dataset studies should accordingly combine complete-window results with class-wise and subject-level analyses. A further distinction is required between strict source-only transfer and unsupervised target-aware transfer. Source-only methods use no target-domain information before prediction, whereas target-aware methods may use unlabeled target observations to estimate statistics, align representations, or guide feature selection. The latter setting remains unsupervised with respect to target labels but is transductive with respect to the available target-feature distribution; it is therefore not equivalent to zero-target-data generalization. 2.3 Wearable domain adaptation and stability-aware feature selection Domain-shift mitigation methods include normalization, distribution alignment, transferable representation learning, contrastive learning, and adversarial adaptation [ 12 , 13 , 15 ]. Recent wearable approaches increasingly learn transferable temporal representations directly from multivariate sensor sequences. Zhang et al. [ 17 ] proposed a contrastive domain-adaptation framework combining time-lag attention with teacher–student and class-conditional alignment mechanisms. Their work illustrates the potential of learned temporal invariance under wearable domain shift. Such methods address a different operating point from compact handcrafted pipelines. They rely on trainable temporal encoders and adaptation losses, whereas feature-based approaches emphasize explicit descriptors, lower complexity, and easier inspection of the selected inputs. These directions are complementary: learned adaptation seeks expressive cross-domain representations, while compact feature selection seeks a transparent and computationally controlled representation. Lighter alternatives based on unlabeled target data include target-statistics normalization, AdaBN-style normalization, mean–variance matching, CORAL, linear-MMD mean matching, and Transfer Component Analysis. These methods reduce marginal source–target mismatch without using target labels, but marginal alignment does not necessarily preserve class-conditional separability. Stability-aware feature selection provides another alternative. A source-only ranking may retain descriptors that are discriminative during training but sensitive to dataset-specific conditions. Incorporating unlabeled source–target distributional information can instead favor descriptors that balance source relevance with cross-domain consistency. Because the target distribution contributes to the ranking, this procedure is appropriately described as unsupervised target-aware feature selection. Unlike Zhang et al. [ 17 ], the present method does not learn a raw-signal temporal encoder, generate target pseudo-labels, or optimize a trainable alignment loss. It operates in an interpretable handcrafted descriptor space and uses unlabeled target distributions only to penalize descriptors with greater source–target instability. 2.4 Compact and edge-compatible wearable analytics Local or edge-based wearable analytics can reduce continuous raw-signal transmission and support processing on smartphones, gateways, or resource-constrained devices [ 18 – 22 ]. Compact descriptors may reduce communication and storage requirements while preserving an interpretable input representation. However, classifier inference time alone does not characterize the complete processing burden, because window construction and descriptor extraction may dominate the software-side cost. Deployment-oriented evaluation should therefore consider representation size, feature-extraction time, model-inference latency, communication payload, predictive reliability, and inter-subject variability together. 2.5 Position of this study Prior wearable studies commonly emphasize single-dataset performance, source-only feature selection, shallow feature alignment, or learned raw-signal representations. Interpretable feature-engineering research demonstrates the value of transparent signal-derived descriptors [ 14 ], while recent domain-adaptation methods demonstrate the potential of learned temporal representations [ 17 ]. The present study examines a complementary question: whether a compact handcrafted representation can be selected by jointly considering source-domain discriminative relevance and unlabeled source–target descriptor stability. The primary protocol uses MHEALTH as the labeled source dataset and PAMAP2 as the external target dataset, while UCI HAR is retained only for exploratory analysis. The proposed selector is compared with the full descriptor space, source-mutual-information-only selection, shallow unsupervised target-feature transformations, and controlled feature-level Source-MLP, Deep CORAL-MLP, and DANN-MLP baselines. The neural comparisons operate within the same handcrafted descriptor space and are not intended as an exhaustive benchmark of raw-signal convolutional, recurrent, transformer-based, contrastive, self-supervised, or foundation-style architectures. The contribution is instead a transparent target-label-free framework that jointly evaluates cross-dataset performance, feature-selection behavior, class-wise reliability, subject-level variability, compactness, latency, and communication payload. 3 Study framework and transfer setting 3.1 Overview This study examines cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring under heterogeneous sensing conditions. A model trained on one dataset may degrade when applied to another because of differences in participants, devices, body placements, sampling rates, acquisition protocols, activity execution, and label taxonomies. The proposed framework combines preprocessing, window-based segmentation, handcrafted descriptor extraction, source-domain relevance estimation, unlabeled source–target stability analysis, compact feature selection, and lightweight classification. Its objective is to retain descriptors that are discriminative in the source domain while remaining comparatively stable under external-domain shift. The primary transfer protocol uses MHEALTH as the labeled source dataset and PAMAP2 as the external target dataset. Their original activities are mapped into three coarse states: sedentary , active , and vigorous . These states are treated as wearable-derived activity indicators rather than clinical or diagnostic labels. The three-state mapping provides a low-complexity operational representation of posture-dominated behavior, routine movement, and more dynamic movement rather than a validated clinical or metabolic-intensity taxonomy. Transfer-direction consistency is also examined by using PAMAP2 as the labeled source dataset and MHEALTH as the unlabeled target dataset under the same fixed operating values. A complementary finer-grained evaluation separates lying, sitting, standing, walking, stair climbing, and running to examine performance when biomechanically similar activities are no longer grouped into the same coarse state. UCI HAR is included only in an exploratory extension and is not used to define the primary protocol, select the main configuration, or support the principal performance claims. Source labels are available during model development, whereas harmonized target labels are sequestered until final evaluation. For the finer-grained evaluation, C = 6, corresponding to lying, sitting, standing, walking, stair climbing, and running. The corresponding joint distributions are P S ( 𝐱 , y ) and P T ( 𝐱 , y ) , (2) and domain shift occurs when P S ( 𝐱 , y ) ≠ P T ( 𝐱 , y ) . (3) In wearable sensing, this discrepancy may arise from differences in sensing hardware, body placement, sampling rate, acquisition conditions, activity composition, participant behavior, or feature–label relationships. Consequently, strong source-domain performance does not necessarily imply reliable target-domain behavior. Three transfer settings are considered: Strict external transfer : the model is trained using the source dataset and evaluated on the target dataset without using target information before prediction. “‘ Unsupervised target-aware feature selection : unlabeled target descriptors are used to estimate source–target stability and select a compact representation, without using target labels. Unsupervised target-feature transformation : source and unlabeled target descriptors are used to estimate a shallow normalization or alignment transformation before prediction. “‘ The term no explicit target-feature transformation means that no post-selection alignment method, such as mean–variance alignment, CORAL, linear-MMD mean matching, or TCA, is applied to the selected target descriptors. It does not exclude the use of unlabeled target distributions during stability-aware feature selection. The target-aware settings assume that an unlabeled batch of target windows is available before deployment or during a calibration phase. They are therefore transductive with respect to the observed target-feature distribution and should not be interpreted as strict zero-target-data generalization. These target-label restrictions are applied unchanged in the primary transfer direction, the reverse transfer direction, and the six-activity evaluation. 3.3 Framework components and scope The framework comprises three principal components. First, descriptor stability is characterized using normalized mean shift, distributional discrepancy, and source-relevance–target-dispersion rank consistency. Second, source-domain relevance and unlabeled cross-domain stability are combined to select a compact descriptor subset. Third, the selected representation is compared with full-feature transfer, source-only feature selection, shallow unsupervised target-feature transformations, and controlled feature-level neural baselines. Source mutual information, descriptor-stability quantities, and the selected feature subset are recomputed after reversing the source and target roles and after replacing the three-state mapping with the six-activity taxonomy. The complete descriptor definitions, stability score, component ablations, target-feature transformations, and leakage-controlled evaluation procedure are provided in Subsections 4.6–4.8. Parameter-sensitivity analyses are used only to characterize robustness around the pre-specified primary configuration and are not treated as target-label-guided model selection. Because overlapping target windows are clustered within participants, complete-window metrics are interpreted together with subject-level summaries. Higher aggregate accuracy or macro-F1 is therefore treated as descriptive target-window improvement rather than definitive evidence of subject-level superiority. The framework is intended as a pre-deployment evaluation of wearable-derived activity indicators under cross-dataset shift. It does not replace clinical diagnosis, validated functional-assessment instruments, expert evaluation, or clinical decision-making. Its conclusions remain specific to the evaluated datasets, sensing conditions, transfer direction, and three-state harmonization. The reverse-direction and six-activity analyses examine the sensitivity of the observed behavior to transfer direction and label granularity but do not remove these scope limitations. Prospective validation, richer participant cohorts, embedded implementation, and independent clinical evaluation remain necessary before real-world healthcare deployment. 4 Materials and methods This section describes the datasets, label harmonization, preprocessing, feature construction, baseline models, stability-aware feature selection, target-feature transformations, evaluation protocol, statistical analysis, and implementation. Target-label access is restricted according to the leakage-control protocol defined in Subsection 4.8 and Algorithm 4. 4.1 Datasets and label harmonization Three public wearable datasets are considered. MHEALTH is the labeled source dataset in the primary transfer protocol. It has a nominal sampling rate of 50 Hz and includes accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, and ECG-related signals. PAMAP2 is the external target dataset. It has a nominal sampling rate of 100 Hz and includes accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, and heart-rate-related signals. ECG- and heart-rate-related channels are excluded so that the primary datasets are represented using their shared inertial and magnetic modalities. In the reverse-direction evaluation, the roles of these two datasets are exchanged while retaining the same shared modalities and descriptor construction. UCI HAR is included only as an exploratory extension. It contains smartphone inertial measurements and differs from the primary datasets in sensor configuration, body placement, acquisition protocol, and original task definition. It is not used to define the primary transfer direction, select the primary feature-selection configuration, tune model parameters, or support the principal performance claims. The two primary datasets are projected onto a common 12-channel inertial–magnetic representation. The retained accelerometer channels are acc_x , acc_y , acc_z , and acc_mag ; the gyroscope channels are gyro_x , gyro_y , gyro_z , and gyro_mag ; and the magnetometer channels are mag_x , mag_y , mag_z , and mag_mag . Because the datasets do not share identical activity taxonomies, their original labels are mapped into three harmonized states: sedentary , active , and vigorous . This pre-specified mapping is an operational abstraction for controlled cross-dataset comparison rather than a unique biomechanical, physiological, or clinical taxonomy. Its practical purpose is to provide a low-complexity monitoring output that distinguishes the least dynamic posture-dominated periods, routine functional movement, and more dynamic activity bouts while reducing incompatibilities between the original dataset taxonomies. These states are not clinical diagnoses, validated functional-assessment categories, or metabolic-equivalent intensity bands. Under consensus movement-behavior terminology, standing is a stationary but non-sedentary behavior [ 23 ]. Because standing is grouped with sitting and lying in the lowest-motion state, the term sedentary is used here only as an internal harmonization label and must not be interpreted as an estimate of consensus-defined sedentary behavior. Activities grouped within the same state may differ in movement pattern, intensity, posture, body involvement, and biomechanical meaning. Activities with similar names may also be performed, sensed, or annotated differently across datasets. The resulting states are therefore interpreted as coarse wearable-derived activity indicators rather than interchangeable biomechanical classes or validated clinical activity levels. Target feature values may be used, but target labels remain unavailable. For each transfer direction, all source-domain relevance quantities, source–target stability indicators, joint scores, and selected feature indices are recomputed after assigning the corresponding datasets to 𝒟 S and 𝒟 T . In the six-activity evaluation, these quantities are recomputed under the six-class source labels and the corresponding unlabeled target-feature distribution. Consequently, neither the reverse-direction Stable-48 subset nor the six-activity Stable-48 subset is inherited from the primary three-state MHEALTH → PAMAP2 experiment. The first stability indicator is the normalized source–target mean shift: Δ k ( μ ) = | μ k S − μ k T | σ k S + σ k T + ϵ , (14) where ϵ > 0 prevents numerical instability. The second indicator measures the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the empirical source and target marginal distributions: D k ( d i s t ) = d J S 2 ( p k S , p k T ) , (15) where the distributions are estimated using 32-bin histograms defined over the same pooled source–target range. The third indicator compares source-domain relevance with unlabeled target-domain dispersion. Source relevance is measured using the mutual information I k S between f k S and the source labels. The target-side dispersion proxy is u k T = log ( 1 + Var ( f k T ) ) . (16) The quantity u k T is not interpreted as target-domain class relevance. High variance may reflect activity variation, sensor noise, participant heterogeneity, acquisition mismatch, or other nuisance factors. It is used only as a weak label-free dispersion and non-degeneracy proxy; source mutual information remains the only class-aware relevance quantity. Let r k S and r k T be the descending ranks of I k S and u k T , respectively. Their normalized discrepancy is Δ k ( r a n k ) = | r k S − r k T | d − 1 . (17) This quantity is treated as a consistency regularizer rather than an estimate of target-label relevance. Each stability indicator is independently min–max normalized: z ~ k = z k − min ℓ z ℓ max ℓ z ℓ − min ℓ z ℓ + ϵ . (18) The stability penalty is S k = w 1 Δ ~ k ( μ ) + w 2 D ~ k ( d i s t ) + w 3 Δ ~ k ( r a n k ) , (19) with w 1 = 0.40 , w 2 = 0.35 , w 3 = 0.25 , w 1 + w 2 + w 3 = 1 . (20) Lower S k indicates greater source–target stability. Because the three component scores lie in [0,1] and their weights sum to one, no additional normalization of S k is applied. After independently normalizing the source mutual-information values to obtain I ~ k S , the joint relevance–stability score is Q k = α I ~ k S − ( 1 − α ) S k . (21) The first K descriptors in the descending Q k ranking are retained: ℱ K * = TopK ( { Q k } k = 1 d ) , | ℱ K * | = K . (22) The pre-specified primary configuration uses K = 48 and α = 0.60 . The value K = 48 retains 17.8% of the original 270-dimensional representation, corresponding to an 82.2% reduction, while preserving descriptors from all five predefined feature families. It was therefore adopted as a moderate compactness operating point. Three matched feature representations are used in the comparative evaluations. Full-270 retains all candidate descriptors, Source-MI-48 retains the 48 descriptors with the highest source mutual information, and Stable-48 retains the 48 descriptors with the highest joint relevance–stability score Q k . Source-MI-48 and Stable-48 are reconstructed independently for each transfer direction and activity taxonomy. The value α = 0.60 assigns 60% of the joint criterion to source relevance and 40% to cross-domain stability. This slightly relevance-dominant balance reflects that source mutual information is the only class-aware quantity, whereas the stability terms are label-free. Neither K = 48 nor α = 0.60 was selected through a grid search using PAMAP2 labels. Both values were fixed before final target-label evaluation. The subsequent sensitivity analyses are therefore reported as post-hoc robustness assessments and are not used to redefine the primary configuration. The same values of K , α , and ( w 1 , w 2 , w 3 ) are retained without target-label-guided adjustment in the reverse transfer and six-activity evaluation. Macro-F1 is emphasized because the harmonized classes are imbalanced and all three activity states are relevant for monitoring. Balanced accuracy is defined as the unweighted mean of the class-wise recalls: B a l a n c e d A c c u r a c y = 1 C ∑ c = 1 C T P c T P c + F N c . (33) For the six-activity evaluation, accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, and class-wise F1 are reported with a normalized 6 × 6 confusion matrix. Class-wise results are used to examine separation between biomechanically similar activities, particularly sitting versus standing and walking versus stair climbing. For a metric M , transfer retention, absolute robustness gap, and relative degradation are defined as R M = M t a r g e t M s o u r c e , Δ M = M s o u r c e − M t a r g e t , G M = ( 1 − M t a r g e t M s o u r c e ) × 100 % . (34) The stability of a selected descriptor subset ℱ is additionally summarized by its mean stability penalty: S ― ( ℱ ) = 1 | ℱ | ∑ k ∈ ℱ S k . (35) Lower S ― ( ℱ ) indicates that the selected descriptors have lower average source–target discrepancy under the stability criterion defined in Equation (19) . For the six-activity evaluation, this quantity is reported for Source-MI-48 and Stable-48 to determine whether the stability-aware subset remains more stable under the finer-grained taxonomy. Because overlapping windows from the same participant are not statistically independent, complete-window results are interpreted as descriptive performance indicators and are complemented by subject-level summaries. For each target subject s , let F 1 , s ( m ) denote the macro-F1 obtained by configuration m . Subject-level performance is summarized using the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and range across retained target subjects. For configurations a and b , the paired subject-level difference is defined as Δ s ( a , b ) = F 1 , s ( a ) − F 1 , s ( b ) . (36) A selected deployment-oriented paired comparison evaluates Stable-48 / align against the Full-270 / none baseline. The comparison is reported using the mean paired difference, a 95% subject-level bootstrap confidence interval, and a two-sided paired-bootstrap p -value. Given the limited number of retained target subjects, this analysis is interpreted descriptively and does not support definitive claims of subject-level superiority. For the reverse-direction and six-activity evaluations, two paired comparisons are specified: Stable-48 versus Full-270 and Stable-48 versus Source-MI-48. Each comparison is summarized using the mean participant-level macro-F1 difference, the number of participants with positive differences, and a 95% participant-bootstrap confidence interval. A two-sided exact sign-flip test is applied to the paired participant-level differences. The two planned p -values within each evaluation are adjusted using the Holm procedure and reported as p H o l m . These participant-level analyses complement the complete-window metrics and are not replaced by window-level significance tests. Subject-level bootstrap confidence intervals are estimated by resampling target subjects with replacement. Window-level confidence intervals are reported only as secondary descriptive estimates because overlapping windows within the same subject are not independent observations. The deployment-balanced configuration is additionally evaluated using class-wise metrics, normalized confusion matrices, ordinal mean absolute error, multiclass Brier score, expected calibration error, subject-level heterogeneity, and a post-hoc vigorous-state threshold analysis. The threshold analysis characterizes the precision–recall trade-off and is not used for model selection or primary performance reporting. 4.10 Efficiency and implementation Compactness is evaluated by comparing the 270-feature and 48-feature representations. The external transfer experiment was more informative than the near-ceiling source-domain results because it directly exposed the effects of cross-dataset shift. Under the pre-specified MHEALTH → PAMAP2 protocol and primary three-state mapping, Stable-48 / none achieved an accuracy of 0.9042 and a macro-F1 of 0.8753, while reducing the representation from 270 to 48 descriptors. Its higher complete-window performance relative to both the full representation and the source-MI-only subset indicates that the improvement is associated with jointly considering source-domain relevance and unlabeled source–target stability rather than dimensionality reduction alone. The reverse PAMAP2 → MHEALTH evaluation preserved the same aggregate ordering under the fixed no-transformation configuration. Stable-48 achieved an accuracy of 0.5811 and a macro-F1 of 0.4400, compared with 0.5720 and 0.4320 for Source-MI-48 and 0.5445 and 0.4257 for Full-270. Stable-48 also had a 23.6% lower mean stability penalty than Source-MI-48. The lower absolute performance indicates substantial directional asymmetry under the evaluated protocol, while the repeated aggregate ordering indicates that the primary finding was not restricted to a single assignment of the two datasets as source and target. The reverse-direction participant analysis provides a more qualified interpretation. Stable-48 exceeded Full-270 for 9 of 10 target participants and Source-MI-48 for 8 participants, with one tie in the latter comparison. However, the corresponding Holm-adjusted tests were not significant. The reverse evaluation therefore supports directional consistency in aggregate performance but does not establish universal or statistically confirmed participant-level superiority. The six-activity evaluation examined whether the stability criterion remained useful after separating lying, sitting, standing, walking, stair climbing, and running. Stable-48 achieved the highest complete-window macro-F1 at 0.4165, compared with 0.4077 for Full-270 and 0.3162 for Source-MI-48. Its mean stability penalty was 0.2305, which was 43.1% lower than the 0.4054 obtained by Source-MI-48. Full-270 nevertheless retained slightly higher accuracy, balanced accuracy, and mean participant-level macro-F1 than Stable-48. The stability-aware subset therefore preserved a macro-F1 and descriptor-stability advantage, particularly over source-only selection, but did not dominate the full representation on every metric. The six-class confusion structure also shows why the finer taxonomy is substantially more difficult. Running remained well recognized, whereas standing was predominantly classified as sitting and walking was predominantly classified as stair climbing. These errors indicate that descriptor stability alone cannot fully resolve biomechanically similar activities when their source and target sensor signatures differ. The three-state taxonomy is consequently best understood as a low-complexity monitoring abstraction that reduces incompatibilities between the original dataset taxonomies, not as a clinical classification system or a substitute for fine-grained activity recognition. The parameter and component analyses qualify these findings. The primary values K = 48 and α = 0.60 were fixed as methodological operating points rather than selected by maximizing target-domain performance. Although K = 24 achieved a higher post-hoc macro-F1, replacing the pre-specified setting on this basis would introduce target-guided selection. Similarly, the component ablation showed that mean shift and the rank-discrepancy term contributed differently, whereas the no-JSD variant performed better under the primary transfer direction. The complete score is retained because it was specified before final target-label evaluation; the K = 24 and no-JSD results are treated as post-hoc findings requiring independent validation. The label-harmonization sensitivity analysis further showed that both the magnitude of the transfer results and the ordering of the tested configurations depend on how activities are assigned to the three states. The principal claims are therefore conditional on the pre-specified mapping and should not be interpreted as invariant to alternative biomechanical or intensity-based taxonomies. The six-activity results reinforce this qualification by showing that separating posture-dominated and locomotor activities changes both the selected descriptors and the resulting class-specific errors. Finally, the primary setting is unsupervised with respect to target labels but target-aware with respect to unlabeled target-feature distributions. It is therefore not a strict zero-target-data deployment scenario. Because target windows are also clustered within a limited number of participants, the complete-window improvements in both transfer directions should be interpreted as descriptive transfer performance rather than definitive evidence of universal or subject-level superiority. 6.2 Role of stability-aware feature selection The feature-count analysis indicates that compact settings between K = 24 and K = 48 remain competitive. The post-hoc K = 24 result suggests that more aggressive compression may be viable, but K = 48 remains the primary setting because it was fixed before final target-label evaluation, preserves descriptors from all five predefined families, and represents the intended moderate compactness operating point. Neither setting should be interpreted as universally optimal. The relevance–stability analysis also favors an intermediate balance. Values of α = 0.50 and α = 0.60 produced similar transfer performance, whereas the strongly source-dominant α = 0.90 setting performed substantially worse. This pattern suggests that source-domain relevance alone may retain descriptors that are discriminative within MHEALTH but less stable under the distributional differences encountered in PAMAP2 . The reverse-direction results extend this interpretation beyond the primary assignment of the two datasets. Under PAMAP2 → MHEALTH transfer, Stable-48 reduced the mean selected-subset stability penalty from 0.3365 for Source-MI-48 to 0.2571, a reduction of 23.6%, while increasing complete-window macro-F1 from 0.4320 to 0.4400. Stable-48 also retained only 48 descriptors while attaining approximately the same mean stability penalty as Full-270, which obtained 0.2575. The six-activity analysis produced a larger separation between the two compact subsets. Stable-48 reduced the mean stability penalty from 0.4054 for Source-MI-48 to 0.2305, corresponding to a 43.1% reduction, while increasing macro-F1 from 0.3162 to 0.4165. The lower penalty is expected from the feature-selection objective, but its co-occurrence with higher macro-F1 in both the reverse-direction and six-activity evaluations supports the intended role of the stability term in filtering source-relevant descriptors that undergo stronger marginal shift. This relationship is not sufficient to guarantee superiority over the full representation for every outcome. In the six-activity evaluation, Full-270 retained slightly higher accuracy, balanced accuracy, and mean participant-level macro-F1 than Stable-48. The stability penalty is therefore best interpreted as a feature-level shift criterion that complements source relevance, rather than as a direct surrogate for target classification performance or participant-level robustness. The feature-family analysis supports this interpretation. Correlation and frequency-domain descriptors were more stable on average, whereas statistical and temporal-dynamics descriptors showed stronger source-domain relevance but greater cross-domain variation. Relevance and stability are therefore complementary properties rather than interchangeable criteria. The component ablation showed that normalized mean shift provided the clearest contribution to aggregate performance and subject-level consistency. The rank-discrepancy term produced a smaller effect and is best interpreted as a weak label-free regularizer rather than an estimate of target-domain class relevance. By contrast, JSD did not provide incremental benefit for the primary transfer direction. This may indicate that histogram-based marginal divergence penalized differences that did not necessarily reduce class separability. Because this ablation finding is based on the primary source–target direction, a simplified mean-shift–rank formulation should not be adopted without independent validation. The mapping-sensitivity results introduce an additional qualification: changing the activity taxonomy modifies the source labels, source mutual information, selected descriptors, target class distribution, and class-specific errors. The six-activity evaluation confirms that the stability-aware subset itself changes when biomechanically similar activities are separated. Stability-aware selection should therefore be evaluated jointly with the adopted harmonization rather than assumed to be taxonomy invariant. 6.3 Comparison with feature-level neural and domain-adaptation baselines Among the tested feature-level neural models, DANN-MLP achieved the highest accuracy and macro-F1, followed by Deep CORAL-MLP and Source-MLP. Within the selected handcrafted descriptor space, this ordering indicates that covariance-based and adversarial adaptation can improve transfer relative to a source-only MLP. Stable-48 / none nevertheless achieved higher complete-window accuracy and macro-F1 than all three neural baselines under the common 48-dimensional representation and target-label-free protocol. This comparison is deliberately restricted to the implemented descriptor space. Source-MLP, Deep CORAL-MLP, and DANN-MLP do not learn directly from raw sensor windows and do not constitute exhaustive benchmarks of convolutional, recurrent, transformer-based, contrastive, self-supervised, or foundation-style time-series models. The results therefore do not establish general superiority over deep learning or neural domain adaptation for wearable activity recognition. 6.4 Main transfer configuration and deployment-balanced operation Stable-48 / none is retained as the main transfer configuration because it provides the strongest complete-window accuracy and macro-F1 among the principal compact settings under the primary mapping. It therefore represents the accuracy-oriented operating point of the proposed framework. Stable-48 / align provides a different trade-off. It combines compactness, high vigorous-state recall, lower subject-level dispersion, and low model-inference latency, but its macro-F1 is lower. It should therefore be described as a deployment-balanced operating point rather than as the best or statistically superior model. The mapping-sensitivity results also show that this ordering is not universal. Stable-48 / align performed better than Stable-48 / none under both alternative mappings. The preferred configuration consequently depends on the activity taxonomy and on whether balanced classification or vigorous-state sensitivity is prioritized. 6.5 Reliability, calibration, and vigorous-state interpretation The class-wise analysis shows that aggregate performance alone is insufficient for interpreting transfer reliability. Under the primary mapping, the deployment-balanced configuration achieves high vigorous-state recall but lower precision because some active windows are classified as vigorous. This behavior may be acceptable in sensitivity-oriented monitoring, but it may generate excessive false positives if isolated window-level outputs are treated as actionable alarms. Vigorous-state behavior also depends strongly on the adopted harmonization. The alternative mappings changed both vigorous-state prevalence and recall, confirming that class-specific reliability cannot be interpreted independently of the underlying activity definition. The six-activity evaluation provides a more demanding assessment of class-specific reliability. Stable-48 retained strong running performance, with a recall of 0.9375 and an F1-score of 0.8592, while lying, sitting, and stair climbing achieved F1-scores of 0.6072, 0.4952, and 0.4952, respectively. Standing and walking were substantially more difficult, with F1-scores of 0.0026 and 0.0397. The confusion matrix shows that 89% of standing windows were classified as sitting and 96% of walking windows were classified as stair climbing. These errors occur between activities with related postural or locomotor signal characteristics and demonstrate that a favorable aggregate stability score does not ensure reliable separation of every activity pair. They also show that the three-state mapping can conceal specific within-state confusions when sitting and standing or walking and stair climbing are grouped into broader monitoring categories. Consequently, the three-state output is suitable only as a coarse wearable-derived monitoring abstraction. Applications requiring reliable posture discrimination, gait characterization, stair detection, or activity-specific clinical interpretation would require validation under the fine-grained taxonomy and may benefit from additional placement-specific features, temporal context, independent calibration, or complementary sensor modalities. The post-hoc threshold analysis illustrates the expected precision–recall trade-off but is not used for model selection or primary reporting. In a deployable system, decision thresholds should be pre-specified or calibrated using an independent validation set defined under the intended activity taxonomy. Temporal aggregation across consecutive windows may further reduce the effect of transient false-positive predictions. 6.6 Compactness, payload, and latency Reducing the representation from 270 to 48 descriptors substantially lowers the estimated communication payload while preserving an interpretable input space. The K = 24 result suggests that further compression may be possible, but this setting requires independent validation before being recommended for deployment. The communication analysis quantifies payload size only and should not be interpreted as measured energy savings. Similarly, the sub-0.03 ms latency corresponds to model inference after descriptor extraction rather than end-to-end execution. In the evaluated software implementation, feature extraction required approximately 30 ms per 5-s window and dominated the processing cost. Sensing, buffering, wireless transmission, device scheduling, and dashboard-side processing were not measured. 6.7 Subject-level heterogeneity and statistical caution Complete-window metrics summarize all target predictions but may conceal inter-individual variability because several overlapping windows originate from each participant. The subject-level analysis confirmed heterogeneous performance across the retained PAMAP2 subjects. Stable-48 / none achieved a higher mean subject-level macro-F1 and a smaller range than the full-feature and source-MI-only baselines, but the improvement was not uniform across participants. The K = 24 configuration also showed a smaller post-hoc range, but this result cannot establish superior subject-level robustness because the setting was identified after target evaluation. The reverse-direction evaluation showed a similar distinction between aggregate and participant-level evidence. Stable-48 achieved a mean participant-level macro-F1 of 0.4402, compared with 0.4320 for Source-MI-48 and 0.4221 for Full-270. Stable-48 exceeded Full-270 for 9 of 10 MHEALTH participants, but the mean paired difference of 0.0181 had a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of [ − 0.0185 , 0.0409 ] and p H o l m = 0.3320 . Relative to Source-MI-48, the mean difference was 0.0082, with 8 positive participant differences, one tie, one negative difference, and p H o l m = 0.1172 . The aggregate reverse-direction ordering is therefore encouraging but does not establish participant-level superiority. Under the six-activity taxonomy, Stable-48 achieved the highest complete-window macro-F1 but not the highest unweighted mean participant-level macro-F1. The participant means were 0.3678 for Stable-48, 0.3743 for Full-270, and 0.2935 for Source-MI-48. The Stable-48 minus Full-270 paired difference was − 0.0065 , with a 95% confidence interval of [ − 0.0528 , 0.0377 ] and p H o l m = 0.7969 . Stable-48 exceeded Source-MI-48 by an average of 0.0743, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.0030, 0.1377], but the Holm-adjusted exact sign-flip result remained non-significant at p H o l m = 0.1875 . The difference between complete-window and participant-mean rankings reflects the unequal number of retained windows per participant and the fact that aggregate metrics weight participants according to their window counts. Reporting both summaries is therefore necessary to distinguish overall target-window performance from consistency across individuals. The deployment-balanced configuration reduced subject-level dispersion but did not achieve the highest complete-window macro-F1, and its paired comparison with the full-feature baseline was not statistically significant. Given the limited number of retained target subjects across the primary, reverse-direction, and six-activity evaluations, the evidence supports cautious descriptive interpretation rather than definitive claims of inter-individual superiority. Larger and more diverse target cohorts are required to assess whether the observed subject-level trends are reproducible. 6.8 Limitations and future work Several limitations should be considered. First, the study is based on secondary analysis of public datasets and does not include prospective daily-life or clinical deployment. The outputs should therefore be interpreted as wearable-derived activity indicators rather than diagnostic decisions, clinical alarms, or substitutes for validated functional assessment instruments. Second, the three-state harmonization necessarily simplifies the original activity taxonomies. Activities grouped within the same state may differ in intensity, posture, movement pattern, and biomechanical meaning, while similarly named activities may be performed or annotated differently across datasets. The sensitivity analysis confirmed that alternative mappings can change class prevalence, transfer performance, vigorous-state recall, and the ordering of the tested configurations. The lowest-motion state also includes standing and should therefore be interpreted as an operational stationary/postural category rather than consensus-defined sedentary behavior. The three states are not validated clinical, functional, or metabolic-intensity categories. The six-activity evaluation reduced this pooling but revealed substantial fine-grained transfer difficulty. Stable-48 retained the highest complete-window macro-F1 and the lowest compact-subset stability penalty, whereas Full-270 achieved slightly higher accuracy, balanced accuracy, and mean participant-level macro-F1. Standing and walking were also frequently confused with sitting and stair climbing, respectively. The present results therefore do not establish uniformly reliable recognition across all six activities. Future studies should define harmonization rules using externally justified criteria, such as measured energy expenditure, metabolic-equivalent ranges, biomechanical similarity, or expert-defined application requirements, and should validate activity-specific performance in larger participant cohorts. Third, the bidirectional validation remains restricted to MHEALTH and PAMAP2 . Reversing their roles provides a distinct source–target direction and reproduces the aggregate ordering of Full-270, Source-MI-48, and Stable-48. However, both directions share the same two datasets, sensor families, and harmonization decisions. Matched validation using an additional dataset and the same frozen operating values is still needed to assess broader cross-dataset generalizability. Fourth, the method uses unlabeled target-domain distributions for stability estimation and is therefore target-aware rather than strictly source-only. Future work should compare this setting with domain generalization, multi-source transfer, limited-target-data adaptation, and self-supervised learning under explicit safeguards against target-label leakage. Fifth, the target-variance quantity in the rank-discrepancy term is only a label-free dispersion and non-degeneracy proxy. It does not estimate target-domain class relevance and may reflect noise, participant heterogeneity, or acquisition mismatch. Its contribution and interpretation should be evaluated across additional datasets and transfer directions. Sixth, the stability weights, K = 48, and α = 0.60 were pre-specified but are not claimed to be universally optimal. The post-hoc K = 24 and no-JSD results suggest potentially useful alternatives, but adopting either on the basis of the current target-domain results would constitute retrospective selection. Additional independent source–target pairs beyond the bidirectional MHEALTH – PAMAP2 evaluation are needed before recommending a different feature count or simplified stability score. Seventh, the vigorous-state threshold analysis is retrospective, and the deployment-balanced configuration retains limited vigorous-state precision. Future work should use independent threshold calibration and examine temporal aggregation of repeated activity predictions. Eighth, the neural comparisons are restricted to feature-level Source-MLP, Deep CORAL-MLP, and DANN-MLP models. They do not represent strong or exhaustive raw-signal deep-learning baselines. Future comparisons should include convolutional, recurrent, transformer-based, contrastive, self-supervised, and foundation-style approaches under matched preprocessing, subject splits, target-label restrictions, and computational constraints. Finally, deployment evidence is limited to software-side latency, model size, and estimated communication payload. Embedded evaluation is needed to measure end-to-end latency, memory demand, feature-extraction cost, wireless overhead, and energy consumption. The limited demographic and clinical metadata in the public datasets also prevent comprehensive assessment of subgroup robustness, fairness, and calibration. Future validation should therefore include larger and more diverse populations, richer metadata, different sensor placements, mobility limitations, and longitudinal monitoring scenarios. 7 Conclusion This study evaluated compact stability-aware feature selection for cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring. The proposed framework combines handcrafted wearable-signal descriptors, unlabeled source–target descriptor-stability analysis, compact feature selection, and lightweight classification. In the primary three-state setting, its outputs are coarse wearable-derived indicators sedentary, active, and vigorous rather than diagnostic decisions, clinical alarms, or validated clinical assessments. These labels operationally represent stationary or posture-dominated behavior, routine functional movement, and high-dynamic movement, respectively. Because the lowest-motion state includes standing, its sedentary label should not be interpreted as consensus-defined sedentary behavior. Under the pre-specified MHEALTH → PAMAP2 protocol and primary three-state mapping, Stable-48 / none achieved an accuracy of 0.9042 and a macro-F1 of 0.8753, while reducing the representation from 270 to 48 descriptors. It achieved higher complete-window accuracy and macro-F1 than the full-feature baseline, the source-MI-only compact subset, the evaluated shallow target-feature-transformation configurations, and the tested feature-level neural adaptation baselines within the common handcrafted descriptor space. These findings indicate that combining source-domain discriminative relevance with unlabeled source–target descriptor stability can improve descriptive target-domain performance while substantially reducing dimensionality. The reverse PAMAP2 → MHEALTH evaluation reproduced the same aggregate ordering using the frozen no-transformation configuration. Stable-48 achieved an accuracy of 0.5811 and a macro-F1 of 0.4400, compared with 0.5720 and 0.4320 for Source-MI-48 and 0.5445 and 0.4257 for Full-270. Stable-48 also reduced the mean compact-subset stability penalty by 23.6% relative to Source-MI-48. The participant-level contrasts were not statistically significant after Holm adjustment. Accordingly, the reverse evaluation supports consistency of the aggregate ranking across both transfer directions but does not establish universal or participant-level superiority. The primary configuration is unsupervised with respect to target labels but target-aware with respect to unlabeled target-feature distributions. It is therefore not a strict zero-target-data generalization setting. Harmonized target labels are accessed only after prediction generation for final evaluation; original activity identifiers are used only to apply the pre-specified activity-inclusion and window-boundary rules. The pre-specified values K = 48 and α = 0.60 are retained as methodological operating points rather than as universally optimal choices. Although the post-hoc K = 24 configuration achieved a higher macro-F1, adopting it on the basis of the current target-domain results would constitute retrospective target-guided selection. This alternative operating point therefore requires independent validation. The component ablation showed that the three stability terms contributed unequally. Mean shift provided the clearest incremental benefit, the rank-discrepancy term had a smaller regularizing role, and the no-JSD variant performed better under the primary transfer direction. The complete score is nevertheless retained as the primary formulation because it was pre-specified, whereas the no-JSD result is treated as a post-hoc diagnostic finding. Additional independent source–target pairs are needed before adopting a simplified stability formulation. The mapping-sensitivity analysis further showed that both transfer performance and configuration ordering depend on the adopted three-state harmonization. Stable-48 / none achieved the highest macro-F1 among the reported compact settings under the primary mapping, whereas Stable-48 / align achieved the highest macro-F1 under both alternative mappings. The principal findings are therefore conditional on the pre-specified taxonomy and should not be interpreted as invariant to alternative biomechanical or intensity-based harmonizations. Under the finer-grained six-activity taxonomy, Stable-48 achieved the highest complete-window macro-F1 at 0.4165, compared with 0.4077 for Full-270 and 0.3162 for Source-MI-48. Its mean stability penalty was 43.1% lower than that of Source-MI-48. Full-270 nevertheless retained slightly higher accuracy, balanced accuracy, and mean participant-level macro-F1, while standing and walking remained difficult to separate from sitting and stair climbing. Stability-aware selection therefore retained a macro-F1 and descriptor-stability advantage under the finer taxonomy, particularly relative to source-only selection, but did not dominate every metric or resolve all biomechanically similar activities. Overall, the results support stability-aware compact feature selection as a transparent and lightweight strategy for cross-dataset wearable activity monitoring under the evaluated protocols. The bidirectional results strengthen the evidence that the aggregate advantage is not confined to one source–target assignment, and the six-activity results show that the stability criterion remains informative when the taxonomy is refined. However, substantial activity-specific and subject-level variability remains, and the evidence does not establish universal robustness, clinical readiness, or general superiority over raw-signal deep-learning methods. Future work should evaluate additional independent source–target pairs, externally justified activity mappings, larger and more diverse cohorts, stronger raw-signal neural and domain-adaptation baselines, prospective daily-life monitoring, independent threshold calibration, temporal aggregation, subgroup robustness, and embedded implementation on wearable or edge devices.
