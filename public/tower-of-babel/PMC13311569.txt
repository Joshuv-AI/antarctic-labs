Why a value operating system now

Across high-income health systems, the trajectory of spending has become a structural concern.1,3 Expenditures grow faster than the wider economy, and they do so in an environment of heightened scrutiny of institutional performance.1 4 5 Clinicians are asked to demonstrate quality, efficiency and fairness, yet the instrumentation available in routine practice is misaligned with these aims.6 7 Dashboards frequently highlight processes that are easy to count rather than outcomes that matter to patients. Cost reports are assembled along accounting lines, are often intransparent and poorly accessible and do neither map to clinical decisions nor to outcomes. While health technology assessment (HTA) helps payers decide what to fund, it does not solve the day-to-day question of which care, as organised locally into episodes and pathways, will produce good outcomes for reasonable episode costs.8 9

This gap is particularly visible at the point where multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) must reconcile competing priorities. Surgeons, oncologists, nursing teams and allied health professionals see the immediate clinical signals—complications, readmissions and functional recovery—while accountants see service-level financials. Without a shared episode-level metric that can be optimised, attempts to improve either side can create misaligned effects on the other. It is therefore unsurprising that activity proliferates in areas that are measurable, whereas ‘value’—outcomes per cost—remains aspirational because it lacks a common, auditable unit of analysis.10,15

The public mandate across many jurisdictions is convergent: services should be effective, appropriate and economically efficient. This mandate has legal expression in different forms and is implemented through HTA processes, periodic benefit review and price–volume instruments. However, these tools predominantly operate ex ante (at the moment of coverage or price negotiation) and at the level of a technology or programme.16,18 The operational problem inside a hospital is ex post, continuous and episode-based: given the mix of patients and decisions actually made, what outcomes are being achieved at what episode cost, and how should the pathway adjust?19 20 (see box 1).

VI, Value Index

A credible answer requires an instrument that is simple to read, strictly standardised and objective.1221,23 As such, a metric that fulfils the requirements of a ‘proper scoring rule’ is needed. The value operating system (VOS) proposed here is an architecture that couples measurement to decision-making. Its executable signal is the Value Index (VI), a 0–100 score with CIs that quantifies outcomes-per-episode-cost. The intention is not to judge clinicians by decree, nor to substitute for guideline development or HTA. Rather, it is to give teams a compass that is conservative by design—transparent in its construction, explicit in its uncertainty and attentive to fairness—so that improvement work and resource stewardship are aligned rather than traded off. In doing so, the VOS addresses three deficits that undermine trust: weak auditability (numbers move without evident causes), brittle analytics (scores that do not show uncertainty promote over-interpretation) and asymmetric transparency (patients and purchasers cannot see the link between outcomes and spend).

Finally, the episode is a pragmatic locus for accountability.22 It matches clinical judgement (index events and horizons are already embedded in practice), supports attribution of outcomes and costs to the same decisions, and is intelligible to both clinicians and payers. The VOS focuses on what is minimally necessary for an honest episode signal rather than attempting comprehensive data capture. This deliberate simplicity, combined with rigorous guardrails, is key to feasibility and acceptance.

Principles and architecture of the VOS

The VOS translates a stable public mandate into a standardised, reproducible and episode-level signal that clinicians can use (architecture shown in figure 1).12 22 24 Its architecture is intentionally modest: a method for defining episodes and measuring the outcomes and costs that matter; a motor that moves data through interoperable pipelines with quality guardrails; and a compass that renders a cautious, interpretable score.

The value operating system: Mandate → Method → Engine → Compass. The Mandate (eg, statutory expectations of effectiveness, appropriateness and efficiency, i.e. WZW) defines what care should achieve. The Method (VBHC) specifies what to measure: patient-relevant outcomes (including PROMs) and harms, aligned with explicit episode-level costs. The Engine executes the pipeline in routine care—interoperable data flows, quality guardrails, governance and auditability. The Compass is the VI (0–100 with 95% CI and traffic-light semantics), expressing outcomes-per-episode-cost in a form clinicians can act on. VI feedback closes the loop by informing pathway redesign and sharpening interpretation of the mandate. PROMs, patient-reported outcome measures; VBHC, value-based health care; VI, Value Index.

The method begins with explicit episode definitions.25 Each episode is anchored to an index event—such as operation date, start of radiotherapy and initiation of systemic therapy—and is assigned a time horizon sufficient to capture early consequences of the pathway. Episodes are defined prospectively with inclusion and exclusion rules, attribution logic for readmissions and reinterventions, and provisions for censoring and competing risks. This preregistration avoids the analytic drift that often erodes credibility once improvement work begins.

Outcomes are purposefully multidomain.26 Clinical effectiveness includes constructs such as local control, avoidance of unplanned reintervention and early functional milestones relevant to the disease. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) provide a direct view of the patient’s experience; their interpretation is tethered to the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) to avoid mistaking trivial changes for meaningful gains.27,31 Harms are not an afterthought: serious toxicity, unplanned ICU admission and readmissions are recorded, weighted according to severity and potential harmfulness to the patient and can cap the composite outcomes score when rates exceed thresholds. The intent is not to punish services but to ensure that high scores cannot be achieved by neglecting safety or by selectively measuring only favourable outcomes.

Costs are captured as episode bundles rather than as line items reconstructed after the fact.12 23 32 Diagnostics, the index intervention, aftercare and attributable complications are included with a declared costing approach (microcosting, time-driven activity-based costing (TDBAC) or tariffs), and currency, price date and deflators are versioned. Costs are normalised against a risk-adjusted benchmark so that the cost score reflects efficiency rather than case-mix.33,36

The motor ensures that the measurement can actually run. Interoperable data structures simplify integration with existing electronic records and finance systems.37,39 Data completeness, latency and outliers are monitored continuously, with automatic suppression when thresholds are breached. Versioning of transformations, mappings and weights permits a full audit trail. Importantly, the user interface is restrained. Clinicians see an episode tile that contains the VI with its CI and traffic light, a PROM trajectory with MCID thresholds, a harm badge and a stacked bar showing episode costs against a benchmark. Managers see a service-line funnel that displays VI against volume with risk-adjusted control limits, alongside a Pareto of cost drivers and statistical process control (SPC) charts that mark the timing of pathway changes. An action register links proposed countermeasures to the metric they are intended to move.

The compass is the VI.4 40 41 It reports outcomes-per-episode-cost on a 0–100 scale calibrated so that 50 corresponds to the benchmark. The VI is conservative by default: it is always accompanied by a 95% CI; thresholds for interpretation are cautious; and a severe-harm veto prevents ‘green’ labels in the presence of excess harm. A grey suppression state is used whenever completeness, latency or model validity rules are not met. The aim is to favour honest uncertainty over spurious precision.

Governance threads through the system. Named roles—clinical, data, methods, product and compliance—own definitions, data quality, models and weights, release cadence and privacy protections. Quarterly value reviews consider outliers and preregistered countermeasures; all analyses carry semantic version tags so that comparisons over time remain interpretable. A public Methods Card documents episode rules, variables, mapping functions, weights and suppression logic. In combination, these features make the score explainable, revisable and legitimate. Full episode specifications, variable dictionaries, PROM windows, cost-bundle rules and suppression criteria are provided in online supplemental S1; figure 1 VOS schematic.

The VI: definition, normalisation, interpretation

The VI is constructed to be easy to read and difficult to game (construction steps in figure 2). It aggregates the minimum information required to express outcomes that matter per episode cost, and it does so with explicit allowance for risk and uncertainty.25 42 43

VI construction cascade. Raw episode inputs (case-mix, outcomes, harms, PROMs and costs) are first normalised to a common 0–100 scale with declared anchors and directionality, then risk-adjusted using a prespecified case-mix. Two component scores are produced: an outcomes score (weighted composite from clinical outcomes, PROMs and harms, with a prespecified harm veto) and a cost score (episode bundle benchmarked to a risk-adjusted median with a tolerance band). These are combined into the VI (0–100) via a monotone mapping calibrated to 50 = benchmark, displayed with a 95% CI, traffic-light interpretation and grey suppression when guardrails fail. Episodes and horizons are defined as described above. Episodes that fail mandatory fields, exceed latency limits or rely on models that do not meet calibration or discrimination criteria are suppressed to grey and excluded from comparative displays. For eligible episodes, outcomes are computed across three domains. Clinical effectiveness and harms are recorded as continuous, binary, ordinal, rate or time-to-event variables as appropriate and then mapped to a common 0–100 scale using predeclared anchors. PROMs are treated with additional care: changes from baseline are interpreted through an MCID ladder that assigns more weight to movements a patient would recognise as meaningful and less to trivial fluctuations; negative changes are mirrored to prevent positive skew. In analogy to the WIN-ratio method, outcomes are evaluated in composite. Formal equations, calibration and uncertainty methods are detailed in online supplemental S2.

Risk adjustment is specified in advance and includes variables that are routinely available and considered prognostic: age, sex, comorbidity, severity or stage, histology where relevant, baseline PROMs and prior treatment. Models are fitted with regularisation as necessary, and their performance is documented with calibration intercept and slope, as well as discrimination metrics suited to the outcome family. Where models fail minimum performance, analyses revert to stratified summaries, and the composite is not computed. This insistence on model validity avoids both over-correction and false precision.

Costs are assembled within the episode window and attributed according to rule. The costing method is declared and applied consistently. To render a cost score that is comparable across contexts, raw costs are normalised against a risk-adjusted benchmark with a tolerance band. The mapping is linear within the band and clipped to prevent leverage from extreme outliers. This approach separates efficiency from case-mix and ensures that changes in the cost score are interpretable in operational terms.

The composite outcomes score is the weighted sum of the normalised, risk-adjusted submeasures. Weights are elicited through a structured process that begins with Delphi to define candidate priorities and proceeds to an analytic hierarchy process in which clinicians and patients judge pairwise trade-offs. The resulting weight vector is versioned and sensitivity-tested. Local variants are allowed when justified by context—for example, a service that places additional emphasis on function—but the version identifier must be published to preserve interpretability across time and place. A severe-harm veto caps the composite outcomes score when rates exceed prespecified thresholds, reflecting the ethical stance that improvements should not be purchased with excess harm. Weight elicitation and the predefined sensitivity plan are described in online supplemental S2.

The VI is then assembled by relating the outcomes score to the cost score and calibrating the result to a 0–100 scale in which 50 equals the benchmark. Rather than fix a parametric form, we use a monotone mapping derived from the reference distribution, with safeguards against denominator artefacts (safeguards in box 1; dashboard artefacts, data lineage and suppression logic are documented in online supplemental S3). The VI is reported with a 95% CI obtained from non-parametric bootstrap resampling at the episode level. Decision thresholds are deliberately conservative: green is reserved for estimates whose entire CI lies above the benchmark; amber indicates uncertainty or proximity to the benchmark; red signals consistent underperformance; and grey denotes suppression for data or model reasons. Every display includes a brief ‘why’ explanation that decomposes the score into its components and shows the risk factors that matter most for the episode class.

A brief example illustrates the mechanics. Consider a sarcoma surgery episode with a 90-day horizon. After risk adjustment, local control and avoidance of unplanned reintervention map to a clinical subscore of 82/100; PROMs exceed the MCID ladder threshold and map to 75/100; harms are low and map to 90/100. With weights of 0.45, 0.35 and 0.20, respectively, the outcomes score is 81.2. The episode cost is CHF 28 500 against a benchmark of CHF 30 000 with a tolerance band of ±20%, which maps to a cost score of 58. Calibration yields a VI of approximately 73 with a 95% CI from 66 to 78. The interpretation is straightforward: outcomes are strong, and costs are modestly favourable; the signal is green because uncertainty does not cross the benchmark and no harm thresholds are breached. Weight elicitation and the predefined sensitivity plan are described in online supplemental S2.

From data to decisions: minimal dataset, guardrails, dashboarding

A practical instrument depends on a dataset that is small enough to collect reliably and rich enough to support honest inference. The minimal episode dataset (required fields in table 1) contains fields for the episode anchor and window, case-mix, outcomes, harms, PROMs and costs, alongside site and version identifiers.44 45 Each field carries a completeness threshold derived from experience with routine data quality. When thresholds are not met, the system withholds the composite and shows a grey state with a reason label. The purpose is not to punish services that are still maturing their data flows but to avoid the false confidence that incomplete information can produce. We refrain from using imputation methods as their performance and usefulness have not yet been studied for VI computations.

Guardrails go beyond completeness.46,48 PROMs require discipline: baseline and horizon windows are tight, missingness is examined for randomness, and response-propensity checks are standard. If non-random missingness is detected, selection adjustments are used where justified; otherwise, the composite is suppressed, and the dashboard calls attention to the issue. Harms have teeth: the veto rule caps the outcomes score when serious adverse events (SAEs) exceed thresholds for the risk stratum. Model validity is checked on every release; failure triggers a reversion to stratified reporting. Outliers are reviewed clinically before any percentile capping or other robust transformation is applied, and any suppression or transformation is documented in the audit trail.

The interface connects measurement to action.3749,53 In the episode tile, clinicians see a compact panel that supports a decision today. The VI and its CI occupy the header, not as a grade but as a concise synthesis of outcomes and costs. Below it, a small trajectory plot shows PROM change from baseline with MCID thresholds shaded, and a harm badge indicates whether any SAEs occurred. A stacked bar decomposes the episode cost into diagnostics, index intervention, aftercare and complications and shows the distance to the benchmark. If a clinician clicks ‘why?’, the panel unfolds to reveal the component scores, the weights applied, the major risk factors and a succinct narrative in plain language. The intention is to make the reasoning behind the number visible without overwhelming the user (decision loop illustrated in figure 3). The service-line funnel addresses variation rather than individual cases. It displays each site or team’s VI against episode volume with risk-adjusted control limits so that common-cause variation is not mistaken for special-cause signals. A Pareto chart identifies the cost components that contribute most to variance from the benchmark. SPC charts track the series over time and mark the dates when pathway changes were introduced. An equity panel shows subgroup VIs with CIs side by side. Finally, an action register links active countermeasures to the metrics they target and records whether predicted effects materialise. This workbench enables teams to move from signal to intervention to verification within a single environment.

Evidence of fitness-for-purpose: validity, sensitivity, equity

To change behaviour responsibly, a new metric must demonstrate that it measures the right construct, that conclusions are not fragile to defensible analytic choices, and that it does not disadvantage particular groups. Our evaluation strategy therefore emphasises validity, robustness and equity (evaluation commitments in box 2; operational safeguards (harm veto, grey suppression and model-validity stops) are codified in onlinesupplemental S1S3).

Claims and falsification map

Claim 1—VI measures ‘outcomes-per-episode-cost’ (construct validity). Falsification: if partial correlations do not show VI increasing with outcomes and decreasing with risk-adjusted costs (holding the other constant), or if directionality reverses in sensitivity analyses, the construct claim fails.

Claim 2—VI is responsive to pathway change (responsiveness). Falsification: if preregistered pathway interventions do not produce aligned step/slope shifts on SPC/ITS at the planned timepoints, responsiveness is not supported.

Claim 3—conclusions are robust to defensible choices (robustness). Falsification: If ±20% wt perturbations, alternative PROM anchors, simpler/regularised risk models, different cost bands/benchmarks, ±14 day windows, or outlier rules produce frequent traffic-light reclassifications beyond the predeclared tolerance, robustness fails.

Claim 4—VI does not disadvantage subgroups after risk adjustment (equity). Falsification: if subgroup VIs show systematic disadvantage with calibrated models, or if calibration parity fails and cannot be remediated, equity is not supported.

Claim 5—governance prevents gaming and spurious certainty. Falsification: If severe-harm vetoes, completeness/latency thresholds or model-validity stops are bypassed without grey suppression, or if audit trails cannot reproduce changes, governance claims fail.

Pass/fail protocol: for each claim, we prespecify pass criteria and grey/suppress conditions in online supplemental files S2; S3. Failures will be reported with root-cause analysis and corrective actions in quarterly value reviews.

Construct validity is assessed in several complementary ways.15 First, monotonicity checks confirm that the VI increases with the outcomes score and decreases with risk-adjusted costs when the other dimension is held constant. Partial correlations and local regression plots help guard against artefacts. Second, known-groups comparisons use strata that are expected to differ—margin status, complexity class or toxicity categories—and examine whether mean VIs are ordered with non-overlapping CIs in the expected direction. The usefulness of the selected groups can be evaluated and adjusted if appropriate. Third, responsiveness is tested by linking preregistered pathway changes to expected signals on SPC charts; step or slope changes that appear when and where anticipated provide convergent evidence. Finally, convergent validity is examined by correlating service-line VIs with independent quality indicators, such as the PROM MCID attainment rate or a complication index, and by comparing VI-based rankings with blinded expert ratings of case vignettes.

Robustness is examined through stress tests that vary each rule or parameter within a reasonable range.15 Weights are perturbed by ±20% and renormalised; outcome anchors are swapped for alternatives (for example, linear PROM mapping vs the MCID ladder); risk adjustment models are replaced with simpler stratifications or regularised alternatives; cost normalisation uses internal historical benchmarks instead of peer medians and widens or narrows tolerance bands; episode windows expand or contract by 2 weeks; and outlier handling toggles between full inclusion and percentile capping (winsorisation). The pass criterion is conservative: traffic-light classifications should rarely change, and when they do, the original CIs should already have suggested caution. The point is not that every choice is arbitrary, but that conclusions are stable across defensible specifications.

Equity is treated as a property to be demonstrated, not assumed.54,56 Subgroup VIs for age, sex, deprivation proxies, distance to centre and relevant intersectional cells are examined with CIs. Calibration of risk models is checked within subgroups, and model fixes or subgroup-specific models are used where needed. PROM response patterns are reviewed for bias, and selection adjustments are applied or suppression enforced if non-random missingness would otherwise distort inference. When signals suggest a disparity, the dashboard highlights the issue and routes it to review with proposed remedies. In this way, the metric itself helps prevent the improvement process from bypassing groups whose outcomes might be harder to improve or less frequently measured.

Use cases and implications

We replace narrow, siloed vignettes with two cross-cycle exemplars that show how the VOS changes decisions when the full episode is counted.

Optimisation versus intensification across the care cycle (sarcoma)

This exemplar contrasts an optimisation strategy—short-course preoperative radiotherapy followed by timely surgery within a disciplined pathway—with therapy intensification that adds agents to standard fractionation. Published data indicate that optimisation can achieve comparable disease control with substantially lower high-grade toxicity and markedly shorter treatment duration.57 58 When the entire episode is tallied, including complications, unplanned revisits and early failures, the difference in total cost of care favours optimisation. In parallel, the integrity of the pathway matters: ‘whoops’ resections outside specialised MDT settings are associated with higher local recurrence. Those failures lengthen the episode through reoperations, reirradiation or systemic therapy, rehabilitation and productivity loss. Within VOS, these dynamics are visible: the outcomes domain reflects similar control but fewer harms under optimisation; the cost domain improves due to fewer fractions, fewer toxicity-driven encounters and fewer salvage procedures; and the VI rises with a CI that communicates caution. Guardrails apply: a harm veto prevents ‘green’ labels in optimisation cohorts if SAEs rise; suppression is enforced if first-contact pathway flags or local-recurrence fields are incomplete. Episode cost bundles include diagnostics, index therapy, aftercare and attributable salvage; costing follows declared methods (eg, TDABC or tariffs) with currency/date versioning. In the service-line funnel, centres with frequent whoops resections and higher local recurrence rates appear below risk-adjusted control limits; countermeasures (early-referral standards, preoperative protocol adherence) are linked to expected VI movement and verified on SPC charts.59,61

Decision support with causal machine learning (targeted radiotherapy)

This exemplar addresses overtreatment that arises when decisions follow population-average effects.62,64 Using causal machine learning, individualised treatment effects for combined local therapy identify a minority of patients who derive clinically meaningful event-free survival benefit from radiotherapy, while many others do not—and some are harmed. VOS turns this into a governed decision rule: offer radiotherapy when predicted ΔEFS at a prespecified time point exceeds a predeclared MCID; otherwise prefer surgery alone (or another modality) with close follow-up. The Methods Card documents model specification, calibration and fairness diagnostics; a model version ID appears on the dashboard; and guardrails enforce grey suppression if subgroup calibration fails. The expected value signal is twofold: in those treated, outcomes improve enough to justify radiotherapy’s added cost and toxicity; in those spared, outcomes remain acceptable while both harms and costs fall. Net effects are higher service-line VI, narrower cost variation and fewer downstream salvage episodes. Equity panels confirm that targeting does not underserve identifiable subgroups. This exemplar demonstrates VOS as a decision engine—not just a reporting layer—by embedding predictive judgements into episode-level accountability.

Implications

These exemplars show how VI complements HTA with postcoverage performance, supports value-aligned contracts that reward measured improvement (not crude cost cuts) and makes explicit the trade-offs that matter to patients. They also clarify portability: episode logic, multidomain outcomes, risk adjustment, uncertainty and governance are universal; only cost benchmarks and, optionally, outcome weights require localisation.

In sum, we specify a VOS and its executable, auditable VI—a risk-adjusted, uncertainty-aware 0–100 outcomes-per-episode-cost signal—designed to steer day-to-day improvement and stewardship at the clinical team level. By embedding governance guardrails (transparency of component scores, prospectively registered weight-elicitation, prespecified sensitivity analyses, equity checks and antigaming rules), the framework aligns pathway decisions with patient-relevant benefit while discouraging unintended incentives. Our approach remains bounded by local cost benchmarks, context-specific weight localisation and data completeness/latency; these constraints are explicit and measured. The forthcoming prospective evaluations of our two exemplars will test real-world performance, including usability, fairness across subgroups and the impact on cost-drivers, thereby informing scale-up, contracting and policy use.
