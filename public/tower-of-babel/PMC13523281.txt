Effectiveness of different intrusion protocols with clear aligner for maxillary anterior teeth in periodontitis: a finite element analysis
Authors: Jingyuan Su, Xiuping Nie, Tian Xiao, Jie Lei, Xin Zhang, Jian Yu, Junxiang Hou, Jie Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtrusion of anterior teeth is a common manifestation of periodontitis. Orthodontic intrusion is an effective approach for improving occlusion and periodontal health. This study aimed to identify the optimal protocols for maxillary incisor intrusion using clear aligner (CA) in patients with periodontitis.MethodsA validated finite element model was established, including the CA, teeth, periodontal ligaments (PDLs), and alveolar bone with three levels of bone height (simulating mild to severe periodontitis). The intrusion angles were varied from 0° to 60° in 10° increments relative to each tooth’s long axis. An intrusion of 0.1 mm was simulated for the maxillary incisors under each periodontal condition. Initial tooth movements and stress distributions of PDLs were evaluated.ResultsBoth central incisors and lateral incisors exhibited absolute intrusion under all conditions, and the amount of intrusion decreased with increasing intrusion angle. At an intrusion angle of 0°, central incisors initially exhibited relative extrusion and lingual tipping, gradually transitioning to relative intrusion and labial tipping as the angle increased. In contrast, lateral incisors consistently displayed relative intrusion with less lingual tipping and earlier tipping transitions. These transitions occurred at lower angles in mild periodontitis.ConclusionsCA designed to intrude maxillary incisors along the tooth’s long axis may not achieve bodily intrusion in periodontitis. Background

Extrusion of anterior teeth is a common clinical manifestation of periodontitis, particularly in moderate to severe cases [1]. As inflammatory processes can destroy periodontal tissue and lead to bone resorption, pathologic tooth migration (PTM) of anterior teeth may occur, including facial flaring, diastema, extrusion, and rotation. The prevalence of PTM in the anterior region has been reported to be 30%, with extrusion accounting for 68% of cases [2]. This condition negatively affects facial aesthetics, deepens overbite, and induces traumatic occlusion, thereby further exacerbating periodontal deterioration [1, 3]. Compared with alternative treatment options, such as tooth splinting, occlusal adjustment, and prosthetic rehabilitation, orthodontic treatment offers distinct advantages for treating anterior tooth extrusion. Orthodontic intrusion has been shown to improve occlusal relationships and periodontal conditions, as shown by reduced inflammation, bone regeneration, and improved attachment levels [4, 5]. Once periodontal inflammation is controlled, orthodontic treatment is not contraindicated for severe adult periodontal disease [5]. Therefore, timely orthodontic intervention is essential for managing pathologically elongated anterior teeth.

In periodontally compromised dentition, reduced alveolar support shifts the center of resistance (CR) apically, which consequently increases the torque acting on the tooth. As a result, a more precise intrusion strategy is needed to avoid uncontrolled crown tipping and orthodontically induced root resorption (OIRR) [6]. In recent years, clear aligner (CA) has gained widespread popularity because of its esthetic appearance, removability, and wearer comfort. For patients with periodontitis, CA provides specific benefits: it facilitates oral hygiene maintenance, avoids significant damage to periodontal tissues, and applies light, intermittent forces that are well tolerated by compromised periodontium [7, 8]. Collectively, these features make CA a suitable option for orthodontic treatment in such patients.

However, studies indicate that CA has limited efficacy in incisor intrusion. Li et al. [9] reported that mandibular incisor intrusion often deviates from the planned direction, and bodily intrusion is rarely achieved. Al-Balaa et al. [10] reported that CA reached 51% accuracy in anterior intrusion, with the maxillary lateral incisors being the most precise (58%). Krieger et al. [11] demonstrated poor efficacy of CA in deep overbite correction, achieving only 14% of the intended movement. These limitations become even more pronounced in periodontally compromised dentition. To date, no study has comprehensively clarified effective protocols for anterior intrusion using CA in periodontitis patients, especially in the maxillary anterior region. Therefore, further studies are needed to optimize CA application.

Finite element analysis (FEA) offers a precise, noninvasive approach to simulate biomechanical responses to external forces [12]. In recent years, FEA has been increasingly utilized to explore the biomechanical mechanisms of the CA [12–14]. For example, Jiang et al. employed FEA to investigate the effects of different retraction protocols with CA and demonstrated that, compared with a bodily retraction design, an angulated design was more effective in achieving controlled anterior tooth movement [13]. This study employed FEA to investigate the effects of different intrusion protocols on maxillary incisors in periodontitis patients, assessing tooth displacements and stress distributions of periodontal ligaments (PDLs) to provide clinical guidance.

Methods

Establishment of the 3D FE model

Tooth morphologies were derived from industrial CT scans of a standard maxillary model (Model i21D-400G; Nissin Dental Products Inc., Kyoto, Japan). The CT data were imported into Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for 3D reconstruction and subsequently optimized in Geomagic Wrap 2016 (3D Systems, USA). The thickness of PDLs were set at 0.25 mm, uniformly extending around the root surface within the alveolar bone [15]. Vertical (2 × 4 × 1 mm) and horizontal (3 × 2 × 1 mm) rectangular attachments were designed in SolidWorks 2015 (Dassault Systèmes, USA) and bonded to canines and premolars, respectively. The thermoforming process of CA was simulated via Masterforce (Angelalign Technology Inc., Shanghai, China) to replicate a clinically realistic, nonuniform aligner thickness distribution (0.55–0.75 mm) [9].

The alveolar bone morphology was determined based on the 2017 classification of periodontal disease [16]. Mild periodontitis (stage I, S1) involves slight alveolar bone loss, with the bone height maintained near the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). Moderate periodontitis (stage II, S2) involves approximately one-third alveolar bone loss, and severe periodontitis (stage III, S3) is modeled with approximately one-half alveolar bone loss, both following the anatomical curvature of the CEJ [14, 17].

The components were assembled in ABAQUS 2016 (Simulia, USA). All components were meshed using tetrahedral elements. Mesh convergence analysis was performed by monitoring the maximum von Mises stress, with convergence defined as a relative change of less than 5% between two successive refinement levels. The final mesh sizes were 0.1 mm for attachments and 0.3 mm for other components. The model components and mesh characteristics are shown in Fig. 1; Table 1, respectively.

Simulation of orthodontic intrusion

Virtual intrusion of the maxillary incisors (0.1 mm) was digitally programmed for each periodontal condition (S1–S3). The intrusion angle was defined in the Y1–Z1 plane of each tooth within the tooth-specific local coordinate system (defined below), with rotation performed around the X1 axis relative to the tooth’s long axis. To assess the biomechanical effects, the intrusion angle was varied from 0° to 60° in 10° increments (Fig. 2A). All four maxillary incisors were intruded simultaneously in each simulation via initial interference/contact interaction. The loading process was simulated based on previous studies [13, 18].

Measurement items and methods

The vertical intrusion of maxillary incisors under different periodontal conditions was evaluated using both local and global coordinate systems. The central incisal edge and apex of the central and lateral incisors, as well as the cusp and apex of the canines, were selected as the measuring points [23].

To better guide clinical practice, two vertical parameters were evaluated [24, 25]:

(1) Absolute intrusion: Defined as the vertical displacement of the crown and root along the Z1-axis. This measurement reflects the movement of the tooth relative to the alveolar bone (positive for absolute intrusion and negative for absolute extrusion).

(2) Relative intrusion: Defined as the vertical displacement of the crown along the Z2-axis. This measurement reflects the visible clinical tooth movement relative to the occlusal plane (positive for relative intrusion and negative for relative extrusion).

Results

Movement of the incisors

Within the present finite element model, both central and lateral incisors exhibited absolute intrusion in all groups, and the amount of intrusion decreased with increasing intrusion angle (Figs. 3A and B and 4A and B). For the central incisor, when the intrusion angle was 0°, it exhibited relative extrusion and lingual tipping. As the angle increased, a gradual shift to relative intrusion was observed, first appearing at 30° (S1), 40° (S2), and 50° (S3), and becoming more evident at higher angles (Fig. 3C). Similarly, a transition toward labiolingual tipping first occurred at 50° (S1), 60° (S2), and 60° (S3), respectively (Fig. 3D).

For the lateral incisor, it showed relative intrusion under all conditions, with a decreased amount as the intrusion angle increased (Fig. 4C). Labiolingual tipping transition was observed earlier than that of the central incisor, occurring at 40° (S1), 50° (S2), and 60° (S3) respectively (Fig. 4D). Additionally, under the same conditions, both the absolute and relative intrusion of the lateral incisor were greater than those of the central incisor, whereas the tendency for lingual tipping was smaller.

Movement of the canine

Under all conditions, the canine consistently exhibited both absolute and relative extrusion, with the greatest absolute extrusion observed at 60° and the greatest relative extrusion at 0° (Fig. 5A–C). In addition, the canine showed distal tipping in all conditions, and this tendency increased with increasing intrusion angle (Fig. 5D). At the same intrusion angle, worsening periodontal conditions further increased both absolute and relative extrusion, as did the tendency for distal tipping.

Discussion

Maxillary anterior tooth extrusion, a typical manifestation of PTM due to periodontitis, leads to significant functional and esthetic problems. Orthodontic intrusion, particularly when combined with regenerative periodontal therapy, has proven to be an effective and reliable treatment strategy. In fixed appliance therapy, intruding maxillary anterior teeth typically requires auxiliary mechanisms such as segmented arches, utility arches, J-hook headgear, or TADs, and labial tipping is often an unavoidable side effect [26]. The continuous forces generated by fixed appliances may also pose additional risks to the vulnerable periodontium. CA, by contrast, delivers intermittent, light forces directly to the tooth surface, with the intrusion direction adjustable through digital treatment planning, while also facilitating oral hygiene and supporting periodontal health [27, 28]. Such a force delivery pattern may further allow PDL recovery and promote bone remodeling in compromised patients. Despite these advantages, controlled incisor intrusion with CA remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly in periodontally compromised patients, often resulting in insufficient overbite correction or unintended tooth flaring [9, 11, 13]. Nevertheless, limited evidence is available regarding the biomechanical effects of different CA intrusion protocols under various periodontal conditions. Therefore, this study utilized FEA to investigate maxillary incisor intrusion in periodontitis, aiming to fill this research gap and provide clinical guidance for optimizing treatment protocols.

FEA has been widely applied as a powerful tool for biomechanical investigations in orthodontics. The accuracy of the finite element model is crucial for ensuring the reliability of the simulation results. During CA treatment, orthodontic force is generated mainly by the aligner, and factors such as its material properties and thickness directly affect the magnitude and distribution of the applied force [29]. Currently, most FEA studies continue to assume a uniform aligner thickness along the crown contour, which oversimplifies the mechanical behavior of the aligner and may compromise the clinical relevance of the simulations [13, 20]. To address this limitation, we replicated the thermoforming process via clinical software, generating a CA model with regionally variable thicknesses ranging from 0.55 mm to 0.75 mm. This approach better captured structural details, allowing for more accurate prediction of tooth displacement and force distribution, thereby enhancing the reliability and clinical relevance of the simulation.

Intrusion, defined as the apical displacement of the tooth, can be assessed either relative to the occlusal plane or along the tooth’s long axis [24], with the latter commonly termed absolute intrusion [25]. This distinction is important because labiolingual tipping of an incisor can produce pseudointrusion, mimicking vertical displacement without true intrusion [30]. However, previous FEA studies have often relied on a single measurement approach, which may not fully capture the complexity of true intrusion [15, 21]. To address this limitation, we developed a dual-metric framework that evaluates intrusion in two directions—along the tooth’s long axis and perpendicular to the occlusal plane—to achieve a comprehensive assessment of vertical tooth movement. Absolute intrusion reflects apical displacement within the alveolar bone and helps optimize force application to reduce occlusal interference and support periodontal health. Relative intrusion, referring to crown movement relative to the occlusal plane and possibly influenced by labiolingual tipping, is more relevant for correcting overbite and improving aesthetics. This combined approach helps clinicians select treatment strategies aligned with specific clinical goals.

In CA therapy, intrusive forces are typically applied at the incisal edge [31], limiting the ability to deliver force through the tooth’s CR. Li et al. [9] reported that mandibular incisors planned to intrude along the long axis failed to achieve bodily intrusion and exhibited lingual crown tipping. However, few studies have assessed this effect in maxillary incisors. Our results showed that at 0° intrusion angles, maxillary central and lateral incisors consistently exhibited lingual tipping at all periodontitis stages, with greater inclination and relative extrusion observed in the central incisors. This may compromise the effectiveness of intrusion and partly explain the limited performance of the CA in deep overbite correction, even in cases where the overbite worsens despite planned intrusion. Our study suggests that adjusting the direction of aligner deformation can reduce labiolingual tipping, increase relative intrusion, and optimize the stress distribution in the PDLs, in line with the findings of Jiang et al. [13]. Specifically, minimal labiolingual tipping of central incisors was observed at intrusion angles of 40°–50° in stage I and 50°–60° in stages II–III periodontitis. For lateral incisors, the least tipping occurred at angles of 20°–30°, 30°–40°, and 40°–50° in stages I, II, and III periodontitis, respectively. In addition, to avoid relative extrusion of central incisors in the simulations, minimum intrusion angles of 20°–30°, 30°–40°, and 40°–50° for stages I, II, and III periodontitis, respectively, were necessary. These results provide clinically relevant targets for force‒vector control in periodontally compromised patients, particularly those with deep overbites or pronounced gummy smiles. They also highlight the inherent challenges of CA therapy, partly due to the full-coverage design and limited appliance stiffness [32], suggesting that effective torque control—especially for central incisors—should be a primary focus in intrusion treatment planning. Notably, adjusting the intrusion angle may offer a practical approach to reduce labiolingual tipping, which is not as straightforward with fixed appliances.

Clinically, a gap between the aligner and the lateral incisors is often observed during anterior intrusion, which our study helps explain. When equal intrusion was planned for both the central and lateral incisors, the latter exhibited greater intrusion amount. This finding is consistent with that of Al-Balaa et al. [10], who reported a higher success rate of intrusion in lateral incisors (58.12%) than in central incisors (51.83%). Lateral incisors exhibited greater intrusion, which may be attributed to their position closer to the anchorage teeth, leading to earlier contact with the aligners [31]. Their smaller root surface area and weaker periodontal support further facilitate movement. Additionally, lateral incisors showed less labiolingual tipping under intrusive forces, indicating more controlled vertical movement. These findings suggest that clinical intrusion plans should account for tooth-specific differences, incorporate overcorrection strategies, and implement material modifications to enhance local stiffness in aligner design [32].

Anchorage stability is a critical determinant of orthodontic efficiency and predictability in CA therapy. In our finite element models, canines acting as anchorage exhibited extrusion, lingual tipping, and distal tipping under all conditions, with distal tipping becoming more pronounced as the intrusion angle increased. This highlights the importance of controlling mesiodistal angulation, which is consistent with previous findings that ranked the mesiodistal angulation of the canines as the second most important factor for predictable anterior intrusion, after the labiolingual angulation of the central incisors [24]. Distal tipping of the canine may contribute to the roller–coaster effect, complicating overbite correction. To enhance anchorage stability and control undesired tipping, auxiliary measures such as strategically placed attachments or planned overcorrection can be considered. Nevertheless, because the canines serve as the principal anchorage units during incisor intrusion, absolute intrusion of the incisors may require absolute anchorage; therefore, adjunctive temporary anchorage devices (TADs) may be considered when stronger anchorage control is necessary.

OIRR is a potential complication of incisor intrusion that warrants close monitoring [33, 34]. In this study, stress was primarily concentrated at the root apex, suggesting a high risk of apical root resorption. While maxillary central incisors are traditionally considered most susceptible to OIRR, this view may be influenced by the limitations of two-dimensional assessments [33, 35]. Recent CBCT studies, such as that by Puttaravuttiporn et al. [36], have demonstrated greater root resorption in lateral incisors, a trend also observed in our study. Lateral incisors exhibited higher peak PDL stress, which may be attributed to their smaller apical areas and the more concentrated force application.

The maximum hydrostatic pressure (MHP: 2–16 kPa) and maximum tolerable stress (MTS: 15–26 kPa) serve as reference thresholds for assessing the biological safety of orthodontic forces, beyond which PDL capillary perfusion may be compromised and structural damage may occur [37]. Previous studies have suggested that von Mises stress can provide meaningful qualitative and quantitative evaluation of PDL mechanical responses, particularly in models involving periodontal breakdown [37–39]. Moga et al. [37] applied the von Mises stress failure criterion to assess PDL stress during gradual periodontal breakdown, reporting that in intact periodontium, von Mises stress remained below MHP and MTS, whereas in reduced periodontium, rotational and tipping movements cervically exceeded MHP. In the present study, the peak von Mises stress values of all anterior teeth ranged from 1.5 to 10.7 kPa, which are of comparable magnitude to previously reported biological reference thresholds. However, individual susceptibility to root resorption varies [34], and lateral incisors exhibited higher stress levels due to their smaller apical areas and more concentrated force application. Therefore, clinicians should closely monitor the progress of intrusion, particularly for lateral incisors, and inform patients about the potential risks.

Alveolar bone loss alters the position of the CR, which in turn affects the biomechanical response to orthodontic forces [40, 41]. Our study revealed that both lingual tipping and relative extrusion increased with increasing severity of bone resorption, necessitating greater intrusion angles for effective movement. Reduced periodontal support also results in increased tooth mobility and a compromised stress distribution in the PDLs [42]. For periodontitis patients, it is therefore critical to apply light forces, maintain precise control over the force direction, and minimize the biological risk to the periodontal tissues.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, owing to individual variability, developing a universal mathematical model remains challenging. Secondly, the FEA simulations focused only on initial tooth displacement and stress distribution, without considering long-term periodontal remodeling. Thirdly, the PDL was assumed to have fully recovered normal mechanical properties after periodontal therapy, which may not fully represent all clinical conditions. Given the complexity of the intraoral environment, further studies are required to validate these findings and to investigate the biomechanical behavior under different PDL recovery states. Finally, this study primarily analyzes displacement and angulation changes along the main biomechanical directions to illustrate key trends. The mesiodistal displacement of the incisors was not the focus of the present study and remains an area for future investigation.

Conclusions
