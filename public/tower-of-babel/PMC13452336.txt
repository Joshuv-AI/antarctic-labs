Dataset of oxidation properties of refractory alloys
Authors: Wei-Chih Lin, Stéphane Gorsse, An-Chou Yeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article presents a curated dataset of high-temperature oxidation experiments on refractory alloys and refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs). The data were compiled from 62 peer-reviewed publications spanning 1956–2023 and supplemented with initial in-house experimental data generated by the authors’ laboratory. For each experiment, alloy composition (at.%), oxidation temperature (°C), exposure time (h), specific mass change in air (mg/cm²), and the data source are reported in a harmonised format. The dataset is openly accessible through an online repository. It is intended to support data-driven analysis, benchmarking, and machine-learning-assisted design of refractory alloys with improved high-temperature oxidation resistance.Keywords: Isothermal oxidation, High temperature oxidation, Machine learning, Thermogravimetric analysis

Background

Refractory alloys and refractory high-entropy alloys are of growing interest for high-temperature applications because of their high melting temperatures and promising mechanical performance at elevated temperatures. However, their oxidation behaviour remains a major limitation for practical use. Experimental oxidation data for these materials are available in the literature, but they are dispersed across many publications, reported in heterogeneous formats, and therefore difficult to reuse for systematic comparison or data-driven analysis. This dataset was created to provide a curated, harmonized, and machine-readable collection of isothermal oxidation results for alloys based on Al, Cr, Hf, Mo, Nb, Si, Ta, Ti, V, W, and Zr, compiled from published literature and supplemented with initial in-house experimental data generated by the authors’ laboratory. It contains alloy compositions, oxidation temperatures, exposure times, specific mass change in air, and bibliographic source information, compiled in a standardized format. This data article is associated with the related research article by Gorsse et al.[63] It provides the curated, harmonized, openly archived, and reusable version of the oxidation dataset together with structured metadata and documentation, so that it can be readily used for comparative studies, benchmarking, and future data-science or materials-informatics applications.

The oxidation experiments span a broad range of conditions relevant to high-temperature structural applications. Oxidation temperatures range from approximately 600 to 1400°C, with exposure times most commonly reported at 3, 4, 10, 20, 50, and 100 h (Fig. 1). The measured specific mass change values extend over more than four orders of magnitude (Fig. 2), from approximately 0.02 mg/cm² to 345 mg/cm², and exhibit a strongly right-skewed distribution characteristic of oxidation datasets that include both protective and non-protective oxide-scale formation. The pronounced skewness of Δm suggests that logarithmic transformation is more appropriate than linear scaling for statistical analysis and machine-learning modelling.

In terms of composition (Fig. 3), the dataset shows dense coverage of alloys based on Ti, Al, Nb, Cr, Mo, and Ta, reflecting their widespread use in refractory alloy and RHEA research. Elements such as Hf, V and W are present but comparatively underrepresented, which mirrors historical experimental trends rather than intentional filtering.

Consistent with this element-wise coverage, the UMAP projections of normalised compositions (Fig. 4) reveal a highly non-uniform exploration of composition space across oxidation temperature ranges. At 600–800 °C, the data are confined to a small number of compact and well-separated clusters, indicating that only a narrow set of closely related alloy families has been investigated. As the temperature increases to 800–1000 °C, the explored space becomes more dispersed but remains uneven, with several small clusters and isolated compositions. In the 1000–1200 °C range, the coverage is particularly sparse, with largely isolated points and little clustering, while at 1200–1400 °C the data again concentrate into a few localized clusters corresponding to targeted studies of specific alloy systems. Together, these observations highlight that, despite dense sampling of certain alloy families, large regions of the high-dimensional composition space remain underexplored, especially at higher temperatures. These maps provide a qualitative overview of dataset coverage and are intended solely for visualisation, not as predictive models.

Each row in the dataset represents one unique oxidation condition, defined by a specific alloy composition, oxidation temperature, and exposure time. In several cases, identical alloy–temperature–time combinations appear multiple times with different reported mass-change values. These duplicated conditions originate from independent experiments reported within the different publications and were retained in the dataset to preserve information on experimental variability and measurement scatter. This design choice enables downstream statistical analysis, uncertainty quantification, and robust machine-learning model training.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The overall workflow used for data collection, curation, and validation is summarised schematically in Fig. 6. The procedure consists of five main stages: systematic literature search, screening based on predefined inclusion criteria, numerical data extraction from tables and Fig.s, data harmonisation and standardisation, and final technical validation prior to dataset release.

Schematic overview of the literature-based workflow used to construct the oxidation dataset, including literature search, screening and inclusion criteria, data extraction, harmonisation, and final validation prior to release in CSV and JSON formats.

A systematic literature survey was conducted to collect high-temperature oxidation data for refractory alloys and refractory high-entropy alloys. Searches were performed using Web of Science and Google Scholar. The main search keywords included combinations of refractory alloy, high-entropy alloy, oxidation, high-temperature oxidation, mass change, isothermal oxidation, and the names of individual refractory elements (Al, Cr, Hf, Mo, Nb, Si, Ta, Ti, V, W, Zr). Reference lists of relevant review articles and key experimental papers were also manually screened to identify additional data sources not captured by keyword searches. All searches were conducted and finalized by December 2023, and no publications released after this date were included.

Only experimental data meeting the following criteria were retained in the dataset. First, alloy compositions were restricted to systems containing only the eleven selected elements: Al, Cr, Hf, Mo, Nb, Si, Ta, Ti, V, W, and Zr. Alloys containing additional metallic or non-metallic elements were excluded. Second, only isothermal oxidation experiments were considered; cyclic oxidation tests or continuously ramped temperature experiments were not included. Third, the oxidation atmosphere was restricted to air, as explicitly stated in the original publication; experiments conducted in controlled oxygen partial pressures, inert atmospheres, or mixed gas environments were excluded.

The retained oxidation temperatures and exposure times correspond to the ranges most commonly reported in the literature, with temperatures spanning approximately 600–1400 °C and exposure times of 3, 4, 10, 20, 50, and 100 h. Only studies reporting specific mass change per unit area (Δm) were included. Oxidation rate constants (e.g., parabolic rate constants, kp) or oxide-scale thickness values were not converted to mass change and were therefore excluded. In cases where both mass change and parabolic rate constants were reported, only the directly measured mass-change values were retained. This choice was made to avoid introducing additional assumptions related to oxide composition, density, scale morphology, and growth kinetics when harmonizing data from different literature sources.

When oxidation data were directly tabulated in the original publication, numerical values were transcribed verbatim. In cases where oxidation results were reported only graphically, data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer(https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer.html). For each Fig., axes were calibrated using the original axis labels and scales, with particular care taken for logarithmic axes. Individual data points were manually digitized by extracting visible values along oxidation curves at the designated exposure times (3, 4, 10, 20, 50, and 100 h). Reported error bars were not digitized and are therefore not included in the dataset.

When multiple curves were present within a single Fig., curve identity was determined based on line style, marker type, and legend information. In cases of overlapping curves or partially ambiguous legends, digitization was restricted to regions where curve attribution could be made unambiguously. Data points that could not be confidently associated with a specific alloy composition or oxidation condition were discarded to avoid misassignment.

All extracted alloy compositions were converted to atomic percent (at.%), regardless of the original reporting format. Composition sums were checked to ensure consistency, with acceptable totals within numerical rounding of approximately 100 at.%. Oxidation mass-change values were converted to a common unit of mg/cm² when necessary, using standard unit conversions.

Column names were normalized across all entries to ensure consistency in spelling, capitalization, and units. Oxidation temperature and exposure time were recorded as numerical values in degrees Celsius and hours, respectively, and bibliographic sources were standardized to include DOI or persistent URLs wherever available.

The final dataset adopts a long-format data structure, in which each row corresponds to one individual oxidation experiment, defined by a unique combination of alloy composition, oxidation temperature, exposure time, and measured specific mass change. This format was chosen in preference to a wide-format representation (e.g., separate columns for mass change at different exposure times) to preserve experimental granularity and to facilitate statistical analysis, visualization, and machine-learning workflows. When multiple measurements were reported for identical alloy–temperature–time conditions, each measurement was retained as a separate row to reflect experimental variability.

To ensure the reliability and internal consistency of the curated oxidation dataset, a multi-step data-quality and validation procedure was applied throughout the extraction and harmonisation process. All data were independently extracted by two curators, and the resulting entries were compared at the level of alloy composition, oxidation temperature, exposure time, and specific mass change. Any conflicting values were systematically traced back to the original source material and resolved through re-examination of tables, Fig.s, or supplementary information. Data points that could not be unambiguously confirmed were conservatively discarded rather than inferred or interpolated.

All alloy compositions were subjected to compositional sanity checks by evaluating the sum of elemental concentrations expressed in atomic percent. Across the final dataset, composition sums cluster close to 100 at.%, with minor deviations attributable to rounding in the original publications. Entries exhibiting larger deviations were individually inspected. Alloys containing elements outside the predefined eleven-element palette (Al, Cr, Hf, Mo, Nb, Si, Ta, Ti, V, W, and Zr), such as B, C, or other minor additions, were excluded entirely. No renormalisation was applied in such cases in order to avoid introducing artificial compositional bias; only alloys explicitly composed of the selected elements were retained.

Specific mass-change values were further validated to ensure physical credibility. Apparent order-of-magnitude inconsistencies, such as suspected factor-of-ten errors arising from unit conversion or transcription mistakes, were flagged and checked against the original source. When confirmation was not possible, the corresponding data points were removed. For alloy systems reported at multiple exposure times under identical temperatures, qualitative time-consistency checks were performed, as mass change is generally expected to increase with exposure time. Deviations from this trend were not automatically excluded, as they may reflect oxide-scale spallation or experimental scatter, but were retained only when explicitly supported by the original publication.

During the curation process, a subset of initially identified candidate data points was excluded or corrected. The primary reasons for exclusion included missing or ambiguous units, oxidation atmospheres other than air, non-isothermal oxidation protocols, compositions containing elements outside the selected palette, and unresolved ambiguities in graph-only data. Corrections were applied only when unequivocal verification from the original source was possible, ensuring conservative data integrity.

Limitations

This dataset aggregates oxidation measurements generated across multiple laboratories, experimental setups, and time periods. Variations in specimen geometry, surface preparation, alloy processing history, and measurement methodology are not explicitly parameterised and therefore contribute to the observed scatter in specific mass change. These factors are inherent to literature-derived datasets and should be considered when interpreting statistical trends or developing predictive models. Consequently, while the dataset enables large-scale comparative and data-driven analyses, it does not capture all experimental variables that may influence oxidation behaviour.

Another important limitation concerns the interpretation of specific mass change as an oxidation metric. The reported values represent the net mass change after oxidation exposure, rather than a direct measurement of oxide scale growth. For refractory alloys containing volatile oxide-forming elements, such as Mo, V, and to a lesser extent Ta and W, oxidation-induced mass change may be partially offset by volatilization-induced mass loss. Therefore, absolute mass change values should be interpreted with caution, since low or even negative mass change does not necessarily indicate limited oxidation damage or superior oxidation resistance.

Oxide scale thickness was not included as a standardized variable in the present dataset because this information is not consistently reported across the collected literature. In addition, when scale thickness values are available, they are often obtained using different characterization methods, measurement locations, and definitions, particularly for multilayered, porous, cracked, or partially spalled oxide scales. These inconsistencies make direct comparison and harmonization difficult across different studies.
