A professor/influencer perspective on science communication
Authors: Chenyao Li, Zhexuan Zhu, Siyuan Ma, Pengxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis backstory is based on an interview with Prof. Pengxin Liu by a student group from ShanghaiTech residential college. The original intention of the interview was to introduce the other identity of Prof. Liu, as a science influencer on social media with over 1.6 M followers, to the undergraduates. Here in this backstory, we share our conversation on the motivation, skill, and outcome for engaging in public science education. The interdisciplinary nature of public communication is highlighted.

Main text

First impressions

S, students: Please briefly introduce your social media account

Liu: The name of my Weibo account is Lin-shoumao (林瘦猫). Starting from 2019 when I moved to ETH Zurich for a postdoctoral position, I decided to share scientific knowledge, mainly on chemistry, with my followers. I gained 0.8 M followers in the first 2 years. After that, I joined Shanghaitech in 2021 and kept managing my account till now.

The contents I post range vastly. In the beginning, to attract a broader audience, I analyzed the ingredients of skincare products for my followers and later shared the correct ways to sterilize hands and surfaces during the pandemic. After 2021, being an assistant professor gave me a chance to communicate with the public about the daily life of a young scientist and principal investigator (PI). Sometimes I also post random stuff like cats and dogs, plants, memes, vlogs, aggressive comments, etc.

S: You mentioned skincare products. Is there a particular reason for you to choose this topic as the breaking point?

Liu: I found it hard to be visible in the beginning. Then I realized that social media is not a place where people head for being educated. Apparently, if I would like to teach the public something, I need to earn their attention first and then their trust.

So, the trick here is to approach from a topic that matters in daily life and resonates with broad audiences. Cosmetics is a fine topic that serves this target. Luckily, the broadcasts and advertisements of almost all daily care products are filled with nonsense and misinformation, which provide plenty of targets that I could shoot at. People are grateful when someone tells them that expenses on nonsense like platinum essence or exotic creatures’ extract are unnecessary and wasteful.

A post with 2.15 M views, showing the steamed pine skin for making planting materials. The process produces a pleasant smell because of the extracted aromatics.

Gradually, I find it not only a great way to attract attention but also important in itself to warn the public about the potential risks of cosmetics. One example is hydroquinone, a compound that was used as a whitening ingredient in daily care products for over 20 years before its potential carcinogenic risk was recognized. After hydroquinone was banned in cosmetics industry, some companies bypassed the regulation by using compounds that are derivatives of hydroquinone. These derivatives function well only because they slowly release hydroquinone after being applied. This practice is legal, profitable, and evil.

S: Do you get questioned on your content’s authenticity, especially when you try to challenge the widely spread advertisements? Or to say, how do you cultivate such a large and dedicated following?

Liu: Yes, quite a lot of questioning at first. People almost always accept the advertisements without questioning, especially when celebrities are the spokespeople. My strategy is to use my academic background and strong personality to deliver my information aggressively. I was eager for quarrels and fights at first, which became part of my “persona.” Getting involved in quarrels made me highly visible. I saw these conflicts as highly necessary, because it is always a tough fight against misinformation. When the public starts to cast even a tiny doubt on advertisements and starts to read the ingredients list more carefully, I know they have gained some basic chemistry knowledge.

Motivation

S: What is your primary motivation to start your account as a science influencer?

Liu: I tried to be an influencer at first only to leave an alternative pathway open, in case I failed to secure a faculty position (laugh). I gradually felt obliged to be active online as an influencer because I felt needed by the public.

A lecture for high school students.

In general, when we talk about public science education, it more often refers to the endeavor to explain someone’s science to the public, especially to pupils and teenagers. We can see a growing number of scientists on social media platforms trying to explain their research to the public in recent years. It is quite common for scientists who work on rocket science, health, animals, dinosaurs, etc. Usually, the goal is believed to be spreading scientific knowledge and promoting scientific thinking among the younger generations.

But for me, I felt myself more needed by grown-ups, especially the elderly. They have never properly received enough scientific education because of historical reasons. Therefore, they are perfect victims of pseudoscience and scams. I found that, for the majority of the public, what is more needed is not the knowledge of advanced science but of the fundamentals. A statistic by China Association for Science and Technology (CAST) in 2025 showed that the proportion of citizens with scientific literacy (scored above 70 out of 100) reached only 15.37%, while the proportion with low scientific literacy (scored below 55 out of 100) reached 55.93%.

S: Why do you think chemistry should be advocated to the public?

Liu: For one thing, while chemistry is fundamental to our daily lives, public understanding of it remains surprisingly thin; for another, the ecological niche of a chemistry influencer is unclaimed in Chinese social media.

As I explained, the lack of basic knowledge among the public creates a vacuum where misinformation thrives. Meanwhile, chemistry is so important that limited public understanding of chemistry will lead to the rapid dissemination of misinformation. We have seen many examples in our society over the years, for instance, the panic buying of table salt, the spraying of bleach over the whole body, and the blind resistance to food additives, to name a few. In this self-media era, the need for accurate and accessible chemistry knowledge has never been more urgent. I do believe my presence as an information source helps the situation.

Besides, chemistry is also called the central science because it interacts with almost all other natural sciences. A lack of general chemistry knowledge undoubtedly means a lack of understanding of daily chemical products, dietetics, medicines, disinfectants, etc. I tried hard to put chemistry and other branches of science into the context of daily lives, so that my content is accessible to the general audience.

Interdisciplinary interaction

S: Could you give us some examples of spreading chemistry within other disciplines?

Liu: The first example, again, is about skincare products. There is this one idiom in the advertising business of cosmetics: the ingredients do not matter, but the process technology matters. The idiom tries to imply that some magical touch on the compounds would endow the products with some magical function, or that the same compound from a “natural” source would be better than a synthesized one.

I happen to have a degree in chemical engineering. Hence, a simple explanation of the fundamentals (momentum transfer, heat transfer, mass transfer, and chemical reaction) easily explodes the myth about the mysterious production process of consumable chemicals. The production process of a cosmetic is no more complicated than making toast. Sometimes, it is also helpful to speak the language of other disciplines. For instance, when discussing the moisturizing effect of skincare products, metaphors like water supply system, surface water, groundwater, and osmosis are quite vivid and understandable.

I also introduced the concept of extraction, repeatedly, by pointing out that this concept is deeply rooted in many other disciplines and in daily life. Extraction is at the core of tea-making, coffee-making, collecting natural products in herbal medicine and aromatics in the perfume industry (similar to the movie “Perfume: The Story of a Murderer”), treatment of pine skin for planting material, and production of soy sauce, among many other occasions.

Another example is the misuse of a homemade fermented liquid from kitchen waste and sugar water. In early 2020, some regional communities donated this type of fermented liquid to local agencies, claiming disinfection and sterilization efficacy. The fermented liquid was diluted and sprayed onto the streets using fog cannon vehicles. This unscientific practice was actually useless and potentially hazardous, as it provided a culture medium for various microorganisms. At the time, I, together with many other science influencers from different educational backgrounds, continuously spoke out. We communicated with media outlets, wrote articles and blogs, and made videos and live streams. Eventually, the spraying was halted. This example inspired me in another way toward public science education as a scientist by actively engaging in social events and influencing policy-making.

S: You mentioned many ways of communicating other than writing articles. What is your consideration on these less academic methods?

Liu: As a trained natural scientist, I find it easy to write articles with strong logic and facts. However, communicating itself is a kind of science that most natural scientists are not well trained in. I also know little about communication theories, which might draw on insights from culture, psychology, and other social sciences. But I do have a strong sense of being emergent, or to say, what is called “wanggan(网感)” in Chinese, an internet user’s perception of information sensitivity. As a long-time user of many social media platforms, and a native of the deepwater Chinese Internet, I usually know what posts can activate spreading and become trending topics. Besides, I do not hesitate to piggyback on social events if some related science is worth discussing.

Future

S: How does being an influencer affect your job and career?

Liu: In one way, being used to translating science into understandable words makes me more comfortable explaining my ideas to people who are not familiar with my research, like students, colleagues, and potential collaborators. And vice versa, I find it easier for me to take in knowledge from other fields and embrace cross-disciplinary collaborations. Right now, the four PhD candidates in my group are working on four different fields, including solution chemistry of titanium,1,2,3,4 heterogeneous thermocatalysis,5,6,7,8 photocatalysis,9,10 and photochemical conversions.

In another way, even before I joined ShanghaiTech, I was known to be an influencer by the president and the dean. Naturally, I gradually fit the role to be responsible for outreach and public education. Over the years, we have built collaborations with high schools, libraries, science museums and planetarium.

In 2024, I was also elected a member of the first Science Popularization Committee of the Chinese Chemical Society (CCS). We have been working closely to promote chemistry with a series of activities. I enjoy this role very much, because it is a great way to serve society.

S: What lies ahead?

Liu: Being a tenure-track assistant professor is always stressful. The first priority for me is to pass my tenure evaluation (laugh).

As for public science education, here I use a topic that I have lectured on every year as an example. When I introduce the Haber-Bosch process, I start with a nasty question: “Why are bird droppings white?” It works every time. From there, I move on to matters that the public worries about: the safety of chemical fertilizers, the recommended amount of protein in the daily diet, and the role of chemical plants.

For the long run, it is the scientists’ job, not others’, to make science fun and accessible to the general public. After all, the research activities of scientists are very often publicly funded. Therefore, I believe being an influencer on social media is a nice way for me to give back to society. I will make sure my public persona never collapses.
