Sharp minimax risks and phase transitions in sparse submatrix detection
Subhajit Goswami, Rajarshi Mukherjee

Abstract
. We study the minimax risk for detecting a sparse elevated-mean Gaussian submatrix inside a larger noisy matrix. When the planted submatrix has size n × n n\times n and the ambient matrix has size N × N N\times N with N = n 1 + α N=n^{1+\alpha} , the classical work of Butucea and Ingster, 2013 identifies the sharp detection boundary around which the minimax risk converges to 0 0 or 1 1 . This paper extends that zero-one theory by determining the precise asymptotic rate of the minimax risk throughout a two-variable phase diagram. Above the detection boundary, we determine the precise exponent for the stretched or super-exponential decay of the risk. Below the boundary, where the risk tends to 1, we identify the exact polynomial order of the rate of convergence up to absolute multiplicative constants. In both of these regimes, the form of the sharp asymptotics changes around the line α + δ = 1 / 2 \alpha+\delta=1/2 where δ \delta indicates the signed distance from the boundary. Finally, on the detection boundary, we show that the minimax risk converges to the non-degenerate constant 1 2 \frac{1}{2} in the very sparse case where n n remains fixed and N → ∞ N\to\infty . Each of these rates corresponds to the risk of a suitably calibrated scan or sum test, whence follow the upper bounds. To show the sharpness of these bounds, we rely on refined second-moment methods applied to random variables chosen carefully according to the particular regime. Our results also extend to the tensor setting.

1.
Introduction

The theory of signal detection in high dimensions has matured significantly in the last three decades. Building on the seminal and foundational work of
Ingster and Suslina, 2003
, the research community has enriched the field with sharp asymptotic minimax separation results across many problems of modern interest, including high-dimensional regression, large matrix and tensor models, and statistical network models. For a given instance of structured signal detection in high dimensions, this program aims to derive the “minimal signal strength” in the problem, as a function of the underlying structure, dimension, and structure required to detect the presence of a signal against high-dimensional background noise. Mathematically, this corresponds to deriving a notion of critical signal strength such that the minimax risk of testing converges to
0/1
depending on whether the signal lies above or below the critical signal strength; see, for instance,
Donoho and Jin, 2004
;
Addario-Berry et al., 2010
;
Arias-Castro et al., 2011b
;
Arias-Castro et al., 2011a
;
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
. In standard
statistical parlance, this translates to characterizing regimes of consistency/inconsistency of a testing problem under study.

However, classical statistics also enjoys a comprehensive theory of asymptotic efficiency of tests that helps go beyond
consistency/inconsistency regimes of testing to a more precise asymptotic characterization of the exact minimax risk of testing. Such a
theory, however, is largely absent from the high-dimensional testing literature, beyond a class of partial results in the context of
Gaussian sequence type models
(
Ligo et al., 2016
;
Mukherjee and Sen, 2020
)
. In order to take further steps to fill this gap, in this paper, we aim to characterize the precise asymptotic behavior of minimax risks for testing the presence of a sparse elevated-mean
n\times n
Gaussian submatrix embedded in a larger
N\times N
matrix of Gaussian noise — the critical signal strength of detection for which was studied in the seminal work of
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
. In the sequel, we will refer to this critical signal strength as
the Butucea–Ingster detection boundary (cf. (
1.7
)).

Our results in the current work reveal a rich two-variable phase transition picture
for this problem which we describe completely; see Figure
1

below. Specifically, above the Butucea–Ingster detection boundary, the minimax risk is
stretched
or
super-exponentially
small (in
n
), and we determine its principal exponential order. Below the boundary, the minimax risk
tends to 1, but at a non-trivial
power-law
rate that we identify up to
absolute multiplicative constants. Thus the quantity
\min\{\mathcal{R}_{n},1-\mathcal{R}_{n}\}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{n}
denotes the minimax risk, changes its order from stretched or super-exponential above the boundary to polynomial below it.
Moreover, within each of these two regimes, the sharp asymptotic form changes at the secondary
critical line

\alpha+\delta=1/2
, which separates the so-called “dense” and “sparse” sub-regimes (see §
1.2
below) in the parametrization
N=n^{1+\alpha}
with
\delta
measuring
the signed distance from the boundary (see (
1.7
)). Finally, on the detection boundary with a fixed submatrix size
n
and
N\to\infty
, the minimax risk converges to
the non-degenerate constant
\frac{1}{2}
.

As expected, a major difficulty in building such a detailed picture is deriving the sharp lower
bounds. We elaborate on this in §
1.3
after introducing the model
and summarizing our main results in the next two subsections.

1.1.
Model, notation and statement of the testing problem

We have the observations forming an
N\times N
matrix
\mathbf{X}=(X_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
with independent entries

(1.1)

X_{ij}\sim N(\theta_{ij},1).

with
\theta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq N
. We are interested in the following class of
parameter spaces, parametrized by a positive integer
n\leq N
and a number
A\geq 0
, for testing against the simple null
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}=\mathbf{0}
, namely

(1.2)

\Theta(A,n,N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}:\exists\ \mathcal{R},\mathcal{C}\subset[N]\mbox{ with }|\mathcal{R}|=|\mathcal{C}|=n\mbox{ s.t. }\theta_{ij}\geq A\\
\mbox{ for }(i,j)\in\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{C}\mbox{ and }\theta_{ij}=0\mbox{ otherwise }\end{array}\right\}.

See, e.g.,
Bhamidi et al., 2017
;
Ma and Wu, 2015
;
Banks et al., 2018
;
Dadon et al., 2024
;
Brennan et al., 2019
;
Gamarnik et al., 2021
;
Oren-Loberman et al., 2026
;
Hajek et al., 2017
;
Kızıldağ et al., 2025
for a non-exhaustive list of works featuring a similar square submatrix setup. Now
given any pair of sequences
A(N),n(N)
indexed by
N
, we can consider the sequence of hypothesis testing problems

(1.3)

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\equiv\mathbf{0}\qquad\mbox{against}\qquad H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A(N),n(N),N).

For a sequence of tests, i.e.,
\{0,1\}
-valued measurable functions
T_{N}=T_{N}(\mathbf{X})

of
\mathbf{X}
, we define the
maximum risk
over
\Theta(A(N),n(N),N)
as

(1.4)

\mathrm{Risk}(T_{N},A(N),n(N))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}_{0}(T_{N}=1)+\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A(N),n(N),N)}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T_{N}=0),

where
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(respectively
\mathbb{P}_{0}
) denotes the joint law of the observations under mean matrix
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(respectively
\mathbf{0}
).

In this paper, we work with pairs
(N,n)
satisfying

(1.5)

N=\lfloor n^{1+\alpha}\rfloor,\qquad\alpha>0\mbox{ (fixed)},

with
n\to\infty
. Accordingly, we choose
n
as the underlying
scale parameter

diverging to
\infty
in the rest of the paper. The
minimax risk
for the testing problem
(
1.3
) can now be defined as:

(1.6)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A(n),\alpha)=\inf_{T_{N}}\mathrm{Risk}(T_{N},A(n),N)

where the infimum is taken over all test sequences
\{T_{N}\}
with
N
as in
(
1.5
). Finally, let

(1.7)

A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha;n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\min\left\{n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)},\sqrt{\frac{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}{n}}\right\},\qquad\delta\in(-1,\infty).

In this parametrization, the Butucea-Ingster detection boundary
(
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
)
can be stated as

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))\to\begin{cases}0,&\delta>0,\\
1,&\delta<0,\end{cases}

as
n\to\infty
for fixed
\alpha
and
\delta
. Thus
\delta
measures signed distance
from the detection threshold. The present paper determines the risk
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))
to principal exponential order when
\delta>0
, the difference
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))
up to absolute constants when
\delta<0
, and the
limiting risk when
\delta=0
. With this notation, we summarize our main results below
before describing them in detail in Section
2
. Along the way, we also
discuss the extension of our results to the
tensor
setting.

Above the detection boundary.

When
\delta>0

(1.11)

\log\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha))\sim-\frac{1}{8}n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}.

Below the detection boundary.

\delta<0

1.3.
Proof overview

Let

(1.15)

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(n;\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{C}\subset[N]\times[N]:|\mathcal{R}|=|\mathcal{C}|=n\}

and for
S\in\mathcal{S}
or, more generally,
S\subset[N]\times[N]
, write

(1.16)

X_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in S}X_{ij}.

The upper bounds on the minimax risks arise from two different types of test statistics, namely

(1.17)

T_{{\rm sum}}^{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{[N]\times[N]}>H\}}\,\,\mbox{ and }\,\,T_{{\rm scan}}^{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{X_{S}}{n}>H\big\}}

referred to as the
sum
test and the
scan
test respectively w.r.t. the threshold
H
(with implicit dependence on
n
). The same statistics were also used
for constructing “good” tests in
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
. For any choice of
the signal strength
A
, computing the optimal value of
H=H_{n}(A)
that minimizes the maximum risk for either of
these tests is rather straightforward and usually involves, as in the case of
T_{{\rm scan}}
, a standard first-moment
computation. Thus we can generate “reasonable” upper bounds on the minimax risk in this manner with an important caveat that the choice between the two types of tests
may be different in the below-boundary regime (where all tests are “bad”, see below) from its above-boundary counterpart. The main and the much more difficult
part is to show that these rates are optimal over all possible tests.

A classical lower bound on the minimax risk is given by the minimum
Bayes risk
under some suitable prior
p
on the parameter space
\Theta(A)=\Theta(A,n)
(recall (
1.2
)). By the Neyman-Pearson lemma, the minimum Bayes
risk is attained by the corresponding likelihood ratio test, i.e., the statistic
\mathbf{1}_{\{L_{p}>1\}}
where
L_{p}
denotes the integrated likelihood ratio under
p
. In view of the obvious “monotonicity” of the probability
measures indexed by
\Theta(A)
with respect to
(\theta_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
,
we can choose, as
p
, the uniform prior
\boldsymbol{\pi}
on the boundary of
\Theta(A)
, i.e., the set

(1.18)

\partial\Theta(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\big\{\boldsymbol{\theta}:\exists\ \mathcal{R}\subset[N],\ \mathcal{C}\subset[N],\ |\mathcal{R}|=n,\ |\mathcal{C}|=n,\theta_{ij}=A\mathbf{1}_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{C}}\big\}

(cf. (
1.2
)). The corresponding integrated likelihood ratio (recall (
1.1
); see also (
4.11
)) is

(1.19)

L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\left(AX_{S}-\frac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}\right).

The core lower-bound problem is to estimate the lower tail of this likelihood
ratio under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.

One obvious problem that one faces in analyzing (
1.19
) is that the summands
comprising
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
are far from being independent. Indeed, the second moment of

L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is governed by overlaps and can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}\exp(A^{2}k\ell)N_{k,\ell}

where
N_{k,\ell}
denotes the number of submatrices intersecting a given submatrix
with number of common rows and columns
k
and
\ell
respectively. In most
situations of interest, this second moment blows up. To remedy this,

Butucea and Ingster, 2013
introduces a “blanket” truncation of
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
; see
(
4.9
) below for a generic form of such truncations. The truncated second
moment typically looks like

(1.20)

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}\exp(q(k\ell,A))\,N_{k,\ell}

where the function
q(k\ell,A)=q(k\ell,A;n)
depends on the underlying
truncation. The truncation in
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
allows the authors to show
that the limiting minimax risk is 1 in the below-boundary regime. However, we cannot
hope to capture the sharp rates and their complex phase transition as revealed by
Figure
1
with a
single
choice of truncation. Before
elaborating on this further, let us note that (
1.20
) can be
interpreted as a
partition function
for a Gibbs measure on the overlap
patterns
(k,\ell)
with
q(k\ell,A)
contributing the energy terms.
This interpretation is not merely speculative. Indeed, in the above-boundary regime,
our sharp exponential rates correspond to the
free energy
for such energy-entropy pairing. We now comment very briefly on the proofs for each of the four
phases in Figure
1
.

Dense above-boundary regime
. In this regime, the upper bound follows
from a sum test. For the lower bound, we apply a Cauchy–Schwarz/Paley–Zygmund type
argument to
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
restricted to a typical event for the sum statistic. As hinted above, the exponent
n^{2\delta}/8
is produced by a free energy computation
where the entropy-energy functional
q(k\ell,A)+\log N_{k,\ell}
(cf. (
1.20
)) is maximized at essentially typical overlaps of order

n^{2}/N
.

Sparse above-boundary regime
. In this case, the optimal upper bound is obtained by a scan statistic. For the lower bound, we first compare the
likelihood ratio to the maximum of
X_{S}
over
S\in\mathcal{S}
. The problem is then
reduced to a second-moment estimate for the number of exceedances of a “high level” by
X_{S};S\in\mathcal{S}
. A careful free energy-type computation then
shows that the dominant contribution has exponent
\frac{\delta^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}
on
the scale
\log|\mathcal{S}|
.

Dense and sparse below-boundary regime
. Below the boundary, the object of interest is the small difference
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
. Interestingly, the upper bounds in both dense and sparse regimes
come from the same sum test while the lower bounds use
Lemma
4.3
, a general lower bound in terms of first and second
moments of truncated-likelihood with very different choices of truncation. A crucial
difference from the above-boundary phase is that the “free energy principle” no
longer holds and the variance of the truncated-likelihood decays at a much slower
power-law rate contributed by several terms rather than a single dominant term. This
is visible in the polynomial rates for
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
. The proof for the
sparse case is particularly delicate.

Critical fixed-size regime
. The result in the critical regime uses
a truncated likelihood ratio similar to the sparse below-boundary regime and shows that, for fixed
n
, all non-identical overlap pairs become negligible while the
truncation removes exactly half of the planted mass, yielding the limit
1/2
.

2.
Main Results

We now state the precise risk asymptotics. Recall the notation
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)=\inf_{T}\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)
and the family
\mathcal{S}
of
n\times n

submatrices of
[N]\times[N]
from (

2.1.
Above-boundary regime
(\delta>0)

The first theorem concerns the dense side of the phase diagram where
\alpha+\delta\leq 1/2
. In this part, the signal
strength
A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)
as given by (
1.7
) is the algebraic rate
n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Dense above-boundary regime)
.

Let
\alpha+\delta\leq 1/2
and
\delta>0
. Then

(2.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))}{n^{2\delta}}=-\frac{1}{8}.

The next theorem covers the sparse scan-dominated sub-regime. We state it for
\alpha+\delta>1/2
, where this is the Butucea–Ingster sparse regime, but the proof gives the same exponent for
every
fixed
\alpha>0
if the sparse regime signal strength
A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n/n}
(recall (
1.7
)) is used. We also give the asymptotic rate corresponding to the signal strength
A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha)=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
for

\alpha+\delta>1/2\geq\alpha
which defines the region where
A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha)
and
A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)
are not equal for
\delta>0
(see (
1.10
) and the surrounding discussion in §
1.2
).

Theorem 2.2

(Sparse above-boundary regime)
.

Let
\alpha+\delta>1/2
and
\delta>0
.

(1)

We have

(2.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))}{\log|\mathcal{S}|}=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}

and the same conclusion holds for all
\alpha>0
and the same choice of signal
strength.

(2)

If also
\alpha\leq 1/2
, then

(2.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha))}{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}}=-\frac{1}{8}.

Since
\log|\mathcal{S}|=(1+o(1))\,2\alpha n\log n
(see (
5.9
) below), part 1 of Theorem
2.2
determines the minimax risk up
to the principal exponential order. The proof of part 2 is essentially a
corollary of the proof of part 1.

2.2.
Below-boundary regime
(\delta<0)

Below the detection boundary, the minimax risk tends to 1. The following two theorems quantify the size of the quantity
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))
. The first result below characterizes the case when
\alpha+\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
– which can arise when
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
or
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
but

negative enough.

Theorem 2.3

(Dense below-boundary regime)
.

Let
\alpha+\delta\leq 1/2
and
\delta<0
. Then

(2.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)))}{\log n}=\delta.

More precisely, there exist absolute constants
c,C\in(0,\infty)
and
n_{0}(\alpha,\delta)<\infty
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}(\alpha,\delta)
,

(2.5)

c\leq\frac{1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))}{n^{\delta}}\leq C.

Next, we move on to the sparse sub-regime, i.e.,
\alpha+\delta>1/2
which implies
that
\alpha>1/2
since
\delta<0
. Somewhat like the part 1 of Theorem
2.2

2.3
below holds for all
\alpha>1/2
provided

A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n/n}

A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n/n}
when

and
A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)
are different for
\delta<0
.
Thus Theorem
2.3
covers the analog of Theorem
2.2

Theorem 2.4

(Sparse below-boundary regime)
.

Suppose that
\alpha>1/2
,
\delta\in(-1,0)
and
A(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n/n}
. Then

(2.6)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A(\delta,\alpha)))}{\log n}=\frac{1}{2}-\alpha.

Moreover, there exist absolute constants
c,C\in(0,\infty)
and
n_{0}(\alpha,\delta)<\infty

n\geq n_{0}(\alpha,\delta)

(2.7)

c\leq\frac{1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))}{\sqrt{(1+\delta)\alpha}\,n^{1/2-\alpha}\sqrt{\log n}}\leq C.

Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
reveal a qualitatively different behavior compared to the above-boundary phase studied in
Theorems

and
2.2
. Specifically, the minimax risks approach their limiting value at a much slower polynomial rate, rather than at a stretched or super-exponential speed. This distinction is also reflected in the proof mechanisms for the two regimes, particularly in the lower bounds. While the above-boundary rates emerge from a single free energy-like term dominating the second moment of a suitably truncated statistic (e.g., the likelihood ratio), the below-boundary rates result from the combined contributions of several distinct terms; see §
1.3
for more details.

(\delta=0)

We finally record a critical result in a fixed-submatrix-size sparse regime. Here

grows to

grows sufficiently slowly as a

Theorem 2.5

(A fixed-size critical limit)
.

For any fixed

(2.8)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}.

Results of similar flavor have appeared previously in the context of Gaussian

(

(

3.
Discussions and Open Directions

3.1.
Connections to existing literature

The results in this paper are
connected to both the classical foundations of statistical efficiency theory and
recent research on sparse signal detection. We expand on them below. We refer the
reader to
(
Mukherjee and Sen, 2020
, Section 1.4)
for more detailed discussions
in a related context.

3.1.1.
Connections to Chernoff exponents and Bahadur efficiency

The viewpoint adopted here is also related to the classical theory of asymptotic error exponents in hypothesis testing. For testing a fixed simple null
P
against a fixed simple alternative
Q
from
m
independent observations, the optimal sum of the two errors is governed, on the exponential scale, by the Chernoff-exponent

-\inf_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}\int\left(\frac{dQ}{dP}\right)^{\lambda}dP,

see, e.g.,
(
Polyanskiy and Wu, 2014
, Corollary 12.1)
. Bahadur’s efficiency
gives another large-deviation comparison of tests, in which the relevant quantity
is the exponential rate at which attained significance levels decay under
alternatives; see, for instance,
Bahadur, 1967
. Both theories ask for
more than consistency: they compare tests by the speed at which their error
probabilities, or related tail probabilities, become small. In this regard, our results can be
viewed as providing an analog of Chernoff exponent and Bahadur efficiency for sparse
submatrix detection problems.

There are two important differences in the present setting. First, the sparse submatrix problem is composite rather than simple-versus-simple. The likelihood ratio that enters the lower bounds is therefore an integrated likelihood ratio over a least favorable prior on submatrices, not a product of i.i.d. one-observation likelihood ratios. Second, the null and alternative approach one another with
n
and
N
, and hence the error need not decay exponentially on the sample-size scale. Indeed, the present results show three distinct behaviors: stretched- or super-exponential decay of
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))
above the boundary, polynomial decay of
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha))
below the boundary, and a non-degenerate limiting risk at criticality.

3.1.2.
Connections to Bhattacharyya affinity

Another useful way to view the minimax risk is through the affinity between the null distribution and a suitably averaged alternative. If
\mathbb{P}_{p}=\int\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,dp(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the mixture induced by a prior
p
on
planted submatrices, the Bhattacharyya affinity is

\rho(\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{p})=\mathbb{E}_{0}\sqrt{L_{p}},

where
L_{p}=d\mathbb{P}_{p}/d\mathbb{P}_{0}
. This quantity directly controls the Bayes testing risk and is
often more closely tied to the optimal testing error than the second moments of likelihood
ratios; see, e.g.,
Bhattacharyya, 1946
;
Addario-Berry et al., 2010
. In particular, controlling the affinity could give a conceptually clean route to sharp lower bounds.

For sparse submatrix detection, however, the affinity is difficult to evaluate directly. The integrated likelihood ratio is a highly dependent average over
\binom{N}{n}^{2}
possible submatrices, and its tails are governed by rare but influential pairs of submatrices with atypically large overlaps. In a sense, the technical core of the paper may be regarded as a way to understand the Bhattacharyya affinity, carried out through more tractable second-moment analysis.

3.1.3.
Connections to sparse Gaussian mixture models

The sparse Gaussian mixture model is a closely related benchmark for rare/weak signal detection. In its simplest form one observes independent variables from

H_{0}:\;Y_{i}\sim N(0,1),\qquad H_{1}:\;Y_{i}\sim(1-\varepsilon_{n})N(0,1)+\varepsilon_{n}N(\mu_{n},1),

and asks for the detection boundary or for sharper error asymptotics. The detection-boundary theory for such models is classical in the higher-criticism literature
Donoho and Jin, 2004
;
Cai et al., 2011
;
Cai and Wu, 2014
;
Arias-Castro and Ying, 2019
. More recently,
Ligo et al., 2016
studied the rate of decay of error probabilities for sparse mixtures in
some
regimes, showing that the relevant decay can be sublinear in the number of observations and can be governed by divergence quantities different from those in the non-sparse case.

However, for the question of interest in this paper, the problems of sparse Gaussian mixtures and sparse submatrix detection are very different. In the mixture model, the likelihood ratio factorizes over observations, so one can often reduce the analysis to large deviations for sums of independent random variables. In the submatrix model, the unknown signal is a combinatorial object with row and column structure; two alternatives are not independent labels but overlapping submatrices. The resulting entropy–energy balance over overlap sizes is responsible for the secondary transition at
\alpha+\delta=1/2
and for the distinction between sum- and scan-dominated regimes.

3.2.
Comparisons with
Mukherjee and Sen, 2020

A particularly close antecedent to the present paper is the work of
Mukherjee and Sen, 2020
on minimax exponents for sparse testing in high-dimensional linear regression. Their paper asks essentially the same second-order question as the one pursued here: after the detection boundary is known, what is the sharp asymptotic size of the minimax testing risk? It also shares an important technical feature with our analysis: the lower bounds are obtained from careful second moment analysis of random variables related to the
(integrated) likelihood ratio.

There is also a qualitative parallel between the two sets of results. In both
problems, the minimax risk has a different scaling on the two sides of the
detection boundary. Below the boundary, the object of interest is the quantity
1-\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
, and the optimal rate is polynomial in the ambient dimension. Above
the boundary, the risk itself tends to zero at a stretched- or super-exponential
rate. Thus both works show that first-order detectability hides a sharper
transition in the magnitude of the optimal error.

At the same time, the phase diagrams are not the same. Mukherjee and Sen treat sparse alternatives in a regression model, including orthogonal and random-design settings, whereas the present paper treats the structured sparse submatrix model of
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
. The geometry of the alternative class is therefore different. In sparse regression the prior is over sparse supports of the unknown parameter vector. In the submatrix problem the dominant combinatorial object is the pair of row and column overlaps between two candidate rectangles. This overlap geometry produces the secondary transition at
\alpha+\delta=1/2
and the sum/scan distinction visible in
Figure
1
.

The comparison is also useful for explaining what is specific to the present contribution. Mukherjee and Sen give a
partial
phase diagram for the regression problem and explicitly identify remaining
gaps
in their Section 4. In particular, the unshaded regions in their Figure 2 correspond to parameter ranges where the minimax risk is not fully characterized; they also conjecture further phase transitions in one sparse regime and point out that matching higher-criticism-type upper bounds by truncated-second-moment lower bounds appears to require new ideas. By contrast, for the canonical square sparse-submatrix model and for the parametrization considered here, the present paper gives a
complete
phase diagram across the above-boundary, below-boundary, and critical regimes.

3.3.
Open directions

Among several questions that can be pursued, we expand upon the questions of adaptation, computational complexity, and other models of signal detection.

3.3.1.
Adaptation

The results in this paper are non-adaptive: the tests and lower bounds are calibrated to the submatrix size
n
and to the location of the signal relative to the boundary. Butucea and Ingster developed adaptive procedures that recover the detection boundary over ranges of unknown submatrix sizes
(
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
)
. A finer and largely open question is whether one can adapt while preserving the sharp risk asymptotics proved here. This is delicate because the optimal exponent changes across regimes: the total sum and the scan statistic contribute the dominant term in different parts of the phase diagram. A single adaptive procedure may therefore be boundary-optimal in the zero-one sense while losing constants or even powers in the sharp-risk scale.

3.3.2.
Computational considerations

The minimax results here are information-theoretic. In the scan-dominated regimes, the natural optimal procedure involves maximizing
X_{S}
over all
S\in\mathcal{S}
, which is computationally infeasible in the worst case. Computational-statistical tradeoffs for submatrix detection were studied by
Ma and Wu, 2015
, who related efficient detection to planted-clique-type barriers in certain regimes (see also the more recent work
Sohn and Wein, 2025
). More broadly, similar computational gaps are known or conjectured in sparse PCA and related planted-structure problems; see, for example,
Berthet and Rigollet, 2013
.

The sharp-risk point of view raises a more refined computational question. Do the polynomial-time tests achieve the same large-deviation exponent above the boundary or the same polynomial difference (from
1
) below it? Conversely, can one prove computational lower bounds at the level of risk exponents rather than merely at the level of zero-one detectability?

3.4.
Other models and further directions

Several extensions are natural. First, one could study rectangular submatrices with
n\neq m
and ambient dimensions
N\neq M
with possibly different values of the exponent
\alpha
in each dimension, where additional regimes may appear. Second, it would be interesting to develop a full critical-window theory, allowing
n\to\infty
and perturbing
A
at scales finer than those considered here. Third, analogous sharp-risk questions can be asked for non-Gaussian exponential-family noise, unknown variance, two-sided alternatives, and heterogeneous signal amplitudes. Finally, the free energy structure of the truncated second moment suggests possible connections with sharper large-deviation principles for overlap distributions in other high-dimensional testing problems.

4.
Preliminaries

In this section we gather some results that are used repeatedly throughout our
proofs. Lemma
4.1
and the subsequent Corollary
4.2

provide “manageable” expressions for certain counts of submatrices which are crucial for obtaining minimax lower bounds via second moment
computations. In Lemma
4.3
, we give a general lower bound
on the minimax risk of tests in terms of first and second moments of
truncated
likelihood ratios. We end the section with some standard results on
normal distributions in one and higher dimensions.

We now proceed to state our first result which gives upper bounds on the fraction
of submatrix pairs with order
n\times n
that intersect at a
k\times\ell

submatrix. We consider the “one-dimensional” version here where we only take into
account the number of common rows
k
(or, equivalently, the number of common
columns
\ell
); cf. (
5.8
) in Section
5
. These bounds, especially in the tractable forms presented in the
lemma below, are crucial for delicate second moment computations which lead to the
lower bounds in our main theorems. They are consequences of Stirling’s formula
combined with careful analysis.

Lemma 4.1

(Asymptotics of binomial coefficients)
.

For any non-negative integer
k
and positive integers
n
and
N
satisfying
k\leq n\leq\tfrac{N}{3}
, we have

(4.1)

\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{N-n}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{k\vee 1}\frac{(n-k)\vee 1}{n}}\exp\big(k\log\tfrac{n^{2}}{Nk}-2Nh(\tfrac{N-n}{N})+2nh(\tfrac{n-k}{n})+Nh(\tfrac{N-2n+k}{N})\big)

where (as in the rest of the paper)
h(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x\log\tfrac{1}{x}
for
x>0
and we
interpret
0\log\infty=0
. Furthermore, if
N\geq n^{\frac{3}{2}}=n^{1+\frac{1}{2}}
(cf. (
1.5
)), we can write the simplified bound

(4.2)

\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{N-n}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{k\vee 1}\frac{(n-k)\vee 1}{n}}\exp(k\log\tfrac{en^{2}}{Nk}-\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}+\tfrac{2kn}{N}).

Proof.

From Stirling’s formula, we have for any positive integers
m>k
,

(4.3)

C^{-1}\leq\frac{\binom{m}{k}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(1-\frac{k}{m})}}\exp\big(k\log\tfrac{m}{k}+(m-k)\log\tfrac{m}{m-k}\big)}\leq C

where
C\in(1,\infty)
. Now consider non-negative integers
k,n
and
N
satisfying
k<n<\frac{N}{2}
.
Using (
4.3
), we can write

(4.4)

\binom{N-n}{n-k}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{(n-k)(1-\frac{n-k}{N-n})}}\exp\big((n-k)\log\tfrac{N-n}{n-k}+(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N-n}{N-2n+k}\big).

We can rewrite the first term inside the exponential as follows:

(4.5)

(n-k)\log\tfrac{N-n}{n-k}=(n-k)\log\tfrac{N}{n}-(n-k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-n}+(n-k)\log\tfrac{n}{n-k}.

As to the other term, we can write

(4.6)

(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N-n}{N-2n+k}=(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-2n+k}-(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-n}.

The expression of
\binom{N-n}{n-k}
resulting from (
4.4
) and
(
4.5
) combined with the expressions of
\binom{n}{k}
and
\binom{N}{n}

obtained from (
4.3
) give us (
4.1
) in the
regime
1\leq k<n\leq\tfrac{N}{3}
. The terminal cases
k=0
and
k=n
can be
verified similarly upon noting that
\binom{n}{k}=1
in both cases and
\binom{N-n}{n-k}=1
in the latter case.

In order to derive (
4.2
), let us revisit (
4.6
) and
bound it as

\begin{split}(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N-n}{N-2n+k}&=(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-2n+k}-(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-n}\\
&\leq(2n-k)-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{(2n-k)^{2}}{N-2n+k}+C\tfrac{(2n-k)^{3}}{(N-2n+k)^{2}}-(N-2n+k)\log\tfrac{N}{N-n}\end{split}

where in the last step we used the (third order) Taylor approximation for
\log(1+x)
. Plugging this bound and
(
4.5
) into (
4.4
), we obtain

\begin{split}\binom{N-n}{n-k}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{(n-k)(1-\frac{n-k}{N-n})}}\exp\big((2n-k)-\tfrac{(2n-k)^{2}}{2(N-2n+k)}+nh(\tfrac{n-k}{n})+(n-k)\log\tfrac{N}{n}-Nh(\tfrac{N-n}{N})\big).\end{split}

for
0\leq k<n
where we used the condition
N\geq n^{\frac{3}{2}}
. Together with
(
4.3
) applied to
\binom{n}{k}
and
\binom{N}{n}
, this yields

(4.7)

\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{N-n}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{k}(1-\frac{k}{n})}\exp\big((2n-k)-\tfrac{(2n-k)^{2}}{2(N-2n+k)}+2nh(\tfrac{n-k}{n})+k\log\tfrac{n^{2}}{Nk}-2Nh(\tfrac{N-n}{N})\big)

for
1\leq k<n
. Now using the (second order) Taylor approximation of
\log(1+x)
, we get the lower bound

Nh(\tfrac{N-n}{N})=(N-n)\log\tfrac{N}{N-n}\geq n-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{n^{2}}{N-n}

for all
N\geq 3n
. On the other hand, from the standard inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
, we have

2nh(\tfrac{n-k}{n})=2(n-k)\log\tfrac{n}{n-k}\leq 2k.

Plugging these two estimates into the right-hand side of (
4.7
) and
using a little bit of algebra, we obtain

\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{N-n}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{k}(1-\frac{k}{n})}\exp(k+k\log\tfrac{n^{2}}{Nk}-\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}+\tfrac{2kn}{N})

for all
1\leq k<n\leq N^{\frac{2}{3}}
. This is precisely the bound (
4.2
)
when
0<k<n
. The cases
k=0
and
k=n
follow in a similar manner
(cf. the second line below (
4.6
)).
∎

In some situations, it is enough to work with the following simplified bound.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any non-negative integer
k
and positive integers
n
and
N
satisfying
k\leq n\leq\tfrac{N}{3}
, we have

(4.8)

\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{N-n}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\leq\exp(Cn-k\log\tfrac{N}{n}).

Proof.

(
4.8
) follows immediately from (
4.1
) upon
noting that
0\leq\inf_{x\in[0,1]}h(x)\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1]}h(x)<\infty

and
h(x)\leq C(1-x)
for
x\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. We skip the verification of
these two properties which are standard.
∎

To obtain tight lower bounds on the minimax risk in below-boundary and critical
regimes, i.e., those covered under Theorems
2.3
–
2.5
, we use the generic inequality in
Lemma
4.3
below. Inequalities of this type are standard in
the literature; see, e.g., display (6.5) in
Mukherjee and Sen, 2020
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given a family of events
\mathbf{E}=(E_{S})_{S\in\mathcal{S}}
(see (
1.15
)) measurable relative to
\mathbf{X}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}
, let us
consider the likelihood ratio
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}
truncated by
\mathbf{E}
defined as

(4.9)

L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\left(AX_{S}-\tfrac{A^{2}n}{2}\right)\mathbf{1}_{E_{S}}

where
X_{S}
is as in (
1.16
) (cf. (
1.19
)). Then we have

(4.10)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})}.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
the mixture measure
\int\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,d\boldsymbol{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
where
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is the uniform prior on
\partial\Theta(A)
(recall (
1.18
)). Using standard change of mean formula for Gaussian
variables (see, e.g., Lemma
4.6
below), we see that

(4.11)

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}=L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\partial\Theta(A)}\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\left(AX_{S}-\tfrac{A^{2}n}{2}\right)

We can now write (see, for instance,
(
Butucea and Ingster, 2013
, Section 5.1)
and also the second paragraph in §
1.3
):

(4.12)

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)&\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)+\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Lpiform}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)+\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}1_{\{L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1\}})\\
&=1-\mathbb{E}_{0}(1-L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}})^{+}\end{split}

where
x^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\max(x,0)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Now decomposing
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
as
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}+(L-L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})
,
which are both non-negative, and using the elementary observation that the
function
x\mapsto x^{+}
is both increasing and subadditive, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)\geq 1-(1-\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}_{0}\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})-L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}\big)^{+}=\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{0}\big|\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})-L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}|.\end{split}

From this (
4.10
) follows immediately upon noting that

\mathbb{E}_{0}\big|\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})-L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}}|\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\mathbf{E}})}

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

To conclude the section, we record a few properties of normal variables
which will be used frequently. These properties are classical and we omit their
proofs.

Lemma 4.4

(Standard normal tail bounds)
.

Letting
\Phi(\cdot)
denote the standard normal CDF, we have

(4.13)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+1}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\leq 1-\Phi(x)=\Phi(-x)\leq\tfrac{1}{2\vee\sqrt{2\pi}x}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}

for all
x\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.5

(Tail probability for bivariate normals)
.

Let
(X,Y)
be distributed as a (bi-variate) normal vector under
\mathbb{P}
with common mean 0, common variance
1
and correlation coefficient
\rho
. Then for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(4.14)

\mathbb{P}(X>a,Y>b)=\mathbb{P}(X>a)\mathbb{P}(Y>b)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\arcsin{\rho}}\exp\big(-\tfrac{a^{2}+b^{2}-2absin\theta}{2\cos^{2}\theta}\big)d\theta.

The final result in the list is the “change of mean” formula for normal
distribution.

Lemma 4.6

(Change of mean formula)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
denote the law of
n
independent standard normal variables. Also for
any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
denote the law
of
n
independent normal variables with means
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and common
variance
1
. Then for (
\mathbb{P}
almost) every
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}}{d\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{y})=\exp(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}y_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}y_{i}^{2})
.

5.
Proofs in the above-boundary regime

In this section we will prove our results for the above-boundary regime
\delta>0
, i.e., Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
.
Their proofs are given in two separate subsections.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Here we will give the proof of Theorem
2.1
which is naturally split into the proofs of
the corresponding upper and lower bound respectively.

Proof of the upper bound.
For the upper bound, consider the sum test (cf. (
1.17
)) given by

T=T_{{\rm sum}}^{N\frac{n^{\delta}}{2}}=\mathbf{1}_{\big\{X_{[N]\times[N]}>N\frac{n^{\delta}}{2}\big\}}.

Since
\frac{X_{[N]\times[N]}}{N}\sim N(0,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, we have

(5.1)

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)=1-\Phi(\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}}}{{\leq}}e^{-\frac{n^{2\delta}}{8}}.

On the other hand,
\tfrac{X_{[N]\times[N]}}{N}\sim N(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},1)

under
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\geq\tfrac{n^{2}A}{N}
and
A=A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
. Therefore

(5.2)

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)=\Phi\big(\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2}-\tfrac{n^{2}A}{N}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:mnregime}}}{{\leq}}\Phi(-\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}}}{{\leq}}e^{-\frac{n^{2\delta}}{8}}

Together (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) imply (cf. (
1.4
))

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)}}{n^{2\delta}}\leq-\frac{1}{8}

which is the upper bound in (
2.1
).

Proof of the lower bound.
We now give the proof for the lower
bound which is more involved. One natural approach would be to truncate the
likelihood ratio by the likely event
\{T=0\}
(under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
) and use Lemma
4.3
. However, the two terms on
the right-hand side of (
4.10
) are of similar (stretched
exponentially small) order, which makes it quite delicate to bound their difference
from below
.
Instead, we take the more direct route of bounding
\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)
or

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)
(see (
4.12
)). To this end we will use a
truncated second moment method applied directly to the event
\{L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1\}
.
More precisely, we have

(5.3)

\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}1_{\{L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1,T=0\}}))^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}1_{\{T=0\}})}

which follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Now note that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}1_{\{L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1,T=0\}})=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1,T=0)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(T=0)-\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1)=\Phi(-\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2})-\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1).\end{split}

So either

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\leq 1)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Phi(-\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2})

in which case we directly obtain

(5.4)

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)}}{n^{2\delta}}\geq-\frac{1}{8},

i.e., the required lower bound in (
2.1
) via the tail bound
(
4.13
) (and in view of (
4.12
))
or

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}1_{\{L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1,T=0\}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\Phi(-\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2}).

So let us assume that the above bound holds.

Next we want to show that

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}1_{\{T=0\}})\leq\Phi(-\tfrac{3n^{\delta}}{2})\exp((1+o(1))n^{2\delta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}}}{{\leq}}e^{-(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2\delta}}{8}}

which then yields the desired lower bound (
5.4
), together with
(
5.5
), via the inequality (
5.3
). Rest of the proof is devoted to verifying
(
5.6
). We need the following result.

Claim.
Given any
B\in[0,\frac{1}{n}]
and
n,N\geq 1
satisfying
n\leq\frac{N}{3}
, let us consider the function
f:[0,n]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
defined as

f(x,y)=x\log\tfrac{n^{2}}{Nx}+y\log\tfrac{n^{2}}{Ny}+2n(h(\tfrac{n-x}{n})+h(\tfrac{n-y}{n}))+N(h(\tfrac{N-2n+x}{N})+h(\tfrac{N-2n+y}{N}))+Bxy

(cf. (
4.1
) and recall that
0\log\infty=0
). Then
f
is continuous, and attains its maximum
at some point in the rectangle
[\frac{n^{2}}{N},\frac{n^{2}}{N}(1+C\frac{n}{N})]^{2}
.

We will validate this Claim at the end and continue with the proof of
(
5.6
). Let us start with (recall (
1.19
) and that
A=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
)

(5.7)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}1_{\{T=0\}})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:Lpi}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{A(X_{S}+X_{S^{\prime}})-A^{2}n}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{[N]\times[N]}\leq N\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2}\big)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm Lem}~\ref{lem:CM}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{A^{2}|S\cap S^{\prime}|}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{[N]\times[N]}\leq N\tfrac{n^{\delta}}{2}-2An^{2}\big)\\
&=\Phi(-\tfrac{3n^{\delta}}{2})\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{A^{2}|S\cap S^{\prime}|}=\Phi(-\tfrac{3n^{\delta}}{2})\sum_{0\leq k,\ell\leq n}\tfrac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}e^{A^{2}k\ell}\end{split}

where

(5.8)

N_{k,\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}{n\choose k}{N-n\choose n-k}{n\choose\ell}{N-n\choose n-\ell}

gives the number of submatrices
S^{\prime}
intersecting a given submatrix
S
at a
k\times\ell
submatrix. Since
\alpha+\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.,
A^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}
, we obtain from
(
4.1
) and our Claim (applied in the second step below) that

\sum_{0\leq k,\ell\leq n}\tfrac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}e^{A^{2}k\ell}\leq n^{2}\max_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}\tfrac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}e^{A^{2}k\ell}\leq n^{2}\exp((1+o(1))\tfrac{A^{2}n^{4}}{N^{2}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:mnregime}}}{{\leq}}\exp((1+o(1))n^{2\delta}).

Plugging this bound into (
5.7
) we obtain (
5.6
). It
remains to verify our claim.

Proof of the Claim.
The continuity of
f
is clear from the definition. The partial derivatives of
f

are given by

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\log\tfrac{(n-x)^{2}}{x(N-2n+x)}+By\mbox{ and }\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\log\tfrac{(n-y)^{2}}{x(N-2n+y)}+Bx.

Since
\max(Bx,By)\in[0,1]
(recall that
(x,y)\in[0,n]^{2}
and
B\in[0,\frac{1}{n}]
), the above gives us that the point of maximum
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})
for

f
is unique and satisfies

\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}\leq x^{\ast}\leq\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}(1+CBy^{\ast})\mbox{ and }\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}\leq y^{\ast}\leq\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}(1+CBx^{\ast}).

In particular, we get that both
x^{\ast}
and
y^{\ast}
are bounded above by

C\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}
. Re-plugging these bounds into the previous display we obtain the
required result. ∎

5.2.
Proofs of Theorem
2.2

This subsection is
primarily
devoted to the proof of
Theorem
2.2
, part 1. In the end we explain how to adapt parts
of the argument to deduce part 2.

Proof of the upper bound.
We will frequently use the following asymptotic expression for
|\mathcal{S}|={\binom{N}{n}}^{2}
which is valid for
all

\alpha>0
(recall from (
1.5
) that
N=\lfloor n^{1+\alpha}\rfloor
) and follows from the Stirling’s formula as in (
4.3
):

(5.9)

\log|\mathcal{S}|=(1+o(1))\,2\alpha n\log n.

Clearly, with
A=A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{\frac{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}{n}}
, we have

(5.10)

\log|\mathcal{S}|=(1+o(1))\tfrac{A^{2}}{2(1+\delta)}\,n^{2}.

Our analysis in the proof of Theorem
2.2
(part 1) is valid for
any

\alpha,\delta>0
and
A
as above.

Proof of the upper bound.
Let us start with the upper bound and
consider the scan test (cf. (
1.17
)) with a carefully chosen threshold, namely

(5.11)

T=T_{{\rm scan}}^{\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}}=\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{X_{S}}{n}>\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}\big\}}\,\,\mbox{where }\tau^{\ast}=\frac{(2+\delta)^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}.

Note that
\tau^{\ast}-1=\frac{\delta^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}(>0)
; cf. (
2.2
).
Since each
\frac{X_{S}}{n}\sim N(0,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, we can write, via a simple union bound,

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)\leq|\mathcal{S}|\,\big(1-\Phi(\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)})\big).

Plugging the upper tail bound from (
4.13
) into the right-hand side above, we obtain

(5.12)

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)\leq e^{-(\tau^{\ast}-1)\log|\mathcal{S}|}.

On the other hand, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\tfrac{X_{S(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{n}\leq\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}\right)

where
S(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{S}
is such that
\theta_{ij}\geq A
for all
(i,j)\in S(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
=0
otherwise. Since
\frac{X_{S(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{n}\sim N(\mu,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with
\mu\geq An
, we get

(5.13)

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq\Phi\left(\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}-An\right).

Now by (
5.10
) and since
\tau^{\ast}=\frac{(2+\delta)^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}
(see (
5.11
)), we can write

\begin{split}&\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}-An=-(1+o(1))\left(\sqrt{2(1+\delta)}-\frac{2+\delta}{\sqrt{2(1+\delta)}}\right)\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}\\
=&-(1+o(1))\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2(1+\delta)}}\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}=-(1+o(1))\sqrt{2(\tau^{\ast}-1)}\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}.\end{split}

Plugging this into (
5.13
) and using (
4.13
)
again, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq e^{-(1+o(1))(\tau^{\ast}-1)\log|\mathcal{S}|}

for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
. Together with (
5.12
) (recall the
definition of
\tau^{\ast}
from (
5.11
)), this implies

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)}}{\log{|\mathcal{S}|}}\leq-(\tau^{*}-1)=-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}

which is the required upper bound in (
2.2
).

Proof of the lower bound.
For the lower bound, one can try the method in the previous subsection and consider
the second moment of the likelihood ratio
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
truncated w.r.t. to
the likely event
\{T=0\}
(under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
). Unfortunately, the second moment
blows up with
n
in this case. Also any approach exploiting
Lemma
4.3
becomes ineffective due to the same reason.
Consequently, we need to find a way to bound
\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)
from below
(cf. (
4.12
)) that does not involve the second moment (truncated or
otherwise) of
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. To this end, using the simple observation
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp(AX_{S})\geq\exp(A\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}X_{S})
, we can write

(5.14)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)&\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\exp(A\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}X_{S})>\exp\big(\tfrac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}+\log{|\mathcal{S}|}\big)\big)\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(A\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}X_{S}>\tfrac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}+\log{|\mathcal{S}|}\big)=\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>\tfrac{An}{2}+\tfrac{\log{|\mathcal{S}|}}{An}\big)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:mathcalSA}+\eqref{def:Tscan}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>(1+o(1))\,\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}\big)\end{split}

(cf. (
5.11
)). Therefore it suffices to bound the probability in the
last line above and we will use second moment method for that. So denoting

Y=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}1_{\big\{\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}\big\}},

we will bound

(5.15)

\mathbb{P}_{0}\Big(\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>(1+o(1))\,\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)}\Big)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y))^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y^{2})}.

Since
\frac{X_{S}}{n}\sim N(0,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
for each
S\in\mathcal{S}
, we
have

(5.16)

\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y)=|\mathcal{S}|\,\big(1-\Phi(\sqrt{2\tau^{*}\log(|\mathcal{S}|)})\big)=e^{-\log|\mathcal{S}|(\tau^{\ast}-1)(1+o(1))}

where we used the lower tail bound in (
4.13
). On the other hand, we
have

(5.17)

\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y^{2})=|\mathcal{S}|\sum_{0\leq k,\ell\leq n}N_{k,\ell}\,p_{k\ell}

where
N_{k,\ell}
is as in (
5.8
) and

p_{k\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|},\tfrac{X_{S^{\prime}}}{n}>(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}\,\big)

with
any

S,S^{\prime}
satisfying
|S\cap S^{\prime}|=k\ell
and
o(1)
denoting
the same function in both cases. The probability depends on
S
and
S^{\prime}
only
through
|S\cap S^{\prime}|=k\ell
since
(\tfrac{X_{S}}{n},\,\tfrac{X_{S^{\prime}}}{n})
is a
bi-variate normal random vector with common mean
0
, common variance 1 and
correlation coefficient
\rho=\frac{k\ell}{n^{2}}
. Thus using Lemma
4.5

(with
a=(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}
), we get

p_{k\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}+\eqref{eq:bvn}}}{{\leq}}\exp\Big(-\tfrac{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}{1+\frac{k\ell}{n^{2}}}(1+o(1))\Big).

Using the transformation of variables
k\to\frac{k}{n}=q
,
\ell\to\frac{\ell}{n}=r
and
k\ell\to\frac{k\ell}{n^{2}}=qr
, where
q,r\in[0,1]
, we can rewrite the above as

p_{k\ell}\leq\exp\Big(-\tfrac{2\tau^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}{1+qr}(1+o(1))\Big).

On the other hand, Corollary
4.2
(recall that
N=\lfloor n^{1+\alpha}\rfloor
) gives us

N_{k,\ell}\leq|\mathcal{S}|e^{Cn}e^{-(k+\ell)\alpha\log n}=|\mathcal{S}|e^{Cn}e^{-(q+r)\alpha n\log n}\leq|\mathcal{S}|e^{Cn}e^{-2\sqrt{qr}\,\alpha n\log n}

where in the final step we used the standard inequality
q+r\geq 2\sqrt{qr}
.
Now plugging the previous two bounds into the right-hand side of (
5.17
) and using (
5.9
)
we obtain

(5.18)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}[Y^{2}]&\leq e^{Cn}n^{2}\exp\big\{-\log|\mathcal{S}|\inf_{0\leq q,r\leq 1}(\tfrac{2\tau^{\ast}}{1+qr}+\sqrt{qr}-2)(1+o(1)\big\}\\
&\>\>=e^{Cn}n^{2}\exp\big\{-\log|\mathcal{S}|\inf_{0\leq s\leq 1}(\tfrac{2\tau^{\ast}}{1+s^{2}}+s-2)(1+o(1)\big\}.\end{split}

Observe that, for any
s\in[0,1]
and since
\tau^{\ast}\geq 1
,

\tfrac{2\tau^{\ast}}{1+s^{2}}+s-2=(\tau^{\ast}-1)+\tfrac{1-s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}\tau^{\ast}-(1-s)=(\tau^{\ast}-1)+(1-s)(\tfrac{1+s}{1+s^{2}}\tau^{\ast}-1)\geq\tau^{\ast}-1(>0).

Hence from (
5.18
), we get

\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y^{2})\leq e^{-\log|\mathcal{S}|(\tau^{\ast}-1)(1+o(1))}.

Substituting this and the lower bound on the first moment from (
5.16
) into the
right-hand side of (
5.15
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y>0)\geq e^{-\log|\mathcal{S}|(\tau^{\ast}-1)(1+o(1))}.

Therefore, in view of (
5.14
)–(
5.15
) (as well as
(
4.12
)) and the definition of
\tau^{\ast}
in (
5.11
), we get

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)}}{\log{|\mathcal{S}|}}\geq-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4(1+\delta)}

which is the desired lower bound in (
2.2
), thus completing the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

We now show how to adapt the above argument in order to deduce part 2 of
Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
, part 2.
We first
outline the proof of the upper bound. To this end let us consider the test
(cf. (
5.11
))

T=T_{{\rm scan}}^{\frac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}}=\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{X_{S}}{n}>\frac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}\big\}}.

From arguments similar to those used for deriving (
5.12
), we
obtain in this case,

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)\leq|\mathcal{S}|e^{-(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}}{8}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Scardinality}}}{{\leq}}e^{-(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}}{8}}.

Notice that we needed the condition
\alpha+\delta>\frac{1}{2}
in the last step.
As to the type-II error, we obtain as in (
5.13
) (with
A=A^{\ast}_{{\rm BI}}(\delta,\alpha)=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
),

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq\Phi\big(\tfrac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}-An\big)=\Phi\big(-\tfrac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}}}{{\leq}}e^{-(1+o(1))\frac{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}}{8}}

for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
. Together the last two displays give us

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)}}{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)}}\leq-\frac{1}{8}.

For the lower bound, we start as in (
5.14
) and obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>1)&\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>\tfrac{An}{2}+\tfrac{\log{|\mathcal{S}|}}{An}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Scardinality}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(\max\limits_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\tfrac{X_{S}}{n}>(1+o(1))\,\tfrac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}\big).\end{split}

In the last step we also used that
A=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
with

\alpha+\delta>\frac{1}{2}
. Now writing
\tfrac{n^{\alpha+\delta}}{2}=\sqrt{2\tau_{n}^{\ast}\log|\mathcal{S}|}
, where
\tau_{n}^{\ast}=(1+o(1))\tfrac{n^{2(\alpha+\delta)-1}}{16\log n}
is bounded away from 1 (cf. below (
5.18
)), the remainder of the proof works exactly as in
the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
∎

6.
Proofs in the below-boundary regime

In this section we prove our results for the below-boundary regime
\delta<0
,
namely Theorems
2.3
and

2.4
. Unlike the stretched and super-exponential rates
in the previous section which are contributed by a single dominant term in the
squared likelihood ratio (for the lower bounds), here we see power law behaviors
emerging from the agglomeration of several terms.

6.1.
Proof of
Theorem
2.3

Let us give the proof of the upper bound (on the risk) first.

Proof of the upper bound.
Taking cue from the proof of the
corresponding part for the above-boundary regime in Section
5
,
let us consider the sum test

T=T_{{\rm sum}}^{0}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{[N]\times[N]}>0\}}.

It is immediate that
\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)=\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
with
A=A^{\ast}(\delta,\alpha)=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
, one has (cf. (
5.2
))

(6.1)

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq\Phi(-n^{\delta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\delta<0)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}-cn^{\delta}.

Combining the above two, we obtain

\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)\leq 1-cn^{\delta}

which is the desired upper bound (on
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
) in
(
2.5
).

Proof of the lower bound.
We will use
Lemma
4.3

without
any truncation. To this end let us
write, in view of (
1.19
),

\mathbb{E}_{0}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}(\exp(A^{2}W_{1}W_{2})),

where
W_{1},W_{2}\sim\mathrm{HG}(N,n,n)
are i.i.d. Hypergeometric variables under

\mathbb{P}
. Now using the convex stochastic ordering between Hypergeometric and Binomial
variables (see, e.g.,
(
Aldous, 1985
)
), we can write

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{0}}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\big(1+\tfrac{n}{N}(e^{A^{2}W_{1}}-1)\big)^{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\exp\big(\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}(e^{A^{2}W_{1}}-1)\big)\big)

where we used the standard inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
in the last step. Since
A^{2}W_{1}=n^{-(1-2(\alpha+\delta))}\leq 1
(recall that
\alpha+\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
), we have
e^{A^{2}W_{1}}-1\leq CA^{2}W_{1}
and hence

\mathbb{E}\big(\exp\big(\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}(e^{A^{2}W_{1}}-1)\big)\big)\leq\mathbb{E}\big(e^{C\frac{n^{2}}{N}A^{2}W_{1}}\big).

Reusing the convex ordering between Hypergeometric and Binomial variables, we thus
obtain

\mathbb{E}_{0}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}\right)\leq\big(1+\tfrac{n}{N}(e^{C\frac{n^{2}}{N}A^{2}}-1)\big)^{n}\leq e^{CA^{2}\frac{n^{4}}{N^{2}}}\leq e^{Cn^{2\delta}}

where in the second step we used
1+x\leq e^{x}
and in the final step we used
A=n^{-(1-\alpha-\delta)}
as well as that
N=\lfloor n^{1+\alpha}\rfloor
.
Now plugging this bound into (
4.10
) in
Lemma
4.3
(with
E_{S}
denoting the underlying full event),
we get (notice that
\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}})=1
)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)\geq 1-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{0}\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{2}\right)-1}\geq 1-\sqrt{e^{Cn^{2\delta}}-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\delta<0)}}{{\geq}}1-cn^{\delta}

which is the required lower bound (on
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
) in (
2.5
). Notice that the condition
\alpha+\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
is crucially used in the entire argument. ∎

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

The proof of Theorem
2.4
has several surprising elements — both technically and conceptually — and is the main highlight of this section.

Proof of the upper bound.
Our argument in the previous subsection
would suggest that a carefully designed scan test like what was used for proving
the corresponding upper bound in the above-boundary regime in §
5.2
should attain the optimal rates. Surprisingly, it turns out that the test considered for the dense region in §
6.1
, i.e,
T{=}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{[N]\times[N]}>0\}}
is still the ‘correct’ test (see
§
1.3
for more detailed discussion). Indeed, using similar
arguments with
A=A(\delta,\alpha)=\sqrt{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n/n}
(see around (
6.1
)) we obtain

(6.2)

\mathrm{Risk}(T,A,n)=\frac{1}{2}+\Phi(-A\tfrac{n^{2}}{N})\leq 1-cA\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}=1-c\sqrt{(1+\delta)\alpha}\,n^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}\sqrt{\log n}

where the bound in the second (equivalently, the third) step is valid since

\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. The above is the required upper bound (on
\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)
) in (
2.7
).

Proof of the lower bound.
The proof of the lower bound is very delicate. We will use Lemma
4.3

with
truncation. In view of the test
T
considered for the upper bound above (cf. the proof of the lower bound in
Theorem
2.1
, part 1), one natural choice for the truncating event(s)
E_{S}
would be
\{X_{[N]\times[N]}\leq B\}
for some suitable
B
. However, one can check that
no
such choice would lead to the required bound. Instead, we truncate as
follows:

(6.3)

L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\big(AX_{S}-\tfrac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{S}\leq B\}}

where
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}An^{2}+\Phi^{-1}(1-A\tfrac{n^{2}}{N})n
. By Lemma
4.3
, we can write

(6.4)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})}\,.

Notice that,

(6.5)

\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm Lem}~\ref{lem:CM}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{S}+An^{2}\leq B\,\big)=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(1-A\tfrac{n^{2}}{N})\big)=1-A\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}.

In view of (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) (and also (
6.2
)), it therefore suffices to prove

(6.6)

\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq CA^{2}\tfrac{n^{4}}{N^{2}}\mbox{ for all $n\geq C(\alpha,\delta)$.}

To this end, let us start by evaluating
\mathbb{E}_{0}[(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})^{2}]
. By (
6.3
), we
can write

(6.7)

\mathbb{E}_{0}((L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})^{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{A^{2}|S\cap S^{\prime}|}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{S}\vee X_{S^{\prime}}\leq q-A|S\cap S^{\prime}|\big)

where
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\Phi^{-1}(1-A\tfrac{n^{2}}{N})n=B-An^{2}
. From (
4.14
), we obtain

(6.8)

\begin{split}&\,\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{S}\vee X_{S^{\prime}}\leq q-A|S\cap S^{\prime}|\big)=p_{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}\\
=&\,\Phi(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\tfrac{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}{n})^{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\arcsin{\rho}}\exp\big(-\tfrac{(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\tfrac{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}{n})^{2}}{1+\sin\theta}\big)d\theta\\
\leq&\,\Phi(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\tfrac{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}{n})^{2}+\exp\big(-\tfrac{(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\frac{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}{n})^{2}}{1+\frac{|S\cap S^{\prime}|}{n^{2}}}\big).\end{split}

In view of (
6.5
), (
6.7
) and (
6.8
), we
can write

(6.9)

\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{1\leq k,\ell\leq n}\frac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}\Big(e^{A^{2}k\ell}\Phi(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\tfrac{k\ell}{n})^{2}-\Phi(\tfrac{q}{n})^{2}+e^{A^{2}k\ell}\exp\big(-\tfrac{(\tfrac{q}{n}-A\frac{k\ell}{n})^{2}}{1+\frac{k\ell}{n^{2}}}\big)\Big)

where
N_{k,l}
is as in (
5.8
). Denoting the sum on the right-hand side above by
\Sigma
, let us split the range of summation
underlying
\Sigma
into three parts, namely

(6.10)

\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3}

where the ranges in
\Sigma_{1}
,
\Sigma_{2}
and
\Sigma_{3}
include all
1\leq k,\ell\leq n
such that
k\ell\leq\tfrac{1}{A^{2}}
,
\tfrac{1}{A^{2}}<k\ell\leq\tfrac{2q}{A}

and
\tfrac{2q}{A}<k\ell\leq n^{2}
respectively. Note that since
A=\sqrt{\frac{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}{n}}
, which also yields (in view of (
4.13
))

(6.11)

\frac{q}{n\sqrt{\log n}}=\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-A\frac{n^{2}}{N})}{\sqrt{\log n}}\in(c(\alpha,\delta),C(\alpha,\delta))\mbox{ for all $n\geq C(\alpha,\delta)$,}

the ranges specified for
\Sigma_{i}
’s are all well-defined, i.e., the left
endpoints are smaller than the corresponding right endpoints for large
n

(depending on
\alpha
and
\delta
). We will now treat each of these sums separately. We will show that

\Sigma_{2}
and
\Sigma_{3}
decay stretched-exponentially fast and are thus of lower
order than the desired upper bound in (
6.6
). It is
\Sigma_{1}
which
governs the
power law
behavior in (
6.6
) and requires a very
careful analysis especially when
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
. Henceforth in this proof
we will implicitly assume that all the statements hold for
n\geq C(\alpha,\delta)
.

Bounding
\Sigma_{3}
.
Since
A\tfrac{k\ell}{n}>2\frac{q}{n}
, i.e.,

A\tfrac{k\ell}{n}-\frac{q}{n}>\frac{q}{n}(>0)
, we get the following from
(
6.9
), the upper bound on
N_{k,\ell}
as given by
(
4.2
) (recall that
N=n^{1+\alpha}
with
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
)
and the upper bound in (
4.13
):

(6.12)

\Sigma_{3}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq n,\\
k\ell>\frac{2q}{A}\end{subarray}}\exp\big(A^{2}k\ell-\tfrac{(A\frac{k\ell}{n}-\tfrac{q}{n})^{2}}{1+\frac{k\ell}{n^{2}}}+k\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{k}+\ell\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{\ell}+Cn^{1-\alpha}\big).

We will need the following result.

Claim.
Let
w,z\in(0,\infty)
be such that
w>\tfrac{z^{2}}{e^{2}}
. Then the function
x\mapsto x\log\tfrac{z}{x}+x^{\ast}\log\tfrac{z}{x^{\ast}}
(cf. (
4.2
)), where
xx^{\ast}=w
, achieves its maximum on
(0,\infty)
at the point
x=\sqrt{w}
.

We postpone the proof of this Claim until the end and continue to the pending
bound on
\Sigma_{3}
. Note that any
k,\ell
included under the summation in
(
6.12
) satisfies

(6.13)

k\ell>2\frac{q}{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:range_q}}}{{\geq}}c(\alpha,\delta)\frac{n\sqrt{\log n}}{A}=c(\alpha,\delta)n^{\frac{3}{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\alpha>\frac{1}{2})}}{{>}}n^{2(1-\alpha)}=\frac{(en^{1-\alpha})^{2}}{e^{2}}

where in the third step we used
A=c(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
.
This enables us use to use the above Claim with
k,\ell
and
k\ell
as
x,x^{\ast}

and
w
respectively for any such values of
k
and
\ell
. Consequently, applying
the transformation
\rho=\frac{\sqrt{k\ell}}{n}
, we can rewrite
(
6.12
) as follows:

(6.14)

\Sigma_{3}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{c(\alpha,\delta)n^{-\frac{1}{4}}\leq\rho\leq 1}\exp\big(A^{2}n^{2}\rho^{2}-\tfrac{(An\rho^{2}-\tfrac{q}{n})^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}+2n\rho\log\tfrac{en^{-\alpha}}{\rho}+Cn^{1-\alpha}\big).

Let us simplify the right-hand side before proceeding further. Recalling that
A=\sqrt{\frac{4(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}{n}}
, we get the equivalent expression

2n\rho\log\tfrac{en^{-\alpha}}{\rho}=-A^{2}n^{2}\rho(\tfrac{1}{2(1+\delta)}-\tfrac{\log\frac{e}{\rho}}{2(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}).

On the other hand, since
An\cdot\frac{q}{n}\leq C(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{n}\log n
— which follows from the choice of
A
above as well as (
6.11
) — and

\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, we have the upper bound

A^{2}n^{2}\rho^{2}-\tfrac{(An\rho^{2}-\tfrac{q}{n})^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}+Cn^{1-\alpha}\leq A^{2}n^{2}\tfrac{\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}+C(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{n}\log n.

Now plugging the above two expressions into the right-hand side of
(
6.14
), we obtain

\Sigma_{3}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{c(\alpha,\delta)n^{-\frac{1}{4}}\leq\rho\leq 1}\exp\big(-A^{2}n^{2}\rho(\tfrac{1}{2(1+\delta)}-\tfrac{\log\frac{e}{\rho}}{2(1+\delta)\alpha\log n}-\tfrac{\rho}{1+\rho^{2}})+C(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{n}\log n\big).

Since
\delta\in(-1,0)
and
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, this yields

(6.15)

\Sigma_{3}\leq Cn^{4}\exp(-c(\alpha,\delta)A^{2}n^{2}n^{-\frac{1}{4}}+C(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{n}\log n)\leq C(\alpha,\delta)\exp(-c(\alpha,\delta)n^{\frac{3}{4}}\log n).

Bounding
\Sigma_{2}
.
In this summation we can afford to ignore the
negative term on the right-hand side of (
6.12
) and write,

\Sigma_{2}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq n,\\
\frac{1}{A^{2}}<k\ell\leq\frac{2q}{A}\end{subarray}}\exp\big(A^{2}k\ell+k\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{k}+\ell\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{\ell}+Cn^{1-\alpha}\big).

Since
k\ell>\frac{1}{A^{2}}\geq c(\alpha,\delta)\frac{n}{\log n}>n^{2(1-\alpha)}
(cf. (
6.13
)), we can again use the Claim below
(
6.12
) to obtain (cf. (
6.14
))

\Sigma_{2}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{c(\alpha,\delta)(n\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\rho\leq C(\alpha,\delta)n^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\exp\big(A^{2}n^{2}\rho^{2}+2n\rho\log\tfrac{en^{-\alpha}}{\rho}+Cn^{1-\alpha}\big).

Since
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, we have
\log\tfrac{en^{-\alpha}}{\rho}\leq-c(\alpha,\delta)\log n
for
\rho>c(\alpha,\delta)(n\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Consequently,

\Sigma_{2}\leq Cn^{4}\sup_{c(\alpha,\delta)(n\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq\rho\leq C(\alpha,\delta)n^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\exp\big(A^{2}n^{2}\rho^{2}-c(\alpha,\delta)n\rho\log n+Cn^{1-\alpha}\big).

The supremum above is clearly achieved at the endpoints leading to

(6.16)

\Sigma_{2}\leq C(\alpha,\delta)\exp(-c(\alpha,\delta)\sqrt{n\log n})

(we also used that
n^{1-\alpha}\leq\sqrt{n}
).

Bounding
\Sigma_{1}
.
As already mentioned, this is the term which
determines the principal order of the variance of
L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime}
. Note that since
k\ell\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}
, i.e.,
A^{2}k\ell\leq 1
for all the underlying
k,\ell
,
we can bound
e^{A^{2}k\ell}-1\leq CA^{2}k\ell
for such pairs. Also we have,
\max(k,\ell)\leq k\ell\leq\frac{n}{2}
. Together with (
6.9
), (
4.2
) and the fact that
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, these observations give us

(6.17)

\Sigma_{1}\leq CA^{2}\sum_{1\leq k\ell\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}}\sqrt{k\ell}\exp\big(k\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{k}+\ell\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{\ell}-2n^{1-\alpha}+2(k+\ell)n^{-\alpha}\big)

We further split it into two cases depending on the value of
\alpha
.

Case
\alpha\geq 1
.
In this case, the sum in (
6.17
) is
dominated by the leading term corresponding to
k=\ell=1
, i.e.,

(6.18)

\Sigma_{1}\leq CA^{2}n^{2(1-\alpha)}=CA^{2}\tfrac{n^{4}}{N^{2}}\quad\mbox{(cf.~\eqref{eq:second_mom_ab}).}

Case
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
.
This is more involved. We start with a
rewriting of (
6.17
) as follows.

(6.19)

\Sigma_{1}\leq CA^{2}\sum_{1\leq k\ell\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}}}\exp(f(k)+f(\ell))

where the function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

(6.20)

f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log x+x\log\tfrac{en^{1-\alpha}}{x}-n^{1-\alpha}+2xn^{-\alpha},\>x\in(0,\infty).

f
is strictly concave and attains its maximum at the point
x^{\ast}\in(0,\infty)
given by the equation

f^{\prime}(x^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2x^{\ast}}+\log\frac{n^{1-\alpha}}{x^{\ast}}+2n^{-\alpha}=0.

Using the facts
\log(1+x)\geq x-\frac{x^{2}}{2}
for
x\geq 0
(a consequence of
Taylor’s expansion) and
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
(we will use this implicitly in the remainder of the proof), we see that

(6.21)

x^{\ast}\in(n^{1-\alpha},n^{1-\alpha}+1)

This range together with the bound
\log(1+x)\leq x
gives us

(6.22)

f(x^{\ast})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log n^{1-\alpha}+1

in view of (
6.20
). Further, since
f
is decreasing on

(x^{\ast},\infty)\subset[n^{1-\alpha}+1,\infty)
, we have

(6.23)

f(x)\leq f(en^{1-\alpha})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:f}}}{{\leq}}-\frac{1}{2}n^{1-\alpha}\mbox{ for all }x\geq en^{1-\alpha}.

Now noting that
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}
and
n^{1-\alpha}>1

(recall that
\alpha<1
), we get from Taylor expansion around
x=x^{\ast}
:

(6.24)

f(x)\leq f(x^{\ast})-\frac{c}{x^{\ast}}(x-x^{\ast})^{2}\mbox{ for all }x\in(0,en^{1-\alpha}).

Putting the previous four bounds together we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}\exp(f(k))&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:fxbnd},\eqref{eq:fbnd2}}}{{\leq}}C\exp(f(x^{\ast}))\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\tfrac{c(x-x^{\ast})^{2}}{x^{\ast}})dx+n\exp(-cn^{1-\alpha})\\
&\leq C\exp(f(x^{\ast}))\sqrt{x^{\ast}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:rangex_ast},\eqref{eq:fxast}}}{{\leq}}Cn^{1-\alpha}.\end{split}

Plugging this bound into (
6.19
), we are finally led to

(6.25)

\Sigma_{1}\leq CA^{2}n^{2(1-\alpha)}=CA^{2}\tfrac{n^{4}}{N^{2}}.

Combining the bounds on
\Sigma_{1}
,
\Sigma_{2}
and
\Sigma_{3}
, we obtain from (
6.9
) and (
6.10
):

\operatorname{Var}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}+\Sigma_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Sigma3bnd4},\eqref{eq:Sigma2bnd4},\eqref{eq:Sigma1bnd2_1},\eqref{eq:Sigma1bnd2_4}}}{{\leq}}CA^{2}\tfrac{n^{4}}{N^{2}}

which is precisely (
6.6
), thus completing the proof. It only
remains to give the

Proof of the Claim.
Let us consider the function
f:[0,\infty)^{2}\to[0,\infty)
defined as
f(x,y)=x\log\tfrac{z}{x}+y\log\tfrac{z}{y}
.
f
is
continuous and is differentiable on
(0,\infty)^{2}
. Furthermore, since
f(x,y)
tends to
-\infty
as
x\to 0
and
y\to\infty
(or
vice versa), we obtain that the function
x\mapsto x\log\tfrac{z}{x}+x^{\ast}\log\tfrac{z}{x^{\ast}}
, with
xx^{\ast}=w
, attains its maximum at some point
x\in(0,\infty)
. Therefore using the method of Lagrange multipliers, we obtain that

x
necessarily satisfies

(6.26)

\log\tfrac{z}{ex}=\lambda x^{\ast}\mbox{ and }\log\tfrac{z}{ex^{\ast}}=\lambda x

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, we have
x\log\tfrac{z}{ex}=x^{\ast}\log\tfrac{z}{ex^{\ast}}
. However, the function
x\mapsto x\log\tfrac{z}{ex}
is strictly monotone on either side of
\tfrac{z}{e^{2}}
. Therefore for (
6.26
) to hold, we need either
x=x^{\ast}=\sqrt{w}
or
x,x^{\ast}
to lie on
the opposite sides of
\tfrac{z}{e^{2}}
. Now since
xx^{\ast}=w>\tfrac{z^{2}}{e^{2}}
,
at least one of
x
and
x^{\ast}
must be larger than
\tfrac{z}{e}
. Consequently, if

x\neq x^{\ast}
, we must have
\log\tfrac{z}{ex}\log\tfrac{z}{ex^{\ast}}<0
which
contradicts (
6.26
) thereby yielding the Claim. ∎

7.
Proof in the critical regime

The sole result that we prove in this section is Theorem
2.5
.
Like with the previous results, we first give the

Proof of the upper bound.
We use the following scan test (cf. (
5.11
)):

T=T_{{\rm scan}}^{2\sqrt{n\log N}}=\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\max_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{X_{S}}{n}>2\sqrt{n\log N}\big\}}.

Since each
\frac{X_{S}}{n}
is distributed as
N(0,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, we can use the upper tail bound in (
4.13
) and a
union bound to deduce

\mathbb{P}_{0}(T=1)\leq N^{2n}(1-\Phi(2\sqrt{n\log N}))\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n\log N}}

(for
n,N>1
). As to the type-II error, let us note that
\frac{X_{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{n}

is distributed as
N(An,1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
(cf. (
5.13
)) and hence with
A=A^{\ast}=2\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{n}}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T=0)\leq\Phi(2\sqrt{n\log N}-An)=\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}

for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta(A)
. Together, the last two displays yield the upper bound in (
2.8
).

Proof of the lower bound.
For the lower bound, let

L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\big(AX_{S}-\tfrac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{S}\leq An^{2}\}}

so that we can write, by (
4.10
),

\mathcal{R}_{n}(A)\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})}.

Using similar argument as in (
6.5
), we get
\mathbb{E}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, all we need to show is that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})=0
. To this end, we can write similarly as in (
6.7
):

\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq\mathbb{E}_{0}((L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})^{2})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}e^{A^{2}|S\cap S^{\prime}|}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{S}\vee X_{S^{\prime}}\leq-A|S\cap S^{\prime}|\big).

We bound these probabilities by (
6.8
) (with
0
in place of
q
) for
all values of
|S\cap S^{\prime}|

except
when
|S\cap S^{\prime}|=n^{2}
(i.e.,
S=S^{\prime}
) in
which case we can use the more direct (and exact) bound, namely (recall that
X_{S}
is
distributed as
N(0,n^{2})
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
)

\mathbb{P}_{0}\big(X_{S}\leq-An^{2}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:normal_tailbnd}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{An}e^{-\frac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}}.

All in all we obtain, as in (
6.9
),

\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq C\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq n,\\
k\ell\neq n^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}e^{A^{2}k\ell}\exp\big(-\tfrac{A^{2}(k\ell)^{2}}{n^{2}+k\ell}\big)+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}e^{A^{2}n^{2}}\frac{1}{An}e^{-\frac{A^{2}n^{2}}{2}}

where
N_{k,l}
is as in (
5.8
) and we already used the upper tail bound for
\Phi(-\tfrac{Ak\ell}{n})
from (
4.13
) in the first
summation.

\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{k\vee 1}\frac{(n-k)\vee 1}{n}}\exp(k\log\tfrac{en^{2}}{Nk}-\tfrac{n^{2}}{N}+\tfrac{2kn}{N})

Since
\frac{N_{k,\ell}}{|\mathcal{S}|}\leq C(n)N^{-k-\ell}
and
A=2\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{n}}
, we can rewrite the above as

(7.1)

\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})\leq C(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k,\ell\leq n,\\
k\ell\neq n^{2}\end{subarray}}\exp\big(-\log N(\tfrac{4(k\ell)^{2}}{n(n^{2}+k\ell)}+k+\ell-4\tfrac{k\ell}{n})\big)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\log N}}.

Now using the standard inequality
k+\ell\geq 2\sqrt{k\ell}
and a
little algebra we obtain

\begin{split}\tfrac{4(k\ell)^{2}}{n(n^{2}+k\ell)}+k+\ell-4\tfrac{k\ell}{n}\geq\tfrac{4(k\ell)^{2}}{n(n^{2}+k\ell)}+2\sqrt{k\ell}-4\tfrac{k\ell}{n}=\tfrac{2\sqrt{k\ell}}{n^{2}+k\ell}(n-\sqrt{k\ell})^{2}.\end{split}

This term is bounded below by
c(n)(>0)
when
k\ell<n^{2}
, i.e.,
k\ell\leq n(n-1)
. Plugging this bound into the right-hand side of (
7.1
) we get
\lim_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\prime})=0
finishing the proof. ∎
