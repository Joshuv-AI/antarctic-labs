ABSTRACT
In recent years, experiments involving micofabricated surface ion traps have grown in complexity,
and as this complexity grows, a common design has emerged in the form of quantum
charge-coupled device architecture. This architecture, already utilized on multiple systems,
supports multiple zones on a device for areas such as memory or computation. The shutting
process between these zones is a process often seen to be minimized. An additional component to
scalable surface trapped-ion experiments includes some form of integrated photonics, as free
space lasers have difficulty scaling to many ions. Here, we discuss recent results in utilizing the
shuttling process with integrated photonics to perform a specific type of gate, as well as
demonstrating state preparation. Furthermore, we show that these gates can be utilized as a type
of optical modulator as the Doppler shift that occurs during shuttling will make the light seen by
shuttled ions different than that seen by stationary ions. Furthermore, we show that the shuttling
operation can be utilized as an optical modulator, where the Doppler shift changes the frequency
from that of a stationary ion.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. QCCD Architecture

Quantum computing trapped-ion architecture is based on chains of ions and the success of such
systems is based upon scaling the number of ions/qubits. Despite trapped-ion quantum computing
having the high fidelities [10], long coherence times[1], and excellent stability[13], it is difficult to
trap large number of ions in a single harmonic well. Quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD)
[14] architecture relies on the mobility of ions between different regions of the device to interact
with larger, stationary chains of ions. The QCCD architecture allows for different zones to
prepare, process, and store information in the qubits. Being able to efficiently utilize these
different zones relies on the efficient movement of the ions between regions of the device.

1.2. Importance of shuttling

The QCCD architecture thus establishes shuttling as an unavoidable operation in this scheme.
Shuttling can also be quite slow compared to other parts of a gate sequence. Ideally, overlapping
shuttling process with another (such as cooling, state preparation, or quantum gates) allows for
higher efficiency. The Doppler shift inherent to shuttling also makes the process velocity selective
and also detuned from the stationary ion. Thus the process is only "turned on" for ions shuttled at
a particular velocity. This ability to effectively turn the process on and off fills a needed role of
light modulation when it comes to addressing large numbers of trapped ions.

Optical modulators are typically employed to control the phase, frequency, and amplitude of light.
These elements are often large, consume power, and are limited in the I/O channels available on a
device. Despite recent work to demonstrate on-device modulators[2], the modulators remain
space-consuming and without the ideal gate fidelities.

shuttling speed, light can be sufficiently detuned from stationary or slower/faster moving ions.
This allows for a single optical modulator to control light at multiple locations on a device based
on the shuttling scheme.

2.3. Vacuum chamber assembly

For this project, a custom vacuum assembly was required. The constructed chamber allows for
optical access through a top window for light delivery to a grating coupler. The grating coupling
enables the coupling of light into on device waveguides that directed light to specific locations on
the trap. We used parts from previously assemblies however it was crucial to the success of the
project that our chamber had comparable state-of-the-art vacuum.
Initially testing of the vacuum chamber (before a device was installed) reached low 10−11 Torr
pressures. These nearly ideal pressures were supported even after trap installation with an
operating pressure measured at approximately 7 × 10−12 Torr. Surface ion traps are sensitive to
stray electric fields. Because of this, it is essential to have portions of the trap nearby establish a
ground reference, and in some ways shield the trap from outside electric fields. It is common to
have a ground plane above a surface ion trap. Because we require optical access to couple light
into the gratings for the on device waveguides we relied on an ITO (indium tin oxide) coated

2.4. Device failure and replacement

Over the course of the two year project, the original RS1630 midscale device failed and needed to
be replaced. An ohmic short developed across the RF contact to the chamber, and ultimately to
the device making it impossible to trap. An unsuccessful attempt was made to “burn off’ the short
by an applied DC current. It remains unclear what the definitive cause of the short may have been
but it is believed that the oven current may have been unnecessarily high causing metallic calcium
to deposit onto the device. Figure 2-7 shows a photo of the damaged RS1630 midscale device that
was removed from the experimental chamber during the project. It is worth noting that because a
thin film of calcium nearly immediately oxidizes in atmosphere it can be difficult to fully test the
cause of failure. With such a thin film deposited, no short was detected after oxidation.

2.5. Control hardware and software

The experiments are controlled through our home built control software, ioncontrol. This is a
python application which interfaces with various hardware components. The experiments here
use a specific software branch of ioncontrol meant to interface with a home-built pulser for
DDS control, NI DAC system for voltage control, and NewFocus motors for beam positioning.
This branch is used throughout the lab space hosting this experiment. However due to hardware
differences, the development of this branch as greatly diverged from the mainline branch used by
QSCOUT.

The pulser hardware consists of an field programmable gate array (FPGA) system meant for
triggering DDS channels that drive acousto-optic modulators (AOMs), pulsing digital outputs, and
receiving digital inputs (such as from a photon counter). An experiment is mediated by a pulse
program loaded into the FPGA, running experiments and reporting data back to the computer.
The pulse program sets DDS frequencies, amplitudes, phases, and applies digital and RF pulses in
sequence to realize an experiment.

Voltages on the trap used for shuttling are supplied by an NI PXI chassis which contains 12 DAC
boards to provide 96 analog channels to drive the electrodes of the trap. The analog signals from
each of these DAC boards is passed into a special PCB board that combines them into two SCSI
cables that go to filter boards attached to the chamber. Each DAC board has a digital output as
well which is routed on the custom PCB board to a BNC output.

The chassis operates in several modes. The standard "static" mode is the typical operation of static
voltages applied to each output channel and can be changed by hand in ioncontrol. Each line is
loaded from a voltage file. Other modes arm the chassis for shuttling. For these modes one needs
to identify a set of "shuttling edges" given by a start line, stop line, and number of steps. A
shuttling sequence consists of a list of these edges. This shuttling sequence can be run in three
ways: (1) "untriggered", (2) "trigger to run the full sequence", and (3) "trigger for each edge".
Additionally these can be operated in "single" or "continuous" mode. The "single" mode will run
the sequence a single time before exiting the "shuttling" state and returning to the "static" mode,
requiring additional inputs to return to the "shuttling" state. "Continuous" mode will run the
shuttling sequence as long as the trigger remains high. Typical operation for a shuttling
experiment would be in "continuous" mode while waiting for a trigger for the full sequence or a
trigger for each edge. Switching to an operational mode outside of the "static" mode will load the
voltage waveform to the chassis and arm it to be triggered during the pulse sequence.

Experiments that involve shuttling require more configuration to set up than stationary ones.
There needs to be some synchronization between the pulses output by the pulser and the shuttling
operation. Given the simplicity of the pulser hardware, the pulser has no knowledge of what
voltage waveforms or waveform sequences are being applied nor their duration. The configuration
relies on TTL pulses between the two pieces of hardware. During the pulse program, when a
shuttling operation occur, a TTL pulse is sent to the NI DAC Chassis to trigger the shuttling
operation. The chassis is configured to provide a response TTL pulse on receipt of the trigger, the
beginning of the sequence, and its end. This handshake informs the pulser when a shuttling
operation is finished during a pulse program. Inside the pulse program, the pulser waits for the

3. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

3.1. Trapping parameters

The RS1630 device had previously only been tested with ytterbium[4] ions. However, the
waveguides specific to calcium on this sister device had not yet been used to trap calcium. The
electrode layout is designed to hold the ion 50 𝜇m above the surface, dictated by the RF electrode
geometry. In order to achieve the higher trap frequencies needed for trapping, a circuit-base
resonator as opposed to a more traditional helical resonator[9]). With a drive frequency of 66
MHz, the trap was operated with radial frequencies of 6.0 MHz and 6.2 MHz and an axial
frequency of 4.2 MHz. It is worth noting that going to lower axial confinement led to higher
heating rate measurements.
The first ion was trapped in the system in April 2024, roughly a quarter of the way through the
project. Lifetimes on this apparatus were above average compared to other room temperature
experiments, seeing multiple lifetimes upwards of 20 hours, with the longest being around 54
hours.

3.2. Grating coupling

In order to couple light into the waveguide that sent the 729 nm light to the ion, we used a grating
coupling with freespace optics (shown in Figure 3-2). The alignment of the freespace beam into
the grating coupling was very sensitive to both the beam’s translational position on the grating, as
well as the three principal axes (pitch, roll, yaw). This presented a fairly large parameter space to
search in order to find the optimal position of the beam that maximized the light out of the
waveguide. In order to achieve the degree of control we required, we combined a translation stage

translation as well as pitch. We used a manual micrometer to control the yaw since it was the least
sensitive degree of freedom. Roll control was accomplished by rotating the polarization of the
freespace beam.

3.3. Shuttling

3.3.1. Voltage solutions

Voltage solutions are prepared by in-house simulation software. The solutions match parameters
such as the axial frequency and electric fields to the particular positions of the ion. The solutions
are generated from ideal models of the ion trap. In reality, the geometry can differ slightly. As
such, it is useful to be able to make adjustments to the solution as the ion is shuttling.
Mid-shuttling adjustments need to be "compiled" into the solution. Code was written to do this
compilation to facilitate easing of the heating rate.
Shuttling solutions are provided as an array of voltages that get updated in steps at a consistent
rate. Due to the small pauses as the voltage array is updated, the ion resonance is at the stationary

frequency for a small amount of time. Additionally, sidebands are convolved onto the lineshape at

3.3.2. Heating rate

Initial heating rates associated with the state preparation and gates initially proved to be rather
high. Using sideband thermometry techniques[12], we measured heating rates on the axial mode
of 0.82+/-0.07 quanta/ms. Using motional Rabi flop fitting[8], we measured a heating rate of
1.5+/-0.1 quanta/ms. The amount of excitation that we measure per shuttled loop was 0.66
quanta/loop. More work in this area needs to be done to identify voltage solutions that minimize
heating during a shuttle, but also restrict the heating or coherent excitation that occurs during the
start or stop of motion. The code for "compiling" solutions mentioned above also has the
capability to build in different velocity profiles into the motion. This allows us to implement
different starting and stopping profiles to address potential sources of heating.

3.5. Transport enabled gates

As described in previous sections, gates can also be performed by using the motion of the ion
through the beam to act as the on/off switch for the beam (the ion moving through the beam is on,
and outside the beam is off). In this experiment, the shuttling speed through the beam was kept
constant and the power of the 729 nm beam was adjusted in order to perform different gates. We
were operating under a scheme in which the user of a quantum computer would be able to control
the amplitude, phase, and frequency of a global beam going into the waveguide on the device.
This global beam would then split and go to various sites on the trap, where the local Doppler
shift could be controlled by varying the speed of each ion at each respective site. Because we only
had one set of laser beams, we were required to return the ion to the center of the interaction
region after each shuttled gate (shown in Figure 3-12), which added considerable time to each
gate. For this experiment, the sequence in Figure 3-12 is referred to as a loop. Each loop

4. CONCLUSIONS

We were successfully able to demonstrate transport enabled gates with integrated optics. We
showed we could achieve full spin polarization and full contrast with five shuttled loops all back to
back to one another. We were limited by the low coherence rates on this setup and had difficulty
employing techniques like gate set tomography to be able to fully characterize the transport
enabled gates. In order for this technology to be fully utilized by the field we know that we need to
be able to characterize our gates more completely. The ion velocity needs additional calibrations
to effectively use shuttled gates.

4.1. Future work

We remain optimistic that with the results shown here, additional experimental work will be
performed, further solidifying transport enabled gates. This work demonstrated the importance of
fully characterizing the shuttling performance and gate fidelities. We believe that the coherent
excitation and limited coherence times proved to be detrimental to the overall performance of our
gates and limiting our ability to use GST to fully characterize the shuttled gates’ performance.
Longer coherence times and better shuttling control should allow for a more direct comparison of
the shuttled versus stationary gates as well as in the impact of the integrated photonics.
