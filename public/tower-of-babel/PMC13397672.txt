Integrated comparative transcriptomics and WGCNA reveal the core transcriptional network and hub genes regulating sucrose accumulation in Saccharum spp
Authors: Ting Yang, Chunyan Kong, Hongbo Liu, Chunjia Li, Xujuan Li, Chunyan Tian, Xiuqin Lin, Xinlong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSucrose accumulation is a key agronomic trait of sugarcane, a primary sucrose-producing crop. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the performance differences between high- and low-sucrose genotypes remain unclear. In this study, two high-sucrose genotypes (YZ08-1609, YZ05-51) and one low-sucrose genotype (CP79-318) were used as materials to systematically analyze the dynamic transcriptional differences between high- and low-sucrose genotypes.ResultsPhenotypic analysis indicated that the high-sucrose genotypes exhibited the characteristic of “early accumulation.” The rapid sucrose accumulation period (S1–S2) in high-sucrose genotypes preceded that in the low-sucrose genotype (S2–S3). Comparative transcriptome analysis revealed that the two high-sucrose genotypes commonly exhibited a conserved regulatory pattern of “enhancing influx while reducing efflux,” characterized by the coordinated upregulation of the starch and sugar metabolism pathway and the coordinated downregulation of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. WGCNA identified 17 co-expression modules. Among them, modules highly significantly positively correlated with sugar-related traits were significantly enriched in pathways such as translation, peptide biosynthetic process, and ribosome, whereas the negatively correlated modules (ME.darkorange, ME.darkseagreen3) were significantly enriched in pathways such as glycolysis and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Further analysis identified multiple key hub genes, including ribosomal protein genes (such as RPS3A, RPL6C) and key enzyme genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (such as PAL1, CAD).ConclusionsThis study identified a core, conserved transcriptional regulatory network in high-sucrose sugarcane genotypes through integrated comparative transcriptomics and WGCNA analyses. This network is associated with efficient sucrose accumulation, and appears to reflect a pattern characterized by enhanced biosynthesis capacity, optimized allocation of carbon sources to sucrose, and restricted diversion to lignin. The research results not only deepen the understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of carbon metabolism in sugarcane but also provide important candidate gene resources for the molecular breeding of high-sucrose traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09232-z.Keywords: Saccharum spp, Sucrose accumulation, Transcriptome sequencing, WGCNA, Candidate genes

Background

Sugarcane (Saccharum spp.) is the most important sugar crop globally, contributing over 80% of the world’s sucrose production [1, 2]. As an efficient C4 plant, it ranks among the top in terms of photosynthetic efficiency crops, enabling it to efficiently convert solar energy into chemical energy [3]. Its unique “source-sink” system enables it to accumulate high concentrations of sucrose in the stem, not only supporting the global sugar supply but also making it an ideal object for studying the mechanisms of sucrose synthesis, transport, and storage [4]. However, there are significant differences in the sucrose accumulation ability and its dynamic changes among different sugarcane genotypes [5, 6]. In-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of sucrose accumulation between high- and low-sucrose genotypes is of great significance for breaking through the bottleneck of traditional breeding and cultivating new high-sucrose genotypes through molecular design [7, 8].

Sucrose accumulation is a complex genetic and developmental process that is precisely regulated by multiple mechanisms, such as the transport of photosynthetic products, sucrose unloading in sink organs, sucrose synthesis and storage, and the competition for carbon source allocation [9, 10]. To date, researchers have published numerous reports on sugarcane sucrose metabolism involving sucrose phosphate synthase [11–12], sucrose synthase [13], invertase [14], fructokinase [15, 16], sucrose transporters [17, 18], transcription factors [19, 20], and plant hormones [21]. These studies have provided important information for revealing the genetic basis of sucrose accumulation in sugarcane and have identified a number of candidate genes that can be used for molecular improvement. However, previous studies still fail to comprehensively clarify the regulatory network of sucrose accumulation, and the identification of key regulatory factors still requires further exploration. Therefore, new technological approaches are needed to advance our understanding.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), as a systems biology method, has been widely used in recent years to identify core gene modules associated with complex traits from large-scale transcriptome data [22]. This method constructs gene co-expression networks, identifies functionally related gene clusters, and correlates them with phenotypic traits. It has been successfully applied in the genetic analysis of various crops such as rice [23], maize [24], wheat [25], peanut [26], etc. In sugarcane research, WGCNA has been widely used in studies on stress responses, disease resistance, etc. For example, Tang et al. [27] used this method to study the response of sugarcane tiller seedlings to drought stress and identified co-expression modules and hub genes that were significantly associated with physiological indicators; Wu et al. [28] screened key genes that positively and negatively regulate disease resistance by comparing the gene expression profiles of susceptible and resistant varieties after inoculation with smut pathogens.

However, in terms of sucrose accumulation, there is currently a lack of systematic analysis of the multi-period transcriptome dynamics of high- and low-sucrose genotypes during the critical period of sucrose accumulation. In particular, co-expression network research based on WGCNA remains relatively limited. Therefore, this study intends to combine multi-period transcriptome sequencing with WGCNA analysis to investigate the gene expression dynamics of high- and low-sucrose genotypes during the sucrose accumulation phase, identify co-expression modules and enriched pathways closely related to sucrose content (SC), and uncover key hub genes that regulate the “source-sink” distribution of carbon assimilates, aiming to provide a solid theoretical foundation and valuable genetic resources for the molecular design breeding of high-sucrose genotypes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The experiment was conducted in 2021 at the experimental base of the Sugarcane Research Institute, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in Kaiyuan City, Honghe Prefecture, Yunnan Province. The soil used was red soil with medium fertility, and conventional water and fertilizer management practices were applied uniformly across all plots. No major disease or pest infestations occurred during the experiment.

The plant materials included two widely promoted high-sucrose genotypes and one low-sucrose genotype. YZ08-1609 is a high-sucrose variety bred by the Sugarcane Research Institute, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China, with an average SC of 16.93% from January to March [29]. YZ05-51 is another high-sucrose variety also bred by the same institute, with an average SC of 15.67% from January to March [30]. CP79-318, a low-sucrose genotype originally introduced from the United States (Canal Point, Florida) and maintained as a parental line in China, served as the control, with an average SC of 6.81% from January to March [5]. On March 1, 2021, healthy seed canes (double-budded setts) were selected for planting. Each variety was planted in 3 rows, with a row length of 10 m and a row spacing of 1.2 m, using a completely randomized block design with three biological replicates.

Based on previous phenological records, sampling was conducted during the late elongation stage—the critical period for rapid sucrose accumulation—at three time points: S1 (September 1), S2 (September 29), and S3 (October 27). At each sampling time, three plants with uniform growth and no mechanical damage were randomly selected from each block. The pith tissue from the second internode from the base (after careful removal of the rind) was collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for RNA-seq analysis. Separately, three additional plants from the same block were selected for juice extraction to measure sugar-related traits. All samples were collected, processed, and preserved independently by biological replicate to ensure data reliability.

Determination of sugar-related traits

The soluble solids content (SSC) was measured using a handheld refractometer (PAL-1, ATAGO Co., Japan). Prior to each measurement session, the refractometer was calibrated with distilled water (zero calibration). A drop of fresh internodal juice was evenly applied to the prism surface, and the SSC value was recorded after the instrument automatically stabilized the reading. Immediately after measurement, the prism was rinsed with distilled water and gently wiped dry with a lint-free soft cloth. SC was measured by automatic saccharimeter, Autopol880 (Rudolph Research Analytical, NJ, USA), Prior to each measurement session, the instrument was calibrated using a standard quartz control plate. The internodal juice was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter to remove suspended impurities and then injected into a 200 mm temperature-controlled polarimeter tube. The optical rotation was measured under constant temperature conditions at 25 °C, and the sucrose content was calculated according to the national standard of China GB/T 10,498—2014.The apparent purity (AP) was calculated according to the formula: AP (%) = (SC / SSC) × 100%. All measurements were performed in a temperature-controlled laboratory at 25 °C.

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from sugarcane stalk pith tissues using TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity (RIN value) was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). All samples had RIN values greater than 7.0 and OD260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0, meeting the library construction standards. Subsequently, sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS® mRNA-seq V3 Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (Vazyme, China). Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the DNA 1000 Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies, 5067 − 1504), which detects fragment sizes ranging from 25 to 1000 bp and concentrations from 0.1 to 50 ng/µL. Qualified libraries with appropriate fragment size and concentration were used for subsequent sequencing. The qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) for 150-bp paired-end sequencing. The sequencing service was provided by Guangzhou Gidio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Bioinformatics analysis of RNA-Seq data

Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control and filtering using Fastp (v0.23.2) to remove adapter sequences, reads containing more than 10% ambiguous bases (N), reads consisting entirely of A bases, and low-quality reads (more than 50% of bases with Q ≤ 20). After filtering, high-quality clean reads were obtained for subsequent analyses. The reference genome and annotation files for the sugarcane cultivar XTT22 (Saccharum hybrid) were obtained from the Sugarcane Genome Database (ScDB, https://sugarcane.gxu.edu.cn/scdb/). Transcripts were assembled using StringTie (v2.2.1), and gene expression levels were quantified using Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (v3.5.2). Genes with |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and an adjusted P-value (FDR corrected) < 0.05 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis for the DEGs were performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.2.2). A Q-value ≤ 0.05 was set as the threshold for significant enrichment.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package in R. The input data was a normalized gene expression matrix comprising 27 samples (3 varieties × 3 growth stages × 3 biological replicates). First, the pickSoftThreshold function was used to select the soft-thresholding power, yielding an optimal soft-thresholding power of β = 12 (scale-free topology fit index R² > 0.85) to satisfy the scale-free network topology criterion. Subsequently, modules were identified using the dynamic tree-cutting algorithm (minModuleSize = 30, mergeCutHeight = 0.25). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the module eigengenes (MEs) and the measured sugar-related traits (SSC, SC, AP). Modules with an absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.7 and P < 0.05 were defined as key modules significantly associated with the target traits. Within each key module, candidate hub genes were selected based on high intramodular connectivity (kWithin) and high module membership (|MM| > 0.8). The top 30 genes satisfying both criteria were chosen for further analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

To validate the reliability of the RNA-Seq data, RT-qPCR analysis was performed on stalk tissues of the three sugarcane varieties at the S2 stage, which showed the most significant phenotypic differences (i.e., the peak rapid sucrose accumulation period for high-sucrose genotypes and the onset period for the low-sucrose genotype). Total RNA was extracted from the stalk tissues of each variety using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Evo-MLV Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate Biology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primers for the selected DEGs were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software (Supplementary Table 1). The glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was used as the internal reference gene [31]. qPCR amplification was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The reaction protocol was as follows: 95 °C for 30 s; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method [32]. Three technical replicates were performed for each sample to ensure the reproducibility and accuracy of the experimental results.

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses for sugar-related traits using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA). Data from three independent biological replicates are presented as mean ± SE. To compare the three sugarcane genotypes and accumulation increments between consecutive stages (ΔS2-S1 vs. ΔS3-S2), one-way ANOVA was used, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Dynamic changes in sugar-related traits

Dynamic changes in sugar-related traits of the three genotypes are shown in Fig. 1. During the key sucrose accumulation period (S1–S3), the AP, SSC, and SC of the high-sucrose genotypes (YZ08-1609 and YZ05-51) were significantly higher than those of the low-sucrose control CP79-318 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Further analysis indicated that, except for the AP accumulation rate of YZ05-51 being higher during S2–S3 than during S1–S2, the accumulation rates of sugar-related traits for both high-sucrose genotypes were generally higher during S1–S2 compared to S2–S3, suggesting that S1–S2 is the crucial stage for sucrose accumulation in high-sucrose genotypes. In contrast, the increase rates of SSC, SC, and AP for the low-sucrose genotype CP79-318 were markedly faster during S2–S3 than during S1–S2, indicating that its rapid sucrose accumulation phase occurs later and is delayed compared to the high-sucrose genotypes (Fig. 1B).

To further verify the “early-accumulation” characteristic, we compared the accumulation increments between consecutive stages for all three sugar-related traits (Supplementary Fig. 1). For high-sucrose genotype YZ08-1609, the increments from S1 to S2 were significantly larger than those from S2 to S3 for AP, SSC, and SC (P < 0.05). For YZ05-51, the same pattern was observed for SSC and SC (P < 0.05), while AP showed no significant difference between the two periods (P > 0.05). In contrast, the low-sucrose genotype CP79-318 exhibited significantly larger increments from S2 to S3 for SSC and SC (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). These results collectively confirm the “early-accumulation” characteristic of high-sucrose genotypes.

Transcriptome sequencing

A total of 27 libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. After stringent quality filtering, an average of 67.5 million clean reads per sample were retained, with an average clean data rate of 99.6%. The average Q30 value (proportion of bases with quality score ≥ 30) was 92.9%, and the average GC content was 50.8%. On average, 86.1% of clean reads were successfully mapped to the reference genome (Supplementary Table S2). These results indicate that the RNA-Seq data are of high quality and suitable for downstream analyses.

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on samples from three sugarcane varieties across three developmental stages, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis based on FPKM values (Supplementary Fig. 2). The results showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 23.65% and 17.80% of the total variance, respectively. Samples in the PCA plot exhibited clear grouping patterns; the three biological replicates from the same stage clustered closely together, indicating high experimental reproducibility. Distinct separations were observed among samples from different varieties and developmental stages, suggesting significant differences in gene expression. Notably, PC2 effectively distinguished the high-sucrose genotypes (YZ08-1609 and YZ05-51) from the low-sucrose genotype (CP79-318), implying that sucrose content variation may be closely associated with global transcriptional changes. The hierarchical clustering heatmap revealed that samples from the same variety or the same developmental stage tended to cluster together, further validating the consistency and reliability of the data. Overall, the clustering analysis showed clear separation of samples from different genotypes.

Analysis of key metabolic pathways during the critical sucrose accumulation period in high- and low-sucrose genotypes

To elucidate the metabolic regulatory basis underlying efficient sucrose accumulation, we compared the gene expression dynamics between high- and low-sucrose genotypes during the critical sucrose accumulation stages (S1 vs. S2, S1 vs. S3). Venn diagrams revealed (Fig. 2A) that in the two comparison sets (S1 vs. S2 and S1 vs. S3), the high-sucrose genotypes YZ08-1609 had 628 commonly up-regulated genes and 1,329 commonly down-regulated genes; YZ05-51 had 566 commonly up-regulated genes and 4,567 commonly down-regulated genes; whereas the low-sucrose genotype CP79-318 had 1,952 commonly up-regulated genes and 6,423 commonly down-regulated genes. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated (Fig. 2B–D) that the up-regulated genes in both high-sucrose genotype, YZ08-1609 and YZ05-51, were significantly enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500) pathway and carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (Calvin cycle, ko00710). Conversely, their down-regulated genes were consistently enriched in carbon-consuming metabolic pathways, most notably phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) (Fig. 2B, C). In YZ08-1609, down-regulated genes were also enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500). In contrast, the low-sucrose genotype CP79-318 exhibited an uncoordinated regulatory pattern (Fig. 2D): while its commonly up-regulated genes were enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500), its commonly down-regulated genes were simultaneously enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and various amino acid biosynthesis pathways. Together, these temporal dynamics (within-genotype comparisons) reveal a conserved transcriptional reprogramming in high-sucrose genotypes that favors sucrose synthesis over competing pathways.

Identification and enrichment analysis of DEGs specific to the rapid accumulation period in high-sucrose genotypes

To further identify specific gene sets that are uniquely altered during the rapid accumulation phase (S1–S2) of high-sucrose genotypes, we next performed between-genotype comparisons. Compared to the low-sucrose control CP79-318, the high-sucrose genotypes YZ05-51 and YZ08-1609 exhibited 70,085 and 65,756 DEGs at the S1 stage, comprising 38,458 and 33,482 up-regulated genes, and 31,627 and 32,274 down-regulated genes, respectively. At the S2 stage, 62,337 and 59,850 DEGs were detected in the two varieties, consisting of 33,121 and 31,405 up-regulated genes, and 29,216 and 28,445 down-regulated genes, respectively (Fig. 3A). Notably, the dynamic changes in gene expression during the rapid accumulation window of high-sucrose genotypes (S1–S2) significantly differed from those in the low-sucrose genotype’s rapid accumulation window (S2–S3) (Fig. 3B). Comparing the DEGs from the high-sucrose genotypes (S1 vs. S2) with those from the low-sucrose genotype (S2 vs. S3), only 879 DEGs were shared between the two groups. In contrast, 1,828 DEGs were specific to the high-sucrose genotypes (potentially involved in regulating the rapid high-sucrose accumulation response), while 3,313 DEGs were specific to the low-sucrose genotype (Fig. 3C).

KEGG analysis further demonstrated that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500), amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (ko00520), pyruvate metabolism (ko00620), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) (Fig. 4B).

To further analyze the key DEGs involved in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500), we mapped this pathway and generated a heatmap of the expression levels of the relevant DEGs (Fig. 4C). In high-sucrose genotypes, sucrose synthesis-related genes SUS1 and SPS were significantly upregulated at S2, whereas the low-sucrose genotype showed higher expression of degradation-related genes (FRK2, GLU2, PGM1) and starch synthesis genes (AGPL3, AGPS2). Construction of weighted gene co-expression network

To systematically identify core gene modules co-varying with sugar-related traits, we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network. This analysis identified a total of 17 gene co-expression modules (Fig. 5A). Module-trait relationship analysis revealed that four modules were significantly correlated with sugar-related traits (Fig. 5B): the ME.coral module (containing 2,047 genes) and the ME.brown module (containing 3,301 genes) showed highly significant positive correlations with both AP and sucrose content (r > 0.7, P < 0.01); the ME.darkorange module (containing 3,250 genes) showed highly significant negative correlations with AP, SSC, SC (|r| > 0.7, P < 0.01); and the ME.darkseagreen3 module (containing 133 genes) showed highly significant negative correlations with both SSC and SC.

Functional enrichment analysis of key module genes

Genes in the positively correlated modules (ME.coral, ME.brown) were significantly enriched in pathways and biological processes such as translation (GO:0006412), peptide biosynthetic process (GO:0043043), and ribosome (ko03010) (Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, genes in the negatively correlated module ME.darkorange were primarily and significantly enriched in single-organism carbohydrate metabolic process (GO:0044275), organic acid metabolic process (GO:0006082), oxidation-reduction process (GO:0055114), as well as pathways including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010) and carbon metabolism (ko01200) (Fig. 6C). KEGG enrichment analysis of genes in the other negatively correlated module, ME.darkseagreen3, showed their primary involvement in pathways such as protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ko04141), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and biosynthesis of amino acids (ko01230) (Fig. 6D).

Mining of hub genes in key modules and interaction network analysis

To further elucidate the key molecular mechanisms regulating sucrose accumulation, this study identified four key modules (ME.coral, ME.brown, ME.darkorange, ME.darkseagreen3) based on WGCNA and selected the top 30 genes with the highest connectivity from each module as candidate hub gene sets (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 2). Through integrated analysis of functional annotations and biological significance, core regulatory genes closely associated with sucrose accumulation were further screened (Supplementary Table 3).

In the positively correlated modules, the hub genes of ME.coral primarily included the proteasome-related gene RPT3 (Sobic.001G458700.1.path5) and several ribosomal protein-encoding genes: RPL6C (Sobic.004G198400.1.path6), RPS3C (Sobic.007G053300.1.path6), RPS3A (Sobic.001G467300.3.path9), and RPS25B (MSTRG.163262). This suggests that this module may participate in the regulation of sugar metabolism by modulating protein synthesis and degradation processes. Hub genes in the ME.brown module included the peroxisomal protein gene PEX14 (Sobic.009G000500.1.path5), the sulfurtransferase gene STR1 (Sobic.008G162000.1.path5), and the gene encoding the glycolytic rate-limiting enzyme PFK3 (Sobic.010G041400.1.path8).

In the negatively correlated modules, the hub gene ADH1 (Sobic.001G097600.1.path4) from ME.darkorange is involved in the glycolysis pathway (ko00010), while TIFY10B (Sobic.002G374100.1.path2) participates in plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), implying that this module might suppress sucrose accumulation through metabolic diversion or hormonal regulation. Furthermore, the hub genes in the ME.darkseagreen3 module—PAL (MSTRG.109226), PAL1 (Sobic.004G220300.1.path4), CAD (Sobic.004G071000.1.path1), and CCOAOMT1 (Sobic.010G052200.1.path5)—are all involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940).

To visualize the expression dynamics of the aforementioned key candidate genes, we generated a heatmap depicting their expression across all samples (Fig. 8). For genes in the positively correlated modules (ME.coral and ME.brown), hub genes such as the ribosomal protein genes RPS3A (Sobic.001G467300.3.path9) and RPS3C (Sobic.007G053300.1.path6) generally exhibited higher expression levels in the high-sucrose genotypes (YZ08-1609 and YZ05-51) compared to the low-sucrose genotype (CP79-318), and maintained relatively high expression during their rapid sucrose accumulation period (S1–S2). In contrast, genes in the negatively correlated modules (ME.darkorange and ME.darkseagreen3), particularly the key enzyme genes PAL1 (Sobic.004G220300.1.path4) and CAD (Sobic.004G071000.1.path1) from the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940), showed higher expression levels in the low-sucrose genotype CP79-318 and lower expression in the high-sucrose genotypes. The expression of these genes exhibited a declining trend at the S3 stage (maturity stage) across all varieties, yet their expression levels remained higher in the low-sucrose genotype than in the high-sucrose genotypes.

To validate the reliability of the RNA-Seq data, this study employed RT-qPCR to measure the expression levels of 9 DEGs across three sugarcane varieties at the S2 stage. These included 5 hub genes selected from the co-expression modules that showed significant differential expression at S2, and 4 DEGs involved in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500). The expression trends of the selected DEGs measured by RT-qPCR were generally consistent with those from RNA-Seq analysis (Fig. 9).

A model for the regulatory mechanism of carbon allocation during the key sucrose accumulation period in sugarcane

To systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the differences in sucrose accumulation between high- and low-sucrose genotypes, this study integrated dynamic phenotypic data, DEGs, and WGCNA results to construct a regulatory model for carbon allocation during the critical sucrose accumulation period in sugarcane (Fig. 10). The model demonstrates that the efficiency of sucrose accumulation in sugarcane stalks depends on the balance of carbon allocation between anabolic and competing catabolic pathways, starting from carbon fixed by the Calvin cycle in photosynthesis (ko00710).

A working hypothesis for carbon allocation regulation during the key sucrose accumulation period in high- and low-sucrose genotypes based on transcriptomic data. Note: This model is primarily constructed based on differential gene expression and WGCNA analyses. The inferred carbon allocation patterns represent a testable hypothesis. Direct validation of metabolic flux direction and magnitude requires future integration of metabolomics, enzyme activity assays, and stable isotope-based flux analysis (e.g., ¹³C-MFA)

In high-sucrose genotypes, the transcriptomic evidence suggests that a greater proportion of photosynthetic carbon may be directed toward sucrose synthesis. This inference is supported by the coordinated upregulation of starch and sucrose metabolism-related genes (e.g., SPS, SuSy) and the downregulation of competitive pathway genes (e.g., PAL, CAD in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. This process is co-driven by two positively correlated gene modules: the ME.coral module, which, by upregulating genes related to ribosome biogenesis (ko03010) and protein translation (e.g., RPT3, RPL6C, RPS3C), significantly enhances the biosynthetic capacity of the sink organ (the stalk), thereby providing ample metabolic potential for sucrose accumulation. Subsequently, the carbon flow is efficiently converted into sucrose via the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (ko00500). Concurrently, only a small fraction of carbon is consumed, primarily through pathways such as glycolysis (ko00010) mediated by the ME.brown module, to maintain basic energy and precursor supply.

In contrast, in the low-sucrose genotype, a substantial portion of photosynthetic carbon is diverted within the sink organ by competing metabolic pathways. The ME.darkorange module activates pathways such as glycolysis (ko00010) and plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), while the ME.darkseagreen3 module reinforces the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940) (involving genes like PAL1 and CAD), irreversibly channeling carbon into structural components like lignin or energy production. This excessive diversion of carbon significantly depletes the carbon precursors available for sucrose synthesis, thereby impairing sucrose accumulation.

In summary, this model reveals, at a systems level, the fundamental difference in sucrose accumulation: the advantage of high-sucrose genotypes lies in establishing a strong “sink strength” and the efficient, directed allocation of carbon flow toward sucrose synthesis; whereas, in the low-sucrose genotype, sucrose accumulation is hindered due to the overconsumption of carbon within the sink by multiple competing pathways.

Discussion

Physiological advantage and molecular basis of “early-accumulation” in high-sucrose genotypes

Phenotypic analysis revealed that the rapid sucrose accumulation period (S1–S2) in high-sucrose genotypes occurred notably earlier than that in the low-sucrose control CP79-318 (S2–S3) (Fig. 1). This “early-accumulation” characteristic serves as a key physiological adaptation strategy. From the perspective of evolutionary ecology, crop phenology is a survival strategy formed by long-term natural and artificial selection to adapt to specific environments and maximize reproductive success [33]. As a typical source-sink crop, the timing of sink strength establishment is of great significance in sugarcane. High-sucrose genotypes might activate efficient unloading and storage mechanisms in the sink organ earlier, enabling them to capture and fix photosynthetic products during the S1–S2 stage when light and temperature resources are abundant. This finding is consistent with the “Sucrose Accumulation Amount (SAA)” concept [5], showing that high-sucrose genotypes obtain a longer effective accumulation period through an earlier start. This advancement in developmental timing is probably a result of artificial directional selection for high sucrose content [34].

Multi-dimensional analysis reveals coordinated regulation of carbon allocation

Dynamic pathway analysis indicated that during the critical sucrose accumulation phase, both high-sucrose genotypes exhibited a conserved metabolic reprogramming pattern: concurrent upregulation of starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500) and downregulation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) (Fig. 2). This coordinated change, which prioritizes sucrose production while suppressing carbon flux into competing secondary metabolic pathways, probably serves as the basis for the robust establishment of the high-sucrose phenotype. As the initiating pathway for the synthesis of secondary metabolites such as lignin, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis typically competes with sucrose accumulation for carbon sources [35]. In this study, this pathway showed complex regulation: an overall downregulation trend in temporal comparisons within high-sucrose genotypes (Fig. 2), yet it emerged as an enriched pathway in inter-genotype comparisons (Fig. 4B). This suggests fine-tuned regulation that maintains basal activity for stress resistance while maximizing carbon flow toward sucrose storage [36].

Further analysis identified 1,828 DEGs specific to high-sucrose genotypes (Fig. 4). KEGG enrichment indicated these genes are involved in starch and sucrose metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism(Fig. 4). The starch and sucrose metabolism pathway directly regulates the balance between sucrose synthesis and degradation [8, 37]. The enhancement of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism reflects stronger precursor supply capacity [38], while pyruvate metabolism enrichment suggests enhanced energy metabolism to support sucrose accumulation [39]. This finding indicates that high-sucrose genotypes, while intensively accumulating sucrose, simultaneously enhance their energy metabolism system to support this highly energy-demanding physiological process.

WGCNA positively correlated modules reveal systemic support for sucrose accumulation

The ME.coral and ME.brown modules, both strongly and positively correlated with sugar-related traits, were significantly enriched in translation (GO:0006412) and ribosome biogenesis (ko03010). Hub genes in these modules were mainly ribosomal protein-encoding genes such as RPS3A and RPL6C (Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 2). This enrichment indicates that high-sucrose genotypes maintain an elevated global protein synthesis capacity, providing the essential translational machinery required for rapid sucrose accumulation [40, 41]. Notably, the ME.brown module also contains PEX14 and PFK3, linking peroxisomal energy metabolism and glycolytic flux to sucrose biosynthesis. PEX14 facilitates fatty acid β-oxidation and contributes to reactive oxygen species scavenging, thereby supporting both cellular energy supply and redox homeostasis [42]. Meanwhile, upregulated PFK3, the rate-limiting phosphofructokinase isoform in glycolysis, likely enhances carbon flux toward sucrose precursors such as UDP-glucose while boosting mitochondrial respiration to meet the heightened ATP demand for biosynthesis [43].

In summary, these co-upregulated modules delineate an integrated transcriptional program in high-sucrose genotypes: coordinated activation of ribosome biogenesis, translation, glycolysis, and peroxisomal metabolism establishes a robust material, energetic, and redox foundation for efficient sucrose accumulation. Nonetheless, functional validation including gene knockdown, enzymatic assays, and metabolic flux analysis is required to confirm causal roles of these pathways in sucrose accumulation.

WGCNA negatively correlated modules reveal carbon competition in the low-sucrose genotype

ME.darkorange and ME.darkseagreen3 are significantly negatively correlated with sugar-related traits. The ME.darkorange module is enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010). Excessive glycolytic activity diverts carbon skeletons from sucrose synthesis toward energy production, directly competing with sucrose accumulation [44]. The ME.darkseagreen3 module was enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940), with hub genes including PAL1, CAD, and CCOAOMT1. The phenylpropanoid and sucrose pathways share common precursors (PEP and erythrose-4-phosphate) [44], representing a classic metabolic trade-off. Consistent with previous reports of a negative correlation between lignin and sucrose [45], our data showed upregulation of PAL1 and CAD in the low-sucrose genotype (Fig. 8). This inverse expression pattern suggests potential competition for carbon resources at the transcriptional level. However, it should be noted that transcriptomic evidence alone cannot directly demonstrate metabolic flux competition; future metabolomic and enzymatic activity analyses are required to quantitatively validate the carbon allocation trade-off between these two pathways.

Regulatory functions of hub genes and molecular breeding potential

Ribosomal protein genes (RPS3A, RPL6C) from positively correlated modules are core regulators of anabolic capacity and serve as candidate targets for breeding. In negatively correlated modules, PFK3, PAL, and CAD act as “valve” genes controlling carbon diversion.While CRISPR/Cas9 may offer future avenues for manipulating these genes [46], applying these techniques to polyploid sugarcane remains challenging due to functional redundancy among homoeologous alleles. Nevertheless, continued advances may enable spatiotemporally specific regulation of PAL or CAD to reduce lignin allocation while maintaining lodging resistance. The stress-responsive transcription factors BZIP23 and TGA2.2 also warrant further investigation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the two high-sucrose genotypes (YZ08-1609 and YZ05-51) and the low-sucrose genotype (CP79-318) used in this study are not near-isogenic lines. Therefore, the observed differences in gene expression and metabolic pathways may reflect broader genetic variation between these genotypes rather than being exclusively attributable to sucrose accumulation per se. Future studies using near-isogenic lines or genetic populations (e.g., F2 or backcross populations) are warranted to validate the causal roles of the identified hub genes and pathways. Second, the inferences regarding carbon allocation and metabolic flux direction in this study are primarily derived from transcriptomic data and KEGG enrichment analysis. While gene expression patterns provide valuable insights into potential metabolic shifts, they do not directly measure actual metabolic flux. Therefore, the proposed model of carbon allocation (Fig. 10) represents a transcriptome-informed hypothesis rather than a definitive demonstration of flux changes. Future studies integrating metabolomics and stable isotope-based metabolic flux analysis (¹³C-MFA) are warranted to quantitatively validate the direction and magnitude of carbon flux changes inferred from this study.

Conclusion

This study, through the integration of multi-stage transcriptome data and WGCNA analysis, systematically deciphered the molecular regulatory network underlying sucrose accumulation in sugarcane. We discovered that high-sucrose genotypes possess an “early-accumulation” characteristic, whose molecular basis lies in a conserved metabolic regulatory program of “enhancing influx while reducing efflux.” Via WGCNA, we identified the core modules and hub genes that may underpin this program, providing a transcriptome-informed framework for future functional validation. These findings not only deepen the understanding of carbon allocation regulation in sink organs but also provide candidate targets for the molecular breeding of high-sucrose sugarcane.
