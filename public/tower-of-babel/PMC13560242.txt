IL17 signaling promotes oocyte developmental competence acquisition during maturation
Authors: Huaxing Zhao, Xiaohua He, Junsong Shi, Xiao Wu, Yalin Liang, Xingwang Wang, Zheng Ao, Xianjun Zhang, Yuxing Zhang, Yanan Li, Yiqian Zhang, Yazheng Dong, Jingfeng Peng, Yunfei Hou, Rong Zhou, Fang Zeng, Linjun Hong, Ting Gu, Huaqiang Yang, Jie Yang, Zheng Xu, Sixiu Huang, Gengyuan Cai, Zicong Li, Zhenfang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDefects in the acquisition of oocyte developmental competence during the maturation process causes subfertility or infertility in animals and humans. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms of oocyte maturation is essential for reproductive biology and medicine. Follicular fluid (FF) is an important microenvironment governing oocyte maturation.MethodsA tandem mass tags (TMT)-based comparative FF proteomic analysis was employed to identify FF proteins that are potentially crucial for oocyte maturation. A very large number of pig and mouse oocytes (approximately 20,000) and embryos (over 13,000, including somatic cell nuclear transfer, parthenogenetic activation, and in vitro fertilization embryos) were used to investigate the effects of identified FF proteins on in vitro oocyte maturation and subsequent in vitro and in vivo embryo development. RNA sequencing, quantitative PCR, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, and immunofluorescence were used to study the expression patterns and action mechanisms of identified FF proteins in oocytes. In addition, intra-oocyte levels of glutathione and reactive oxygen species were measured to assess redox homeostasis.ResultsInterleukin 17D (IL17D) was identified as an important FF protein and it is significantly upregulated in porcine FF during oocyte maturation. IL17D promotes oocyte maturation by enhancing bidirectional communication between oocytes and cumulus cells, via upregulating CX43 expression and transzonal projections, which helps to maintain oocyte redox homeostasis and nuclear–cytoplasmic synchrony. IL17D treatment of oocytes enhances subsequent in vitro and in vivo full-term embryo development by modulating lipid metabolism and histone modification reprogramming. IL17D exerts its function via activating IL17 signaling through binding to CD93. Two other IL17 family members, IL17A and IL17F, also enhance oocyte maturation quality. IL17D displays a conserved expression pattern and function in pig and mouse oocytes.ConclusionsThis study reveals the critical roles of IL17D in regulating oocyte developmental competence acquisition during maturation by activating IL17 signaling. The findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlining oocyte developmental potential acquisition and may help to develop methods for efficient production of oocytes for assisted reproduction.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00971-3.Keywords: Oocyte, Follicular fluid, Proteome, IL17D, IL17 signaling, Cell communication

Introduction

Oocyte maturation is the final stage of oogenesis yielding a competent egg to support early embryo development. The developmental competence acquired during this phase determines the quality and health of mature oocytes, which is essential for the normal growth of early embryos [1–3]. Defects in gaining oocyte developmental ability during in vivo maturation cause subfertility and even infertility in humans and animals [4, 5]. Inadequate oocyte competence acquisition during in vitro maturation also leads to inefficient generation of in vitro embryos, such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) and somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) embryos, for assisted reproduction in humans and livestock [6–8]. A detailed understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying the acquisition of oocyte developmental potential during the maturation process could be beneficial for improvement of both human reproductive health and livestock reproduction performances.

Oocyte maturation is a complex biological process involving numerous distinctive events such as cAMP-regulated breakdown of germinal vesicle [9], proliferation and expansion of cumulus cells [10], and establishment of communication between the oocyte and cumulus cells [11]. Failure in any of these critical steps may result in oocyte maturation defects and subsequent embryo development impairment. However, the key molecules and signaling pathways that regulate the complex oocyte maturation process still remain largely unknown.

Follicular fluid (FF) is a unique microenvironment that plays an important role in regulating the maturation of oocytes. FF is mainly composed of blood plasma exudates and follicular cell secretions, which contains a variety of regulatory molecules such as proteins, RNAs, metabolites, hormones, cytokines, and polysaccharides [12–14]. The abundance of these components in FF is changed dynamically during mammalian oocyte maturation [15–17]. It has been reported that the levels of some factors, such as estrogen, melatonin, amphiregulin and interleukins, in FF can reflect the quality or developmental ability of mature oocytes [18–21].

In this study, we performed a comparative proteomic analysis of porcine mature follicular fluid (pMFF) and immature follicular fluid (pIFF) to identify novel factors involved in porcine oocyte maturation. A potentially important cytokine called interleukin 17D (IL17D) was identified from our proteomic analysis results. Subsequently, the roles and action mechanisms of IL17D in oocyte developmental competence acquisition were investigated. We demonstrated that IL17D-mediated activation of IL17 signaling plays an important role in regulating pig and mouse oocyte maturation and subsequent embryo development.

Materials and methods

Animals

FF donor Duroc sows (about 14 months old) purchased from Guangdong Wens Breeding Swine Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China) were housed under suitable room temperature with free feeding and drinking. Cumulus cell–oocyte complex (COC) donor female mice (6- to 8-week-old Kunming mice) were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China) and housed in ventilated cages with a standard 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle. The animal experimental protocols used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of South China Agricultural University (approval number: 2020f005, approval date: 3 April 2020).

Collection of in vivo follicular fluid and in vitro oocyte culture medium

The collection of in vivo FF and in vitro oocyte culture medium were performed as previously described [8]. Briefly, Duroc sows were continuously treated with 20 mg altrenogest (Yofoto, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China) per sow per day for 18 days, and then injected with 1000 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG, Yofoto, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China). After 40 h, ovaries of estrous sows were surgically exposed and follicular fluid was collected from preovulatory follicles (diameter > 15 mm) on ovaries with just ovulated follicles. This FF was defined as pig mature follicular fluid (pMFF). The pig immature follicular fluid (pIFF) was collected from immature follicles (3–5 mm in diameter) of nonestrous Duroc sows by surgery. Fresh culture medium (FCM) and mature culture medium (MCM), which were collected at 44 h after culturing with porcine COCs, were also collected for analysis. Following the removal of cell debris by centrifugation, all samples were stored in liquid nitrogen for later use.

Tandem mass tags (TMT) proteomics

The FF samples were individually milled to a powder in a mortar with liquid nitrogen and quickly transferred to a precooled centrifuge tube. After using the ProteoMinerTM protein enrichment kit (Bio-Rad, California, USA) to remove the high-abundance proteins in FF, the products were mixed with the lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8), 8 M Urea, and 0.2% SDS, and incubated with ultrasonication on ice for 5 min and subjected to centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with 2 mM DTT for 1 h at 56 °C after adding sufficient iodoacetic acid to the sample and incubating for 1 h at room temperature. Then the sample extract was added with four volumes of cold acetone, vortexed well, and placed at −20 °C for 2 h to overnight. Following centrifugation, the pellet was collected and washed twice with cold acetone. The pellet was dissolved in a dissolution buffer containing 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.5) and 8.0 M urea. Protein concentration was determined with a Bradford assay. The supernatant from each sample, containing precisely 0.1 mg of protein, was digested with Trypsin Gold (Promega, Wisconsin, USA) for 16 h at 37 °C. After trypsin digestion, the peptide was desalted with a C18 cartridge to remove the high urea, and desalted peptides were dried by vacuum centrifugation. According to manufacturer’s instructions, desalted peptides were labeled with TMT 10-plex reagents (TMT10plexTM Isobaric Label Reagents Set, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA USA). Finally, after HPLC fraction, samples were subjected to proteomic analysis using an EASY-nLCTM 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA USA) coupled to an Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA USA) operating in the data-dependent acquisition mode. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis was performed by Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

The Proteome Discoverer 2.2 software was used for searching the raw files against Sus scrofa 11.1 of the NCBI database. To ensure reliability of data, peptides and proteins with false discovery rates (FDR) < 5% were considered confident, and proteins with at least a unique peptide were selected for subsequent analysis. For pMFF and pIFF samples, a Student’s t-test was used to determine protein abundance difference between two groups. As described previously, parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) proteomics was used to validate the reliability of TMT proteomic data [8]. The principal component analysis (PCA) of pMFF and pIFF was performed using the R package factoextra [22]. The enrichment of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was analyzed using a hypergeometric test. In brief, all differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were mapped to the KEGG database, thereby calculating the number of DEPs in each pathway. Then, a hypergeometric test was applied to calculate significantly enriched pathways with all identified proteins as background. The R package clusterProfiler 4.0 was used for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [23].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Frozen FF and in invtro maturation (IVM) media were thawed on ice and then gradually equilibrated to room temperature for the IL17D ELISA assay. The porcine IL17D ELISA assays were performed using an IL17D ELISA kit (Jinmei, Jiangsu, China; cat. no. JM10019P2), and a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA). Oocytes were collected for the cAMP ELISA assay. Approximately 100 oocytes were treated with 200 μL cell lysis buffer and then vortexed to fully release the intra-oocyte cAMP. The cAMP ELISA assays were performed using a cAMP ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA; cat. no. KGE002B), and a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA).

Porcine oocyte collection and in vitro maturation

Ovaries of gilts were obtained from a local slaughterhouse and transported to the laboratory in 0.9% saline (w/v) containing penicillin-G (100 IU/mL) and streptomycin sulfate (100 mg/L) at 25–30 °C within 6 h. COCs were aspirated from the antral follicles ((3–6 mm in diameter) and recovered under a stereomicroscope. Only COCs with at least three layers of compact cumulus cells and homogeneous cytoplasm were selected for IVM. Approximately 50 COCs were cultured in IVM media under mineral oil at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 44 h. According to purpose of each oocyte IVM experiment, the IVM medium was supplemented with the following drugs: IL17D recombinant protein (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China; cat. no. CG96), metoclopramide (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China; cat. no. HY-17382), IL17A (Pepro Tech, New Jersey, USA; cat. no. 200-17-5UG), IL17B (Pepro Tech, New Jersey, USA; cat. no. 200–28), IL17C (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA; cat. no. 2306-ML-025), IL17E (Pepro Tech, New Jersey, USA; cat. no. 200–24), and IL17F (Pepro Tech, New Jersey, USA; cat. no. 210-17F-5UG). The IVM medium was prepared as we previously reported [24]. The matured COCs were treated with DPBS containing 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase to remove the surrounding cumulus cells. The oocytes with the first polar body in the perivitelline space were considered as mature.

Production and culture of embryos

Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) and parthenogenetic activation (PA) of porcine embryos were conducted as we previously described [24]. Briefly, for production of SCNT embryos, the matured oocytes and donor cells were suspended in TCM-199 medium plus 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) containing 7.5 μg/mL cytochalasin B (CB). After oocytes were enucleated, they were stained with 1 g/mL Hoechst 33,342 and examined under UV-light irradiation to exclude oocytes that were not completely enucleated. A single donor cell from a Duroc boar was injected into the perivitelline space of each enucleated oocyte. The constructed oocytes were electrically fused using two direct current pulses of 150 V/mm for 50 μs in 0.28 mol/L mannitol supplemented with 0.1 mM MgSO4 and 0.01% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). The fused oocytes were cultured in PZM-3 medium for 1 h, and then activated by two direct current pulses of 100 V/mm for 20 μs in 0.28 mol/L mannitol supplemented with 0.1 mM MgSO4 and 0.05 mM CaCl2. These activated embryos were incubated in PZM-3 medium containing 5 mg/mL CB for 4 h at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. For production of PA embryos, the matured oocytes were electrically activated using two direct current pulses of 150 V/mm for 50 μs in 0.28 mol/L mannitol supplemented with 0.1 mM MgSO4 and 0.01% PVA. The activated embryos were cultured in PZM-3 medium supplemented with 5 mg/mL CB in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 38.5 °C for 4 h. These embryos were cultured in fresh PZM-3 medium under mineral oil at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 6 days. The cleavage and blastocyst rates were assessed at 2 and 6 days after embryo activation.

The embryo transfer was conducted as previously reported [25], but with some adjustments. In brief, SCNT embryos cultured for 20 h in vitro were examined and the abnormal embryos were removed. Only embryos with intact cytoplasm or normal cleavage were employed for embryo transfer. These normal SCNT embryos were loaded into a transparent transfer tube, placed in a portable incubator before transferring into surrogate sows. Estrus-synchronized Landrace and Yorkshire sows in parity 2–5 and showing natural standing estrus within 30–50 h were used as embryos recipients. These sows were anesthetized with ketamine (25 mg/ kg body weight) and xylazine (1.1 mg/ kg body weight) for induction, and 3% of isoflurane for maintenance. A single oviduct was exposed through surgery. The SCNT embryos with 100 μL culture medium were placed directly using a syringe into the oviduct of sow. One month after embryo transfer, the pregnancy status of the surrogate sows was monitored by ultrasound equipped with a convex transducer. If spontaneous farrowing did not occur until gestation day 116, the surrogate sows were injected with a prostaglandin analog (cloprostenol, 200 μg/surrogate), and after about 24 h they delivered vaginally under supervision or with assistance. The total number of newborn cloned piglets (including live born and still born) in each litter was recorded to calculate the average litter size of farrowed surrogates.

Mouse oocyte maturation and IVF embryo preparation

Firstly, 6- to 8-week-old female mice were superovulated by injecting 10 UI PMSG followed by 10 UI human chorionc gonadotropin (hCG) after 48 h. To collect germinal vesicle (GV) stage COCs, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at 48 h after PMSG injection, and COCs were retrieved by manual rupturing of antral ovarian follicles. GV stage COCs were cultured in M2 medium under mineral oil for 18 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, to obtain in vitro matured COCs. To collect in vivo matured COCs, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at 18 h after hCG injection, and COCs were isolated from oviduct ampullae. COCs were treated with DPBS containing 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase by repeatedly pipetting to remove surrounding cumulus cells. Human tubal fluid (HTF) medium under mineral oil was pre-cultured in a humidified atmosphere (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 4 h. Sperm collected from the cauda epididymides of 10-week-old male mice were dispersed in HTF medium for 1 h. Subsequently, sperm were introduced into COC-containing HTF medium at a final concentration of 4 × 105/mL. After 6 h of co-incubation, zygotes displaying two pronuclei were selected under stereomicroscopy and transferred to KSOM medium (Potassium Simplex Optimization Medium; Aibei Biotechnology, Nanjing, China; cat. no. M1450) under mineral oil (37 °C, 5% CO2). The two-cell rate, four-cell rate, and blastocyst rate of IVF embryos were assessed at 24, 48, and 120 h post-fertilization, respectively.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The matured oocytes and four-cell stage cloned embryos were washed thrice with DPBS at 37.5 °C. Then, 20 oocytes or 5 four-cell stage embryos were randomly selected and transferred into a PCR tube containing 10 μL RNA lysis buffer, respectively, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Overall, 50 COCs were selected to remove oocytes, and the suspension containing cumulus cells (CCs) was centrifuged at 800 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. Cell pellets were washed thrice with DPBS at 4 °C, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80 °C. RNA extraction, library construction, and RNA sequencing were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Each group had three biological replicates. For bioinformatics analysis, filtered clean reads were mapped to the Sus scrofa 11.1 reference genome. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R package DESeq2 [26]. Biological process of Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and KEGG enrichment were performed by the R package clusterProfiler. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed on String (https://cn.string-db.org/) and visualized in Cytoscape 3.7 software.

Immunofluorescence

Blastocysts, oocytes, and COCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. P0099-100 mL) for 15 min and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. P0096-100 mL) for 20 min. Following incubation with QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. P0260) for 1 h, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies as follows: anti-H3K27me3 antibody (CST, Massachusetts, USA; cat. no. 5499S), anti-H3K9ac antibody (CST, Massachusetts, USA; cat. no. 9649S), anti-CX43 antibody (CST, Massachusetts, USA; cat. no. 3512S), anti-IL17D antibody (Bioss, Beijing, China; cat. no. bs-2612R), and anti-CD93 antibody (Santa Cruz, Dallas, USA; cat. no. sc-365172) diluted 1:100 at 4 °C. The blastocysts, oocytes, and COCs were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. A0423) or Cy3-conjugated affiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, Chicago, USA; cat. no. SA00009-1) diluted 1:200 in the dark for 1 h. Chromosomes were stained with DAPI staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. C1006) for 15 min in the dark. The fluorescence was observed using epifluorescence microscope (Ti2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany); fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Analysis of transzonal projections (TZPs)

For analysis of TZPs, COCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. P0099-100 mL) for 30 min and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. P0096-100 mL) for 20 min. COCs were stained with 100 nM TRITC Phalloidin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China; cat. no. 40734ES75) for 15 min to visualize the TZPs in the dark. Chromosomes were stained with DAPI staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; cat. no. C1006) for 15 min in the dark. Finally, images of equatorial cross sections of oocytes were acquired with a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Glutathione (GSH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurements

The total intracellular GSH level was determined using the CellTracker™ Blue CMF2HC dye (4-chloromethyl-6,8-difluoro-7-hydroxycoumarin; Invitrogen, USA). This cell-permeant dye is nonfluorescent until it reacts with intracellular thiols, primarily GSH, to produce a blue-fluorescent conjugate. Briefly, a group of oocytes were washed in PBS–PVA and then incubated in M2 medium containing 10 μM CMF2HC dye for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, the oocytes were washed thoroughly three times in fresh PBS–PVA to remove excess dye, and the fluorescence was observed using an epifluorescence microscope.

Similarly, the intracellular ROS level was detected using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA, Yesen, China). This cell-permeant probe is de-esterified intracellularly and then oxidized by a broad spectrum of ROS to form the highly green-fluorescent product DCF. A separate group of oocytes were incubated in M2 medium supplemented with 10 μM H2DCFDA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Following incubation, the oocytes were washed three times in PBS–PVA, and the fluorescence was observed using an epifluorescence microscope.

For both assays, a minimum of 10–20 oocytes per experimental group were analyzed. The fluorescence intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) and are presented as mean fluorescence intensity per oocyte or as relative values normalized to the control group.

Quantitative real-time PCR

As previously reported by us [24], total RNA was performed using HiPure Total RNA Nano Kit (Magen, Guangdong, China; cat. no. R4125-02) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed with SYBR Green using an ABI StepOnePlus Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). For controls: 18S was used as an internal control in oocyte samples and β-actin was used as an internal control in cumulus cells samples. The primer sequences are showed in Supplementary Table S1.

Molecular docking prediction

The amino acid sequences of the target proteins were obtained from the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). For proteins with unknown 3D structures, we used the Swiss-model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) for 3D modeling of the target proteins, and only the predicted structures with more than 30% sequence identity were used for further analysis. When selecting the model, large GMAE values and QMEAN values close to 0 were selected as much as possible. Subsequently, ZDOCK (https://zdock.umassmed.edu/) was used for molecular docking prediction, and the docking model was visualized in PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for Windows. Statistical comparisons were made with analysis of variance (ANOVA), Student’s t-tests, and chi-squared tests when appropriate. The data were considered statistically significant when the P-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Proteomic profiling of pMFF and pIFF

To screen potentially important FF proteins that regulate porcine oocyte maturation, pMFF and pIFF were collected for TMT-based proteomic analysis (Fig. 1A). In total, 2898 proteins were identified from 13,182 quantified peptides. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a high quantitative reproducibility among three biological replicates in the same group (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs; foldchange ≥ 1.5; P-value < 0.05; Supplementary Data 1) between pMFF and pIFF revealed that 173 and 607 DEPs were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, in pMFF compared with pIFF (Fig. 1C). To validate the TMT proteomic results, we performed a PRM proteomic analysis and found a high positive correlation between TMT and PRM proteomic data, indicating that the TMT results were reliable (Fig. 1D). The DEPs between pMFF and pIFF were highly enriched in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) processes involved in ribosomes, spliceosomes, DNA replication, cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions, and so on (Fig. 1E). To further investigate the biological processes occurring in the FF during oocyte maturation, the KEGG pathways of all identified proteins were evaluated by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The results showed that proteins in FF were mainly enriched in ligand–receptor interaction-related pathways, including neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, ECM–receptor interactions, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that many FF proteins participate in intercellular communication in the follicle during oocyte maturation. However, only the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway was significantly enriched in both KEGG and GSEA analyses, suggesting that this pathway plays a key role in oocyte maturation. GSEA results showed that proteins involved in the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway are the leading-edge subset of upregulated proteins in pMFF (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these data not only characterize the proteomic dynamics of the FF but also highlight the importance of intercellular crosstalk, which particularly dependents on cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, in follicles during in vivo pig oocyte maturation.

IL17D is a potentially important regulator of oocyte maturation

The above data implies that TMT-identified DEPs enriched in the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway play a critical role in oocyte maturation. These FF proteins included eight upregulated and two downregulated DEPs in pMFF (Fig. 2A). We preferentially focused on the upregulated DEPs in pMFF, because it is feasible to validate the effects of these upregulated FF proteins on oocyte maturation by supplementation of them to oocyte IVM medium. Among the eight upregulated DEPs associated with the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway, SDF1 firstly attracted our notice as we have previously found that it significantly improves pig oocyte maturation and subsequent embryo development [24]. By analyzing our RNA sequencing data of cumulus cells (CCs) from SDF1-treated and control cumulus cells–oocyte complexes (COCs), we found that SDF1 enhances the maturation rate and quality of porcine oocytes, probably mainly by activating the IL17 signaling pathway in CCs, as IL17 signaling was the top one enriched KEGG pathway (Fig. 2B). All the DEGs involved in this pathway were upregulated in the SDF1 treatment group (Fig. 2C), and four DEGs (IL6, IL8, FOS, and JUN) in this pathway have the highest interaction with other DEGs (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, one of the major upstream activators of IL17 signaling, IL17D, was also enriched in the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathway, and it was upregulated in FF during in vivo oocyte maturation (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S1). Therefore, we speculated that the IL17D protein in FF participates in the regulation of oocyte maturation by modulating the IL17 signaling pathway. This hypothesis was further supported by analyzing the NCBI gene database-derived IL17D expression profiles in different tissues of pig, mouse, and human, which indicated that IL17D is highly expressed in the ovary of the three analyzed species (Fig. 2E–G). Furthermore, our ELISA results confirmed that IL17D protein level increases in FF during in vivo pig oocyte maturation, but decreases in culture medium during in vitro oocyte maturation (Fig. 2H).

Supplementation of IL17D during IVM enhances pig oocyte maturation and subsequent in vitro and in vivo full-term embryo development

To investigate whether IL17D can improve porcine oocyte maturation, immature pig COCs were cultured with in vitro maturation (IVM) media supplemented with 0 ng/mL, 5 ng/mL, 50 ng/mL, 100 ng/mL, and 200 ng/mL of IL17D protein. Compared with the control (0 ng/mL) group, 50 ng/mL of IL17D treatment significantly increased, while 5 ng/mL and 100 ng/mL of IL17D supplementation tended to increase, the oocyte maturation rate (Table 1). To further examine whether IL17D can enhance oocyte maturation quality, besides increasing oocyte maturation rate, the influence of IL17D addition during oocyte IVM on subsequent embryos development was investigated. Only somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) and parthenogenetic activation (PA) embryos were used for examination, because porcine in vitro fertilization (IVF) embryos usually have a high incidence of polyspermy [27, 28]. Both the SCNT and PA embryos produced from 50 ng/mL of IL17D-treated oocytes developed to the blastocyst stage more efficiently than the nontreated group (Table 2). Moreover, the in vivo full-term developmental efficiency of SCNT embryos generated from 50 ng/mL of IL17D-incubated oocytes increased approximately by about one-fold (Table 3). Together, these results indicate that IL17D plays a key role in oocyte maturation.

Supplementation of IL17D during oocyte IVM improves subsequent SCNT embryo development by modulating lipid metabolism and histone modification

To elucidate the mechanisms by which supplementation of IL17D during oocyte IVM enhances subsequent development of SCNT embryos, gene expression patterns of four-cell stage SCNT embryos cloned from 0 ng/mL and 50 ng/mL of IL17D-treated oocytes were analyzed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Fig. 3A). PCA suggested that two groups of SCNT embryos have a different gene expression profile (Fig. 3B). Compared with the control SCNT embryos (the 0 ng/mL group), expressions of 452 and 453 genes were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, in the 50 ng/mL group of embryos (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Data 2). According to the GO biological process and KEGG analysis, the DEGs between two groups of SCNT embryos were mainly annotated to metabolism-related pathways, many of which were involved in lipid metabolism, including cholesterol metabolism, the PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism (Fig. 3D and E). These lipid metabolism processes have been shown to play important roles in the reprogramming capacity of embryos [29–32]. Since histone methylation and acetylation levels reflect the reprogramming ability of SCNT embryos [33], the levels of H3K27me3 and H3K9ac in two groups of SCNT embryos were measured by immunofluorescence staining at the blastocyst stage to compare their reprogramming capacities. Supplementation with IL17D during oocyte IVM reduced the H3K27me3 level (Fig. 3F and H), but increased H3K9ac abundance (Fig. 3G and I) in subsequently generated SCNT embryos. These results indicate that IL17D treatment during oocyte IVM enhances subsequent SCNT embryos via regulating lipid metabolism and histone modification.

IL17D activates meiotic maturation-related pathways to enhance oocyte maturation

To investigate how IL17D regulates the maturation quality of oocytes, we collected matured oocytes from IL17D-treated and control COCs for RNA-seq (Fig. 4A). Two groups of oocytes displayed a different transcriptome profile, as revealed by PCA clustering (Fig. 4B). A total of 1075 DEGs were identified (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Data 3), and these genes were enriched in many pathways in biological processes of GO (Fig. 4D) and KEGG databases (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, four KEGG pathways that affect oocyte maturation, including ovarian steroidogeneses [34], ECM–receptor interactions [35], gap junctions [36], and focal adhesions [37], were enriched (Fig. 4E). The majority of DEGs in these four pathways were upregulated in the IL17D supplementation group (Fig. 4F), suggesting that IL17D activates these pathways to promote oocyte maturation. We also found that the gap junction pathway is at the center of the interaction network of enriched KEGG pathways (Fig. 4G), which suggests that IL17D-mediated activation of the gap junction pathway may play a key role in oocyte maturation.

IL17D enhances oocyte maturation by promoting gap junction protein CX43 expression and TZP formation

Gap junctions mainly rely on the CX43 protein to form their structural basis in porcine COCs. Our RNA-seq data revealed that IL17D increased CX43 mRNA levels in matured oocytes by more than 30-fold (Fig. 5A). This result was further confirmed by the qPCR result (Fig. 5B). The transcriptome data also showed that the mRNA levels of the AP1 family members (FOS and JUN), which are the transcription factors of CX43 [38], also were increased in IL17D-treated oocytes (Fig. 5C). These results imply that IL17D enhances CX43 expression via activating its transcription factors, the AP1 family members, which are the downstream factors in the IL17 signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, CX43 protein levels in oocytes treated with IL17D were higher than those in the control group at 11 and 44 h post-incubation (Fig. 5D and E). Interestingly, the transcript level of CX43 in cumulus cells was also upregulated by IL17D (Fig. 5F), indicating that IL17D improves the formation of gap junction channels between the oocyte and cumulus cells.

CX43 proteins are located at the tips of transzonal projections (TZPs), which are the structural basis of intercellular communication between the oocytes and cumulus cells. To examine whether IL17D also promote TZP formation in treated COCs, the TZP number was compared between two groups of oocytes at different time points. IL17D-treated oocytes have a higher number of TZPs than control oocytes at 11 and 44 h after treatment (Fig. 5F and G). The enhanced gap junction and TZP formation in IL17D-treated oocytes resulted in elevation of the intraoocyte level of cAMP, which can be moved from cumulus cells to the oocyte via gap junctions to promote oocyte maturation [9, 39], at 22 h and 44 h after IL17D addition (Fig. 5H). IL17D treatment also increased the level of glutathione (GSH), which also is transported from cumulus cells to the oocyte via gap junctions to regulate oocyte maturation [40], in matured oocytes (Fig. 5I and J). The IL17D-mediated elevation of GSH levels significantly suppressed reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in treated oocytes (Fig. 5K and L). These results suggest that IL17D enhances bidirectional communication of substances between the oocytes and cumulus cells via promoting gap junction formation, which not only facilitates the exchange of signaling molecules but also critically maintains the oocyte’s redox homeostasis by increasing GSH levels.

IL17D activates the IL17 signaling pathway in cumulus cells to promote oocyte maturation

To further explore how IL17D regulates oocyte maturation, we collected mature-stage cumulus cells from IL17D-treated and control COCs for RNA-sequencing (Fig. 6A–C). The DEGs (Supplementary Data 4) identified by RNA-seq were mainly enriched in cytokine-related pathways, including response to cytokines, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions, viral protein interactions with cytokines and cytokine receptors, and the IL17 signaling pathway (Fig. 6D and E). Notably, the DEGs enriched in the IL17 signaling pathway were mostly upregulated in IL17D-treated cumulus cells (Fig. 6F), suggesting that IL17D regulates oocyte maturation via activating the IL17 signaling pathway in cumulus cells.

IL17D enhances communication between the oocytes and cumulus cells by increasing ligand and receptor expression

According to the IL17 signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S1), IL17 family members can activate the expression of transcription factor family AP1 to regulate transcription of a large group of cytokines, which include chemokines. In line with this, in treated cumulus cells, IL17D changed the expression of genes involved in response to cytokines, and interactions between cytokines and cytokine receptor pathways (Fig. 6D and E). These processes are fundamental for intercellular crosstalk, which is necessary for normal oocyte maturation [34, 41, 42]. To evaluate whether IL17D affects the intercellular communication of COCs, we constructed a ligand–receptor interaction dataset (Supplementary Data 5) according to previous reports [43, 44] and analyzed our transcriptome data of oocytes and cumulus cells. The analysis results indicated that the expression of most of the ligand and receptor genes were significantly upregulated in COCs after IL17D stimulation (Fig. 7A). These ligands and receptors form an autocrine and/or paracrine signaling network to mediate cell–cell communication in COCs (Fig. 7A). Notably, the transcript abundance of several factors that are beneficial for oocyte maturation, including CXCL12 (SDF1), NGF, FGF2, CTGF, and AREG [24, 45–47], was significantly increased in the IL17D group (Fig. 7B), suggesting that IL17D improves oocyte maturation by triggering expression of these intercellular signals (Supplementary Fig. S2).

IL17D improves porcine oocyte maturation and subsequent embryo development by binding to CD93

For a long time, the receptor of IL17D has been unknown, but a recent study showed that CD93 is a receptor of IL17D in mice [48]. In order to assess whether porcine IL17D can also bind to CD93, we first analyzed the binding energy between porcine IL17D and CD93 using a molecular docking prediction tool. The binding energy was calculated to be −31.2 kcal/mol, indicating a stable binding between IL17D and CD93 ( Fig. 8A; Supplementary Table S2). Moreover, the immunofluorescence results demonstrated that CD93 protein is expressed in porcine oocytes and cumulus cells (Fig. 8B). Metoclopramide (MCP) has been shown to effectively block CD93 signaling [49]. We then employed MCP as a CD93 inhibitor to investigate whether IL17D enhances oocyte maturation via activating CD93 signaling. No adverse effect was observed when MCP was solely added to oocyte IVM medium, but MCP abolished the positive effects of IL17D on oocyte maturation and subsequent embryonic development (Table 4; Supplementary Table S3). These results suggest that during pig oocyte IVM, CD93 is not activated when no IL17D is added to IVM medium, but it is required to mediate the beneficial effects of IL17D on oocyte maturation when IL17 is supplemented to IVM medium. On the basis of the major action mechanisms of IL17D in improving oocyte maturation, which were found in our earlier results in Fig. 5, we examined the influence of MCP on IL17D treatment-increased intracellular cAMP concentrations, CX43 expression, and TZP formation in oocytes. In line with our previous observations, IL17D supplementation increased oocyte cAMP level, CX43 protein abundance, and TZP counts, but these IL17D-induced beneficial effects were eliminated by MCP treatment (Fig. 8C–G). These results further confirm that IL17D improves oocyte maturation via binding the receptor CD93 to enhance oocyte maturation and subsequent embryo development.

IL17D expression pattern and function are conserved in porcine and murine COCs

The IL17D protein level is decreased in IVM medium during in vitro pig oocyte maturation (Fig. 2H), and addition of the IL17D receptor inhibitor MCP alone to IVM medium has no adverse effects on oocyte maturation (Table 4, Supplementary Table S2, and Fig. 8C–G). These findings suggest that the synthesis of IL17D protein is insufficient during in vitro pig oocyte maturation. To verify this speculation, the IL17D protein level was examined by immunofluorescence in in vivo- and in vitro-derived mature- and immature-stage pig COCs. The results indicated that IL17D protein abundance remains stable in porcine oocytes and cumulus cells of in vivo-derived COCs, whereas it decreases in oocytes as well as cumulus cells of in vitro-derived COCs (Fig. 9). Furthermore, in mice, the IL17D protein level also is stable in COCs during in vivo oocyte maturation, but declines in cumulus cells and tend to decline in oocytes during in vitro oocyte maturation (Fig. 10). These results imply that, in both pigs and mice, inadequate production of IL17D protein in in vitro matured COCs leads to a decrease in IL17D protein level in IVM medium, which may negatively affect in vitro oocyte maturation, while stable synthesis of IL17D protein in in vivo matured COCs results in an elevation of IL17D protein concentration in FF, which may promote in vivo oocyte maturation. To examine whether IL17D also plays positive roles in mouse oocyte developmental potential acquisition, IL17D protein was added to the IVM medium of mouse oocytes. The results showed that IL17D significantly increased the maturation rate of mouse oocytes and the cleavage rate of subsequent IVF embryos, although it has no significant effect on IVF embryo development at the four-cell and blastocyst stages (Tables 5 and 6). These data indicate that IL17D is a conserved regulator of oocyte maturation across mammalian species.

Effects of other IL17 family members on pig oocyte maturation

The above results have demonstrated that IL17D-mediated activation of the IL17 signaling pathway enhances oocyte maturation. However, the IL17 signaling pathway is known to be regulated by six IL17 family members including IL17A, IL17B, IL17C, IL17D, IL17E, and IL17F. These IL17 members, according to the KEGG database, can be divided into at least five subgroups on the basis of their receptors and downstream signal transduction (Supplementary Fig. S1). To test whether other IL17 family members, like IL17D, could also promote oocyte maturation, IL17A, IL17F, IL17B, IL17C, and IL17E were respectively supplemented during pig oocyte IVM. Although IL17A and IL17F had no effect on the maturation rate of oocytes (Supplementary Table S4), the addition of 50 ng/mL of IL17A during oocyte IVM tended to increase the blastocyst rate of SCNT embryos (35.56% versus 18.27%, P = 0.065) and PA embryos (45.72% versus 35.59%, P = 0.052), while supplementation of 50 ng/mL of IL17F during oocyte IVM significantly increased blastocyst formation of SCNT embryos (29.99% versus 21.35%, P < 0.05), and tended to improve the blastocyst rate of PA embryos (Supplementary Table S5). Nevertheless, IL17B, IL17C, and IL17E did not affect oocyte maturation rate (Supplementary Table S6) and subsequent embryo development (Supplementary Table S7). These results not only further confirm the important roles of the IL17 signaling pathway in oocyte maturation but also suggest that IL17A, IL17D, and IL17F belong to the same IL17 subgroup, and hence play a similar role in enhancing oocyte maturation, yet other IL17 subfamily members have no effect on oocyte maturation.

Discussion

In the present study, we have characterized the global protein profile of FF during pig oocyte maturation. Functional analysis of identified FF proteins reveals that many of them are involved in pathways related to ligand–receptor interactions, including neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, ECM–receptor interactions, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 1). This suggests that FF contains abundant proteins mediating cellular communication, as binding of ligands to receptors is a major way mediating intercellular crosstalk [50, 51]. We also found that the levels of at least eight cytokines were upregulated in FF during oocyte maturation. Several cytokines have been found to participate in follicle development, oocyte maturation, and ovulation [52, 53]. There is increasing evidence showing that ovarian follicles are the site of inflammatory responses during oocyte maturation [54, 55], and cytokines are involved in inflammatory response. Therefore, the upregulated cytokines in FF, as IL17D was demonstrated in this study and SDF1 was shown in our previous study, may also play important roles in oocyte maturation.

Our transcriptome results show that the IL17 signaling pathway is activated in the cumulus cells of pig COCs treated with SDF1 or IL17D (Figs. 2 and 6), both of which enhance oocyte maturation and subsequent embryo development [24]. The IL17 signaling pathway also is reported to be involved in ovarian follicle growth, oocyte maturation quality, and early embryo development in mice [56]. Moreover, the IL17 signaling pathway contains a downstream transcription factor family called AP1 (Supplementary Fig. S1), whose expression is significantly increased in periovulatory granulosa cells, and this upregulation of AP1 is necessary for the rise of prostaglandins in mature human follicles [57]. Periovulatory elevation of AP1 expression also is critical for metabolic changes and cholesterol synthesis in granulosa cells of mature human follicles [58]. A knockout study indicates that ovarian expression of AP1 is essential for successful ovulation in mice [59]. All these findings, together with the results from the present study, indicate that the IL17 signaling pathway plays an important role in oocyte developmental competence acquisition during the maturation process. Nevertheless, no fertility defects were observed in mice with null mutations of the IL17D gene or its receptor gene, CD93 [48, 60, 61]. This unexpected outcome probably could be explained by the fact that another cytokine found in the FF by our proteomic analysis, SDF1, can also activate the IL17 signaling pathway in COCs during maturation, and hence compensate for the function of IL17D.

In the mammalian antral follicles, CCs surround and connect to the oocyte via TZPs, forming the COCs [62]. TZP tips of CCs establish major substance delivery channels with the oocyte membrane via gap junctions [41]. These channels allow various molecules, such as cAMP, amino acid, and RNA, to be transferred between CCs and the oocyte, thus regulating oocyte maturation [42, 63]. Active bidirectional communication via gap junctions between the oocyte and CCs are necessary for oocyte maturation and acquisition of developmental competence [41, 42]. Gap junctions and TZPs are predominantly composed of CX43, whose expression is regulated by the downstream transcription factor AP1 in the IL17 signaling pathway [38, 64]. Notably, downregulation of CX43 expression during oocyte maturation disrupts gap junction channels and TZP-mediated molecule transportation, causing a decrease of intra-oocyte cAMP and GSH levels [40, 65]. The decrease of cAMP level accelerates oocyte nuclear maturation, resulting in asynchronous maturation of the cytoplasm and nucleus and thereby compromising oocyte maturation quality [39]. Oocyte cytoplasmic maturation encompasses not only the redistribution of organelles and the dynamics of the cytoskeleton but also the critical process of molecular maturation, during which maternal mRNAs are transcribed, stored in a stable but inactive form, and regulated for timely translational activation [3, 66, 67]. However, following the isolation of COCs from the follicular environment, the rapid decline in intra-oocyte cAMP levels leads to the accelerated consumption of maturation-promoting factor [36]. This triggers the spontaneous resumption of meiosis. Consequently, the aforementioned cytoplasmic processes, including organelle redistribution and the establishment of maternal mRNA storage and regulatory mechanisms, fail to complete fully, resulting in incomplete cytoplasmic maturation. This asynchrony between precocious nuclear maturation and insufficient cytoplasmic preparation is a primary cause of diminished oocyte developmental competence following IVM. In addition, GSH synthesized by cumulus cells can be directly delivered to the oocyte via gap junctions. This GSH transfer is crucial for the oocyte’s capacity to scavenge free radicals and for ensuring subsequent normal embryo development [40, 68]. Our data demonstrate that IL17D increases the formation of gap junction by promoting CX43 expression via activation of the IL17 signaling. Therefore, IL17D may ameliorate oocyte nuclear maturation via the IL17D–AP1–CX43 axis. This functional axis promotes synchronous nucleocytoplasmic maturation and enhances the antioxidant capacity of oocytes by inducing structural remodeling of gap junction, which enhance communication between cumulus cells and oocytes.

It is widely accepted that follicle development is governed by the oocyte. Through timely and orderly secretion of signaling factors, the oocyte finely regulates the metabolism, proliferation, and differentiation of CCs and other follicular somatic cells to intermediate the intercellular crosstalk in COCs [42, 69]. Similarly, receiving signals from the follicular somatic cells is also important for the normal growth of the oocyte [34, 41, 42]. IL17 family members activate AP1 to regulate expression of a large group of cytokines and chemokines (Supplementary Fig. S1), which play an important role in cell-to-cell communication [34, 41, 42]. This is consistent with the downstream signaling pathways of IL17D found in this study (Supplementary Fig. S2). In fact, our RNA-seq data of cumulus cells and oocytes indicates that IL17D treatment promotes expression of many ligands and receptors, which participate in the intercellular crosstalk in COCs. Therefore, IL17D not only enhances gap junction-mediated substance exchange but also promotes ligand–receptor interaction-directed signaling transduction in COCs, suggesting that IL17D and the IL17 signaling pathway are important for bidirectional communication between cumulus cells and the oocytes during maturation.

Supplementation of IL17D during oocyte IVM changes subsequent SCNT embryos’ gene expression patterns, and the affected genes are mainly involved in lipid metabolism-related pathways including cholesterol metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid biosynthesis, and sphingolipid metabolism. All these metabolic pathways have been shown to play critical roles in early embryo development [29–32]. Lipid metabolism not only provides energy for early embryonic development but also generates acetyl-CoA [70], which is vital for histone modification, especially for histone acetylation in early embryos [71, 72]. In line with this, our results show that supplementation of IL17D during oocyte IVM enhances histone acetylation and reduces histone methylation in SCNT embryos. The alternation of lipid metabolism in SCNT embryos produced from IL17D-treated oocytes may be related to the potential actions of IL17D on oocyte lipid metabolism, as a downstream factor in the IL17 signaling pathway, AP1, can promote cholesterol synthesis in mature oocytes [58], and induction of IL17 signaling can activate the PPAR signaling pathway [73], which is closely related to lipid metabolism [74, 75].

We found that IL17D is highly expressed in the ovary of all three analyzed species, including humans, mice, and pigs. Furthermore, during in vivo or in vitro oocyte maturation, the expression pattern and function of IL17D in COCs are similar between pigs and mice. These results suggest a conserved role of IL17D in mammalian oocyte maturation. While IL17 family receptors typically form homodimers or heterodimers to transduce upstream IL17 family member signals [76, 77], it has been demonstrated that IL17D protein did not show detectable binding to any homodimers or heterodimers of the canonical IL17 receptor family members [48]. Although the present study and another study [48] identify CD93 as a receptor mediating IL17D downstream signaling, the possibility remains that CD93 may form heterodimeric complexes with other IL17 receptor family members, and these heterodimeric receptors could be recognized and bound by IL17D in a cell type- or context-specific manner. Further studies using receptor knockdown or co-immunoprecipitation in COCs would help to clarify whether CD93 functions independently or in concert with other IL17 family receptors to mediate IL17D signaling. Although the addition of IL17D, IL17A, and IL17F during pig oocyte maturation enhances subsequent embryo development, IL17D supplementation significantly improves oocyte maturation rates, while IL17A and IL17F supplementation have no significant influence on oocyte maturation rate. The differential effects between IL17D and IL17A/IL17F on pig oocyte maturation rates may arise from their variations in receptor usage or downstream signaling specificity. IL17D exerts its function through binding to receptor CD93, whereas IL17A and IL17F typically signal via IL17RA/RC heterodimers (Supplementary Fig. S1), which may trigger distinct cellular responses. In addition, differences in activity or stability between IL17D protein and IL17A/IL17F proteins may also contribute to their differences in the effects on porcine oocyte maturation rates.

Conclusions

IL17D promotes porcine oocyte maturation and subsequent in vitro as well as in vivo embryo development by binding to receptor CD93 to activate the IL17 signaling pathway. IL17D supplementation during oocyte IVM enhances substance exchange via increasing gap junction protein CX43 expression and TZP formation, and improves intercellular communication by promoting ligand and receptor expression in COCs. Addition of IL17D to oocyte IVM medium promotes subsequent embryo development by modulating lipid metabolism as well as histone modification. This study discovered that IL17D is not only an immune regulator but also an oocyte maturation regulator. The findings from this study contribute to a better understanding of FF molecule-mediated regulation of oocyte maturation. In addition, the present study provides an approach to improve the efficiency of obtaining in vitro oocytes and embryos for assisted reproduction.
