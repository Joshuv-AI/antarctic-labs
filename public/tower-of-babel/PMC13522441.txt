Chiral polariton transport enabled by optical spin Hall effect in perovskite waveguides
Authors: Mateusz Kędziora, Andrzej Opala, Maciej Zaremba, Helgi Sigurðsson, Barbara Piętka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractControlling the spin degree of freedom of light at the microscale is crucial for advancing photonic information processing. Spin-polarized light propagation, combined with strong optical nonlinearities, unlocks new functionalities in compact photonic circuits and active spin-optronic devices. Lead halide perovskite exciton-polaritons uniquely combine room-temperature operation, pronounced nonlinearities, and versatile microstructuring, making them a powerful platform for spin-based photonic technologies. Here, we demonstrate polarized edge emission from polariton condensates in perovskite single crystals predesigned into a microwire, forming natural, DBR-free cavity. Above threshold, we observe a distinct waveguiding optical spin Hall effect pattern in both real- and reciprocal-space emission, accompanied by pseudospin phase-locking arising from coherence between opposite edges. Beyond static polarization textures, we achieve spin-resolved polariton edge lasing with chirality exceeding 80% and spin-polarized signal propagation over tens of micrometers. These results establish CsPbBr3 waveguides as a promising easy-to-fabricate platform for on-chip spin-coded information transport and nonlinear spin-optoelectronics.Subject terms: Polaritons, Bose-Einstein condensates

Introduction

The development of optical materials and photonic structures that sustain spin-dependent phenomena in the nonlinear regime is a key driver of progress in spin-optoelectronics. Recent efforts have focused on realizing optical spin Hall and valley Hall effects1–3, generating currents of pure spin-polarized light detected as circularly polarized (CPL) emission, and implementing spin-selective topological photonic states. Such functionalities are typically achieved by engineering periodic potentials, either through nanostructuring of the substrate4 or direct patterning of the light-emitting medium5.

A promising platform for realizing spin-polarized states with large optical nonlinear responses is provided by exciton-polaritons (henceforth polaritons), which are the quantum superposition of excitons and photons in confined systems such as microcavities6, waveguides7 and metasurfaces8. They exhibit pronounced optical nonlinearities and susceptibility towards electric and magnetic fields coming from their exciton component, and rapid timescales and small effective mass from their photonic component9. They can undergo a power-driven non-equilibrium phase transition into a macroscopic coherent state referred to as a polariton condensate at elevated temperatures that emits coherent light10. In confined photonic systems, such as planar cavities formed from distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs), the optical spin Hall effect (OSHE)6,11,12 emerges as a key phenomenon in polariton spin transport13,14 and topological polaritonics15. The OSHE for polaritons arises from splitting between the in-plane transverse-electric and transverse-magnetic (TE-TM) photon modes, resulting in effective spin-orbit coupling (SOC) of polaritons and separation of coherent spin currents tunable through optical means. OSHE has been demonstrated in GaAs-based microcavities6, organic crystals16, liquid crystal cavities1,17, organic microbelts18, and recently in perovskite-filled dielectric cavities12, to name a few.

Owing to their spatial design flexibility, facile synthesis, scalability, and excellent lasing performance19, lead-halide perovskites are well established as one of the most important semiconductor platforms in modern photonics, enabling strong polariton nonlinearities and long-range transport at room temperature20. Demonstrating spin phenomena such as the OSHE and strong circularly polarized light (CPL) emission in perovskites has required substantial effort, including precise crystal or layer microstructuring21, fabrication of metasurfaces followed by spin-coating of perovskite layers4, and incorporation of birefringent liquid crystal to tailor the polarization state of the emitted light22. Perovskite microcavities have already proven effective for polarization control23 and linearly polarized lasing24, owing to their intrinsic in-plane optical birefringence, and have even enabled the realization of a spin-NOT logic gate12. These advances position perovskites as a highly versatile platform for integrating spin-resolved photonic functionalities with nonlinear polariton physics under ambient conditions.

In this work, we demonstrate spontaneous formation of highly polarized spatial emission patterns and polariton spin currents at room temperature in inorganic perovskite crystal CsPbBr3 waveguides without any need for additional complex structurization or birefringent dopants. Remarkably, due to the high-quality waveguide facets, which form a natural optical microcavity, the condensation occurs without external Bragg mirrors. We show simultaneous polariton condensation in two energy-degenerate modes of opposite spatial parity and orthogonal polarization, shown schematically in Fig. 1a. The geometry of the natural cavity supports edge lasing originating from a polariton condensate above the light cone. In the far field, the emission from the two opposite edges exhibits interference fringes, evidencing the coherence of the emitted light. Owing to its interferometric nature, the observed emission angle cannot be associated with a unique wavevector.

In real space, we observe a characteristic linear polarization pattern associated with dominant TE lasing (Fig. 1b), accompanied by a pronounced OSHE pattern that emerges after the condensation threshold shown schematically in Fig. 1c. This represents a form of OSHE in a nonequilibrium condensate of polaritons in the waveguide geometry. It is worth mentioning that conventional OSHE is reduced in a planar cavity for CsPbBr3 at room temperature due to linear birefringence and therefore splitting of the H and V modes at k = 0 23,24, and only possible in cubic perovskites such as FAPbBr312. Beyond static polarization textures, we further demonstrate that asymmetric nonresonant pumping can drive almost pure chiral condensation (above 80% CPL) with opposite circular polarizations at the two waveguide edges.

Results and discussion

We used monocrystalline microwires made of cesium lead bromide to demonstrate polarization patterns in the edge-leaking condensate. The crystals were grown using microfluidically-assisted pseudomorphosis25. Despite the small Q factor (in the range of a few hundreds) of such a natural microcavity formed between two opposite edges of the crystal, exciton polaritons can still form even without the use of external DBRs, thanks to the very large exciton oscillator strength in perovskites25,26. A photo taken with a Nomarski-contrast microscope shows an example crystal in Fig. 2a. Crystallized from solution at room temperature, CsPbBr3 has an orthorhombic crystallographic structure in which the perpendicular axes have different lengths, resulting in birefringence of the material with ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices24,27. Elongated perovskite crystal wires grown in the order of micrometers in length act as polariton ridge waveguides, but since the guided modes are totally internally reflected, they cannot be probed efficiently through standard far-field spectroscopy measurements. However, the polariton dispersion can be measured using a grating on the surface of a waveguide to allow light to couple in and out of the waveguide through higher diffraction orders (Bragg scattering)26.

To probe the photoluminescence of the perovskite, we use a nonresonant, 435 nm V-polarized, picosecond pulsed laser with a Gaussian beam profile of 6 μm diameter focused on the center of the microwire. Spatial and polarization resolved spectra across the microwire for a power corresponding to 0.2 threshold power (Pth is in the range of 200 μJ/cm2) are shown in Fig. 2b. Here, vertical (V) and horizontal (H) emission refers to polarization across (y) and along (x) the wire.

The emission originates from two distinct regions: excitonic emission at 2.35 eV from the wire center, and red-shifted polariton emission from the wire edges. Cross sections taken from the wire center (Fig. 2c, upper panel) and edges (Fig. 2c, middle panel) show that exciton emission in orthogonal linear polarizations differs only in intensity, with no spectral shift. In contrast, the polariton emission exhibits similar overall intensity for both polarizations, but distinct spectral profiles due to orthogonal confinement (i.e., across the wire), with Fabry-Perot peaks slightly more closely spaced in energy for horizontal polarization corresponding to the higher wavenumber of TE polaritons for a given energy. It is worth noting that for excitation powers far below threshold, the exciton-related emission directly under the excitation spot is higher than the polariton one at the edges. Above the condensation threshold, we can observe a significant increase in emission intensity only at the edges, which is shown in Fig. 2c for 1.2 Pth power (bottom panel).

Above threshold, condensation of polaritons takes place into two standing wave modes across the microwire with coherent emission from the opposite-facing facets25. We note that our far-field measurements allow us to probe the light scattered out from the edges and not the whole standing wave within. This edge-lasing behavior shows marked polaritonic condensate features with large blueshift as a function of pump intensity, a linewidth collapse and slow broadening with power, a nonlinear input-ouput curve, and redistribution of modal intensities due to thermal-assisted scattering (see SI for details). The macroscopic coherence of the condensate is evidenced in the sharp fringe contrast when interfering the emission between the opposite facets (discussed in more detail in25 and below). In our geometry, the horizontal polarization (TE) is found to be several times stronger than the vertical (TM) [see Fig. 2c, bottom panel], implying that the TE branch possesses lower losses.

Interestingly, the pair of TE and TM polarized condensate spectral peaks in Fig. 2c coincide energetically despite the different curvatures of the TE and TM dispersion relations. The reason for this energy-degenerate condensation is due to the strong spin-anisotropic polariton-polariton interactions28,29, which give rise to parametric scattering pathways that invert the linear polarization of the polariton. One mode, H-polarized, has a lower threshold and lases (condenses) first and subsequently feeds particles into the neighboring V-polarized mode at the same energy but different wavenumber. Energy-degenerate parametric scattering was recently reported as a mechanism of a supersolid phase transition in III-V semiconductor waveguides30, but is more commonly known to invert the emission linear polarization in the parametric oscillator regime in anisotropic planar cavities31 and quasi-one-dimensional microwires32. More recently, energy-degenerate parametric scattering of polaritons showing polarization inversion was reported in microcavities with perovskite and liquid crystal under nonresonant pumping33.

To reconstruct experimental data, we decomposed the dielectric function34 into a Lorentzian (excitonic) contribution and a non-resonant background: ε(E) = εex(E) + εbg(E). The photonic TE/TM modes were then computed using the Fourier Modal Method S4 package35 (rigorous coupled wave analysis), taking only the non-resonant part of the dielectric function (i.e., without the exciton). Note that, because of the large contrast in the wire’s dimensions, d = 600 nm in thickness (height), w = 6 μm in width, and 6 mm in length, we can treat its guided modes as quasi-TE and TM modes in a rectangular waveguide, instead of belonging to an infinite slab geometry. We then fitted the maxima in the calculated reflectance map with a photon dispersion relation ETE/TM(k)=E0TE/TM+α1TE/TMk+α2TE/TMk2 within the wavevector range of interest, 17–24 μm−1. The results are plotted as solid lines in Fig. 2d (see SI for more details). The quadratic term is required because the simulated spectra show a clear deviation from a purely linear dependence of E(k) over this wavevector range. Physically, this term captures the weak bending of the photonic modes caused by an increase of the effective refractive index due to the non-resonant background part of the dielectric function (see also section S3 in SI). In the SI, we additionally calculate the guided photonic TE and TM mode solutions to Maxwell’s equations assuming a slab perovskite geometry and constant refractive index of the perovskite nc = 2.35, which corresponds to CsPbBr3 operating around 560 nm, sufficiently far away from excitonic resonance34.

Figure 3a shows the Stokes parameters of the edge-emitted light measured above the condensation threshold. The results evidence a strongly polarization anisotropic “flower petal” emission pattern coming from the wire’s edges. The phenomena can be understood in terms of directionally-dependent precession of the polariton condensate macroscopic pseudospin. The pseudospin is defined S=Ψ†σ^Ψ, where σ^ is the Pauli matrix vector and Ψ(r)=[ψ+(r),ψ−(r)]T is a spinor order parameter describing the polariton condensate. Namely, ψ± polariton components correspond to spin-up and spin-down excitons along the out-of-plane direction of the microwire, which are explicitly coupled to σ± circularly polarized photons. The Stokes vector, therefore, gives a direct measure of the polariton condensate spin dynamics through emitted photons. To demonstrate coherence in the polariton emission across the microwire, we angularly resolved the emission spectra. Interference between coherent emission from the two edges of the microwire gives rise to a distinct fringe pattern in the far field (reciprocal space), with the fringe spacing determined by the crystal wire width. The clear contrast between the fringes evidences that light emitted from the two edges is coherent and phase-locked. We note that wider microwires can enable multi-wavelength emission (condensation into a ladder of modes), which blurs the intensity of OSHE (see SI for the details), as well as wavelength switching driven by mode competition25.

Figure 4 a shows the spectra measured at a pump power of 1.2 Pth, resolved into its six principal polarizations, for a relatively narrow perovskite microwire of width 6 μm within a small range of angles for greater clarity. Figure 4b, c show the corresponding S1 and S3 Stokes parameters in a wider range of angles. As discussed in Fig. 2c, the condensate can be divided between two neighboring standing waves across the wire resulting in two spectral peaks seen as two lines in Fig. 4a. An odd parity standing wave corresponds to a far field emission with minimum intensity at normal incidence (emission from wire edges is anti-phase), and an even parity standing wave as one with a maximum intensity at normal incidence (emission from wire edges is in-phase). The measurements in Fig. 4a reveal that the energy-degenerate H and V modes (S1) exhibit opposite parity: at 2.298 eV, the horizontal mode is odd while the vertical mode is even, whereas for the next lasing mode at 2.281 eV, the behavior is reversed. For diagonal polarizations (S2), the parity of the modes is the same, while for the circular polarization components (S3), no clear even or odd character is observed, as the intensity at θ = 0 shows neither a maximum nor a minimum. This asymmetric emission profile in the far field circular polarization is a consequence that circular polarization is a superposition of linearly polarized light ∣σ±=12(∣H∓i∣V) and therefore both even- and odd-parity mode profiles in ∣H and ∣V contribute to the far-field circular emission profile.

However, the presence of two modes blurs the far-field pattern in time-integrated measurements. To overcome this, we chose a slightly narrower wire of width w = 5.0 μm such that neighboring standing waves are spectrally separated more, and we obtain clear single-mode emission25. This results in a clearer polarization pattern in the far field, shown in Fig. 5a for all Stokes components. At small angles along the wire θx ≈ 0 the H-polarization is dominant, corresponding to strong condensate emission from a TE polarized standing wave. At higher angles ∣θx∣ > 0, polaritons undergo spin-precession within their lifetime, resulting in V-polarized emission as fringes with spacing Δk = π/w = 0.63 μm−1. Importantly, we observe the momentum-space separation of the polariton spins seen both in the diagonal (S2) and circular (S3) Stokes components forming the characteristic quadrupole typical of the OSHE in planar cavities6.

The OSHE effect, in combination with the structure of CsPbBr3 microwires, allows us to demonstrate spin-separated polariton transport in a waveguide geometry shown in Fig. 6. By employing asymmetric optical pumping, we realize an edge splitter that selectively favors polariton propagation in one direction, which separates into opposite spin currents, due to the OSHE, observable along the two waveguide edges. To ensure spatially homogeneous signal collection, asymmetric pumping was performed in a transmission configuration using a Gaussian beam elongated in the y-direction and excitation at a small (less than 5∘) angle. The circularly polarized emission distributions above the condensation threshold are shown in Fig. 6a. The intensity cross-section taken along the upper (UE) and lower (LE) edges (see Fig. 6b), shows a gradual decay over 20 μm and intensity beats due to the interference between the wire walls. While the total intensity is nearly identical on the two edges, the S3 parameter (Fig. 6c) has equal magnitude but opposite sign, reaching ∣S3∣ = 0.85, among the highest values reported for room-temperature polariton systems. Angle-resolved spectra in Fig. 6d, e show a pronounced asymmetry in the distribution of the two circular polarizations, in sharp contrast to the more balanced emission observed under symmetric pumping conditions illustrated in Fig. 4. We note that our experiments are performed over multiple realizations of the condensate (multiple pulses) and, due to spontaneous symmetry breaking upon condensation, one might expect equal amounts of polaritons, on average, flowing left and right. Under this condition, one retrieves the four-lobed S3 pattern as seen in Fig. 3. However, the clear kx > 0 (momentum along wire to the right) preference of the emission underlines the bias from the pump on the condensate. The scale of our separated spin-currents is similar to what has been reported in planar perovskite cavities1,12, but a clear advantage of our platform is that coherence emerges spontaneously (i.e., the laser imprints no information) with clear nonlinear behavior as a function of nonresonant pump power around condensation, as well as requires no vertical stacks as mirrors and instead strong-coupling forms under total-internal reflection in the waveguide25.

In summary, we have demonstrated strongly polarized spin currents due to pronounced OSHE in polariton condensates formed in CsPbBr3 crystals shaped into waveguides and operating at room temperature. By generating an extended polariton condensate, probed through edge lasing and polarization-resolved measurements, we revealed spin textures characteristic of the OSHE in both real and reciprocal space. These experimental observations are supported by theoretical calculations of polarization patterns for waveguide polaritons populating adjacent TE and TM modes at the same time.

Unlike earlier realizations, primarily in GaAs-based planar microcavities at cryogenic temperatures, our approach requires neither distributed Bragg reflectors nor complex heterostructures, enabling a far simpler and more scalable platform. The use of non-resonant pumping and non-equilibrium condensation places the effect firmly in the nonlinear regime, opening new opportunities for nonlinear spin-optronic functionalities. Taking profit on this capability and the waveguide pre-designed shape, we demonstrated edge-separated circularly polarized condensates with high chirality and long-range propagation. This combination of room-temperature operation, strong nonlinearity, and spin-selective transport paves the way for practical spin filters and spin splitters for future spin-based photonic and computing architectures.

Methods

Crystallization of perovskites

Perovskite micro-wires were produced by crystallization in spatially defined templates made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Uncured PDMS (Sylgard 184,9:1 base to curing agent) was placed on a gallium arsenide master template and heated for 10 min at 140 degrees Celsius to solidify. The finished template was then transferred to a glass slide that had been washed successively in soapy water, acetone, and isopropanol. The perovskite solution was prepared by mixing equal amounts of CsBr and PbBr2 (both from Alfa Aesar) in DMSO (Sigma Aldrich). Approximately 1 microliter of a 0.2 molar CsPbBr3 solution was added to the template, which was then sealed in a plastic container and stored in a refrigerator for 1 day, followed by 2 days at room temperature. After opening, slow crystallization occurs over approximately 1–2 days.

Optical measurements

The excitation laser was a Fluence Jasper X0 with a Fluence Harmony optical parametric amplifier and a collecting objective Nikon LU Plan ELWD 50 × magnification objective with a numerical aperture of 0.55. The signal was collected on a high-resolution Andor Marana CMOS camera or Princeton Instruments. SpectraPro HRS500 spectrometer with PIXIS CCD camera. Polarization optics (half-waveplate, quarter-waveplate and linear polarizer) calibrated to resolve six main polarizations were placed at the detection path.
