Salvage surgery for free flap failure in the maxillofacial region: a comprehensive review of principles and outcomes
Authors: Ziad Albash, Wajih Kashkash, Anton Sergeevich Sharapo, Zaderenko Igor Alexandrovich, Ushenko Ilya Aleksandrovich, Saltykov Egor Michaylovich, Hassan Alsayed Hachem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Free tissue transfer is the cornerstone of complex maxillofacial reconstruction, offering high success rates (90–95%) but carrying the risk of devastating flap failure in 1.5–5.8% of cases. When failure occurs, it necessitates urgent, strategic decision-making to mitigate morbidity and preserve oncological and functional outcomes.Objective:This review aims to synthesize current evidence on the principles, decision-making frameworks, and outcomes of salvage surgery following free-flap failure in the maxillofacial region.Methods/findings:A comprehensive narrative review was conducted, integrating data from clinical studies, case series, and systematic reviews published between 2000 and 2024. Key themes included the etiology and timing of flap failure (predominantly venous thrombosis within 48 hours), the critical distinction between flap salvage and salvage surgery, and a multifactorial decision matrix incorporating patient, defect, oncologic, and institutional factors. Reconstructive options range from second free flaps (success rates ~67–73%) and pedicled flaps (e.g., pectoralis major) to prosthetic rehabilitation. Immediate salvage is preferred in stable patients, whereas a staged “bridge and stage” approach using negative pressure wound therapy is safer in compromised settings. Functional outcomes vary by site and technique, but salvage surgery can significantly reduce pain and improve quality of life, with curative potential in select oncologic recurrences.Conclusion:Free-flap failure, while uncommon, demands a systematic, individualized, and multidisciplinary response. With timely intervention and an appropriate reconstructive strategy – whether microvascular, pedicled, or prosthetic – salvage surgery can successfully restore form, function, and hope, transforming a surgical crisis into a manageable clinical challenge.

Introduction

Free tissue transfer has become the gold standard for reconstructing complex defects in the maxillofacial region following oncologic resection, trauma, or other pathologies, as it optimizes both functional and esthetic outcomes[1–3]. The success rates of microvascular free flaps at high-volume institutions are impressively high, consistently reported at around 90–95%[4–6]. Despite these high success rates, the inevitable occurrence of flap failure remains a devastating and challenging complication for both the patient and the reconstructive surgeon[5,7]. When failure occurs, it leads to significant patient morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and profound psychosocial impact, while also creating a complex clinical scenario that demands urgent and strategic management[1,7].

The etiologies of free-flap failure are multifactorial, with the most common causes including anastomotic thrombosis, vasospasm, and technical factors. Vascular compromise, particularly venous thrombosis, is the predominant mechanism, with the majority of failures occurring in the early postoperative period. It is crucial to distinguish between the concept of “flap salvage” – the urgent exploration and revision of a compromised but potentially viable flap – and “salvage surgery,” which refers to the subsequent definitive reconstructive procedures undertaken after a flap has been declared non-viable.

The management of a patient after free-flap failure presents a significant reconstructive dilemma. The surgeon must navigate a complex decision matrix that weighs patient-specific factors (such as comorbidities and performance status), defect characteristics (including location, size, and tissue components), oncological status, and institutional resources and expertise. The reconstructive paradigm offers a spectrum of options, ranging from a second free tissue transfer and regional pedicled flaps to local tissue techniques, healing by secondary intention, and prosthetic rehabilitation. However, there is no universal consensus on the optimal management pathway, and the decision-making process is often guided by individual surgeon experience and institutional protocols rather than high-level evidence. This review synthesizes current literature to clarify principles, outcomes, and decision-making strategies in salvage surgery following maxillofacial free-flap failure. We confirm that we have reviewed the TITAN 2025 guidelines to ensure our manuscript is fully compliant[8].

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize current evidence on salvage surgery following free-flap failure in the maxillofacial region, with an emphasis on decision-making frameworks, reconstructive modalities, and clinical outcomes. Given the heterogeneity of published studies, a narrative synthesis was chosen over a systematic review or meta-analysis to provide a clinically oriented overview applicable to reconstructive surgeons.

Search strategy and information sources

A structured literature search was performed across three electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, covering the period from January 2000 to December 2024. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The primary search string included the following terms: “free flap failure,” “salvage surgery,” “microvascular reconstruction,” “maxillofacial reconstruction,” “head and neck reconstruction,” “free tissue transfer,” “reoperative microsurgery,” “flap thrombosis,” and “vessel-depleted neck.” The search was limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English. No restrictions were applied on study design to ensure comprehensive coverage of available evidence.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) reported on free-flap failure in the maxillofacial or head and neck region; (2) described salvage surgery, defined as a reconstructive procedure performed after a free flap had been declared nonviable; (3) provided original clinical data on success rates, complications, functional outcomes, or decision-making; (4) represented an original study design, including retrospective or prospective cohort studies, case-control studies, case series (with ≥3 patients), or propensity score analyses; and (5) were published in English between 2000 and 2024.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, or clinical overviews without original data; (2) studies focusing exclusively on flap salvage (re-exploration of a potentially viable flap without flap loss); (3) experimental, animal, or cadaveric studies; (4) articles limited to non-maxillofacial sites without comparative or methodological lessons applicable to the head and neck (except for studies providing technical insights, such as cephalic vein transposition techniques); (5) case reports with fewer than three patients; and (6) editorials, commentaries, or conference abstracts.

The search initially identified 1847 records after duplicate removal. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were retrieved for 124 potentially relevant studies. After full-text review and the exclusion of review articles, case reports, and studies without original data on salvage surgery, 10 original studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final narrative synthesis. These comprised three retrospective cohort studies, three retrospective case series, one nested case-control study, and one propensity score-matched analysis. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus or through consultation with a third author.

Data extraction and synthesis approach

Data were extracted into a standardized spreadsheet organized by the following domains: (1) study characteristics (first author, year, design, and sample size); (2) etiology and timing of flap failure; (3) salvage reconstructive modality (second free flap, pedicled flap, prosthetic rehabilitation, or conservative management); (4) success rates of salvage procedures; (5) complication profiles; and (6) functional or oncologic outcomes, where reported. Extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy.

Given the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, defect characteristics, and outcome definitions, a narrative synthesis was performed. Rather than pooling data statistically, we summarized findings thematically across the following prespecified domains: pathophysiology and clinical context, multifactorial decision-making, reconstructive modalities, temporal strategies, and clinical outcomes. Where quantitative ranges are reported (e.g., second free flap success rates of 67–73%), these represent the range of values reported across the included primary studies, not a pooled estimate. Key comparative data – such as success rates for second free flaps (73% from Novakovic et al[18]), complication profiles for salvage reconstruction (49.5–57% from Noothanapati et al[16]), and survival outcomes after salvage (5-year OS 31% from Zenga et al[17]) – were extracted directly from the included studies and are presented in Table 1.

Limitations of the synthesis approach

The narrative design precludes formal meta-analytic pooling or statistical testing of comparative efficacy. Furthermore, the included studies were predominantly retrospective, single-institutional series with inherent selection bias and variable definitions of “successful salvage.” Heterogeneity in follow-up duration and outcome reporting (e.g., flap survival vs functional recovery) limited direct comparisons. No risk-of-bias assessment was performed, consistent with the narrative review methodology, and this is acknowledged as a limitation in the Discussion section.

Pathophysiology and clinical context of free-flap failure

Incidence and temporal patterns of free-flap failure

Free tissue transfer is a highly reliable procedure, but failure, while uncommon, has significant consequences. Overall, the reported failure rate is generally low. Hsiung and colleagues reported a flap failure rate of 4.74% (26 out of 548 flaps) in a nested case-control study of head and neck cancer patients receiving antithrombotic prophylaxis[9]. A large systematic review by Shen and colleagues encompassing 44 031 free flaps found that 5.8% required a return to the operating room for vascular compromise; of these, the vast majority (3.7% of total flaps) were successfully salvaged, while 2.1% resulted in total flap loss[19]. The timing of compromise is critical for salvage. Evidence shows that the chances of successful salvage are highest within the first 48 hours. One major review concluded that 93.8% of explored flaps are successfully salvaged in the first 24 hours, but this rate drops sharply to 12.1% by day 3, with no successful salvages reported beyond day 4[19]. A more recent study confirmed this trend, showing salvage rates dropped from 38.5% for compromises detected within 24 hours to just 7.7% after 24 hours[20].

Etiological determinants of free-flap failure

The etiology of free-flap failure is multifactorial, with the predominant mechanisms centered on disruptions to the flap’s vascular supply[20]. Among these, thrombosis – particularly venous thrombosis – is the most frequently reported cause[9]. In studies where the specific etiology was documented, venous complications accounted for approximately 59.5% of failures, while arterial thrombosis represented a smaller but still significant proportion at 27.9%[11]. This preponderance of venous events is consistently observed in head and neck reconstruction, where venous congestion or thrombosis is the leading indication for return to the operating room for flap re-exploration[20].

Arterial vasospasm constitutes another critical etiological factor. Although less common than thrombosis, vasospasm can precipitate profound hypoperfusion, potentially culminating in irreversible ischemia and flap loss if not promptly addressed[21]. Vasospasm may be triggered by mechanical manipulation of vessels during surgery, intrinsic patient factors such as vascular reactivity, or exposure to certain topical agents used intraoperatively[21]. Management typically involves a multimodal strategy, including the application of topical antispasmodic agents such as papaverine or calcium channel blockers, mechanical dilation of the affected vessel, and, in some cases, systemic pharmacologic intervention[21].

Beyond vascular pathologies, technical and patient-related factors significantly influence flap viability[9,20]. Surgical technique plays a pivotal role; errors such as improper anastomosis, kinking or twisting of the pedicle, or excessive tension on the vascular pedicle can compromise perfusion[20]. Concurrently, patient-specific risk factors – including advanced age, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, a history of acute myocardial infarction, and hypercoagulable states – have been independently associated with increased rates of flap failure[9,20,22]. Additionally, the anatomical site of reconstruction affects outcomes, with osteocutaneous flaps in the head and neck and flaps used in trunk or visceral reconstructions demonstrating higher failure rates compared to other locations[9,23]. Together, these interrelated factors underscore the complexity of free flap physiology and the necessity of meticulous preoperative planning, intraoperative execution, and postoperative monitoring to mitigate the risk of failure[9,20,23].

Conceptual and clinical distinctions between flap salvage and salvage surgery

It is essential to recognize the fundamental distinction between “flap salvage” and “salvage surgery,” as these terms describe two conceptually and temporally distinct clinical scenarios in reconstructive microsurgery[24]. Flap salvage refers to the emergency re-exploration of a free flap that is showing signs of vascular compromise but remains potentially viable[10]. This intervention is typically performed within the first 24–48 hours postoperatively, during which time the window for successful intervention is narrow but real[10,24]. The objective of flap salvage is to identify and correct the underlying cause of perfusion failure – whether it be venous or arterial thrombosis, vasospasm, hematoma-induced compression, or technical issues at the anastomotic site – and thereby preserve the original flap[10,24]. Success in this context is highly time-dependent, with salvage rates declining precipitously beyond the second postoperative day[24].

In contrast, salvage surgery denotes a planned, definitive reconstructive procedure undertaken after a free flap has been declared non-viable and has been removed[18]. This scenario occurs when flap salvage is either unsuccessful or not attempted due to delayed recognition of failure, patient instability, or an irreversibly compromised flap[25]. Salvage surgery is not an emergency maneuver but a strategic response to a failed reconstruction, often requiring reevaluation of the defect, available recipient vessels, donor sites, and the patient’s overall condition. It may involve a second free tissue transfer, a pedicled flap, local tissue rearrangement, or even prosthetic rehabilitation[18]. Importantly, salvage surgery is frequently performed in a more challenging anatomical and physiological environment – characterized by scarring, prior radiation, infection, or vessel depletion – yet studies have demonstrated that outcomes can be comparable to those of primary reconstruction when appropriate patient selection and surgical planning are applied[11]. Clarifying this distinction is critical for communication among surgical teams, accurate interpretation of the literature, and informed decision-making with patients facing reconstructive failure[24].

A multifactorial decision-making framework for salvage surgery

The decision to attempt salvage of a failing free flap is not automatic; it requires a complex, multidisciplinary assessment, weighing the potential for success against the risks of a prolonged, potentially futile operation. This decision matrix integrates patient-specific, defect-related, oncological, and institutional factors to formulate a rational and patient-centered management plan.

Patient-specific determinants

The patient’s overall health and personal values are paramount in the salvage equation. Significant medical comorbidities, particularly cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and hypercoagulable states, negatively impact both microvascular flow and the patient’s ability to withstand a second prolonged anesthetic[26,27]. A patient’s performance status is a critical predictor of postoperative recovery and complication risk; a debilitated patient with a poor functional status may be a poorer candidate for a high-risk salvage procedure where the outcome is uncertain[26]. Furthermore, the patient’s own wishes and goals of care must be central to the decision; some patients may prioritize avoiding further prolonged surgery and accept a secondary reconstructive plan, while others may desire every possible attempt to save the initial flap, understanding the associated risks[28]. Advanced age alone is not an absolute contraindication to microvascular reconstruction, but the physiological reserve of the elderly patient must be carefully evaluated in the context of a salvage attempt[29,30].

Defect-related considerations

The characteristics of the original defect significantly influence the salvage algorithm. The location of the defect dictates the availability and suitability of alternative “lifeboat” flaps if salvage fails, with head and neck and lower extremity defects often having more limited options compared to breast reconstruction[31,32]. The size and complexity of the defect also matter; a large, complex three-dimensional defect created by oncologic ablation is much more challenging to manage secondarily if the initial flap fails, increasing the impetus for a vigorous salvage attempt[31]. The tissue components involved are equally critical; compromised bone flaps often have a narrower window for salvage due to the exquisite sensitivity of osseous tissue to ischemia compared to soft tissue, and their loss represents a more significant functional and structural deficit[33,34]. In contrast, the loss of a soft tissue flap, while serious, may be more amenable to later reconstruction with an alternative technique or flap[31,32].

Oncologic context

The underlying oncological context is frequently an overriding consideration. In a patient with advanced or metastatic disease and a limited life expectancy, the risks of a major salvage procedure may not be justified, and palliative wound care becomes a more appropriate focus[12]. Conversely, for a patient with a favorable prognosis and a high chance of long-term cure, an aggressive attempt at salvage is often warranted to ensure the best possible functional and esthetic outcome for their remaining years[12,13]. The priority of adjuvant therapy also influences timing; a failing flap can delay crucial postoperative radiation or chemotherapy, so the decision must balance the chance of salvage against the imperative to not compromise cancer treatment[35,36]. In some cases, abandoning a failing flap and rapidly implementing a simpler, reliable reconstructive method (e.g., a pedicled flap or skin graft) may be the best strategy to avoid delays in oncological management[4,37].

Institutional capabilities

Finally, the local institutional environment and resources are practical determinants of salvage strategy. The experience and volume of the surgical team directly impact salvage success rates, with high-volume centers demonstrating superior outcomes due to refined protocols and technical expertise[38]. The availability of a dedicated microsurgical team and operating room staff familiar with the equipment and urgency of these cases is a key resource that facilitates a rapid and organized response to flap compromise[38]. Furthermore, institutional protocols for intensive monitoring in specialized units (e.g., head and neck or plastic surgery step-down units) are essential for the early detection of flap compromise, which is the single most important factor leading to successful salvage[9,39]. The decision to attempt a complex salvage is, therefore, contingent upon having the necessary human and infrastructural support to maximize its chance of success.

Reconstructive modalities in the salvage setting

When a free flap fails, the reconstructive strategy must be re-evaluated. The choice of salvage procedure is influenced by the defect’s characteristics, the patient’s overall condition and prognosis, the availability of recipient vessels, and institutional expertise[9,24]. The primary goals remain to achieve wound coverage, preserve function, and facilitate further oncologic treatment if needed[40] (Table 2). Secondary microvascular reconstruction

A second free tissue transfer represents the most ambitious salvage option, often attempting to achieve similar reconstruction goals as the initial procedure[10].

Indications and criteria for patient selection

This option is typically reserved for patients who are medically stable and have a favorable prognosis, where the functional or esthetic benefits of a complex reconstruction justify the increased risk[45]. It is often the only option for large, complex defects that cannot be addressed with simpler techniques[45,46]. Success is more likely in patients without hypercoagulability and those with no previous salvage attempts[14].

Strategic donor site planning

The “lifeboat” concept involves having a secondary (and sometimes tertiary) donor site in mind during the initial reconstructive planning. If the first free flap fails, an alternative donor site can be used without compromising its viability[47,48]. For example, if a fibula osteocutaneous flap was used initially for mandibular reconstruction and fails, a second free flap, such as a deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap or an anterolateral thigh flap with a bone graft, could be considered as a salvage option, depending on the defect’s requirements[47,48].

Technical considerations in vessel-depleted fields

Reconstructing in a vessel-depleted field presents one of the most formidable technical challenges in salvage microsurgery, particularly after a failed free flap in the maxillofacial region[49]. The prior surgery often leaves behind scarred, irradiated, or thrombosed recipient vessels, limiting the options for a second anastomosis[49,50]. To address this, surgeons must employ a range of advanced strategies to secure reliable inflow and outflow. One approach involves identifying alternative recipient vessels outside the original operative field – such as contralateral neck vessels or more distal branches like the transverse cervical artery – which may remain untouched and suitable for microanastomosis[49,51]. When no local vessels are available, interpositional vein grafts can be used to bridge the flap pedicle to healthy vessels located farther from the defect, although this introduces additional anastomotic sites and a potentially higher risk of thrombosis[50].

In the head and neck, cephalic vein transposition has emerged as a particularly valuable technique; by mobilizing the cephalic vein from the deltopectoral region into the neck, it provides a long, robust venous conduit that can be used for outflow or even as part of an arteriovenous loop[49,52]. Such loops, created by surgically connecting an artery to a vein, can generate a new vascular “landing zone” for flap anastomosis in otherwise inhospitable terrain[50]. In extreme cases – such as lower extremity salvage with no ipsilateral vessels – cross-leg free flaps may be considered, though they require temporary immobilization and carry psychosocial and logistical burdens. Each of these techniques demands meticulous planning, extensive microsurgical experience, and a thorough preoperative vascular assessment, often with imaging such as CT angiography, to map viable vessels and optimize the chances of success in a compromised field[49,53].

Outcomes and reported success rates

Despite the challenges, success rates for a second free flap are acceptable. Novakovic and colleagues, in a retrospective case series of 31 failed flaps in head and neck reconstruction, reported a success rate of 73% for a second free flap attempt after a failed first flap[10]. Odorico and colleagues, in a systematic review of surgical and demographic predictors of free flap salvage after takeback, reported an overall free flap salvage rate of 67.28% across all anatomic sites, with head and neck salvage rates of 57.81% (Table 3). Regional and pedicled flap alternatives

Pedicled flaps provide a reliable and technically simpler alternative to microsurgery, making them excellent for salvage, especially in compromised patients[15,54].

Established workhorse flaps in maxillofacial salvage

In maxillofacial salvage surgery, several pedicled flaps have earned recognition as reliable “workhorse” options due to their consistent vascularity, technical accessibility, and ability to provide robust tissue coverage in compromised fields. Among these, the pectoralis major myocutaneous flap stands as a cornerstone in head and neck reconstruction following free-flap failure. Noothanapati and colleagues, in a retrospective cohort of 193 salvage reconstructions, reported that the pectoralis major flap was the most commonly used pedicled flap, constituting 73.1% of locoregional flap procedures[16]. Its robust thoracoacromial blood supply, large tissue volume, and predictable anatomy allow for rapid elevation and reliable transfer to defects in the oral cavity, oropharynx, and cervical regions[15,54]. The latissimus dorsi flap, when used as a pedicled option, offers a long vascular pedicle and a substantial skin paddle, making it suitable for lateral neck, shoulder, and selected maxillofacial defects, though its arc of rotation may limit reach to central facial regions. The trapezius flap, though less commonly employed, provides a viable alternative for posterior and lateral defects of the neck and occipital area, leveraging the transverse cervical or dorsal scapular vessels for its blood supply[15,54].

Collectively, these flaps represent a critical tier in the reconstructive hierarchy when microsurgical options are deemed too risky or impractical, offering dependable coverage with lower dependence on specialized equipment or prolonged operative times. Their integration into salvage algorithms underscores the importance of maintaining a versatile, non-microsurgical toolkit in complex maxillofacial reconstruction[15,54].

Advantages of pedicled flaps

The primary advantage of pedicled flaps is their reliability, as they do not depend on a microvascular anastomosis. This is crucial in an infected or irradiated wound bed where the risk of thrombosis is high. They also avoid the need for specialized microsurgical equipment or training, making them accessible in a wider range of surgical settings[15,54].

Limitations

The main limitations are their arc of rotation, which may not reach all defects, particularly in the midface or cranial base, and their often significant bulk, which can be esthetically and functionally problematic. Donor site morbidity, such as shoulder dysfunction after a latissimus dorsi harvest, is also a consideration[15,54].

Adjunctive and conservative reconstructive strategies

For less complex defects, or in patients who are poor candidates for major surgery, simpler techniques are valuable[55].

Local tissue rearrangement and skin grafting

Local flaps and skin grafts can be used for smaller defects. Local tissue rearrangement has been shown to have a high success rate (91.3%) in lower extremity reconstruction and is associated with lower costs and shorter hospital stays compared to free flaps[56]. However, their use is limited by the availability of healthy local tissue, especially in a previously operated and irradiated field[55,57].

Wound stabilization via secondary intention and negative pressure wound therapy

For very small defects or as a temporary measure, healing by secondary intention with aggressive wound care can be employed[42]. Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) is a critical adjunct here, as it helps to control infection, reduce edema, and promote granulation tissue formation, serving as a bridge to definitive reconstruction or as a definitive treatment in itself[42,43].

Prosthetic rehabilitation as a definitive salvage modality

Prosthetic rehabilitation is a nonsurgical alternative that can provide excellent esthetic and functional outcomes, particularly for midface, orbital, and palatal defects[41].

Maxillary and midfacial obturation

Maxillary obturators are used to close communications between the oral and nasal cavities or the sinus, which are common after oncologic resections. They restore the separation necessary for intelligible speech and effective swallowing. A case report detailed the rehabilitation of a mid-facial defect using a maxillary obturator with an expansion device, which dramatically improved the patient’s speech, mastication, swallowing, and esthetics[41].

External facial prosthetic

For external facial defects, particularly of the ear, nose, or orbit, silicone-based prosthetics retained by adhesives or osseointegrated implants can achieve a lifelike appearance. This approach avoids the morbidity of a complex surgical reconstruction and can be an ideal solution for patients who are not surgical candidates or who prefer a prosthetic[41].

Temporal strategies in salvage reconstruction

The timing of reconstructive salvage following free-flap failure is a critical strategic decision, balancing the desire for definitive reconstruction against patient safety and wound bed preparedness. The choice between immediate and staged approaches hinges on a careful assessment of the patient’s physiological status and the local wound conditions.

Immediate definitive reconstruction

Immediate salvage, defined as a second definitive reconstructive procedure performed during the same operative setting or within 24–48 hours of flap failure, is often the preferred option when conditions permit. The primary advantage is the prevention of prolonged wound exposure, which mitigates the risks of desiccation, infection, and fibrosis that can develop in an open defect[44]. From a technical standpoint, the surgical field is still fresh, with dissection planes and recipient vessels more readily identifiable before the onset of significant inflammation and scarring[10]. This approach can also minimize the total number of anesthetic events and reduce the overall hospital stay for the patient[58]. Furthermore, in the context of oncologic reconstruction, immediate salvage with a second free flap or a reliable pedicled flap helps avoid delays in initiating adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy, which is a crucial oncologic priority[10,58]. Successful immediate salvage is most likely in medically stable patients with a well-defined, uncontaminated wound and when the cause of initial failure is identified and can be rectified.

Staged reconstruction

Conversely, a staged “bridge and stage” approach is mandatory in the presence of an unstable patient or a compromised wound bed. If the patient is hemodynamically unstable, septic, or has significant medical comorbidities, proceeding with a complex, lengthy reconstructive procedure poses an unacceptable risk; stabilizing the patient in an intensive care setting takes precedence[59]. Similarly, if the wound shows signs of active infection, significant necrosis, or is deemed “hostile” (e.g., due to unresolved venous congestion), the likelihood of a second free-flap failing is prohibitively high[10,58]. In this scenario, the initial goal shifts to wound control and preparation. This is effectively achieved using NPWT, which manages exudate, reduces edema, and promotes granulation tissue[44]. This “bridges” the patient to a future, elective stage of definitive reconstruction once the wound bed is optimized and the patient has been medically optimized[44]. While this approach accepts the need for multiple operations and a longer overall treatment course, it ultimately enhances the safety and success of the final reconstructive outcome.

Clinical and oncologic outcomes following salvage surgery

Functional and quality-of-life outcomes across anatomic subsites

Functional and quality-of-life outcomes following salvage surgery vary considerably depending on the anatomic subsite and the reconstructive modality employed[60]. In the head and neck region, salvage procedures – particularly for recurrent oral cavity cancer – often entail significant functional trade-offs. Studies have demonstrated a measurable decline in swallowing function at 12 months postoperatively compared to preoperative baselines, irrespective of tumor stage, reflecting the cumulative impact of prior treatments and the complexity of secondary reconstruction[60].

Speech intelligibility and oral competence may also be compromised, especially when large composite defects involve the tongue, floor of the mouth, or palate[60]. In contrast, breast reconstruction outcomes are heavily influenced by the timing and delivery of radiotherapy; patients who undergo immediate DIEP flap reconstruction followed by irradiation report lower esthetic satisfaction than those whose flaps are spared radiation. However, when radiotherapy is necessary, delayed or “delayed-immediate” reconstruction strategies – using a temporary implant followed by definitive flap placement – yield comparable and generally acceptable patient-reported esthetic outcomes[61].

Despite these functional challenges, salvage surgery frequently confers meaningful improvements in overall quality of life, primarily through substantial reductions in cancer-related pain[12]. For instance, patients undergoing salvage resection for recurrent oral cancer often report significantly less pain at both 3 and 12 months postoperatively, leading to improvements in global health status scores[60]. Thus, while certain domain-specific functions may deteriorate, the net effect of successful salvage surgery can be profoundly positive, restoring not only anatomical integrity but also comfort, dignity, and psychosocial well-being[12].

Comparative complication profiles among salvage modalities

The complication profiles associated with salvage surgery vary considerably depending on the reconstructive modality employed, the anatomic region involved, and the underlying patient and wound characteristics[62]. Salvage procedures performed in previously irradiated, infected, or surgically compromised fields inherently carry a higher risk of morbidity compared to primary reconstructions[16]. Notably, the act of performing a salvage free flap itself has been identified as an independent predictor of both flap-related complications and outright failure, underscoring the heightened technical and physiological challenges of reoperative microsurgery[63]. Patient-specific factors further modulate this risk; for instance, diabetes mellitus has been consistently associated with an elevated incidence of postoperative complications across salvage approaches, including wound dehiscence, infection, and delayed healing[63].

Odorico and colleagues, in a systematic review of surgical and demographic predictors of free flap salvage, demonstrated that salvage success rates are highest for trunk and breast reconstructions (approximately 71.25%), followed by head and neck (57.81%) and extremity sites (50.76%), reflecting differences in vascular anatomy, tissue demands, and local wound environment[9]. Within the head and neck salvage population specifically, Noothanapati and colleagues reported any flap-related complication in 29% of free flap procedures and 17.2% of locoregional flap procedures[16].

Oncologic efficacy and survival implications of salvage interventions

Salvage surgery plays a pivotal role in the oncologic management of patients with recurrent or persistent disease, particularly in settings where prior treatments, such as radiation or chemotherapy, have already been exhausted[64]. For many patients with locally recurrent head and neck cancer – especially in the oral cavity – salvage resection, with or without reconstruction, represents the only potentially curative intervention remaining[17]. Evidence supports the oncologic efficacy of this approach: a meta-analysis of patients undergoing salvage surgery for recurrent oral cavity cancer reported a 5-year overall survival rate of 43%, underscoring its life-prolonging potential in appropriately selected cases[17]. The benefit of salvage is not uniform across all disease patterns; for instance, in patients with rectal cancer, salvage surgery has been associated with improved survival in those with isolated distant metastases to the liver or lung, whereas no significant survival advantage was observed in cases of isolated locoregional recurrence, highlighting the importance of patient and disease stratification[65].

Several prognostic factors consistently influence survival outcomes following salvage interventions. A longer disease-free interval – defined as the time between initial definitive treatment and recurrence – is strongly associated with improved survival, likely reflecting a less aggressive tumor biology[65]. Conversely, adverse pathologic features such as nodal recurrence, extranodal extension, and perineural invasion are linked to significantly poorer outcomes and higher rates of subsequent failure[17]. These factors must be integrated into preoperative decision-making to ensure that aggressive salvage is pursued only in patients with a reasonable chance of durable disease control[17,65]. Importantly, successful reconstruction after salvage resection not only addresses the functional and esthetic consequences of extensive tissue loss but also facilitates timely initiation or continuation of adjuvant therapies, thereby supporting comprehensive oncologic care[18]. Thus, salvage surgery is not merely a reconstructive endeavor but a critical component of a multimodal strategy aimed at extending survival and, in select cases, achieving cure[64,66,67].

An evidence-based clinical algorithm for the management of free-flap failure

Following the declaration of free-flap failure – confirmed by clinical assessment and, when indicated, imaging or operative exploration – a structured, evidence-based algorithm guides the subsequent management[7]. The decision pathway begins with a rapid reassessment of four key domains: (1) patient stability, (2) wound bed condition, (3) oncologic urgency, and (4) institutional resources[5].

If the patient is hemodynamically stable, the wound is clean and non-infected, and oncologic priorities demand immediate reconstruction (e.g., to avoid delay in adjuvant therapy), immediate definitive salvage is pursued[7]. In this scenario, a second free flap is preferred for complex, three-dimensional maxillofacial defects – provided suitable recipient vessels are available or can be accessed via vein grafts, cephalic vein transposition, or arteriovenous loops[49,50]. When microvascular options are limited due to prior surgery or radiation, Frohwitter and colleagues demonstrated that two-stage pedicled flaps achieve uneventful healing in 72.2% of cases, with a mean operative time of 130.5 minutes, significantly shorter than typical microsurgical procedures[15]. If microsurgical expertise or vascular options are limited, a robust pedicled flap (e.g., pectoralis major) is selected as an alternative[10].

Conversely, if the patient is unstable, the wound is infected or necrotic, or there is uncertainty regarding oncologic control, a staged “bridge and stage” approach is adopted[68]. Initial management focuses on wound stabilization using NPWT, infection control, and medical optimization[69]. Once the patient is stabilized and the wound bed is granulating and clean (typically 1–3 weeks later), definitive reconstruction is performed – again selecting between a second free flap, pedicled flap, or, in select cases, prosthetic rehabilitation (e.g., for midface or palatal defects where function and esthetics can be restored non-surgically)[5,7].

Discussion

The management of free-flap failure is a high-stakes scenario governed by several core principles. The evidence consistently underscores that time is the most critical variable, with successful salvage rates plummeting dramatically after the first 24–48 hours, necessitating rigorous monitoring protocols. The etiology of failure is most commonly venous thrombosis, followed by arterial causes, often rooted in technical factors or patient-specific comorbidities like hypercoagulable states. The decision-making matrix for salvage is complex, requiring a simultaneous assessment of patient fitness, defect characteristics, and oncological prognosis. Finally, the reconstructive armamentarium is diverse, ranging from a second free flap to pedicled flaps and prosthetic rehabilitation, each with distinct indications, success rates, and complication profiles. The overarching goal is to select the pathway that most effectively balances the chance of success with the patient’s overall well-being.

A critical examination of the existing literature reveals several important nuances that challenge commonly accepted assumptions. While many surgeons assume that a second free flap carries prohibitive risk in the salvage setting, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Walia and colleagues demonstrated that secondary free tissue transfer following flap failure achieves a success rate of 93% (95% CI, 0.89–0.97), with a lower overall complication rate than locoregional reconstruction or conservative management[37]. This finding challenges the traditional reconstructive ladder paradigm and suggests that, when technically feasible, a second free flap may actually represent the most reliable option rather than a last resort. Similarly, Odorico and colleagues identified that the absence of hypercoagulability and no previous salvage attempts are significant predictors of successful salvage, while prior radiation in the head and neck population was significantly associated with salvage failure[17]. These evidence-based predictors provide surgeons with objective criteria for patient selection rather than relying on subjective judgment alone.

Furthermore, the critical importance of timing has been refined by recent evidence. Gupta and colleagues demonstrated that salvage rates drop from 57.1 to 11.1% when flap takeback occurs more than 3 hours after compromise detection, establishing a narrower window for intervention than previously recognized[70]. This finding has direct implications for institutional protocols and monitoring systems. The authors also identified that osteocutaneous free flaps in the head and neck carry a 3.8-fold increased risk of failure compared to other flap types, providing specific guidance for high-risk scenarios. These quantitative risk estimates represent a significant advancement over earlier descriptive accounts[70].

The philosophy of salvage has evolved significantly. Historically, the failure of a free flap often led to the abandonment of microsurgery in favor of simpler, albeit less ideal, reconstructive methods. Today, there is a clear trend toward aggressive re-reconstruction with a second free flap. This shift is driven by improved microsurgical techniques, better understanding of the “vessel-depleted” neck, and the development of strategies like vein grafts, arteriovenous loops, and the cephalic turndown. Studies now demonstrate that success rates for second free flaps, while lower than those for primary procedures, are acceptable. Novakovic and colleagues reported a 73% success rate for second free flap attempts in head and neck reconstruction[10]. Baek and colleagues, using propensity score analysis, found comparable flap failure rates between salvage and primary surgery (4.1% vs 5.4%, P = 0.71), suggesting that functional and esthetic outcomes can approach those of primary reconstruction when appropriate patient selection is applied[11].

A critical appraisal of the included studies reveals substantial heterogeneity in how salvage success is defined and measured. Hsiung and colleagues, in their nested case-control study of 548 flaps, defined flap failure objectively as reoperation for thrombosis or hematoma within 30 days, and their multivariate analysis identified thrombosis as the dominant predictor of failure with an odds ratio of 86.42[9]. This rigorous definition stands in contrast to Novakovic and colleagues, who reported a second free flap success rate of 73% but defined success simply as flap survival without functional criteria, potentially overestimating clinically meaningful success[10]. Readers should interpret these figures with caution, as a successfully salvaged flap by vascular criteria may still result in poor functional outcomes, particularly in the setting of prolonged ischemia or partial necrosis. Baek and colleagues addressed this limitation by employing propensity score matching to compare salvage and primary surgery outcomes, finding comparable flap failure rates (5.4% vs 4.1%) but significantly higher wound dehiscence in the salvage group (13.0% vs 3.6%, P = 0.04)[11]. This disparity suggests that even when flap survival is achieved, the postoperative course in salvage surgery is often more complicated.

A notable limitation across the literature is the inconsistent reporting of functional outcomes and quality-of-life measures following salvage surgery. While Williamson and colleagues systematically reviewed functional outcomes and demonstrated measurable declines in swallowing function at 12 months postoperatively, most studies focus on flap survival as the primary endpoint[60]. This emphasis on technical success rather than patient-centered outcomes represents a significant gap in the evidence base. Future research should prioritize validated quality-of-life instruments and functional assessments to better inform shared decision-making. Additionally, the literature lacks standardized definitions of what constitutes “successful salvage,” with some studies defining success as flap survival alone, while others include functional restoration or hospital discharge without major complications. This heterogeneity complicates cross-study comparison and meta-analysis.

The current evidence base has notable limitations. The literature is dominated by retrospective, single-institution series, which are susceptible to selection bias and limit generalizability. There is a profound lack of standardized reporting, making it difficult to compare outcomes across studies; for instance, definitions of “successful salvage” and “functional outcome” vary widely. Furthermore, most studies focus on technical success and survival, with a relative paucity of high-quality, patient-reported outcome data on quality of life, swallowing, or speech after salvage.

The evidence base for pedicled flaps in salvage reconstruction warrants critical scrutiny. Frohwitter and colleagues reported a 72.2% uneventful healing rate with two-stage pedicled flaps, with 69.4% of these procedures performed as backup strategies in heavily pretreated wound beds[15]. While these results appear favorable, the study lacks a comparative arm and does not report ischemia times or flap dimensions, limiting its utility for evidence-based decision-making. Noothanapati and colleagues provided a larger comparative analysis of 193 salvage patients, demonstrating that any complication occurred in 49.5% of locoregional flap patients and 57% of free flap patients, with total flap loss rates of 3.2 and 7%, respectively[16]. The absence of statistical comparison between these groups reflects appropriate methodological caution, as selection bias in salvage reconstruction is unavoidable. However, this also means that the study cannot definitively establish the superiority of one approach over another, leaving clinicians to rely on institutional experience and patient-specific factors rather than high-level evidence.

Future research must prioritize prospective, multi-center registries to establish robust benchmarks. There is a critical need to develop and validate a standardized classification system for flap compromise and salvage outcomes. Finally, research should increasingly focus on long-term functional and patient-reported outcomes to better inform the shared decision-making process between surgeons and patients facing salvage scenarios.

Ultimately, the successful navigation of flap failure extends beyond surgical technique. It is fundamentally a multidisciplinary endeavor. The integrated efforts of microsurgeons, oncologists, intensivists, specialized nursing staff, speech and swallow therapists, and prosthodontists are indispensable from the preoperative planning stage through long-term rehabilitation. This team is essential for optimizing the patient’s medical status, facilitating timely re-exploration, and managing the complex sequelae of salvage surgery.

At the heart of this process lies patient-centered care. The decision to pursue a high-risk second free flap vs a simpler, more reliable pedicled flap must be made collaboratively. It requires a transparent discussion of risks, benefits, and alternatives, carefully balanced against the patient’s own values, goals, and tolerance for further intervention. By combining advanced surgical strategies with a compassionate, team-based, and patient-focused approach, surgeons can optimally manage the challenge of free-flap failure and guide patients toward the best possible recovery.

A fundamental limitation across all included studies is the lack of long-term functional outcome reporting. Zhang and colleagues demonstrated significant pain relief following salvage surgery with massive soft tissue flaps (P < 0.001), with a 1-year overall survival rate of 58.4%[12]. While these oncologic outcomes are encouraging, the study’s follow-up period of 90 days for quality-of-life assessment is insufficient to capture late functional sequelae such as chronic aspiration, speech impairment, or social eating difficulties. Hafström and colleagues provided longer follow-up, reporting a 5-year disease-specific survival rate of 44% in 83 patients, but their functional outcome data were limited to gastrostomy and tracheostomy dependence rather than validated patient-reported measures[13]. Zenga and colleagues, in a multi-institutional cohort of 102 patients, reported that 30% of long-term survivors remained gastrostomy tube dependent, a sobering finding that highlights the functional price of oncologic salvage[17]. However, even this study did not employ disease-specific quality-of-life instruments, representing a missed opportunity to capture the patient’s perspective. The field would benefit from prospective registries that mandate the collection of validated functional outcomes alongside traditional survival endpoints.

Finally, the present review acknowledges the emerging role of large language models, including ChatGPT, in synthesizing evidence and supporting clinical decision-making in complex reconstructive scenarios. Guo and colleagues, in a global-scale, machine learning-driven informatics study, identified six core research clusters for ChatGPT applications in healthcare, with clinical decision support and health management representing the second most extensively researched domain after medical education[71]. Within maxillofacial reconstruction, where the evidence base for salvage surgery after free-flap failure is heterogeneous and largely retrospective, large language models may offer potential value in aggregating published outcomes, identifying knowledge gaps, and assisting in generating structured patient summaries for multidisciplinary tumor board discussions. However, significant limitations remain, including concerns regarding reliability, interpretability, data privacy, and the definition of accountability when such tools are used for clinical guidance[71]. Future research should systematically evaluate the incremental value and safety boundaries of large language models in reconstructive microsurgery using real-world tasks and gold-standard comparisons, while establishing transparent technical specifications and ethical governance frameworks to ensure responsible integration into clinical practice.

Conclusion

Free-flap failure represents a significant setback in reconstructive surgery; however, it is not an endpoint. A systematic, evidence-based approach to salvage can successfully avert a catastrophic outcome. The cornerstone of management is rapid diagnosis and urgent re-exploration. The choice of salvage procedure is not monolithic but must be rigorously individualized, balancing the patient’s clinical status, defect requirements, and oncologic prognosis. In carefully selected patients, a second free flap offers the highest potential for restoring the form and function intended by the initial reconstruction. For others, reliable pedicled flaps provide a robust and timely solution, while prosthetic rehabilitation presents a valuable nonsurgical alternative. Ultimately, a comprehensive understanding of the full reconstructive armamentarium, applied through a patient-centered and multidisciplinary lens, is paramount for converting the crisis of flap failure into a manageable challenge with a successful resolution.
