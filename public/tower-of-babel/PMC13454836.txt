Impella Support as a “Bridge to Nowhere”
Authors: Koichiro Hori, Riku Arai, Jun Sasaki, Tetsuro Nagao, Suguru Migita, Keisuke Kojima, Yasuo Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImpella and other percutaneous mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices are increasingly used for cardiogenic shock complicating acute myocardial infarction. Although they provide rapid hemodynamic stabilization, some patients do not achieve myocardial recovery and are not candidates for heart transplantation or durable MCS. In such situations, Impella may become a “bridge to nowhere”, raising ethical and clinical questions about continuation or withdrawal.Methods and ResultsWe present illustrative cases of acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock managed with Impella and reviewed through multidisciplinary ethical deliberation. In Case 1, support was defined as a time-limited trial with a predefined exit strategy, followed by transition to palliative-focused care. In Case 2, escalation from Impella CP to Impella 5.5 stabilized hemodynamics and enabled recovery of decision-making capacity; however, the patient declined heart transplantation and long-term MCS, resulting in elective withdrawal of Impella support and initiation of end-of-life care.ConclusionsWhen recovery or durable therapy is not achievable or acceptable, Impella can unintentionally function as a “bridge to nowhere”. Multidisciplinary deliberation and early integration of palliative care are essential for goal-concordant withdrawal decisions.

Central Figure

Percutaneous mechanical circulatory support (MCS) using Impella has become an essential therapeutic option for patients with cardiogenic shock complicating acute myocardial infarction (AMI).1 Early initiation of Impella support can stabilize hemodynamics and allow time for revascularization and myocardial recovery in selected patients. However, these advances have also introduced ethical challenges not fully addressed by current clinical frameworks. Although Impella provides effective circulatory support, it does not inherently define an exit strategy when myocardial recovery is insufficient.

Impella CP and Impella 5.5 differ not only in flow capacity but also in duration of support: approximately 8 days and up to 30 days, respectively. These time frames can function implicitly as time-limited trials (TLTs), requiring reassessment of prognosis, treatment goals, and patient values. When recovery does not occur, continuation or escalation of support requires renewed ethical deliberation.

In contemporary practice, particularly in patients with a large infarct burden such as broad anterior AMI, Impella CP is frequently initiated before percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) as a bridge to decision-making. If cardiac function remains inadequate after initial stabilization, escalation to Impella 5.5 may be considered to allow further assessment of candidacy for heart transplantation or durable MCS as destination therapy.

Nevertheless, when neither myocardial recovery nor transition to definitive long-term therapy is feasible or desired, prolonged mechanical support may unintentionally result in a “bridge to nowhere”. In such situations, clinicians, patients, and families face ethically complex decisions regarding continuation or withdrawal of support despite preserved hemodynamics.

We present 2 illustrative cases that exemplify distinct trajectories of Impella-supported cardiogenic shock: one in which Impella was positioned as an explicit time-limited trial from the outset, and another in which escalation to Impella 5.5 enabled recovery of decision-making capacity but ultimately was a “bridge to nowhere”. Through these cases, we discuss ethical considerations and propose a practical framework for goal-concordant end-of-life care in the Impella era.

A 76-year-old woman presented with cardiogenic shock due to AMI caused by complete left main coronary artery occlusion. On admission, her blood pressure was 80/62 mmHg and serum lactate was 4.7 mmol/L. Initial 12-lead ECG showed ST-segment elevation in leads V2–6, I, aVL, and aVR, with reciprocal ST-segment depression in leads II, III, and aVF. Impella CP was implanted before primary PCI, followed by successful drug-eluting stent implantation. Despite revascularization, persistent hemodynamic instability with right ventricular failure required escalation to combined venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and Impella support (ECPELLA).

To assess the potential for meaningful myocardial recovery, multiple clinical parameters were considered. In this patient, the infarct was large, as reflected by extremely elevated peak creatine kinase/creatine kinase-MB levels (11,792/695 U/L), which have been shown to correlate with extensive myocardial necrosis and poor functional recovery.2 The infarction involved complete occlusion of the left main coronary artery, a lesion known to be associated with adverse outcomes.3 Advanced age and persistent cardiogenic shock with right ventricular failure requiring ECPELLA support were also regarded as unfavorable prognostic factors.4 Taken together, these findings suggested a low likelihood of clinically meaningful myocardial recovery.

From admission, the patient retained decision-making capacity and consistently stated that intensive care would be acceptable only if meaningful functional recovery, specifically independent ambulation and activities of daily living, could be achieved. Heart transplantation and durable MCS as destination therapy were deemed inappropriate based on medical assessment.

Accordingly, MCS was explicitly defined as a TLT of approximately 1 week. The duration of the TLT was determined based on several considerations. Impella CP is intended for short-term MCS, and prolonged use, particularly via femoral access, is associated with increasing risks of vascular complications, infection, and bleeding.5 In this case, no significant improvement in hemodynamics or cardiac function was observed within the time frame, and inflammatory markers increased and anemia progressed by day 7–8. Therefore, the 1-week TLT was considered a reasonable and clinically appropriate window to reassess recovery potential and align further treatment with the patient’s goals of care. A multidisciplinary ethics conference was convened early, incorporating cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, intensivists, intensive care unit nurses, palliative care specialists, and a medical social worker, together with the patient’s surrogate decision-maker. The conference confirmed that escalation to Impella 5.5 was not appropriate in this case. Although Impella 5.5 permits up to 30 days of support, its use presupposes 1 of 2 exit strategies: native cardiac recovery with device weaning, or bridging to heart transplantation or destination therapy-left ventricular assist device (DT-LVAD). Native cardiac recovery was considered highly unlikely given the clinical trajectory described above. Heart transplantation was contraindicated due to the patient’s advanced age, and DT-LVAD was deemed inappropriate primarily due to the absence of a dedicated caregiver. As neither exit strategy was realistically achievable, escalation to Impella 5.5 was not pursued, and further care was aligned with the patient’s previously expressed goals.

After reassessment revealed insufficient myocardial recovery and no realistic pathway toward the patient’s minimum acceptable quality of life, ECPELLA support were withdrawn in a planned and ethically structured manner. Care goals were transitioned to palliative-focused management in accordance with the patient’s expressed values. The patient was transferred out of the intensive care unit on day 20 and subsequently received palliative-focused care in the general ward. She was later transferred to another hospital and died on day 56, corresponding to 48 days after withdrawal of Impella CP support.

A 60-year-old man presented with cardiogenic shock after extensive AMI. Initial blood pressure was 79/65 mmHg and serum lactate was 3.6 mmol/L. ECG showed ST-segment elevation in leads V1–4 and aVR. Primary PCI was performed for complete occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending artery. Despite intra-aortic balloon pump support and inotropic therapy, hemodynamic status could not be maintained, and Impella CP was implanted on day 3. Persistent instability required escalation to Impella 5.5 on day 18.

Escalation to Impella 5.5 resulted in sustained hemodynamic stabilization, successful extubation, and recovery of decision-making capacity. After escalation to Impella 5.5, support was reduced from P6 to P3 over 4 days. Even at P3, the patient remained dependent on dobutamine (5 µg/kg/min) and milrinone (0.25 µg/kg/min). Repeated attempts to reduce either agent caused worsening pulmonary congestion, renal dysfunction, and hypotension, indicating failed weaning despite stepwise support reduction. Support below P3 was not sustainable because reduction to P2 repeatedly triggered backflow alarms and automatic return to P3. Thus, P3 was the lowest practically achievable level of Impella 5.5 support. Although right heart catheterization on day 36 showed relatively preserved hemodynamics, these findings reflected dependence on Impella 5.5 and high-dose catecholamines rather than true myocardial recovery. From a medical perspective, bridging to heart transplantation or durable MCS as DT was considered potentially feasible at that stage. However, severe renal dysfunction persisted despite Impella support and inotropic therapy. On day 36, the Japanese HeartMate Risk Score was calculated to estimate prognosis after LVAD implantation. Based on creatinine 3.71 mg/dL, albumin 2.60 g/dL, international normalized ratio 1.1, and age 60 years, the score was 4.57 at our institution, and 3.76 assuming management at a high-volume LVAD center, both indicating high risk. These findings suggested a low likelihood of favorable 1-year survival after implantation.

After regaining the ability to communicate, the patient clearly declined heart transplantation and long-term MCS, expressing a preference for discharge home and avoidance of prolonged life-sustaining treatment. Although circulatory stability was maintained with Impella 5.5, myocardial recovery remained insufficient. At this point, continued mechanical support no longer aligned with the patient’s goals of care and effectively constituted a “bridge to nowhere”. A multidisciplinary ethics conference was convened to reassess therapeutic appropriateness, patient preferences, quality of life, and family and social context.

Following confirmation of the patient’s wishes and consensus among the care team, Impella support was electively withdrawn, and palliative-focused end-of-life care was implemented. After device removal, pulmonary congestion gradually worsened, and the patient developed dyspnea and low cardiac output syndrome with severe distress and restlessness, consistent with end-stage heart failure. Morphine 10 mg/day was administered for symptom relief as part of palliative care. A multidisciplinary palliative care team, including the attending physician, psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, and a clinical psychologist, also provided psychosocial support for the patient and his wife. The patient was transferred out of the intensive care unit on day 59 and died on day 67, corresponding to 14 days after withdrawal of Impella 5.5 support.

Discussion

Both cases illustrate a growing but insufficiently discussed challenge in contemporary cardiovascular care: how to appropriately implement end-of-life care in patients supported with Impella who do not achieve myocardial recovery and do not proceed to definitive long-term therapies. In patients with extensive myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock, lack of hemodynamic or functional improvement during the early phase of temporary MCS is often indicative of limited recovery potential, underscoring the importance of predefined reassessment windows.2 Importantly, these scenarios should not be interpreted as failures of Impella therapy. Rather, they reflect the inherent limitations of mechanical support when myocardial recovery is not achieved or when the patient’s values do not align with available therapeutic options.

A unique feature of Impella, particularly Impella 5.5, is its ability to maintain stable hemodynamics over a prolonged period. Although advantageous for bridging patients to recovery, transplantation or informed decision-making, it may also obscure therapeutic limits. Stable circulation may lead clinicians and families to regard ongoing support as beneficial even when no realistic path to recovery or acceptable quality of life exists. This gap between physiologic stability and meaningful clinical benefit lies at the core of the ethical dilemma.

Many institutions struggle with whether and when Impella withdrawal is ethically permissible. Withdrawal of mechanical support is often emotionally and ethically challenging, especially when vital signs remain stable. However, as these cases show, Impella withdrawal is not treatment abandonment. When further disease-modifying therapy is no longer feasible or desired, it represents a transition to goal-concordant care.

A TLT provides a practical framework. By defining the goals, duration, and reassessment points of mechanical support at initiation, clinicians can align expectations among patients, families, and care teams. In Case 1, early clarification of intent enabled a transition to palliative care. In Case 2, escalation to Impella 5.5, though justified as a bridge to decision-making or potential transplantation, still resulted in a “bridge to nowhere” when patient preferences became clear and recovery did not occur. In both cases, multidisciplinary ethics conferences enabled transparent, shared decision-making by integrating medical appropriateness, patient preferences, quality of life, and family or social context. Thus, support withdrawal was based not on a single clinician’s judgment, but on a collective, ethically informed process.

At our institution, routine multidisciplinary discussions on ongoing management occur during weekly ward rounds and involve cardiologists, intensivists, nurses, physiotherapists, and clinical engineers. In contrast, formal ethics conferences are convened when major treatment decisions are required. These conferences include cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, intensivists, intensive care unit nurses, palliative care team members, medical social workers, and other relevant professionals. In the present cases, the conference was held about 3 weeks after Impella 5.5 implantation, when the patient’s condition had stabilized and wishes regarding long-term MCS had become clear. The 4 perspectives used in our structured ethical deliberation (i.e., medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, and contextual features) were based on the 4-box method proposed by Jonsen and colleagues.6

The Japanese clinical context further underscores the importance of such frameworks. Limited availability of heart transplantation, the substantial social and caregiving burdens associated with long-term MCS, and cultural considerations regarding end-of-life care necessitate careful, individualized decision-making. In addition, national recommendations in Japan regarding end-of-life care in emergency and intensive care settings emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary decision-making and respect for patient preferences.7 As Impella use continues to expand, similar ethical challenges are likely to become increasingly common.

Conclusions

Impella has transformed the management of cardiogenic shock after AMI by providing durable circulatory support. However, it does not eliminate the need for ethically grounded end-of-life decision-making. When myocardial recovery is insufficient and definitive long-term therapies are neither feasible nor desired, Impella may become a “bridge to nowhere”, but as these cases show, ethically guided withdrawal of Impella support, based on multidisciplinary deliberation and respect for patient values, is appropriate and necessary in the Impella era.
