Arsenic trioxide promotes a glue‑like interaction to drive STUB1-mediated NPM-ALK degradation in ALK+ ALCL
Authors: Guiming Li, Jun Wang, Shiguang Ye, Lixin Zhou, Yanli Sun, Bowen Li, Yunshuo Zhao, Yi Ding, Yu Zeng, Bin Xue, Guangming Wang, Fangce Wang, Xiu Luo, Aibin Liang, Ellen L Weisberg, Wenjun Zhang, Wenchao Lu, Jing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nucleophosmin-anaplastic lymphoma kinase (NPM-ALK) fusion constitutively activates ALK tyrosine kinase, driving oncogenesis in anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL). While arsenic trioxide (ATO) exhibits therapeutic potential, its precise mechanism remains elusive, limiting clinical application. Here, we used a combined approach of E3 ligase library screening, ATO chemical proteomics, and NPM-ALK immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry to reveal that ATO regulates NPM-ALK stability via the E3 ligase STUB1. Clinically, high STUB1 expression correlates with improved survival, identifying it as a candidate favorable prognostic biomarker in NPM-ALK+ ALCL. Mechanistically, ATO promotes a glue‑like interaction that stabilizes the ternary complex of STUB1 and NPM-ALK, promoting ubiquitination at K174 and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Furthermore, structural modeling, domain mapping, and point mutations support a proposed arsenic-dependent interaction model involving STUB1 Cys83, Cys103, and NPM-ALK Cys599, which may modulate the STUB1–NPM-ALK interface and enhance their interaction, thereby promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Crucially, we demonstrate that STUB1 overexpression or pharmacological activation using the FDA-approved cardiac glycoside Deslanoside synergizes profoundly with ATO to inhibit tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a mature T-cell lymphoma driven in its ALK-positive subtype by the oncogenic NPM-ALK fusion, which constitutively activates signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT and represents a central therapeutic target [1–4]. Although frontline chemotherapy and ALK inhibitors achieve high initial response rates, relapse and therapeutic resistance remain major clinical challenges [1, 5–7]. Arsenic trioxide (ATO), a clinically established inducer of oncoprotein degradation in acute promyelocytic leukemia, has shown activity in ALK+ ALCL by promoting NPM-ALK destabilization, yet its molecular mechanism remains unclear [8–10]. In this study, we identify that arsenic trioxide promotes a glue‑like interaction supporting a proposed arsenic-dependent interaction model involving STUB1 Cys83, Cys103, and NPM-ALK Cys599, driving K174 ubiquitination and degradation of NPM-ALK, and establish STUB1 as a candidate favorable prognostic biomarker whose pharmacological activation synergizes with ATO to overcome resistance.

Using an integrated screening strategy that combined (1) an E3 ligase library screen (overexpression system), (2) biotinylated ATO (Biotin-As) chemical proteomics with competitive ATO pretreatment (overexpression system), and (3) NPM-ALK immunoprecipitation (IP)–mass spectrometry in NPM-ALK+ Karpas‑299 cells (endogenous system), we identified STUB1 as the top‑ranked, and ultimately the sole, E3 ligase selectively recruited by ATO to bind NPM‑ALK and promote its degradation (Fig. 1a, Figure S1). Co-IP assays using purified STUB1-TPR and endogenous Karpas-299 cells confirmed that STUB1 directly binds ATO (Fig. 1b). In multiple ALCL datasets, STUB1 expression was elevated in ALK+ ALCL and correlated with better outcomes and overall survival (Fig. 1c, d, Figure S2). STUB1 knockdown (KD) partially reduced ATO‑mediated NPM-ALK degradation, whereas STUB1 overexpression (OE) enhanced it, demonstrating STUB1‑dependent regulation. Neither manipulation altered NPM‑ALK transcript levels, supporting a post‑translational mechanism (Fig. 1e, f, Figure S3a–c). These results demonstrate that ATO induces NPM-ALK degradation via STUB1 recruitment.

Unbiased proteomics identified NPM-ALK as among the most significantly downregulated proteins upon ATO treatment, suggesting a relative tendency toward selective impact on the NPM-ALK axis (Fig. 1g, Figure S3d). Thermal shift assays confirmed direct binding between ATO and STUB1 (Fig. 1h). Reciprocal Co-IP confirmed STUB1 targets ALK (not NPM), and gradient transfection showed STUB1 drives dose‑dependent degradation of ALK fusions (Figure S4a–b). In a DEFUSE system [11], ATO rescued ~ 50% of cells from AP1903‑induced death (DC₅₀ = 177.9 nM), indicating ATO degrades full‑length ALK by removing the F–C death switch (Figure S4c–e). These data establish that ATO degrades ALK independently of NPM, which may extend to other ALK‑driven malignancies beyond ALK fusions. ATO dose-dependently enhanced STUB1–ALK interaction in both overexpression and endogenous systems (Fig. 1i, Figure S5), and NanoBiT assays revealed robust ternary complex formation (Fig. 1j). Collectively, these data suggest that ATO engages STUB1 to promote ALK degradation, consistent with a glue‑like mechanism.

Having established that ATO-mediated STUB1 engagement is centered on ALK, domain mapping and NanoBiT assays identified the ALK C‑terminus as essential for STUB1 interaction (Fig. 1k–m, Figure S6a). Mutagenesis revealed Cys599 as the primary ATO‑binding residue on NPM‑ALK (C599A abolished ATO‑dependent ternary complex formation), whereas Cys639 was dispensable (Fig. 1n, Figure S6b–c). On STUB1, docking and mutagenesis identified Cys83/Cys103 as critical residues, as both mutants abrogated the ATO response (Fig. 1o, Figure S6d–f). These findings support a model in which ATO may engage a bidentate arsenic-mediated interaction involving STUB1 Cys83/Cys103 and NPM-ALK Cys599, which is associated with formation of the ternary complex and subsequent degradation of oncogenic NPM-ALK (Fig. 1p).

ATO treatment enhanced STUB1‑dependent polyubiquitination of NPM‑ALK (Fig. 2a, Figure S7a). LC–MS/MS identified K174 (within the kinase activation loop) as the predominant ubiquitination site. The K174A mutation abolished polyubiquitination and STUB1‑induced degradation, confirming its critical role (Fig. 2b–d, Figure S7b–c). STUB1 knockdown partially attenuated both ATO‑induced growth inhibition and apoptosis (Fig. 2e, Figure S8a–c), whereas STUB1 overexpression reduced NPM‑ALK protein and p‑AKT signaling, inhibited proliferation, enhanced apoptosis, and synergized with ATO (Fig. 2f–h, Figure S8d–e). Thus, STUB1 is a major contributor to ATO‑mediated growth inhibition and apoptosis. The STUB1 activator Lanatoside C (Lac) [12] suppressed proliferation; combining ATO with Lac further enhanced cytotoxicity and abolished long‑term growth (Fig. 2i). Deslanoside (Des), a clinically preferred derivative of Lac that shares the same active metabolite in vivo, also binds STUB1 and enhances STUB1-mediated ubiquitination activity, showed superior potency (lower DC50 and EC50) and more effectively suppressed downstream signaling (Fig. 2j, Figure S9–10). Notably, the ATO–Des combination enhanced degradation and inhibited cell proliferation in extended assays, with stronger effects than ATO–Lac, suggesting Des as a preferred partner for ATO and pointing to its translational potential in ALK+ ALCL (Fig. 2k–l, Figure S11).

We next assessed therapeutic efficacy in vivo. STUB1 overexpression in Karpas‑299 cells delayed tumor formation in NSG mice and prolonged survival (no tumors for 62 days; one death over 100 days), demonstrating strong therapeutic potential (Fig. 2m–p, Figure S12). The STUB1 activator Deslanoside (Des, 5 mg/kg) significantly inhibited tumor growth (59.5% reduction) without toxicity (Fig. 2q–t). In an aggressive high‑burden model, ATO (7.5 mg/kg) or Des alone each reduced tumor growth, but the combination of ATO + Des produced marked synergistic suppression and significantly improved survival, with only one mouse death versus controls (Fig. 2u–w). All treatments were well‑tolerated with no weight loss (Fig. 2x). These findings establish that STUB1 activation (via overexpression or Des) effectively suppresses ALK+ ALCL in vivo, and combining Des with ATO yields superior efficacy, supporting clinical translation of this dual‑agent strategy for aggressive ALK+ ALCL.

The study still has some limitations. While our biochemical and modeling analyses support a molecular glue-like mechanism, the ternary complex structure remains unsolved. Deslanoside binds and activates STUB1, yet the structural basis is unknown. In addition, the discrepancy between biochemical binding affinity and cellular potency, as well as the use of supraphysiological concentrations in proteomic and adduct profiling assays, suggests that the nature and extent of target engagement under physiological conditions require further clarification. Validation in patient-derived models and prospective studies is needed. Additionally, the prognostic versus predictive value of STUB1 requires clarification in larger independent cohorts. Addressing these limitations will refine STUB1‑directed degradation strategies.
