Stem cell-based tactics in the remodeling and treatment of intestinal diseases
Authors: Yanian Ren, Shanshan Zhang, Jiageng Wang, Yuanxin Wang, Mingyi Xu, Lei Zhang, Keli Ge, Yongli Chu, Leisheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntestinal diseases are imposing a prevalent socioeconomic burden worldwide, which is largely attributed to the disrupted homeostasis of gut-immune crosstalk including gut microbiota dysbiosis and immunomodulatory imbalance. Stem cells are unique cell populations with self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation properties, and current literature has indicated the remarkable applications in early embryogenesis, disease remodeling and novel cytotherapy via diverse modes of action (e.g., direct- or trans-differentiation, cytokine paracrine, and bidirectional immunomodulation). Of them, human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and the derivatives, including the self-organizing organoids, have been vastly employed for intestinal disease remodeling, while mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) and the microvesicles are adopted for multifarious intestinal disorder intervention. In this review article, we mainly focus on the progressions in stem cell-based disease remodeling (intestinal lineage and organoid induction from hPSCs) and cellular therapy for intestinal diseases (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, infectious intestinal diseases, and colorectal cancer), together with the underlying repair mechanisms (e.g., target genes and signaling pathways). Furthermore, the promising prospective and the concomitant challenges of stem cell-based preclinical and clinical investigations upon intestinal disorders are also outlined, including ethical considerations and supervision, heterogeneity and standardization, integration with novel biotechnologies (e.g., single-cell RNA sequencing, intracellular tracing, gene-editing and three-dimensional bioprinting). Collectively, our findings will supply profitable references and facilitate the development of stem cell-based mechanistic investigation and regenerative medicine for intestinal diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05124-z.Keywords: Stem cell therapy, Intestinal organoids, Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), Signaling pathways, Precision medicine

Introduction

Intestinal diseases are a category of digestive diseases that affect the functions or structures of digestive organs such as the small intestine and large intestine [1, 2]. The common symptoms of intestinal diseases include abdominal pain, diarrhea, bloody stools, and digestive disorders [3]. The major subtypes of intestinal diseases are infectious intestinal diseases (e.g., bacterial dysentery, viral enteritis), inflammatory bowel diseases (e.g., Crohn's disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC)), functional bowel diseases (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome), and tumoral lesions (e.g., intestinal polyps, colorectal cancer) [4]. For decades, a variety of in vitro multicellular models have been reported for simulating intestinal functions and investigating mechanisms, including Caco-2 (enterocytes), NCI-H716 (enteroendocrine cells), HT29-MTX (goblet cells), and M cells [5]. However, the inherent defects and multifaceted variations of both cell line models and experimental animal models compared with the in vivo intestinal microenvironment, as well as the resultant conclusions upon pathogenesis should not be neglected [6].

Longitudinal studies have indicated the profound correlation between gut mucosal immune system and intestinal health [7]. The spatiotemporal homeostasis of intestinal microbial communities and host immune cells plays a decisive role in maintaining the intestinal immune homeostasis as well as the concomitant physiological immune response [8]. The dysregulated homeostasis in gut microbiota and the resultant immune imbalance plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of diverse intestinal disorders and even intestinal tumors via orchestrating the intestinal permeability and metabolite signalings [9, 10]. For instance, Güemes-González et al. put forward the therapeutic prospect of intestinal permeability for intestinal disorders via regulations from molecules (e.g., ions, peptides) to pathogenic microorganisms [11]. Simultaneously, the imbalance of immune cells that orchestrates immune responses is recognized as the core trigger of intestinal inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) such as regulatory T cells and pro-inflammatory intestinal T cell subsets [9, 12]. The microbial dysregulation in composition and shifts commonly deteriorates the activation of proinflammatory-associated signal cascades such as Toll-like receptor (TLR), aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, JAK/STAT and NF-κB signaling [2, 8, 13].

The increasing incidence of intestinal diseases and the inherent limitations of the current conventional treatments (e.g., substantial side effects, potential enteric neurotoxicity, and high costs) have driven an urgent demand for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies including the stem cell-based precision medicine [14, 15]. Generally, stem cells can be divided into totipotent stem cells (e.g., fertilized egg), pluripotent stem cells (e.g., embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells), multipotent stem cells (mesenchymal stem/stromal cells), and unipotent stem cells (e.g., hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), neural stem cells) [16–18]. Stem cells of diverse subtypes possess self-renewal property and are competent for intestinal lineage differentiation, and thus serve as advantageous sources for intestinal disease remodeling and cellular therapy [19–21]. For instance, Miao et al. reported the generation of vascularized intestinal organoids from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) for the recapitulation of the intestinal features and endothelial-epithelial crosstalk, while Ren et al. and Jwo et al. outlined the advancements in hiPSC-derived intestinal organoids for uncovering the IBD heterogeneity-related robust regulatory frameworks and hiPSC-derived neoantigens in colorectal cancer, respectively [22–24].

In this review article, we summarize the state-of-the-art renewal of stem cell-based research upon diverse intestinal disease remodeling and novel cytotherapy, together with the multifaceted challenges and remarkable prospects. As to disease remodeling, we outline the current progress in hPSC differentiation towards multiple intestinal lineage cells (e.g., intestinal stem cells, intestinal epithelial cells, immune-related cells and interstitial cells) and the three-dimensional (3D) organoids (e.g., intestinal organoids, organoid-on-a-chip, and organoid-based clinical trials). As to stem cell-based cytotherapy, we describe the landscape of stem cells and derivatives for intestinal disease intervention, including IBD, infectious intestinal diseases and colorectal cancer (CRC), together with the related intestinal disorders in clinical trials. Collectively, this review aims to supply references for the directions and perspectives of stem cell-based tactics for dissecting the mechanisms of intestinal diseases and facilitating innovative therapeutic strategies (Table 1).

Methods

Data collection and analysis

For the indicated raw data collection, we turned to the ClinicalTrials.gov website of National Institutes of Health (NIH) of the United States (https://clinicaltrials.gov/). As to organoid-based clinical trials, we searched the information with the keywords “intestinal disease” (Condition/disease) and “organoid” (Intervention/treatment), and a total number of 33 registered organoid-based clinical trials were enriched for dissecting intestinal disorders (up to December 31, 2025).

As to clinical trials of stem cell-based therapeutics, we searched the information with the keywords “intestinal disease” (Condition/disease) and “stem cells” (Intervention/treatment), and a total number of 227 registered clinical trials were enriched for dissecting intestinal disorders (up to September 30, 2025).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted as we described with the GraphPad Prism software V5 [25, 26] (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). In brief, the categories and the status of clinical trials were shown as pie graph, while the number and distribution of registered clinical trials were shown as line charts.

Stem cell differentiation towards intestinal lineage cells

Differentiation into intestinal epithelial cells (IECs)

The small and large intestinal epithelium functions essentially in nutrition, drug absorption and metabolism [27]. IECs contain transporters and metabolic enzymes, and function essentially in the metabolic and absorption processing of nutrients and medications. Differing from experimental disease models and epithelial cell models, IECs derived from hPSCs eliminate the possible disparities in pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies attributed to the variations in species and gene expression profiling [28] (Fig. 1, Table 2). For instance, Saari et al. compared the generation of small intestinal epithelial cells (SIECs) from hiPSCs under diverse culture conditions, and found the combination of serum-free medium and xeno-free substrata was competent for functional SIEC induction with enterocyte-specific cellular functionality [27]. This study represents significant progress for the induction of functionally mature IECs from hPSCs under xenogeneic-free system, and thus prompts the large-scale preparation of intestinal cells under good manufacturing practice (GMP) [29, 30]. For functional assessment and disease remodeling, Janssen et al. verified that hiPSC-derived IECs showed a comparable capacity to human primary IECs for the transport of chemicals including per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs), while Yamada et al. differentiated hiPSCs into SIECs for elucidating intracellular SARS-CoV-2 virus infection and drug development [31, 32]. Taken together, these findings put forward the promising prospects of hPSCs for IEC preparation and the concomitant mechanisms (Figs. 1, 2). However, the long-lasting unsolved issues during IEC induction from hPSCs are the main obstacles for translational applications in intestinal disease administration, including the low differentiation efficiency and purity, high cost and time consumption, ethical risk, insufficient maturity and potential tumorigenicity [33–36]. Of note, the safety and toxicity profile of hPSC-derived IECs should be treated seriously and solved carefully due to the karyotypic instability risks, teratoma risk from residual undifferentiated hPSCs, or systematic adverse event data from completed trials.

Differentiation into intestinal differentiated cells

To date, a certain number of two-dimensional (2D) and 3D protocols have been developed for the differentiation of hPSCs into intestinal differentiated cells, including absorptive enterocytes and secretory cells (Paneth cells, enteroendocrine cells, and goblet cells) [37–39] (Figs. 1, 2, Table 2). Currently, specific cell types of intestinal cells have been derived from hPSCs for illuminating intestinal development and regenerative medicine purposes via orchestrating signaling pathway networks, including Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, Notch signaling pathway, and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling [40]. However, the inherent limitations of hiPSCs and the derivatives (e.g., intestinal cells and organoids) for mimicking the in vivo microenvironment of patients with intestinal diseases as well as the multifaceted variations in cellular phenotype and omics features owing to the ex vivo culture stimulations should not be neglected [34, 41].

As to secretory cells like enterocytes, the induction of enterocyte-like cells from hiPSC-derived intestinal stem cells is achieved with the defined cocktail stimulation for drug transport- and metabolism-associated pharmacokinetic testing [37]. Meanwhile, with the aid of small molecule compounds, enterocytes are derived from hiPSCs for drug-metabolizing enzyme activity measurement via an intermediate stage for intestinal stem cell induction [42]. Instead, Paneth cells mainly serve as guardians in niche homeostasis and intestinal inflammation, and only limited literature has reported the sustained generation of Paneth cells with the exception of the cytokine-JAK-STAT signaling and Wnt/β-catenin signaling for enhancing Paneth cell lineage tracing and restricting Lgr5+ IESC differentiation into Paneth-like niche cells [43].

Enteroendocrine cells in the intestinal crypts are derived from common precursor cells under the sequential modulation by key transcription factors from absorptive enterocyte lineages [44]. At present, in vitro enteroendocrine cell induction and maturation from hPSCs are available via both inhibiting Notch signaling and the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors (Math1, Neurog3) [44]. Goblet cells secret mucin 2 (MUC2) in gut mucus and transmit the antigens in the intestine to the antigen-presenting cells via the GC-associated antigen passages (GAPs), which thus participate in intestinal barrier and immune regulation functions [45]. Therefore, the versatile investigators in the field have achieved the generation and maturation of goblet cells in functional conjunctival epithelium from hPSCs and conjunctival epithelial stem/progenitor cells under cytokine cocktail stimulation (e.g., EGF, KGF) for exploring intestinal development and pharmaceutical research, together with the underlying mechanisms (e.g., iron metabolism, Notch signaling) [46–50] (Figs. 1, 2, Table 2). These findings emphasize the feasibility of programming hPSCs into diverse intestinal differentiated cells for intestinal disease modeling and novel cellular therapeutics, yet the translational gaps between hPSC-based models and human intestinal diseases remain unresolved, including the safety concerns (tumorigenicity, ectopic tissue, alloimmunity) [51].

Differentiation into interstitial cells

Among interstitial cells, MSCs are located aside the intestinal stem cells (ISCs), and play an essential role in the proliferation and differentiation of small intestinal epithelial stem cells and their derivatives [28]. For decades, we and other investigators in the field have concentrated on large-scale and cost-effective generation of MSCs from hPSCs including the heterologous human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and the autologous hiPSCs via cell programming approaches (e.g., small molecule cocktails, gene overexpression including Yamanaka factors) and organoid technologies via modulating certain signaling cascades (e.g., WNT, PDGFR, JAK/STAT, and BMP signaling) [28, 33, 36, 51, 52]. For example, we and Wang et al. reported the generation of MSCs from hPSCs by modulating the BMP/MSX2 signaling cascades, which revealed considerable mitigative effect upon dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute colitis [33, 53]. These hPSC-derived MSCs (hPSC-MSCs) rather than those isolated from adult tissues provide overwhelming novel avenues for monitoring the bioprocess and molecular mechanisms during early mesenchymal differentiation [33, 54]. Furthermore, the organoid-derived MSCs resemble the trophocytes essential for LGR4+ ISC maintenance by secreting diverse trophic factors (e.g., R-spondin, WNT, BMP inhibitors) [28]. Simultaneously, despite the considerable consistency between hPSC-MSCs and adult tissue-derived counterparts, the further dissection of the similarities and otherness in biological functions and molecular features is the prerequisite for intestinal disease treatment and potential druggability [33, 55, 56]. Collectively, these data suggest the potent immunomodulatory property of stem cell-derived interstitial cells like MSCs as novel and ideal candidates for intestinal diseases (Figs. 1, 2, Table 2). Of note, most of the literature upon hPSC-MSCs is fundamental research at the preclinical stage for safety and effectiveness assessment in animal disease models rather than clinical outcome data from completed trials.

Differentiation into special cell types

In the intestinal system, certain special cell types like neurons and Tuft cells also function essentially for signal perception, nutrient metabolism and immune defense. As to neural lineage induction, investigators reported the hPSC-derived nitrergic neurons via inhibiting PDGFRs for the amelioration of gut dysmotility in mice, which robustly improved gastrointestinal motility and thus provided new frameworks for understanding chronic constipation and intestinal pseudo-obstruction [57]. Similarly, enteric neural crest cells (ENCCs) are also generated from hPSCs, which further facilitated the establishment of submucosal and myenteric ganglia in tissue-engineered small intestine and thus hold potential for enteric neuropathy treatment [58]. As to tuft cells characterized by chemosensory features in the intestinal epithelium for the group 2 innate lymphoid cell (ILC2) activation, researchers reported the tuft differentiation and acetylcholine production from IL-4-induced macaque intestinal organoids [59].

Of note, intestinal immune-related cells have also been differentiated from hPSCs, and the immune cells are able to temporally migrate to the mucosa that resembles the in vivo intestinal lymphoid follicles [60–63] (Fig. 1, Table 2). Intestinal microfold cells (M cells) in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue are characterized by pocket features for immune cells (e.g., T cells, B cells, and DCs) in the intestinal tract, which are involved in the bioprocess of bacterial transcytosis and the initiation of immune responses, together with applications in cancer immunotherapy [64, 65]. For microfold cell induction and human intestinal organogenesis, Bouffi and colleagues turned to human intestinal organoid (HIO) induction from hPSCs, and observed the immune cell aggregates and epithelial microfold cells in mice with the humanized immune system [60]. Very recently, functional tissue-resident macrophages have been induced from HIOs, which could persist for 2 weeks in vitro and for over 12 weeks in vivo, and are thus qualified for molecular mechanism studies of intestinal diseases including IBD [66]. Therefore, the rapid and simple derivation strategies to generate intestinal immune cells and neuronal spectra from hPSCs provide alternative avenues for dissecting intestinal bioprocess and pathobiology (Figs. 1, 2, Table 2). Notwithstanding, the inherent deficiency of the current protocols for differentiating hPSCs into special cell types and for functional assessment as mentioned above (e.g., multi-step manipulation, time- and money-consuming, cell purity and maturity) have largely hindered the application research upon intestinal diseases.

Stem cell-based organoids for intestinal disease diagnosis

Intestinal organoids

Organoids are 3D self-organizing models with remarkable structure and functional hallmarks for faithfully recapitulating the key features of the in vivo organs [25, 26, 67, 68]. Intestinal organoids serve as miniature hollow spheres with the representative intestinal epithelial structure, and thus effectively simulate the ecological niche of ISCs and the derivatives (e.g., IECs) [69]. For decades, a variety of conventional targets (e.g., Desmoglein-2, CLDN-2) and novel molecular targets (e.g., IL-22-pSTAT3 SP, SIRT2) as well as potential therapeutic approaches (e.g., sex hormones, bacterial indoleacrylic acid) have been identified with the aid of stem cell-based intestinal organoid models for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [70]. To some extent, the current intestinal models can retain the genetic and transcriptomic profile of in situ intestinal tissue and function as powerful pharmacological tools to deepen our understanding of IBD pathogenesis (e.g., gene mutations, the defect of antimicrobial peptide activities, and abnormal inflammatory state) and therapy personalization [71, 72]. Notably, it’s crucial to develop modified strategies for the faithful simulation and homogeneity of intestinal organoids by optimizing culture systems (e.g., medium, ECM scaffolds, bioreactors, detection technologies) and intestinal organoid transplantation [70, 73].

Current literature extensively reported the induction of HIOs and human colonic organoids (HCOs) from hPSCs for disease diagnosis and drug toxicity tests, together with the crosstalk with immune cells [74, 75] (Fig. 3, Table 3). For the efficient induction of mesenchyme-free HIOs, a directed differentiation protocol by utilizing the CDX2 knock-in reporter hiPSCs is established for the enrichment of intestinal progenitors and CFTR function evaluation [76]. Instead, hiPSC-derived colon organoids are employed for dissecting the virological characteristics of SARS-CoV-2 virus and the related coronaviruses (SC2r-CoVs) [77]. Furthermore, hPSC-derived HCOs are applied for the faithful recapitulation of the molecular signatures and inflammatory responses of patients with UC, and thus offer novel tools for understanding the pathologic mechanisms and developing effective therapeutic approaches [78]. Taken together, these findings highlight the broad application spectrum of diverse intestinal organoids in fundamental research and clinical practice (Fig. 3, Table 3). In particular, hPSC-derived intestinal organoids rather than those from tissues are adequate to mimic the early development of intestine and the mutation accumulation in intestinal diseases, yet the further applications for clinical purposes in current situations remain challenging due to the abovementioned inherent limitations.

Biomimetic organoid-on-a-chip (OoC)

The intestinal organoids have resolved certain long-standing challenges related to immortalized and cancer cell cultures, yet the closed topology as well as the relatively immature state partially hinders their applications [79, 80]. The emergence of micro-engineered OoC has approved the spatiotemporal investigations into pharmacological screening and immunotherapy responses of patients with refractory diseases including intestinal tumors [81] (Fig. 3). By developing a small intestine-on-chip based on hiPSC-derived intestinal epithelial cells, investigators are able to monitor the self-organized epithelial, mesenchymal, and neural cells [79]. With the aid of the complete methodological framework of microfluidic intestine-on-chip systems, IECs as well as myofibroblasts and neurons are respectively generated and self-organized in the top and bottom channels, which can capture the diversity of small intestinal epithelial barrier and resemble the multiple compartments of human small intestine [79]. Meanwhile, the hiPSC-derived personalized OoC platforms can be employed for recapitulating organ biology, host genetics and pharmacogenetic research [82].

As to mechanistic investigations, the microfluidic intestinal OoC has been reported to simulate the ultrafast oxygen dynamics mediated by hyperactivated NF-κB signaling during intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury [83]. Interestingly, by integrating hiPSC-derived visceral sensory ganglion organoids with HCOs on a microfluidic device, researchers can gain insights into the developmental trajectories and molecular signatures of gut-nerve-brain axis [84]. Collectively, OoC technology in combination with the related novel technologies (e.g., scRNA-seq, 3D bioprinting, in vivo tracing, gene-editing technique) has provided open topology and elevated platforms for both physiological and pathological intestinal research (e.g., pathogenesis, drug discovery, toxicology) (Fig. 3). These findings indicated the stem cell-derived OoCs as ideal model systems for monitoring the spatiotemporal landscapes of intestinal physiology (e.g., intestinal mucosa, intestinal epithelium) of the intestinal tract and precision medicine, yet the high costs and the technological complexity as well as the certain safety risks remain problematic for intestinal disease diagnosis and intervention.

Organoid-based clinical trials

Considering the robust advantages of organoids in disease simulation and pharmaceutical tests, stem cell-based organoids have been applied to simulate the intestinal diseases and the resultant precision medicine (Fig. 3). According to the ClinicalTrials.gov website of the NIH of the United States (https://clinicaltrials.gov/), 33 registered organoid-based clinical trials are enriched for dissecting intestinal disorders with the keywords (Condition/disease: intestinal disease; Intervention/treatment: organoid), including 16 interventional trials and 17 observational ones (Fig. 4a, Additional Information: Additional Table S1). Among them, 16 trials are recruiting, 5 trials have completed, 4 trials are not recruiting (Active not recruiting, 1; Not recruiting, 3), 1 trial is withdrawn, and the status of 7 trials is unknown (Fig. 4b). As to the location distribution, France, China and Italy are the top 3 countries with the most registered organoid-based trials among the 15 countries (Fig. 4c). With the aid of the line chart based on the number of the diseases (termed “Condition (Log2 N11)”) and the corresponding enrolled cases (termed “Enrollment (Log10 N12)”), we found the stem cell-based organoids were applied for diverse intestinal disorders, including CRC, colon cancer, intestinal disease, IBD, squamous cell carcinoma of the anus, gut microbiota, hypersensitivity, rectal cancer, cystic fibrosis, gut inflammation, cephalic duodenopancreatectomy, enterocolitis, irritable bowel syndrome, radiation enteritis, and pediatric intestinal pseudo-obstruction (PIPO). Of them, CRC and IBD rank as the top two diseases with the most registered trial number and enrollment, respectively (Fig. 4d, Table 4).

Organoid-based clinical trials for intestinal diseases

Considering the limited information in registered trials, the prognostic outcomes (e.g., safety profile, primary endpoints, adverse events, and primary/secondary outcomes) and potential hazards (e.g., immunogenicity, tumorigenic risks, and infectious pathogens) should be taken seriously and checked before in vivo transplantation [85]. For example, Okamoto et al. provided a critical discussion of the first human organoid transplantation trial and addressed immunogenicity concerns for both autologous and allogeneic intestinal epithelial organoid transplantation, while Solitano et al. introduced the therapeutic potential of organoid transplantation for tissue repair with detailed protocols and safety assessment frameworks [86, 87]. In detail, the patient-specific IECs from biopsies provide the potential to expand intestinal lineages and enable the following transplantation into the impaired intestinal areas, yet the long-term efficacy of intestinal organoids and the derivatives remains controversial due to the limitations of current organoid technologies [86, 88]. In terms of the preclinical and clinical applications, there are accumulating evidences of organoid-based explorations for intestinal tissue reconstruction and personalized medicine for disease intervention. Overall, therapeutic application of intestinal organoids may provide alternative options for epithelial restitution and mucosal healing of patients with diverse intestinal diseases in the near future.

Stem cells and derivatives for intestinal disease intervention

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

IBD is a chronic and relapsing inflammatory disorder consisting of UC and CD, which is characterized by continuous immune dysregulation of the intestinal mucosa, and coincides with shifts in the composition of the microbiota [70, 89, 90] (Fig. 3). Current literature has put forward the concept of stem cell-based regimens for IBD intervention, with particular emphasis on mesenchymal stem/stromal cells and their unique immunosuppressive capability. For instance, investigators in the field have identified MSCs of diverse origins for the remission of UC and CD such as hPSC-derived MSCs (hPSC-MSCs), placental-derived MSCs (P-MSCs), umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs), uterine myometrium-derived MSCs (UM-MSCs), adult tissue-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs), and bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) [91–95]. For the reinforced efficacy of MSCs upon IBD, genetic manipulations and bioengineering with biomaterials are conducted by investigators in the field (e.g., CCR2 or CXCR4 overexpression, the GelMA hydrogel, TLR5high-CMNP nanovesicles, and 3D-printed hydrogel-based suppositories) [96–100].

Meanwhile, mechanism studies have confirmed the bidirectional modulatory property of MSCs and paracrine of microvesicles (e.g., exosomes) for intestinal disorder administration both in clinical and preclinical investigations [96, 101–103], and certain candidate modulators (e.g., TLR3, SETD2, TSG-6) and signaling pathways have also been identified during the process (e.g., AMPK-FXR signaling, ELF3/miR-342-3p/KDM6B axis, TGF-β signaling) [101, 104–108]. Meanwhile, HSCs are also effective in the treatment of IBD via reinforcing epithelial function and improving microbiota dysbiosis and metabolites, while intestinal organoids are anticipated to conquer the limitations of current therapeutic strategies and thus act as potential treatment options for intestinal diseases including IBD [85]. Taken together, both the gene-edited and non-gene-edited MSCs as well as the concomitant microvesicles supply novel avenues for overcoming the intractable IBD (Fig. 3). With regard to MSC-based therapy, therapeutic potential and efficacy in patients with IBD or related intestinal diseases have been proven to be influenced by route-dependent variability (e.g., intraperitoneal vs. intravenous), subtypes of MSCs (CD106+, CD146+), and MSC-macrophage interactions, which collectively provide clear mechanistic explanations for contradictory outcomes [103, 109, 110].

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC)

As a life-threatening intestinal disorder, NEC has caused a certain morbidity and mortality in premature infants owing to the complexity in pathogenesis and limitations in therapeutic options [92]. Therewith, investigators have turned to stem cell-based therapies for conquering the incurable gut complication of prematurity like NEC [111] (Fig. 3). For instance, researchers have confirmed the ameliorative effect of MSCs upon epithelial morphology and intestinal damage in the experimental NEC model, and have demonstrated the effect of intraperitoneal MSC infusion upon intestinal injury in murine NEC models [112, 113]. However, the accompanying limitations and challenges of using stem cell-based treatments to resolve the complexities associated with intestinal disease treatment, including NEC should not be neglected. For instance, Zhang et al. and Zhao et al. observed the alterations in biological properties (e.g., cell viability, homing capacity) of MSCs and even the contradictory therapeutic efficacy after long-term ex vivo passage [114, 115]. We and our collaborators further verified the multifaceted variations in cellular phenotype and transcriptomic signatures of diverse MSCs under serum-containing and serum-free conditions [116]. These findings highlight the necessity and urgency of considering the technical, biological, and regulatory barriers through quality control for developing stem cells into effective clinical products [56, 117].

In the meantime, investigators have proposed the safety and treatment prospects of MSC-derived secretomes for the treatment of NEC models and early-stage clinical trials [118]. For example, Hwang and colleagues took advantage of extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from thrombin-preconditioned MSCs, and verified the alleviative effect upon experimental NEC mouse-derived organoids [119]. Furthermore, Weil et al. and Rager et al. elucidated the therapeutic effect and paracrine mechanisms of MSCs against NEC-related hypoxic injury via enhancing the repair and regeneration of fetal intestinal epithelial cells, while Chen et al. highlighted the enhanced paracrine effects of BM-MSCs with prolyl hydroxylase 2 (PHD2) silencing upon NEC-associated intestinal damage via a NF-κB-dependent manner [120–122]. These findings indicate that the stem cell-derived exosomes or EVs are advantageous sources for novel acellular therapy, yet the deficiency of applicable and standardized quality-control methods as well as the challenges in large-scale production further restrict the innovation of therapeutic products and their druggability [56]. Collectively, these data suggest the rescue effect of MSCs and the EVs for the intestinal injury during NEC, together with the indispensability of GMP for stem cell-based products and clinical applications.

Colorectal cancer (CRC)

CRCs rank as one of the leading malignancies and the prevalent tumors with high incidence and lethality rate. Worse still, the multifaceted limitations and adverse effects of conventional therapies further escalate the treatment prognosis [123]. In recent years, stem cells of diverse kinds have emerged as promising approaches for several cancer cytotherapy approaches, including HSCs for leukemia [124, 125], MSCs for anticancer-related fibrosis (e.g., breast and ovarian cancers) [126, 127], and stem cell-derived immune cells (e.g., natural killer cells, macrophages) for anti-cancer [63, 128–130]. The application of MSCs for CRC intervention and tumorigenesis remains controversial, which is intensely different from that for inflammatory-based disorders with promising outcomes [131]. Ocansey et al. previously summarized the encouraging achievements of MSC-based regimens in IBD and the related CRC as well as the challenges, including the potential involvement in tumor progression and the possible overestimation of efficacy [132]. Notwithstanding, autologous and allogeneic MSC-based iatreusis upon CRC is still confronted with the double-edge effects and tumor-associated mesenchymal cells involved in disease development and progression [132–135].

In spite of the controversial debates in CRC treatment, the feasibility of MSCs for confronting CRC-related fibrosis via suppressing the extracellular matrix (ECM)- and pro-fibroblast-associated genes (e.g., HGF, TSG-6) is available [136]. Consistently, researchers have demonstrated the significant outcomes of radiation-induced colorectal fibrosis after intraperitoneal injection of AD-MSCs and consecutive UC-MSCs, respectively [137, 138]. Of note, the novel chimeric antigen receptor-transduced T cells and natural killer cells (CAR-Ts, CAR-NKs) derived from hPSCs or HSCs have extensively improved the outcomes in patients with solid tumors including CRC [139–142]. Mechanistically, the underlying pro- vs anti-tumor mechanisms of stem cells and the derivatives are diverse and complex. For example, natural killer cells attenuate CRC progression via ligand-receptor binding, granzyme and perforin release, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and inflammatory factors (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α) [143, 144]. Instead, neutrophils are adequate to facilitate CRC progression via suppressing natural killer cells [145]. Taken together, stem cell-based cytotherapy is also a double-edged sword in CRC treatment in clinical practice, and should be conducted according to the specific scenario, especially in the tumor microenvironment (TME) with dual functions in CRC immunosurveillance and immunoediting [131] (Fig. 3). In detail, the cytokine cocktails in the TME are capable of establishing an immunosuppressive milieu, and thus impact the functional phenotypes of distinct cell populations (e.g., MSCs) and determine the development and metastasis of CRCs. For example, the secreted cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IDO) can influence the immunomodulatory capacity and immune responses of MSCs in the TME, and deteriorate angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis of CRC [146]. Even though the vast reports upon MSC-related biofunction in the TME, yet the detailed mechanistic explanation for why MSCs are pro-tumorigenic in some CRC contexts and anti-tumorigenic in others — including factors such as differing paracrine profiles, passage number, and TME cytokine concentration thresholds — remains insufficiently elaborated by the investigators in the field.

Clinical trials of stem cell-based therapeutics

To investigate the global landscape of stem cell-based therapeutics for intestinal disorders, we turned to the abovementioned ClinicalTrials.gov website, and a total number of 227 registered clinical trials were enriched with the keywords (Condition/disease: intestinal disease; Intervention/treatment: stem cells) by September 30th, 2025. Of them, those with the “withdrawn” status or that did not concurrently fulfill the subjects were eliminated, and 126 items were further enriched including 103 interventional trials and 23 observational trials (Fig. 5a, Additional Information: Additional Table S2). As shown in Fig. 5b, 62 trials have been completed (41 trials with the “Completed” status, 20 trials with the “Terminated” status, and one with the “Suspended” status), 11 trials are not recruiting (5 trials with the “Active not recruiting” status, 6 ones with the “Not recruiting” status), 16 trials are recruiting and 37 trials are unknown (Additional Information: Additional Table S2). As to the clinical stages of the registered trials, 78 trials are in the early stages (early phase, 1 trial; phase 1, 23 trials; phase 1/2, 35 trials; phase 2, 19 trials), 35 trials are in the not available (N/A) stage, 16 trials are in phase 3 stage, and only one trial is in phase 4 stage (Fig. 5c, Additional Information: Additional Table S2). As to the location distribution of the 126 trials, United States (46 trials), Spain (16 trials) and China (16 trials) are the top 3 countries with the registered stem cell-based clinical trials for intestinal disorders (Fig. 5d, Additional Information: Additional Table S2).

Clinical trials of stem cell-based research for intestinal diseases

According to those advanced or completed clinical trials, MSC-guided treatment in intestinal diseases has been extensively reported. As to one representative phase Ⅰ-Ⅱ trial (trial No. NCT01157650) for fistulous CD intervention, clinicians assessed the primary and secondary outcomes (security and tolerance) of autologous adipose tissue-derived MSC (AD-MSC) implant, and verified the satisfactory prognosis such as fistula healing efficiency, improvements in quality of life and systemic CD, which were confirmed by the follow-up visits (1, 4, 12, 24 weeks, and 1 year) and relative literature [147–149]. In another trial (trial No. NCT01541579) by Panés J et al. with 212 enrolled patients (including 105 cases with placebo, 107 cases with AD-MSCs), an expanded allogeneic AD-MSC product (Cx601) was applied for CD-related complex perianal fistulas treatment, and an improvement in combined remission rate and a decline in related adverse events were collectively observed in patients with MSC infusion [150]. Meanwhile, various investigations including 18 human randomized controlled trials or single-arm studies (n = 360) and 28 animal works (n = 567) have highlighted the unexpected safety and effectiveness (e.g., CD clinical parameters scores, C-reactive protein levels, inflammatory bowel disease questionnaire) of MSC-based “biological therapies” for CD, which attributes to the low cellular immunogenicity and immunomodulation [151]. Considering the variations in trial registrations, phases, and geographic distribution, the synthesis of clinical outcome data is urgently needed from the viewpoints of efficacy endpoints, response rates, or adverse event profiles from completed trials in a structured way. For example, the short-term effects and long-term effects of MSC-based regimens upon patients with intestinal disorders. Taken together, these state-of-the-art meta-analyses and clinical trials collectively put forward the feasibility and reliability of MSC-based therapeutic strategies for intestinal disorders. Notably, considering the complicated or contradictory outcomes during early and preliminary investigations, the integration of quality control and spatiotemporal metabolokinetics of MSCs and hPSC-derived products is the inevitable routine for investigational new drug application for intestinal diseases [56, 152, 153].

Prospective and challenges

The intestine serves as the largest organ for nutrient absorption and metabolism in the body, and the dysregulations of immune homeostasis and microbial imbalance commonly result in various intestinal disorders and the concomitant complications. Due to the limitations and adverse effects of traditional drug therapy and surgical treatment, it’s imperative to assess the novel stem cell-based strategies for disease modeling, drug discovery and innovative cytotherapy. Since the year of 1907, the 3D “in a dish” organoids have been extensively reported for disease remodeling and pharmacological screening purposes [154]. Herein, we conduct a systematic review of the current literature upon stem cell-related disease remodeling and regenerative medicine, and highlight the feasibility and practicality of stem cells and derivatives (e.g., intestinal lineages and organoids) for investigating the pathogenesis and intervention for intestinal diseases.

Organoid maturation and vascularization

Current literature has further highlighted the feasibility of direct differentiation of hPSCs into HIOs or intestinal tissue with neuron and immune cell lineages both in vitro and in vivo, which tremendously accelerates the transformation application of stem cells for intestinal disease administration [66, 155, 156]. Instead, Jun and the colleagues demonstrated the pivotal role of interleukin-2 (IL-2) in the maturation of hPSC-derived intestinal organoids and the generation of crypt and villus domains [157]. Simultaneously, Takahashi et al. introduced the suspension culture system for the efficient generation and maturation of HIOs from hPSCs by utilizing a rotating bioreactor, which offered a reliable platform for translational medical applications [158].

Intestinal organoids derived from adult intestinal tissues and hPSCs can recapitulate the key features of the original organ physiologically and pathologically, yet the generation of vascularized intestinal organoids with immune cells from hiPSCs for mechanistic exploration and transformation purposes is still urgently needed [28, 159]. Despite certain consistency in disease modeling, hPSC-derived intestinal organoids can encompass epithelium and mesenchyme, while those generated from adult ISCs or Lgr5+ stem cells are more accessible to researchers and clinicians in the field for long-term organoid culture [85]. That is, both the hPSC-derived ISCs and adult tissue-derived ISCs possess good value for precision medicine, and the choice of using appropriate types of models depends on the specific experimental objectives and design.

In the meantime, there are common limitations and challenges of current organoid systems to be addressed in recapitulating the inflammatory microenvironment and maintaining acute inflammatory phenotype during ex vivo culture of intestinal organoids [85]. Therewith, current intestinal organoids possess certain limitations for disease remodeling (e.g., lack of vascularization, immune cells, microbiota). For example, the application of organoids for dissecting microbiota-organoid interactions relevant to IBD pathogenesis is still difficult largely attributes to the deficiency of feasible organoid-microbiota co-culture models. Therefore, a certain number of researchers turn to alternative microbial extract for mimicking the microbial dysbiosis or host-microbe interaction in vivo. For the purpose, investigators have reported hPSC-derived intestinal organoids with native organ-specific endogenous vascular endothelial cells and enteric nervous system, respectively [160, 161]. Considering the multifaceted pathogenetic factors and etiopathogenesis during intestinal diseases, the further optimization of stem cell-based intestinal models with structural and functional maturation should encompass host genetic factors, microbial flora, immune system dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction and microenvironmental stimulators [85]. Meanwhile, the identification of differentiation trajectories and molecular characteristics (e.g., BMP ligands, EMT-related polarity) along the crypt-villus axis from hPSCs or ISCs toward the mature absorptive and secretory cell lineages is also essential for dissecting intestinal diseases [79, 162].

Standardization, quality control, and biobanking

Simultaneously, the standardization of stem cell-based protocols is critically imperative due to the long-lasting challenges such as reproducibility in organoid systems, the heterogeneity of patient-derived samples and the technical limitations associated with manufacturing clinical-grade products [163, 164]. For example, the novel preparation of transplantable organoids using only three ingredients and the generation of live buds under chemically defined stimulation have been reported, respectively [68, 165–167]. With the aid of self-assembled intestinal organoids, functional intestinal epithelial stem cells and enterocytes were robustly generated from PDGFRα+ MSCs in the intestinal epithelial cell stroma [28, 168].

The generation and cryopreservation of definitive endoderm for large-scale intestinal lineage preparation from hPSCs under defined and scalable culture conditions (chemically-defined, Xeno-free medium) are of equal importance [30, 169]. For improving the comparability and reproducibility of stem cell-derived intestinal organoids, the recommended guidelines with crucial parameters have been highlighted, including culture conditions, quality control measures, critical quality attributes, and functional assessments [164]. Due to the multidimensional variations and the potential large-scale application of stem cell-based fundamental research and clinical application, there’s also an urgent demand for the establishment of stem cell biobanks and organoid biobanks [116, 170–172].

Novel technology integration

Of note, pioneering investigators in the field have turned to integrating intestinal OoC technology and the in vivo transplantation for the proof-of-concept demonstration of pathogenesis (e.g., ROS accumulation) and functional maturation. Meanwhile, machine learning algorithms integrated with transcriptomic technologies (e.g., RNA-seq, scRNA-seq) effectively outline the key genetic signatures and signaling pathways in intestinal organoid-based models [173] (Fig. 3). However, the current organoid construction systems cannot completely recapitulate the complex in vivo environment of the human intestine, and reveal certain functional defects (e.g., neurogenesis, deficiency in vessels, smooth muscle cells, immune cells, host cell-microorganism interactions) and tissue differences [70, 174].

Therewith, the faithful recapitulation of the architecture, functionality, organogenesis, and genetic signature of original intestinal tissues needs continuous technological innovation and breakthroughs in organoid model systems (e.g., 3D bioprinting, nanobiomaterials, microfluidic chip, microbial environment, physicochemical stimulators). Meanwhile, it’s noteworthy that the significant transcriptional differences between organoid-derived cells and the original in vivo counterparts further emphasize the necessity of optimizing organoid models for intestinal disease modeling [23]. Additionally, investigators have also introduced alternative biomaterials (e.g., PEG-4MAL hydrogels, synthetic hydrogels, poly (ethylene glycol)/RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp)) analogous to native human tissues rather than Matrigel for the generation, maintenance, differentiation, and in vivo delivery of human organoids from hPSCs [175–177]. These persistent attempts and multifaceted improvements upon the preparation of hPSC-derived organoids will robustly facilitate the standardization and application of stem cell-based regenerative medicine.

Ethical considerations and regulatory framework

Of note, the ethical norms and criteria for the stem cell-based intestinal studies should be updated according to the new requirements and challenges in the field [25, 26]. Nevertheless, all the procedures and applications of stem cell-based therapies must fulfill the ethical considerations, including ethics approval, the donor consent, clinical guidelines, organoid transplantation, and the Declaration of Helsinki [178, 179].

Organoids derived from hPSCs or adult tissues are unique models for illuminating homo-specific characteristics of organ development and disease pathogenesis, and the HIOs are advantaged resources for intestinal repair and regeneration attribute to their capacity for recapitulating both mesodermal and endodermal components of human intestine [180–182] (Fig. 3). Overall, the burgeoning field of stem cell-based tactics, and in particular, the well-established models (hPSCs and the intestinal derivatives, MSCs and organoids) have provided tremendous prospects for intestinal disease remodeling and treatment in the near future.
