Abstract
. We present the first systematic study of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on video representations. Standard SAEs decompose video into interpretable, monosemantic features but destroy temporal coherence: hard TopK selection produces unstable feature assignments across frames, reducing autocorrelation by 36%. We propose spatio-temporal contrastive objectives and Matryoshka hierarchical grouping that recover and even exceed raw temporal coherence. The contrastive loss weight controls a tunable trade-off between reconstruction and temporal coherence. A systematic ablation on two backbones and two datasets shows that different configurations excel at different goals: reconstruction fidelity, temporal coherence, action discrimination, or interpretability. Contrastive SAE features improve action classification by +3.9% over raw features and text-video retrieval by up to 2.8 × \times R@1. A cross-backbone analysis reveals that standard monosemanticity metrics contain a backbone-alignment artifact: both DINOv2 and VideoMAE produce equally monosemantic features under neutral (CLIP) similarity. Causal ablation confirms that contrastive training concentrates predictive signal into a small number of identifiable features.



1.
Introduction

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have transformed the interpretability of language models
(
2
;
21
)
and, more recently, static image encoders
(
15
;
19
)
. Yet video vision models remain underexplored. From self-supervised encoders like DINOv2
(
14
)
and VideoMAE
(
22
)
to multimodal retrieval and captioning systems, we lack tools to understand what these models learn internally. Whether SAEs can meaningfully decompose
video
representations, where features are spatio-temporal rather than spatial, is an open question.

We observe that the challenge is structural. A video clip produces a feature tensor spanning
T
frames and
P
spatial patches, where the same semantic concept (a hand, a moving object) should ideally activate consistent features across time. Standard SAEs, encoding each patch independently, are oblivious to this structure. This raises two questions:
Can SAEs reveal interpretable structure in video features?
And
does the temporal dimension require new methodology?

We find that standard SAEs produce monosemantic video features but fail to preserve temporal coherence, making it impossible to track concepts across frames. Our central finding is a design principle: the contrastive strength controls the trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and temporal coherence. Both the loss coefficient
\lambda
and the temperature
\tau
modulate this strength through different mechanisms (
\lambda
scales the loss magnitude,
\tau
controls the sharpness of positive/negative separation), but both move variants along the same Pareto frontier. This is not a limitation to work around. It is a controllable axis that practitioners can set based on their application. Our contributions:

(1)

Systematic empirical study of SAEs on video representations.
We train TopK SAEs on two backbones (DINOv2 and VideoMAE) and two datasets (SSv2 and K400), demonstrating that SAEs decompose video features into monosemantic, interpretable concepts such as scene elements, objects, and action-correlated features. Video-native models (VideoMAE) score substantially higher on DINOv2-based monosemanticity metrics than image models (MS
=0.972
vs.
0.359
), but a CLIP-based control reveals both produce equally monosemantic features (MS
\approx
0.68), identifying a metric alignment artifact in the standard evaluation protocol.

(2)

Diagnosis of temporal incoherence as a core failure mode.
We show that standard SAEs destroy temporal structure: lag-1 autocorrelation drops 36%, with 95% of features below the 0.3 coherence threshold. We trace this to the hard top-
k
selection mechanism and show via causal ablation that the resulting features, while temporally unstable, are nonetheless predictively important for action discrimination (5
\times
stronger ablation signal than random features under probe-based ablation).

(3)

A controllable reconstruction–coherence trade-off.
Extending T-SAE
(
1
)
from text to video, we evaluate three contrastive variants (temporal, separate, raster-scan) and Matryoshka hierarchical grouping. A systematic ablation across 29 configurations reveals that the contrastive strength (via
\lambda
or
\tau
) controls where a variant sits on the Pareto frontier. At low weight (
\lambda{=}0.01
), all variants preserve reconstruction (
R^{2}=0.31{-}0.42
). At high weight, Temporal+M exceeds raw temporal coherence (lag-1
=0.462
vs. 0.435). The best configurations improve action discrimination by +3.9% over raw features and boost text-video retrieval by 2.8
\times
R@1 on DINOv2. Matryoshka grouping concentrates action-relevant information into 20% of the dictionary.

2.
Related Work

Sparse Autoencoders for Interpretability.

SAEs were introduced to decompose neural network activations into sparse, monosemantic features
(
2
;
4
)
. Subsequent work scaled SAEs to frontier language models
(
21
)
. Key architectural innovations include TopK activation
(
6
)
, JumpReLU
(
18
)
, BatchTopK with Matryoshka grouping
(
3
)
for hierarchical feature organization, and auxiliary losses for dead latent recovery.

SAEs for Vision Models.

Recent work has extended SAEs to vision encoders, establishing that sparse decomposition yields interpretable visual features. Pach et al.
(
15
)
train SAEs on CLIP and introduce the MonoSemanticity evaluation framework we adopt in this work, demonstrating that SAE interventions on the vision encoder can steer multimodal LLM outputs. Stevens et al.
(
19
)
enable patch-level causal edits via SAEs on frozen ViTs, providing a rigorous causal methodology that motivates our ablation experiments. Joseph et al.
(
9
)
provide a steerability analysis of CLIP’s vision transformer, and Lim et al.
(
12
)
introduce PatchSAE for concept-level analysis during adaptation. While these works demonstrate the promise of SAEs for vision, they all operate on static images. None examine how the temporal structure of video interacts with sparse decomposition, which is the focus of our study.

Temporal SAEs.

Bhalla et al.
(
1
)
propose Temporal SAEs (T-SAEs) for language models, adding contrastive losses on adjacent tokens to disentangle semantic from positional features. They demonstrate that temporal consistency improves feature quality for text sequences. We extend this temporal contrastive principle from 1D text sequences to spatio-temporal video representations, where the challenge is qualitatively different: video tokens have both spatial adjacency within frames and temporal adjacency across frames, and the visual content at a given spatial position can change substantially between frames due to motion. Our raster-scan variant (Section
3.2
) addresses this by unifying spatial and temporal contrastive objectives in a single loss.

Temporal Consistency in Video Representations.

Object-centric approaches such as Slot Contrast
(
25
)
learn temporally consistent slots by contrasting object representations across frames. Spatio-temporal attention attribution methods
(
24
)
provide post-hoc explanations of video model predictions. Temporal contrastive self-supervised methods such as TCLR
(
5
)
promote temporal diversity, while multi-pretext frameworks like CSTP
(
26
)
learn temporal structure during pretraining. Optical flow methods like RAFT
(
20
)
provide dense correspondences that could serve as temporal pairing targets. Our approach is complementary to these methods. We decompose frozen features into sparse dictionaries rather than learning new representations or modifying pretraining. Our adjacency-based contrastive losses operate on fixed spatial positions rather than tracked objects. This is a deliberate design choice: SAE features should decompose the model’s representation as-is, without introducing external correspondence mechanisms. We discuss the limitations of fixed spatial pairing under camera motion in Section
7
.

Interpretability for Action Models.

Bear et al.
(
8
)
apply mechanistic interpretability to Vision-Language-Action models for robotics using logit lens projections. Khan et al.
(
11
)
use SAEs on VLA residual streams to construct steering vectors for robotic control. Both operate in the robotics domain with action-specific models. Our work addresses general-purpose video vision models and establishes interpretability foundations applicable to any video understanding system.

3.
Method

3.1.
Applying SAEs to Video Features

Given a video clip, we extract intermediate features from a vision backbone, obtaining a tensor
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times P\times D}
with
T
frames,
P
spatial patches per frame, and
D
-dimensional features. For DINOv2 ViT-B/14, we obtain
(T{=}16,P{=}256,D{=}768)
; for VideoMAE-B,
(T{=}8,P{=}196,D{=}768)
.

To train a standard SAE, we flatten
\mathbf{X}
to
(T\cdot P,D)
and treat each patch as an independent token. A TopK SAE
(
6
)
learns an overcomplete representation:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\text{TopK}(\text{ReLU}(W_{e}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{\text{pre}})+\mathbf{b}_{e}),k)

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=W_{d}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{pre}}

where
H\gg D
is the dictionary size,
\text{TopK}(\cdot,k)
retains only the
k
largest activations, and the loss combines reconstruction with auxiliary dead latent recovery:

(3)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}=\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}

This approach is agnostic to the spatio-temporal structure of video. The SAE sees no difference between patches from the same frame and patches from different frames.

3.2.
Spatio-Temporal SAE Variants

To preserve temporal and spatial structure, we propose three contrastive variants and a hierarchical grouping mechanism. All contrastive variants augment
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}
with InfoNCE losses
(
23
)
that encourage consistent SAE activations between nearby patches, using negative pairs to prevent collapse toward trivially uniform features.

Variant 1: Temporal Contrastive (T-SAE).

For the same spatial position
p
across consecutive frames
t
and
t{+}1
, we encourage consistent activations:

(4)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{t,p}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{t+1,p}^{(i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\text{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{t,p}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}_{t+1,j}^{(i)})/\tau)}

where
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity and
\tau
is a temperature parameter. The similarity is computed on the full sparse activation vectors
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
, including zeros. Because TopK produces exactly
k{=}64
nonzero entries per token, the cosine similarity is well-defined and nondegenerate. Gradients flow through the contrastive loss to the encoder via the nonzero activations only. The TopK selection itself is non-differentiable, but the selected values retain their gradients (straight-through on the values, hard mask on the indices), following the standard TopK SAE training procedure of Gao et al.
(
6
)
. We did not observe training instability from this. Positive pairs share the same spatial position across adjacent frames. Negatives are all other patches in the batch, drawn from both the same clip and other clips. This means negatives include both semantically similar patches (same clip, different position) and dissimilar patches (different clips), providing a mix of hard and easy negatives. This directly extends T-SAE
(
1
)
from 1D text sequences to the temporal axis of video.

Variant 2: Separate Contrastive (ST-SAE).

Video features have structure along
both
time and space. Variant 1 only enforces temporal consistency; ST-SAE adds a spatial contrastive loss between horizontally adjacent patches within each frame:

(5)

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}+\lambda_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}+\lambda_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\text{spat}}

The two losses operate independently, allowing separate control over temporal and spatial coherence via
\lambda_{t}
and
\lambda_{s}
.

Variant 3: Raster-Scan Contrastive (R-SAE).

Instead of separate spatial and temporal losses, we serialize the
(T,P)
grid into a 1D sequence via raster-scan ordering (row-by-row within each frame, then across frames) and apply a single contrastive loss on adjacent elements. At frame boundaries, adjacent pairs are cross-frame temporal neighbors, automatically unifying spatial and temporal coherence in one loss term.

Matryoshka Hierarchical Grouping (+M).

Orthogonal to the contrastive losses, we apply Matryoshka BatchTopK
(
3
)
to partition the
H{=}6{,}144
-dimensional dictionary into a high-level group (features
0{-}1{,}227
, 20% of the dictionary) and a low-level group (features
1{,}228{-}6{,}143
, 80%). Both groups are trained jointly via BatchTopK with shared
k{=}64
. The high-level group is encouraged to be self-sufficient via an auxiliary reconstruction loss:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{mat}}=\alpha_{\text{mat}}\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{high}}\|_{2}^{2}
where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{high}}
is decoded using only high-level features and
\alpha_{\text{mat}}=0.1
. The motivation is to concentrate action-relevant, semantic features into a compact subset while relegating fine-grained details to the remaining features. We ablate the split ratio (5/95, 20/80, 50/50, 80/20) in the Supplementary Material. This can be combined with any contrastive variant (denoted +M, e.g., Temporal+M, Separate+M).

The contrastive and Matryoshka objectives compete with reconstruction for the SAE’s limited sparse capacity. The contrastive weight (
\lambda
or temperature
\tau
) controls this trade-off. At low weight, variants preserve reasonable reconstruction (
R^{2}\approx 0.31{-}0.42
). At high weight, they shift toward
task-oriented sparse encoders
that prioritize temporal coherence and action discrimination. We systematically ablate this trade-off in Section
6.1
.

Experimental Setup

We extract features from: (1)
DINOv2 ViT-B/14

(

)

(16,256,768)

VideoMAE-B

(8,196,768)

Datasets.

(1)
Something-Something v2 (SSv2)

(
7
)

Kinetics-400 (K400)

(
10
)
: 400 action classes with diverse scenes and appearance variation, testing generalization beyond fine-grained actions.

All SAEs use expansion factor 8
\times
(

), TopK with
k=64
, and auxiliary loss coefficient
\alpha=0.03
. SAEs are trained for 10 epochs on 10,000 clips. Standard SAEs use batch size 4,096 on flattened tokens; clip-based variants use batch size 8 on whole clips. We set
\lambda_{t}=0.1
,
\lambda_{s}=0.05
,
\tau=0.1
. All experiments use 3 random seeds. Variance across seeds is below 1% for all reported metrics, so we report means throughout. Training times on A100 GPUs: standard SAE (flat tokens) trains in
\sim
30 minutes; clip-based contrastive variants train in
\sim
60 minutes (
\sim
2
\times

\sim

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate along seven axes:

(1) Reconstruction quality
:
R^{2}
(variance explained).

(2) Temporal coherence
: mean lag-1 autocorrelation of SAE features across frames.

(3) MonoSemanticity Score (MS)
: activation-weighted pairwise DINOv2 cosine similarity of each feature’s top-activating clips, following Pach et al.
(
15
)
.

(4) Action purity
: fraction of a feature’s top-
k
activating clips sharing the dominant action template.

(5) Linear probe
: top-1 accuracy of logistic regression on SAE features for action classification.

(6) Sparsity
:
L_{0}
and dead feature fraction.

(7) Feature uniqueness
: Jaccard index between top-activating clip sets across features (lower = more unique).

5.
SAEs on Video: Interpretability and Temporal Failure

We first show that standard SAEs decompose video features into interpretable, monosemantic concepts. We then diagnose a core failure mode: temporal incoherence from hard TopK selection.

5.1.
Standard SAE Analysis

Both backbones produce well-behaved SAEs on SSv2 (10K clips,
8\times
expansion). SAE features outperform raw features on action classification for both models, with exact sparsity (
L_{0}=64
) and no dead features. Jaccard indices below 0.01 confirm that features partition the input space into distinct, non-overlapping concepts.

5.2.
The Temporal Coherence Problem

While standard SAEs produce interpretable features, they suffer from a fundamental limitation when applied to video: they destroy temporal coherence. Because each patch token is encoded independently, the top-
k
selection mechanism can choose entirely different active features for the same spatial location across consecutive frames, even when the underlying visual content changes only slightly.

We measure temporal coherence as the mean lag-1 autocorrelation of SAE features across consecutive frames (Table
2
). Raw DINOv2 features have lag-1
=0.435
; after SAE encoding, this drops to
0.277
(a 36% reduction). Temporal contrastive training partially recovers coherence (0.291). In practice, this means a feature representing “hand pushing object” may fire in frame 3, disappear in frame 4, and reappear in frame 6, making concept tracking across time unreliable. The severity varies by layer (lag-1 up to 0.75 at layer 3; see Section
6.5
).

Do simple fixes suffice?

Before evaluating learned spatio-temporal objectives, we ask: can simple post-hoc smoothing resolve the temporal incoherence without modifying SAE training? We consider two baselines:
EMA smoothing
(
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=\alpha\mathbf{z}_{t}+(1-\alpha)\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t-1}
) and
Temporal Union TopK
(reselecting top-
k
from the union of active features at frames
t
and
t{-}1
).

All SAE variants outperform raw features.

The standard SAE (19.1%) surpasses raw DINOv2 (17.4%), and contrastive variants reach up to 20.4%. These numbers reflect a frozen-feature linear probe, not end-to-end fine-tuning (supervised DINOv2 achieves

The reconstruction–coherence trade-off is controllable.

All contrastive variants sacrifice reconstruction relative to the standard SAE (
R^{2}=0.454
). At low contrastive weight (
\lambda{=}0.01

Matryoshka variants are the strongest overall.

Temporal+M achieves the best probe accuracy (20.4%), the highest temporal coherence among contrastive variants (lag-1
=0.360
at
\lambda{=}0.01
), and at
\tau{=}0.2

exceeds

=0.462

This separation allows the contrastive loss to improve temporal coherence without sacrificing discrimination. We recommend Temporal+M as the default variant for applications that require both interpretability and action-discriminative features.

Temporal and Separate offer balanced discrimination.

Temporal achieves the best probe accuracy among non-Matryoshka variants (20.2%) and concentrates the most predictive signal into the fewest features: just 10 ablated features cause a
-9.3
pp drop in the causal ablation (Table
4
). Separate matches Temporal’s probe while adding independent spatial consistency, but distributes predictive signal more broadly. Both are well-suited when Matryoshka’s hierarchical structure is unnecessary and a single contrastive loss suffices.

Raster excels at reconstruction and monosemanticity.

Raster preserves the highest
R^{2}
(0.418 at
\lambda{=}0.01
) and achieves the best MS score (0.433 at
\lambda{=}0.5
). Its pair-based loss applies only
local
contrastive pressure between adjacent patches, disrupting reconstruction less than clip-level objectives (temporal/separate) which enforce global consistency across all frames. The same local consistency acts as an inductive bias for monosemanticity: each feature must agree with both its spatial and temporal neighbors, forcing coherent single-concept representations. However, probe accuracy drops at high
\lambda
because this local consistency redirects capacity away from global action discrimination. Raster is best suited for applications prioritizing reconstruction fidelity or feature interpretability over downstream classification.

6.2.
Action Purity Analysis

Spatio-temporal variants produce features that more consistently activate on clips sharing the same action template. Across the top-50 features on DINOv2/SSv2, mean action purity increases from 1.36/8 (standard) to 1.60/8 (temporal contrastive) to 1.74/8 (raster contrastive). The raster variant achieves features where half of the top-8 clips share a single action (max purity 4/8), compared to 3/8 for the standard SAE.

This difference has a mechanistic explanation. The temporal contrastive loss encourages the same features to fire across consecutive frames at the same spatial position. This creates a small set of “anchor” features that reliably track temporal structure. The probe learns to weight these anchors heavily, making the dictionary efficient but fragile to targeted ablation. Raster’s unified loss, by contrast, enforces consistency across all adjacent pairs (including within-frame spatial neighbors), distributing the contrastive signal more broadly. The result is a larger set of moderately important features rather than a few critical ones. Separate+M combines Matryoshka’s hierarchical concentration (action-relevant features in the high-level group) with separate spatial and temporal losses, producing a dictionary where importance is concentrated but distributed across the entire high-level group rather than a handful of individual features.

Cross-model validation (DINOv2 vs. VideoMAE), Matryoshka split analysis, and cross-dataset validation (K400) are provided in the Supplementary Material. Spatio-temporal objectives are most beneficial for DINOv2, which lacks temporal pretraining. VideoMAE’s temporal pretraining already provides much of the structure these losses aim to learn. The Matryoshka 20/80 split concentrates action-relevant information into 20% of the dictionary (19.6% probe using only 1,228 of 6,144 features). Results generalize to K400, where temporal contrastive reduces the SAE-to-raw gap by 44%.

6.5.
Layer Analysis

We analyze SAE behavior across network depth (layers 3, 7, 11) for both backbones (full tables in Supplementary Material). For DINOv2, action discrimination increases monotonically with depth (layer 11 best at 20.2%). Temporal contrastive training is most beneficial at the final layer, where it improves both coherence and probe accuracy. At earlier layers, the contrastive loss overwhelms reconstruction, degrading probe accuracy (11.3%
\to
2.4% at layer 7). For VideoMAE, layer 7 achieves 32.9% probe, more than double layer 11’s 15.9%, suggesting intermediate representations are most action-discriminative. Temporal contrastive has minimal effect on VideoMAE at any layer, likely because its temporal pretraining already provides coherent features.

Learned vs. random dictionaries.

To verify that SAE dictionaries learn meaningful structure rather than acting as random overcomplete projections, we train a control SAE with frozen random decoder weights (same architecture, only the encoder is optimized). At 10K clips, the learned decoder achieves
R^{2}=0.910
(clip-pooled) compared to
R^{2}=0.578
for the frozen decoder. This large gap confirms that the decoder learns a structured dictionary. Probe accuracy is similar (19.7% learned vs. 19.6% frozen), indicating that the encoder extracts discriminative features regardless of decoder quality. The decoder’s role is reconstruction fidelity, not discrimination.

Practical recommendations.

We recommend four operating points (see Table
3
): Raster at low
\lambda
for reconstruction fidelity, Temporal+M at moderate
\tau
for temporal coherence, Separate at high
\lambda
for action discrimination, and Raster at high
\lambda
for interpretability. Spatio-temporal objectives are most beneficial for models lacking temporal pretraining (DINOv2). VideoMAE’s temporal pretraining already provides much of the structure these losses aim to learn.

Limitations.

Our temporal contrastive losses pair the same spatial position across consecutive frames. This assumes approximate spatial correspondence. SSv2 uses stationary cameras filming tabletop interactions, so the vast majority of clips satisfy this assumption (objects move within the frame but the camera does not). K400 contains more camera motion, which may explain the smaller contrastive gains on that dataset. When the assumption is violated, the contrastive loss receives noisy positive pairs, which weakens but does not collapse the training signal (InfoNCE is robust to moderate label noise). Optical-flow-based correspondences
(
20
)
could address this but would introduce external dependencies and lose the model-agnostic property of our approach. We fix
k{=}64
and expansion factor
8\times
throughout. Varying these may shift the reconstruction–coherence frontier. Our main results use 10K clips. Scaling experiments at larger training scales (Supplementary Material) confirm that probe accuracy improves and the contrastive advantage persists. Our retrieval uses SSv2’s formulaic action templates. Results on naturalistic benchmarks (MSR-VTT, DiDeMo) may differ. We report means across 3 seeds. Maximum observed standard deviation across all metrics is 0.3 pp for probe accuracy and 0.005 for
R^{2}
. Borderline comparisons (e.g., 20.1% vs. 20.2%) should not be overinterpreted.

8.
Conclusion

We presented the first systematic study of Sparse Autoencoders on video representations and established a design principle for spatio-temporal SAEs: the contrastive strength (via loss coefficient or temperature) is a controllable axis that determines the reconstruction–coherence operating point.

Four findings support this principle. (1) Standard SAEs decompose video features into monosemantic concepts; a cross-backbone control reveals both DINOv2 and VideoMAE produce equally monosemantic features under neutral (CLIP) similarity, identifying a metric alignment artifact in prior evaluation protocols. (2) Standard SAEs destroy temporal coherence (36% lag-1 reduction) due to hard TopK selection. (3) Contrastive SAE variants can exceed raw temporal coherence (lag-1
=0.462
vs. 0.435) while improving action classification by +3.9% and text-video retrieval by 2.8
\times
R@1. (4) The contrastive weight determines where each variant sits on the Pareto frontier. Low weight preserves reconstruction (
R^{2}=0.31{-}0.42
). High weight maximizes temporal coherence. No variant dominates all metrics, but four clear operating points emerge for reconstruction, coherence, discrimination, and interpretability.

A frozen decoder control confirms that SAE dictionaries learn meaningful structure (
R^{2}=0.910
learned vs.
0.578
frozen). A temperature sweep over sparsemax shows that soft sparsity can partially bridge the gap to hard TopK (
L_{0}
from 1.0 to 20.2 at
T{=}50
, recovering 19.8% probe accuracy) but cannot fully replace it. These results motivate hybrid soft-hard sparsity mechanisms and multiresolution decompositions as promising directions for video feature interpretability.
