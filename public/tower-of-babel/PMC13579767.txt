Identification of IMP-8-carrying Comamonas thiooxydans strains from a diarrheal sample: genomic insights into plasmid-borne carbapenemase in an environmental bacterium
Authors: Luyao Ma, Tongxi Hu, Jinjuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe proliferation of carbapenem-resistant bacteria, facilitated by carbapenemase enzymes, constitutes a significant public health threat. Although the metallo-β-lactamase gene blaIMP-8 has been identified in various bacterial species, its occurrence in Comamonas thiooxydans within the human intestinal tract is not well understood.MethodsThree bacterial strains were isolated from an intestinal perforation patient's diarrheal samples using MacConkey agar with meropenem. Species identification was done via MALDI-TOF MS, and the IMP gene's presence was confirmed by PCR. Complete genome sequencing used Illumina and Nanopore platforms, while Southern blot confirmed the plasmid location of blaIMP-8. Comparative genomic analysis, using Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI), was performed on 481 Comamonas genomes. Core-genome SNP analysis assessed the genetic relatedness of the three isolates. ResultsThe three isolates were identified as Comamonas thiooxydans, carrying blaIMP-8 on plasmids. The earliest strain, L4020hy, also had a ~ 10 kb insertion for the NorR regulator. Among 481 Comamonas genomes, 93 were conspecific C. thiooxydans (≥ 95% ANI), including these isolates. Phylogenetically, they were closest to the environmental isolate Comamonas sp. A23. SNP analysis showed genetic distinctiveness among the clinical isolates, with 403, 322, and 251 core-genome SNPs between L4020 vs. L4037, L4020 vs. L4040, and L4037 vs. L4040, respectively.ConclusionThis study reports the identification of multiple strains of Comamonas thiooxydans harboring a blaIMP-8-containing plasmid within the human intestinal tract. This finding highlights the potential for C. thiooxydans to serve as a reservoir and possible vector for carbapenem resistance genes within the human gut, warranting further surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13085-z.Keywords: Comamonas thiooxydans, Diarrheal sample, Plasmid, IMP-8

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global health challenges of the twenty-first century, threatening decades of progress in modern medicine. The World Health Organization has identified carbapenem-resistant bacteria as critical priority pathogens, underscoring the urgent need for intensified surveillance and the development of effective countermeasures (https://www.who.int/en/news-room/detail/27-02-2017-who-publishes-list-of-bacteria-for-which-new-antibiotics-are-urgently-needed). Carbapenems, a class of β-lactam antibiotics with the broadest spectrum of activity, have long served as last-resort agents for treating severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens. However, the rapid emergence and global dissemination of carbapenem resistance, primarily mediated by carbapenemase enzymes, has severely compromised their clinical efficacy [1]. Among these enzymes, IMP-8, a member of the metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) family, represents a particularly concerning class of resistance determinants due to their ability to hydrolyze nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, and their resistance to inhibition by clinically available β-lactamase inhibitors. The IMP (imipenemase) family of MBLs, initially identified in Japan in the 1990 s, has since disseminated globally and now comprises numerous variants exhibiting distinct geographic distributions and host associations[2]. The specific variant IMP-8 has since been reported sporadically, predominantly in Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with the majority of documented cases originating from Asian countries [3, 4]. In 2024, the first IMP-8-producing Enterobacter hormaechei strain was described, carrying the blaIMP-8 gene on a novel plasmid [5]. This strain, designated K432, carried the blaIMP-8 gene on a novel 45.8 kb plasmid (pK432-IMP) belonging to an unclassified incompatibility group. Notably, this plasmid also harbored qnrS1 and additional resistance determinants, highlighting its role as a mobile platform for multidrug resistance dissemination [5]. Further investigations have revealed that IMP-8-encoding plasmids exhibit substantial genetic plasticity, with recombination events facilitating the acquisition of accessory resistance regions, such as class 1 integrons and truncated transposons, across diverse bacterial hosts [6, 7]. In parallel, an IncR plasmid harboring blaIMP-4, a close homolog of blaIMP-8, has been shown to evolve through the accumulation of genetic segments derived from multiple plasmids, a process that may facilitate conjugative transfer despite the absence of a tra operon [7]. More broadly, IMP-encoding plasmids, such as the IncN1-type pIMP-HZ1, have been demonstrated to disseminate among Klebsiella pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa in clinical settings. This dissemination is characterized by extensive fragmentation and rearrangement of accessory regions, which likely enhances plasmid adaptability across diverse bacterial genetic backgrounds [6].

Within this broader context of plasmid-mediated dissemination of IMP-type carbapenemases, increasing attention has been directed toward environmental bacteria as underrecognized reservoirs of clinically relevant resistance genes. The genus Comamonas comprises aerobic, non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli commonly found in environmental sources such as soil, water, and wastewater treatment facilities [8]. Traditionally considered environmental saprophytes with low pathogenic potential, Comamonas species have been increasingly recognized as opportunistic pathogens capable of causing human infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or those with underlying medical conditions [9]. Comamonas thiooxydans, first described as a sulfur-oxidizing bacterium isolated from activated sludge, exhibits remarkable metabolic versatility and environmental adaptability [10]. Although clinical reports involving C. thiooxydans remain infrequent, documented infections include bacteremia, peritonitis, and wound infections, often associated with healthcare exposure or invasive procedures. To date, to the best of our knowledge, no plasmid-borne blaIMP-8 has been reported in Comamonas thiooxydans strains isolated from human intestinal tract samples. Although an IMP-8–producing C. thiooxydans strain of human origin, isolated specifically from a urinary tract infection, that isolate harbored the blaIMP-8 gene on the chromosome. Therefore, the plasmid-mediated dissemination of blaIMP-8 in C. thiooxydans within the human intestinal niche remains undocumented, represents a significant shift in our understanding of environmental bacteria as emerging reservoirs and vectors of clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance [11]. In a previous study, the C.thiooxydans isolate designated strain ZDHYF418 exhibited resistance to aztreonam, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides, while displaying intermediate susceptibility to meropenem [11]. Genomic analysis revealed that blaIMP-8 is chromosomally located within a Tn402-like class 1 integron, flanked by transposase genes and additional resistance determinants, including aac(6’)-Ib-cr. This genetic configuration suggests that horizontal gene transfer (HGT), potentially originating from Enterobacterales, may have facilitated its acquisition [11]. This observation aligns with broader antimicrobial resistance (AMR) trends, where environmental bacteria serve as reservoirs of resistance genes that can be transferred to clinical pathogens through plasmids, integrons, and transposons [12, 13]. The human intestinal tract, characterized by its densely populated microbial ecosystem, offers an optimal environment for HGT, as close interspecies interactions and antibiotic-driven selective pressures promote the exchange of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) [14, 15]. This study represents the first documented instance of an IMP-8 plasmid identified in C. thiooxydans isolated from the intestinal tract of a patient. Consequently, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the genomic architecture and phenotypic characteristics of this strain, while emphasizing the need to expand AMR surveillance beyond classical pathogens to include environmental bacteria that may serve as reservoirs for high-risk resistance determinants.

Materials and methods

Strain isolation and genetic identification

Between July 26 and 28, 2021, fecal samples were collected on three consecutive days from a patient diagnosed with intestinal perforation. The samples were inoculated onto MacConkey agar plates supplemented with 2 µg/mL meropenem and incubated at 37 °C for 20 h to selectively isolate carbapenemase-producing strains, in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [16]. Single colonies were subsequently selected and subcultured for further analysis. Bacterial species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Genomic DNA was extracted using the BeadPure Bacterial DNA Kit B (Zenpio, China). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were conducted with primers targeting the IMP, VIM, KPC, and NDM genes to screen for the presence of carbapenemase-encoding resistance determinants [17].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 15 antimicrobial agents were determined using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2026 guidelines (M100). The tested agents included piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain.

WGS and genomics analysis of IMP-8-producing C. thiooxydans isolates.

Whole-genome sequencing was performed using a combination of short-read Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing (Novogene, Beijing) and long-read Oxford Nanopore sequencing platforms. For Illumina sequencing, libraries were constructed from sonicated genomic DNA fragments (~ 350 bp) using the Nextera XT kit. Raw reads were quality filtered using Fastp (v0.23.1) to remove adapters and low-quality bases. For Nanopore sequencing, unsheared genomic DNA was prepared with the SQK-LSK114 kit, with base calling conducted in high-accuracy mode using Guppy (v0.17.1) and real-time sequencing monitored via MinKNOW. Hybrid genome assembly was carried out using Unicycler (v0.4.7). Genome annotation was performed using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP, v6.6), and acquired antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using ResFinder 4.1. Species identity was confirmed by Average Nucleotide Identity based on BLAST (ANIb) analysis [18]. Plasmid sequence alignments were performed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) hosted by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Comparative visualizations of IMP-8–carrying plasmids were generated using the BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG, v0.95) [19]. Structural comparisons of genetic regions harboring the blaIMP-8 gene were conducted using Easyfig version 2.2.3 [20].

Plasmid analysis

Plasmid profiling of C. thiooxydans isolates was conducted using S1 nuclease–pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (S1-PFGE), followed by Southern blot hybridization. Briefly, genomic DNA was digested with S1 nuclease and separated by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis. The resolved DNA fragments were transferred onto a nylon membrane and hybridized with a digoxigenin-labeled IMP-8–specific probe for detection [21, 22]. The transferability of blaIMP-8-harboring plasmids was assessed through conjugation experiments, using colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa PAO1Co as the recipient strain. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller–Hinton agar supplemented with 2 mg/L colistin and 2 mg/L meropenem and were subsequently confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS and PCR analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis of C. thiooxydans isolates

A total of 481 C. thiooxydans genome assemblies were retrieved from the NCBI database. Following quality control and Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis, 93 conspecific strains were retained for further analysis, a threshold of ≥ 95% ANI was applied against the C. thiooxydans type strain to define conspecific strains. These strains, together with the three C. thiooxydans isolates obtained in the present study, were subjected to core-genome single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis using Snippy (v4.6.0) with Comamonas testosteroni strain G1 (GenBank accession number CP067086.1) as the reference genome. To account for the confounding effects of homologous recombination on phylogenetic inference, recombinant regions were identified and removed from the core-genome alignment using Gubbins (v3.4.3). The resulting core-genome alignment was used to construct a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree with FastTree (v2.1.11). Tree visualization and annotation were performed using the Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL, https://itol.embl.de/) platform.

Results and discussion

Between July 26 and 28, 2021, diarrheal samples were collected on three consecutive days from a patient with intestinal perforation, and carbapenem-resistant bacteria were selectively cultured on MacConkey agar supplemented with 2 µg/mL meropenem. All recovered isolates were identified as Comamonas thiooxydans, an environmental bacterium not routinely associated with human intestinal infections. Due to the limitations of the ethical approval and informed consent obtained at the time of sample collection in 2021, we are unable to provide additional patient data, including antibiotic history, co-detection of other organisms, or clinical relevance. This is a key limitation of our study.All three isolates exhibited similar resistance profiles, including resistance to ceftazidime, Cefepime, Aztreonam, Gentamicin, Tobramycin and ciprofloxacin, but remained susceptible to imipenem and several other agents (Table 1). The isolation of this species, carrying a clinically significant carbapenemase gene, from a clinical intestinal sample raises the possibility that environmental bacteria can colonize the human gut while harboring resistance determinants of clinical concern. This observation challenges the conventional separation between environmental and clinical microbiology and raises the possibility that such bacteria may participate in clinically relevant AMR dynamics [23, 24]. PCR-based screening for carbapenemase-encoding genes (IMP, VIM, KPC, and NDM) revealed the presence of the IMP gene in all three isolates, while assays for VIM, KPC, and NDM genes were negative, and Southern blot analysis confirms this determinant to a large plasmid of approximately 240 kb in each strain (Fig. 1A). The shared presence of blaIMP among genetically distinct isolates indicates that plasmid carriage, rather than clonal expansion, underlies their persistence. Although conjugation experiments indicated that the blaIMP harboring plasmids were non-conjugative under laboratory conditions, this does not prevent mobilization in vivo. The genetic context of blaIMP-8, including the conserved structure comprising a DDE-type integrase/transposase/recombinase–tniB–tniQ–recombinase family protein–aac(6′)-Ib–blaIMP-8–intI1, provides genomic evidence for the presence of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) that could, in principle, facilitate horizontal gene transfer. However, it is important to emphasize that direct evidence for such transfer events within the human gut is currently lacking. While horizontal gene transfer via helper plasmids, natural transformation, or bacteriophage-mediated transduction remains a theoretical possibility within the complex ecological context of the human intestine, the findings of this study do not allow us to conclude that such transfer has actually occurred. Further studies, such as in vivo or in vitro gut models, are needed to experimentally assess the mobilization potential of this plasmid [25, 26]. The colonization of the gut by environmental bacteria carrying a carbapenemase gene identifies a potential reservoir for clinically significant resistance genes within the human microbiome.[27, 28], This scenario could theoretically lead to the exposure of the gut microbial community to these genes, highlighting a potential pathway for resistance gene persistence and dissemination that merits further investigation.

Whole-genome sequencing confirmed the gene as blaIMP-8, a carbapenemase that is rarely reported within the genus Comamonas. Indeed, prior to this study, only a single clinical report described an IMP-producing Comamonas isolate, carrying IMP-8 from human sources (Urinary tract infection), which carried the gene on the chromosome[11], Critically, the present study represents the first report of a plasmid-borne blaIMP-8 in C. thiooxydans isolated from the human intestinal tract, contrasting with its previously documented chromosomal location in a urinary tract isolate. This apparent rarity likely reflects systematic under detection, as environmental bacteria are typically excluded from routine clinical surveillance, a bias that recent studies have shown can vague the true prevalence of carbapenemase genes in clinical specimens [29]. Comparative plasmid analysis revealed that the blaIMP-8 carrying plasmids from the three isolates shared an identical backbone, with the plasmid from strain L4020 (248,115 bp) containing an additional ~ 10 kb region relative to those from strains L4037 and L4040 (238,210 bp). This insertion encoded the nitric oxide reductase transcriptional regulator norR (Fig. 1B), providing direct evidence of ongoing plasmid evolution, this insertion likely resulted from recombination, transposition, or horizontal gene transfer events within the gut environment. While the functional consequence of this insertion in the intestinal niche remains to be experimentally determined, the presence of norR is noteworthy given the established role of nitric oxide in host immune responses and gut microbial ecology[30, 31], This observation illustrates how resistance plasmids can accumulate genetic elements with potential adaptive functions beyond antimicrobial resistance. Analysis of the genetic environment surrounding blaIMP-8 exhibited the highest similarity with p447-IMP (accession number: KY978631), the ZDHYF418 chromosome (accession number: CP063057.1), and pWF1033-KPC-IMP (accession number: CP158493.1). A conserved structure comprising DDE-type integrase/transposase/recombinase–tniB–tniQ–recombinase family protein–aac(6′)-Ib–blaIMP-8–intI1 was identified (Fig. 1C). While the conserved mobile genetic element structure surrounding blaIMP-8 provides genomic evidence for mobilization potential, direct experimental evidence for horizontal gene transfer within the human gut is currently lacking. The near identity of this region across unrelated plasmids and chromosomal contexts supports dissemination via a shared mobile genetic element rather than vertical inheritance, this finding is consistent with the concept that environmental resistomes can function as reservoirs and hubs for resistance gene exchange [24, 32]. All identified antimicrobial resistance genes were exclusively located on the plasmids, with no resistance determinants from the ResFinder database detected on the chromosomes of the three isolates (Table S3).

Furthermore, a total of 481 Comamonas genome sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database. ANI analysis identified 93 genomes (Table S1) as belonging to C. thiooxydans, exhibiting ≥ 95% similarity to the study isolates (Fig. 2). Phylogenetic clustering (Fig. 3) showed that the three isolates were closely related to one another but distinct from other strains. After filtering for homologous recombination using Gubbins, The final core-genome alignment used for phylogenetic analysis comprised 52,616,935 bp. Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) was performed using Snippy with the complete genome of Comamonas testosteroni strain G1 (GenBank accession number CP067086.1) as the reference. This analysis identified 403 SNPs between strains L4020 and L4037, 322 SNPs between L4020 and L4040, and 251 SNPs between L4037 and L4040 (Table S2), providing no evidence of recent clonal transmission. These substantial core-genome SNP differences (403, 322, and 251 SNPs) among isolates collected only two to three days apart are inconsistent with recent clonal expansion and instead support co-infection with multiple distinct C. thiooxydans strains. These SNP differences were identified using high-quality genomes assembled from a hybrid Illumina and Nanopore sequencing approach. The observed SNP counts are orders of magnitude higher than the expected error rate of these platforms (< 0.1%), confirming they represent genuine biological variation rather than technical artifacts. Considering the typical mutation rate of bacteria, the observed hundreds of SNP differences between isolates collected within days strongly suggests they represent distinct, independently acquired lineages rather than recent clonal variants. In summary, while we acknowledge the theoretical possibility of combined technical and evolutionary artifacts, the weight of evidence—including high-quality complete genomes, SNP counts far exceeding expected microevolutionary rates, and independent plasmid structural variations—strongly supports our conclusion that the three C. thiooxydans isolates represent genetically distinct, co-colonizing lineages. We have carefully considered alternative explanations and maintain that this interpretation is the most parsimonious based on the available genomic data. Furthermore, the observed plasmid differences—specifically, the ~ 10 kb norR insertion in the L4020hy plasmid compared to the L4037hy and L4040hy plasmids —are likely attributable to recombination, transposition, or horizontal gene transfer events occurring within the complex gut microbial ecosystem. This plasmid variation does not contradict but rather reinforces the conclusion of non-clonal, multi-lineage colonization. Comparison with their closest phylogenetic relative, the environmental soil isolate Comamonas spp. A23, revealed substantially larger SNP distances of 59,895 (L4020hy vs. A23), 60,169 (L4037hy vs. A23), and 60,064 (L4040hy vs. A23), confirming that the clinical isolates are genetically distinct from this environmental strain. Instead, these data support intestinal colonization by multiple independent lineages. The closest phylogenetic relative was Comamonas spp. A23 (GCA 004522605.1), an environmental soil isolate, further suggesting an environmental origin for both the bacterium and its resistance plasmid [33]. This observation aligns with ecological studies demonstrating that environmental bacterial populations exhibit high genetic diversity and can seed multiple lineages into human-associated niches [34]. Collectively, these findings may indicate that environmental bacteria can carry and potentially disseminate clinically important resistance determinants into human-associated niches, highlighting their role in the ecology of AMR. The detection of blaIMP-8 in C. thiooxydans suggests a potential pathway that warrants further investigation linking environmental reservoirs, the human microbiome, and clinically important resistance determinants, though direct evidence for such transmission remains to be established. The presence of an environmental bacterium carrying a carbapenemase plasmid in the human gut suggests an acquisition event that could have originated from environmental sources. Potential exposure routes might include contact with contaminated water, food, or wastewater, highlighting the importance of non-healthcare settings in the dissemination of carbapenemase-producing organisms [35].

Taken together, this study indicated that current AMR surveillance frameworks, which primarily target classical pathogens such as Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa, are insufficient to capture the full landscape of resistance gene dissemination. Expanded surveillance strategies incorporating environmental and opportunistic bacteria, supported by genomic and metagenomic approaches, are essential for the early detection of emerging resistance threats [36]. Viewed through a One Health perspective, the convergence of environmental, human, and microbial factors observed here underscores the need for integrated interventions addressing antimicrobial resistance across ecological boundaries. The acquisition of the norR insertion in the L4020 plasmid, together with the substantial chromosomal SNP differences between isolates, is consistent with the hypothesis that the three C. thiooxydans strains may represent distinct lineages that have independently acquired and modified the IMP-8 plasmid. However, given the single-patient nature of this study, this remains a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. This study is limited by the analysis of isolates obtained from a single patient, which restricts broader inferences regarding prevalence and transmission. In addition, the absence of longitudinal and environmental sampling prevents assessment of colonization persistence and definitive source attribution. Finally, although plasmid transfer was not observed under laboratory conditions, horizontal gene transfer may still occur within the complex ecological context of the human gut. Additionally, this study is limited by the absence of dedicated analyses for chromosomal point mutations associated with carbapenem resistance. While our analysis focused on acquired resistance genes, we did not systematically screen for resistance-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or other chromosomal mutations. In future work, tools such as AMRFinderPlus (NCBI) or PointFinder could be employed to detect such chromosomal determinants, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the resistance mechanisms.

From a One Health perspective, the convergence of environmental, host, and microbial factors observed in this study underscores the need for integrated interventions across ecological boundaries to address antimicrobial resistance. Potential preventive directions include expanding surveillance to encompass environmental and opportunistic bacteria for early warning, strengthening monitoring of non-healthcare setting, and continuing to optimize antibiotic stewardship strategies to reduce selective pressure.

Conclusion

In summary, the identification of IMP-8-producing C. thiooxydans strains from a diarrheal sample represents an interesting observation that suggests the possibility that the human intestinal tract could serve as a niche for the acquisition of such genes, although this remains to be confirmed in larger studies for the acquisition of plasmid-encoded carbapenemase genes by environmental bacteria. This finding provides preliminary genomic insight into the potential context of blaIMP-8 carriage, though further studies with larger cohorts are needed. Within the limitations of a single-patient study, this work provides genomic insight into the genetic mechanisms underpinning resistance emergence and supports the relevance of environmental bacteria as potential reservoirs of clinically significant resistance determinants. Furthermore, recombination-filtered phylogenetic analysis showed the distinct lineages of these co-colonizing strains, offering a refined understanding of their genomic diversity within the human gut. While broader epidemiological inferences are not warranted, these findings suggest that expanding AMR surveillance to include environmental and opportunistic bacteria may be warranted, and that further studies with larger cohorts and environmental sampling are needed to assess the true scale of this phenomenon.
