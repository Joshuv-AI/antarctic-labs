An unprecedented outbreak of Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis in kept Iberian wild goats (Capra pyrenaica)
Authors: Marta Pérez-Sancho, A Perelló, T García-Seco, C Herranz, MC Arnal, L Torre-Fuentes, M Ugarte-Ruiz, JA Ortiz, M Hernández, D Fernández de Luco, J Álvarez, L Domínguez, C Gortázar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella spp. is one of the main causes of food-borne zoonotic infections in the European Union with Enteritidis being the most reported serovar. Salmonella is commonly associated with domestic animals, although it has also been associated with wildlife. The domestic goat (Capra hircus) appears to have a relatively low susceptibility to this pathogen. Salmonella has been described in game meat of different wild ruminant species; however, limited information is available regarding the susceptibility of wild goats, which may represent a source of human infection.In this study, we describe the epidemiological, molecular, microbiological and pathology findings of an unusual outbreak of clinical salmonellosis in captive Iberian wild goat (Capra pyrenaica) in Spain. The outbreak, plausibly caused by S. Enteritidis (9,12:g, m:-), was characterized by abortions, neonatal mortality and sporadic cases of septicaemic salmonellosis in adult wild goats. Most isolates showed the same Minimum Inhibitory Concentration profiles using the Thermo Scientific™ Sensititre™ EU Surveillance Salmonella/E. coli EUVSEC3 AST panel. Seven isolates were sequenced and classified as ST11 showing the same set of 140 virulence genes. The genomes differed by 0–1 SNP (NC_011294.1:g.2862805G > A).Due to the unusual circumstances of the outbreak, the origin of the outbreak was also investigated through non-invasive sampling for nucleic acid detection using sponges pre-hydrated with an isotonic surfactant liquid and for bacteriology. Salmonella was exclusively detected from wild-goat enclosures, allowing to presumably rule out other potential animal-sources of infection, including peridomestic/wild animals such as pigeons, rats, rabbits and partridges present and analysed in the farm surroundings, although another untested animal source could still be involved.The unusual presentation of this Salmonella outbreak could be explained by the convergence of epidemiological and ecological factors (infection origin linked to peridomestic animals or humans, management conditions which may favour pathogen emergence, potentially increased susceptibility of wild ruminants to infection or other factors which may be involved). Background

Non-typhoidal Salmonella is one of the main causes of food-borne zoonotic infections in the European Union, second only to Campylobacter spp. 70% of these infections are caused by serovar Enteritidis, which is the most commonly reported serovar in laying hens and the second in broiler chicken, although it has been also associated with other animal species [1, 2]. Its global spread is likely linked to international trade of breeding poultry stocks [3].

Both domestic and wildlife animals can be infected by shared zoonotic pathogens including those of the genus Salmonella [4]. The limited studies on goats suggest that domestic goats (Capra hircus) have a relatively low susceptibility to Salmonella spp. infection [5] and serovar Enteritidis is not frequently isolated from this host species [6]. However, sporadic cases have been reported in which goats were carriers and became sources of human infections. When clinical signs appear in goats, abortions are a common finding [7]. A report from Taiwan described a severe outbreak of salmonellosis in 2- to 3-day-old goat kids caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Havana, characterized by high mortality and clinical signs such as weakness, lameness, and diarrhoea [8].

In a large survey in northeastern Spain, three Iberian wild goats (Capra pyrenaica) (representing 0.96% of the sampled population) were found positive to Salmonella, including one belonging to serovar Enteritidis. In the same region, a fatal case of septicaemic salmonellosis caused by Salmonella Enteritidis was reported in an 11-year-old male Iberian wild goat [9]. Additionally, S. arizonae was isolated from an asymptomatic Iberian wild goat in a study on pathogen carriage conducted in southern Spain [10]. In contrast, a recent study found no Salmonella-positive samples among Iberian wild goats in western Spain [11]. To date, there have been no recent reports of Salmonella spp. infection in Alpine ibex (Capra Ibex) [12, 13]. In wild animal populations, humans and domestic animals may serve as common sources of infection, and the strains infecting wildlife could reflect the circulating serovars in the environment [9].

Few EU countries conduct routine surveillance for Salmonella spp. in game meat, and this occurs only in small numbers and without covering all animal species, particularly not including Capra species such as the Alpine ibex in the Alpine region and the Iberian wild goat in the Iberian Peninsula [14]. The ISO 6579-1:2017/A1:2021 establishes the methodological approach for detection, enumeration and serotyping of Salmonella in the food chain. However, alternative methodological approaches based on bacterial pre-enrichment and DNA detection by PCR have been explored for Salmonella in wild animals [15]. Molecular characterization of Salmonella isolates has provided useful information for assessing the potential epidemiological links among isolates recovered from wild hosts and other animals or humans [2, 16].

Environmental detection of Salmonella often involves the collection of faecal samples or the use gauze swabs, environmental sponge swabs, or disposable foot covers, where the swabs or the foot covers are then placed into buffered peptone water (BPW) and processed for culture and identification [e.g. 17, 18, 19]. Additionally, environmental S. Enteritidis DNA can also be detected using rapid lateral flow-based immunodiagnostic assays [20], loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) [21] and PCR techniques [22].

In this study, we describe the epidemiological, molecular, microbiological and pathology findings of an unusual outbreak of clinical salmonellosis in captive Iberian wild goat in Spain. The outbreak, caused by S. Enteritidis, was characterized by abortions, neonatal mortality and sporadic cases of septicaemic salmonellosis in adult wild goats. Due to the unusual circumstances of the outbreak, its origin was also investigated. We applied non-invasive sampling using sponges pre-hydrated with an isotonic surfactant liquid to collect environmental nucleic acids [23, 24]. We hypothesize that the convergence of several epidemiological and ecological factors (i.e., infection origin from peridomestic/humans on the farm + breeding conditions that could lead to the emergence of the infection, potential increased susceptibility of wild ruminants to infection, etc.) may explain the occurrence and severity of this outbreak.

Case presentation

Study population

The Iberian wild goat farm was in a 4-ha fenced property in Spain, at 900 m altitude a.s.l. The wild goat farm had three enclosures, one for females and kids (0.8 ha), one for adult males (3 ha) and one for handling and quarantine purposes (0.2 ha), which was placed between the other two. Since the farm was new, the main goal was to establish a large group of reproductive females. Males were kept until they reached 10 years of age to be sold or released back into the field. In 2021, a total of 43 female goats and 23 billy goats were present on the farm. A small group of six wild goats was sold in 2021 to a second game farm. In 2022 and 2023, a total of 86 and 93 animals, respectively, were present on the farm.

Animals were fed with commercial feed, fresh alfalfa and ad libitum dried alfalfa. Periodically, health controls were successfully carried out (tuberculosis and brucellosis). Vaccines against Clostridium and Pasteurella were routinely administered.

The wild goat farm shared spaces with a red-legged partridge (Alectoris rufa) farm. The partridge farm was divided in two facilities, a chick-rearing building with adjacent full-fenced open-air aviaries, and the cages for adult partridge reproductive pairs, which were located in a separate open-air facility (Supplementary material 1). The chick building and aviaries were divided into 8 units and a corridor and were at a distance of 270 m from the male wild goat enclosure. The adult partridge cages were at a distance of 50 m from the female wild goat enclosure. Anthropophilic birds had access to the wild goat enclosures and could potentiallyact as bridge hosts and contaminate the partridge aviaries and the reproduction cages through the upper wire mesh. Rodents, lagomorphs, and small carnivores could potentially enter the wild goat enclosures through small spaces between the doors and the fences, or by climbing over the fence.

Case history

In 2021, 2022 and 2023 most of the pregnant female goats (47%, 32% and 60%, respectively) experienced late-term abortions or gave birth to weak offspring that typically survived less than 48 h (2–3 weeks maximum), and died a few days or weeks later with signs of septicaemia (3 kids survived in 2021; 1 kid survived in 2022; 3 kids survived in 2023).

In 2023–2024, a multidisciplinary study was conducted including epidemiological, clinical, pathological and microbiological investigations aimed at (i) determining the aetiology of the outbreak (first clinical manifestations were detected in 2021) and (ii) investigating the source of infection. In May 2023, a field visit was carried out for clinical evaluation, necropsies were carried out and samples collected for anatomopathological and bacteriological investigations. In July 2023, a field visit was carried out to perform an epidemiological survey (structured questionnaires including 80 open-ended and closed questions about characteristics of the herd, management of carcasses, pasture management, feeding, presence of other animals, etc. as previously used by our group) and sampling (for environmental DNA detection and bacteriology [22]). During this visit, samples from a dead kid goat (M517/23) were collected and submitted for bacteriological analysis. The field visit and the corresponding epidemiological survey revealed the presence of pigeons and other peridomestic birds in the enclosures, as well as the in the dried alfalfa which may represent a source of Salmonella infection. In June 2024, samples from two weak born animals that died were subjected to microbiological determinations.

In May 2023, during a field visit, necropsies were performed in two adult animals (M515/23 and M516/23) and seven kid goats. Samples from these animals (the two adults animals and two kid-goats) were submitted to Universidad de Zaragoza (Spain) for anatomopathological analysis. Besides, samples from the two adult animals were sent to Universidad Complutense for bacteriological determinations (Supplemental Table 2).

The adult billy-goat M516/23 (of 2.5/5 fat score) showed high parasitisation by ticks. In the pectoral region there was a lump of soft consistency with thick purulent material inside. The cutaneous projection of the carpal joints (on both forelimbs) showed hair loss and keratosis. The mediastinal lymph node was enlarged; the left lung presented a soft cranial mass with thick purulent material, and the lung parenchyma was emphysematous. The mesenteric lymph node was enlarged and the heart showed areas of pale surface and ecchymosis in the muscle of the left ventricular chamber. The spleen was enlarged (292 g) with a bulge of splenic tissue and showing black dots in some areas. The right kidney was congested and had very dark discoloration on the surface of the renal cortex (possible pseudomelanosis). The other animal M515/23 was a young adult female goat which showed a poor body condition (fat score 2 out of 5) with signs of chronic diarrhoea (perianal region and hocks with diarrheal faeces). The mammary gland and nipples showed purulent material. The spleen was enlarged (308 g) with a bulge of splenic tissue. The lung was congested with some interstitial oedema in the diaphragmatic lobes. The liver was slightly friable and enlarged, and the gallbladder was enlarged, associated with bile retention. The right kidney presented inflammation, less consistency and congestion.

Four out of seven kids showed nasal discharge, and one a soiled perineal region. All of them presented lung congestion and oedema. In six of them, the abomasum showed abundant coagulated milk, and the small rumen was filled with plant remains and soil. One showed an empty abomasum. The small intestine showed mucous content, and the large intestine had plenty of meconium.

Histology

The seven kids of Iberian < 2-week-old-goats found dead in May 2023 were subjected to necropsy. All were kept at -20 °C since collection until the necropsy was performed. Some degree of autolysis was observed. Only two kids were sampled for histopathology due to the advanced autolysis in five of them. In addition, the two 2-year-old goats (M515/23 and M516/23) found dead and necropsied were also analysed for histology. Tissue samples from lungs, liver, spleen, kidney and small intestine were preserved in 10% buffered formaldehyde. Sections of 5 μm were stained with haematoxylin and eosin technique.

Microbiology

Microbiological analyses were performed in two stages. Firstly, general bacteriological analyses were carried out on clinical samples (liver, vaginal swab, mammary gland swab, nasal swab, spleen, etc.) from animals (adult billy goat M516/23 [nasal, lung and joint swabs] and female-goat M515/23 [liver, vaginal/mammary gland swabs ] as well as three kid goats: M517/23 [liver, spleen and nasal/vaginal and brain swabs], M1337/24 [spleen, meconium and hemoperitoneum swab] and M1338/24 [spleen and rectal swab]) (Table 1) as follows: samples were processed and surface plated on Columbia blood agar (bioMérieux, Madrid, Spain) under aerobic and anaerobic conditions for 48 h at 37 °C. Identification of the isolates was performed by MALDI-TOF using a protein-peptide extraction protocol according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Germany). Spectra acquisition was performed using UltrafleXtreme equipment (Bruker Daltonik) in the linear and positive mode using FlexControl software (version 3.4) in an automatic mode. Isolates were identified by comparing their MALDI profiles with those included in the BDAL Bruker database using MALDI Biotyper software (Bruker Daltonik) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics

Bacterial DNA from a selection of seven isolates (Table 2) was extracted and purified using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit according to manufacturer´s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) for whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Library preparation was performed using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq platform. The raw reads generated in this study have been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive under accession number PRJEB90984.

Raw reads were assessed for quality using FastQC [26] and processed with Trimmomatic [27] to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Genome assemblies were generated using SPAdes [28] and assembly quality was evaluated with QUAST [29]. Multilocus Sequence typing (MLST) was performed using the mlst software [30] referencing the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org). Assembled genomes were screened for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes using ResFinder [31], and point mutations associated with AMR were detected using PointFinder [32]. Plasmid replicons were detected with PlasmidFinder [33] and virulence genes were identified using Abricate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate).

A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based approach was used to evaluate the genetic relationship between the seven isolates. Trimmed reads were mapped against the reference genome S. Enteritidis genome P125109 (NC_011294.1) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) [34] and SAMtools [35]. Variant calling was generated by BCFtools [36] using ‘mpileup’ and ‘call’ options, and the resulting SNPs were filtered by removing those with a base quality < 30 and a mapping quality score < 30. The number of SNPs was evaluated from the resulting VCF (Variant Call Format) files to assess the genetic differences among the isolates. In addition, diversity among the core genome shared by all strains, identified through Roary [37], was evaluated using the assemblies built using SPAdes [38] and annotated using Prokka [39].

Molecular detection of Salmonella spp. from environment and animals

Sponge sampling (n = 12) for environmental DNA (eDNA) was carried out in 2023 in wild goat feeders and water troughs from the two enclosures (male n = 2; female n = 2), from feeders and drinking devices for partridge chicks (n = 4), from the cages of the adult partridges (n = 2), the employee toilet surface (n = 1), and from the nearby church tower used as pigeon roost and nesting site (n = 1). Additionally, we sampled 4 eDNA sponges on the feeders and water troughs of a second wild goat farm. Sponges for Salmonella DNA detection were also taken from the mammary gland as well as the perineal, hock, abdominal and sternal region skin surfaces of a necropsied female wild goat (n = 4), and from two abscesses (pectoral skin and cranial lung lobe) and the perineal, hock, abdominal and sternal region skin surfaces of a necropsied male wild goat (n = 3) (Table 5).

This environmental/animal sampling was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, the extraction of DNA was carried out using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen Hilden, Germany), starting from the pellet obtained after centrifuging 900 µL of the sample (15mL of isotonic surfactant liquid in which the sponge had previously been hydrated) for 3 min at 13.000 rpm. Salmonella DNA from these samples was detected by PCR [40].

Results

Histology

The two-year-old female (M516/23) histologically showed a suppurative infiltration by neutrophils in the mammary gland (Fig. 1) and generalized congestion. Multiple tiny foci of necrosis and neutrophilic infiltration were observed in liver, lung and spleen (Fig. 2). The two-year-old male (M515/23) had consolidated areas in the cranial lung lobes. Histologically, it showed a suppurative infiltration by neutrophils into alveolar lumens. Also, multiple foci of necrosis and neutrophilic infiltration were observed in the liver. Kid goat (M517/23) showed interstitial pneumonia, congestive kidneys, ruminal and reticular impaction, and congestive liver and renal cortex.

Microbiology

Bacteriological results of M516/23 and M515/23 are summarized in Table 1. Salmonella spp. was recovered from all samples (liver, vaginal swab and mammary gland) from M515/23. The billy goat M516/23 yielded a Salmonella-positive culture on liver but this bacterium was not detected on nasal, lung and joint swabs. In contrast, sponges recovered from different animal surface lesions in both animals (M515/23: skin, breastbone, mammary gland, hock, perineal and M516/23: breastbone, hock, perineal) yielded positive results to Salmonella PCR (Table 1). Salmonella was identified in samples from liver, spleen, faeces and brain of kid goat M517/23 (negative results were found on nasal, rectal and vaginal swabs) (Table 1). All isolates were confirmed as S. Enteritidis (9,12:g, m:-) (Table 2).

For characterization of Salmonella, the antimicrobial resistance profile of six isolates (three from goat M515/23 and two from kid 517/23 and M1338/24) were determined. Most isolates showed the same MIC profiles being susceptible to EUVSEC3 panel (Table 3).

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics

A selection of 7 Salmonella isolates recovered from female goat M515/23, Billy goat M516/23, kid M517/23 and kid M1138/24 were identified as serotype Enteritidis (9,12:g, m:-) and subjected to WGS (Table 2). These seven sequenced isolates were classified as sequence type 11 (ST11). None of them showed any AMR determinants. The analysis of putative plasmid sequences revealed that all isolates harboured only the IncFIB and IncFII replicons. In addition, all isolates carried the same set of virulence genes (n = 140) with the reference genome (NC_011294.1:g.2862805G > A) and by 0–1 SNP when considering the core genome except for one isolate from 2024 (Table 2), that was separated by 13 SNPs from the other six isolates.

Molecular detection of Salmonella spp. from environmental samples. To identify Salmonella hotspots and transmission dynamics, an environmental sampling was designed using sponges pre-hydrated with an isotonic surfactant liquid to collect environmental nucleic acids in different points (Table 4). Samples recovered from feeders and drinkers located on female-goat enclosures were positive to eDNA. No other samples (Billy-goats enclosures, employee toilet, dovecot, second game farm enclosures and partridge corrals) tested positive.

Discussion and conclusions

Salmonella spp. has been described in different game meat species such as wild boars [41, 42] or wild ruminants [43]. However, information in this regard is limited in Iberian wild goats. In fact, to our knowledge, our group described the first, and to date the only, fatal salmonellosis case in a male Iberian wild goat [9]. In this study, we describe an unprecedented outbreak in farmed Iberian wild goat characterized by reproductive failures and perinatal mortality (almost 100%) and occasional septicaemic fatalities in adults where S. Enteritidis was detected in affected adult and kid goats.

As the consumption of game meat is becoming more frequent because it is perceived as organic and sustainable [4], it is necessary to consider establishing the same hygiene controls while obtaining food from game species. Although wild goat meat is rarely consumed by Spanish hunters, the population of this species is expanding but remains susceptible to important demographic changes due to diseases (particularly sarcoptic mange) [44]. Valldeperes et al. (2023) recently revised the main diseases of relevance on Iberian wild goat, with particular interest in those affecting captive individuals [45]. These authors showed the relevance of sarcoptic mange, infectious keratoconjunctivitis or caseous lymphadenitis. However, Salmonella, particularly serovar Abortusovis, is only mentioned as an abortive agent of low prevalence in Iberian wild goat (0.2–0.8%). Gomez-Guillamón et al., (2020) revealed also low prevalence of selected pathogens (not including Salmonella) among Andalusian Iberian wild goat populations [46]. Surprisingly, our results suggest that Salmonella could be linked to outbreaks with dramatic consequences in fenced Iberian wild goat, becoming a potential source of infection for other animals and humans. Several factors may lead to the occurrence of S. Enteritidis in this unprecedented outbreak in farmed game ruminants: high-density conditions on an Iberian wild goat farm, which may favour transmission and persistence, a higher susceptibility of Iberian wild goat to Salmonella infection and/or molecular features in the strain involved in the outbreak or the co-existence of other pathogens/predisposing factors which were unnoticed in spite of the epidemiological, molecular, microbiological and pathological investigations.

First, it must be considered that the risk of salmonellosis may be greater in those cases in which game animals are in high density kept in captivity [47] (farmed game, as those of the present report). In these cases, the risk of transmission to humans would not be so much through food as through direct or indirect transmission from the animal itself, when they share the same environments (farm operators, veterinarians) [41], all of which is favoured by the greater density of individuals in the same space, the overabundance, the entry and exit of individuals from different sources [41] or closer contacts with domestic livestock and humans [48]. In the case of captive Iberian wild goat, infectious diseases as caseous lymphadenitis or pneumonia have been described as more prevalent and severe than in free-ranging ones [45]. In fact, most reported cases of clinical disease caused by Salmonella in wild ruminants have been described in captive animals [49].

Second, potential different Salmonella-susceptibility among ruminant species could explain the disease severity on Iberian wild goat. This has been previously described in other infectious diseases. For example, domestic goats are recognized as highly susceptible to Mycobacterium bovis infection [50]. On the contrary, Iberian wild goat seems to be relatively resistant to tuberculosis infections as no positive samples were found among 205 animals examined in Spain [51]. Domestic goats have shown low levels of Salmonella infection [52, 53, 54] although Salmonella presence on goat-milk has been previously described [55, 56], even as source of outbreaks on goats [57]. Previous results have suggested a low prevalence of Salmonella in Iberian wild goat although fatal salmonellosis has sporadically been described [9, 10].

Third, molecular features could shed light on the origin of the outbreak. The high degree of similarity (0–1 SNP) of sequenced isolates obtained from several animals over 2023 indicates that a single source was likely behind all cases. The outbreak strain was included in a clade genetically linked with the so-called Atlantic clade [3] or MGT14-CC13 [58] (data not shown), that was predominantly associated with human clinical isolates (and less so with S. Enteritidis isolates from livestock) in a recent study including a large collection of Spanish strains [59] with the closest isolates (separated by > 10 SNPs) were of human and food origin, whereas the closest animal isolate (from a laying hen) was > 40 SNPs away from the outbreak strains (Supplementary material 3). In the case of wild birds, a higher prevalence of Salmonella has been described in animals in contact with livestock or human activities [15]. In the present study, the lack of Salmonella-positive samples in potential animal-source of infection (i.e. peridomestic animals as pigeons, rats or rabbits, partridge farm, water, feed, etc.) in the farm surroundings may allow us to exclude some potential sources of infection (although other animal source not analysed could be involved). In addition, the molecular features of the S. Enteritidis recovered in this case may suggest an anthropogenic origin [41].

Fourth, the potential existence of other factors/pathogens which may be involved in the outbreak could not be absolutely ruled out. However, Salmonella enteritidis could be considered a primary pathogen, even an etiological agent of fatal salmonellosis in Iberian wild goats [9]. It can cause reproductive alterations in humans [60], mice [61], or even in ruminants such as water buffalo [62] or sheep [63]. Anatomopathological results do not suggest viral infections and the findings corresponded with bacteriological infection. Analyses to investigate other potential bacterial pathogens were performed: samples were subjected to general bacteriological examination, and Salmonella was recovered from adults (liver, vaginal swab, mammary gland) and kids (liver, spleen, faeces and brain). Further analysis for specific Salmonella detection was later implemented (ISO 6579–1:2017). Isolation of the same Salmonella strain in adults and kids was in different organs which suggests a septicaemic process. No other signs, epidemiological data, clinical analysis, etc. suggested the potential implication of other viral/bacteriological agents. The animals had been recently acquired, fed with commercial feed, fresh alfalfa and ad libitum dried alfalfa. Periodically, health controls were successfully carried out (tuberculosis and brucellosis).

Among the limitations of our study, sample sizes were often low due to practical difficulties. Although sampled animals were representative of the clinical picture of the farm, positive Salmonella results were limited to two adults and two kids (female goat M515/23, Billy goat M516/23, kid M517/23 and kid M1138/24) and environmental samples from goat-enclosures. Other investigations, for example, for viral co-infection were not carried out, although, as abovementioned, the results showed a consistent presence of Salmonella in adults and kids, the pathology findings which corresponded with bacteriological systemic infection, and there was a lack of systematic isolation of other potential abortifacient bacteria in adults/kids in the general bacteriological analysis. All these facts suggest Salmonella as the primary agent of this unprecedented outbreak in Iberian wild goat although other unnoticed pathogen could not be absolutely discarded. Another limitation is the unknown origin of the Salmonella strain causing this outbreak. Sample sizes for other animal species were limited and thus, we cannot completely rule them out as sources of this Salmonella strain. Environmental samplings have been gaining interest in animal health in the last few years. Although several aspects still need to be defined for their appropriate validation for different purposes [64], an approach based on pre-hydrated sponges has been successfully implemented for eDNA detection for different pathogens (bacteria, virus) [17, 65, 66]. The usefulness of this methodology to investigate contact risks in multi-host settings has been demonstrated [23]. In the case of Salmonella, previous studies have demonstrated the usefulness of environmental samplings for exploratory risk factor assessment. Thus, in UK broiler farms, Salmonella-positive samples were collected around the houses, especially if these borders were of grass and soil rather than of concrete and gravel, with the highest proportions of positive samples being from drainage, farm tracks, and water [19]. Herrero-Garcia et al. (2024) demonstrated the suitability of pre-hydrated sponges to detect Salmonella in outdoor hoofstock [22]. Ofori et al. (2023) detected Salmonella in different farm environments (including dust and soil) suggesting that they may act as important reservoirs of this pathogen [67]. In our study, environmental sampling (for bacteriology and eDNA detection) revealed the presence of Salmonella on different points (feeders, drinkers, dust, shoe covers) from male and female goat enclosures, i.e., those settings where Salmonella was present in animals with clinical signs. Our results demonstrated the suitability of environmental surveillance to detect Salmonella in the environment of infected animals where environmental contamination may occur and could represent a source of reinfection or where cleaning and disinfection programs should be reinforced.

This study provides new insights into the epidemiology of Salmonella in Iberian wild goat, a potential reservoir of pathogenic-serovars with dramatic consequences for kept animals. Different authors have highlighted the need to improve Salmonella surveillance at the wildlife-livestock interface due to the potential role of wild animals as pathogenic serovar reservoirs [68]. This is particularly relevant in the case of the Iberian wild goat where new diagnostic methods, especially non-invasive tools, are needed [45]. The non-invasive approach may contribute to implementing Salmonella epidemiological surveillance in less-explored hosts.
