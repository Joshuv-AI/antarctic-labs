A knowledge graph-driven paradigm for holistic symptom management: development and evaluation of a nurse-led KG-QA system in community palliative care
Authors: Xu Yan, Ran An, Guozhen Liu, Youqing Wang, Yan Jiang, Qiaoqin Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity nurses play a pivotal role in palliative care but face barriers in managing complex symptoms, such as fragmented knowledge and a lack of community-tailored evidence-based guidance, impairing clinical efficiency. The aim of this study was to develop and evaluate a knowledge graph-based question-answering system for symptom management in community palliative care.MethodsA three-phase codesign study guided by the Knowledge-to-Action framework was conducted. Phase 1 (Knowledge Creation): A Symptom Management Knowledge Base (Knowledge Product I) was developed through a codesign process involving a multidisciplinary expert panel. This panel adapted a knowledge base created by researchers through systematic evidence synthesis, employing FAME criteria for contextual adaptation. Phase 2 (Action Cycle: Implementation): A semantically structured knowledge graph (Knowledge Product II) was constructed via automated extraction by software developers, followed by manual verification by researchers. Based on this graph, a question-answering system was created and implemented as a WeChat mini-program, resulting in a practical KG-QA system (Knowledge Product III). Phase 3 (Action Cycle: Evaluation): The system’s acceptability, usability, and perceived usefulness and ease of use were assessed among experts and community nurses during a two-week evaluation period using the Clinical Nursing Information System Effectiveness Evaluation Scale and the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire, which is grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model.ResultsThe knowledge base comprises 225 evidence items for nine symptoms; the knowledge graph integrates ten entity types, 11 relationship categories, 442 entities and 668 relationships, with the system supporting four query interfaces and three search methods. The evaluations demonstrated high perceived usefulness and ease of use, with strong scores for acceptability (102.25 ± 16.21; 110.56 ± 9.90) and usability (2.47 ± 1.98; 2.23 ± 1.93).ConclusionThe question-answering system bridges the evidence-practice gap via a nursing-process paradigm, offering a potentially scalable model that aligns with national policies pending further validation. However, these findings are based on a small‑scale, single‑region, short‑term evaluation relying largely on subjective measures. Future research should explore its long-term clinical outcomes and cross-setting scalability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02198-7.Keywords: Community health services, Palliative care, Symptom management, Knowledge graph, Question-answering system

Background

Given the global trend of an aging population and increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases, China faces particularly severe demographic and health challenges. By 2033, the country is projected to become a superaged society [1], placing a disproportionate caregiving burden on households. This burden is further exacerbated by China’s substantial cancer burden, which accounts for 23.7% of the global incidence and 30% of cancer-related mortality [2]. In response to the dual pressures of an aging society and high cancer burden, community and home-based palliative care has emerged as a strategic, person-centered solution to alleviate these mounting challenges [3].

Effective management of complex, dynamic, and often cooccurring symptoms—such as the pain–dyspnea–delirium triad [4]—is fundamental to this care model and relies heavily on nurses. In China, however, community nurses face substantial challenges in symptom management practices. First, patient symptom profiles are overwhelmingly complex. Up to 94% of end-stage patients experience two or more concurrent symptoms, with those suffering from metastatic cancer averaging 14 symptoms [4, 5]. This high symptom burden not only intensifies psychological distress but also exponentially impacts patients’ quality of life. Second, profound structural inequities create severe resource constraints. There is a widespread scarcity of community-suited equipment [6, 7] and critical medications, with access to potent analgesics being only 31% of that available in tertiary hospitals [6, 8]. Third, training gaps and fragmented knowledge hinder practice. A shortage of trained professionals and limited palliative care competency leave community nurses lagging behind their specialized hospital counterparts [9, 10]. This disparity is evident even in early-adopter regions such as Shanghai, suggesting even more severe conditions in less developed areas [11]. Moreover, clinical guidelines and drug databases are siloed, focusing narrowly on individual symptoms and obscuring critical inter-symptom relationships. For example, information on managing morphine-induced respiratory depression in COPD patients and alternative fentanyl patch options is fragmented across sources, forcing nurses to manually reconstruct pathways and delaying timely decision-making.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) offer promising technological solutions to the complex challenges faced in resource-limited community settings. By integrating diverse data sources into a unified semantic network structured as entity-relation-entity triples, KGs dismantle information silos, clarify intricate relationships, and provide an easily navigable knowledge structure. This transformative potential is exemplified by high-impact biomedical KGs such as PubMed Knowledge Graph 2.0, which integrates over 36 million papers, 1.3 million patents, and 480,000 clinical trials to support cross-domain scientific discovery and literature mining [12]. Similarly, Wu et al. [13] developed an ontology-guided clinical decision support system via a KG. This system provides interpretable, personalized health examination recommendations, matches expert clinicians and outperforms standardized packages (96.3% precision, 84.8% recall). These implementations demonstrate that structured, semantically integrated knowledge organization advances professional practice, setting a precedent for effective KG-based support systems in community nursing.

Although current clinical KG applications demonstrate transformative potential in areas such as drug repurposing, which involves identifying new therapeutic uses for existing drugs by integrating and analyzing heterogeneous biomedical data like disease-drug-gene associations [12] and diabetes management [14], their focus is primarily on medical diagnosis, medication management, or direct patient engagement. This limited scope does not align with the holistic, process-oriented approach of nursing, rendering existing KGs inadequate for capturing the comprehensive care processes essential in community palliative care. This paradigm gap leads to practical challenges: disconnected nonpharmacological guidance hampers effective symptom management, whereas fragmented educational resources obstruct the teaching of self-care skills to patients and caregivers. As a result, families often find themselves unprepared to manage symptom exacerbations before professional help arrives, potentially compromising the continuity of care.

In this work, we codesigned, developed, and evaluated a knowledge graph-based question-answering (KG-QA) system for community nurses engaged in palliative care symptom management in collaboration with key stakeholders, including palliative care specialists, community nurses, and software developers.

Methods

Study design and theoretical framework

This study utilized an integrated theoretical framework (Fig. 1)—an adapted FAME-enhanced Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) model—that offers a comprehensive structure to address challenges in resource-limited community settings. The KTA framework was selected because it uniquely separates knowledge creation from the action cycle, offering a structured pathway from problem identification to sustained knowledge use—a feature lacking in frameworks focused primarily on barrier identification (e.g., PARIHS [15], CFIR [16], TDF [17]). Its knowledge creation funnel (inquiry, synthesis, product) directly matches our phased development of the symptom management knowledge base (Knowledge Product I → II → III). The iterative action cycle (identifying problems, adapting to local context, assessing barriers, selecting interventions, monitoring, evaluating, sustaining) enables continuous refinement, essential for resource-limited community settings. Given KTA’s established application in nurse-led implementation research, we chose it as the core framework and further enhanced it with FAME criteria (feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, effectiveness) for contextual adaptation and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) for evaluation to meet the specific needs of community palliative care. Thus, this framework establishes a closed-loop, iterative knowledge translation system with two main phases:

Theoretical framework diagram of the research Project – an adapted FAME‑enhanced KTA Model. This integrated model illustrates the systematic process of knowledge translation: (1) The FAME-enhanced KTA cycle guides the development and implementation of the KG-QA system, translating Knowledge Product I (synthesized evidence) into a contextually adapted Knowledge Product II, which is subsequently operationalized as the KG-QA system—the Knowledge Product III. (2) During the implementation and sustained use phases, the TAM framework is used to evaluate this product, where metrics of perceived usefulness and ease of use monitor its adoption. These evaluation findings then feed back into the action cycle, informing future iterations of the system and establishing a framework for continuous improvement

Knowledge creation

A thorough review of existing evidence was conducted to gather data on core symptoms and their management — a process that was completed in approximately 3 months — resulting in the creation of the first knowledge product (Knowledge Product I). Using the FAME criteria as the adaptation filter, experts contextualized this evidence and developed a tailored knowledge base (Knowledge Product II). This narrative evidence was then organized into a semantic KG through iterative entity and relation extraction, creating a machine-readable model for the system.

Action cycle

The KG was transformed into a question-answering system — an implementation phase that lasted approximately 4 months — with a browser/server architecture and natural language processing modules, and was deployed as a WeChat mini-program, effectively converting knowledge into a practical tool (Knowledge Product III). The TAM was then used to assess the system’s impact and adoption potential during a 2‑week evaluation period, with a focus on perceived usefulness and ease of use. The evaluation results were reintegrated into the model, supporting a closed-loop iteration process. This approach ensures rigorous knowledge translation from evidence synthesis to clinical practice while continuously improving the tool.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB No. IRB00001052-24181) on January 23, 2025. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study.

Construction of the knowledge base (knowledge creation)

Identification of core symptoms and framework development

Research on symptom clusters demonstrates that physical symptoms and psychosocial issues can be evaluated and managed independently within specialist palliative home care, with an emphasis on physical symptom burden during initial assessments [18]. Consequently, we intentionally concentrated the current KG‑QA system on physical symptoms, recognizing that psychological symptoms (e.g., anxiety, depression), nutritional concerns (e.g., anorexia), social issues (e.g., caregiver burden), and spiritual distress will be incorporated in future iterations. To build the knowledge base for these somatic symptoms, we conducted a systematic search of databases, such as PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI, and Wanfang, for literature on community palliative care symptom management policies, guidelines, and expert consensus. This approach aligns with the knowledge inquiry and synthesis phase of the KTA. Through thematic analysis and coding, we extracted and categorized symptom-related information, identified frequently occurring symptoms, and summarized management strategies. Core symptoms were selected on the basis of their prevalence and burden in the literature, as well as their relevance to community nurses’ daily challenges identified in preliminary field assessments [19]. To finalize the core symptoms and the knowledge base framework, a multidisciplinary expert panel consensus meeting was convened. The panel was recruited from multiple institutions across Beijing based on predefined inclusion criteria, including medical and nursing managers, frontline physicians, and registered nurses. None of the panel members were from the researchers’ own workplace. The inclusion criteria were: (i) at least ten years of field experience and recognized authority in palliative care; (ii) possession of an intermediate or higher professional title; (iii) demonstrated expertise in community‑based end‑of‑life care; and (iv) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Their specialties covered both medicine and nursing, ensuring multidisciplinary input. After an expert panel review, we constructed and refined the knowledge base framework, achieving the KTA goal of synthesizing and organizing knowledge for practical application. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 1.1.

Systematic evidence retrieval and contextual adaptation

On the basis of core symptoms, we systematically retrieved, evaluated, and synthesized evidence, organizing the findings into a preliminary knowledge base aligned with the drafted framework. This approach aligns with the KTA stage for developing implementable knowledge products (refer to Supplementary Materials 1.2). To ensure applicability in community-home settings, the same multidisciplinary expert panel previously mentioned engaged in contextual adaptation, operationalizing the adaptation phase. The panel employed the FAME strategy to refine evidence by removing or modifying unsuitable components, establishing a community-specific adoptability threshold guided by the principles of safety primacy and community feasibility. This process ensured that the final knowledge base (Knowledge Product II) was both evidence-based and contextually actionable. Further details are provided in Supplementary Materials 1.3.

Development of the KG-QA system (action cycle: implementation)

Metamodel construction

The KG meta-model was developed via Knowledge Product II. Within a standardized framework, we established ontological categories and relational rules to convert narrative evidence into structured, machine-actionable knowledge representations. This structural translation is a crucial component of the KTA, facilitating the practical application of knowledge in sociotechnical systems. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.1.

Iterative development and validation

The KG was developed via an iterative approach that combines a pretrained model with automated extraction and manual review. This iterative process guarantees the KG’s accuracy and reliability, enhances its precision and adaptability, and furthermore aligns with the KTA’s principle of continuous refinement. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.2.

KG-QA system development

A browser/server architecture was implemented for the KG-QA system, which was deployed as a WeChat mini-program. WeChat, a highly popular social media platform in China, was chosen for its extensive accessibility, seamless integration into daily life, and user-friendly design [20]. This selection aimed to enhance accessibility, a critical factor for the sustained utilization of knowledge under the KTA. To ensure perceived usefulness and ease of use, the system was designed with multiple interactive interfaces and query modalities: graph-based exploration, natural language input, and direct text retrieval. For natural language queries, a four-stage processing pipeline was built: entity extraction, semantic classification, automated query generation, and result retrieval, with responses delivered in plain language supplemented by supporting evidence links. This user-centered development process operationalized Knowledge Product II into the deployable KG-QA system (Knowledge Product III), aligning with the KTA objective of translating evidence-based knowledge into practice. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.3.

Evaluation of the KG-QA system (action cycle: evaluation)

This phase corresponds to the KTA’s stages of monitoring knowledge use and evaluating outcomes. Guided by the TAM, the evaluation concentrated on system performance and key adoption determinants, specifically perceived usefulness and ease of use. These factors are well-documented predictors of the intention to integrate technology in practice [21].

Expert evaluation

The KG-QA system was evaluated by the same multidisciplinary expert panel that participated in earlier study phases, including symptom identification and contextual adaptation. This ensured that evaluators possessed a comprehensive understanding of the system’s foundation and context, a prerequisite for assessing perceived usefulness within the TAM. Sixteen deidentified cases of community-based palliative care, characterized by a life expectancy of less than six months and complete documentation, were selected through purposive sampling. Subsequently, these cases were randomly assigned using a random number table, with each expert assigning two distinct cases. For each case, the experts formulated three symptom management queries, performed simulated searches, and evaluated the responses via the Clinical Nursing Information System Effectiveness Evaluation Scale (CNISEES) [22] and the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire (PSSUQ) [23]. Within the TAM, perceived usefulness was evaluated via the net benefits and information quality dimensions of the CNISEES, whereas perceived ease of use was evaluated via the system quality dimension of the CNISEES and the usability and interface quality dimension of the PSSUQ. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Community nurse evaluation

The usability testing literature indicates that five to eight participants can uncover 80%–85% of usability issues [24]. Considering the constraints of the community setting, we conducted a preliminary evaluation with a purposive sample of 10–15 community palliative care nurses. After receiving structured training, nurses used the system in their community-home care practice for two weeks. Following the trial, we quantitatively evaluated the system’s adoption potential via the CNISEES and PSSUQ. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Discussion

This study aims to address three key challenges faced by community palliative care nurses: the complexity of symptom profiles, resource constraints in community home-based settings, and fragmented information sources. Our KG-QA system directly confronts these issues through its core architectural features. First, a contextually adapted knowledge base ensures that information remains relevant and feasible despite resource constraints. Second, this knowledge is consolidated into a single, accessible tool—a WeChat Mini Program—via an integrated graph structure, providing actionable guidance for nurses. Finally, multipath query interfaces facilitate streamlined access to this synthesized knowledge, allowing for flexible retrieval.

Guided by the KTA, a key insight emerged: the necessity for a rigorous and structured filtering mechanism during the adaptation phase, particularly in resource-limited settings. Although the knowledge creation phase developed a comprehensive evidence base (Knowledge Product I), our pre-implementation surveys revealed systemic constraints—such as limited access to analgesics, scarce equipment, and insufficient training [19]—that could render some high-quality evidence unsafe or impractical. To address these challenges, we introduced and empirically validated the contextual adaptation filter model (the FAME-enhanced KTA framework). In this model, Knowledge Product I serves as the input. While KTA emphasizes adaptation, it lacks a specified mechanism. This gap is filled by the FAME criteria, which function as a rigorous, safety-focused filter to establish a community-specific adoptability threshold. As shown in Fig. 1, the FAME-enhanced KTA model ensures that evidence is not only synthesized but also systematically stress-tested against practical realities of feasibility, appropriateness, and meaningfulness before implementation. The output of this process is a contextually adapted evidence base (Knowledge Product II), which directly informed the development of our KG-QA system. Additionally, this model incorporates a built-in feedback mechanism, where findings from ongoing evaluations (e.g., TAM-based assessments) are designed to feed back into the evidence base, ensuring the system’s continuous evolution and closing the KTA loop. This process establishes a strict adoptability threshold for the community environment. The deliberate exclusion of ten evidence items, such as palliative sedation, was driven by safety concerns, highlighting that feasibility and safety are as crucial as efficacy. The refinement of 18 other items was guided by two core principles: safety primacy and community feasibility. These principles offer a replicable decision-making framework, and the resulting model serves as a transferable model for evidence implementation in resource-constrained settings. This model directly addresses pervasive implementation failures, such as the multilevel barriers that undermine the adoption of evidence-based therapies despite robust support [25] or the collapse of theoretically sound packages when poorly tailored to local contexts [26].

This study contributes a resource-adapted knowledge base and a novel nurse-led KG paradigm to address critical gaps in community palliative care support tools. First, it delivers a context-sensitive evidence base (Knowledge Product II) through the FAME-enhanced KTA framework. This process led to the strategic inclusion of nonpharmacological interventions—a crucial yet often overlooked component in community nursing. To address this gap, our system offers a resource-sensitive framework that integrates these interventions, ensuring that they are accessible, actionable, and tailored for home and community settings. Guided by the FAME-enhanced KTA framework, we focused on nonpharmacological interventions requiring minimal equipment or training, organizing them into two categories. The first category includes easily implementable techniques such as cold or heat therapy; external traditional Chinese medicine methods such as moxibustion and auricular point sticking for pain management [27–29]; fan therapy for dyspnea relief [30–33]; and abdominal massage for constipation [34–36]. These approaches can be effectively administered by community nurses with brief training. The second category features low-cost, resource-efficient strategies, including breathing exercises that require no specialized devices [30–33], fever management using common household items such as tepid sponging and ice packs [28, 37–40], and warm-water enemas combined with dietary modifications for constipation [34, 36, 37, 41].

Beyond mere content innovation, the system’s primary contribution lies in its paradigmatic shift within the KG—specifically, the nursing-process paradigm. This shift addresses a fundamental challenge in community palliative care: reconciling the holistic, generalist approach of nursing and the specialized, fragmented nature of conventional knowledge systems. While existing KGs excel in areas such as diagnosis and pharmacology, they fall short in representing the process-oriented logic essential to nursing. Our system is designed to reflect the clinical workflow of community nurses by structuring knowledge around symptom management pathways rather than disease entities. By integrating multisource evidence filtered through FAME criteria within the KTA, we construct a cohesive knowledge network that incorporates often-overlooked elements into a structured symptom-to-care continuum. This model features a dual-layered architecture, combining detailed vertical pathways for individual symptoms with horizontal links across related conditions. For example, the pain management pathway integrates both pharmacological and nonpharmacological therapies with essential nursing care. It also models clinical trajectories, such as opioid-induced constipation, which is linked to corresponding nursing protocols and self-management guidance [28, 29, 34, 35, 42–46]. Cross-symptom queries, such as those for constipation, seamlessly retrieve appropriate interventions from the dedicated knowledge subgraph, including acupuncture, auricular point therapy, and patient education [28, 34, 36, 37, 41, 47]. This graph-based structure fundamentally reorganizes nursing knowledge into an interconnected network, visually mapping symptom-intervention-evidence relationships. It creates an interpretable, explorable resource that complements professional judgment. Consequently, it enables holistic symptom management by linking entities such as pain to multiple nonpharmacological pathways, allowing nurses to efficiently select evidence-informed options aligned with available resources and patient-specific goals. This dual innovation—context-adapted content within a nursing-centered paradigm—shifts both the role of the nurse and the function of the technology. It turns nurses from passive information seekers into active, informed practitioners and shifts the KG from a static repository to a dynamic tool for person-centered care.

The system gained compelling validation from both expert and nurse evaluations. These TAM-based findings now feed directly into the KTA Action Cycle, operationalizing the built-in feedback mechanism of our model and initiating a new iteration of continuous improvement. The observed divergence highlights its dual strengths in terms of scientific rigor and practical utility. Experts, having codeveloped the knowledge base through FAME-based adaptation, assigned the highest ratings to Net Benefits (21.63 ± 4.21), thereby confirming the system’s clinical logic and positioning it as a validated Knowledge Product III within the KTA. Nurses—the primary end-users—rated Information Quality (24.06 ± 2.18) and Interface Quality (1.94 ± 1.87) most favorably, confirming its alignment with real-world workflows and its success in fulfilling the core TAM determinants of perceived usefulness and ease of use. These strong scores indicate that the system effectively facilitates cognitive offloading and informational empowerment in practice. This helps alleviate nurses from the burden of laborious information synthesis and enhances their decision-making confidence, allowing them to focus more on clinical reasoning and person-centered care. This is particularly advantageous in resource-limited settings where specialist support is scarce. By directly addressing the initial triad of challenges, the system shows potential to become a scalable tool that could support tiered healthcare delivery under the “Healthy China 2030” framework [48], operationalizing its “Internet + Healthcare” vision [49]. Of note, given the preliminary nature of this single‑region, short‑duration study and its reliance on subjective measures, these findings should be interpreted as initial evidence. Rigorous multicenter, longer‑term evaluations with objective outcomes are required before stronger claims of scalability or policy alignment can be made.

Strengths and limitations

This study has two key strengths. Methodologically, it introduces a FAME-enhanced KTA framework that provides a rigorous, context-aware model for adapting evidence to resource-limited community settings—a replicable advancement to implementation science. Technically, it pioneers a nurse-led KG paradigm that restructures evidence into an intuitive, dual-layered network, effectively bridging the gap between conventional KG technology and the holistic, process-oriented logic of nursing practice.

Several limitations are also acknowledged, which further suggest directions for future research. Although the repeated involvement of the expert panel provides contextual depth, it may introduce bias; however, this risk is mitigated by the use of standardized evaluation tools. The two-week evaluation period was sufficient for initial assessment but was insufficient to gauge long-term impact on clinical workflows or patient outcomes. While the nurse sample size met usability heuristics and was supported by validated scales, a larger cohort would increase the generalizability of the results. The rigorous knowledge base reflects only a July 2024 evidence snapshot, excluding advanced hospital-centric interventions to ensure community feasibility, which might reduce perceived comprehensiveness. Technologically, the system is optimized for single-symptom queries, with complex multisymptom case interpretation remaining a key challenge. Finally, Beijing-based evaluation requires further validation across China’s diverse healthcare settings to ensure broader applicability.

Conclusions

This paper details the design and development of a nurse-led KG-QA system—orchestrated through a FAME-enhanced KTA framework—that pioneers a nursing-process paradigm for KGs and translates evidence into practice via a WeChat mini-program. This work bridges the evidence-practice gap by embedding specialist knowledge into community nursing, delivering a scientifically grounded tool that supports frontline providers, improves care quality, and advances the goal of tiered healthcare.

(3) Pathway C (Direct Text Search): From the Modular Access Interface (Level 2, Fig. 4B), clicking “Knowledge Retrieval” opens the Text-Based Search Interface (Level 3, Fig. 4E). Entering a natural language query (e.g., “adverse reactions of opioids”) returns a list of entities. Clicking an entity (e.g., “constipation”) transitions the view to the Graph-Based Query Interface (Level 3, Fig. 4F) focused on that node, from which users can click “View” to proceed to the Specific Text Interface (Level 4, Fig. 4G)
