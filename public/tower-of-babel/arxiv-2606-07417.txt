An optimal local theory for reaction-diffusion equations driven by non-trace-class noise Thanks: The first author is a member of GNAMPA (INdAM). The second and third authors have received funding from the VICI subsidy VI.C.212.027 of the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research (NWO)
Antonio Agresti, Fabian Germ, Mark Veraar

Abstract
. We study local well-posedness for a class of stochastic reaction-diffusion equations driven by multiplicative, possibly colored, noise. The interaction between rough stochastic forcing and polynomial nonlinearities naturally leads to solutions with low spatial regularity, making the treatment of the nonlinear terms delicate. Our main contribution is a general local existence and uniqueness theory for SPDEs with rough noise and highly irregular initial data. The framework also yields new results in standard noise regimes, including trace-class noise and space-time white noise. We identify the critical initial-data spaces for a wide range of nonlinearities, and we establish instantaneous parabolic regularization, general blow-up criteria, and sufficient conditions for positivity preservation. We apply the abstract theory to several prototypical models, including the stochastic Allen-Cahn, Burgers, Fisher-KPP, and coupled Gray-Scott equations. Finally, in the one-dimensional space-time white-noise setting, we combine our local theory with existing global a priori results in a highly singular regime.

1.
Introduction

Reaction-diffusion equations play a central role in the mathematical modeling of chemical reactions, population dynamics, phase separation, ecology, and pattern formation.
Classical examples include the Allen–Cahn equation, the Fisher–KPP equation, the Lotka–Volterra system, and the Gray–Scott and Brusselator models.
In the deterministic setting, the theory of reaction-diffusion equations is highly developed; see, for instance,
[
12
,
26
,
33
,
50
,
49
,
53
]
and the references therein.
Depending on the structure of the nonlinearities, one has a rich body of results on local and global well-posedness, regularity, positivity, and asymptotic behavior.
At the same time, even in the deterministic case, obtaining global well-posedness is delicate in general, and further structural assumptions are often indispensable.

Stochastic perturbations arise naturally in reaction-diffusion models when one wishes to account for uncertainty in parameters, unresolved microscopic interactions, or random forcing acting on the system.
Stochastic reaction-diffusion equations have therefore received substantial attention in recent years; see, for instance,
[
15
,
16
,
14
,
13
,
21
,
37
,
39
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
and the references therein.
However, compared to the deterministic literature, the stochastic theory is still much less complete, especially for systems and for equations with rough multiplicative noise.
A large part of the available theory relies on strong coercivity or dissipativity assumptions, and many results concern equations or settings in which the noise or initial data are comparatively regular.
In particular, for weakly dissipative systems of reaction-diffusion type, many natural questions remain open. In the recent work
[
46
]
, progress was made for weakly coercive
triangular systems driven by non-trace-class noise.

Previous work on local and global well-posedness
[
4
,
5
]
by the first and third authors treated several weakly dissipative stochastic reaction-diffusion systems with
trace-class noise
and
transport noise
, including Lotka–Volterra, Brusselator, and Gray–Scott type models.
This showed that one can go substantially beyond the fully coercive setting in concrete examples.
Nevertheless, many important problems remain open.
A major obstacle is the lack of a sufficiently flexible local theory capable of handling rough multiplicative noise and highly irregular initial data.
Thus, such a local theory is not only of independent interest, but also forms the natural starting point for future developments on global well-posedness and, subsequently, on more refined dynamical questions.

The purpose of the present paper is to develop such a local theory in the case of possibly non-trace-class noise.
More precisely, we study stochastic reaction-diffusion equations of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}u_{i}-\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}t&=\Big[\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u))+f_{i}(\cdot,u)\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(t),\\
u_{i}(0)&=u_{0,i},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

on a bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, together with the Dirichlet boundary condition

\displaystyle u|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0

Our results also have versions for Neumann boundary conditions, but we will focus on the Dirichlet case for clarity.

In the above, we assume without loss of generality that the dimension of the system is equal to the number of noise terms, which can be achieved by adding trivial rows or columns. Here
u=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
is the unknown process, the nonlinearities may have polynomial growth, and the independent driving Brownian noises
(W^{j})_{j=1}^{\ell}
are allowed to be multiplicative and non-trace-class.

A central difficulty is that the combination of rough stochastic forcing and superlinear nonlinearities naturally leads to solution spaces of low spatial regularity.
In this context, the term
critical
refers to the borderline regularity suggested by the natural scaling of the equation.
At this level, the linear part, the nonlinear drift terms, and the noise have the same strength under parabolic rescaling; below this level one should not expect a general well-posedness theory based on the same methods.
Thus, the critical spaces describe the largest natural classes of initial data for which the equation is still expected to be locally well posed.
As a consequence, the nonlinear terms must be treated at the level of distributions, and the identification of the correct critical initial-data spaces becomes a delicate issue.

The main novelty of the present work lies in the combination of two recent ingredients. The improved abstract theory from
[
9
]
provides local well-posedness in critical spaces once the deterministic and stochastic nonlinearities satisfy suitable mapping estimates. The estimates of
[
1
]
provide precisely the required bounds for multiplication operators composed with non-trace-class covariance operators.
Together, these tools allow us to treat rougher stochastic perturbations than in
[
4
]
, and to remove several technical restrictions on the noise which commonly appear in the literature.
In particular, even in situations involving trace-class noise as considered in
[
4
]
, the improved abstract framework now enables us to treat more general classes of equations, and, in particular, identify larger critical classes of initial data.

Our main result is a local existence and uniqueness theorem for
stochastic reaction-diffusion equations with non-trace-class multiplicative
noise and highly irregular initial data. The theorem is formulated in a scale
of Sobolev–Besov spaces and identifies the scaling-critical initial-data
spaces for broad classes of polynomial nonlinearities. In addition to local
well-posedness, we prove instantaneous parabolic regularization, derive
blow-up criteria in low-regularity norms, and establish positivity preservation under natural structural assumptions. The coefficients of the leading elliptic operator are allowed to be merely measurable in time and in
\omega
. In a one-dimensional space-time white-noise setting, we show how the local theory can be combined with recent results from
[
21
,
27
]
to obtain global well-posedness in a highly singular regime. This method is based on our instantaneous regularization results, blow-up criteria, and a suitable restarting procedure.
The same method can be used to extend global well-posedness results to the case where the initial data have very low (negative) smoothness. In particular, this applies to the results in
[
15
,
39
,
46
,
55
,
56
]
as long as one assumes that the local Lipschitz constants have polynomial growth, which holds in many physically relevant scenarios.

1.1.
Scaling and criticality for SPDEs with non-trace-class noise

It is well-known in the context of PDEs that, by using scaling arguments, one can identify natural, scaling-critical regularity thresholds for well-posedness, which are often
sharp
. This is also possible for reaction-diffusion equations with non-trace-class noise such as (
1.1
). This reveals the maximal order of growth of the nonlinearities and the intrinsic scaling of the SPDE under consideration. Building on this, one can determine the lowest Sobolev regularity/index for which the system of SPDEs (
1.1
) is well-posed.

Scaling invariance.

We now explain, at a heuristic level, how the critical exponents arise from the different nonlinear terms in (
1.1
).
The purpose of the following scalar equation is not to introduce an additional model, but to isolate the three mechanisms present in the general system: a reaction term
f(u)
, a divergence-form term
\operatorname{div}F(u)
, and a multiplicative noise term
g(u)\partial_{t}W
.
To make the scaling transparent, we replace these nonlinearities by pure powers with independent growth parameters. Thus, we consider

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=|u|^{1+\rho_{1}}+\dv(e|u|^{1+\rho_{2}})+|u|^{1+\rho_{3}}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}

(1.2)

where
e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is fixed.
Here the first term represents the scaling of
f
, the second that of

\operatorname{div}F
, and the third that of the multiplicative noise.
The use of monomials is only for the scaling calculation; the results below
apply to general locally Lipschitz nonlinearities with the corresponding
polynomial growth. We assume that the driving noise satisfies

\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm law}}}{{=}}\lambda^{-1-d/2+d/\zeta}\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}(t,x),\quad\text{for a $\zeta\in[2,\infty]$.}

(1.3)

The previous condition suggests that
\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}
lies between a space-time white noise (
\zeta=\infty
), and the white-in-time noise (
\zeta=2
), depending on the value of the coloring
\zeta
. Details are given in Subsection
2.1.3
. A noise satisfying the assumptions of our results and the scaling (
1.3
) up to a logarithmic correction is provided in
[
1
, Subsection 1.1]
.

It is well known in the context of PDEs that scaling arguments often reveal
the borderline regularity for well-posedness. For stochastic reaction-diffusion equations of the form (
1.1
) the same idea can be used to identify which growth rates of the drift and noise
are critical, and which initial-data spaces are naturally associated with them. The calculation below should therefore be understood as a guide to the structure of the assumptions and results that follow.

Suppose that
u
is a solution to (
1.2
) and define, for
\lambda>0
, the dilated process
u_{\lambda}
under the parabolic scaling:

u_{\lambda}(t,x)=\lambda^{\sigma}u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x).

(1.4)

Clearly,

\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}(t,x)-\Delta u_{\lambda}(t,x)=\lambda^{2+\sigma}\big[\partial_{t}u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)-\Delta u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)].

(1.5)

Next, we analyze the scaling of the nonlinearities in (
1.2
). First, note that

|u_{\lambda}|^{1+\rho_{1}}=\lambda^{\sigma(\rho_{1}+1)}|u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)|^{1+\rho_{1}}

where we see that the above has the same scaling as the linear operator (
1.5
) if

2+\sigma=\sigma(\rho_{1}+1)\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad\sigma=2/\rho_{1}.

(1.6)

Second, for the divergence-type nonlinearity, we have

\dv(e|u_{\lambda}|^{1+\rho_{2}})(t,x)=\lambda^{1+\sigma(1+\rho_{2})}\dv(e|u|^{1+\rho_{2}})(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x),

the scaling of which corresponds to the linear part (
1.5
) if

2+\sigma=1+\sigma(1+\rho_{2})\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad\sigma=1/\rho_{2}.

(1.7)

Finally, for the noise term in (
1.5
), from the scaling of the noise (
1.3
), it follows that

|u_{\lambda}(t,x)|^{1+\rho_{3}}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}(t,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm law}}}{{=}}\lambda^{1+d/2-d/\zeta+(1+\rho_{3})\sigma}|u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)|^{1+\rho_{3}}\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{W}^{(\zeta)}(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x),

which coincides with the scaling in (
1.5
) if

\sigma=\frac{1}{\rho_{3}}\Big(1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}\Big).

(1.8)

Throughout this manuscript, we impose that
\zeta\in[2,\infty]
is such that the right-hand side above is positive (see Assumption
2.3
).

Summarizing the previous scaling analysis in (
1.6
)-(
1.8
), the various nonlinearities in (
1.5
) have the same scaling if and only if we have

\text{fully critical relations:}\quad\rho_{1}=2\rho_{2},\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho_{3}=\frac{\rho_{1}}{2}\Big(1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}\Big).

(1.9)

Critical spaces.

The scaling invariance of local solutions
u\mapsto u_{\lambda}
as in (
1.4
) reveals the optimal regularity threshold for well-posedness of (
1.1
).
To compute critical spaces for the latter, one considers the map induced by (
1.4
) on the initial data
u_{0}
:

u_{0}\mapsto u_{0,\lambda}:=\lambda^{\sigma}u_{0}(\lambda\cdot),

(1.10)

where
\sigma=2/\rho_{1}
and (
1.9
) holds. In particular, the relations (
1.6
), (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are also satisfied.
A function space
X
on
\mathcal{O}
for the initial data is
critical
if it is locally invariant under the mapping (
1.10
).
For the homogeneous version of
X
this translates to a global invariance under the mapping
u_{0}\mapsto u_{0,\lambda}
.
In particular, critical spaces are given by the following Lebesgue and Besov space

L^{\frac{d\rho_{1}}{2}}(\mathcal{O})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B^{\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})\ \text{ for }\ q,p\in(1,\infty).

(1.11)

Indeed, the corresponding homogeneous versions satisfy
\|u_{0,\lambda}\|_{L^{\frac{d\rho_{1}}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\frac{d\rho_{1}}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, and

\|u_{0,\lambda}\|_{\dot{B}^{\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}}}_{q,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\lambda^{\sigma}\|u_{0}(\lambda\cdot)\|_{\dot{B}^{\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}}}_{q,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\eqsim\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}}}_{q,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where the implicit constant is independent of
\lambda>0
.
One can check that
\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho_{1}}
is the only value of the smoothness
s
for which the homogeneous Besov
\dot{B}^{s}_{q,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is scaling invariant under the mapping (
1.10
), and similarly for the integrability
\frac{d\rho_{1}}{2}
among Lebesgue spaces.
Independently of the chosen structure of the function spaces, the Sobolev index of a critical space always amounts to

-2/\rho_{1},\quad\text{ (Critical Sobolev index)}

(1.12)

which is shown to be uniquely identified from the above scaling argument.

If one of the scaling identities (
1.6
), (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) is not satisfied, then the corresponding nonlinearity is
not
critical. If that is the case and a strictly smaller
\rho_{i}
is used, then the corresponding term vanishes in the limit

\lambda\to\infty
. In this sense, the scaling procedure identifies
the nonlinearities of highest order, matching
the scaling of the principal part.

1.2.
Illustration: optimal local well-posedness for scalar SPDEs

The preceding scaling calculation predicts the critical growth of the
nonlinearities and the corresponding initial-data spaces. The following
simplified statement (which follows from Corollary
2.16
) shows how this prediction is realized by our main theorem in a more concrete scalar situation:

\mathrm{d}u-\Delta u\,\mathrm{d}t=\big[f(u)+\dv(F(u))\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(u)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)

(1.13)

on a bounded smooth domain
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with Dirichlet boundary condition. Here the intuitive meaning of the noise term is

W(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}\varphi_{n}w_{n}(t),

where
(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are standard i.i.d. Brownian motions.
We assume
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
with
\varphi_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and the coefficients satisfy
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
, where
the space

\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
denotes the space of sequences
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
for which
\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}<\infty
, with the norm

\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}^{\zeta}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mu_{n}|^{\zeta}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}^{2},

and
\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}=\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\infty}}
.
The parameter
\zeta\geq 2
quantifies the spatial roughness of the covariance of the noise (
\zeta=2
being trace-class noise, and
\zeta=\infty
being space-time white noise). In the literature the orthonormal system
\varphi
is often taken as the eigensystem of
-\Delta
. For general smooth domains one typically has
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}=\infty
. For special domains such as an interval or a cube the eigenfunctions satisfy
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}<\infty
. Note that in our setting one need not choose the eigensystem of
-\Delta
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that for the noise coefficient
\mu\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
, the intensity
\zeta\geq 2
satisfies the condition

\zeta<\frac{2d}{d-2}\quad\text{ whenever }\quad d\geq 2.

Suppose that the nonlinearities
f
,
F
and
g
satisfy the locally Lipschitz bounds

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{\rho}+|y|^{\rho})|x-y|,

\displaystyle|F(x)-F(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{\rho/2}+|y|^{\rho/2})|x-y|,

\displaystyle|g(x)-g(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{\rho_{3}}+|y|^{\rho_{3}})|x-y|,

where
\rho>\max\Bigl\{\frac{2}{d},\frac{4}{d+2}\Bigr\}
and
\rho_{3}=\frac{\rho}{2}(1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta})
. Suppose that
q\in[2,\infty)
satisfies

\frac{2}{\rho(\rho+1)}<\frac{d}{q}<1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}+\min\Bigl\{\frac{d}{\rho+1},\frac{2}{\rho}\Bigr\}.

(1.14)

Then one can choose suitable temporal parameters
(p,\kappa)
so that (
1.13
) admits a unique maximal local solution
(u,\sigma)
in a suitable space for initial data
u_{0}
in a critical Besov space

\prescript{}{D}{B}^{\,\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}).

Moreover,
u
a.s. regularizes to

u\in C^{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}_{\loc}((0,\sigma)\times\overline{\mathcal{O}}),\quad\text{for $\theta_{1}\in[0,1/2)$ and $\theta_{2}\in\big[0,1-\tfrac{d}{2}+\tfrac{d}{\zeta}\big)$.}

Note that the growth of the Lipschitz constants is in line with the scaling we found in Subsection
1.1
. Moreover, the space of initial data is
critical
for (
1.13
) from a PDE point of view, and therefore the local well-posedness result in Theorem
1.1
is
sharp
.

The condition in (
1.14
) is somewhat technical and we do not know whether it is optimal. It enables us to identify critical spaces of initial values. In concrete situations it turns out to be rather flexible.

The expression
\lambda:=1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}
appears several times in the above result and is related to the regularity of the noise. Due to the condition on
\zeta
one can check that
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Note that the space regularity of the solution
u
is
C^{\lambda-}
, and the regularity of the noise is
C^{\lambda-1-}
.

For concreteness of the conditions on the parameters, consider
d\geq 3
and
\rho\geq 2
. Then the condition becomes

\frac{2}{\rho(\rho+1)}<\frac{d}{q}<1+\frac{2}{\rho}-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}.

Thus, we prove well-posedness with initial value in critical space
\prescript{}{D}{B}^{d/q-2/\rho}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})
with smoothness

\frac{d}{q}-\frac{2}{\rho}\approx-\frac{2}{\rho+1}+\varepsilon\quad\text{ for }\quad\varepsilon>0.

Finally, we point out that if
f=0
we can allow for more irregular initial data, see Corollary
2.14
.

Example 1.2

(Cubic nonlinearity
f
)
.

For
\rho=2
and
d=3
, this simplifies to
\zeta\in[2,6)
with the following restriction on

:

\frac{1}{3}<\frac{3}{q}<\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{\zeta},

(1.15)

and

.

, one obtains the largest possible class of initial values. This can be summarized as

The above theorem is representative for the general picture developed below.
The identification of such critical spaces is important for at least two reasons.
First, these spaces are natural from the point of view of scaling and therefore provide a conceptually satisfactory local well-posedness theory.
Second, they form the right starting point for future global arguments.
Indeed, once local solutions are available in critical spaces, the regularization results proved in this paper show that solutions immediately enter better spaces, and our blow-up criteria reduce global well-posedness questions to the derivation of suitable a priori bounds in norms that are often accessible in concrete models.

We expect that the framework developed here will be useful in a number of further directions, e.g. global well-posedness, and long-time behavior for stochastic reaction-diffusion systems with rough noise and rough initial data.

2.1.
Setting

Consider the following system of SPDEs on
\mathcal{O}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}u_{i}-\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}t&=\Big[\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u))+f_{i}(\cdot,u)\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(t),\\
u_{i}(0)&=u_{0,i},\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

where
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
\ell\geq 1
is an integer.
Here
u=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
is the unknown process. We write

\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{j}(a^{j,k}_{i}\partial_{k}u_{i})

in distributional sense. Coupling only takes place through the nonlinear mappings
F
,
f
and
g
.

2.1.1.
Function spaces with Dirichlet boundary conditions

In this subsection we fix the notation for the function spaces used below (see
[
2
,
8
]
for details). Let

\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded
C^{2}
-domain and let
q\in(1,\infty)
. We
write
q^{\prime}
for the conjugate exponent of
q
.

Let
\Delta_{D}
denote the strong Dirichlet Laplacian on
L^{q}(\mathcal{O})
, i.e.

\mathsf{D}(\Delta_{D})=H^{2,q}(\mathcal{O})\cap H^{1,q}_{0}(\mathcal{O}),\qquad\Delta_{D}u=\Delta u.

Equivalently, we set

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q}(\mathcal{O}):=H^{2,q}(\mathcal{O})\cap H^{1,q}_{0}(\mathcal{O}),\qquad\prescript{}{D}{H}^{0,q}(\mathcal{O}):=L^{q}(\mathcal{O}),

and

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1,q}(\mathcal{O}):=H^{1,q}_{0}(\mathcal{O})=\{u\in H^{1,q}(\mathcal{O}):u|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0\}.

The negative part of the scale is defined by duality. More precisely,

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}):=(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))^{*},

where the duality is understood with respect to the extension of the

L^{2}
-pairing. The intermediate spaces are obtained by complex interpolation:
for
-2\leq s_{0}<s_{1}\leq 2
and
\theta\in(0,1)
we put

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O}):=[\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{0},q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{1},q}(\mathcal{O})]_{\theta},\qquad s=(1-\theta)s_{0}+\theta s_{1}.

In particular, this leads to
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{0,q}(\mathcal{O})=L^{q}(\mathcal{O})
. The spaces
H^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})
can be introduced in the same way.

We shall also use the corresponding real interpolation spaces. For

p\in[1,\infty]
,
-2\leq s_{0}<s_{1}\leq 2
, and
\theta\in(0,1)
, we define

\prescript{}{D}{B}^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}):=(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{0},q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{1},q}(\mathcal{O}))_{\theta,p},\qquad s=(1-\theta)s_{0}+\theta s_{1}.

By the reiteration theorem this definition is independent of the particular
choice of
s_{0},s_{1}
and
\theta
.
The space
\prescript{}{D}{B}^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})
is continuously embedded into the usual
Besov space
B^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})
whenever
s\in(0,2)
and
s\neq 1/q
.

Let us recall the standard identifications with the usual Bessel potential and
Besov spaces on
\mathcal{O}
. For
s\in(0,2)
with
s\neq 1/q
one has

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})=\begin{cases}H^{s,q}(\mathcal{O}),&s\in(0,1/q),\\[5.69054pt]
\{u\in H^{s,q}(\mathcal{O}):u|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0\},&s\in(1/q,2).\end{cases}

Similarly, for
s\in(0,2)
with
s\neq 1/q
,

\prescript{}{D}{B}^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})=\begin{cases}B^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}),&s\in(0,1/q),\\[5.69054pt]
\{u\in B^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}):u|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0\},&s\in(1/q,2).\end{cases}

We shall frequently use the following Sobolev embeddings for the Dirichlet
scale. If
-2\leq s_{1}\leq s_{0}\leq 2
,
1<q_{0}\leq q_{1}<\infty
, then

s_{0}-\frac{d}{q_{0}}\geq s_{1}-\frac{d}{q_{1}}\ \text{implies}\ \prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{0},q_{0}}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s_{1},q_{1}}(\mathcal{O}).

Moreover, for
s\geq 0
and
s-d/q>\alpha\geq 0
with
\alpha\notin{\mathbb{N}}
, one has

\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow H^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})\ \text{and}\ \prescript{}{D}{B}^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow B^{s}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{O}}).

2.1.2.
Nonlinearities and coefficients

The following is the main assumption on the coefficients and nonlinearities.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
d,\ell\geq 1
be integers. We say that Assumption
2.1
(s,q,p,\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
holds if
q\in[2,\infty)
,
p\in(2,\infty)
,
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}>0
,
s\in[0,1)
and for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
the following hold:

(1)

(Regularity of the coefficients)

a_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
that are
\mathscr{P}\otimes\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{O})
-measurable, and there exists an
M
such that a.s. for all
t\in[0,\infty)
,

\|a^{j,k}_{i}(t,\cdot)\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}\leq M,

(2)

(Parabolicity)

There exists
\nu>0
such that a.s. for all
t\in[0,\infty)
,
x\in\overline{\mathcal{O}}
and
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}a_{i}^{j,k}(t,x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\geq\nu|\xi|^{2}.

(3)

(Local Lipschitzness)
For all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the maps

\displaystyle F_{i}^{k},\ f_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}\to{\mathbb{R}},\qquad g_{i}^{j}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R},

are
\mathscr{P}\otimes\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{O})\otimes\mathscr{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{\ell})
-measurable, and satisfy

F_{i}^{k}(\cdot,0),\ f_{i}(\cdot,0)\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}),\qquad g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,0)\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}),

and a.s. for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
x\in\mathcal{O}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
, for some
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}>0
,

\displaystyle|f_{i}(t,x,y)-f_{i}(t,x,y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|y|^{\rho_{1}}+|y^{\prime}|^{\rho_{1}})|y-y^{\prime}|,

\displaystyle|F_{i}^{k}(t,x,y)-F_{i}^{k}(t,x,y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|y|^{\rho_{2}}+|y^{\prime}|^{\rho_{2}})|y-y^{\prime}|.

\displaystyle|g_{i}^{j}(t,x,y)-g_{i}^{j}(t,x,y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|y|^{\rho_{3}}+|y^{\prime}|^{\rho_{3}})|y-y^{\prime}|.

The above assumption is in its most general form. It may help the reader to consider a special choice, which in most cases does not lead to any restrictions:

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\zeta\in[2,\infty]
be the parameter related to the noise intensity which will be introduced in Assumption
2.3
below. Let
\rho>0
. Recall from Subsection
1.1
that the following choice for
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}
is natural as it leads to the same scaling behavior in the SPDE:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}=\rho,\qquad\rho_{2}=\frac{\rho}{2},\qquad\rho_{3}=\frac{\rho}{2}\Big(1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}\Big).

The conditions on
\zeta
ensure that
\rho_{3}\in(0,\rho/2]
. Here the end-point
\rho/2
only occurs if
\zeta=2
(i.e. trace-class noise).

2.1.3.
The noise term

Let us formalize the structure of the stochastic forcing driving the system.
Let
W^{j}=R^{j}B^{j}
, where
B^{1},\ldots,B^{\ell}
are independent
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-cylindrical Brownian motions. Moreover,
R^{j}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}))
is given by

\displaystyle R^{j}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}^{j}e_{n}^{j}\otimes\varphi_{n}^{j},

(2.2)

for suitable intensities
(\mu_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}
, an orthonormal basis
(e_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}
and an orthonormal system
(\varphi_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
. In other words, the covariance
R^{j}(R^{j})^{*}
of
W^{j}(1)
is given by

R^{j}(R^{j})^{*}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mu_{n}^{j}|^{2}\varphi_{n}^{j}\otimes\varphi_{n}^{j}.

Formally, the above corresponds to having

W^{j}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}^{j}\varphi_{n}^{j}w^{j}_{n}(t),

where
(w^{j}_{n})_{j,n}
is a family of standard independent Brownian motions.

The stochastic integral with respect to
W^{j}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(r)=\int_{0}^{t}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)R^{j}\,\mathrm{d}B^{j}(r).

(2.3)

Thus, we need that
g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)R^{j}\in L^{2}(0,t;\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})))
a.s. to ensure the stochastic integrability. Here
\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),Z)
denotes the
\gamma
-radonifying operators from
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
into a Banach space
Z
. For details on
\gamma
-radonifying operators we refer the reader to
[
32
, Chapter 9]
. For details on stochastic integration in spaces such as
H^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
, the reader is referred to
[
48
, Section 4]
.

To formulate the following assumptions concerning the structure of the noise, we introduce, for an orthonormal system

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j}=(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}^{j}
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
such that
\varphi_{n}^{j}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
the space

\displaystyle\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})=\begin{cases}\{(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})}^{\zeta}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\mu_{n}|^{\zeta}\|\varphi_{n}^{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}<\infty\},&\zeta\in[2,\infty),\\
\ell^{\infty},&\zeta=\infty.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Henceforth we fix orthonormal systems
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j}
in (
2.2
)
and impose the following relation on
\zeta
and
d
whenever
\mu^{j}\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})
for
\zeta<\infty
.

Assumption 2.3
.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
,
(\varphi^{j}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
form an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
such that
\varphi^{j}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
for each
n\geq 1
. The sequence
\mu^{j}=(\mu_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})
, where

\zeta\in[2,\infty]
is arbitrary if
d=1
and is such that

\displaystyle\zeta<\frac{2d}{d-2}\quad\text{whenever}\quad d\geq 2.

(2.5)

In many places throughout the paper we need that, for
\zeta\geq 2
the number

\displaystyle 1-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{\zeta}\in(0,1],

(2.6)

which is always satisfied in
d=1
and in
d\geq 2
leads precisely to the condition (
2.5
).

A key theorem for handling the noise term is our following recent result
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
on sharp estimates associated with (
2.1
) provided Assumption
2.3
is satisfied. We state the main result of that latter paper below, as it provides an important ingredient for several of the proofs of the results below. It is a key in estimating the
\gamma
-norm of
g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)R^{j}
which appears when bounding (
2.3
). Below we let
M_{g}h=gh
denote multiplication by
g
for
h\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
.

Theorem 2.4

(Sharp bounds for coloured noise)
.

Let
R^{j}
be as in (
2.2
) with associated orthonormal system
(\varphi_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
such that
\varphi^{j}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
for each
n\geq 1
.
Let
q\in(1,\infty)
and
s\in[0,d)
. Suppose that either

(1)

\zeta\in(2,\infty)
,
\eta\in(1,q)
,
\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\zeta}
and
\frac{1}{\eta}-\frac{1}{\zeta}<\frac{1}{2}
,

(2)

\zeta=2
,
\eta\in(1,q]
and

\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{\eta}
;

(3)

\zeta=\infty
,
2<\eta<q<\infty
and

\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}
.

Then for each
g\in L^{\eta}(\mathcal{O})
and
\mu^{j}\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})
, the operator
M_{g}R^{j}:L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\to\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
is
\gamma
-radonifying and satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|M_{g}R^{j}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))}\lesssim\|\mu^{j}\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})}\|g\|_{L^{\eta}(\mathcal{O})},

(2.7)

where the constant depends on the parameters
(d,s,q,\eta,\zeta)
.

Similar results hold for other boundary conditions and are immediate from
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
.
Through Theorem
2.4
, one can often check that
M_{g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)}R^{j}
is in
L^{2}(0,t;\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})))
a.s. under suitable conditions on
u
and
g_{i}
. This will be used in Section
2.2
below to obtain the well-definedness of the stochastic integral in (
2.3
).

In the existing literature on non-trace-class noise, it is common practice to assume that the functions
(\varphi_{n}^{j})_{n\geq 1}
are the
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunctions of a leading differential operator. A key advantage of our framework is that the sharp
\gamma
-radonifying bounds in Theorem
2.4
above rely exclusively on the
\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})
-norm and suitable norm of
g
. By decoupling the noise basis from the differential operator, our approach provides a highly flexible local theory that easily accommodates general orthonormal systems and domains where explicit eigenfunction bounds are unavailable.

Let us comment on a common choice in the literature to see what
\mu\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}^{j})
means.

Example 2.5

(Eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian)
.

A canonical choice in the literature is to take the orthonormal system
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}=(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as the
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-normalized eigenfunctions of the negative Dirichlet Laplacian
-\Delta
on a bounded smooth domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, associated with eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\to\infty
.

According to standard spectral estimates (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
) and Weyl’s law (
\lambda_{n}\sim n^{2/d}
), the eigenfunctions satisfy the asymptotic bound:

\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\lambda_{n}^{(d-1)/4}\sim n^{\frac{d-1}{2d}}.

(2.8)

In this setting, the spatial coloring condition
\mu\in\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
requires that the sequence of scalars
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies the summability constraint:

\sum_{n\geq 1}|\mu_{n}|^{\zeta}n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}<\infty.

(2.9)

For further comparison with the literature the reader is referred to
[
1
, Section 6.3]
.

2.2.
Local existence, uniqueness, and regularity

Henceforth we view equation (
2.1
) as an abstract evolution equation of the form (
4.1
), with the following choice of spaces:

\displaystyle X_{0}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}),\quad X_{1}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}),

(2.10)

in which case the interpolation spaces take the form

\displaystyle X_{1-\tfrac{1+\kappa}{p},p}=\prescript{}{D}{B}^{1-s-2\tfrac{1+\kappa}{p}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}),\quad X_{\theta}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s+2\theta,q}(\mathcal{O}),\quad\text{for $\theta\in(0,1)$.}

(2.11)

For simplicity in the notation we write
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})
instead of
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O};{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell})
. The latter is needed since we use systems.

Definition 2.6

(Strong
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution)
.

Let
s\in[0,1)
,
q\in[2,\infty)
,
p\in(2,\infty)
, and
\kappa\in[0,p/2-1)
. Let
\tau
be a finite stopping time, and let
u_{0}\in L^{0}_{\mathscr{F}_{0}}(\Omega;\prescript{}{D}{B}^{1-s-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O}))
be a given initial condition.

A strongly progressively measurable process
u=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}:[0,\tau]\times\Omega\to\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
is called a
strong
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
to (
2.1
) on
[0,\tau]
if, almost surely,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{p}(0,\tau,w_{\kappa};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))\cap C([0,\tau];\prescript{}{D}{B}^{1-s-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})),

\displaystyle\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u)),

\displaystyle\,f_{i}(\cdot,u)\in L^{p}(0,\tau,w_{\kappa};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})),

\displaystyle g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)R^{j}

\displaystyle\in L^{p}(0,\tau,w_{\kappa};\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))).

and for all
t\in[0,\tau]
and
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, the following holds as an equality in
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
almost surely:

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)-u_{0,i}-\int_{0}^{t}\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}(r))\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u(r)))+f_{i}(\cdot,u(r))\Big]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\tau]}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u(r))\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(r).

Since
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})=(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1+s,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))^{*}
and
s\in[0,1)
, the conditions on
\dv F_{i}(\cdot,u)
are understood via the duality form

\langle\dv F_{i}(\cdot,u),\phi\rangle=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}F_{i}(\cdot,u)\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\phi\in\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1+s,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}).

Since the elements of
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
are functions for
s\in[0,1)
, the composition
F_{i}(\cdot,u)
is well-defined in a pointwise sense. The same applies to
f_{i}(\cdot,u)
and
g^{j}_{i}(\cdot,u)
, where for the latter, due to Theorem
2.4
, it is enough to prove
g_{i}^{j}(u)\in L^{p}(0,\tau,w_{\kappa};L^{\eta}(\mathcal{O}))
a.s. for a suitable
\eta\in(1,q]
.
From the conditions in Theorem
2.9
below, one can check that the integrability assumptions of the above Definition
2.6
are fulfilled. This will be clear from the proofs.

Due to the above integrability conditions, and standard measurability properties, the deterministic integrals exist as Bochner integrals in
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
(see
[
31
, Chapter 1]
), and since
q\in[2,\infty)
, the stochastic integral exists as
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
valued Itô integral (see
[
48
, Section 4]
).

An advantage of working with spaces of distributions is that it allows to consider strong solutions even though the objects are not defined pointwise. Under the right regularity conditions on
u
, it is equivalent to use analytically weak solutions (see Remark
2.7
(
i
)). The advantage of the latter is that the integrals all become real-valued. In special cases, one can also consider mild solutions (see Remark
2.7
(
ii
)). The advantage of mild solutions is that the integrals do take values in spaces with positive spatial smoothness at least for almost all
t
.

Remark 2.7

(Other solution concepts)
.

The strong equality in
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
formulated in Definition
2.6
is closely related to other standard solution concepts in the SPDE literature
[
20
,
57
]
.

(i)

Analytically weak solutions:
Because the SPDE holds as a strong equality in the base space
X_{0}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
, it automatically satisfies the equation in the classical analytically weak (or distributional) sense. By taking the duality pairing with any test function
\phi\in\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm d}}}{{\hookrightarrow}}\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1+s,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})
(where
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1
), one can integrate by parts to transfer all the spatial derivatives onto
\phi
. Almost surely, for all
t\in[0,\sigma]
and
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}u_{i}(t)\phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathcal{O}}u_{0,i}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}u_{i}(r)\dv(a_{i}^{T}\cdot\nabla\phi)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\big(f_{i}(\cdot,u(r))\phi-F_{i}(\cdot,u(r))\cdot\nabla\phi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}^{j}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\sigma]}(r)\int_{\mathcal{O}}g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u(r))\varphi_{n}^{j}\phi\,\mathrm{d}w_{n}^{j}(r),

where

w^{j}_{n}(t)=B^{j}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}\otimes e^{j}_{n}),

where
(B^{j})_{j=1}^{\ell}
are the
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-cylindrical Brownian motions as in Subsection
2.1.3
.
Thus, a solution obtained through our
L^{p}(L^{q})
-framework satisfies the SPDE in the standard weak sense commonly used in PDEs without the need to artificially weaken the solution definition itself. If
u,\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u)),f_{i}(\cdot,u)
and
g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)
satisfy the integrability conditions in Definition
2.6
and satisfies the above integral identity, then it is a strong
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution in the sense of Definition
2.6
.

(ii)

Mild solutions:
In the special case that
a_{i}
is independent of
(t,\omega)
, one has that
-A_{i}
generates a strongly continuous analytic semigroup on
L^{q}(\mathcal{O})
. Moreover, using suitable extrapolation theory, one can check that it extends to a semigroup on
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
which regularizes to spaces of functions. In that case, assuming enough smoothness of
u
one can equivalently formulate the equation component-wise as a.s. for all
t\in[0,\sigma]

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)=e^{-tA_{i}}u_{0,i}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-r)A_{i}}\Big[\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u(r)))+f_{i}(\cdot,u(r))\Big]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-r)A_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\sigma]}(r)g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u(r))\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(r).

Next we continue to define local and maximal solutions.

Definition 2.8

(Maximal local
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution)
.

Let the parameters and initial data
u_{0}
be as in Definition
2.6
.

(1)

(Local Solution)
A pair
(u,\sigma)
is called a
local
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
to (
2.1
) if
\sigma:\Omega\to[0,\infty]
is a stopping time,
u:[0,\sigma)\to\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
is strongly progressively measurable, and there exists an increasing localizing sequence of stopping times
(\sigma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}=\sigma
almost surely, and for each
n\geq 1
, the stopped process
u|_{[0,\sigma_{n}]}
is a strong
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution on
[0,\sigma_{n}]
.

(2)

(Uniqueness)
A local
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
(u,\sigma)
is called
unique
if for any other local
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
(v,\tau)
with the same initial data,
u=v
almost surely on
[0,\sigma\wedge\tau)
.

(3)

(Maximality)
A unique local
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
(u,\sigma)
is called

if for any other unique local solution
(v,\tau)
with the same initial data, it holds that
\tau\leq\sigma
almost surely, and
u=v
on
[0,\tau)
.

The following is our main result on existence and uniqueness of (
2.1
). To describe the precise time-regularity we need the weighted vector-valued Bessel potential spaces. For details the reader is referred to
[
2
,
3
,
8
]
. We recall the definition here. Define the weight
w_{\kappa}^{a}(t):=(t-a)^{\kappa}
for real
a
and
\kappa\geq 0
.
For
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
,
I=(a,b),p\in(1,\infty)
,
\theta\in(0,1)
and a Banach space
X
, we define the vector-valued Bessel potential spaces by

\displaystyle H^{\theta,p}(I,w^{a}_{\kappa};X):=[L^{p}(I,w_{\kappa}^{a};X),W^{1,p}(I,w^{a}_{\kappa};X)]_{\theta},

(2.12)

where
W^{1,p}(I,w^{a}_{\kappa};X)
is the Sobolev space of regularity
1
and integrability
p
. If
w^{a}_{\kappa}=1
, then we omit it from the notation.

Next we state a general local well-posedness result for
\zeta<\infty
. The case
\zeta=\infty
and

(i.e. multiplicative white noise) will be considered in Theorem
2.22
below.

Theorem 2.9

(Local existence and uniqueness)
.

Let Assumption
2.1
(s,q,p,\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})

hold, let
\kappa\in[0,\tfrac{p}{2}-1)

and consider (

3.
Applications

In this section, we apply our abstract well-posedness and regularization framework to several prototypical stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). While our main theorems are stated in terms of general growth parameters
\rho_{i}
, these bounds are deeply connected to the canonical nonlinearities that drive physical, biological, and fluid-dynamical models.

In particular, we focus on polynomial nonlinearities of cubic (
\rho_{1}=2
) and quadratic (
\rho_{1}=1
and
\rho_{2}=1
) type. These structures are not merely mathematical test cases, but rather represent the fundamental leading-order interactions in nonlinear systems:

•

Cubic non-divergence nonlinearities (
\rho_{1}=2
):
Arising naturally in models of phase separation and quantum field theory (such as the Ginzburg-Landau or Allen-Cahn equations), cubic drifts model systems driven by symmetric double-well potentials, where the nonlinearity drives the separation of states. See Subsection
3.1
.

•

Quadratic non-divergence nonlinearities (
\rho_{1}=1
):
In biological and chemical models (like the Fisher-KPP or coupled Lotka-Volterra systems), quadratic terms representing binary interactions dictate population dynamics and reaction rates. See Subsection
3.2
.

•

Quadratic divergence nonlinearities (
\rho_{2}=1
):
In fluid dynamics, advective transport fundamentally generates quadratic divergence-form nonlinearities, driving the energy cascade in the Burgers and Navier-Stokes equations. See Subsection
3.3
.

•

Finally, in Subsection
3.4
we consider more general
\rho_{1}
, and focus on the case (white noise)
\zeta=\infty
. We present applications to some rather academic superlinear examples in which we can establish global existence and uniqueness for rough initial data.

The interplay between polynomial growth and singular stochastic forcing typically restricts solutions to distribution spaces of very low regularity. The examples below illustrate how our abstract framework translates directly to these specific physical models. Based on Theorems
2.9
and
2.12
, we identify the exact critical scaling regimes for the Allen-Cahn, Burgers, and Fisher-KPP equations, thereby guaranteeing local well-posedness for highly irregular initial states.

In a forthcoming paper, we will improve our current local results to global ones. This will be done through the regularization and blow-up criteria of Theorem
2.12
and Corollary
2.21
and sophisticated energy bounds.

Example 3.4

(The Stochastic Gray-Scott model)
.

To illustrate our framework’s capacity to handle coupled cubic systems, we consider the stochastic Gray-Scott model. This system is famous for its rich array of pattern-forming behaviors (such as Turing spots, stripes, and spirals) driven by an autocatalytic reaction (

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}u-\dv(a_{1}\cdot\nabla u)\,\mathrm{d}t&=(-uv^{2}+r_{f}(1-u))\,\mathrm{d}t+g_{1}^{1}(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}W^{1}(t)+g_{1}^{2}(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2}(t),\\

Here,

Because the growth parameter (

) is mathematically identical to that of the scalar Allen-Cahn equation of Examples

, the exact same geometric scaling restrictions on the parameter space apply.
Thus, we obtain the same local theory and critical spaces. Global existence for the stochastic Gray-Scott model is much harder to obtain than for fully coercive systems. For the case of transport noise and

, global well-posedness was recently established in
[
5
]
for the more general Brusselator model for

and
d=3
. Global well-posedness for certain triangular systems for the rougher situation

was recently considered in
[
46
]
. The blow-up criteria of Corollary
2.21
set the stage to obtain many further global well-posedness results, for rough classes of initial data.

Finally, Remark
3.3
also applies to the presented Gray-Scott model.

Remark 3.15
.

The above result can be seen as a partial extension of
[
21
, Theorem 1.9]
. As is clear from the proof our result also relies on the latter. In the latter paper only locally Lipschitzness of
f
and
g
is required, whereas we have an assumption on the polynomial growth of order
\rho
of the Lipschitz constant. On the other hand, they require that the initial data is in
\prescript{}{D}{C}^{\varepsilon}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
and we consider initial data from the much larger space of distributions
\prescript{}{D}{B}^{-\frac{2}{\rho+1}+\varepsilon}_{q_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{O})
with
q_{\varepsilon}=\big(\frac{2}{(\rho+1)\rho}+\varepsilon\big)^{-1}
. By Sobolev embedding and taking
\varepsilon>0
small enough, one sees that our initial value space contains initial data from
L^{v}(\mathcal{O})
for any

. In particular, if
\rho=2
, this leads to initial values from
L^{v}(\mathcal{O})
with

.

In an even more recent paper,
[
27
]
showed that the polynomial growth of order
\rho
of the Lipschitz constant bound can be removed for

-initial data, provided the noise is bounded. In contrast, our work handles initial data that is significantly rougher (distributions in

) and allows for unbounded noise. To handle the extreme initial roughness and unbounded noise simultaneously, we demonstrate that a polynomial constraint (
\rho_{1}
) on the local Lipschitz geometry is sufficient to bridge the solution into the globally well-posed continuous regime. Our results show that for nonlinearities
f
and
g
with polynomially growing Lipschitz constants, the stated Problems 1.4 and 1.5 in
[
27
]
have positive answers. The case of general locally Lipschitz functions remains open. A typical example which is not covered by our setting is
f(u)=\sin(e^{u})
.

Indeed, a simple calculation gives

\int_{a}^{\sigma}\|u^{i}(t)\|_{X_{1}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{a}^{\sigma}\|u^{i}(t)\|_{X_{1}}^{p_{i}}t^{\frac{\kappa_{i}p}{p_{i}}}t^{-\frac{\kappa_{i}p}{p_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\left(\int_{a}^{\sigma}\|u^{i}(t)\|_{X_{1}}^{p_{i}}t^{\kappa_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{p}{p_{i}}}\left(\int_{a}^{\sigma}t^{-\frac{\kappa_{i}p}{p_{i}-p}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1-\frac{p}{p_{i}}}.

Hence, both
u^{0}
and
u^{1}
belong to
L^{p}(a,\sigma;X_{1})
almost surely.
Since
A
has stochastic maximal
L^{p}
-regularity on bounded time-intervals (see below Assumption
4.2
), it follows from
[
8
, Prop. 3.11]
that
u^{0}=u^{1}
a.s. on
[a,\sigma]
.
∎

4.2.
Maximal regularity for divergence form operators in extrapolation spaces

In this subsection we give conditions which imply that a second order divergence form operator
L
has maximal
L^{p}
-regularity as defined in Definition
4.1
.
This is used in the proof of Theorem
2.9
in Subsection
4.3
.
For simplicity, we only consider scalar equations in this subsection, which is enough for our purposes. However, it is possible to extend it to (complex) systems using the appropriate extension of the ellipticity condition (see
[
24
, Section 5]
). As the deterministic setting allows more flexibility in the parameters, we will allow
p,q\in(1,\infty)
and
\kappa\in(-1,p-1)
in this subsection

There is an extensive literature on maximal regularity for second order differential operators both in divergence and non-divergence form (see
[
30
,
22
,
38
]
). The main new aspect below is that we consider the differential operator on the space

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})\quad\text{with domain}\quad X_{1}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}),

(4.9)

with
s\in[-1,1]
and
q\in(1,\infty)
. Here
\mathcal{O}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded
C^{2}
-domain.

Assumption 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded
C^{2}
-domain. Let

1<p,q<\infty
and
\kappa\in(-1,p-1)
. Let
a=(a^{j,k})_{j,k=1}^{d}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{O}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
be measurable and assume there exist
M>0
,
\nu>0
such that

\displaystyle\|a^{j,k}(t,\cdot)\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}

\displaystyle\leq M,\ \ t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}a^{j,k}(t,x)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}

\displaystyle\geq\nu|\xi|^{2},\ \ \ t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\mathcal{O}},\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Under Assumption
4.11
, we define
L(t):\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q}(\mathcal{O})\to L^{q}(\mathcal{O})

by
L(t)u=-\operatorname{div}(a(t,\cdot)\nabla u)
.
Integrating by parts twice, one finds

\langle L(t)u,\psi\rangle=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}u(x)\,\operatorname{div}\big(a(t,x)^{T}\nabla\psi(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\psi\in\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}).

This implies that we can also view
L
as a
\mathcal{L}(L^{q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}))
-valued function. By complex interpolation we see that
L:[0,T]\to\mathcal{L}(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))
for any
s\in[-1,1]
.

Using
s=1
, one can define very weak solutions as in
[
41
,
42
]
. Moreover, using the latter setting we will obtain a result on maximal
L^{p}
-regularity for any
s\in[-1,1]
.

Theorem 4.12

(A Lions–Magenes type result)
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.11
holds.
Let
s\in[-1,1]
and let the family
L:[0,T]\to\mathcal{L}(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))
be defined as above. Then
L
has maximal
L^{p}(0,T,w_{\kappa})
-regularity with constant
C
which only depends on
T,M,\nu,p,q,\kappa,d,\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

In the proof we use general
A_{p}
-weights in time, since we need duality and time-reversal arguments in the proof. One can check that the definition of maximal
L^{p}
-regularity extends to general
A_{p}
-weights in time. For the theory of
A_{p}
-weights, the reader is referred to
[
28
, Chapter 7]
. Recall that the dual weight
w^{\prime}=w^{-1/(p-1)}
is in
A_{p^{\prime}}
. Moreover,
w(t)=t^{\kappa}
is in
A_{p}
if and only if
\kappa\in(-1,p-1)
.

Maximal
L^{p}(0,T,w)
-regularity can be equivalently stated as the boundedness of

\mathcal{M}:L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))\to L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))),

where
\mathcal{M}f=u
, is defined as the unique
u\in L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
,

u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}-L(r)u(r)+f(r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Therefore, by complex interpolation and
[
32
, Theorem 2.2.6]
, it is enough to prove
\mathcal{M}
is well-defined and bounded for
s=-1
and
s=1
.

Step 1 – Case
s=-1
:
For
\mathcal{O}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\mathcal{O}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}
, maximal
L^{p}(0,T,w)
-regularity follows from
[
24
, Theorems 5.2 and 5.4]
. The case of bounded
C^{2}
-domains can be deduced from this by standard localization/partition of unity arguments (see
[
25
,
35
,
40
]
). In these references, the passage from the whole-space and half-space estimates to bounded
C^{2}
-domains is obtained by the usual localization and boundary-flattening argument. The change of variables preserves uniform ellipticity and produces only lower-order terms with coefficients bounded in terms of
M
and
\mathcal{O}
; these are absorbed by the standard perturbation theorem for maximal regularity. The resulting constants depend only on
T,M,\nu,p,q,\kappa,d
and
\mathcal{O}
.

Step 2 – Case
s=1
:

Let
\widetilde{L}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{L}(\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}),L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
be defined by

\widetilde{L}(t)\psi:=-\operatorname{div}\big(a(t,\cdot)^{T}\nabla\psi\big)=-\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}a^{k,j}(t,\cdot)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi-(\partial_{j}a^{k,j})(t,\cdot)\partial_{k}\psi.

From Step 1 with weight
\widetilde{w}(t):=w^{\prime}(T-t)
, a bounded perturbation, and a time-reversal one can see that
the backward problem

\displaystyle-v^{\prime}(t)+\widetilde{L}(t)v(t)=g(t),\qquad v(T)=0,

(4.10)

with
g\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))

has a unique solution
v\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
, where a solution is defined in the strong sense as

v(t)-\int_{t}^{T}\widetilde{L}(r)v(r)\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{t}^{T}g(r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Moreover, by the maximal regularity of Step 1:

\|v\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))}.

Furthermore, the above identity also implies that
v\in W^{1,p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
.

Now, to prove maximal
L^{p}
-regularity, choose an arbitrary
f\in L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}))
. For
g\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
let
v_{g}\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
denote the solution to (
4.10
). Define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{f}(g)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f(t),v_{g}(t)\rangle_{\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.11)

Then
\Lambda_{f}
is linear and by the regularity estimate for the backward problem

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{f}(g)|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}))}\|v_{g}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime},\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}))}\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))}.

Therefore, since
(L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})))^{*}=L^{p}(0,T,w;L^{q}(\mathcal{O}))
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure), there exists a unique
u_{f}\in L^{p}(0,T,w;L^{q}(\mathcal{O}))
such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{f}(g)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{f}(t),g(t)\rangle_{L^{q}(\mathcal{O}),L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.12)

and
\|u_{f}\|_{L^{p}(0,T,w;L^{q}(\mathcal{O}))}=\|\Lambda_{f}\|\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T,w;\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-2,q}(\mathcal{O}))}
.

It remains to check that
u_{f}
is the solution to the original parabolic problem. For
u_{f}
to be a solution we need to show that for all
t\in[0,T]
,

u_{f}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}-L(r)u(r)+f(r)\,\mathrm{d}r.

For this it is enough to check that for all
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,T)

\int_{0}^{T}u_{f}(t)\zeta^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}L(r)u_{f}(r)\zeta(r)-f(t)\zeta(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

This is equivalent to for all
\psi\in\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})

\int_{0}^{T}\langle u_{f}(t),\psi\rangle\zeta^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}[\langle u_{f}(r),\widetilde{L}(r)\psi\rangle-\langle f(t),\psi\rangle]\zeta(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

To prove the latter identity, it suffices to set
g(t)=-\zeta^{\prime}(t)\psi+\widetilde{L}(t)\psi\zeta(t)
, and note that
v_{g}=\zeta\psi
and combine this with (
4.11
) and (
4.12
).

By linearity it is enough to prove uniqueness for
f=0
. Let
u\in L^{p}(0,T,w;L^{q}(\mathcal{O}))
be such that for all
t\in[0,T]

u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}L(r)u(r)\,\mathrm{d}r=0.

Then an approximation argument shows that for every

\displaystyle\varphi\in W^{1,p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))\cap L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))

(4.13)

with
\varphi(T)=0

one has

\int_{0}^{T}\langle u(t),\varphi^{\prime}(t)-\widetilde{L}(t)\varphi(t)\rangle_{L^{q},L^{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Indeed, this can first be checked for
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,T])\otimes\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})
and then extended by density, first to
C^{1}([0,T];L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))\cap C([0,T];\prescript{}{D}{H}^{2,q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
and then to the general case (
4.13
).

Now, let
g\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T,w^{\prime};L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O}))
be arbitrary, and let
v_{g}
be the solution of the backward adjoint problem

-v_{g}^{\prime}(t)+\widetilde{L}(t)v_{g}(t)=g(t),\qquad v_{g}(T)=0.

Taking
\varphi=v_{g}
in the preceding identity gives (in the beginning of Step 2 we have seen that
v_{g}
has the required regularity)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle u(t),g(t)\rangle_{L^{q},L^{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle u(t),v_{g}^{\prime}(t)-\widetilde{L}(t)v_{g}(t)\rangle_{L^{q},L^{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Since
g
was arbitrary, it follows that
u=0
.

The same argument applies on every subinterval
(a,b)\subset(0,T)
after the translation
t\mapsto t-a
, with the weight
w_{\kappa}^{a}(t)=(t-a)^{\kappa}
. Since the
A_{p}
-characteristic of
w_{\kappa}^{a}
is independent of
a
and
b
, the constants are uniform. This gives maximal regularity in the sense of Definition
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

For
s=0
many sufficient conditions are known under which an elliptic second order operator
A
with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions has maximal
L^{p}
-regularity in the case the coefficients are
(t,x)
-dependent. See for instance
[
23
, Theorem 8.1]
and
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
for
p,q\in(1,\infty)
,
C^{1}
-domains
\mathcal{O}
, and with coefficients measurable in time and VMO in space (at least unweighted).

One would expect that if the coefficients
a^{j,k}\in C^{|s|+}(\mathcal{O})
, then the maximal
L^{p}
-regularity results remain valid on
\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
. Such results for space and time-dependent
a^{j,k}
seem unavailable in the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions. In the periodic case and on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such results can be found in
[
6
, Theorem 5.2]
, where even the stochastic setting is covered. It might be possible to apply a similar technique as in the latter paper (but without the stochastic part) and reduce to the case
s=0
by applying
(-\Delta)^{-(1+s)/2}
to the equation and use perturbation arguments.

Remark 4.14
.

In case the coefficients
a^{j,k}
are time-independent, we can also consider
L(t)u=\dv(a\nabla u)
with the Neumann-type boundary condition
(a\nabla u)\cdot n=0
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
. Indeed, this follows from the same method as before again applying a deformed version of
[
24
, Theorem 6.4]
.

Time-dependent coefficients would lead to time-dependent boundary conditions. In principle, this could be something one could study for some values of
s
such as
s=0
. Then the domain of the operator remains time-independent which is something we need in Subsection
4.1
.

Proofs of the local well-posedness and identification of critical spaces

Before we turn to the proof of Theorem

We define the following spaces on the domain
\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})\quad\text{and}\quad X_{1}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{1-s,q}(\mathcal{O})

(4.14)

so that by interpolation

\displaystyle X_{1-\tfrac{1+\kappa}{p},p}

(4.15)

{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
-valued spaces.

The idea will be to apply Theorem
4.5
, for which we need to verify that
Assumption
4.2
holds, with the mappings

\displaystyle(Au)_{i}:=-\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}),\quad(\Phi_{2}(\cdot,u))_{i}:=\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,u)-F_{i}(\cdot,0)),\quad(\Phi_{1}(\cdot,u))_{i}:

\displaystyle=f_{i}(\cdot,u)-f_{i}(\cdot,0)

(4.16)

and
\phi_{i}=\dv(F_{i}(\cdot,0))+f_{i}(\cdot,0)
for

.

For the noise term we use
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{\ell}
and define
\Psi(t,\omega,u)\in\gamma(\mathcal{U},X_{1/2})
as

(\Psi(\cdot,u)h)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}M_{g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,u)-g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,0)}R^{j}h^{j},\qquad h:=(h^{1},\ldots,h^{\ell})\in\mathcal{U},

Note that an
(s,q,p,\kappa)

) with the above choices of the spaces.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

Since
q\in[2,\infty)
, the spaces
X_{0}
and

are UMD and have type
2
(see
[
31

For the reference operator
A_{0}

4.2

-\Delta
(see
[
8

L^{p}

4.12
applied pointwise in
\Omega
, and using that
A
has a diagonal structure.

For the
\Phi_{1}
-estimate, we compute, for
\alpha_{1}\in[0,1-\tfrac{1+\kappa}{p})

\displaystyle\|f_{i}(u)-f_{i}(v)\|_{X_{\alpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\lesssim}}\|f_{i}(u)-f_{i}(v)\|_{L^{\eta_{1}}(\mathcal{O})}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(1+|u|^{\rho_{1}}+|v|^{\rho_{1}})(u-v)\|_{L^{\eta_{1}}(\mathcal{O})}

(4.19)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\lesssim}}(1+\|u\|_{L^{(\rho_{1}+1)\eta_{1}}(\mathcal{O})}^{\rho_{1}}+\|v\|_{L^{(\rho_{1}+1)\eta_{1}}(\mathcal{O})}^{\rho_{1}})\|u-v\|_{L^{(\rho_{1}+1)\eta_{1}}(\mathcal{O})}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\lesssim}}(1+\|u\|_{X_{\beta_{1}}}^{\rho_{1}}+\|v\|_{X_{\beta_{1}}}^{\rho_{1}})\|u-v\|_{X_{\beta_{1}}},

(4.21)

where in (ii) we applied Hölder’s inequality and in (i) and (iii) we used the Sobolev embeddings

\displaystyle\rho_{1}\big(\beta_{1}-1+\frac{1+\kappa}{p}\big)+\beta_{1}\leq 1+\alpha_{1},\quad\alpha_{1}\in\big[0,1-\frac{1+\kappa}{p}\big),\,\beta_{1}\in\big(1-\frac{1+\kappa}{p},1\big).

Requiring that the range for

\displaystyle 1-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}-\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}+1}\frac{d}{q}<s<\min\Big\{1+\frac{d}{\rho_{1}+1}-\frac{d}{q},\,d+1-\frac{d}{q}-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p},1+\frac{d\rho_{1}}{q}-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}\Big\},

Similarly, for

, one has

X_{\theta}=[X_{0},X_{1}]_{\theta}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-1-s+2\theta,q}(\mathcal{O})\ \ \text{ for }\theta\in(0,1).

Hence,
X_{1/2}=\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O})
. Moreover, for all
v\in X_{1}
, it follows from the boundedness of the coefficients in Assumption
4.19
that

\|\dv(bv)+c\,v\|_{X_{0}}\lesssim\|v\|_{L^{q}(\mathcal{O})},

and

\|\chi v\,R_{\mu}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),X_{1/2})}\lesssim\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}\|v\|_{L^{\eta}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|\mu\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}\|v\|_{L^{q}(\mathcal{O})},

where in the above we applied Theorem
2.4
(
1
) with
\eta
close to
q
(this choice is allowed due to the first in (
4.111
)). As
X_{(1+s)/2}=L^{q}(\mathcal{O})
and
s<1
, it follows that the operators
v\mapsto\dv(bv)+cv
and
v\mapsto\chi v\,R_{\mu}
are lower-order. The claimed estimates (
4.113
) in the case
v_{0}=0
are a consequence of
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
and

(X_{0},X_{1})_{1-\frac{1+\kappa}{p},p}=\prescript{}{D}{B}^{1-s-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\mathcal{O}}),

where in the last step we used the third inequality in (
4.111
).

In case
v_{0}\neq 0
, we can apply
[
8
, Proposition 3.11]
and use
\prescript{}{D}{C}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow B^{1-s-2\frac{1+\kappa}{p}}_{q,p}(\mathcal{O})
by the second condition in (
4.111
).
∎

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
2.24
is the following result.

Lemma 4.21

(Positivity for linear SPDEs with rough noise)
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.19
holds with
\psi_{j}\equiv 0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, and that

v_{0}\geq 0\ \text{ a.e.\ on }\ \Omega\times\mathcal{O},\quad\text{ and }\quad\phi\geq 0\ \text{ a.e.\ on }\ {\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}.

Let
s\in[0,1)
,
q\in[2,\infty)
,
p\in(2,\infty)
, and
\kappa\in[0,p/2-1)
be such that (
4.111
) holds.
Then the maximal
(s,q,p,\kappa)
-solution
v
to (
4.108
) satisfies

v\geq 0\ \text{ a.e.\ on }\ {\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}.

(4.114)

For convenience of the reader, we include a direct proof of this standard result.

Proof.

For simplicity, we consider the case
\ell=1
, the other follows analogously. Thus, we write
\chi=\chi_{1}
and
\mu=\mu^{1}
.
Arguing as in
[
4
, Lemma A.1]
, the claim follows by combining the maximum principle in
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
and an approximation argument.
By replacing
\phi
by
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\tau_{n}]}\phi
where

\tau_{n}=\inf\{t\in[0,\infty)\,:\,\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\mathcal{O}))}\geq n\}\quad\inf\varnothing:=\infty,

and
v_{0}
by
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|v_{0}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\leq n\}}v_{0}
for some
n<\infty
, it suffices to consider
\phi
and
v_{0}
with finite moments of any order. From the last assertion in Lemma
4.20
there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|v(t)\|_{C(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}^{p}\Big]\leq C_{0}.

(4.115)

where
(s,q,p,\kappa)
are as in (
4.111
).
Fix
N\geq 1
and
T\in(0,\infty)
. Let

\mu^{(N)}_{k}:=\mu_{k}
for
k\leq N
and
\mu^{(N)}_{k}=0
otherwise.
Clearly,

\mu^{(N)}\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
.
Hence, from standard regularity theory of SPDEs (see e.g.,
[
43
, Chapter 4]
), there exists a unique progressively measurable process

v^{(N)}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega;H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O}))\cap L^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{O})))

satisfying the SPDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}v^{(N)}=\big[\dv(a\cdot\nabla v^{(N)}+bv^{(N)})+cv^{(N)}+\phi\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\chi v^{(N)}R_{\mu^{(N)}}\,\mathrm{d}B,\\
&v^{(N)}(0)=v_{0},\qquad v^{(N)}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.116)

Since
\mu^{(N)}\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})
, well-known positivity results for linear SPDE with trace class noise (see e.g.,
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
) imply that (
4.114
) holds with
v
replaced by
v^{(N)}
.
Note that

w^{(N)}:=v-v^{(N)}

solves the linear SPDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}w^{(N)}=\big[\dv(a\cdot\nabla w^{(N)}+bw^{(N)})+cw^{(N)}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\big({\chi w^{(N)}}R_{\mu^{(N)}}+\chi vR_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}B,\\
&w^{(N)}(0)=0,\qquad w^{(N)}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.117)

Arguing as in Lemma
4.20
,
Assumption
4.19
ensures the existence of a constant
C>0
such that, for all
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w^{(N)}(t)\|_{C(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}^{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi vR_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))}^{p}\,t^{\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.118)

If
\zeta<\infty
, then from Theorem
2.4
(
1
), it follows from (
4.115
) and the choice of
(\mu^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
that

\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w^{(N)}(t)\|_{C(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}^{p}\big]\leq C\,\|\mu-\mu^{(N)}\|_{\ell^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S}_{\varphi})}^{p}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\to}}0,

In the case
\zeta=\infty
, we need to argue differently. Here, we employ the convergence result for
\gamma
-radonifying operators in
[
32
, Theorem 9.1.14]
.
Let
P_{N}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}(e_{1},\dots,e_{N})
. Note that

M_{\chi v}R_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}=M_{\chi v}R_{\mu}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{N}).

where
M_{\chi v}
denotes the multiplication by
\chi v
.
Hence, from Theorem
2.4
(
3
) and
[
32
, Theorem 9.1.14]
, it follows that

M_{\chi v}R_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\to}}0\ \text{ in }\ \gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))\ \text{ a.e.\ on }(0,T)\times\Omega.

Moreover, from the ideal property of
\gamma
-radonifying operators
[
32
, Theorem 9.1.10]
, we have

\|M_{\chi v}R_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))}\leq\|M_{\chi v}R_{\mu}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))},

as
\|I-P_{N}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}))}\leq 1
, and by combining the Lebesgue domination theorem, Theorem
2.4
(
3
) and (
4.115
), we infer

\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w^{(N)}(t)\|_{C(\overline{\mathcal{O}})}^{p}\big]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\chi vR_{\mu-\mu^{(N)}}\|_{\gamma(L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\prescript{}{D}{H}^{-s,q}(\mathcal{O}))}^{p}\,t^{\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\to}}0.

Hence, (
4.114
) follows from the above and
v^{(N)}\geq 0
a.e. on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}
as proven below (
4.116
).
∎

With the above result at our disposal, we can prove Theorem
2.24
.

Proof of Theorem
2.24
.

One can follow the same method as in
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
, which is based on a linearization procedure. We point out the main differences only.

Step 1: (Reduction) It suffices to consider
u_{0}\in L_{\mathscr{F}_{0}}^{\infty}(\Omega;\prescript{}{D}{C}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O}))
for some
\alpha>0
in Theorem
2.24
.

In light of the instantaneous regularization result (
2.17
) in Theorem
2.12
and Proposition
4.9
with the choices made at the beginning of Subsection
4.3
,
the claim of Step 1 follows verbatim from Steps 1 and 2 of
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
. Here we used the extension of the compatibility result stated in Proposition
4.9
. Moreover, to approximate
u_{0}
, we can use the heat semigroup to regularize while preserving positivity.

Step 2: Smooth initial data.

From Step 1, we assume that
u_{0}\in L_{\mathscr{F}_{0}}^{\infty}(\Omega;\prescript{}{D}{C}^{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{O}}))
for some
\alpha>0
.
As in
[
4
, Theorem 2.13]
, we consider the following variant of (
2.1
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}u_{i}^{+}-\dv(a_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}^{+})\,\mathrm{d}t&=\Big[\dv(F_{i}^{+}(\cdot,u^{+}))+f_{i}^{+}(\cdot,u^{+})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}g_{i}^{j,+}(\cdot,u^{+})\,\mathrm{d}W^{j}(t),\\
u_{i}^{+}(0)&=u_{0,i},\end{aligned}\right.

(4.119)

where, for

f_{i}^{+}(\cdot,y)=f_{i}(\cdot,y\vee 0),\quad F_{i}^{+}(\cdot,y)=F_{i}(\cdot,y\vee 0),\ \ \text{ and }\ \ g_{i}^{j,+}(\cdot,y)=g_{i}^{j}(\cdot,y\vee 0),

and

The idea behind the use of the system of SPDEs in (

2.30
) as the vector

has all non-negative entries. Note that the nonlinearities

2.21
and Proposition
4.17

are also valid for the system of SPDEs (

Let

be the maximal

\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{\zeta}<s_{0},\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ 0<1-s_{0}-2\frac{1+\kappa_{0}}{p_{0}}-\frac{d}{q_{0}}<\alpha.

(4.121)

For instance, the above holds for

close to

and therefore

u^{+}\in C([0,\sigma^{+});\prescript{}{D}{C}(\overline{\mathcal{O}}))\ \text{ a.s. }

Due to the pathwise regularity of

in the previously displayed formula, we can follow Step 4 from the proof of

u^{+}\geq 0\ \text{ a.e.\ on }\ [0,\sigma^{+})\times\Omega\times\mathcal{O}.

Here we use Lemmas
4.20
and
4.21

Arguing as in the argument below

The positivity of

4.122
