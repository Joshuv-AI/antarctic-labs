CaC: Advancing Video Reward Models via Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Concentrating
Jiyuan Wang, Huan Ouyang, Jiuzhou Lin, Chunyu Lin, Dewen Fan, Boheng Zhang, Haonan Fan, Fei Zuo, Jia Sun, Huaiqing Wang, Honglie Wang, Yiyang Fan, Zhenlong Yuan, Zijun Li, Yongrui Heng, Guosheng Lin, Fan Yang, Tingting Gao, [4pt] BJTU

Abstract
Recently, video generation models have achieved remarkable progress in global visual fidelity. However, when synthesizing complex dynamic content, they frequently produce sparse subtle anomalies . Existing video reward models primarily emphasize overall visual quality and text alignment; yet as generation quality improves, these coarse-grained defects become less frequent, while the sparse structural, temporal, and physical anomalies become an increasingly important bottleneck. Therefore, in this paper, we propose Concentrate and Concentrate (CaC), a coarse-to-fine anomaly reward model based on Vision-Language Models. During inference, it first conducts a global temporal scan to anchor anomalous time windows, then performs fine-grained spatial grounding within the localized interval, and finally derives robust judgments via structured spatiotemporal Chain-of-Thought reasoning. To equip the model with these capabilities, we construct the first large-scale generated video anomaly dataset with per-frame bounding-box annotations, temporal anomaly windows, and fine-grained attribution labels. Building on this dataset, we design a three-stage progressive training paradigm. The model initially learns spatial and temporal anchoring through single- and multi-frame supervised fine-tuning, and then is optimized by a reinforcement learning strategy based on two-turn Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Beyond conventional accuracy rewards, we introduce Temporal and Spatial IoU rewards to supervise the intermediate localization process, effectively guiding the model toward more grounded and interpretable spatiotemporal reasoning. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CaC can stably “concentrate” on subtle anomalies, achieving a 25.7% accuracy improvement on fine-grained anomaly benchmarks and, when used as a reward signal, CaC reduces generated-video anomalies by 11.7% while improving overall video quality.

Recent advances in video generation models
[

,

,

have significantly improved the visual fidelity of generated videos. However, when producing complex and dynamic content, these models still frequently exhibit hallucinations
[

]
, including structural distortions
[
17
,

]
, physical-law violations
[

, temporal inconsistencies
[

, and corrupted text rendering
[
35
,

]
. Unlike the well-defined anomalies observed in other areas, anomalies in generated videos are far more complex and often manifest as
subtle
violations of physical/logical priors.

Recently, the reinforcement learning (RL) paradigm has significantly enhanced the capabilities of generative models by employing reward models to evaluate outputs and drive optimization. In the domain of video generation, there is a critical need for a robust reward model capable of providing stable and accurate feedback on video anomalies, thereby advancing video generation quality.

While existing video reward models
[
66
,
63
,
68
,
71
,
19
,
62
]
play a pivotal role in aligning human preferences, they still largely emphasize visual quality and text alignment, leaving structural distortions and localization errors relatively under-explored.
Unlike global visual quality issues, hallucinations in generated videos are often spatiotemporally sparse, appearing only in a few frames or occupying small localized regions.
Consequently, identifying these subtle errors requires fine-grained spatiotemporal perception to isolate weak anomaly signals from large amounts of normal context, yet these faint signals are often overwhelmed by current models’ global video-level reasoning.
To bridge this gap, video reward modeling requires a dedicated mechanism that can actively concentrate on these sparse anomalies through spatiotemporal focusing, filtering out the vast normal context to enable fine-grained reasoning.

In this paper, we propose
Concentrate and Concentrate (CaC)
, a VLM-based reward model with a coarse-to-fine two-turn localization strategy for focusing on subtle anomalies. During inference, CaC first conducts a global scan to anchor the anomalous temporal range, followed by fine-grained spatial grounding within the selected time window. This design not only reduces the burden of spatiotemporal search, but also mitigates VLM hallucinations by deriving final judgments from interpretable spatiotemporal CoT reasoning.

To enable this coarse-to-fine reasoning ability, we construct a three-stage training framework. First, we train the model to recognize and localize anomalies. Given single frames extracted from videos, the model learns anomaly comprehension and bbox grounding, laying a foundation for subsequent video-level detection.
Second, we extend training to multi-frame inputs, enabling the model to capture temporal errors in dynamic scenes. This stage jointly trains global video scanning and fine-grained video-clip localization, ensuring that the model outputs the formats required for two-turn interaction.
Finally, we employ two-turn GRPO to optimize the VLM’s reasoning trajectory, enabling it to produce more stable and accurate judgments. We design a diversified reward mechanism, including format verification, temporal IoU, spatial IoU, attribution IoU, and result accuracy, which guides the model toward more accurate spatiotemporal localization and interpretable anomaly judgment.

To support the above pipeline, we construct the first large-scale dataset for sparse anomalies in generated videos, with expert annotations of temporal segments, anomaly types, and
per-frame bboxes
. Although labor-intensive, these annotations are crucial: 1) they improve CoT ground-truth (GT) construction by allowing video clips with GT spatiotemporal annotations to be fed into the foundation model, effectively reducing hallucinations during label generation compared with prior work
[
19
,
66
]
; 2) they provide references for attribution and temporal/spatial IoU rewards in the third training stage, guiding the model toward more interpretable reasoning through explicit localization.

Extensive experiments on our anomaly benchmark demonstrate that CaC reliably detects sparse anomalies, achieving
81.7%
overall accuracy and improving over the strongest baseline by 25.7%. On a harder split with increasingly sparse anomalies, CaC’s advantage grows as anomalies become shorter in duration and smaller in spatial extent. CaC also improves accuracy by 19.9% on the external Skyra-derived subset, providing an out-of-domain generalization. More importantly, when used as a reward signal for downstream video generation, CaC reduces generated-video anomalies by 11.7% and improves overall video quality, demonstrating that its judgments provide actionable optimization feedback. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We construct the first large-scale comprehensive dataset for sparse anomalies in generated videos, providing anomaly types, reasons, temporal windows, and per-frame bboxes. These annotations support high-quality CoT synthesis for SFT and provide verifiable reward signals for the RL stage.

•

We propose CaC, a coarse-to-fine two-turn reward model that recognizes sparse video anomalies through hierarchical spatiotemporal focusing. Its structured judgments can be converted into reward feedback for reducing anomalies in generated videos.

•

We develop a three-stage progressive pipeline for CaC training. In the RL stage, we further introduce temporal, spatial, and attribution IoU rewards to guide the VLM toward grounded, interpretable anomaly CoT reasoning and stable judgments.

3
Method

3.1
Dataset Preparation

To enable the model to detect subtle hallucinations in generated videos, we construct a large-scale dataset with fine-grained spatiotemporal anomaly annotations, specifically targeting sparse video distortions. The overall construction pipeline is illustrated in Fig.
2
.

Data Collection and Taxonomy.

We collect images and prompts describing complex scenes and motions, and synthesize videos using SoTA video generation models, including Kling
[
50
]
, Sora
[
5
]
, and Wan
[
52
]
. We manually screen the outputs and curate
\sim
25K videos covering broad anomaly types. As shown in the top-right of Fig.
2
, we build a new anomaly taxonomy designed to help VLMs identify subtle anomalies and support the subsequent three-stage training.
Single-frame recognizable anomalies
(Stage 1) include object distortion and human distortion, with common subtypes such as object deformation and extra limbs. We also include character anomalies, which are largely overlooked by existing reward models.
Multi-frame anomalies
(Stage 2) include motion anomalies and physical violations, covering common issues such as flickering and object disappearance. Moreover, we further filter these anomalous videos by excluding
global quality issues
such as text-video mismatch and low aesthetic quality, retaining 21,396 videos,
\sim
80% of the screened videos. This highlights that localized and sparse spatiotemporal anomalies are becoming increasingly dominant failure modes in current video generation.

The processing pipeline for our CaC dataset. Please refer to Sec.
3.1
for details.

CoT Synthesis with Human Annotation.

High-quality CoT data can improve the effectiveness of reward models
[
19
]
. To construct such data for sparse video anomalies, we design an annotation pipeline that combines expert human labels with VLM-based CoT synthesis, extending prior practices
[
47
,
48
,
63
]
. As shown in Fig.
2
(left), we uniformly downsample each video into frame sequences, which are examined frame by frame by human experts. For anomalous samples, annotators label the
temporal segments
,
anomaly types
,
underlying reasons
, and
affected-region bboxes
. Given that subtle anomalies are difficult to annotate accurately, we organize 50 video evaluation experts, including 40 annotators and 10 reviewers, and adopt a two-round review mechanism where errors are returned for correction. We further randomly check 10% of the annotations to verify bbox and anomaly-type accuracy. Detailed annotation procedures are provided in Appendix
B.1
.
Based on these annotations, as shown in Fig.
2
(bottom), we feed the original video, human-annotated GT, and cropped anomalous frames into Gemini-2.5-Pro
[
11
]
, and design a four-step reasoning template to encourage comprehensive VLM analysis. Since some sparse anomalies are extremely difficult for VLMs to recognize, we introduce a generate-verify process, where the generated CoT and human annotations are sent back to Gemini to check their consistency. Samples verified within three rounds are deemed
salient anomalies
; those verified within five rounds are deemed
non-salient anomalies
; and the remaining samples are discarded, as they may be overly difficult or affected by human annotation errors. Ultimately, we retain 15,374 anomalous training samples. The detailed prompt templates are provided in Appendix
B.2
.

We further pair the anomalous set with an equal number of normal videos constructed using the same pipeline, resulting in a dataset of approximately 30K videos. More dataset details are provided in Appendix
B.3
, with additional statistics visualized in Fig.
S1
.

3.2
Multi-stage Progressive Training for Hierarchical Concentrating

Based on the constructed dataset, we build a 3-stage progressive training pipeline, as shown in Fig.
3
.

Stage 1: Single-frame Warmup.

Existing pre-trained VLMs are typically unexposed to anomaly scenes in generated videos. We therefore first teach the VLM to recognize and localize these distortions from anomalous frames. As shown in Fig.
3
(Stage 1), each training sample contains an extracted anomalous frame, the video prompt, and the corresponding CoT reasoning sequence constructed in Sec.
3.1
. This sequence includes the judgment, reasoning, and
spatial bbox
coordinates of the affected region. Through this stage, the model acquires a structured CoT format and establishes a mapping from visual cues to anomaly attribution and spatial grounding.

Stage 2: Multi-frame SFT.

After establishing frame-level anomaly understanding and spatial grounding capabilities, we move to video-level training. As shown in Fig.
3
(Stage 2), we propose a joint training strategy that prepares the model for both global temporal scanning and fine-grained spatial localization in the subsequent GRPO stage.
Specifically, the model is optimized simultaneously on two types of video inputs: uniformly sampled (4 fps) global videos for localizing anomalous
temporal regions
within the full context, and densely sampled (8 fps) clips for precise
anomaly localization
after temporal focusing. These clips are cropped from the full videos as pure abnormal/normal temporal windows, and are all shorter than 1s. Using the constructed CoT supervision (Sec.
3.1
), the model learns the spatial and temporal CoT reasoning format required by the third-stage two-turn video input and acquires basic video anomaly recognition capability.

Stage 3: Two-Turn GRPO.

To further strengthen the model’s multimodal spatiotemporal reasoning and improve the reliability of its CoT process, we adopt two-turn GRPO fine-tuning. As shown in Fig.
3
(Stage 3), given an instruction
c
and a video
f
, the model generates a structured anomaly prediction
y
in a predefined format. The prediction contains two rounds: in the first round, the model takes the uniformly sampled video
f
and predicts the anomaly judgment,
temporal boundaries
, and anomaly type, denoted as
y^{1}
. For each rollout, the video is then cropped along its predicted
time window
to obtain a localized clip
f_{i}^{\prime}=\text{crop}(f,y_{i}^{1})
. In the second round, the model takes the densely sampled
f^{\prime}
together with the CoT derived from
y^{1}
, and predicts the anomaly judgment,
spatial bboxes
, and anomaly type, denoted as
y^{2}
. The complete response is defined as
y_{i}=[y_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{2}]
.

During training, for each input
(c,f)
, GRPO samples a group of
G
rollouts
\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{G}\}
from the old policy (VLM). Each
y_{i}
is evaluated by a weighted combination of rewards, and the rewards are normalized within the group to obtain the advantage
\hat{A}_{i}
. The policy is optimized by maximizing:

J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=J_{\text{clip}}(\theta)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),

(1)

where
\pi_{\theta}
and
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denote the fine-tuned and original VLMs, respectively,
J_{\text{clip}}(\theta)
is computed from
\hat{A}_{i}
, and
\beta
controls the KL strength. See Appendix
C.1
for details and the formal definition of GRPO. We next introduce these rewards.

3.3
Reward for CaC

Format Reward
evaluates the structural correctness of outputs. We require each response to be a parsable JSON object satisfying the stage-specific, status-conditioned schema: valid judgment for all samples; empty anomaly list if predicted normal; and anomaly taxonomy with a valid time window or frame-indexed bboxes in the first/second turn if predicted anomalous. The format reward is defined as:

R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(y_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{2}\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{Valid}_{t}(y_{i}^{t}),\\
-1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

Invalid or unparsable outputs are penalized, while semantically incorrect predictions cannot receive additional positive reward merely by being well formatted.

Status Reward
evaluates anomaly judgment accuracy by comparing the predicted status with the GT status. Similar to
R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(\cdot)
, it assigns
0
to correct status predictions and
-1
otherwise. For normal videos, a correct prediction can directly pass the status check in the first turn, while a false prediction is penalized and cannot receive IoU-based rewards because no GT anomaly exists. For anomalous videos, the model passes the status check only when its predictions are correct across both turns. Although anomalous samples can receive additional IoU-based rewards, these rewards are gated by correct multi-turn prediction and accurate attribution/localization; simply predicting “abnormal” therefore brings no benefit. For GT-normal samples, IoU terms are masked out, and GRPO normalizes rollouts per input, avoiding cross-sample bias.

Attribution Type IoU Reward
evaluates the model’s ability to identify five anomaly types introduced in Sec.
3.1
(object/human distortions; text/motion anomalies; physics violations), guiding the model to reach its judgment through accurate attribution CoT. For anomalous samples, we extract the anomaly type sets from the
t
-th turn prediction and the GT annotations, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{t}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{gt}}
, and compute their set IoU:

R_{\mathrm{type}}(y_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{2}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{t}\cap\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{gt}}|}/{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{t}\cup\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{gt}}|}

(3)

Temporal IoU Reward
evaluates the accuracy of the temporal intervals identified in the first turn. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{pred}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{gt}}
denote the sets of predicted and GT anomalous frame indices, respectively. The temporal reward is computed as:

R_{\mathrm{temp}}(y_{i})={|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{pred}}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{gt}}|}/{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{pred}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{gt}}|}.

(4)

Spatial IoU Reward
evaluates the precision of the frame-level bboxes predicted in the second turn. We compute spatial IoU on temporally matched frames,
i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{pred}}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{gt}}
, where
M
is the number of GT bboxes on these frames. The reward is formulated as the mean IoU:

R_{\mathrm{spa}}(y_{i})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{IoU}(b_{m}^{\mathrm{pred}},b_{m}^{\mathrm{gt}}),\ M>0;\quad 0,\ \text{otherwise}.

(5)

Together, these temporal and spatial IoU rewards encourage more accurate temporal grounding and spatial localization, leading to more reliable and interpretable CoT reasoning.

Reward Aggregation.
In Sec.
3.1
, each GT anomaly receives a saliency label
s_{n}\in\{\text{salient},\text{non-salient}\}
based on its verification difficulty. For anomalies with varying recognition difficulty, it is unreasonable to penalize all prediction errors equally. Therefore, we introduce a saliency discount factor
\gamma(s_{n})
and define the final reward as:

R(y_{i})=w_{1}R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(y_{i})+w_{2}\bar{\gamma}_{i}R_{\mathrm{stat}}(y_{i})+m_{i}\big(w_{3}\widetilde{R}_{\mathrm{type}}(y_{i})+w_{4}\widetilde{R}_{\mathrm{temp}}(y_{i})+w_{5}\widetilde{R}_{\mathrm{spa}}(y_{i})\big),

(6)

where
m_{i}
masks IoU terms for GT-normal samples, set to
1
/
0
for GT-anomalous/normal samples, respectively.
The
w_{i}
values denote the weights of the respective reward components, and we set
(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5})=(1,2,2,2,5)
.
\bar{\gamma}_{i}
is set to
1
for GT-normal samples and otherwise denotes the average saliency discount over GT anomalies in the sample, and
\widetilde{R}_{k}=1-\bar{\gamma}_{i}(1-R_{k})
for
k\in\{\mathrm{type},\mathrm{temp},\mathrm{spa}\}
. We set
\gamma(s_{n})=0.5/1
for non-salient/salient anomalies, respectively.

3.4
Two-turn Inference Strategy for Hierarchical Concentrating

Existing video reward models typically sample frames at fixed temporal intervals, feeding all sampled frames into the model simultaneously for inference. However, capturing subtle or fleeting anomalies inherently demands denser sampling, which incurs prohibitive computational overhead when processing long videos. In this work, we propose the Coarse-to-Fine Two-turn Inference strategy to efficiently localize anomalies.

First, the input video is uniformly downsampled to obtain a sparse frame sequence (4 fps), which is fed into the VLM for global temporal scanning. The model initially detects anomalies in the video; if detected, it outputs the anomalous time window, along with its attribution analysis. Second, we extract the video clip corresponding to the predicted time window and densely resample it (8 fps). This clip is then fed back into the VLM for fine-grained spatial reasoning, where the model confirms the anomalies and outputs the anomaly attribution and spatial bboxes. The first turn therefore proposes a candidate anomalous window, while the second turn makes the final anomaly decision. If the second turn does not confirm the anomaly, the video is conservatively classified as normal.

Reward Interface.

CaC is used as a reward model through a structured-to-scalar interface. Given a video
v
and its instruction
p
, CaC produces a structured response, and we convert the final judgment into a smooth scalar reward using the VLM’s vocabulary probabilities. Specifically, we read the probabilities of “normal” and “abnormal”, denoted as
P_{\mathrm{norm}}
and
P_{\mathrm{abn}}
, and define:

R_{\mathrm{CaC}}(v,p)={P_{\mathrm{norm}}}/({P_{\mathrm{norm}}+P_{\mathrm{abn}}}).

(7)

Thus, CaC assigns higher rewards to likely normal videos and lower rewards to likely anomalous ones.
The structured CoT, type, time window, and bbox can serve as interpretable evidence.

Implementation.

We adopt Qwen3-VL-8B
[
2
]
as the foundational model for CaC. All training stages unfreeze the full pipeline, including the ViT encoder, the adapter, and the LLM. In the warmup stage, we fine-tune the model with LoRA (rank
=
32) at a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
. In the Multi-frame SFT stage, we switch to full-parameter tuning with the same learning rate. In the RL stage, we perform two-turn GRPO for 250 steps with a learning rate of
1.0\times 10^{-6}
, a rollout group size of
G=8

C.2

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented CaC (Concentrate and Concentrate), a coarse-to-fine reward model for detecting and localizing subtle anomalies in generated videos. By combining a two-turn inference strategy, progressive three-stage training, and verifiable spatiotemporal rewards, CaC mitigates the attention dilution caused by the extreme sparsity of video anomalies and produces more reliable grounded judgments. We further introduced a dataset with per-frame bbox annotations to support both training and evaluation in this setting. Extensive experiments show that CaC consistently outperforms existing video reward models, detector-style baselines, and general VLMs, while downstream alignment results further confirm its value as an effective reward signal for improving video generation quality.

Appendix B
Dataset Construction and Details

B.1
Detailed Annotation Guidelines

We provide the complete annotation protocol for the CaC dataset, which comprises four stages:
frame extraction and inspection
,
anomaly structured descriptions
,
bounding box grounding
, and
quality assurance
. We assemble a dedicated team of 50 video evaluation experts (40 annotators and 10 senior reviewers), all of whom complete a calibration session with exemplar videos covering our five predefined anomaly categories prior to formal annotation. The protocol operates on generated videos and does not rely on personal user metadata.

B.1.1
Frame Extraction and Inspection

As described in Sec.
3.1
, each video is first uniformly downsampled to extract a sequence of frames. Human experts then inspect each frame sequentially to detect anomalies. This frame-level examination ensures that both single-frame defects and multi-frame anomalies are captured. Videos in which no anomaly is identified in any frame are labeled as normal samples.

B.1.2
Anomaly Structured Description

For each of the five predefined categories in our taxonomy, annotators determine whether the anomaly type is present via binary classification. A single video may be associated with multiple categories simultaneously. The five categories are:
Object Distortion
(deformation, mutation, or clipping artifacts in inanimate objects),
Human Distortion
(limb deformity, abnormal fingers, extra limbs, or body splitting in human subjects),
Motion Anomalies
(unnatural trajectories, jitter/flicker, or sudden disappearance/appearance of entities across frames),
Physical Violations
(unsupported floating, interpenetration, or other behaviors contradicting real-world physics), and
Character Anomalies
(unreadable text, abnormal layout, or inconsistent textual content across frames).

Annotation Format.
For each detected anomaly, annotators provide a structured description that encodes the frame range, the affected objects, the anomaly types, and the underlying reason. The temporal boundaries are recorded as start and end indices on the uniformly sampled frame sequence, rather than timestamps. This unified format enables frame-level grounding of each anomaly instance while the underlying reason field captures the semantic context behind the anomalies. When multiple anomaly instances belong to the same category, they are concatenated within a single group; anomalies belonging to different categories are recorded in separate groups following the category ordering defined in the taxonomy. This hierarchical scheme enables unambiguous parsing of annotations into per-category anomaly groups, each containing one or more frame-grounded instances.

Annotation Conventions.
To resolve ambiguities during annotation, we establish several guiding principles. When an anomaly is detectable from a single frame (
e.g.,
, a deformed hand or unreadable text), it is assigned to a single-frame category (Object Distortion, Human Distortion, or Character Anomalies) even if it persists across multiple frames, whereas multi-frame categories (Motion Anomalies and Physical Violations) are reserved for anomalies whose identification inherently requires cross-frame comparison. To distinguish Object Distortion from Physical Violations, the former captures structural deformation of the object itself, while the latter targets implausible interactions between objects or with the environment. For Character Anomalies, annotators compare textual content across the full frame sequence when needed, and inconsistent text is annotated with the complete frame range over which the inconsistency is observable, even if each individual frame appears plausible in isolation.

B.1.3
Bounding Box Grounding

For each identified anomaly instance, annotators draw a tight bounding box around the anomalous region in the corresponding frames within the annotated frame range. Bounding boxes are stored as
(x_{\min},y_{\min},x_{\max},y_{\max})
in the Qwen-style normalized coordinate system, where each frame axis is mapped to the range
[0,1000]
. The following principles guide this process:

•

Each bounding box must fully encompass the relevant distorted region such that the anomaly can be identified solely from the visual content within the box, without relying on surrounding context.

•

Multiple bounding boxes may be assigned to a single anomaly instance when the corresponding distortion manifests in multiple disjoint regions within the same frame.

•

When partial occlusion occurs, annotators approximate the full extent of the affected area based on visible cues and structural priors.

•

Bounding boxes should be as tight as possible, avoiding unnecessary inclusion of irrelevant visual content to minimize interference from unrelated structures.

•

For multi-frame anomalies that involve entity state transitions (
e.g.,
, sudden disappearance, appearance, or abrupt transformation), bboxes are annotated on the
boundary frames
where the transition occurs. Specifically, for disappearance events, the bounding box is placed on the last frame in which the entity is still visible, enclosing its full extent at that moment. For sudden appearance events, the bounding box is placed on the first frame in which the entity becomes visible. This convention anchors the bbox annotation to the most informative frame while the structured description captures the full frame range of the anomaly.

To guarantee annotation reliability, we adopt a rigorous
two-round
review mechanism. In the first round, each completed annotation is independently verified by a senior reviewer, who examines the anomaly types, frame boundaries, bboxes, and underlying reasons; any inconsistencies are flagged and returned to the original annotator for correction, and this cycle repeats until the reviewer approves the annotation. In the second round, we randomly sample 10% of the accepted annotations for a spot-check audit conducted by a separate group of reviewers, verifying the accuracy and consistency of both the bboxes and the anomaly types. Only batches achieving an inter-annotator agreement rate above 95% are accepted into the final dataset; those falling below this threshold are returned for complete re-annotation.

B.2
Prompt Templates for CoT Synthesis

In this section, we provide a detailed overview of the prompt templates used throughout the CoT data construction process. As described in Sec.
3.1
, we feed each video along with its manual annotations into Gemini-2.5-Pro
[
11
]
to synthesize structured reasoning chains. The core synthesis prompt is shown in Figs.
S4
and
S5
. Specifically, we supply the annotated anomaly types, temporal spans, underlying reasons, and corresponding bboxes to guide the model toward the difficult regions while still requiring it to re-examine the visual evidence. The model is instructed to follow a four-step reasoning template: first performing detailed frame-level inspection, then analyzing cross-frame consistency and motion, then making an overall anomaly assessment with concrete attribution, and finally producing a structured JSON output with the anomaly status, frame range, label, reason, problem region, and per-frame bboxes. Although the ground-truth annotations are provided, they are treated as guidance rather than answers to be copied; the model must independently verify whether the visual content supports the annotated anomaly. This design reduces hallucinated rationales
[
39
,
38
,
37
]
and yields higher-quality CoT labels for subtle anomalies that are difficult for VLMs to discover from the raw video alone.

For the verification stage, the generated CoT is checked against the human annotations to ensure that the reasoning chain is not only plausible but also aligned with the verified labels and localization; the verification prompt is shown in Fig.
S6
. For anomaly presence and type predictions, we perform exact string matching against the ground-truth labels to determine correctness; for the temporal segments and spatial bounding boxes, we compute the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) between the predicted and ground-truth regions, and consider a prediction correct if the IoU exceeds 0.5. A sample passes verification only when all criteria are simultaneously satisfied. This verification is necessary because GT-conditioned generation may still produce inconsistent reasoning, incorrect attribution, or imprecise localization, especially for non-salient anomalies. The process is repeated for up to five rounds—samples passing within the first three rounds are deemed salient anomalies, those passing in the fourth or fifth round are deemed non-salient anomalies, and those failing all five rounds are discarded to avoid introducing noise. By incorporating detailed descriptions of all five anomaly categories from our taxonomy into both prompts, we ensure that the synthesized CoT data faithfully reflects the fine-grained spatiotemporal reasoning required for video anomaly detection, thereby providing high-quality supervision for our three-stage progressive training pipeline.

Appendix C
Additional Training and Inference Details

C.1
GRPO Formulation

In this section, we provide the detailed formulation of the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objective. For simplicity, we denote the overall conditioning context at step
t
as
X_{i,t}
, which encapsulates the task instruction
c
, the original or cropped video frames (
f
or
f^{\prime}
), and the previously generated tokens
y_{i,<t}
across the two autoregressive stages. In the second turn, the cropped frames are rollout-specific: each
f_{i}^{\prime}
is constructed from the frame-index window predicted in the corresponding first-turn response
y_{i}^{1}
. If the predicted window is empty, invalid, or cannot be parsed into frame indices, we do not construct
f_{i}^{\prime}
and the rollout terminates after the first turn.

For each input
(c,f)
, the old policy samples a group of
G
complete two-turn rollouts
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
. Each rollout receives a scalar reward
R(y_{i})
from the weighted reward function in Sec.
3.3
. We compute the group-relative advantage by normalizing rewards within the group:

\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{R(y_{i})-\mathrm{mean}(\{R(y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{R(y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G})+\epsilon_{A}},

(8)

where
\epsilon_{A}
is a small constant for numerical stability.

During training, the surrogate objective is maximized. The clipped surrogate objective
J_{\text{clip}}(\theta)
restricts the policy update to prevent excessively large step sizes, and is formulated at the token level over all generated tokens in the rollout as:

J_{\text{clip}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\min\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i},\text{clip}\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_{i}\right),

(9)

where
r_{i,t}(\theta)
is the probability ratio between the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the old policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
:

r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid X_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid X_{i,t})},

(10)

and
\epsilon
is the clipping hyperparameter.

To prevent the fine-tuned model
\pi_{\theta}
from deviating too far from the initial reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, a KL divergence penalty is applied. Following standard GRPO implementations, we employ an unbiased estimator of the KL divergence.
The KL penalty is then written as:

D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\left(\rho_{i,t}-\log\rho_{i,t}-1\right),\rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i,t}\mid X_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid X_{i,t})}.

(11)

Combining these components yields the full GRPO objective:

J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(c,f)\sim\mathcal{D},\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[J_{\text{clip}}(\theta)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\right],

(12)

where
\beta
is the coefficient controlling the strength of the KL penalty. By calculating advantages relative to the group rather than using an external value network, GRPO significantly reduces the memory overhead while maintaining stable optimization
[
25
,
21
]
.

Training Implementation Details

We adopt Qwen3-VL-8B
[
2
]
as the backbone of CaC and train the full model pipeline
[
53
,
31
,
13
]
across all stages, including the vision encoder, adapter, and language model. The three training stages use disjoint data splits, and each stage keeps a 1:1 ratio between normal and anomalous samples. The first stage learns single-frame anomaly grounding from about 20K frames. The anomalous frames are sampled from 5K videos that contain single-frame recognizable anomalies, while normal frames are randomly sampled from normal videos. The second stage performs multi-frame SFT with about 20K full-video samples and 15K local clips. Full-video samples are collected from the CaC dataset to train global temporal localization. Clip-level samples are cropped from the full videos and restricted to within 1 second. For anomalous videos, we require the cropped clip to fully cover the annotated anomalous region, because partial crops of cross-frame inconsistency events may introduce ambiguous or incorrect labels. The third stage constructs 7.5K training instances for two-turn GRPO from a non-overlapping data split.

For optimization and compute, the warmup stage uses LoRA with rank 32 and a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
, and is trained for 9,330 steps on 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, taking about 11.5 hours. The multi-frame SFT stage switches to full-parameter tuning with the same learning rate, and is trained for 7K steps on 8 H200 GPUs, taking about 30.5 hours. The RL stage runs GRPO with a learning rate of
1.0\times 10^{-6}
, rollout group size
G=8
, batch size 64, and gradient accumulation over 32 steps; it is trained for 250 steps on 16 H200 GPUs, taking about 42 hours.

Appendix E
Limitations and Broader Impact

CaC is designed as a specialist reward model for sparse structural, temporal, and physical anomalies in generated videos, and therefore should not be used as the only objective for downstream alignment. We have to combine CaC with UnifiedReward: the general reward preserves text-video alignment and holistic quality, while CaC provides complementary anomaly-sensitive feedback. However, we believe this is also a future direction: as video generation becomes increasingly realistic, reliable evaluation may require multiple expert reward models working together rather than a single monolithic reward.

Due to limited computational resources, our experiments are still restricted in scale. Although our training already uses 16-card and 32-card H200-level settings for the most expensive stages, broader multi-seed studies, larger downstream alignment sweeps, and evaluation on substantially longer videos would require considerably more compute. We therefore leave more exhaustive scaling analysis to future work.

The dataset construction also relies on fine-grained human annotations of frame ranges and bounding boxes. These annotations improve supervision quality, but they are costly and may still contain subjective ambiguity in borderline cases. We hope that releasing and further improving CaC can support future work on more automated fine-grained annotation pipelines, reducing the manual burden while preserving localization quality.

Finally, although the current RL formulation obtains a continuous reward through token-probability-based optimization, the reward feedback is still applied at the generated-video level. While CaC predicts temporal windows and spatial boxes, the optimization signal is not yet immediately mapped back to the exact anomalous frames or regions in the generator’s denoising process. A promising future direction is to combine anomaly localization with more localized RL guidance, so that the predicted spatiotemporal boxes can directly indicate where and how generation should be corrected.

From a broader-impact perspective, CaC can support safer and more reliable video-generation systems by exposing subtle structural, temporal, and physical artifacts that are often missed by holistic evaluators. At the same time, improving anomaly detection and reward feedback may also help optimize generators toward more realistic synthetic media. Future release and deployment should therefore consider appropriate access control, usage restrictions, provenance tracking, and monitoring for harmful or misleading applications.
