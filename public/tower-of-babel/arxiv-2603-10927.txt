Sharp Eigenfunction Bounds on the Torus for large p p
Daniel Pezzi

Abstract
. We prove the discrete restriction conjecture holds with no loss when p > 2 ​ d d − 4 p>\frac{2d}{d-4} and d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 . That is, we show optimal L p L^{p} bounds for eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on the square torus for large values of p p . This improves the results Bourgain and Demeter. Our proof method is a refinement of the circle method approach previously used to establish results with a subpolynomial loss. This represents the first sharp L p L^{p} bounds for eigenfunctions on the torus since the work of Cooke and Zygmund. We present applications to bounds for spectral projectors and the additive energy of lattice points on higher dimensional spheres. These results are similarly sharp. We also prove results with a logarithmic loss that hold in a wider range of p p .

Introduction

1.1.
Background

Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be the square torus. We may view functions on this manifold as
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose values are completely determined by its values on a fundamental domain
[0,1]^{d}
. If

(1)

f(x)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{F}(f)(\mathbf{k})e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}.

The plane waves
e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}\coloneq e(\mathbf{k}\cdot x)
are eigenfunctions

That is, we project onto integer lattice point frequencies that lie on the circle of radius

Conjecture 1.1

(The Discrete Restriction Conjecture)
.

Let
p\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
. Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}
be the square torus. Suppose

is an eigenfunction of

(3)

\left\lVert e_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}(N^{\frac{d-2}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}+1)\left\lVert e_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

The extra
O_{\epsilon}(N^{\epsilon})

See

\mathcal{F}_{d,N}\coloneq\{\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},...,k_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|\mathbf{k}|=N\}.

Then it is well known that the following bounds hold for the cardinality of lattice points.

(4)

\#\mathcal{F}_{d,N}\lesssim_{\epsilon}N^{\epsilon}N^{d-2+\epsilon}.

Lattice points on the sphere are more regularly distributed as the dimension increases. The
\epsilon

1.1

[
4

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 5

p>\frac{2d}{d-4}

\left\lVert e_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim N^{\frac{d-2}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}\left\lVert e_{N}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

This result contains the first sharp bounds for toral eigenfunctions since the work of Cooke and Zygmund. Cooke proved the result in the course of showing a Cantor-Lebesgue Theorem

We also able to improve the

Theorem 1.3

1.3.
Some Applications

We present two applications of our main results to bounds for approximate eigenfunctions and bounds for the additive energy of spheres.

1.4.
A quick tour of Discrete Restriction

To explain the intuition behind
1.1
, we will present the more general concept of constructive interference. Let
\Xi
be a set of frequencies
\xi
such that
|\xi|\sim N
and
\#\Xi=M
. Consider the sum

f(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}e(\xi\cdot x).

Clearly
f(0)=M
. By continuity, we have
|f(0)|\geq M/2
is a ball of radius
\sim N^{-1}
centered at
0
. We then have

\int_{B(0,1)}|f(x)|^{p}dx\gtrsim M^{p}N^{-d}.

The constructive interference of the exponentials, which is a manifestation of the uncertainty principle, forces a function that is very large on a small neighborhood of a point. In the case of the torus, when
d\geq 5
, we have
M\sim N^{d-2}
. The same computation then gives the first bound on the right hand side of
1.1
. When
d=2,3
or
4
, we have a factor of
N^{\epsilon}
.

This point focusing example is a general phenomena that saturates
L^{p}
norms of eigenfunctions (or, more generally, quasimodes). However, the two terms in the discrete restriction conjecture balance at
p=\frac{2d}{d-2}
. On generic manifolds we expect the critical value to be
p=\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}
. The difference is due to the degenerate number of lattice points on spheres as compared to arbitrary
1
-separated sets, which gives a global count of
N^{d-2}
as opposed to
N^{d-1}
.

The second term on the right hand side of
1.1
can be seen by considering a single plane wave. For an example with full support, one can generate a host of random examples analyzed with Khintchine’s inequality that experience maximum destructive interference. The difference in the saturating example leads to the differences in techniques used for
p>\frac{2d}{d-2}
and
p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
. Harmonic analysis techniques seem most suited for smaller value of
p
, while number theory works well with the analysis of exponential sums experiencing constructive interference. See
[
9
]
for an exploration of this dichotomy in the case of spectral projectors. There, sharp estimates are proven both using the circle method and cancellation between exponential sums.

Notation

We use
|\cdot|
to denote both the magnitude of a vector and the Lebesgue measure of a set; which interpretation is being used is always clear from context. We use
\#A

We denote
\mathbf{k}

\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(k_{1},...,k_{d})
. The variable

\mathbb{T}^{d}

We write

. We use similar notation for

. We write

We use

We will write

To ease comparison, we have tried where possible to emulate the notation of

2.1.
Preliminary estimates

Let
K(x)
be the convolution kernel (over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
) of
P_{N}
. As
K(x)
is the sum of exponentials whose frequencies are given by the lattice points on
NS^{d-1}
, it is difficult to analyze directly. It can be written as

(10)

K(x)\coloneq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot x).

It is beneficial to represent this discrete sum in a continuous way. The integration kernel
K(x)
can be realized as

(11)

K(x)=\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma(k/N)e(kx_{j}+k^{2}t)\Bigr)e(-\lambda t)dt.

The function
\gamma
is a smooth bump function supported in the ball of radius
\lesssim 1
. We note that everything in the integrand is
1
-periodic, and so the integration can be viewed as being over (a rescaled version of)
S^{1}
. The above representation selects out
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda=0
, as in any other case the integral is
0
by periodicity. Taking inspiration from the circle method from analytic number theory, we expect the integrand to be larger near rational times with small denominator. In this instance, it is productive to cut around times with very small denominator, as the rest of the function will have small Fourier coefficients.

Fix
N\leq Q\leq N^{2}
. Define

D_{Q}\coloneq\{q:Q\leq q\leq 2Q,\,q\text{ is prime}\}.

We then consider

R_{Q}\coloneq\{\frac{a}{q}:q\in D_{Q},(a,q)=1\}.

It is immediate that
\#R_{Q}\sim Q^{2}(\log Q)^{-1}
. Let
1_{[-1/20,1/20]}\leq\eta\leq 1_{[-1/10,1/10]}
be a Schwartz bump function. Define

(12)

\eta_{Q}=c_{Q}\sum_{a/q\in R_{Q}}\eta((t-\frac{a}{q})Q^{2}).

The constant
c_{Q}
is chosen so that
\int_{[0,1]}\eta_{Q}dt=1
. Note that
c_{Q}\sim\log Q
by direct computation. This will help us get an improved bound for Fourier coefficients on our error term later. We now define

K^{Q}\coloneq\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}G(t,x_{j})e(-\lambda t)\eta_{Q}(t)dt.

Here,
G(t,x_{j})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma(k/N)e(kx_{j}+k^{2}t)
is the 1-dimensional Schrödinger propagator on the square torus. We now seek to bound the size of
K^{Q}
and the size of the Fourier transform of
K-K^{Q}
. To achieve the first task, we need a bound on
G(t,x)
(we drop the subscript
i
momentarily). A sharp bound can be proven for all time using classical methods, however we also require an estimate that applies near fractions of very large denominator.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
q\geq N
be an odd integer. Let
t=\frac{a}{q}+\varphi
where
(a,q)=1
and
|\varphi|\lesssim\frac{1}{q^{2}}

|G(t,x)|\lesssim\sqrt{q}.

Before we prove this, let us analyze

k
based on
q

k=rq+k_{1}

\displaystyle G(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{q-1}e(k_{1}^{2}a/q)\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma(\frac{k_{1}+rq}{N})e((rq+k_{1})x+(rq+k_{1})^{2}\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{q-1}e(k_{1}^{2}a/q-k_{1}m/q)\Bigr)\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(y/N)e((x+\frac{m}{q})y+\varphi y^{2})dy\Bigr)

We note that

|S(a,m,q)|\lesssim q^{-1/2}.

4.1

Proof of

Fix

|J(x,\varphi,m,q)|\lesssim N.

As we are considering times very close to a rational number, this estimate is superior to any van der Corput estimate. However, we will use (non) stationary phase to reduce the number of
m

J(x,\varphi,m,q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(y/N)e((x+\frac{m}{q})y+\varphi y^{2})dy.

\displaystyle\lesssim q^{1/2}.

q
prime. A direct calculation shows

|\{t\in[0,1]:|G(t,x)|\geq 2^{s}\}|\lesssim\sum_{q\lesssim N^{2}2^{-2s}}\frac{\phi(q)}{q2^{2s}}\lesssim N^{2}2^{-4s}(\log(\frac{N}{2^{s}}))^{-1}.

Here,
\phi(q)
is the Euler totient function, which counts the number of integers less than
q
and coprime to it. The conclusion of
Proposition

2.1
says that, on the support of
\eta_{Q}
, we have

|G(t,x)|\lesssim\sqrt{Q},

as we have localized to small neighborhoods are rationals with large denominators. We then immediately have via Hölder’s inequality and our normalization constant
c_{Q}
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert G(t,x)\right\rVert_{L^{d}(\text{supp}\,\eta_{Q})}^{d}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log Q\,N^{2}\sum_{N^{d/2}\leq 2^{s}\lesssim\sqrt{Q}}(\log(\frac{N}{2^{s}}))^{-1}2^{s(d-4)}+\log QN^{\frac{d}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log Q}{\log(NQ^{-1/2})}\,N^{2}Q^{\frac{d-4}{2}}.

∎

It seems difficult to get around the
\log Q
loss here as we will eventually take
Q=N^{2}
in some regimes. The proof of the next proposition will illustrate another
\log Q
loss. To continue, we prove that the Fourier transform of
K-K^{Q}
is small.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any
d\geq 1
and
N\leq Q\leq N^{2}
, we have

(17)

||{\mathcal{F}(K-K^{Q})}||_{\infty}\lesssim\log Q\,Q^{-1}

Proof.

We compute the Fourier coefficient of
K-K^{Q}
as

\mathcal{F}(K-K^{Q})(\mathbf{k})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}G(t,x_{j})\Bigl(1-\eta_{Q}(t)\Bigr)e(-\lambda t)e(-\mathbf{k}\cdot x)dtdx.

After exchanging the order of integrating, we see that the integral with respect to
x
is only non-zero when
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},...,k_{d})
where
k_{j}
come from the sum in the definition of
G(t,x_{j})
. This then leaves us with only a Fourier transform with respect to time, and so

\mathcal{F}(K-K^{Q})(\mathbf{k})=\widehat{1-\eta_{Q}}(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\gamma(\frac{k_{j}}{N}).

Define
l=|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda
. The bump functions do not alter our conclusions as they equal
1
if
l=0
. As
1-\eta_{Q}(t)
has mean zero, we get

\mathcal{F}(K-K^{Q})(\mathbf{k})=0\text{ if }|\mathbf{k}|^{2}=\lambda.

If
l\neq 0
, the constant term drops out and we compute the Fourier transform of
\eta_{Q}
. Recalling (
12
), we have

(18)

\widehat{1-\eta_{Q}}(l)\sim c_{Q}Q^{-2}\hat{\eta}(\frac{l}{10Q^{2}})\sum_{q\in R_{Q}}\sum_{a=1}^{q-1}e(la/q).

This follows from basic properties of the Fourier transform. Recall the
c_{Q}
factor is
\sim\log Q
. As the sum is over roots of unity, the inner sum is
-1
if
q
does not divide
l
and
q-1
otherwise. Therefore, we have

\widehat{1-\eta_{Q}}(l)\sim\log Q\,Q^{-2}\hat{\eta}(\frac{l}{10Q^{2}})\Bigl(-\#D_{Q}+\sum_{q\in D_{Q},\,q|l}q\Bigr).

We have
\#D_{Q}\sim Q(\log Q)^{-1}
and
q\sim Q
. Suppose
l
has less than 10 factors in
D_{Q}
. Then the triangle equality immediately implies that

|\widehat{1-\eta_{Q}}(l)|\sim\log Q\,Q^{-2}|\hat{\eta}(\frac{l}{10Q^{2}})|\cdot 10Q\lesssim\log Q\,Q^{-1}.

Now suppose
l
has
S
factors with
S\geq 10
. Then
l\gtrsim Q^{S}
. However,
\eta
is Schwartz and so enjoys rapid decay.

|\hat{\eta}(z)|\lesssim(1+|z|)^{-100}.

Therefore
|\hat{\eta}(\frac{l}{10Q^{2}})|\lesssim Q^{-100\cdot(S-2)}
. The triangle inequality then gives

|\widehat{1-\eta_{Q}}(l)|\lesssim\log Q\,Q^{-2}Q^{-100\cdot(S-2)}SQ\lesssim\log QQ^{-1},\,S\geq 10.

So in any case we conclude.
∎

2.2.
Proof of
\log
-loss result

We now prove
Theorem

1.3
. Following
[
4
]
, assume
\|a_{\xi}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{d,\lambda})}=1
. Define

F(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}a_{\xi}e(x\cdot\xi).

Recall the superlevel sets
E_{\alpha}
for this function are given by

E_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:|F(x)|>\alpha\}.

This allows us to define the oscillatory indicator functions as

f(x)=\frac{F(x)}{|F(x)|}1_{E_{\alpha}}(x).

These functions have the following crucial property which is trivially verified.

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}dx=|E_{\alpha}|.

By the definitions of these functions, Plancherel’s Theorem, and Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\alpha|E_{\alpha}|\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bar{F}(x)f(x)dx=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}\bar{a}_{\xi}\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi),

which implies

\alpha^{2}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}\leq\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}|\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)|^{2}=\langle K*f,f\rangle.

From Proposition 4.1 in
[
4
]
, we also have that

(19)

||{K^{Q}}||_{\infty}\lesssim Q^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon}.

Recall we can split our kernel as

K=K^{Q}+(K-K^{Q}).

This gives

\displaystyle|\langle K*f,f\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle K^{Q}*f,f\rangle+\langle(K-K^{Q})*f,f\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq||{K^{Q}}||_{\infty}\langle||{f}||_{1},|f|\rangle+||{\widehat{K-K^{Q}}}||_{\infty}\langle|\hat{f}|,|\hat{f}|\rangle

\displaystyle\leq||{K^{Q}}||_{\infty}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}+||{\widehat{K-K^{Q}}}||_{\infty}|E_{\alpha}|.

We now optimize in
Q
using (
19
) or
Proposition

2.4
, with the goal of making
||{K^{Q}}||_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}
. Having done this, we get

|E_{\alpha}|\lesssim\log Q\,\alpha^{-2}Q^{-1}.

Where
\alpha
and
Q
are now related by
\alpha^{2}\sim\log Q\,(\log(N/\sqrt{Q}))^{-1}\,N^{2}Q^{\frac{d-4}{2}}
or
\alpha^{2}\sim Q^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon}
.

This yields for all
d\geq 5

(20)

|E_{\alpha}|\lesssim\begin{cases}\quad N^{\epsilon}\alpha^{-\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}}&\text{ if }N^{\frac{d-1}{4}}\lesssim\alpha\lesssim N^{\frac{d-1}{3}+\epsilon_{0}}\\
\quad(\log N)^{1+\frac{2}{d-4}}N^{\frac{4}{d-4}}\alpha^{-\frac{2d-4}{d-4}}&\text{ if }\alpha\gtrsim N^{\frac{d-1}{3}+\epsilon_{0}}\end{cases}.

Here,
\epsilon_{0}>0
is arbitrary. The bound for large heights can be improved to just a
\log N
factor if
\alpha\lesssim N^{\frac{d-2}{2}-\epsilon_{0}}
. Note that the bound
eq.

16
is vacuous if
d\leq 4
. The bound
|E_{\alpha}|\lesssim N^{\epsilon}\alpha^{-\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}}
actually holds for all level sets as a consequence of the conjecture being proven by decoupling when
p\leq 2(d+1)/(d-1)
. We can thus view this result as improving the level set bound for larger heights.

It is a standard fact that

\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{p}=\int_{0}^{||{F}||_{\infty}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha.

The torus being the domain of integration does not factor into the above equality. We use (
20
) and compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}\alpha^{p-1}\alpha^{-\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}}d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\epsilon}\alpha^{p-\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}}|_{0}^{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\epsilon}N^{\frac{d-1}{3}(p-\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1})}.

Which is
\lesssim N^{\frac{p(d-2)}{2}-d}
when
p>\frac{2d}{d-3}
and
d\geq 6
or
p>6
when
d=5
. We use our second bound for the large heights. If
d\geq 5
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log N)^{1+\frac{2}{d-4}}\,N^{\frac{4}{d-4}}\int_{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\alpha^{p-1}\alpha^{-\frac{2d-4}{d-4}}d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log N)^{1+\frac{2}{d-4}}\,N^{\frac{4}{d-4}}\alpha^{p-\frac{2d-4}{d-4}}|_{N^{\frac{d-1}{3}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log N)^{1+\frac{2}{d-4}}\,N^{\frac{4}{d-4}}N^{\frac{d-2}{2}(p-\frac{2d-4}{d-4})}

\displaystyle\sim(\log N)^{1+\frac{2}{d-4}}\,N^{\frac{p(d-2)}{2}-d}.

And so we are done.

3.
A sharp argument

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.2
.

3.1.
Preliminary estimates

The convolution kernel (on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
) of
P_{N}
is given by

(21)

K(x)\coloneq\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot x).

Taking inspiration from the circle method from analytic number theory, we replace this discrete sum with a more continuous equivalent. It is a straightforward consequence of periodicity that

(22)

K(x)=\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}G(t,x_{j})e(-\lambda t)dt,

where

(23)

G(t,x_{j})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma(k/N)e(kx_{j}+k^{2}t).

The function
\gamma
is a Schwartz bump function such that
1_{[-1,1]}\leq\gamma
. Because of the cancellation occurring in the time integral, the only essential property of this function is that it is 1 when
k\sim N
. Importantly,
G(t,x_{j})
in its given form is the Schrödinger propagator on the circle. Additionally, we view the integral in (
22
) as being over (a rescaled version of)
S^{1}
which has been identified with
[0,1]
in the obvious way.

The motivating insight from the circle method is that any expression of the form (
22
) should be large when
t
is near a rational with small denominator. We shall quantify this intuition and use it to obtain bounds on
K(x)
, which we shall then convert to
L^{p}
norms of eigenfunctions.

In this section we use different definitions for the parameters as compared to
Section

2
. Let
2\leq Q<N/100
be dyadic. We also define the dyadic
2^{s}
by
Q<2^{s}<N
. Let
\eta
be a smooth bump function identically
1
on
\frac{1}{4}\leq|t|\leq\frac{1}{2}
and supported in
\frac{1}{8}\leq|t|\leq 1
. W define

R_{Q}=\{\frac{a}{q}:Q\leq q<2Q,(a,q)=1\},

and

(24)

\eta_{Q,s}(t)=\sum_{a/q\in R_{Q}}\eta((t-a/q)N2^{s}).

Note we place no restriction on
q
other then it must be
\sim Q
. Clearly
\#R_{Q}\sim Q^{2}
. Our definition of
\eta
implies

\text{supp}\,\eta_{Q,s}=\{|t-\frac{a}{q}|\sim\frac{1}{N2^{s}},\frac{a}{q}\in R_{Q}\}.

When
2^{s}\sim N
, we modify the bump function
\eta
so that
\eta_{Q,s}
is supported on
|t-\frac{a}{q}|\lesssim N^{-2}
. This will not the affect bulk of our analysis. It is trivially verified that the supports of
\eta_{Q,s}
do not overlap.

We now split the kernel into three pieces. For the first, let
\eta_{0}
be an appropriate compactly supported bump function that is
1
near
0
. We define

\displaystyle K_{0}\coloneq\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}G(t,x_{j})e(-\lambda t)\eta_{0}(Nt)dt,

\displaystyle K_{Q,s}\coloneq\int_{[0,1]}\prod_{j=1}^{d}G(t,x_{j})e(-\lambda t)\eta_{Q,s}(t)dt,

\displaystyle K_{err}\coloneq K-K_{0}-\sum_{Q,s}K_{Q,s}.

The first piece of the kernel is supported only around
t=0
. We could alter the lower bound on
Q
to include this portion of the domain in the definition of the
K_{Q,s}
, but we will present a simpler and distinct argument for
K_{0}
. We recall that we identify
[0,1]
with
S^{1}
to make sense of this definition. The cutoff function
\eta_{Q,s}
is supported in time near rationals with denominator
\sim Q
at a distance
\sim(N2^{s})^{-1}
. The error
K_{err}
defined by a cutoff supported near times that cannot be well approximated by rationals of denominator
\lesssim N
.

We recall two key inputs from number theory, Dirichlet’s Lemma and the sharp bound for the Schrödinger propagator for the torus.

Lemma 3.1

(Dirichlet’s Lemma)
.

For every
t\in[0,1]
, there exists a
2\leq q\leq N
and
a
with
(a,q)=1
such that
t=\frac{a}{q}+\varphi
, where
|\varphi|\leq\frac{1}{Nq}
. If
t
is near
0
or
1
, we allow
a=0
or
1
respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\leq a<q<N
with
(a,q)=1
. For
t
such that
|t-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{1}{qN}
we have that

(25)

|G(t,x)|\lesssim q^{-1/2}\min\{N,|t-\frac{a}{q}|^{-1/2}\}.

See Lemma 3.18 in
[
3
]
for a proof of the second lemma, the first is classical. The estimate for
K_{0}
is an instance of a more general phenomena for local operators.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 5.
Let
2\leq p\leq\infty
. Let
K_{0}
be as before. Then for all
F\in L^{p^{\prime}}
, the Hölder dual of
L^{p}
, we have

(26)

\left\lVert K_{0}*F\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim N^{d-2-\frac{2d}{p}}\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Proof.

First we write

K_{0}(x)=\int_{[0,1]}\eta_{0}(Nt)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k_{j}}e(x_{j}k_{j}+k_{j}^{2}t)e(-\lambda t)dt.

We may take the Fourier transform with respect to time, as after some trivial identifications we can replace
[0,1]
with integration over
\mathbb{R}
. Then

K_{0}(x)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}N^{-1}\widehat{\eta_{0}}(N^{-1}(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda))e(\mathbf{k}\cdot x).

Recall that
\eta_{0}
is Schwartz, and so its Fourier transform enjoys rapid decay outside of
B(0,c)
for some
c
. By the lattice point counts, we bound the number of
\mathbf{k}
such that
|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda=X
with
X\lesssim N
by
N\cdot N^{d-2}
, where there is no additional
\epsilon
factor as
d\geq 5
. By the triangle inequality, we therefore have

(27)

||{K_{0}}||_{\infty}\lesssim N^{d-2}.

We also bound the Fourier coefficients. To compute

\mathcal{F}(K_{0})(\mathbf{k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\int_{[0,1]}\eta_{0}(Nt)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k}\gamma(\frac{k_{j}}{N})e(x_{j}k_{j}+k_{j}^{2}t-\lambda t)dt\Bigr)e(-\mathbf{k}\cdot x)dx,

we simply note that the only expressions which depends on
x
inside the
t
integral are the exponentials
e(x_{j}k_{j})
. Using the cancellation in
x
and taking the Fourier transform with respect to time yields

\mathcal{F}(K_{0})(\mathbf{k})=N^{-1}\widehat{\eta_{0}}(N^{-1}(|\mathbf{k^{2}}|-\lambda))\prod_{j=1}^{d}\gamma(\frac{k_{i}}{N}).

Here,
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},...,k_{d})
. The triangle inequality then implies

(28)

||{\mathcal{F}(K_{0})}||_{\infty}\lesssim N^{-1}.

The bounds (
27
) and (
28
) immediately imply, for any
F
, the bounds

\displaystyle||{K_{0}*F}||_{2}\lesssim N^{-1}||{F}||_{2},

\displaystyle||{K_{0}*F}||_{\infty}\lesssim N^{d-2}||{F}||_{1}.

The result follows from interpolation.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Our proof in dimension
2,3,4
will also imply the sharp bound for
K_{0}*F
, but with an additional
\epsilon
factor. Recall such a factor is necessary in
L^{p}
estimates because of the lower bound for the number of lattice points on spheres in low dimensions has such a factor. The advantage of isolating the local contribution is that we are now comfortable proving stronger bounds for the other pieces of the kernel without fear of a contradiction, although this does not play into our proof.

The main difficultly will be in estimating
K_{Q,s}
. We have the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

For each dyadic
2\leq Q<\frac{1}{100}N
and
Q<2^{s}<N
, we have

(29)

\left\lVert K_{Q,s}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim(N2^{s})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}Q^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}.

Using the bound in
Lemma

3.2
and Hölder’s inequality will prove the result almost immediately. We provide an ostensibly more complicated proof where we write out
K_{Q,s}(x)
in full using the formulation reached after the statement of
Proposition

2.4
. This is for the purposes of illustrating the difficulty in improving (
29
). This gives an expression for
K_{Q,s}(x)
as

(30)

\int_{[0,1]}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{d}S(a,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\frac{a}{q})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{d}J(x_{j},\varphi,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\varphi)\Bigr)\eta_{Q,s}(t)dt.

This obviously unwieldy expression can be simplified. Recall that
\eta_{Q,s}(t)
localizes to
\sim(N2^{s})^{-1}
neighborhoods of rational
a/q
such that
q\sim Q
and
(a,q)=1
. The first factor in the sum is independent of
\varphi
; it only depends on
a
and
q
. Because of this, let us define

(31)

\mathcal{S}(m,q)\coloneq\sum_{(a,q)=1}\prod_{j=1}^{d}S(a,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\frac{a}{q}).

This term will appear naturally when we integrate, while the term involving
J
will be dealt with by similar techniques to what we used in
Section

2
.

By the triangle inequality we have

(32)

|\mathcal{S}(m,q)|\lesssim q^{1-\frac{d}{2}}.

Now we prove
Proposition

3.5
.

Proof.

Momentarily drop the index
j
. Recall

J(x,\varphi,m,q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma(y/N)e((x+\frac{m}{q})y+\varphi y^{2})dy.

In contrast to the previous section,
q\geq N
. The trivial inequality and stationary phase imply as before that

|J(x,\varphi,m,q)|\lesssim\min\{N,|\varphi|^{-1/2}\}.

However, as we are dealing with smaller
q
, only
\sim 1
of the
m
will significantly contribute. Recall that if

|x+\frac{m}{q}|\lesssim N^{-1},

we use the trivial estimate, but by repeated integration by parts we have that

|x+\frac{m}{q}|\sim 2^{j}N^{-1}

implies

|J(x,\varphi,m,q)|\lesssim_{A}2^{-jA}\min\{N,|\varphi|^{-1/2}\}.

There are
\sim 2^{j}
such
m
, and only
\sim 1
integers
m
such that
|x+\frac{m}{q}|\lesssim N^{-1}
. By integrating by parts a finite number of times not depending on
N
, we get enough decay in
2^{j}
that we may sum the series. Because of this we have

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\bigl|\prod_{j=1}^{d}J(x_{j},\varphi,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\varphi)\bigr|\lesssim\min\{N^{d},|\varphi|^{-d/2}\}.

We need some care before we deploy this bound. Recalling (
30
), we will first break the domain up based on the disjoint components of
\eta_{Q,s}
, which are centered around fractions
a/q
. On each of these components
S(a,m,q)
is constant as it does not depend on
\varphi
, and so we can sum in
a
and
q
. Additionally,
J(x,\varphi,m,q)
does not depend on
a
and so has the same values on any component of the support of
\eta_{Q,s}
. This gives after a change of variables

\sum_{q\sim Q}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{S}(m,q)\int_{E}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{d}J(x_{j},\varphi,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\varphi)\Bigr)\eta_{Q,s}(t)dt.

Here,
E
is a set of measure
\sim(N2^{s})^{-1}
. As we have absorbed the sum in
a
, we now use the triangle inequality. Our bound for
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
is independent of
m
, so much like in the proof of
Lemma

2.3
we focus on the
J
term to sum.

Over the domain we are integrating in the expression for
K_{Q,s}
, we have that
|\varphi|\sim(N2^{s})^{-1}
. So this bound becomes

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\bigl|\prod_{j=1}^{d}J(x_{j},\varphi,m_{j},q)e(-\lambda\varphi)\bigr|\lesssim(N2^{s})^{d/2}.

When
2^{s}\sim N
we use the trivial estimate. Returning to (
30
), we see the support of
\eta_{Q,s}
means we are integrating over a set of measure
(N2^{s})^{-1}
, but with a sum over
a
and
q
. The sum over
a
is absorbed into the definition of
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
, and so by the triangle inequality and (
32
) we have

|K_{Q,s}(x)|\lesssim(N2^{s})^{d/2-1}\sum_{q\sim Q}q^{1-\frac{d}{2}}.

We conclude by summing.

|K_{Q,s}(x)|\lesssim(N2^{s})^{d/2-1}Q^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}.

∎

Now we import a bound for the error term.

Proposition 3.6
.

We have

(33)

||{K_{err}}||_{\infty}\lesssim N^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon}.

This is a direct consequence of (2.15) in
[
2
]
, taking
Q\sim 2^{s}\sim N
. Note that
Proposition

3.5
would imply
||{K_{err}}||_{\infty}\lesssim N^{\frac{d}{2}}
, and so the reader may wonder why are are not trying to improve (
29
) using cancellation like is done in cited works. The reason is subtle and will be fully elaborated on in
Section

4.2
in the context of motivating computations. For now, we say we need estimates that hold for all
Q
, and the improved estimates in
[
4
]
only hold for

for small

if

errors are intolerable. As the error term is essentially the case

, there is no harm having an
\epsilon

It will also be beneficial to compute the magnitude of the Fourier transform of
K_{Q,s}
. Such an estimate can be used in two ways, for proving
L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
bounds or for a specific argument involving level sets. In this section we will use the latter. In the following estimates, it is important again that no
\epsilon
-losses appear in the parameters
N
and
2^{s}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

For any
Q,s,
and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have

(34)

\mathcal{F}(K_{Q,s})(\mathbf{k})\lesssim\frac{Q^{2}}{N2^{s}}.

Proof.

When computing
\mathcal{F}(K_{Q,s})(\mathbf{k})
, we note that

G(t,x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e(kx+k^{2}t^{2})

is the only
x
dependence in the definition of
K_{Q,s}
. So, like in the proof of
Proposition

3.3
, we may directly take the Fourier transform in
x
and then in time, using the small support of
\eta_{Q,s}
to view the transform in time as occurring on
\mathbb{R}
. Along with the product structure on the torus, this gives that

\mathcal{F}(K_{Q,s})(\mathbf{k})=\widehat{\eta_{Q,s}}(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda)\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma(k_{i}/N).

The smooth cutoffs play no role in our analysis going forward. Let
l=|\mathbf{k}|^{2}-\lambda
. Using linearity and the definition (
24
), we compute
\widehat{\eta_{Q,s}}
as

\widehat{\eta_{Q,s}}(l)=(N2^{s})^{-1}\hat{\eta}(\frac{l}{N2^{s}})\sum_{\frac{a}{q}\in R_{Q}}e(la/q).

We conclude by
\#R_{Q}\sim Q^{2}
and the triangle inequality.

\mathcal{F}(K_{Q,s})(\mathbf{k})\lesssim(N2^{s})^{-1}Q^{2}.

∎

These are all the estimates required for our proof.

3.2.
Proof of sharp result

We have for general
F
that

\displaystyle||{F*K_{Q,s}}||_{2}\lesssim\frac{Q^{2}}{N2^{s}}||{F}||_{2},

\displaystyle||{F*K_{Q,s}}||_{\infty}\lesssim(N2^{s})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}Q^{-\frac{d-4}{2}}||{F}||_{1}.

We wish to interpolate these these two estimates and sum over
Q
and
s
. The estimate will always be largest when
2^{s}\sim N
, and we will be able to proceed when the power of
Q
is negative, which means the estimate is dominated by the
Q\sim 1
terms. Importantly, we have the correct powers of
N
and
2^{s}
without any
\epsilon
-loss which, in the end, gives us the desired sharp estimate that agrees with the local term
K_{0}
, albeit in a restricted range of
p
. Interpolation yields

(35)

||{F*K_{Q,s}}||_{p}\lesssim(N2^{s})^{\frac{d-2}{2}-\frac{d}{p}}Q^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d-4}{2}}||{F}||_{p^{\prime}}.

The power of
Q
is negative precisely when
p>\frac{2d}{d-4}
. This yields

||{F*\sum_{Q,s}K_{Q,s}}||_{p}\lesssim N^{d-2-\frac{2d}{p}}||{F}||_{p^{\prime}},\quad p>\frac{2d}{d-4}.

Recall that this is the same bound as for
K_{0}
. For ease of notation define
K_{main}=K_{0}+\sum_{Q,s}K_{Q,s}
. The above bound therefore holds for
K_{main}
when
p>\frac{2d}{d-4}
by the triangle inequality. We are now in position to prove the result.

Remark 3.8
.

Even with a bound that was finite for
d=4
, we would not prove corresponding sharp bounds because of the
\epsilon
-loss present in the lattice point count. This manifests both in the local estimate and in the
L^{\infty}
bound used in the computation below.

Following
[
4
]
, assume
\|a_{\xi}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{d,\lambda})}=1
. Define

F(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}a_{\xi}e(x\cdot\xi).

Recall that the level sets
E_{\alpha}
for this function are given by

E_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:|F(x)|>\alpha\}.

This allows us to define the oscillatory indicator functions as

f(x)=\frac{F(x)}{|F(x)|}1_{E_{\alpha}}(x).

These functions have the following crucial property which is trivially verified.

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}dx=|E_{\alpha}|.

By the definitions of these functions, Plancherel’s Theorem, and Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\alpha|E_{\alpha}|\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bar{F}(x)f(x)dx=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}\bar{a}_{\xi}\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi),

which implies

\alpha^{2}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}\leq\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{F}_{d,N}}|\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)|^{2}=\langle K*f,f\rangle.

Recall we can split our kernel as

K=K_{main}+K_{err}.

For
p>\frac{2d}{d-4}
, we have the following estimate.

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle K*f,f\rangle=\langle K_{main}*f,f\rangle+\langle K_{err}*f,f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq||{f}||_{p}||{K_{main}*f}||_{p^{\prime}}+N^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon}\langle|f|,|f|\rangle

\displaystyle\leq N^{d-2-\frac{2d}{p}}|E_{\alpha}|^{\frac{2}{p^{\prime}}}+N^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}.

If
\alpha^{2}\gtrsim N^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\epsilon_{0}}
, then the rightmost term can be absorbed into the left hand side. We then simplify to

(36)

|E_{\alpha}|\leq N^{\frac{p(d-2)}{2}-d}\alpha^{-p},\quad p>2d/(d-4).

The result now follows from standard estimates. Compute

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F|^{p}=\int_{N^{\frac{d-1}{4}+\epsilon_{0}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha+\int_{0}^{N^{\frac{d-1}{4}+\epsilon_{0}}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha.

The second term is handled by the estimate
|E_{\alpha}|\lesssim N^{\epsilon}\alpha^{-\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}}
, which is a corollary of the
\ell^{2}
-decoupling theorem. For the first term we use (
36
). Let
p>p_{1}>2d/(d-4)
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{N^{\frac{d-1}{4}+\epsilon_{0}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\alpha^{p-1}|E_{\alpha}|d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{p_{1}(d-2)}{2}-d}\int_{N^{\frac{d-1}{4}+\epsilon_{0}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\alpha^{p-1}\alpha^{-p_{1}}d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{p_{1}(d-2)}{2}-d}\alpha^{p-p_{1}}\rvert_{N^{\frac{d-1}{4}+\epsilon_{0}}}^{N^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{p_{1}(d-2)}{2}-d}\cdot N^{\frac{d-2}{2}(p-p_{1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{p(d-2)}{2}-d}.

The result follows.

4.
Exponential Sum Estimates

In the previous arguments, two exponential sums arose. In this section, we sketch the proof of the required bounds for the generalized quadratic Gauss sums and explore why the bounds used for Kloosterman and Sailé sums in previous works are insufficient for our purposes.

Recall that the generalized quadratic Gauss sums
S(a,m,q)
are given for integers
a,m,q
such that
(a,q)=1
by

(37)

S(a,m,q)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}e(\frac{ak^{2}+mk}{q}).

We use a different sign convention for
m
in this section but this has no bearing on our results. We also have the following expression which plays the role of the singular series from the circle method.

(38)

\mathcal{S}(m,q)=\sum_{(a,q)=1}(\prod_{j=1}^{d}S(a,m_{j},s)e(-\lambda\frac{a}{q})).

4.1.
Gauss Sum Estimates

For the generalized quadratic Gauss sum, we wish to prove the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
q\geq 2
, we have

(39)

|S(a,m,q)|\lesssim q^{-1/2}.

We may further assume that
(a,q)=1
, however the multiplicative nature of quadratic Gauss sums means this reduction is redundant for the bound we are trying to state. We will show that these sums exhibit square root cancellation. The
S(a,m,q)
generalize the standard quadratic Gauss sums

S(1,0,q)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}e(\frac{k^{2}}{q}).

It is known this sum exhibits square root cancellation, which we prove here for pure primes.

Lemma 4.2

(Standard Gauss Sum Bound)
.

For any
p
a prime with
p\geq 2
, we have

|S(1,0,p)|\leq p^{-1/2}.

Proof.

If
p=2
the result is trivial. Assuming
p
is odd we have

\displaystyle p^{2}|S(1,0,p)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}e(k^{2}/p)\overline{\sum_{l=1}^{p-1}e(l^{2}/p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\sum_{l=0}^{p-1}e((k^{2}-l^{2})/p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\sum_{l=0}^{p-1}e((k+l)(k-l))/p).

We now make the change of variables
\alpha=k+l
and
\beta=k-l
. This gives

p^{2}|S(1,0,p)|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{p-1}\sum_{\beta=0}^{p-1}e(\frac{4\alpha\beta}{p}).

The assumption that
p
is odd means that multiplication by
4
as we sum over the cyclic groups is an isomorphism, and so we can replace it with
1
. If
\alpha\neq 0
, then by the geometric series formula the sum in
\beta
is
0
. Otherwise, it is
p
. This gives

p^{2}|S(1,0,p)|^{2}=p.

Which obviously allows us to conclude.
∎

To prove an identical bound for any odd natural number
q
, one may use the imported
Lemma

4.5
below. For a more elementary proof, we refer to
[
14
]
. For even numbers, there may be an additional factor of
\sqrt{2}
if
q
is a multiple of
4
, see Corollary 1.2.3 in
[
1
]
for a full statement.

Now we state a series of technical results which will use, along with the more general version of
Lemma

4.2
, to prove
Proposition

4.1
.

Lemma 4.3

(Gauss sums are multiplicative)
.

Assume
q=p_{1}p_{2}
with
(p_{1},p_{2})=1
. Then

(40)

S(a,m,q)=S(ap_{2},m,p_{1})S(ap_{1},m,p_{2}).

Proof.

We compute directly

\displaystyle S(ap_{2},m,p_{1})S(ap_{1},m,p_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p_{1}-1}e(\frac{ap_{2}k^{2}+mk}{p_{1}})\sum_{l=0}^{p_{2}-1}e(\frac{ap_{2}l^{2}+ml}{p_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p_{1}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{p_{2}-1}e(\frac{ap_{2}^{2}k^{2}+p_{2}mk+ap_{1}^{2}l^{2}+p_{1}ml}{p_{1}p_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p_{1}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{p_{2}-1}e(\frac{a(p_{2}k+p_{1}l)^{2}+m(p_{2}k+p_{1}l)}{p_{1}p_{2}})

Because of our assumption that
a,p_{1},
and
p_{2}
are all coprime, the Chinese Remainder Theorem implies that
p_{2}k+p_{1}l
runs through all integers mod
p_{1}p_{2}
as
k
and
l
vary. This enables the change of variables
z=p_{2}k+p_{1}l
, which yields

S(ap_{2},m,p_{1})S(ap_{1},m,p_{2})=\sum_{z=0}^{p_{1}p_{2}-1}e(\frac{az^{2}+mz}{p_{1}p_{2}})=S(a,m,q).

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
4|q
and
m
is odd. Then

(41)

S(a,m,q)=0.

Proof.

Because of multiplicativity it is sufficient to show the result with
q=2^{k}
with
k\geq 2
. We have

S(a,m,2^{j})=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}e(\frac{ak^{2}+mk}{2^{j}}).

Let us now compute what values
ak^{2}+mk
takes as
k
varies. Because
a
and
m
are odd,
ak^{2}+mk
is even for every value of
k
. Moreover, suppose we have two solutions
k_{1},k_{2}
of

ak^{2}+mk+c=0\,(\text{mod }2^{j}),

and that
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are the same parity. Then

ak_{1}^{2}-ak_{2}^{2}+mk_{1}-mk_{2}=(k_{1}-k_{2})(a(k_{1}+k_{2})+m)=0\,(\text{mod }2^{j}).

The term
a(k_{1}+k_{2})+m
is always odd. This forces

(k_{1}-k_{2})=0\,(\text{mod }2^{j}).

Therefore, we can have, at most, one odd solution and one even solution. As
k
varies in
\{0,..,2^{j}-1\}
, the value
ak^{2}+mk
runs through each even number in
\mathbb{Z}/2^{j}\mathbb{Z}
exactly twice by basic counting. Therefore we can write the sum as

S(a,m,2^{j})=2\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}e(\frac{2k}{2^{j}}).

Because
j\geq 2
, the above sum is 0 by the geometric series formula.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
aq+m
is even. Then

(42)

|S(a,m,q)|=|\frac{q}{a}|^{-1/2}|S(-q,-m,a)|.

Proof.

This is Theorem 1.2.2 in
[
1
]
with our normalization.
∎

These results are enough to prove the required bound for all natural numbers
q
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

We always assume
(a,q)=1
. Because of multiplicativity, we may reduce to proving

|S(a,m,p^{j})|\leq p^{-j/2}

for all primes
p
nd integers
a,j
such that
j\geq 1
and
(a,q)=1
.

Suppose
p
is odd. Then
p^{j}
is as well. In what follows, denote
x^{*}
the multiplicative inverse of
x
modulo
p^{j}
and
(\frac{a}{p^{j}})
the Jacobi symbol. Direct computation gives

\displaystyle S(a,m,p^{j})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{j}}e(-4^{*}a^{*}m^{2}/p^{j})\sum_{k=0}^{p^{j}-1}e(a/p^{j}(k^{2}-2k2^{*}a^{*}m+4^{*}(a^{*})^{2}m^{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{j}}e(-4^{*}a^{*}m^{2}/p^{j})\sum_{k=0}^{p^{j}-1}e(k^{2}a/p^{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{j}}e(-4^{*}a^{*}m^{2}/p^{j})(\frac{a}{p^{j}})S(1,0,p^{j}).

So the result follows in the case from the bound on the standard quadratic Gauss sum. Now suppose
p^{j}=2^{j}
for some j. The result is trivial if
j=1
. If
j\geq 2
, then
2^{j}
is divisible by
4
. If
m
is odd, then
Lemma

4.4
immediately gives the result as the sum is
0
. If
j
is even, then we can apply
Lemma

4.5
with
q=2^{j}
to get

|S(a,m,2^{j})|=|\frac{2^{j}}{a}|^{-1/2}|S(-2^{j},-m,a)|.

The result then follows as
a
is coprime to
2^{j}
, and is therefore odd, after another application of multiplicativity.
∎

These bounds suffice for our results in
Section

2
. In
Section

3
, we sum over
a
in the expression for
K_{Q,s}
, yielding
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
. Ostensibly the sum over
a
gives additional cancellation, however we will argue in the following subsection this is insufficient for proving sharp results.

4.2.
Difficulty in improving the range of
p

We wish to use cancellation in the sum over
a
to get an improved bound in
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
. For this purpose, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

The function
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
is multiplicative in
q
. If
q
is odd, it has the following expression.

(43)

\mathcal{S}(m,q)=S(1,0,q)^{d}\sum_{(a,q)=1}\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)^{d}e((-\nu/q)a^{*}-(\lambda/q)a),

where
\nu=-4^{*}(m_{1}^{2}+...+m_{d}^{2})
.

The proof is standard, but we do not present it as we will not prove results in this subsection. Recall that as the Jacobi symbol takes on the values
0,1,-1
, the expression for
\mathcal{S}(m,q)
reduces to an expression involving Kloosterman sums when
d
is even and Sailé sums when
d
is odd. We now recall the definitions of these sums.

Definition 4.7
.

We define a Kloosterman sum as

(44)

\textbf{Kl}(\alpha,\beta,q)\coloneq\sum_{(a,q)=1}e(\frac{a\alpha+a^{*}\beta}{q}).

And the Sailé sums

(45)

\textbf{Sa}(\alpha,\beta,q)=\sum_{(a,q)=1}(\frac{a}{q})e(\frac{a\alpha+a^{*}\beta}{q}).

For
q
a prime power, we have square root cancellation and each can be bounded by
\lesssim q^{1/2}
, see
[
6
]
for example. For general
q
, the best bound we can hope for both these sums is

\textbf{Kl}(\alpha,\beta,q),\textbf{Sa}(\alpha,\beta,q)\lesssim\tau(q)\sqrt{\gcd(\alpha,\beta,q)}\sqrt{q}.

Here
\tau(q)
is the number of divisors of
q
. As we assume
(a,q)=1
, the bound simplifies in our case to

\textbf{Kl}(\alpha,\beta,q),\textbf{Sa}(\alpha,\beta,q)\lesssim q^{\epsilon}\sqrt{\gcd(\lambda,q)}\sqrt{q}.

This is the bound given by (2.12) in
[
2
]
. The power of
q^{\epsilon}
is inconsequential for our purposes. The factor
\sqrt{\gcd(\lambda,q)}
is not. If
q|\lambda
, this means we get no cancellation in the sum and we default to using the trivial bound
\lesssim q
from the triangle inequality.

An implicit step in
[
2
]
and
[
4
]
is the following. Restrict to
Q\leq N^{\epsilon_{0}}
and use the triangle inequality. For
Q\geq N^{\epsilon_{0}}
, apply the above bound. The reason for this is that the additional factors of
\sqrt{(\lambda,q)}
build up in a fatal way. If
Q
is moderately sized, there cannot be too many
q
that divide
\lambda
as
\lambda\leq N^{2}
. However, for very small
q
, it may be the case that
\lambda
has many divisors in the denominators of
R_{Q}
.

Take
Q\sim\log\log N
, for example, and consider the integer formed by

X=\prod_{q\sim Q}q.

This integer satisfies
X\lesssim(\log\log N)^{\log\log N}\lesssim N
. So if
X|\lambda
, we may only use the trivial estimate for every
q\sim Q
. With our interpolation, we desire a negative power of
Q
, so our estimate is dominated by the
Q\sim 1
terms. Therefore, we must use the trivial estimates near
Q\sim 1
, which restricts the range of
p
we can apply our argument in.

A similar phenomenon explains why we cannot improve the bound
Proposition

3.7
to
Q^{1+\epsilon}
like (14) in
[
4
]
. For small
Q
, the best we can hope for is the triangle inequality. The improved bound for moderate
Q\gtrsim N^{\epsilon_{0}}
does not change our final bound as we are limited by the terms
Q\sim 1
.

Remark 4.8
.

If we assume
\lambda
does not have many divisors, we do get an improved bound. In the ideal situation, we have
\lambda
being prime, and the techniques of this paper give the sharp results for
p>\frac{2(d+1)}{d-3}
for
d\geq 5
by using the full square root cancellation in Kloosterman and Sailé sums. Intermediate bounds can be proven based on the structure of the divisors of
\lambda
, but as we are interested in the strongest bound for the full set of eigenvalues we do not record them here.

5.
Comparison with result with loss

In this section we will catalog the differences between our proofs and the proof in
[
4
]
.

A more careful integration by parts argument allows us to prove a no loss version of the dispersive type estimate for
G(t,x)
. This is already known by other methods when
q\geq N
. For the
\log
-loss argument had to show the same holds near rationals with very small denominator.

A more careful analysis of
\mathcal{F}(K-K^{Q})
gives that the
Q^{\epsilon}
bound there is in fact a
\log
coming from the normalization constant
\log Q
. Such a factor is always present if we take our denominators to be primes. Taking the set of denominators to be all numbers eliminates this loss, but then the roots of unity argument used to simplify the expression (
18
) no longer holds, and a loss is incurred there regardless.

When proving the sharp result, our philosophy is to take any power of
Q
as long as we do not have an
\epsilon
-loss in the parameters
N
and
2^{s}
. This gives us worse bounds compared to previous works and prevents us from using more involved calculations. Additionally, our intolerance of losses prevents us from improving over the trivial bound in a few places, most notably (
32
) and (
34
). It seems different techniques are needed to improve these bounds.
