 Numerical Software
 Cindy Rubio-González (PI)
 University of California, Davis

                                        December 31, 2025

1 Executive Summary

This is the final technical report for the project “Towards Scalable Precision Tuning of Numerical
Software,” funded by the Department of Energy (DOE), Office of Science, Office of Advanced
Scientific Computing Research (ASCR) under award number SC0020286. The report summarizes
the work done over the funding period (9/2019-8/2025), focusing on the development of automated
techniques to balance the floating-point precision and performance of numerical software. The
research resulted in nine publications including top-tier venues (SC, ICSE, ICS, ISC, ARITH), two
Best Paper Awards (Hans Meuer Award at ISC 2023 and Best Paper Award at ARITH 2025), and
the release of eight open-source software tools.

2 Major Goals

The primary objective of this project was to address the reliability and performance challenges
of numerical software by automating the exploration of precision-performance trade-offs. While
high floating-point precision ensures reliability, it often leads to significant performance overheads;
conversely, reduced precision can accelerate computation but may introduce catastrophic errors.
This project was organized into two main research thrusts:

 1. Scalable Precision Tuning: Devising novel algorithms and tools for scalable floating-point
 precision tuning, and collaborating with application developers (e.g., weather and climate
 modeling) to evaluate tuning effectiveness in real-world scenarios.

 2. Robust Precision Tuning: Creating advanced testing and analysis techniques to iden-
 tify inputs that trigger high numerical errors and other numerical problems to improve the
 reliability of numerical programs.

3 Technical Accomplishments

This section details the project’s technical accomplishments, organized into the two primary re-
search thrusts: Scalable Precision Tuning and Robust Precision Tuning. The contributions range
from developing predictive models for mixed-precision performance and accuracy to evaluating
automated tuning in weather and climate models. This work has produced a suite of methodolo-
gies and tools that enable developers to maximize computational performance while ensuring the
numerical accuracy required for scientific discovery.

3.1 Predicting Performance and Accuracy for Precision Tuning
We developed FPLearner, a deep learning-based framework that accelerates mixed-precision
tuning by predicting the performance and accuracy of numerical programs. While traditional
dynamic tuners suffer from scalability bottlenecks due to the requirement for exhaustive program
executions, FPLearner replaces costly runs with rapid model predictions. By representing mixed-
precision programs as graph structures, our approach utilizes Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to
model complex program dependencies. Experimental results show that FPLearner improves the
efficiency of state-of-the-art dynamic tuners by an average of 25.5% (up to 61%) while maintaining
or improving tuning quality. This work was published at the International Conference on Software
Engineering (ICSE) 2024 [8]. The tool is open-source and accessible at https://github.com/
ucd-plse/FPLearner.

3.2 Precision Tuning for Weather and Climate Models
We conducted a pioneering case study on floating-point precision tuning for weather and climate
models, addressing a critical gap in high-performance computing (HPC) research. While previous
efforts in this domain relied on manual expert intervention or low-precision emulation—which often
obscures actual hardware speedups—our work utilizes automated, performance-guided techniques
at a production scale. Leveraging a custom-built Fortran transformation tool, we applied precision
tuning to computational hotspots in three real-world models: MPAS-A, ADCIRC, and MOM6.
Our analysis identifies key lessons learned and provides a framework of best practices for feasible
precision tuning in complex, large-scale domains. This work was published at the International
Workshop on Software Correctness for HPC Applications co-located with SC 2024 [6] and the tool
is publicly available at https://github.com/ucd-plse/PROSE.

3.3 Exposing Large Errors and Compiler-Induced Numerical Inconsistencies
Robust precision tuning requires a deep understanding of the edge cases where numerical accu-
racy collapses. In floating-point computation, large errors refer to significant deviations from the
mathematically expected result, often triggered by phenomena such as catastrophic cancellation
or precision loss in sensitive algorithms. Closely related are compiler-induced numerical inconsis-
tencies, which occur when the same source code produces divergent outputs due to differences in
how compilers, or different optimization levels (e.g., -O2 vs. -O3), reorder instructions and apply
floating-point optimizations. Identifying the inputs and programs that trigger these behaviors is
essential for robustness; if a program is numerically unstable under different compilers or inputs,
precision tuning results cannot be trusted as reliable across different execution environments.

3.3.1 Symbolic Execution for Error-Inducing Numerical Inputs
We developed FPGen, the first symbolic-execution framework capable of generating multi-dimensional
floating-point inputs that maximize numerical error. A key innovation of our approach is an au-
tomated program transformation that inserts explicit checks for precision loss and catastrophic
cancellation at critical program points. This allows the symbolic execution engine to treat nu-
merical instability as a searchable property, uncovering errors that were previously unreachable.
Prior to FPGen, symbolic-execution tools lacked the mechanisms to effectively trigger numerical
inconsistencies. Our evaluation shows that FPGen identifies errors in 33% more programs than
state-of-the-art techniques, and the errors discovered are, on average, more than two orders of mag-
nitude larger. This research was published at the International Conference on Software Engineering

(ICSE) 2020 [2] and the tool is available at https://github.com/ucd-plse/FPGen.

3.3.2 Input Range Generation for Compiler-Induced Numerical Inconsistencies
We developed CIGEN, an automated technique designed to identify input ranges that trigger
compiler-induced numerical inconsistencies. CIGEN utilizes a multi-phase workflow that inte-
grates input-partitioned and coverage-based sampling, input clustering, and optimization algo-
rithms to uncover previously unknown problematic inputs. In an evaluation across 175 GNU
Scientific Library (GSL) functions, CIGEN demonstrated a 53.4% improvement over the state
of the art in detecting inconsistencies. Furthermore, by integrating CIGEN with Ciel [4], we
successfully identified 21 GSL functions where multiple, distinct sources of compiler-induced in-
consistency were captured across different input ranges. This work was published at the In-
ternational Conference on Supercomputing (ICS) 2024 [5] and the tool is publicly available at
https://github.com/LLNL/CIGEN.

3.3.3 Program Generation for Compiler-Induced Numerical Inconsistencies
We developed LLM4FP, the first framework that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to au-
tomatically generate floating-point programs designed to trigger numerical inconsistencies across
different compilers and optimization levels. Unlike traditional randomized testing tools that often
produce semantically simple or invalid code, LLM4FP utilizes a dual-strategy approach: Grammar-
Based Generation to create syntactically valid programs from scratch, and Feedback-Based Muta-
tion to refine programs that have successfully exposed inconsistencies. In our experimental evalua-
tion, LLM4FP achieved an inconsistency detection rate of 29.33%—roughly 2.5× the effectiveness
of existing methods. Notably, over 98% of the inconsistencies identified by LLM4FP involved subtle
deviations in normal numerical values rather than extreme cases like NaNs or infinities, demon-
strating its superior ability to uncover complex compiler-induced errors. This work was published
at the International Workshop on Software Correctness for HPC Applications co-located with SC
2025 [9] and the tool is available at https://github.com/ucd-plse/LLM4FP.

3.4 Isolation of Compiler-Induced Numerical Inconsistencies
While the previous section focused on the automated generation of inputs and programs to expose
numerical instability, this section addresses the critical challenge of isolation. Once an inconsistency
is detected—where divergent results occur across different compilation paths—developers must
pinpoint the exact root cause within the source code. Unlike input generation, which aims to find
where a program fails, isolation identifies the specific lines of code or mathematical expressions
that are hypersensitive to compiler optimizations. Automating this localization is a prerequisite
for robust precision tuning, as it allows developers to apply selective precision enhancement only
to the problematic code regions, thereby maximizing performance gains while maintaining strict
numerical consistency.

3.4.1 Code Line Isolation for CPU Programs
We developed pLiner, an automated technique for localizing the source code responsible for
compiler-induced numerical inconsistencies in CPU applications. These inconsistencies—where a
program yields divergent results when using different compilers or optimization levels—undermine
the reliability of scientific software. pLiner employs a novel combination of hierarchical code iso-
lation and selective precision enhancement to pinpoint the specific code regions that determine

numerical stability. In our experimental evaluation, pLiner successfully isolated problematic code
in 87% of cases, representing a substantial advancement over the 6% success rate of state-of-the-art
methods. This work was published at Supercomputing Conference (SC) 2020 [3] and the tool is
publicly available at https://github.com/LLNL/pLiner.

3.4.2 Expression Isolation in Heterogeneous Programs
We developed Ciel, an automated approach for localizing numerical inconsistencies induced by
compiler optimizations at the expression level in heterogeneous applications. Ciel utilizes a re-
cursive bisection search algorithm with adaptive granularity to guide a floating-point precision
enhancement strategy. This methodology allows for the efficient identification of specific pro-
gram expressions responsible for numerical deviations. In experimental evaluations across diverse
heterogeneous applications, Ciel achieved 99.4% precision in isolating inconsistencies and demon-
strated performance comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art methods for CPU-only programs.
Ciel [4] was published at the International Supercomputing Conference (ISC) 2023, where it
received the Hans Meuer Award (Best Research Paper). Ciel is open-source and available at
https://github.com/LLNL/Ciel.

3.5 More Robust Exception Handling in Numerical Libraries
A fundamental component of numerical robustness is the reliable management of floating-point ex-
ceptions, such as division-by-zero, overflow, and invalid operations. In large-scale HPC applications,
exception handling is frequently inconsistent; numerical libraries may suppress exceptions by failing
to propagate NaN or Infinity values to the output, or they may behave differently across hardware
architectures. This lack of standardization undermines precision tuning, as it prevents developers
from using hardware flags as a reliable signal for precision loss. The following subsections describe
our efforts to establish community standards for exception propagation and to develop automated
tools that verify whether libraries adhere to their stated exception-handling policies.

3.5.1 Consistent Exception Handling for the BLAS and LAPACK
We proposed a first-of-its-kind standard for consistent exception handling in the BLAS and LA-
PACK libraries, addressing decades-old inconsistencies in how these fundamental routines manage
floating-point exceptions. While the IEEE 754 standard defines exceptional values (NaN and Infin-
ity), existing linear algebra implementations often handle them inconsistently—either silently sup-
pressing them or producing unreliable results that can mislead users. Our work, developed through
a broad cross-institutional collaboration between academia, U.S. National Labs, and industry, de-
fines a rigorous propagation policy: exceptional values created during or input to a routine must
propagate to the output unless there is a clear mathematical justification for their removal. This
specification provides a critical roadmap for standardizing reliability across the high-performance
computing (HPC) software stack and serves as the formal baseline for next-generation verification
tools, such as EXCVATE (described in the next subsection). This research was presented at the
International Workshop on Software Correctness for HPC Applications co-located with SC 2022 [1].

3.5.2 Testing Exception Handling
We developed EXCVATE, an automated framework designed to verify the correctness of exception-
handling policies in numerical libraries. While prior research focused primarily on generating inputs
to trigger floating-point exceptions, EXCVATE addresses the critical challenge of ensuring that

these exceptions—once they occur—are handled and reported consistently according to a library’s
specification. The tool employs a novel two-phase approach: first, it utilizes binary instrumentation
to cheaply spoof exceptions by overwriting registers with exceptional values (e.g., NaNs or Infinities)
mid-execution; second, for any identified policy violations, it uses an SMT solver to back-calculate
concrete, valid inputs that reify the buggy behavior. In an evaluation of 26 BLAS functions across
multiple implementations and compilers, EXCVATE identified five significant exception-handling
failures where exceptions were silently suppressed or incorrectly propagated. This work was awarded
the Best Paper Award at the 32nd IEEE Symposium on Computer Arithmetic (ARITH 2025) [7]
and can be found at https://github.com/ucd-plse/EXCVATE.

4 Professional Leadership and Community Engagement

Beyond research dissemination and tool development, the PI demonstrated sustained leadership in
the high-performance computing (HPC) community through the organization of major technical
events. In collaboration with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), the PI co-organized
six consecutive editions (2019–2024) of the International Workshop on Software Correctness for
HPC Applications (CORRECTNESS), held annually at the Supercomputing (SC) conference. The
PI’s leadership extended to inter-agency coordination as a co-organizer of the DOE/NSF Workshop
on Correctness in Scientific Computing at PLDI 2023. Furthermore, the PI prioritized educational
outreach by co-developing and presenting a tutorial at SC 2024 titled “Tools to Diagnose and Repair
Floating-Point Errors in Heterogeneous Computing Hardware and Software,” in partnership with
the University of Utah and the University of Washington.

5 Concluding Remarks

Over the duration of this award, the project successfully transformed the analysis of numerical
software from a manual, expert-intensive process into a scalable, automated discipline. By develop-
ing a comprehensive ecosystem of tools, we have provided researchers with the means to optimize
floating-point precision without sacrificing scientific integrity. Our case studies on production-scale
weather and climate models (MPAS-A, MOM6) demonstrate that these techniques are practically
essential for the next generation of heterogeneous HPC architectures. Furthermore, through our
leadership of the CORRECTNESS workshop series and the proposal of new exception-handling
standards, we have fostered a more robust and collaborative research community. The open-source
software and methodologies resulting from this work provide a lasting foundation for ensuring the
efficiency and reliability of scientific applications.
