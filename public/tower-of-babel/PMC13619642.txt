A stable tensorial approach for inversion of adsorption potential energy from isotherm data

Márcio Oliveira Alves, Felipe Silva Carvalho, João Pedro Braga

Abstract

AbstractContextUnderlying the adsorption mechanism is not a simple task due to the complexity of the process. In this context, statistical mechanics provides a versatile approach with relatively low computational cost for describing adsorption phenomena. An alternative strategy consists of obtaining the adsorption potential energy from experimental data through the solution of an inverse problem. In this work, the adsorption potential energy was determined directly from adsorption isotherms using experimental datasets for both aqueous- and gas-phase adsorption systems. The proposed methodology was compared with the traditional two-step procedure, in which the second adsorption virial coefficient is first determined and subsequently used for inversion of the potential energy. Due to the ill-posed nature of the problem, the conventional approach exhibits significant instability and error propagation. In contrast, the direct inversion method provides excellent results, achieving values of R2∼0.99.MethodsA methodology grounded in statistical physics was developed to obtain the adsorption potential energy directly from isotherm data. The proposed approach formulates the problem as a tensor contraction, eliminating the need for the traditional two-step procedure involving the intermediate determination of the second adsorption virial coefficient.Keywords: Adsorption, Potential energy, Tensorial contraction, Inverse problem

Introduction

Adsorption is an efficient, versatile, and cost-effective method for removing contaminants from water and wastewater. Therefore, developing high-performance adsorbent materials is essential to improve removal efficiency. These materials have increased adsorption capacity, enabling faster and more effective treatment. In addition, advances in adsorbent design contribute to more sustainable and economical solutions, benefiting both the environment and water resource management [–].

Despite the widespread use of adsorption, achieving a comprehensive understanding of its mechanisms remains a challenge due to the complexity of the process. This complexity arises from the numerous sub-processes and intricate interactions between the adsorbent, adsorbate, and solvent []. Adsorption studies are typically conducted experimentally to evaluate equilibrium isotherms and process kinetics. These experiments provide valuable data for understanding how variables such as temperature, concentration, and time affect adsorption behavior [, ].

In a theoretical approach, advanced techniques such as electronic structure calculations [], molecular dynamics simulations [], and transport property studies [] can be employed. In particular, electronic structure calculations, often based on methods such as density functional theory (DFT), allow the precise determination of energy levels and interactions within the adsorbate-adsorbent, providing valuable information on adsorption energies and geometric configurations. While experimental isotherms are often analyzed using empirical models such as Langmuir and Freundlich, these do not provide the detailed physical insight required to fully understand the adsorption process []. On the other hand, quantum chemical methods and molecular dynamics simulations are computationally demanding and may not provide an accurate description of adsorption on heterogeneous and porous surfaces [].

An alternative approach consists in employing models based on statistical mechanics, which provide valuable insights into the adsorption process while maintaining a low computational cost. Ben Lamine et al. proposed a more realistic framework for studying adsorption phenomena based on Hill’s adsorption model [, ]. This method allows the application of a series of models to fit experimental adsorption isotherms and extract microscopic information, such as the number of layers, orientation anchorage, and number of adsorption sites. This approach has been demonstrated to be both powerful and versatile in describing adsorption processes across various adsorbates and adsorbents [–].

Another methodology grounded in statistical mechanics was introduced by Radke and Prausnitz []. Based on the theory of dilute solutions proposed by McMillan and Mayer [], the adsorbent surface is treated as an external field influencing the solution. In this framework, the amount of material adsorbed on the surface is described by a virial expansion, in which each virial coefficient depends on the interaction potential between the adsorbed molecules and the surface. This formulation is consistent with Henry’s law, since the second virial coefficient (SVC) can be obtained from the slope of the isotherm at low concentrations. Consequently, if the adsorption potential energy (APE) is known, the SVC can be determined, allowing direct calculation of the adsorption isotherm. Conversely, determining the APE from experimental SVC data constitutes an inverse problem [].

Experimental data inherently contain noise due to measurement errors. As a result, the inverse problem is ill-posed, meaning that the solution may be non-unique, unstable with respect to the data, or may not exist []. To address such issues, methods such as singular value decomposition [], Tikhonov regularization [], and Hopfield neural networks [] are commonly employed. In a recent study, we applied Hopfield neural network to derive the APE from SVC data [].

Since experimental adsorption isotherms are widely used in adsorption studies, an important approach is to determine the APE directly from these data. In this work, a generalized methodology is introduced to obtain the APE directly from adsorption isotherms, eliminating the need to first determine the SVC. To this end, a novel framework is proposed in which the inverse problem is formulated as a tensor contraction in a multiway setting, enabling direct estimation of the APE from isotherm data. The methodology is validated using different experimental datasets for both liquid- and gas-phase adsorption. By improving the generality and robustness of APE determination, this approach provides a powerful tool for predicting adsorption energies from experimental data.

Results and discussion

Adsorption datasets and inversion strategy

Since the second virial coefficient corresponds to the linear intercept, the isotherm data must exhibit near-linear behavior at low concentrations. Therefore, the experimental dataset should include a sufficient number of points in this concentration range at different temperatures. To validate the methodology, experimental datasets exhibiting these characteristics were selected for adsorption systems in both aqueous and gas phases. For adsorption in aqueous solution, two datasets were selected for validation of the proposed methodology. The first dataset was obtained from the work of Oueslati et al., in which the adsorption of Reactive Red 120 dye (RR 120) on activated carbon (AC) was investigated []. The second dataset is avaliable on Calvete et al. works and refers to the adsorpiton of Procion Red MX 3Bdye (PR-3B) on AC. These datasets provide extensive coverage of low-concentration measurements exhibiting near-linear behavior. Additionally, the study reports adsorption isotherms measured at six different temperatures, making this dataset particularly suitable for evaluating the efficiency of the model. For the selection of gas-phase adsorption data, the same criteria were applied, and the dataset reported by Sghaier et al. on CO2 adsorption over AC was selected. []. In both cases, the experimental data available in graphical form were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer software [].

The Lennard–Jones 10-4 potential, given by Eq. , was selected for the inversion procedure because it is widely employed to describe adsorbate–surface interactions and requires only a small number of adjustable parameters. However, the inversion strategy proposed in this work is not restricted to a specific potential model. Other adsorption potentials, such as the Steele potential [], can also be incorporated into the same framework without modification of the underlying methodology.

General trends of the virial coefficients

Regarding the virial coefficients obtained for both systems (RR120 and CO2 adsorption), the observed temperature dependence reflects the balance between intermolecular interactions and thermal effects. A decrease in the value of B1 (SVC) with increasing temperature is observed, which occurs due to the reduction in the adsorption energy. In particular, for the adsorption of RR120, which occurs in solution, the interactions between the adsorbate and the surface are more favorable than those between the dye molecules and the solvent.

The negative values of C2 indicate that adsorption is dominated by repulsive interactions or competition for adsorption sites. This effect is more pronounced at lower temperatures, where reduced thermal motion enhances the influence of intermolecular interactions. As the temperature increases, thermal agitation weakens these interactions, leading to less negative values and indicating a shift toward entropy-dominated behavior. The stronger negativity observed in the aqueous phase suggests that additional factors such as solvation and steric constraints further enhance competitive effects compared to the gas phase.

In contrast, the slightly positive values for D3 indicate that many-body correlations contribute cooperatively to adsorption. These correlations are more significant at lower temperatures, where structured arrangements of adsorbed molecules are more stable. As temperature increases, thermal motion disrupts these arrangements, reducing the magnitude of the third virial coefficient and indicating a diminished role of higher-order interactions.

Sensitivity and robustness of model 3

To evaluate the sensitivity and robustness of Model 3, a series of analyses was performed. The adsorption dataset of Reactive Red 120 (RR120) on activated carbon (AC) was selected for this analysis because it contains the largest number of experimental data points among the datasets considered, providing a more rigorous test of the method. Furthermore, all calculations were carried out using the third-order expansion with the (A0) correction, as this configuration yielded the most accurate results in the previous analyses.

The first assessment examined the dependence of the inversion procedure on the initial estimates of the nonlinear parameters, (ε) and (Ad). Since the remaining parameters enter the model linearly, they were determined directly using the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. A set of randomly generated initial guesses for (ε), ranging from 0.1 to 10 (kT), and (Ad), ranging from 0.01 to 1 (g-1 L), was employed, and the inversion procedure was repeated for each condition.

The sensitivity analysis showed that the inversion results were remarkably insensitive to the initial estimates, since the standard deviation of ε/kT and Ad was only 9.93×10-8 and 1.31×10-6, respectively. These extremely small deviations demonstrate that the inversion converges to essentially the same solution over a broad range of initial estimates, indicating a very low sensitivity of the obtained APE parameters to the initial guesses.

The robustness of Model 3 was further assessed by introducing controlled artificial noise into the experimental isotherm data for the adsorption of R120 onto AC. Normally distributed random errors corresponding to 1, 5, and 10 % of the experimental values were independently added to the original dataset. For each noise level, 100 perturbed isotherms were generated using randomly sampled errors, and the inversion procedure was subsequently performed for each realization. The mean values of the estimated parameters ε and Ad, together with their corresponding standard deviations, are presented in Table .

As can be seen, the parameter ε, which represents the depth of the adsorption potential well, exhibits a high degree of stability with respect to the introduction of artificial noise. Its mean values remain close to the value obtained from the original experimental dataset, while the corresponding standard deviations remain relatively small even at the highest noise level considered. This behavior indicates that the estimation of the potential well depth is not significantly affected by moderate perturbations of the experimental data.

In contrast, the parameter Ad exhibits a substantially larger dispersion as the noise level increases. This behavior is expected because Ad is defined as the product of two quantities, namely the surface area A and the minimum potential coordinate d. Consequently, variations in the experimental data can be propagated through this product, resulting in greater variability in the estimated value of Ad. This behavior is also consistent with the inherent characteristics of ill-posed inverse problems, for which different combinations of model parameters may provide similarly satisfactory descriptions of the available data. Therefore, the relatively large standard deviations observed for Ad should not necessarily be interpreted as a failure of the inversion procedure, but rather as an indication of the greater sensitivity of this composite parameter to perturbations in the input data.

Despite this sensitivity, the overall quality of the inversions remains high, as indicated by the R2 and RMSD values obtained for the perturbed datasets. Even when artificial errors of up to 10 % were introduced, the inversion procedure continued to provide satisfactory fits to the resulting isotherms. This result demonstrates that Model 3 maintains its predictive capability under controlled perturbations of the experimental data. It is worth noting that the original experimental measurements already contain an intrinsic level of experimental uncertainty; therefore, the successful recovery of the adsorption parameters after the deliberate introduction of additional random errors provides further evidence of the numerical robustness of the proposed inversion procedure. If the model were highly unstable with respect to perturbations in the input data or strongly dependent on the particular realization of the experimental errors, substantial deterioration of the R2 and RMSD values would be expected as the noise level increased. The absence of such a pronounced deterioration supports the robustness of Model 3 against experimental data perturbations.

To further assess the numerical stability of the inversion procedure, the condition number of the concentration matrix involved in the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse was evaluated for each temperature. The condition number was calculated as κ(C(k))=σmax/σmin, where σmax and σmin are the largest and smallest singular values of C(k), respectively []. For the RR120/AC dataset with model 3, the condition numbers ranged from 6.79×103 to 3.33×104 across the investigated temperatures. These values indicate a moderately conditioned inversion problem, with the matrices remaining sufficiently far from numerical singularity. Therefore, although the polynomial structure of the concentration matrix introduces a certain degree of conditioning, the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse does not lead to severe numerical instability for the dataset investigated. This result is consistent with the sensitivity and noise analyses presented above, which showed that the recovered parameters remain stable under changes in the initial estimates and perturbations of the experimental data.

Conclusion

In this work, a new methodology is proposed to determine the APE directly from adsorption isotherm data, based on Henry’s law adsorption. The traditional approach involves a two-step procedure, in which the SVC is first obtained from the isotherm data and subsequently used for inversion of the APE. However, due to the ill-posed nature of the problem, this approach introduces significant instability and error propagation during the inversion process.

The proposed approach, referred as Model 3, formulates the problem as a tensorial contraction and was evaluated using three experimental datasets: adsorption of RR120 and PR-3B dyes on AC in aqueous solution and CO2 adsorption on AC in the gas phase. In all cases, increasing the polynomial order and including the baseline term A0 improved the inversion results. Although these modifications increase the number of model parameters, the use of adjusted R2 and RMSD, which penalize model complexity, confirms the improvement. Under these conditions, the method achieves R2∼0.99, consistent with the best-performing adsorption models. In contrast, Models 1 and 2, which rely on a two-step procedure, do not exhibit a consistent trend and, in some cases, yield inversion results with R2<0.7, highlighting the inherent instability of the approach.Additionally, Model 3 demonstrated robust performance in the presence of artificially introduced experimental errors and remarkable stability with respect to different initial estimates of the nonlinear parameters, supporting the reliability of the proposed inversion procedure.

Overall, the results suggest that direct inversion of the APE within the isotherm fitting framework provides a more stable strategy for treating the inverse problem under the conditions investigated. By avoiding intermediate steps and reducing error propagation, the proposed methodology allows a consistent description of adsorption behavior for the studied systems. Nevertheless, the applicability of the approach depends on the availability of experimental adsorption isotherms that obey Henry’s law and contain a sufficient number of experimental points in the linear low-concentration region at different temperatures. Under these conditions, the methodology represents a promising framework for the determination of adsorption potential energies directly from experimental isotherm data.
