Efficient preservation of old methane-derived organic carbon in deep-sea surface sediments
Authors: Rui Bao, Yangli Che, Mingzhi Liu, Naiyu Zhang, Yang Zhou, Lihua Dong, Shen Li, Owen A Sherwood, Min Yu, Zongheng Chen, Shengxiong Yang, Nan Wang, Longhai Zhu, Jiawang Chen, Mutai Bao, Haoshuai Li, Luonan Chen, Weifeng Wu, Haiyue Ma, David J Burdige
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethane released from the subseafloor is significantly attenuated during upward migration, yet the preservation of methane-derived organic carbon (OC) in global deep-sea sediments remains poorly understood. Here, we measured carbon isotopes coupled with temperature-ramped analyses on various methane seepage sediments. Our results reveal a correlation between the radiocarbon (14C) content in deep-sea surface sediments and sulfate-methane transition depth. Notably, our findings suggest that a substantial amount of 14C-depleted OC may originate from deep-seated methane and is efficiently preserved in surface seepage sediments. We propose that the efficient OC preservation is related to microbial-mediated aggregate formation at the seawater-sediment interface, where physical occlusion within coarse-grained matrices reduces oxygen availability and enhances OC stability. We estimate that at least 6 Tg C of methane-derived OC are preserved annually in surface sediments of the global continental slopes. This process may play a non-negligible role in OC preservation in sediments, reducing methane emission into the atmosphere.Subject terms: Carbon cycle, Marine chemistry, Geochemistry

Introduction

Methane is a potent greenhouse gas with a warming potential approximately 28 times relative to carbon dioxide over a 100-year timescale1,2. Substantial amounts of methane are trapped in ice-like solid hydrates in subseafloor sediments2–4. There is evidence that methane from the subseafloor can be released into the atmosphere, which is thought to be a non-negligible driving factor of rising surface temperature on Earth5,6. The decomposition of hydrates results in the generation and upward emission of methane gas. In situ underwater observations in the deep sea have revealed the visible release of methane bubbles from the seabed sediments into seawater7,8. It is therefore essential to investigate the processes of seafloor methane emissions and their transformation products.

Methane emitted from subseafloor sediments serves as an energy source for microbial communities, driving a cascade of biogeochemical processes within seafloor ecosystems. A substantial portion of this methane is consequently consumed and microbially transformed in marine sediments4,9,10. This microbial-mediated transformation can lead to the incorporation of methane carbon into organic matter11, and the subsequent preservation of this methane-derived organic carbon (OC) in sediments. The methane-derived OC may constitute a potentially important carbon pool with implications for climate change. Its transformation and preservation are directly relevant to the regulation of methane emissions.

Central to these processes is the methane that migrates from deeper geological layers, yet its upward flux is predominantly controlled by sulfate-coupled anaerobic oxidation occurring within the sulfate-methane transition (SMT) zone12,13. Consequently, the depth of the SMT serves as a vital indicator of the potential for methane to reach the surface seabed. Methane emissions appear to be widespread across various marine sedimentary environments14,15. However, the extent to which methane-derived OC is preserved in marine sediments in relation to variations in SMT depth remains inadequately characterized on a broader spatial scale. This represents a significant knowledge gap in our understanding of methane cycling and its ultimate fate as a greenhouse gas.

Quantifying the preservation of OC derived from methane transformation across the vast seafloor remains challenging. Cold seeps, as extreme oceanic environments with high methane fluxes16, offer natural settings to isolate the role of methane transformation in OC preservation within marine sediments. Moreover, observations of intermittent methane emissions from the seafloor suggest that methane continues to migrate across the sediment–water interface over extensive areas14,15. Therefore, cold seeps serve as critical windows for investigating the mechanisms and extent of methane-derived OC preservation.

The surface sediment layer acts as a critical control point, regulating benthic methane release and serving as the final barrier preventing seafloor methane from entering the water column13. Investigating methane transformation in surface seep sediments is therefore essential—not only for understanding methane-driven OC preservation, but also for assessing whether this carbon pool may ultimately contribute to atmospheric methane. This knowledge gap is particularly evident in deep-sea cold seep systems, which act as “distributed micro-valves” modulating global seabed methane emissions. However, limited attention has been paid to the quantitative contribution and preservation efficiency of methane-derived OC in marine surface seepage sediments, particularly regarding its impact on global OC sequestration.

In this study, we employ machine learning in conjunction with statistical analysis to investigate the potential global prevalence of methane-converted OC. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the preservation of methane-converted OC, we examine sediments collected from extreme environments of methane seepage in a series of cold seep habitats located in the northern South China Sea, where significant methane release from underlying sediments has been documented16,17. Temperature-ramped analyses, alongside stable and radiocarbon isotope measurements (δ13C and Δ14C), are conducted on bulk and specific-grain-size sediments to identify and estimate the contribution of methane. Finally, we focus specifically on surface sediments, assessing the activation energy of OC and organo-mineral associations across different grain size fractions to explore the key processes governing the preservation of methane-derived OC.

Results and Discussion

Global prevalence of methane-derived OC preservation

To assess the global impact of methane transformation on OC burial, we compile literature data on global SMT depths, OC contents, and Δ14C values in marine surface sediments. Based on these datasets, we generate global distribution maps and perform regression analyses (Fig. 1a–c). We find the increased OC contents and negative Δ14C signatures in surface sediments with SMT depth decreases (Fig. 1d–f). Given that methane derived from subseafloor is of ancient origin (old 14C age, depleted in 14C)18, this negative Δ14C bias with shallowing SMT depth points to the incorporation of methane-derived carbon into deep-sea surface sedimentary OC.

Furthermore, the increased OC content shown in Fig. 1e implies that methane transformation contributes not only 14C-old carbon but also additional OC burial in surface sediments. These results may provide independent evidence for the contribution of deep-sourced ancient methane-derived carbon to the deep-sea surface sediments, suggesting that the upward migration of the methane barrier enhances OC burial. On a global scale, methane migrating upward from deep sediments may thus represent an important component of the OC pool in surface marine sediments, shaping both its content and 14C isotopic composition.

Given that the preservation of deep-sourced methane-derived OC in surface sediments appears to be a worldwide phenomenon, our finding may provide a unified explanation for the ubiquitous observation of elevated OC contents and aged 14C ages in marine surface sediments, whose features are traditionally attributed primarily to terrestrial inputs and reworked marine OC19. Our results suggest that methane-derived OC burial may exert far-reaching influences on global scales, including in regions where active methane seepage is not detectable, yet this phenomenon is more pronounced at active seep sites. The methane transformation and OC preservation thus likely represent a pervasive process operating across the seafloor with varying intensities.

In this context, cold seeps with intensive methane emissions, often underlain by dissociating hydrates that release 14C-depleted (“14C-dead”) methane18, represent end-member environments where methane-derived carbon contributions are most pronounced. Given their global distribution and high emission fluxes, seepage systems warrant particular attention when assessing the role of methane-derived OC in marine carbon cycling.

More 14C-depleted carbon signatures in seepage top sediments

To further investigate the signatures of methane-derived OC preservation in deep-sea extreme systems, we collect push cores from six distinct benthic seepage habitats in the South China Sea using a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) and analyze carbon isotopic compositions. These cores encompass a range of typical cold seep settings: sediments colonized by mussels around active methane seeps (Fig. 2c1–d1, sites #02 and #15), sediments covered by bacterial mats (Fig. 2a1–b1, sites #04 and #07), sediments adjacent to authigenic carbonate precipitates, and those covered by dead mussel shells (Fig. 2e1–f1, sites #06 and #05). These habitats represent different development stages of methane seepage (active, developmental, and recessional stages, respectively), providing a natural chronosequence to examine how methane-derived OC preservation evolves over the lifespan of a seep system.

We find that the most pronounced methane signals occur at sites #02 and #15, where surface sediments exhibit the highest OC contents ( > 2.0%) and the most depleted Δ14C values (the oldest 14C ages: 9970 ± 50 and 9200 ± 40 yr BP) among all samples (Fig. 2c2–d2). These Δ14C values are substantially more negative than those of marine sinking particulate OC (POC) in the South China Sea (Δ14C values > –600‰ and 14C ages <7000 yr)20, indicating a significant contribution of ancient methane-derived carbon. In contrast, sediments from developmental and recessional stages show relatively consistent or slightly decreasing OC contents with depth, accompanied by a progressive downcore decline in Δ14C values (Fig. 2a2–b2, e2–f2), which is consistent with steady depositional conditions and OC degradation in non-extreme deep-sea sediments21. Notably, while Δ14C generally decreases with depth in these non-active stages, the most depleted values at active methane seepage stages appear in surface sediments rather than deeper layers. These evidence further underscores the role of active methane emission in delivering 14C-depleted carbon to the seafloor surface.

Building on this evidence, our findings further reveal that the incorporation of methane-derived OC into surface sediments is dynamically linked to the evolution of methane emissions, specifically, to the intensity and history of methane emissions. Methane ascending from deeper sediments is characterized by near-dead 14C signatures (Δ14C ≈ −999‰)18, and its transformation into OC is most pronounced at active methane seepage stages, where surface sediments exhibit the strongest 14C depletion and highest OC contents (Fig. 2c2–d2). In contrast, sediments from recessional stages show markedly lower OC contents and Δ14C values that are substantially less depleted, indicating that the methane-derived carbon signal is largely absent once active seepage ceases. This stark contrast between active and non-active stages points to a critical distinction: the efficient preservation of methane-derived OC is not a ubiquitous feature of cold seeps, but rather a phenomenon tightly coupled to intensive methane seepage. Understanding the mechanisms that facilitate enhanced preservation during intensive methane seepage is crucial. Such insights may be pivotal in deciphering the often subtle and challenging-to-detect imprint of methane-derived OC preservation on a global scale.

Methane-derived OC preservation in the surface sediments

Elucidating the preservation mechanisms of methane-derived OC will advance our understanding of how seafloor methane emissions modulate the marine carbon cycle and potentially drive climate feedbacks on geological timescales. Here, we employ ramped pyrolysis/oxidation (RPO) to characterize the thermogram patterns and activation energy distributions of OC in those sediments. Our results reveal that intensive methane seepage sites (sites #02 and #15) exhibit distinct carbon dioxide (CO2) thermograms (Fig. 2c3−d3), and activation energy distributions (Fig. 2c4−d4), compared to other sites. Specifically, the surface sediments (1 cm) from sites #02 and #15 show a pronounced high-temperature CO2 peak that is absent in deeper layers, suggesting a greater abundance of refractory organic matter components and higher thermal activation energy in the surface carbon pool. In contrast, the thermogram shapes and activation energy patterns at developmental (sites #04, #07) and recessional (sites #06, #05) stages remain largely invariant with sediment depth. These results imply that active methane seepage not only delivers ancient carbon to the surface sediments but also facilitates the preferential preservation of methane-derived OC in a more refractory form, likely enhancing its potential for long-term burial in surface sediments.

We decompose the CO2 thermograms from surface sediments of different methane seepage stages using a Gaussian distribution model (F1, F2, F3; Fig. 3a). The thermograms from developmental (sites #04, #07) and recessional (sites #06, #05) stages can be deconvolved into three components centered at 370 °C (F1), 550 °C (F2), and 750 °C (F3; Fig. 3a). In contrast, active stages (sites #02, #15) exhibit a distinct thermogram characterized by a sharp peak centered at 480 °C (Fig. 3a). The contribution of high-temperature OC components (PCH4-C = (F2 + F3)/(F1 + F2 + F3)×100%) is substantially larger at active sites than at other sites, coinciding with the oldest bulk OC 14C ages observed in their surface sediments (Fig. 3b), which indicates that the methane-derived OC preserved in intensive methane seepage sediments is composed of refractory, energy-intensive forms that require higher thermal energy for degradation. Collectively, our results reveal a coherent link between CO2 thermogram patterns and OC 14C ages across methane seepage evolutionary stages, suggesting that the enhanced preservation efficiency of methane-derived OC, particularly during active seepage, is tightly coupled to the intensity of ancient methane fixation in surface sediments. These findings demonstrate that active methane emission not only delivers ancient carbon to the seafloor but also promotes its selective preservation in a form resistant to diagenetic alteration.

We further employ RPO coupled with in-depth carbon isotopic analyses both on the surface (1 cm) and deeper (5 cm) sediments at active methane seepage sites (Fig. 4). Our results show that the δ13C values of each thermal fraction (T1-5) in surface sediments (from −21.0‰ to −26.6‰) are significantly more negative than those in deeper sediments (from −20.1‰ to −23.8‰, Fig. 4a, b). In addition, lower Δ14C values of each thermal fraction also appear in the surface sediments rather than in the deeper layer (Fig. 4a, b). These isotopic signatures of the thermal intervals from deep to surface sediments converge towards the methane end-member (Fig. 4c, arrow), demonstrating the presence and accumulation of methane-derived OC in surface sediments. This finding is also consistent with 14C analyses of bulk sediments (Fig. 3b).

Furthermore, the most depleted Δ14C signatures in surface sediments occur in the higher-temperature CO2 fractions (T3–T5; Fig. 4a, b), which correspond to the F2 + F3 Gaussian components (Fig. 3a). As the higher-temperature CO2 release is typically linked to more refractory organic matter22, the association between Δ14C signatures and higher-temperature CO2 fractions indicates not only the incorporation of ancient methane-derived carbon, but also its stable preservation in surface sediments. Quantitative estimations based on 14C mass balance reveal that methane-derived carbon accounts for 63% and 59% of the OC in surface sediments at the intensive methane seepage sites (#02 and #15), respectively, substantially higher than in deeper intervals (14% to 10%; Supplementary Table 1). These calculated proportions (mean 61 ± 2%) at sites #02 and #15 are consistent with those independently derived from Gaussian decomposition (PCH4-C = 62 ± 10%), corroborating that the F2 + F3 components (high-temperature components) are dominated by methane-derived OC (Fig. 3a). Together, these thermochemical and isotopic evidence demonstrate that methane-derived OC accumulating in surface sediments of intensive methane seepage regions has both ancient and refractory properties that confer enhanced preservation potential and underlie its efficient retention at the seafloor.

Grain size-specific preservation of methane-derived OC

To disentangle the influence of grain size on OC preservation, we measure RPO and the isotopic compositions of OC across different grain size fractions at active sites #02 and #15 (See Methods). RPO thermograms of the different grain-size fractions reveal a distinct pattern: surface sediments (1 cm) are dominated by high-temperature peaks, whereas deeper sediments (5 cm) exhibit flattened distributions, particularly in the coarse fraction (63−250 µm and >250 µm; Fig. 5a, b). Compared to fine fractions, the enhanced thermochemical stability and higher activation energy of coarse fractions (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicate that methane-derived OC is predominantly preserved in coarse fractions with a more refractory form. This inherently higher resistance to degradation23,24, likely favors long-term preservation of methane-derived OC in surface sediments. In the meantime, the coarse fractions in surface sediments exhibit the highest OC contents (Supplementary Fig. 2). This pattern contrasts with typical marine sediments, where fine fractions generally host higher OC content25. Notably, these coarse fractions also display the most depleted δ13C and Δ14C values, which are substantially lower than those in deeper layers (Fig. 5c, d), suggesting preferential accumulation of methane-derived OC in coarse fractions in the surface sediment.

In aerobic surface sediments, methanotrophic communities assimilate methane for growth, producing biomass and microbial by-products that adhere to mineral surfaces26,27. While microbial-derived carbon may be conventionally regarded as a relatively labile fraction, its incorporation into coarse sediments can enhance long-term preservation of methane-derived OC. Critically, the surface enrichment of ancient carbon (Fig. 2c2−d2), is strongly associated with aerobic methanotrophs (function gene: pmoA) rather than anaerobic methanotrophs (function gene: mcrA; Supplementary Fig. 3), implicating aerobic methane oxidation (AeOM) as the important driver of surface OC accumulation. Aerobic methanotrophs assimilate methane-derived carbon at faster rates than their anaerobic counterparts28, efficiently converting it into biomass that contributes directly to sedimentary OC. In contrast, anaerobic methanotrophs are slow growers10, with most transformed methane carbon partitioned into inorganic pools. This functional divergence is reflected in gene abundances: mcrA represents only 1.2% and 38.5% of pmoA abundance in surface sediments at sites #02 and #15, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). This microbial activity may promote the agglomeration of sediment particles into coarse-grained aggregates.

Within these aggregates, microbial colonization and extracellular polymeric substances may modify fine-scale aggregate morphology29, and promote the development of complex pore networks and tortuous internal pathways30, as illustrated by contrasting pore structures between less-colonized (Fig. 6a) and heavily colonized aggregates (Fig. 6b). These architectures physically influence solute transport within aggregates31, thereby potentially restricting oxygen diffusion and oxidative attack, while aerobic methanotrophic activity within coarse-grained aggregates may further reduce oxygen availability, creating steep redox gradients between exterior and interior.

Superimposed on this physical occlusion, multiple complementary mechanisms may operate in concert. First, microfossil biomineral matrices—abundant in coarse fractions (Supplementary Fig. 4)—exhibit rich pore structures that increase specific surface area32 (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. 5a−b), providing sorption sites for ancient, refractory methane-derived OC. Second, microbial by-products facilitate the accumulation of proteinaceous functional groups (e.g., amide and carboxylic carbon)33, necessitating greater energy to break these bonds compared to non-methane-affected sediments. Third, microbial transformation of OC into cellular biomass may involve chemical modifications that enhance organic matter resistance to decomposition34,35.

This multi-faceted protection mechanism is supported by OC/surface area (SA) ratios in coarse fractions, which substantially exceed the range expected from simple mineral-surface sorption models (OC loading ratios32: 0.4–1.0 mg C/m2; Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Notably, while SA does not increase from the 63–250 µm to the >250 µm fraction, OC/SA rises sharply. This decoupling implicates that pore occlusion, rather than adsorption alone, as the dominant preservation mechanism. Steep redox gradients between the aggregate exterior and interior, sustained by aerobic methanotrophic activity, reinforce the aggregates as self-protective micro-environments, enabling methane-derived OC to persist in aerobic surface sediments that would otherwise favor its oxidation. Larger aggregates with greater internal volume for such shielding correspondingly host a higher proportion of ancient OC. Collectively, these analyses establish physical occlusion within sedimentary aggregates as the important mechanism enabling methane-derived OC to persist in aerobic surface sediments—a finding that raises further questions about the long-term fate of this carbon pool during burial and diagenesis.

A methane-derived carbon sink on continental slopes

To constrain the significance of this process, we employ a two end-member model to estimate methane-derived OC contributions (See Methods). At the local scale, the active Haima cold seep in the South China Sea preserves approximately 52 t C/yr of methane-derived OC in surface sediments, which is substantially higher than in deeper sediments (11 t C/yr) and comparable to reported methane emission fluxes from the northern US Atlantic margin (11–68 t C/yr)15. These estimates demonstrate that methane-derived OC preservation is already a non-negligible process at the scale of individual seep systems, pointing to its potentially larger role across methane-prone continental margins.

Our global synthesis demonstrates that the preservation of methane-derived OC in surface sediments is not a regional anomaly but a widespread phenomenon across diverse marine environments (Fig. 1). On global continental slopes, where vast methane hydrate reservoirs are highly susceptible to dissociation, this process reaches its quantitative peak: up to 6.1 Tg C/yr of methane-derived OC could be preserved in surface sediments. This estimate accounts for over 12.2% of the methane-derived carbon emitted from global seafloor sediments36 and represents 1.8–4.3% of the total OC accumulation in global marine sediments28,37. These findings reveal that methane-derived OC preservation is not merely a localized curiosity but a quantitatively significant component of the global carbon budget.

From subsurface sequestration to surface accumulation: old methane-derived OC in deep-sea surface sediments

This preservation manifests through two fundamentally different regimes, distinguished by the depth of the SMT zone and its interplay with methane flux intensity (Fig. 7). In settings where the SMT resides at depth, representative of non- or inactive seepage systems, anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) drives carbonate precipitation near the SMT zone38 and the physical encapsulation of organic matter within carbonate matrices39. Methane-derived OC is thus primarily sequestered at depth, with limited upward accumulation into surface sediments. Above the SMT, the OC pool is dominated by settling material from the upper water column, resulting in progressively increasing 14C ages with depth toward the SMT (Fig. 7a).

Conceptual framework illustrating the role of sulfate-methane transition zone (SMTZ) in regulating the preservation of methane-derived OC in marine sediments.

a In the deeper SMTZ, methane-derived OC is primarily preserved through anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM), driving the physical encapsulation of organic matter and carbonate-bound OC precipitation near the SMTZ, which results in progressively older OC 14C ages (14Corg age) with increasing depth toward the SMTZ. b In the shallow SMTZ, aerobic oxidation of methane (AeOM) supplants AOM as the dominant transformation pathway near the surface sediment. The coupled action of AeOM, well-packed organo-mineral aggregates, and complex pore structure enables efficient transformation and preservation of deep-sourced methane into surface OC pools, resulting in older 14Corg age in the surface sediments.

In stark contrast, at active seep sites where the SMT zone shoals toward the seafloor, a fundamentally different preservation regime emerges (Fig. 7b). Here, the flux of deep-sourced methane to surface sediments increases dramatically, enabling AeOM to supplant AOM as the dominant transformation pathway near the surface sediment40. Aerobic methanotrophs proliferate at the sediment–water interface, secreting abundant extracellular polymeric substances that bind mineral grains, microbial cells, and organic matter into large organo-mineral aggregates. These aggregates develop complex, fine-pored, and highly tortuous internal structures that limit oxygen penetration and inhibit oxidative degradation41, resulting in anomalously old 14C ages in surface sediments (Fig. 7b)—a finding with important implications for understanding OC aging in marine environments.

Together, these processes define a continuum of methane-derived OC preservation controlled by SMT depth: from subsurface sequestration in AOM-dominated systems to surface accumulation in active seep environments driven by AeOM. In active seeps, the coupled action of AeOM, aggregate formation, internal redox zonation, and carbonate-mediated protection enables efficient transformation of deep-sourced methane into stable surface OC pools. Active seep sites thus emerge as global hotspots for methane-derived OC preservation, revealing that the sediment–water interface, rather than the deep SMT zone alone, can serve as the primary locus of methane-derived carbon burial when methane fluxes are sufficiently high (Fig. 7).

Our findings provide biogeochemical evidence that microbial fixation, chemical modification, and physical protection act in concert to promote the preservation of methane-derived OC. This mechanism has far-reaching implications: by sequestering methane-derived OC in surface sediments, this preservation pathway moderates its release into the hydrosphere, thereby reducing CO2 production from AeOM in the water column and potentially mitigating ocean acidification42,43. Given the ubiquitous distribution of aerobic methanotrophs in sediments, analogous transformation and preservation of microbial-mediated methane-derived OC may occur in other methane-active environments, including terrestrial wetlands44. While our findings demonstrate that SMT shoaling and methane-derived carbon contribute to elevated OC contents and aged Δ14C signatures in surface sediments, this interpretation does not invalidate traditional views. It is well established that pre-aged terrestrial inputs and reworked marine organic matter also play roles in shaping sedimentary OC ages across many settings19. Nevertheless, the two mechanisms—methane-derived carbon preservation versus external supply of old carbon—are not mutually exclusive. They operate concurrently, with their relative importance varying regionally. While aged carbon from terrestrial or reworked carbon sources remains important, the key advance of our study is the discovery of a large-scale spatial control mechanism, the depth of the sulfate-methane transition zone, that subtly but significantly governs the preservation of newly generated methane-derived old carbon across global continental margins, offering a process-based explanation beyond existing views.

The efficient preservation of methane-derived OC in surface sediments thus buffers methane emissions, facilitates deep-sourced carbon burial at the sediment–water interface, and may exert a non-negligible influence on global climate regulation over geological timescales. By transforming a potent greenhouse gas into a stable sedimentary carbon pool, this previously overlooked pathway adds a dimension to our understanding of the marine carbon cycle and its feedbacks to climate change.

Methods

Sampling

During the R/V Haiyang VI and Dongfanghong III scientific expeditions in 2020 and 2022, push cores from six submarine habitats (#02, #04, #05, #06, #07, #15) were retrieved from the active Haima (16˚50΄ N, 110˚30΄ E) and Site F cold seeps (22˚07΄ N, 119˚17΄ E) located on the northern slope of the South China Sea. The cores were collected by manipulator arms of the ROV, and the submarine seepage landscapes were simultaneously observed and recorded when sampling (Fig. 2a1−f1). The sediment cores were sectioned at 1-cm intervals and then frozen at −20 °C until further processing. Sediments collected from the inner cores were stored in sterile tubes for identifying microbial gene abundance.

Sediment sieving

In order to excavate the mechanisms of methane-derived OC preservation at a higher resolution, sediments from the surface (1 cm) and deeper (5 cm) layers at active sites #02 and #15 were selected for separation and geochemical characterization analyses of different grain size fractions. The samples were dry-sieved using a Sonic Sifter Separator (Model L3P-25, Advantech Manufacturing, INC, USA) into five grain size fractions ( <20, 20–32, 32–63, 63–250, and >250 μm). The fractionated samples were stored in pre-combusted (450 °C, 5 h) glass vials for analysis of OC content, δ13C, and Δ14C, and RPO.

Global data analysis

Data compilation, pre-processing of Δ14C and OC content

We collected the available Δ14C data and corresponding location information of marine sediments worldwide from the MOSAIC database (version 2.0)45. In addition, we integrated OC content data that is stored in the Pangaea46 (Pangaea dataset DOI: 10.1594/PANGAEA.733692) and MOSAIC (version 2.0) databases45 (Supplementary Dataset S1). We then applied preprocessing procedures to ensure the validity and reliability of the data. Firstly, we excluded records containing invalid values (e.g., non-numeric entries or OC values outside a physically reasonable range) and removed records with missing key variables (e.g., OC or water depth) to ensure the completeness and reliability of the dataset. Secondly, we selected OC content and Δ14C data from depths <5 cm below the seafloor (labeled as top sediments in subsequent descriptions), as sediments in this depth range were considered to have a significant methane signature in our study. Thirdly, we selected OC content and Δ14C data distributed within water depth >700 m to minimize the influence of terrestrial-sourced organic matter inputs, particularly in the context of potential methane seepage47. Fourthly, in cases where multiple measurements were available at a given geographic location, we calculated the average value of these measurements to ensure only one OC content or Δ14C data at each geographic location. The obtained OC content data after pre-processing included 4918 records and was presented in Supplementary Fig. 6a.

OC content prediction

In order to obtain a more comprehensive OC content distribution on a global scale, we predicted OC content using the Random Forest (RF) machine learning method. RF is an ensemble learning algorithm that constructs multiple regression trees and makes predictions by calculating the mean response across individual trees, thereby improving model stability and predictive accuracy48. To train the model, we collected several relevant environmental variables and examined their relationships with OC content. These variables included temperature, chlorophyll concentration, and oxygen concentration of surface seawater, water depth, salinity, and other factors (Supplementary Dataset S2), which were considered to play important roles in reflecting key characteristics of the sedimentary environment and variation of OC content49.

To obtain a more reliable estimate of spatial predictive performance, we adopted a k-fold cross-validation strategy based on nearest neighbors using the knndm() function in the CAST package in R50–52 among the available validation strategies53,54. This approach constructs spatial folds by matching the nearest-neighbor distance structure between the training–testing samples and the prediction domain, thereby providing a more realistic validation framework for spatial prediction. We optimized the model by adjusting key parameters, such as the number of trees (500) and maximum depth of trees (100), within the RF algorithm. Experimental results show that the RF model with random cross-validation achieved a prediction accuracy (R2) of 51% on the testing sets (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The validation of the global applicability of predicted OC contents was detailed in Supplementary Text 1 and Supplementary Figs 6–9.

The sedimentation rate used for this calculation was constructed using a depth-dependent approach. In regions with water depths of 700-2000 m, sedimentation rates were estimated using the empirical model55, which predicted sedimentation rate from water depth, distance to coast, and sea-surface POC. In regions deeper than 2000 m, the sedimentation rate was referred from the global sedimentation-rate dataset56, which was based on a K-nearest-neighbor (KNN) machine-learning approach trained on a large compilation of measured sediment accumulation rates. Importantly, this dataset provided per-cell uncertainty estimates, allowing us to explicitly quantify the propagation of uncertainty into SMT depth (Supplementary Fig. 10). These two components were then merged into a unified global sedimentation-rate field, which served as the basis for subsequent SMT-depth estimation.

Global distributions of OC content and SMT depth

To achieve a globally visualized distribution of OC content, we initially projected the pre-processed OC content data points onto global raster maps. A global raster map was constructed with latitude and longitude as the coordinate axes, segmenting the Earth’s surface into uniform grid cells, each representing a specific spatial scale. By calculating the Euclidean distance between the measured data points and the grid network based on their latitude and longitude, we mapped the collected data points to the corresponding grid cells. Each grid cell was assigned an OC content value. In cases where a grid cell contained multiple data values, we calculated the average to determine a single valid value for that grid cell. Additionally, the predicted OC contents were simultaneously projected onto this map. This methodology effectively integrated both collected and predicted OC content data, resulting in a continuous dataset with global spatial coverage. A separate global grid map was also constructed for the estimated SMT depths. The global distribution maps for OC content and SMT depth in top marine sediments were shown (Fig. 1a–b, Supplementary Datasets S3 and S4).

To evaluate the impact of spatial resolution on our simulation results, we performed global raster projections of OC content and SMT depth at three different resolutions: 1°×1° (180×360 grids), 0.5° × 0.5° (360 × 720 grids), and 0.25° × 0.25° (720 × 1440 grids). The correlations between SMT depth and OC content were found to be remarkably consistent across all three resolutions, indicating that variations in data resolution do not affect our conclusions. Therefore, we chose the results at the highest spatial resolution to present in Fig. 1, while results at the other resolutions were included in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Statistical analyses

To investigate the correlation between the SMT distribution depth and OC content globally, we first paired the OC content values (Fig. 1a) and SMT data (Fig. 1b) based on the same latitude and longitude information. We then divided the SMT depth at 15 m-interval within 0–400 m (Supplementary Fig. 12). Owing to the absence of matched SMT and OC data at depths greater than 405 m, the analysis between SMT depth and OC is therefore restricted to the 0-405 m depth range. We calculated the percentage of grids with higher OC content (assumed to be >1%) within each 15 m interval (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, we calculated the mean SMT distribution depth and mean OC content within each interval and fitted their relationship (Fig. 1e).

Similarly, to excavate the relationship between SMT depth and the Δ14C values of OC, we first paired the SMT data and Δ14C values (data from regions with water depths greater than 700 m, Supplementary Dataset S5) according to their latitude and longitude information. Given the scarcity of matched Δ14C and SMT depth data pairs at depths exceeding 100 m, the analysis of SMT depth and Δ14C was confined to the 0–100 m depth range. The data pairs were then grouped into 15-m intervals according to SMT depth, and the median SMT depth and Δ14C value were calculated for each interval. The pretreatment methodologies of sediments used for OC analyses were reported previously57. Briefly, the sediments were freeze-dried and then finely ground. RPO coupled OC isotopic analysis

Programmed thermal decomposition was performed on sediments to separate different organic matter components based on their thermochemical stability differences. Prior to measurements, the sediments were subjected to HCl rinsing treatment to remove IC, following the detailed procedures described in the “OC content and isotopic analysis” section. Approximately 5–8 mg of IC-removed sediments were loaded into a quartz sample reactor with a quartz filter. After purging the oven with helium, the oven temperature was ramped from room temperature to 900 °C at a constant heating rate of 5 °C/min65. Carbonaceous organic compounds and pyrolysis products desorbed during the ramp-up process were quantitatively converted to CO2, which was subsequently removed from the oxidizing reactor with a carrier stream of helium and measured for concentration.

To quantitatively compare the stability of organic matter across sediment samples, we calculated the E based on the evolved CO2 thermograms as described previously66. This calculation selected ω = 1010 s‒1 as the Arrhenius pre-exponential factor for all samples, and was conducted in Python programming language v.3.6 by the ‘rampedpyrox’ package (See reference for detailed description)66. Furthermore, we isolated the evolved CO2 thermograms from surface sediment pyrolysis into three components (F1, F2, F3) using a first-order Gaussian distribution model. This method can be used to identify the composition of multi-source organic matter and was successfully applied to determine sediment sources67.

The CO2 thermograms from the decomposition of volatilized components in surface (1 cm) and deep (5 cm) sediments at #02 and #15 sites were integrated into five temperature windows (T1: 80 − 320 °C; T2: 320 − 391 °C; T3: 391 − 484 °C; T4: 484 − 570 °C; T5: 570 − 900 °C)22. The evolved CO2 gas generated from each thermal interval was trapped for further carbon isotopic (δ13C and Δ14C) measurements on the AMS at NOSAMS (Supplementary Table 3).

Assuming the presence of tens of thousands of active seeps covering approximately 2.05×104 km2 globally4, we estimated that around 0.003 Tg C/yr of methane-derived OC may be preserved in surface sediments at these seeps. Furthermore, given the widespread distribution of methane hydrates on continental slopes, where hydrates are prone to dissociate and release methane, we estimated that approximately 6.1 Tg C/yr of methane-derived OC may be preserved in surface sediments of the global continental slopes covering around 41 million km2.

Three-dimensional (3D) structure acquisition and pore network quantification

The three-dimensional (3D) internal structures of organo–mineral aggregates were characterized using X-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), following previously established procedures for sample collection, wet preservation, scanning, and image analysis29,70. Two representative organomineral aggregates that were collected from surface sediments in the Tay estuary, Scotland, were represented herein29. To preserve the hydrated internal architecture and minimize structural artefacts associated with dehydration, resin embedding or drying, we applied wet-scanning aggregates adapted from previous work70. Prior to scanning, individual aggregates from marine surface sediments were isolated and transferred into sealed containers suitable for wet micro-CT imaging29. Scans were acquired at a voxel size of 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 μm3, which enabled submicron-scale visualization of aggregate interiors, including mineral particles, organic-rich domains, and internal pore space. Raw projection data were reconstructed into 3D image stacks using the standard reconstruction workflow70. The reconstructed volumes were then cropped to retain the target aggregate and processed for phase segmentation, allowing the distinction of aggregate solids from pore space in 3D.

Pore networks were subsequently extracted and quantified following the protocols detailed in Lawrence et al.71. Pore networks were classified into pores and throats using a skeletonization algorithm72, in which individual pore segments were identified from the skeleton, and throats were defined as the connections between adjacent pores. The analysis was performed directly on intact 3D volumes; it thus preserved the spatial relationships between pore domains and surrounding organo–mineral matrices, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for evaluating physical protection of organic matter within aggregate interiors.
