Pupil size as a sensitive marker of acute stress dynamics
Authors: Geoffrey Millour, Alexandre Coste, Ludovic Molle, Romuald Lepers, Christophe Hausswirth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to investigate pupil size as a sensitive and immediate marker of acute stress, by analyzing its evolution in response to two different stress-inducing cognitive tasks aiming to elicit distinct stress responses: a traditional Stroop task and a socially evaluative competitive Stroop task. Thirty participants were randomly assigned to either the traditional (G-trad, N = 14) or the competitive (G-comp, N = 16) Stroop groups. Stress-related measures—including pupil dilation, heart rate variability (RMSSD and SDNN), respiratory rate, blood pressure, salivary cortisol and α-amylase levels, and perceived stress (visual analog scale)—were recorded at baseline and during the 20-min recovery period. In addition, pupillary, cardiac, and respiratory responses were continuously recorded throughout the stress-inducing task, while perceived stress was assessed between task blocks. Significant increases in pupil dilation and perceived stress were observed only in G-comp during the task. Salivary cortisol increased significantly in G-comp after the Stroop, while α-amylase increased in both groups, though more markedly in G-comp. In this group, both cortisol and α-amylase exhibited a delayed peak, occurring 10 min post-task, before partially declining, reflecting a transient but intense hormonal stress response. No significant difference between the groups was found for RMSSD, SDNN, respiratory rate and blood pressure. Overall, pupil dilation was the only physiological measure recorded during task performance that differentiated between the traditional and competitive conditions. By contrast, hormonal markers exhibited delayed responses, while the other physiological measures failed to discriminate between conditions, supporting pupillometry as a non-invasive, real-time indicator of acute stress dynamics.Keywords: Social evaluation, Sympathetic nervous system, Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, Mydriasis, Hormonal responses

Introduction

Stress is a fundamental component of human adaptation, enabling individuals to respond effectively to environmental challenges. Importantly, stress does not uniformly impair performance; its effects depend on its intensity, duration, and context. Moderate acute stress can enhance alertness and cognitive efficiency, consistent with the inverted U-shaped relationship between arousal and performance described by the Yerkes–Dodson law [1]. In contrast, excessive stress, particularly in socially evaluative contexts such as examinations or competitive situations, can overwhelm cognitive resources and degrade performance, leading to increased error rates and impaired decision-making [2,3]. Stress can be categorized as either acute or chronic: acute stress refers to the body's immediate response to a specific stressor, typically short-lived and adaptive, while chronic stress arises from prolonged exposure to a persistent stressor or from a failure to return to a homeostatic state, as observed in individuals experiencing long-term traumatic stress [4]. The accurate assessment of acute stress is therefore crucial in high-stakes environments where optimal performance and rapid decision-making are required. In the medical field, healthcare professionals frequently face acute stress during emergency procedures, which can impair key cognitive functions [5]. Similarly, in aviation, pilots are exposed to time-critical and high-pressure situations where stress can lead to performance degradation and increased error rates [6]. Monitoring acute stress responses in these contexts is therefore essential not only to ensure safety and performance, but also to implement timely stress management interventions [7]. Given its immediate impact on psychophysiological functioning, quantifying acute stress through physiological measures may provide a valuable tool to enhance performance, reduce errors, and protect health in demanding professions [4,8].

Stressful situations, particularly those involving cognitive demands, trigger two key physiological systems: the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS). The HPA axis regulates the secretion of cortisol, a glucocorticoid essential for managing prolonged stress and restoring homeostasis [2]. Cortisol remains the gold-standard biomarker of stress responses and systemic regulation [9,10]. SNS activation triggers rapid autonomic adjustments, including increased heart rate (HR), reduced heart rate variability (HRV) [11,12], and elevated respiratory rate [13,14]. It also induces pupillary dilation (mydriasis), a fast-reacting indicator of autonomic arousal and cognitive effort [[15], [16], [17]]. In parallel, levels of salivary α-amylase rise in response to adrenergic stimulation, providing a non-invasive biomarker of sympathetic activation [[18], [19], [20]]. However, cortisol and α-amylase responses appear with a time lag, making them unsuitable for real-time stress monitoring. While cardiorespiratory parameters are more immediate, they may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle differences in stress intensity [21]. In this context, pupillometry represents a particularly promising approach for capturing rapid and subtle variations in stress responses [22]. Pupil size is closely linked to activity in the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system—a key regulator of attention, arousal, and stress responses [17]. LC-NE activation enhances norepinephrine release, suppresses parasympathetic control, and results in pupil expansion [15]. This response reflects both cognitive and emotional load and offers continuous, non-invasive measurement of neurophysiological reactivity [23]. Consistent with this view, previous studies have shown that pupillary responses are sensitive not only to cognitive demand, but also to emotional and social modulation of stress responses. For example, pupillometry has been shown to reflect variations in emotional stress reactivity during socially supportive or emotionally salient situations [24,25], further supporting its relevance as a dynamic index of cognitive-emotional arousal. Importantly, the LC-NE system is closely interconnected with the HPA axis and contributes to the coordination of rapid autonomic and endocrine stress responses. While LC-NE activation supports immediate vigilance and arousal adjustments, cortisol release reflects a slower neuroendocrine cascade involved in stress adaptation and homeostatic regulation [26,27]. Yet, it remains to be determined whether pupillary responses can reliably discriminate slightly different stress conditions, especially when the cognitive task remains constant and only the emotional or motivational context changes by adding social-evaluative pressure.

In laboratory settings, acute stress is commonly elicited using cognitively demanding tasks such as the Stroop task [28], which engage attentional control and executive functions [4]. These paradigms offer the advantage of precisely controlling task difficulty while enabling the assessment of physiological responses during task performance. Previous studies have shown that pupil dilation reliably reflects cognitive load and attentional conflict during Stroop task execution. Increased pupil size has consistently been observed during incongruent Stroop conditions, reflecting the greater executive and attentional demands associated with conflict resolution [29,30]. In addition, pupillometry has been shown to capture rapid modulation of autonomic arousal during Stroop performance depending on the emotional or social context, including stress-buffering effects induced by social support [24]. However, cognitive tasks alone generally induce only moderate stress responses and may not be sufficient to robustly activate the HPA axis. In contrast, stressors involving a social-evaluative component, such as the Trier Social Stress Test, elicit stronger endocrine and autonomic responses [10], highlighting the critical role of social evaluation in stress reactivity. Recent work further suggests that introducing competitive or socially evaluative elements into the Stroop task, while preserving identical cognitive demands, can significantly intensify stress responses through the combined activation of the SNS and HPA axis [31]. Competition, in particular, introduces a psychological threat to self-worth or social status, thereby amplifying emotional and physiological reactivity [2].

The main objective of this study was to investigate whether pupil dilation can serve as a sensitive and reliable marker of acute stress, by comparing responses to two cognitively identical Stroop tasks, one presented in a neutral context and the other incorporating a competitive dimension. We hypothesized that the competitive condition would elicit a stronger overall stress response across multiple physiological and subjective domains. Specifically, we expected pupillary responses to show the most immediate and condition-sensitive modulation during task performance, reflecting rapid autonomic reactivity. Subjective stress ratings were expected to be higher in the competitive condition. Cardiovascular and respiratory measures were expected to reflect task-related stress responses in both conditions but to provide less discrimination between conditions, whereas endocrine markers (salivary cortisol and α-amylase) were expected to exhibit delayed stress-related changes across the experimental timeline.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty individuals (18 females and 12 males) were recruited for the study (mean ± standard deviation [SD]: age 44 ± 12 years; weight 73.1 ± 16.2 kg; height 1.70 ± 0.09 m; BMI 25.0 ± 4.6 kg/m2). Inclusion criteria required participants to be between 18 and 70 years old, and to be in good general health without any history of cardiovascular, neurological, or psychiatric disorders. Participants were also required to have normal or corrected-to-normal vision to ensure accurate performance on a computerized Stroop task. Exclusion criteria included regular use of medications affecting the cardiovascular or endocrine systems, current smoking, pregnancy, and participation in other clinical trials or stress-related studies within the last three months. Additionally, individuals with a history of color blindness or any condition affecting cognitive performance (e.g., attention deficit disorder) were not included. Participants were recruited from an existing database of potential participants, which included approximately 400 individuals who had previously expressed interest in taking part in scientific studies. All volunteers were screened via an initial online questionnaire to determine eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Those who met the criteria were invited for an in-person visit to confirm eligibility and complete the informed consent process. All participants were informed about the study's objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits. They provided written informed consent before participating. The study protocol was approved by the National Ethics Committee (CER STAPS) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (ethical approval: IRB00012476-2024-13-11-352). This study was not pre-registered, as it was designed as an exploratory investigation of pupillary responses to socially evaluative stress.

Study design

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a group performing a traditional Stroop task (G-trad, N = 14) and a group performing a competitive Stroop task (G-comp N = 16). The characteristics of both groups are presented in the Results section. The Stroop task was programmed using MATLAB (The MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) with the Psychtoolbox extension. It began with a 10-trial practice phase, which participants could repeat if necessary to ensure proper task comprehension. The task then consisted of three blocks of 60 trials each (50% congruent and 50% incongruent). During congruent trials, the color word matched the font color (e.g., the word “red” displayed in red), whereas in incongruent trials, the color word and the font color were mismatched (e.g., the word “red” displayed in blue). Participants were instructed to identify the ink color as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing the corresponding key on the keyboard. Responses were made using the following keys.

•Red = W

Red = W

•Green = X

Green = X

•Blue = 2 (on the numeric keypad)

Blue = 2 (on the numeric keypad)

•Yellow = 3 (on the numeric keypad)

Yellow = 3 (on the numeric keypad)

For each trial, participants had up to 2 s to respond. Regardless of whether a response was made or not, the next stimulus was automatically presented after this interval. Accordingly, the stimulus-onset asynchrony (the time between the onset of one stimulus and the onset of the next) was fixed at 2 s throughout the task. To enhance task engagement and maintain cognitive load, auditory feedback was provided exclusively for incorrect responses. The three blocks of 60 trials were separated by a 30-s rest period, allowing participants brief recovery periods while maintaining cognitive engagement. In the competition condition, participants were informed immediately before the task that they would be competing against other participants. They were told that 11 participants had already completed the task, although this information was fictitious. To enhance social-evaluative pressure, a ranking was displayed at the end of each Stroop block, indicating progressively lower standings: 9th out of 12 after the first block, 10th out of 12 after the second block, and 11th out of 12 after the third block. This ranking was ostensibly based on both reaction time and accuracy. In addition, phrases accompanied these rankings, pushing participants to improve their performance. For example, after the first block, participants saw the following message: “With a 9th place out of 12, you are at the bottom of the ranking. Every position gained is a challenge. It is imperative to react now before it becomes too late to catch up.”. This competitive setup aimed to induce social-evaluative threat, amplifying the stress response beyond the cognitive challenge of the traditional Stroop task [31].

The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 1. It spanned 50 min, starting with a baseline phase (−20 to 0 min), followed by the Stroop task phase (0 to +10 min), and ending with a recovery phase (+10 to +30 min). The protocol was designed to assess physiological and subjective indicators of stress at multiple time points. During the baseline phase, participants completed the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS [32]). Salivary samples were then collected, blood pressure was measured, and participants rated their perceived stress using a visual analog scale (VAS) [33]. To ensure the stabilization of cardiorespiratory parameters, participants remained still for 1 min before each physiological measurement and before starting the Stroop task. HR, HRV, respiratory frequency, and pupil size were then recorded for 1 min. The Stroop task was divided into three successive blocks (Block 1, Block 2, Block 3), with continuous monitoring of HR, HRV, respiratory frequency, and pupil size throughout the entire duration of the task. Participants also completed the stress VAS during the recovery between Block 1 and Block 2 in order to capture subjective stress without excessively interrupting task engagement. In the recovery phase, all parameters were measured again immediately after the Stroop task and then every 10 min for up to 30 min post-task. As in previous phases, a 1-min stabilization period preceded each measurement, followed by a 1-min recording of HR, HRV, respiratory frequency, and pupil size.

Materials and measurements

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS): Participants completed the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS [32]), a widely used psychological instrument that assesses the extent to which individuals perceive their lives as stressful. Of the ten questions that make up the PSS-10, six assess negative experiences, while four assess positive experiences. Responses were given on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), with reverse scoring for positive questions. The total score ranges from 0 to 40, with the following interpretation: “low” stress for 0–13 points, “moderate” stress for 14–26 points, and “high” stress for 27–40 points.

Salivary Samples (Cortisol and α-Amylase): Saliva samples were collected to assess cortisol and alpha-amylase (sAA) responses to acute stress, following standard procedures [34]. To minimize the impact of external factors on the biological stress markers [31], participants were asked to adhere to the following guidelines.

•Circadian rhythm control: Participants were instructed to wake up at least 3 h before the study session, and all testing sessions began after 11:30 a.m. to reduce the influence of circadian variations in hormone concentrations.

Circadian rhythm control: Participants were instructed to wake up at least 3 h before the study session, and all testing sessions began after 11:30 a.m. to reduce the influence of circadian variations in hormone concentrations.

•Physical activity: Participants were advised to refrain from vigorous physical activity for at least 3 h before the study.

Physical activity: Participants were advised to refrain from vigorous physical activity for at least 3 h before the study.

•Nutrition: To control for potential dietary influences, participants were instructed to avoid alcohol consumption on the day of the study and to abstain from eating and drinking (except for water) for at least 1 h before the session.

Nutrition: To control for potential dietary influences, participants were instructed to avoid alcohol consumption on the day of the study and to abstain from eating and drinking (except for water) for at least 1 h before the session.

Saliva was collected using Salivette® (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Numbrecht, Germany). Participants were instructed to place the cotton swab in their mouth during 3 min without chewing. Tubes were labeled with the participant ID, date, and time of collection and samples were immediately frozen at −20 °C until analysis. For laboratory analysis, samples were thawed and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature according to standard procedures. Cortisol concentrations were determined using the laboratory's validated immunoassay protocol, and alpha-amylase activity was measured using the laboratory's standard enzymatic assay. The laboratory provided a report with concentrations for each participant and time point. All saliva samples were safely disposed of via the DASRI waste management system after analysis. One participant from the G-trad was excluded from cortisol and α-amylase analyses due to insufficient saliva volume preventing reliable biochemical assessment.

Pupil size: Pupil size, expressed in millimeters (mm), was measured using the Tobii Pro X3-120® eye tracker (Tobii Technology, Danderyd, Sweden) at a sampling rate of 120 Hz. The device was calibrated before each test according to the manufacturer's guidelines to ensure measurement accuracy. Data were recorded and preprocessed simultaneously using MATLAB. Pupil diameter was recorded separately for the left and right eyes. During measurements performed before and after the Stroop task, participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a white cross displayed at the center of a black computer screen for 1 min. During the Stroop task, pupil diameter was continuously recorded throughout the three blocks; however, only the first minute of each block was retained for analysis, and the mean pupil diameter across the three blocks was calculated. To account for transient changes in pupil size associated with illumination adaptation [15], the first 5 s of each recording period were excluded from the analysis. Pupil data preprocessing was based on the validity information provided by the Tobii system. Invalid samples were exported as missing values (NaN) and excluded from subsequent analyses. These invalid samples corresponded to periods during which the eye tracker was unable to reliably estimate pupil diameter, including blink-related signal loss. No interpolation or signal reconstruction was performed. Mean pupil diameter was then computed from all valid samples within the remaining 55 s of each 1-min recording period. This approach was consistent with the objective of the study, which was to quantify sustained changes in mean pupil diameter over predefined recording windows rather than transient within-period fluctuations or event-related pupil responses. Pupil diameter was first averaged separately across valid samples from the left and right eyes, and the two eye-specific mean values were subsequently averaged to obtain a single pupil diameter value for each participant and time point. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation during pupil recordings to minimize the influence of eye movements. All measurements were conducted under stable and controlled lighting conditions. The light level (≈150 lux) was measured using a Light Meter S8608 (Dr. Meter, China) positioned at the same height as the participants' right eye and oriented toward the screen to approximate the amount of light reaching the eyes [35].

Heart Rate and Heart Rate Variability: Heart rate (HR, in bpm) and time-domain HRV metrics, including RMSSD and SDNN (in ms), were recorded using a Polar H10 chest strap (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) connected to the Elite HRV application (Elite HRV Inc., United States). RR intervals were processed using the HRVTool MATLAB toolbox [36]. Artefactual fluctuations exceeding 15% of the previous interval were removed using the HRV. RRfilter function, while preserving physiologically relevant variability. Time series were visually inspected to ensure signal quality, and segments with excessive noise or signal loss were excluded from further analysis.

Respiratory Frequency: Respiratory frequency was recorded using the Sniff Logic device (Sniff Logic, Israel) and measured in real-time via MATLAB. Participants were instructed to keep their mouths closed and breathe through their noses for the duration of the measurements. This setup allowed for precise synchronization with the pupil dilation recordings.

Blood Pressure: Blood pressure was measured in the supine position immediately following cortisol sample collection using an automated arm blood pressure monitor (HBP-1120, Omron Corporation, Kyoto, Japan).

Stress Visual Analog Scale: Subjective stress was assessed using a VAS with a sliding indicator. The front of the scale presented the prompt “Indicate how stressed you feel at this moment?” and consisted of linear continuum anchored by “None” and “As bad as it could be”, representing the lowest and highest levels of stress, respectively [33]. Participants adjusted the slider to indicate their current level of stress. The reverse side of the scale displayed a numerical range from 0 to 100 mm, from which the investigator recorded the corresponding score.

Statistical analysis

All data were recorded in a case report form, then preprocessed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Redmond, Washington, USA), MATLAB, and the JAMOVI statistical analysis software (version 2.3.28, Sydney, Australia). The normality of the distribution, homogeneity of variances, and sphericity assumption were tested using the Shapiro-Wilk, Levene, and Mauchly tests, respectively. A two-way mixed-design ANOVA (Stroop task condition × Time) was conducted to assess the effects of the Stroop task condition and Time on physiological and subjective (VAS) stress indicators. Pupillary, cardiac, and respiratory measures were continuously recorded during the Stroop task and segmented into five time periods, whereas VAS followed the same structure. Endocrine and blood pressure measures were assessed at discrete time points only before and after the Stroop task (four time periods). Additional analyses were conducted at the block level to characterize within-task pupil and cardiorespiratory dynamics. Given the unequal sex distribution between the traditional and competitive Stroop conditions, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for the primary outcome (pupil dilation) by including sex as an additional between-subject factor in the mixed-design ANOVA. When the sphericity assumption was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η2p) for the ANOVA, following the guidelines by Cohen [37] and Lakens [38], where 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively. For significant main effects and interactions, multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc tests to identify significant pairwise differences. Additionally, participants' characteristics, perceived stress measured by the PSS, and performance (accuracy and reaction time) during the Stroop task were compared using t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests if the variances were not homogeneous or the distributions were not normal. Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for the t-tests, with 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively [37]. All results are presented as mean ± SD with individual values displayed. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. No statistical outliers were excluded from the analyses.

Salivary cortisol and α-Amylase

Fig. 2 shows salivary cortisol and α-amylase concentrations before the Stroop task and during recovery for both groups. For cortisol, significant main effects of Time (F(1.71, 46.06) = 159.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.855) and Group (F(1, 27) = 53.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.662), as well as a significant Time × Group interaction (F(1.71, 46.06) = 101.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.790), were observed. Between-group comparisons revealed higher cortisol concentrations in the G-comp at all post-task time points. For α-amylase, significant main effects of Time (F(1.51, 40.73) = 269.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.909) and Group (F(1, 27) = 181.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.870), as well as a significant Time × Group interaction (F(1.51, 40.73) = 70.50, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.723), were found. α-amylase levels increased in both groups following the Stroop task, with a markedly stronger response in the G-comp, showing significantly higher concentrations at 10- and 20-min post-task. Pupil size

Fig. 3 shows pupil size across the different experimental phases and during the three Stroop blocks. During the experimental phases, a significant main effect of Time was observed (F(3.03, 84.92) = 21.78, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.438), along with a significant Time × Group interaction (F(3.03, 84.92) = 3.70, p = 0.014, η2p = 0.117), whereas no main effect of Group was found (F(1, 28) = 0.07, p = 0.789, η2p = 0.003). Post-hoc analyses indicated that pupil size increased during the Stroop task in the G-comp compared to baseline and recovery periods, whereas no comparable change was observed in the G-trad. Additional analyses examining pupil dilation across the three Stroop blocks revealed a significant main effect of Block (F(1.39, 37.45) = 8.43, p = 0.003, η2p = 0.238), whereas no main effect of Group was observed (F(1, 27) = 0.56, p = 0.462, η2p = 0.020), and no significant Group × Block interaction was found (F(1.39, 37.45) = 0.07, p = 0.868, η2p = 0.003). The results indicate a progressive decrease in pupil dilation across the three blocks. To assess whether the unequal sex distribution between the traditional and competitive Stroop conditions influenced the main findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed by including sex as an additional between-subject factor in the mixed-design ANOVA. The significant Time × Stroop task condition interaction remained significant after accounting for sex (F(4,104) = 4.23, p = 0.003, η2p = 0.140). No significant Time × Sex interaction (F(4,104) = 1.48, p = 0.213, η2p = 0.054) or Time × Stroop task condition × Sex interaction (F(4,104) = 0.93, p = 0.449, η2p = 0.035) was observed, indicating that there was no evidence that sex accounted for the observed Time × Stroop task condition effect or moderated the temporal pattern of pupil dilation.

Cardiorespiratory and blood pressure parameters

Fig. 4 presents cardiorespiratory and blood pressure measures across the different experimental phases. Respiratory rate showed a significant main effect of Time (F(3.02, 84.62) = 7.04, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.201), whereas no main effect of Group (F(1, 28) = 0.109, p = 0.743, η2p = 0.004) or Time × Group interaction (F(3.02, 84.62) = 1.71, p = 0.171, η2p = 0.058) was observed. Heart rate also exhibited a significant main effect of Time (F(2.76, 77.31) = 37.00, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.569), with no significant main effect of Group (F(1, 28) = 0.80, p = 0.380, η2p = 0.028) or Time × Group interaction (F(2.76, 77.31) = 1.63, p = 0.192, η2p = 0.055). HRV indices changed significantly over time for both RMSSD (F(2.76, 77.33) = 10.09, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.265) and SDNN (F(4, 112) = 3.42, p = 0.011, η2p = 0.109). However, no significant main effects of Group (RMSSD: F(1, 28) = 0.642, p = 0.430, η2p = 0.022; SDNN: F(1, 28) = 2.09, p = 0.159, η2p = 0.070) or Time × Group interactions (RMSSD: F(2.76, 77.33) = 0.407, p = 0.732, η2p = 0.014; SDNN: F(4, 112) = 0.57, p = 0.682, η2p = 0.020) were observed. Systolic blood pressure remained stable across the experimental phases, with no significant main effect of Time (F(3, 84) = 0.466, p = 0.706, η2p = 0.016), Group (F(1, 28) = 0.50, p = 0.485, η2p = 0.018), or Time × Group interaction (F(3, 84) = 1.077, p = 0.363, η2p = 0.037). Overall, these cardiorespiratory and cardiovascular measures exhibited time-dependent variations but did not discriminate between the traditional and competitive Stroop conditions.

Perceived stress (visual analog scale)

A significant main effect of Time on perceived stress measured with the VAS was observed (F(4, 112) = 38.14, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.577), along with a significant Time × Group interaction (F(4, 112) = 5.10, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.154), whereas no main effect of Group was found (F(1, 28) = 1.32, p = 0.260, η2p = 0.045) (Fig. 5). Discussion

The present study investigated whether pupil size can serve as a sensitive marker of acute stress by comparing responses to two cognitively identical Stroop tasks, one performed in a neutral context and the other incorporating a socially evaluative competitive dimension. Consistent with our hypotheses, pupil dilation emerged as the only physiological marker capable of discriminating between the two experimental conditions during task performance. Salivary cortisol and α-amylase showed delayed but significant increases, particularly during the competitive task, whereas cardiorespiratory measures exhibited time-dependent changes without discriminating between conditions. Subjective stress ratings (VAS) increased in both groups, with a more pronounced rise in the competitive condition.

Pupil dilation reflects sympathetic nervous system activation and is closely linked to LC-NE activity, enabling rapid autonomic responses to cognitive and emotional challenges [17,23]. Importantly, pupil size is known to vary as a function of multiple factors, including mental effort, environmental uncertainty, and emotional arousal [39]. This multidimensional sensitivity makes pupillometry a particularly rich, yet complex, index of neurophysiological reactivity. A key challenge is therefore to determine whether pupillary responses can discriminate subtle variations in stress when cognitive demands are held constant and only the evaluative or motivational context is manipulated. In the present study, the modulation of pupil size observed between the traditional and competitive conditions indicates that the added social-evaluative context influenced stress-related pupillary dynamics despite identical cognitive demands. This modulation likely reflects the multifaceted nature of acute stress responses, involving coordinated changes in emotional arousal, motivational engagement, and autonomic activation. These findings highlight the relevance of pupillometry for capturing context-dependent variations in acute stress reactivity. In addition, the temporal modulation of pupil size during the Stroop task further supports its sensitivity to rapid fluctuations in autonomic arousal across experimental phases and task demands. This time-dependent effect may reflect a reduction in novelty and emotional reactivity as participants become increasingly familiar with the task structure across blocks, a phenomenon previously shown to attenuate arousal-related responses during repeated or sustained task exposure [40].

Cortisol and α-amylase displayed slower responses, consistent with the temporal dynamics of the HPA axis and adrenergic stimulation, respectively [41]. Levels of both markers were significantly greater in the competitive condition, confirming the effectiveness of the competitive Stroop task in eliciting a higher stress response, in line with prior work [31]. Although these measures discriminated between conditions, their slower temporal dynamics and delayed availability due to analytical constraint limit their ability to capture rapid fluctuations in stress responses, highlighting the added value of pupillometry for real-time assessment.

The SNS activation, as reflected by the increase in salivary α-amylase following the Stroop tasks, typically elicits rapid autonomic responses, such as increases in heart rate, decreases in heart rate variability [11,12], elevated respiratory rate [13,14], and increases in blood pressure under conditions of varying task demand [42]. Consistent with prior work demonstrating robust cardiovascular reactivity during Stroop paradigms when task demands are manipulated [42], cardiovascular variables in the present study showed clear time-dependent changes but did not differ between the traditional and competitive conditions. This pattern may reflect the moderate intensity of the stressor and the fact that both conditions involved comparable cognitive demands, which could have led to similar levels of sustained task engagement. In addition, inter-individual differences in autonomic responsiveness may have reduced the ability of these measures to discriminate between conditions [21,43]. The absence of significant modulation of cardiorespiratory and HRV indices across Stroop blocks may further suggest limited sensitivity to short-term within-task contextual changes under the present experimental conditions. In contrast to the temporal dynamics observed in pupil dilation, cardiovascular and respiratory parameters remained relatively stable throughout task execution, suggesting that they may primarily reflect sustained cognitive engagement and general autonomic activation rather than rapid context-dependent fluctuations.

Subjective stress ratings (VAS) largely mirrored the pupillometry findings. In the competitive condition, perceived stress increased during the Stroop task and declined during recovery, while in the traditional condition, changes were smaller and primarily observed between the Stroop task and the recovery. This pattern suggests that participants’ self-reported stress closely reflects the physiological changes captured by pupil dilation, supporting the ecological validity of pupillometry. However, subjective measures have inherent limitations: participants may underreport or overreport stress due to social desirability or limited introspective access to their physiological state [44]. In contrast, pupillometry provides continuous, real-time indices of autonomic and cognitive arousal [23], offering a more sensitive assessment of acute stress dynamics [22].

These findings highlight pupil size as a real-time marker of acute stress. Pupillometry could thus be applied in high-stakes environments such as healthcare or aviation, where monitoring stress may help identify situations associated with increased vulnerability to performance decrements that are not directly explained by changes in cognitive demand [5,6]. It may also complement well-being interventions by providing objective feedback on their effectiveness [4,7,8]. Overall, pupillometry represents a promising tool for monitoring stress dynamics in cognitively demanding settings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the study was based on a relatively small sample of healthy adults (N = 30), which should be taken into account when interpreting the robustness and generalizability of the findings. In addition, although both male and female participants were included, the sex distribution was not fully balanced between the traditional and competitive Stroop conditions. A sensitivity analysis including sex as an additional between-subject factor indicated that the main pupil dilation findings remained robust after adjustment for sex, with no evidence that sex influenced the temporal pattern of pupil dilation or moderated the effect of Stroop task condition over time. Nevertheless, the study was not powered to investigate sex-specific effects, and the potential influence of sex distribution on secondary physiological outcomes should therefore be considered when interpreting these measures. Second, pupillary responses can be influenced by ambient lighting, fatigue, or eye movements, which requires careful experimental control and calibration in applied settings. Third, hormonal and subjective measures (VAS) were collected at discrete time points to minimize interference with task performance, limiting the temporal resolution of HPA-axis and adrenergic dynamics as well as the granularity of subjective stress assessment. In particular, the single-item VAS may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of subjective stress, despite allowing repeated assessments with minimal disruption to the task. In addition, HRV measures acquired during cognitive task execution should be interpreted with caution, as HRV is classically defined under relatively stationary physiological conditions and may be influenced by task-related non-stationarity and transient autonomic dynamics. Fourth, although the inclusion of a cognitive control condition (traditional Stroop) allowed for the isolation of the additional impact of competitive stress relative to cognitive load alone, the absence of a non-task control condition prevents disentangling stress-specific effects from time-related or task-unspecific physiological fluctuations. Finally, while the Stroop paradigm successfully elicited measurable acute stress responses, it may represent a relatively moderate stressor compared to more potent socially evaluative stress protocols such as the TSST. It is well established that stronger stress induction paradigms can produce larger cardiorespiratory responses, which may improve the ability of these measures to discriminate between conditions. However, the fact that pupil dilation differentiated between conditions despite the relatively moderate intensity of the stressor suggests that pupillometry may be particularly sensitive to subtle variations in stress responses. Future studies should therefore examine stronger stress induction paradigms, continuous hormonal monitoring, and complementary autonomic markers such as electrodermal activity to further characterize the respective sensitivity and temporal dynamics of different stress biomarkers and to improve ecological validity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, pupil dilation emerges as a rapid, sensitive, and non-invasive indicator of acute stress, capable of capturing subtle variations in stress induced by social-evaluative context under conditions of equivalent cognitive demand. These findings support its use in experimental research and suggest its potential for real-time monitoring of stress in applied settings, particularly in high-stakes environments.
