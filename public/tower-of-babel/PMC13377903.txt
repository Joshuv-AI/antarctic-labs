Mitigating risk in audiology and speech therapy practice: Essential risk management and control strategies for private and public practices in resource‐constrained health systems
Authors: Katijah Khoza‐Shangase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective risk management is central to patient safety, regulatory compliance, and organizational sustainability in healthcare. In South Africa, audiology and speech therapy practitioners operate within a system characterized by resource constraints, regulatory complexity, and socio‐economic inequality, resulting in differentiated risk exposure across practice settings. This study explored risks encountered by practitioners and the strategies used to mitigate these risks. A qualitative design was employed in 2025 with 92 practitioners. Data were analyzed thematically, supported by descriptive and inferential analyses. Four interrelated risk domains emerged, with sectoral differences reflected across thematic and quantitative findings. Public sector practitioners reported significantly higher clinical risk, including diagnostic errors (65%) and delayed treatment (70%), particularly in rural settings (χ 2 = 18.21, p < 0.01). Additionally, operational risks such as staff shortages (75%) and equipment failure (70%) were also more prevalent. In contrast, private practitioners reported greater financial risk related to income volatility and overhead costs (70%; t = 3.12, p < 0.01). Legal and regulatory risks were evident across both sectors, while mitigation strategies included telehealth adoption and community partnerships. These findings demonstrate structurally produced risk, highlighting the need for integrated, context‐sensitive risk governance frameworks in resource‐constrained health systems.

INTRODUCTION

Risk management is a foundational component of healthcare governance, encompassing the systematic identification, assessment, and mitigation of threats to patient safety, professional practice, and organizational sustainability. 1 , 2 Within audiology and speech therapy, risk management extends beyond clinical decision‐making to include operational continuity, financial viability, data protection, and regulatory compliance. 3 , 4 Failures in these domains can result in adverse patient outcomes, professional misconduct claims, reputational damage, and service disruption.

In South Africa, risk management occurs within a uniquely complex healthcare environment shaped by a dual public–private system, entrenched socio‐economic inequality, and uneven resource distribution. 5 , 6 Public sector practitioners frequently operate in under‐resourced facilities characterized by high patient volumes, staffing shortages, and aging infrastructure, increasing exposure to clinical and operational risk. In contrast, private practitioners face risks associated with financial sustainability, regulatory compliance, and market competition, particularly within a highly regulated professional environment governed by the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) and national data protection legislation such as the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA). 7 , 8

Internationally, healthcare risk management has evolved toward integrated, system‐level approaches that emphasize prevention, quality improvement, and organizational learning rather than individual fault. 9 , 10 However, much of this literature is grounded in high‐income contexts (HICs) with established infrastructure, insurance systems, and technological supports. In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including South Africa, structural constraints fundamentally alter risk exposure and limit the feasibility of conventional risk mitigation strategies. 11 , 12

Despite growing recognition of risk governance as a critical component of healthcare quality, empirical research on risk management within audiology and speech therapy, particularly in LMIC contexts, remains limited. Existing studies tend to privilege clinical risk while underexamining operational, financial, and regulatory dimensions of practice management. This gap is particularly consequential in South Africa, where practitioners must navigate complex, intersecting risks across sectors. Audiology and speech therapy practices, often small, decentralized, and resource dependent, represent underexplored sites of risk production and mitigation, particularly in LMICs. This is especially important given that, unlike hospital‐based professions, these practitioners frequently operate with limited institutional support while simultaneously managing clinical, operational, and business‐related responsibilities.

This study, therefore aimed to explore the risk profiles of South African audiology and speech therapy practitioners and to examine the strategies employed to mitigate clinical, operational, financial, and regulatory risks within public and private practice settings. By situating these findings within both national and international risk management literature, the study contributes contextually grounded evidence to inform professional training, institutional policy, and health system governance.

METHODOLOGY

Aim and objectives

The aim of this study was to explore risk exposure and risk management strategies among South African audiology and speech therapy practitioners across public and private practice settings.

Specifically, the study sought to: (1) identify the primary clinical, operational, financial, and regulatory risks encountered in practice management; (2) examine sectoral and contextual differences in risk exposure across public and private practices; and (3) explore the strategies practitioners employ to mitigate identified risks within resource‐constrained and regulatory environments.

Study design and setting

A qualitative, exploratory study design was adopted 13 to capture practitioners’ lived experiences of risk and risk management. The study was conducted across South Africa and included participants practicing in urban, peri‐urban, and rural contexts within both public and private healthcare sectors.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were registered audiologists or speech therapists with a minimum of one year of professional experience and active involvement in practice management or operational decision‐making. Purposive sampling was used to ensure diversity across sector, geography, and years of experience. Recruitment occurred through professional associations, healthcare networks, and direct invitations. A total of 92 practitioners participated, with equal representation from public and private sectors.

Data collection

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted during a single data‐collection phase in 2025, either in person or telephonically, depending on participant preference and feasibility. Interviews lasted approximately 45–60 min and explored perceived risks, risk mitigation strategies, implementation challenges, and sector‐specific risk dynamics. All interviews were audio‐recorded with consent and supplemented by field notes.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed thematically using Braun and Clarke's 14 six‐phase framework. NVivo software supported coding and theme development. To complement qualitative analysis, descriptive statistics and inferential tests (chi‐square and independent t‐tests) were conducted using SPSS to explore sectoral differences in reported risks and mitigation strategies. This mixed analytical approach enabled both in‐depth exploration of practitioner experiences and structured comparison of sectoral patterns, strengthening the alignment between qualitative insights and quantitative trends.

Trustworthiness and ethics

Credibility was enhanced through member checking and peer debriefing, while dependability was ensured through a documented audit trail. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of the XXX Human Research Ethics Committee (Non‐Medical) (Protocol XXX). All participants provided informed consent, and data were anonymized and securely stored in accordance with institutional and legal requirements.

Thematic Findings

Theme 1: Clinical risk exposure and mitigation

Participants identified clinical risks, including diagnostic errors, delayed intervention, and patient non‐compliance, as central concerns in practice. These risks were more frequently reported by public sector practitioners, who linked them to high patient volumes, limited access to diagnostic tools, and constrained follow‐up capacity.

“Working in a rural public facility means we often don't have access to updated diagnostic tools, which increases the risk of misdiagnosis.” (Public sector participant 6, 2025)

“Patient compliance is a major issue, especially in communities where there is limited understanding of the importance of follow‐up care.” (Private sector participant 42, 2025)

Theme 2: Operational and financial risk

Operational and financial risks emerged as a distinct theme, with marked sectoral differences. Public practitioners primarily reported operational challenges, including staff shortages and equipment failures, which were perceived to increase workload, burnout, and the likelihood of error. In contrast, private practitioners emphasized financial risks related to fluctuating patient volumes, high overhead costs, and income instability.

Summary of results

The findings reveal distinct yet intersecting risk profiles among South African audiology and speech therapy practitioners across public and private sectors. Public practitioners were disproportionately affected by clinical and operational risks associated with resource constraints, while private practitioners faced heightened financial and regulatory risks linked to practice sustainability and compliance. Across both sectors, practitioners demonstrated adaptive capacity through innovative risk mitigation strategies, including telehealth and community engagement. Importantly, these patterns, evident across Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, demonstrate that risk is experienced as cumulative and interdependent rather than discrete, with clinical risk often exacerbated by operational failures and regulatory constraints.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that risk management in South African audiology and speech therapy practice extends well beyond individual clinical competence or procedural compliance. Instead, risk is structurally produced through the interaction of sectoral inequalities, regulatory expectations, and persistent resource constraints within a deeply stratified health system. The findings further demonstrate that risk exposure is unevenly distributed and contextually shaped across public and private sectors, necessitating differentiated and system‐aware approaches to risk governance.

From a healthcare risk management perspective, these findings reinforce the inadequacy of linear or siloed risk models in professional practice settings. As illustrated in Theme 2 (Operational and Financial Risk) and Theme 4 (Adaptive Risk Mitigation Strategies), participants consistently described how staffing shortages, equipment failures, and financial instability interacted to amplify clinical risk rather than operating as discrete challenges. Clinical errors, operational breakdowns, financial strain, and regulatory non‐compliance were therefore experienced as mutually reinforcing pressures within a constrained system. This aligns with contemporary risk governance frameworks that emphasize complexity, interdependence, and organizational learning rather than individual fault attribution. 2 , 10

Public sector practitioners were disproportionately exposed to clinical and operational risks (see Tables 2 and 3), particularly those related to diagnostic delays, treatment errors, staffing shortages, and equipment failures. These findings are consistent with broader evidence from LMICs, which shows that chronic underinvestment in public health systems shifts systemic risk onto frontline clinicians, often resulting in moral distress, burnout, and compromised quality of care. 15 , 16 , 17 They also align with emerging work on health system resilience, which emphasizes how constrained systems redistribute risk to frontline providers while relying on adaptive coping mechanisms. 18 , 19 In rural and peri‐urban settings, limited access to diagnostic technologies, specialist referral pathways, and continuity‐of‐care mechanisms further compounded clinical risk, increasing the likelihood of delayed intervention and suboptimal outcomes. Importantly, these risks cannot be attributed to individual practitioner failure; rather, they reflect structural conditions that constrain safe and effective practice, reinforcing calls for health system accountability rather than clinician‐level blame.

In contrast, private sector practitioners reported heightened exposure to financial and regulatory risks (see Tables 3 and 4), including income volatility, escalating overhead costs, and uncertainty related to compliance with HPCSA marketing regulations and the POPIA. These findings align with international literature on small and solo healthcare practices, which highlights the vulnerability of clinicians operating as entrepreneurs within competitive and increasingly regulated healthcare markets. 20 , 21 In the South African context, extreme socio‐economic inequality intensifies these risks, as private practitioners are required to balance affordability, ethical obligation, and financial sustainability in a setting where a large proportion of patients are uninsured or underinsured. 22 , 23 This tension highlights the inadequacy of risk frameworks that treat financial risk as separate from ethical or clinical considerations. Importantly, financial risk in this context cannot be disentangled from ethical and clinical risk, as income instability directly shapes service accessibility, consultation time, and continuity of care, challenging assumptions that financial risk is external to patient safety.

Legal and regulatory risk emerged as a cross‐cutting concern across both sectors (see Table 4), particularly in relation to patient confidentiality and data protection. While private practitioners highlighted compliance challenges associated with digital record‐keeping and marketing restrictions, public sector practitioners emphasized infrastructural barriers, such as shared consultation spaces and overcrowded facilities, that undermine confidentiality in practice. These findings resonate with critiques of principlist bioethics and regulatory models that assume infrastructural adequacy and institutional capacity, thereby generating compliance risk by design in under‐resourced settings. 9 , 24 This finding suggests a need for regulatory bodies to adopt risk‐informed, context‐sensitive guidance that distinguishes between wilful non‐compliance and structurally constrained practice environments, particularly within public sector and small private practice contexts. Accordingly, regulatory expectations must be more closely aligned with contextual realities if they are to support, rather than inadvertently undermine, ethical and safe practice.

Notably, practitioners across both sectors demonstrated adaptive capacity through innovative risk mitigation strategies (see Table 5), including telehealth adoption, incident reporting systems, and community partnerships. Telehealth emerged as a particularly salient strategy, functioning simultaneously as a clinical, operational, and access‐related risk mitigation tool. This finding aligns with growing evidence that digital health interventions, when contextually implemented, can reduce travel‐related risks, improve continuity of care, and partially offset workforce shortages in LMICs. 25 , 26 , 27 Community partnerships were especially prominent among public sector practitioners, reflecting evidence that cross‐sector collaboration and NGO engagement serve as critical risk buffers in resource‐constrained health systems. 19 However, reliance on such adaptive strategies also signals systemic gaps, raising questions about sustainability when risk mitigation depends heavily on individual ingenuity rather than institutional support.

These findings also resonate with qualitative research in adjacent healthcare fields, including nursing, primary care, and hospital management, where risk is similarly conceptualized as emerging from systemic constraints rather than individual practitioner failure. However, what distinguishes audiology and speech therapy is the decentralized, small‐practice nature of service delivery, often characterized by solo or small‐group practices operating with limited institutional buffering. This convergence of clinical, operational, and financial responsibilities intensifies exposure to intersecting risk domains and highlights the need for tailored risk governance frameworks specific to allied health contexts.

Collectively, these findings suggest that effective risk management in South African audiology and speech therapy practice requires a shift away from individualized risk responsibility toward integrated, system‐aware risk governance. Such an approach must recognize the interdependence of clinical, operational, financial, and regulatory risks and address the structural conditions that shape practitioners’ exposure to harm.

Implications for healthcare risk management

The findings of this study have direct implications for healthcare risk managers, particularly those operating in resource‐constrained and dual‐sector health systems. First, the interdependence of clinical, operational, financial, and regulatory risks highlights the need for integrated enterprise risk management approaches that move beyond siloed risk categories. Risk managers should priorities system‐level assessments that account for how infrastructure limitations, workforce shortages, and financial pressures interact to shape patient safety outcomes. Second, the marked sectoral differences identified in this study suggest that risk mitigation strategies must be context‐specific. In public sector settings, priority areas include infrastructure investment, workforce strengthening, and equipment maintenance systems to reduce clinically significant risk exposure. In private practice, financial risk buffering mechanisms, regulatory clarity, and compliance support are critical to sustaining safe and ethical service delivery. Third, the adaptive strategies identified, particularly telehealth and community partnerships, highlight opportunities for scalable, system‐supported interventions. Risk managers should consider formalizing these strategies within organizational policies and national frameworks to ensure sustainability and equitable access. Finally, these findings reinforce the importance of aligning risk governance with Just Culture principles, 28 recognizing that many risks originate from structural conditions rather than individual practitioner negligence. Effective risk management in this context requires shared accountability across institutions, regulators, and policymakers.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly when discussing risk and professional vulnerability. Second, while the sample was geographically diverse, practitioners in deeply remote or underserved rural contexts may remain underrepresented, potentially underestimating the magnitude of risk in these settings. Third, the cross‐sectional design captures risk perceptions at a single point in time and does not account for how risk exposure and mitigation strategies evolve over the professional life course or in response to policy and system changes. Finally, although the mixed qualitative, quantitative approach strengthened analytic depth, the study did not directly assess patient outcomes or institutional risk metrics. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs, multi‐stakeholder perspectives, and integration of organizational‐level data to further strengthen evidence on risk governance in audiology and speech therapy practice. In addition, the study focused on practitioner perspectives and did not include the views of regulators, managers, or patients, which may have provided further insight into institutional risk governance and accountability. Incorporating multi‐level stakeholder perspectives represents an important avenue for future research.

CONCLUSION

This study examined risk exposure and mitigation strategies among South African audiology and speech therapy practitioners, demonstrating that risk in practice management is structurally produced and unevenly distributed across public and private sectors. Public sector practitioners faced heightened clinical and operational risks driven by resource scarcity, infrastructural limitations, and workforce constraints, including inadequate equipment and limited spaces that compromise diagnostic accuracy and patient confidentiality. In contrast, private practitioners navigated financial volatility and complex regulatory environments shaped by market pressures, restrictive reimbursement structures, and socio‐economic inequality, particularly in relation to marketing, sustainability, and compliance. Across both sectors, practitioners nevertheless exhibited adaptive capacity through innovative strategies such as telehealth adoption, task‐shifting, and community partnerships, highlighting professional resilience in the face of systemic constraints.

The findings highlight the urgent need for risk management frameworks that are contextually grounded, sector‐sensitive, and institutionally supported. Such frameworks should be embedded within institutional governance structures and professional training pathways, rather than relying on informal, practitioner‐led risk mitigation. Strengthening clinical risk management requires targeted practitioner training in culturally responsive communication and patient education to enhance adherence and reduce preventable clinical errors, alongside increased investment in public healthcare infrastructure to address equipment shortages and improve diagnostic and intervention accuracy. Addressing operational and financial risks further necessitates the development of public–private partnerships to optimize resource sharing and reduce inefficiencies, as well as financial support mechanisms, such as subsidies or low‐interest loan schemes, to buffer private practitioners against income instability and escalating operational costs.

Equally critical is the need to support legal and regulatory compliance through systemic reform. Infrastructure upgrades in public facilities are essential to ensure patient privacy and confidentiality, while clearer, more practicable guidance from regulatory bodies such as the HPCSA, particularly regarding ethical marketing and digital practice, would enable private practitioners to navigate compliance requirements without compromising service viability. Importantly, responsibility for managing risk must not rest solely with individual practitioners but be distributed across healthcare institutions, professional councils, funders, and policymakers.

By foregrounding practitioner experiences within a deeply unequal health system, this study contributes empirically to the healthcare risk management literature from an LMIC perspective. It challenges compliance‐oriented and individualized models of risk management, advocating instead for integrated approaches that promote innovation, including expanded telehealth training and cross‐sector collaboration, while simultaneously addressing structural constraints. In doing so, the study highlights the importance of aligning risk governance with system‐level realities and health system resilience priorities in resource‐constrained settings. Such approaches are essential for safeguarding patient care, protecting practitioner wellbeing, and ensuring the long‐term viability of audiology and speech therapy services in South Africa and comparable contexts. Advancing risk governance in allied health professions is essential not only for reducing harm but for strengthening the resilience, legitimacy, and sustainability of health systems operating under conditions of chronic constraint.
