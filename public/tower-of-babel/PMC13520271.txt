Adult Fasciola hepatica in the rectus abdominis muscle: a case report
Authors: Seyed Ali Hashemi, Mitra Shayeghi-Kelyani, Saeid Ebrahimi, Amir Bairami, Mohammad Zibaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFascioliasis is a zoonotic disease in regard to public health, which is sporadically reported from different parts of the world. Adult worms infect the liver and bile ducts, but extrahepatic involvement and the development of parasitic myositis and muscle mass caused by Fasciola hepatica are uncommon and very rare.Case presentationA 55-year-old Iranian female presented with a history of abdominal pain, epigastric tenderness without pallor and jaundice, along with significant weight loss for the past five months. Microscopic examination of the stool did not identify adult worms or parasite eggs. Laboratory findings were almost normal, while erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 32 mm/hour, triglyceride 330 mg/dL and eosinophil 6%. Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-Hepatitis C virus (HCV) were non-reactive, whereas Hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb) was 260.7 IU/L. Ultrasound of the tissue revealed a mass in the abdominal wall in the right upper quadrant. After her admission to the hospital, a flatworm emerged from the rectus abdominis muscle of the patient. Histopathological examination of the excised lesion showed parasite-induced myositis with abscess formation and a granulomatous foreign body reaction. Based on the morphological characteristics of the worm and serological assessment, the worm was identified as Fasciola hepatica. The patient was treated with triclabendazole and underwent clinical and paraclinical follow-up for 90 days.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of diagnosing Fasciola hepatica in the differential diagnosis of unexplained subcutaneous or intramuscular nodules, especially in patients with a history of travel to endemic areas. The current case is one of the rarest cases of extrahepatic involvement (muscular fascioliasis) reported in Iran, a country where Fasciola infection is endemic in its northern regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-06168-8.

Introduction

Fascioliasis is an important food-borne zoonotic disease and is considered neglected tropical diseases by the World Health Organization. The WHO report indicates that between 2.4 to 17 million people are currently infected with the parasite, with a further 180 million at risk in Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, Asia, and South America [1]. In studies conducted in Iran between 1999 and 2019, the overall prevalence of Fasciola infection in Iran was reported to be approximately 6% [2].

Fasciola hepatica has an indirect life cycle that requires a mammalian definitive host and a freshwater snail intermediate host. The infection usually occurs in humans when they eat plants infected with metacercariae, such as watercress, or drink contaminated water. The larvae pass through the intestinal wall and migrate to the liver and bile ducts, causing biliary fibrosis or obstruction and dilation [3].

Fascioliasis in humans has two distinct clinical phases (acute and chronic) that may present with or without symptoms depending on the worm load, duration, and stage of infection. The clinical manifestations are abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, cholangitis, cholecystitis, jaundice, urticaria and eosinophilia [4].

A few cases of human ectopic fascioliasis were reported in the literature. The parasite usually inhabits the liver and bile ducts, but occasionally the larvae develop from a deviated course and in the peritoneal cavity or other ectopic foci. In humans, ectopic lesions are most often found in the gastrointestinal tract, heart, blood vessels, pleural cavity, cervical and inguinal lymph nodes, eye, brain, and skeletal muscle [5].

Its migratory nature and nonspecific clinical presentation often lead to misdiagnosis, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [6]. Hence, we are presenting a rare case of fascioliasis of rectus abdominis muscle which is the first from Iran to the best of our knowledge. This case highlights an extremely rare presentation of intramuscular fascioliasis in the rectus abdominis, contributing to the limited global literature on such atypical locations. It underscores the need for clinicians to consider Fasciola adult worm or eggs in unusual sites for timely diagnosis and management.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old Iranian woman presented to the surgical department with complaints of abdominal pain and epigastric region tenderness associated with loss of weight for 5 months, which progressed significantly over the last 3 months. The patient exhibited no fever, or alterations in bowel habits. She showed no signs of liver involvement, and her other body systems remained largely unaffected. She had no significant past medical history such as icterus, abdominal distension, pruritus, chronic fatigue, nausea and vomiting, anorexia, urine or stool discoloration, ascites, or hepatic encephalopathy, which are known complication of Fasciola infection. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the epigastric and right upper quadrant.

Complete blood count showed a leukocyte count of 9.5 × 103/µL with eosinophilia (9%), and mildly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (32 mm/hour). Histopathological examination of the sample revealed parasitic myositis with abscess formation and a foreign body-type granulomatous reaction, consistent with an inflammatory response to a parasitic infection involving the muscle tissue. The granulomas represented collections of immune cells reacting to the parasite or its remnants as a persistent foreign irritant. No dysplasia or evidence of malignancy was observed. The leaflet-shaped worm was confirmed as an adult Fasciola hepatica in the rectus abdominis muscle. Serological testing was performed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which detected antibodies against the excretory-secretory proteins of adult Fasciola hepatica in the patient's serum.

The patient was treated with triclabendazole 10 mg/kg twice daily for 2 days post-operatively, which effectively improved her clinical condition. The patient was followed over the next 3 months after discharge, during which time the eosinophilia resolved, serological assessments were negative, and no complaints were reported.

Discussion

Fascioliasis is a zoonosis caused primarily by adult worms of Fasciola species. The most common organs involved are the liver and bile ducts, and involvement of other organs is rare [3].

Following ingestion, the metacercariae excyst in the duodenum and penetrate the intestinal wall to enter the peritoneal cavity. Subsequently, the larvae traverse the liver parenchyma and enter the biliary ducts, where they develop into adult flukes and lay eggs. Occasionally, larvae reach ectopic sites, including the lungs, subcutaneous tissue, pancreas, genitourinary tract, brain, and eyes [5].

In a review study the highlights of ectopic fascioliasis in humans were documented [5]. Although rare, extrahepatic migration can occur through several mechanisms. Hematogenous spread, in which flukes or larvae enter the hepatic veins or portal circulation and disseminate to organs such as the lungs, brain, eyes, pancreas, and genitourinary tract. Other possible route of involvement includes direct tissue migration, where immature flukes may migrate irregularly from adjacent tissues and localize aberrantly in subcutaneous tissue, abdominal wall muscles (for example, rectus abdominis), and peritoneum, rather than reaching the bile ducts. Finally, less well-characterized route is lymphatic spread, whereby flukes may access lymphatic vessels and migrate to regional or distant lymph nodes and tissues [6]. In our patient, adult worm, granulomas, and abscess were found in the soft tissue of the abdominal wall and the upper part of the rectus abdominis muscle. There were no lesions within the cavity containing Fasciola hepatica and no evidence of parasite eggs in the stool, blood and/or lymphatic vessels. Therefore, it can be concluded that the parasite developed in the rectus abdominis muscle through direct penetration and not through blood and lymphatic vessels. Adult worms are usually not found in the muscles and similar cases are very rare. Some reports have confirmed the migration and presence of adult Fasciola worms in the muscle and sub-epithelial lesion of the gastric distal body [7].

Stool examination to detect adult worms or eggs, serological tests, and imaging findings are commonly used to diagnose Fasciola infection in humans. Serological assessment, such as ELISA, is effective in the invasive stage of fascioliasis, when antibodies against Fasciola species usually appear 2–4 weeks after exposure. According to earlier studies, ELISA is highly effective in the diagnosis of Fasciola infection, with sensitivity and specificity rates of 96.7% and 91.2%, respectively [8]. In the present patient, who underwent surgery for a muscle tumor, the postoperative ELISA test was positive for Fasciola hepatica.

The importance of imaging in the diagnosis of fascioliasis and other parasitic infection is well established. Ultrasound findings have proven useful in identifying muscular fascioliasis, which manifests as myositis with parasitic abscesses. Muscular fascioliasis is characterized by the presence of adult Fasciola species or their eggs in muscle tissues [9]. Imaging in our case showed an ill-defined, moderately echogenic, and infiltrative mass in the rectus abdominis muscle in the RUQ.

Based on morphological features, a definitive diagnosis could be established through histopathological analysis following surgical excision of the lesion or direct parasite extraction. The adult fluke exhibits a smooth, leaf-like morphology with a conical protrusion at the anterior end [10]. The parasitic myositis with abscess formation and foreign body granulomatous reaction in the rectus abdominis muscle was characteristic in muscular fascioliasis.

Conclusion

The present case highlights the importance of considering unusual locations such as the rectus abdominis muscle for ectopic fascioliasis. A combination of surgical excision and anthelminthic therapy was effective, underscoring the need for heightened clinical awareness and a multidisciplinary approach.
