Red Ear Syndrome: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Zaki Alwatban, Wasan Alharbi, Muneera Al Khalifa, Mohammed Al Shehabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Red ear syndrome (RES) is a rare disorder characterized by episodic erythema, warmth, and burning pain of the external ear. RES is frequently associated with migraine. We report the case of a 35‐year‐old male with a long‐standing history of poorly controlled migraine who presented with recurrent bilateral auricular erythema and burning pain, predominantly affecting the left ear. Symptoms were triggered by migraine attacks, heat exposure, and ear manipulation and relieved by cooling measures. Clinical examination, laboratory investigations, and imaging were normal. RES was diagnosed after excluding infectious and inflammatory causes. Treatment with indomethacin and magnesium resulted in complete symptom resolution. RES should be considered in patients with recurrent auricular erythema and burning pain, particularly in those with migraine. Careful clinical assessment and systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses are crucial for identifying RES and preventing inappropriate management.

1. Introduction

Red ear syndrome (RES), also known as auricular erythermalgia, is a rare clinical condition first described by Lance in 1994 and further characterized in 1996 [1–4]. RES is defined by paroxysmal episodes of unilateral or bilateral erythema of the external ear, accompanied by warmth and burning pain [1]. These episodes are typically precipitated by mechanical or physiological stimuli, including touching or rubbing the ear, heat exposure, migraine attacks, neck movement, chewing or jaw motion, physical exertion, and emotional stress [4, 5].

The true incidence and prevalence of RES remain unknown, largely owing to the limited number of reported cases; thus, the condition is regarded as rare [4]. The pathophysiology of RES is not fully understood; however, it is generally classified into primary (central) and secondary (peripheral) diseases [6], with some cases demonstrating overlapping features of both mechanisms [7]. Primary RES is typically idiopathic or associated with migraine and trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (TACs) [5]. In contrast, secondary RES has been associated with upper cervical spine pathology and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction [5].

In this study, we present a case of RES in a patient with migraine, accompanied by a comprehensive review of current literature, with particular emphasis on clinical features, proposed pathophysiological mechanisms, differential diagnosis, and management strategies.

2. Case Presentation

A 35‐year‐old male with a long‐standing history of poorly controlled migraine since childhood presented to the otorhinolaryngology clinic with recurrent episodes of bilateral ear redness associated with pain. The patient had previously attended multiple clinics and had been repeatedly treated for otitis externa without clinical improvement.

At presentation, he described episodic redness of both ears, predominantly involving the left ear, accompanied by sensations of warmth, burning, and throbbing pain. Similar but milder symptoms were occasionally noted in the right ear. These episodes were temporally associated with unilateral left temporal headaches consistent with his known migraine and were accompanied by visual aura. Symptoms were intermittent, self‐limiting, and recurrent.

Exacerbating factors included migraine attacks, exposure to hot weather, and ear rubbing, whereas symptom relief was achieved with cooling measures and improvement in headache. The patient denied hearing loss, tinnitus, otorrhea, TMJ dysfunction, dental problems, neck pain, rheumatologic or joint symptoms, and nasal or allergic complaints. The patient reported a positive family history of migraine in his father.

On physical examination, bilateral erythema of the auricles with increased warmth was observed, without tenderness or fluctuation (Figure 1). Otoscopic examination revealed normal external auditory canals and intact tympanic membranes bilaterally. The remainder of the otorhinolaryngologic examination, including head and neck assessment and TMJ evaluation, was unremarkable (Figure 2).

Laboratory investigations, including routine blood tests and a rheumatologic workup for systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s syndrome, and relapsing polychondritis, were within normal limits. Computed tomography of the TMJs showed no abnormalities. After excluding alternative diagnoses such as otitis externa, erysipelas, eczematous dermatologic conditions, and relapsing polychondritis, a diagnosis of RES was established.

The patient was treated with indomethacin (25 mg once daily for 3 weeks) and magnesium supplementation (600 mg once daily) and was advised to avoid known triggering factors. He reported marked clinical improvement at 1‐month follow‐up, with complete resolution of ear redness, warmth, and burning sensations (Figure 3). No adverse or unanticipated events were reported.

3. Discussion

RES is a rare clinical entity characterized by recurrent episodes of unilateral or bilateral erythema, warmth, and burning pain of the external ear [1]. Despite increasing recognition in the literature, its underlying pathophysiology remains poorly understood [8]. Several mechanisms have been proposed and can be broadly categorized into central and peripheral mechanisms [7].

Central mechanisms primarily involve dysfunction of the brainstem trigeminoautonomic network. Goadsby and Lipton proposed the trigeminoautonomic circuit dysregulation theory, suggesting impaired regulation between trigeminal nerve activation and facial parasympathetic outflow. According to this hypothesis, stimulation of the trigeminal nerve by various triggers leads to abnormal parasympathetic responses, resulting in vasodilation and pain [4, 7, 9]. In addition, Raielli et al. proposed that activation of the trigeminovascular system leads to the release of vasoactive substances, resulting in vasodilation, erythema, and pain [4, 7, 10]. These central mechanisms are supported by the strong association between RES and headache disorders, particularly migraine.

Furthermore, peripheral mechanisms have been implicated, particularly in secondary forms of RES. Lance et al. suggested that antidromic discharge from the upper cervical nerve roots (C2‐C3), often related to cervical spine pathology, leads to the release of vasodilatory neuropeptides, resulting in auricular erythema and pain. TMJ dysfunction has also been proposed as a peripheral trigger. In this model, non‐noxious stimuli such as heat, touch, or chewing activate a local axon reflex, causing antidromic nerve discharges that result in neurogenic inflammation, vasodilation, and pain [3, 4, 7].

The diagnosis of RES remains primarily a diagnosis of exclusion. A broad differential diagnosis must be considered, including otitis externa, erysipelas, eczematous dermatologic conditions, relapsing polychondritis which preserve ear lobule, and other inflammatory or infectious causes of auricular erythema and pain [11, 12]. A comprehensive ENT examination is essential, supported by basic and targeted laboratory investigations. Evaluation for cervical spine and TMJ pathology is mandatory in suspected cases of secondary RES. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and TMJ should be considered in patients with high clinical suspicion or associated neurological symptoms [5].

Our literature review of 45 articles comprising 100 reported cases of RES (Tables 1 and 2) indicated that the condition predominantly affected females (63%), with a mean age of 36.6 years. The left ear was most commonly involved (36%), followed by bilateral involvement (24%). Primary RES was the most frequent subtype (58%), followed by secondary and mixed forms (21% each). Headache disorders were present in 62% of the cases, with migraine accounting for 44%, further supporting the involvement of trigeminoautonomic and trigeminovascular mechanisms. Identifiable triggering factors were reported in 73% of the cases, reinforcing the contribution of both central and peripheral stimuli.

Several unusual presentations highlight the heterogeneity of RES. Arjona et al. reported cases of RES following herpes zoster infection attributed to injury of the upper cervical nerve roots and a case triggered by sexual activity in a patient with Chiari Type I malformation, likely due to transient compression of the cerebellar tonsils during exertion [21]. Picco et al. described a pediatric case characterized by burning ear pain associated with subjective tinnitus, transient hearing loss, auditory hallucinations, and vertigo, despite normal ENT and audiological evaluations [27]. Drug‐induced RES has been reported, particularly in association with cytarabine (Ara‐C), an antineoplastic agent used in hematological malignancies such as acute myeloid leukemia [26, 38, 41]. Additionally, Soares et al. described RES following tympanoplasty, hypothesizing that local neuronal injury during surgery triggered symptom onset, supported by rapid symptom relief with topical lidocaine and neuropathic pain medications [44].

No specific or universally effective treatment for RES has been established. Consequently, management strategies are largely guided by the proposed underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [4, 5]. Treatment of primary RES focuses on controlling the associated headache disorder, whereas the management of secondary RES targets the underlying causes. In our case, we started the patient on magnesium supplementation and indomethacin believing that it will target migraine and TACs. Magnesium supplementation is a good option for migraines, and indomethacin is an excellent option for TACs [50, 51].

In our literature review, management strategies demonstrated considerable variability, with therapies largely targeting the presumed underlying mechanisms rather than the syndrome itself. Pharmacological treatment was primarily dominated by neuropathic pain and migraine‐oriented therapies, with gabapentin being the most frequently reported medication, followed by indomethacin and amitriptyline. This observation supports the growing evidence suggesting that neuropathic mechanisms, central sensitization, trigeminovascular activation, and migraine‐related pathways may contribute significantly to RES pathogenesis. Gabapentin was commonly utilized in cases associated with neuropathic pain, cervical pathology, or temporomandibular disorders, whereas indomethacin appeared particularly useful in patients with overlapping TACs and paroxysmal headache syndromes. Similarly, the frequent use of migraine prophylactic therapies including flunarizine, verapamil, topiramate, magnesium supplementation, triptan‐based therapies, and calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP)–related agents further reinforces the close association between RES and migraine‐spectrum disorders. Nonpharmacological interventions were similarly heterogeneous and predominantly focused on trigger avoidance and symptomatic relief. Cooling measures, including ice packs, cold environments, and cold‐water application, were frequently reported as effective acute symptomatic strategies, likely due to their vasoconstrictive effects and modulation of neurovascular activation. Furthermore, several cases required more targeted interventions directed toward identifiable secondary causes, including dental appliances for temporomandibular disorders, medication discontinuation for drug‐induced cases, nerve blocks, cervical root interventions, neurovascular decompression procedures, and other surgical approaches for structural pathology. More recently, advanced therapies such as onabotulinum toxin A have emerged as potential options for refractory cases although evidence remains limited to isolated reports.

Therapeutic outcomes remain highly variable. Many reported cases require multiple treatment trials with limited benefit, whereas others demonstrate spontaneous resolution. In selected cases of secondary RES, surgical intervention has been necessary as part of definitive management [3, 25].

A limitation in this case is that RES is a rare disease, and the diagnosis is mainly established by excluding other diseases with similar clinical presentations. Furthermore, there is currently no clear evidence supporting racial or ethnic predilection in RES. However, this approach also represents a strength, as comprehensive evaluation and careful exclusion of alternative conditions further strengthened and supported the diagnosis of RES.

4. Conclusion

RES is a rare and often underrecognized condition that should be considered in patients presenting with recurrent auricular erythema and burning pain, particularly in those with migraine. This case highlights the importance of a thorough evaluation and exclusion of alternative diagnoses to avoid misdiagnosis and mismanagement. Early recognition of RES may facilitate appropriate treatment, reduce unnecessary interventions, and improve patient outcomes.
