Decentralized Inexact Cubic Newton Method with Consensus Procedure
ARTEM AGAFONOV 1, 2, 3, ANTON NOVITSKII 3, 4, ALEXANDER ROGOZIN 3, YURY SOKOLOV 3, DMITRY KAMZOLOV 5, ALEXANDER G. DYAKONOV 2, MARTIN TAKÁČ 1, ALEXANDER GASNIKOV 3,4,6

Abstract
Distributed optimization is widely used in large-scale and privacy-preserving machine learning, where each agent stores a local objective and communicates only with its neighbors in a connected network. We study decentralized second-order optimization and focus on consensus procedures that approximately average local iterates, gradients, and Hessians through neighbor-to-neighbor communications. We propose a general Decentralized Cubic Newton method for convex optimization under L 1 L_{1} -smoothness of gradients and L 2 L_{2} -Lipschitz continuity of Hessians, and develop a theory that accurately tracks the inaccuracies caused by consensus and by disagreement between local iterates. Under these assumptions, the method matches the iteration complexity of the exact Cubic Newton method and requires only additional polylogarithmic communication-round overhead to reach the necessary consensus accuracy. We further propose an Accelerated Decentralized Cubic Newton method for strongly convex objectives and show that it matches the iteration complexity of the exact Accelerated Cubic Newton method, again with only additional polylogarithmic communication-round overhead. Finally, although the general method requires exchanging full d × d d\times d Hessian matrices, we show how it can be implemented for generalized linear models by transmitting only vectors, making the approach substantially more practical in high dimensions.

Proximal methods
. A different approach to decentralized optimization is based on ADMM-type primal-dual proximal methods
[
25

. ADMM is applied when local functions have simple structure that allows cheap computation of proximal operator
\prox_{f}^{\gamma}(x)=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y}\left(f(y)+1/(2\gamma)\|{y-x}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

Implementing a distributed second order method is nontrivial
. We now turn to second-order methods. They are algorithms that can compute not only function values and gradients, but also second order information, that are Hessians
\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x)
. Developing a distributed Newton method even in a centralized setting (i.e. with full aggregation) turns out to be nontrivial. Consider two quadratic functions
f_{1}(x)=(x+1)^{2}
and
f_{2}(x)=(2x-1)^{2}
. Let a distributed Newton method make two local steps with a following aggregation, i.e.
x_{1}^{k+1/2}=x^{k}-(\nabla^{2}f_{1}(x^{k}))^{-1}\nabla f_{1}(x^{k})
,
x_{2}^{k+1/2}=x^{k}-(\nabla^{2}f_{2}(x^{k}))^{-1}\nabla f_{2}(x^{k})
and
x^{k+1}=(x_{1}^{k+1/2}+x_{2}^{k+1/2})/2
. However, such a method will jump out of the global minimum. Indeed, local minima are
x_{1}^{*}=-1
and
x_{2}^{*}=1/2
and global minimum is
x^{*}=1/5
. Starting from
x^{0}=x^{*}
we get to
x^{1}=-1/4
. This simple example shows that a straightforward implementation of the Newton method is not convergent.

Non-distributed Newton
. A standard Newton iteration
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\gamma_{k}(\nabla^{2}f(x^{k}))^{-1}\nabla f(x^{k})
with unit stepsize
\gamma_{k}=1
is known to achieve local quadratic convergence, but is non-convergent if started from an arbitrary point
[
30
]
. Global convergence is achieved via line search for
\gamma_{k}
. In the modern literature, regularized versions of Newton method are mostly used. Cubic regularization of Newton method was proposed in
[
31
]
. Gradient regularization of Hessian was studied in
[

]
. In
[

\|{\nabla^{2}f(y)-\nabla^{2}f(x)}\|\leq\bar{L}_{2}\|{y-x}\|
), all mentioned regularized versions of Newton method achieve
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\bar{L}_{2}D^{3}/\varepsilon})

characterizes the distance to solution from starting point. The same convergence rate holds for damped Newton method, as shown in
[
35
]

Centralized Newton
. We first discuss centralized second order methods. Exchanging Hessians between the nodes is the communication bottleneck of distributed methods. In the case when local functions are loss functions of generalized linear models
[
36
,
37
]

38

Decentralized Newton
. Considering decentralized second order methods, the results in the current literature are not well developed. Newton method with assumption on closeness of local summands was derived in
[
42
]

In contrast, our proposed method relies only on standard Lipschitz continuity, making it robust to data heterogeneity.
Decentralized quasi-Newton methods were proposed in

Problem setup and notation

We consider decentralized optimization over a deterministic time-varying undirected communication network with
m
nodes. Individual graphs in the sequence need not be connected; connectivity is imposed through the contraction condition in Assumption
3
.
Each node
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
has access to a local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. The node can compute function values
f_{i}(x_{i})
, gradient values
\nabla f_{i}(x_{i})
and Hessian values
\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x_{i})
.
The global optimization objective is given in (
1
). The notation used in the paper (for non-accelerated Decentralized Cubic Newton method) is summarized in Table

Assumption 1
.

We assume that each function
f_{i}
is convex. Then the global objective
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x)
is convex as well.

Assumption 2

We assume that each function
f_{i}
is
\mu_{i}
-strongly convex with
\mu_{i}>0

f(x)=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x)

\bar{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}

Consensus routine.

We assume that nodes are connected via a deterministic time-varying network represented by a sequence of graphs
\{\mathcal{G}^{k}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
with a common vertex set
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,m\}
. With each graph, we associate a mixing matrix
{W}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

Assumption 3
.

The sequence of mixing matrices
\{{W}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

(Network compatibility) For each
k\geq 1
,
[{W}^{k}]_{ij}=0
if
(i,j)\notin\mathcal{E}^{k}
and
i\neq j
.

2.

(Double stochasticity) For each
k\geq 1
, we have
[W^{k}]_{ij}\geq 0
for any
i,j=1,\ldots,m
,
{W}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{m}
,
\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}{W}^{k}=\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{m}
is a vector of all ones.

3.

(Contraction property) There exist an integer
\tau\geq 1
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for all
k\geq\tau-1
and any matrix
{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, it holds that

\left\|{W}_{\tau}^{k}{U}-\bar{{U}}\right\|_{F}\leq(1-\lambda)\|{U}-\bar{{U}}\|_{F},

(3)

where
{W}_{\tau}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{W}^{k}{W}^{k-1}\dots{W}^{k-\tau+1}
and
\bar{{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}{U}
(a matrix where each row is the average vector
(\bar{u}^{k})^{\top}
).

The contraction property in Assumption
3
generalizes several assumptions in the literature.

•

Time-static connected graph:
{W}^{k}={W}
. In this classical case, the network is connected at every iteration, meaning
\tau=1
. The one-step contraction factor is given by
\lambda=1-\sigma_{2}({W})
, where
\sigma_{2}({W})
denotes the second largest singular value of
{W}
.

•

Sequence of connected graphs: every
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is connected. Here we also have
\tau=1
, and the parameter is defined by the worst-case spectral gap:
\lambda=1-\sup_{k\geq 0}\sigma_{2}({W}^{k})
.

•

\tau
-connected graph sequence (i.e. for every
k\geq 0
graph
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}^{k}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{k}\cup\mathcal{E}^{k+1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{E}^{k+\tau-1})
is connected
[
14
]
.
For
\tau
-connected graph sequences it holds
1-\lambda=\sup_{k\geq 0}\sigma_{\max}({W}_{\tau}^{k}-\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top})
. In this general setting, the graphs
\mathcal{G}^{k}
in the sequence are not required to be connected individually; connectivity is only required over the union of edges across any
\tau
consecutive rounds.

Remark 1
.

For time-static graphs, mixing matrix
W
can be replaced by a matrix polynomial
P_{K}(W)
of degree
K=\lceil\sqrt{\chi}\rceil
. Taking
P_{K}(W)
as a Chebyshev polynomial with correct coefficients enables to make it well-conditioned, i.e.
\chi=1/(1-\sigma_{2}(P_{K}(W)))=\mathcal{O}(1)

[
19
,
50
]
. Thus, one communication round will be replaced by
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\chi})
communication rounds, but the effective condition number will be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(1)
. As a result, for static graphs a communication complexity factor
\tau/\lambda=1/\lambda=\chi
in Table
I
reduces to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\chi})
. The only difference is to replace Consensus procedure (
4
) with accelerated consensus (see i.e. Algorithm 2 in
[
19
]
).

During every single communication round, the agents exchange information according to the rule
{U}^{k+1}={W}^{k}{U}^{k}
. In particular, the contraction property holds for
\tau
-connected graphs with Metropolis weights choice for
{W}^{k}
, i.e.

[{W}^{k}]_{ij}=\begin{cases}1/(1+\max\{q_{i}^{k},q_{j}^{k}\})&\text{if }(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{k},\\
0&\text{if }(i,j)\notin\mathcal{E}^{k},\\
1-\mathop{\textstyle\sum}\limits_{(i,m)\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}[{W}^{k}]_{im}&\text{if }i=j,\end{cases}

where

denotes the degree of node
i
in graph

.

The contraction property above ensures that repeated application of the mixing matrices drives local variables to their average.
Accordingly, in the convergence analysis we model consensus as an abstract routine and capture its effect via suitable accuracy bounds introduced below.
The contraction property will later be used to relate these bounds to the number of communication rounds.

III
Decentralized Cubic Newton

In this section, we propose a decentralized variant of the Cubic Regularized Newton method.

Local model of the objective.

We begin by introducing the local model used by each node to perform the update. This model is constructed using inexact local estimates of the first- and second-order derivatives obtained via the consensus procedure.

Definition 1

(Local inexact cubic model)
.

Given the local point
\hat{x}_{i}^{k}
and local inexact derivatives
\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k}),\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
, define the local smoothed model of the objective

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}^{k}_{i}(x;\hat{x}^{k}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k}),x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\right\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})(x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}),x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\right\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\delta_{1,k}}{2\gamma}+\left(\tfrac{\gamma\delta_{1,k}+\delta_{2,k}}{2}\right)\|x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\|^{2}+\tfrac{L}{6}\|x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\|^{3}

where
\delta_{1,k},\delta_{2,k}\geq 0
are regularization parameters and

is a smoothing parameter.

Compared to standard Taylor models (e.g.,
\phi(x;y)
or the inexact tensor model
[

), the model (
12

This constant does not affect the minimizer in (
15

[

) and suggests that similar analysis techniques could be applied.

However, a key difference in our setting is that the cubic step is performed locally at each node, while the convergence guarantees must be established for the global objective evaluated at the averaged iterate
\bar{x}^{k}
. As a result, the standard analysis cannot be applied directly, since it typically controls the descent of a single sequence of iterates, whereas here multiple local sequences are generated and only their average is used in the analysis. This leads to additional error terms, which, as we show, can be controlled by increasing the number of consensus steps.

The Method.

The proposed method is a decentralized variant of the Cubic Regularized Newton. At every iteration, each node first approximates the network average iterate by consensus, obtaining
\hat{x}_{i}^{k}
. It then computes local first- and second-order derivatives at
\hat{x}_{i}^{k}
and applies consensus again to obtain approximations of the global gradient
\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
and Hessian
\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
. Using these quantities, node
i
builds the local inexact cubic model
\hat{\omega}_{i}^{k}(x;\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
and sets
x_{i}^{k+1}
to its minimizer. The resulting method is summarized in Algorithm
1
. All scalar parameters, namely
L
,
\gamma
,
\delta_{1,k}
,
\delta_{2,k}
, and the prescribed numbers of consensus rounds, are common algorithmic parameters known to all nodes. The local computations are node-specific only through the local functions
f_{i}
and the local consensus outputs.

An iteration of the method can be visualized as in Figure

.

Starting from approximately synchronized states
\hat{x}_{i}^{k}
, obtained via consensus (
13
), each node computes local first- and second-order derivatives at
\hat{x}_{i}^{k}
and applies consensus to obtain approximations of the global gradient
\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
and Hessian
\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
. Using these quantities, node
i
constructs its local inexact cubic model
\hat{\omega}_{i}^{k}(x;\hat{x}_{i}^{k})
.

Each node then independently minimizes its local model, producing the new iterate
x_{i}^{k+1}
(see Figure
1
, red points). Since these models rely on approximate global information, the resulting points
x_{i}^{k+1}
are generally different across nodes and become spatially dispersed.

This dispersion is the primary source of error introduced by decentralization. At the next iteration, the consensus step contracts these points toward their average, producing new approximately synchronized states
\hat{x}_{i}^{k+1}
. Throughout this process, our theoretical analysis tracks the trajectory of the exact average
\bar{x}^{k}
(purple trajectory), demonstrating that it effectively performs a global cubic Newton step despite the local approximations.

The cubic subproblem (
15
) is solved locally at each node using standard routines for cubic regularization
[
31
]
. In practice, one can employ implementations such as
[
52
]
. The computational cost of solving this subproblem is typically of order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
per iteration.

As a result, the method is fully decentralized: all nodes operate in parallel using only local computations and communication with neighbors.

The convergence analysis below quantifies how these consensus errors affect the quality of the local model and the descent of the global objective.

Convergence Analysis.
We begin with a one-step descent lemma.

Lemma 2

\displaystyle f(x_{i}^{k+1})\leq\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\right.

\displaystyle\left.f(x)+\tfrac{L+\bar{L}_{2}}{6}\|x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\|^{3}\right.

(20)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\gamma\delta_{1,k}+\delta_{2,k}\right)\|x-\hat{x}_{i}^{k}\|^{2}\right\}+\tfrac{\delta_{1,k}}{\gamma}.

Next, we transform the result of Lemma

into a bound on the averaged iterate
\bar{x}^{k+1}
, which will be used to derive convergence guarantees for
f(\bar{x}^{k+1})-f(x^{*})
. Averaging (

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{m}\mathop{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{m}f(x_{i}^{k+1})\leq\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\biggl\{f(x)+\tfrac{4(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{6}\|x-\bar{x}^{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle+2(\gamma\delta_{1,k}+\delta_{2,k})\|x-\bar{x}^{k}\|^{2}\biggr\}+\tfrac{\delta_{1,k}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x},k}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1,k}+\delta_{2,k})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x},k}^{2}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\hat{\mathrm{err}}_{k}\end{subarray}},

Based on this inequality, the process guarantees that the sequence of averaged iterates

f(\bar{x}^{k+1})\leq f(\bar{x}^{k})+\tfrac{\delta_{1,k}}{\gamma}+\hat{\mathrm{err}}_{k}.

Suppose that the inaccuracy regularization parameters

and consensus errors

\delta_{1,k}\leq\delta_{1}

In this section, we present the accelerated variant of the decentralized Cubic Newton method, listed as Algorithm

.
Unlike the basic method (Algorithm
1
), the accelerated scheme maintains three local sequences

\{x_{i}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
,
\{y_{i}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, and
\{v_{i}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
Here
x_{i}^{k}
is the main iterate, obtained as the minimizer of the local cubic model,
y_{i}^{k}
is the minimizer of a local estimating function, and

v_{i}^{k}
is an interpolation point at which the local cubic model is constructed.

At iteration
k

v_{i}^{k}
, then runs consensus on
\{v_{j}^{k}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and obtains
\hat{v}_{i}^{k}
.
Next, node
i
computes the local derivatives at
\hat{v}_{i}^{k}
and uses consensus to approximate the averaged gradient and Hessian by

\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{v}_{i}^{k})
and
\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{v}_{i}^{k})
.
These quantities are then used to define a local inexact cubic model
\hat{\nu}^{k}_{i}(x;\hat{v}^{k}_{i})
. Following
[
51
]
, the accelerated cubic model contains only quadratic additional regularization:

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}^{k}_{i}(x;\hat{v}^{k}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{g}_{i}(\hat{v}_{i}^{k}),x-\hat{v}_{i}^{k}\right\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\hat{H}_{i}(\hat{v}_{i}^{k})(x-\hat{v}_{i}^{k}),x-\hat{v}_{i}^{k}\right\rangle

(34)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\delta_{2,k}}{2}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{k}\|^{2}+\tfrac{L}{6}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{k}\|^{3}.

After the cubic step is computed, the method performs one additional consensus procedure on the gradients

\{\nabla f_{j}(x_{j}^{k+1})\}_{j=1}^{m}
. This quantity is used to update the local estimating sequence
\psi_{i}^{k}(x)
, initialized as

\psi_{i}^{1}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}f(x_{i}^{1})+\tfrac{\bar{\kappa}_{2,0}}{2}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{0}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\bar{\kappa}_{3,0}}{6}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{0}\|^{3},

(35)

and updated according to

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{k+1}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{k}(x)+\tfrac{\bar{\kappa}_{2,k}-\bar{\kappa}_{2,k-1}}{2}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{0}\|^{2}

(36)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\bar{\kappa}_{3,k}-\bar{\kappa}_{3,k-1}}{6}\|x-\hat{v}_{i}^{0}\|^{3}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\alpha_{k}}{A_{k}}\left(f(x_{i}^{k+1})+\left\langle\hat{g}_{i}(x_{i}^{k+1}),x-x_{i}^{k+1}\right\rangle\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{2}\|x-x_{i}^{k+1}\|^{2}\right),

where

A_{0}=1,~~A_{k}=\textstyle{\prod}_{j=1}^{k}(1-\alpha_{j}).

(37)

The particular choice of coefficients
\alpha_{k}
is specified later in the convergence analysis.

Although
\psi_{i}^{k}(x)
explicitly contains function values, the algorithm only requires its minimizer.
Therefore, additive constants in
\psi_{i}^{k}(x)
do not affect the actual updates.

Convergence Guarantees and Proof Sketch.

In this subsection, we focus on the strongly-convex setting and provide convergence guarantee for Accelerated Decentralized Cubic Newton (Algorithm
2
). Our analysis follows the estimating-sequence framework for accelerated second-order and high-order methods

[
49
,
53
,
51
]
.

The main goal is to establish lower and upper bounds on the local estimating sequences
\psi_{i}^{k}(x)
defined in (
36
). In the exact single-node setting, estimating-sequence arguments typically yield bounds of the form
[
49
]

\displaystyle\tfrac{f(x^{k+1})}{A_{k}}\leq\psi^{k+1}(y^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\psi^{k+1}(x^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{f(x^{*})}{A_{k}}+\mathcal{O}\left(L\|x^{0}-x^{*}\|^{3}\right),

which imply

f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{*})\leq A_{k}\mathcal{O}\left(L\|x^{0}-x^{*}\|^{3}\right).

Thus, the convergence rate is determined by the choice of the weights
A_{k}
. In the strongly-convex case, one may choose
A_{k}=(1-\alpha)^{k}
, which leads to a linear rate of the form

f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{*})\leq(1-\alpha)^{k}\mathcal{O}\left(L\|x^{0}-x^{*}\|^{3}\right).

Using the
\mu
-strong convexity bound

\|x^{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{2}{\mu}\bigl(f(x^{0})-f(x^{*})\bigr),

this can be rewritten as

f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{*})\leq(1-\alpha)^{k}\,\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{L\bar{R}}{\mu}\left(f(x^{0})-f(x^{*})\right)\right),

where
\bar{R}
is a bound on the distance to the solution. Our decentralized analysis recovers the same estimating-sequence mechanism at the local level, with additional error terms caused by consensus inaccuracies and disagreement between local iterates.

At a high level, the proof proceeds in three steps. First, we establish upper and lower bounds on the local estimating sequences
\psi_{i}^{k}(x)
. Second, we control the additional consensus and disagreement errors. Third, we combine these results to obtain an intermediate linear-rate estimate with accumulated inexactness terms and then specialize it to explicit per-iteration conditions ensuring an
\varepsilon
-solution.

The detailed proofs are deferred to Appendix
B-B
. In particular, Appendix
B-B
first proves an intermediate estimate (Theorem
6
) with accumulated consensus and disagreement terms, and then derives the explicit sufficient conditions stated below in Theorem
3
.

To obtain an explicit accelerated convergence bound, we additionally assume that iterates remain in a bounded neighborhood of the solution. Assumptions of this type are standard in the analysis of accelerated globally convergent high-order methods with inexact gradients; see, e.g.,
[
51
, Assumption 2]
and
[
54
, (Opt)]
. In contrast, for related acceleration schemes with unbiased stochastic gradients, such a boundedness assumption is not required; see
[
55
]
.

Assumption 6
.

Let
\{x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k},v_{i}^{k},\hat{v}_{i}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by Algorithm
2
. Assume that there exists
\bar{R}>0
such that for all
k\geq 1
,

\max_{i\in[m]}\Bigl\{\|x_{i}^{k}-x^{*}\|,\|y_{i}^{k}-x^{*}\|,\|\hat{v}_{i}^{k}-x^{*}\|,\|v_{i}^{k}-x^{*}\|\Bigr\}\leq\bar{R}.

(41)

We initialize the method from a common point
x_{i}^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}x^{0}
for all
i\in[m]
. Since
v_{i}^{0}=x_{i}^{0}
and consensus leaves identical inputs unchanged, we have
\hat{v}_{i}^{0}=x^{0}
for all
i\in[m]
. Hence,

\|\hat{v}_{i}^{0}-x^{*}\|=\|v_{i}^{0}-x^{*}\|=\|x^{0}-x^{*}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}R.

We now state the main strongly-convex convergence result for the Algorithm

Theorem 3

Let Assumptions

This quadratic dependence on the dimension

This observation motivates the design of more communication-efficient implementations, in particular via structured or compressed Hessian representations. Such improvements are possible, for example, in the case of generalized linear models, which we discuss in the next section.

V
Implementation Aspects for Generalized Linear Models

The main limitation of proposed Algorithms
1
and
2
is the exchange of Hessians between the nodes of the network. Exchanging matrices incurs a high communication cost. A possible way to overcome communication limitations is running the method on loss functions of generalized linear models
[
36
]
. Consider a problem of decentralized training. Each node locally holds a dataset characterized by
\left\{a_{ij}\right\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
, where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are feature vectors. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote model weights and let local functions have form
f_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f_{ij}(x)
and
f_{ij}(x)=\varphi_{ij}(a_{ij}^{\top}x)
. Matrices of second derivatives are computed as
\nabla^{2}f_{ij}(x)=\varphi_{ij}^{\prime\prime}(a_{ij}^{\top}x)a_{ij}a_{ij}^{\top}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to once communicate datasets
\left\{a_{ij}\right\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
to the neighboring nodes and then only exchange vectors
h_{i}(x)=(\varphi_{i1}^{\prime\prime}(a_{i1}^{\top}x)\ldots\varphi_{i\ell}^{\prime\prime}(a_{i\ell}^{\top}x))
. Therefore, the cost of a single communication will be reduced from
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
. Moreover, work
[
36
]
also proposed to exchange compressed vectors
\mathcal{C}(h_{i}(x))
, where the compression operator leaves only several nonzero components of
h_{i}(x)
and zeroes out all the rest entries. Using compressed information exchange additionally decreases communication complexity.

VI
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we studied consensus-based decentralized second-order optimization and proposed Cubic Regularized Newton methods for both convex and strongly convex settings. Our analysis explicitly tracks the inexactness arising from consensus errors and disagreement between local iterates, and shows that these effects can be controlled to recover the iteration complexity of exact Cubic Newton methods up to logarithmic communication overhead.

We deliberately do not focus on communication efficiency and restrict our attention to a relatively simple setting of generalized linear models, which allows us to highlight the core algorithmic and analytical ideas without additional technical overhead.

Several directions remain open. First, it would be interesting to develop communication-efficient extensions based on Hessian compression, low-rank or quasi-Newton surrogates, and lazy or randomized Hessian updates. Second, our accelerated analysis relies on a boundedness assumption on the iterates; removing this assumption would further strengthen the theory. Finally, it would be natural to extend the present framework beyond the deterministic convex setting, including stochastic objectives, nonconvex problems, and broader classes of decentralized second-order methods.

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\Biggl\{\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}\Biggr\}.

Proof.

From (

is a minimizer, it is enough to consider points on the segment between

and
x^{*}
, using the definitions of

, we obtain for any

we obtain:

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f\bigl(\bar{x}^{k}+\alpha_{k}(x^{*}-\bar{x}^{k})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+2\alpha_{k}^{2}(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\|x^{*}-\bar{x}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}\|x^{*}-\bar{x}^{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl\{\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}\biggr\}.

By the strong convexity of

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{k})f(\bar{x}^{k})+\alpha_{k}f(x^{*})

\displaystyle\quad-\tfrac{\bar{\mu}\alpha_{k}(1-\alpha_{k})}{2}\|\bar{x}^{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\alpha_{k}^{2}(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\|\bar{x}^{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{k}^{3}\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\|\bar{x}^{k}-x^{*}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl\{\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}\biggr\}.

Subtracting

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{k+1})

\displaystyle-f(x^{*})\leq(1-\alpha_{k})\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{k})-f(x^{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|\bar{x}^{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}\biggl[\bar{\mu}(1-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle-4\alpha_{k}(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})-\alpha_{k}^{2}\tfrac{4(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\|\bar{x}^{k}-x^{*}\|\biggr]

This inequality holds if we ensure that

\alpha^{2}\tfrac{4(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}D\leq\bar{\mu}/4

\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, the first condition becomes

. These conditions are satisfied by our choice of
\alpha
in (

Therefore, the bracket is non-negative, and we have:

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{k+1})-f(x^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{k})-f(x^{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}.

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}^{N+1})-f(x^{*})\leq(1-\alpha)^{N+1}\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{0})-f(x^{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle+\biggl[\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}\biggr]\mathop{\textstyle\sum}_{i=0}^{N}(1-\alpha)^{i}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)^{N+1}\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{0})-f(x^{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\biggl\{\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma}+\tfrac{2(L+\bar{L}_{2})}{3}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{3}+2(\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}^{2}\biggr\},

where in the last step we used the geometric series bound

Proof.

Firstly:

\displaystyle\gamma\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma\bigl(\hat{\Delta}_{g|\hat{x}}+2\bar{L}_{1}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}\bigr)+\bigl(\hat{\Delta}_{H|\hat{x}}+2\bar{L}_{2}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{32}+\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{16}+2\left(\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{64(\bar{L}_{1}/D+\bar{L}_{2})}\right)\left(\tfrac{\bar{L}_{1}}{D}+\bar{L}_{2}\right)

where the last inequality holds as

. Hence, we obtain:

\gamma\hat{\Delta}_{g|\hat{x}}+\hat{\Delta}_{H|\hat{x}}+2(\gamma\bar{L}_{1}+\bar{L}_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}\leq\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{16\alpha},

and then:

\alpha\leq\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{16\bigl(\gamma\hat{\Delta}_{g|\hat{x}}+\hat{\Delta}_{H|\hat{x}}+2(\gamma\bar{L}_{1}+\bar{L}_{2})\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}\bigr)}.

and using

, we get:

\displaystyle\delta_{2}=\hat{\Delta}_{H|\hat{x}}+2\bar{L}_{2}\hat{\Delta}_{\hat{x}}\leq\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{16}+2\bar{L}_{2}\left(\tfrac{\alpha\varepsilon}{24\bar{L}_{1}D}\right)=\tfrac{\bar{\mu}}{16}+\tfrac{\alpha\varepsilon\bar{L}_{2}}{12\bar{L}_{1}D}.

Now, by the result of Theorem

f(\bar{x}^{N+1})-f(x^{*})\leq(1-\alpha)^{N+1}\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{0})-f(x^{*})\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\left(\delta_{1}D+\hat{\mathrm{err}}\right),

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times\bigl(f(\bar{x}^{0})-f(x^{*})\bigr)=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof of Remark
3
.

Proof.

In the general analysis (

to keep iterates bounded. However, in the strongly convex regime, the error accumulation is bounded uniformly: according to Theorem
5
, unrolling the recurrence for any
k\geq 0
(in particular, for
k=N
), we obtain:

Hence, our method is monotone up to a small inaccuracy

(

Theorem

.

,

,

, and Assumption

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{H|\hat{v},k}

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{g|x,k+1}

(126d)

run with parameters chosen as for all

,

Proof.

126a
)–(
126c
