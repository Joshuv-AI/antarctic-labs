A high-resolution dataset on costs and greenhouse gas emissions of battery recycling in China
Authors: Haoyang Li, Han Hao, Xin Sun, Hao Dou, Lang Mai, Tingyuan Kang, Boyu Liu, Zongwei Liu, Fuquan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of electric vehicle deployment is expected to lead to a surge in retired batteries over the coming decades, drawing increasing attention to battery recycling as part of sustainability transitions. However, existing assessments of battery recycling costs and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are often conducted at coarse spatiotemporal scales, limiting their ability to capture regional heterogeneity and long-term dynamics. Here, we present a high-resolution dataset of costs and GHG emissions for hydrometallurgical recycling processes in China, covering 337 prefecture-level cities from 2020 to 2060 and disaggregated across six typical battery cathode chemistries. The dataset is constructed using a process-based framework that integrates spatial and temporal heterogeneities in battery retirements, factor costs, and energy mixes. By providing a consistent and structured representation of recycling impacts, this dataset enables comparative analyses across regions and time horizons and supports further research on secondary material impact assessment and recycling network design.

Background & Summary

The rapid expansion of electric vehicle (EV) deployment has been accompanied by a growing stock of traction batteries1,2. As these batteries reach the end of their service life, annual retirements are expected to increase to the terawatt-hour scale over the coming decades3. To mitigate the environmental risks associated with end-of-life batteries and to recover critical metals such as lithium, nickel, and cobalt, battery recycling has received increasing attention worldwide4,5. A wide range of battery recycling technologies is under active development and evaluation for technical and economic performance6,7. From a sustainability perspective, the use of recycled materials is widely recognized as an effective means of reducing the carbon footprint of battery supply chains8–10. Consequently, whether and to what extent battery recycling can reduce the climate change impacts associated with battery materials has become a key consideration in policy discussions11–14.

Existing assessments of battery recycling costs and climate change impacts are commonly based on techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) approaches15,16. Widely used tools and models, such as EverBatt, provide process-level estimates of recycling performance under standardized assumptions for representative countries or regions17. In addition, a growing body of studies has reported cost and emission estimates for recycling batteries with typical cathode chemistries, such as lithium iron phosphate (LFP) and nickel-cobalt-manganese oxide (NCM), across the main recycling routes, including pyrometallurgical18,19, hydrometallurgical20–22, and direct recycling processes23,24. Pyrometallurgical recycling uses high-temperature treatment to recover valuable metals, hydrometallurgical recycling relies on leaching, separation, and purification steps, and direct recycling aims to preserve and regenerate cathode active materials. Among these routes, hydrometallurgical recycling is currently a mature and widely applied industrial route, making it well suited to recycling impact assessment based on industrially grounded process data.

Recycling costs and environmental impacts can vary substantially across regions and over time due to differences in factor costs, energy mixes, and other local conditions8,10,25. Yet existing assessments generally rely on national or regional average assumptions26–28, offering limited resolution for city-level analyses. Most current assessments are static, providing only snapshot estimates. They do not capture how recycling costs and environmental impacts may evolve over time as deployment scales, technologies mature, and external conditions change. As a result, existing studies are not well suited to high-resolution assessment under nationwide spatiotemporal heterogeneity or to applications in recycling network design. In addition, existing assessment frameworks tend to focus on established recycling routes. Emerging approaches, such as upcycling pathways that recover higher-value materials through more integrated, end-to-end processes, remain underrepresented despite their growing industrial relevance.

To address these gaps, we construct the dataset using process-based TEA and LCA approaches, explicitly incorporating spatial and temporal heterogeneities in battery retirements, factor costs, and energy mixes. The dataset reports recycling costs and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with hydrometallurgical processes with direct precursor synthesis, resolved by six typical battery chemistries and at the prefecture level in mainland China over the period 2020–2060. This configuration represents an industrially relevant recovery pathway that links battery recycling to secondary precursor production. Recycling costs are further decomposed into ten components following a classification framework that distinguishes capital and operating costs, while GHG emissions are reported across three components based on emission sources. By providing high-resolution, process-based battery recycling impacts across cities, years, and battery chemistries, the dataset enables allocation-based assessment of secondary material impacts and provides a consistent basis for spatially explicit recycling network design.

Methods

The dataset construction followed a structured workflow that assembled information from multiple data sources, transformed raw inputs into process- and city-specific parameters, and applied process-based accounting to quantify recycling costs and GHG emissions, as outlined in Fig. 1. Specifically, data from official statistics, industry reports, power sector, and peer-reviewed literature were preprocessed and parameterized to derive process parameters, factor costs, and grid emission factors. These inputs were subsequently integrated within a process-based accounting framework that quantified recycling costs through techno-economic analysis and GHG emissions through life cycle assessment.

System boundary

This dataset quantified the unit costs and GHG emissions of recycling retired lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) using an industrial-scale hydrometallurgical route. The system boundary was limited to on-site material recovery operations within the recycling facility, including battery dismantling, pyrolysis, shredding, leaching, purification, extraction, and crystallization. Upstream activities such as removal of batteries from vehicles, storage, and transportation, and downstream processes after precursor synthesis were excluded.

The dataset covered mainland China, including 337 prefecture-level administrative units, from 2020 to 2060. Six representative LIB chemistries were considered: lithium iron phosphate (LFP), lithium manganese oxide (LMO), nickel-cobalt-manganese oxides with low-, medium-, and high-nickel content (NCM-L, N:C:M = 1:1:1; NCM-M, 6:2:2 or 5:2:3; NCM-H, 8:1:1 or 9:0.5:0.5), and lithium nickel-cobalt-aluminum oxide (NCA).

Data sources

The dataset integrated information from multiple sources, including official statistics, industry reports, power sector data, and peer-reviewed literature. Battery retirement volumes were derived from the Transport Impact Model (TIM), which projects the installation volume of LIBs based on Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth, vehicle sales elasticity, EV penetration, and powertrain technology progress29–32. TIM provided time-resolved trajectories of vehicle stock, sales, and battery retirements at the prefecture level, forming the basis for estimating annual volumes of retired batteries from 2020 to 2060. The translation from installed capacity to retirement flows was based on a degradation-informed lifetime representation that jointly accounts for calendar aging and cycle aging, rather than assuming a single exogenous lifetime33,34.

The recycling process was based on mature, industrial-scale hydrometallurgical practice documented in Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) reports and operated by a leading battery recycling enterprise, with reported technical recovery rates exceeding 95% for lithium and over 99% for cobalt and nickel35. Unlike conventional routes that recover nickel, cobalt, and manganese as sulfates or chlorides, the process considered here was configured for the direct synthesis of NCM precursor as the final product. High-nickel NCM precursor (NCM 811) was adopted as the benchmark output due to its higher economic value and broad relevance in contemporary battery manufacturing.

Cost-related inputs included labor wages, industrial land prices, equipment prices, utility prices (industrial electricity, natural gas, and water), and prices of process chemicals. Manufacturing wage data were obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook 202436, industrial land prices from the Qianzhan Database37, and equipment prices from Overseadia38. Prices of basic reagents used in hydrometallurgy such as sulfuric acid were sourced from SunSirs39, while prices of chemicals containing critical metals including lithium, nickel, and cobalt were obtained from the Shanghai Metals Market (SMM)40. Industrial electricity tariffs, incorporating peak-valley pricing and capacity payments, were compiled from public announcements released by the State Grid Corporation of China (SGCC) and the China Southern Power Grid Company Limited (CSG)41,42. Industrial natural gas and water prices were collected from announcements issued by the National Development and Reform Commission of China43. Grid emission factors were compiled from the literature and the China Regional Power Grids Carbon Dioxide Emission Factors published by the China Academy of Environmental Planning under the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China44,45. GHG emissions associated with other energy inputs and their CO2-equivalent values were calculated using GREET46–48. Together, these sources provided spatially differentiated factor costs for battery recycling and GHG-related parameters for electricity and other energy inputs across China.

Data preprocessing and parameterization

Process inventories for battery recycling were constructed using a process-based accounting framework that represented industrial-scale hydrometallurgical recycling operations. The inventory was organized around the recycling process flow and plant-level operating parameters, including production capacity, operating time, labor requirements, land use, equipment and utility requirements, as well as material inputs and outputs for different battery chemistries. Parameter values were derived and calibrated using information from EIA reports for mature recycling facilities. All estimates in the dataset were based on a single reference plant capacity of 100,000 tonnes, adopted as a representative industrial-scale benchmark to ensure comparability across cities, years, and battery chemistries; however, this standardized assumption does not capture plant-size heterogeneity or associated scale effects in real-world recycling systems.

Using this framework, we parameterized the material and energy requirements associated with recycling one kilogram of retired battery for each chemistry, forming the basis for subsequent cost and GHG accounting. To ensure consistency across heterogeneous data sources, all cost-related inputs were harmonized to a common spatial resolution of 337 prefecture-level administrative units. City-level data were used whenever available. When cost information was only reported at the provincial level, province-average values were assigned uniformly to all prefecture-level units within the corresponding province. All monetary inputs were converted to constant 2023 US Dollars (USD) before further analysis. Observed factor prices for 2023 were used as baseline values, and costs reported in Chinese Yuan (CNY) were converted to USD using the average 2023 exchange rate of 1 USD = 7.05 CNY. This harmonization strategy ensured full spatial coverage while preserving observed regional cost differentials.

GHG emission factors for electricity consumption were processed in a manner consistent with the cost data harmonization. Provincial grid emission factors published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment were used for the period from 2020 to 2035. Projections compiled from the peer-reviewed literature were used to extend emission factors to 2060 and were also employed to cross-check the consistency of trends and assumptions for the 2020–2035 period, ensuring temporal continuity. In regions where grid-specific data were limited or unavailable, emission factors were inferred from neighboring power grids with similar generation structures under conservative assumptions. This approach maintained smooth temporal transitions in grid carbon intensity while retaining complete spatial representation. Here, revenue referred to gross sales income from recycled products, rather than net profit. Output prices were treated as fixed benchmark values in the cost accounting framework, rather than as market forecasts, so as to avoid conflating process-level cost evolution with exogenous volatility in product prices. Accordingly, the revenue-linked cost terms in the dataset reflect a benchmark economic setting rather than projected market dynamics. General and administrative costs were assumed to be 65% of labor costs, capturing overheads associated with management, administration, and supporting services.

Spatial variation in recycling costs was represented primarily through the factor cost terms expected to capture the most important local differences across prefecture-level cities. Specifically, land, labor, utilities, waste disposal, and G&A were differentiated at the city level using location-specific input data. By contrast, several other cost components were treated as spatially uniform under the modelling assumptions. Plant-related costs were linked to equipment procurement and therefore were not differentiated at the prefecture level, while maintenance and insurance were derived proportionally from those costs. Raw material prices were also treated as spatially uniform, and R&D costs were calculated as a fixed proportion of revenue rather than being city-specific. This treatment was intended to capture the most influential local factor-cost variation in the dataset while maintaining a transparent and tractable accounting framework.

Learning effects were applied at the component level to reflect heterogeneous learning potentials across different cost categories. A learning rate of 2% was assigned to cost terms associated with land, raw materials, utilities, waste disposal, and R&D, reflecting the limited scope for cost decline in these categories under a relatively mature industrial hydrometallurgical route. Accordingly, these terms were assumed to decrease only modestly with accumulated experience. A learning rate of 5% was assigned to plant-related terms represented by ISBL and OSBL, which in turn affected annualized plant costs as well as maintenance and insurance costs. This choice captured moderate learning effects associated with large-scale plant deployment, including gains from engineering standardization, repeated project delivery, equipment procurement, and installation efficiency improvements. A higher learning rate of 10% was applied to labor costs, with G&A costs declining proportionally through their linkage to labor. This higher value reflected the substantial scope for learning in labor-intensive activities, where accumulated operational experience, improved worker proficiency, and the gradual substitution of manual tasks by mechanized or automated processes were expected to drive faster cost reductions.

Learning was driven by nationally aggregated cumulative collected retired battery volumes to reflect spillover effects across firms and locations. In the implementation, LFP was linked to its own cumulative collected volume, whereas the other five chemistries were linked to the cumulative collected volume of non-LFP batteries, because these chemistries are processed through a different class of recycling lines associated with cobalt- or nickel-containing cathode systems, rather than the distinct recovery route used for LFP batteries.

Spatial variation in recycling GHG emissions was represented primarily through provincial differences in the emission context. Provincial emission factors were used in the accounting framework, and all prefecture-level cities within the same province-level administrative region were assigned the same emission factor. The reported GHG components in the dataset, processing, upstream materials, and waste treatment, thus retained spatial differences across provinces, while remaining subject to the same provincial-level assumptions within each province. This treatment was intended to reflect the dominant regional heterogeneity relevant to recycling GHG emissions while maintaining consistency in the process-based accounting framework.

A learning rate of 2% was assigned to emission terms associated with processing, upstream materials, and waste treatment. This choice reflected the relatively mature state of the industrial hydrometallurgical route, under which large emission reductions from learning alone were not expected. Instead, these emission terms were allowed to decline only modestly with accumulated experience, through incremental reductions in material and utility requirements and more efficient waste management. Learning was driven by the same nationally aggregated cumulative collected retired battery volumes used in the cost accounting, with separate learning trajectories specified for LFP and non-LFP chemistries. Changes in electricity emission factors were modeled separately through grid decarbonization assumptions.

A cut-off life cycle convention was adopted for retired lithium-ion batteries, under which end-of-life batteries entered the recycling system with zero upstream burdens. Accordingly, the reported recycling emissions captured only processes occurring within the recycling facility and associated waste treatment, as defined by the system boundary, and were independent of both upstream battery manufacturing stages and downstream use of recycled materials in subsequent material or battery production.

Data Records

The dataset is deposited in the figshare repository50 and consists of two Excel workbooks: Automotive_Material_Recycling_China.xlsx and Definitions.xlsx. Automotive_Material_Recycling_China.xlsx is the main data file and contains three worksheets: Notes, Cost, and GHG. The Notes worksheet provides a brief overview of the dataset and explains the organization of the data files and worksheets, as summarized in Table 7. The Cost and GHG worksheets contain the core records for recycling costs and recycling GHG emissions, respectively. In both Cost and GHG worksheets, each row represents one specific combination of a prefecture-level city, a battery chemistry, and a recycling cost or GHG emission component. The first five columns identify the region, battery chemistry, component, indicator, and unit. The remaining columns report the corresponding values at five-year intervals from 2020 to 2060.

Technical Validation

Internal consistency was assessed using a uniform set of accounting checks applied across all cities, years, and battery chemistries. For both recycling costs and GHG emissions, component-level values were verified against the predefined decomposition frameworks, and aggregated totals were reproduced by summing their corresponding subcomponents for each city-year-chemistry entry. All reported values were checked to ensure non-negativity and dimensional consistency with their assigned units (USD per tonne for costs; t CO2-eq per tonne for GHG emissions). The resulting data matrix provided complete coverage of all 337 prefecture-level cities over the full study period from 2020 to 2060.

Consistency was further examined along the major data axes of chemistry, geography, and time. Cross-chemistry comparisons were checked against the expected differences implied by the underlying process inventory, including the higher upstream material requirements and associated burdens of nickel- and cobalt-containing chemistries relative to LFP. Cross-city variation was also examined for consistency with the spatial heterogeneity embedded in factor prices and grid emission factors. In addition, temporal trajectories were reviewed to ensure smooth evolution over time without spurious discontinuities, in line with the applied learning curve and the projected evolution of grid emission factors. Together, these checks supported the interpretation of the dataset as a self-consistent accounting system that can be compared across cities, years, and battery chemistries.

Robustness was evaluated by testing whether the main spatial, temporal, and technological patterns remained stable under plausible variations in key modeling inputs. Perturbations to baseline factor prices changed absolute cost levels but did not materially affect the overall spatial gradients across cities or the cross-chemistry ordering of costs, indicating that the observed cost structure primarily reflected systematic differences in process requirements rather than sensitivity to specific price assumptions. The dataset was also tested under alternative learning rate specifications. Although learning rates influenced the absolute level and long-term trajectory of both costs and GHG emissions, they did not materially change the relative positioning of cities or the overall ordering of battery chemistries. A similar pattern was observed for GHG emissions: alternative grid emission factor trajectories affected the magnitude and pace of emission reductions, but the main cross-city contrasts and cross-chemistry differences remained broadly consistent. These results indicated that the dominant emission patterns were governed by regional energy mixes rather than artifacts of a specific parameter setting.

To assess external plausibility, the reported recycling cost and GHG emission estimates were compared with results from the EverBatt model under a comparable plant-scale setting17. EverBatt provided point estimates based on a conventional hydrometallurgical recycling configuration. Its reported costs were expressed in 2022 USD, but the underlying configuration was taken to represent contemporary operating conditions. For comparability, EverBatt cost estimates were adjusted for inflation and converted to a 2023 price basis. Under this comparison, EverBatt estimates generally fell within or close to the ranges reported in this dataset across battery chemistries, while preserving broadly consistent cross-chemistry rankings, as shown in Tables 8 and 9. This comparison supported the external plausibility of the reported magnitudes. The generally higher costs in this dataset reflected differences in system boundary, process configuration, and underlying industrial input prices relative to EverBatt. In particular, this dataset represented an integrated industrial setup spanning dismantling to direct precursor synthesis, while spatial heterogeneity further broadened the reported ranges across cities.
