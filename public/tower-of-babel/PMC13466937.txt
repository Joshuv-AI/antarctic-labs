Increased MAP-1 and lectin complement activation capacity in Klinefelter syndrome
Authors: Simon Chang, Jesper Just, Anne Skakkebæk, Emma B Hasselholm, Christian Nielsen, Peter Garred, Claus H Gravholt, Anna-Marie Bloch Münster, Yaseelan Palarasah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextKlinefelter syndrome (KS) is a congenital condition linked to immune dysregulation and metabolic dysfunction with an increased risk of autoimmune, metabolic, and cardiovascular diseases.ObjectiveWe examined complement activation through the lectin pathway in men with KS and its relationships with hypogonadism, body composition, and fibrinolytic function.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study comparing men with KS with age-matched controls. We measured lectin pathway complement activation capacity, individual lectin complement factors including the lectin pathway regulator MAP-1, and the main complement split product, C3dg, using ELISAs. Body composition was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and fibrin clot lysis was analyzed via turbidometry. Additionally, we analyzed skeletal muscle mRNA expression of the MASP1 gene splice variant MASP1-201, which encodes MAP-1.ResultsLectin pathway activation capacity was higher in 45 men with KS compared to 45 male controls (mean ± SD, 106.2 ± 51.6% vs 64.0 ± 30.1%, P < .0001). Serum MAP-1 levels were also elevated in KS patients. MASP1-201 expression was increased in KS. Serum C3dg levels were not different between KS and controls. The lectin pathway activation capacity was not strongly linked to sex hormone levels and only had a weak association with body fat. The lectin pathway contributed to fibrin clot lysis variability in KS, and levels of the fibrinogen–C3dg complex were similar in both KS and controls.ConclusionMen with KS demonstrate increased lectin complement pathway activation capacity, and increased MAP-1 and MASP1-201 expression. Altered immune function could be a specific characteristic of KS contributing to the comorbidity burden.

Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY; KS) is a congenital condition associated with a high comorbidity burden (1). Men with KS develop hypergonadotropic hypogonadism with low testosterone levels and obesity with accumulation of visceral fat (1). This trajectory of metabolic dysfunction has long been believed to be the main factor causing high rates of cardiometabolic disorders in KS. However, emerging evidence suggests that specific genetic modifications in KS contribute to the clinical phenotype and comorbidity risk independent of hypogonadism (2-4). Also, it is becoming increasingly evident that changes in immune function affect overall homeostasis and the risk of developing a wide spectrum of diseases beyond classical autoimmune diseases, including neurological, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases (5). Both sex hormones and chromosomal sex affect immune function and the risk of autoimmune disease (6), with potential benefits to immune function from correcting long-term hypogonadism (7). KS is associated with an increased risk of autoimmune diseases and altered immune function (1, 4, 8-10), suggesting dysregulation of immune pathways. Some studies have indicated that immune function in KS could be driven by gene dosage effects related to the X chromosome (11). However, it remains unclear how and to what extent hypogonadism, testosterone replacement therapy (TRT), or genetic predisposition contribute to immune function in KS.

The complement system is a key part of the innate and adaptive immune system and plays a role in pathological pathways leading to different diseases (12), impacting the risk of diabetes (13), cardiovascular disease (14), and thrombosis (15). Obesity is a well-established general disease risk factor and pro-inflammatory marker that may contribute to increased complement activation (16). Men with KS present with increased adipose tissue accumulation, even after correction of hypogonadism (2). Furthermore, increased activation potential of the complement system could contribute to the 20% lifetime risk of venous thrombosis seen in KS (17). This risk is comparable to some classical thrombophilias, far exceeding the risk in other thrombosis-prone conditions, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, and is on par with the most thrombogenic types of cancer (18). Several proteins in the complement system have been shown to impact the formation and resolution of venous thrombi in a pro-coagulative and anti-fibrinolytic manner (19-25), promoting immunothrombosis.

In the innate immune system, the lectin pathway of complement is responsible for recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns via pattern recognition molecules (PRMs), such as mannose-binding lectin (MBL), ficolins, and collectins. Alterations in the activation and regulation of the lectin pathway have been implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases (26). In this study, we investigated the potential for activation of the lectin pathway of the complement system and associations with testosterone, body composition, and fibrinolysis in KS.

Materials and methods

Participants

Men with non-mosaic KS ± TRT of 18 to 70 years of age were eligible for inclusion and were matched by age and years of education to male controls without any history of TRT, as described elsewhere (27). The exclusion criteria included prior thrombosis, current anticoagulant therapy or use of platelet inhibitors, current use of narcotics, diabetes mellitus, and prior severe head trauma. For this study, all participants were examined at inclusion in a cross-sectional design. All participants were examined in a clinically stable condition without signs of acute infection or inflammatory disease, and none received medications known to directly modulate complement activation.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Central Denmark Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics (1-10-72-131-15) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (1-16-02-47215). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and the study was registered with Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02526628).

Blood sampling

Morning blood samples were collected from an antecubital vein after overnight fasting.

Evaluation of complement activity and individual factors

Plasma levels of complement PRMs (MBL, ficolin-2, ficolin-3, and collectin-11), MAP-1, and the complement activation split product C3dg were measured in citrated plasma samples using previously described sandwich ELISAs (28-35). In brief, sandwich ELISAs for MBL, ficolin-2, ficolin-3, collectin-11, MAP-1, and C3dg were performed as follows: MaxiSorp 96-well ELISA plates (Nunc, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, Denmark) were coated with in-house produced monoclonal capture antibodies specific for each analyte: anti-MBL (clone HYB131-1) (RRID:AB_416087), anti-ficolin-2 (clone FCN216) (RRID:AB_3739529), anti-ficolin-3 (clone FCN334) (RRID:AB_3739532), anti-collectin-11 (clone HYB17) (RRID:AB_3739533), anti-MAP-1 (clone 20C4) (RRID:AB_3739521), and anti-C3dg (clone 16-39-06) (RRID:AB_3739535). After blocking with 1% BSA in TBS-T, plasma samples and standards were applied in duplicate.

Bound antigen was detected using biotinylated in-house produced monoclonal antibodies—anti-MBL (clone HYB131-1), anti-ficolin-2 (clone FCN219), anti-ficolin-3 (clone FCN334), anti-collectin-11 (clone HYB15) (RRID:AB_3739536), anti-MAP-1 (clone 8B3), and C3dg using biotinylated polyclonal rabbit antibodies (anti-C3dg antibody) (DAKO A0063) (RRID:AB_578478), followed by streptavidin-HRP. Plates were developed with o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (OPD), and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

These assays were optimized for automated analysis in a 384-well format on the Biomek FX robotic system (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA).

The lectin pathway activation capacity, which is the functional MBL-induced activity of the lectin pathway of the complement system, was assessed as previously described (36). In brief, MaxiSorp ELISA plates were coated with mannan (Sigma catalog no. 7204) (MBL-mediated lectin pathway). Serum samples were diluted in lectin-specific TBS buffer (10 mM Tris, 145 mM NaCl; 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mg/mL SPS, pH 7.4) with 0.05% Tween-20. Complement activation on the plates was measured as C3 deposition and detected using mouse anti-C3 (measured by monoclonal anti-human C3, clone C3 F1–8 at 2 µg/mL) (RRID:AB_416045), an antibody described previously (36) followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG pAb (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK). Samples were run in duplicate, and lectin pathway activation capacity was expressed as a percentage relative to the capacity obtained from a standard pool of serum from 10 healthy individuals. The plates were developed with OPD and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm (32, 37).

Fibrinogen, fibrin clot lysis, and fibrinogen–C3dg complex

Total fibrinogen concentrations were measured in citrated plasma using a commercially available immunoturbidimetric assay (STA Liatest Fibrinogen, Diagnostica Stago, Asnières-sur-Seine, France). The assay quantifies fibrinogen based on the formation of antigen–antibody complexes, and measurements were performed on a STA-R Max automated coagulation analyzer (Stago) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Results were reported in g/L using the instrument's built-in calibration standards (38), and fibrin clot lysis was assessed as the change in turbidity per hour after application of a lysis mixture containing 50 µg/mL tissue plasminogen activator on top of pre-prepared fully polymerized clots. The C3dg–fibrinogen complex was quantified using a combination of previously described sandwich ELISAs (32, 37), with the following details. MaxiSorp 96-well ELISA plates (Nunc, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with a monoclonal anti-C3dg capture antibody (clone 16-39-06) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with 1% BSA in PBS-T, plasma samples and assay standards were applied in duplicate.

Bound C3dg–fibrinogen complexes were detected using a biotinylated polyclonal rabbit anti-human fibrinogen antibody (RRID:AB_2894406), followed by streptavidin-HRP. Plates were developed with OPD (Sigma-Aldrich), and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Sex hormones, body composition, and liver function

Total testosterone and estradiol were assessed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. Luteinizing hormone (LH) was quantitatively determined by an immunoassay (Cobas, Roche Diagnostics Limited, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Body composition was assessed by dual-energy X-ray 129 absorptiometry using a Hologic QDR2000/w osteodensitometer (Hologic, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Plasma albumin was determined calorimetrically, and alanine transaminase was measured by enzymatic reaction (Atellica CH, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany).

Gene expression in muscle

The analysis was based on total RNA sequencing of skeletal muscle biopsies from the right vastus lateralis of the quadriceps muscle, as previously described (39) (data accessible at the European Genome Phenome Archive [EGA], EGAS00001006996). Among the study participants, RNA sequencing data was available for 21 KS men treated with TRT and 15 control males. Briefly, the paired-end fastq files underwent quality checks using FastQC (Babraham Bioinformatics). Adaptor sequences were removed, and low-quality ends were trimmed using Trim Galore (Babraham Bioinformatics). The clean fastq files were then used as input for quasi-mapping for quantifying transcript expression using Salmon (40). A decoy-aware transcriptome index was built based on the hg38 transcriptome, and selective alignment was carried out, using the cleaned fastq pairs as input. Salmon transcript-level quantifications were imported into R using tximeta (41) to generate transcript-level count estimates with associated reference transcriptome metadata (hg38). The transcript counts were normalized in DESeq2 (42). DESeq2-normalized counts for MASP1 transcripts were then extracted and compared between groups.

Weighted gene correlation network analysis

Weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA, v1.70.3) (43) was applied to identify co-expressed genes from the RNA-seq data. Variance-stabilized counts for all detected genes were used as input. Signed co-expression networks were created using a one-step approach. Adjacency was calculated by choosing an appropriate soft thresholding power with an approximate scale-free topology. Gene clustering was performed on the signed Topology Overlap Matrix by hierarchical clustering, identifying modules via the blockwiseModules function. Module eigengenes were calculated via the moduleEigengenes function. Eigengene significance and corresponding P-values were obtained for selected module-trait associations. Intramodular connectivity and gene significance for specific traits were extracted for each module of interest.

Statistics

Distribution of data was evaluated by inspecting histograms and direct quantile-quantile plots. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and between-group comparisons were performed using the Student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. Associations were evaluated using univariate linear regression after transforming all nonnormally distributed variables. Analysis was performed using StataIC 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). No formal power analysis was performed as KS is relatively rare. All available patients meeting inclusion criteria were included to maximize the study size and strength of analysis.

Results

We included 45 men with KS (27 on TRT and 18 without TRT) and 45 age-matched controls. The study population demographics and data on metabolism, sex hormones, hematology, coagulation and fibrinolysis have been published previously (27, 44). Briefly, men with KS had higher total body fat percentage compared with controls (Table 1), with further higher total body fat percentage in untreated vs treated KS (Mean ± SD, 34.2 ± 8.5 vs 27.4 ± 5.6, P = .002). Similarly, as a group, men with KS had lower total testosterone and higher LH levels compared with controls (Table 1), but this was largely due to the low levels of total testosterone and high LH in untreated vs treated KS (Table 1). Liver function, reflected by albumin and alanine transaminase, was not different between men with KS and controls, and was unaffected by TRT (Table 1).

Lectin complement pathway activation capacity and regulation

The lectin pathway activation capacity was higher in men with KS than in controls (P < .001, Fig. 1A). The lectin pathway is initiated when PRMs (MBL, ficolins, or collectin-11) bind at the site of endothelial injury or to a pathogen and form a complex with MASP1/2 (Fig. 2), leading to further downstream activation of complement factors. Reflecting the increased capacity for lectin complement activation in KS, the median serum MBL levels in KS were almost 50% higher than those in the controls; however, due to large variability and clustering in the lower range, this was not statistically significantly different (P = .09, Fig. 1B). Still, lectin pathway activation capacity in both men with KS and controls was associated with serum MBL levels (log-transformed β [95% CI], 30.5 [20.3; 40.8], P < .001, R2 = 0.48 and 14.5 [7.5; 21.5], P < .001, R2 = 0.29 respectively, Fig. 3A). The levels of ficolin 2, ficolin 3, and collectin-11 did not differ between men with KS and controls.

We found further evidence of altered complement regulation in KS. MAP-1 inhibits lectin-mediated complement activation by competing with MASPs for binding to PRMs, such as MBL (Fig. 2) (45). Serum MAP-1 levels were higher in men with KS than in controls (P = .001, Fig. 1C). Furthermore, serum MAP-1 levels were associated with both MBL (P < .001, R2 = 0.14) and lectin pathway activation capacity (P < .001, R2 = 0.15), reflecting the concomitant upregulation of complement-activating and regulatory factors in KS.

To evaluate the resting activity of the complement system, we assessed the serum levels of C3dg in our samples. Serum levels of C3dg did not differ between KS and controls (Table 1), indicating non-differential complement activation in vivo in the resting state. To evaluate pre-sampling cross-talk between complement and fibrinolysis, we further assessed the presence of fibrinogen–C3dg complex in our samples. We were able to demonstrate the presence of fibrinogen–C3dg complex in samples from both men with KS and controls (Table 1). The levels of the fibrinogen–C3dg complex were not different between men with KS and controls, again supporting non-differential complement activity in the resting state prior to blood sampling.

Complement, sex hormones, and body composition

As previously demonstrated, sex hormone levels have a severe impact on body composition in men with KS (2) potentially further affecting complement regulation. However, lectin pathway activation capacity was not different between treated and untreated men with KS, and lectin pathway activation capacity was not directly associated with total or free levels of testosterone or estradiol. Similarly, lectin pathway activation capacity was not directly associated with total body fat percentage in the combined KS and control groups. However, we observed an association between lectin pathway activation capacity and total body fat percentage in treated KS only (β [95% CI], 4.4 [0.9; 8.0], P = .02, R2 = 0.21).

Since MBL levels explained almost half the variability in lectin pathway activation capacity among men with KS, we proceeded to evaluate the associations between MBL, sex hormones, and body composition in KS. MBL was not different between treated and untreated men with KS (Fig. 1B) and MBL in KS was not associated with testosterone, estradiol, or total body fat percentage.

We then applied multivariate regression to assess the contribution of sex hormones and body composition beyond MBL levels to the variability of lectin pathway activation capacity. First, we constructed a model with lectin pathway activation capacity as the dependent variable and all measured complement factors as covariates. This model, including MBL, MAP-1, ficolin-2, ficolin-3, and collectin-11 predicted 42% of the variability in lectin pathway activation capacity in men with KS (F-test P < .0001, adjusted R2 = 0.42). Further addition of sex hormones (testosterone, estradiol), sex hormone regulation (luteinizing hormone), and body fat percentage increased the prediction to 45% of the variability in lectin pathway activation capacity among men with KS (F-test P = .0003, adjusted R2 = 0.45). Lastly, since MBL is produced in the liver, MBL levels may be affected by liver function. However, we observed no association between MBL and alanine transaminase or albumin levels in men with KS.

Similarly, we were interested in examining whether changes in sex hormone levels and alterations in body composition caused by TRT in KS were associated with complement regulation expressed by MAP-1 levels. MAP-1 was not different between treated and untreated men with KS (Fig. 1C) and we observed no association between serum MAP1 levels and sex hormones or total body fat percentage in KS. In contrast, we observed an inverse association between MAP1 and total testosterone levels in the controls (log-transformed β [95% CI], −49.1 [−86.1; −12.0], P = .01, R2 = 0.15).

Complement and fibrinolysis

As previously shown (2), fibrinogen levels were increased and fibrin clot lysis was severely reduced in KS compared with controls (Table 1). We aimed to examine whether complement activation contributes to the fibrinolytic deficit in KS. We observed a weak association between fibrin clot lysis and lectin pathway activation capacity using the combined data from all participants (β [95% CI], 0.7 [−1.2; −0.2], P = .005, R2 = 0.09, Fig. 3B), but not in men with KS exclusively. As such, the very large variation in lectin pathway activation capacity seen across our participants only explained very little (9%) of the very large variability in fibrin clot lysis across the participants. We know from our previous study that, analogous to the relationship between lectin pathway activation capacity and MBL levels, fibrin clot lysis is highly dependent on fibrinogen levels and body composition in KS (2). As such, using multivariate linear regression, fibrinogen and body fat percentage explained 22% of the variability in fibrin clot lysis among men with KS (F-test P = .005, R2 = 0.22). Adding lectin pathway activation capacity to the model increased the prediction to 27% of the variability in fibrin clot lysis among men with KS (F-test P = .004, R2 = 0.27).

MAP-1 gene expression

Complement factors are predominantly expressed in the liver. However, through alternative splicing of the lectin pathway-associated gene MASP1, 3 different isoforms MASP-1, MASP-3, and MAP1 are generated, of which the splice variant coding for MAP-1 (MASP1-201) is highly expressed in heart muscle, but also in skeletal muscle among other tissues (46). Due to the increased levels of MAP-1 in Klinefelter syndrome, we assessed the expression of the MASP1-201 gene transcript in skeletal muscle. MASP1-201 expression was significantly higher and almost doubled in men with KS compared with controls (Fig. 4A). MASP1-201 expression in muscle was not associated with total testosterone, total body fat percentage, or total body lean mass, indicating a potential syndrome-specific expression pattern. We then performed a WGCNA analysis on gene expression data exclusively from KS to identify genes with expression patterns associated with expression of MASP1-201. We identified a single network of co-expressed genes (“white” module) with an eigengene value, representing the overall module gene expression profile, that correlated with MASP1-201 expression (Fig. 4B). The eigengene of the white module was further associated with BMI (R2 = −0.72, P = .0003) and lean body mass (R2 = −0.62, P = .008). Because the white module eigengene was inversely correlated with MASP1-201 expression, we identified genes of interest within this module by ranking the genes on module membership (strength of correlation with the white module eigengene) and gene significance for MASP1-201 (strength of association with MASP1-201 expression). We identified BRD4, NOLC1, CAMK2B, ATN1, ZNF805, ZNF70, IRS2, and NFRKB as the strongest representatives of the white module most closely linked to MASP1-201 expression (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

It has long been suspected that immune competence in KS could be altered (9, 47). Men with KS exhibit elevated rates of autoimmune and atopic diseases and are believed to be more susceptible to infectious diseases (1). In this study, we provide the first evidence of increased capacity for activation of the lectin pathway of the complement system in men with KS, indicating that alterations in innate immunity may contribute to the clinical phenotype of KS. In addition to classical autoimmune disorders (12), the increased complement activation capacity in KS could contribute to the observed high risk of diabetes (13), cardiovascular disease (14), and thrombosis (15) also seen among men with KS (1). Traditionally, the morbidity pattern seen in KS, including autoimmune diseases, has been seen as a consequence of long-term male hypogonadism (48). Increasing evidence suggests that specific genome-wide alterations in KS directly contribute to the clinical phenotype beyond the immediate effects of hypogonadism. The data presented here further add to the idea of syndrome-specific regulation of complement in KS. The large variation in testosterone levels and body fat percentage observed among men with KS was only weakly associated with lectin pathway activation capacity and levels of individual complement pathway proteins. For example, lectin pathway activation capacity was predicted by MBL levels, with no association between MBL levels, sex hormones, or body composition among men with KS. This indicates that complement regulation in KS is somewhat unaffected by the substantial changes in androgenicity and metabolism following TRT (1, 2). Whether the same could be seen in other conditions treated with TRT for hypogonadism is currently not known.

Furthermore, we demonstrated increased expression of MASP1-201 in KS that was unrelated to sex hormones or body composition. We thus hypothesize that differentially expressed genes between KS and controls contribute directly to the severity of complement activation alterations in KS (3). The MBL protein is synthesized in the liver following transcription of the MBL2 gene, and studies have found a clear association between many common allelic variants and blood MBL levels (49). MBL levels were not associated with liver function reflected in alanine transaminase levels. As such, although MBL levels in the current study were not significantly elevated in men with KS, differences in the distribution of MBL2 variants in KS and controls could contribute to the increased potential for lectin pathway activation capacity among men with KS. The MBL2 gene, like most genes coding for complement system proteins, is primarily expressed in the liver. We did not have access to liver biopsies that would be needed to further elucidate whether differential expression of complement genes occurs in the liver of men with KS. However, our finding of increased MASP1-201 expression in the skeletal muscle of men with KS further supports that the differential expression of genes involved in complement regulation contributes to the altered complement function in KS. In addition, increased MASP1-201 expression in skeletal muscle was associated with reduced expression of a co-expressed gene set (the white module), including key genes involved in sustaining muscle growth and regeneration (BRD4, NOLC1), mediating insulin/insulin-like growth factor-1-induced anabolism (IRS2), supporting muscle contractility (CAMK2B), adaptating to muscle injury or stress (NFRKB), and generally supporting transcriptional programming (ATN1, ZNF805, ZNF70). Downregulation of these co-expressed genes in KS muscle could indicate that anabolic signaling and maintenance of muscle mass are challenged in KS, which would lead to muscle atrophy, metabolic impairment, and a shift towards an inflammation-prone muscle state with a reduced capacity for normal protein synthesis. From this, it becomes increasingly intriguing why the correction of hypogonadism seemingly cannot affect the expression of MASP-201 in KS.

Notably, MAP-1 is primarily expressed in cardiomyocytes (45), speculatively as a compensatory mechanism to prevent increased complement activation in the heart. Innate immune responses, including complement activation, are key mediators in the early phases of myocardial injury, and inhibition of the lectin pathway has been demonstrated to protect against myocardial ischemia and preserve myocardial function following ischemia (50). The potential of MAP-1 to regulate the risk or severity of myocardial infarction has not been established; however, MAP-1 has been demonstrated to similarly inhibit lectin pathway activation during myocardial ischemia, improving cardiac function following ischemia in mice (50). Considering that MAP-1 was consistently increased in KS, it is intriguing that men with KS do not seem to be affected by myocardial infarctions to a degree reflecting the much elevated prevalence of classic cardiovascular risk factors among men with KS (17, 51). Thus, MAP-1 upregulation could represent a highly specific karyotype-phenotype trait in KS; an inborn regulator of cardiac inflammation with the potential for reducing risk and severity of myocardial ischemia. This needs to be further explored in future studies.

It is important to note that despite emphasizing the potential genetic aspects of complement regulation in KS, our study still found a positive contribution of the combined effects of treating hypogonadism, expressed as increased testosterone levels and lowered body fat, on complement activation capacity. The proinflammatory capacity of adipose tissue is well recognized. Evidence from non-KS populations also shows complement system dysfunction in obese subjects (16) and lowered inflammation after weight loss (52). In addition, a previous study found that levels of complement factors were positively associated with BMI across 99 patients with type 2 diabetes and 99 age- and sex-matched controls (53). TRT in KS is capable of modulating the distribution between lean and fat mass, with significant loss of adipose tissue with testosterone supplementation (2, 54). This supports the continued emphasis on optimizing TRT in KS, thereby reducing body fat and alleviating proinflammatory responses.

We hypothesized that increased lectin pathway-mediated complement activation capacity could contribute to the very low fibrinolytic capacity in KS (2). Available evidence suggests that activation of the lectin pathway enhances fibrin clot stability and impairs fibrinolysis (14). For example, lectin pathway enzymes MASP-1 and MASP-2 have been shown to catalyze the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, facilitate fibrin cross-linking via Factor XIII activation, and inhibit fibrinolysis through activation of the thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) (14). In addition, studies have shown direct interactions between complement pathway proteins and fibrinogen affecting the fibrinolytic capacity (23, 25). We were only able to show a weak inverse association between lectin pathway activation capacity and fibrin clot lysis, and only a small added effect of its capacity to predict fibrin clot lysis in men with KS in a model that included fibrinogen and body fat percentage. One apparent reason for this could be the lack of statistical power due to our sample size. However, another highly plausible reason could be that the assays available to us are not capable of fully representing the very specific conditions in vivo when complement, coagulation, and other systems are concomitantly activated in a highly localized environment. Complement activation is a complex process involving cellular components of blood, the endothelium, and other tissues at the site of activation. We were unable to assess fibrin clot lysis in blood clots that were prepared under simultaneous activation of both the coagulation and complement systems. Therefore, the elevated capacity for complement activation in KS did not affect the structure of the clots investigated in our fibrin clot lysis assay. We showed that the preformed levels of fibrinogen–C3dg were not different between KS and controls, indicating that complement factors, to a lesser extent, are expected to implicate the tissue factor-induced clotting of plasma samples ex vivo, if complement is not intentionally activated as part of the process. Our novel finding of fibrinogen–C3dg in both KS and controls highlights that complement and hemostasis are indeed intertwined. With the reduced fibrinolytic capacity and increased lectin pathway activation capacity seen in KS, this condition could represent a model system for further investigating the interplay between complement and fibrinolysis.

We present the first comprehensive assessment of lectin complement activation capacity in men with KS, applying highly specific assays targeting both lectin-mediated complement activation and its individual PRMs and the overall complement activation split-product C3dg. This was a cross-sectional study, and the data cannot establish causality or assess the impact of changes in complement activation capacity over time. Also, our estimates could be affected by insufficient sample size even though we included a relatively large cohort of men with this somewhat rare condition. Similarly, our study was not designed to directly associate complement activation capacity and clinical thrombotic events, or to directly assess the effect on thrombotic risk of interventions aimed at specifically regulating complement activation. Such studies would require large cohorts with many thrombotic outcomes and are not likely to be conducted in the context of KS.

In conclusion, we show that increased lectin complement activation capacity is likely a distinct feature of KS, with syndrome-specific upregulation of both activating and inhibitory lectin pathway proteins, and only a minor role in regulating complement through hypogonadism, TRT, and body composition. Specifically, we show upregulation of MASP-201 in KS regardless of sex hormones or body fat. The in vivo complement activation level, measured by C3dg, did not differ between KS and control groups. We also reveal an interaction between complement and fibrinolysis by detecting fibrinogen–C3dg complexes in both KS and controls, but under the current conditions, we were not able to definitively demonstrate increased complement capacity in KS as a significant predictor of reduced fibrinolytic capacity.
