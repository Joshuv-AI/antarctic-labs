Abstract
. We find a new definition of colored edge graphs of finite algebras in the case of minimal Taylor algebras, a definition which includes the graphs invented by A. Bulatov. Next we proceed to reprove the main results of A. Bulatov’s theory in the case of minimal Taylor algebras and in our setting, finding several simplifications compared to the more general case of smooth algebras Bulatov considered.



1.
Introduction

This paper, the first in a series of at least three, is concerned with the Constraint Satisfaction Problem, and its computational complexity. For three decades or so, the main focus in the investigation of computational complexity of the Constraint Satisfaction Problem was the Dichotomy Conjecture of Feder and Vardi, stated in
[
26
]
, which said that, for any finite relational structure
\mathbb{A}
the complexity of
CSP(\mathbb{A})
can be either tractable, or NP-complete. We start the investigation of the theories behind the two proofs of the Dichotomy Conjecture, by D. Zhuk
[
46
]
and by A. Bulatov
[
19
]
, and try to simplify them, connect them and find simpler methods than either by combining their ideas. The first effort towards unification of these proofs was in the seminal paper
[
3
]
, where the minimal Taylor algebras were introduced as the proper setting in which Dichotomy can be analyzed. We will work within minimal Taylor algebras throughout our project.

In this paper our main focus is on simplifying the initial part of the colored edge theory of A. Bulatov. After distilling the important properties colored edges need to satisfy, we call them Edge Axioms, we will prove that Bulatov’s edges satisfy them. We define another type of colored edges which also satisfy Edge Axioms, but are significantly easier to define than Bulatov’s. Next, we prove that any colored graphs which satisfy Edge Axioms also satisfy the initial results of Bulatov’s theory. We are confident that the remainder of Bulatov’s theory, within the framework of minimal Taylor algebras, would be correct for any graphs satisfying the Edge Axioms (with Bulatov’s original proofs). These initial results we prove are related to binary absorption, and we manage to simplify some of the proofs of Bulatov from
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
and a theorem from
[
3
]
. In the penultimate section, we review the construction by M. Maróti which uses unary polynomials and binary absorption, and we apply it to prove a connection between a partial result by Zhuk and another one by Bulatov. We finish the paper with an announcements of results we will prove in subsequent papers.

2.1.
Universal algebra

We assume the reader has a thorough understanding of the basic notions of Universal Algebra, the readers who need help with basic Universal Algebra are advised to look up the missing parts in
[
24
]
, or
[
42
]
,
[
28
]
and
[
29
]
. The notions of the centralizer condition and the centralizer (some call it the annihilator) from Commutator Theory, which are used in Section 8, are defined in the book
[
27
]
. In this paper we will deal with finite and idempotent algebras, so we assume that any algebra we mention is finite and idempotent, unless stated otherwise. Here we state the results and define the notions which are less standard, or more obscure, but will be used in the paper.

Following A. Bulatov, we define the hypergraph of proper subuniverses:

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra. By
H({\mathbf{A}})
we denote the set of all proper subuniverses, i.e.,
H({\mathbf{A}}):=\{B\subsetneq A:B\in{\mathrm{Sub}\>{\mathbf{A}}}\}
.

We consider
H({\mathbf{A}})
as a hypergraph, so that we can use the related terminology of paths, connectedness etc. Next we recall the definition of a tolerance and A. Bulatov’s notions of a link tolerance and link congruence.

Definition 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra. A
tolerance
of
{\mathbf{A}}
is a reflexive, symmetric and compatible binary relation on
{\mathbf{A}}
. The transitive closure of a tolerance is, of course, a congruence. When the transitive closure of the tolerance
\tau
on
{\mathbf{A}}
is
A\times A
, we say that
\tau
is
connected
. When the only tolerances on
{\mathbf{A}}
are the diagonal and the full relation, we say that
{\mathbf{A}}
is
tolerance-free
.

Definition 3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
be algebras and
{\mathbf{R}}\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
. For any
i\leq n
, the
i
th
link tolerance
of
{\mathbf{R}}
, denoted by
tol_{i}R
is defined by

\begin{gathered}tol_{i}R:=\{(a,b)\in A_{i}\times A_{i}:(\exists a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})\\
(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})\in R\text{ and }(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},b,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})\in R\}\end{gathered}

The transitive closure of
tol_{i}R
is the
i
th link congruence of
{\mathbf{R}}
, denoted by
lk_{i}R
.

Note that
tol_{i}R
can be defined even if
R
has no algebraic structure, but then it would be just a symmetric relation. Reflexivity of
tol_{i}R
follows from subdirectness of
R
and compatibility of
tol_{i}R
from the compatibility of
R
.

We define the neighbors of a point just for binary relations, though the extension to
n
-ary relations is possible.

Definition 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
be idempotent algebras and
{\mathbf{R}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
. For any
a\in A
, the set of right
R
-neighbors of
a
, denoted by
[a]R
, are the set
\{x\in B:(a,x)\in R\}
. Similarly, the set of left
R
-neighbors of
b\in B
, denoted by
R[b]
is
\{x\in A:(x,b)\in R\}
.

Both
[a]R
and
R[b]
are subuniverses of
{\mathbf{B}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively, which is proved by using primitive positive definitions (for short, pp-definitions) and idempotence.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an idempotent algebra and
\tau
a connected tolerance of
{\mathbf{A}}
. Then either
\tau=A^{2}
, or
H({\mathbf{A}})
is a connected hypergraph.

Proof.

Assume
\tau\neq A^{2}
. We call the maximal subsets
B
of
A
which satisfy
B^{2}\subseteq\tau
the tolerance classes. Each tolerance class is a proper subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
since
B=\cap\{\tau[b]:b\in B\}
and
\tau[b]
is a subuniverse, as we mentioned before this Proposition. Moreover, for any pair
a,b\in A
such that
(a,b)\in\tau
there exists at least one tolerance class
B
of
\tau
such that
a,b\in B
. Hence, from connectedness of
\tau
follows the connectedness of
H({\mathbf{A}})
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
be idempotent algebras and
{\mathbf{R}}\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
be such that
tol_{1}R
is a connected tolerance. Then

(1)

tol_{2}R
is also a connected tolerance.

(2)

If there exists
b\in B
such that
R[b]=A
, then
tol_{1}R=A^{2}
.

(3)

If
tol_{1}R\neq A^{2}
, then
H({\mathbf{A}})
is a connected hypergraph.

(4)

Assume that
A={\mathrm{Sg}}(a_{1},a_{2})
for some
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
. In that case,
tol_{1}R=A^{2}
iff there exists some
b\in B
such that
R[b]=A
.

Proof.

To prove (1), note that
tol_{1}R
is a connected tolerance iff
R
, viewed as a bipartite graph between the disjoint unions of
A
and
B
, is connected, iff
tol_{2}R
is connected. (2) is obvious. (3) follows from Proposition
1
. For the direction
\Rightarrow
of (4), note that from
tol_{1}R=A^{2}
there must exist some
b\in B
such that
(a_{1},b),(a_{2},b)\in R
. But then
A={\mathrm{Sg}}(a_{1},a_{2})=R[b]
. The direction
(\Leftarrow)
of (4) is statement (2).
∎

2.2.
CSP

The Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP for short) has several equivalent definitions, each offering slightly different language to express the same thing. We will use definitions from
[
37
]
, which were based on
[
1
]
.

Definition 5
.

By a
constraint language
we mean a set
\Gamma
of relations (of any arities) on the same nonvoid base set
A
.

Definition 6
.

Given a constraint language
\Gamma
on the set
A
, we define an instance of
CSP(\Gamma)
as any ordered triple
(V,A,{\mathcal{C}})
where
V
is called the set of variables, while
{\mathcal{C}}
is a set whose elements are called constraints. Each constraint is an ordered pair
(S,R)
, where
S\subseteq V
is the constraint scope, while
R\subseteq A^{S}
is such that there exist an integer
n
and a surjective mapping
\varphi:n\rightarrow S
such that
R\circ\varphi\in\Gamma
. Here
R\circ\varphi=\{g\circ\varphi:g\in R\}
.
R
is the constraint relation of the constraint
(S,R)
. A mapping
f:V\rightarrow A
is a solution to the instance
(V,A,{\mathcal{C}})
of
CSP(\Gamma)
if for every constraint
(R,S)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
f|_{S}\in R
.

Note that the way Feder and Vardi postulated their conjecture implies that we may consider only the case when
\Gamma
is finite, but we do not make that requirement here, yet. On the other hand, we do assume that the domain
A
is finite throughout this paper. We will usually assume that the variable set
V
is
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. If we close
\Gamma
under intersection, and also under permutation and identification of coordinates (if
\Gamma
was finite, it will remain finite after those closures), then we may assume, without loss of generality, that different constraints have different scopes. This is because we may intersect all constraint relations with the same scope and replace all of those constraints with the single constraint.

Definition 7
.

Let
P
be an instance of CSP
(\Gamma)
. We say that
P
is
(k,l)
-minimal if

•

for any
S\subseteq V
,
|S|\leq l
, there is precisely one
i
such that
S_{i}=S
(
l
-density) and

•

whenever
(S,R)
and
(S^{\prime},R^{\prime})
are constraints such that
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
and
|S^{\prime}|\leq k
, then
R^{\prime}=R|_{S^{\prime}}
(
k
-consistency).

By using the
(k,l)
-minimality algorithm (see e.g.
[
1
]
), for any given fixed numbers
k\leq l
we can transform, in polynomial time, a given instance of
CSP(\Gamma)
into an equivalent
(k,l)
-minimal instance. Whenever an instance is at least
(1,1)
-minimal, the second condition implies that each constraint relation
R
is a subdirect product of
{\mathbf{A}}_{j}
,
j\in S
, where
(\{j\},A_{j})
are constraints in
{\mathcal{C}}
.

According to
[
33
]
, the complexity of the
CSP(\Gamma)
depends on the compatible operations (polymorphisms) of the relational structure
(A,\Gamma)
. Therefore, it suffices to verify the conjecture for
\Gamma={\sf{SP}}_{fin}({\mathbf{A}})
for some finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
. More precisely (to conform to our definitions) we may assume that
\Gamma=\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\sf{S}}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
and we will denote such
\Gamma
by
\Gamma({\mathbf{A}})
. The impact of this assumption on
\Gamma
is that now
\Gamma
contains all full finite powers of
A
and that
\Gamma
is closed under intersections and products (along with permutations and identifications of variables assumed earlier), thus
\Gamma
is a
relational clone
.

This justifies our construction where we move from an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
to one of its term reducts
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
. Namely, any relation compatible with all operations of
{\mathbf{A}}
is compatible with all term operations of
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
, as well. Therefore, any instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is an instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})
, so if we can solve any instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})
in polynomial time, we can do the same with instances of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
.

We may assume that the relational structure
(A,\Gamma)
has no endomorphisms except for automorphisms. Namely, if
\varphi
is an endomorphism which maps
A
onto a proper subset, then the set of instances of
CSP(\Gamma)
which have a solution is precisely the same as the set of instances of
CSP(\Gamma|_{\varphi(A)})
on the domain
\varphi(A)
which has a solution (in the nontrivial direction, just compose the solution of
CSP(\Gamma)
with the endomorphism). Such relational structures for which all endomorphisms are automorphisms are called
cores
.

For
(A,\Gamma)
a core, the complexity of
CSP(\Gamma)
equals the complexity of
CSP(\Gamma^{c})
, which is
\Gamma
augmented with all one-element unary relations. If
\Gamma=\Gamma({\mathbf{A}})
, then
\Gamma^{c}=\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}^{id})
, where
{\mathbf{A}}^{id}
is the idempotent reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{A}}^{id}
is an algebra whose operations are all idempotent term operations in
{\mathrm{Clo}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
. So, we justified the focus on idempotent finite algebras and assume from now on that all algebras are such. For more details about the reductions in this and the previous paragraph, cf. Theorems 4.4 and 4.7 of
[
22
]
.

As was proved in
[
22
]
, if
{\mathbf{A}}
has no Taylor term (to be defined later) then
CSP(\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}))
is NP-complete. In
[
22
]
it is conjectured that in the converse case the
CSP(\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}))
is in P. A proof of this, the Algebraic Dichotomy Conjecture, in the papers
[
46
]
and
[
19
]
thus also confirmed the original Dichotomy Conjecture by Feder and Vardi. It matters to us that, if
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra with a Taylor term, we can replace
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
with
CSP({\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})
such that
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
is a term reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}
which still contains some Taylor term. This is the idea behind minimal Taylor algebras, which we will explore in the next section.

2.3.
Multisorted CSP and templates

Already when we reduce an instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
to a
(k,l)
-minimal one, we introduced subuniverses
A_{i}
of
{\mathbf{A}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Instead of
R\leq A^{S}
, we can consider
R
as a subdirect product of
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in S\}
. For some reductions we will also need
A_{i}
to be homomorphic images of
{\mathbf{A}}
(or of its subalgebra), or a retract of
{\mathbf{A}}
(or of its subalgebra).

Now we define a
template
of CSP.

Definition 8
.

A class
{\mathcal{T}}
of isomorphism types of similar finite algebras is called a CSP template if
{\mathcal{T}}
is closed under homomorphic images and subalgebras. If, additionally, a template is closed under unary polynomial retracts, we call it an M-template.

Note that we do not allow isomorphic algebras to appear more than once in a template, which is why we speak of isomorphism types, rather than algebras themselves. The reason is, if the language of a finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite, then there are only finitely many algebras, up to isomorphism, which can be obtained from
{\mathbf{A}}
by taking subalgebras, homomorphic images and retracts. This will allow us to reduce a multisorted CSP instance by making the template a smaller set of isomorphism types. Now we define the multisorted CSP instance.

Definition 9
.

Given a template
{\mathcal{T}}
, a (multisorted) instance
P
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
is the triple
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
. Here
D=\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in V\}
, where each
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is isomorphic to some algebra in
{\mathcal{T}}
, is the tuple of domains, while
{\mathcal{C}}
is the set of constraints. Each constraint is an ordered pair
(S,R)
, where
S\subseteq V
, while
R\leq\prod\limits_{i\in S}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is a subdirect product. A mapping
f\in\prod\limits_{i\in V}A_{i}
is a solution to the instance
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
if for every constraint
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
f|_{S}\in R
.

Of course, we can take as the template
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}})
, the set of isomorphism types of all finite algebras which can be obtained from a finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
by taking homomorphic images, subalgebras and retracts. Then each
(1,1)
-minimal instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
. Moreover, if
{\mathbf{A}}
has a finite language, then
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}})
is finite, as we said above.

2.4.
Taylor terms

A term
t
of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is a Taylor term if it is idempotent and satisfies a finite set of equations which is not satisfied by any projection operation on a set with more than one element. W. Taylor made an equivalent, but more specific condition for Taylor terms, but we will work with cyclic terms, an even more restrictive condition, which was proved in
[
5
]
to be equivalent to Taylor terms within the finite algebras (and locally finite varieties of algebras). First we define a cyclic term:

Definition 10
.

A term
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a cyclic term of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
iff the following two identities hold in
{\mathbf{A}}
:

\begin{gathered}t(x,x,\dots,x)\approx x\text{ and}\\
t(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\approx t(x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}).\end{gathered}

Theorem 3

(Theorem 4.2 of
[
5
]
)
.

A finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
has a Taylor term iff
{\mathbf{A}}
has a cyclic term of any prime arity
p
such that
p>|A|
.

Subsequently, M. Siggers proved in
[
43
]
that a finite algebra has a Taylor term iff it has a term with 6 variables which satisfies some specified (finite) set of identities, and K. Kearnes, P. Marković and R. McKenzie improved this to a four-variable term in
[
34
]
. We will need just the existence of those two characterizations, so we omit their exact equations.

Definition 11
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra. We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a
set
if every term interprets in
{\mathbf{A}}
as some projection. We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
is
affine
if there exist a ring
{\mathbf{R}}
and a left
{\mathbf{R}}
-module
{\mathbf{M}}
such that the clones of polynomial operations
{\mathrm{Pol}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
and
{\mathrm{Pol}\>{\mathbf{M}}}
are equal.

In the case of idempotent affine algebras, we may assume without loss of generality that the
{\mathbf{R}}
-module
{\mathbf{M}}
is faithful, so for every term
t(x_{1},\dots x_{n})
there exist elements
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\in R
such that

\begin{gathered}t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(x_{1},\dots x_{n})=r_{1}x_{1}+\dots+r_{n}x_{n}\text{ and}\\
r_{1}+r_{2}+\dots+r_{n}=1.\end{gathered}

The following is a summary of several well-known results in the case of finite idempotent Taylor algebras:

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite idempotent Taylor algebra. The following are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}
is affine.

(2)

There exists a ring
{\mathbf{R}}
and an
{\mathbf{R}}
-module
{\mathbf{M}}
with universe
A
such that the clone of all idempotent operations of
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathrm{Clo}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
are equal.

(3)

{\mathbf{A}}
is quasi-affine.

(4)

{\mathbf{A}}
is Abelian.

(5)

There exists a congruence
\Theta
of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
such that the diagonal relation
0_{A}
is a
\Theta
-class.

We give another condition which forces an algebra to be affine:

Theorem 5
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite idempotent Taylor algebra and
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{3}
. For every
a\in A
define the relations
R_{a,1}:=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}:R(a,x,y)\}
,
R_{a,2}:=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}:R(x,a,y)\}
and
R_{a,3}:=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}:R(x,y,a)\}
. If for every
a\in A
all three relations
R_{a,1}
,
R_{a,2}
and
R_{a,3}
are graphs of bijections of the set
A
, then
{\mathbf{A}}
is affine.

Proof.

Another way to look at
R
is to note that, for any
a,b\in A
there exists a unique
c
in
A
such that
R(a,b,c)
, so
R
is the graph of an operation from
A^{2}
into
A
. Moreover, as there exist unique
d\in A
such that
R(d,b,a)
and unique
e\in A
such that
R(b,e,a)
, thus
R
is the graph of a quasigroup operation
r(x,y)
on the set
A
, and such that
r
commutes with all operations of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Define the relation
S(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
of
A^{4}
by
(\exists z)R(x_{1},y_{1},z)\wedge R(x_{2},y_{2},z)
.
S
is compatible relation with
{\mathbf{A}}
(we have its pp-definition from
R
) and it is the kernel of the quasigroup operation
r:A^{2}\rightarrow A
. From all we know about
R
, it follows that
S
, viewed as a binary relation between its first two coordinates and its last two coordinates, is an equivalence relation on the set
A^{2}
, i.e., it is a congruence on
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
.

Fix some
a\in A
. Since
{\mathbf{A}}
is idempotent,
\{a\}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
and the bijection whose graph is
R_{a,3}(x,y)
is pp-definable from compatible relations
\{a\}
and
R
, so the bijection
R_{a,3}
is an automorphism of
{\mathbf{A}}
, denote it
\varphi
. So, if we define
S^{\prime}(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}):=S(\varphi(x_{1}),y_{1},\varphi(x_{2}),y_{2})
, it is another congruence of the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
. Moreover, one of its classes (the one where the quantified
z
in the definition of
S
is chosen to be
a
) is

\{(\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{2}),\varphi(x_{2})):x_{1},x_{2}\in A\}.

Since
\varphi\in{\mathrm{Aut}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
, it follows that the diagonal relation
0_{A}
is a congruence class of the congruence on
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
defined by
S^{\prime}
. According to Theorem
4

(5)\Rightarrow(1)
,
{\mathbf{A}}
is affine.
∎

A key concept to L. Barto’s and M. Kozik’s approach to Taylor algebras and the study of the Constraint Satisfaction problem is absorption, defined below.

Definition 12

(Definition 3.3 of
[
3
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra and
B\subseteq A
. We call
B
an
n
-
absorbing set
of

if there is a term operation
t\in{\mathrm{Clo}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}}
such that
t(\mathbf{a})\in B
whenever
\mathbf{a}\in A^{n}
and

.

If, additionally,
B
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
, we write
B\lhd_{n}{\mathbf{A}}
, or

when the arity is not important.

in

]
.

(Definitions 2.2 and 5.6 of
[
3
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra and

. We say that

is a

if for every

there exists a coordinate

is such that

of

f(\mathbf{a})\in B

is such that

. We say that

Some easy observations are that projective subuniverses must, indeed, be subuniverses, and that in every nontrivial clone any binary operation which is not a projection witnesses the fact that a strongly projective subuniverse is 2-absorbing.

3.
Minimal Taylor algebras

By using either

On any finite set, there exist finitely many Taylor clones such that any Taylor clone contains one of them.

A finite algebra

By the remarks above Definition

Every finite Taylor algebra has a term reduct which is a minimal Taylor algebra.

There is a result by D. Zhuk which connects binary absorbing subuniverses, ternary absorbing subuniverses, and congruence classes of “polynomially complete” congruences with the Constraint Satisfaction Problem. This is the following theorem, which follows from Theorems 5.5 and 5.6 of

We do not define the Z-irreducible, (1,1)-minimal and cycle consistent instances, but we note that, in polynomial time, any instance can either be solved, or transformed to such an instance which is equivalent to the original one. Suppose that

Simplifying slightly, Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem allows us to keep reducing the instance until we are left with an instance which has an affine factor in each

Lemma 15

Let

Let

4.
Edge Axioms

In this section we fix a pseudovariety
{\mathcal{V}}
generated by a finite minimal Taylor algebra, and define the directed graphs
as({\mathbf{A}})
and
sm({\mathbf{A}})
for all algebras
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

We don’t define our edges explicitly. Instead, we list several properties we call
Edge Axioms
, and any assignment of pairs of directed graphs each minimal Taylor algebra in
{\mathcal{V}}
which satisfies these axioms are our edge-colored graphs.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras generated by a single finite minimal Taylor algebra. For each
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, fix
as({\mathbf{A}})=(A;\rightarrow_{as})
and
sm({\mathbf{A}})=(A;\rightarrow_{sm})
, two directed graphs whose vertex set is the universe of
{\mathbf{A}}
. We denote
\rightarrow_{s}:=\rightarrow_{as}\cap\rightarrow_{sm}
,
s({\mathbf{A}}):=(A;\rightarrow_{s})
, and
asm({\mathbf{A}}):=(A;\rightarrow_{as}\cup\rightarrow_{sm})
. We say that the class of triples
\{({\mathbf{A}},as({\mathbf{A}}),sm({\mathbf{A}})):{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
is a (finitely generated) pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras with colored edges if the following
edge axioms
are satisfied:

Base Axiom 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
{\mathbf{B}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}
be an affine subalgebra. Then for all
a,b\in B
,
a\rightarrow_{as}b
.

Base Axiom 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
{\mathbf{B}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}
be a subalgebra with a majority term. Then for all
a,b\in B
,
a\rightarrow_{sm}b
.

Base Axiom 3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and for
a,b\in A
let there exist a term
t(x,y)
such that
t
acts on the set
\{a,b\}
as a semilattice operation with the absorbing element
b
. Then
a\rightarrow_{s}b
.

Homomorphism Axiom 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let
f:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
be a homomorphism. If
a\rightarrow_{as}b
in
as({\mathbf{A}})
(respectively,
a\rightarrow_{sm}b
in
sm({\mathbf{A}})
), then
f(a)\rightarrow_{as}f(b)
in
as({\mathbf{B}})
(respectively,
f(a)\rightarrow_{sm}f(b)
in
sm({\mathbf{B}})
).

In other words, Homomorphism Axiom 1 claims that each algebraic homomorphism between algebras in
{\mathcal{V}}
is also a graph homomorphism of their as-graphs and their sm-graphs.

Homomorphism Axiom 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let
f:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
be a homomorphism. If

\begin{gathered}f(a)\rightarrow_{as}f(b)\text{ in }as({\mathbf{B}}),\,f(a)\rightarrow_{sm}f(b)\text{ in }sm({\mathbf{B}}),\\
\text{or }f(a)\rightarrow_{s}f(b)\text{ in }s({\mathbf{B}}),\end{gathered}

then, for every
b^{\prime}\in A
such that
f(b^{\prime})=f(b)
and
{\mathrm{Sg}}(\{a,b^{\prime}\})
is minimal among
\{{\mathrm{Sg}}(\{a,c\}):c\in A\text{ and }f(c)=f(b)\}
, we have

a\rightarrow_{as}b^{\prime}\text{ in }as({\mathbf{A}}),\,a\rightarrow_{sm}b^{\prime}\text{ in }sm({\mathbf{A}}),\text{ or }a\rightarrow_{s}b^{\prime}\text{ in }s({\mathbf{A}}),

respectively.

Relational Axiom 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
. If

a_{1}\rightarrow_{as}a_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{A}},\,b_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}b_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{B}},\,(a_{1},b_{2})\in R\text{ and }(a_{2},b_{1})\in R,

then
(a_{2},b_{2})\in R
.

Relational Axiom 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
. If

\begin{gathered}a_{1}\rightarrow_{as}a_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{A}},\,b_{1}\rightarrow_{as}b_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{B}},\\
(a_{1},b_{1})\in R,\,(a_{1},b_{2})\in R\text{ and }(a_{2},b_{1})\in R,\end{gathered}

then
(a_{2},b_{2})\in R
.

Relational Axiom 3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{C}}
. If

\begin{gathered}a_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}a_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{A}},\,b_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}b_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{B}},\,c_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}c_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbf{C}},\\
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})\in R,\,(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})\in R\text{ and }(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})\in R,\end{gathered}

then
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{2})\in R
.

The following easy consequence of the Edge Axioms justifies why, in our notation
\rightarrow_{s}
,
\rightarrow_{as}
etc., we don’t specify the algebra.

Proposition 17
.

Let
\{({\mathbf{A}},as({\mathbf{A}}),sm({\mathbf{A}})):{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
be a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras with colored edges,
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
{\mathbf{B}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}
and
a,b\in B
. Then
a\rightarrow_{as}b
in
as({\mathbf{A}})
iff
a\rightarrow_{as}b
in
as({\mathbf{B}})
and
a\rightarrow_{sm}b
in
sm({\mathbf{A}})
iff
a\rightarrow_{sm}b
in
sm({\mathbf{B}})
.

Proof.

Consider the identity embedding as a homomorphism
f:{\mathbf{B}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
. Then Homomorphism Axiom 1 immediately gives direction
(\Leftarrow)
, while Homomorphism Axiom 2 implies the direction
(\Rightarrow)
, considering that
f(b)=f(b^{\prime})
implies
b=b^{\prime}
(since
f
is the identity map).
∎

5.
Graphs which satisfy the edge axioms exist

In this section we will prove that, in the case of minimal Taylor algebras, A. Bulatov’s edges, defined in
[
14
]
, satisfy our edge axioms. Next we will define pairs of directed graphs in a different way, and these graphs will also satisfy the edge axioms on a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras.

5.1.
Bulatov’s edges satisfy Edge Axioms

First of all, A. Bulatov’s edges are defined for “smooth” algebras, so first we have to verify that minimal Taylor algebras are “smooth”.

Definition 16
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite Taylor algebra.
{\mathbf{A}}
is
smooth
if, for any similar algebra
{\mathbf{B}}
, any
a,b\in A
and any homomorphism
\varphi:{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,b)\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
, if either

(a)

there exists a term
t(x,y)
such that
(\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\};t^{{\mathbf{B}}})
is a 2-element semilattice, or

(b)

there exists a term
m(x,y,z)
such that
(\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\};m^{{\mathbf{B}}})
is a 2-element majority algebra,

then
\{x\in A:\varphi(x)\in\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}\}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that, in his definition of “thick edges”, A. Bulatov needed to consider the case when
\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
had one operation which acted as a semilattice and another which acted as the majority operation. This case can’t occur in minimal Taylor algebras, since if
\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
has a semilattice operation, then it is a minimal Taylor algebra which has a 2-absorbing subuniverse, say
\{\varphi(b)\}
. Thus, by Theorem
10
,
\{\varphi(b)\}
is also a strongly projective subuniverse and the equations
m(\varphi(a),\varphi(a),\varphi(b))=m(\varphi(a),\varphi(b),\varphi(a))=m(\varphi(b),\varphi(a),\varphi(a))=\varphi(a)
become impossible for any idempotent term
m(x,y,z)
.

Also note the following: If we denote by
C:=[a]_{\ker\varphi}
and
D:=[b]_{\ker\varphi}
in either of the cases (a) and (b) considered by Definition
16
, then
C,D,C\cup D={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b)
are all subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}
and that
D\lhd_{2}(C\cup D)
if case (a) applies and
t(\varphi(a),\varphi(b))=t(\varphi(b),\varphi(a))=\varphi(b)
, while
C\lhd_{3}(C\cup D)
and
D\lhd_{3}(C\cup D)
if case (b) applies.

Proposition 18
.

Any finite minimal Taylor algebra is smooth.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{C}}
be the subalgebra of
{\mathbf{B}}
whose universe is
\varphi({\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,b))
. According to Theorem
8
, both
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,b)
and
{\mathbf{C}}
are minimal Taylor algebras. From the fact that the semilattice operation and the majority operation are both Taylor operations and Theorem
7
follows that
\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{C}}
. Therefore, the inverse image
\varphi^{-1}(\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\})
is a subuniverse of
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,b)
, so
{\mathbf{A}}
is smooth.
∎

What follows are A. Bulatov’s definitions of the three types of directed edges in smooth algebras. To distinguish them from the edges that satisfy our Edge Axioms, we will denote A. Bulatov’s edges by
\rightarrow_{s}^{B}
,
\rightarrow_{a}^{B}
and
\rightarrow_{m}^{B}
for the semilattice, affine and majority edges, respectively.

Definition 17
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a minimal Taylor algebra and
a,b\in A
a pair of distinct elements. We say that
ab
is a
semilattice edge
(in the sense of Bulatov), and write

, if there exists a term

such that
(\{a,b\};t)
is a 2-element semilattice such that
t(a,b)=t(b,a)=b
.

We note that the semilattice edges
\rightarrow_{s}^{B}
in A. Bulatov’s smooth algebras depend on the choice of a binary operation
t
(which Bulatov stipulates to have additional properties), in particular Bulatov claims that the orientation of a semilattice edge depends on this choice. In minimal Taylor algebras, though, a simple application of Proposition
18
and Theorem

gives

Corollary 19
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a minimal Taylor algebra and
a,b\in A
two distinct elements. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

a\rightarrow_{s}^{B}b
.

(2)

\{a,b\}\in{\mathrm{Sub}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\{b\}\lhd_{2}\{a,b\}
.

(3)

\{a,b\}\in{\mathrm{Sub}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\{b\}
is a strongly projective subuniverse of
\{a,b\}
.

The truth of condition (3) above clearly depends only on the minimal Taylor algebra, not on any choice of term operations, since the strong projectivity is realized by any cyclic term of
{\mathbf{A}}
(all variables of a cyclic term are essential).

Also, Bulatov’s edges
\rightarrow_{s}^{B}
are really the only possible edges in pseudovarieties of minimal Taylor algebras, as seen in the next lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be any finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras with pairs of digraphs such that the class
\{({\mathbf{A}},\rightarrow_{as},\rightarrow_{sm}):{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
satisfies Edge Axioms. If

,

If

, we apply Relational Axiom 1 to

and

which acts on

.

On the other hand, from

\{a,b\}
is a subuniverse of

which is a two-element semilattice with absorbing element

.
∎

We plan to use A. Bulatov’s results about his edges mostly as blackbox here, but in order to do that we need to prove they apply to a pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras. Namely, Bulatov extended his edges from a single smooth algebra to a finite class of smooth algebras, as we saw in Theorem
21
, while the pseudovariety in which we have defined our Edge Axioms is an infinite set of isomorphism types. So we need the following result.

Theorem 22
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite minimal Taylor algebra and
{\mathcal{V}}
the pseudovariety of finite minimal Taylor algebras
{\mathbf{A}}
generates. If
|A|^{2}=n
, and
t(x,y,z)
satisfies the majority (respectively, minority) condition on any algebra in
\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
, then
t(x,y,z)
satisfies the majority (resp., minority) condition on any algebra
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

If
t(x,y,z)
satisfies the additional conditions (a)-(c) of Theorem
21
on
\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
, then it satisfies the same conditions throughout
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
a,b\in B
be such that
\varphi
is a surjective homomorphism which maps
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{B}}}(a,b)
onto the two-element majority algebra. Since
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathsf{HSP}_{fin}({\mathbf{A}})
, there exists some positive integer
k
, some
{\mathbf{C}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{k}
and some surjective homomorphism
\psi:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
. Select two elements

such that
\psi(a^{\prime})=a
and
\psi(b^{\prime})=b
. Clearly,
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{C}}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})={\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}^{k}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Therefore,
\varphi\circ\psi
maps
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}^{k}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
onto the two-element majority algebra.

Now we inductively select coordinates
i_{1},i_{2},\dots
in
k
in the following way: Let
a^{\prime}(i_{1})\neq b^{\prime}(i_{1})
and if
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{j}
are selected, and, if such exist, select some
c,d\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}^{k}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
.
c\neq d
, such that
\mathrm{pr}_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{j}}c=\mathrm{pr}_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{j}}d
. Then select
i_{j+1}
so that

. Hence,
\mathrm{pr}_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{j}}{\mathrm{Sg}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
will keep increasing as
j
increases, until the projection becomes the same size as
|{\mathrm{Sg}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})|
. This will happen at most when
j=n
, since there are at most
n
many possible values for
(a^{\prime}(i),b^{\prime}(i))
, and when
a^{\prime}(i)=a^{\prime}(i^{\prime})
and
b^{\prime}(i)=b^{\prime}(i^{\prime})
, then
c(i)=c(i^{\prime})
and
d(i)=d(i^{\prime})
, so the coordinate
i^{\prime}
is redundant. So, for some selection

of coordinates,
j\leq n
, we obtain an isomorphism
\tau
which maps the projection
\mathrm{pr}_{I}{\mathrm{Sg}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
onto
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. Denote
a^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{pr}_{I}a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{pr}_{I}b^{\prime}
. We get that
\varphi\circ\psi\circ\tau
maps
{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}^{I}}(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})
onto the two-element majority algebra with
\varphi\circ\psi\circ\tau(a^{\prime\prime})=\varphi(a)
and
\varphi\circ\psi\circ\tau(b^{\prime\prime})=\varphi(b)
. Since
{\mathbf{A}}^{I}\in\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
, thus the two-element majority algebra on
\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
is also in
\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
, and since
t(x,y,z)
satisfies the majority condition for all algebras in
\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
, we get that

acts as the majority operation on
\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\}
, thus fulfilling condition (a) of Definition
18

is an identity and since it holds on

{\mathcal{V}}
.

The statement about the minority condition has the same proof as above, we just replace the phrase “the two-element majority algebra” with “an affine algebra” everywhere. If
t(x,y,z)
satisfies the additional conditions (a)-(c) of Theorem
21
on

is analogous as we are once again checking homomorphic images of 2-generated subuniverses.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}

{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proposition 27
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras such that
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
is a two-element semilattice,
A=\{a,b\}
and
\{b\}\lhd_{2}\{a,b\}
. Then
a\rightarrow_{a}^{B}b
iff there exists an affine algebra in
{\mathcal{V}}
, while
a\rightarrow_{m}^{B}b
iff there exists a two-element majority algebra in
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that there is no 2-element majority algebra in
{\mathcal{V}}
. Since
{\mathcal{V}}
is closed under taking subalgebras and homomorphic images, the assumptions of the majority condition are never met, so that condition is vacuously true for any projection operation, e.g.
t(x,y,z)=x
satisfies the majority condition. But
b\notin\{a\}={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(a,b,b))
, so
a\rightarrow_{m}^{B}b
does not hold.

Similarly, if there is no affine algebra in
{\mathcal{V}}
, we conclude that the first projection satisfies the minority condition (though not condition (a) from Theorem
21
). Hence we can take the first projection to be the operation
t(x,y,z)
from the definition of the minority edges. But again,
b\notin\{a\}={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(a,a,b))
.

On the other hand, if there is a 2-element majority algebra
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, then consider any term
t(x,y,z)
which satisfies the majority condition. Let
{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y)
be the free algebra, let
\varphi:{\mathbf{F}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
be the homomorphism such that
\varphi(x)=a
and
\varphi(y)=b
, and let
\psi:{\mathbf{F}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
be some homomorphism such that
\psi(\{x,y\})=B
. Denote by
\alpha:=\ker\varphi
and
\beta:=\ker\psi
. Since

\begin{gathered}\mbox{}[t(a,a,b)]_{\beta}=[t(a,b,a)]_{\beta}=[t(b,a,a)]_{\beta}\\
\neq[t(a,b,b)]_{\beta}=[t(b,a,b)]_{\beta}=[t(b,b,a)]_{\beta},\end{gathered}

we obtain that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{F}}}}}
depends on all three variables. On the other hand, from
\{b\}\lhd_{2}\{a,b\}
we obtain
[y]_{\alpha}\lhd_{2}{\mathbf{F}}
, so by Theorem
10

[y]_{\alpha}
is a strongly projective subuniverse of
{\mathbf{F}}
. Hence,

[t(x,y,y)]_{\alpha}=[t(y,x,y)]_{\alpha}=[t(y,y,x)]_{\alpha}=[y]_{\alpha},

and after applying
\varphi
, we obtain
t(a,b,b)=t(b,a,b)=t(b,b,a)=b
, and therefore
a\rightarrow_{m}^{B}b
.

The proof that, if there exists an affine algebra
{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, then
a\rightarrow_{a}^{B}b
is similar (just take some 2-generated subalgebra of
{\mathbf{C}}
, instead of the whole
{\mathbf{C}}
, and then proceed as in the previous paragraph).
∎

5.2.
Our edges satisfy Edge Axioms

Next we define a pair of directed graphs,
as({\mathbf{A}})
and
sm({\mathbf{A}})
, on each algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
in the finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras
{\mathcal{V}}
in a different way, and prove that these graphs also satisfy the Edge Axioms. These new digraphs depend only on the algebra on which they act, not on the rest of
{\mathcal{V}}
, and we believe their definition is easier and more natural than Bulatov’s graphs.

Definition 21
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras,
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and
a,b\in A
.

(1)

a\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}b
in
{\mathbf{A}}
if for every cyclic term
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of
{\mathcal{V}}
,

b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c(a,a,...,a,b)).

(2)

a\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b
in
{\mathbf{A}}
if for every binary term
t(x,y)
, every cyclic term
c(x_{1},...,x_{n})
of
{\mathcal{V}}
and every
k>0
such that
k\leq n/2
,

b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(b,c(\underbrace{a,a,\dots,a}\limits_{k},\underbrace{b,b,\dots,b}\limits_{n-k}))).

(In the expression
c(a,a,\dots,a,b,b,\dots,b)
, the first
k
variables are evaluated as
a
, while the remaining
n-k
variables are evaluated as
b
.)

Notation.
For shorter notation, following
[
41
]
, we will use the expression
c(a^{k}b^{n-k})
instead of
c(\underbrace{a,a,\dots,a}\limits_{k},\underbrace{b,b,\dots,b}\limits_{n-k})
.

We define the
full composition
of terms
s(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
and
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, denoted by
s\lhd t
, as the term

\begin{gathered}(s\lhd t)(x_{1},\dots,x_{mn}):=\\
s(t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),t(x_{n+1},\dots,x_{2n}),\dots,t(x_{(m-1)n+1},\dots,x_{mn})).\end{gathered}

Theorem 28
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras and for each
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
let
as({\mathbf{A}})=(A;\rightarrow_{as}^{Z})
and
sm({\mathbf{A}})=(A;\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z})
be the digraphs defined in Definition
21
. Then
\{({\mathbf{A}},as({\mathbf{A}}),sm({\mathbf{A}})):{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
satisfies the Edge Axioms.

Proof.

Base Axiom 1.
Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, let
{\mathbf{B}}
be an affine subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}
and let
c
be a cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
. If
{\mathbf{A}}
is the full idempotent term reduct of some faithful
{\mathbf{R}}
-module, then we know

c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha x_{i}

for some
\alpha\in R
such that
n\alpha=1
in
{\mathbf{R}}
. If we iterate the full composition of
c
with itself, i.e., if
c_{1}:=c
and
c_{k+1}:=c\lhd c_{k}
, inductively we obtain

c_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n^{k}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{k}}\alpha^{k}x_{i},

where we know
n^{k}\alpha^{k}=1^{k}=1
.

Now,
\alpha
is an endomorphism of the finite Abelian group
(A;+)
, so, just like any mapping of a finite set into itself, there exists an idempotent power (for composition) of
\alpha
, i.e., there exists some
m
such that
\alpha^{2m}=\alpha^{m}
. Let us compute

\displaystyle c_{m}(a,\dots,a,b)

\displaystyle=c_{m}(c_{m}(a,\dots,a,b),c_{m}(a,\dots,a,b),\dots,c_{m}(a,\dots,a,b))

\displaystyle=n^{m}\alpha^{m}(1-\alpha^{m})a+n^{m}\alpha^{2m}b

\displaystyle=n^{m}(\alpha^{m}-\alpha^{m})a+n^{m}\alpha^{m}b=b.

Moreover, for each
k
we have that

c_{k+1}(a,\dots,a,b)=c(a,\dots,a,c_{k}(a,\dots,a,b))\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c_{k}(a,\dots,a,b)),

so, inductively, each
c_{k}(a,\dots,a,b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c(a,\dots,a,b))
. We obtain, as desired,
b=c_{m}(a,\dots,a,b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c(a,\dots,a,b))
, and so
a\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}b
.

Base Axiom 2.
Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, let
{\mathbf{B}}
be a majority subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
a,b\in B
. Since
{\mathbf{A}}
is a minimal Taylor algebra and there is a ternary term which acts as the majority on
\{a,b\}
, we know that
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b)=\{a,b\}
, so we may as well assume
B=\{a,b\}
. Let
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a cyclic term on
{\mathcal{V}}
and
k\leq n/2
. Consider

p(x,y)=c(x^{k}y^{n-k}).

(We’re using the notation we established just after Definition
21
). As the two-element majority algebra only has the trivial binary operations,
p(x,y)
it is either equal to
x
or to
y
on
\{a,b\}
.

If
p(x,y)=x
, by the cyclic equations,

c(y^{n-k}x^{k})=x,

which implies that
k\neq n-k
(that is
k<n/2<n-k
). Moreover, we would get

\displaystyle c(x^{k}y^{n-k})

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle c(x^{n-k}y^{k})

\displaystyle=y\text{ and}

\displaystyle c(x^{n})

\displaystyle=x

which, together with
k<n-k<n
contradicts the well-known fact that the term operations on the two-element majority algebra are monotone with respect to order
a<b
.

Hence,
p(x,y)=y
. Again, by the cyclic equations,

c(y^{n-k}x^{k})=y,

which implies that
k\neq n-k
(that is
k<n-k
).
Therefore,

c(a^{k}b^{n-k})=b.

To prove the desired conclusion
a\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b
, we need to prove for any binary term
t(x,y)
that

b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(b,c(a^{k}b^{n-k})))={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(b,b))={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b),

which is trivially true.

Base Axiom 3.
If
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
\{a,b\}\in{\mathrm{Sub}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
is a two-element meet-semilattice with absorbing element
b
and neutral element
a
, then any cyclic term
c
of
{\mathcal{V}}
, when interpreted in the semilattice
\{a,b\}
, must have every variable essential. Hence
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
equals on
\{a,b\}
to the meet of all its variables. So
c(a,a,\dots,a,b)=b
and
a\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}b
. Also, for any binary term
t(x,y)
and
k\leq n/2
,

t(b,c(a^{k}b^{n-k}))=t(b,b)=b,

so

b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b)={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t(b,c(a^{k}b^{n-k}))).

Thus
a\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b
, too, so
a\rightarrow_{s}^{Z}b
, as required.

Homomorphism Axiom 1.
Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, let
\varphi:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
be a homomorphism, and let
a\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}b
or
a\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b
. If
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is any cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
,
k\leq n/2
and
t(x,y)
is any binary term, we have that either
b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,c(a^{n-1}b))
, or
b\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,t(b,c(a^{k}b^{n-k})))
. In the first case this implies
\varphi(b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),c(\varphi(a)^{n-1}\varphi(b)))
, while in the second case this implies
\varphi(b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),t(\varphi(b),c(\varphi(a)^{k}\varphi(b)^{n-k})))
. Homomorphism Axiom 1 follows directly.

Homomorphism Axiom 2.
Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, let
\varphi:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
be a homomorphism, and let
\varphi(a)\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}\varphi(b)
or
\varphi(a)\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}\varphi(b)
. Select
b^{\prime}\in\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(b))
such that
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
is minimal (with respect to inclusion) among the sets
\{{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c):\varphi(c)=\varphi(b)\}
.

If
\varphi(a)\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}\varphi(b)
and
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
, then there exists a binary term
p(x,y)
such that

p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{n-1}\varphi(b)))=\varphi(b).

Now we denote
b^{\prime\prime}:=p^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{n-1}b^{\prime}))
and compute

\displaystyle\varphi(b^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi(p^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{n-1}b^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{n-1}\varphi(b^{\prime})))

\displaystyle=p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{n-1}\varphi(b)))=\varphi(b).

By its definition,
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
and also
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{n-1}b^{\prime}))
, so
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})\subseteq{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
. On the other hand, from
\varphi(b^{\prime\prime})=\varphi(b)
, by the choice of
b^{\prime}
it can’t happen that
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})\subsetneq{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
, so
b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})
. Hence,
b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{n-1}b^{\prime}))
and thus
a\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}b^{\prime}
.

On the other hand, if
\varphi(a)\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}\varphi(b)
,
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
,
k\leq n/2
and
t(x,y)
is any binary term, then there exists a binary term
p(x,y)
such that

p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),t^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(b),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{k}\varphi(b)^{n-k})))=\varphi(b).

Denote
b^{\prime\prime}:=p^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(b^{\prime},c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{k}(b^{\prime})^{n-k})))
. Again we note that
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
and also
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(b^{\prime},c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{k}(b^{\prime})^{n-k})))
. We compute

\displaystyle\varphi(b^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi(p^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a,t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(b^{\prime},c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{k}(b^{\prime})^{n-k}))))

\displaystyle=p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),t^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(b^{\prime}),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{k}\varphi(b^{\prime})^{n-k})))

\displaystyle=p^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a),t^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(b),c^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\varphi(a)^{k}\varphi(b)^{n-k})))=\varphi(b).

Since
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
, we obtain
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})\subseteq{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
. On the other hand, from
\varphi(b^{\prime\prime})=\varphi(b)
, by the choice of
b^{\prime}
it can’t happen that
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})\subsetneq{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime})
, so
b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b^{\prime\prime})
. Moreover, since
b^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(b^{\prime},c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{k}(b^{\prime})^{n-k})))
, it follows that
b^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(b^{\prime},c^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a^{k}(b^{\prime})^{n-k})))
holds, so by definition
a\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b^{\prime}
.

Relational Axiom 1.
Suppose that
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
{\mathbf{R}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
,
a_{1}\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}a_{2}
in
{\mathbf{A}}
,
b_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b_{2}
in
{\mathbf{B}}
and
(a_{1},b_{2}),(a_{2},b_{1})\in R
. Let
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
. Since
a_{2}\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}}(a_{1},c(a_{1}^{n-1}a_{2}))
, there exists a term

such that
t(a_{1},c(a_{1}^{n-1}a_{2}))=a_{2}
. Applying this term in
{\mathbf{R}}
we compute

t\left(\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{bmatrix},c\left(\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{n-1}\begin{bmatrix}a_{2}\\

t(b_{2},c(b_{2}^{n-1}b_{1}))\end{bmatrix}.

So, we conclude that

. By the definition of sm-edges, from

and

, we obtain that

, i.e.,

, as desired.

Relational Axiom 2.
Suppose that
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
{\mathbf{R}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
,
a_{1}\rightarrow_{as}^{Z}a_{2}
in
{\mathbf{A}}
,

. Then there exists a term

so that

. We compute in

\displaystyle t\left(c\left(\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\

So,

, i.e.,

Relational Axiom 3.
Suppose that
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
{\mathbf{R}}\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{C}}
,

in
{\mathbf{A}}
,
b_{1}\rightarrow_{sm}^{Z}b_{2}
in
{\mathbf{B}}

(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2}),

(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2}),

. Consequently,

. Since

, there exists a binary term

such that

. We compute in

\displaystyle t_{1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\

6.
Basic consequences of Edge Axioms

First we prove some basic consequences of the edge axioms. To shorten the formulations of theorems, for the rest of this section, we assume that
{\mathcal{V}}
is a finitely generated pseudovariety of minimal Taylor algebras and that for each
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
there are two directed graphs fixed,
as({\mathbf{A}})=(A,\rightarrow_{as})
and
sm({\mathbf{A}})=(A,\rightarrow_{sm})
, so that the class
\{({\mathbf{A}},as({\mathbf{A}}),sm({\mathbf{A}}):{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}})\}
satisfies the Edge Axioms. (We note here that we only need
{\mathbf{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(2)
to be finite, but in all applications of our theory we will have a finitely generated
{\mathcal{V}}
). We denote
\rightarrow_{s}:=\rightarrow_{as}\,\cap\,\rightarrow_{sm}
and
\rightarrow_{asm}:=\rightarrow_{as}\,\cup\,\rightarrow_{sm}
, as before.

Lemma 29
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
a\rightarrow_{asm}b
in
{\mathbf{A}}
. Then there exists a term
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
such that
x_{1}
and
x_{n}
are essential variables of
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}
and that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,c_{2},\dots,c_{n-1},b)=b
for some choice of
c_{i}\in A
.

Proof.

If
a=b
there is nothing to prove (use any Taylor operation of
{\mathbf{A}}
, it is idempotent and has at least two essential variables), so we suppose that
a\neq b
.

Suppose first that
a\rightarrow_{as}b
holds. From Relational Axiom 2 follows that
(b,b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}^{2}}(\{(a,b),(a,a),(b,a)\})
. Therefore, there exists a ternary term
t
such that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,a,a)=t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,a,b)=b
. By idempotence it follows that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,a,a)=a
, and hence the first and the third positions are both essential in the term operation
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}
.

Next, suppose that
a\rightarrow_{sm}b
. Then
(b,b,b)\in{\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}^{3}}(\{(a,b,b),(b,a,b),(b,b,a)\})
follows from Relational Axiom 3. Therefore, there exists a ternary term
t(x,y,z)
such that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,b,b)=t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,a,b)=t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,b,a)=b
. By idempotence,
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,a,a)=a
, hence
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,b,a)
can not be simultaneously equal to
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,b,a)=b
and to
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,a,a)=a
. Thus, at least one of the first two positions of
t(x,y,z)
is essential in
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}
, and the same argument shows that in any pair of variables of
t(x,y,z)
at least one is essential in
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}
. It follows that
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}
has at least two essential variables, and thus
t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a,b,b)=t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,a,b)=t^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(b,b,a)=b
finishes the lemma.
∎

Corollary 30

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}

. This contradicts the fact that

∎

Next we consider the following stronger variants of the Base Axioms:

Stronger Base Axiom 1

.

, while the second statement is Corollary

.

Stronger Base Axioms 1 and 2

The next proposition is a part of A. Bulatov’s Proposition 24 from

Proposition 32
.

There exists a binary term
f(x,y)
such that
{\mathcal{V}}\models f(x,y)\approx f(x,f(x,y))
and for any
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and any
a,b\in A
, either
f(a,b)=a
or
a\rightarrow_{s}f(a,b)
. Moreover, whenever
a\rightarrow_{s}b
, then
f(a,b)=f(b,a)=b
.

Proof.

Take any cyclic term of
{\mathcal{V}}
and make all of its variables, except the first one, equal to obtain the term
t(x,y)
. For all
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and any
a,b\in A
such that
a\rightarrow_{s}b
, Corollary
19
implies that
B=\{a,b\}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
\{b\}
is a strongly projective subuniverse of
{\mathbf{B}}
. Moreover, each variable of a cyclic term is essential (also in the subalgebra
{\mathbf{B}}
), so
t(a,b)=t(b,a)=b
for every
a\rightarrow_{s}b
in
{\mathbf{A}}
. Every term we will construct in this proof will be a two-variable term in the language
\{t\}
in which the variables
x
and
y
will both occur, so the basic properties of semilattices imply that for all
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, any
a,b\in A
such that
a\rightarrow_{s}b
and all terms, those terms act on
\{a,b\}
as the semilattice operation with the absorbing element
b
.

Now we iterate the term
t
in the first variable:

t_{1}(x,y):=t(x,y)\text{ and }t_{i+1}(x,y):=t(x,t_{i}(x,y)).

It is easy to compute that for
n:=|F_{{\mathcal{V}}}(2)|
and
k=n!
it holds that
{\mathcal{V}}\models t_{k}(x,t_{k}(x,y))\approx t_{k}(x,y)
.

Next, we denote

and compute

{\mathcal{V}}\models t_{k}(x,q(x,y))=t_{k}(x,t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x)))\approx t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x))=q(x,y),

and thus

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{V}}\models q(q(x,y),x)=t_{k}(t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x)),t_{k}(x,t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x))))\approx\\
t_{k}(t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x)),t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x)))\approx t_{k}(x,t_{k}(y,x))=q(x,y).\end{gathered}

Finally, we use another iteration in the first variable

q_{1}(x,y):=q(x,y)\text{ and }q_{i+1}(x,y):=q(x,q_{i}(x,y)).

As before for

,

{\mathcal{V}}\models q_{k}(x,q_{k}(x,y))\approx q_{k}(x,y).

We prove by an induction on

that for all

,

, and suppose that it holds for

. Then

{\mathcal{V}}\models q_{i+1}(q(x,y),x)=q(q(x,y),q_{i}(q(x,y),x))\approx q(q(x,y),q(x,y))\approx q(x,y).

By substituting

for

we obtain

{\mathcal{V}}\models q_{k}(q(x,q_{k-1}(x,y)),x)\approx q(x,q_{k-1}(x,y)),

which can be written differently as

{\mathcal{V}}\models q_{k}(q_{k}(x,y),x)\approx q_{k}(x,y).

We choose for

imply that for all
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and all

, either
f(a,b)=a
, or
a\rightarrow_{s}f(a,b)
in
{\mathbf{A}}
. Moreover, we know that for all
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and all
a,b\in A
such that

in
{\mathbf{A}}
,

∎

.

The next theorem and its proof are our version of Theorem 5 of

. Its proof is shorter than in

, and we use much less theory: in
[

, A. Bulatov uses the Tame Congruence Theory of D. Hobby and R. McKenzie

Theorem 33
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Then
asm({\mathbf{A}})
is weakly connected.

Proof.

We use an induction on
|A|
. When
|A|=2
, the Post lattice reveals that there are only four minimal clones with a Taylor term, the two semilattice clones, the affine clone and the clone generated by the majority operation. Moreover, any Taylor clone contains one of these four, so these are all minimal Taylor clones. These clones have weakly connected asm-graphs by Base Axioms 1-3.

For the inductive step, let
a,b\in A
be two distinct elements. We have to prove that
a
is weakly connected to
b
. First note that if
B={\mathrm{Sg}}(a,b)\neq A
then by the inductive assumption
a
is weakly connected to
b
in
asm({\mathbf{B}})
, and then we can apply Proposition
17
. Moreover, we easily prove the following:

Claim 1
.

If
a
and
b
are connected in the hypergraph
H({\mathbf{A}})
, then
a
and
b
are weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{A}})
.

Proof of the claim.

The assumption implies that there exists a sequence
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
of proper subuniverses of
A
which connect
a
to
b
. In other words,
a\in C_{1}
,
b\in C_{k}
and for all
i<k
,
C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}\neq\emptyset
. Select
a=c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{k}=b
so that for each
1\leq i<k
,
c_{i}\in C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}
. For each
i<k
, by the inductive assumption,
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}
are weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{C}}_{i+1})
. By Proposition
17
,
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}
are also weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{A}})
, and the concatenation connects
a
and
b
.
∎

Claim 2
.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
has a nontrivial congruence
\alpha
, then
a
and
b
are weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{A}})
.

Proof of the claim.

By the inductive assumption,
[a]_{\alpha}
and
[b]_{\alpha}
are weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{A}}/\alpha)
. This means that we can select some
\alpha
-blocks
[a]_{\alpha}=[c_{0}]_{\alpha},[c_{1}]_{\alpha},\dots,[c_{k}]_{\alpha}=[b]_{\alpha}
such that for each
i<k
, either
[c_{i}]_{\alpha}\rightarrow_{asm}[c_{i_{1}}]_{\alpha}
, or
[c_{i_{1}}]_{\alpha}\rightarrow_{asm}[c_{i}]_{\alpha}
. Now, using the natural homomorphism from
{\mathbf{A}}
onto
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha
and Homomorphism Axiom 2, we can select
d_{i}
and
e_{i}
,
i\leq k
, so that
d_{i},e_{i}\in[c_{i}]_{\alpha}
,
a=d_{0}
,

and for all
i<k
we have that at least one of

and
d_{i+1}\rightarrow_{asm}e_{i}
holds. Moreover, since each
[c_{i}]_{\alpha}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
which is strictly smaller, thus for all
i\leq k
, by the inductive hypothesis we know that
d_{i}
and
e_{i}
are weakly connected in
G_{asm}({\mathbf{A}})
. Therefore,
a
and
b
are weakly connected, as desired.
∎

Claim 3
.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
has a nontrivial tolerance
\tau
, then
a
and
b
are weakly connected in
asm({\mathbf{A}})
.

Proof of the claim.

By Claim 2, we can assume that
{\mathbf{A}}
is simple. Since the transitive closure of

is a congruence, either this closure is

, in which case
\tau=0_{{\mathbf{A}}}
, or this closure is

, in which case

is connected. By Proposition

, if

is connected and
\tau\neq 1_{{\mathbf{A}}}
, then

is connected, so

and

are weakly connected by Claim 1.
∎

Claim 4
.

For any
{\mathbf{B}}
and any
{\mathbf{R}}\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
, we can assume that either

is the graph of a surjective homomorphism from
{\mathbf{B}}
onto
{\mathbf{A}}
, or there exists some

.

Proof of the claim.

For the congruence

, by Claim 2 the only options are

and
lk_{1}R=1_{{\mathbf{A}}}
. Assume that

. Using the assumption that

and according to Theorem
2
, if

, then we are done by Claim 3. So, we can assume that

, using again Theorem

and the fact that

, we obtain that there exists some

such that

. On the other hand, if
lk_{1}R=0_{{\mathbf{A}}}
, then

is the graph of a surjective homomorphism from
{\mathbf{B}}
onto
{\mathbf{A}}
.
∎

Claim 5
.

For any
c,d\in A
which are not in the same weak component of
asm({\mathbf{A}})
, the relation
R_{cd}:={\mathrm{Sg}}^{{\mathbf{A}}^{2}}((c,d),(d,c))
is the graph of an automorphism
\varphi_{cd}\in{\mathrm{Aut}\>{\mathbf{A}}}
such that
\varphi_{cd}(c)=d
,
\varphi_{cd}(d)=c

\varphi_{cd}^{2}=id

Proof of the claim.

Since

and

, and thus

is not the graph of an automorphism, then by Claim 4 there exists some

. If

and

. Thus,

and

are weakly connected, a contradiction. Hence

∎

Claim 6
.

If

is not weakly connected, then the automorphism group

acts transitively on
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Proof of the claim.

Consider any

. If

and

\varphi_{cd}
maps

to

. On the other hand, if

to

We assume from now on that

and

are not weakly connected in

Next we consider

, i.e., it is invariant under permutations of coordinates, since its generating set is symmetric. Let

Claim 7
.

(a,a,a)\notin S
and
(b,a,b)\notin S
.

Proof of the claim.

If

is also obtained from the assumption

. Applying the automorphism

Claim 8
.

\mathrm{pr}_{1}T\cap\mathrm{pr}_{2}T\neq\emptyset

Let

{\mathbf{A}}

Now we have

p\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&a&b\\
a&b&a\\

d\\
c\end{bmatrix}\in S\;\text{ and }\;q\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&a&b\\
a&b&a\\

e\\

Claim 9
.

\mathrm{pr}_{1}T=A
.

Proof of the claim.

Suppose first that

,

,

Now suppose that

onto

Claim 10

\mathrm{pr}_{i,j}S=A^{2}

We prove it for

\mathrm{pr}_{1,3}S\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{2}

∎

Denote by

Claim 11
.

For any

,

is the graph of a bijection.

Proof of the claim.

First of all, Claim 10 and the idempotence of

Suppose first that

. In that case, either

is not connected to

, or to

. By the inductive assumption, either
{\mathrm{Sg}}(a,d)=A
or
{\mathrm{Sg}}(b,d)=A

(b,a),(d,a)\in S^{a}

, hence

Now, let

, and let

be the graph of an automorphism of
{\mathbf{A}}
. We select some

which is not connected to

in

and such that

Because of Claim 4, we know that

Let

We say that an M-template
{\mathcal{T}}
is M-reduced if every maximal-sized algebra in
{\mathcal{T}}
among those without binary absorbing subuniverses can not be eliminated. “SMB algebras”, which were considered in
[
37
]
, are Taylor algebras with two fundamental operations
d(x,y,z)
and
x\wedge y
which are defined by some equations. In the special case of SMB algebras, the authors were able to apply Lemma
40
to reduce any instance to an M-reduced instance in which every maximal-sized
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
which has a binary absorbing subuniverse has a nice structure. In particular, there was a “neutral element”
1
in each such algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
such that, in the language of Bulatov’s edges,
1\rightarrow_{s}x
for all
x\in A
. Moreover, the element
1
really acted neutrally: for each term, if we evaluate one of its variables as 1, the obtained polynomial is equal to some other term in one fewer variable.

Using this structure, the authors of
[
37
]
proved that an M-reduced template has a solution iff its restriction to variables which have no binary absorbing subuniverses has a solution. In the case of SMB algebras, if
{\mathbf{A}}
has no proper binary absorbing subuniverses, then
{\mathbf{A}}
has no s-edges at all, i.e., there is no subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}
which has a proper binary absorbing subuniverse. It has been known since
[
7
]
that such algebras have “few subpowers”, so they are tractable. This is a shortcut to proving the tractability of SMB algebras, and the nice structure of M-reduced instances was proved using just the binary absorbing part of Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem (Theorem
12
). It seemed possible that this binary absorbing part of Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem can be proved separately from the rest of the proof of Dichotomy Theorem, and then applied to prove the full Dichotomy just like in the case of SMB algebras.

Thus, we tried to generalize these ideas from SMB algebras to minimal Taylor algebras. Indeed, we were able to prove that any maximal-sized algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
in an M-reduced template has a single maximal (i.e., source) strong component in
asm-({\mathbf{A}})
(it also has a single minimal one, by Corollary
37
). Moreover, we were able to overcome the potential problem with the M-templates, since minimal Taylor algebras might not be closed under taking retracts. By choosing term reducts of those retracts which are minimal Taylor, Definition
25
and Lemma
40
can be modified to apply to minimal Taylor algebras.

However, there exists a minimal Taylor algebra
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
which can not be eliminated using Lemma
40
, but it has more than one element in its sole maximal strong component of
{\mathbf{A}}
. The example below appeared first in
[
8
]
, as Example 4.10.2.

Example 1
.

The algebra
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}=(\{0,1,2,3\};f)
is defined below.

{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
has the ternary cyclic operation

given by:

•

when
\{x,y,z\}=\{1,2,3\}

•

•

On any

It can be proved that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a minimal Taylor algebra, and it has no neutral element. This is a problem since the ideas of
[

involved adding a solution that goes through all neutral elements to the instance, and then applying Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem concluding that there must exist some other solution which is never equal to the neutral element. As opposed to a single neutral element which must be a subuniverse (by idempotence),

is not a subuniverse, so adding solution(s) which are always in

may generate new solutions which are not in

.

The next result, taken from

{\mathcal{T}}

9.
Concluding remarks and further research

In this paper we introduced a different take on Bulatov’s colored edges, by focusing on the properties such objects ought to have and then finding various graphs which fit these properties. We managed to reprove some of the results in the initial part of Bulatov’s theory just from these abstract properties, and there is no doubt in our minds that the whole theory can be restated in this way. Next, we simplified the proofs of Theorem 33, Theorem 36 and Corollary 38 from their original proofs in
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
and
[
3
]
. Finally, we analyzed the approach via consistent maps by M. Maróti and found reasons why it does not generalize fully from SMB algebras to minimal Taylor algebras. However, we managed to apply Bulatov’s result in the case of “large centralizers”, with subdirectly irreducible domains of variables, to prove the binary absorbing part of Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem in that case.

In subsequent projects, some authors of this paper together with G. Gyenizse and M. Kozik proceeded to investigate the two proofs of the Dichotomy Theorem, and discovered further connections between them. However, since colored edges are shown to be less useful in these subsequent results, much less than in investigating binary absorption, we split these results off into a separate paper, which is in production. More recently, another subset of authors of this paper, together with M. Maróti, managed to prove the Dichotomy Theorem using consistent maps and a stronger version of Zhuk’s Reduction Theorem, but only its part related to binary absorption.
