A haemato-neuro-renal diagnostic challenge in advanced HIV: multiple opportunistic infections including novel Mycobacterium chelonae pyelonephritis and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder with myelopathy
Authors: Shubham Lodha, Ankit Das, Bhavya Sharda, Mahadev Meena, Anand Kumar Maurya, Sagar Khadanga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advanced HIV disease predisposes individuals to a wide and often concurrent spectrum of complications arising from opportunistic infections, immune dysregulation, direct viral injury, and treatment effects. In tuberculosis-endemic, resource-limited settings, disseminated tuberculosis frequently dominates clinical reasoning for multisystem disease in profoundly immunosuppressed patients — a cognitive default that risks failing to identify concurrent, independently treatable conditions. We describe a 35-year-old man with advanced HIV infection from central India who simultaneously developed parvovirus B19-associated marrow suppression, xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis due to co-infection with carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli and Mycobacterium chelonae, and symptomatic HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) with myelopathy. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of M. chelonae pyelonephritis in a patient with HIV infection. This case report describes the diagnostic considerations for advanced HIV presenting as concurrent multisystem disease in a tuberculosis-endemic, resource-limited setting, and the clinical consequences of moving beyond the presumption of a single unifying aetiology.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is characterised by progressive immune dysfunction that predisposes affected individuals to a wide spectrum of complications, which may arise from direct HIV-mediated damage, opportunistic infections ranging from typical to rare, treatment-related effects, and inflammatory processes [1]. Patients with advanced HIV may therefore present with multisystem involvement reflecting the simultaneous unfolding of disparate aetiologies — a clinical tableau of competing diagnoses rather than a single unifying condition. Particularly in tuberculosis-endemic regions, tuberculosis remains a primary and often sole diagnostic consideration. However, the aetiological spectrum for complications such as neurological dysfunction, cytopenias, and renal disease is exceptionally broad. Neurological manifestations in HIV encompass opportunistic infections and primary HIV-associated conditions such as HAND and myelopathy — the latter being diagnoses of exclusion. Among haematological complications, parvovirus B19 — a common childhood pathogen — is a well-recognised cause of life-threatening bone marrow suppression in HIV, often underdiagnosed because impaired humoral immunity may result in absent serological responses [2], [3]. Renal involvement in patients with HIV have higher risk of complicated urinary tract infections that may progress to destructive pathology [4]. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria, though uncommon causes of genitourinary disease, are increasingly recognised as extrapulmonary pathogens — yet data on genitourinary NTM infection specifically in this population remain scarce [5].

Attributing multisystem disease to a single unifying aetiology, thus risk overlooking concurrent or alternative pathologies with distinct management implications, underscoring the importance of rigorous parallel diagnostic evaluation. The approach of single unifying etiology may thus risk overlooking concurrent or alternative pathologies with distinct management implications. Rigorous parallel diagnostic evaluation carries profound therapeutic consequences compared with conventional attribution of multisystem disease to a single unifying aetiology. We present a case that illustrates this principle: a patient with advanced HIV, persistent pancytopenia, progressive neuropsychiatric symptoms, and pyelonephritis, in whom comprehensive evaluation identified not a unifying diagnosis but three distinct concurrent aetiologies — parvovirus B19-associated myelosuppression, xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis due to co-infection with carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli and Mycobacterium chelonae, and direct HIV-mediated neurological disease. To our knowledge, pyelonephritis caused by M. chelonae has not been previously described in patients with HIV infection, expanding the recognised spectrum of non-tuberculous mycobacterial disease in advanced HIV. This case further illustrates the diagnostic challenges and clinical consequences of pursuing diagnoses beyond the obvious presumption.

Case report

A 35-year-old male daily-wage worker was referred for evaluation of multisystem complaints following diagnosis of HIV-1 infection at an outside facility approximately three months prior, with a baseline CD4 count of 60 cells/µL. He was a reformed alcoholic (abstinent for two years, previous intake 30–60 mL of country liquor daily for three years) and tobacco chewer, with no other substance use. He was initiated on antiretroviral therapy (ART) consisting of tenofovir, lamivudine, and dolutegravir after clinical exclusion of active pulmonary tuberculosis. Despite reported full adherence to ART, newer complaints emerged along with persistence of existing symptoms, thus prompting admission for further evaluation.

His symptoms had begun 12 months earlier with intermittent low-grade fever. Neuropsychiatric symptoms, which emerged concurrently, began with irrelevant speech, progressing to fixed delusions involving family members and auditory hallucinations. He subsequently developed progressive bilateral lower-limb weakness for two months and dysuria for one month. Motor involvement developed as acute-onset, rapidly progressive spastic paraparesis that confined him to bed within one month. Urinary symptoms — initially retention, then incontinence accompanied by dysuria — developed in parallel. He reported more than 10 kg of unintentional weight loss over six months. There was no history of headache, seizures, visual disturbance, dysphagia, bowel dysfunction, cough, haemoptysis, dyspnoea, abdominal pain, vomiting, or diarrhoea.

On examination, the patient was cachectic with severe pallor. There was no icterus, clubbing, pedal oedema, or lymphadenopathy. Multiple small (1–3 mm) skin-coloured nodular lesions with central umbilication were present over the face and limbs. Neurologically, he was alert but disoriented with slurred speech. Higher mental function testing demonstrated deficits in executive function and recent memory, fulfilling criteria for multi-domain cognitive impairment. Meningeal signs were absent; cranial nerve examination and fundoscopy were normal, with no papilloedema, choroidal tubercles, or features of CMV retinitis. Motor examination showed spasticity and symmetric power of 3/5 in both lower limbs, with exaggerated patellar and ankle reflexes, bilateral extensor plantar responses, and absent jaw jerk. Upper-limb tone and power were normal. Sensory examination was unremarkable. Baseline haemogram, renal function, and liver function tests, performed prior to ART initiation, were within normal limits except for hypoalbuminaemia.

At admission, haemoglobin was 10 g/dL with further haematological deterioration becoming apparent over subsequent weeks: haemoglobin declined to a nadir of 3·5 g/dL, with associated leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, persisting for more than two months and requiring more than ten units of packed red blood cell transfusions. Peripheral smear revealed anisopoikilocytosis with macro-ovalocytes, teardrop cells, microcytes, and elliptocytes. Mean corpuscular volume was elevated at 103 fL; reticulocyte count was inappropriately low at 2·4%, consistent with a hypoproliferative anaemia. Serum vitamin B12, folate, iron indices, and triglycerides were within normal limits; ferritin was markedly elevated at 3886 ng/mL. Bone marrow biopsy revealed a hypocellular marrow with no infiltration or haemophagocytosis; Ziehl–Neelsen stain, CBNAAT, MGIT culture, bacterial culture, fungal stains, and fungal cultures were all negative. Epstein–Barr virus DNA PCR was performed and returned negative, excluding EBV-associated myelosuppression. PVB19 IgM serology was negative at this stage; PVB19 PCR, which would have been the preferred confirmatory test, could not be performed due to non-availability at our institution. Given the multisystem presentation in a tuberculosis-endemic region, empirical antitubercular therapy (ATT) was initiated approximately two weeks into the admission with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, fluconazole prophylaxis, pyridoxine supplementation, and dose adjustment of dolutegravir during rifampicin co-administration were commenced alongside.

Acute kidney injury developed during admission, with creatinine rising to 3·13 mg/dL. Urinalysis revealed haematuria, pyuria, and moderate proteinuria. Ultrasonography demonstrated a right staghorn calculus with gross hydroureteronephrosis and sonographic features of pyelonephritis. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen (Fig. 1) confirmed a right staghorn calculus with gross hydronephrosis and imaging features consistent with xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP), including the characteristic bear paw sign. Urine culture grew carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli in significant colony counts. Expanded mycobacterial evaluation of urine was simultaneously pursued: specimen was divided into four aliquots for direct acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear microscopy, cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (CB-NAAT), mycobacterial growth indicator tube (MGIT) culture, and Line Probe Assay (LPA), respectively. Direct smear microscopy was performed using fluorochrome staining with Auramine-O and examined according to standard laboratory guidelines; the smear was reported as negative for AFB. Sample digestion and decontamination were carried out using the N-acetyl-L-cysteine–sodium hydroxide (NALC–NaOH) method. CB-NAAT was performed using the GeneXpert® MTB/RIF system in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. This fully automated cartridge-based molecular platform integrates sample processing, nucleic acid extraction, amplification, and real-time detection. The assay employs hemi-nested real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) with molecular beacon technology, using five overlapping fluorescent probes targeting the 81-base-pair rifampicin resistance–determining region of the rpoB gene specific to the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. The test result was negative for M. tuberculosis, with no evidence of rifampicin resistance. Mycobacterial culture using the MGIT system remained negative after 42 days of incubation. Further, a Line Probe Assay was performed on the decontaminated urine sample for species-level identification of non-tuberculous mycobacteria. DNA extraction was carried out using the GenoLyse® kit as per the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplification was performed using biotinylated primers supplied with the assay kit. The amplified products were then subjected to reverse hybridisation with species-specific oligonucleotide probes immobilised on nitrocellulose strips. Detection was achieved through colorimetric development, and interpretation was based on the banding pattern compared with the manufacturer’s reference template. The LPA demonstrated probe positivity consistent with Mycobacterium chelonae. He received intravenous meropenem and colistin for ten days for CRE pyelonephritis, and underwent percutaneous pigtail nephrostomy drainage, with rapid subsequent improvement in renal function. Clarithromycin was initiated for M. chelonae infection and continued for eight months. The progressive, rather than fluctuating, course of multi-domain cognitive deficits, with interference in activities of daily living, argued against acute delirium and prompted evaluation for an underlying major neurocognitive disorder. CSF analysis revealed normal glucose, mildly elevated protein (68 mg/dL), and 2 lymphocytes/µL. A comprehensive infectious CSF workup — including M. tuberculosis (CBNAAT, ZN stain), Cryptococcus (CrAg, India ink), CMV PCR, HSV PCR, JC virus PCR, VDRL, KOH, bacterial culture, Japanese encephalitis IgM, and West Nile virus IgM — was entirely negative. MRI brain (Fig. 2) showed discrete T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the periventricular white matter and bilateral centrum semiovale, without diffusion restriction; MRI spine showed no intrinsic cord signal abnormality. Serum copper levels, like serum B12, was within normal limits, excluding both as aetiology for the myelopathy. HIV-associated neurological disease was considered, supported by persistent multi-domain cognitive deficits with functional decline, spastic paraparesis with bladder dysfunction, characteristic imaging findings, and comprehensive exclusion of CNS opportunistic infections and structural or metabolic alternatives. At approximately 6 months from ART initiation (8 weeks after initial admission), virological suppression was achieved and CD4 count had risen to approximately 100 cells/µL. Repeat PVB19 IgM serology at this point demonstrated seroconversion, coinciding temporally with rapid haematological recovery: haemoglobin rose to 8·0 g/dL and reticulocyte count reached 9–14%, consistent with restoration of erythropoiesis without specific antiviral therapy.

The diagnostic conclusions were reached through a combination of targeted positive evidence and systematic exclusion of competing aetiologies, as detailed in Table 1, — driven by the recognition that in advanced HIV, multisystem disease may reflect concurrent rather than unifying pathology. Parvovirus B19-associated marrow suppression was diagnosed on the basis of hypoproliferative pancytopenia with hypocellular bone marrow, absence of infiltrative or nutritional causes, and IgM seroconversion coinciding with immune reconstitution and spontaneous haematological recovery. Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis due to co-infection with carbapenem-resistant E. coli and M. chelonae was established by the pathognomonic CT bear paw sign, significant bacteriuria on urine culture, and LPA identification of M. chelonae on expanded mycobacterial urine workup. Symptomatic HAND was diagnosed in accordance with Frascati criteria, supported by neuropsychological documentation of impairment in at least two cognitive domains — executive function and episodic memory — with functional decline, after comprehensive exclusion of opportunistic CNS infections, metabolic, and structural aetiologies through CSF analysis and advanced neuroimaging. HIV-associated myelopathy was established as a diagnosis of exclusion, supported by progressive spastic paraparesis with bladder dysfunction in the setting of normal spinal imaging, normal CSF, and normal serum copper levels. Treatment included targeted antimicrobial therapy directed towards identified opportunistic infections, along with continuation of ART, cotrimoxazole and fluconazole prophylaxis. ATT, started empirically, was complicated by drug-induced liver injury (peak AST 316 U/L; total bilirubin 6·3 mg/dL), necessitating temporary modification to a streptomycin-, levofloxacin-, and ethambutol-based regimen. Following hepatic recovery, isoniazid and pyrazinamide were permanently discontinued, while rifampicin and ethambutol were reintroduced — not as antitubercular therapy, but as components of the NTM regimen for M. chelonae — alongside clarithromycin and amikacin for an initial intensive phase of three months, followed by a continuation phase of rifampicin, ethambutol, and clarithromycin for twelve months. At last follow-up, the patient had achieved complete haematological recovery, normalised renal function, and virological suppression. Neurological symptoms had partially improved, with residual lower-limb contractures.

Discussion

Advanced HIV disease often defies Occam's razor, as multisystem manifestations may arise from multiple concurrent pathological processes rather than a single unifying aetiology. This case illustrates that complexity: our patient developed simultaneous complications stemming from direct retroviral injury, immune dysregulation, and opportunistic infections. Given the patient’s residence in a tuberculosis-endemic region, disseminated tuberculosis was the primary initial differential, prompting initiation of empirical ATT. The absence of supportive microbiological, radiological, and biochemical evidence for tuberculosis, alongside the depth of immunosuppression, drove a broader evaluation. Systematic multisystem diagnostic workup ultimately identified concurrent aetiologies — parvovirus B19-associated marrow suppression, carbapenem-resistant E. coli and M. chelonae-associated pyelonephritis, and HIV-associated neurological disorders.

Cytopenias and Parvovirus B19 in advanced HIV infection

Persistent cytopenias in people with HIV have diverse aetiologies (Table 3), ranging from causes seen in the general population — such as anaemia of chronic disease and nutritional deficiencies — to HIV-specific conditions including direct viral suppression of haematopoiesis, medication toxicity, and opportunistic infection-mediated marrow suppression or infiltration [2]. Among opportunistic pathogens causing cytopenias, parvovirus B19 deserves particular consideration. While classically associated with aplastic crises in patients with chronic haemolytic anaemias, PVB19 IgG has been detected in approximately 40% of HIV-infected patients, though only a subset develops clinically significant anaemia [6]. Diagnosis of PVB19 infection relies on detection of IgM/IgG antibodies or viral DNA by PCR. In advanced HIV, however, profound immunosuppression may render antibody responses severely blunted — meaning serology may be genuinely negative despite active infection, rather than falsely so. In this context, PCR-based detection of viral DNA is the preferred diagnostic modality; where PCR is unavailable, as in our setting, repeat serology after immune reconstitution with ART may unmask a previously absent antibody response and confirm the diagnosis retrospectively [8].

Our patient, with profound immunosuppression, presented with prolonged transfusion-dependent pancytopenia with hypoproliferative indices, bone marrow hypocellularity, and excluded nutritional and infiltrative causes — a clinical picture strongly suggestive of PVB19-associated aplasia. Initial PVB19 IgM serology was negative, consistent with the blunted antibody responses seen in profound immunosuppression, and PCR was unavailable. Repeat serology performed after approximately 2 months of initial admission demonstrated IgM seroconversion coinciding with virological suppression and partial immune reconstitution (CD4 count rise of approximately 50 cells/µL). The simultaneous resolution of cytopenias with brisk reticulocytosis (9–14%), without PVB19-specific antiviral therapy, provides strong indirect evidence that immune reconstitution alone was sufficient to clear the infection and restore erythropoiesis. As immune function improves on ART, patients mount an effective humoral response adequate to reduce viraemia and allow marrow recovery. This temporal relationship — serological conversion concurrent with immune recovery and haematological improvement — reflects the established course of PVB19-associated marrow suppression in HIV [9].

Recovery across the three cell lines followed a dissociated, biphasic pattern: total leukocyte count and platelet count began recovering earlier in the course, while haemoglobin recovery remained delayed until PVB19 IgM seroconversion was documented later in the admission. We attribute the earlier myeloid and megakaryocytic recovery to resolution of generalised marrow suppression from active HIV viraemia and the infective burden of xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis, both being brought under control through ART-driven virological suppression and targeted antimicrobial therapy with source control. Erythroid recovery, in contrast, remained suppressed until PVB19 clearance through immune reconstitution — consistent with the virus's selective erythroid tropism, which persists independently of resolution in other lineages — offering a plausible mechanistic explanation for the biphasic trajectory observed.

This case thus emphasises several important diagnostic and therapeutic principles regarding PVB19 in advanced HIV. First, in advanced HIV with profound immunosuppression, cytopenias warrant investigation beyond common aetiologies to include conditions with heightened risk in this population — PVB19-associated aplasia being a prime example, often overlooked in favour of more familiar causes. Second, a negative PVB19 IgM at presentation must not be interpreted as exclusionary in profoundly immunosuppressed patients — repeat serology following immune reconstitution remains a valid and important diagnostic strategy. Third, in advanced HIV, effective ART with resultant immune reconstitution may itself be sufficient to restore erythropoiesis in PVB19-associated aplasia, without recourse to PVB19-specific therapy — making early ART optimisation the primary therapeutic objective.

Non-tubercular mycobacterium and genitourinary Infections in HIV

Urinary tract infections in immunocompromised patients are frequently more severe, with a broader aetiological spectrum than in the general population. Multidrug-resistant bacterial UTIs are increasingly reported, with an association between resistance and more severe immunosuppression. Beyond classical uropathogens, opportunistic and atypical organisms including M. tuberculosis, NTM, and Candida species are encountered [4].

NTM, though most commonly associated with pulmonary disease, are recognised causes of extrapulmonary infection, including genitourinary disease, in the presence of specific risk factors [10]. Among NTMs, rapidly growing mycobacteria — particularly M. abscessus — are most frequently implicated in genitourinary disease [5]. In contrast, M. chelonae, classically associated with cutaneous and soft-tissue infections, has been reported only rarely as a cause of genitourinary infection, predominantly in transplant recipients or other profoundly immunocompromised hosts [11].

In advanced HIV, the confluence of impaired cell-mediated immunity, MDR pathogens, and polymicrobial infections can culminate in aggressive, tissue-destructive phenotypes. One such severe complication is xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis — a rare granulomatous form of chronic pyelonephritis associated with long-standing obstruction and polymicrobial infection [12]. Consequently, diagnostic approach to UTI in advanced HIV must extend beyond standard urine culture to include mycobacterial and fungal cultures supplemented by molecular diagnostics such as line probe assays, particularly when presentations are severe, imaging suggests destructive pathology, or the infection is refractory to empirical therapy.

In our patient, who developed xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis, expanded microbiological evaluation revealed co-infection with carbapenem-resistant E. coli and M. chelonae (identified via LPA). The pathogenesis of M. chelonae pyelonephritis in this case warrants mechanistic consideration. M. chelonae is an environmental, rapidly growing mycobacterium of low intrinsic virulence and negligible urotropism, found predominantly in cutaneous and soft-tissue disease — renal involvement has not, to our knowledge, been previously reported in HIV infection. In our patient, several pathological factors appear to have converged beyond the baseline of severely compromised cell-mediated immunity. A staghorn calculus causing urinary obstruction impaired mechanical pathogen clearance while simultaneously disrupting urothelial integrity, compromising local epithelial innate defences and facilitating mycobacterial urotropism. The resultant chronic obstructive inflammatory milieu promoted granuloma formation — the pathological hallmark of XGP. Concurrent carbapenem-resistant E. coli pyelonephritis likely compounded this further, with bacterial-mediated urothelial disruption and tissue destruction creating an additional permissive environment for secondary M. chelonae invasion. The convergence of these three factors provides a plausible mechanistic framework for an otherwise inexplicably rare event.

HIV-associated neurological conditions

Neurological complications in advanced HIV range from opportunistic infections — notably tuberculosis, cryptococcosis, toxoplasmosis, and cytomegalovirus — to primary HIV-associated neurological conditions including HAND, vacuolar myelopathy, and sensory neuropathies. Their development has historically been correlated with the degree of immunosuppression, though this association is not uniformly observed [13]. The pathogenesis of these primary conditions involves a complex interplay of immune-mediated damage and potential viral invasion of neural tissue. Beyond direct viral invasion, HIV−1 proteins Nef and Vpr are increasingly implicated in HAND pathogenesis via extracellular vesicle-mediated neurotoxicity, disrupting myelin integrity and dysregulating neuroinflammatory pathways even during effective ART. This offers a plausible contributor to persistent cognitive impairment independent of active systemic viraemia, relevant to patients such as ours who develop neurological symptoms despite virological suppression.

HAND classically manifests as cognitive impairment, motor dysfunction, and behavioural change, reflecting diffuse frontal-subcortical pathology. While no definitive diagnostic test exists, neuroimaging often reveals cerebral atrophy, basal ganglia volume loss, or non-specific white matter hyperintensities [14]. Diagnosis of HAND has historically been guided by the Frascati research nosology, which classifies HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders into three stages of increasing severity — asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI), mild neurocognitive disorder (MND), and HIV-associated dementia (HAD) — requiring documented impairment in at least two cognitive domains with corresponding functional consequences for symptomatic stages [15]. However, the Frascati criteria have been challenged in recent literature: a 2023 consensus statement noted that these criteria may misclassify more than 20% of cognitively healthy individuals as impaired, and proposed a revised framework incorporating biomarker-based diagnostic approaches [16]. In resource-limited settings where such biomarkers remain inaccessible, the Frascati framework continues to represent the most pragmatic available diagnostic standard. HAND remains a diagnosis of exclusion regardless of the nosological framework applied, necessitating thorough investigation of alternative causes including opportunistic CNS infections, metabolic disorders, and substance use. HIV-associated myelopathy, characterised by spastic paraparesis, sensory ataxia, and bladder dysfunction, is similarly a diagnosis of exclusion based on clinical presentation alongside spinal imaging and CSF studies excluding alternative myelopathies. Non-specific spinal imaging findings such as cord signal abnormalities or atrophy have been described, but normal cord imaging is also reported [17]. The co-occurrence of both diagnoses, as seen here, has been reported previously, consistent with shared pathophysiological mechanisms and the emerging concept of these conditions representing a spectrum — sometimes termed vacuolar encephalomyelopathy — rather than entirely distinct entities [18].

In our patient, progressive neuropsychiatric decline and spastic paraparesis with bladder dysfunction prompted a comprehensive workup. Opportunistic infection, metabolic, and structural aetiologies were systematically excluded through CSF analysis and advanced imaging. Neuropsychological assessment demonstrated impairment in at least two cognitive domains — executive function and episodic memory — accompanied by clinically significant decline in activities of daily living, collectively satisfying the Frascati criteria for symptomatic HAND; HIV-associated myelopathy was concurrently established as a diagnosis of exclusion on the basis of progressive spastic paraparesis and bladder dysfunction in the setting of normal spinal imaging and cerebrospinal fluid.

Combination ART has substantially changed the course of HIV neurology, reducing the incidence of HIV-associated dementia by approximately 50% and modifying the phenotypic expression of HAND [19]. The cornerstone of management is virological suppression with a well-tolerated regimen. Regimens incorporating drugs with higher central nervous system penetration-effectiveness (CPE) scores have been proposed to enhance neurocognitive outcomes, but the clinical benefit remains debated, with meta-analyses showing no consistent advantage over standard suppressive therapy [20]. Adjunctive therapies targeting downstream neuroinflammatory pathways remain an active area of investigation.

This case illustrates a principle that extends beyond any single diagnosis: in advanced HIV, particularly in tuberculosis-endemic and resource-limited settings, the instinct to converge on a unifying aetiology carries real clinical risk. Each of the three concurrent conditions identified here — PVB19-associated aplasia, M. chelonae pyelonephritis, and direct HIV-mediated neurological disease — required distinct investigation, distinct management, and would have been missed or delayed had diagnostic reasoning stopped at any earlier point. The resource constraints that shaped our approach — empirical ATT in the absence of confirmatory microbiological evidence, serial rather than concurrent serological testing, unavailability of PVB19 PCR, CSF HIV viral load and dedicated mycobacterial PCR across a wide range of sample types — are not unique to our setting; they reflect the reality of HIV care across much of the high-burden world. For clinicians managing advanced HIV globally, this case reinforces that diagnostic breadth, systematic parallel evaluation, and willingness to pursue expanded microbiological testing beyond standard panels are not academic luxuries — they are the difference between treating a presumption and treating the patient.

Conclusion

This case represents, to our knowledge, the first reported instance of Mycobacterium chelonae pyelonephritis in a patient with HIV infection, and demonstrates that advanced HIV disease may simultaneously manifest as concurrent, independently treatable conditions spanning multiple organ systems. The systematic exclusion of tuberculosis as a unifying diagnosis, alongside expanded microbiological evaluation and parallel multisystem workup, was essential in reaching the correct diagnoses and directing appropriate therapy. As combination ART extends survival in HIV, clinicians globally — particularly in tuberculosis-endemic, resource-limited settings — will increasingly encounter patients in whom diagnostic parsimony is not a virtue but a hazard.
