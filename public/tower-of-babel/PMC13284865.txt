What's Behind DOOR Number 2? Recent Applications From Post-Hoc Analyses of Infectious Diseases Randomized Clinical Trials
Authors: Jason M Pogue, Ryan K Shields
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This editorial explores the impact and interpretation of desirability of outcome ranking (DOOR) analyses compared to traditional endpoints in recent infectious diseases pharmacotherapy literature.

In 2 recent publications in Open Forum in Infectious Diseases, researchers present findings of Desirability of Outcome Ranking (DOOR) analyses in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for the treatment of Acute Bacterial Skin and Skin Structure Infections (ABSSI) [1] and duration of therapy for non-Staphylococcal bacteremia [2]. First, Howard-Anderson and colleagues developed a novel DOOR endpoint for ABSSI and reanalyzed the OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 RCTs comparing omadacycline and linezolid [1]. Second, Ong and colleagues utilized data from the BALANCE RCT comparing 7 versus 14 days of therapy for bloodstream infections to create and analyze a paradigm shifting DOOR endpoint to compare duration of therapy [2]. The authors aimed to move from a theoretical benefit of short course therapy (ie, DOOR-RADAR, where the tiebreaker between patients with identical outcomes is duration of therapy) to a documented one (ie, DOOR-FRAME, where the tiebreaker is the subsequent development of resistance). These 2 publications represent a growing trend in infectious diseases pharmacotherapy where hierarchical clinical endpoints (HCE) are used to compare therapeutic regimens rather than binary ones. Given the widespread adoption of HCE in trial design, and in some cases, as a primary endpoint, it is important to consider how these analyses may or may not change our interpretations from traditional binary endpoints.

DOOR was developed on the patient-centric premise that binary “all or nothing” analyses have the potential to miss clinically important differences between treatment arms. For example, if the endpoint of interest in a VAP trial is clinical failure at the test of cure, this endpoint would equate a patient who was alive, cured, and liberated from the ventilator with a patient who was alive, cured, remained on the ventilator, and developed acute kidney injury secondary to treatment. This may theoretically lead to “no difference” between treatment arms in a binary outcome measure; however, applying an ordinal endpoint based on the priority of clinicians, patients, and/or investigators may identify important differences. The overall goals are to create a more holistic assessment of the patient outcome, account for competing risks of endpoints, and provide a more detailed risk to benefit assessment of efficacy and safety. Accordingly, DOOR endpoints are reported as the probability that a random patient who receives treatment A would have a better outcome than a random patient who received treatment B with significance defined as the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval being >50% (ie, the patient has a >50% chance of a better outcome with the intervention). Similar approaches have been successfully employed with Win Ratio (WR) and Win Odds in cardiovascular pharmacotherapy, which are also increasingly being employed in infectious diseases pharmacotherapy.

While DOOR is an innovative endpoint for outcomes assessment, the question remains whether such analyses provide new interpretations over traditional endpoints. For registry trials, experts have developed and analyzed DOOR endpoints for HAP/VAP [3], cUTI [4], cIAI [5], and ABSSI [1]. These DOOR analyses rank outcomes that range from best (alive, no events) to worst (death) using the number of events occurring among survivors. This event rate commonly includes one event representing clinical cure, one for serious adverse events (SAEs), and one for infectious complications. In subsequent DOOR analyses of published RCTs, the noninferiority that was demonstrated with traditional binary endpoints has been similar to the nonsignificant differences demonstrated in the overall DOOR or its various components. For example, in the analysis by Howard-Anderson and colleagues, the overall DOOR findings for omadacycline in OASIS-1 (50.6%, 95% CI 47.4%–53.7%) and OASIS-2 (52.1%, 95% CI 49.2%–55.0%) [1] were not meaningfully different than the difference of the binary endpoint of an investigator assessed clinical response post-treatment in both trials [6]. This is perhaps not surprising because the rate of other DOOR components (death, infectious complications, and SAEs) were infrequent, and therefore the DOOR was principally driven by clinical response.

HCE analyses have similarly been applied to other landmark infectious diseases trials with a consistent theme of affirming conclusions reached by traditional binary endpoints. For example, while a DOOR analysis supported immunoguided discontinuation of valganciclovir prophylaxis when compared with fixed duration prophylaxis for cytomegalovirus disease among kidney transplant recipients treated with antithymocyte globulin (66.1% (95% CI, 64.4%–67.7%)), an assessment of endpoints for efficacy (ie, incidence of CMV disease 0% vs 2.7%; P = .24) and safety (ie, incidence of neutropenia 9.2% vs 37.8%; P < .01) leads to the same conclusion [7]. Likewise, DOOR and traditional endpoints have reached the same conclusion for the DOTS [8] and CAMERA-2 [9, 10] trials. In the current DOOR-FRAME analysis by Ong and colleagues, the modest, but insignificant improvement of the DOOR for short course therapy (46.8% (95% CI 40.4%–53.2%)) with the addition of the FRAME component (47.4% (95% CI 40.9%–53.8%)) is consistent with the slightly higher incidence of the tiebreaking binary outcome of antimicrobial resistant organisms or C. difficile infection within 180 days between groups (6.9% vs 8.5%) [2].

Thus, the question remains, if there are potentially significant differences in safety and efficacy outcomes that are better reflected by ordinal endpoints, then how do we explain the findings to date that have not identified those differences? First, it may simply be that when competing outcomes diverge between treatment arms, binary (or composite) endpoints can be appropriately used to perform a risk to benefit analysis. For example, in CAMERA-2, patients with MRSA bacteremia treated with combination therapy had numerically higher rates of mortality (21% vs 16%; P = .28), statistically lower rates of persistent bacteremia at day 5 (11% vs 20%; P = .02), and statistically higher rates of acute kidney injury (23% vs 6%; P < .001) compared with monotherapy [9]. While the impact on duration of bacteremia is intriguing, the increased rates of AKI and potentially mortality would likely lead one to avoid combination therapy, even though the overlap of individual endpoints is unknown. A subsequent DOOR analysis incorporating these (and other) factors found that combination therapy had a 54.5% (95% CI 48.9%–60.1%) probability of a worse outcome—in other words, no significant difference [10]. Taken together, these data highlight that an overall summary statistic may not meaningfully change the interpretation, and in some instances, outcomes that differ in their degree of clinical relevance may offset each other when solely considered as equal “events” (eg, is persistent bacteremia truly equivalent to acute kidney injury?) leading to a conclusion of no difference, when an important difference exists. Thus, clinicians must be cognizant of which events are included in DOOR assessments, and how each component is contributing to the overall probability of a better or worse outcome. While stakeholders have made the case that clinical failure, SAE's, and infectious complications are of equal importance, the latter 2 components, particularly in registry RCTs, may have varying causal associations to the study drug, and their inclusion as equal events increases the likelihood that no difference will be identified in an overall DOOR endpoint. Furthermore, for some disease states (eg, cUTI and ABSSI) analyses have demonstrated that few subjects have >1 event, and therefore binary comparisons of these events essentially provide the same output. While tiebreakers such as shorter duration of therapy or potential ecological impact of a narrower spectrum therapy have been employed to achieve “significant” DOOR probabilities in favor of the shorter course or narrower agent, these endpoints are somewhat predetermined, and they do not change the real-world interpretation from binary endpoints combined with the stewardship assumption that shorter or narrower is better.

Clearly, DOOR is an innovative way to assess clinical outcomes, and we applaud leaders in the infectious diseases’ community for creating a novel approach to enhance our understanding and translation of RCT data into clinical practice. That said, DOOR analyses performed to date have not identified nuanced differences that could not also be ascertained by traditional binary or composite endpoints. Reflecting on the 2 recent OFID publications and the broader literature base, we consider DOOR an important complementary approach to assess comparative effectiveness, but not yet a replacement for traditional primary endpoints. To overcome some of the potential limitations, we encourage future analyses that evaluate ordinal outcomes in a stepwise manner wherein a clear hierarchy of clinically important outcomes most likely to be influenced by the study treatment can be assessed sequentially. Whether this is performed via DOOR, WR, or another innovative HCE, such analyses may prove useful in determining the net benefit (or deficits) with specific interventions, should they exist.
