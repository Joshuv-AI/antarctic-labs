Thue-Morse sequence and groups of intermediate growth
Rostislav Grigorchuk, Yaroslav Vorobets

Abstract
We consider the substitution subshift generated by the Thue-Morse substitution 0 → 01 0\to 01 , 1 → 10 1\to 10 . We prove that the topological full group of the subshift contains a subgroup of intermediate growth. Namely, one group from the family known as the Grigorchuk groups embeds into this group. To obtain our main result, we prove an embedding theorem for topological full groups, and also develop a technique to prove isomorphism of groups using the Schreier graphs.

Introduction

There are many topics where the interests of theory of dynamical systems
and algebra converge. The interconnection between these two mathematical
disciplines goes both ways. Theory of dynamical systems is looking for
algebraic invariants to use in various classification problems. Besides, a
lot of dynamical systems are of algebraic origin (see
[
Sch
,
LS
]
). On the other hand, algebra (first of all, group theory)
uses ideas and methods of theory of dynamical systems for new constructions
and for solving old problems as demonstrated, for instance, in the recent
book by V. Nekrashevych
[
Ne2
]
.

This paper is centered around one specific topic on the intersection of
theory of dynamical systems with group theory, namely, the topological full
groups associated with Cantor minimal systems.

The
Cantor minimal systems
are classical objects of study in
topological dynamics. Such a system
(X,T)
consists of a Cantor set
X

and a homeomorphism
T:X\to X
, which defines a continuous action of the
infinite cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}
on
X
. The action is to be minimal in the
sense that it allows no closed invariant set other than the empty set and

X
. We are interested in systems coming from symbolic dynamics. Recall
that the two-sided
shift
over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
(of at least
two symbols) is the transformation
\sigma
of the space
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of
bi-infinite sequences of symbols from
\mathcal{A}
that acts by the rule

\sigma(\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots)=\ldots y_{-2}y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
, where
y_{n}=x_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
A
subshift
is the restriction of
\sigma
to a closed shift-invariant
set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Any subshift is uniquely determined by the set
of all forbidden words (i.e., finite strings of symbols that must not occur
in elements of
\Omega
) or, equivalently, by the set of all admissible
words. Two major classes of subshifts are subshifts of finite type and
minimal subshifts. They represent two different kinds of dynamic behavior
(chaotic vs. regular). A subshift of finite type is determined by
specifying a finite number of forbidden words. The minimal subshifts are
not so easy to construct. The main source of examples are the substitution
subshifts generated by primitive substitutions. A
substitution
over
an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a procedure that replaces every symbol from the
alphabet with a certain word over
\mathcal{A}
. Suppose that the substitution
preserves an infinite sequence
\xi=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
of symbols from
\mathcal{A}
,
and let
\bigl(\Omega(\xi),\sigma\bigr)
be the subshift for which the admissible
words are words that occur infinitely often in
\xi
. Then it is called a

substitution subshift
. The substitution is
primitive
if,
starting with any symbol and applying the substitution several times, we
eventually end up with a word that includes all symbols in the alphabet.
If this is the case then the subshift
\bigl(\Omega(\xi),\sigma\bigr)
is
necessarily a Cantor minimal system (see, e.g., the books

[
AS2
,
Fo
,
LM
]
).

The
Thue-Morse
substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
over the binary alphabet

\{0,1\}
, given by
0\to 01
,
1\to 10
, is among the simplest and most
famous substitutions that appear in many areas of mathematics. Starting
with
0
and repeatedly applying the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
, we obtain a
sequence of finite binary strings
0,01,0110,01101001,\dots
where each
string extends the previous one. This results in an infinite binary string

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}=01101001\dots
invariant under the substitution rule. The string

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
is known as the
Thue-Morse sequence
. Since the substitution

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
is primitive, the sequence
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
generates a minimal subshift over
the binary alphabet
\{0,1\}
, called the
Morse subshift
. One
instance where the sequence
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
appears in algebra is a criterion of
nilpotency of a group in terms of semigroup identities, which uses the idea
of iteration in the spirit of the Thue-Morse substitution as shown
implicitly by A. Maltsev
[
Mal
]
and repeated in an explicit form in

[
BP
]
. This is also related to the so-called Zimin words (see,
e.g., the book
[
Sa
]
), which are used in theory of rings. L. Rowen

[
Ro
]
suggested that
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
may be the key to solving one of
the most famous problems in theory of rings, the Koethe conjecture. For
many more applications of the Thue-Morse sequence in many different
contexts, see the survey
[
AS1
]
.

The automaton computing
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
and the automaton generating the
lamplighter group.

Here is another curious connection of the Thue-Morse sequence to group
theory. The sequence
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
is
2
-automatic, which means there is an
automaton that, given a number
n
in its binary form as input, computes
the
n
-th symbol of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
. If the symbols are counted starting from

0
, the task is done by a
2
-state automaton shown in Figure

1
on the left (see
[
AS2
]
).
It is an acceptor-like automaton in which the result is determined by the
terminal state. Now let us consider a transducer automaton with the same
transition machine shown in Figure
1
on the right. This
automaton outputs one symbol per each symbol of input. Choosing one of the
states as initial, the automaton generates a transformation on the set of
binary strings. The transformation happens to be related to a Gray code.
Recall that a Gray code is an ordering of numbers
0,1,\dots,2^{k}-1
such
that the binary representations of any two neighbors differ in a single
digit. Given a number
n
as a
k
-digit binary input, the automaton
outputs the position of
n
in the Gray code. The two states of the
automaton give rise to two transformations, which in turn generate a
transformation group. The group turns out to be the lamplighter group,
i.e., the wreath product of an infinite cyclic group with a group of order

2
(see Theorem 7 in
[
BG+
]
).

The notion of a
topological full group
(TFG) is relatively recent.
Such a group can be associated to any homeomorphism
T:X\to X
, where
X

is a Cantor set. Informally, the TFG of
T
, denoted
[[T]]
, consists of
all homeomorphisms of
X
that locally coincide with elements of the cyclic
group generated by
T
(see Definition
3.1
). After the paper

[
GPS
]
by T. Giordano, I. Putnam and C. Skau, the TFG emerged as an
algebraic invariant of topological dynamics of the system
(X,T)
provided
it is a Cantor minimal system (see Theorem
3.2
).

The TFG’s of Cantor minimal systems are groups with unique properties.
Already in
[
GPS
]
it was shown that they are indicable (admit a
homomorphism onto
\mathbb{Z}
). The systematic study of algebraic properties of
groups
[[T]]
was spearheaded by H. Matui
[
Ma1
]
who showed that the
commutator subgroup
[[T]]^{\prime}
is simple and, in the case of a minimal
subshift, finitely generated. The studies were continued by Matui

[
Ma2
]
, K. Medynets and the first author
[
GM1
,
GM2
]
, K.
Juschenko and N. Monod
[
JM
]
, and others. Any finite group and
the free abelian group of infinite rank embed into
[[T]]
. If the system

(X,T)
is not topologically conjugate to a subshift, then the group

[[T]]
is locally virtually abelian (i.e., every finitely generated
subgroup
H\subset[[T]]
contains an abelian subgroup of finite index in

H
). Otherwise the lamplighter group embeds into
[[T]]^{\prime}
(see

[
Ma2
]
). The latter implies exponential growth of the group

[[T]]^{\prime}
as the lamplighter group contains a free semigroup on two
generators. Further remarkable properties of TFG’s include non-elementary
amenability
[
JM
]
and factorization of
[[T]]^{\prime}
into a product
of two locally finite subgroups
[
Ma1
,
GM1
]
. Neither
[[T]]
nor

[[T]]^{\prime}
can be finitely presented but an effective description of the
system
(X,T)
may help to obtain an infinite presentation. For instance,
if
(X,T)
is a subshift such that all admissible words form a recursive
language, then the commutator subgroup
[[T]]^{\prime}
allows a recursive
presentation and, as a consequence, has a decidable word problem (see

[
GM2
]
). The maximal subgroups of TFG’s were studied by the
authors in
[
GV
]
.

N. Matte Bon
[
M-B
]
observed that the TFG of a Cantor minimal
system may contain a subgroup of intermediate (faster than polynomial but
slower than exponential) growth. Namely, each group from a family

\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
constructed in
[
Gr1
]
embeds into
the TFG of a minimal subshift. The growth of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
is intermediate if
the sequence
\omega
is not eventually constant. The groups
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
have
many other unusual properties. They are branch, just-infinite groups, most
of them are torsion groups (that is, every element has finite order). Each
group acts transitively on levels of the binary rooted tree, and the
Schreier graphs of those actions have line-like shapes. This defines a
linear order on each level that gives rise to a Gray code (see

[
M-B
]
). The code is the same for all groups
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
and,
somehow, it is related to the Gray code computed by the automaton in Figure

1
. Each group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
also acts naturally on the
boundary of the binary rooted tree, and the Schreier graphs associated with
the orbits of that action also have line-like shapes. The latter fact was
used in numerous articles for various purposes, in particular, to initiate
the study of a joint spectrum of self-similar groups (see
[
BG
]
)
or to construct first examples of simple groups of intermediate growth

[
Ne1
]
. Linear structure of the Schreier graphs will be important in
this paper as well.

Among groups
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
, a special role belongs to the groups

\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
associated to periodic
sequences
012012\dots
and
010101\dots
. Unlike generic groups in the
family, these two are self-similar groups, namely, each of them naturally
embeds as a subgroup of finite index into the wreath product of itself with
the group of order
2
. The two groups share many properties but there are
also essential differences. For example, the first group is torsion while
the second one has elements of infinite order. Each maximal subgroup in

\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
has finite index while
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
admits
maximal subgroups of infinite index.

The Schreier graphs of the group
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
were studied by the
second author in
[
Vo2
]
. D. Lenz, T. Nagnibeda and the first author
observed in
[
GLN
]
that
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
can be embedded into the
TFG of the substitution subshift generated by a substitution

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}
given by
a\to aca
,
b\to d
,
c\to b
,
d\to c
.
They used this fact to link the spectral problem for Schreier graphs of

\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
to the spectral theory of random Schroedinger
operators. The substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}
is not primitive but,
in fact, the associated subshift coincides with the subshift generated by a
primitive substitution
a\to ac
,
b\to ad
,
c\to ab
,
d\to ac
(see

[
ADQ
]
). Note that the TFG of this subshift is finitely
generated. An explicit generating set was found in
[
Vo3
]
.

The substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}
occurs naturally in the study of
the group
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
. Indeed, the group is generated by four
elements denoted
a,b,c,d
and the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}

induces an injective endomorphism of
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
. This was first
observed by I. Lysenok
[
Ly
]
who obtained the following
presentation of the group
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}
by generators and relators:

\bigl\langle a,b,c,d\mid 1=a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=d^{2}=bcd=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}^{k}\bigl((ad)^{4}\bigr)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}^{k}\bigl((adacac)^{4}\bigr),\ k=1,2,\dots\bigr\rangle.

Recursive group presentations of this kind are called

L
-presentations
by analogy with the
L
-systems that are used for
the study of formal languages and in some other areas of science. Note
that the above presentation is optimal in the sense that no relator is
redundant (as shown in
[
Gr2
]
). Interestingly, this optimality is
preserved after the profinite completion of the group
\mathcal{G}_{(012)^{\infty}}

(as proved by M. G. Benli
[
Be
]
).

Remarkably, the group
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
also admits an
L
-presentation

\bigl\langle a,c,d\mid\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{k}(r)=1,\ r\in R,\ k\geq 0\bigr\rangle,

where the set of basic relators
R
is

\{a^{2},(cd)^{2},(ad)^{4},[d,ac]^{2},[d,cac]^{2},[d,acaca]^{2},[d,acacac]^{2},[d,cacacac]^{2}\}

and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is the substitution given by
a\to aca
,
c\to d
,
d\to c
.
It was obtained by Y. Muntyan
[
Mu
]
in his dissertation. It is
not known yet whether this presentation is optimal. We do not need the
presentation in this paper, but the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
will be very
important in what follows. Just like
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}
, the
substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is not primitive. However, as we will show in this
paper, the associated substitution subshift is topologically conjugate to
the subshift generated by the
period doubling
substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}
:

x\to xy
,
y\to xx
, which is primitive. Note that the substitution
subshifts generated by the substitutions
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Ly}}
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}

belong to a family of Toeplitz subshifts studied in
[
Vo1
]
.

The main result of this paper adds another item to the list of properties
of the group
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
showing its connection to the classical
Thue-Morse sequence.

Theorem 1.1
.

The group of intermediate growth
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
embeds into the
topological full group of the substitution subshift generated by the
Thue-Morse substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
as well as into the TFG of the substitution
subshift generated by the period doubling substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}
.

The embeddings in Theorem
1.1
can be described explicitly (see
Proposition
5.15
at the end of the paper).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the following general result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(X_{1},T_{1})
and
(X_{2},T_{2})
be aperiodic Cantor systems. If

(X_{2},T_{2})
is a continuous factor of the system
(X_{1},T_{1})
then the TFG

[[T_{2}]]
embeds into
[[T_{1}]]
.

Given a factor map
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
, the induced embedding

\mathcal{E}_{f}:[[T_{2}]]\to[[T_{1}]]
in Theorem
1.2
can
be described explicitly. Namely, every homeomorphism
S_{2}\in[[T_{2}]]
is
given by
S_{2}(y)=T_{2}^{\nu(y)}(y)
,
y\in X_{2}
for some continuous function

\nu:X_{2}\to\mathbb{Z}
. Then the homeomorphism
S_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{f}(S_{2})
is given
by
S_{1}(x)=T_{1}^{\nu(f(x))}(x)
,
x\in X_{1}
.

Theorem
1.1
inspires a number of questions and topics to explore.
We would like to formulate some of them.

Problem 1.3
.

Suppose
T_{\mathsf{s}}
is a substitution subshift generated by a primitive
substitution
\mathsf{s}
.

(i)

Under what condition on the substitution
\mathsf{s}
the topological full
group
[[T_{\mathsf{s}}]]
of the subshift
T_{\mathsf{s}}
contains a group of
intermediate growth?

(ii)

Under what conditions on
\mathsf{s}
the group
[[T_{\mathsf{s}}]]
contains a
subgroup of Burnside type (i.e., finitely generated infinite torsion group)?

In particular, what can be said about the TFG of the subshift generated by
the Fibonacci substitution
0\to 01
,
1\to 0
? Or the Rudin-Shapiro
substitution
a\to ab
,
b\to ac
,
c\to db
,
d\to dc
? Or the Chacon
substitution
0\to 0012
,
1\to 12
,
2\to 012
?

There are other remarkable groups that have finite
L
-presentations. One
example is the iterated monodromy group (IMG) of the polynomial
z^{2}+i
.
This group first appeared in
[
BGN
]
and then in many other
articles including
[
GSS
]
where a finite
L
-presentation for

\mathrm{IMG}(z^{2}+i)
was found. It is quite similar to the above
presentation of
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
but uses a substitution
a\to b
,
b\to c
,
c\to aba
. This substitution preserves no infinite sequence.
Instead, there are three sequences cyclically permuted by the
substitution. Each of the three sequences generates the same minimal
subshift. Note that the group
\mathrm{IMG}(z^{2}+i)
acts naturally on the
boundary of a rooted binary tree but, unlike the groups
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
, the
Schreier graphs of orbits are not of linear shape.

Problem 1.4
.

Does the group
\mathrm{IMG}(z^{2}+i)
embed into the TFG of a minimal Cantor
system? Does it embed into the TFG of a substitution subshift generated by
a primitive substitution?

We should also mention that connections between the Thue-Morse substitution
and self-similar groups were previously explored in the paper

[
BCT
]
where the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
was used to construct
a certain self-similar group that turned out to be the IMG of the rational
map
f(z)=\bigl(z-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}\bigr)^{-1}
. Note that this group can be
generated by a
3
-state automaton and, in fact, it is featured in the
classification
[
BG+
]
of such groups (see Automaton no.
2853 in
[
BG+
]
).

(Theorem

2
Substitution subshifts

An
alphabet

\mathcal{A}
is a finite set consisting of more than one
element. Elements of
\mathcal{A}
are referred to as
letters
or

symbols
. Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}
denote the set of all finite strings

x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}
of letters from
\mathcal{A}
(including the empty one

\varnothing
). We refer to them as
words
over
\mathcal{A}
and write
without any delimiters. The number of letters in a word is called its

length
. Elements of
\mathcal{A}
are identified with words of length
1
.
For any words
u,w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
let
uw
denote their
concatenation
: if

u=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{m}
and
w=y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{n}
, where each
x_{i}
and
y_{j}

is a letter, then
uw=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{m}y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{n}
. This operation is
associative and turns
\mathcal{A}^{*}
into a monoid. Likewise, for any finite list
of words
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{k}
their concatenation is denoted
w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{k}
. The concatenation of
k
copies of the same word
w
is denoted

w^{k}
. We say that a word
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a
prefix
of a word

w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
if
w=uv
for some
v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
. We say that
u
is a

subword
of
w
if
w=vuv^{\prime}
for some
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

For any nonempty set
S
let
\mathcal{A}^{S}
denote the set of all functions

\xi:S\to\mathcal{A}
. In the cases
S=\mathbb{N}
(positive integers),
S=-\mathbb{N}

(negative integers), and
S=\mathbb{Z}
(all integers), we represent an element

\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{S}
as respectively an infinite string
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
, a
left-infinite string
\ldots x_{-3}x_{-2}x_{-1}
, and a bi-infinite string

\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
, where
x_{i}=\xi(i)
for each
i\in S
. Notation for bi-infinite strings includes a dot that serves as a
reference point. We say that a nonempty word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a

subword
of the string
\xi
if
w=x_{n}x_{n+1}\ldots x_{m}
for some
integers
n
and
m
,
n\leq m
such that
\{n,n+1,\dots,m\}\subset S
.

Given a word
w=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, an infinite string

\eta=y_{1}y_{2}\ldots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and a left-infinite string
\zeta=\ldots z_{2}z_{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
, we define
concatenations

w\eta
,
\zeta w
,
and
\zeta.\eta
as respectively the infinite string
x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
, the left-infinite string
\ldots z_{2}z_{1}x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}
,
and the bi-infinite string
\ldots z_{2}z_{1}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
. We say that a
word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a
prefix
of an infinite string
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}

if
\xi=w\eta
for some
\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Note that for any words

u,w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
and strings
\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
, we
have
(uw)\eta=u(w\eta)
and
\zeta(uw)=(\zeta u)w
so that expressions

uw\eta
and
\zeta uw
make sense. On the other hand, usually

\zeta.(u\eta)\neq(\zeta u).\eta
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},\dots
be an infinite list of words in
\mathcal{A}^{*}
in which at
most finitely many words are empty. Then there exists a unique string

\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that each finite concatenation
w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{k}
,

k\geq 1
is a prefix of
\eta
. We denote
\eta
as an
infinite
concatenation

w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}\ldots
. If all
w_{i}
are copies of the same word

w
, we also use notation
w^{\infty}
. Likewise, the
left-infinite
concatenation

\ldots w_{3}w_{2}w_{1}
is defined as the only left-infinite
string
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
k\geq 1
we have

\zeta=\zeta^{\prime}(w_{k}\ldots w_{2}w_{1})
for some
\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
.

Next we are going to introduce the
shift
and
subshifts
over
an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, which are topological dynamical systems on the set

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and its subsets. First we need a topology on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. We
regard
\mathcal{A}
as a discrete topological space, then
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is endowed
with the
product topology
. Given any nonempty finite set

S_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}
and any function
\omega_{0}:S_{0}\to\mathcal{A}
, let

\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(S_{0},\omega_{0})
denote the set of all functions
\omega:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathcal{A}

such that the restriction of
\omega
to
S_{0}
coincides with
\omega_{0}
. Sets
of the form
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(S_{0},\omega_{0})
, referred to as
cylinders
, are
open subsets of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, they form a base of the topology on

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The topological space
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact and metrizable.
Convergence in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the pointwise convergence of functions. In
terms of bi-infinite strings, a sequence of strings
\omega^{(k)}=\ldots x^{(k)}_{-2}x^{(k)}_{-1}x^{(k)}_{0}.x^{(k)}_{1}x^{(k)}_{2}x^{(k)}_{3}\ldots
,

k=1,2,\dots
converges to a string
\eta=\ldots y_{-2}y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}\ldots
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
if for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
the
sequence of letters
x^{(1)}_{n},x^{(2)}_{n},x^{(3)}_{n},\dots
eventually
coincides with the constant sequence
y_{n},y_{n},y_{n},\dots
.

Definition 2.1
.

The (two-sided)
shift
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a transformation
of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, denoted
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
or simply
\sigma
, that sends any
function
\omega:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathcal{A}
to a function
\eta:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathcal{A}
given by

\eta(n)=\omega(n+1)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In terms of bi-infinite strings,

\sigma(\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots)=\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}x_{1}.x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\ldots
. A
subshift
over
\mathcal{A}
is the restriction
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y

of the shift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
to a nonempty closed set
Y\subset\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such
that
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(Y)=Y
.

The shift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}
is invertible and maps cylinders onto cylinders,
hence it is a homeomorphism of the topological space
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
onto
itself. Any subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
is a homeomorphism of the compact
topological space
Y
onto itself.

Given an infinite string
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Omega(\xi)
denote the
set of all bi-infinite strings
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that every subword of

\omega
is also a subword of
\xi
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the set
\Omega(\xi)
is nonempty and closed,
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega(\xi))=\Omega(\xi)
. Every subword of any string in

\Omega(\xi)
occurs infinitely many times as a subword of
\xi
.

Proof.

For any bi-infinite string
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the shifted string

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)
has exactly the same subwords as
\omega
. It follows that

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega(\xi))=\Omega(\xi)
. Next we prove that
\Omega(\xi)
is
a closed set. Given a nonempty word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
of length
k\geq 1
and any

n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\Sigma_{w,n}
be the set of all bi-infinite strings
\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
y_{n}y_{n+1}\ldots y_{n+k-1}=w
. The set
\Sigma_{w,n}
is a cylinder. The complement of

\Omega(\xi)
is the union of cylinders
\Sigma_{w,n}
over all words

w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
that are not subwords of
\xi
and over all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
the complement is an open set. Then the set
\Omega(\xi)
is closed.

Next we prove that every subword
u
of any string
\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
occurs
infinitely often as a subword of
\xi
. Let
\xi=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
and

\omega=\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
. If
u
is a subword of length
s\geq 1

of
\omega
, we have
y_{m}y_{m+1}\ldots y_{m+s-1}=u
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
for any
k\geq 1
the word
u_{k}=y_{m-k}y_{m-k+1}\ldots y_{m-1}y_{m}y_{m+1}\ldots y_{m+s-1}
is a subword of length
s+k
of
\omega
.
Since
\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
, the same word
u_{k}
is also a subword of the string

\xi
. That is,
x_{n_{k}}x_{n_{k}+1}\ldots x_{n_{k}+k+s-1}=u_{k}
for some

n_{k}\geq 1
. Then
x_{n_{k}+k}x_{n_{k}+k+1}\ldots x_{n_{k}+k+s-1}
is another
occurrence of
u
as a subword of
\xi
. Since
n_{k}+k\geq k+1
, there are
infinitely many numbers of the form
n_{k}+k
,
k\geq 1
. Therefore
u
occurs
infinitely often as a subword of the string
\xi
.

It remains to prove that the set
\Omega(\xi)
is not empty. Let
W_{\xi}
be
the set of all nonempty words that occur infinitely many times as subwords
of
\xi
. Since the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is finite while the string
\xi
is
infinite,
W_{\xi}
contains at least one
1
-letter word. Let us show that
for any word
w\in W_{\xi}
there exist letters
l,r\in\mathcal{A}
such that the
word
lwr
belongs to
W_{\xi}
as well. Let
s
be the length of
w
.
There is a sequence
\{n_{k}\}
,
2\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\ldots
, such that

x_{n_{k}}x_{n_{k}+1}\ldots x_{n_{k}+s-1}=w
for all
k\geq 1
. Since the
alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is finite, we can find letters
l,r\in\mathcal{A}
such that

(x_{n_{k}-1},x_{n_{k}+s})=(l,r)
for infinitely many values of
k
. Then

lwr\in W_{\xi}
.

Now it follows by induction from the above that there exist two sequences
of letters
r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\dots
and
l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},\dots
such that for any

k\geq 1
the word
w_{k}=l_{k}l_{k-1}\ldots l_{2}l_{1}r_{0}r_{1}r_{2}\ldots r_{k}
belongs
to
W_{\xi}
. Then the bi-infinite string
\omega=\ldots l_{2}l_{1}r_{0}.r_{1}r_{2}\ldots
belongs to the set
\Omega(\xi)
so that this set is not empty.
Indeed, any subword
u
of
\omega
is also a subword of
w_{k}
for all
sufficiently large
k
. Since each
w_{k}
is a subword of
\xi
, so is
u
.
∎

In view of Lemma
2.2
, the restriction of the shift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}

to the set
\Omega(\xi)
, where
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, is a subshift. We refer to
it as the subshift generated by the string
\xi
. Even though
\xi
does
not belong to
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the shift directly relates it to the set

\Omega(\xi)
via the following construction.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any infinite string
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
and left-infinite string

\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
, the set of all limit points of the sequence

\zeta.\xi,\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
in

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
coincides with
\Omega(\xi)
.

Proof.

Let
\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
be the bi-infinite string
\zeta.\xi
.
Then
\xi=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
. For any
k\geq 0
we have
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(\zeta.\xi)=\ldots x^{(k)}_{-1}x^{(k)}_{0}.x^{(k)}_{1}x^{(k)}_{2}\ldots
, where

x^{(k)}_{n}=x_{n+k}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider an arbitrary bi-infinite
string
\omega=\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. First assume that

\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
. For any
k\geq 1
the word
w_{k}=y_{-k}\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}\ldots y_{k}
is a subword of
\omega
of length
2k+1
. Hence it
is also a subword of
\xi
. That is,
x_{n_{k}}x_{n_{k}+1}\ldots x_{n_{k}+2k}=w_{k}
for some
n_{k}\geq 1
. Moreover,
n_{k}
can be chosen
arbitrarily large since
w_{k}
occurs infinitely many times as a subword of

\xi
(due to Lemma
2.2
). Therefore we can build the sequence

\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
inductively in such a way that
1<n_{1}+1<n_{2}+2<n_{3}+3<\ldots
. Then
\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{n_{k}+k}(\zeta.\xi)\}_{k\geq 1}
is a subsequence
of the sequence
\zeta.\xi,\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
. This subsequence converges to
\omega
since

x^{(n_{k}+k)}_{m}=x_{m+n_{k}+k}=y_{m}
whenever
k\geq|m|
.

Conversely, assume that
\omega
is a limit point of the sequence

\zeta.\xi,\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
. That is,

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{k_{i}}(\zeta.\xi)\to\omega
as
i\to\infty
, where
0\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<k_{3}<\ldots
. Take any nonempty subword
u
of the string
\omega
.
We have
y_{n_{1}}y_{n_{1}+1}\ldots y_{n_{2}}=u
for some integers
n_{1}
and

n_{2}
,
n_{1}\leq n_{2}
. The convergence implies that the word

x^{(k_{i})}_{n_{1}}x^{(k_{i})}_{n_{1}+1}\ldots x^{(k_{i})}_{n_{2}}=x_{n_{1}+k_{i}}x_{n_{1}+1+k_{i}}\ldots x_{n_{2}+k_{i}}
coincides with
u
for all
sufficiently large
i
. If
i
is large enough then, additionally,

n_{1}+k_{i}\geq 1
so that
u
occurs as a subword of the string
\xi
. Thus
every subword of
\omega
is also a subword of
\xi
, which means that

\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

A
substitution
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a map

\mathsf{s}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
. The substitution can be
applied term by term to any finite or infinite string
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
of
letters from
\mathcal{A}
, which yields a finite or infinite concatenation

\mathsf{s}(x_{1})\mathsf{s}(x_{2})\mathsf{s}(x_{3})\ldots
. Hence the map

\mathsf{s}
induces a transformation of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
and a transformation of

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We use the same notation for the substitution and both
induced transformations.

Let
\mathsf{s}
be a substitution over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Suppose
z
is
a letter in
\mathcal{A}
such that the word
\mathsf{s}(z)
begins with
z
and
has length greater than
1
. That is,
\mathsf{s}(z)=zw
for some nonempty
word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
. Consider a sequence of words

z,\mathsf{s}(z),\mathsf{s}^{2}(z),\dots
obtained by repeatedly applying the
substitution to the letter
z
. For any
k\geq 1
we have
\mathsf{s}^{k}(z)=\mathsf{s}^{k-1}(zw)=\mathsf{s}^{k-1}(z)\mathsf{s}^{k-1}(w)
. Note that

\mathsf{s}^{k-1}(w)\neq\varnothing
since
w\neq\varnothing
. Hence every
word in the sequence is a prefix of the next word and of smaller length
than the next word. It follows that, as
k\to\infty
, words

\mathsf{s}^{k}(z)
in a sense converge to an infinite string. To be
precise, there exists a unique string
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that each

\mathsf{s}^{k}(z)
is a prefix of
\xi
. Clearly,
\mathsf{s}(\xi)=\xi
.
Moreover,
\xi
is the only infinite string that begins with the letter
z

and is preserved by the substitution
\mathsf{s}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The
substitution subshift
generated by a substitution
\mathsf{s}

over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)
, where
\xi

is an infinite string preserved by
\mathsf{s}
.

In this paper we are interested in substitution subshifts generated by
three substitutions. The first one is the
Thue-Morse
substitution

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
over the alphabet
\{0,1\}
given by
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(0)=01
,
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(1)=10
. The
second one is the so-called
period doubling
substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}
over
the alphabet
\{x,y\}
given by
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x)=xy
,
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(y)=xx
. The third one is
a substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
over the alphabet
\{a,c,d\}
given by
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(a)=aca
,

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(c)=d
,
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(d)=c
.

Since the word
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(0)=01
begins with
0
and the word
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(1)=10
begins
with
1
, the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}
preserves a unique infinite string

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
that begins with
0
and another infinite string

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
that begins with
1
.

Lemma 2.6
.

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
are obtained from each other by changing all 0s to 1s
and all 1s to 0s. Both strings generate the same substitution subshift.

Proof.

Consider a substitution
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}
over the alphabet
\{0,1\}

given by
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(0)=1
,
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(1)=0
. We obtain
that
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(0)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(0)=10
and

\mathsf{s}_{(01)}\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(1)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(1)=01
. It follows that

\mathsf{s}_{(01)}\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(\xi)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi)
for any word or
infinite string
\xi
over the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. In particular,

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}))=\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}))=\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
. Since the string
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})

begins with
1
, we have
\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})=\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
due to
uniqueness of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
. In other words,
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}

by changing all 0s to 1s and all 1s to 0s. Then also

\mathsf{s}_{(01)}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime})=\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
.

For any
k\geq 1
we obtain
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(0)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(01)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(0)\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(1)
and

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(1)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(10)=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(1)\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(0)
. As a consequence, the word

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(1)
is a subword of
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(0)
while the word
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(0)
is a
subword of
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(1)
. Any subword
w^{\prime}
of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
is also
a subword of its prefix
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(1)
for all sufficiently large
k
. Since

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(1)
is a subword of
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(0)
, which is a prefix of the string

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
, it follows that
w^{\prime}
is a subword of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
as well. Conversely,
if
w
is a subword of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
, then for some
k\geq 1
it is a
subword of the prefix
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k}(0)
, which is a subword of
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{k+1}(1)
,
which is a prefix of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
. Hence
w
also a subword of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
. Thus
the strings
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime}
have the same subwords. Therefore

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})=\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}^{\prime})
so that both strings generate the same subshift.
∎

Since the word
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x)=xy
begins with
x
, the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}

preserves a unique infinite string
\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}\in\{x,y\}^{\mathbb{N}}
that begins with

x
. Since the word
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(y)=xx
does not begin with
y
, the substitution

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}
preserves no infinite string that begins with
y
. Likewise, the
substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
preserves a unique infinite string

\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}\in\{a,c,d\}^{\mathbb{N}}
that begins with
a
and no infinite string that
begins with
c
or
d
.

Let us explore the dynamics of the substitution subshifts

\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
. First we need detailed descriptions of the
strings
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
,
\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}
and
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The
n
-th symbol of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
is
0
if the number of 1s in the
binary expansion of the number
n-1
is even, and
1
otherwise.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
x_{n}
denote the
n
-th symbol of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
. Then

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
. As
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})=\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
, we also have

\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(x_{1})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(x_{2})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(x_{3})\ldots
. Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(0)
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(1)
are
both words of length
2
, we obtain that
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(x_{i})=x_{2i-1}x_{2i}
for all

i\geq 1
. Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(0)=01
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}(1)=10
, we further obtain that

x_{2i-1}=x_{i}
while
x_{2i}\neq x_{i}
.

For any
i\geq 2
the binary expansion of the number
(2i-1)-1=2(i-1)
is
obtained from the binary expansion of
i-1
by appending
0
to it while
the binary expansion of
2i-1
is obtained by appending
1
. Since

x_{2i-1}=x_{i}
and
x_{2i}\neq x_{i}
, it follows that the lemma holds for

n=2i-1
and
n=2i
whenever it holds for
n=i
. Besides, the lemma holds
for
n=1
and
n=2
as
x_{1}=0
and
x_{2}=1
. Since any
n\geq 3
can be
represented as
2i-1
or
2i
for some integer
i
,
2\leq i<n
, the lemma
follows by strong induction on
n
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
n=2^{k}m
, where
k
is a nonnegative integer and
m
is a positive odd
integer. Then the
n
-th letter of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}
is
x
if
k
is
even, and
y
if
k
is odd.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
x_{n}
denote the
n
-th letter of
\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}
. Then

\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
. As
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})=\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}
, we also have

\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x_{1})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x_{2})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x_{3})\ldots
. Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x)
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(y)
are
both words of length
2
, we obtain that
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x_{i})=x_{2i-1}x_{2i}
for all

i\geq 1
. Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(x)=xy
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{pd}}(y)=xx
, we further obtain that

x_{2i}=y
if
x_{i}=x
, and
x_{2i}=x
if
x_{i}=y
. Besides,
x_{n}=x
for any
odd
n
. Now the lemma easily follows by induction on
k
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
n=2^{k}m
, where
k
is a nonnegative integer and
m
is a positive odd
integer. Then the
n
-th letter of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is
a
if
k=0
,
c

if
k
is odd, and
d
if
k
is even and positive.

Proof.

Let
W
be the set of all words of the form
ay_{1}ay_{2}\ldots ay_{s}
, where
each
y_{i}
is
c
or
d
. We have
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ac)=acad
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ad)=acac
. It
follows that the set
W
is invariant under the substitution
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
.
Besides,
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
doubles the length of every word in
W
. Since
ac
is a
prefix of the infinite string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})=\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
, each of the
words
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ac),\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{2}(ac),\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{3}(ac),\dots
is also a prefix of
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
.
All these words belong to
W
and their length gets arbitrarily large. We
conclude that
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
, where
x_{n}=a
if
n
is odd, and

x_{n}=c
or
d
if
n
even.

We have
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})=\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(x_{1}x_{2})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(x_{3}x_{4})\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(x_{5}x_{6})\ldots
.
Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ac)
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ad)
are both words of length
4
, it follows
that
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(x_{2i-1}x_{2i})=x_{4i-3}x_{4i-2}x_{4i-1}x_{4i}
for all

i\geq 1
. Since
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ac)=acad
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(ad)=acac
, it further follows
that
x_{4i}=d
if
x_{2i}=c
, and
x_{4i}=c
if
x_{2i}=d
. Besides,

x_{n}=c
for any even
n
not divisible by
4
. Now the lemma easily
follows by induction on
k
.
∎

The description of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
obtained in Lemma

allows
to produce explicit examples of bi-infinite strings in the set

.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\overleftarrow{\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}}
denote the left-infinite string obtained by

\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
backwards. Then the bi-infinite strings

\overleftarrow{\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}}c.\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
and
\overleftarrow{\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}}d.\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
belong to

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
.

Proof.

Let
\omega=\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
be one of the strings

\overleftarrow{\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}}c.\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
and
\overleftarrow{\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}}d.\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
. That
is,
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
,
x_{-n}=x_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, and
x_{0}=c
or

d
. We need to show that every subword of
\omega
is also a subword of

\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
. Lemma
2.9
implies that for any

depends only on the largest power of

that divides

. As a

for any integers

such that

. It follows that for any

the words
w_{k}=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{2^{k}-1}
,
w^{-}_{k}=x_{1-2^{k}}\ldots x_{-2}x_{-1}

Any subword
u
of the string
\omega
is also a subword of
w^{-}_{k}x_{0}w_{k}
for

. By the above,
w^{-}_{k}x_{0}w_{k}=w_{k}x_{0}w^{+}_{k}

Lemma

,
x_{2^{k}}=c
if

x_{2^{k}}=d
if

is

for some

.
∎

Definition 2.11
.

A homeomorphism
T:X\to X
of a compact topological space
X
onto itself
is called
minimal
if there is no closed set
Y\subset X
different
from the empty set and
X
such that
T(Y)=Y
. An equivalent condition is
that for any point
x\in X
the
two-sided orbit

\{T^{n}(x)\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is dense in
X
.

Minimality of a homeomorphism
T
is a sign of very regular recurrence in
the dynamical system defined by
T
.

Proposition 2.12
.

The subshifts
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
are minimal.

Note that minimality of the first two subshifts in the proposition is well
known. We are going to present a unified proof for all three subshifts.
First let us observe that minimality of a subshift can be described in
purely combinatorial terms.

Lemma 2.13
.

A subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
is minimal if and only if any two bi-infinite
strings in
Y
have the same subwords.

Proof.

For any nonempty word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
of length
k\geq 1
and any

n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\Sigma_{w,n}
be the set of all bi-infinite strings
\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
y_{n}y_{n+1}\ldots y_{n+k-1}=w
. Each of the sets
\Sigma_{w,n}
is a cylinder. It is easy
to observe that these cylinders form a base of the topology on

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Further, for any
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
let

U_{w}
be the set of all strings in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that admit
w
as a
subword. Obviously,
U_{w}
is the union of the cylinders
\Sigma_{w,n}

over all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
U_{w}
is an open set. Besides,

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma_{w,n})=\Sigma_{w,n-1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which implies
that
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(U_{w})=U_{w}
.

Consider an arbitrary subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
. It follows from the above
that for any nonempty word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, the set difference
Y\setminus U_{w}

is a closed set and
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(Y\setminus U_{w})=Y\setminus U_{w}
. Assuming
the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
is minimal, we obtain that
Y\setminus U_{w}
is
either the empty set or
Y
. Hence
w
is either a subword of every string
in
Y
or a subword of no string in
Y
. Since this holds for any nonempty
word
w
, we conclude that any two strings in
Y
have the same subwords.

Now assume the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
is not minimal, that is, there is a
nonempty closed set
Y_{0}\subset Y
different from
Y
such that

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(Y_{0})=Y_{0}
. Take any point
\omega\in Y\setminus Y_{0}
. Since the
cylinders of the form
\Sigma_{w,n}
constitute a base of the topology, we
can find one of them that contains
\omega
and is disjoint from the closed
set
Y_{0}
. Since
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(Y_{0})=Y_{0}
, it follows that the set
U_{w}
is
also disjoint from
Y_{0}
. We obtain that
w
is a subword of the string

\omega
but not a subword of any string in
Y_{0}
. Thus two strings in
Y
do
not always have the same subwords.
∎

For subshifts of the form
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)
, where
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
minimality is equivalent to a very restrictive combinatorial property of
the string
\xi
(usually called
uniform recurrence
).

Lemma 2.14
.

The subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)
, where
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, is minimal if
and only if for any nonempty word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
that occurs infinitely many
times as a subword of
\xi
, there exists
N\geq 1
such that
w
is a
subword of every word of length at least
N
that is a subword of the
string
\xi
.

Proof.

Take any left-infinite string
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
. Let
\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
be the bi-infinite string
\zeta.\xi
. First we
show that any nonempty word
w
that occurs infinitely often as a subword
of
\xi
is also a subword of some string
\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
. Let
s
be the
length of
w
. There is a sequence of integers
\{n_{k}\}
,
0\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\ldots
, such that
x_{n_{k}+1}x_{n_{k}+2}\ldots x_{n_{k}+s}=w
for
all
k\geq 1
. Since
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact, the sequence

\{\sigma^{n_{k}}(\zeta.\xi)\}_{k\geq 1}
has a limit point
\omega
. By
construction,
\omega=\zeta_{1}.w\xi_{1}
for some
\xi_{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
and

\zeta_{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
. In particular,
w
is a subword of the string

\omega
. Clearly,
\omega
is also a limit point of the sequence

\zeta.\xi,\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
. Hence

\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
due to Lemma
2.3
.

Next we show that for any word
w
that is not a subword of all
sufficiently long subwords of
\xi
, there is a string
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi)

such that
w
is not a subword of
\omega^{\prime}
. For any
N\geq 1
let
w_{N}
be a
subword of
\xi
of length at least
N
that does not admit
w
as a
subword. We have
w_{N}=x_{n_{N}}x_{n_{N}+1}\ldots x_{m_{N}}
for some
n_{N}
and

m_{N}
, where
n_{N}\geq 1
and
m_{N}-n_{N}\geq N-1
. The sequence

\{\sigma^{n_{2k}+k}(\zeta.\xi)\}_{k\geq 1}
has a limit point
\omega^{\prime}=\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
. Since
n_{2k}+k\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, it
follows that the string
\omega^{\prime}
is also a limit point of the sequence

\zeta.\xi,\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
. Hence

\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi)
due to Lemma
2.3
. Any subword
u
of

\omega^{\prime}
is also a subword of the word
u_{s}=y_{-s}y_{-s+1}\ldots y_{s-1}y_{s}

for some
s\geq 1
. By construction,
x_{n_{2k}+k-s}\,x_{n_{2k}+k-s+1}\ldots x_{n_{2k}+k+s}=u_{s}
for infinitely many values of
k
. If
k\geq s+1
then

n_{2k}+k-s>n_{2k}
and
n_{2k}+k+s\leq n_{2k}+2k-1\leq m_{2k}
. It follows
that
u_{s}
is a subword of the word
w_{2k}
for some
k
. Then
u
is
also a subword of
w_{2k}
so that
u\neq w
.

Now we can prove the lemma. Assume that the string
\xi
does not have the
combinatorial property formulated in the lemma. That is, some nonempty
word
w
occurs infinitely many times as a subword of
\xi
, but
w
is not
a subword of all sufficiently long subwords of
\xi
. By the above there
exist strings
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi)
such that
w
is a subword of
\omega
but
not a subword of
\omega^{\prime}
. Then it follows from Lemma
2.13

that the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)
is not minimal. Conversely, assume
that the string
\xi
has the combinatorial property formulated in the
lemma. Take an arbitrary
\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
and let
w
be a subword of

\omega
. By Lemma
2.2
, the word
w
occurs infinitely often as a
subword of
\xi
. Hence for some
N\geq 1
every subword of
\xi
of length
at least
N
admits
w
as a subword. Clearly, any bi-infinite string

\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi)
has a subword
w^{\prime}
of length
N
. Since
w^{\prime}
is also a
subword of
\xi
, it admits
w
as a subword. Then
w
is a subword of

\omega^{\prime}
as well. We obtain that every subword of
\omega
is, in fact, a
subword of all strings in
\Omega(\xi)
. As the choice of
\omega
was
arbitrary, we conclude that any two strings in
\Omega(\xi)
have the same
subwords. By Lemma
2.13
, the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)

is minimal.
∎

In the remainder of this section we are going to establish relations
between the subshifts
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,

\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and
\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
T_{1}:X_{1}\to X_{1}
and
T_{2}:X_{2}\to X_{2}
be homeomorphisms of topological
spaces. Suppose there exists a continuous map
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
such that
f

is onto and
fT_{1}=T_{2}f
so that the following diagram is commutative:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}X_{1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&X_{1}\\[1.99997pt]
f\Big\downarrow&&\phantom{f}\Big\downarrow f\\[2.5pt]
X_{2}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}&X_{2}\end{array}

Then we say that
T_{2}
is a
continuous factor
of
T_{1}
while
T_{1}

is a
continuous extension
of
T_{2}
. In the case the map
f
is

k
-to-one for some
k\geq 1
, we say that
T_{2}
is a
k
-to-one continuous
factor of
T_{1}
. In the case
f
is a homeomorphism, we say that the map

T_{2}
is
topologically conjugate
to
T_{1}
.

Proposition 2.16
.

The subshift
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
is a two-to-one continuous factor
of the subshift
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
. The subshift

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
is topologically conjugate to

\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
.

To prove Proposition
2.16
, we are going to use the
so-called
block factor maps
, which are helpful for establishing
relations between subshifts. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
be two alphabets.
Suppose that for some
k\geq 1
we have a function
\phi:\mathcal{A}_{1}^{k}\to\mathcal{A}_{2}
.
This function induces a map
f_{\phi}:\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that sends
any bi-infinite string
\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
over the alphabet

\mathcal{A}_{1}
to a bi-infinite string
\ldots y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
over

\mathcal{A}_{2}
given by
y_{n}=\phi(x_{n},x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+k-1})
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows from the construction that for any cylinder

\Sigma\subset\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}
the pre-image
f_{\phi}^{-1}(\Sigma)
is a finite
union of cylinders in
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence the map
f_{\phi}
is
continuous. Also by construction,
f_{\phi}
intertwines the shifts

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}
, that is,

f_{\phi}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}f_{\phi}
. The map
f_{\phi}
is called a

k
-block factor map. Note that each shift is itself a
2
-block factor
map.

Lemma 2.17
.

Given an infinite string
\xi_{1}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots\in\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\mathbb{N}}
, let

\xi_{2}=y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}\ldots
be an infinite string in
\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

y_{n}=\phi(x_{n},x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+k-1})
for all
n\geq 1
. Then

f_{\phi}(\Omega(\xi_{1}))=\Omega(\xi_{2})
.

Proof.

Take any left-infinite string
\zeta_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-\mathbb{N}}
. Then

f_{\phi}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1})=\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2}
for some left-infinite string

\zeta_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}^{-\mathbb{N}}
. By Lemma
2.3
,
\Omega(\xi_{1})
is the
set of all limit points of the sequence
\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1},\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1}),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{2}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1}),\dots
while

\Omega(\xi_{2})
is the set of all limit points of the sequence
\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2},\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}(\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2}),\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{2}(\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2}),\dots
. Since

f_{\phi}\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}f_{\phi}
, it follows by induction on
m
that

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{m}(\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2})=f_{\phi}\bigl(\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{m}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1})\bigr)
for all
m\geq 0
. Hence the
first sequence is mapped by
f_{\phi}
onto the second sequence. Assume

\omega\in\Omega(\xi_{1})
. Then
\omega
is the limit of some subsequence

\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{m_{i}}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1})\}_{i\geq 1}
of the first sequence.
Continuity of the map
f_{\phi}
implies that

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{m_{i}}(\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2})\to f_{\phi}(\omega)
as

so that

f_{\phi}(\omega)\in\Omega(\xi_{2})
. Therefore

f_{\phi}(\Omega(\xi_{1}))\subset\Omega(\xi_{2})
.

Conversely, assume
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi_{2})
. Let

\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}^{m_{j}}(\zeta_{2}.\xi_{2})\}_{j\geq 1}
be a subsequence of the
second sequence converging to
\omega^{\prime}
. The respective subsequence

\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{m_{j}}(\zeta_{1}.\xi_{1})\}_{j\geq 1}
of the first sequence need
not converge, but it has a limit point due to compactness of

\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Any limit point

\omega\in\Omega(\xi_{1})
. Besides,

due

f_{\phi}
. We conclude that
\Omega(\xi_{2})\subset f_{\phi}(\Omega(\xi_{1}))
. Thus
f_{\phi}(\Omega(\xi_{1}))=\Omega(\xi_{2})
.
∎

First consider a function

.

The
2

3
Topological full groups

Let
X
be a
Cantor set
. This means that
X
is a compact
metrizable topological space that is totally disconnected and has no
isolated points. The topology on the Cantor set is generated by

clopen
(i.e., both closed and open) sets. For instance, the space

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of bi-infinite strings over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a Cantor set.
Each cylinder is a clopen subset of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T:X\to X
be a homeomorphism of the Cantor set
X
onto itself. The

topological full group
of
T
, denoted
[[T]]
, is a transformation
group consisting of all homeomorphisms
F:X\to X
that can be given by

F(x)=T^{\nu(x)}(x)
,
x\in X
for some continuous function
\nu:X\to\mathbb{Z}
.

If a function
\nu:X\to\mathbb{Z}
is continuous then it is locally constant and
takes only finitely many values. Then nonempty level sets of
\nu
form a
finite partition of the Cantor set
X
into clopen sets. Thus every
element of the topological full group
[[T]]
is “piecewise” a power of

T
. The group
[[T]]
is countable (as there are only countably many
clopen subsets of
X
).

The topological full group
[[T]]
is
ample
, which means that any
homeomorphism of the Cantor set
X
that locally coincides with elements of
the group is itself in
[[T]]
. Moreover, it is the smallest ample
collection of homeomorphisms that contains the cyclic group generated by

T
. For more details and proofs of these facts, see Section 2 of the
paper
[
GV
]
.

In the case
T
is a minimal homeomorphism of a Cantor set (such a
transformation is called a
Cantor minimal system
), the (isomorphism
class of the) topological full group
[[T]]
is an almost complete
invariant of the dynamics of
T
as shown by Giordano, Putnam and Skau

[
GPS
]
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
GPS
]
)
.

Suppose
T_{1}:X_{1}\to X_{1}
and
T_{2}:X_{2}\to X_{2}
are minimal homeomorphisms of
Cantor sets. Then the topological full groups
[[T_{1}]]
and
[[T_{2}]]
are
isomorphic if and only if
T_{1}
is topologically conjugate to
T_{2}
or

T_{2}^{-1}
.

Next we show that Theorem
3.2
applies to the substitution
subshifts
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
, and, moreover, the topological full groups of
these subshifts are complete invariants of their dynamics.

Proposition 3.3
.

The subshifts
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
are Cantor minimal systems. Each of these
subshifts is topologically conjugate to its inverse.

Minimality of all three subshifts is already established by Proposition

2.12
, but we need to show that
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
are Cantor sets.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T
be a homeomorphism of a Cantor set
X
and let
Y\subset X
be a
nonempty closed set such that
T(Y)=Y
. Suppose that the restriction
T|Y

is a minimal homeomorphism of
Y
. Then the subset
Y
is either finite or
a Cantor set.

Proof.

Since
Y
is a closed subset of the Cantor set
X
, it is compact,
metrizable and totally disconnected. Therefore
Y
is itself a Cantor set
if and only if it has no isolated points. Since
T
is a homeomorphism of

X
and
T(Y)=Y
, isolated points of
Y
are mapped to isolated points of

Y
while non-isolated points are mapped to non-isolated points. It
follows that
T(Y_{0})=Y_{0}
, where
Y_{0}
is the set of all non-isolated
points of
Y
. As
Y_{0}
is a closed subset of
Y
, minimality of the
restriction
T|Y
implies that
Y_{0}
is the empty set or
Y
. In the case

Y_{0}
is empty, every point of
Y
is isolated. Then compactness of
Y

implies that this set is finite. In the case
Y_{0}=Y
, there are no
isolated points in
Y
. Then
Y
is a Cantor set.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

The set
\Omega(\xi)
is finite if and only if the infinite string
\xi
is
eventually periodic.

Proof.

Let
\xi=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
be an infinite string over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
First assume that
\xi
is eventually periodic. That is,
\xi=wuuu\ldots

for some words
w,u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, where
u\neq\varnothing
. Let
n_{0}
be the
length of
w
and
p
be the length of
u
. Then for any left-infinite
string
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
the sequence

\sigma^{n_{0}}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma^{n_{0}+p}(\zeta.\xi),\sigma^{n_{0}+2p}(\zeta.\xi),\dots

converges in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
to the bi-infinite string
\omega=\ldots uu.uu\ldots
. Consequently, for every
i
,
0\leq i\leq p-1
, we have

\sigma^{n_{0}+i+kp}(\zeta.\xi)\to\sigma^{i}(\omega)
as
k\to\infty
. Any subsequence
of the sequence
\zeta.\xi,\sigma(\zeta.\xi),\sigma^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
shares
infinitely many terms with at least one of the subsequences

\{\sigma^{n_{0}+i+kp}(\zeta.\xi)\}_{k\geq 1}
,
0\leq i\leq p-1
. It follows that

\omega,\sigma(\omega),\dots,\sigma^{p-1}(\omega)
are the only limit points of that
sequence. By Lemma
2.3
, these are also the only points of
the set
\Omega(\xi)
so that
\Omega(\xi)
is finite.

Conversely, assume that the set
\Omega(\xi)
is finite. Take any

\omega\in\Omega(\xi)
. Since
\sigma(\Omega(\xi))=\Omega(\xi)
, it follows that

\sigma^{p}(\omega)=\omega
for some
p\geq 1
. Then
\omega=\ldots uu.uu\ldots
, where

u
is a word of length
p
. We claim that for some
k\geq 1
the string

\xi
has no subwords of the form
u^{k}v=uu\ldots uv
, where
v
is a word
of length
p
different from
u
. Assume the contrary. Then there exists
a sequence of words
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\dots
of length
p
different from
u

such that for any
k\geq 1
the word
u^{k}v_{k}
is a subword of
\xi
. This
means existence of nonnegative integers
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\dots
such that

x_{n_{k}+1}x_{n_{k}+2}\ldots x_{n_{k}+(k+1)p}=u^{k}v_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. Take
any left-infinite string
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathbb{N}}
. Since
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is
compact, the sequence
\{\sigma^{n_{k}+kp}(\zeta.\xi)\}_{k\geq 1}
has a limit
point
\omega^{\prime}
. By construction,
\omega^{\prime}=\ldots uuu.v\eta
for some word
v

of length
p
different from
u
and some
\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Since

n_{k}+kp\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, the string
\omega^{\prime}
is also a limit point
of the sequence
\zeta.\xi,\sigma(\zeta.\xi),\sigma^{2}(\zeta.\xi),\dots
. Hence

\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega(\xi)
due to Lemma
2.3
. Then each of the
strings
\sigma^{p}(\omega^{\prime}),\sigma^{2p}(\omega^{\prime}),\sigma^{3p}(\omega^{\prime}),\dots
belongs to

\Omega(\xi)
as well. It is easy to observe that all these strings are
distinct, which contradicts the fact that
\Omega(\xi)
is a finite set.

We have established that for some
k\geq 1
words of the form
u^{k}v
, where

v
is a word of length
p
different from
u
, do not occur as subwords of
the string
\xi
. The word
u^{k}
does occur as a subword of
\xi
since it
is a subword of
\omega
. Hence
x_{n+1}x_{n+2}\ldots x_{n+kp}=u^{k}
for some

n\geq 0
. Note that whenever
x_{n+ip+1}x_{n+ip+2}\ldots x_{n+ip+p}=u
for

k
consecutive values of
i
, the same should hold for the next value of

i
. Now it follows by induction that
x_{n+ip+1}x_{n+ip+2}\ldots x_{n+ip+p}=u
for all
i\geq 0
. Thus the string
\xi
is eventually
periodic.
∎

For any word
w
, let
\overleftarrow{w}
denote the same word written
backwards. The word
w
is called a
palindrome
if

\overleftarrow{w}=w
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that any subword of a string
\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
can be extended to a
palindrome subword of
\xi
. Then the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|\Omega(\xi)
is
topologically conjugate to its own inverse.

Proof.

Consider a map
R:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that flips any bi-infinite string

\ldots y_{-2}y_{-1}y_{0}.y_{1}y_{2}\ldots
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
around turning it into
the string
\ldots y_{2}y_{1}y_{0}.y_{-1}y_{-2}\ldots
. The map
R
is an
involution that maps cylinders onto cylinders. This implies that
R
is a
homeomorphism. It is easy to see that
R\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}=\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}R
. It
follows that for any subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
, the restriction

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|R(Y)
is also a subshift. Moreover,
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|R(Y)
is
topologically conjugate to the inverse of
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
. Hence to prove
the lemma, it is enough to show that
R(\Omega(\xi))=\Omega(\xi)
. A string

\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
belongs to
\Omega(\xi)
if and only if every subword of

\omega
is also a subword of
\xi
. By construction, a word
u
is a subword
of
R(\omega)
if and only if
\overleftarrow{u}
is a subword of
\omega
. It
follows that a string
\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
belongs to
R(\Omega(\xi))
if and
only if for every subword
u
of
\eta
, the word
\overleftarrow{u}
is a
subword of
\xi
.

If a word
u
is a subword of a word
w
, then the word
\overleftarrow{u}

is a subword of
\overleftarrow{w}
. In the case
w
is a palindrome,
u

is a subword of
w
if and only if
\overleftarrow{u}
is a subword of

w
. Since any subword of the string
\xi
can be extended to a palindrome
subword of
\xi
, any word
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a subword of
\xi
if and only if

\overleftarrow{u}
is a subword of
\xi
. Now it follows from the above
that
R(\Omega(\xi))=\Omega(\xi)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

By Proposition
2.12
, the subshifts

\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and

\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
are minimal. Let us prove that
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
,

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
and
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
are Cantor sets. In view of Lemma

3.4
, it is enough to show that all three sets are
infinite. By Proposition
2.16
, the subshift

\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
is a continuous factor of

\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
while the subshift
\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})

is topologically conjugate to
\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
. This means, in
particular, that the set
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
can be mapped onto
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
while

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
can be mapped onto
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
. It follows that the sets

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})
and
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
are infinite if
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
is infinite.

It remains to prove that the set
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
is infinite. In view of
Lemma
3.5
, it is enough to show that the string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is
not eventually periodic. If an infinite string
\xi=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
is
eventually periodic with period
p
, then for any
n\geq 1
the sequence of
letters
x_{n},x_{n+p},x_{n+2p},\dots
is eventually constant. We are going
to show that the latter is not the case when
\xi=\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
. Any integer

p\geq 1
can be represented as
2^{k}m
, where
k\geq 0
,
m\geq 1
and
m
is
odd. Let
n_{p}=2^{k}
. Then for any
s\geq 1
the number

n_{p}+2sp=2^{k}+2^{k+1}sm
is divisible by
2^{k}
but not divisible by

2^{k+1}
. Since
m
is odd, the number
m^{2}
leaves remainder
1
after
division by
4
, and so does the number
(4s+1)m^{2}
for any
s\geq 1
.
Therefore the number
n_{p}+(4s+1)mp=2^{k}+2^{k}(4s+1)m^{2}
is divisible by

2^{k+1}
but not divisible by
2^{k+2}
. Now it follows from Lemma

2.9
that
x_{n_{p}+2sp}=x_{2^{k}}
for all
s\geq 1
,

x_{n_{p}+(4s+1)mp}=x_{2^{k+1}}
for all
s\geq 1
, and
x_{2^{k+1}}\neq x_{2^{k}}
. As a consequence, the sequence

x_{n_{p}},x_{n_{p}+p},x_{n_{p}+2p},\dots
is not eventually constant.

We proceed to the second statement of the proposition. Suppose

\mathsf{s}
is a substitution over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
that substitutes
every symbol of
\mathcal{A}
with a palindrome. Then for any word
w=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
,

\overleftarrow{\mathsf{s}(w)}=\overleftarrow{\mathsf{s}(x_{1})\mathsf{s}(x_{2})\ldots\mathsf{s}(x_{n})}=\overleftarrow{\mathsf{s}(x_{n})}\ldots\overleftarrow{\mathsf{s}(x_{2})}\,\overleftarrow{\mathsf{s}(x_{1})}=\mathsf{s}(x_{n})\ldots\mathsf{s}(x_{2})\mathsf{s}(x_{1})=\mathsf{s}\bigl(\overleftarrow{w}\bigr).

As a consequence, the substitution
\mathsf{s}
transforms palindromes into
palindromes. Note that the words
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(a)=aca
,
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(c)=d
and

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}(d)=c
are palindromes. The words
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2}(0)=0110
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2}(1)=1001

are also palindromes. By the above the substitutions
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2}

transform palindromes into palindromes. It follows by induction that for
any
k\geq 1
the words
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{k}(a)
and
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2k}(0)
are palindromes.
Recall that each
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{k}(a)
is a subword of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
. Moreover,
any subword of
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is also a subword of
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{Mu}}^{k}(a)
for all
sufficiently large
k
. Likewise, each
\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2k}(0)
is a subword of the
string
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
and any subword of
\xi_{\mathrm{TM}}
is also a subword of

\mathsf{s}_{\mathrm{TM}}^{2k}(0)
for all sufficiently large
k
. Now it follows from Lemma

3.6
that each of the subshifts
\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})

and
\sigma_{\{0,1\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{TM}})

By Proposition

A transformation

Theorem 3.8

Suppose that
T_{1}
and

are aperiodic homeomorphisms of Cantor sets
and

To prove Theorem

of the group
[[T]]
, where
T
is an aperiodic homeomorphism of a Cantor

[

]
(see the
construction of a group
G
right before Theorem 5.2 in

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
T
is a homeomorphism of a Cantor set
X
and
\ell:X\to\mathbb{Z}
is a
continuous function. Then the map
h_{T,\ell}:X\to X
defined by

h_{T,\ell}(x)=T^{\ell(x)}(x)
,
x\in X
, is continuous.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
the map
h_{T,\ell}
coincides with a continuous map

T^{n}
on the set
\ell^{-1}(n)
. Since the function
\ell
is continuous,
the set
\ell^{-1}(n)
is clopen, which implies that
h_{T,\ell}
is
continuous on that set as well. As
X
is the disjoint union of the sets

\ell^{-1}(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the lemma follows.
∎

Let
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
denote the set of all continuous functions
\ell:X\to\mathbb{Z}
.
Given a homeomorphism
T
of a Cantor set
X
, for any functions

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
we define a function

\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}:X\to\mathbb{Z}
by
(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2})(x)=\ell_{1}\bigl(T^{\ell_{2}(x)}(x)\bigr)+\ell_{2}(x)
for all
x\in X
. Note that
the formula involves addition and composition of functions. We never use
multiplication of functions in what follows.

Lemma 3.10
.

\oplus_{T}
is a well defined operation on
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any continuous functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}:X\to\mathbb{Z}
,
the function
\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}
is continuous as well. Note that

\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}+\ell_{2}
. The map
h_{T,\ell_{2}}

is continuous due to Lemma
3.9
. Then
\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}

is continuous as a composition of two continuous functions. Finally,

\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}
is continuous as the sum of two continuous functions

\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}
and
\ell_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

h_{T,\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}}=h_{T,\ell_{1}}h_{T,\ell_{2}}
for all

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

For any
x\in X
we have
(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2})(x)=\ell_{1}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))+\ell_{2}(x)
. Then

h_{T,\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}}(x)=T^{\ell_{1}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))+\ell_{2}(x)}(x)=T^{\ell_{1}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))}T^{\ell_{2}(x)}(x).

On the other hand,

h_{T,\ell_{1}}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))=T^{\ell_{1}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))}h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x)=T^{\ell_{1}(h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x))}T^{\ell_{2}(x)}(x).

Hence
h_{T,\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2}}=h_{T,\ell_{1}}h_{T,\ell_{2}}
everywhere on

X
.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

\oplus_{T}
is an associative operation on
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

For any functions
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
, we have
\ell\oplus_{T}\ell^{\prime}=\ell h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}+\ell^{\prime}
. Besides,
h_{T,\ell\oplus_{T}\ell^{\prime}}=h_{T,\ell}h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}
due to Lemma
3.11
. Given arbitrary
functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2})\oplus_{T}\ell_{3}=(\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}+\ell_{2})\oplus_{T}\ell_{3}=(\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}+\ell_{2})h_{T,\ell_{3}}+\ell_{3}=\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}h_{T,\ell_{3}}+\ell_{2}h_{T,\ell_{3}}+\ell_{3},

\displaystyle\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}(\ell_{2}\oplus_{T}\ell_{3})=\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}\oplus_{T}\ell_{3}}+(\ell_{2}\oplus_{T}\ell_{3})=\ell_{1}h_{T,\ell_{2}}h_{T,\ell_{3}}+\ell_{2}h_{T,\ell_{3}}+\ell_{3}.

Thus
(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}\ell_{2})\oplus_{T}\ell_{3}=\ell_{1}\oplus_{T}(\ell_{2}\oplus_{T}\ell_{3})
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{0}_{X}
denote the zero function on
X
. It is easy to see that
\mathbf{0}_{X}

is the identity element of the operation
\oplus_{T}
. Hence
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a
monoid with respect to
\oplus_{T}
. We denote by
C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})
the
set of all invertible elements of this monoid. Then
C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is
a group with respect to the operation
\oplus_{T}
.

Next we define a map
H_{T}:C(X,\mathbb{Z})\to C(X,X)
by
H_{T}(\ell)=h_{T,\ell}
for
all
\ell\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
. The map
H_{T}
is well defined due to Lemma

3.9
. In view of Lemma
3.11
,
H_{T}
is a
homomorphism of the monoid
C(X,\mathbb{Z})
with the operation
\oplus_{T}
to the
monoid
C(X,X)
with the operation of composition.

Lemma 3.13
.

H_{T}\bigl(C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})\bigr)=[[T]]
.

Proof.

It follows from Definition
3.1
that

[[T]]=H_{T}(C(X,\mathbb{Z}))\cap\mathop{\mathrm{Homeo}}(X)
, where
\mathop{\mathrm{Homeo}}(X)
is the group of all
homeomorphisms of
X
. Hence it is enough to show that for any function

\ell\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
, the map
H_{T}(\ell)=h_{T,\ell}
is a homeomorphism if
and only if
\ell
is invertible with respect to the operation
\oplus_{T}
.
First assume that
\ell
is invertible, and let
\ell^{\prime}
be its inverse. We
have
\ell\oplus_{T}\ell^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}\oplus_{T}\ell=\mathbf{0}_{X}
. Then it follows from
Lemma
3.11
that
h_{T,\ell}h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}=h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}h_{T,\ell}=h_{T,\mathbf{0}_{X}}=\mathrm{id}_{X}
. Hence the map
h_{T,\ell}
is invertible and its
inverse
h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}
is continuous (due to Lemma
3.9
).
Therefore
h_{T,\ell}
is a homeomorphism.

Conversely, assume that
h_{T,\ell}
is a homeomorphism of
X
. Define a
function
\ell^{\prime}:X\to\mathbb{Z}
by
\ell^{\prime}(x)=-\ell\bigl(h_{T,\ell}^{-1}(x)\bigr)

for all
x\in X
. Since
h_{T,\ell}
is a homeomorphism, the function

\ell^{\prime}
is well defined and continuous. Hence
\ell^{\prime}\in C(X,\mathbb{Z})
. By
construction,

\ell^{\prime}\oplus_{T}\ell=\ell^{\prime}h_{T,\ell}+\ell=(-\ell)h_{T,\ell}^{-1}h_{T,\ell}+\ell=-\ell+\ell=\mathbf{0}_{X}.

Then
h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}h_{T,\ell}=h_{T,\mathbf{0}_{X}}=\mathrm{id}_{X}
due to Lemma

3.11
. Since the map
h_{T,\ell}
is invertible, it follows
that
h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}=h_{T,\ell}^{-1}
. Consequently,
\ell\oplus_{T}\ell^{\prime}=\ell h_{T,\ell^{\prime}}+\ell^{\prime}=\ell h_{T,\ell}^{-1}+\ell^{\prime}=-\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}_{X}
. We
conclude that the function
\ell
is invertible with respect to the
operation
\oplus_{T}
and
\ell^{\prime}
is its inverse.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Suppose that the homeomorphism
T
is aperiodic. Then the homomorphism

H_{T}
is one-to-one. Moreover, the restriction of
H_{T}
to the group

C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism onto the group
[[T]]
.

Proof.

If
T
is aperiodic, then
T^{n}(x)\neq x
for all points
x\in X
and
positive integers

. As
T
is invertible, it follows that for any
x\in X
and

we have
T^{n_{1}}(x)\neq T^{n_{2}}(x)
whenever
n_{1}\neq n_{2}
. In particular,
h_{T,\ell_{1}}(x)=T^{\ell_{1}(x)}(x)\neq T^{\ell_{2}(x)}(x)=h_{T,\ell_{2}}(x)
whenever
\ell_{1}(x)\neq\ell_{2}(x)
. As a
consequence, the homomorphism
H_{T}
is one-to-one.

By Lemma
3.13
,
H_{T}\bigl(C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})\bigr)=[[T]]
.
Hence the restriction of
H_{T}
to the group
C^{\times}_{T}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a
homomorphism onto the group
[[T]]
. In the case when the homomorphism

H_{T}
is one-to-one, the restriction is an isomorphism onto
[[T]]
.
∎

Any continuous function
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
induces a map
\Phi_{f}:C(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})\to C(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
given by
\Phi_{f}(\ell)=\ell f
for all

.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
T_{1}:X_{1}\to X_{1}
and
T_{2}:X_{2}\to X_{2}
are homeomorphisms of Cantor
sets and
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
is a continuous function such that
fT_{1}=T_{2}f
.
Then the map
\Phi_{f}
is a homomorphism of the monoid
C(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
with
operation
\oplus_{T_{2}}
to the monoid
C(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
with operation

\oplus_{T_{1}}
.

Proof.

First we prove that
fT_{1}^{n}=T_{2}^{n}f
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. For
n\geq 0
, the
proof is by induction on
n
. Indeed, the case
n=0
is trivial, and if

fT_{1}^{k}=T_{2}^{k}f
for some
k\geq 0
then

fT_{1}^{k+1}=(fT_{1}^{k})T_{1}=(T_{2}^{k}f)T_{1}=T_{2}^{k}(fT_{1})=T_{2}^{k}(T_{2}f)=T_{2}^{k+1}f.

In the case
n<0
, we have
-n>0
so that
fT_{1}^{-n}=T_{2}^{-n}f
by the
above. Then

fT_{1}^{n}=(T_{2}^{n}T_{2}^{-n})fT_{1}^{n}=T_{2}^{n}(T_{2}^{-n}f)T_{1}^{n}=T_{2}^{n}(fT_{1}^{-n})T_{1}^{n}=T_{2}^{n}f(T_{1}^{-n}T_{1}^{n})=T_{2}^{n}f.

Given functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in C(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
, we need to show that

\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell_{2})=\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1})\oplus_{T_{1}}\Phi_{f}(\ell_{2})
. For any
x\in X_{1}
we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl(\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell_{2})\bigr)(x)=(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell_{2})(f(x))=\ell_{1}\bigl(T_{2}^{\ell_{2}(f(x))}(f(x))\bigr)+\ell_{2}(f(x)),

\displaystyle\bigl(\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1})\oplus_{T_{1}}\Phi_{f}(\ell_{2})\bigr)(x)=(\ell_{1}f\oplus_{T_{1}}\ell_{2}f)(x)=\ell_{1}f\bigl(T_{1}^{\ell_{2}f(x)}(x)\bigr)+\ell_{2}f(x).

Since
\ell_{2}(f(x))=\ell_{2}f(x)
is an integer, the map
fT_{1}^{\ell_{2}f(x)}

coincides with the map
T_{2}^{\ell_{2}f(x)}f
. It follows that

\bigl(\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1}\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell_{2})\bigr)(x)=\bigl(\Phi_{f}(\ell_{1})\oplus_{T_{1}}\Phi_{f}(\ell_{2})\bigr)(x)
.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.15
, the restriction of

\Phi_{f}
to the group
C^{\times}_{T_{2}}(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
is a homomorphism to the
group
C^{\times}_{T_{1}}(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
. This group homomorphism is one-to-one
provided that the function
f
is onto.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.15
, the map
\Phi_{f}
is a homomorphism of the
monoid
C(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
with operation
\oplus_{T_{2}}
to the monoid

C(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
with operation
\oplus_{T_{1}}
. Take any function
\ell\in C(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
invertible with respect to
\oplus_{T_{2}}
and let
\ell^{\prime}
be
its inverse. Then
\ell\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}\oplus_{T_{2}}\ell=\mathbf{0}_{X_{2}}
, which implies that
\Phi_{f}(\ell)\oplus_{T_{1}}\Phi_{f}(\ell^{\prime})=\Phi_{f}(\ell^{\prime})\oplus_{T_{1}}\Phi_{f}(\ell)=\Phi_{f}(\mathbf{0}_{X_{2}})=\mathbf{0}_{X_{1}}
. Hence
the function
\Phi_{f}(\ell)
is invertible with respect to
\oplus_{T_{1}}

and
\Phi_{f}(\ell^{\prime})
is its inverse. We obtain that
\Phi_{f}
maps
invertible elements of the monoid

to invertible elements of
the monoid
C(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
. Consequently, the restriction of
\Phi_{f}
to the
group
C^{\times}_{T_{2}}(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
is a homomorphism to the group

C^{\times}_{T_{1}}(X_{1},\mathbb{Z})
.

If the map
f
is onto then for any functions
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
on

X_{2}
we have
\ell_{1}f=\ell_{2}f
if and only if
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
. It follows
that the map
\Phi_{f}
is one-to-one, and so is its restriction to

C^{\times}_{T_{2}}(X_{2},\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

.

Suppose

Definition 3.17
.

Let

.

4
Groups of intermediate growth

Let
G
be an infinite, finitely generated group. Suppose
A
is a finite
generating set for
G
. Let
A_{\pm}
denote the subset of
G
consisting of
all generators in
A
and their inverses. Any word over the alphabet

A_{\pm}
can be interpreted as a product in the group
G
. This gives rise
to a map
h_{G,A}:A_{\pm}^{*}\to G
, which is a homomorphism of the monoid

A_{\pm}^{*}
onto
G
. For any element
g\in G
, the length of the shortest
word in the pre-image
h_{G,A}^{-1}(g)
is called the
length
of
g

(relative to the generating set
A
). For any integer
n\geq 1
let

\gamma_{G,A}(n)
be the number of all elements of the group
G
of length at
most
n
. The function
\gamma_{G,A}
is called the
growth function
of

G
. The asymptotic behavior of
\gamma_{G,A}(n)
as
n\to\infty
is referred
to as the
growth
of the group
G
. Clearly,
\gamma_{G,A}(n)
does not
exceed the number of words of length at most
n
in
A_{\pm}^{*}
, which is

1+m+m^{2}+\dots+m^{n}=(m^{n+1}-1)/(m-1)
, where
m
is the number of elements
in
A_{\pm}
. Hence the growth of
G
is at most exponential.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G
be an infinite, finitely generated group with a finite generating
set
A
. We say that the group
G
has
exponential growth
if

\gamma_{G,A}(n)\geq c^{n}
for some
c>0
and all sufficiently large
n
. We
say that the group
G
has
polynomial growth
if
\gamma_{G,A}(n)\leq n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
and all sufficiently large
n
. We say that
the group
G
has
intermediate growth
if the growth of
G
is
slower than exponential but faster than polynomial, namely, for any

c,\alpha>0
we have
n^{\alpha}<\gamma_{G,A}(n)<c^{n}
for all sufficiently large

n
.

Definition
4.1
is not sensitive to the choice of a generating
set for the group
G
. Suppose
B
is a different finite generating set
for
G
. Let
k_{1}
be the maximal length of elements of
B
relative to
the generating set
A
. Then any product of
n
elements of
B
or their
inverses can be written as a product of at most
k_{1}n
elements of
A
or
their inverses. Therefore
\gamma_{G,B}(n)\leq\gamma_{G,A}(k_{1}n)
for all

n\geq 1
. Likewise,
\gamma_{G,A}(n)\leq\gamma_{G,B}(k_{2}n)
for all
n\geq 1
, where

k_{2}
is the maximal length of elements of
A
relative to the generating
set
B
. It follows that whenever the growth function
\gamma_{G,A}
is of
exponential, polynomial or intermediate growth, the growth function

\gamma_{G,B}
exhibits the same kind of growth.

Note that if an infinite, finitely generated group is not of exponential or
polynomial growth, it need not have intermediate growth. Indeed, there are
groups with growth oscillating between exponential and sub-exponential.

Now we are going to describe a family of groups of intermediate growth
constructed by the first author in
[
Gr1
]
. Each group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
of
the family is labeled by an infinite ternary string

\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
is generated by a set
A_{\omega}

consisting of four transformations
a
,
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
. In
the original construction in
[
Gr1
]
, the generators were acting on
the interval
[0,1]
. We consider a modification in which the generators
act on the set
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
of infinite binary strings. This
modification agrees with the original construction when we identify any
binary string
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots
with the number

2^{-1}x_{1}+2^{-2}x_{2}+2^{-3}x_{3}+\dots
.

The generator
a
is common for all groups in the family. It is defined by

a(0\xi)=1\xi
and
a(1\xi)=0\xi
for all
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. That is,

a
changes the first symbol in every binary string. The generators

b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
are defined using the following recursive
rules: for any
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
l\in\{0,1,2\}
and

\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\displaystyle b_{2\omega}(0\xi)=c_{1\omega}(0\xi)=d_{0\omega}(0\xi)=0\xi,

\displaystyle b_{0\omega}(0\xi)=b_{1\omega}(0\xi)=c_{0\omega}(0\xi)=c_{2\omega}(0\xi)=d_{1\omega}(0\xi)=d_{2\omega}(0\xi)=0a(\xi),

\displaystyle b_{l\omega}(1\xi)=1b_{\omega}(\xi),\qquad c_{l\omega}(1\xi)=1c_{\omega}(\xi),\qquad d_{l\omega}(1\xi)=1d_{\omega}(\xi).

The string
1^{\infty}=111\ldots
is the only element of
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

fixed by
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
for all
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Any
other string
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is uniquely represented as

\xi=1^{k}0x\eta
, where
x\in\{0,1\}
,
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
k\geq 0
, and

1^{k}
denotes the symbol
1
repeated
k
times (
1^{0}=\varnothing
). Then
two of the transformations
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
change only the
symbol
x
in the string
\xi
while the third one does not change
\xi
at
all. Which one of the three fixes
\xi
is determined by the
(k+1)
-th
symbol of the ternary string
\omega
. Namely,
\xi
is fixed by
b_{\omega}
,

c_{\omega}
or
d_{\omega}
if the
(k+1)
-th symbol of
\omega
is respectively
2
,

1
or
0
.

It follows from the construction that
a
,
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
are
involutions. Besides,
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
commute with one
another, and
b_{\omega}c_{\omega}=d_{\omega}
,
b_{\omega}d_{\omega}=c_{\omega}
and
c_{\omega}d_{\omega}=b_{\omega}
. Therefore one of the transformations
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and

d_{\omega}
can be dropped from the generating set
A_{\omega}
as redundant.

It is easy to see that for any
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and any
n\geq 1
the
first
n
symbols of strings
a(\xi
),
b_{\omega}(\xi)
,
c_{\omega}(\xi)
and

d_{\omega}(\xi)
are uniquely determined by the first
n
symbols of the string

\xi
and the first
n-1
symbols of the string
\omega
. As a consequence,
for any
n\geq 1
the natural action of the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
on

\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
induces an action on binary words of length
n
regarded as
prefixes of infinite binary strings. Thus we obtain a length-preserving
action of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
on the set
\{0,1\}^{*}
of all binary words. Note that
the action of the generators
a
,
b_{\omega}
,
c_{\omega}
and
d_{\omega}
can be given
by the same recursive formulas as their action on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, only
this time
\xi
is an arbitrary word in
\{0,1\}^{*}
and we also need to let

a(\varnothing)=b_{\omega}(\varnothing)=c_{\omega}(\varnothing)=d_{\omega}(\varnothing)=\varnothing
for all
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
acts transitively on binary words of any given length.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the length
k
of the binary words,
simultaneously for all
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}
. The case
k=0
is trivial as

\varnothing
is the only word of length
0
. Now assume that the lemma
holds for words of some length
k\geq 0
and every
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}
. Any
binary word of length
k+1
can be written as
0w
or
1w
, where
w
is a
word of length
k
. Any infinite ternary string
\omega
can be written as

l\omega^{\prime}
, where
l\in\{0,1,2\}
and
\omega^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We obtain
that
b_{\omega}(1w)=1b_{\omega^{\prime}}(w)
,
c_{\omega}(1w)=1c_{\omega^{\prime}}(w)
and

d_{\omega}(1w)=1d_{\omega^{\prime}}(w)
. Further, out of three words
b_{\omega}(0w)
,

c_{\omega}(0w)
and
d_{\omega}(0w)
, two coincide with
0a(w)
while the third one
is
0w
. Consequently, two of three words
ab_{\omega}a(1w)
,
ac_{\omega}a(1w)

and
ad_{\omega}a(1w)
coincide with
1a(w)
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the set of all
elements
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\omega^{\prime}}
such that
g(1w)=1g^{\prime}(w)
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\omega}

and all words
w
of length
k
. It is easy to see that
S^{\prime}
is a subgroup
of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega^{\prime}}
. By the above,
a,b_{\omega^{\prime}},c_{\omega^{\prime}},d_{\omega^{\prime}}\in S^{\prime}
.
Therefore
S^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{\omega^{\prime}}
. By the inductive assumption, the group

\mathcal{G}_{\omega^{\prime}}
acts transitively on words of length
k
. It follows that all
words of length
k+1
that begin with
1
are in the same orbit of the
action of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
. Since the generator
a
changes only the first symbol
in each word of length
k+1
, we conclude that the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
acts
transitively on binary words of length
k+1
.
∎

The set
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
of infinite binary strings is endowed with the
product topology. The topology is generated by the cylinder sets of the
form
w\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}=\bigl\{w\eta\mid\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\bigr\}
, where

w\in\{0,1\}^{*}
. The fact that the action of the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
on

\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
induces a length-preserving action on finite binary strings
implies that
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
acts on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
by homeomorphisms.

Lemma 4.3
.

Every orbit of the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
is dense in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Let
O
be any orbit of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
. By Lemma
4.2
,
the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
acts transitively on binary words of any given length.
It follows that any binary word
w
occurs as a prefix of some string in

O
. In other words, the orbit
O
has a point in the cylinder

w\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus
O
is dense in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

In the case a ternary string
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is eventually
constant, it is not hard to show that the group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
has a cyclic
subgroup of finite index. Hence the growth of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
is polynomial in
this case. In all other cases, the growth turns out to be intermediate.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
Gr1
]
)
.

If a string
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is not eventually constant, then the
group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
has intermediate growth. Moreover,

\gamma_{\mathcal{G}_{\omega},A_{\omega}}(n)\geq\exp(\alpha\sqrt{n})
for some
\alpha>0
and
all sufficiently large
n
. If, additionally, for some
m\geq 1
any
m

consecutive symbols of
\omega
include
0
,
1
and
2
then

\gamma_{\mathcal{G}_{\omega},A_{\omega}}(n)\leq\exp(n^{\beta})
for some
\beta<1
and all
sufficiently large

.

In this paper we are interested in the group
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
labeled by
the periodic string
(01)^{\infty}=010101\ldots
. The growth of this group
is much faster than the upper bound in Theorem
4.4
. The
precise rate of growth was established by Erschler
[
Er
]
as a
particular case of the following general result.

Theorem 4.5

(
[
Er
]
)
.

Suppose that a string
\omega\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
contains no

and for

consecutive symbols of

and

. Then

\exp\left(\frac{n}{\log^{2+\varepsilon}(n)}\right)\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{G}_{\omega},A_{\omega}}(n)\leq\exp\left(\frac{n}{\log^{1-\varepsilon}(n)}\right)

for any

and all sufficiently (depending on

.

Note that our notation for the groups

and their generators is

).
We reformulated Theorem

(Corollary 2
′
in

.

.

5
Schreier graphs

In this section we prove Proposition
4.6
, which will allow
us to prove Theorem
3.18
as well as Theorem
1.1
. The
proof of Proposition
4.6
is going to rely on the use of the
Schreier graphs.

A
graph
is a combinatorial object that consists of
vertices

and
edges
related so that every edge joins two vertices or a vertex
to itself (in the latter case the edge is called a
loop
at that
vertex). Vertices joined by an edge are called its
endpoints
. We
allow multiple edges joining the same pair of vertices as well as multiple
loops at the same vertex. We consider graphs with
undirected
edges,
which means that the two endpoints of a non-loop edge are not ordered.
Also, our graphs have labeled edges, that is, every edge is assigned a

label
.

The vertices of a graph are pictured as dots or small circles. An edge is
pictured as an arc joining its endpoints. The label of an edge is written
next to the edge.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be a graph with vertex set
V_{1}
and edge set
E_{1}
. Let

\Gamma_{2}
be a graph with vertex set
V_{2}
and edge set
E_{2}
. An onto
map
f:V_{1}\to V_{2}
is called a
covering
of the graph
\Gamma_{2}
by
the graph
\Gamma_{1}
(and then
\Gamma_{1}
is said to
cover

\Gamma_{2}
) if there exists an onto map
h:E_{1}\to E_{2}
such that for any
edge
e\in E_{1}
the endpoints of
h(e)
are images of the endpoints of
e

under
f
and the label of
h(e)
matches the label of
e
. The covering

f:V_{1}\to V_{2}
is called an
isomorphism
of the graph
\Gamma_{1}

onto the graph
\Gamma_{2}
(and then
\Gamma_{1}
is said to be

isomorphic
to
\Gamma_{2}
) if
f
is bijective and the associated
map
h:E_{1}\to E_{2}
can be chosen to be bijective.

A
path
of length
n\geq 0
in a graph
\Gamma
is a sequence of its
vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
along with a sequence of edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}

such that for any
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
the edge
e_{i}
joins the vertex

v_{i-1}
to
v_{i}
. We say that the path connects the vertex
v_{0}
to

v_{n}
. The graph
\Gamma
is called
connected
if any two vertices
of
\Gamma
are connected by a path. The length of the shortest path
connecting a vertex
u
to a vertex
v
is called the
distance
from

u
to
v
in the graph. This distance function turns the vertex set of a
connected graph into a metric space.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
A
. Let

\alpha:G\curvearrowright X
be an action of
G
on a set
X
and
O
be an
orbit of that action. The
Schreier graph

\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
of the orbit
O
of the action
\alpha
of the
group
G
relative to the generating set
A
is a graph with vertex set
O

and edges labeled by elements of
A
. For any
u,v\in O
and
l\in A
the
graph
\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
has a (unique) edge with label
l

connecting the vertex
u
to
v
if and only if
lu=v
or
lv=u
within the
action
\alpha
. In the case the generators in
A
are indexed by letters of
an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, we might regard edge labels in

\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
as elements of
\mathcal{A}
.

If
l\in A
is a generator of the group
G
then any vertex
v\in O
of the
Schreier graph
\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
is joined to
lv
and
l^{-1}v

by edges with label
l
. It follows that the graph

\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
is connected. Moreover, for any
v\in O
and

g\in G
the distance between vertices
v
and
gv
in this graph does not
exceed the length of the element
g
relative to the generating set
A
.

We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}(G,A)
the set of all Schreier graphs of the form

\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
. If a generator
l\in A
is an involution
(
l^{-1}=l
), then the conditions
lu=v
and
lv=u
are equivalent for all

u,v\in O
. Hence the action of
l
on the orbit
O
is uniquely recovered
from the graph
\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
. In the case when all generators
in
A
are involutions, the graph
\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(G,A;\alpha,O)
allows to
reconstruct the action of the entire group
G
on
O
. Therefore in that
case, elements of the set
\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}(G,A)
encode all transitive actions of the
group
G
.

Definition 5.3
.

Given an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, we define a group
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
by

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}=\langle\mathcal{A}\mid z^{2}=1,\ z\in\mathcal{A}\rangle
. That is, elements of

\mathcal{A}
are considered nontrivial involutions, and the group
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
is
freely generated by them.

Lemma 5.4
.

A graph
\Gamma
with labeled edges belongs to
\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A})
if and
only if the following conditions hold:

•

the graph
\Gamma
is connected;

•

all edge labels are in
\mathcal{A}
;

•

for any vertex
v
of
\Gamma
and any
z\in\mathcal{A}
there is a unique edge
with
v
as an endpoint and labeled by
z
.

Proof.

Any Schreier graph is connected. For a graph of the form

\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A};\alpha,O)
, all edge labels are in
\mathcal{A}
.
Furthermore, for any
u,v\in O
and
z\in\mathcal{A}
, the relations
zu=v
and

zv=u
are equivalent since
z
is an involution. Hence for any
v\in O

and
z\in\mathcal{A}
, the vertex
v
is an endpoint of a unique edge with label

z
, which joins
v
to
zv
.

Conversely, assume that a graph
\Gamma
satisfies all three conditions of
the lemma. Let
V
be the vertex set of
\Gamma
. For any
z\in\mathcal{A}
and

v\in V
, the vertex
v
is an endpoint of a unique edge with label
z
.
Let
T_{z}(v)
denote the vertex of
\Gamma
joined to
v
by that edge.
This defines a family of transformations
T_{z}
,
z\in\mathcal{A}
of the set
V
.
Since edges of the graph
\Gamma
are undirected, it follows that each

T_{z}
is an involution. Then there exists a homomorphism

\phi:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathrm{Sym}(V)
of the group
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
to the group

\mathrm{Sym}(V)
of all invertible transformations of
V
such that

\phi(z)=T_{z}
for all
z\in\mathcal{A}
. The homomorphism
\phi
induces an action

\alpha:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\curvearrowright V
given by
gv=\bigl(\phi(g)\bigr)(v)
for
all
g\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
v\in V
. Note that any two vertices of
\Gamma

joined by an edge are in the same orbit of the action
\alpha
. Since the
graph
\Gamma
is connected, it follows that the action is transitive. By
construction,
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A};\alpha,V)
.
∎

Let
\xi
be a word
x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, an infinite string

x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\ldots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
or a bi-infinite string
\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Suppose that there are no double
letters in
\xi
. Then we define a graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
with labeled
edges as follows. The vertex set
V
of the graph is
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
if

\xi
is a word of length
n\geq 0
,
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
if
\xi
is an
infinite string, and
\mathbb{Z}
if
\xi
is a bi-infinite string. For any

i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\{i-1,i\}\subset V
, we connect the vertex
i
to

i-1
by a unique edge with label
x_{i}
. These are the only non-loop edges
of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
. Further, for any
i\in V
and
x\in\mathcal{A}
such that
the vertex
i
is not connected to
i-1
or
i+1
by an edge with label

x
, we add a unique loop at
i
with label
x
. These are the only loops
of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
.

By construction,
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
is a connected graph with a linear
structure. All edge labels are letters in
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\xi
contains no
double letters, for any
i\in V
and
x\in\mathcal{A}
the vertex
i
is an
endpoint of a unique edge with label
x
. Hence the graph

\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
belongs to
\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A})
due to Lemma

5.4
. For examples of such graphs, see Figures
3
,

4
and
5
.

Suppose
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
is a subshift over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
such that for
any letter
z\in\mathcal{A}
the generalized
2
-cycle
\delta_{z}
is defined on
Y
.
Let
G_{Y}
be the subgroup of the topological full group
[[\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y]]

generated by involutions
\delta_{z}
,
z\in\mathcal{A}
. The group
G_{Y}
is uniquely
determined by
\mathcal{A}
and
Y
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Under the above assumptions, for any bi-infinite string
\xi\in Y
the
orbit of
\xi
under the action of the group
G_{Y}
is

\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. If the string
\xi
is not periodic
then the Schreier graph of its orbit relative to the generating set

\{\delta_{z}\mid z\in\mathcal{A}\}
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
.

Proof.

Since
\xi\in Y
and each of the generalized
2
-cycles
\delta_{z}
,
z\in\mathcal{A}

is defined on
Y
, the string
\xi
has no double letters. Let
\xi=\ldots x_{-1}x_{0}.x_{1}x_{2}\ldots
. Then
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)=\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}.x_{i+1}x_{i+2}\ldots
for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the Schreier graph
of the orbit of
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)
under the action of the group
G_{Y}
, for
any
z\in\mathcal{A}
the vertex
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)
is an endpoint of a unique edge
with label
z
. This edge joins
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)
to

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i-1}(\xi)
if
z=x_{i}
, to
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i+1}(\xi)
if

, and to itself otherwise.

Let
O
be the orbit of the string
\xi
under the action of the group

G_{Y}
and
\Gamma
be the Schreier graph of that orbit relative to the
generating set
\{\delta_{z}\mid z\in\mathcal{A}\}
(with edge labels in
\mathcal{A}
). Since
each generator is piecewise a power of the subshift
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}|Y
, the set

\{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is invariant under the action of

.
Therefore the orbit
O
is contained in this set. On the other hand, it
follows from the above that for any

the strings

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}(\xi)
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{A}}^{i-1}(\xi)
are in the same orbit of the
group
G_{Y}
. We conclude that

\phi
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Since the group
G
is generated by involutions
g_{z}
,

, there
exists a group homomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\to G

for
all

. This homomorphism is onto. The restriction of the action

\psi_{O}:G\to\mathrm{Sym}(O)

\psi=\psi_{O}\pi
is a
homomorphism of the group
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
to
\mathrm{Sym}(O)
. The

induces an action
\beta:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\curvearrowright O

and

is an orbit of the action
\alpha
, the natural action of the group

\psi_{O}(G)

\pi

Lemma 5.12
.

Suppose

are graphs such that

\Gamma_{1}
covers
\Gamma_{2}
. Then
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{1})\subset\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
V_{1}
be the vertex set of
\Gamma_{1}
and
V_{2}
be the vertex set of

\Gamma_{2}
. There exist transitive actions

\alpha_{1}:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\curvearrowright V_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\curvearrowright V_{2}
such that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A};\alpha_{1},V_{1})
and

\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{\mathop{\mathrm{Sch}}}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{A};\alpha_{2},V_{2})
. The actions give rise
to group homomorphisms
\psi_{1}:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathrm{Sym}(V_{1})
and

\psi_{2}:\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathrm{Sym}(V_{2})
. Then
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{1})
is the kernel
of
\psi_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{2})
is the kernel of
\psi_{2}
. Suppose

is a covering of the graph
\Gamma_{2}
by the graph

\Gamma_{1}
. Then the equality
\bigl(\psi_{1}(z)\bigr)(v)=u

\bigl(\psi_{2}(z)\bigr)(f(v))=f(u)

.

of
the group
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
belongs to this set, we conclude that

\psi_{2}(g)f=f\psi_{1}(g)
for all

.

If

then
\psi_{1}(g)
is the identity map on

\psi_{2}(g)f=f\psi_{1}(g)=f
. Since the map

is onto, it follows
that
\psi_{2}(g)

. Equivalently,

. Thus
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{1})\subset\mathcal{K}(\Gamma_{2})
.

Lemma 5.13
.

\displaystyle W_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[.011]_{\{0,1\}}\cup[.100]_{\{0,1\}}\cup[.01011]_{\{0,1\}}\cup[.10100]_{\{0,1\}},

\displaystyle W_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[.00]_{\{0,1\}}\cup[.11]_{\{0,1\}},

\displaystyle W_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[.0100]_{\{0,1\}}\cup[.1011]_{\{0,1\}}.

Proof.

Suppose
T_{1}:X_{1}\to X_{1}
and
T_{2}:X_{2}\to X_{2}
are aperiodic homeomorphisms
of Cantor sets, and
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
is a continuous map that is onto and
satisfies
fT_{1}=T_{2}f
. By Theorem
3.8
, there exists a
one-to-one homomorphism
\Psi:[[T_{2}]]\to[[T_{1}]]
. An explicit description
of
\Psi
can be extracted from Lemmas
3.14
and

3.16
. Namely, if
F\in[[T_{2}]]
is a homeomorphism given by

F(x)=T_{2}^{\nu(x)}(x)
,
x\in X_{2}
, where
\nu:X_{2}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a continuous
function, then its image
\Psi(F)
is given by

\bigl(\Psi(F)\bigr)(x)=T_{1}^{\nu(f(x))}(x)
for all
x\in X_{1}
. In the
case when
F=\delta_{U;T_{2}}
for some clopen set
U\subset X_{2}
, we obtain
that
\Psi(F)=\delta_{f^{-1}(U);T_{1}}
.

By Proposition
4.6
, there is a one-to-one homomorphism

h:\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}\to[[\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})]]
such that

h(z)=\delta_{z}
for all
z\in\{a,c,d\}
. Recall that
\delta_{z}
is the short
notation for the generalized
2
-cycle

\delta_{[.z]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}});S}
, where

S=\sigma_{\{a,c,d\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
. Let

f_{\phi_{2}}:\{a,c,d\}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\{x,y\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be the block factor map
considered in Lemma
2.19
. In view of the lemma, the
restriction of
f_{\phi_{2}}
to the set
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
can be regarded a
homeomorphism onto
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
. Let
g_{1}
be the inverse of that
homeomorphism. Then
g_{1}S_{1}=Sg_{1}
, where
S_{1}=\sigma_{\{x,y\}}|\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
.
Hence
g_{1}
induces a one-to-one homomorphism
\Psi_{1}:[[S]]\to[[S_{1}]]
as
described above. Then
h_{1}=\Psi_{1}h
is a one-to-one homomorphism of the
group
\mathcal{G}_{(01)^{\infty}}
to
[[S_{1}]]
. For any
z\in\{a,c,d\}
we obtain
that
h_{1}(z)=\Psi_{1}(\delta_{z})=\delta_{U^{\prime}_{z};S_{1}}
, where
U^{\prime}_{z}=g_{1}^{-1}\bigl([.z]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})\bigr)=f_{\phi_{2}}\bigl([.z]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})\bigr)
.

It follows from the description of the string
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
(Lemma
2.9
)
that any occurrence of the letter
d
in
\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}}
is followed by
ac
.
Hence
[.d]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})=[.dac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
.
Any occurrence of the letter
a
is followed by either
c
or
dac
. Hence
the set
[.a]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
is the union of

[.ac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
and
[.adac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\cap\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
.
Recall that the
1
-block factor map
f_{\phi_{2}}
is induced by the
function
\phi_{2}:\{a,c,d\}\to\{x,y\}
given by
\phi_{2}(a)=\phi_{2}(d)=x
and

\phi_{2}(c)=y
. Therefore
f_{\phi_{2}}\bigl([.c]_{\{a,c,d\}}\bigr)\subset[.y]_{\{x,y\}}
,
f_{\phi_{2}}\bigl([.ac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\bigr)\subset[.xy]_{\{x,y\}}
,
f_{\phi_{2}}\bigl([.dac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\bigr)\subset[.xxy]_{\{x,y\}}
and
f_{\phi_{2}}\bigl([.adac]_{\{a,c,d\}}\bigr)\subset[.xxxy]_{\{x,y\}}
. Note that
[.c]_{\{a,c,d\}}
,
[.ac]_{\{a,c,d\}}
,

[.dac]_{\{a,c,d\}}
and
[.adac]_{\{a,c,d\}}
are disjoint sets that cover

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{Mu}})
. As observed in the proof of Lemma
2.19
, the
word
xxxx

\xi_{\mathrm{pd}}
. Hence the set

[.xxxx]_{\{x,y\}}
is disjoint from
\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
. Consequently,

\Omega(\xi_{\mathrm{pd}})
is covered by disjoint sets
[.y]_{\{x,y\}}
,

[.xy]_{\{x,y\}}
,
[.xxy]_{\{x,y\}}

[.xxxy]_{\{x,y\}}
