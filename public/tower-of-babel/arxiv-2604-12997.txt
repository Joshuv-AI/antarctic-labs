Abstract
. We establish sufficient conditions on discrete subsets of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} for them to form a uniqueness or a non–uniqueness pair for the fractional Laplacian. Specifically, assuming that f = 0 f=0 on Λ \Lambda and that ( − Δ ) s ​ f = 0 (-\Delta)^{s}f=0 on M M , where Λ , M ⊂ ℝ d \Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} are discrete, we find sufficient conditions on these sets that force f f to vanish identically, and we provide examples in which non–uniqueness occurs. Some of the ideas used in the proofs also extend to a broader class of multiplier operators.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Uniqueness and non–uniqueness pairs

In this article, we are interested in understanding the following type of question:

Question A
.

Given
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, spaces
\mathsf{H}
and
\mathsf{S}
of functions defined in
\Omega
, an operator
T:\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{S}
, and subsets
\Lambda,M\subset\Omega
, under what conditions on
\Lambda,M
, and
\mathsf{H}
does
f\in\mathsf{H}
satisfying
f|_{\Lambda}=0
and
T(f)|_{M}=0
necessarily vanish identically?

If this happens, we say that
(\Lambda,M)
is a
uniqueness pair
for the operator
T
in
\mathsf{H}
, denoted as
\mathsf{UP}_{T}
for
\mathsf{H}
. Otherwise,
(\Lambda,M)
is called a
non–uniqueness pair
and is denoted as
\mathsf{NUP}_{T}
for
\mathsf{H}
. This kind of question is classic, but the notion of uniqueness pairs has attracted significant attention in recent years, particularly in connection with problems arising in Fourier analysis. We define the Fourier transform for
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

When
T=\mathcal{F}
, those are known as
Fourier uniqueness pairs
and are often connected to rigidity phenomena resulting from the influence of the uncertainty principle.

Perhaps the most classical example arises from the Beurling–Malliavin theorem
[
5
]
, which provides a description of those sets
\Lambda
for which
(\Lambda,\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\delta,\delta))
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
or a
\mathsf{NUP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathsf{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, given that the condition
M=\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\delta,\delta)
forces the Fourier transform to be supported in
(-\delta,\delta)
. In particular, we know that
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
and
M=\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathsf{H}
, while
\Lambda=c\mathbb{Z}
and
M=\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
for any constant
c>1
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathsf{H}
.

A remarkable result about Fourier uniqueness pairs was recently obtained by Radchenko and Viazovska
[
23
]
. As a consequence of an interpolation formula that they established for
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{even}}(\mathbb{R})
, which is the space of even functions in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows that the pair
(\Lambda,M)
forms a
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{even}}(\mathbb{R})
, with

\Lambda=M=\left\{\pm~\sqrt{n}\mathrel{:}n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

This result is notable for several reasons. First, both sets
\Lambda
and
M
are discrete, showing that rigidity phenomena may arise even under highly sparse sampling conditions. Second, their construction relies on certain arithmetic structures closely related to the solution of the sphere packing problem in dimensions
8
and
24

[
8
,
28
]
, and raises the question of whether this result has a more algebraic or analytical nature.

Motivated by this question, Ramos and Sousa
[
24
]
showed that

(1.1)

\mathbb{Z}_{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{\pm~n^{\alpha}\mathrel{:}n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

forms with itself a
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
whenever
\alpha<1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
. Later, Kulikov, Nazarov, and Sodin
[
20
]
extended the result of
[
24
]
to the full parameter range
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. They also introduced the more general notion of supercritical and subcritical pairs, together with corresponding
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\mathsf{NUP}_{\mathcal{F}}
results associated with these sets. All of these previous results hold in one dimension. For higher dimensions, Adve
[
2
]
obtained a result for discrete subsets forming a
\mathsf{UP}_{\mathcal{F}}
or
\mathsf{NUP}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Although the Fourier transform provides a classical example in which uniqueness pairs can be studied, more recent developments have shown that similar questions naturally arise in the context of partial differential equations, where the notion of uniqueness is closely related to propagation and continuation properties of solutions. A notable example in this direction is given by
Heisenberg uniqueness pairs
, introduced by Hedenmalm and Montes-Rodríguez
[
16
]
in the study of the Klein–Gordon equation. Analogous uniqueness results have been investigated for evolution equations. In particular, consider the Schrödinger equation

(1.2)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta u+Vu&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],\\
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

Under mild regularity assumptions on the potential
V
, Kehle and Ramos
[
18
]
showed that the time–one solution operator
E_{1}
, which maps the initial datum to the value of the corresponding solution at time
t=1
associated with (
1.2
), admits uniqueness pairs. More precisely, for
0<\alpha<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, they proved that
(\Lambda^{1}_{\alpha}(c_{1}),\Lambda^{1}_{\alpha}(c_{2}))
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{E_{1}}
for a suitable
\mathsf{H}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R},(1+|x|)\,\mathrm{d}x)
, where

(1.3)

\Lambda^{d}_{\alpha}(c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{c(\log|k|)^{\alpha}\frac{k}{{|k|}}\mathrel{:}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},k\neq 0\right\}.

An important aspect of these results is that the uniqueness sets are discrete, and similar conclusions are obtained for certain nonlinear versions of (
1.2
).

Related phenomena have already been observed regarding unique continuation for harmonic functions in the upper half–space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
. Specifically, let
\mathcal{U}\subset C^{1}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}})
denote the subspace of harmonic functions, and let
D:\mathcal{U}\to L^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}})
be defined by
D(u)=|\partial_{\nu}u|
, where
\partial_{\nu}u
denotes the normal derivative. It has long been known that, for every nonempty open set
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the pair
(O\times\{0\},O\times\{0\})
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{D}
for
\mathcal{U}
. Such results can be traced back, for example, to Riesz
[
25
]
.

For dimension
d=1
or for nonnegative functions in higher dimensions, the above result still holds if the assumption that the underlying set is open is replaced by the weaker requirement that it has positive Lebesgue measure (see
[
1
, Introduction]
for a brief discussion). Interestingly, for
d\geq 2
, there exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of positive Lebesgue measure such that
(E\times\{0\},E\times\{0\})
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{D}
for
\mathcal{U}
, as proved by the Bourgain–Wolff construction in
[
6
]
.

Assuming sufficient regularity, the behavior of the operator
D
can be further understood through the Caffarelli–Silvestre extension
[
7
]
. In the case
s=\frac{1}{2}
, this extension realizes the fractional Laplacian as the Dirichlet–to–Neumann operator associated with the harmonic extension problem in the upper half–space. In particular, if
u
is the harmonic extension of its boundary trace, then the normal derivative appearing in the definition of
D
agrees on the boundary, up to a multiplicative constant, with
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
(see Subsection
2.2
).

This naturally leads to the question of whether analogous uniqueness pairs hold for the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
, with
0<s<1
.

1.2.
Main results

We will be interested in tackling Question
A
concerning the fractional Laplacian as an operator and discrete sets
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
0<s<1
, we write
(-\Delta)^{s}:\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{S}
, where the function spaces
\mathsf{H}
and
\mathsf{S}
will be specified later, and we abbreviate
\mathsf{UP}_{(-\Delta)^{s}}
and
\mathsf{NUP}_{(-\Delta)^{s}}
by
\UP
and
\NUP
, respectively.

To study uniqueness and non–uniqueness pairs on discrete sets, we first describe the geometric setup. Given an injective function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a discrete set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we measure the distribution of
\Gamma
under the change of variables induced by
F
. Assuming that
F(\mathbb{R}^{d})
contains balls of arbitrarily large radius, consider

M_{F}(\Lambda,r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{\frac{\#(F(\Gamma)\cap B)}{|B|}\mathrel{:}B\subset F(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ a ball and }|B|=r\right\}\quad\text{for }r>0,

and

(1.4)

\mathrm{D}_{F}^{+}(\Gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\limsup_{r\to\infty}\sup M_{F}(\Gamma,r),\qquad\mathrm{D}_{F}^{-}(\Gamma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf M_{F}(\Gamma,r).

As usual, the previous values are allowed to lie in
[0,\infty]
. Motivated by the one--dimensional case
1
1

1

See Remark
2.8
.
, we define for
\alpha,c>0
, the changes of variables
G_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Phi_{\alpha,c}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

(1.5)

G_{\alpha}(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(y)|y|^{1/\alpha},\qquad\Phi_{\alpha,c}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\tfrac{x}{|x|}e^{(|x|/c)^{1/\alpha}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{|x|>0\right\}}(x).

Our first result concerns uniqueness pairs related to such functions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathsf{H}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
0<\alpha,s<1
, and
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be discrete. Set
\beta=\alpha^{-1}
. Then
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\UP
for
\mathsf{H}
in each of the following cases:

(i)

d\geq 1
, and there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c_{1}}}(\Lambda)>0
and
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c_{2}}}(M)>0
.

(ii)

d=1
, and there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c_{1}}}(\Lambda)>0
and
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{\beta,c_{2}}}(M)>0
.

(iii)

d=1
, and there exist
c>0
such that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c}}(\Lambda)>0
and
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{G_{\alpha}}(M)>0
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, if
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
vanishes on
\{c_{1}\log(|k|){|k|^{-1}k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}}
and
(-\Delta)^{s}f
vanishes on
\{c_{2}\log(|k|){|k|^{-1}k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, then
f
must be identically zero. The same conclusion holds if
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
vanishes on
\{\pm c\log(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(-\Delta)^{s}f
vanishes on
\{\pm n^{\alpha}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
c>0
and
0<\alpha<1
. Indeed, this follows directly from the fact that the sets
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{d}(c)
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\alpha}
from (
1.3
) and (
1.1
), respectively, satisfy

(1.6)

\mathrm{D}_{\Phi_{\alpha,c}}^{\pm}(\Lambda_{\alpha}^{d}(c))=1=\mathrm{D}_{G_{\alpha}}^{\pm}(\mathbb{Z}_{\alpha}).

Notice that it follows by definition that if
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{T}
for
\mathsf{H}
, then it is also a
\mathsf{UP}_{T}
for every
\mathsf{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathsf{H}
. So, it is natural to consider uniqueness pairs for function spaces with the lowest possible regularity. However, there is a minor point concerning the choice of the spaces. If
T:\mathsf{H}\to\mathsf{S}
is an operator such that
T(f)
is prescribed on a discrete set, then
\mathsf{S}
must be a space of functions for which pointwise evaluation is well defined, rather than a space whose elements are defined only up to almost everywhere equivalence. This means that in the discrete setting, the
\UP
problem requires stronger regularity assumptions than those provided by
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Nevertheless, we remark that the first two items of Theorem
1.1
hold in the larger space
\mathsf{H}=X_{s,\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which will be introduced in Subsection
2.1
, but we state our Theorem in the Schwartz space for the sake of convenience.

We next turn to non–uniqueness phenomena. In the opposite direction of the uniqueness pair situation, when
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{T}
for
\mathsf{H}
, then it remains a
\mathsf{NUP}_{T}
for every
\mathsf{H}^{\prime}
with
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{H}^{\prime}
. Hence, the smaller the space, the stronger the result about non–uniqueness. With this in mind, and motivated by
[
20
]
, we introduce for
0<s<1
and
0<p,q<\infty
the Gelfand–Shilov spaces

(1.7)

\mathcal{S}_{s}^{(p,q)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mathrel{:}\exists\,b=b_{f}>0\text{ such that }\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|e^{b|x|^{p}}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)|e^{b|x|^{q}}<\infty\right\},

and we will proceed to identify
\NUP
in such highly regular spaces, which are subspaces of the Schwartz class, and therefore will imply a corresponding result for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For the sake of comparison, let us recall some corresponding
\UP
results for open sets available in the literature. In this direction, Ghosh, Salo, and Uhlmann
[
13
]
proved a low–regularity result showing that, for every
0<s<1
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, every nonempty open set
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the property that
(O,O)
is a
\UP
for
H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Further strong unique continuation results in this setting can be found in
[
11
,
19
,
26
]
.

In stark contrast, there exist open sets
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(O,O)
does not enjoy the uniqueness pair property for the Fourier transform. In fact, even more pathological phenomena can occur: there are nonzero functions
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that both
h
and
\widehat{h}
vanish on an open neighborhood of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Indeed, for
0<r<\frac{1}{2}
, take
f,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B(0,r))
and
a=(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set
f_{a}(x)=f(x-a)
,
g_{a}(x)=g(x-a)
, and
O_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{:}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb{Z}^{d})<\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}-r\}
. Let
G(\xi)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}g_{a}(m-\xi)
, which is a smooth
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
–periodic function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\{c_{k}\}
denotes the Fourier coefficients of
G
, then
\{c_{k}\}
is rapidly decaying and

h(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{k}f_{a}(x-k)

belongs to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover, both
h
and
\widehat{h}
vanish at
O_{r}
, given that
\widehat{h}(\xi)=\widehat{f_{a}}(\xi)G(-\xi).

In view of this, and the results by Ghosh, Salo, and Uhlmann
[
13
]
, the fractional Laplacian may be viewed as exhibiting a stronger form of nonlocality than the Fourier transform, at least from the perspective of their behavior on open sets. Motivated by this, and by Theorem
1.1

(iii)
together with the results of
[
20
,
23
,
24
]
discussed previously, we are led, for fixed
0<s<1
in dimension
d=1
, to ask for which values of
\alpha
the set
\mathbb{Z}_{\alpha}
forms with itself a
\UP
. If this stronger form of nonlocality persists at the discrete level, then such a criterion should be possible. Our next result shows that this is indeed not the case.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
0<\alpha,p,q<1
and consider
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}
be discrete. If both
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(M)
are finite, then
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{\frac{1}{2}}
for
\mathcal{S}_{1/2}^{(p,q)}(\mathbb{R})
.

Hence, in view of (
1.6
), Theorem
1.2
implies that for every fixed
0<\alpha,\beta<1
there exists a nonzero function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f
vanishes on
\mathbb{Z}_{\alpha}
and
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}f
vanishes on
\mathbb{Z}_{\beta}
. This is a major difference compared to the Fourier transform, where such a range of non–uniqueness phenomena does not hold
[
20
,
24
]
.

Furthermore, the proofs of Theorem
1.1

(iii)
and Theorem
1.2
imply that the same pairs yield uniqueness and non–uniqueness, respectively, for the
Hilbert transform
, as stated in Corollary
1.3
below. Here the Hilbert transform is defined, for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, as

{\bf H}f(x)=\mathrm{p.v.}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x-t)}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
0<\alpha,p,q<1
and
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}
be discrete.

(i)

If
\exists\,c>0
such that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c}}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{G_{\alpha}}(M)
are positive, then
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{\bf H}
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

If both
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(M)
are finite, then
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{\bf H}
for

\mathcal{S}_{\bf H}^{(p,q)}(\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\mathrel{:}\exists\,b=b_{f}>0\text{ such that }\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|e^{b|x|^{p}}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|{\bf H}f(x)|e^{b|x|^{q}}<\infty\right\}.

While the proof of Theorem
1.2
is one–dimensional and relies on the specific choice
s=\frac{1}{2}
, it is still possible to derive a non–uniqueness result in higher dimensions by strongly exploiting the structure of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This is detailed in the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
s>0
and
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
(A\mathbb{Z}^{d},A\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is a
\NUP
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Finally, Theorems
1.1

(i)
and
1.4
admit extensions to a broader class of multiplier operators. These generalizations are presented in Theorems
6.1
and
6.2
, see Section
6
.

2.2.
The fractional Laplacian

For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
0<s<1
, we define the fractional Laplacian of order
s
via Fourier transform as

(2.2)

\mathcal{F}\left((-\Delta)^{s}f\right)(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,|\xi|^{2s}\widehat{f}(\xi).

Furthermore, (
2.2
) provides a natural definition of the fractional Laplacian for
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Alternatively,
(-\Delta)^{s}
can be realized via the Caffarelli–Silvestre extension
[
7
]
, which interprets the fractional Laplacian as a Dirichlet–to–Neumann operator. More precisely, consider the extension
u:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
solving

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}(y^{1-2s}\nabla u)=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+},\\
u(\cdot,0)=f&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

with
y>0
denoting the extended variable. Assume in addition that
u
is smooth and weakly vanishes as
y\to\infty
. Then the fractional Laplacian of
f
can be recovered through the weighted normal derivative

(2.3)

(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)=-C_{s}\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}y^{1-2s}\partial_{y}u(x,y),

where
C_{s}=2^{2s-1}\frac{\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(1-s)}
is a normalization constant. In particular, for the Schwartz class setting, this formula (
2.3
) coincides with the well–known singular integral representation

(2.4)

(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+s)}{-\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(-s)}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)-f(w)}{|x-w|^{d+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}w,

which provides a direct link to the Fourier definition, as (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) are equivalent in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and we refer to
[
21
]
for a detailed proof of this equivalence. Moreover, it is well known that if
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
(-\Delta)^{s}f
belongs to the class

(2.5)

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mathrel{:}(1+|x|^{d+2s})D^{\gamma}\psi(x)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ for every }\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}\right\}.

Since our focus is on discrete uniqueness pairs, we require at least a notion of pointwise evaluation for the fractional Laplacian. The space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by itself is not sufficient for this purpose. On the other hand, working in the Schwartz class is one way to guarantee it, although it is not strictly necessary. A more flexible approach is to impose suitable local regularity together with integrability conditions at infinity. Under these assumptions, as shown in
[
27
]
, for a function
f
in the space
X_{s,\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see (
2.1
)), the fractional Laplacian can be defined via the pointwise formula (
2.4
).

The advantage of working with
X_{s,\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is that we ensure more than pointwise values, but also that the regularity of
f
is quantitatively transferred to
(-\Delta)^{s}f
. In fact, if
f\in X_{s,\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
f\in C_{s,\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. So, from this and from
[
27
, Propositions 2.5 and 2.6]
, it follows that
(-\Delta)^{s}f
is a Hölder continuous function with Hölder continuity exponent
\sigma
and

(2.6)

\big[(-\Delta)^{s}f\big]_{C^{0,\sigma}}\lesssim_{s,\sigma,d}[f]_{C_{s,\sigma}}.

The first important notion we introduce in this subsection is the upper Beurling density with respect to a measure.

We also define the notion of separated sequences.

2.4.
de Branges and model spaces

We begin by recalling the basic structure of de Branges spaces. A function
E
is called Hermite–Biehler if
E
is entire, which means
E\in\mathscr{H}(\mathbb{C})
, and

|E^{\ast}(z)|<|E(z)|\quad\text{ for }z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

. If

\mathcal{H}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\left\{F\in\mathscr{H}(\mathbb{C})\mathrel{:}F/E\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})\text{ and }F^{\ast}/E\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})\right\},

equipped with the norm

\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}|E(x)|^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

It is a standard theorem of de Branges that

is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
[
9

,

3.
Uniqueness pairs

3.1.
From zeros to decay

The goal of this subsection is to show that the assumed set of zeros in Theorem
1.1
is sufficiently dense to induce the desired decay of the associated functions. As part of our approach, we work with a suitably chosen discrete mesh in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that preserves asymptotic information. The following proposition shows that a mesh
\Lambda
with
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{\alpha,c}}(\Lambda)>0
is sufficiently fine in the sense of an exponential–power decay.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha,c>0
,
\Phi_{\alpha,c}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be as in (
1.5
), and consider
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be discrete. Assume that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{\alpha,c}}(\Lambda)>0
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C>0
, which depends on
\delta
, such that for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
satisfying

(3.1)

|y-\lambda|\leq Ce^{-\delta(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}.

Proof.

First,
\Phi_{\alpha,c}
is a bijective function from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
onto
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\xi|>1\}
, with inverse
\Psi_{\alpha,c}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
given by

\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,c(\log|\xi|)^{\alpha}\dfrac{\xi}{|\xi|}.

By assumption,
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{\alpha,c}}(\Lambda)>0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
such that
0<\varepsilon<\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{\alpha,c}}(\Lambda)
. Using the definition of lower density, there exists
r_{1}>0
such that for every
r\geq r_{1}

\inf\left\{\frac{\#(\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\Lambda)\cap B)}{|B|}\mathrel{:}B\subset\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ a ball and }|B|=r\right\}\geq\varepsilon.

Equivalently, for every ball
B\subset\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
|B|=r\geq r_{1}
, we have

(3.2)

\#(\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\Lambda)\cap B)\geq\varepsilon|B|.

Choose
R>0
so large that
\omega_{d}R^{d}\geq r_{1}
and
\varepsilon\omega_{d}R^{d}\geq 1
, where
\omega_{d}=|B(0,1)|
. Let
B\subset\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be any ball of radius
R
. Then

|B|=\omega_{d}R^{d}\geq r_{1}
, and hence (
3.2
) gives

\#(\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\Lambda)\cap B)\geq\varepsilon\omega_{d}R^{d}\geq 1.

Thus every ball of radius
R
contained in
\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
intersects
\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\Lambda)
. Now let
\xi\in\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that it satisfies
|\xi|>R+1
. Then

B(\xi,R)\subset\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Hence

(3.3)

B(\xi,R)\cap\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\Lambda)\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{for all }|\xi|>R+1.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. We claim that there exist constants
C,R_{0}>0
, such that for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|y|\geq R_{0}
, there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
satisfying

(3.4)

|y-\lambda|\leq Ce^{-\delta(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
|y|
is large, with
e^{(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}>2R+2
and define
\xi=\Phi_{\alpha,c}(y)
. Then, by construction,
|\xi|>2R+2
and (
3.3
) implies that there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that

|\xi-\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\lambda)|\leq R.

Write
\eta=\Phi_{\alpha,c}(\lambda)
. In particular,
|\eta-\xi|\leq R
, and therefore, using the inverse function

(3.5)

|y-\lambda|=|\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\xi)-\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\eta)|.

The goal is to apply the mean value inequality. For
|w|>2
, write

\Psi_{\alpha,c}(w)=\phi(|w|)\frac{w}{|w|},

where
\phi(r)=c(\log r)^{\alpha}
. For a smooth radial map of the form
G(w)=\phi(|w|)w/|w|
, one has the standard decomposition of the differential into radial and tangential parts

DG(w)h=\phi^{\prime}(|w|)\Big\langle h,\frac{w}{|w|}\Big\rangle\frac{w}{|w|}+\frac{\phi(|w|)}{|w|}\left(h-\Big\langle h,\frac{w}{|w|}\Big\rangle\frac{w}{|w|}\right).

Applying this to
\Psi_{\alpha,c}
, we obtain
\phi^{\prime}(r)=c\alpha(\log r)^{\alpha-1}/r
, and hence for
|w|>2

(3.6)

\left\|D\Psi_{\alpha,c}(w)\right\|\leq|\phi^{\prime}(|w|)|+\frac{\phi(|w|)}{|w|}=\frac{c\alpha(\log|w|)^{\alpha-1}}{|w|}+\frac{c(\log|w|)^{\alpha}}{|w|}\lesssim_{\alpha,c}\frac{(\log|w|)^{\alpha}}{|w|}.

Moreover, every point
w
on the segment
[\xi,\eta]
satisfies

|w|\geq|\xi|-|w-\xi|\geq|\xi|-R\geq R+2>2,

so (
3.6
) is valid along the whole segment. Hence, by the mean value inequality applied to
\Psi_{\alpha,c}
on the convex segment
[\xi,\eta]
,

|\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\xi)-\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\eta)|\leq|\xi-\eta|\sup_{w\in[\xi,\eta]}\left\|D\Psi_{\alpha,c}(w)\right\|.

Using
|\xi-\eta|\leq R
and (
3.6
), we get

|\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\xi)-\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\eta)|\lesssim_{\alpha,c}R\sup_{w\in[\xi,\eta]}\frac{(\log|w|)^{\alpha}}{|w|}.

Since
|w|\geq|\xi|-R
for
w\in[\xi,\eta]
and the function
r\mapsto(\log r)^{\alpha}/r
is decreasing for sufficiently large

, it follows, after possibly enlarging the implicit constants, that

|\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\xi)-\Psi_{\alpha,c}(\eta)|\lesssim_{R,\alpha,c}\frac{(\log|\xi|)^{\alpha}}{|\xi|}.

Combining this with (
3.5
), we obtain

(3.7)

|y-\lambda|\lesssim_{R,\alpha,c}\frac{(\log|\xi|)^{\alpha}}{|\xi|}.

Since
|\xi|=e^{(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}
, if we write
t=(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}
, then
\log|\xi|=t
, and (
3.7
) becomes
|y-\lambda|\lesssim_{\alpha,c,R}t^{\alpha}e^{-t}
. For
0<\delta<1
, the function
t^{\alpha}e^{-(1-\delta)t}
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Hence

|y-\lambda|\lesssim_{\alpha,c,R,\delta}e^{-\delta(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}

for all
y
such that
e^{(|y|/c)^{1/\alpha}}\geq 2R+2
. This is equivalent to
|y|\geq R_{0}
, where
R_{0}=c(\log(2R+2))^{\alpha}
.

So (
3.4
) follows, and by increasing the constant
C
, it yields the global bound (
3.1
).
∎

∎

Next, we prove the non–uniqueness part of Corollary
1.3
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3

(ii)
.

Fix sequences
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}^{+}_{G_{\alpha}}(M)
are finite, and set
r=\max\left\{p,q\right\}
. Let
h\in\mathcal{S}_{1/2}^{(r,r)}(\mathbb{R})
be the nonzero function defined in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. By construction
\mathrm{supp}~\widehat{h}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which implies
{\bf H}(h)(x)=-ih(x)
. Since
h
vanishes at
\Lambda\cup M
and
|h(x)|\lesssim e^{-c|x|^{r}}
for some
0<c<1
, we have
h\in\mathcal{S}_{\bf H}^{(r,r)}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{S}_{\bf H}^{(p,q)}(\mathbb{R})
, and the result follows.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We first establish a few preliminary facts. Consider
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
Q=[0,1)^{d}
. Define

(4.12)

\Lambda=A\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad\Lambda^{*}=A^{-t}\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad Q_{A}=A^{-t}Q.

Because
Q_{A}
is a fundamental domain for the dual lattice
\Lambda^{*}
, the family
\{Q_{A}+\ell\mathrel{:}\ell\in\Lambda^{*}\}
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, a change of variables combined with Fubini’s theorem yields

f(Ak)=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}\int_{Q_{A}+\ell}e^{2\pi iAk\cdot w}\widehat{f}(w)\,\mathrm{d}w=\int_{Q_{A}}\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}e^{2\pi iAk\cdot(\xi+\ell)}\widehat{f}(\xi+\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

For
\ell\in\Lambda^{*}
, we have
A^{t}\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Thus
Ak\cdot\ell=k\cdot A^{t}\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which implies
e^{2\pi iAk\cdot\ell}=1
. Then

(4.13)

f(Ak)=\int_{Q_{A}}e^{2\pi iAk\cdot\xi}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}\widehat{f}(\xi+\ell)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

and in the same way

(4.14)

(-\Delta)^{s}f(Ak)=\int_{Q_{A}}e^{2\pi iAk\cdot\xi}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}|\xi+\ell|^{2s}\widehat{f}(\xi+\ell)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Motivated by the previous identities, consider the unitary linear map

U:L^{2}(Q_{A})\to L^{2}(Q),\qquad(Uh)(\xi)=|\det A|^{-1/2}h(A^{-t}\xi).

Then for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we have
U(e^{2\pi iAk\cdot\xi})(w)=|\det A|^{-1/2}e^{2\pi ik\cdot w}
. Since the family
\{e^{2\pi ik\cdot w}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is complete in
L^{2}(Q)
, it follows by unitarity of
U
that
\{e^{2\pi iAk\cdot w}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is complete in
L^{2}(Q_{A})
.

In particular, the vanishing of the sequences
\{f(Ak)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and
\{(-\Delta)^{s}f(Ak)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is equivalent to the vanishing, for a.e.
\xi\in Q_{A}
, of the two periodized functions

(4.15)

F_{A}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}\widehat{f}(\xi+\ell),\qquad G_{A}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{*}}|\xi+\ell|^{2s}\widehat{f}(\xi+\ell).

Let
v
be the nonzero vector given by
v=A^{-t}e_{1}\in\Lambda^{*}
. For
\xi\in Q_{A}
, define

a_{1}(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi+v),\qquad a_{2}(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi+2v),\qquad a_{3}(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi+3v).

If
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{f}
is contained in

(4.16)

B_{A}=(Q_{A}+v)\cup(Q_{A}+2v)\cup(Q_{A}+3v),

then the only possibly nonzero terms in the sums defining
F_{A}(\xi)
and
G_{A}(\xi)
are precisely those corresponding to
v,2v,3v
. Thus, the conditions
F_{A}(\xi)=0
and
G_{A}(\xi)=0
become the system

(4.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{1}(\xi)+a_{2}(\xi)+a_{3}(\xi)&=0,\\
|\xi+v|^{2s}a_{1}(\xi)+|\xi+2v|^{2s}a_{2}(\xi)+|\xi+3v|^{2s}a_{3}(\xi)&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Now we will construct nontrivial smooth functions
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
on
Q_{A}
that satisfy the given linear relations and ensure that the resulting patch
\widehat{f}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The main idea is that, for each fixed
\xi\in Q_{A}
, the unknowns
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
satisfy two linear homogeneous equations, so the solution space has dimension at least one. We exhibit a convenient one–parameter family of solutions.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
Q=[0,1)^{d}
, and consider again the sets defined in (
4.12
).

Let
v=A^{-t}e_{1}\in\Lambda^{*}
and
B_{A}
as in (
4.16
). Note that the set
H_{v}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{:}|\xi+2v|=|\xi+v|\}
is a proper affine hyperplane. Indeed, we have
|\xi+2v|^{2}-|\xi+v|^{2}=2\xi\cdot v+3|v|^{2}
, so

H_{v}=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mathrel{:}\xi\cdot v=-\tfrac{3}{2}|v|^{2}\right\},

which is an affine hyperplane because
v\neq 0
. In particular,
Q_{A}\setminus H_{v}
contains a nonempty open set
O
with
\overline{O}\subset Q_{A}\setminus H_{v}
. Choose a nonzero smooth function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(O)
, and define
a_{3}(\xi)=\varphi(\xi)
. We should have by (
4.17
) that
a_{1}(\xi)=-a_{2}(\xi)-a_{3}(\xi)
. Substituting this into the second constraint of (
4.17
) gives

\left(|\xi+2v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}\right)a_{2}(\xi)+\left(|\xi+3v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}\right)a_{3}(\xi)=0.

Therefore, the last equation can be solved for
a_{2}

(4.18)

a_{2}(\xi)=-\varphi(\xi)\left(\frac{|\xi+3v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}}{|\xi+2v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}}\right)

and then

(4.19)

a_{1}(\xi)=-a_{2}(\xi)-a_{3}(\xi)=-\varphi(\xi)\left(1-\frac{|\xi+3v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}}{|\xi+2v|^{2s}-|\xi+v|^{2s}}\right).

Since the quotients on the right–hand side of (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) are smooth functions of
\xi
on
Q_{A}
(on a neighborhood of
\mathrm{supp}~\varphi\subset\overline{O}
, both numerator and denominator are smooth, and the denominator never vanishes), we conclude that
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are in
C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{A})
. Using these functions, we define
\widehat{f}
at
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the natural shifts

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{jv+Q_{A}\right\}}(\xi)a_{j}(\xi-jv)

(observe that
kv+Q_{A}\subset B_{A}
,
k=1,2,3
).

Since
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are in
C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{A})
, patching these three pieces together yields a
C^{\infty}
function compactly supported inside
B_{A}
. Thus
\widehat{f}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{A})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Since
\varphi\not\equiv 0
we have
\widehat{f}\not\equiv 0
, hence by Fourier inversion
f\not\equiv 0
. By construction, for every
\xi\in Q_{A}
the triple
(a_{1}(\xi),a_{2}(\xi),a_{3}(\xi))
satisfies (
4.17
) pointwise. Therefore the periodized functions
F_{A}(\xi)
and
G_{A}(\xi)
defined in (
4.15
) vanish pointwise on
Q_{A}
. Plugging back into (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we obtain Theorem
1.4
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The function
f
constructed in the proof of Theorem
1.4
belongs to a strict subclass of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, the pair
(A\mathbb{Z}^{d},A\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is a
\NUP
not only for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, but already for the subspace
\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mathrel{:}\widehat{f}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
. Since

, the Paley–Wiener theorem implies that

extends to an entire function on

of exponential type.

5.
Unique continuation revisited

We can interpret Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.4
within the boundary framework introduced in Subsection
2.2
. Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be the smooth function that weakly vanishes as
y\to\infty
satisfying

\begin{cases}\mathrm{div}(y^{1-2s}\nabla u_{f})=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+},\\
u_{f}(\cdot,0)=f&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

It follows from (
2.3
) that the vanishing of
(-\Delta)^{s}f
on a set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is equivalent to the vanishing of the weighted normal derivative
\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}y^{1-2s}\partial_{y}u_{f}(x,y)
for
x\in\Lambda
. Therefore, our main results admit the following formulation.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
0<\alpha,s<1
,
c>0
and
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Consider
G_{\alpha}
and
\Phi_{\alpha,c}
as in (
1.5
).

(i)

If both the trace of
u_{f}
and
\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}y^{1-2s}\partial_{y}u_{f}(x,y)
are zero on
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
discrete, with
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi_{1,c}}^{-}(\Lambda)>0
, then
u_{f}\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
. In dimension
d=1
, the same conclusion holds if
u_{f}(\cdot,0)
vanishes on
\Lambda
while the weighted normal derivative vanishes on
M
, where
M,\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
are discrete with
\mathrm{D}_{\Phi_{1,c}}^{-}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}_{G_{\alpha}}^{-}(M)
both positive.

(ii)

There exists a nontrivial solution
u_{f}
whose trace and weighted normal derivative are zero on
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. If
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
and the dimension is
d=1
, the same holds with
A\mathbb{Z}
replaced by a discrete set
\Lambda
with
\mathrm{D}_{G_{\alpha}}^{+}(\Lambda)<\infty
.

Thus, the density of the boundary sets determines whether the simultaneous vanishing of Dirichlet and Neumann forces triviality, giving the uniqueness pairs a unique continuation property on the boundary for a degenerate elliptic equation. These results can be viewed as a discrete version of the phenomena discussed in the
Introduction
for harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}
. For dimension
d=1
, it is known, see
[
1
]
, that uniqueness holds whenever the underlying set has positive measure, while the Bourgain–Wolff construction
[
6
]
provides higher–dimensional examples of sets with positive measure where uniqueness fails.

Corollary
5.1
shows that even infinite sets of measure zero can lead either to unique or non–unique continuation. This phenomenon occurs in all dimensions, but our method yields a more refined result in dimension
d=1
. In a discrete setting, what governs this behavior is not the measure but the asymptotic growth of the set at infinity: sparse sets that grow slowly, such as
\left\{\pm c\log(n)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, still impose
u_{f}\equiv 0
, while sets that are less dense at infinity, such as
\left\{\pm c\,n^{\alpha}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, admit nontrivial solutions.

6.
Generalizations for multiplier operators

Let
m:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous function that is polynomially bounded. Define the operator
T_{m}
on the Schwartz class as

(6.1)

\widehat{T_{m}(f)}=m\widehat{f}.

We can formulate our main results in terms of uniqueness and non–uniqueness pairs for such more general multipliers. The results are stated below, with the details left to the reader.

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem
1.1
–revisited)
.

Suppose there exists a nonempty closed set
S\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

(i)

m
is real–analytic on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S
;

(ii)

writing
\tau=\inf\left\{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|\mathrel{:}z\in S\right\}
and
\Omega_{c}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\mathrel{:}|\mathrm{Im}(z)|<c\right\}
, for no
c>\tau
doest there exist a meromorphic function on
\Omega_{c}\setminus S
whose germ at each point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus S
agrees with the germ of
m
at
x_{0}
.

Let
\Lambda,M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be discrete. If there exist
0<c_{1},c_{2}<(2\pi\tau)^{-1}
such that
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c_{1}}}(\Lambda)>0
and
\mathrm{D}^{-}_{\Phi_{1,c_{2}}}(M)>0
, then
(\Lambda,M)
is a
\mathsf{UP}_{T_{m}}
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Theorem 6.2

(Theorem
1.4
–revisited)
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
Q_{A}=A^{-t}[0,1)^{d}
and
\Lambda^{*}=A^{-t}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Suppose that there exist distinct
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in\Lambda^{*}
such that the function

g_{j}(\xi)=m(\xi+v_{1})-m(\xi+v_{j}),\qquad j\in\{2,3\},

are nonzero for all
\xi\in Q_{A}
. Assume also that
m
is
C^{\infty}
on an open neighborhood of
B_{A}
, where
B_{A}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{3}(v_{j}+Q_{A})
. Then
(A\mathbb{Z}^{d},A\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is a
\mathsf{NUP}_{T_{m}}
for
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

6.1.
Examples

We conclude with some examples of multipliers covered by the previous results.

6.1.1.
Shifted fractional Laplacian

Let
s,\lambda>0
and

m(\xi)=(|\xi|^{2}+\lambda)^{s}.

The operator in (
6.1
) coincides with the fractional power of the shifted Laplacian
T_{m}=(-\Delta+\lambda)^{s}.

In this setting, Theorem
6.2
applies. In addition, if
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, then Theorem
6.1
also holds.

6.1.2.
Unimodular multipliers

Let
\lambda\geq 0
,
s>0
, and consider the Fourier multiplier

m(\xi)=e^{i(|\xi|^{2}+\lambda)^{s}}.

The case
s=\frac{1}{2}
,
\lambda=0
corresponds to the time evolution of the wave equation, while
s=1
,
\lambda=0
yields the free Schrödinger propagator, both at time
t=1
. The choice
s=\frac{1}{2}
and
\lambda=1
is associated with the solution of the Klein–Gordon equation, also at time
t=1
. Operators of this type have been studied with respect to their boundedness properties, see for instance
[
4
]
. In our context, if
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, then Theorem
6.1
applies.

6.1.3.
Mixed multipliers

Let
s,\gamma>0
, and

m(\xi)=\frac{e^{i|\xi|^{2s}}}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\gamma/2}}.

This class combines oscillatory behavior with polynomial decay and has appeared in different contexts, see
[
17
]
. In the present framework, Theorem
6.2
is applicable. Furthermore, when
s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, Theorem
6.1
is also valid.

Appendix A
Proof of property
(\mathsf{P})

The following proposition is based on a construction due to Baranov, Borichev, and Havin
[
3
, Lemma 4.2]
. In that work, the case
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
is treated in connection with a different problem. For completeness, we provide here a proof for
0<\alpha<1
. Moreover, throughout this appendix, the relations
\asymp
and
\lesssim
are allowed to depend on
\alpha
, and this dependence is omitted from the notation.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

(A.1)

x_{n}=n^{\alpha},\qquad y_{n}=\begin{cases}n^{\alpha-1},&0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
1,&\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

z_{2n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,x_{n}+iy_{n},\qquad z_{2n+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,-x_{n}+iy_{n}.

Then the product of (
2.12
) defines an inner function. Let
E_{\ell}
denote the associated Hermite–Biehler function with phase
\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}
. Set
\beta=\alpha^{-1}
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(A.2)

|\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}(x)|\lesssim_{k}\ell(1+|x|)^{k\left(\beta-1\right)}.

Furthermore, (
2.16
) holds and

(A.3)

\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{\prime}(x)\asymp\ell(1+|x|)^{\beta-1}.

Proof.

First of all, it is clear from (
A.1
) that (
2.12
) is an inner function, given that the Blaschke condition (
2.11
) is verified for the zero set.

We begin by proving (
A.2
) and (
2.16
). By the mean value theorem, for each
n\geq 1
there exists
\xi_{n}\in(n,n+1)
such that

\Delta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,x_{n+1}-x_{n}=(n+1)^{\alpha}-n^{\alpha}=\alpha\xi_{n}^{\alpha-1}.

Since
\xi_{n}\asymp n
, it follows that

(A.4)

\Delta_{n}\asymp n^{\alpha-1}.

Consequently, for
n\asymp N

(A.5)

\Delta_{n}\asymp\Delta_{N},\qquad y_{n}\asymp y_{N}.

In particular,

(A.6)

\Delta_{N}^{-1}\asymp N^{1-\alpha}.

We next recall the representation of the derivatives of
\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}
. Let
P_{v}(u)
denote the (non–normalized) Poisson kernel for
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, defined by

P_{v}(u)=\frac{v}{u^{2}+v^{2}},\qquad u\in\mathbb{R},\ v>0.

Using the formula (
2.14
) for
\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{\prime}
, we have

(A.7)

\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{\prime}(x)=\ell\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(P_{y_{n}}(x-x_{n})+P_{y_{n}}(x+x_{n})\bigr).

We now justify differentiation to arbitrary order. For each integer
m\geq 0
,

(A.8)

|P_{v}^{(m)}(u)|\lesssim_{m}\frac{v}{(u^{2}+v^{2})^{1+\frac{m}{2}}},\qquad u\in\mathbb{R},\ v>0.

Fix now
k\geq 1
and let
K\subset\mathbb{R}
be compact. For all
n
sufficiently large, the estimate (
A.8
) with
m=k-1
gives

|P_{y_{n}}^{(k-1)}(x\pm x_{n})|\lesssim_{k}\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{k+1}}

uniformly in
x\in K
. Now

\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{k+1}}=\begin{cases}n^{\alpha-1-\alpha(k+1)}=n^{-1-\alpha k},&0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
n^{-\alpha(k+1)},&\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

and both series are summable. Hence, using the Weierstrass
M
–test, we conclude that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(P_{y_{n}}^{(k-1)}(x-x_{n})+P_{y_{n}}^{(k-1)}(x+x_{n})\bigr)

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, by the standard theorem on termwise differentiation of uniformly convergent series of smooth functions

(A.9)

\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}(x)=\ell\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(P_{y_{n}}^{(k-1)}(x-x_{n})+P_{y_{n}}^{(k-1)}(x+x_{n})\Bigr).

Applying (
A.8
) with
m=k-1
, we have from (
A.9
) that
|\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}(x)|\lesssim_{k}\ell S_{k}(x)
, where

S_{k}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{(k+1)/2}}+\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x+x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{(k+1)/2}}\right).

Set
s=\frac{k+1}{2}
. Then
s>\frac{1}{2}
, and we may rewrite

(A.10)

S_{k}(x)=S_{k}^{-}(x)+S_{k}^{+}(x),

where

S_{k}^{-}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}},\qquad S_{k}^{+}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x+x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}.

Since the left–hand side of (
A.10
) defines an even function, it suffices to consider
x\geq 0
. In the compact interval
[0,2]
, the function
\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}
is continuous, therefore bounded, and consequently (
A.2
) is immediately obtained after enlarging the constant if necessary.

We may assume from now on that
x\geq 2
, and we choose the unique integer
N
such that

x_{N}\leq x<x_{N+1}.

Since
x_{N}=N^{\alpha}
, we have

(A.11)

N\asymp x^{\beta},\qquad\Delta_{N}^{-1}\asymp N^{1-\alpha}\asymp x^{\beta-1}.

Claim
.

If
s>\frac{1}{2}
, then

(A.12)

\sum_{N/2<n<4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim y_{N}^{1-2s}\left(1+\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right).

Proof.

By (
A.5
), one has
y_{n}\asymp y_{N}
throughout the range
N/2<n<4N
. Next, if
n\leq N-1
, then

x-x_{n}\geq x_{N}-x_{n}=\sum_{j=n}^{N-1}\Delta_{j}\gtrsim(N-n)\Delta_{N},

where we used (
A.5
) to compare
\Delta_{j}
with
\Delta_{N}
for
j\asymp N
. Likewise, if
n\geq N+2
, then

x_{n}-x\geq x_{n}-x_{N+1}=\sum_{j=N+1}^{n-1}\Delta_{j}\gtrsim(n-N-1)\Delta_{N}.

Hence

\sum_{N/2<n<4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim y_{N}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(m^{2}\Delta_{N}^{2}+y_{N}^{2})^{s}}.

Factoring out
y_{N}^{2s}
gives

y_{N}^{1-2s}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\bigl(1+m^{2}(\Delta_{N}/y_{N})^{2}\bigr)^{s}}.

Now for every
a>0
and every
s>\frac{1}{2}
,
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+a^{2}m^{2})^{-s}\lesssim 1+a^{-1}
, by comparison with the convergent integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t^{2})^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}t
. Applying this with
a=\Delta_{N}/y_{N}
proves (
A.12
) and the claim follows.
∎

We now estimate
S_{k}^{-}(x)
. We split the sum into three regions.
First, if
n\leq N/2
, then

x-x_{n}\geq x_{N}-x_{\lceil N/2\rceil}\gtrsim N^{\alpha}.

Therefore

\sum_{n\leq N/2}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim N^{-2\alpha s}\sum_{n\leq N/2}y_{n}.

We estimate the partial sums of
y_{n}
separately in the two regimes. For
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have that
\sum_{n\leq N}y_{n}=\sum_{n\leq N}n^{\alpha-1}\lesssim N^{\alpha}
by comparison with
\int_{1}^{N}t^{\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}t
. If
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, then
y_{n}=1
, and therefore
\sum_{n\leq N}y_{n}=N
. Thus

\sum_{n\leq N/2}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\begin{cases}N^{-2\alpha s}N^{\alpha}=N^{-\alpha k},&0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
N^{-2\alpha s}N=N^{1-\alpha(k+1)},&\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1.\end{cases}

Since
\Delta_{N}^{-k}\asymp N^{k(1-\alpha)}
by (
A.6
), and the preceding powers of
N
are no larger than a fixed constant times
N^{k(1-\alpha)}
for
N\geq 1
, we obtain

(A.13)

\sum_{n\leq N/2}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

Second, if
n\geq 4N
, then
x_{n}-x\gtrsim x_{n}
, because
x<x_{N+1}=(N+1)^{\alpha}
and
x_{n}=n^{\alpha}
with
n\geq 4N
. Hence

\sum_{n\geq 4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\sum_{n\geq 4N}\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{2s}}=\sum_{n\geq 4N}\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{k+1}}.

Thus

\sum_{n\geq 4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\begin{cases}\sum_{n\geq 4N}n^{-1-\alpha k}\lesssim N^{-\alpha k},&0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
\sum_{n\geq 4N}n^{-\alpha(k+1)}\lesssim N^{1-\alpha(k+1)},&\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1.\end{cases}

Again these quantities are bounded by a constant multiple of
\Delta_{N}^{-k}
, and therefore

(A.14)

\sum_{n\geq 4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

Third, for the middle block
N/2<n<4N
, the claim (
A.12
) yields

\sum_{N/2<n<4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim y_{N}^{1-2s}\left(1+\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right)\lesssim y_{N}^{-k}\left(1+\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right),

because
2s-1=k
. We now compare this with
\Delta_{N}^{-k}
. If
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then
y_{N}\asymp\Delta_{N}
by (
A.4
), and so

y_{N}^{-k}\left(1+\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right)\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

If
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, then
y_{N}=1
, and therefore

y_{N}^{-k}\left(1+\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right)=1+\Delta_{N}^{-1}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k},

because
k\geq 1
and
\Delta_{N}\leq 1
for all sufficiently large
N
(finitely many remaining values of
N
are absorbed into the implicit constant).
Hence

(A.15)

\sum_{N/2<n<4N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x-x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

Combining (
A.13
), (
A.14
), and (
A.15
), we obtain

(A.16)

S_{k}^{-}(x)\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

We estimate
S_{k}^{+}(x)
in the same spirit. In contrast with the analysis of
S_{k}^{-}
, here it is enough to split the sum into the two ranges
n\leq N
and
n>N
. Indeed, since
x,x_{n}>0
, we always have

x+x_{n}\geq x\qquad\text{and}\qquad x+x_{n}\geq x_{n}.

We use these bounds separately according to the range of
n
. If
n\leq N
, then
x+x_{n}\geq x\asymp N^{\alpha}
, so

\sum_{n\leq N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x+x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\lesssim N^{-2\alpha s}\sum_{n\leq N}y_{n}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k},

by the same argument used in the estimate of the left tail. If
n>N
, then
x+x_{n}\geq x_{n}
, and therefore

\sum_{n>N}\frac{y_{n}}{\bigl((x+x_{n})^{2}+y_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{s}}\leq\sum_{n>N}\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{2s}}=\sum_{n>N}\frac{y_{n}}{x_{n}^{k+1}}\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k},

exactly as in the estimate of the right tail. Hence

(A.17)

S_{k}^{+}(x)\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}.

From (
A.16
) and (
A.17
) we conclude that
S_{k}(x)\lesssim\Delta_{N}^{-k}
. In particular, (
2.16
) follows. Moreover, we have
|\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}(x)|\lesssim_{k}\ell\Delta_{N}^{-k}
. Using (
A.11
), we finally obtain
|\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{(k)}(x)|\lesssim_{k}\ell x^{k(\beta-1)}
for all
x\geq 2
.
As noted earlier, the same upper bound is trivial on
[-2,2]
after adjusting the constant. This finishes the proof of (
A.2
).

For the first derivative lower bound, choose
M=\left\lfloor c\frac{y_{N}}{\Delta_{N}}\right\rfloor
, where
c>0
is small enough that
1\leq M\leq N/4
for all large
N
(this is indeed possible because
y_{N}/\Delta_{N}\asymp 1
if
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}
, while
y_{N}/\Delta_{N}\asymp N^{1-\alpha}=o(N)
if
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
). For
0\leq m\leq M
,

0\leq x-x_{N-m}\leq x_{N+1}-x_{N-m}=\sum_{j=N-m}^{N}\Delta_{j}\lesssim(m+1)\Delta_{N}\lesssim y_{N}.

Moreover, since
0\leq m\leq M\leq N/4
, we have

\frac{3N}{4}\leq N-m\leq N.

Hence, by (
A.5
), for every
0\leq m\leq M
we have
y_{N-m}\asymp y_{N}
, and

\frac{y_{N-m}}{(x-x_{N-m})^{2}+y_{N-m}^{2}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{y_{N}}.

Summing over
m
throughout this range and using in (
A.7
) that the summands are nonnegative, we obtain

\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{\prime}(x)\geq 2\ell\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{y_{N-m}}{(x-x_{N-m})^{2}+y_{N-m}^{2}}\gtrsim\frac{M+1}{y_{N}}\ell\asymp\Delta_{N}^{-1}\ell.

Together with (
A.2
) and (
A.11
), this proves
\varphi_{\alpha,\ell}^{\prime}(x)\asymp\ell x^{\beta-1}
for
x\geq 2
. Since the right–hand side of (
A.7
) is continuous and strictly positive on
[-2,2]
, we conclude (
A.3
) and the result follows.
