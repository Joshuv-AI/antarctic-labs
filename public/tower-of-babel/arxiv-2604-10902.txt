Entropic independence via sparse localization
Vishesh Jain, Huy Tuan Pham, Thuy-Duong Vuong

Abstract
. Entropic independence is a structural property of measures that underlies modern proofs of functional inequalities, notably (modified) log-Sobolev inequalities, via “annealing” or local-to-global schemes. Existing sufficient criteria for entropic independence typically require spectral independence and/or uniform bounds on marginals under all pinnings, which can fail in natural canonical-ensemble models even when strong mixing properties are expected. We introduce sparse localization : a restricted localization framework, in the spirit of Chen–Eldan, in which one assumes ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -independence only for a sparse family of pinnings (those fixing at most c ​ n cn coordinates for any c > 0 c>0 ), yet still deduces quadratic entropic stability and entropic independence with an explicit multiplicative loss of order c − 1 c^{-1} . As an application, we give a rigorous proof of approximate conservation of entropy for the uniform distribution on independent sets of a given size in bounded degree graphs.



1.
Introduction

Functional inequalities such as (modified) log-Sobolev inequalities (mLSIs) are a standard route to sharp
mixing time bounds, concentration inequalities, and quantitative stability for high-dimensional discrete
distributions.
A widely-used modern strategy for proving mLSIs is an annealing (or interpolation) approach:
one constructs a path of intermediate measures

\nu=\nu^{(0)}\rightsquigarrow\nu^{(1)}\rightsquigarrow\cdots\rightsquigarrow\nu^{(T)},

and proves an mLSI for
\nu
in two conceptually distinct steps:

(i)

Reduction to an easy regime.
Show that entropy does not decay too quickly along the path,
typically via an entropy-factorization / entropic contraction estimate.

(ii)

Handling the easy regime.
Prove an mLSI for
\nu^{(T)}
,
using model-specific arguments in a tractable regime.

Entropic independence, which is the focus of this paper, is concerned with step (i).

We focus on distributions on the
k
-slice
\binom{[n]}{k}
, where
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
For a probability distribution
\mu
on
\binom{[n]}{k}
, define its normalized one-site marginals

q_{\mu}
as a distribution on
[n]
:

q_{\mu}(i)\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mu}[i\in S]}{k}\qquad(i\in[n]).

Definition 1.1

(Entropic independence)
.

A probability distribution
\nu
on
\binom{[n]}{k}
is
C
-entropically independent
if for every

\mu
on
\binom{[n]}{k}
with
\mu\ll\nu
,

\operatorname{KL}(q_{\mu}\|q_{\nu})\;\leq\;\frac{C}{k}\,\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\nu).

Entropic independence has been used repeatedly as a modular hypothesis for deriving mLSIs for natural Markov chains on combinatorial state spaces; see, e.g.,

[
6
,
1
,
2
]
and references therein.

1.1.
Existing criteria for entropic independence

There are, by now, several powerful general frameworks that imply entropic independence from
correlation or influence information. We highlight three that are most relevant to this paper. We refer the reader to
Section

3
for various definitions.

(1)

Chen–Liu–Vigoda
[
6
]
show that spectral independence together with suitable marginal
boundedness hypotheses yields entropy factorization and mLSIs for a broad class of Markov chains.
A salient feature is that these hypotheses are formulated uniformly over pinnings/conditionings of the
base measure; this uniformity is crucial in the inductive/local-to-global machinery underlying their approach.

(2)

A different line of work by Anari et. al
[
1
,
2
]
derives entropic
independence from strong global structure such as fractional log-concavity (which is equivalent to spectral independence under all tilts) and related
high-dimensional expansion properties of generating polynomials. These conditions are extremely powerful, but do not hold as widely.

(3)

Chen–Eldan
[
5
]
introduce a martingale framework which implies entropic independence
under an appropriate spectral independence hypothesis that is again formulated uniformly over all pinnings, together
with additional regularity/tameness conditions on marginals. We also refer the reader to independent and concurrent work of Chen, Feng, Yin, and Zhang
[
4
]
, which proves optimal mLSIs for general anti-ferromagnetic models.

A common thread is that all these criteria require controlling either (i) spectral independence, or typically, (ii) both spectral independence and marginal behavior,
under a very broad family of conditionings (in fact, all conditionings). Unfortunately, in many applications (such as those involving conservative dynamics,
[
8
,
9
]
, see also
Section

6
), the “all pinnings” requirement in these criteria is too stringent. This mismatch is especially acute in so-called annealing arguments (i.e. reduction to the easy regime): this step typically explores only a family of intermediate measures that retain many of the properties of the original measure, whereas “full”
localization may generate highly adversarial conditionings with extreme marginals or qualitatively different
behavior.

1.2.
Our contribution

The main conceptual message of this paper is that one does not need uniform control under all pinnings.
Instead, we introduce a sparse localization framework: we only require independence bounds for
pinnings that fix at most a
c
-fraction of the coordinates, for any
c>0
, and we derive entropic independence with an
explicit loss of a factor
c^{-1}
in constants.

Sparse localization has two key advantages:

(i)

It matches the restricted conditioning landscape in applications such as
[
8
]
, where
independence estimates are available along the relevant localization path but not uniformly
over all pinnings.

(ii)

Sparse pinnings produce measures close to the original one: by forbidding near-total pinning,
sparse localization avoids creating highly degenerate intermediate distributions, in contrast to full
localization schemes that may explore a much larger and less stable family of conditionings.

Our main theorem shows that marginal boundedness of the base measure together with sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence implies entropic
independence. Informally, sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence requires a uniform operator norm bound on the
influence matrix under all pinnings that touch at most
cn
coordinates (see

Section

3
for the precise definition).

Theorem 1.2

(Entropic independence)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\binom{[n]}{k}
. Suppose that:

(a)

(Marginal lower bound)
for every
i\in[n]
, either
\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\nu}[i\in S]=0
or

\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\nu}[i\in S]\geq b
for some
b\in(0,1)
; and

(b)

(Sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence)
the pushforward
\widetilde{\nu}
on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is

\alpha
-
\ell_{2}
-independent restricted to
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{Sparse}_{c})
, i.e. for every feasible
u\in\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{Sparse}_{c})
,

\|\Psi(\mathcal{R}_{u}\widetilde{\nu})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\alpha.

Then
\nu
is
C
-entropically independent with
C=2\alpha/(bc)
.

Remark 1.3
.

When
c=1
, sparse localization becomes full localization and
Theorem

1.5
recovers the Chen–Eldan quadratic
stability paradigm
[
5
, Theorem 42]
. The point of our framework is that it remains effective when
independence or marginal boundedness is available only for a very sparse family of pinnings, at the price of the explicit factor

c^{-1}
. The power of this comes from the fact that for very sparse pinnings
u
,
\mathcal{R}_{u}\nu
often has quantitatively similar independence and marginal boundedness properties as
\nu
.

Remark 1.4
.

Our assumptions are phrased in terms of
\ell_{2}
-independence i.e. operator norm bounds on the influence matrix, as opposed to spectral norm bounds. We discuss the relation between these notions in
Section

3
.

Our entropic independence theorem is obtained via a quadratic stability inequality on the cube, which may be
of independent interest.

Theorem 1.5

(Quadratic stability from sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Fix
c\in(0,1]
and
\alpha>0
.
If
\nu
is
\alpha
-
\ell_{2}
-independent restricted to
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{Sparse}_{c})
, then for every

\mu\ll\nu
,

\|m(\mu)-m(\nu)\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{8\alpha}{c}\,\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\nu).

Proof techniques

The proof of
Theorem

1.5
follows the Chen–Eldan localization blueprint but introduces
two new modular ingredients that enable restricted (sparse) pinning hypotheses.

•

In
Section

4
we prove a restricted localization Lipschitz lemma
(
Proposition

4.1
):
\ell_{2}
-independence over pinnings in
\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V})
implies Lipschitz control of
the mean map
v\mapsto m(\mathcal{T}_{v}\widetilde{\nu})
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
(and we apply this with

\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}
). This step is where we ensure the localization martingale only visits pinnings that
remain in the allowed family.

•

In
Section

5
we replace the “full” convex duality step of
[
5
]

by a sparse convex duality argument: we apply the Donsker–Varadhan variational formula
(
Theorem

2.7
) but restrict test functions to sparse linear functionals, and we compute the resulting
restricted quadratic conjugate explicitly in terms of Ky Fan norms (
Lemma

5.5
). This is the
unique point where the factor
c^{-1}
enters, through the inequality comparing the Ky Fan norm to
\|\cdot\|_{2}

(
Lemma

5.4
).

Theorem

1.2
follows in a relatively simple manner from
Theorem

1.5
using a standard inequality upper bounding the KL divergence by the
\chi^{2}
-divergence. This step only uses marginal boundedness for the base measure.

Application: down-up walk on fixed size independent sets

In
Section

6
, we discuss an application of
Theorem

1.2
to proving annealing (i.e. reduction to the easy regime) for the uniform distribution on fixed size independent sets in bounded degree graphs. This step is crucial in the program of
[
8
]
for proving optimal mixing of the so-called down-up walk on fixed size independent sets throughout the algorithmic tractability regime; however, as was pointed out to us by Zongchen Chen and Mario Morellini, this step does not follow from any previous arguments in the literature (contrary to what is claimed in
[
8
]
), precisely due to the fact that previous methods for proving entropic independence require spectral independence and/or marginal boundedness for all pinnings of the base measure, which is not available here. Our argument in
Section

6
fixes this critical gap.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Tilts, pinnings, and sparse vectors

We work on the discrete cube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. For a probability measure
\nu
on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, write

m(\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]\in[-1,1]^{n},\qquad m_{i}(\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X_{i}],

for the mean vector, where
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\sim\nu
.

For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the tilt (or external field) operator
\mathcal{T}_{v}
on measures by

(2.1)

\frac{d(\mathcal{T}_{v}\nu)}{d\nu}(x)=\frac{e^{\langle v,x\rangle}}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{\langle v,X\rangle}]},\qquad x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}.

For
u\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
, define the pinning operator
\mathcal{R}_{u}
by conditioning:

(2.2)

\mathcal{R}_{u}\nu:=\nu\bigl(\,\cdot\mid X_{i}=u_{i}\text{ for all }i\text{ with }u_{i}\neq 0\,\bigr),

whenever the conditioning event has positive
\nu
-probability.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the sign function by

\mathrm{sign}(x)=\begin{cases}1\qquad&x>0\\
0\qquad&x=0\\
-1\qquad&x<0.\end{cases}

For a set of vectors
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
to be the downward-closure of the vectors
\{\operatorname{sign}(v):v\in\mathcal{V}\}
. Formally,

(2.3)

\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V}):=\Bigl\{s\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}:\text{there exists }v\in\mathcal{V}\text{ such that }s_{i}\in\{0,\operatorname{sign}(v_{i})\}\ \forall i\in[n]\Bigr\}.

Remark 2.1
.

For our applications in
Section

4
, it is important that we work with the downward-closure, as defined above, and not just with the set of all sign vectors themselves.

Definition 2.2

(Sparse vectors)
.

Fix
c\in(0,1]
.
Define

\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}:=\Bigl\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ |\{i\in[n]:v_{i}\neq 0\}|\leq\lceil cn\rceil\Bigr\}.

Note that

\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{Sparse}_{c})=\{-1,0,1\}^{n}\cap\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}.

2.2.
Entropy and divergences

For probability measures
\mu
and
\pi
on a common finite set
\Omega
, we write
\mu\ll\pi
to mean that

\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
. In this case,
\frac{d\mu}{d\pi}(\omega)=\mu(\omega)/\pi(\omega)
.

Definition 2.3

(Entropy functional)
.

Let
\pi
be a probability measure on a finite set
\Omega
and let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]>0
.
Define

\Ent_{\pi}[f]:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\log\frac{f}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f\log f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f].

Definition 2.4

(KL and
\chi^{2}
divergence)
.

Let
\pi,\mu
be probability measures on a finite set
\Omega
with
\mu\ll\pi
. Define

\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{d\mu}{d\pi}}}\right]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu(\omega)\log\frac{\mu(\omega)}{\pi(\omega)}.

Also define

\chi^{2}(\mu\|\pi)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\frac{(\mu(\omega)-\pi(\omega))^{2}}{\pi(\omega)}.

The following standard fact follows using the elementary inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
for all
x\geq 0
.

Fact 2.5
.

Let
\pi,\mu
be probability measures on a finite set
\Omega
with
\mu\ll\pi
. Then

\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\pi)\leq\chi^{2}(\mu\|\pi).

Definition 2.6

(
f
-tilt and KL identity)
.

Given
\pi
a probability measure on
\Omega
and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f>0
,
define the tilted measure
\mu
by

\frac{d\mu}{d\pi}(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f}.

Then

(2.4)

\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\pi)=\frac{\Ent_{\pi}[f]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f}.

Theorem 2.7

(Donsker–Varadhan variational formula)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on a finite set
\Omega
with
\mu\ll\nu
. Then

\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\nu)=\sup_{\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\varphi]-\log\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[e^{\varphi}]}}\right\}.

Moreover, if
g=\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}
, the supremum is attained by
\varphi^{*}=\log g+C
for any constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.3.
From
\binom{[n]}{k}
to the cube

We use the standard embedding
\binom{[n]}{k}\hookrightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
sending

S\mapsto\mathbf{1}_{S}
, followed by the map
\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}^{n}
given by
x\mapsto 2x-\mathbf{1}
.
For a measure
\mu
on
\binom{[n]}{k}
, denote its pushforward on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
by
\widetilde{\mu}
.
Then for each
i\in[n]
,

m_{i}(\widetilde{\mu})=2\,\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mu}[i\in S]-1.

In particular,

(2.5)

\|m(\widetilde{\mu})-m(\widetilde{\nu})\|_{2}^{2}=4\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mu}[i\in S]-\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\nu}[i\in S]}}\right)^{2}.

3.
Influence matrices and sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence

3.1.
Correlation matrices and notions of independence

For a measure
\nu
on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, define the covariance matrix

\mathrm{Cov}(\nu)_{ij}:=\mathrm{Cov}_{\nu}(X_{i},X_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X_{i}X_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X_{i}]\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X_{j}],

and variances
\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})=\mathrm{Cov}(\nu)_{ii}
. When
\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})=0
, coordinate
i
is deterministic under
\nu
.
In this case we interpret influence/correlation matrices as acting on the active coordinate set

A(\nu):=\{i:\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})>0\}
(i.e. by restricting to the principal submatrix indexed by
A(\nu)
).
All operator norms below are taken on this active subspace.
Whenever
\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})>0
for all
i\in A(\nu)
, define the correlation matrix

\mathrm{Cor}(\nu):=\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})\bigr)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\nu)\,\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})\bigr)^{-1/2},

and the influence matrix

\Psi(\nu):=\mathrm{Cov}(\nu)\,\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})\bigr)^{-1}.

(Here
\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i}))
denotes the diagonal matrix on
A(\nu)
with entries

\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})
.)
We remark that when viewing
\nu
as a distribution over
2^{[n]}
induced by the map
x\mapsto\{i:x_{i}=1\}
then the entries of
\Psi(\nu)
can be rewritten as

\Psi(\nu)_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\nu}[i\in S|j\in S]-\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\nu}[i\in S|j\not\in S]

Definition 3.1

(limited
\ell_{2}
-independence)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
with
0\in\mathcal{S}
. For
\alpha>0
, we say that
\nu
is
\alpha
-
\ell_{2}
-independent restricted to
\mathcal{S}
if for any
u\in\mathcal{S}
,

\|\Psi(\mathcal{R}_{u}\nu)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\alpha.

Specializing to the case of
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{Sparse}_{c})
, gives the following.

Definition 3.2

(sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. For
\alpha>0
and
c\in(0,1]
, we say that
\nu
is
c
-sparse
\alpha
-
\ell_{2}
-independent if for any
u\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}\cap\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}
,

\|\Psi(\mathcal{R}_{u}\nu)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\alpha.

\ell_{2}
-independence and comparisons to other independence notions

Our results are phrased in terms of
\ell_{2}
-independence. This is closely related to other standard notions in the literature.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and write
\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})=1-m_{i}(\nu)^{2}
.
Let
\operatorname{Cov}(\nu)
be the covariance matrix,
\Cor(\nu)
the correlation matrix, and
\Psi(\nu)
the influence
matrix. On active coordinates,

\Psi(\nu)=D^{1/2}\Cor(\nu)\,D^{-1/2},\qquad D:=\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})).

Consequently, if the marginals are bounded away from
\pm 1
in the quantitative sense that

(3.1)

\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(X_{i})\geq\sigma^{2}\qquad\text{for all active }i,

then the two operator norms are comparable:

(3.2)

\|\Psi(\nu)\|_{\op}\ \leq\ \sigma^{-1}\|\Cor(\nu)\|_{\op}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\Cor(\nu)\|_{\op}\ \leq\ \sigma^{-1}\|\Psi(\nu)\|_{\op}.

In words, under this assumption, spectral independence, which refers to boundedness of the operator norm (or spectral radius, since these quantities are the same for a symmetric matrix) of the correlation matrix, is equivalent to
\ell_{2}
-independence up to a factor of
\sigma^{-1}
.

It is well-known that a sufficient condition for
\alpha
-spectral independence is that either the maximum absolute row-sum of
\Psi(\nu)
(denoted by
\|\Psi(\nu)\|_{\infty\to\infty}
) is bounded by
\alpha
, or the maximum absolute column-sum of
\Psi(\nu)
(denoted by
\|\Psi(\nu)\|_{1\to 1}
) is bounded by
\alpha
. In fact, in many applications, spectral independence is proved by establishing one of these stronger properties. Using the standard matrix inequality

(3.3)

\|A\|_{\op}\ \leq\ \sqrt{\|A\|_{1\to 1}\,\|A\|_{\infty\to\infty}},

we observe that in settings where one can control both row and column sums of influences,
\ell_{2}
-independence follows automatically. For instance, in the case of the hardcore model in the uniqueness regime, it is known that both
\|\Psi\|_{\infty\to\infty}
(see
[
7
]
) and
\|\Psi(\nu)\|_{1\to 1}
(see
[
3
]
) are bounded by a constant depending only on the relative gap to uniqueness.

4.
Sparse localization and Lipschitzness of the mean map

This section proves a sparse adaptation of
[
5
, Lemma 68]
. We state the proposition below for a general family of directions
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
; later we specialize to

\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mathcal{V}
. For
\alpha>0
, suppose that
\nu
is
\alpha

\ell_{2}
-independent restricted to
\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V})
. Then, for every
v\in\mathcal{V}
,

\|m(\mathcal{T}_{v}\nu)-m(\nu)\|_{2}\leq 4\alpha\|v\|_{2}.

Remark 4.2
.

In
[
5
, Lemma 68]
, this statement is proved for the special case
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We note that arXiv v2 of
[
5
]
contains a typographical error wherein
\ell_{2}
-independence is replaced by the weaker assumption of spectral independence; the correct statement with
\ell_{2}
-independence appears in arXiv v1 of
[
5
]
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

Fix
v\in\mathcal{V}
. We will construct a continuous-time jump process
u(t)\in\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V})
(initialized at
u(0)=0
) and an associated measure-valued process

\mu_{t}:=\mathcal{R}_{u(t)}\mathcal{T}_{(1-t)v}\nu

such that
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration corresponding to
\{u(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Note that

\mu_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{v}\nu,\qquad\mu_{1}=\mathcal{R}_{u(1)}\nu.

In
[
5
]
, such a process is constructed for arbitrary
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but with
u(t)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
. We will follow exactly the same construction: our assumption that
v\in\mathcal{V}
will guarantee that
u(t)\in\mathrm{sign}(\mathcal{V})
.

Martingale construction

We describe the construction of
u(t)
. Initialize
u(0)=0
. For each
i\in[n]
, define

s_{i}:=\operatorname{sign}(v_{i})\in\{-1,0,+1\},\qquad a_{i}:=|v_{i}|\geq 0,

so that
s_{i}a_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. For
u\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, define the updated state
u^{(i)}=(u^{(i)}_{1},\dots,u^{(i)}_{n})
by

u^{(i)}_{j}:=\begin{cases}s_{i}&j=i,\\
u_{j}&j\neq i.\end{cases}

Given time
t\in[0,1]
and
u(t)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
, define the deterministic measure

(4.1)

\pi_{t,u}:=\mathcal{R}_{u(t)}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{(1-t)v}\nu\bigr).

Then define the instantaneous jump rate for coordinate
i
by

(4.2)

\lambda_{i}(t,u(t)):=\begin{cases}a_{i}\bigl(1+s_{i}\,m_{i}(\pi_{t,u})\bigr)&\qquad\text{for }i\text{ with }u_{i}=0,\\
0&\qquad\text{for }i\text{ with }u_{i}\neq 0\end{cases}

Note that, since
m_{i}(\pi_{t,u})\in[-1,1]
, we have
\lambda_{i}(t,u(t))\in[0,2a_{i}]
and the total rate is bounded by
2\|v\|_{1}
uniformly in
(t,u(t))
.

We define
u(t)
to be the time-inhomogeneous continuous-time Markov chain with generator
\mathcal{L}
acting on
F:\{-1,0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(\mathcal{L}_{t}F)(u)=\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}(t,u)\,\bigl(F(u^{(i)})-F(u)\bigr)=\sum_{i:\,u_{i}=0}\lambda_{i}(t,u)\,\bigl(F(u^{(i)})-F(u)\bigr).

In words, at each time
t
, every coordinate
i
which is not already
\pm 1

5.1.
Ky Fan norms and a sparse quadratic conjugate

Definition 5.3

(Ky Fan
m
-norm)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
m\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
define

\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}\;:=\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
|S|\leq m\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{i\in S}x_{i}^{2}.

Equivalently, if
|x|_{(1)}\geq|x|_{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq|x|_{(n)}
are the absolute values of coordinates of
x

sorted in nonincreasing order, then

\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{m}|x|_{(j)}^{2}.

Lemma 5.4

(Ky Fan
m
-norm vs.
\ell_{2}
norm)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
m\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Then

\frac{m}{n}\,\|x\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}\;\leq\;\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The upper bound is immediate. For the lower bound, write the squared coordinates in nonincreasing order:
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}\geq 0
, where
a_{j}:=|x|_{(j)}^{2}
. Then

\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}.

Since the average of the top
m
terms is at least the average of all
n
terms,

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j},

which rearranges to

\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\;\geq\;\frac{m}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}.

But
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=\|x\|_{2}^{2}
, giving

\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{m}{n}\,\|x\|_{2}^{2}.\qed

Lemma 5.5

(Sparse quadratic conjugate)
.

Fix
c\in(0,1]
and
m:=\lceil cn\rceil
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and define

\Phi_{c}(x)\;:=\;\sup_{v\in\mathrm{Sparse}_{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\langle x,v\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}}}\right\}.

Then for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\Phi_{c}(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\,\|x\|_{2,(m)}^{2}\;\geq\;\frac{c}{2\varepsilon}\,\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Fix
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leq m
and restrict to vectors
v
supported on
S
. Then

\langle x,v\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}v_{i}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}v_{i}^{2}}}\right).

Each summand is maximized at

, yielding

\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(v)\subseteq S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\langle x,v\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}}}\right\}=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}^{2}.

Maximizing over

The final inequality follows from

5.4

∎

\|\Psi(\mathcal{R}_{u}\mu_{r}(H))\|_{\op}\ \leq\ \alpha(\Delta,\delta/2,\gamma/2),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.5

(Entropic independence for
\mu_{r}
)
.

Fix
\Delta\geq 3
and
\delta,\gamma>0
. There exists
C=C(\Delta,\delta,\gamma)>0
such that the following holds.
For every graph
H
of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and every integer
r
satisfying

\gamma|V(H)|\leq r\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)|V(H)|
, the measure
\mu_{r}(H)
is
C
-entropically independent.

Proof.

Combine the marginal bound
Lemma

6.2
(giving
b=b(\Delta,\delta,\gamma)>0
)
with sparse
\ell_{2}
-independence
Lemma

6.4
(giving
c,\alpha
),
and apply
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

Lemma 6.6

(Density window along the localization chain)
.

Fix
d\in(0,1]
and suppose
\ell\geq dk
. Set
T:=k-\ell
, and for
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
write

n_{t}:=|V(G_{t})|
.
Then for every
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
,

d\gamma\,n_{t}\;\leq\;k-t\;\leq\;(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)\,n_{t}.

Consequently, conditional on
G_{t}
, the measure
\nu_{t}=\mu_{k-t}(G_{t})
lies in the parameter regime of

Proposition

6.5
with lower-density parameter
d\gamma
and upper-density slack
\delta
.

Proof.

The identification
\nu_{t}=\mu_{k-t}(G_{t})
conditional on
G_{t}
is exactly

Lemma

6.1
.

For the lower bound, since
n_{t}\leq n
and
t\leq T=k-\ell
, we have

k-t\;\geq\;\ell\;\geq\;dk\;\geq\;d\gamma n\;\geq\;d\gamma n_{t}.

For the upper bound, note from (
6.1
) that

n_{t}\;\geq\;n-(\Delta+1)t.

Therefore

\frac{k-t}{n_{t}}\;\leq\;\frac{k-t}{\,n-(\Delta+1)t\,}\;=:\;h(t).

A direct computation gives

h^{\prime}(t)=\frac{(\Delta+1)k-n}{\bigl(n-(\Delta+1)t\bigr)^{2}}.

Since
k\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)n
and
(\Delta+1)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)<1
, we have

(\Delta+1)k-n\;\leq\;\bigl((\Delta+1)(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)-1\bigr)n<0.

Hence
h
is decreasing on
[0,T]
, and so

\frac{k-t}{n_{t}}\;\leq\;h(t)\;\leq\;h(0)=\frac{k}{n}\;\leq\;(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta).

This proves the claim.
∎

We can now prove the entropy-conservation statement
Equation

6.3
.

Proof of
Equation

6.3
.

This is a direct consequence of the “local-to-global” entropy argument from

[
1
, Appendix B]
.
For completeness, we sketch the deduction in our notation.

Choose a constant
d=d(\Delta)>0
such that
\ell\geq dk
, and write
T:=k-\ell

(so
\nu_{T}=\nu_{k-\ell}
).
For each
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}
, let
\mu_{t}
be the
f
-tilt of
\nu_{t}
, i.e.

d\mu_{t}/d\nu_{t}=f/\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}f
.
Then
\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\nu_{t})=\Ent_{\nu_{t}}[f]/\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}f
by (
2.4
).
The standard entropy decomposition for subset localization (the same telescoping device used in

[
1
, Appendix B]
) gives

\mathbb{E}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Ent_{\nu_{t+1}}[f]\mid\nu_{t}}}\right]=\Ent_{\nu_{t}}[f]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}[f]\cdot\operatorname{KL}(p_{t}\|q_{t}),

where
q_{t}
is the single-vertex distribution induced by
\nu_{t}
and
p_{t}
is the corresponding
single-vertex distribution induced by
\mu_{t}
.

By
Lemma

6.6
, conditional on
G_{t}
the realized measure
\nu_{t}
is of the form

\nu_{t}=\mu_{k-t}(G_{t})

with

d\gamma\,|V(G_{t})|\leq k-t\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{c}(\Delta)\,|V(G_{t})|.

Therefore
Proposition

6.5
applies pathwise with lower-density parameter
d\gamma
, and shows that

\nu_{t}
is
C
-entropically independent with

C=C(\Delta,\delta,d\gamma).

Hence

\operatorname{KL}(p_{t}\|q_{t})\;\leq\;\frac{C}{k-t}\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{t}\|\nu_{t})=\frac{C}{k-t}\cdot\frac{\Ent_{\nu_{t}}[f]}{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}f}.

Substituting yields

\mathbb{E}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Ent_{\nu_{t+1}}[f]\mid\nu_{t}}}\right]\;\geq\;\Ent_{\nu_{t}}[f]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{C}{k-t}}}\right).

Taking expectations and iterating from
t=0
to
t=T-1
gives

\mathbb{E}[\Ent_{\nu_{T}}[f]]\;\geq\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{j=\ell+1}^{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{C}{j}\Bigr)}}\right)\Ent_{\nu}[f].

Since
\ell\geq dk
and
C=C(\Delta,\delta,d\gamma)
is independent of
n
, for all sufficiently
large
n
we have
C/j\leq 1/2
for every
j\in\{\ell+1,\dots,k\}
; the finitely many remaining
values of
n
can be absorbed into the final constant. Therefore

\prod_{j=\ell+1}^{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{C}{j}\Bigr)\;\geq\;\exp\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-2C\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{k}\frac{1}{j}}}\right)\;\geq\;\exp\!\bigl(-2C\log(k/\ell)\bigr)\;\geq\;\exp\!\bigl(-2C\log(1/d)\bigr)\;=:\;c_{0}(\Delta,\delta,\gamma)\;>\;0.

This completes the proof.

∎
