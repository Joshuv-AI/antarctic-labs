Exploring the impact of measurement approaches on disability prevalence: findings from a comprehensive child survey
Authors: Ahmet Ozturk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding how disability prevalence differs between social and medical models across socioeconomic contexts helps inform policies aimed at improving the well-being of children with disabilities. Additionally, consistent data collection methods are essential to support the shift from medical to social models of disability. This study examined associations between two measures of disability: parent-reported functional limitations based on the Washington Group/UNICEF Child Functioning Module (CFM) and medically documented impairments.MethodsWe analyzed data from Türkiye’s 2022 child survey, which provided a comprehensive assessment of parent-reported functional limitations and medically documented impairments in 14,705 children aged 0–17 from 9,010 households. Using principal component analysis and logistic regression, we identified and tested associations between the two measures.ResultsDisability prevalence was 2% based on medically documented impairments but rose to 14.1% when using the CFM, highlighting significant discrepancies across measures. The CFM responses suggested three distinct sub-populations: (1) physical, sensory, and cognitive domains, (2) social domains, and (3) emotional domains, with each group displaying unique socioeconomic characteristics. Additionally, children with medical disability reports were three times more likely to report symptoms of depression or anxiety than those without such reports, underscoring the importance of integrating functional and clinical measures for a comprehensive understanding of disability.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the CFM’s functional approach provided a complementary perspective to the medical model, offering global comparability and a fuller profile of childhood disability. This dual-model approach supports disability-inclusive policies by capturing a broader spectrum of functional needs, thereby aiding resource allocation and public awareness initiatives.Keywords: Disability measurement, Disability models, Washington Group/UNICEF Child Functioning Module, Socioeconomic characteristics

Background

Disability is a complex and evolving concept that includes aspects of body function and structure, capacity, and performance, as well as the interaction between an individual and their environment [1–3]. Over time, various conceptual models have emerged to understand and explain disability, often represented by the medical and social models. The medical model views disability as a problem of the individual, arising from disease, trauma, or other health conditions, and requiring medical care or behavioral adjustment [4]. In contrast, the social model frames disability as a socially created problem, resulting from environmental and attitudinal barriers that restrict participation. Addressing disability within this framework requires collective social action to ensure accessibility and inclusion, emphasizing disability as a human rights issue [4]. These perspectives have been synthesized within the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF), which uses a biopsychosocial approach to integrate biological, individual, and social dimensions of health and functioning [4].

Established in 2001, the Washington Group on Disability Statistics (WG) is a United Nations (UN) initiative aimed at improving the quality and comparability of disability statistics worldwide. Its primary objective is to foster global collaboration in health statistics by developing standardized disability measures suitable for use in censuses and national surveys [5–7]. The WG’s mission is to produce essential data on disability that can be compared across countries and regions. To guide the development of its question modules, the WG adopted the WHO ICF as its conceptual framework [5–7]. Initially focused on designing disability questions for adults aged 18 and older, the WG later identified the need for a specialized module to assess child functioning and disability, enabling the collection of comparable data for children globally [5].

In this context, United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and the Washington Group on Disability Statistics have developed the Child Functioning Module (CFM) for use in censuses and surveys to meet the demand for comparable data collection for children with disabilities worldwide. The module aims to provide a population-level estimate of the number and proportion of children with functional difficulties. It covers children aged 2–17 and assesses difficulties in various areas of functioning. Consistent with the biopsychosocial model of disability, the module focuses on the presence and extent of functional difficulties rather than body structure or conditions. In addition to questions on areas related to physical, sensory, and cognitive functioning, the CFM also includes questions related to difficulties in psychosocial functioning, such as managing emotions, accepting changes, controlling behavior, and making friends [2, 8]. This approach is in line with the social model of disability, which emphasizes the role of environmental and attitudinal barriers in restricting the participation of individuals with impairments [1, 9, 10].

The CFM includes two versions tailored to different age groups: one for children aged 2–4 years and another for those aged 5–17 years, both typically administered to mothers or primary caregivers. This module assesses functional difficulties across various domains, including vision, hearing, mobility, communication/comprehension, behavior and learning. For the younger age group (2–4 years), the CFM also includes questions on dexterity and playing, while for older children (5–17 years), it examines self-care, remembering, focusing attention, coping with change, relationships and emotions. By measuring the degree of difficulty in these areas, the CFM identifies children at a higher risk of limited participation, helping to quantify the impact of unaccommodating environments on their lives [8].

The CFM’s structure and rigorous design make it a valuable tool for national and global disability monitoring. The module has been extensively reviewed by experts, tested across various countries, and validated for cultural understanding, thus ensuring reliable and cross-culturally sensitive data collection [8]. This has positioned the CFM as a crucial tool for identifying barriers to participation and promoting inclusive policies for children with disabilities worldwide.

While the functional approach to disability has been well established in fields such as rehabilitation—particularly following the introduction of the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health in 2001 [4]—its integration into large-scale statistical data collection and population-level monitoring has been more recent. In this context, tools such as the Washington Group/UNICEF Child Functioning Module represent an important step in operationalizing the functional model for use in censuses and surveys.

In March 2017, a joint statement issued by various UN agencies and Member States, disability organizations, and other stakeholders recommended the CFM module as the appropriate tool for disaggregating Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) data on children [2]. Accordingly, the CFM was included in the Türkiye Child Survey conducted by the Turkish Statistical Institute (TURKSTAT) in collaboration with UNICEF Türkiye and the Ministry of Family and Social Services of Türkiye between October 10 and December 16, 2022 [11]. The survey collected data on both functional limitations using the CFM module and medically documented impairments based on parent reports.

For medically documented impairments, respondents reported whether the child possessed official medical documentation confirming the presence of a disability, such as visual or hearing disability reports issued by authorized medical boards. These questions aimed to capture disabilities documented by medical professionals during the survey period, although no direct medical assessments were conducted as part of the survey.

In Türkiye, the use of the WG/UNICEF CFM is gaining prominence, particularly as the country seeks to align its disability data collection efforts with international standards. This alignment is crucial, as traditional data collection methods may underreport the true prevalence of childhood disability.

In this study, we used a unique dataset to explore associations between two different measures of disability: self-reported functional limitations based on the Washington Group/UNICEF CFM questions and medically documented impairments reported by parents. Both types of questions were asked to the same respondents, helping to reduce potential biases related to differences in respondent populations and survey implementation. The 2022 child survey included data on 14,705 children aged 0–17 living in 9,010 households. We applied principal component analysis and logistic regression models to explore and test associations.

The rationale for this study stemmed from the need to better understand the relationships between self-reported functional limitations, as captured by the WG/UNICEF CFM, and medically documented impairments in Türkiye. Previous research [12–17] has explored the relationships between different measures of disability in various contexts, highlighting discrepancies in prevalence estimates and their implications for disability identification. However, comprehensive analyses that integrate both functional and clinical measures within the same dataset remain limited. This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between these two measures of disability and contributing to the growing body of international research on disability measurement. The findings have important implications for evidence-based policymaking and the implementation of disability-inclusive practices in health, education, and social services.

Methods

Data

The study relied on a unique dataset from the Türkiye Child Survey, which collected information on both functional limitations and medically documented impairments based on parent-reported data. Respondents, primarily mothers or primary caregivers, provided information about their child’s functional abilities and the presence of medically documented disabilities. Importantly, both types of questions were asked to the same set of respondents. This consistency ensures that the estimates are robust to potential biases that might otherwise arise from differences in survey methodology. Specifically, if these questions had been administered in separate surveys or to distinct groups of individuals, the results could have been confounded by variability in respondent characteristics. By asking both sets of questions to the same individuals, the study minimizes these risks and enables a more reliable comparison between the two measures.

TURKSTAT conducted the survey in cooperation with UNICEF Türkiye and the Ministry of Family and Social Services of Türkiye between October 10 and December 16, 2022. The survey utilized a multi-stage cluster sampling method to ensure national representativeness. In the first stage, clusters (blocks) consisting of approximately 100 household addresses, each with at least one child aged 0–17 years, were selected as the primary sampling units with a probability proportional to their size. In the second stage, 10 household addresses were selected from each sampled cluster by systematic random selection method, resulting in a total of 9,010 households [11].

The sampling frame was created based on the National Address Database and Address-Based Population Registration System, which include all households with at least one child aged 0–17 years. The final sample comprised 14,705 children living in 9,010 households. To ensure that the survey findings are nationally representative, population weighting was applied, including steps for computing design weights, adjusting for non-responsiveness, integrative calibration, trimming, and calculating the overall inflation factor. These methodological steps allow the data to be generalized to the national level with a high degree of confidence, ensuring reliable country-wide estimates [11].

Information was obtained primarily from mothers, who constituted 98% of all respondents. In cases where the mother did not live in the same household or was not alive, the father, grandmother, aunt, or another primary caregiver provided the information. The survey collects data on education, living conditions, early childhood development, health, disability, quality of school life, breastfeeding and nutrition, and social and cultural participation for children aged 0–17 years. It also includes household-level information, such as housing characteristics, income, social transfers, and places or institutions within walking distance of the household. Moreover, in compliance with ethical standards, informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the survey [11].

To assess medically documented impairments, the survey included a series of dichotomous questions aimed at capturing parent-reported information about official medical documentation confirming the presence of a disability. Respondents, typically mothers or primary caregivers, were first asked whether the child had a disability report issued by an authorized medical board indicating disability status. Additional dichotomous questions asked whether the child had specific medically documented impairments, including mental disability, hearing disability, visual disability, orthopedic disability, language disorder, mental and emotional illness, learning disorder, and pervasive developmental disorder. These questions specifically sought to identify children with disabilities documented by recognized medical boards during the survey period. Since multiple conditions could be reported for the same child, children may have had more than one medically documented impairment. It is important to note that the survey did not involve direct medical assessments or clinical evaluations; all information was based on parent-reported data.

For functional limitations, the Washington Group/UNICEF Child Functioning Module was used to identify children aged 5–17 years with functional difficulties. The CFM is specifically designed to collect information on children aged 5–17 years (or aged 2–4 years), with a knowledgeable proxy respondent providing information for each child [8]. According to the standard UNICEF [18] syntax, children were categorized as having functional difficulties if at least one domain was coded as “A lot of difficulty” or “Cannot do at all” (“Every day” for Anxiety or Depression). This approach ensures consistency with the guidelines for analyzing CFM data and provides an internationally comparable measure of functional limitations. The survey has questions on all domains of the module including vision, hearing, mobility, self-care, communication, learning, remembering, concentrating, accepting change, controlling behavior, making friends, anxiety, and depression. In this study, we only used the CFM on children aged 5–17 years.

Since the medically documented impairments and CFM captures difficulties across multiple domains, a child may experience challenges in more than one area simultaneously. Therefore, the sub-groups identified through this study are not mutually exclusive, as a child can belong to more than one sub-group if they present difficulties across different domain clusters.

Empirical specification

In this study, we applied binary logistic regression to analyze and compare disability prevalence across different definitions. This statistical method is well-suited for binary outcomes, where the dependent variable takes on two possible values—in this case, whether a person was reported to have any difficulties based on the CFM. Binary logistic regression allowed us to estimate the likelihood (odds) of an individual being classified as having a disability, while considering various factors. We used robust standard errors to account for potential violations of regression model assumptions, ensuring more reliable hypothesis testing. Age, gender, household income, and mother’s years of schooling were included as control variables.

However, the mother’s years of schooling were not directly reported in the survey. Instead, the highest level of education the individual had ever attended was recorded. To estimate the mean years of education, levels were assigned numerical values: not attended school or preschool was considered 0 years, primary school as 5 years, elementary school as 8 years, high school as 11 years, 2- or 3-year higher education as 13 years, undergraduate as 15 years, master’s degree as 17 years, and doctorate as 20 years. Similar approaches have been used in previous studies, such as Aydemir and Kirdar [19] and Ozturk and Tumen [20]. These studies estimated the mean years of schooling conditional on the highest completed schooling level, utilizing Turkish Demographic and Health Surveys, which include data on both graduation levels and years of schooling.

The survey also included variables related to items the child had at home, including whether the child had his/her own room. The 15 questions on possessions explored the following items: desk for studying, quiet work environment, mobile phone, computer, tablet, software for education, internet connection, calculator, dictionary, additional resource books, magazine, literature books, artwork, art books, and musical instrument. Since the total of 16 questions were highly correlated and measured similar characteristics, we applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensions. The main aim of PCA is to explain the variance-covariance structure of a set of correlated variables through a few uncorrelated variables, called principal components, which retain most of the information in the original dataset [21, 22].

Based on this analysis, two principal components were retained for use in the regression models. The first component captures overall household economic resources, as it reflects consistently positive loadings across all items. The second component represents a contrast between arts-related items (e.g., artwork and art books) and more traditional or technology-oriented items (e.g., desk, internet access, and dictionary), and can be interpreted as reflecting differences in household preferences toward arts versus technology.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Figure 1 presents the percentage of children aged 5–17 years with functional difficulty across the WG/UNICEF CFM domains. Overall, the most prevalent disabilities were observed in the psychosocial domains: anxiety ranked first with 7.3%, followed by depression at 4.7%. The prevalence of disability in the psychosocial functioning domains was 2.1% for accepting change, 1.6% for controlling behavior, and 2.1% for making friends. For the remaining eight domains related to physical, sensory, and cognitive functioning, the prevalence ranged from 0.2% to 1.5%.

Patterns in socio-demographic characteristics also differed across disability domains. For instance, school attendance rates were highest among children reporting anxiety and depression, exceeding 90%, and remained close to 90% for the other psychosocial domains. In comparison, school attendance was approximately 80% for children with difficulties in physical, sensory, and cognitive domains. Similar differences were observed in maternal education and household income levels. Children reporting anxiety and depression, as well as those experiencing difficulties in accepting change, controlling behavior, and making friends, were more likely to come from households with relatively higher levels of income and maternal education compared to children with physical, sensory, and cognitive difficulties.

These descriptive patterns suggested that the CFM domains did not form a single homogeneous structure and that certain domains tended to cluster together conceptually and empirically. In particular, domains related to seeing, hearing, walking, self-care, communication, learning, remembering, and concentrating displayed similar prevalence and socioeconomic profiles and were therefore grouped under a physical, sensory, and cognitive category. The psychosocial domains appeared to form two relatively distinct patterns. Difficulties related to accepting change, controlling behavior, and making friends shared similar demographic and socioeconomic distributions and were grouped as a social functioning category, whereas anxiety and depression showed a separate profile and were grouped as an emotional functioning category.

Based on these observations, three disability groups were defined for the analytical procedures. The first group consists of children with functional difficulty in at least one domain of seeing, hearing, walking, self-care, communication, learning, remembering, or concentrating, referred to as the physical, sensory, and cognitive group. The second group consists of children with functional difficulty in at least one domain of accepting change, controlling behavior, or making friends, referred to as the social group. The third group consists of children with functional difficulty in at least one domain of anxiety or depression, referred to as the emotion group. These groups are not mutually exclusive, and a child may belong to more than one group if difficulties are reported across multiple domains.

Comparison of disability prevalence and association across definitions

Table 2 presents the odds ratios of the logistic regression exploring the associations between different disability populations. The group of physical, sensory, and cognitive disabilities was found to be highly associated with the group of children with a medical disability report, referred to as the medically documented disability group (Model 1). When gender, age, years of mother’s education, and household income were controlled, the odds ratio of this relationship was 42.8 (95% confidence interval: 30.7–59.5). This finding indicates that a child with a medical disability report is 42.8 times more likely to have difficulties in one of the eight domains within the physical, sensory, and cognitive group than a child without such a report.

When examining the association between social domains and the medically documented disability group, the odds ratio declined by nearly half compared to the association between the physical, sensory, cognitive group and the medically documented disability group (Model 3). The same regression was conducted for the third comparison, which examined the association between the emotion group and the medically documented disability group (Model 5). Although a highly significant association was still observed, the odds ratio dropped sharply to 3.0 (95% confidence interval: 2.1–4.3). This finding highlights that, compared to children without disability medical reports, those with a disability report are three times more likely to exhibit signs of depression or anxiety.

We presented the same estimates in Models 2, 4, and 6 of Table 2, additionally controlling for the two principal components derived from the 16 variables related to items in the children’s room. Importantly, the overall pattern of results remained consistent, indicating that the inclusion of these additional control variables did not substantially alter the observed associations. This robustness highlights the stability of the findings across different model specifications and sub-populations.

As the association between the physical, sensory, and cognitive domains group and the medically documented disability group was found to be quite high, we conclude that these groups are highly similar (Models 1–2). The association between the social domains and the medically documented disability group weakened by half, while the association between the emotion domains and the medically documented disability group was much weaker than the other two associations.

Finally, in Table 3, we compared the socio-economic characteristics of four disability groups to those of the overall population. Among these groups, children with medically documented disabilities showed the most significant socio-economic differences from the general population. For instance, this group had the highest percentage of younger children, a lower school attendance rate (approximately 80%), a smaller proportion of mothers with higher education, and a lower share of families in the highest income bracket.

Additionally, we observed that among the disability groups defined by the CFM, the medically documented disability group appears most similar to the physical, sensory, and cognitive domains. As shown in Table 3, socio-economic characteristics such as school attendance, maternal education, and income distribution display comparable patterns between children in the physical, sensory, and cognitive domains and those in the medically documented disability group.

In contrast, the emotional domains (e.g., anxiety and depression) displayed a socio-economic distribution that closely resembled the overall population, indicating fewer socio-economic differences between children with emotional disabilities and the general population. Lastly, Table 3 demonstrates a moderate association between the socio-economic characteristics of the social domains (accepting change, controlling behavior, or making friends) and those of the medically documented disability group. This association is weaker compared to the stronger relationship observed between the medically documented group and the physical, sensory, and cognitive domains.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the prevalence and types of disabilities among children aged 5–17 by comparing medically documented impairments with functional limitations measured by the CFM. The findings revealed a substantial difference in prevalence rates, with medically documented disabilities accounting for only 2% of children, compared to 14.1% identified through the CFM’s functional approach. Similar to previous studies (e.g., Kohen et al. [24]), the CFM captured a broader spectrum of children with functional limitations, including those with less visible or undiagnosed disabilities. This highlights the tool’s importance for inclusive policymaking and comprehensive disability monitoring. Notably, the CFM’s emphasis on psychosocial difficulties proved particularly significant, as these accounted for the largest proportion of children with disabilities in this study—a pattern consistently observed in other countries [2, 24]. Comparable variations have also been reported in adult populations [16].

The categorization of CFM disability domains into three distinct groups—physical, sensory, and cognitive; social; and emotional—underscores the diverse nature of disabilities and highlights the need for tailored interventions to address specific challenges. This study’s distinction between social and emotional categories within psychosocial difficulties offers a nuanced perspective compared to prior findings, such as those by Zia et al. [25], who identified two broader groups: motor/cognition and mood. Their exploratory factor analysis of CFM data in Uganda revealed a two-factor structure, with domains such as seeing, hearing, walking, self-care, communication, learning, remembering, concentrating, accepting change, behavior control, and making friends grouped under “Motor and Cognition,” and anxiety and depression categorized as “Mood.”

Additionally, the association between medical disability reports and the likelihood of functional limitations in psychosocial domains, particularly depression and anxiety, highlights the often-overlooked mental health needs of children with documented disabilities, a finding echoed in studies like de Castro et al. [26]. These observations signal the need for holistic disability policies that encompass physical, cognitive, and mental health needs for comprehensive support.

Traditional disability estimates have also largely relied on clinical definitions, primarily serving administrative and legislative purposes. While these clinical criteria remain essential for legal frameworks, the CFM has demonstrated its value as a complementary tool by capturing a broader spectrum of functional limitations. This approach enhances disability assessment by providing globally and regionally comparable data. Such data have the potential to raise public awareness about disability prevalence, much like the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) scores have influenced educational outcomes. Notably, reporting prevalence rates individually across CFM domains could offer policymakers deeper insights into the functional needs within their populations. This granularity supports the development of disability-inclusive policies and enables the precise allocation of resources to meet the diverse needs of each disability sub-group.

This study benefited from the use of a nationally representative dataset integrating both functional and medically documented impairments, enabling a direct comparison of two widely used disability measurement approaches. By asking both types of questions to the same respondents—mothers or primary caregivers—the study minimized biases arising from differences in respondent populations, ensuring consistency and comparability between measures. The findings highlight the importance of combining functional and clinical approaches to capture the full spectrum of childhood disabilities, particularly in identifying psychosocial challenges that may be overlooked in medically documented impairments. Moreover, this study contributes to global disability research by providing evidence from Türkiye, a context that aligns its disability data collection with international standards, making the findings relevant for cross-country comparisons and policy development.

However, this study faced several methodological constraints. The reliance on parent-reported data for medically documented impairments introduced potential biases, as reports may have varied depending on caregivers’ awareness, perceptions, or access to healthcare. This limitation underscores the need for caution when interpreting results, particularly given the likelihood of underreporting medical diagnoses due to systemic barriers or limited clinical engagement. Additionally, the study design did not permit causal inferences in regression analyses, restricting the scope of conclusions. The absence of data on key factors such as paternal education necessitated the use of proxy indicators, which may not have fully captured household socio-economic dynamics. Furthermore, the three disability groups defined in the study were not mutually exclusive, and some children could belong to more than one group due to overlapping functional difficulties across domains. As a result, group-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously, as some observed associations may partly reflect co-occurring difficulties rather than entirely distinct functional profiles.

Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the critical value of comprehensive and flexible tools like the CFM in capturing a fuller picture of disability prevalence and its impact, particularly in psychosocial health domains. As socio-economic and cultural contexts continue to evolve, further research into caregiver-reported data, functional approaches, and culturally sensitive methodologies remains essential for refining disability measurement and informing the development of inclusive policies.

Conclusion

This study provided an in-depth examination of disability prevalence and characteristics in Türkiye, utilizing a unique dataset that captured both functional limitations through the Washington Group/UNICEF Child Functioning Module and medically documented impairments. The findings demonstrated that the choice of disability measurement—functional versus medical—substantially influences prevalence estimates and the observed relationships with socio-demographic characteristics.

By directly comparing these two approaches within the same dataset, the study highlights the complementary nature of functional and medical measures in identifying different dimensions of childhood disability. In particular, the CFM captures a broader spectrum of functional difficulties, including psychosocial domains that may not be reflected in medically documented impairments.

Data were collected by TURKSTAT in accordance with the ethical approval of the “Ethics Commission of Turkish Statistical Institute”. More information about the Commission can be found at https://www.tuik.gov.tr/Kurumsal/Etik_Komisyonu.

Moreover, in compliance with ethical standards, informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the survey. Information was obtained mainly from mothers (in cases where the mother did not live in the same household or was not alive, the father, grandmother, aunt, etc. provided the information as the primary caregiver).
