Abstract
Restricted Syndrome Decoding (ResSD) is a variant of linear code decoding problem where each of the error’s entries must belong to a fixed small set of values. This problem underlies the security of CROSS, a post-quantum signature scheme that is one of the Round 2 candidates of NIST’s ongoing additional signatures call. We show that solutions to this problem can be deduced from vectors of a particular structure and a small norm in newly constructed codes, in both Hamming and Euclidean metrics. This allows us to reduce Restricted Syndrome Decoding to both code-based (Regular Syndrome Decoding) and lattice-based problems (Closest Vector Problem, List of Short/Close Vectors), increasing the attack surface and providing new insights into the security of ResSD. We evaluate our attacks on CROSS instances both theoretically and experimentally on reduced parameters.

Our contribution

Our primary goal is to better understand the Restricted Syndrome Decoding (ResSD) problem and expand the surface of analysis techniques applicable to it. Given a parity-check matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and a syndrome
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
, the problem consists in finding an error
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{e}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}={\mathbf{s}}
with each entry of
{\mathbf{e}}
belonging to a fixed subset
E
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Although CROSS has also a version using ResSD
with a subgroup of
E^{n}
, in this work we focus on the basic version (called CROSS-R-SDP). The main family of algorithms for solving the original syndrome decoding problem is called ISD (Information Set Decoding), and almost all existing algorithms for solving ResSD are adaptations of ISD. There is an exception in recent work
[
14
]
that studies algebraic techniques based on Gröbner basis, as well as hybrid attacks. We depart from these methods with several new ideas.

(Reduction of ResSD to regular syndrome decoding)

The first idea consists, instead of using
{\mathbf{H}}
directly, in considering the new parity check matrix

{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}r_{1}{\mathbf{H}}_{1}&\dots&r_{z}{\mathbf{H}}_{1}\ \|\ \dots&\dots\ \|\ r_{1}{\mathbf{H}}_{n}&\dots&r_{z}{\mathbf{H}}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

where
{\mathbf{H}}_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
column of
{\mathbf{H}}
and
r_{i}
are the elements of
E
. One can immediately see that if
{\mathbf{e}}
was a solution to our original ResSD problem, then if we denote
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}
the vector of length
zn
which in the
i^{\text{th}}

z
-size block has exactly one 1 at the position that corresponds to the value of the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
{\mathbf{e}}
, we will have
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{s}}
. So
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}
is a special solution of a regular syndrome decoding instance, because its nonzero values contain only ones. We call this kind of vectors
light-regular
vectors. That is why we add
n
parity-check equations imposing a sum equal to 1 on each block (similarly to the so-called “encoding regularity” from
[
26
]
). We obtain a new parity-check matrix
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(2n-k)\times zn}
. We adapt some ISD algorithms to this new particular problem, obtaining rather high complexities because of the high rate of the code admitting
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}
as a parity-check matrix.

(Reduction of light-regular decoding (and ResSD) to CVP)

Noticing that light-regular vectors also minimize the Euclidean norm among solutions, we create the lattice containing this code and convert the regular syndrome decoding problem into a closest vector problem. The idea is similar to lattice-based attacks against code-based problems using the Lee metric
[
33
]
. But the large dimension of our lattice which in addition has many short vectors makes this instance hard to solve. We nevertheless improve the attack by using a “hybrid” method consisting in “guessing” a certain number of blocks. This reduces the dimension of the lattice, and if we enumerate all vectors close to our new target, the solution will be among them. We combine this already hybrid attack with a “truncation method” consisting in guessing of a certain number of coordinates per block, giving better complexities.

(Direct reductions from ResSD to ListCVP/ListSVP)

Another idea is based on the fact that given
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
a\neq 0
and
{\mathbf{b}}=(b,\dots,b)
, we can rewrite
{\mathbf{e}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}={\mathbf{s}}
into
(a{\mathbf{e}}+{\mathbf{b}}){{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}=a{\mathbf{s}}+{\mathbf{b}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}
. If we denote
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}=a{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}=a{\mathbf{s}}+{\mathbf{b}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}
, they both constitute an instance of ResSD with solution’s entries lying in
aE+b
. Thus, we can perform an affine substitution of the set of error’s values to minimize a parameter that we called the affine diameter of
E
. This, combined with the truncation method, leads to solutions of small Euclidean norms that allow us to use lattice-based attacks again, while preserving the dimension
n
of the problem. This results in a reduction of ResSD to a list-closest-vector-problem (which consists in listing the vector close up to a certain distance to the target) and to the list-shortest-vector-problem (the same, but the target is the zero vector) over lattices. Remarkably, certain parameters of ResSD instances heuristically lead to a degeneration of these list-decoding problems into basic CVP and SVP instances. This generalizes the observation of the CROSS designers
[
4
]
that ResSD with a two-element set
E
corresponds to a subset-sum problem.

These attacks are based on statistics on the error distributions. In CROSS, the set
E
of values for the error is a multiplicative subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which allows us to randomize the solution of the ResSD instance. This allows us to convert weak-key attacks into standard probabilistic attacks.

,
p
prime, is denoted by
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The symbol
\triangleq
is used to define the object on the left side. We denote by
\textsf{Card}\left(S\right)
the cardinality of the set
S
. Vectors are denoted by bold lowercase letters and indexed from 1, matrices are denoted by bold capitals. For every matrix
{\mathbf{M}}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
its

{\mathbf{0}}
are, respectively, the identity matrix of size

for every integer

2.1
Linear codes

Definition 1
(Linear code)

A linear code
\mathcal{C}\subset{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}
of dimension
k
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of dimension
k
. It can be represented by two equivalent ways:

•

With a generator matrix
{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
whose rows are a basis of
\mathcal{C}
:

\mathcal{C}=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{G}}\;|\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\right\}

•

With a parity-check matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
that is a generator matrix of
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
:

\mathcal{C}=\left\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\;|\;{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathbf{0}}\right\}

In particular, we have
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathbf{0}}
and
\mathcal{C}
does not change if its generator or parity-check matrix is multiplied on the left by an invertible matrix.

Notation 1

\mathcal{C}({\mathbf{G}})
is the linear code that admits
{\mathbf{G}}
as a generator matrix, and
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}({\mathbf{H}})
is the code that admits
{\mathbf{H}}
as a parity-check matrix.

We say that a generator matrix
{\mathbf{G}}
is in
systematic form
if it is in the form
({\mathbf{I}}_{k},{\mathbf{R}})
.

Restricted syndrome decoding

There are several variations of Restricted Syndrome Decoding problems (
ResSD
) in the literature. Since we focus on the analysis of CROSS, we use the definition given in the specification, which we slightly generalize to include derived instances that we will analyze.

Problem 1
(
ResSD
)

Given
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
, find
\vec{e}\in E^{n}
such that
\vec{e}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}=\vec{s}
, where
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a predetermined subset of size
z
.

In the CROSS specification
[
5
]
and in
[
14
]
, the set
E
is chosen to be a multiplicative subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
q=p
is a prime. We provide the general definition as in
[
8
]
, since we will also consider “truncated” instances where
E
is a subset of the original multiplicative subgroup. We also focus on the full-weight instance, although
[
15
,
8
]
further considered a case where the number of coordinates
e_{i}=0
is fixed.

The following two statements show basic manipulation methods for
ResSD
instances and are a formalization of the “Shifting E” paragraph from
[
5
]
.

Proposition 1
(Affine Shifting of
E
)

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with solution’s entries in a set
E=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{z}\}
. We denote by
{\mathbf{u}}=(1,\dots,1)
the vector of length
n-k
. For any
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
a\neq 0
, define a new instance
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}

of
ResSD
with solution’s entries in the set
E^{\prime}
, where
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}=a^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}
,
{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{s}}+ba^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}
,
E^{\prime}=aE+b\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
If there exists an algorithm solving
\left({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\right)
over
E^{\prime}
in time
T
with success probability
\rho
, then there exists an algorithm solving
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})
over
E
in time
T+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
with success probability
\rho
.

Proof

Computing
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})
can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. Let us then suppose that we know
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}\in E^{\prime n}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}{{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}}^{{\intercal}}={\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\ {\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}(a^{-1}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}})={\mathbf{s}}+ba^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad a^{-1}({\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}-b{\mathbf{u}}){{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}={\mathbf{s}}

We also remark
{\mathbf{e}}\triangleq a^{-1}({\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}-b{\mathbf{u}})
has entries in

a^{-1}E^{\prime}-a^{-1}b=a^{-1}(aE+b)-a^{-1}b=E

So
{\mathbf{e}}
that can be computed in linear time is a solution to our problem.∎

Proposition 2
(Multiplicative Randomization)

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with
restriction set

E=\left\{{\omega^{i}\mid 0\leq i<z}\right\}
for a primitive
z^{\text{th}}
root of unity
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then, one can sample in polynomial time a new instance
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
of
ResSD
over the same restriction set
E
, such that the sets of solutions to the two instances are in bijection computable in
\mathcal{O}(n)
and the image of any solution to
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})
is uniformly distributed over
E^{n}
.

Proof

Sample a vector
{\mathbf{c}}
uniformly at random from
E^{n}
and let
{\mathbf{C}}
be the diagonal matrix constructed from
{\mathbf{c}}
. Observe that a solution
{\mathbf{e}}
to the initial problem satisfies

{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\mathsf{T}},

where
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
, and
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{C}}
has every coordinate multiplied by a random element from
E
. Observing that because
\omega
is a primitive root, for any
e_{0}\in E
the mapping

e\mapsto e_{0}e
is a bijection from
E
to itself, this concludes the proof.∎

The following method is inspired by hybrid methods in regular syndrome decoding (see below and in
Section 3
). It further exploits the multiplicative subgroup structure to provide stronger attack guaranties.

Proposition 3
(Multiplicative Truncation)

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with an existing solution
{\mathbf{x}}
whose entries lie in
E=\left\{{\omega^{i}\mid 0\leq i<z}\right\}
for a primitive
z^{\text{th}}
root of unity
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then, for any integer
z^{\prime}
with
1\leq z^{\prime}\leq z
, one can efficiently sample a
ResSD
instance
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
such that, with probability at least
\rho
(over sampling), the entries of the solution will lie in
E^{\prime}=\left\{{\omega^{i}\mid 0\leq i<z^{\prime}}\right\}
, where
\rho=\left({z^{\prime}/z}\right)^{n}
,
and a solution to
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}})
in
E^{\prime n}
can be efficiently converted into a solution to
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})
in
E
.

Proof

Let
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}})
be sampled using multiplicative randomization (
2
) and let
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
be the image of
{\mathbf{x}}
under the bijection from the statement.
Clearly, every coordinate of
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
lands in
E^{\prime}
with probability
z^{\prime}/z
, independently of other coordinates. Therefore,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
belongs to
E^{\prime n}
with probability
\rho
. Recovering
{\mathbf{x}}
from any solution
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
amounts to
n
multiplications (by the diagonal of
{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}
); the result clearly belongs to
E^{n}
and satisfies the parity check equation.∎

Remark 1

This proposition is heavily based on the fact that
E
is a multiplicative subgroup. For general
E
, it is possible to apply a variant of the truncation similar to hybrid attacks on the regular syndrome decoding problem described in
[
18
,
52
]
. Concretely, we do not modify the parity check matrix and simply guess
z^{\prime}
out of
z
possible values of each error coordinate separately. This has the same probability
1-\binom{z-1}{z^{\prime}}/\binom{z}{z^{\prime}}=z^{\prime}/z
per coordinate. However, the key difference from the multiplicative truncation is that one cannot force a particular subset of
E
, and every coordinate will have its own randomly chosen subset.

Finally, we also describe a basic memoryless attack that will be used for comparison with other low-memory methods. Note that the CROSS security analysis document
[
4
]
only describes attacks with the best time complexity, which have impractical memory complexities.

Proposition 4

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with an existing solution whose entries lie in a set
E
with
|E|=z
. Then, it can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}z^{k})
and negligible memory.

Proof

Write
{\mathbf{H}}=({\mathbf{I}}_{n-k},{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime})
so that for
{\mathbf{e}}=({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}^{T}={\mathbf{x}}^{T}+{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{y}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}^{T}
. Enumerate
z^{k}
candidates for
{\mathbf{y}}
and verify that
{\mathbf{x}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}^{T}-{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{y}}^{T}
belongs to
E^{n-k}
(which can be done in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
field operations per guess).
∎

Regular syndrome decoding

We recall the Regular Syndrome Decoding problem (
RegSD
).

Definition 2

For
n=zt
, a vector
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of Hamming weight
t
is
regular
if it can be written
{\mathbf{e}}=({\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{t})
where each
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{z}
has Hamming weight 1.

Problem 2
(
RegSD
)

Given
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and

\vec{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
,
n=zt
, find a regular vector
\vec{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of Hamming weight
t
such that
\vec{e}{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\intercal}}=\vec{s}
.

Definition 3

In this work, we will often consider regular vectors over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
having only one value on their nonzero entries. We are going to call such vectors
light-regular vectors
and denote by
L_{t}^{n}
the set of those of Hamming weight
t
.

2.2
Lattices

An
n
-dimensional ball of radius
R
, denoted
\mathcal{B}_{n,R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consists of all vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
having the Euclidean norm at most
R
. Its volume is

\Vol{\mathcal{B}_{n,R}}\triangleq\frac{R^{n}\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}=R^{n}\cdot\Vol{\mathcal{B}_{n,1}}.

Definition 4
(Lattice)

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
. The
k
-dimensional
lattice
generated by
{\mathbf{A}}
is
\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{A}})\triangleq\left\{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{A}}\ |\ {\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\right\}
. The length of its shortest nonzero vector (in terms of the Euclidean norm) is denoted
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{A}}))
.
The
volume
of
{\mathbf{A}}

We now describe fundamental lattice-related problems.
The following standard heuristic is used to approximate the number of lattice vectors of norm below a given radius
r

Heuristic 1
(Gaussian Heuristic (GH))

For a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the number of lattice points inside a ball
\mathcal{B}_{n,R}
is approximately

|\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{B}_{n,R}|\approx\frac{\Vol{\mathcal{B}_{n,R}}}{\Vol{\mathcal{L}}}.

In particular,
under GH, the shortest vector is approximated as

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})~\approx~\left({\Vol{\mathcal{B}_{n,1}}}\right)^{-1/n}\left({\Vol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)^{1/n}~\approx~\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi e}}\left({\Vol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)^{1/n}.

Below, we describe standard lattice problems and their list-decoding variants and summarize the best solving complexities based on standard heuristics. All mentioned algorithms are probabilistic.

Bi-Regular Permutations

We should first consider the conditions for our matrix
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}
to be set in systematic form. Let
{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{zn\times zn}
be a permutation matrix and we denote
\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1},\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{2}\right)\triangleq\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{P}}
, where
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}
is a square matrix of size
2n-k
.
{\mathbf{P}}
must be selected in order to have
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}
invertible. So, because of the
n
first rows of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}
, there must be at least one column of each block in
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}
, or there will be empty rows. Moreover, there should not be three columns or more from the same block in
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}
, or there will be a linear dependency between columns. Indeed, let us consider
z^{\prime}\leq z
columns of the same block. By construction of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}
there is a linear dependency between them if and only if there exists a nonzero vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{z^{\prime}}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{z^{\prime}}x_{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{z^{\prime}}r_{i}x_{i}=0

where
r_{i}
are some elements of
E
(which are all distinct). This system admits a nonzero vectorial solution if and only if
z^{\prime}\geq 3
.

That is why we introduce the notion of bi-regular permutations which is inspired by regular permutations introduced in
[
26
]
. It allows us to have exactly one or two columns of each block in
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}
.

Definition 6

Let
{\mathbf{e}}=({\mathbf{e}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{z})^{n}
. For a permutation matrix
{\mathbf{P}}
let

{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{P}}=({\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n}^{\prime},{\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n}^{\prime\prime},{\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime})

with
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime}
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime\prime}
are vectors with one entry, and
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{z-2}
.
{\mathbf{P}}
is
bi-regular
if each
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime}
,
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime\prime}
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}
are formed only by coordinates from
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
.

Since columns from different blocks cannot have linear dependencies (because of the distinct rows of ones in the
n
first rows), we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 5

Let
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(2n-k)\times zn}
defined as in
5
from some
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
. For
{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{zn\times zn}
a permutation matrix, if
{\mathbf{P}}
is bi-regular then
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{P}}
can be put in systematic form by doing linear operations on its rows.

Permutation-Based ISD

This algorithm from
[
26

was a direct adaptation of the original Prange algorithm
[
43
]
to the regular context. Although we are in a non-binary context, we can almost directly take the version of
[
26

. Given an instance
\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{s}}}\right)
(defined as in
5
), the algorithm randomly chooses a bi-regular permutation matrix
{\mathbf{P}}
for computing
\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1},\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{2}\right)\triangleq\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{P}}
, where
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(2n-k)\times(2n-k)}
and
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(2n-k)\times((z-2)n+k)}
. Then it computes
{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\triangleq\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}^{-1}_{1}\widetilde{{\mathbf{s}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n-k}
and checks whether
\left|{{\mathbf{e}}_{1}}\right|_{\textsf{H}}=n
. If this is the case, it returns the solution
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}={\mathbf{P}}^{-1}({\mathbf{e}}_{1},{\mathbf{0}})
. If not, the algorithm starts the process again.

Remark 3

If the vector
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
has Hamming weight
n
, it will also be light-regular since the linear constraints of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}
impose the sum on each of the
n
blocks to be equal to 1.

Remark 4

In the notation of
6

({\mathbf{e}}_{n-k+1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n}^{\prime\prime})
and
({\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime},\dots,{\mathbf{e}}_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime})
to be equal to
{\mathbf{0}}
.

Assuming that the original solution is unique (as expected with CROSS parameters), we can see that the probability for choosing a right bi-regular permutation is exactly the probability of guessing:

•

for
n-k
blocks, a set of two entries to which the nonzero value belongs.

•

for
k
blocks, the exact position of the nonzero value.

We obtain a probability of success that is

\left(\dfrac{z-1}{\binom{z}{2}}\right)^{n-k}z^{-k}=2^{-k}\left(\dfrac{2}{z}\right)^{n}

(1)

The total average number of iteration of our adapted Permutation-Based ISD being the inverse of the probability of success, the complexity of the algorithm is at least in
\mathcal{O}\left(2^{k}\left(\dfrac{z}{2}\right)^{n}\right)
.

P_{i}=\dfrac{\binom{k+\ell-n/2}{i}\binom{n-k-\ell}{n/2-p/2-i}}{\binom{n/2}{p/2}}\quad\text{and so}\quad\mathcal{P}_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k+\ell-n/2}P_{i}\ 2^{-i}

For the case where
k+\ell\leq n/2
, we have

\mathcal{P}=\sum_{i=0}^{k+\ell}P^{\prime}_{i}\ 2^{-i}\quad\text{where}\quad P^{\prime}_{i}=\dfrac{\binom{k+\ell}{i}\binom{n/2-k-\ell}{n/2-p/2-i}}{\binom{n/2}{p/2}}

We can now estimate the hole complexity by (when
k+\ell\geq n/2
):

\dfrac{\mathcal{T}_{0}}{\mathcal{P}_{0}\mathcal{P}_{1}\mathcal{P}_{2}}=\dfrac{n2^{n/2-p/2}\max\left(\binom{n/2}{p/2}(z-2)^{p/2},\binom{n/2}{p/2}^{2}(z-2)^{p}/q^{\ell}\right)}{\binom{n/2}{p/2}^{2}\left(\dfrac{z-2}{z}\right)^{p}\left(\dfrac{2}{z}\right)^{n-p}\sum_{i=0}^{k+\ell-n/2}\dfrac{\binom{k+\ell-n/2}{i}\binom{n-k-\ell}{n/2-p/2-i}}{\binom{n/2}{p/2}}2^{-i}}\\
=\dfrac{n2^{n/2-p/2}\max\left((z-2)^{p/2},\binom{n/2}{p/2}(z-2)^{p}/q^{\ell}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{z-2}{z}\right)^{p}\left(\dfrac{2}{z}\right)^{n-p}\sum_{i=0}^{k+\ell-n/2}\binom{k+\ell-n/2}{i}\binom{n-k-\ell}{n/2-p/2-i}2^{-i}}

Memory complexity is proportional to
\textsf{Card}\left(L_{1}\right)=\binom{n/2}{p/2}(z-2)^{p/2}
.

Conclusion

We have seen that adapting the ISD algorithms for our case does not give competitive attacks. The main reason is because the rate of the regular code is
\dfrac{(z-2)n+k}{zn}
, which tends to be high when
z
is not too small, as in CROSS where
z=7
. This means that one must guess a large number of variables while applying the adapted Prange algorithm or its refinements.
The complexity could probably be reduced by using other ISD variants but not enough to make it competitive.
The fact that it is more effective to apply the adapted ISD directly to the original
ResSD
problem as was done in the CROSS specification could possibly have been anticipated.
Indeed, we saw in
(
1
)
that our Prange’s average number of iterations is
2^{k}\left(\dfrac{z}{2}\right)^{n}
, which is strictly greater than
z^{k}
because
n>k
. And we can notice that if we “adapt” Prange’s algorithm to the original
ResSD
problem directly, we will have to “guess”
k
values that can take
z
values each (this is the attack of
4
), making the average number of iterations equal to
z^{k}
.

There are also algebraic (or hybrid) algorithms for solving
RegSD
as studied in
[
18
,
52
]
, in both binary and non-binary cases. However, although they are likely to provide better parameters (the algorithm described in
[
52
]
has the lowest complexities in many parameters regimes), it would also probably not be enough to have interesting complexities. Indeed, if we observe the gaps between their complexities and the ones of adapted ISD given in
[
52
, Tab.2]
for codes of approximately the same lengths as ours, the gain is up to 15 bits.

From now on, we will work in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with

\mathbb{F}_{p}

For every matrix

or vector
{\mathbf{v}}

(or

For any linear code

that contains

Definition 7

Remark 5

If

Then we can easily check the following proposition.

Proposition 6

Let

be a linear code. The mapping

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}\

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{u}})

forms a surjection from

onto

and

.

From the structure of the generator matrix

one can immediately deduce the volume of the lattice, equal to the determinant of the matrix.

Proposition 7

For any linear code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
of dimension

Reduction

For the reduction, we will firstly need the two following lemmas giving relations between our code and our lattice.

Lemma 2

Let
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
be such that
\left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}\leq\sqrt{n}
. Then
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\triangleq\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*})
belongs to
\widetilde{S}

Proof

Let us denote
\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}=\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{u}}^{*})
. We have
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{s}}}
because
\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}\right)
thanks to

. If we suppose that
\left|{\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}}\right|_{\textsf{H}}>n
, then we will obviously have
\left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}>\sqrt{n}
which is not possible. So
\left|{\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}}\right|_{\textsf{H}}\leq n
and by

, so
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\widetilde{S}
.∎

Lemma 3

Let
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\widetilde{S}
and we denote
{\mathbf{e}}^{*}\triangleq\mathbb{Z}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}})
. Then
\left|{{\mathbf{e}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{n}
and
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\triangleq{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{e}}^{*}
belongs to
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Proof

By
1
we know that
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in L^{zn}_{n}
, so
\left|{{\mathbf{e}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{n}
. Let us denote
\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}=\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{u}}^{*})

\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{{\intercal}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{s}}}-\widetilde{{\mathbf{s}}}={\mathbf{0}}

\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}\right)
and by
6

.∎

We now compute the distance of a solution of our
CVP
instance to the target.

Lemma 4

If we assume that
\widetilde{S}\neq\emptyset
, then we have

S^{*}=\left\{{{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\ |\ \left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{n}}\right\}

Proof

We assumed
\widetilde{S}\neq\emptyset
, so we can choose
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}_{1}\in\widetilde{S}

{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1}={\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{e}}^{*}_{1}

\widetilde{{\mathbf{u}}}_{1}\triangleq\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1})

3

\left|{{\mathbf{e}}^{*}_{1}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{n}
and

. So we have obtained that
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1}\in\left\{{{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\ |\ \left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{n}}\right\}
. This means by the definition of

that

S^{*}=\left\{{{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\ |\ \left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=R}\right\}\quad\text{for\ some}\quad 0\leq R\leq\sqrt{n}.

(2)

We now assume that

.
Let
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{2}\in S^{*}
. We denote
{\mathbf{e}}^{*}_{2}\triangleq{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{2}

\left|{{\mathbf{e}}^{*}_{2}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=R

Proposition 8

If we assume that
\widetilde{S}\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{Z}^{zn}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{F}_{p}^{zn}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\ \mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*})

\widetilde{S}

Proof

We first show that

distinct such that

\displaystyle\left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}\quad

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left|{{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}^{*}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}+p\left|{{\mathbf{k}}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\quad\quad

\displaystyle\geq\quad\quad\sqrt{n}\quad\quad+p\left|{{\mathbf{k}}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}

Conversely, we obviously have

Theorem 4.1

\left(\mathcal{L}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\right),{\mathbf{y}}^{*}\right)

Proof

Computing

. Let us then consider
\varphi
and
\psi
the mappings as defined in
1
and
8
respectively. We then have
\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}
which is a bijection between the solutions of both of our problems, and computing a preimages of this mapping can be done in linear time.∎

This reduction comes from the same ideas as those of

5
Lattice-based Modelings: Reduction of ResSD to List-CVP and List-SVP without expansion

We also propose another heuristic, but more direct and more compact lattice-based modeling of
ResSD
. The key idea is that the set
E
of allowed values can be modeled by a set of “small” values around a well-chosen center. Furthermore, the values can be scaled by a nonzero constant which increases the number of such embeddings. The effect is stronger when the set is small or has a certain structure. For this purpose, we will
use the multiplicative truncation (
3
) to reduce the size of the set
E
.

We start with the notion of
an affine diameter
in
Subsection 5.1
, which quantifies how close the values of the set
E
can be packed together under an affine substitution. In
Subsection 5.2
, we describe the general shape of the reduction with an arbitrary center
\mu
and a radius
R
. Then, in
Subsection 5.3
, we analyze the specialization of the reduction based on the affine diameter, which leads to a concrete deterministic reduction to
List-CVP
. In
Subsection 5.4
, we continue with a stronger but probabilistic reduction to
List-CVP
based on the standard deviation of the set
E
and the mean of
E
as the center. We show in
Subsection 5.5
that for an integral center the problem can be converted to
List-SVP
by manipulating the
ResSD
instance before the reduction. Remarkably, for some
ResSD
instances (low code rate and compact restriction set),
one can heuristically deduce reductions to pure
CVP
and
SVP
. Finally, in
Subsection 5.6
we conclude with applying the reductions to CROSS-R-SDP instances and verifying them experimentally.

5.1
Affine diameter

Definition 8

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
|E|=z>0
. We define the
affine diameter

D_{E}
of the set as

\min_{a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{p},a\neq 0}~\max_{x\in E}~\mathbb{Z}(ax+b).

(3)

Remark 8

For computational purposes, a small optimization is to use the equivalent expression

D_{E}=\min_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\{0\},x_{0}\in E}~\max_{x\in E}~\mathbb{Z}(ax-ax_{0}),

which reduces the number of
b
to be tested from
p
to
|E|
. To see that it is true, observe that, for a fixed
a
, the minimum in (
3
) is achieved when one of the elements of
E
is mapped to 0. Otherwise, one can subtract the smallest integer from
\mathbb{Z}(ax+b)
without wrapping modulo
p

a

It is easy to see that

is the smallest length of a continuous segment that contains all elements of

. We will show how to use a small affine diameter to convert

into a lattice-based problem. Before that, we will study the notion itself further.

The case of

in the definition. In the case of

.

5.2
Reduction to List-CVP

The following theorem shows the close relation between solution vectors to a (restricted) syndrome decoding problem and the lattice associated to the corresponding linear code (as defined in
Section 4
). One has to keep
1
(Affine Shifting) in mind, since it allows us to change the set
E
which can lead to different behavior of the Euclidean distances.

Theorem 5.1

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD

with solution entries in a set
E
.
Let positive
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exists an
n
-dimensional lattice
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of volume
p^{n-k}
and a vector
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, constructible in time polynomial in
n
, such that there exists an embedding
\varphi
of the solutions
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
,
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}
, into the lattice, such that

\left|{\varphi({\mathbf{e}})-{\mathbf{v}}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}}.

(4)

This embedding and its left inverse are computable in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof

We first construct
\mathcal{L},{\mathbf{y}}
and
\varphi
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the linear code that admits
{\mathbf{H}}
as a parity check matrix.
Let
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
be an arbitrary solution to
{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{H}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})~~\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n},

(5)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\vec{\mu}-\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{a}})~~\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(6)

\displaystyle\varphi:{\mathbf{e}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{e}})-\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{a}})~~\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},

(7)

where
\vec{\mu}=(\mu,\ldots,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We now prove the claims.
Clearly,
\mathcal{L}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
are constructible efficiently.
We now prove that
\varphi
produces a vector in
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
be a solution to the SD problem:
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}
.
Then,

\mathbb{F}_{p}(\varphi({\mathbf{e}})){\mathbf{H}}^{T}=({\mathbf{e}}-{\mathbf{a}}){\mathbf{H}}^{T}=0,

and so
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\varphi({\mathbf{e}}))
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
, which by
6
implies that
\varphi({\mathbf{e}})
belongs to
\mathcal{L}
.
Furthermore, we have

\varphi({\mathbf{e}})-{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{e}})-\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{a}})-\vec{\mu}+\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{a}})=\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{e}})-\vec{\mu}

which implies (
4
).
Finally, the map
\psi:{\mathbf{y}}\mapsto\mathbb{F}_{p}({\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{a}})
is a left inverse of
\varphi
and it is clear that both
\phi
and
\psi
can be computed in linear time.∎

As a corollary, we derive the generic reduction of
ResSD
to
List-CVP
, which is parametrized by the “restriction center”
\mu
which should be close to all elements of
E
, and the radius
R
bounding the distance from this center.

Corollary 2

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with solution entries in a set
E
.
Let positive
\mu,R\in\mathbb{R},\mu\geq 0,R>0
, and define

\rho=\Prob_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}(E)\\
1\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i}-\mu)^{2}\leq R^{2}}\right\}.

(8)

Let a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and a vector
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined as in
Theorem 5.1
.
Then, with probability
\rho
over solutions to the
ResSD
problem,
a solution to the
List-CVP
instance
(\mathcal{L},{\mathbf{v}})
with distance upper bound
R
and
N
output vectors
can be converted into a solution to the
ResSD
instance in time
\mathcal{O}(nN)
.

Proof

From
Theorem 5.1
it is clear that solutions
{\mathbf{e}}
satisfying the bound in (
8
) belong to the constructed
List-CVP
instance within radius
R
. Recovering the
ResSD
solution can be done in linear time per candidate vector, since it only requires addition of a constant vector and testing the restriction to
E
.
∎

Remark 9

Under the Gaussian Heuristic and using
7
, we note that when
R\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi e}}\Vol^{1/n}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi e}}p^{1-k/n}
, the number of vectors in the lattice satisfying the distance bound
R
is a small constant. In this case, one can heuristically reclassify the
List-CVP
formulation as a simple
CVP
. In contrast,
Theorem 4.1
is a non-heuristic precise reduction to
CVP
.

Remark 10

Note that the success probability is taken over the instances of the initial problem, and so is essentially a weak-key attack when
\rho<1
.
For a fixed
\mu
, by varying the distance upper bound
R
we can explore different trade-off points between the
List-CVP
complexity and the success probability. However, reaching a very high success probability would be extremely costly. Luckily, we have an alternative in the case where
E
is a multiplicative subgroup, as in CROSS.
The natural randomization method (
2
) allows the reduction to be performed multiple times, each time having a fresh success probability
\rho
. Here, one needs to note that the reduction probability in the theorem is over the possible error-vector solutions, and the randomization technique perfectly randomizes these vectors.

5.3
Analysis based on affine diameter

A possible choice for
\mu
and
R
is naturally based on the affine diameter. The following proposition describes a precise compact reduction based on this quantity.

Proposition 9

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with solution entries in a set
E
. Then, in time polynomial in
n
and linear in the output size, this problem can be reduced to a
List-CVP
problem over an
n
-dimensional lattice of volume
p^{n-k}
and distance
\sqrt{n}D_{E}/2
.

Proof

We first apply
1
to obtain an equivalent
ResSD
instance with
E^{\prime}=\left\{{0,\ldots,D_{E}}\right\}
and then apply
2
with
\mu=D_{E}/2
and
R=\sqrt{n}D_{E}/2
, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i}-\mu)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\frac{D_{E}}{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{nD_{E}^{2}}{4},

implies the success probability
\rho=1
exactly. The lattice from
2
(and
Theorem 5.1
) has volume
p^{n-k}
by
7
.
∎

An interesting question one may ask is for which
ResSD
parameters
n,k,p,D_{E}
the problem reduces to pure
CVP
(which would be solvable in heuristic time
2^{0.292n+o(n)}
by
Heuristic 3
)? The following analysis is under the GH.

The expected length of the shortest nonzero vector (or distance from a random point) is
\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi e}}\Vol^{1/n}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi e}}p^{\frac{n-k}{n}}
. When this quantity is larger than
R=\sqrt{n}D_{E}/2
, we can expect a unique solution coinciding with the image of the solution to the
ResSD
problem under the reduction. Therefore, we need

D_{E}<\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi e}}p^{1-k/n}\approx 0.48p^{1-k/n}.

This bound benefits from smaller
D_{E}
, larger
p
, and lower code rate.

Regarding the overall sparsity, a natural requirement is to expect roughly 1 random solution to the problem, which means
z^{n}\approx p^{n-k}
,
z=|E|
(see
[
5
]
), and thus the bound becomes
D_{E}<0.48z
.
Clearly, this is not possible for
z\geq 3
. Therefore, this reduction with
(\mu,R)
based on the affine diameter never reduces to pure
CVP
in interesting cases. However, the reduction may still be useful for two reasons. First, we can apply multiplicative truncation (
3
), which makes the problem sparser by eliminating some elements of
E
. The combination of truncation and reduction may thus be more powerful. Second, the
List-CVP
formulation can still lead to efficient attacks, since the enumeration complexity gradually increases from sieving
2^{0.292n+o(n)}
, with increasing
List-CVP
radius. In the next subsection, we will evaluate these options, but together with better choices of
\mu
and
R
.

5.4
Analysis based on the standard deviation of E

Although setting
\mu,R
in the reduction based on the affine diameter
D_{E}
is a natural option and leads to good theoretical bounds and deterministic success (
\rho=1
), stronger attacks may be achieved when
\rho<1
is allowed.

Another natural choice of
\mu
is the one that minimizes the expected value of the distance (where the expectation is over
{\mathbf{e}}\in E^{n}
)

\left|{\varphi({\mathbf{e}})-{\mathbf{v}}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}}.

Since it is not straightforward to compute,
we will first minimize the expected square of the distance instead.

Proposition 10

The expected value of the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}
, where each
e_{i}
is sampled independently and uniformly from a set
E
, is minimized at
\mu=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{r\in E}\mathbb{Z}(r)
.

Proof

By linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}}\right\}=n\mathbb{E}\left\{{\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{0})-\mu}\right)^{2}}\right\}=\frac{n}{|E|}\sum_{r\in E}\left({\mathbb{Z}(r)-\mu}\right)^{2},

where the second equation is due to
e_{0}
being sampled uniformly from
E
.
The latter expression is minimized at
\mu=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{r\in E}\mathbb{Z}(r)
.
∎

Setting
\mu
to the average value of
\mathbb{Z}(E)
means that the expected value of the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}
being minimized is equal to
n\sigma^{2}(E)
where
\sigma^{2}(\mathbb{Z}(E))
is the variance of a random variable sampled uniformly from
E
.

For computing the success probability of the reduction, it is more convenient to work with the median of the distance instead of the mean. This would give a good estimate on the
List-CVP
distance in the reduction with the success rate
\rho=50\%
. For this purpose, we will heuristically assume that the mean approximates well the median. Note that taking the median commutes with squaring, so that the median of the distance is equal to the square root of the median of the squared distance, which by the heuristic coincides with the mean of the squared distance, which is in turn minimized and calculated in
10
. We will also evaluate the quality of this heuristic experimentally.

Heuristic 5

The median value
M_{E,n}
of the distance
d=\left|{\varphi({\mathbf{e}})-{\mathbf{v}}}\right|_{\textsf{E}}
(using
\mu=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{r\in E}\mathbb{Z}(r)
) is well approximated by the square root of the mean of
d^{2}
:

M_{E,n}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{|E|}\cdot\sum_{r\in E}^{z}{(\mathbb{Z}(r)-\mu)^{2}}}.

5.5
Reduction to List-SVP

In this subsection, we describe a trick that allows one to convert the problem into
List-SVP
/
SVP
using the fact that the problem originates from linear codes, where the corresponding decoding and low-weigh codeword problems are more closely related.

Proposition 11

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD

with solution entries in a set
E
. Let
\mu
be a positive
integer
. Then, there exists an
n
-dimensional lattice
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of volume
p^{n-k-1}
, constructible in time polynomial in
n
, such that there exists an embedding
\varphi
of the solutions
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
,
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}
, into the lattice, such that

\left|{\varphi({\mathbf{e}})}\right|_{\textsf{E}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\mathbb{Z}(e_{i})-\mu}\right)^{2}}.

(9)

This embedding and its left inverse are computable in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof

First, we apply
1
with
a=1
and
b=-\mu
to obtain a new
ResSD
problem
({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})
with restriction
E^{\prime}=\{r-\mu\mid r\in E\}
.
Second, choose an arbitrary invertible matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
such that

{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime T}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}={\mathbf{s}}^{\prime\prime T}.

Let
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime}={\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
. Clearly, the sets of solutions of
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime T}={\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}
and of
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime T}={\mathbf{s}}^{\prime\prime}
are identical. Now set
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k-1)\times n}
to
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime}
with the first row removed, which only increases the set of solutions in the new
ResSD
instance
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
(by an expected factor of
p
).

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the linear code that admits
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime\prime}
as a parity check matrix.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})~~\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n},

(10)

\displaystyle\varphi:{\mathbf{e}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbb{Z}({\mathbf{e}})-\vec{\mu}~~\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},

(11)

where
\vec{\mu}=(\mu,\ldots,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{L}
is constructible efficiently.
Let
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
be a solution to the original SD problem:
{\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{H}}^{T}={\mathbf{s}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{F}_{p}(\varphi({\mathbf{e}}))^{T}={\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\mathbb{F}_{p}(\varphi({\mathbf{e}}))^{T}={\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}({\mathbf{e}}-\vec{\mu})={\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime T}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T},

and so
\mathbb{F}_{p}(\varphi({\mathbf{e}}))
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
, which by
6
implies that
\varphi({\mathbf{e}})
belongs to
\mathcal{L}
. The norm equation (
9
) follows directly from the definition of
\varphi
. It is evident that
\phi
has left inverse and both are computable in linear time.
∎

This directly implies the
List-SVP
reduction, with proof as in
2
.

Corollary 3

Let
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(n-k)\times n}\times\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n-k}
be an instance of
ResSD
with the solution’s entries in a set
E
.
Let a nonnegative integer
\mu
and a positive real
R
and define

\rho=\Prob_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}(E)\\
1\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i}-\mu)^{2}\leq R^{2}}\right\}.

Let a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be defined as in
11
.
Then, with probability
\rho
over solutions to the
ResSD
problem,
a solution to
List-SVP
over
\mathcal{L}
with distance upper bound
R
and
N
output vectors
can be converted into a solution to the
RegSD
instance in time
\mathcal{O}(N)
.

This reduction is of theoretical and practical interest.
For parameters implying
R<\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, under GH, the constructed
List-SVP
problem degenerates to a pure
SVP
problem, which is an interesting result for a code-based problem.
The reduction provides an alternative to the Kannan embedding
[
36
]
, which is the most used technique for converting a
CVP
instance into an
SVP
instance.
SVP
algorithms are often more performant than
CVP
in practice. However, in our analysis below, we only consider strongest generic methods (
Heuristic 2
and
Heuristic 3
) which have the same complexity for
SVP
and
CVP
(perhaps having a small difference hidden in the omitted polynomial factors).

and running sieve and enumeration algorithms. The complexities are adapted to achieve success rate of 90%. Up to the correction, total time is equal to the product of the inverse of the truncation probability and the sum of sieve and enumeration costs.

The first step in the attack is to truncate the instance from
z=7
to
z^{\prime}\leq z
. According to
3
, this has a probability of success
(z^{\prime}/z)^{n}
, which means that
(z/z^{\prime})^{n}
attempts are needed on average. We increase this number of attempts by a factor of four to reach the success probability
1-e^{-4}\approx 98\%
. Here, we use the Poisson distribution as the limit of the binomial distribution. This defines the first factor in the final time complexity.

The second step consists in applying the
List-CVP
reduction from
2
with the distance upper bound
R
being the median of the distribution, approximated using
Heuristic 5
. Since the median only ensures the success rate of
50\%
, we repeat the entire process four times, each time using multiplicative randomization (
2
), increasing the success rate to
15/16\approx 94\%
.

The third step is to solve the resulting
List-CVP
instance by enumeration, using the complexity estimate from
Heuristic 4
.
The latter always includes the sieving cost, which actually dominates in instances where the target solution is expected to be unique. Due to the pruning used in
Heuristic 4
, we repeat the process four times to reach
98\%
success rate of the step. The total success rate is
90\%
. This step also dominates memory complexity which by
Heuristic 3
is
2^{0.208n+o(n)}
.

We remark that the analysis potentially omits polynomial complexity factors in sieving (which is relevant for instances degraded to
CVP
) and in enumeration (which is relevant for
List-CVP
cases). This is in part due to the lack of a precise analysis of the sieving complexity. We believe that this leads to an acceptable accuracy for security analysis purposes, and this simplification is in line with the common literature.

From the results, we can draw several conclusions. First, the best attacks are always obtained from the truncation to
z^{\prime}=3
, which coincidentally is the largest
z^{\prime}
for which
List-CVP
reduces to
CVP
. Second, the attacks do not threaten the CROSS parameters even with potentially improved sieving and enumeration algorithms (note that, in current analysis, enumeration always incurs additional overhead in comparison to the expected number of vectors in the output). Indeed, for
z^{\prime}\leq 3
, the probability of successful truncation is always less than
2^{-n}
. For
z^{\prime}\geq 4
, the ratio of the expected number of vectors in the considered ball
\mathcal{B}_{n,R}
to the probability of successful truncation is always larger than
2^{n}
.

We performed experiments to verify the correctness of the reduction. The setup consists of a standard laptop with an Intel i7 CPU @ 3 GHz and 32 GB RAM. The software used is the SageMath computer algebra
[

]

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown and studied reductions of the recent Restricted Syndrome Decoding problem to the more traditional Regular Syndrome Decoding problem with the particularity of light-regular errors, as well as to the lattice-based search (
SVP
,
CVP
) and enumeration problems (
List-SVP
,
List-CVP
).
The reductions range from exact, deterministic and relatively large output instances (dimension
zn
), to heuristic, probabilistic and compact instances (dimension
n
).
We believe that our results shed new light on the
ResSD
problem and open new research directions, such as finding more connections between the paradigms, finding new reductions, advancing analysis of the resulting instances.
For the CROSS signature, we obtained some new time-memory trade-off points, although our current reductions and attacks do not threaten its security.
