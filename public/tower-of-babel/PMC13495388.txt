Successful one-stage thoracoscopic repair of esophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula and associated foregut duplication cyst in a neonate: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Xiaoxia Wu, Bingliang Li, Hongxia Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula (EA-TEF) complicated by foregut duplication (FD) cyst is an exceedingly rare congenital anomaly. Since the first description in 1956, only 20 cases have been reported, with open thoracotomy representing the standard surgical approach. We present a case of successful one-stage thoracoscopic repair of this complex association in a neonate.Case presentationA male neonate weighing 2080 g was diagnosed with Gross type C EA-TEF. At 3 days of age, right thoracoscopic exploration identified a 1-cm FD cyst on the distal esophageal wall. Complete cyst enucleation with mucosal preservation and muscle layer reconstruction was performed, followed by fistula ligation and primary esophageal anastomosis. Histopathology confirmed FD cyst with stratified squamous, columnar, and simple flat epithelium. The postoperative course was complicated by pneumonia, suspected anastomotic leak, and anastomotic stricture requiring three dilations. At 10-month follow-up, the infant demonstrated catch-up growth (weight 8 kg, 10th percentile; height 72 cm, 25th percentile) with no recurrence of fistula or cyst.ConclusionsOne-stage thoracoscopic repair of EA-TEF with concurrent FD cyst enucleation appears technically feasible in selected neonates. The magnified visualization afforded by thoracoscopy may, in some cases, facilitate intraoperative identification of FD. However, this approach does not eliminate anastomotic complications inherent to EA surgery. Patient selection, surgical expertise, and long-term functional outcomes require further investigation.

Background

Esophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula (EA-TEF) affects approximately 2.4 per 100,000 births and requires prompt surgical intervention [1]. Its association with foregut duplication (FD) cysts—congenital malformations arising from aberrant foregut budding during the third to sixth week of gestation—is exceptionally rare, with only 20 cases documented since 1956 [2–19].

Recent literature suggests that foregut duplication cysts, although rare, are increasingly identified owing to advances in imaging, yet preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, particularly in neonates with associated anomalies [20]. Although open thoracotomy has been the standard approach for EA-TEF with FD since the 1960s, minimally invasive techniques have increasingly been adopted in pediatric surgery, with potential advantages in visualization and reduced surgical trauma. To our knowledge, no case of simultaneous thoracoscopic repair of EA-TEF and FD cyst in neonates has previously been reported. Herein, we describe this case and discuss its clinical implications.

Literature review methodology

We conducted a structured literature search to identify all reported cases of EA-TEF associated with FD. The search was performed in May 2026, across three databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase. The search strategy combined the following keywords and MeSH terms: “esophageal atresia”, “racheoesophageal fistula”, “foregut duplication”, “esophageal duplication”, “foregut cyst”, “duplication cyst”, and “thoracoscopy”” or “thoracotomy”. No language restrictions were applied, and the search covered publications from 1956 to 2026. Two authors (X.W. and B.L.) independently screened titles and abstracts, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Inclusion criteria were foregut duplication associated with EA-TEF confirmed by surgery, histology or autopsy. Exclusion criteria were isolated foregut duplication without EA, and review articles without original cases.

Case presentation

A male newborn weighing 2080 g was delivered via cesarean section at 38 weeks of gestation and was transferred to our hospital on the second day of life due to suspected EA following the failure to pass a nasogastric tube. A plain chest radiograph and esophagography confirmed the diagnosis of EA-TEF. Preoperative chest CT was performed to assess the gap between the two esophageal pouches, no cystic lesion was identified. Preoperative echocardiography identified a small atrial septal defect, renal ultrasonography was normal.

At 3 days of age, right thoracoscopic exploration was performed under general anesthesia with single-lung ventilation. A 5-mm camera port was placed at the fifth intercostal space along the inferior angle of the right scapula. Two 3-mm working ports were positioned at the fourth and sixth intercostal spaces along the mid-axillary line, respectively. The gap between the upper esophageal pouch and the tracheoesophageal fistula measured approximately 2 cm. A well-circumscribed, 1-cm cystic lesion embedded in the muscular wall of the distal esophagus, 1 cm distal to the fistula (Fig. 1A). Segmental resection would have generated considerable anastomotic tension, therefore, given the clear cyst-tissue demarcation, we attempted enucleation at the cyst-esophageal wall interface. The cyst did not communicate with the esophageal lumen, and its intact capsule was readily dissected from the mucosa. Following complete separation, the mucosa remained intact and the adjacent wall appeared viable, with no ischemic changes. We therefore proceeded with enucleation, meticulously reconstructing the muscular layer with interrupted 5 − 0 absorbable sutures (Fig. 1B). The tracheoesophageal fistula was ligated with 5 − 0 absorbable suture and divided. A moderate-tension, single-layer end-to-end esophageal anastomosis was performed using interrupted 5 − 0 absorbable sutures (Fig. 1C), and the overall anatomy is depicted in the line diagram (Fig. 2).

Esophageal stricture was diagnosed at 2 months postoperatively, necessitating three balloon dilations at 2, 4, and 6 months. At 8-month follow-up, esophagoscopy and contrast study demonstrated patent anastomosis without fistula recurrence or cyst regrowth. At 10 months, the infant weighed 8 kg (10th percentile for age) with height 72 cm (25th percentile), demonstrating gradual catch-up growth. Weight-for-age Z-score was − 2 to -1. The patient remains under active surveillance.

Discussion and conclusions

Esophageal atresia (EA) and foregut duplication (FD) share a common embryological origin, both arising from aberrant development of the primitive foregut. During the third to sixth weeks of gestation, the foregut divides into ventral (respiratory) and dorsal (esophageal) segments. Experimental evidence suggests that failure of the foregut to detach from the notochord at the appropriate time may underlie the coexistence of these malformations [21].

Based on our structured literature search, a total of 21 cases of EA-TEF with FD, including the present case, have been documented since Wiersema’s initial description in 1956 (Table 1) [2–19]. Among these, Gross type C was the most common subtype (n = 15, 71.4%), followed by type A (n = 4, 19.0%) and type B (n = 2, 9.5%). No cases of Gross type D or E have been reported in association with FD [2–19]. Among these cases, 13 exhibited cystic duplication and 8 exhibited tubular duplication. Of the cystic cases, 7 were localized to the esophageal wall (thoracic/abdominal), while the remaining 6 were located in the mediastinum or stomach. Age at diagnosis ranged from 3 days to 7 years, however, given heterogeneous reporting, with some early cases documented only as “at primary repair” or “at autopsy”, calculating a reliable median age is not feasible. Histopathologically, duplication walls typically contained gastrointestinal and respiratory epithelium, with ectopic gastric mucosa and pancreatic tissue identified in several cases.

Reported cases of EA associated with FD

The clinical spectrum of EA-FD association ranges from asymptomatic lesions detected incidentally during imaging or surgery to compressive symptoms (dyspnea, dysphagia) caused by mass effect on the trachea or esophagus, or even malignant transformation [5, 11, 22, 23]. Balakrishnan et al. documented that approximately 80% of children with isolated foregut duplication become symptomatic within the first two years of life [24]. The preoperative detection rate of FD in EA patients remains disconcertingly low. The patients required an average of 2.8 surgical procedures to achieve definitive treatment [16]. This diagnostic delay may be partly attributable to the restricted visualization of the distal esophagus beyond the immediate operative field in thoracotomy.

Our case represents an evolution in surgical management: whereas four prior cases achieving one-stage open repair for cystic duplication were constrained to segmental esophageal resection—Narasimharao and Santamaria resected the esophageal segment containing the cyst, and Knod performed “short segmental resection secondary to ischemia of the adjacent esophageal wall” —our thoracoscopic technique permitted complete cyst enucleation with intact mucosal preservation and muscle layer reconstruction [8, 10, 16, 17]. More recently, Shchapov reported one-stage thoracoscopic repair of EA with a tubular duplication in a neonate, and Kroczek and Lipa described thoracoscopic management of a mediastinal foregut duplication cyst in long-gap type A EA following delayed detection at 12 months [18, 19]. Both prior reports involved tubular or mediastinal cystic duplications; our case is unique in representing one-stage thoracoscopic enucleation of an intramural cystic foregut duplication concurrent with primary EA-TEF repair. Collectively, these experiences demonstrate that thoracoscopic management of EA-associated foregut duplications, whether cystic or tubular detected at primary repair or delayed, is technically achievable, though patient selection and anatomical factors profoundly influence outcomes.

For cystic FD in the distal esophagus, simultaneous correction during initial EA repair is feasible and advisable, reducing the necessity for staged procedures. Surgical strategy should be individualized based on anastomotic tension and vascular supply. Options include enucleation with muscular preservation (as in our case) or segmental resection when the cyst wall is inseparable from the esophageal mucosa or when the adjacent wall is ischemic [8, 11, 16].

It is noteworthy that while thoracoscopy may enhance FD detection through magnified visualization, this potential advantage is based on theoretical considerations and our single-case experience rather than comparative data. Furthermore, thoracoscopy does not eliminate the postoperative morbidity inherent to EA repair in vulnerable neonates. Our 28-day hospitalization exceeded the 10-day discharge reported by Narasimharao and the uneventful courses described by Spataru [8, 17]. The weight-for-age Z-score of -2 to -1 at 10-month follow-up, while indicating catch-up growth, suggests that nutritional recovery in low-birth-weight infants remains challenging irrespective of operative technique.

Several risks specific to thoracoscopy in low-birth-weight neonates warrant consideration. These include limited working space, reduced tolerance to pneumothorax and hypercapnia, and the steep learning curve associated with thoracoscopic EA repair [25–28]. Our case demonstrates feasibility in a 2080-g infant, but we caution that such approaches should be undertaken only by surgeons with substantial experience in both thoracoscopic EA repair and neonatal minimally invasive surgery.

Our experience yields three principal conclusions. First, thoracoscopy may enhance intraoperative awareness of FD, underscoring the importance of maintaining heightened vigilance for this coexisting anomaly during EA repair. Second, simultaneous thoracoscopic FD resection and EA-TEF repair demonstrates the technical feasibility of minimally invasive management for this complex association in selected patients. Third, patient selection requires individualization; although low birth weight does not preclude thoracoscopy, anastomotic technique quality remains the primary determinant of outcomes.

This single-case report cannot establish superiority of thoracoscopy over thoracotomy. The rarity of EA-TEF with FD precludes randomized comparison; meaningful conclusions will require international registry collaboration. Furthermore, 10-month follow-up, while demonstrating structural integrity, remains insufficient to assess long-term esophageal motility, stricture recurrence, or gastroesophageal reflux disease. These represent significant limitations that should guide interpretation of our findings.

In conclusion, one-stage thoracoscopic repair of EA-TEF with concurrent FD cyst enucleation appears technically feasible in selected neonates when performed by experienced surgeons. The procedure may offer advantages in FD detection and esophageal preservation compared with historical open series, though postoperative complications align with inherent risks of EA surgery in low-birth-weight infants. Surgeons should maintain heightened awareness of this rare association, with thoracoscopic exploration considered when expertise and patient factors permit. Further multi-center collaboration is needed to define optimal patient selection criteria and long-term functional outcomes.
