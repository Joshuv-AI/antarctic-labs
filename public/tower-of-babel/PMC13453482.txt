The impact of immune checkpoint inhibitors on infection in patients with solid malignancies: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Chunlan Wu, Luying Wan, Qing Wu, Ping Li, Jingyi Li, Yifan Xu, Lina Zhu, Xianhe Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) revolutionised cancer treatment but may alter infection risk. The effect of ICIs on infection susceptibility remains unclear.MethodsWe searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and EMBASE from database inception to October 10, 2025 for randomised controlled trials (RCTs). The primary endpoint was the risk ratio (RR) of all-grade infections; the secondary endpoint was the RR of severe (Grade 3–5) infections. Stratified analyses by ICI agent, regimen, treatment timing, infection site, and cancer type were performed separately for treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger's test, and trim-and-fill; heterogeneity was evaluated by I2 statistic. This study is registered with PROSPERO, number CRD420251231682.FindingsA total of 141 RCTs (87,484 participants) were included. In TEAE-based analyses, ICIs were associated with increased risks of any-grade infections [RR = 1.25; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.12–1.39; I2 = 71.1%] and severe infections (RR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.17–1.44), driven by symmetric comparisons (ICI vs. Placebo and ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy). For TRAEs, no significant increase was seen for any-grade infections (RR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.84–1.22; I2 = 67.3%) or severe infections (RR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.81–1.14). However, symmetric comparisons again showed increased TRAE risks for ICI vs. placebo and ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy. Publication bias was suggested for any-grade infectious TRAEs (Egger's P = 0.015); after trim-and-fill correction, the pooled RR was strengthened (RR = 1.24).InterpretationThe relationship between ICI therapy and infection risk is nuanced and context-dependent. In symmetric comparisons, ICIs are associated with modestly increased TEAE- and TRAE-based infection risks. This risk should be weighed against the expected survival benefit, underscoring the need for individualised assessment. Future prospective studies with time-to-event analyses and standardised infection surveillance are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between ICI exposure and infection risk.FundingThere was no funding source for this study.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, Infection, Adverse events, Meta-analysis, Solid tumours

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Clinical observations suggest a heightened risk of infections among patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), yet individual trials have yielded conflicting results and previous meta-analyses were limited by small sample sizes. Consequently, the overall infection risk profile—including effects by ICI type, regimen, and timing—remains poorly characterised across diverse solid tumours and treatment settings. To inform this background, we searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and EMBASE from inception to October 10, 2025 for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of ICI regimens versus non-ICI controls with infection outcomes in solid tumours, using terms related to ICIs and specific drug names without language restriction, and identified 141 eligible studies (mostly of moderate quality with some concerns regarding blinding in open-label designs, reflecting the pre-existing evidence base).

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the largest and most comprehensive meta-analysis to date evaluating infection risk associated with ICI therapy across diverse solid tumours and treatment contexts, comprising 141 RCTs and 87,484 participants. Our analysis demonstrates that the relationship between ICI therapy and infection risk is nuanced and context-dependent, varying substantially by comparator type and treatment regimen. In symmetric comparisons (ICI vs. placebo or ICI plus identical chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy alone), ICIs were associated with modestly increased overall (Treatment-Emergent Adverse Events, TEAE-based) and treatment-related (Treatment-Related Adverse Events, TRAE-based) infection risks. These findings clarify the modest but detectable association between ICIs and infection risk in unbiased comparisons and highlight the importance of distinguishing symmetric from asymmetric trial designs when interpreting safety data.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with existing evidence, our findings suggest that ICI therapy is associated with a modest increase in any-grade infectious complications in settings where confounding is minimised (symmetric comparisons). The absolute risk increase is small, and treatment decisions should balance this risk against the expected survival benefit of ICIs on an individualised basis. In higher-risk subgroups (e.g., those receiving perioperative ICI or ICI plus chemotherapy), proactive monitoring for infections warrants consideration. Importantly, the elevated risk observed in the perioperative setting likely reflects the trial designs (predominantly ICI vs. placebo or ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy) rather than a unique biological hazard of the perioperative period; nevertheless, enhanced vigilance in surgical patients is reasonable. Future research should move beyond crude event counts to person-time incidence rates to better quantify time-at-risk bias. Additionally, the biological mechanisms underlying ICI-associated infection risk—including the roles of corticosteroid use, compensatory immunosuppressive pathways, and immune reconstitution-like phenomena—warrant investigation. Finally, clinical or genomic biomarkers should be identified for risk stratification and targeted prevention.

Introduction

Cancer treatment has undergone a paradigm shift with the advent of immunotherapy, most notably through immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).1, 2, 3, 4 By blocking key inhibitory pathways, including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), ICIs potentiate anti-tumour immunity mediated by T cells, achieving durable clinical responses across a spectrum of malignancies.5, 6, 7

The broad immunomodulatory nature of ICIs, while known for inducing immune-related adverse events (irAEs), may also paradoxically heighten susceptibility to infections beyond the induction of autoimmunity. Indeed, clinical observations indicate a higher frequency of infections among patients receiving ICI therapy. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that PD-L1 inhibitors markedly increased the risk of pneumonia compared to chemotherapy or placebo.8 Furthermore, a comprehensive review of registration studies found that respiratory tract infections were the most frequently reported infectious events among ICI-treated patients, followed by urinary tract infections.9 Real-world cohort studies have further quantified this risk, reporting that approximately 19% of patients with non-small cell lung cancer receiving nivolumab develop infections requiring antimicrobial therapy, with bacterial infections (particularly pneumonia) predominating, followed by viral (influenza, varicella-zoster virus) and fungal (Aspergillus, Candida) infections.10 Beyond these common pathogens, the literature has documented a spectrum of opportunistic infections associated with ICI use, including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia,11 invasive aspergillosis,12 cytomegalovirus reactivation,13 and reactivation of chronic viral infections such as hepatitis B14 and herpes zoster.10 However, the evidence remains conflicting, as individual phase III trials have not consistently demonstrated increased infectious complications. In the GEMSTONE-302 trial of sugemalimab plus chemotherapy in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the incidence of any-grade infection was 13.43% in the immunotherapy group versus 13.21% in the chemotherapy group.15 Similarly, the KEYNOTE-122 trial of pembrolizumab in nasopharyngeal carcinoma reported any-grade infection in 20.69% of the ICI arm versus 25.89% of the chemotherapy arm.16 These discrepancies may reflect differences in tumour types, prior therapies, concomitant chemotherapy regimens, the specific ICI agent used (PD-1 vs. PD-L1 vs. CTLA-4 inhibitors), and the definition and ascertainment of infectious events across studies. Consequently, the impact of ICIs on infection risk remains inadequately examined, and conclusive evidence is scarce.

This study aims to systematically assess the impact of ICIs on infection risk. Elucidating this relationship is critical to optimising clinical management and improving patient safety during ICI therapy.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251231682).

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the association between ICI therapy and infection risk in patients with solid malignancies. We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that met the following criteria: (1) enrolled adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with histologically confirmed solid tumours. For hepatocellular carcinoma [HCC], a radiological diagnosis in the setting of cirrhosis, consistent with accepted guidelines,17,18 was also considered eligible; (2) compared any ICI-based regimen (monotherapy or combination) with a non-ICI control. For trials comparing ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone, we required that the chemotherapy regimens be identical in both arms to isolate the specific effect of ICIs on infection risk; and (3) reported the incidence of infectious adverse events graded by Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 3.0 or later. Studies were excluded for any of the following reasons: (1) non-randomised study design; (2) inappropriate control group (e.g., lacking a non-ICI comparator); (3) duplicate publication; (4) specific publication types (reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, and trial protocols); (5) lack of extractable infection outcome data; (6) full text not available in English; and (7) unpublished data or studies. Summary estimates were extracted directly from published reports. Sex of participants (male/female) was extracted from each included RCT as reported by the original investigators; no additional methods were used to determine sex, and gender identity was not reported in the primary studies. Race and ethnicity data were not extracted because the original trials did not consistently report these data; therefore, analyses by race or ethnicity could not be performed.

We conducted comprehensive literature searches in PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and EMBASE for relevant studies published from database inception to October 10, 2025 (the final search date). The search was performed in two stages: an initial search covering the period from inception to February 15, 2025, and a final update covering February 16, 2025 to October 10, 2025, to ensure inclusion of the most recent publications.

The search strategy combined comprehensive keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH) related to immune checkpoint inhibitors (“immune checkpoint inhibitors”, “PD-1”, “PD-L1”, “CTLA-4”, “ICI”, “immunotherapy”, “programmed cell death protein 1”, “programmed cell death protein ligand 1”, and “cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4”) and both international and investigational agent names (including pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, tremelimumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, camrelizumab, toripalimab, penpulimab, cemiplimab, adebrelimab, sugemalimab, avelumab, durvalumab, cadonilimab, and amivantamab) without language restriction, with standardised filters for randomised controlled trials. This electronic search was supplemented by manual screening of reference lists from retrieved articles. Trial registries were not searched but we did search for unpublished data, none of which were included. Further details regarding the systematic review and meta-analysis of studies, including the full search strategy, are provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

An independent duplicate screening of titles and abstracts was conducted by two reviewers (CW and LW), who also performed the subsequent full-text review and data extraction. All disagreements were initially resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Any persistent discrepancies were adjudicated by a third independent reviewer (QW) to reach a final decision.

Ethics

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published RCTs. All data were extracted from peer-reviewed publications and did not involve the collection of primary data from human participants. Therefore, ethics approval from an institutional review board was not required, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the study design (systematic review of published aggregate data).

Statistical analysis

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study using the revised Cochrane tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials (RoB2).19 Risk of bias was assessed across five specific domains according to the revised Cochrane tool (RoB2): (1) bias arising from the randomisation process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. Studies were rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk according to the tool's predefined criteria. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined as follows: low risk of bias if all domains were judged as low risk; some concerns if the study raised some concerns in at least one domain; and high risk of bias if the study was judged to be at high risk in at least one domain. All disagreements were initially resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Any persistent discrepancies were adjudicated by a third independent reviewer to reach a final decision.

The overall strength of evidence for the pooled outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.20,21 Evidence derived from the included RCTs (assessed for risk of bias with RoB2) was initially considered high quality and was then evaluated against standard GRADE criteria, including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias, and other relevant considerations. Evidence was downgraded by one or two levels based on the following criteria: (1) Risk of bias—downgraded by one level if more than 33.3% of the weight in a meta-analysis came from studies at moderate or high risk of bias, or by two levels if more than 33.3% of the weight came from studies at high risk of bias; (2) Indirectness—downgraded by one level if more than 33.3% of the weight came from partially indirect studies, or by two levels if more than 33.3% of the weight came from indirect studies; (3) Inconsistency—downgraded by one level if, despite a consistent direction of effect across studies, significant unexplained heterogeneity was present (I2 > 50%), or by two levels if the direction of effect was inconsistent and heterogeneity was significant (I2 > 50%); (4) Imprecision—downgraded by one level if the 95% confidence interval for the effect size crossed one of the minimal clinically important difference (MID) thresholds, or by two levels if it crossed both; (5) Publication bias—downgraded by one level if the funnel plot showed suspicion (e.g., visual asymmetry or Egger's test P < 0.05), or by two levels if there is convincing evidence of publication bias (e.g., missing studies confirmed by contour-enhanced funnel plot or evidence of selective reporting).

Infections were defined as any adverse event classified under the “Infections and infestations” system organ class according to the CTCAE version used in each trial. Severe infections were defined as those of grade 3 or higher, with fatal events (grade 5) included in this definition.

The safety outcomes were assessed based on adverse event data reported in the original trials. We prioritised the extraction of Treatment-Related Adverse Events (TRAEs) when explicitly reported by the study investigators. For trials that reported only Treatment-Emergent Adverse Events (TEAEs) without causality assessment, TEAE data were extracted. Consequently, general surgical morbidities or post-operative complications not attributed to the study drug were excluded, unless they were specifically reported as treatment-related by the study authors. All analyses were performed separately for TEAEs and TRAEs.

The primary endpoint was the risk ratio (RR) of all infection events in patients receiving ICI compared to those not receiving immunotherapy. The secondary endpoint was the RR for severe infections. Pooled RRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association between ICI and infectious adverse events. Absolute risk differences (ARDs) were calculated as the difference in infection incidence between the ICI and control arms.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on cancer types, infection site, ICI agent type, treatment regimen, and timing of treatment (perioperative, locally advanced, or advanced). Additionally, we stratified by infection adverse event attribution (TRAE vs. TEAE), as well as by the timing and nature of infections, including those occurring during neoadjuvant therapy, related to surgery, or in the postoperative period. In the comparison of ICI monotherapy versus chemotherapy alone, we performed a subgroup analysis stratified by the risk of febrile neutropenia associated with the chemotherapy regimens in the control arm. Based on the classification used in previous studies,22,23 chemotherapy backbones were categorised as “high-risk” (expected incidence of febrile neutropenia >20%) or “intermediate-risk” (expected incidence 10–20%). Because none of the included trials employed a chemotherapy regimen with a low risk (<10%) in the control group, a low-risk subgroup could not be evaluated.

Heterogeneity was evaluated with the I2 statistic and Cochran's Q test. Given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included trials, we applied random-effects models for all primary analyses a priori, irrespective of the observed I2 statistic. This approach provides more conservative summary estimates by incorporating both within- and between-study variability. Moreover, to investigate sources of heterogeneity, we performed separate meta-regressions for any-grade TRAE (with treatment regimen as moderator) and any-grade TEAE (with cancer type as moderator). The proportion of between-study variance explained by moderators (R2) and residual heterogeneity (I2) were estimated, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger's test, and the trim-and-fill method.24 To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by iteratively removing each study and recalculating the pooled risk ratios.

The following post-hoc analyses were performed: (1) separate reporting of TEAEs and TRAEs; (2) subgroup analysis by chemotherapy intensity (combined versus single-agent); (3) subgroup analysis by febrile neutropenia risk category; (4) subgroup analysis by timing of adverse events (neoadjuvant, surgery-related, postoperative); (5) meta-regression analyses; and (6) reporting of ARDs. All other analyses were prespecified in the PROSPERO protocol (CRD420251231682).

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) with the meta, metafor, robvis, and dplyr packages.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source for this study.

Results

A total of 37,826 potentially relevant articles was identified. After removing 14,201 duplicates, 22,786 irrelevant records were excluded based on title and abstract screening. The full texts of the remaining 839 articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 697 articles were excluded for the following reasons: duplicate study cohort (n = 29), lack of data on infection rates (n = 173), inappropriate control group (n = 161), non-RCT design (n = 45), trial protocol only (n = 56), or publication type being a review or meta-analysis (n = 234). Consequently, 141 studies were included in the final meta-analysis (see Supplementary Materials for the full list of recruited studies and additional details). The PRISMA flow diagram is shown as Fig. 1.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram of the study selection process. RCTs: randomised controlled trials.

This analysis encompassed 87,484 participants from 141 trials (comprising 11 phase II, 2 phase II/III, and 121 phase III). The most common cancer types were non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC; n = 43, 30.50%), oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC; n = 10, 7.09%), breast cancer (BC; n = 9, 6.38%), small cell lung cancer (SCLC; n = 9, 6.38%), renal cell carcinoma (RCC; n = 8, 5.67%), urothelial carcinoma (UC, n = 8; 5.67%), and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC; n = 8, 5.67%), among others. With regard to treatment setting, the majority of trials enrolled patients with advanced or metastatic disease (n = 110, 78.01%), followed by those conducted in the perioperative setting (n = 20, 14.18%) and those involving locally advanced disease treated with radical radiotherapy (n = 9, 6.38%). Regarding ICI class, PD-1 inhibitors were evaluated in 75 trials (53.19%), PD-L1 inhibitors in 48 trials (34.04%), and combinations of PD-1/PD-L1 plus CTLA-4 inhibitors in 15 trials (10.64%). In terms of treatment regimen, the most common comparison was ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone (n = 75, 53.19%), followed by ICI monotherapy versus chemotherapy alone (n = 35, 24.82%), ICI monotherapy versus placebo (n = 19, 13.48%), and ICI versus targeted therapy (n = 12, 8.51%). In the pooled population, the proportion of male patients was 38.81% in the ICI group and 31.58% in the control group. For any-grade infections, the incidence of TEAE was 25.92% (95% CI: 20.10–31.75) in the ICI group compared with 20.80% (95% CI: 16.32–25.27) in the control group; for TRAE, the corresponding rates were 9.97% (95% CI: 6.94–13.01) and 9.01% (95% CI: 6.70–11.32), respectively. For severe (Grade 3) infections, the TEAE rate was 8.91% (95% CI: 6.15–11.68) among ICI-treated patients versus 6.62% (95% CI: 4.46–8.78) among controls, while the TRAE rate was 3.52% (95% CI: 2.45–4.60) and 3.69% (95% CI: 2.80–4.58), respectively. Fatal infections (TEAE) occurred in 0.74% (95% CI: 0.50–0.98) of the ICI group and 0.76% (95% CI: 0.53–0.98) of the control group; for fatal TRAE, the rates were 0.20% (95% CI: 0.11–0.29) and 0.39% (95% CI: 0.26–0.51), respectively. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Supplementary Table S2 lists the specific treatment regimens used in the ICI and control arms for each trial that reported any-grade infection events. In the primary analysis using TEAEs, an increased risk of any-grade infections (RR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.12–1.39; I2 = 71.1%; absolute risk difference [ARD] = 4.70% [2.62–6.77]; Fig. 2A) and of severe infections (RR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.17–1.44; I2 = 31.5%; ARD = 1.51% [0.83–2.19]; Supplementary Figure S1A) was observed in patients receiving ICIs. By contrast, the risk of fatal infections was similar between the two groups (RR = 1.02; 95% CI: 0.83–1.25; I2 = 0%; Supplementary Figure S2A). In analyses restricted to TRAEs, no increase was observed for any-grade infections (RR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.84–1.22; I2 = 67.3%; Fig. 2B), severe infections (RR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.81–1.14; I2 = 45.9%; Supplementary Figure S1B), or fatal infections (RR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.62–1.02; I2 = 0%; Supplementary Figure S2B).

When stratified by treatment regimen based on TEAEs, the association between ICI and infection risk differed markedly by comparator type. Compared with placebo, ICI was associated with a considerably increased risk (RR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.12–1.90; ARD = 8.87% [−0.17 to 17.91]). The risk increase was more modest when ICI plus chemotherapy was compared with chemotherapy alone (RR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.12–1.49; ARD = 5.67% [2.88–8.46]). ICI monotherapy did not differ materially from chemotherapy alone (RR = 1.06; 95% CI: 0.84–1.32) (Fig. 3A). In the analysis restricted to TRAEs, ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone also showed an increased infection risk (RR = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.17–1.52), and ICI versus placebo showed a similarly increased risk (RR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.09–1.94), whereas ICI monotherapy was associated with a lower infection risk than chemotherapy alone (RR = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.31–0.71; ARD = −3.86% [−5.46 to −2.25]) (Fig. 3B). For targeted therapy, no symmetric counterpart exists; therefore, these estimates are inherently confounded and should be interpreted with caution.

To address the potential confounding from asymmetric control arms, we further explored the heterogeneity within the ICI monotherapy versus chemotherapy alone trials by stratifying these trials by the intensity of the chemotherapy regimen used in the control arm. All combined chemotherapy regimens were platinum-based doublets. In TEAE-based analyses (Supplementary Figure S3A), there was an increased risk of any-grade infection with ICI monotherapy when compared with combined chemotherapy (RR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.02–1.46), but no meaningful difference when compared with single-agent chemotherapy (RR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.54–1.36). Meanwhile, TRAE-based analyses (Supplementary Figure S3B) demonstrated that ICI monotherapy was consistently associated with a lower risk of treatment-attributable infections irrespective of chemotherapy intensity (single-agent: RR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.33–0.86; combined: RR = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.21–0.69), suggesting a lower attributable risk with ICI monotherapy.

Additionally, based on TRAE analysis, compared with chemotherapy alone, ICI monotherapy was linked to a markedly lower risk of febrile neutropenia (RR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.07–0.25; P < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis stratified by the risk of febrile neutropenia for the chemotherapy regimen, a lower risk with ICI was found in the intermediate-risk group (RR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.06–0.22) than in the high-risk group (RR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.13–4.41). Although the test for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.051), this potential trend may be limited by only two studies in the high-risk stratum (Supplementary Figure S4).

Stratification by ICI agent type showed that for any-grade infectious TEAE, PD-L1 inhibitors (RR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.06–1.30; ARD = 3.43% [0.78–6.07%]), and PD-1 inhibitors (RR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.07–1.52; ARD = 4.59% [1.79–7.39%]) were associated with an increased risk, while no such increase was observed for the combination of PD-1/PD-L1 plus CTLA-4 (RR = 1.84; 95% CI: 0.74–4.57). For any-grade infectious TRAEs, there was no appreciably increased risk with any ICI type (Supplementary Figure S5).

In analyses stratified by treatment setting using TEAEs, ICI was associated with an increased risk of infection in the perioperative setting (RR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.08–1.38; ARD = 2.64% [0.44–4.84]), in patients with locally advanced disease receiving radical radiotherapy (RR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.02–3.85; ARD = 15.38% [−7.51 to 38.26]), and in those with advanced disease (RR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.06–1.39; ARD = 4.48% [2.01–6.95]) (Fig. 3C). For TRAEs, an increased risk was observed only in the perioperative setting (RR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.92; ARD = 3.79% [1.11–6.46]), whereas no increase was seen for advanced disease (RR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.71–1.13) or locally advanced disease (RR = 1.19; 95% CI: 0.91–1.56) (Fig. 3D).

We also performed an exploratory subgroup analysis based on TEAEs stratified by the timing of adverse events (neoadjuvant, surgery-related, and postoperative). The pooled RR for postoperative infections was 1.50 (95% CI: 1.30–1.73; ARD = 6.21% [1.60–10.83]), whereas neoadjuvant infections and surgery-related infections showed no discernible increase (Supplementary Figure S6). However, the postoperative subgroup predominantly includes ICI versus placebo trials (which have higher baseline RRs, as shown in Fig. 3A), and the small number of neoadjuvant trials (n = 3) limits reliable inference.

Subgroup analyses by primary tumour type showed distinct patterns. For TEAE, significantly elevated risks were found for gynaecological cancer (RR = 1.15 [1.02–1.29], ARD = 2.16% [0.03–4.29]). For TRAE, only SCLC demonstrated a noteworthy increase (RR = 1.54 [1.12–2.12] ARD = 1.54% [−0.24 to 3.33]); no other cancer types had evident associations (Supplementary Figure S7).

Analysis of the most common infection sites—respiratory infections, sepsis or septic shock, and genitourinary infections—yielded divergent results depending on the outcome definition. For TEAEs, the ICI group was linked to an elevated risk for respiratory infections (RR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.14–1.49) and genitourinary infections (RR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.05–1.29), but no such elevation was observed for sepsis or septic shock (RR = 1.06; 95% CI: 0.83–1.35). Conversely, for TRAEs, there was no increase in risk for any site (all 95% CIs contained 1) (Supplementary Figures S8–S10).

The I2 values were 71.1% for any-grade TEAEs and 67.3% for any-grade TRAEs, indicating moderate to considerable heterogeneity. To explore the sources of this heterogeneity, we conducted meta-regression analyses. For any-grade infectious TEAEs (moderator: cancer type), the model also showed a notable overall effect (R2 = 46.8%, P = 0.0002), although residual heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 71.1%). For any-grade infectious TRAEs (moderator: treatment regimen), the meta-regression explained a sizeable proportion of the between-study variance (R2 = 56.9%, P < 0.0001), with moderate residual heterogeneity (I2 = 54.7%) (Supplementary Figure S11).

For infectious TEAEs, funnel plots were symmetric, and Egger's tests were not appreciable for any-grade (P = 0.71; Supplementary Figure S12A), severe (P = 0.36; Supplementary Figure S12B), and fatal events (P = 0.36; Supplementary Figure S12C), indicating no evidence of publication bias. For infectious TRAEs, meaningful bias was detected for any-grade (P = 0.015; Supplementary Figure S12D) and severe events (P = 0.0025; Supplementary Figure S12E). Trim-and-fill correction shifted the random-effects RR to 1.24 (95% CI: 0.97–1.59; P = 0.082; Supplementary Figure S12G) for any-grade infectious TRAEs and to 1.08 (0.87–1.34; P = 0.47; Supplementary Figure S12H) for severe infectious TRAEs, suggesting that publication bias may have attenuated the true effect. No bias was found for fatal TRAEs (P = 0.93; Supplementary Figure S12F).

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by iteratively removing each study. The pooled RRs remained stable across all exclusions, indicating that no single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the overall result. These findings confirm the robustness of our meta-analysis (Supplementary Figures S13–15).

The risk of bias, assessed using the RoB2 tool, is summarised in Supplementary Figure S16. Eight studies (5.67%) were rated as high risk, primarily due to lack of blinding. Some concerns were raised for 64 studies (45.39%), while the remaining 69 studies (48.94%) were judged to be at low risk.

According to the GRADE framework, high-certainty evidence was observed for two comparisons (ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy and ICI versus placebo) for TRAE. Moderate-certainty evidence supported an increased risk of any-grade TEAE, perioperative infections (both TEAE and TRAE), urinary/genital tract infections (TEAE), sepsis (TEAE), fatal infections (both TEAE and TRAE), and the TEAE outcomes of PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4 inhibitors, as well as ICI versus placebo, and ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy (TEAE). Discussion

To our knowledge, this meta-analysis of 141 RCTs involving 87,484 patients represents the most comprehensive evaluation of infection risks associated with ICI therapy across diverse solid tumours and treatment contexts to date. Our large-scale data demonstrate that the relationship between ICI therapy and infection risk is nuanced and context-dependent, varying substantially with comparator type, treatment regimen, and clinical setting.

Notably, in symmetric comparisons (placebo-controlled or ICI plus chemotherapy vs. the same chemotherapy backbone trials), ICIs were associated with modestly increased risks of both TEAE-based and TRAE-based infection. Although the overall TEAE estimate may be partly influenced by time-at-risk bias from prolonged ICI survival, the similar increases in TEAEs and TRAEs indicate that this bias is unlikely to be the sole driver, suggesting a modest association between ICIs and infection risk. According to the flexible Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) framework,25 these RR values slightly exceed the small-effect threshold (Cohen's d = 0.2, corresponding to RR ≈ 1.20), suggesting a modest but potentially detectable clinical magnitude in higher-risk populations. Thus, while the absolute risk increase is modest, proactive monitoring should be considered in higher-risk subgroups, and clinical decisions should balance this risk against survival benefits on a case-by-case basis.

In contrast, the overall TRAE result was null (RR = 1.01), which might intuitively weaken causal inference. However, this null estimate appears to be largely driven by confounding from asymmetric comparisons. In the ICI versus chemotherapy asymmetric comparison, the chemotherapy arm showed higher infection-related TRAEs, a finding consistent with chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression, whereas lower TRAE rates were observed with ICIs (RR = 0.47). This pattern of lower rates in the ICI arm pulled the pooled estimate toward null (RR = 1.01). Similarly, ICI ± tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) versus a different TKI lacks a symmetric control; such estimates represent net differences between two active regimens, not an isolated ICI effect. For TEAEs, the lack of an overall increase may reflect a balance between the higher baseline infection risk of chemotherapy (which would favour ICI) and the longer survival in ICI arms (which would increase cumulative events via time-at-risk bias). Therefore, the overall null should not be interpreted as evidence negating a potential infection risk related to ICI; in symmetric comparisons that reduce confounding, a modest risk signal persists.

Why did ICI monotherapy show an association with increased TEAEs versus doublet chemotherapy but not versus single-agent chemotherapy? One speculative explanation is that doublet chemotherapy trials were predominantly frontline, where ICI prolongs survival and introduces time-at-risk bias, whereas the single-agent trials were later-line, with minimal survival benefit and thus minimal bias. Hence, the modest increase in doublet comparisons may reflect time-at-risk bias, but this remains a hypothesis that requires validation with patient-level time-to-event data.

Beyond these methodological considerations, the fundamental biological distinction between cytotoxic chemotherapy and ICIs provides a framework for understanding the observed infection risk patterns. Cytotoxic agents cause myelosuppression and neutropenia that directly impair innate immunity.10 ICIs block inhibitory receptors on T cells to reinvigorate anti-tumour immunity, suggesting that infection susceptibility during ICI therapy arises from immune dysregulation rather than direct immunosuppression.13 Several mechanisms may contribute: (1) concomitant chemotherapy, especially myelosuppressive regimens, directly increases infection risk26; (2) frequent use of corticosteroids to manage irAEs may create iatrogenic immunosuppression11; (3) emerging preclinical evidence suggests context-dependent effects of ICIs on host-pathogen interactions, including compensatory immunosuppressive pathways or exaggerated immunopathology, and PD-1/PD-L1 signalling may play subtle roles in B-cell differentiation, antibody production, and neutrophil function, which could theoretically influence host defence against specific pathogens.27, 28, 29, 30 Collectively, infection risk reflects the cumulative burden of multimodal treatment, irAE management, and host-immune interactions, rather than an isolated drug effect.

It is worth noting that the observed increase in infection risk in the perioperative setting is likely driven by the comparator types (ICI vs. placebo or ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy) rather than a true biological effect of the perioperative setting itself. As shown in our subgroup analyses, these comparator types are consistently associated with an increased infection risk. Given the scarcity of studies with identical comparator types across different settings, we could not statistically separate the comparator effect from the setting effect. Nonetheless, given the survival benefit in earlier-stage patients, the risk-benefit balance still favours ICI therapy, provided infection prevention is emphasised.

We observed divergent correlations between ICI agent types and the risk of infection. Based on TEAE data, treatment with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors was linked to a higher incidence of infection. Of note, although there is evidence that combined PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade predisposes patients to severe irAEs requiring immunosuppression, our analysis did not detect a corresponding increase in infection risk. This null finding is provisional, constrained by the small number of trials in this subgroup and the lack of patient-level data on immunosuppressive therapy. Future studies with larger sample sizes, or meta-analyses directly comparing these regimens with sufficient trials, are needed to definitively determine whether there is a higher infection risk with dual ICI therapy compared to ICI monotherapy.

As reported in the results, moderate to appreciable heterogeneity was present for both TEAEs (I2 = 71.1%) and TRAEs (I2 = 67.3%), which is expected given the broad inclusion criteria across tumour types, ICI agents, treatment lines, and comparator regimens. Meta-regression identified cancer type and comparator regimen as influential moderators, explaining 46.8% and 56.9% of the between-study variance, respectively. Residual heterogeneity likely stems from unmeasured factors such as differences in baseline infection risk, corticosteroid use, and diagnostic surveillance intensity.

We also note that in several subgroup analyses, the fixed-effect model yielded estimates that differed from the random-effects model. This discrepancy is expected when there is substantial between-study heterogeneity, as the fixed-effect model ignores this variability and gives more weight to larger studies, potentially overestimating precision. Given the clinical and methodological diversity of the included trials (e.g., variations in cancer types, treatment settings [perioperative vs. advanced], ICI agents [PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4], comparator regimens [chemotherapy, targeted therapy, placebo], and infection definitions/reporting), the random-effects model, which accounts for both within-study and between-study variance, provides a more appropriate and conservative summary estimate.31,32

Our study has several limitations. First, and most importantly, our analysis is based on crude cumulative event counts rather than exposure-adjusted incidence rates or time-to-event analyses. Because ICI therapy often prolongs survival, longer follow-up in experimental arms could increase cumulative infection events even if the instantaneous risk per unit time is unchanged. This ‘time-at-risk’ bias may therefore lead to overestimation of the relative risk of infection, particularly for TEAE-based estimates. Second, the considerable clinical and methodological diversity across the included trials led to moderate to appreciable heterogeneity, which may affect the precision of our pooled estimates. Third, although this meta-analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence, encompassing 141 RCTs and 87,484 participants, infection events in the original trials were derived from adverse event reporting rather than prospective surveillance with standardised diagnostic criteria. This likely introduces some degree of misclassification and underreporting. Fourth, the included RCTs enrolled selected patient populations with better performance status and fewer comorbidities than real-world patients. Consequently, our findings likely underestimate the true infection burden in routine clinical practice, and real-world observational studies are needed to complement our results. Fifth, we were unable to assess sex differences in infection risk due to the lack of individual patient data and sex-stratified reporting—a notable limitation given the well-recognised sex-based differences in immunity. Finally, race and ethnicity data were not consistently reported, which precluded subgroup analyses and limited our ability to assess potential confounding by socioeconomic or structural factors.

Overall, the relationship between ICI therapy and infection risk is nuanced and context-dependent. In symmetric comparisons (ICI vs. placebo and ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy), ICIs are associated with modestly increased overall and treatment-related infection risks. Although the absolute risk increase is small, proactive monitoring warrants consideration in higher-risk subgroups; for all patients, treatment decisions should balance this modest risk against the survival benefit of ICIs on a case-by-case basis.
