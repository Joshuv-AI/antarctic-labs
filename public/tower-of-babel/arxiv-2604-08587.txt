Approximate covariant quantum error correction across radical-pair proteins: cryptochrome narrows the nuclear spin coherence gap
Hikaru Wakaura

Abstract
Quantum brain proposals require that coherence survives on behaviorally relevant timescales, yet the gap between spin coherence times and neural decision windows has remained a quantitative obstacle. Here we evaluate approximate covariant quantum error correction (CQEC)—a single, physically transparent purification protocol constrained by the Eastin–Knill theorem—across two radical-pair proteins parameterized by ab initio spin Hamiltonian calculations: monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) and cryptochrome (CRY, PDB 4I6G). Both share a three-layer architecture ( 31 P nuclear spin memory, electron spin interface, classical electrochemistry) and identical hyperfine coupling ( A = 200 A=200 MHz), but differ 16-fold in nuclear T 2 T_{2} : 3.2 ms (MAO-A) versus 52 ms (CRY). The decisive test is whether CQEC preserves coherence over the 200 ms Schultze-Kraft veto window. We map each protein’s T 2 T_{2} gap onto an effective simulation decoherence rate ( γ veto = T 2 ​ gap / 2 ​ T sim \gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=T_{2}~\text{gap}/2T_{\mathrm{sim}} ): 3.08 for MAO-A, 0.19 for CRY. At γ veto = 0.19 \gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=0.19 , CQEC maintains L ↔ \leftrightarrow R tunneling coherence of 0.83 (trajectory-average 95% CI [0.76, 0.79]; versus 0.12 without correction, × \times 6.9 improvement). At γ veto = 3.08 \gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=3.08 , coherence collapses to 0.012 even with CQEC. A T 2 T_{2} sensitivity analysis shows that this result is robust: even at T 2 = 26 T_{2}=26 ms (half the CRY estimate), CQEC-protected coherence remains 0.69. A matched classical Markov baseline produces only monotonic relaxation, confirming that the CQEC-maintained oscillatory dynamics are a genuinely quantum signature. We show, however, that no single protein optimizes both layers: CRY’s shorter electron T 2 e T_{2}^{e} (0.53 ns versus 1.1 ns) worsens Layer 2 recovery fidelity. This layer–protein tradeoff, together with unresolved challenges in state preparation and spatial entanglement distribution, defines the next quantitative targets for quantum brain research.

I
Introduction

The quantum brain hypothesis faces a concrete quantitative challenge: nuclear spin coherence times in candidate proteins (
T_{2}\sim
ms) are orders of magnitude shorter than behaviorally relevant timescales (
\sim
200 ms for the Schultze-Kraft veto window
Schultze-Kraft et al. 2016
;
\sim
1 s for deliberate decisions). Whether error correction can bridge this gap depends not only on the protocol but on the specific protein environment—a dependence that has not been systematically quantified.

Previous work has established the three-layer quantum brain architecture using MAO-A parameters
Tegmark 2000
;
Fisher 2015
;
Hore and Mouritsen 2016
and evaluated multiple error correction paradigms including decoherence-free subspaces, dynamical decoupling, and purification protocols
Eastin and Knill 2009
;
Faist et al. 2020
;
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
. Here we take a complementary approach: rather than combining multiple QEC paradigms, we apply a
single
protocol—approximate covariant purification—across
two
radical-pair proteins to isolate the effect of protein-specific parameters on coherence preservation. Crucially, we map the abstract simulation decoherence parameter onto protein-specific physical timescales via a veto-window scaling (
\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=T_{2}~\text{gap}/(2T_{\mathrm{sim}})
), enabling direct comparison of CQEC efficacy at behaviorally relevant timescales.

The two proteins share the FAD cofactor and identical
31
P hyperfine coupling (
A=200
MHz) but differ dramatically in nuclear coherence: MAO-A (
T_{2}=3.2
ms, neurotransmitter catabolism
Koch and Hepp 2006
;
Rodgers and Hore 2009
) and cryptochrome (CRY,
T_{2}=52
ms, circadian regulation and magnetoreception
Ritz et al. 2000
;
Maeda et al. 2008
). This 16-fold difference generates qualitatively different
T_{2}
gaps relative to the 200 ms behavioral threshold.

Approximate covariant error correction.

We use the covariant swap test with projector
\Pi=\bigoplus_{E}(I_{E}+\mathrm{SWAP}_{E})/2
within each energy sector
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
. This achieves approximate (not exact) error correction, consistent with the Eastin–Knill prohibition on exact covariant QEC with continuous symmetry
Eastin and Knill 2009
;
Faist et al. 2020
. The protocol is a purification scheme whose performance we characterize numerically—it is not a full QEC code with proven threshold. We apply this single protocol identically to both proteins to ensure that any performance differences arise from physical parameters, not methodological choices.

II
Results

II.1
Protein-dependent
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
reveals a two-dimensional dichotomy

The effective decoherence rate per gate,
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=t_{\mathrm{gate}}/T_{2}
, depends on both the layer (nuclear versus electron) and the protein (Table

). For Layer 1 (nuclear spin memory,
d=4

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.54\times 10^{-6}

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=9.56\times 10^{-8}

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=4.55

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=9.43

(0.53 ns at Earth field) is half that of MAO-A (1.1 ns).

This creates a layer–protein tradeoff absent from single-protein analyses: CRY is strongly superior at Layer 1 but inferior at Layer 2. No single protein optimizes both quantum layers simultaneously.

At Layer 2, the protein dependence emerges: MAO-A achieves single-evaluation CQEC recovery fidelity

II.3
Veto-window scaling: from per-gate rates to behavioral timescales

The per-gate
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
characterizes single-operation fidelity but does not answer the biologically relevant question: does coherence survive over the 200 ms veto window? Over this timescale, nuclear spins undergo
N=200~\mathrm{ms}/t_{\mathrm{gate}}\approx 4\times 10^{7}

\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\times N=\frac{200~\mathrm{ms}}{T_{2}}

(1)

\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=61.6

\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=3.82

To simulate veto-window dynamics in a computationally tractable Lindblad integration (total simulation time

\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}/(2T_{\mathrm{sim}})

\exp(-2\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}T_{\mathrm{sim}})=\exp(-\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}})

0.2

III
Discussion

Relationship to multi-paradigm approaches.

Previous work has integrated multiple QEC paradigms (decoherence-free subspaces, dynamical decoupling, purification, symmetry gauging, catalytic recovery) into composite stabilizers. Our approach is deliberately simpler: by using a single protocol across two proteins, we isolate the contribution of protein-specific parameters from methodological complexity. The two approaches are complementary—the multi-paradigm framework answers “what QEC resources does biology provide?” while our analysis answers “which proteins provide the best parameters for a given protocol?”

The layer–protein tradeoff.

If Layer 1 (nuclear memory) and Layer 2 (electron interface) must function coherently, then either (i) a single protein with balanced parameters exists but has not yet been characterized, or (ii) different proteins serve different layers. However, our veto-window analysis suggests a third possibility: (iii) Layer 2 coherence is not required because information extraction proceeds classically through Layer 3 readout (singlet yield
\to
neurotransmitter modulation). This is the most parsimonious interpretation.

CQEC correction frequency.

Our simulation applies CQEC 10 times over the 200 ms veto window, corresponding to one purification event every
\sim
20 ms. This timescale is comparable to the CRY photocycle (
\sim
10 ms for FAD reoxidation
Maeda et al. 2008
). Whether biochemical cycles can implement the covariant swap test remains an open question. The
T_{2}
sensitivity analysis shows that reducing the correction frequency degrades coherence gracefully rather than catastrophically.

CRY as the preferred experimental target.

CRY offers three advantages over MAO-A: (i) its radical pair (FAD
∙-
/Trp
∙+
) is experimentally confirmed
Maeda et al. 2008
, whereas MAO-A’s radical pair status is unconfirmed; (ii) its circadian signaling pathway is immune to the homeostatic neurotransmitter buffering that confounds MAO-A pharmacological tests; (iii) CQEC maintains 83% coherence over the veto window for CRY versus 1.2% for MAO-A—a 70-fold advantage.

What remains unresolved.

Four challenges persist:

(i)

State preparation at 310 K
: Preparing
(\left|\uparrow\uparrow\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow\downarrow\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
from the near-maximally mixed thermal state (
kT\approx 27
meV
\gg
nuclear Zeeman splitting) remains the most critical open problem.

(ii)

Spatial entanglement distribution
: Coherence across synaptic clefts (
\sim
20 nm) or inter-neuronal distances (
\mu
m–mm) is not addressed.

(iii)

Metabolic cost
: CQEC requires ancilla preparation (Landauer minimum
\sim
0.07 eV per cycle).

(iv)

Classical sufficiency
: Drift-diffusion
Ratcliff and McKoon 2008
and accumulator models
Schurger et al. 2012
account for behavioral data without quantum mechanics.

Connection to free will and veto dynamics.

The Libet readiness potential
Libet et al. 1983
and Schultze-Kraft veto window
Schultze-Kraft et al. 2016
define a 200 ms temporal window for motor decision cancellation. Our veto-window-scaled simulation shows that CRY nuclear spins maintain 83% tunneling coherence over this window with CQEC, while MAO-A retains only 1.2%. We emphasize the “luck objection”
Mele 2006
: quantum indeterminacy does not constitute agency, and classical models
Schurger et al. 2012
already account for the Schultze-Kraft data.

IV
Conclusion

By applying a single approximate covariant purification protocol across two radical-pair proteins with veto-window-scaled decoherence, we establish four results:

(i)

The
T_{2}
gap is protein-dependent: CRY’s 52 ms nuclear coherence gives
\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=0.19
, versus
\gamma_{\mathrm{veto}}=3.08
for MAO-A. CQEC maintains 83% coherence for CRY but only 1.2% for MAO-A over the 200 ms veto window (
\times
6.9 improvement for CRY, 70-fold advantage over MAO-A).

(ii)

CRY Layer 1 is the first identified parameter regime where error-corrected coherence quantitatively survives a behaviorally relevant timescale at physically calibrated decoherence rates. This remains a simulation result contingent on the estimated CRY
T_{2}
; however, the
T_{2}
sensitivity analysis demonstrates robustness down to
T_{2}=26
ms (coherence 0.69).

(iii)

A layer–protein tradeoff exists: CRY is superior at Layer 1 (
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=10^{-8}
versus
10^{-6}
) but inferior at Layer 2 (
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=9.4
versus
4.5
). The most parsimonious resolution is that Layer 2 coherence is unnecessary and information extraction proceeds classically.

(iv)

The oscillatory quantum signature in the decision model and its absence in the classical stochastic baseline are robust across the CQEC-effective regime (
\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}<0.35
), confirming that the quantum–classical distinction is observable wherever sufficient coherence is maintained.

These results transform the quantum brain question from a debate about whether coherence can survive
in principle
to a quantitative identification of the protein, layer, and timescale at which coherence is preserved: CRY, Layer 1, 200 ms.

Appendix A
Two-qubit dephasing decay verification

The veto-window scaling assumes single-qubit off-diagonal decay
\exp(-2\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}T_{\mathrm{sim}})
. We verified this by comparing the analytical prediction for the
\left|01\right\rangle\left\langle 10\right|
element against numerical Lindblad evolution in the full 2-qubit decision Hamiltonian (with zero tunneling and no relaxation, isolating the pure dephasing contribution). At
\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}=0.05
, the numerical off-diagonal magnitude exceeds the single-qubit analytic prediction by a factor of 4.5; at
\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}=0.2
, by a factor of 400. This discrepancy arises because the Hamiltonian’s confidence coupling (
J=0.3\Delta
) partially protects cross-sector coherences. The single-qubit prediction is therefore a conservative lower bound on coherence, and our reported bridging results underestimate the actual coherence maintained at each protein’s
T_{2}
gap.

Appendix B
Approximate covariance and the Eastin–Knill constraint

Exact covariant QEC for continuous symmetries is prohibited
Eastin and Knill 2009
. Our scheme achieves
\varepsilon
-approximate covariance: the infidelity between the covariant and non-covariant purified states scales as
O(1/d^{2})
per energy sector. For
d=4
, this gives
\varepsilon\approx 0.06
; for
d=8
,
\varepsilon\approx 0.016
.

Appendix C
Classical stochastic baseline construction

The classical comparison model is a two-state Markov chain on
\{L,R\}
with transition probability per step
p=\Delta^{2}dt/(\Delta^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{deph}}^{2})
, capped at 0.5. Initial state is uniform random. This matches the quantum tunneling rate and noise regime while removing all quantum interference effects. We average over 1,000 realizations for ensemble statistics.

Appendix D
Ancilla resource accounting

The recursive covariant swap test with
n=4
rounds consumes
2^{4}=16
copies. For
d=4
, each copy requires 16 matrix elements, and the computation proceeds sequentially. The biological realization of 16 independent copies of a nuclear spin state within the
\sim
20 ms inter-CQEC window remains an open question.

Appendix E
Thermodynamic cost analysis

Each CQEC cycle costs at minimum
\sim 4\times kT\ln 2\approx 0.07
eV (Landauer bound for 2 qubits at 310 K). At one cycle per 20 ms (10 corrections per veto window), this is
\sim 5.6\times 10^{-19}
W per qubit—negligible compared to the brain’s 20 W budget. This estimate does not account for biological implementation inefficiencies.
