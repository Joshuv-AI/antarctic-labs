Information-Theoretic Bounds for Sparse Covariance Estimation in the Vertical-Split Distributed Model
Jing Yee Tan, Guangyue Han

Abstract
We study the minimax estimation error for distributed covariance matrix estimation in the vertical-split (feature-split) setting, where two agents each observe different coordinates of m m i.i.d. sub-Gaussian samples and communicate a limited number of bits to a central server. While 26 established nearly tight bounds for dense (unstructured) cross-covariance matrices, we investigate whether imposing elementwise s s -sparsity on the cross-covariance C 21 C_{21} can reduce the required communication and sample complexity. In contrast to the horizontal-split setting, where 11 showed that sparsity does not reduce communication cost for mean estimation, we prove that sparsity does help for cross-covariance estimation in the vertical split. Specifically, we establish minimax lower bounds showing that the communication budget per agent scales as B k = Ω ⁡ ( σ 4 ​ d k ​ s ′ ​ log ⁡ ( d 1 ​ d 2 / s ′ ) / ε 2 ) B_{k}=\Omega(\sigma^{4}d_{k}\,s^{\prime}\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s^{\prime})/\varepsilon^{2}) and the sample complexity for cross-covariance estimation as m = Ω ⁡ ( σ 4 ​ s ′ ​ log ⁡ ( d 1 ​ d 2 / s ′ ) / ε 2 ) m=\Omega(\sigma^{4}\,s^{\prime}\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s^{\prime})/\varepsilon^{2}) , where s ′ = s ∧ d min s^{\prime}=s\wedge d_{\min} . For the 1 1 -sparse case, this yields an exponential improvement from d 1 ​ d 2 d_{1}d_{2} to log ⁡ ( d 1 ​ d 2 ) \log(d_{1}d_{2}) compared to the dense rate. Our lower bounds are established via Fano’s method with an explicit sparse packing using a Varshamov–Gilbert-type argument for signed partial permutation matrices combined with the Conditional Strong Data Processing Inequality of 26 . We show the bounds are tight with a matching achievable scheme, based on covering-net quantization and entry-wise hard thresholding, that attains the s s -sparse lower bound up to polylogarithmic factors.



1
Introduction

Statistical estimation in distributed environments has become a fundamental problem in modern data science. In a typical formulation, data is spread across
m
agents who observe independent or correlated samples of an unknown parameter; each agent compresses its local data into a limited number of bits and sends the result to a central server, which must produce an accurate estimate. A central goal of the theory is to characterize the minimax tradeoff between the communication budget, the number of samples, and the estimation error. For dense (unstructured) parameters, a line of work beginning with
32
and
19
established that in the horizontal split, where each of
K
machines holds a subset of
m
i.i.d. samples and observes all
d
coordinates, the communication cost must scale linearly in the ambient dimension
d
to achieve the statistical minimax rate. Since high-dimensional models often possess intrinsic low-dimensional structure, these results motivate a natural meta-question:
How does structural knowledge of the parameter, such as sparsity, affect the communication cost of distributed estimation?

For distributed mean estimation in the horizontal split,
19
showed that a simple thresholding-based protocol can exploit the
s
-sparsity of the mean vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to trade communication for estimation error, saving a factor of
d/K
in one of the two quantities. They conjectured that this tradeoff is essentially optimal.

11
confirmed this conjecture by proving that, even in the fully interactive (multi-round) communication model, the total communication must scale with
d
, not
s
, via
\Omega(\min\{m,d\}K)
to achieve the statistical minimax error for
s
-sparse Gaussian mean estimation.

This result reveals a striking phenomenon:
sparsity helps statistically but not in communication
. It is well known that in centralized estimation, the minimax rate for a
s
-sparse mean scales with the intrinsic dimension
s
, not on the ambient dimension
d
. One might therefore expect that distributed protocols could similarly exploit sparsity to reduce communication.
11
showed this intuition is wrong. They showed that with
s
-sparsity assumptions, it is impossible to improve both the loss and communication so that they depend on the intrinsic dimension
s
rather than the ambient direction
d
. This negative result raises the question of whether such phenomenon is universal:
does sparsity always fail to reduce communication cost?

The
vertical
(feature) split provides a fundamentally different distributed model. Here, all
k
agents observe the
same
set of
m
samples, but each agent sees only a subset of coordinates: Agent
i
observes the features
\{X_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{m}
corresponding to dimensions
d_{i}
, with
\sum_{i}d_{i}=d
. The natural estimation target is the
cross-covariance matrix

C_{21}=\mathbb{E}[(X_{2}-\mathbb{E}X_{2})(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}X_{1})^{\top}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
, since the diagonal blocks
C_{kk}
can be estimated locally without communication.
26
recently characterized the minimax distortion for dense (unstructured) covariance estimation in this setting with
K=2
agents: under the Frobenius norm, the communication budget per agent must scale as
B_{k}=\Omega(\sigma^{4}d_{1}d_{2}d_{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
, and they constructed an achievable scheme matching this bound up to polylogarithmic factors. A key feature of the vertical split is that the agents’ data is
correlated within each sample
:
(X_{1}^{(t)},X_{2}^{(t)})
are jointly distributed for every
t
. Braverman’s D-SDPI, which requires conditional independence across machines, therefore does not apply. To handle this correlation, Rahmani et al. introduced the
Conditional Strong Data Processing Inequality
(C-SDPI), which quantifies information contraction through a state-dependent channel.

The
vertical
(feature) split is a natural model for settings in
which multiple parties hold different attributes of the same individuals. In healthcare, one institution may record genomic data while another stores clinical outcomes for the same patient cohort; in finance, a bank observes
transaction histories while a credit bureau holds repayment records for the same customers (
26
). In such settings, the
quantity of primary interest is the
cross-covariance
between the
feature sets held by different agents, for instance, the correlation
between genetic markers and treatment response, or between spending patterns and default risk, since each agent can estimate its own marginal statistics locally without communication. The vertical split also arises in multi-sensor systems where different instruments measure different physical quantities of the same phenomenon. Unlike the horizontal split, where the communication channel transmits information about independent random variables, the vertical-split channel is
state-dependent
: the message sent by one agent is statistically dependent on the data held by the other, because both observe the same underlying samples. This correlation structure makes the vertical split both practically important and information-theoretically distinct from the well-studied horizontal setting.

A natural question left open by
26
is whether
sparsity
in the cross-covariance
C_{21}
can reduce the communication cost in the vertical split. If
C_{21}
has at most
s
non-zero entries (equivalently, only
s
out of
d_{1}d_{2}
cross-agent feature pairs are correlated), does the communication budget drop?

In this paper, we show that,
in contrast to the horizontal split, sparsity does reduce the communication cost for covariance estimation in the vertical split
. For
s
-sparse cross-covariance estimation, the communication budget per agent drops from
\Omega(\sigma^{4}d_{1}d_{2}d_{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
in the dense case to
\Omega(\sigma^{4}s\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s)\,d_{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
, an improvement from
d_{1}d_{2}
to
s\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s)
in the dimension dependence. The structural reason is that the framework used in the vertical split does not possess a mechanism to inflate the communication bound beyond what the packing number dictates. Restricting to
s^{\prime}
-sparse hypotheses reduces the packing number from
\log|\mathcal{V}|\sim d_{1}d_{2}
to
\log|\mathcal{V}|\sim s^{\prime}\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s^{\prime})
, and this reduction propagates directly into the communication lower bound.

This result has practical implications for distributed systems in which cross-agent dependencies are inherently sparse. A similar sparsity arises in high-dimensional multi-omics studies, where one measures hundreds or thousands of features (e.g., metabolites or proteins) on a handful of samples. There, the correlation structure among the measured features is sparse and block-structured: only variables belonging to a common biological process are strongly associated, while the vast majority of pairs are effectively independent (
23
).

Main Contributions

We study the minimax estimation error for distributed covariance matrix estimation (DCME) in the vertical-split setting under elementwise sparsity constraints on the cross-covariance matrix
C_{21}
. Let
s^{\prime}:=s\wedge(d_{1}\wedge d_{2})
and write
L\sim\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s^{\prime})

(i)

s
-Sparse minimax lower bounds

(Theorems
4.1
and
4.3

For cross-covariance estimation under the Frobenius norm,

\mathfrak{M}_{F}^{(\mathrm{cross})}=\Omega\!\left(\sigma^{2}\sqrt{s^{\prime}\log\!\Big(\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{s^{\prime}}\Big)\Big(\frac{d_{1}}{B_{1}}\vee\frac{d_{2}}{B_{2}}\Big)}\right).

\varepsilon
, the communication budget per agent must satisfy

27
to the Fano method.

(ii)

Matching achievable scheme
(Theorem
4.6

(iii)

\ell_{0}
-sparse vectors to the partial permutation constraint required by the C-SDPI framework, and is the combinatorial ingredient enabling the

2.1
Data Model and Vertical Split

Let
Z=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional random vector with
X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
X_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, and
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
. The distribution of
Z
belongs to the family
\mathcal{P}=\mathrm{subG}^{(d)}(\sigma)
of
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian distributions. We observe
m
i.i.d. copies
Z^{(1)},\dots,Z^{(m)}\sim P\in\mathcal{P}
.

In the
vertical
(feature) split with
K=2
agents, Agent 1 observes
\{X_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
(the first
d_{1}
coordinates of every sample) and Agent 2 observes
\{X_{2}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
(the last
d_{2}
coordinates). Both agents see all
m
samples, but different coordinates. We write

\mathbf{X}_{k}:=\big(X_{k}^{(1)},\dots,X_{k}^{(m)}\big)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times m}

(1)

for Agent
k
’s full dataset.

The covariance matrix of
Z
admits the block decomposition

C\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\big[(Z-\mathbb{E}Z)(Z-\mathbb{E}Z)^{\top}\big]\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}\\
C_{21}&C_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
C_{kk}=\mathrm{Cov}(X_{k})
and
C_{21}=\mathbb{E}[(X_{2}-\mathbb{E}X_{2})(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}X_{1})^{\top}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
is the cross-covariance matrix. The diagonal blocks
C_{11}
and
C_{22}
can each be estimated locally by the respective agent without communication; the cross-covariance
C_{21}
is the block that
requires
inter-agent communication, making it the natural estimation target.

2.2
Communication Model and Minimax Objective

We consider the simultaneous (one-shot, non-interactive) communication
model of
26
.

Definition 2.1

(DCME and DCCME Schemes)
.

A
Distributed Covariance Matrix Estimation
(DCME) scheme with
parameters
(\sigma,m,d_{1:2},B_{1:2})
consists of two encoder
functions

\mathcal{E}_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times m}\to[1:2^{B_{k}}]

producing messages
M_{k}=\mathcal{E}_{k}(\{X_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m})
for

k=1,2
, and a decoder

\mathcal{D}\colon[1:2^{B_{1}}]\times[1:2^{B_{2}}]\to\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d\times d}
, outputting
\hat{C}=\mathcal{D}(M_{1},M_{2})
.
A
Distributed Cross-Covariance Matrix Estimation
(DCCME) scheme
replaces the decoder with

\mathcal{D}_{21}\colon[1:2^{B_{1}}]\times[1:2^{B_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
, outputting
\hat{C}_{21}
.

The distortion is measured under the Frobenius norm:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{C}-C\|_{F}\bigr]
for DCME and

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{C}_{21}-C_{21}\|_{F}\bigr]
for DCCME.
The
minimax distortions
are

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{F}(\sigma,m,d_{1:2},B_{1:2},s)

\displaystyle\;:=\;\inf_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{D}}\;\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{C}-C\|_{F}\big],

(3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{F}^{(\mathrm{cross})}(\sigma,m,d_{1:2},B_{1:2},s)

\displaystyle\;:=\;\inf_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{D}_{21}}\;\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{C}_{21}-C_{21}\|_{F}\big],

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{s}
is the sparse distribution class defined below.
Since
\|\hat{C}-C\|_{F}^{2}\geq 2\|\hat{C}_{21}-C_{21}\|_{F}^{2}
, any
DCME scheme induces a DCCME scheme, giving

\mathfrak{M}_{F}\geq\mathfrak{M}_{F}^{(\mathrm{cross})}
.

2.3
Sparsity Model

We impose an
elementwise hard sparsity
constraint on the
cross-covariance.

Definition 2.2

(Support)
.

For a matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
, the
support
is

S(E):=\{(j,i)\in[d_{2}]\times[d_{1}]:E_{ji}\neq 0\}
,
and
|E|_{0}:=|S(E)|
is the number of nonzero entries.

Definition 2.3

(Symmetric difference)
.

For two supports
S,S^{\prime}\subset[d_{2}]\times[d_{1}]
, the

symmetric difference
is

S\triangle S^{\prime}:=(S\setminus S^{\prime})\cup(S^{\prime}\setminus S)
,
i.e., positions where exactly one of
S,S^{\prime}
has a nonzero.

Definition 2.4

(Sparse Cross-Covariance Class)
.

For an integer
1\leq s\leq d_{1}d_{2}
, the
s
-sparse cross-covariance
class is

\mathcal{S}(s)\;:=\;\big\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}:|A|_{0}\leq s\big\},

and the corresponding
distribution class is

\mathcal{P}_{s}\;:=\;\big\{P\in\mathrm{subG}^{(d)}(\sigma):C_{21}(P)\in\mathcal{S}(s)\big\}.

The dense setting of
26
corresponds to

s=d_{1}d_{2}
.

Remark 2.5

(Hard vs. soft sparsity)
.

We emphasise that our sparsity model is the
hard
(exact)

\ell_{0}
constraint
|C_{21}|_{0}\leq s
, requiring at most
s
entries
to be non-zero. This contrasts with the
soft
sparsity models
common in centralised covariance estimation, such as the
\ell_{q}
-ball
constraint
\sum_{i,j}|C_{ij}|^{q}\leq R_{q}
for
q\in(0,1]
of
27
. Soft sparsity permits many small but non-zero entries, whereas hard sparsity forces exact zeros. We work with hard sparsity for two reasons. First, for jointly Gaussian
Z
,
(C_{21})_{ij}=0
if and only if
(X_{2})_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(X_{1})_{j}
, so each zero entry corresponds to a pair of cross-agent features that are statistically independent; an
s
-sparse cross-covariance means that only
s
out of
d_{1}d_{2}
feature pairs carry any correlation. This sparsity–independence equivalence is the structural property that interacts with the vertical split and drives the improvement in our bounds. Second, the hypothesis families in our lower bound proofs are constructed from matrices in
\{-1,0,+1\}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
, and the packing analysis relies on exact zeros to ensure each hypothesis lies in
\mathcal{P}_{s}
.

to
X_{2}
depends on the unknown cross-covariance
C_{21}
, which plays the role of a latent state

. This state-dependence means the standard SDPI does not directly apply.
26

, which averages the contraction over the state
distribution.

To state it in the form used in this paper, consider a standardised
model where
X_{1}\sim N(0,I_{d_{1}})
and

X_{2}\mid(X_{1},V\!=\!v)\sim N(A_{v}X_{1},\;I_{d_{2}}-A_{v}A_{v}^{\top})
,
so that the conditional relationship is the Gaussian channel

X_{2}\;=\;A_{v}\,X_{1}+Z_{v},\qquad Z_{v}\sim N(0,\,I_{d_{2}}-A_{v}A_{v}^{\top})\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;X_{1},

(6)

where
V
is a random variable indexing the cross-covariance

A_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
with

A_{v}A_{v}^{\top}\preceq I_{d_{2}}
, and
X_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp V
.

Theorem 3.11

(C-SDPI for Gaussian Mixtures;

(
26
, Proposition 3.7)
)
.

Under the channel model (
6
), the conditional SDPI
coefficient equals

s\big(P_{X_{1}},\,T_{X_{2}|X_{1},V}\,\big|\,P_{V}\big)\;=\;\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[A_{V}^{\top}A_{V}\big]\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(7)

Moreover, the C-SDPI coefficient tensorises: for the full dataset

\mathbf{X}_{1}=(X_{1}^{(1)},\dots,X_{1}^{(m)})
passed through
m
independent
copies of the same channel with a fixed state
V
,

s\big(P_{\mathbf{X}_{1}},\,T_{\mathbf{X}_{2}|\mathbf{X}_{1},V}\,\big|\,P_{V}\big)\;=\;s\big(P_{X_{1}},\,T_{X_{2}|X_{1},V}\,\big|\,P_{V}\big)\;=\;\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[A_{V}^{\top}A_{V}\big]\big\|_{\mathrm{op}},

(8)

independent of
m

(
26
, Theorem 3.1)
.

3.7
Fano’s Inequality and the Averaged Fano Method

We now recall Fano’s method for minimax lower bounds and develop the two specialisations used in this paper: a basic version (without auxiliary randomness) used in the 1-sparse warm-up, and an
averaged
version (with auxiliary randomness
W
) used in the general
s
-sparse proof.

General Setup

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of distributions, and let
\theta\colon\mathcal{P}\to\Theta
be a parameter of interest residing in a metric space
(\Theta,\|\cdot\|)
(in our case,
\Theta
is the space of covariance matrices or cross-covariance matrices equipped with the Frobenius norm). The goal is to approximate
\theta(P)
for an unknown
P\in\mathcal{P}
from data
X\sim P
.

A finite subset
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}=\{P_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathcal{P}
is called
2\delta
-separated
if
\|\theta(P_{v})-\theta(P_{v^{\prime}})\|\geq 2\delta
for all
v\neq v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
. Fano’s method reduces the estimation problem to a hypothesis testing problem: if no test can reliably distinguish which
P_{v}
generated the data, then no estimator can achieve error smaller than
\delta
.

Standard Fano Inequality

Lemma 3.21

(Fano’s Inequality;

(
31
, Corollary 15.12)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}=\{P_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathcal{P}
be
2\delta
-separated.Let
V
be drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{V}
, and given
V=v
, let
X\sim P_{v}
. Then

\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\;\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\;\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\|\hat{\theta}(X)-\theta(P)\|\big]\;\geq\;\delta\left(1-\frac{I(V;\,X)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}\right).

(12)

We refer the reader to
31
for the proof.

Application to the DCME/DCCME Setting (Without
W
)

Similar to
26
, the following variant of Fano’s Inequality is utilized:

Lemma 3.22

(Fano for DCME/DCCME)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}=\{P_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathrm{subG}^{(d)}(\sigma)
be a family of
|\mathcal{V}|
distributions with covariance matrices
C_{v}
and cross-covariance matrices
D_{v}:=C_{v,21}
. Define the pairwise separations

\rho_{\mathrm{dist}}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(v,v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}^{2}\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|C_{v}-C_{v^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{dist}},\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{dist}}^{(\mathrm{cross})}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(v,v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}^{2}\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|D_{v}-D_{v^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{dist}}.

Let
V
be uniform on
\mathcal{V}
, and given

\mathbf{Z}=\{Z^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{v}

\mathbf{X}_{1}
and

respectively. Then for any DCME (respectively, DCCME) scheme with parameters

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{F}:=\inf_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},D}\ \sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dist}}(\widehat{C},C)\right]

.

The bound in Lemma

reduces the minimax lower bound problem to two tasks:
(i)
constructing a well-separated family

distributions with covariance matrices

and cross-covariance matrices

\rho_{\mathrm{dist}}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{W}\\

(15)

Lemma 5.1

For

Remark 5.2

If

We adapt the probabilistic argument in

Each element of

(26)

To construct such an

Choose which entries to remove

and Bernstein’s inequality respectively, and sums over entries to obtain the Frobenius bound. The complete calculation is given in Appendix

7
Discussion

We have established near-optimal minimax bounds for distributed covariance matrix estimation in the vertical-split model under elementwise
s
-sparsity of the cross-covariance
C_{21}
. The communication budget per agent drops from
\Omega(\sigma^{4}d_{k}d_{1}d_{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
in the dense setting (
26
) to
\Omega(\sigma^{4}d_{k}s^{\prime}\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s^{\prime})/\varepsilon^{2})
under
s
-sparsity, and a matching achievable scheme based on covering-net quantization and entry-wise hard thresholding confirms that this improvement is tight up to polylogarithmic factors.

Open problems.

We present a few possible extensions of the work.

Unknown sparsity level.

Our achievable scheme requires knowledge of
s
to calibrate the threshold
\lambda^{*}
. In practice,
s
is rarely known. Whether a protocol that requires no prior knowledge of
s
can achieve the same
s\log(d_{1}d_{2}/s)
rate in the vertical split remains open.

Other structural assumptions.

Beyond elementwise sparsity, questions on whether other structural assumptions on covariance matrices such as bandedness, low-rank structure, Toeplitz, and group sparsity similarly reduce communication cost are natural extensions of the present work.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\tilde{\varepsilon}}{16}+\frac{33\sigma^{2}\tilde{\varepsilon}}{512}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\tilde{\varepsilon}}{7}.

Thus, it is clear that

~\sqrt{d_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{C}_{kk}-C_{kk}\|_{op}|\mathcal{E}^{c}]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{7}.

Cross-Covariance Error Analysis

The proof strategy is, we first bound

Decomposing the threshold error.

Deterministic bound on

(conditioned on

Probabilistic bound on
