Determinants of the continuum of maternal healthcare service utilization in Nigeria: Evidence from the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey

Gideon Ikemdinachi Nwankwo, Rejoice Uche Obiora, Delight Mawufemor Agbi, Bisola Olubiyi, Daniel Mumba, Herbert Tafadzwa Kazonda, Isaac Isiko, Edson Mwebesa

ABSTRACT
Background: Maternal mortality remains a major public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, with Sub-Saharan Africa bearing a disproportionate burden. Nigeria accounts for over one-quarter of all maternal deaths worldwide. This reflects gaps in access to and continuity of maternal healthcare. The continuum of maternal healthcare, which includes antenatal care (ANC), skilled delivery, and postnatal care (PNC) is critical for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. This study examines the determinants of continuum of maternal healthcare service utilization in Nigeria. Methods: This study utilized data from 13,596 women (weighted n = 14,183) from the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey. Maternal Health Services Utilization (MHSU) was operationalized as a composite outcome (none, partial, adequate) based on ANC attendance, place of delivery, maternal and child PNC. Multilevel multinomial logistic regression models were fitted to assess individual, household, and contextual predictors. Variables significant at p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were included in multivariable models. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results: Overall, 44.0% of women did not utilize any services, 44.4% partially utilized services, and only 11.6% achieved adequate utilization. Women with secondary or higher education were significantly more likely to achieve adequate utilization (Relative Risk Ratio (RRR) = 12.10, 95% CI: 8.72, 16.81). Wealth status, media exposure, employment, and health insurance were positively associated with utilization, while higher parity and decision-making barriers reduced utilization. Rural residence was associated with lower utilization (RRR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.29, 0.49). Distance to health facilities was not significant in the adjusted model. Conclusion: Maternal healthcare service utilization in Nigeria remains suboptimal, with significant inequities driven by socioeconomic status, education, and women’s autonomy. Strengthening the continuum of care requires multisectoral interventions addressing structural and social determinants of health.

Introduction Maternal mortality remains a critical global public health challenge, with approximately 287,000 maternal deaths recorded worldwide in 2020, the vast majority of which occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and were largely preventable [ 1 , 2 ]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for nearly 70% of the global burden, reflecting persistent inequities in access to quality maternal healthcare services [ 1 , 3 ]. Despite global commitments under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), progress in reducing maternal mortality has been uneven, particularly in high-burden countries [ 3 ]. The continuum of maternal healthcare services utilization has been widely recognized as an effective strategy for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. This approach emphasizes the integrated utilization of essential services across pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period, including antenatal care (ANC), skilled delivery, and postnatal care (PNC) [ 4 , 5 ]. Completion of this continuum is critical, as discontinuities at any stage significantly increase the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [ 5 , 6 ]. In line with this, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a minimum of eight ANC contacts during pregnancy to optimize health outcomes [ 7 ]. However, evidence from LMICs indicates that many women do not complete this continuum, with substantial drop-offs between service components [ 5 , 8 ]. Nigeria remains one of the countries most affected by poor maternal health outcomes, contributing disproportionately to the global burden. Recent estimates indicate that Nigeria accounts for over one-quarter (28.3%) of all maternal deaths worldwide, with approximately 8,200 deaths and a maternal mortality ratio of 1,047 per 100,000 live births [ 3 ]. Although a considerable proportion of women access at least one ANC visit, only 43% of births are assisted by skilled birth providers, while 39% of births occur in health facilities and 59% take place at home [ 9 – 11 ]. These discontinuities underscore systemic challenges within the healthcare system, as well as socioeconomic and cultural barriers influencing maternal healthcare utilization. Nigerian women from poor households face financial barriers to accessing care, including direct healthcare costs and transportation expenses, while those living in rural and underserved communities experience greater difficulties due to long distances, poor road networks, limited transportation, and inadequate availability of skilled providers and health facilities [ 12 – 15 ]. In addition, women's autonomy in healthcare decision-making and the influence of husbands and other family members often shape decisions regarding when and where to seek maternal healthcare [ 12 , 16 , 17 ]. Sociocultural preferences for home delivery and the use of traditional birth attendants further contribute to lower utilization of skilled and facility-based maternity care [ 14 , 17 , 18 ]. Several studies have identified multiple determinants of maternal healthcare utilization in Nigeria and similar settings. Individual-level factors such as maternal education, parity, employment status, and media exposure, alongside household and contextual characteristics including wealth status, place of residence, and regional disparities, have been consistently associated with service uptake [ 19 , 20 ]. Additionally, women’s autonomy and decision-making capacity particularly regarding financial access play a crucial role in influencing healthcare utilization [ 19 , 21 ]. However, much of the existing evidence has focused on isolated components of maternal healthcare rather than examining the continuum of care as a composite outcome. Therefore, this study aims to examine the determinants of continuum of maternal healthcare utilization in Nigeria using data from the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS). Specifically, it operationalizes maternal health service utilization as a composite outcome encompassing antenatal care attendance, place of delivery, and postnatal care for both mother and child, and applies a multilevel multinomial logistic regression framework to identify individual, household, and contextual factors associated with varying levels of utilization. Methods Study design and data source This study is a secondary analysis of data from the 2024 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS), a nationally representative cross-sectional household survey conducted by the National Population Commission of Nigeria with technical support from the Demographic Health Survey (DHS) program and funding from the United States Agency for International Development (USAID). The NDHS employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design. In the first stage, enumeration areas are selected as primary sampling units, stratified by state and urban or rural residence. In the second stage, households within selected clusters are systematically sampled. All women aged 15–49 years who were usual residents of selected households or who slept in the household the night before the survey were eligible for interview. The analytic sample for this study comprised 13,596 women with a recent live birth (weighted n = 14,183) for whom complete data on all outcome components were available. Full details of the NDHS sampling procedures and questionnaires [ 22 ]. The NDHS received ethical approval from the National Health Research Ethics Committee of Nigeria. Secondary analysis of publicly available DHS data does not require additional institutional ethics approval. Variables Outcome variable. The primary outcome was Maternal Health Services Utilization (MHSU), operationalized as a composite indicator derived from four service components: (1) antenatal care attendance, classified as inadequate (fewer than eight visits) or adequate (eight or more visits), consistent with WHO recommendations; (2) place of delivery, classified as non-facility-based or facility-based; (3) postnatal care for the child, classified as received (yes) or not received (no); and (4) postnatal care for the mother, classified as received (yes) or not received (no). These four binary components were combined to create a three-category ordinal outcome: not utilized, defined as women who did not use any of the four service components; partially utilized, defined as women who used one, two, or three of the four components; and adequately utilized, defined as women who used all four components. The not-utilized category served as the reference group in all regression analyses. Independent variables. Individual-level covariates included: age group (15–24, 25–34, 35 or older), education level (none, primary, secondary or higher), current marital status (unmarried, married), current employment status (not employed, employed), birth interval (less than 24 months, 24 months or more, not applicable for first births), parity (continuous), media exposure (not exposed, exposed), decision-making autonomy (not a big problem, a problem), and medical insurance coverage (no, yes). Media exposure was defined as exposure to radio or television. Decision-making autonomy was operationalized as a binary variable indicating whether the respondent reported that obtaining permission to seek medical care or obtaining the money needed for treatment was a big problem; women reporting either barrier was classified as having a decision-making problem. Household and contextual variables included: wealth index (poor, middle, rich), place of residence (urban, rural), distance to health facility (not a big problem, big problem), and sex of household head (male, female). Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, and the distribution of MHSU was examined across all predictors. Weighted chi-squared tests for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for parity were used to assess bivariate associations with MHSU. All estimates accounted for the complex survey design using sampling weights, clustering, and stratification. Multilevel multinomial logistic regression was used to model the three-category MHSU outcome while accounting for the clustered structure of the DHS data, in which women are nested within enumeration areas. Before fitting the main models, a null model containing only the random intercept (Model 0) was estimated to assess the extent to which clustering at the enumeration area level contributed to variation in MHSU. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was computed for each outcome category relative to the reference category using the formula: I C C = σ u 2 σ u 2 + π 2 3 where π²/3 ≈ 3.29 represents the variance of the standard logistic distribution. An ICC greater than 0.10 was considered indicative of meaningful clustering, justifying the use of multilevel modelling [ 23 ]. The multilevel multinomial model is expressed as: n ( P ( Y i j = k ) P ( Y i j = B ) ) = β 0 k + X i k ′ β k + u i k where P ( Y i k = k ) is the probability of subject i in category k , X i k ′ β k are the fixed effects for predictors X on category k , u i k is the random effect for individual i in category k and u i ~ N ( 0 , σ u 2 ) . Multivariable analyses were conducted sequentially across three models. Model 1 included individual-level variables only; Model 2 included household and contextual variables only; and Model 3 included all variables simultaneously. Potential confounders identified based on theoretical considerations and existing literature were considered for inclusion regardless of their statistical significance in bivariate analyses. All variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were considered eligible for inclusion in the multivariable models. Model comparisons were done using Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC). Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and variables with VIF < 5 were included in the final model. Results are expressed as relative risk ratios (RRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all multivariable analyses. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis. All analyses were conducted in Stata 15.0, incorporating survey weights and the complex sampling design throughout. Results Sample characteristics A total of 13,596 women of reproductive age were included in the analysis (weighted n = 14,183). In this sample, almost half were aged 25–34 years (49.0%), nearly half of the participants (47.1%) had no formal education, and 41.5% had attained secondary or higher education. Almost all respondents were married (95.6%), and more than half (56.2%) were currently employed. Media exposure was approximately equal, and the median parity was almost 3 (IQR: 2–5). Most women (87.5%) reported that decision-making was not a major problem, and most respondents (97.5%) had no medical insurance. At the household level, nearly half (47.8%) of respondents were classified as poor. The majority (62.5%) resided in rural areas, and only 27.7% reported that distance to a health facility was a significant problem. Most households were male headed (89.7%). Discussion This study examined the determinants of the continuum of maternal healthcare service utilization among women of reproductive age in Nigeria using nationally representative data from the 2024 NDHS. The findings reveal that the majority of women in Nigeria fail to complete the full continuum of maternal care: 44.0% did not utilize any of the four assessed service components, and only 11.6% achieved adequate utilization of all four. These patterns reflect a pervasive failure of continuity across the care pathway that is not unique to Nigeria but is characteristic of sub-Saharan Africa more broadly. Studies from Ethiopia and Tanzania have similarly documented low completion of the maternal care continuum, largely attributable to drop-offs between antenatal care and skilled delivery, and between delivery and postnatal care [ 24 , 25 ]. The Nigerian evidence presented here reinforces that improving maternal health outcomes will require strategies focused not only on expanding access to individual services but on sustaining women's engagement across the entire continuum of care. Education emerged as the strongest individual-level determinant of maternal health service utilization in this study, a finding consistent with a large body of evidence from comparable settings. Women with secondary or higher education were over twelve times more likely to achieve adequate utilization in the fully adjusted model, and the magnitude of this association exceeded that of any other predictor. This is consistent with findings from Ghana, where higher educational attainment is strongly associated with increased antenatal care attendance and skilled birth attendance [ 26 ] and with evidence from Uganda and Kenya showing that education enhances women's capacity to recognize obstetric complications, navigate health systems, and demand quality care [ 27 , 28 ]. The attenuation of the education effect between Model 1 and Model 3, from an RRR of 31.71 to 12.10, is itself an important finding. It indicates that the strength of the association was reduced after accounting for household wealth, urban-rural residence, and other covariates included in the fully adjusted model. However, because this study used a cross-sectional association model and did not conduct formal mediation analysis, this attenuation should not be interpreted as evidence that household wealth or urban residence mediates the relationship between education and maternal health service utilization. The persistence of a strong, association between education and utilization after adjusting for these covariates underscores the importance of educational attainment as a key correlate of maternal health service utilization in Nigeria. Household wealth demonstrated a strong and consistent association with maternal healthcare utilization across all models, though its effect attenuated substantially in the combined model, reflecting the confounding contribution of individual-level factors. In the household-only model, rich women were over 48 times more likely to achieve adequate utilization compared to poor women, a finding consistent with evidence from Uganda and Burkina Faso where financial constraints, including direct costs such as user fees and transport, as well as indirect costs such as time away from work and childcare, are identified as major barriers among women of lower economic status [ 29 , 30 ]. Even in the fully adjusted model, the wealth gradient remained large and statistically significant, indicating that financial access is an independent constraint on maternal care-seeking that is not fully explained by the higher educational attainment, urban residence, and media exposure that tend to accompany higher household wealth. Rural residence was associated with lower utilization of maternal health services, consistent with findings across sub-Saharan Africa. In countries such as Malawi and Ethiopia, rural women report lower use of skilled delivery and postnatal care due to weaker health infrastructure and limited availability of skilled providers [ 31 , 32 ]. A notable finding in this study, however, is that self-reported distance to a health facility was not independently associated with utilization in either the household or the combined model after controlling for place of residence and other covariates. This pattern has been observed in other settings, including parts of Rwanda, where perceived quality of care, user costs, and sociocultural factors appear to exert greater influence on maternal care-seeking than physical distance alone [ 33 ]. The absence of a significant distance effect here may also reflect limitations in how DHS surveys capture access barriers: the binary categorization of distance as a problem does not capture variation in transport availability, road quality, travel time, or opportunity costs, all of which may be more proximate determinants of utilization than the simple self-reported measure. These considerations suggest that conventional survey measures may underestimate the true role of geographic access barriers and that their policy relevance should be interpreted with caution. Media exposure and employment were both positively associated with maternal health service utilization. Studies from Ethiopia and Senegal indicate that exposure to mass media improves awareness of maternal health services and promotes care-seeking behavior [ 34 , 35 ]. Employment likely reflects financial independence and decision-making capacity, facilitating access to care. Decision-making autonomy itself was a critical determinant; women who reported difficulties in making health-related decisions were less likely to use services, consistent with evidence from Mali, where limited autonomy constrains timely maternal care [ 36 ]. Interventions promoting women’s empowerment and involving men in maternal health education could therefore enhance service utilization. The inverse association between parity and adequate utilization is consistent with patterns reported in Ghana and Ethiopia, where higher-parity women are less likely to use formal maternal healthcare, possibly because prior uncomplicated deliveries reduce perceived need for professional care or because accumulated childcare burdens reduce the time and resources available for health service use [ 37 , 38 ]. This pattern is concerning because complications such as uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, and obstructed labor may be more, not less, likely with increasing parity in settings with limited obstetric capacity. Targeted messaging that emphasizes the specific and persistent risks associated with each pregnancy, regardless of prior birth experience, is essential to address this misconception. Women from female-headed households were more likely to utilize maternal health services, a finding that may reflect greater female control over household resources and health-related decisions in the absence of a male household head [ 39 ]. Although medical insurance coverage was very low in this sample (2.5%), it was significantly associated with higher utilization across all models. This is consistent with evidence from Ghana's National Health Insurance Scheme and Rwanda's community-based Mutuelles de Santé program, both of which have been shown to improve access to maternal care by reducing the financial barriers associated with direct out-of-pocket payments at the point of service [ 40 , 41 ]. The low coverage observed in this study reinforces the case for expanding insurance coverage in Nigeria, particularly through community-based and government-subsidized schemes that are accessible to rural and poor populations. Strengths and limitations This study has several important strengths. The use of nationally representative data from the 2024 NDHS provides a large and geographically diverse sample that supports generalizable inferences about the determinants of maternal healthcare utilization across Nigeria's heterogeneous population. The multilevel analytical framework appropriately accounts for the clustered structure of the DHS data and allows the quantification of community-level contributions to variation in utilization over and above individual and household predictors, as confirmed by the ICC values from the null model. The composite operationalization of the continuum of care as a single three-category outcome represents an improvement over studies that examine individual service components in isolation and enables a more comprehensive assessment of continuity of care. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of the NDHS prevents causal inference; the associations reported here should be interpreted as evidence of factors statistically associated with utilization rather than as evidence of causal effects. Self-reported ANC attendance is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the binary classification of adequate ANC as eight or more visits, a threshold that may be difficult to recall accurately over the typical reference period. The composite outcome itself may mask heterogeneity in service use patterns across the four components; future studies could examine the pathways through the continuum more granularly. The multilevel framework captures variation across enumeration areas but cannot account for unmeasured community-level factors such as health worker availability, facility quality, and sociocultural norms around childbirth, all of which may contribute to the observed cluster-level variation. Finally, the low prevalence of medical insurance in the sample (2.5%) limits statistical power for the insurance effect estimate, and results in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution. Conclusion Maternal healthcare service utilization in Nigeria remains deeply suboptimal. Only one in nine women achieves adequate utilization of all four components of the maternal care continuum, and nearly half do not access any component at all. This study demonstrates that these patterns are not randomly distributed but are systematically shaped by education, household wealth, place of residence, women's decision-making autonomy, media exposure, employment, and health insurance coverage. The consistent and independent effects of these determinants across all three models confirm that inequities in maternal care utilization in Nigeria are embedded in multiple and interacting layers of social, economic, and structural disadvantage. Addressing these inequities will require interventions that go well beyond conventional supply-side improvements in facility availability. Sustained investment in girls’ education is essential, not only as a direct determinant of maternal health service use but as a pathway to better economic outcomes, greater autonomy, and improved capacity to navigate health systems. Financial protection mechanisms, including expanded community-based and national health insurance coverage, are needed to reduce the cost barriers that prevent poor women from completing the continuum of care. Communication strategies through mass media and community health workers should be sustained and expanded, with particular attention to messages that address the specific risks of each pregnancy, regardless of prior birth experience and that engage men and families in supporting maternal care-seeking. Rural areas require targeted investment in infrastructure, skilled workforce deployment, and community health worker programs to reduce the utilization gap between urban and rural populations. Taken together, the evidence from this study points to the need for a comprehensive, multisectoral approach that simultaneously addresses structural barriers, strengthens women's empowerment, and ensures that financial and geographic access to the full continuum of maternal care is equitable across all segments of the Nigerian population.
