Using interactive learning activities to predict learning outcomes in medical education: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Guang Xu, David K Strom, Sumathilatha Sakthi-Velavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActive learning methods are increasingly used in medical education to enhance engagement and retention; however, limited evidence links performance on embedded formative assessments to summative examination outcomes in large-group pre-clinical settings. Interactive Learning Activities (ILAs) incorporating low-stakes quizzes were implemented in a first-year Cardiopulmonary–Renal (CPR) systems course. This study aimed to determine whether ILA quiz performance was associated with summative examination outcomes and to explore student perceptions across academic performance tiers.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods study was conducted across two academic years (2022: n = 156; 2023: n = 160). ILAs included pre- and post-session quizzes, contributing 5% to the final course grade. Linear regression and correlation analyses examined associations between mean ILA quiz scores and summative examination performance. Students were stratified into tertiles of final course grade for survey comparisons. An anonymous post-course survey collected Likert-scale and open-ended responses. Quantitative survey data were analyzed using chi-square tests, and qualitative responses were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework.ResultsThe ILA quiz scores demonstrated consistent moderate-to-strong positive correlations with overall examination performance in both cohorts (2022: R = 0.641; 2023: R = 0.672). Combined analysis confirmed a stable association (R = 0.632). Association was evident early in the semester, by the 6th ILA. Survey responses (25.6% response rate) indicated generally positive perceptions of ILAs. Lower-performing students reported comparatively high perceived learning benefits, whereas middle-tier students more frequently expressed concerns about quiz-related stress and workload (p < 0.05 for selected items). Qualitative themes highlighted engagement, retrieval practice, and timely feedback as strengths, with graded timing and stress as primary concerns.ConclusionPerformance on low-stakes ILA quizzes was consistently associated with summative examination outcomes in a large-group pre-clinical course. Embedded formative assessments may serve both instructional and early indicator functions, supporting early identification of students who may benefit from additional academic support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09496-x.Keywords: Active learning, Medical education, Formative assessment, Cognitive load, Student engagement, Academic performance

Background

Active learning strategies are instructional approaches that engage students directly in the learning process [1]. They have been associated with improved engagement and knowledge retention [2, 3]. Grounded in Constructivist Learning Theory and informed by Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), active learning emphasizes integrating new information with prior knowledge through experience, interaction, and reflection, positioning student engagement as essential to effective learning [4, 5]. Cognitive Load Theory further distinguishes between intrinsic, extraneous, and germane cognitive load, which together influence how learners process information and respond to instructional design.

In medical curricula, embedding structured assessment within active learning may provide pedagogical benefits and actionable insight into student progress. Common structured approaches include Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Team-Based Learning (TBL), both of which incorporate preparatory work and application exercises [6–9]. These models are typically implemented in small-group settings and may be difficult to scale within large preclinical courses.

A defining component of TBL and other active learning models is the integration of both individual and team low-stakes assessments [6]. These assessments support retrieval practice and provide formative feedback to identify gaps in understanding [10–14]. However, fewer studies have examined whether assessments embedded within active learning are associated with objective summative outcomes [10, 15–17]. This gap is particularly relevant in early medical curricula, where timely identification of students who may benefit from additional academic support is important.

To address this gap, Marian University College of Osteopathic Medicine implemented Interactive Learning Activities (ILAs) within the Cardiopulmonary–Renal (CPR) course. These sessions incorporated pre- and post-activity quizzes and were informed by principles consistent with Constructivist and Cognitive Load Theories to promote active engagement while managing cognitive load [4, 5].

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether students’ performance on low-stakes ILA quizzes was associated with summative examination outcomes in the CPR course. The secondary objectives were to evaluate student perceptions of ILAs and associated quizzes across performance tiers and to identify opportunities to refine instructional and assessment strategies. Through quantitative and qualitative analyses, this study aimed to examine the role of embedded formative assessments within a large-group active learning model.

Methods

Design and implementation of ILAs

At MU–WCOM, the CPR course is a first-year systems course that integrates multiple disciplines and is supported by several summative assessments that contribute to the final grade.

To support learning, faculty incorporated 16 ILAs, delivered weekly alongside traditional lectures (Supplementary Table 1). These sessions were designed to promote active problem-solving and self-assessment [18]. Students received preparatory materials prior to each ILA.

During sessions, faculty presented clinical cases in a large-group format with structured opportunities for brief peer discussion and faculty-guided clarification [19]. Faculty elicited volunteer responses, probed reasoning, and provided expert clarification during the session. ILAs typically included case-based discussion and, where appropriate, use of audience response tools or brief small-group interaction.

Depending on the topic, one or more content experts facilitated each ILA (Supplementary Table 1). The format varied by session but typically included discussion of one to four clinical cases.

While sharing certain features with TBL, such as preparatory work, case application, and embedded assessment, ILAs did not include permanent teams and therefore constitute a distinct large-group active-learning format. The design was informed by principles consistent with CLT, including content segmentation, alignment between preparatory and in-session materials, scaffolded questioning, and real-time feedback [5, 20, 21]. In this context, intrinsic load was determined by content complexity; extraneous load was addressed through structured session design and alignment of materials; and germane load was supported through retrieval practice and case-based integration.

Each ILA included a five-question pre- and post-quiz composed of multiple-choice items aligned with session objectives. The pre-quiz assessed students’ preparation, whereas the post-quiz evaluated learning gains. Quizzes were administered individually, and the quiz scores accounted for 5% of the overall course grade. Scores and answer rationales were released within one to two days to provide timely feedback. The quizzes were brief and consistently formatted to reinforce retrieval practice while minimizing extraneous cognitive load [20]. However, features such as timing and grading may have contributed additional extraneous load for some learners, particularly when preparation time was limited. The quizzes were open-book, open-notes, consistent with prior work on formative assessment [14]. Multidisciplinary facilitation, embedded assessments, and interactive pacing supported integration of content within the ILAs [22].

Prior course evaluations indicated that students generally viewed ILAs positively, although opinions about the graded ILA quizzes were mixed. Some students also perceived quizzes as a source of stress or limited instructional value. These differing perceptions highlighted the need to examine both the association between ILA quiz performance and summative outcomes, as well as student perspectives across academic performance levels.

Study design and setting

We conducted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study at MU-WCOM [23]. The Marian University Institutional Review Board reviewed the protocol and granted exemption under federal regulations (IRB protocol CR-S22.159).

Participants

We collected de-identified performance data from first-year osteopathic medical students enrolled in the CPR course in 2022 (n = 156) and 2023 (n = 160). Only students who completed the course were included. ILA scores were excluded for excused absences, and no demographic identifiers were linked to the data.

Quantitative analysis

A correlational design was used to examine the relationship between students’ ILA scores and summative examination performance, consistent with the study’s primary objective. Linear regression was used to evaluate the association between average ILA quiz scores and examination scores. Additional analyses examined associations between individual ILAs and their corresponding examination blocks. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Analyses were performed using SPSS, and data visualization was conducted using GraphPad Prism. Assumptions of linear regression, including linearity and approximate normality of the variables, were assessed visually using histograms and scatterplots. Survey development and administration.

We developed an online survey in Qualtrics XM comprising 12 items: 11 Likert-scale questions assessing students’ perceptions of ILAs and associated quizzes, and one open-ended item for additional feedback. Items were developed based on course objectives and reviewed by three faculty members for content validity. The survey was pilot-tested and revised for clarity. Because survey items assessed multiple constructs (e.g., engagement, stress, perceived value), internal consistency reliability (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not calculated. Survey questionnaire

Students were stratified into tertiles (cohorts A, B, and C) based on final course grade to explore differences in performance across levels. Surveys were distributed anonymously at the end of the semester. Participation was voluntary, and no identifying data were collected. Likert-scale responses were treated as ordinal data. Descriptive statistics (median, interquartile range, mean, and standard deviation) were calculated. Group differences across academic tiers were examined using chi-square tests.

Qualitative analysis

Open-ended responses were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework [24]. Two researchers independently coded responses, developed a codebook, and identified themes through an iterative consensus process [25]. This analysis supported interpretation of student perceptions and identification of areas for instructional improvement.

Thematic analysis of open-ended survey data

Positive themes included appreciation for high-quality faculty instruction, knowledge gained from structured case discussions, and the audience response system. Negative themes included quiz preparation, concerns regarding graded and timed quizzes, mandatory attendance, and perceived time constraints. Additionally, cohort B reported the greatest dissatisfaction with stress and workload. Representative comments reflected perceived tension between formative intent and performance pressure.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a consistent positive association between ILA quiz performance and summative examination outcomes across two consecutive cohorts. These findings support prior research linking formative assessment with summative performance in medical education [26].

The ILAs were designed to help learners focus on clinically relevant problem-solving while segmenting complex material into manageable components. The integration of brief quizzes may have supported retrieval practice and metacognitive reflection, both associated with improved knowledge consolidation [10–14, 18].

Grounded in Constructivist Learning Theory and principles of CLT, the ILAs were structured to promote active integration of prior knowledge while minimizing extraneous cognitive load [4, 5].

From a cognitive load perspective, the ILAs were intended to balance intrinsic load associated with complex content, reduce extraneous load through structured delivery, and support germane load through retrieval practice and case-based reasoning. Although causality cannot be inferred, these findings suggest that structured formative assessment within active learning may align with principles associated with improved performance on high-stakes assessments.

ILA quiz performance as a potential indicator of performance

The ILA quiz performance was associated with summative assessment outcomes. This association was evident early in the semester (6th ILA), suggesting potential value as an early indicator of student performance.

Early identification of students who may benefit from targeted academic support is an important consideration in medical education [27]. While causality cannot be established, the observed associations suggest that quiz performance may serve as a bellwether and provide a useful indicator of student progression. These findings suggest that ILA performance may provide faculty with a reliable tool to address student performance in a timely manner, thereby advocating for changes that can improve it.

Additionally, performance on ILA correlated well with their corresponding examination blocks.

These findings suggest alignment between ILA content and summative assessment expectations.

These findings highlight the potential dual role of embedded quizzes: reinforcing learning during active sessions and providing insight into evolving academic performance.

Importantly, these findings reflect association rather than causation due to shared underlying factors such as academic preparation, study strategies, or motivation. This suggests that ILA quiz performance may be associated with individual exam and overall course performance. This potential indicator complements their instructional value and highlights the importance of structured, low-stakes assessments in content-dense curricula.

Instructional adjustments and their impact

Comparison across academic years revealed differences in the strength of associations between individual ILAs and examination blocks. Instructional modifications after AY 2022, including adjustments to discussion time, scheduling, and case complexity, coincided with changes in the strength of certain ILA–exam associations. Certain ILAs (1, 4, 6, 11, 12, 14, 15) were consistently associated with exam performance across both years.

For example, ILA-10 (ECG interpretation) demonstrated a stronger association with Exam 3 performance in AY 2023 after incorporation of more foundational scaffolded examples. This finding suggests improved alignment between session structure and assessment expectations, although causality cannot be confirmed.

Conversely, some ILAs (2, 3, 5, 13, and 16) that were significant in AY 2022 were not statistically significant in AY 2023. Potential contributing factors may include faculty transitions, variation in quiz item distribution, or changes in examination emphasis.

These observations are consistent with prior research emphasizing alignment between instructional design and assessment structure [28]. However, because multiple design variables changed simultaneously (faculty composition, case number, polling use, scaffolding), the influence of specific ILA components could not be interpreted.

Student perceptions

Survey findings demonstrated generally positive perceptions of ILAs across all cohorts. These findings are consistent with the literature, indicating that interactive and technology-enhanced engagement strategies can improve learner satisfaction [29, 30]. Differences in perceived benefits were observed across academic tiers.

Cohort C reported comparably high perceived benefit from ILAs. This may reflect the value of structured case discussion and guided retrieval practice for learners who benefit from additional scaffolding. Cohort B, by contrast, reported comparatively lower perceived benefit on Likert-scale items and contributed proportionally more qualitative comments describing quiz-related stress. These differences suggest that learners at different performance tiers may experience formative assessments differently, reflecting differences in how intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load are experienced across groups.

Lower-performing students may benefit from structured scaffolding that reduces intrinsic load, whereas middle-tier students may experience increased extraneous load from timed, graded quizzes, which may diminish perceived benefit. Differences also emerged regarding preferred case volume per session. Across cohorts, three cases per ILA were the most frequently selected option. However, Cohort C demonstrated relatively greater preference for one or two cases compared with other tiers. This finding may reflect differences in management of intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load or pacing preferences across performance groups [5]. Preference for fewer cases may reflect attempts to manage intrinsic load, whereas preference for more cases may support germane load through repeated application and integration. These preferences reflect differences in management of intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load across groups [5, 31].

Thematic insights from student feedback

Qualitative responses reflected both perceived instructional value and stress related to the graded, timed structure of quizzes.

Students described ILAs as beneficial for integrating complex material and identifying gaps in understanding. One student noted:

“They helped me find gaps in my knowledge and how to apply them.”

The comments suggest that students perceived quizzes as mechanisms for retrieval practice and metacognitive reflection, consistent with literature supporting the role of frequent low-stakes testing in reinforcing learning [10–14, 32, 33].

Students also valued the integration of interactive elements, particularly Poll Everywhere. One respondent stated:

“Allowing us to engage through poll everywhere challenges us individually, but also working together with classmates gives an opportunity for collaborative learning when facilitated appropriately.”

Such comments highlight the perceived benefit of combining individual accountability with peer interaction, aligning with principles of active learning and social constructivism [4]. The ILAs that used this tool incorporated real-time modifications based on student performance, offering a personalized learning experience that adjusted to individual needs [34]. Students likely valued the audience response system for promoting engagement and reinforcing concepts, consistent with prior literature [29, 30].

Students described that ILAs promoted accountability:

“I loved that the ILAs kept me accountable with keeping up with the lectures in time.”

These responses suggest that ILAs functioned as structured checkpoints within the course.

The top negative themes included concerns about quiz-related stress, particularly grading and timing. A student noted:

“The timed nature of the quizzes early in the morning increased my own personal stress as the semester went on.”

Some respondents questioned the value of graded quizzes:

“When it was ungraded, I felt like I could sit and learn, talk to my classmates, and figure things out.”

These responses reflect a tension between accountability and psychological comfort, a balance frequently discussed in the formative assessment literature [35–37]. From a cognitive load perspective, timed quizzes and grading may increase extraneous load, while performance pressure may further amplify overall cognitive burden, particularly when sessions occur early in the day [5, 20].

Qualitative responses suggested variation across academic tiers. Cohort C students more frequently described ILAs as helpful for clarifying difficult concepts, whereas cohort B students contributed proportionally more comments related to stress and workload.

Although qualitative sample sizes were limited, this pattern aligns with the quantitative finding that perceived benefit varied by academic tier, suggesting that formative assessment structures may be experienced differently across performance groups.

These findings highlight the importance of careful calibration in formative assessment design. While retrieval practice and accountability may enhance learning, excessive emphasis on grading or timing may diminish perceived benefit for some learners [10–14].

From a cognitive load perspective, optimizing ILAs may involve reducing extraneous load (e.g., adjusting quiz timing, reconsidering grading pressure, and clarifying expectations), calibrating intrinsic load (e.g., aligning case complexity with learner progression), and supporting germane load through structured retrieval practice and feedback. Such adjustments may improve both learning outcomes and learner perceptions across performance tiers.

Limitations

Despite these associations, the study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single institution and within a single preclinical course, which may limit generalizability. A control group using traditional instruction was not included, which limits direct comparison of instructional approaches. The correlational design precludes causal inference and observed associations may be influenced by unmeasured factors such as prior academic metrics (e.g., MCAT, undergraduate GPA), study strategies, or motivation. These factors were not directly measured and may confound observed associations. Internal consistency of the survey instrument was not formally assessed. Survey response rates were modest and self-reported survey responses introduced the possibility of response bias, as students may have been influenced by their overall course experience when evaluating ILAs [38]. Long-term outcomes, such as retention across subsequent courses or performance on licensing examinations, were not assessed.

Future directions

This study contributes to the growing literature on scalable active learning models in medical education. Future research should examine generalizability across multiple institutions and curricular contexts to determine whether similar associations emerge in diverse educational environments. Longitudinal studies tracking student performance across subsequent courses or licensing examinations may clarify whether early formative assessment signals persist over time.

Future investigations incorporating multivariate modeling or controlled variation in quiz structure (e.g., graded versus ungraded formats) may help clarify the independent contribution of embedded formative assessment. Exploring faculty perspectives may provide insight into implementation challenges and sustainability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provided empirical evidence that ILA performance was consistently and positively associated with both examination outcomes and final course grades, suggesting that embedded formative assessments within large-group active learning sessions may offer insight into student progression within a content-dense medical course. While most students reported positive experiences with ILAs, qualitative responses also identified stress related to graded, timed quizzes, highlighting the importance of thoughtful calibration in formative assessment design. Variation in perceptions across academic tiers further suggests that formative assessment structures may be experienced differently by learners with different profiles. These findings contribute to ongoing efforts to develop scalable active learning models that balance instructional rigor with learner support. Future refinement of ILAs, guided by performance data and student feedback, may strengthen alignment between instructional design and assessment outcomes while preserving student engagement.
