Squeezed-state radiation in shockwave scattering: QCD-Gravity double copy


Abstract
Gluon and graviton radiation in strong field shockwave scattering are described by effective Lipatov vertices, with the graviton Lipatov vertex proportional to the bilinear of its QCD counterpart. We show here that the n-particle gluon radiation spectrum can be described as a generalized Susskind-Glogower (gSG) squeezed coherent state and discuss the properties of such squeezed states. The double copy structure of the radiative frameworks suggests that multi-graviton radiation can be similarly described as a gSG state. We examine the physical parameter space and show that very large squeezing parameters ∼ ln ⁡ ( n ¯ ) \sim\ln({\bar n}) (where n ¯ {\bar{n}} is the mean graviton occupancy) are feasible for nearly minimal uncertainty configurations of the gSG state. Quantum noise in the corresponding gravitational wave spectrum is enhanced above the sensitivity of current and future gravitational wave detectors. Our results point to the importance of a comprehensive study of the strong field Lipatov regime of gravitational radiation.

While the scattering of classical shockwaves is natural to consider in Einstein gravity, it is at face value deeply surprising in QCD, where one expects the physics to be fundamentally quantum. It is hence important at the outset to address how such a (semi-)classical picture arises in QCD before proceeding further with a discussion of this double copy as more than a mathematical curiosity. The relevant kinematics is the high energy multi-Regge asymptotics of perturbative QCD, where

The possibility that copious gravitational radiation can be emitted in a laser-like squeezed state is very interesting because quantum noise can be strongly enhanced in this radiation. [

In the CGC/Glasma, it is unclear whether it is meaningful to talk about particle/energy number states of gluons, firstly, because the fields are strongly nonlinear, and secondly, due to the difficulty in defining a gauge invariant order parameter for the condensate. (\Delta p)^{2}\approx D(r)(1-p)
are used in place of the series expression, producing smooth curves all the way to
p\to 1
. We observe that the two regions join continuously since the values from the asymptotic formulas and those from the numerical estimation of the sums agree to a good degree.

For a typical gravitational wave measured by LIGO,
{\bar{n}}=4\cdot 10^{36}

[
96
]
. The squeezing parameter from our computation is then
|\xi|\sim 42
; this enhances the amplitude for quantum noise from
\sim 10^{-35}
m to
\sim 10^{-17}
m, which is significantly above the required sensitivity. Since the gravitational flux is proportional to the square of the strain amplitude of the source
[
97
]
, Lipatov radiation from primordial sources corresponding to much larger strain amplitudes, such as supermassive black hole mergers, or extreme mass ratio inspirals, can further bring signals of quantum noise into detectable ranges.

Whether the noise measured is truly quantum is however a much more difficult question. A nice discussion of this issue can be found in
[
24
]
. The gSG squeezed state characterizing both the Glasma and
gravitational radiation in shockwave collisions is fundamentally a quantum state because it is an eigenstate of the Barnett-Pegg phase operator. However, the corresponding probability distribution is a negative binomial distribution (satisfying super-Poisson statistics), which could be either classical or quantum, and therefore cannot be easily distinguished from a distribution of purely classical origins. In contrast, sub-Poisson statistics are a smoking gun for quantum effects. Despite significant recent developments in novel proposals to detect single gravitons
[
96
,
92
,
58
,
82
]
, these too may be inconclusive, as argued in
[
24
]
. An interesting question in this regard is whether an unambiguous sub-Poissonian signal due to quantum corrections to the gSG state can be detected by measuring
correlations between “graviton clicks” in two or more such spatially separated detectors
[
74
]
, analogously to measurements of Hanbury-Brown–Twiss (HBT) correlations. Baym’s Zakopane lectures
[
15
]
, and a more recent review by Aspect
[
9
]
provide excellent introductions to HBT interferometry from sub-femtometer scales to cosmological ones.

5
Summary and Outlook

We discussed here a double copy of gluon and graviton radiation from shockwave collisions in QCD and Einstein gravity, respectively. The gravitational effective Lipatov vertex controlling the emission of gravitons from the interaction of two reggeized gravitons emitted in the shockwave collision is proportional to a bilinear of its QCD counterpart. In QCD, multi-particle production in the CGC EFT can be understood as
t
-channel iterations of the underlying
2\rightarrow 3
process, with the
n
-particle probability obtained by averaging over the color charge distributions of fast (“large x”) partons in each of the shockwaves. This distribution is a negative binomial distribution (NBD), and we showed that it can be described as a generalized Susskind-Glogower (gSG) intermediate phase squeezed coherent state, characterized by the average multiplicity
{\bar{n}}
and the NBD parameter
r
. Both of these quantities are computed in the CGC EFT.

We discussed the possibility that the non-equilibrium Glasma formed as a result of such “laser-like” copious gluon production (with
{\bar{n}}\sim Q_{S}^{2}S_{\perp}/\alpha_{S}
) is a superfluid state, as is the case for overoccupied ultracold Bose gases. Further, if the state is a strongly squeezed gSG state, one possible consequence (discussed previously for far-from-equilibrium dynamics in condensed matter physics) is the rapid decoherence and thermalization of Glasma. This possibility is very intriguing in the context of the rapid thermalization and strong flow observed for the QGP formed in ultrarelativistic heavy-ion collisions.

The shockwave computation in gravity of single inclusive graviton radiation in the Lipatov regime follows exactly analogously to the QCD case. It is therefore very plausible that the resulting n-graviton spectrum follows an NBD distribution as for the Glasma, and is similarly a gSG squeezed state. Since the NBD parameter
r
in this case is unknown, we explored the behavior of the squeezing parameter
\xi
for small deviations
\delta
from the minimum uncertainty state, as a function of
{\bar{n}}
and
r
. For
r>1
, and

{\bar{n}}\gg r
, large values of
\xi
are feasible. In particular, we find that
\xi\propto ln({\bar{n}})
. Since
{\bar{n}}
can be very large (
\sim 10^{36}
for gravitational waves detected by LIGO), our result is very suggestive that variances due to fluctuations on the quantum scale
L_{\rm Planck}\sim 10^{-35}
meters can be significantly enhanced towards scales that are accessible at current and future gravitational wave detectors.

Our conclusion relies on the assumption that the double copy motivated NBD/gSG description holds for multi-graviton radiation in strong gravitational fields. It will therefore be important to extend our shockwave computations of Lipatov radiation in the dilute-dilute and (ideally) dilute-dense analytical frameworks to two-and multi-graviton emissions. In addition to confirming that this correspondence is robust, it will be useful to examine closely how two-graviton correlations can be modified by Hawking-type quantum effects, and whether these lead to sub-Poisson statistics. Not least, since our computations are for ultrarelativistic shockwave collisions, it will be important to understand the
1/\gamma
corrections to this picture, where
\gamma
is the Lorentz factor.

To prove Eq. (

\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)^{n}|0\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(n+r)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{n!}|n\rangle~.

(5.1)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)^{n}\,|n\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,z^{n}\,|n\rangle

(5.2)

which readily gives the relation in Eq. (
3.8
).

Proof of (
5.1
) by induction

We first prove the general relation

\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}~|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+r}~|n\rangle~.

(5.3)

To do so, denote
\hat{O}\equiv\hat{N}+r
, for which we have

\hat{O}|n\rangle=(\hat{N}+r)|n\rangle=(n+r)|n\rangle~.

(5.4)

We want to find the action of an operator
\hat{Q}
on
|n\rangle
such that
\hat{Q}^{2}=\hat{O}
. Note that
\hat{O}
is diagonal on the Fock basis. Its matrix elements takes the following form

[\hat{O}]=\begin{pmatrix}r&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&1+r&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&2+r&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&3+r&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}~.

(5.5)

Now because the square root of a diagonal matrix, is simply the square root of the individual diagonal elements, the matrix elements of
\hat{Q}
take the form

[\hat{Q}]=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{r}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
0&\sqrt{1+r}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&\sqrt{2+r}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&\sqrt{3+r}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}~.

(5.6)

Therefore,
\hat{Q}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+r}~|n\rangle
. This proves Eq. (
5.3
).

We will now use this in the proof of Eq. (
5.1
) by induction. Assume that Eq. (
5.1
) is true for an arbitrary but fixed positive integer
m
. We can then show that it is true for
m+1
as well:

\displaystyle\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)^{m+1}|0\rangle=\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)^{m}|0\rangle=\left(a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+r)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{m!}|m\rangle

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+r)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{m!}\left(\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}\right)|m\rangle=a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\frac{(m+r)\Gamma(m+r)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{m!}|m\rangle~

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(m+r)\Gamma(m+r)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{m!}\sqrt{m+1}|m+1\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(m+r+1)}{\Gamma(r)}}\sqrt{(m+1)!}|m+1\rangle~,

(5.7)

which is the desired result proving Eq. (
3.8
).
Note that we used the identity Eq. (
5.3
) for
\sqrt{\hat{N}+r}|m\rangle
in the last term of the second line, and subsequently,
a^{\dagger}|m\rangle=\sqrt{m+1}|m+1\rangle
.

To prove Eq. (
3.10
), we begin by using the identities:

\displaystyle a|n\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle~,

\displaystyle(\hat{N}+r-1)^{-1/2}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=(n+r-1)^{-1/2}|n\rangle~,

\displaystyle\hat{A}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n+r-1}}|n-1\rangle~.

(5.8)

This gives

\displaystyle A|z;r\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-|z|^{2})^{r/2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,z^{n}\,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n+r-1}}\,|n-1\rangle.

(5.9)

Relabeling the summation index
n\mapsto n+1
yields

\displaystyle A|z;r\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-|z|^{2})^{r/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{\,n+1}\sqrt{\binom{n+r}{n+1}}\,\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{\sqrt{n+r}}\,|n\rangle.

(5.10)

We now use the binomial identity

\binom{n+r}{n+1}\,\frac{n+1}{n+r}=\binom{n+r-1}{n}.

(5.11)

Hence, the coefficient of
|n\rangle
becomes

(1-|z|^{2})^{r/2}\,z^{\,n+1}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-1}{n}}|n\rangle=z\,(1-|z|^{2})^{r/2}\,z^{n}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-1}{n}}|n\rangle.

(5.12)

But this is simply
z
times the coefficient of
|n\rangle
in the original state
|z;r\rangle
.
Therefore we have established Eq. (

Appendix B: Coherent states and Squeezed states

An excellent short pedagogical review of coherent and squeezed states is given in
[
10
]
.
Here we summarize some of the relevant results for this work for the convenience of readers; interested readers should however consult this review and the references within.

B.1: ‘Linear’ Coherent State in a Harmonic Oscillator

Some basic definitions:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{P}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}\hat{X}^{2}\,\,\,;\,\,\,[\hat{X},\hat{P}]=i\hbar\,\,\,;\,\,\,\hat{X}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,(a+a^{\dagger}),\quad\hat{P}=-i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}}\,(a-a^{\dagger}),

(5.13)

\displaystyle[a,a^{\dagger}]=1,\quad N=a^{\dagger}a,\quad H=\hbar\omega\left(N+\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

(5.14)

Fock basis:

|n\rangle=(a^{\dagger})^{n}/\sqrt{n!}\,|0\rangle\,\,,\,\,a|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}\,|n-1\rangle\,\,,\,\,a^{\dagger}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle~\,\,,\,\,\langle n|n\rangle=1~.

(5.15)

The last equality follows from
a^{n}(a^{\dagger})^{n}=(N+1)(N+2)\cdots(N+n)
with
N
being the number operator
N=a^{\dagger}a
and
\langle 0|0\rangle=1
.

Glauber coherent state states
|\alpha\rangle
are defined as eigenstates of the annihilation operator
a

\displaystyle a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle,\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}~,\qquad\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle=1~

(5.16)

which satisfy

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\alpha|a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha,\quad\langle N\rangle_{\alpha}\equiv\langle\alpha|N|\alpha\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}~,

(5.17)

The states saturate the Heisenberg uncertainty bound. To see this saturation, one can compute the variances (uncertainty) in position and momentum:
\langle\alpha|(\Delta X)^{2}|\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha|X^{2}|\alpha\rangle-\langle\alpha|X|\alpha\rangle^{2}
,
\langle\alpha|(\Delta P)^{2}|\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha|P^{2}|\alpha\rangle-\langle\alpha|P|\alpha\rangle^{2}
. For instance,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha|X|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\ \langle\alpha|(a+a^{\dagger})|\alpha\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\alpha+\alpha^{*})~,

(5.18)

\displaystyle\langle\alpha|X^{2}|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\ \langle\alpha|(a+a^{\dagger})^{2}|\alpha\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\left((\alpha+\alpha^{*})^{2}+1\right)~,

(5.19)

\displaystyle\langle\alpha|(\Delta X)^{2}|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}~.

(5.20)

and likewise for
\langle\alpha|(\Delta P)^{2}|\alpha\rangle
, to give

\displaystyle\langle(\Delta X)^{2}\rangle_{\alpha}\langle(\Delta P)^{2}\rangle_{\alpha}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4}~.

(5.21)

Expansion in Fock basis:

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle~,

(5.22)

The probability of finding the coherent state in an
n
-particle Fock state

|\langle n|\alpha\rangle|^{2}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}\;~\qquad(\text{Poisson distribution with mean }|\alpha|^{2}).

(5.23)

Displacement operator definition and properties:

\displaystyle D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha a^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}a}\,\,;\,\,D^{\dagger}(\alpha)aD(\alpha)=a+\alpha\,\,;\,\,D(\alpha+\beta)

\displaystyle=e^{\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha\beta^{*}-\alpha^{*}\beta)}D(\alpha)D(\beta).

(5.24)

The coherent state is defined via the displacement operator as

|\alpha\rangle=D(\alpha)|0\rangle
and satisfies the
non-orthogonality and overlap relations,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle=\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha^{\prime}|^{2}+\alpha^{*}\alpha^{\prime}\right)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|^{2}}e^{i{\rm Im\,}(\alpha^{*}\alpha^{\prime})},

(5.25)

and the completeness relation
\frac{1}{\pi}\int d^{2}\alpha\;|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|=\mathbb{I}
. This is derived using Eq. (
5.22
) and the identity
\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|=\mathbb{I}
.

B.2: Squeezed Coherent states in Harmonic Oscillator

Define the single mode squeezing operator

\displaystyle S_{\eta}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta a^{2}-\eta^{*}a^{\dagger 2}\right)}~,

(5.26)

which generates the squeezed state
|\eta\rangle=S_{\eta}|0\rangle
from the Fock vacuum. This state can also be represented as an expansion in the Fock basis. In order to do so, we will disentangle
S_{\eta}
into
SU(1,1)
basis elements. First define operators

\displaystyle K_{-}=\frac{1}{2}a^{2}~,\qquad K_{+}=\frac{1}{2}a^{\dagger 2}~,\qquad K_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}\right)~.,

(5.27)

which generate the
su(1,1)
algebra:
[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm}~,[K_{-},K_{+}]=2K_{0}
. Now define
\eta=|\eta|e^{i\phi}
. Then the
su(1,1)
disentangled form of
S_{\eta}
,
S_{\eta}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta a^{2}-\eta^{*}a^{\dagger 2}\right)}=e^{-(\eta K_{-}-\eta^{*}K_{+})}
is

\displaystyle S_{\eta}=\exp\!\big(\gamma\,K_{+}\big)\;\exp\!\big(-2\ln(\cosh|\eta|)\,K_{0}\big)\;\exp\!\big(-\gamma^{*}\,K_{-}\big)~,

(5.28)

with
\gamma=e^{-i\phi}\,\tanh(|\eta|)
.
In terms of
a,a^{\dagger}
the squeezing operator is then

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta a^{2}-\eta^{*}a^{\dagger 2}\right)}=\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-i\phi}\tanh(|\eta|)\,a^{\dagger 2}\big)\,\exp\!\big(-\ln\!\big(\cosh|\eta|\big)\,(a^{\dagger}a+\tfrac{1}{2})\big)\,\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{+i\phi}\tanh(|\eta|)\,a^{2}\big)~.

(5.29)

With this representation, we find

\displaystyle|\eta\rangle=S_{\eta}|0\rangle=\big(\cosh|\eta|\big)^{-1/2}\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-i\phi}\tanh(|\eta|)\,a^{\dagger 2}\Big)|0\rangle=\big(\cosh|\eta|\big)^{-1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{n}\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}|2n\rangle\,,

(5.30)

where we used
a^{\dagger n}|0\rangle=\sqrt{n!}|n\rangle
in the last equality. The probability of finding the state
|\eta\rangle
in an
2n
particle Fock state (by construction) is given by

\displaystyle p_{n}=|\langle 2n|\eta\rangle|^{2}=(\cosh|\eta|)^{-1}\frac{\tanh^{2n}(|\eta|)(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}~.

(5.31)

One can verify that this probability distribution (which is no longer Poisson) is properly normalized,
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}=1
. Further, upon inspection we find that this distribution is actually a negative binomial with parameters
r=1/2
and
p=\tanh^{2}|\eta|
. This follows simply from the binomial identity

\displaystyle\binom{n+\frac{1}{2}-1}{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}~.

(5.32)

We also present results for various expectation values in this state. First of all since only even Fock states contribute, we have
\langle{a}\rangle=0
. Consequently,
\langle{X}\rangle=\langle{P}\rangle=0
. The expectation of the number operator is
\langle{N}\rangle=\sinh^{2}|\eta|
. Further, for real
\eta
a simple calculation gives

\displaystyle\langle{a}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh|\eta|\tanh|\eta|}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}\tanh^{2n}|\eta|=\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2|\eta|).

(5.33)

The last result uses
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}p^{n}=p(1-p)^{-3/2}
with
p=\tanh^{2}|\eta|
.
Inserting into the quadrature variance formula:

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle a^{2}\rangle+\langle a^{\dagger 2}\rangle+2\langle N\rangle+1\right)~,\qquad\langle P^{2}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle a^{2}\rangle+\langle a^{\dagger 2}\rangle-2\langle N\rangle-1\right)~,

(5.34)

gives the result:

\displaystyle(\Delta X)^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{2|\eta|}~,\qquad(\Delta P)^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-2|\eta|},

(5.35)

Hence, the squeezing parameter
\xi=\frac{1}{4}\ln\left((\Delta P)^{2}/(\Delta X)^{2}\right)
is simply
\xi=-|\eta|
and the state is

minimum uncertainty
:
(\Delta X)^{2}(\Delta P)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}
.

Appendix C: Uncertainty relations for the gSG state

We will compute here the root mean square product
\Delta X\Delta P
(in other words, the uncertainty relation) for the generalized Susskind-Glogower state.
Defining

c_{n}=(1-|z|^{2})^{r/2}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,z^{n}\,,

(5.36)

we can write the gSG state in Eq. (
3.6
) as

|z;r\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}|n\rangle.

(5.37)

The expectation value of the number operator is (replacing further
|z|^{2}=p
),

\displaystyle\langle N\rangle\equiv{\bar{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n|c_{n}|^{2}=(1-p)^{r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\binom{n+r-1}{n}p^{n}=(1-p)^{r}\cdot\frac{rp}{(1-p)^{r+1}}=\frac{rp}{1-p}\,,

(5.38)

or equivalently
p={\bar{n}}/({\bar{n}}+r)
.
To compute
\Delta X
and
\Delta P
, we will first compute
the expectation value of the annihilation operator
a
in the gSG state. Since

a|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle,

(5.39)

we get

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle=\sum_{m,n}c_{m}^{*}c_{n}\langle m|a|n\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n-1}^{*}c_{n}\sqrt{n}\,,

(5.40)

and substituting Eq. (
5.36
) in this expression gives

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{r}z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{n\binom{n+r-2}{n-1}\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,p^{n-1}.

(5.41)

Defining

\displaystyle A_{1}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-2}{n-1}\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,p^{n-1}\,,

(5.42)

we can rewrite the expectation value as

\displaystyle\langle a\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{r}z\,A_{1}(p).

(5.43)

To the best of our knowledge, it is not possible to express
A_{1}(p)
in terms of known functions.

Likewise, the expectation of
a^{2}
in the gSG state gives

\displaystyle\langle a^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{r}z^{2}A_{2}(p)\,,

(5.44)

where

\displaystyle A_{2}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\sqrt{n(n-1)}\sqrt{\binom{n+r-3}{n-2}\binom{n+r-1}{n}}\,p^{n-2}\,.

(5.45)

Since the position and momentum operators are defined as

X=\frac{a+a^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad P=\frac{a-a^{\dagger}}{i\sqrt{2}}\,,

(5.46)

their expectation values in the gSG state are

\displaystyle\langle X\rangle=(1-p)^{r}A_{1}(p)\frac{z+z^{*}}{\sqrt{2}}~,~\qquad\langle P\rangle=(1-p)^{r}A_{1}(p)\frac{z-z^{*}}{i\sqrt{2}}~,

(5.47)

To calculate the corresponding variances, we also need to compute

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle a^{2}\rangle+\langle a^{\dagger 2}\rangle+2\langle N\rangle+1\right)~,\qquad\langle P^{2}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle a^{2}\rangle+\langle a^{\dagger 2}\rangle-2\langle N\rangle-1\right)~.

(5.48)

Plugging in Eqs. (
5.38
), (
5.43
) and (
5.44
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[(1-p)^{r}(z^{2}+z^{*2})A_{2}(p)+2\frac{rp}{1-p}+1\right],

(5.49)

\displaystyle\langle P^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[-(1-p)^{r}(z^{2}+z^{*2})A_{2}(p)+2\frac{rp}{1-p}+1\right].

(5.50)

Hence, the variances
\Delta X^{2}=\langle X^{2}\rangle-\langle X\rangle^{2}
,
\Delta P^{2}=\langle P^{2}\rangle-\langle P\rangle^{2}
, are

\displaystyle\Delta X^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{rp}{1-p}+\frac{(1-p)^{r}}{2}(z^{2}+z^{*2})A_{2}(p)-\frac{(1-p)^{2r}}{2}(z+z^{*})^{2}A_{1}(p)^{2}\,.

(5.51)

\displaystyle\Delta P^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{rp}{1-p}-\frac{(1-p)^{r}}{2}(z^{2}+z^{*2})A_{2}(p)+\frac{(1-p)^{2r}}{2}(z-z^{*})^{2}A_{1}(p)^{2}\,.

(5.52)

We see immediately that the state
|z;r\rangle
is in general not a minimum certainty state. However in the

limit, we should recover the minimum certainty Glauber coherent state. We will verify this below.
In order to take the

limit appropriately we need to rescale
z
(recall

was
|z|^{2}
) as

z=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{r}},\qquad p=\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{r}~,\qquad(\alpha~\text{fixed})~.

(5.53)

In this limit we have the following asymptotics for various terms that appear in the variances:

\displaystyle(1-p)^{r}

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}\left(1-\frac{|\alpha|^{4}}{2r}+\cdots\right),

(5.54)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{r}e^{|\alpha|^{2}}\left(1+\frac{|\alpha|^{4}+|\alpha|^{2}}{2r}+\cdots\right),

(5.55)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=re^{|\alpha|^{2}}\left(1+\frac{(|\alpha|^{2}+1)^{2}}{2r}+\cdots\right).

(5.56)

Plugging these expansions in the formula for the variances in (
5.51

\Delta X^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4r}\left(\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{*2}\right)+O(r^{-2})

(5.57)

\Delta P^{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4r}\left(\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{*2}\right)+O(r^{-2})

(5.58)

So that

\displaystyle\Delta X^{2}\Delta P^{2}=\frac{1}{4}+O(r^{-2})~,

(5.59)

Hence the leading deviation from the minimal uncertainty state appears at order

.

p<1
and diverges as
p\to 1
. In this regime, most of the contribution comes from the tail of the sum where
m
is large. Using

\binom{m+r-1}{m}\sqrt{m+r}\sim m^{r-1/2}/\Gamma(r)
, writing
p\sim e^{-\epsilon}
and approximating the sum with an integral we get

A_{1}\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(r)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{r-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-t\epsilon}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(r+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(r)\,\epsilon^{r+\frac{1}{2}}}.

(5.60)

Therefore,

(1-p)^{2r}p\,A_{1}(p)^{2}\approx\epsilon^{2r}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(r+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(r)^{2}\,\epsilon^{2r+1}}=\frac{\Gamma(r+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(r)^{2}\,\epsilon}.

(5.61)

Next we look at the sum
A_{2}(p)
defined in Eq. (
3.17
). Again, in the
p\to 1
limit, most the contribution comes from the tail of the sum. At large
m
, the factor
\sqrt{(m+r)(m+r+1)}
in the summand can be approximated as

\displaystyle\sqrt{(m+r)(m+r+1)}\approx m+r+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8(m+r)}~.

(5.62)

Plugging this into Eq. (
3.17
), the sum can be carried out in closed form,

\displaystyle A_{2}(p)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(1-p)^{-r}(p-2r-1)}{2(p-1)}-\frac{\,{}_{2}F_{1}(r,r;r+1;p)}{8r}~,

(5.63)

Along with the prefactor
(1-p)^{r}p
, we obtain the following asymptotics in small
\epsilon=1-p
:

\displaystyle(1-p)^{r}pA_{2}(p)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{r}{\epsilon}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-r\right)-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8(r-1)}\right)\epsilon+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)~.

(5.64)

Inserting these results into the expression of
(\Delta X)^{2}
and
(\Delta P)^{2}
in Eq. (
3.19
) and Eq. (
3.20
), the leading order results are

\displaystyle(\Delta X)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(2r-\frac{2\,\Gamma(r+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}}{\Gamma(r)^{2}}\right)\equiv\frac{C(r)}{\epsilon}~,

(5.65)

\displaystyle(\Delta P)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8(r-1)}\right)\epsilon\equiv D(r)\epsilon~.

(5.66)
