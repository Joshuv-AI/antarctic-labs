“We are not sick, so why take medicine?” Community perspectives on barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies for IPTsc in Ghana
Authors: Joseph Sam, Emmanuel Asampong, Harriet Affran Bonful, Dora Dadzie, Mahnaz Vahedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntermittent preventive treatment for school-aged children (IPTsc) is a newly recommended malaria chemoprevention strategy targeting populations that increasingly sustain malaria transmission in sub-Saharan Africa. Ghana recently added IPTsc into its national malaria strategy, yet evidence on community acceptability and implementation strategies in non-trial settings remains limited. This study explored perceived barriers, facilitators, and preferred implementation strategies for IPTsc in a high-transmission district in Ghana.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted in four junior high schools in the Hohoe Municipality. Data were collected through five focus group discussions (FGDs), comprising four FGDs with school-aged children and one FGD with caregivers. Additionally, three key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with two headteachers and the municipal School Health Education Programme coordinator. Transcripts were analysed using Attride-Stirling’s thematic network analysis framework.ResultsPerceived barriers included concerns about drug origin, fear of side effects, administering medicines to asymptomatic children, tablet size and taste, and doubts about programme continuity. Facilitators centred on perceived benefits such as reduced malaria episodes, improved school attendance, lower household treatment costs, and community willingness to support IPTsc if adequate sensitisation was provided. Preferred implementation strategies included school-based delivery complemented by community outreach, administration by trained health professionals, clear communication on drug timing and food intake, and early parental involvement.ConclusionIn this setting, perceived acceptability of IPTsc was shaped less by doubts about effectiveness and more by concerns about trust, safety, delivery, and programme continuity. These findings suggest that context-sensitive IPTsc implementation in similar high-transmission settings should prioritise community engagement, involvement of health professionals, and adaptive delivery models that link schools with community health platforms.

Background

Progress in malaria control efforts over the past two decades has led to significant reductions in morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. These gains have been driven primarily by interventions targeting children under five years of age and pregnant women [2]. As a result, the malaria burden has shifted towards school-aged children, who account for about 50% of infections in many endemic settings [3, 4].

School-aged children (aged 5–15 years), often asymptomatic, have the highest prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum infection in sub-Saharan Africa [5]. They act as reservoirs that sustain transmission to younger children and adults [5]. Recurrent and chronic malaria infections in school-aged children are associated with anaemia, impaired cognitive function, reduced school attendance, and poorer educational performance [6, 7]. This has long-term implications for human capital development in Sub-Saharan Africa. The World Health Organisation (WHO) in 2022 recommended intermittent preventive treatment for school-aged children (IPTsc) as a targeted strategy for addressing individual and community-level malaria burden [8]. IPTsc involves the administration of full therapeutic doses of antimalarial drugs to school-aged children at predefined intervals, irrespective of infection status [8]. IPTsc treats existing asymptomatic infections and provides a period of post-treatment prophylaxis, thereby reducing parasite prevalence and interrupting community-level transmission [9]. It can be delivered through school-based or community-based platforms in areas of moderate to high malaria endemicity [10]. While the seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) intervention is targeted at younger children, IPTsc is given to school-aged children in both seasonal and perennial transmission settings, with flexibility in drug choice and dosing frequency [11].

IPTsc is a promising addition to the malaria control toolkit. Studies across Sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated varying levels of effectiveness and feasibility across different epidemiological settings, drug regimens, delivery platforms, and outcome measures [8]. For instance, IPTsc studies in Mali, Uganda, Kenya, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Tanzania have reported reductions in asymptomatic parasitaemia, clinical malaria, and anaemia, as well as improvements in school attendance and learning outcomes, using different drug regimens and delivery platforms [9, 10, 12–15].

In Ghana, school-aged children experience high rates of asymptomatic malaria infections, which contribute to sustained community transmission, particularly in high-endemic districts [16, 17]. In response to this burden and aligned with updated WHO guidance, the National Malaria Elimination Programme has added IPTsc to Ghana’s 2024–2028 malaria strategic plan, with phased introduction in selected high-transmission districts, including the Hohoe Municipality [18]. Hohoe is considered a high-endemic malaria district with year-round transmission and seasonal peaks [19]. As a newly introduced intervention, IPTsc represents a significant addition to Ghana’s malaria control arsenal, but its success will depend on community acceptance and health system readiness.

Evidence from post-trial and implementation studies in other African settings indicates that IPTsc uptake is shaped by factors such as trust in drug safety and perceptions of preventive treatment for asymptomatic children [9, 20]. However, much of the existing evidence has emerged mainly from trial-experienced communities where repeated exposure to research activities and health interventions may have influenced perceptions and acceptance. Less is known about how caregivers, school-aged children, and school stakeholders interpret and negotiate IPTsc in routine, non-trial settings. Preventive treatment for apparently healthy children in these settings may conflict with local understandings of illness, medicine use, and trust in health interventions [21]. Understanding these context-specific perceptions is essential to inform acceptable and sustainable implementation of IPTsc. Acceptability in this study was informed by Sekhon et al.’s Theoretical Framework of Acceptability, and was used as a guide to explore the perceived barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies for IPTsc among community stakeholders in a high-burden malaria district in Ghana.

Methods

Research design

The qualitative data reported in this paper were extracted from a larger study titled ‘Perceived Acceptability of Intermittent Preventive Treatment among School-Aged Children (IPTsc) in the Hohoe Municipality, Ghana’. The parent study assessed the perceived acceptability of IPTsc among school-aged children and their caregivers, and further explored perceived barriers, facilitators, and potential implementation strategies. The study included both quantitative and qualitative components, however, this paper reports only the qualitative findings. The quantitative component is being prepared for separate publication.

An exploratory qualitative study design was used to explore perceived barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies for IPTsc.

Study context

Empirical research was conducted between June 2025 and August 2025 in four purposively sampled junior high schools (JHS) in the Hohoe Municipality. Hohoe Municipality is one of the 18 municipal districts in the Volta Region of Ghana. It has an entomological inoculation rate of about 65 infectious bites per person-year [19]. As a high transmission municipality, Hohoe is one of the priority areas for the National Malaria Elimination Programme’s (NMEP) new IPTsc intervention. This is in line with updated WHO guidance, which recommends IPTsc in areas with moderate or high malaria endemicity [8]. Conducting the study in Hohoe supports and informs a national priority initiative (IPTsc) in a relevant setting.

Study population

The research participants comprised school-aged children, their caregivers, headteachers, and the School Health and Education Programme (SHEP) coordinator for the Hohoe Municipality. School-aged children between 10 and 15 years, enrolled in JHS at the time of the study, were recruited. Children within this age group generally have stronger literacy and numeracy skills, which improves their ability to understand the study objectives and accurately record their responses [5]. Headteachers and the municipal school health education programme (SHEP) coordinator were included in the study as key informants. Headteachers were included based on their institutional roles in school health implementation. The SHEP coordinator was also included because of her municipal-level role in coordinating school health activities across schools in the municipality.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants were selected using purposive maximum variation sampling. This strategy was used to capture diverse perspectives across participant categories, schools, sex, and age groups. For school-aged children, variation was sought across the four participating schools, male and female groups, and three age brackets: 10–11 years, 12–13 years, and 14–15 years. In all, 30 school-aged children participated in the focus group discussions.

In addition to the maximum variation strategy, practical eligibility and recruitment conditions were applied. Participants were included if they were available during the data collection period, willing to participate, able to provide assent or consent as appropriate, and able to communicate in the interview language. These conditions were applied to support ethical recruitment and meaningful participation, but they were not treated as dimensions of variation.

Caregivers were purposively selected from the catchment communities of the participating schools, with linkage established through enrolled school-aged children. For each school, two caregivers, one male and one female, were recruited to capture gendered perspectives. In total, eight caregivers participated in the FGD. Key informants, comprising two headteachers and the municipal SHEP coordinator, were purposively selected based on their institutional and programmatic roles. Although four schools participated in the study, only two headteachers were interviewed because of their availability during the data collection period. In total, three key informants were interviewed.

Data collection

Focus group discussions (FGDs) were the primary data collection method because they enabled exploration of shared perceptions regarding the barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies for IPTsc among school-aged children and caregivers.

In all, five focus group discussions—four among school-aged children and one among caregivers were conducted. School-aged children in groups were segmented by gender (i.e. male-only FGDs and female-only FGDs) for gender-specific exploration of challenges to and acceptance of IPTsc. Also, segmenting the discussants by gender contributed to making participants more comfortable when expressing their opinions or sharing their experiences within the group context. To limit the effect of any participant dominating the discussion, all participants were constantly encouraged, especially the quieter ones, to speak, share their opinions, and agree and disagree with others where they felt the need to do so. With a mix of directed and non-directed facilitation, the effects of dominant participants on the rest of the other participants’ responses were significantly minimised. Issues raised and discussed during FGDs were summarised and orally presented to participants to confirm, alter or reject at the end of the discussion. This was to make sure that the information collected accurately represented what participants said.

All focus groups were held in a quiet classroom convenient to both the participants and the research team. Groups consisted of 7–9 participants. Except for two children (under five years) who accompanied two women (caregivers), no other person was allowed to sit in the discussions. The presence of the accompanying children did not substantially disrupt the discussions. FGDs lasted about 60 to 90 min. Although the primary language of the discussions was English, occasional clarification and code-switching into Twi and Ewe were permitted where participants found it easier to express themselves. Responses provided in local languages were translated into English during transcription. The use of familiar local expressions during discussions helped minimise communication barriers and improve participant comfort.

To complement the FGDs, 3 key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with two headteachers and the SHEP coordinator for the municipality. All KIIs were conducted in the offices of the respective key informants; were audio-recorded alongside handwritten notes; and lasted between 20 and 30 min.

Two female research assistants (MPH students from the University of Ghana) were trained and assisted the principal investigator (JS) in conducting the interviews. Discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ assent and supplemented with field notes documenting non-verbal cues and contextual factors. The principal investigator (JS) is a public health researcher with prior familiarity with the Hohoe Municipality through previous academic and field engagements. However, there were no prior personal or professional relationships with participants before the study. To minimise the influence of researcher assumptions and power dynamics during data collection, the discussions were facilitated using open-ended and non-leading questioning techniques. Emerging interpretations were also discussed among members of the research team during coding and thematic development. The involvement of multiple researchers in reviewing transcripts and coding decisions further supported reflexive interpretation and analytic rigour.

Instruments

An open-ended thematic guide was used to facilitate the conduct of all focus group discussions. This instrument was modified into three: a relatively simple version with lay language, which was used for the school-aged children, a standard guide for caregivers and a short version for key informant interviews (S1-S3). These instruments were complemented by a short multi-item demographic questionnaire to collect socio-demographic information from all participants. The instruments focused on exploring the perceived barriers, facilitators and implementation strategies to IPTsc implementation. The tools were pilot tested in two similar schools outside the study sites to assess clarity, comprehension, and flow. Minor revisions made after piloting included: simplifying wording for school-aged children and adjusting the sequencing and probing of some selected questions.

Analysis

All interviews were transcribed using intelligent verbatim transcription and analysed thematically using Attride-Stirling’s thematic network analysis framework. This approach was used because it provides a systematic process for moving from initial codes to basic themes, organising themes, and global themes [22]. Analysis began with repeated reading of transcripts and field notes to gain familiarity with the data. Members of the research team reviewed the transcripts, and brief transcript summaries were prepared to document key issues emerging from each interview and focus group. A preliminary codebook was then developed based on the study objectives and the interview guide domains. This initial codebook provided deductive codes related to barriers, facilitators, and implementation strategies. During coding, additional inductive codes were added to capture issues emerging from participants’ accounts.

All transcripts were imported into MAXQDA for coding. Initial coding was conducted by the principal investigator, with a subset of transcripts independently reviewed by one member of the research team using the preliminary codebook. Coding was done separately for school-aged children, caregivers, and key informants to preserve differences in perspectives across participant groups. The coded data were then compared and integrated across groups to identify converging and contrasting views. Emerging codes, coding decisions, and theme development were regularly reviewed and discussed among members of the research team. Areas of disagreement or ambiguity were resolved through discussion and re-examination of the relevant transcript segments. This iterative team-based process was used to improve consistency in coding and strengthen analytic rigour.

Data collection and analysis continued until thematic sufficiency was reached, meaning that additional transcripts produced limited new insights relevant to the study objectives. After coding, related codes were grouped into basic themes, which were then organised into broader organising themes and global themes in line with Attride-Stirling’s thematic network analysis framework as shown in Fig. 1. The final thematic framework was reviewed against the coded data to ensure that themes were grounded in participants’ accounts. Verbatim quotations were selected to illustrate key themes and are presented using standardised participant identifiers (i.e. participant category, gender, and age where applicable). The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research was used as a reporting checklist to guide reporting of the methods and findings [23].

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC 039/04/25). Institutional permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the Ghana Education Service and the participating schools in the Hohoe Municipality. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. All adult participants provided written and verbal informed consent before participation, while school-aged children provided assent and written consent was obtained from their caregivers. Participants were informed of the purpose of the study, the nature of their involvement, and their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty or loss of benefits.

Measures were taken to protect participants’ privacy and confidentiality throughout the study. Focus group discussions and key informant interviews were conducted in private settings to minimise interruptions and ensure confidentiality. Participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and would be used only for research purposes. No personally identifying information was included in transcripts or reports, and pseudonyms were used during transcription and reporting to maintain anonymity. To minimise deductive disclosure, identifiable institutional details were removed from quotations and participant tables during reporting.

All interviews and focus group discussions were audio-recorded only after obtaining explicit consent from participants. Participants were informed about the purpose of the audio recording and were given the opportunity to decline recording if they wished. Audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored on password-protected computers accessible only to members of the research team. All digital files were encrypted and stored in secure folders to prevent unauthorised access. The data will be retained for five years following publication of the study in accordance with institutional research data management guidelines, after which the recordings will be permanently deleted.

Participants did not receive monetary compensation for participating in the study. However, caregivers who travelled to participate were reimbursed for transportation costs, and refreshments were provided after focus group discussions.

Results

The findings are organised around three domains aligned with the study objectives: perceived barriers to IPTsc implementation, perceived facilitators to IPTsc implementation, and implementation strategies for IPTsc. Within each domain, several sub-themes emerged, reflecting participants’ perspectives across stakeholder groups. In all, the study included 41 participants across five focus group discussions and three key informant interviews. The FGDs comprised school-aged children and caregivers, while the KIIs involved school-level and municipal-level stakeholders engaged in school health implementation. Perceived barriers to IPTsc implementation

Barriers to IPTsc implementation clustered around three organising themes: trust, safety, and programme sustainability. These concerns were expressed across stakeholder groups, although caregivers tended to emphasise drug origin, experimentation, and parental authority. Children focused more on the meaning of taking medicines when they were not visibly ill and on the practical experience of swallowing tablets.

Trust and legitimacy of IPTsc medicines

Concerns about trust centred on the origin of IPTsc medicines, fear of experimentation, and discomfort with medicines being given outside direct medical prescription. Caregivers were most concerned and expressed distrust of the source of the medicine, if it was to come from outside the country. They feared that their community would be used to test unproven drugs and would be exploited for research. This concern was reflected in accounts from caregivers and a headteacher:“If the medicine is from Ghana, then we know it’s okay…but if it’s from outside, like somebody wants to test a project on us, then I say no” (Caregiver 5, Female, 50 years).“Some of us may be worried because maybe you might bring a medicine that is not the malaria medicine you say … so when you give the drugs to the child, bring it home for us to see or call us to come and see the type of medicine you are giving” (Caregiver 2, Female, 37 years).“Some will accept, some will fear … even the folic acid [iron-folate tablet supplementation given to adolescent girls in schools], some of the parents are saying they are trying to ‘fertilise’ their children, especially the females.” (Headteacher 1, Male, 48 years).

Safety and medicine characteristics

Concerns about safety and medicine characteristics formed another major barrier. Caregivers drew on previous experiences with malaria preventive medicines and worried that IPTsc might cause fever, vomiting, or other adverse reactions. These concerns did not necessarily reflect rejection of malaria prevention itself, but anxiety about how children might react to the medicine.“When my son took the first dose [malaria preventive medicine she received during a visit to the child welfare clinic], his temperature was very high, and he had to be admitted for a week. If it should be the same medicine, then the side effects are what I have a problem with. Not the medicine itself, but the side effects”(Caregiver 3, Female, 49 years).“Is it the same medicine as the one given to children when they go for weighing? That malaria medicine for babies, or is it a different medicine? That medicine makes the children more sick” (Caregiver 2, Female, 37 years).

Programme sustainability

Beyond concerns about the medicine itself, participants raised questions about whether IPTsc would be sustained over time. Caregivers worried that the programme might begin with enthusiasm but stop because of funding constraints or changes in government priorities. This concern was shaped by previous experiences of interventions that were introduced in communities but later discontinued. They emphasised that the programme should be sustained over time and not end after one year. For instance, a 50-year-old female caregiver who also served as a headteacher said, “…not that they will start this year and then next year say they don’t have funds to procure more medicine. You know, these things come into play if they change the government. Is it the current government that is trying to make this work?” (Headteacher 2, Female, 50 years).

Children and school health stakeholders also expressed concern about continuity. Their accounts suggested that acceptability would depend not only on initial introduction, but also on whether the programme was seen as reliable and sustained.“I advise them because the medicine is good … so they should be bringing it year after year” (SAC 7, Male, 13 years).“Some people come and do this, and we don’t hear anything again, so come back to us at the grassroots and we’ll help to sustain the programme” (SHEP Coordinator, Female, 54 years).

Perceived facilitators of IPTsc implementation

Facilitators to IPTsc implementation are grouped around three organising themes: perceived benefits of IPTsc, conditional support and willingness to participate, and community sensitisation and trusted engagement structures. While participants generally viewed IPTsc positively, support was often dependent on adequate sensitisation, visible community involvement, and trust-building processes.

Perceived benefits of IPTsc

In addition to being critical, some participants acknowledged the perceived benefits of IPTsc. Caregivers mentioned the high cost and stress of treating malaria episodes and saw IPTsc as a way to prevent regular hospital visits and save money on drugs. School staff also mentioned that IPTsc would improve school attendance and learning, as malaria frequently causes children to miss classes. A common theme was that if children were not falling ill, they would attend school regularly and concentrate better. Children valued the idea of preventing malaria rather than treating it, which they felt would keep them healthy and active. A 14-year-old female child said, “It will prevent us from getting sick of malaria or going to the hospital when we have to learn” (SAC 16, Female, 14 years).

Caregivers linked IPTsc to reduced household burden, while a headteacher emphasised improved school participation: “I also think it's a good idea in the sense that, in terms of the money aspect, if a child is sick, it's a whole lot of responsibility on the parents. So, this one is going to assist the child, the parents” (Caregiver 7, Female, 48 years).“I support this idea because it will allow students’ participation fully in the term. As a mother, this will also give me the peace of mind to work” (Caregiver 5, Female, 50 years).“You see, malaria is one of the problems we are faced with here. It leads to children absenting themselves from school, so it will be good to have the medicine.” (Headteacher 1, Male, 48 years).

Conditional support and willingness to participate

Beyond recognising the potential benefits of IPTsc, participants expressed willingness to support implementation and participate in the programme. However, this support was often conditional upon adequate explanation, trust in the intervention, and reassurance from health and school authorities. Caregivers were willing to allow their children to participate in IPTsc and cooperate with health workers once they were adequately informed about the programme. Children also described their willingness to encourage peers to participate if they observed positive outcomes following implementation.“I would like us to take that medicine because some of us sleep without using the mosquito net, and even when the mosquitoes bite us, we don't even feel it. So, I think it is a good idea to take that medicine. I will tell my friends to also take it because it will help all of us” (SAC 11, Female, 12 years).“It’s a good idea because some parents in rural areas may not be able to afford the malaria drugs when their child gets sick. Through this programme, we can help them.” (SHEP Coordinator, Female, 54 years).“The SHEP coordinators and opinion leaders can help us reach the schools and churches quickly” (Headteacher 2, Female, 50 years).

Community sensitisation and trusted engagement structures

Implementation strategies

Across all focus groups and key informant interviews, schools were identified as the most appropriate platform for the implementation of IPTsc. Participants perceived schools as organised spaces where children could be easily reached, monitored, and supported during drug administration. Caregivers and school staff noted that schools already serve as delivery points for other health interventions, such as deworming and iron–folate supplementation, which made school-based IPTsc implementation appear familiar and feasible. A headteacher emphasised “administering it in the schools will be effective and easier because schools provide an existing system for coordination, supervision, and follow-up” (Headteacher 2, Female, 50 years).

The SHEP coordinator and a caregiver also added, “Schools and PTAs remain the most structured platforms for effective administration and monitoring of school health programs” (SHEP coordinator, Female, 54 years).“Giving the medicine in school will be easier, but parents should be invited to see what is being given” (Caregiver 5, Female, 50 years).

Participants also expressed strong preferences regarding who should administer IPTsc. Across all groups, there was a clear expectation that IPTsc should be administered by trained health professionals rather than teachers or non-clinical personnel. Caregivers and children associated drug administration with medical authority and professional competence and expressed discomfort with medicines being given outside a clinical setting. This concern was closely linked to fears of “drug abuse” or taking medicines without a doctor’s recommendation. A 15-year-old female child questioned the legitimacy of IPTsc by stating, “Is it not drug abuse? The medicine wasn’t given to you by a doctor, and you are taking it every term … I think it’s not good for us” (SAC 24, Female, 15 years). This reflects a normative belief that medicines should only be administered by qualified health personnel.

The SHEP coordinator and headteachers acknowledged their willingness to support IPTsc implementation but agreed that their role should be complementary rather than primary. They described their role as facilitating access, coordinating schedules, mobilising parents, and supporting sensitisation activities, while leaving actual drug administration to health workers. Overall, participants consistently favoured a context-adapted implementation approach that combined school-based IPTsc delivery with complementary community outreach and visible involvement of trained health professionals. Across stakeholder groups, this hybrid delivery preference emerged as one of the clearest and most consistent implementation recommendations identified in the study.

Discussion

This paper explored the perceived barriers, facilitators and implementation strategies for IPTsc among school-aged children, caregivers and school staff. The study found that while many community stakeholders recognised the potential benefits of IPTsc, support for the intervention was often conditional upon trust, adequate sensitisation, and confidence in safe programme delivery. Participants further emphasised context-specific implementation preferences, particularly the use of a hybrid school-community delivery model, administration by trained health professionals, and sustained community engagement through trusted local structures.

Perceived barriers to IPTsc implementation

A major barrier to IPTsc acceptability is concerns about the origins of IPTsc drugs and distrust of foreign-driven health initiatives. Caregivers expressed fears that children might be used as experimental subjects for externally funded programmes. Similar concerns were reported in a post-trial study in the DRC, where caregivers had suspicion towards IPTsc because it was given for free [20]. This may be worse in situations where communities have limited prior exposure to malaria chemoprevention. The persistence of these concerns can be interpreted within a broader socio-historical context of power asymmetries in global health. These concerns may reflect broader anxieties surrounding externally driven health interventions, particularly in contexts where communities have limited involvement in programme design and decision-making. Although these issues were not explored directly in the present study, previous research in Ghana has highlighted how limited community engagement during programme planning may influence trust in public health interventions [24]. The findings here suggest the need to prioritise community ownership and co-creation of the intervention. National and district health authorities should position IPTsc as a Ghana-led intervention within the national malaria policy, rather than as a donor initiative. Engagement strategies should prioritise transparency around drug sourcing, regulatory approval, safety monitoring and continuity of the programme.

Administering antimalarial drugs to children who are not visibly ill was perceived as drug abuse by some participants, especially when drugs are not administered by trusted health professionals. This is in line with perceptions about malaria chemoprevention programmes in Eastern Africa, where parents suggested IPTsc should be given after laboratory results confirmed infections [20]. IPTsc in this context conflicts with local illness frameworks that associate medication with symptomatic disease. This contradiction between chemoprevention and community illness narratives can be interpreted through the medical anthropology of health belief systems. In many settings, medicine is commonly associated with visible illness and professional diagnosis [25]. IPTsc contradicts this logic by targeting apparently healthy children, making caregivers perceive the intervention as unnecessary or harmful. For successful implementation, IPTsc must include clear, repeated explanations of preventive medicine concepts, delivered in culturally appropriate terms. School health platforms alone are insufficient if caregivers do not understand the reason for prophylactic treatment. Health workers and teachers should be equipped to explain IPTsc using everyday analogies that align prevention with protection rather than treatment. Several studies conducted in trial-experienced or implementation-exposed settings have documented relatively high acceptance of preventive malaria treatment among caregivers when the purpose of prophylactic medication was clearly explained. For example, community-based studies of IPTsc in Kenya and Malawi reported improved caregiver acceptance following repeated programme exposure and sensitisation activities [14, 26]. However, these contexts may differ from the present study setting, where IPTsc has not yet been routinely implemented, and community familiarity with malaria chemoprevention remains limited.

The fear of side effects and safety concerns was also highlighted as a barrier to IPTsc uptake. Despite acknowledging the effectiveness of antimalarial drugs, concerns about vomiting, dizziness, and long-term health consequences were highlighted. This finding is consistent with previous IPTsc trials and chemoprevention studies, where fear of adverse events has been shown to undermine acceptability despite strong evidence of safety [10, 27]. This concern reflects a rational risk assessment rather than resistance. In contexts where access to emergency care is limited, even minor side effects are perceived as potentially serious. In addition, previous experiences with antimalarial drugs shape community narratives more than statistical safety data. As such, proactive pharmacovigilance communication is essential. Programmes should normalise discussion of mild side effects, clearly distinguish them from severe reactions, and establish visible referral mechanisms. Failure to address side effects openly may reinforce rumours and undermine trust. While safety concerns were prominent in the present study, other IPTsc trials conducted in communities with repeated exposure to malaria chemoprevention reported that caregivers became more reassured about drug safety over time [15]. In such contexts, community confidence increased over time as participants became familiar with the intervention and observed its benefits.

Participants expressing strong concern that IPTsc would be short-lived due to changing government priorities and funding cuts, situate this study within current global health discussions on malaria funding. Caregivers cited past experiences with discontinued social programmes following funding cuts or political transitions. These concerns may reflect broader uncertainties surrounding the continuity of externally supported malaria interventions in some sub-Saharan African settings. Although the intervention differs substantially from IPTsc, the recent suspension of the transgenic mosquito project in Burkina Faso illustrates how changes in political, regulatory, or public support environments can affect the continuity of malaria control initiatives [28]. In contexts where communities have previously observed interruptions to externally supported programmes, perceptions that interventions may be temporary or politically contingent could reduce confidence in long-term programme sustainability. Positioning IPTsc within Ghana’s long-term malaria strategic plan, rather than as a pilot or donor-funded initiative, may enhance confidence. Cross-party policy endorsement and institutionalisation within the school health education programme structures could further address these concerns.

Perceived facilitators of IPTsc implementation

Despite these concerns, participants perceived IPTsc as beneficial in reducing malaria burden, improving school attendance and learning outcomes, and lowering household treatment costs. These perceived benefits are broadly consistent with dimensions of acceptability described within Sekhon’s Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (perceived effectiveness and ethicality) and are consistent with evidence from IPTsc trials across sub-Saharan Africa [14, 26, 27, 29]. These findings suggest that although many participants recognised the potential benefits of IPTsc, acceptance was often conditional upon trust in the intervention, clear explanations of preventive treatment, reassurance about safety, and confidence in how the programme would be delivered. Participants were therefore not uniformly opposed to IPTsc but were negotiating the perceived benefits of malaria prevention against concerns about safety, legitimacy, and appropriate delivery. Communication strategies, in effect, should highlight these benefits valued by caregivers and children. Framing IPTsc as both educational and a health intervention may increase uptake. Individual support for IPTsc will also increase if stakeholders are adequately engaged.

Children reported willingness to encourage peers after observing benefits, while caregivers and school staff emphasised the importance of involvement in decision-making processes. Participants described a conditional form of communal readiness for IPTsc that depended heavily on intensive sensitisation and the active involvement of trusted community structures. Traditional leaders, opinion leaders, and community networks were identified as critical facilitators. Social cognitive theories and implementation science frameworks confirm the role of peer endorsement and trusted intermediaries in health behaviour adoption [30, 31]. This conditional readiness suggests that IPTsc uptake is socially mediated rather than individually determined. Acceptability may strengthen when communities are engaged as active partners in programme design and delivery. When communities are engaged as partners rather than targets, resistance decreases, and legitimacy increases. Structured community engagement strategies that go beyond information dissemination to include dialogue, negotiation, and feedback are needed.

IPTsc implementation strategies

A hybrid implementation approach that combines school-based delivery with community outreach to reach out-of-school children was preferred. This finding is consistent with modelling studies and implementation research demonstrating that exclusive reliance on schools may increase inequities [4, 5, 9]. This preference reflects community awareness of exclusion risks and a desire for inclusive coverage. Hybrid models acknowledge the social realities of school attendance and mobility. IPTsc delivery strategies could integrate SHEP platforms with CHPS and community health outreach to maximise coverage. Stakeholders further emphasised the need for IPTsc implementation to be tailored to local realities, including school calendars, community norms, and health system capacity. This aligns with implementation science literature emphasising contextual adaptation over rigid fidelity [14]. National guidelines should allow structured flexibility rather than uniform implementation. Local adaptation enhances relevance and ownership. For policy implications, adaptive implementation frameworks should permit district-level modification within national standards.

Study implications

These findings have important implications for malaria control policy and implementation planning in Ghana and similar high-transmission settings. At the policy level, the integration of IPTsc into national malaria strategies should include clear guidance on community engagement, communication around drug safety and sourcing, and long-term programme sustainability to strengthen public trust. The findings strongly support the use of schools as the primary delivery platform for IPTsc, complemented by community outreach to reach out-of-school children and children absent during school-based administration. Participants further preferred administration by trained health professionals, while teachers, SHEP coordinators, community health workers, and community leaders were viewed as important for mobilisation, sensitisation, follow-up, and referral support. However, reliance on health professionals for school-based delivery may have workforce implications during large-scale implementation, particularly in resource-constrained settings. The findings also suggest that IPTsc implementation strategies should remain adaptable to local realities, including school attendance patterns, food intake before dosing, and parental involvement during administration. Lastly, the study provides baseline evidence from a non-trial setting and highlights the need for further implementation research during routine scale-up, including assessment of staffing models, adherence, adverse event monitoring, and long-term programme sustainability.

Conclusion

This study found that while school-aged children, caregivers, and school stakeholders perceived IPTsc as beneficial, acceptance was conditional upon trust, adequate sensitisation, safe delivery, and confidence in programme sustainability. Participants preferred a context-sensitive implementation approach that combines school-based delivery with community outreach and administration by trained health professionals. Efforts to implement IPTsc in this setting should include a strong community engagement and intervention co-creation strategy to ensure community ownership. Although these findings are perceived assessments on IPTsc, they provide insights into the potential motivating factors and barriers that should be addressed for the successful routine delivery of IPTsc in the Hohoe Municipality and similar endemic settings.
