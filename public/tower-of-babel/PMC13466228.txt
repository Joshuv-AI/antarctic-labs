Prepectoral versus Retropectoral Direct to Implant Breast Reconstruction after Skin-reducing Mastectomy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Caterina M. Nava, Daniel Correia, Salvatore Giordano, Gauthier Zinner, Carlo M. Oranges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Skin-reducing mastectomy (SRM) enables single-stage implant-based breast reconstruction (IBBR) in women with large or ptotic breasts. The optimal implant plane after SRM remains debated. Retropectoral placement provides reliable coverage but is associated with pain and animation deformity, whereas prepectoral placement avoids muscle dissection but requires well-vascularized and thicker skin flaps.Methods:A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO ID: 1019974). Databases were searched in April 2025. Eligible studies reported outcomes of SRM followed by single-stage prepectoral or retropectoral IBBR. The primary outcome was breast complications; secondary outcomes included aesthetic and patient-reported outcomes (PROs). A random-effects proportional meta-analysis was performed.Results:Twenty-six studies comprising 863 patients and 1106 reconstructions were included (432 prepectoral and 674 retropectoral). The pooled complication rate was similar between prepectoral and retropectoral reconstruction (20% versus 25%), with no statistically significant differences in any specific complication, including implant loss, skin necrosis, nipple-areolar complex necrosis, wound dehiscence, superficial epidermolysis, capsular contracture, and rippling. PROs favored prepectoral placement, with consistently high BREAST-Q scores across psychosocial, sexual, and satisfaction domains.Conclusions:In women with large ptotic breasts undergoing SRM with single-stage IBBR, prepectoral and retropectoral reconstruction show comparable overall safety. Given the favorable PRO trends, prepectoral placement may be preferred, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Takeaways

Question: In skin-reducing mastectomy (SRM) with single-stage implant-based breast reconstruction (IBBR) in ptotic breasts, do outcomes differ between prepectoral and retropectoral implant placement?

Findings: In this meta-analysis of 26 studies (1106 reconstructions), overall complication rates did not differ significantly between planes. Patient-reported outcomes were higher with prepectoral placement.

Meaning: SRM with single-stage IBBR appears safe in well-selected patients regardless of implant plane.

INTRODUCTION

The evolution of mastectomy techniques has been driven by the dual goals of oncologic safety and improved aesthetic outcomes. Skin-sparing mastectomy, introduced in the early 1990s, marked a turning point by preserving the breast skin envelope and inframammary fold, thus allowing more natural reconstructions compared with previous mastectomy approaches.1,2 However, in women with large or ptotic breast, simple skin preservation often led to redundant, poorly vascularized flaps, with increased risks of wound complications and unsatisfactory cosmetic results.3–5 To address this limitation, the concept of skin-reducing mastectomy (SRM) was introduced, combining oncologic principles with reduction mammoplasty techniques. By employing a Wise-pattern or inverted-T incision, SRM reduces the skin envelope and enables single-stage implant-based breast reconstruction (IBBR) while reshaping the breast mound.1,4

However, the optimal plane of implant placement after SRM remains debated. Retropectoral placement positions the implant partially beneath the pectoralis major, whose inferior border is then sutured to a de-epithelialized dermal flap to create a dermomuscular pocket over the implant, as described by Nava et al.1 This provides robust soft-tissue coverage and reduces visible rippling, especially in thin patients, but requires muscle disinsertion. As a result, it is associated with pain, animation deformity, increased bleeding, increased seroma rate, functional impairment of the chest wall, and increased recovery time.1,3,4,6–8

In contrast, prepectoral reconstruction, in which the implant is placed entirely above the pectoralis major muscle has re-emerged as an alternative in the last decade. This technique avoids muscle dissection, reducing pain, animation deformity, and hospitalization, but requires adequate skin flap vascularity to avoid complications such as necrosis and implant exposure.3,4,9–11

Both planes can be reinforced with acellular dermal matrices (ADMs), which provide additional coverage to increase the thickness of the lower flap and to better fixate the inframammary fold. However, their high cost and limited availability prevent widespread use.12–15

Given the rising popularity of prepectoral reconstruction and the long-standing experience with retropectoral techniques, a systematic evaluation of outcomes is essential in the setting of SRM. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare prepectoral and retropectoral single-stage IBBR following SRM, focusing on postoperative complications, aesthetic results, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).

METHODS

We followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines for reporting study results.16 Our systematic review and meta-analysis study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration ID: 1019974).

Search Strategy

The review question was formulated based on the PICOS framework (population, intervention, comparison, outcome, study design) and included adult women with large or ptotic breasts undergoing SRM and single-stage IBBR. Prepectoral and retropectoral implant placement were compared; the primary outcome was postoperative complications (Table 1).

Article Selection

We included English-language studies of adult women who underwent SRM followed by single-stage IBBR. Animal or cadaveric work, other mastectomy types, autologous reconstructions, tissue expanders and secondary implant reconstructions were excluded. Eligible designs were randomized controlled trials, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and case series with more than 5 patients. Studies had to report the primary outcome to qualify for analysis. All records retrieved on April 11, 2025 were imported into Rayyan and screened independently by two reviewers (C.M.N. and D.C.), with disagreements resolved in consultation with the senior author (C.M.O.).

Outcomes Definition

The primary outcome was defined as any postoperative complication occurring at the breast after single-stage prepectoral or retropectoral implant placement, whereas the secondary outcome was defined as aesthetic outcomes and patient satisfaction.

For inclusion in the meta-analysis, a complication had to be reported in at least 3 articles; otherwise, it was only described in the Results section. Complications were documented on a per-breast basis.

Statistical Analysis and Quality Assessment

The data were analyzed for proportional meta-analysis using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) version 4.3.2 with the meta-package. Articles were dichotomized in 2 groups: prepectoral or retropectoral implant placement. A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian–Laird method was applied to calculate pooled complication rates of each method, with studies weighted by inverse variance (and incorporating between-study variance, τ2, under the random-effects model). Study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Q-statistic P value. I2 values <30% indicate low heterogeneity and values above 70% indicate significant heterogeneity.17 Forest plots were generated to display pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Differences in pooled proportions were then compared for statistical significance using a 2-tailed z-test. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

Risk of bias for nonrandomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale; higher scores (8–9) were considered indicative of higher methodological quality and lower risk of bias.18

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively by implant plane, P values were not calculated because groups were drawn from separate noncomparative studies and pooled comparisons would not appropriately account for between-study clustering and heterogeneity. For continuous variables, study-size–weighted means were calculated as ∑(ni×x¯i)/∑ni,with ranges reported as the minimum–maximum of study means. For binary variables, results are presented as n/Navailable (%), where Navailable is the sum of patients (or breasts) across studies reporting the variable.

RESULTS

The initial search yielded 373 citations. After removing 60 duplicates, 313 titles remained. Of these, 261 were excluded based on title and abstract, leaving 52 articles for full-text assessment. Six reports could not be retrieved in full text, and 20 were excluded after full-text review for the following reasons: Becker expander studies (n = 3), insufficient extractable data (n = 4), wrong outcome (n = 6), other technique (n = 5), and case series with 5 or fewer participants (n = 2). Ultimately, 26 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review, comprising 18 retrospective and 8 prospective studies (Fig. 1).1,3,4,7,13,19–39

Overall Complications

In this meta-analysis, the pooled complication rate was lower in the prepectoral group (0.20; 95% CI: 0.11–0.33; I2 = 84%) compared to the retropectoral group (0.25; 95% CI: 0.19–0.31; I2 = 60%), though this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.43) (Figs. 2, 3). Regarding additional complications, the pooled rate of hematoma was lower in the prepectoral group at 0.02 (95% CI: 0.01–0.05; I2 = 0%) compared to 0.04 (95% CI: 0.02–0.06; I2 = 0%) in the retropectoral group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.17). The incidence of seroma was identical between the two approaches, with a rate of 0.03 in both the prepectoral (95% CI: 0.01–0.05) and retropectoral groups (95% CI: 0.02–0.06), and no significant difference was observed (P = 1.00).

Implant exposure occurred less frequently in the prepectoral group, with a rate of 0.03 (95% CI: 0.01–0.07; I2 = 0%), compared to 0.07 (95% CI: 0.03–0.15; I2 ≈ 60%) in the retropectoral group (P = 0.38). Similarly, the rate of superficial epidermolysis was lower in the prepectoral cohort at 0.03 (95% CI: 0.00–0.16; I2 = 0%) versus 0.09 (95% CI: 0.04–0.17; I2 = 56%) in the retropectoral group (P = 0.25).

Capsular contracture was reported at a lower rate in the prepectoral group (0.01; 95% CI: 0.00–0.21; I2 = 0%) than in the retropectoral group (0.05; 95% CI: 0.01–0.18; I2 = 56%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.56). Implant rippling was reported exclusively in the prepectoral group, with a rate of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.18; I2 = 0%).

The infection rate was also lower in the prepectoral group, recorded at 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.05; I2 = 0%) compared to 0.02 (95% CI: 0.01–0.07; I2 = 0%) in the retropectoral group (P = 0.62). Finally, revision surgery was less frequently reported in the prepectoral group, with a pooled rate of 0.01 (95% CI: 0.00–0.14; I2 = 28%) versus 0.15 (95% CI: 0.01–0.67; I2 = 42%) in the retropectoral group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.42).

Physical well-being also showed favorable outcomes, ranging from 66.9 ± 14.67 to 87.1 ± 13.5,31 suggesting good physical recovery in the prepectoral group. Data on preoperative satisfaction were limited but still positive, with Lembo et al reporting 69.32 ± 8.6.20

Response rates to the BREAST-Q were generally excellent, with full participation (100%) reported in 5 studies,3,7,19,20,31 and 90% in 1,37 of 8 total studies reporting the BREAST-Q questionnaire results. Only 1 study had a notably lower response rate of 17.9%, which may limit interpretation in that case.13

Overall, these results reflect a high level of patient satisfaction and well-being following prepectoral breast reconstruction, supported by strong response rates and consistent reporting across studies.

PROs for retropectoral breast reconstruction were reported in only two studies, making a meta-analysis not feasible. These studies provided data across various BREAST-Q domains.27,35 Reported scores were generally high, with Salgarello et al,27 noting postoperative satisfaction at 80.4 ± 11.9 and physical well-being at 88.4 ± 7.5, whereas Lisa et al35 reported even higher postoperative satisfaction (up to 93.3 ± 5.0). Despite these encouraging findings, the small number of studies underscores the lack of consistent reporting of PROs in the retropectoral group.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared prepectoral and retropectoral single-stage IBBR after SRM. The analysis of 26 studies and over 1100 reconstructed breasts shows that overall complication rates are broadly comparable, though differences in complication patterns and PROs provide important insights for surgical decision-making.

In this meta-analysis, the pooled complication rate was 20% for prepectoral placement and 25% for retropectoral placement. Although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.43), the trend favored the prepectoral approach. Importantly, heterogeneity was high in several pooled analyses, particularly within the prepectoral cohort (I2 up to 84%). Despite this variability, prepectoral reconstruction demonstrated lower rates of ischemic complications, including implant loss, skin necrosis, NAC necrosis, wound dehiscence and implant exposure. Superficial epidermolysis followed a similar trend, occurring in 3% of prepectoral cases compared with 9% of retropectoral cases. These complications are particularly relevant in SRM, where the 2 long superior flaps that close down to the inframammary fold may become ischemic because of their length and thinness, leading to wound healing complications at the inverted-T scar.1,41–43

Flap vascularity after SRM mainly depends on the subdermal plexus supplied by internal mammary and lateral thoracic perforators.44 Creating a thin SRM flap can disrupt this plexus, further reducing perfusion to the critical T-point, particularly in prepectoral implant placement.3,21 The trend toward lower ischemic complications in the prepectoral group in our analysis may be explained by preferential selection of higher-risk cases with thinner or more compromised mastectomy flaps for retropectoral implant placement. Nevertheless, other meta-analyses and large cohort studies have reported similar findings, with lower rates of ischemic complications such as skin necrosis, NAC necrosis, and wound dehiscence after prepectoral compared with retropectoral reconstruction.40,45,46

In the literature, implant loss is usually also more common with prepectoral implant placement in several large matched-cohort and multicenter studies, with explantation rates up to 23% for prepectoral versus 4.8% for retropectoral in some analyses.47–49 In our meta-analysis, however, there was a nonsignificant trend toward lower implant loss in the prepectoral group. This apparent discrepancy may also reflect the fact that selected prepectoral studies are more recent by 5–10 years than retropectoral series, potentially benefiting from advances in patient selection and perioperative care.

In contrast, rippling was reported exclusively in the prepectoral plane (1%), reflecting reduced soft-tissue coverage, which is typically correctable with fat grafting.50 Seroma and hematoma rates were comparable between groups, suggesting that such complications are likely not related to implant position.51

The use of ADMs has become an important adjunct in SRM reconstructions.7 By reinforcing the pocket and acting as a biologically active interface, ADMs provide additional implant coverage, reduce tension on mastectomy flaps, and lower the risk of capsular contracture.52–54 Their role is particularly prominent in prepectoral placement, where the implant can be fully wrapped, whereas in retropectoral reconstructions, ADMs are more often applied as a lower-pole patch.4,35,55 In our meta-analysis, capsular contracture was reported less frequently in the prepectoral group (1%), where 46.8% of patients received ADM compared with the retropectoral group (5%), where 9.5% of patients received ADM, consistent with the proposed protective effect of ADM support.3,39

Retropectoral placement requires detachment of the pectoralis muscle, which has been consistently associated with animation deformity, increased postoperative pain, and longer recovery.7,13 Although these endpoints were not uniformly quantifiable in this meta-analysis, the consistently higher patient-reported physical well-being scores after prepectoral reconstruction support the advantage of preserving the muscle plane.3,37 PROs further emphasize the strengths of the prepectoral approach. Across multiple series, psychosocial, sexual, and satisfaction scores were consistently high, ranging from 70 to 90 on BREAST-Q domains.3,37 Although the limited retropectoral data also reported favorable satisfaction,34,35 the inconsistent reporting limits comparison.

Taken together, these results suggest that prepectoral reconstruction after SRM is at least as safe as retropectoral, with potential advantages in pain, function and patient satisfaction, though confirmation from larger prospective studies is needed.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis restricted to SRM with single-stage prepectoral versus retropectoral IBBR. The main limitations are the predominance of retrospective cohort designs, likely selection bias favoring thicker flaps for prepectoral placement and substantial heterogeneity in how complications and PROs were reported. Moreover, heterogeneity in surgical practice across centers and the absence of a uniform control group are inherent limitations. Finally, most retropectoral series predate the prepectoral cohorts by 5–10 years, so temporal improvements in technique and perioperative care may partly explain the observed differences between groups.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, prepectoral reconstruction appears at least as safe as retropectoral placement after SRM in women with large ptotic breasts, with potential benefits in morbidity, function, and patient satisfaction when flap quality is adequate. Larger prospective studies with standardized reporting and long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these results and refine patient selection.
