Parental Perceptions on Antenatal Information About Extremely Preterm Birth: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Gonzalo Solís‐García, Laura Valladares Salado, María Carmen Bravo, María Sánchez‐Holgado, Sara López Magallón, Carlos Zozaya Nieto, Aranzazu Fernandez Sanchez, Francisco López Sánchez, Adelina Pellicer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimAntenatal information is essential for families facing the possibility of an extremely preterm (EPT) birth. Their information needs are not always clear and communication can be challenging during this stressful, uncertain time. We explored parental perceptions about antenatal information provided during perinatal counselling about EPT births.MethodsThis Spanish qualitative study comprised parents of infants born between 23 + 0 and 26 + 6 weeks of gestation in 2023–2024, who received antenatal counselling in a tertiary setting. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 15 families after their infants were discharged from the neonatal intensive care unit. These were analysed using inductive thematic analysis, in which themes and subthemes were identified.ResultsFour main themes emerged. These were: making sense of complex antenatal information, the key roles of context and communication style, emotional journeys around extreme prematurity and supporting better perinatal care. Satisfaction with counselling was generally high, but parents wanted more practical, empathic approaches that respected their individual values and supported their emotional needs. There were wide variations in the level of detail required.ConclusionEffective antenatal counselling about EPT births should empower parents by respecting their values, supporting their emotional needs and providing resources for informed decision‐making.Keywords: antenatal information, decision‐making, edge of viability, extremely preterm, parental perceptions

Introduction

Extremely preterm (EPT) birth is the leading cause of child mortality and disability in Western countries [1]. Although mortality and morbidity have decreased over the last three decades, outcomes still show substantial room for improvement, especially in infants born before 26 weeks of gestation [2, 3].

Infants born EPT have higher risks of mortality and neurodevelopmental complications as they approach the edge of viability. These include cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, language difficulties and behavioural problems [4]. At 23 weeks, less than half of the infants admitted to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) will survive without severe neurodevelopmental impairment [2]. Uncertainty about prognoses and the potential for adverse outcomes contribute to parental stress [5]. The long‐term impact of these challenges on families' quality of life [6] underscores the importance of providing good‐quality antenatal information. This information must promote understanding and adapt to the needs of each family [7]. Such information can help families manage stress and support early mother–infant bonding, but discussing the risks may also lead to higher anxiety levels [8]. The quality of antenatal consultations depends on communication style and delivery, as well as the information provided [9]. Previous studies have shown limited consistency between the information provided and what the family understands during this stressful time [10]. This can lead to communication challenges and reduce trust in the medical team [11].

Parents expect more than just objective data about their child's prognosis [12] to guide them through the information and decision‐making process [13]. They want an approach that considers their individual context and values [13], which may differ widely across cultures and social groups. The aim of our study was to explore the perceptions that parents had about the information they received during perinatal consultations before the birth of their EPT infant.

Methods

Study Design and Subjects

This was a qualitative study that was designed using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines, which provide a 32‐item checklist for interviews [14]. We included the parents of infants born EPT at the Hospital La Paz in Madrid, Spain, who received an antenatal consultation between 23 + 0 and 26 + 6 weeks of gestation. The participants comprised the parents of 13 singletons and two sets of twins, and the 15 interviews were conducted between October 10, 2024 and April 24, 2025.

Our maternity unit provides resuscitation and active treatment from 23 + 0 weeks. Parents receive counselling to help them contribute to shared decision‐making between 23 + 0 and 23 + 6 weeks, and active treatment and palliative care are offered as options. Lung maturation with steroids, neuroprotection with magnesium sulphate and active resuscitation are the default treatment options for pregnancies over 24 + 0 weeks.

Consultations were carried out by neonatologists, alone or jointly with obstetricians. These usually took place on the obstetric ward, as all the mothers had been admitted for high‐risk pregnancies and no dedicated consultation space was available. A scripted summary of a typical perinatal consultation is shown in Table S1.

We excluded the parents of outborn infants, those who were not fluent in Spanish or English, those below 18 years of age or who were cognitively impaired. All infants born during the study period were screened, and eligible families were approached for informed consent. A flexible sample size was planned, which initially targeted 10 interviews, with the possibility of extending this to 15–20, depending on thematic saturation.

Interviews and Data Collection

The subsequent interviews about the consultations were conducted by two neonatology staff physicians (GSG, LVS), who were both trained in qualitative methods, with both parents or just the mother. Researchers contacted the parents to introduce the study and ask for their consent. All the interviews took place after the infants were discharged from the NICU, with 11 carried out in person in a private room in the hospital, and four on the telephone. The interviews were semi‐structured and used a predefined script of open‐ended questions (Table 1). We asked the parents why they had an antenatal consultation, and their expectations of what this would involve. They were also asked how they felt, what information they remembered, and whether any aspects of it could have been improved. Epidemiological data were retrieved from electronic records and the parents were asked about socioeconomic factors. Any problems with the interview script were discussed after the first four interviews, including the wording of some of the questions, and an extra specific question on discussions around long‐term outcomes. The interviews were recorded with the permission of the parents, then transcribed and de‐identified for analysis purposes.

Data Analysis

The analysis was carried out by using the inductive reflexive method proposed by Braun and Clarke [15, 16, 17]. This approach provides a flexible, yet rigorous, framework for identifying and interpreting patterns within qualitative data. It is particularly well‐suited for exploring topics when the perspectives of patients are central to the process. It acknowledges that the subjectivity and reflexivity of researchers play a role in the analysis. The analysis comprised: familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the report.

The two investigators (GSG, LSV) independently analysed all the recordings and coded the themes and subthemes. They summarised the participants' views and included quotes related to each theme. The investigators then compared their findings and created a combined report. Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved by internal discussions.

Ethics

The La Paz Research Ethics Committee approved the study (PI‐6252) before the start of the recruitment process. The parents provided written, informed consent for their anonymized interviews to be used.

Results

There were 29 children born during the recruitment period of June 1st 2023 to December 31st 2024. The parents of 14 children were excluded: 11 had not received any antenatal information, two of their children were outborn and there were language difficulties in one case. We conducted 15 interviews between October 10th, 2024 and April 24th, 2025. Of those, 11 (73.3%) were in person and 4 (27.7%) were carried out by telephone. Data saturation was achieved after 15 interviews and recruitment was stopped. The median duration of the interviews was 40 min, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 30–50 min. Both parents were involved in eight (53.3%) of the interviews and only the mother was interviewed in the other seven (46.7%). The median gestational age at the time of the antenatal counselling was 24 weeks (IQR 23–25). Four families were counselled between 23 + 0 and 23 + 6 weeks of gestation and were therefore offered a choice between active resuscitation and palliative care. The remaining 11 families were counselled from an active treatment standpoint, in accordance with the NICU protocol. Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the study sample.

Thematic Analysis

Four themes were extracted from the qualitative analysis, with subthemes identified for each of them. These were: making sense of complex antenatal information, the key roles of context and communication style, emotional journeys around extreme prematurity and supporting better perinatal care (Figure 1). We have included a number of quotes from the parents to illustrate each theme.

Summary of identified themes.

Theme 1: Making Sense of Complex Antenatal Information

Understanding and Recalling Information

Most parents reported that the explanations were clear and adapted to their level of understanding. However, some of them felt overwhelmed by the amount or complexity of the information. Several parents said that they forgot most of the information received but remembered the feelings associated with the information.

Mother 1: I do not recall anything about that day. I was very nervous.

Scope of the Information and Topics Mentioned

Most parents reported receiving information about general prognosis and survival, the logistics of neonatal resuscitation and the future neonatal admissions. Families reported the positive feelings they had experienced during situations in the NICU when those situations had been already discussed during the antenatal consultation.

Discussions about prognoses, including the risk of death, were handled differently at various gestational ages. The possibility of death was mentioned in most cases. Some parents valued directness and specific mentions of the odds of death, while others preferred to focus on positive outcomes. Most parents remembered sequelae being mentioned, and half of them recalled specific discussions about neurodevelopmental prognoses.

Extra topics that parents would have appreciated included the risk of infection, the need for intravenous access, and the difficulties with catheters and bloodwork. Some parents said that practical NICU logistics and resources to support parents could have prepared them better for the early admission.

Some parents said they were asked to take part in clinical research studies during the consultation and felt comfortable agreeing. However, some struggled to remember what the studies were about.

Father 4: We talked about the possibility of bad things happening over the first days, and future long‐term problems.

Level of Detail and Use of Statistics

Most parents perceived the amount of information as adequate and wanted as much information as possible. Some said that they would have appreciated further details during follow‐up consultations.

An issue that was voiced less frequently was that they felt the information was overwhelming. In two cases, one of the parents wanted further details, while the other parent just wanted general information about the delivery and prognosis.

Some parents received detailed information about the complications of preterm birth. This was perceived as positive in some cases, but others reported negative feelings, especially when those complications did not happen afterwards.

There were variations in how the parents perceived the use of statistics. Some appreciated specific numbers about survival and risks, finding them reassuring or necessary for decision‐making. Others felt that statistics increased their anxiety or were not relevant for single cases. In general, more families with adverse outcomes saw statistics and percentages as unhelpful when they looked back. Parents felt it was reasonable to be asked whether they wanted more statistics and specific figures or more general information about risks.

Mother 7: I would have preferred more details, but separated into different conversations to be able to process them … maybe a summary first and then more details on the second day … that way the information would have been easier to digest.

Decision Making at the Edge of Viability

Parents were asked about possible courses of action at 23 weeks of gestation, in line with the hospital protocol, and these included active resuscitation and palliative and comfort care. Some parents appreciated being asked about this and found the information useful to make a decision. Others did not consider palliative care as an option and wanted to go ahead with treatment in any case. Survival, quality of life prognoses, and religious beliefs were mentioned as factors that influenced decision making.

Mother 6: It is a hard situation, but if you have to make difficult decisions, it is better to have information beforehand … I like to participate in the decision, knowing everything, even if it is hard to cope with it.

Evaluating the Information Provided and How Realistic It Was

Most parents said that they received good quality information and 13 awarded it a general score of nine or 10 out of 10. However, one family gave it a score of one and another gave it a score of two. The correlations between these scores and their experiences in the NICU varied. All the families, but one, reported that it was useful to receive the information before admission.

Mother 3: The interview was phenomenal, the duration was right, he explained things well and answered our questions, with touches of optimism.

Theme 2: The Key Roles of Context and Communication Style

Physical Context

Most of the consultations took place in patient rooms on the antenatal ward and all of the participants felt these were quiet enough. The information was given while standing in front of the mother's bed in thirteen cases, while in two cases the doctor was sitting on a chair. One family said that the presence in the same room of another family who had a healthy pregnancy was upsetting.

In two cases, the parents had to have the discussions with the neonatology staff physicians in the delivery room or operating room, right before the delivery. This was seen as inevitable due to the acute situation by both families, but also as something that could have been improved.

Father 12: He sat on a chair beside the bed, relaxed, to tell us everything. We really appreciated that. … There was someone else that gave us information on other days, leaning against the wall. We had the impression that he was in a rush and it had to be quick.

Temporal Context and Follow‐Up Consultations

The timing of the antenatal consultations varied, ranging from days or weeks before the infant was born to a few minutes before delivery. In general, parents preferred to be approached as early as possible and to have opportunities for follow‐up consultations when the delivery did not progress immediately. This would have helped them to understand the information better and ask further questions.

Some parents would have appreciated a visit from the neonatal team even before viability, so that they could explain the situation and the timing of a possible active resuscitation. Others acknowledged that a consultation before viability might have contributed to negative feelings of anticipated loss.

Mother 11: I would have appreciated receiving information from the first day that I arrived at the hospital, when they were still trying to stop the delivery.

Multidisciplinary Involvement and Continuity of Care

Families appreciated the involvement of both neonatologists and obstetricians and continuity in the team that they dealt with. Several pointed out the importance of professionals introducing themselves and explaining their roles. Some parents found that having too many people present during the consultation was overwhelming and they preferred a small number of professionals.

Mother 9: We would have been more comfortable if there had been only one or two people giving us the information … it was scary to have so many people in the room.

Empathy and Communication Style

Parents valued it when professionals communicated with empathy, used accessible language, and showed that they understood their emotional state. A neutral and realistic tone was preferred, and calmness and confidence were highly valued. Some parents felt reassured by knowing that the neonatologists had experience of caring for similar infants. They trusted the person who was responsible for the future care of their baby after they met them.

Father 3: The non‐verbal language was very good, as it transmitted calmness and reassurance. They were calm, explained things one at a time and used a soft voice.

Theme 3: Emotional Journeys Around Extreme Prematurity

Prior Information About Preterm Birth

The information the families received was very variable. A few families had previous experience with preterm birth, either in their own family or among friends, but most had little information about the risks. Two parents had worked with children with special needs, who had been born preterm, which added to their anxiety about the uncertain prognosis. Some had searched online before the consultation but found no reassurance on websites or social media.

Mother 6: I would look it up on Internet, but most times this made things worse … there was no reliable information.

Initial Reactions to the Risk of Preterm Birth

Most of the parents were scared about the uncertainty of having an EPT infant, as well as mentally and physically tired. They described feelings of shock, disbelief, injustice and fear when they learnt about the risk of an EPT birth. Some said they could not recall the initial information they had been given or that their memories were blurred.

Mother 2: I was shocked and did not really know what was happening. I couldn't believe it … I did not understand why the midwives were asking me to decide on a name, I didn't want to pick one because she was not going to be born just yet.

Anxiety, Hope and Adaptation

After the initial shock, parents said that they fluctuated between anxiety and hope. Some found comfort, while others struggled with ongoing uncertainty. All the parents that commented on their emotional state before and after the consultation found the information reassuring, irrespective of their infants' gestational ages or prognoses.

Father 12: We were very tired and frightened, but the way that the neonatologist explained everything to us gave us a feeling of reassurance.

Theme 4: Supporting Better Perinatal Care

Emotional Support From Family and Professionals

Support from partners, families and healthcare professionals played a critical role in coping with the emotional impact of an EPT birth. The presence of both parents during the consultations helped them to understand the information that was being given to them and provided emotional support. Several parents identified a gap in psychological support during the antenatal period and suggested integrating mental health professionals into the consultation process.

Father 12: They waited for the two of us to be in the hospital to come and have discussions and that was a good thing.

Written and Visual Materials

Some parents appreciated receiving infographics that summarised outcomes, as these helped them process the information. Others preferred not to receive them, because they contributed to their anxiety, especially when they were given to them without any context or explanation. All parents agreed that it would be good to be asked beforehand if they wanted infographics to be provided.

Three of the families said that actual photos of preterm babies or NICU equipment could have helped them prepare for the admission. One parent did not think that real pictures would be appropriate and suggested graphic depictions or drawings.

Father 14: It would have given us peace to know what he was going to look like, because we were picturing him as something terrifying.

Tailored Information

Parents emphasized the need for information to be tailored to their individual contexts, values and emotional states. They said that neonatologists should explore the information needs of the family and personalize the consultation, with more or less detail, accordingly.

Mother 4: The most important thing is to manage expectations … for us, being realistic but leaving room for hope and insisting that you are going to fight for them.

Discussion

Our qualitative study provides insight into parental perceptions of antenatal counselling of EPT infants in a tertiary care setting. The thematic analysis summarized four main themes that captured the complexity of parental experiences. These were making sense of complex antenatal information, the key roles of context and communication style, emotional journeys around extreme prematurity, and supporting better perinatal care. The findings displayed wide diversity in parental experiences. There was good satisfaction in general, but also gaps in the information provided and several suggestions for improvement.

Parents consistently valued professionals demonstrating both clinical expertise and clear, empathic communication that acknowledged their emotional state. However, they showed significant variations when it came to their preferences about the level of details and statistics they wished to receive. Similar preferences about statistical results were reported by Guillen et al. [7], who found that 72% of parents wanted to know outcome statistics. However, Roscigno et al. [11], found that statistics did not heavily influence parental decisions about resuscitation, compared to their own ethical, religious and spiritual values. They concluded that a more compassionate and less statistical‐oriented approach might be preferred by families during antenatal counselling.

The parents in our study agreed about the need to explore communication needs in each case and tailor the information individually. This was similar to a qualitative study by de Boer et al. [18] that advocated engaging in open and personalized discussions about family values. Gaucher et al. [19] reported that mothers wanted personalized information, with some parents requesting more practical information about how to be involved in their infants' care. Our parents came from diverse social and geographical backgrounds. This heterogeneity was reflected in their varied information needs and in the different preferences and suggestions they expressed regarding language, tone, and the use of statistics. Discussions were different at 23 weeks of gestation, when decisions needed to be made about active or palliative care, compared to those ages when active resuscitation was performed by default.

The emotional shock and impact of the consultation on the parents' feelings about an EPT birth were consistently brought up during the interviews. Parents were shocked and often said that they could not recall the specific information given to them by the neonatologists. However, they vividly remembered the feelings associated with those antenatal consultations. Randriamboarison et al. reported that parents felt disorientation and anxiety [13] and highlighted the importance of empathic communication, which could help families adjust, cope with distress, build trust, and empower these families to participate in shared decision‐making.

Some interesting suggestions for improvements were raised by the parents. Most said they would have preferred to have been approached as early as possible during the mother's admission. They also said they would have valued more follow‐up visits so that they could ask additional questions. Although a multidisciplinary approach was preferred, they felt that the number of professionals present during the first interview with the parents should have been limited. Written and multimedia materials were helpful, but should be used with caution, especially handouts detailing statistics. The content of the antenatal information should be personalized for every family and it should include practical information about the NICU and parental resources. Our findings underscore the importance of individualized support that addressed parents' needs and emotional trajectories. It also reinforces previous research that called for family‐centred models of counselling.

Strength and Limitations

The study had several strengths, including the robust qualitative approach, and the fact that we included the families of infants with different gestational ages, cultures and outcomes. This allowed us to explore different perspectives. However, some limitations must also be acknowledged. The single‐centre design may have limited the external validity of our findings. There could have been recall bias, because the parents were interviewed three to 16 months after the antenatal consultation. The use of some telephone interviews may have influenced the content and depth of the responses. Most of the parents had university degrees and were of Spanish origin, which could make it difficult to transfer the findings to other contexts. Future studies should include different populations and explore the effectiveness of different counselling models.

Conclusion

Understanding and addressing parental perceptions is essential for improving antenatal counselling of infants born EPT, especially at the edge of viability. The parents in our study valued empathic communication and information tailored to their needs. There were wide variations in their preferences for statistical information and the level of detail they received. Effective antenatal counselling should empower parents by respecting their values and supporting their emotional needs, while providing adequate resources for informed decision‐making.
