Room-temperature ferroelectricity in NaNbO3 membrane
Authors: Yunfan Wang, Zihao Zheng, Lele Ren, Yifeng Du, Jinming Guo, Shujun Zhang, Ce-Wen Nan, Bao-Wen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sodium niobate (NaNbO3), traditionally recognized as an antiferroelectric material, has garnered significant attention for its potential in energy storage, electromechanical devices, and electrocaloric applications. Central to unlocking its full potential is precise control over its ferroic orders, particularly the transition between antiferroelectric and ferroelectric phases. In this study, we propose a topochemical conversion strategy for fabricating pure ferroelectric NaNbO3 membranes with a P21ma orthorhombic phase regardless of membrane thickness. Using Scanning transmission electron microscopy, we identify two distinct regions with large ( > 10°) or small ( < 1°) octahedral tilts in the ferroelectric NaNbO3 membranes. Polarization analyses in these regions reveal that the ferroelectricity originates from the out-of-plane displacement of Nb5+ cations. Additionally, the pure ferroelectric NaNbO3 membranes demonstrate thickness-dependent piezoelectric enhancement, achieving a d33 value of 93.7 pm/V at 30 nm thickness, as well as pronounced resistance switching behaviors. This study introduces an innovative molten salt environment-controlled synthesis strategy, enabling efficient production of high-quality pure ferroelectric NaNbO3 membranes, and advancing their integration into next-generation functional devices.

Introduction

NaNbO3 is renowned for its exceptionally rich sequence of continuous phase transitions, often described as the most complex perovskite system1,2. At room temperature, it typically exists in an antiferroelectric (AFE) P phase (orthorhombic, Pbma), where the antiferroelectricity originates from the antiparallel displacement of Nb5+ cations3,4. Notably, the AFE phase can be transitioned into a ferroelectric (FE) phase. Due to its high polarization strength ( ~ 40 μC/cm2)5, and environmental friendliness, FE NaNbO3 has demonstrated considerable potential for applications in energy storage6–10, piezoelectricity11–14, electrocaloric effects15–17, and ferroelectric memory storage18. Extensive efforts have been devoted to stabilize the FE phase of NaNbO3 at room temperature by inducing transitions from the AFE state. In NaNbO3 ceramics, the FE phase has been achieved through strategies including the application of E-fields19, chemical composition modification16,20, and grain size tailoring21,22. For example, upon the application of E-fields, the translational antiferroelectric domains convert into wedge-shaped ferroelectric domains, partially and irreversibly transforming the AFE P phase into the FE Q phase (orthorhombic, P21ma)19. Chemical composition modification involved introducing dopants with large tolerance factors and electronegativity differences, which promotes the formation of random polar nano-regions and the tilting of oxygen octahedra, enhancing ferroelectric relaxation behavior17,20. Moreover, as the grain size of NaNbO3 ceramics decreases, internal stresses increase, shifting the phase transition temperature and triggering nanoscale AFE P phase to the FE Q phase transformation21. While in NaNbO3 thin films, the FE phase can be stabilized at room temperature through the application of epitaxial strain11,23–25. These films exhibit thickness-dependent phase transitions similar to those observed in NaNbO3 ceramics, displaying a single FE phase below 40 nm thickness, while a mixed phase state with ordered coexistence of FE and AFE was observed above this critical thickness. First-principles calculations reveal that the surface effects at reduced thickness lower the relative energy of the FE phase, making it more favourable than the AFE phase at the nanoscale24.

Nevertheless, the energy difference between the AFE and FE phases in pure NaNbO3 is only 2.4 meV/f.u.26, making the intrinsic stabilization of a pure FE phase at room temperature challenging. Consequently, the difficulty in synthesizing the pure FE phase has significantly hindered the comprehensive investigation of the ferroelectric properties of NaNbO3. Recently, Harikrishnan et al.27 employed multislice electron ptychography (MEP) to study the ferroelectricity of strain-stabilized FE NaNbO3. Their findings revealed that the polarization originates from anion displacements relative to the niobium sublattice and demonstrated that the oxygen octahedral tilting and polarization order parameters are not linearly coupled. However, as the octahedral tilting mode was substrate-induced rather than intrinsic, the fundamental role of octahedral tilting in NaNbO3’s ferroelectric behaviour remains insufficiently understood.

In this study, we utilized a topochemical conversion strategy to synthesize pure FE NaNbO3 membranes. By precisely controlling the molten salt environment—a critical yet frequently overlooked factor—we were able to modulate the competing rates of two side reactions during the topochemical conversion process. This allowed us to synthesize NaNbO3 membranes with varying morphologies, phases, and ferroelectric properties. The FE NaNbO3 membrane synthesized in this study exhibited a pronounced preferential growth along the (010) plane and could be mechanically exfoliated down to a thickness of 30 nm. Table S1 provided a detailed comparison of the advantages and limitations of this approach relative to the actively studied chemical etching method28–31. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) analysis revealed two distinct regions within the FE NaNbO3 membrane, characterized by distinct octahedral tilt angles—one high and one low. Subsequent investigation showed that these regions exhibited significant out-of-plane (OOP) polarization displacements. Additionally, long-range ferroelectric order was observed, with no evidence of a ferrielectric (FiE) phase. Notably, the ferroelectric switching behaviors exhibited variations with membrane thickness. Furthermore, we observed notable thickness-dependent piezoelectric enhancement and pronounced resistance switching behaviors in the FE NaNbO3 membrane.

Figure 2d shows the Raman spectrum of the NaNbO3 membranes at room temperature. Specific Raman modes are denoted by Tr (translational), L (librational), B (bending), and S (stretching), corresponding respectively to the displacement of Na⁺ ions relative to the NbO6 octahedra, roto-vibrational motion of the oxygen octahedra, bending and stretching motion of the O–Nb bonds of the NbO6 octahedra35,36. In Fig. 2d, the NbO6 octahedra predominantly exhibit three vibrational modes, namely, ν1, ν2, and ν5. Among these, ν5 represented a triply degenerate symmetric O–Nb bending motion associated with the displacement of the Nb5+ cation and the tilting of the NbO6 octahedra. For membrane synthesized in pure KCl, the fewer Raman-active modes in the spectrum indicated a highly symmetric cubic structure37. With increasing NaCl, the relative intensity of ν5 mode gradually increased (compared to the adjacent ν52 peak), suggesting tensile deformation of the NbO6 octahedra. This deformation increases the correlation length of atomic displacements and enhances polarizability, thereby improving the FE properties38. To verify the phase structure of NaNbO3, the Raman peaks of the sample corresponding to the red line in Fig. 2d were fitted and compared with those of the FE Q and AFE P phases, as reported in the literature (Fig. 2e, f, and Table S2)39–41. The fitting results revealed a closer match with the FE Q phase, supporting the phase assignment.

To further confirm the purity of the ferroelectric phase, we conducted the temperature-dependent Raman spectrum (Fig. S5). Previous studies have shown that the AFE P phase of NaNbO3 transformed into the FE rhombohedral phase below −100 °C, leading to an abrupt change in the low-frequency Raman peaks (below 100 cm−1)39. Therefore, the absence of such changes in our FE NaNbO3 membrane below −100 °C suggests the absence of any residual AFE phases. Compared to the Curie temperature (TC) in the range of 250 °C–370 °C reported in the literature (with the differences attributed to variations in the preparation techniques)42,43, our FE NaNbO3 membrane (n(NaCl): n(KCl) = 3:1) exhibits a higher TC of approximately 400 °C, as evidenced by abrupt changes in Raman characteristic peaks at this temperature, as shown in Fig. S5c. Figure 2g shows the second harmonic generation (SHG) spectra of NaNbO3 membranes synthesized under varying molten salt ratios. No significant SHG signal was observed from the mixed phase NaNbO3 membrane synthesized in pure KCl, while the intense SHG signals from the other FE NaNbO3 membranes confirmed their non-centrosymmetric FE nature. Moreover, the non-centrosymmetry of NaNbO3 membranes synthesized in pure NaCl is weakened. This may be attributed to the incorporation of interstitial Na+ caused by the high Na+ concentration, which promotes an increase in oxygen vacancy defects44. The presence of these vacancies leads to a weakening of the symmetric O-Nb bending motion, thereby weakening its polarity. To further clarify the point group symmetry of the FE phase, we conducted SHG measurements on the FE NaNbO3 membrane (n(NaCl): n(KCl) = 3:1) under both vertical (Fig. 2h) and horizontal (Fig. 2i) polarization configurations. The experimental data were then fitted using the SHG tensor based on the mm2 point group symmetry. Given the space group P21ma corresponds to the mm2 point group, the excellent fitting results further confirm the orthorhombic structure of the FE phase in the NaNbO3 membranes24.

To gain deeper insight into the phase structure, we conducted a detailed characterization of the cross-sectional structures of both mixed phase (n(NaCl): n(KCl)=0:10) and FE phase (n(NaCl): n(KCl)=3:1) NaNbO3 membranes, as shown in Fig. 3. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images (Fig. 3a, b) and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (Fig. S6) revealed an unexpected layered structure in the mixed phase NaNbO3 membrane, different from the typical perovskite structure. Previous reports have generally described the molten salt primarily as a catalyst, rather than a reactant, with the synthesized NaNbO3 membrane adopting a simple perovskite structure43. Therefore, the synthesized mixed phase NaNbO3 membrane was initially expected to be free of K incorporation and maintain a simple perovskite structure. However, atomic-resolution energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Fig. 3c) showed a clear absence of Na and an enrichment of K in the layered region, confirming that the formation of the layered structure was induced by the incorporation of K atoms from the KCl molten salt. This observation was also corroborated by the Rietveld refinement XRD patterns shown in Fig. S3. Furthermore, the presence of K-enriched regions in the EDS elemental mapping (Fig. S7) indicated that these K-enriched layers were heterogeneously distributed within the mixed phase NaNbO3 membrane. Figure 3d presents schematic diagrams of three distinct structures observed within these layered regions. The top two schematics illustrated the substitution of Na atoms by K atoms at the A-site, leading to two distinct dislocation phenomena between adjacent perovskite units (1, 2). The enrichment can be explained by the formation of A-site vacancies after removing the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\left({{{\rm{Bi}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{O}}}}_{2}\right)}^{2+}$$\end{document}Bi2O22+ layers. These vacancies, located between the NbO6 octahedral layers, were then preferentially filled by reactive K atoms to stabilize the new layered structure. The bottom schematic depicted the occupation of interstitial sites by K atoms between two adjacent perovskite units (3), which were likely a result of the high K+ concentration in the molten salt environment. Therefore, we can conclude that the molten salt not only acts as a catalyst for the topochemical conversion but also facilitates the incorporation of its cations into the NaNbO3 membrane structure through A-site substitution or intercalation. Interestingly, the atomic contents of K and Na in the NaNbO3 membranes synthesized under varying molten salt ratios were analyzed by using EDS and atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) (Fig. S8 and Table S3). The EDS elemental mapping (Figs. S10, S11) confirmed that the FE NaNbO3 contained only trace amounts of K atoms. We further investigated the oxygen octahedral tilting behavior of the FE phase NaNbO3 via annular bright-field scanning transmission electron microscope (ABF-STEM) images. Due to the tolerance factor (t = 0.973) being less than 1, NaNbO3 naturally favors octahedral tilting11. In the FE phase, the average octahedral tilt angle is typically around 7°27,46. Interestingly, local magnified images shown in Fig. 3g, h revealed that the FE NaNbO3 membrane in our study consisted of two distinct regions—one with an exceptionally high title angle exceeding 10° and another with a very low tilt angle below 1°.

In summary, the molten salt environment firstly determines the dominant cation (Na+ or K+) in the reaction system. A KCl-rich environment (e.g., n(NaCl): n(KCl)=0:10) promotes the incorporation of larger K+ ions into the NaNbO3 lattice via A-site substitution. This causes lattice expansion and charge redistribution, destabilizing the pure NaNbO3 structure and leading to mixed phases. With sufficient NaCl, Na⁺ becomes dominant, suppressing K+ reactivity and favors the direct formation of the stable FE phase. With a further increase in NaCl content (e.g., n(NaCl): n(KCl)>8:2), the excessive Na+ concentration not only alters the A-site substitution rate of Na+ (rNa+), but may also elevate the concentration of oxygen vacancies, thereby affecting the morphology of the NaNbO3 membrane and weakening its polarity. Moreover, the salt composition also changes key thermodynamic parameters (eutectic point, enthalpy, heat capacity) and the system’s viscosity in the high-temperature transformation47,48. In essence, the molten salt acts as a reactive medium that directly provides ions for substitution and as a tunable solvent that governs the reaction’s thermodynamics and kinetics, together determining the final crystalline phase.

Piezoelectric and ferroelectric responses of FE NaNbO3 membrane

To evaluate the piezoelectric and FE properties of the FE NaNbO3 membrane, we employed piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) to assess the effective piezoelectric strain coefficient d33 and the FE response. A local alternating electric field (at voltage VAC) was applied using a conductive atomic force microscopy tip, inducing the converse piezoelectric response in the NaNbO3 membranes. The vertical amplitude of the probe tip (Atip) was then measured as a function of the applied AC bias voltage (VAC), and the slope of the fitting line was used to determine the local d33 value at the specific point. (See Methods for details.) As shown in Fig. S15, six points were selected uniformly on each NaNbO3 membrane to evaluate the piezoelectric response50, with the average d33 for each membrane presented in Fig. 5a. Alterations in the molten salt environment resulted in an increased preferential growth (f(010)) of the FE phase, leading to an increase in the average d33 from 29.5 pm/V to 48.4 pm/V. As previously discussed, the enhanced preferential growth along the (010) plane increased the fraction of low tilt angle regions, thereby reducing the average octahedral tilt angle. Additionally, we observed an intriguing thickness-dependent enhancement of the piezoelectric properties in the FE NaNbO3 membranes (n(NaCl): n(KCl) = 3:1). Figure 5a shows that the d33 increased with decreasing thickness, with this effect becoming progressively more pronounced at thinner scales. When the thickness was reduced to 30 nm, the d33 reached 93.7 pm/V—surpassing the reported values for other lead-free 2D FE systems of similar thickness (Fig. S16). Figure 5b, c show typical butterfly curves and 180° phase switching signals at varying thicknesses, demonstrating the FE properties of the NaNbO3 membrane. A progressive increase in the coercive field was observed as the thickness decreased (Table S5). Notably, the butterfly curves exhibited a slight negative voltage shift, an effect commonly observed in ultrathin ferroelectric films and often attributed to non-ferroelectric artifacts like charge injection52,53. Furthermore, the observed asymmetries likely stem from experimental factors, such as work function differences, rather than the intrinsic properties of the NaNbO3 membranes54.

To further investigate the thickness-dependent OOP switching behavior, domain switching experiments were conducted. Figure 5d–i show the OOP amplitude and phase images of FE NaNbO3 membrane (n(NaCl): n(KCl) = 3:1) with three different thicknesses (60, 40, and 30 nm), acquired after writing box-in-box patterns with opposite tip voltages ( − 10 and +10 V). Under identical bias condition, a pronounced increase in OOP amplitude was observed with decreasing the thickness of NaNbO3 membranes. This trend aligns with the thickness-dependent enhancement of piezoelectric properties shown in Fig. 5a. However, the corresponding OOP phase images revealed that thinner membranes exhibited reduced switching behavior in regions subjected to negative bias. This was likely due to an increase in the coercive field and its shift towards negative voltages. For instance, as shown in Fig. 5c (black curve), a + 10 V bias readily drove the 30 nm FE NaNbO3 membrane to a saturated phase, indicating full polarization switching in the positively biased region. In contrast, due to the elevated coercive field and its negative shift, the membrane failed to reach saturation amplitude under a −10 V bias, which hindered full polarization reversal in the negatively biased region.

Resistance switching behavior of FE NaNbO3 membrane

We further employed conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) to investigate the OOP resistance switching behavior of the FE NaNbO3 membranes (n(NaCl): n(KCl) = 3:1). The principle of C-AFM is shown in Fig. 6a, b, during the forward voltage sweep from 0 to 10 V (process 1), the NaNbO3 membrane remained in a low-resistance state (LRS) and transitioned to a high-resistance state (HRS) as the voltage was swept from 10 V to 0 V (process 2). Subsequently, the LRS was re-established during the negative voltage sweep from 0 to −10 V (process 3) and reverted to the HRS during the subsequent sweep from −10 V to 0 V (process 4)55. Figure 6c–e and Fig. S17 display the consecutive I-V curves of the FE NaNbO3 membranes with varying thicknesses. As the thickness decreased, the switching voltage from HRS to LRS in process 1 increased. Comparative analysis from Table S5 reveals that, for the FE NaNbO3 membranes of the same thickness, the voltage required for resistance switching matched that required for polarization switching. When the applied voltage exceeded the coercive voltage, the current increased sharply, indicating a transition of the FE NaNbO3 membrane from the HRS to the LRS. Notably, during the polarization reversal process, non-ferroelectric factors such as vacancy migration may still contribute to the ferroelectric resistance switching56. In conclusion, this study demonstrates the successful fabrication of FE NaNbO3 membrane by precise controlling the molten salt environment and conducted a comprehensive investigation of their room-temperature ferroelectric properties. The resulting membrane exhibits a stable P21ma space group symmetry and retains robust ferroelectricity at room temperature. Analyses of the polarization vectors and oxygen octahedral tilting in both high and low tilt angle regions revealed that the ferroelectricity originates from the OOP displacement of Nb5+ cations. Additionally, the regions with high tilt angle showed pronounced octahedral distortion, whereas regions with low tilt angle displayed significant lattice compression. The FE NaNbO3 membrane exhibits an intriguing thickness-driven enhancement in piezoelectric properties, with the thinnest membranes achieving a d33 coefficient of 93.7 pm/V, surpassing other similar thickness lead-free 2D ferroelectrics. The membrane also exhibits clear OOP and IP resistance switching behaviors, underscoring its potential in ferroelectric memory applications. Altogether, this study presents a significant advancement in the synthesis and understanding of intrinsic ferroelectric properties in FE NaNbO3, highlight the potential of molten salt–mediated synthesis for producing high-performance nanoscale ferroelectric materials suitable for next-generation piezoelectric and memory technologies.

Methods

Synthesis of BiNN5 precursor by molten salt method

According to previous reports, the Bi2.5Na3.5Nb5O18 (BiNN5) precursor was synthesized using the molten salt method43. Na2CO3 (Aladdin, 99.99%), Bi2O3 (Aladdin, 99.99%), and Nb2O5 (Aladdin, 99.99%) powders were used as reactants and mixed according to the stoichiometry. NaCl-KCl (Aladdin, 99.99%, molar ratio 5:5) powders were added to the mixture at a mass ratio of 1:1, followed by ball-milling in ethanol for 36 h. The dried powders were heated to 1100 °C and maintained for 4 h in a sealed alumina crucible. Finally, the resulting product was ground into a fine powder and thoroughly washed multiple times with hot deionized water to remove the molten salts, yielding the BiNN5 precursor.

Synthesis of NaNbO3 membrane via topochemical conversion

The as-prepared BiNN5 precursor and excess Na2CO3 (Aladdin, 99.99%) were mixed in a molar ratio 4:6 using magnetic stirring for 4 h to avoid destroying the morphology. Notably, NaCl and KCl could be combined in varying proportions to create different molten salt environments. The mixture was subsequently positioned within a sealed alumina crucible and sintered at 1100 °C for 2 h (The holding temperature varied slightly depending on the varying molten salt ratios). After sintering, the product was ground into a fine powder and washed multiple times with hot deionized water to remove the molten salts. Subsequently, it was washed once with 8 mol/L HNO3, followed by several additional washes with hot deionized water to ensure complete removal of the Bi2O2 byproducts originating from the exfoliation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\left({{{\rm{Bi}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{O}}}}_{2}\right)}^{2+}$$\end{document}Bi2O22+ oxide layers.

Mechanical exfoliation of NaNbO3 membrane

In a typical procedure, NaNbO3 membrane was sandwiched between two pieces of tracing paper, and a plastic rod was then rolled unidirectionally over the surface of the tracing paper. Notably, the plastic rod was moved at a constant speed in one direction during calendering to apply a uniform, unidirectional shear force to the NaNbO3 membrane, thereby facilitating the effective initiation of plane slip. After the treated NaNbO3 membrane was repeatedly cleaved with thermal release tape to obtain thinner NaNbO3 membranes, the tape was then vertically pressed onto a plasma-cleaned silicon substrate and heated on a heating stage at 100 °C for 2 min to successfully transfer the exfoliated membranes onto the substrate.

Characterization

The NaNbO3 membranes were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (SmartLab SE, Rigaku) to investigate their crystalline phases. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JSM 7610 F Plus, JEOL) was used to observe the dimensions and morphology of the NaNbO3 membranes. Raman spectra were recorded at various temperatures using a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM Odyssey, Horiba) equipped with 532 nm laser excitation. The piezoelectric response was obtained using a conductive platinum/iridium-coated silicon cantilever in a Piezoelectric force microscope (PFM) (Cypher ES, Oxford). C-AFM (Cypher ES, Oxford) images were performed to evaluate the out-of-plane ferroelectric polarization switching property.

Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) measurements and analysis

STEM specimens were prepared by a dual-beam Focused Ion Beam (FIB, Helios 5 UC, ThermoFisher Scientific). The specimens were bonded on commercial Cu grids and then thinned down by Ga ions with accelerating voltage and beam current of 30 kV and 2.5 nA, and finally 2 kV and 40 pA were used for the precise removal of surface amorphous layers. The thicknesses of investigation areas were about 30–40 nm. A double spherical aberration (Cs) corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM, Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV was employed to characterize the atomic-scale structures, chemical compositions. The convergence angle in STEM mode and high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) acceptance angles were 25 mrad and 39–200 mrad, respectively. The polarization vector data were obtained by calculating the displacement of B-site atoms from the center of four neighboring A-site atoms via the customized Matlab scripts.

Measurement of the effective piezoelectric coefficient (d33)

A local alternating electric field (at voltage VAC) was applied via an atomic force microscopy tip to induce the converse piezoelectric response at the contact point between the probe tip and the NaNbO3 membrane. Subsequently, the vertical vibration amplitude of the probe tip was measured and then fitted using the simple harmonic oscillator (SHO) model. The resulting fitted amplitude (Atip) was then employed as a proxy for the OOP deformation amplitude of the sample (Asample), seen in Fig. S15.

To determine the effective piezoelectric coefficient (d33), a series of VAC values were applied, and the corresponding Atip values were then recorded. A linear fit of Atip as a function of VAC was performed using the least-squares method, and the slope of the fitting line was taken as the local d33 value at that specific point. Considering the highly localized nature of the applied electric field, measurements were conducted at six uniformly distributed locations across the membrane surface to ensure statistical reliability. Finally, the average of these six d33 values was considered as the effective piezoelectric coefficient (d33) of the NaNbO3 membrane.

Device fabrication

Firstly, the NaNbO3 membranes were transferred onto a 285 nm-thick SiO2 substrate using Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps and selected under an optical microscope. Subsequently, a photolithography process was employed to define the counter electrode with a channel width of 2 μm. Cr/Ag/Cr electrodes were then patterned onto the defined regions by magnetron sputtering with a thickness of 40 nm. The I-V characteristics of the NaNbO3-based memory device were measured using a probe station (Y-4, Wuhan Yuxin) coupled with a semiconductor parameter analyzer (2636B, Keithley) under ambient conditions.
