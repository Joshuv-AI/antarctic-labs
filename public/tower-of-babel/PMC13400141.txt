A predictive corticospinal model for pain perception
Authors: Xiao-Min Lin, Xiao-Shuo Zhang, Hang Zhou, Xiu-Yi Han, Zhao-Xing Wei, Tor D Wager, Irene Tracey, Ji-Xin Liu, Cun-Zhi Liu, Ya-Zhuo Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPain perception arises from integrated corticospinal circuits, yet most neuroimaging biomarkers are brain centric. We develop the Corticospinal Pain Intensity Pattern, a multivariate model trained and validated on 330 simultaneous corticospinal fMRI data. Across independent datasets, the model predicts pain intensity more accurately than cortical signatures and generalizes to electrical pain, while remaining insensitive to itch and observed pain. The model further tracks analgesia induced by transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation in healthy participants. In a chronic pain cohort, corticospinal model expression derived from low-frequency spontaneous activity predicts baseline pain and longitudinal changes closely mirror treatment-induced pain relief. A corticospinal hidden Markov model reveals that dynamic transitions between pro- and anti-nociceptive states underpin static spectral abnormalities. Together, these findings establish a corticospinal biomarker that bridges experimental and clinical pain by linking task-evoked and spontaneous neural activity.Keywords: corticospinal fMRI, pain biomarker, neuromodulation, chronic pain, acupuncture

Introduction

Pain, a multidimensional experience shaped by the interplay of sensory, affective, and cognitive processes, arises from dynamic interactions across neuraxial circuits spanning the spinal cord and brain. Classical models posit that nociceptive signals are relayed through the spinal dorsal horn neurons to thalamocortical pathways, culminating in integrated representations within a distributed “pain matrix” encompassing the somatosensory cortices, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus, as well as other brain regions involved in pain processing, such as the periaqueductal gray (PAG) and amygdala.1,2 However, despite advances in brain-centric biomarkers like the Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS),3,4,5 the spinal cord’s role in pain encoding remains underexplored, largely due to technical barriers in acquiring reliable simultaneous brain and spinal fMRI signals.6 This omission is critical because, as the first site of nociceptive integration, spinal circuits gate peripheral inputs and modulate ascending signals.7 However, their contribution to subjective pain perception and predictive biomarker development remains unresolved. Consequently, this presents a gap in our understanding of how the full neuroaxis, including both the brain and spinal cord, jointly contributes to pain perception.

Contemporary multivariate approaches, such as multi-voxel pattern analysis (MVPA), have revolutionized pain neuroscience by capturing distributed neural representations that transcend traditional univariate activation models. Unlike conventional mass-univariate General Linear Model (GLM) analyses that identify isolated pain-active regions, MVPA-based biomarkers like the NPS decode pain intensity through spatially distributed cortical patterns, achieving good predictive accuracy and mechanistic interpretability.3,4,5 For instance, the NPS has been demonstrated to effectively differentiate neural responses to social rejection-related stimuli3,8 and aversive visual stimuli9 from nociceptive-specific activation patterns. Despite these advances in cortical imaging and multivariate modeling, most work to date has been conducted in healthy volunteers under tightly controlled experimental paradigms. In parallel, there is growing interest in developing neuroimaging-based pain biomarkers that can augment clinical assessment by providing objective, mechanistically grounded readouts to complement self-report. Recent reviews not only highlight substantial progress but also emphasize that very few candidates have been rigorously validated in real-world chronic pain populations or linked to longitudinal treatment response.10,11 Neuroimaging signatures such as the NPS have begun to show predictive and pharmacodynamic utility in osteoarthritis12 and other chronic pain conditions, and resting-state functional MRI-based classifiers can distinguish chronic pain patients from pain-free controls to some extent.13 However, these advanced brain-centric models face critical limitations. They implicitly assume that pain is encoded through cortical representations, neglecting dynamic interactions across the neuroaxis and failing to consider the complex, bidirectional communication between brain regions and the spinal cord, which play a crucial role in shaping the experience of pain.14 This oversight critically constrains our capacity to explain how top-down psychological processes like nocebo effects shape pain perception, as these phenomena likely involve dynamic neuraxial integration between supraspinal expectations and spinal gating mechanisms.15 This oversight is particularly consequential given converging evidence that spinal circuits gate nociceptive inputs through ascending/descending modulation processes inaccessible to brain-only MVPA frameworks.

Recent methodological breakthroughs in simultaneous corticospinal fMRI have fundamentally transformed our capacity to investigate dynamic neuraxial interactions.16 The seminal work by Tinnermann and her colleagues exemplifies how this simultaneous imaging paradigm is indispensable for uncovering multilevel pain regulation.15 By concurrently capturing spinal dorsal horn responses and supraspinal activity, their study revealed that expectation-driven nocebo effects are mediated through a functional cascade along the rostral anterior cingulate-PAG-spinal cord axis. Crucially, such synchronization avoids temporal confounds of sequential scans and ensures physiological coherence between spinal and supraspinal signals, which is indispensable for modeling real-time neuraxial interactions. This technological leap opens unprecedented opportunities for clinical translation. A corticospinal pain model could identify distinct and complete pathological signatures. For example, aberrant spinal horn motor-sensory coupling may drive central sensitization in chronic neuropathic pain.14 In fibromyalgia, disrupted descending inhibition from PAG to cervical cord might amplify widespread hyperalgesia.17 Crucially, such neuraxial biomarkers may guide neuromodulation therapies, for instance, determining whether a patient’s mechanical allodynia originates from spinal GABAergic interneuron loss (targeting spinal cord stimulation)18,19 vs. impaired prefrontal-PAG top-down control (favoring repetitve transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS] over motor cortex).20

Here, we bridge this gap by leveraging simultaneous corticospinal fMRI to derive a multivariate pattern that integrates supraspinal and cervical spinal pain activation signals. We ask whether corticospinal patterns outperform brain- or spinal-only models in predicting pain in healthy participants, whether this signature generalizes across sensory modalities and cognitive contexts, and whether its expression tracks neuromodulation-induced analgesia and chronic pain-related abnormalities (Figure 1A).

Results

To construct and validate a comprehensive predictive model capable of decoding integrated neuraxial representations of pain, we conducted a multi-stage investigation across four independent cohorts (total N = 330; Table S1). First, leveraging simultaneous corticospinal fMRI, we acquired extensive nociceptive neuroimaging data in a discovery cohort (study 1, n = 95) to develop the Corticospinal Pain Intensity Pattern (CsPIP) using a multivariate least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-principal component regression (LASSO-PCR) machine learning algorithm (Figure 1C). To rigorously assess the model’s generalizability and specificity, we subsequently applied the CsPIP to independent datasets involving distinct sensory modalities (pain vs. itch; study 2, n = 40), nociceptive inputs (electrical stimulation; study 3, n = 50), and cognitive contexts (pain empathy; study 4, n = 60). Furthermore, we evaluated the biomarker’s sensitivity to neuromodulation-induced analgesia (transcutaneous electrical nerve-stimulation [TENS]) and explored its clinical utility in decoding resting-state dynamics in chronic pain patients (irritable bowel syndrome [IBS]; study 5, n = 83, after exclusion). Below, we first delineate the univariate functional architecture that serves as the basis for our multivariate modeling.

fMRI analysis during the pain task revealed robust activation within canonical nociceptive processing networks. Whole-brain contrasts identified significant blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal increases (cluster-forming threshold Z > 3.1, family-wise error (FWE corrected p < 0.05) in regions consistent with the “pain matrix,” including the bilateral insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), and thalamus. The primary (S1) and secondary (S2) somatosensory cortices similarly exhibited task-related activation, with spatial extents overlapping known somatotopic representations of forearm pain stimulation. Crucially, non-parametric analysis (threshold-free cluster enhancement [TFCE] corrected, 10,000 permutations) revealed distinct activation patterns in the cervical spinal cord. Significant positive activation was observed in the C6 dorsal horn (peak voxel TFCE corrected, p = 0.02), while concurrent negative BOLD responses were detected in the ventral horn at the same vertebral level (Figure 1B).

Development of the corticospinal pain intensity pattern

To predict pain intensity, we employed a LASSO-PCR algorithm to decode nociceptive intensity representations from distributed neural patterns. First, we divided the dataset from study 1 (n = 92, after exclusion) into training (n = 61) and hold-out dataset (n = 31) to develop and evaluate our predictive model (Figure 1C). Specifically, corticospinal contrast parameter estimates (COPEs) from each run across all participants were used as input features to predict pain intensity (i.e., each participant has two COPEs). The resulting voxel-wise weight patterns, termed CsPIP, were iteratively trained on images from 90% of participants and tested on the remaining 10% via 10-fold cross-validation, with participant-level stratification to minimize data leakage. The cross-validated model achieved a significant predictive accuracy of r = 0.36, p < 0.0001 (Figure 2A). Validation on the hold-out dataset further confirmed generalizability (r = 0.47, p = 0.0001, Figure 2B).

Predictive weight interpretation of the CsPIP

Then, we performed permutation testing to obtain an unbiased estimate of accuracy (p < 0.001). To identify corticospinal regions robust to the CsPIP, we conducted bootstrapping tests (10,000 iterations) with correction. This analysis revealed stable predictive weights in bilateral anterior insula, dACC, cerebellum, supplementary motor area (SMA), and other brain regions, along with spinal cord segments at C2/C6 dorsal horn and C4 ventral horn (Figure 2D). Although the spatial features of CsPIP demonstrate substantial overlap with the NPS across most brain regions (e.g., dACC, insular, occipital gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, SMA, thalamus, and cerebellum), CsPIP exhibits a distinct pattern in the primary motor cortex (M1) that is absent in the NPS pattern. To elucidate the large-scale functional topology of the CsPIP, we stratified the voxel-wise predictive weights into seven canonical functional brain networks and the spinal cord (Figure 2C). This network-level decomposition revealed that positive predictive weights were predominantly anchored in large-scale networks such as somatomotor network and ventral attention network, aligning with the established roles of these systems in sensory-discriminative and salience processing during nociception.

External validation

To assess the generalizability of the model to independent data and distinct sensory modalities, we performed an external validation using an independent cohort (study 2, n = 39, after exclusion). In this study, participants underwent a factorial design involving both pain and itch stimuli at high and low intensities. Behavioral ratings confirmed the effectiveness of pain manipulations (Figure 3A). High-intensity pain stimuli elicited substantially higher ratings than low-intensity pain (high pain: mean = 6.89 ± 1.54; low pain: 4.99 ± 1.42; paired t test: t(38) = 10.77, p < 0.001). We next examined whether CsPIP generalized to predict individual differences in pain sensitivity in this independent cohort. For each condition, we conducted separate tests using CsPIP to assess the neural representations associated with each level of pain intensity. The model achieved a predictive accuracy of r = 0.52, with p < 0.001 for high pain intensity. For low pain intensity, the model’s prediction was significant, with r = 0.34 and p = 0.04. (Figure 3B). To further quantify the diagnostic utility of the signature, we used a two-forced-choice framework to classify high-pain trials against each of the other three conditions (i.e., low pain, low itch, high itch). The CsPIP reliably discriminated high pain from low pain (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.52–0.76, p = 0.032). Crucially, the model maintained high specificity by successfully distinguishing high pain from low itch (AUC = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.61–0.83, p < 0.001) and high itch (AUC = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.55–0.78, p = 0.010), with all classification performances significantly exceeding chance levels (Figure 3C).

Because pain is highly dynamic and can fluctuate on the order of seconds,5 we next asked whether CsPIP captures trial-to-trial variations in self-reported pain intensity (Figures 3D and 3E). Using all 2,340 trials from 39 participants, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model predicting trial-wise rating from CsPIP expression, condition, and their interaction, while accounting for random intercepts and random slopes at the participant level. Critically, the interaction was highly significant (β = 0.29, Z = 4.07, p < 0.001, 95% CI = 0.15–0.44), indicating that the slope linking CsPIP output to subjective intensity differed across conditions. CsPIP predictions closely tracked trial-to-trial rating fluctuations in pain blocks but showed markedly weaker coupling in itch blocks (Figure 3E). To quantify this effect at the individual level, we computed trial-wise within-subject correlations between CsPIP predictions and ratings for each participant and condition using Spearman’s ρ (Figure 3D). Across participants, within-subject correlations were reliably positive for pain condition (mean ρ = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.09–0.23; one-sample test against zero: t(38) = 4.63, p < 0.001). In contrast, correlations were substantially attenuated and not different from zero for low- and high-itch conditions (mean ρ = 0.04, 95% CI = −0.02–0.10; t(38) = 1.44, p = 0.16). Direct comparison confirmed that pain conditions exhibited stronger corticospinal-behavior coupling than itch conditions (paired t test, t(38) = 2.51, p = 0.017). These results demonstrate that CsPIP not only predicts inter-individual variability in pain sensitivity but also captures rapid intra-individual fluctuations in perceived pain intensity, whereas trial-wise itch ratings are largely decoupled from the signature. Finally, we assessed the calibration of CsPIP predictions at the single-trial level (Figure 3F). Trials were binned according to predicted intensity, and we plotted mean observed ratings against mean predictions separately for pain and itch. For pain trials, the calibration curve exhibited a strong positive linear relationship, indicating that CsPIP predictions clearly tracked the graded changes in perceived pain intensity. In contrast, itch trials showed no significant linear relationship, resulting in an essentially flat calibration function. Together with the behavioral and classification analyses, these results reinforce the pain specificity of the corticospinal signature.

To further test the generalizability of CsPIP, we introduced an independent dataset using brief electrical stimulation of the skin as the nociceptive modality (study 3, n = 50). CsPIP responses were positively correlated with mean electrical-pain ratings (r = 0.45, p = 0.001; Figure 3G). Thus, a signature originally derived from heat pain generalized to a distinct nociceptive input pathway, suggesting that CsPIP captures modality-independent aspects of pain representation. To better understand why CsPIP generalized across nociceptive modalities, we trained a LASSO-PCR model directly on the electrical-pain data and quantified pattern similarity between this electrical-pain model and CsPIP within each region of a 275-parcel Brainnetome-based atlas covering cortical, subcortical, cerebellar, brainstem, and spinal structures. Strikingly, 81 of 275 regions showed significant positive pattern similarity after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05/275; Figure 3H), indicating a substantial overlap between the spatial representations supporting heat and electrical pain. We then functionally characterized the top 20 overlapping regions using meta-analytic functional-behavioral decoding (Figure 3I). The resulting term profile was dominated by constructs related to perception and cognition, including “perception,” “vision,” “cognition,” “attention,” and “memory,” as well as emotion-related terms.

Comparisons between CsPIP and other models

We also used the same pipeline to re-train two models, the brain model (BM) and the spinal cord model (SCM), using only the brain or spinal cord data from study 1. However, neither model reached the predictive accuracy of the CsPIP (Figure 3J). The BM achieved a training result of r = 0.31, p < 0.001, with test results in study 1 showing r = 0.45, p < 0.001, and in study 2, a low-pain prediction of r = 0.13, p = 0.42, and high-pain prediction of r = 0.35, p = 0.03. Similarly, the SCM yielded a training result of r = 0.29, p = 0.001, with study 1 test results showing r = −0.08, p = 0.56, and study 2 low-pain and high-pain predictions of r = 0.23, p = 0.15, and r = 0.10, p = 0.53, respectively.

To benchmark our multivariate model against established pain biomarkers, we evaluated the predictive utility of the NPS3 and Stimulus-Intensity Independent Pain Signature (SIIPS1)21 on our test set (Figure 3J; Table S2). For each participant’s pain response, we computed pattern responses by calculating the dot product between the predefined pattern weight vector and corresponding whole-brain parametric maps. In study 1, the NPS demonstrated significant predictive performance in both training and test sets, with significant correlations to subjective pain ratings in the training set (r = 0.21, p < 0.05) and maintained predictive validity in the test set (r = 0.28, p < 0.05). In study 2, NPS-derived scores showed significant correlation in high-pain condition (r = 0.51, p < 0.001), but no significant correlation with low-pain conditions (r = 0.09, p = 0.58). In study 3, the NPS achieved marginally significant predictive performance (r = 0.24, p = 0.089). However, the SIIPS-derived scores neither predicted pain responses in either dataset of study 1 (r = 0.11, p = 0.21; r = 0.22, p = 0.09) nor did they predict pain responses in the low-pain condition of study 2 (r = 0.02, p = 0.89) or in study 3 (r = 0.08, p = 0.56), showing significant predictive utility only for the high-pain condition in study 2 (r = 0.38, p < 0.05).

We further benchmarked the primary linear model against nonlinear alternatives to validate the model selection (Figure S1). The linear LASSO-PCR model substantially outperformed both nonlinear algorithms across all validation datasets. In the internal test set (study 1), while the LASSO-PCR maintained robust predictive accuracy (r = 0.48), the support vector regression (SVR) with an radial basis function kernel (r = 0.23) and Random Forest (r = 0.39) models demonstrated considerably lower performance. Crucially, this performance gap widened in the external validation (study 2): unlike the CsPIP, which successfully generalized to independent datasets; both nonlinear models failed to predict pain intensity in the external cohorts, yielding non-significant or negative correlations (SVR: r = −0.01 for low pain, r = −0.26 for high pain; Random Forest: r = −0.01 for low pain, r = 0.20 for high pain). These findings indicate that the encoding of pain intensity within distributed corticospinal circuits is best captured by linear multivariate patterns, whereas nonlinear models likely suffered from overfitting given the high-dimensional nature of the neuroimaging features.

Pain specificity of the CsPIP

In the pain specificity analyses, we first asked whether CsPIP would generalize to non-painful somatosensory states. As noted above, CsPIP failed to predict subjective intensity in either low- or high-itch conditions in study 2 (Figures 3D–3F), despite robust prediction of pain ratings in the same participants. In study 4, we applied the CsPIP model to a pain empathy experiment involving 58 healthy participants (after exclusions). Each participant underwent corticospinal fMRI while observing another person being subjected to a series of electric shocks that were either painful or non-painful. These trials utilized video stimuli designed to elicit vicarious pain or neutral observation, after which participants rated the perceived intensity of the shock delivered to the person on the screen (see STAR Methods for details). We computed CsPIP signature responses for both the painful and painless observation conditions and found no significant correlation between the CsPIP response and the participants’ empathy scores (painful condition: r = 0.03, p = 0.81; painless condition: r = 0.02, p = 0.88).

Sensitivity of the CsPIP to neuromodulation-induced pain changes

As part of study 1, 61 individuals were randomly selected to the study 1-TENS subcohort for the TENS experiment, allocating 30 to active TENS and 31 to sham stimulation; their baseline data were used to train and validate the CsPIP model (Figure 4A). A 2 (group: TENS vs. sham) × 2 (side: treated left hand vs. untreated right hand) × 2 (time: pre- vs. post-treatment) mixed-effects ANOVA on pain-intensity ratings revealed a significant three-way interaction, F(1, 59) = 37.02, p < 0.001, indicating that analgesia differed across groups and sides over time. To clarify this effect, we ran separate 2 (side) × 2 (time) repeated-measures ANOVAs within each group (Figures 4B and 4D). In the active-TENS group, a significant side × time interaction emerged for pain intensity, F(1, 29) = 38.80, p < 0.001. Follow-up paired t tests showed significant reductions on the treated left side (pain intensity: t(29) = 6.740, Bonferroni corrected p < 0.001), whereas no change occurred on the contralateral untreated right side (pain intensity: t(29) = 0.064, Bonferroni corrected p = 0.95). However, no significant interaction effects (F(1,30) = 0.712, p = 0.405) were observed in the sham group.

To determine whether CsPIP captured these behavioral changes, we computed pre-minus-post difference scores for both self-reported pain and CsPIP output and examined their correlations separately for each forearm (Figures 4C and 4E). In the sham group, the association was nonsignificant on both the left side (r = 0.13, p = 0.477) and the right side (r = −0.04, p = 0.828). In the active-TENS group, by contrast, no significant association was observed on the untreated right side (r = 0.14, p = 0.452), whereas a significant positive correlation emerged on the treated left side (r = 0.36, p = 0.049). Notably, the traditional NPS failed to reach statistical significance in capturing this effect (r = 0.34, p = 0.063), highlighting a stronger predictive performance by CsPIP. Finally, we tested the SIIPS pattern and observed a significant correlation with analgesic change on the treated left forearm (r = 0.45, p = 0.012), suggesting that SIIPS may be more sensitive to TENS-related pain modulation than CsPIP in this dataset (Figure S2).

Spectral power of spontaneous activity within CsPIP serves as a state-dependent marker of pain relief

We first assessed whether the CsPIP generalizes to resting-state fMRI data in chronic pain patients with IBS, a prototypical visceral pain disorder characterized by recurrent abdominal pain. In this cohort, 83 patients in both the real electroacupuncture and sham groups underwent resting-state fMRI before and after an 18-session electroacupuncture treatment course, allowing us to test whether changes in CsPIP expression track longitudinal modulation of ongoing clinical pain. Consistent with the fundamental differences between task-evoked and intrinsic brain activity,4,22 the model failed to predict clinical pain scores when applied directly to the temporally averaged time series (r = −0.08, p = 0.49). We reasoned, however, that although the temporal phase of spontaneous fluctuations is stochastic, the spatial topography of intrinsic low-frequency BOLD activity might be preserved in chronic pain. Indeed, prior work has shown that the deviation of low-frequency BOLD fluctuations is a robust signature of chronic pain.11,23 To test this hypothesis, we transformed the resting-state signal into the frequency domain and computed the fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations ([fALFF] 0.01–0.13 Hz) as a spectral index of local neural excitability.

Based on baseline behavioral data (PHQ-9 and IBS-SSS scores), we used K-means clustering to partition the patients into high-burden and low-burden phenotypes. Strikingly, when the CsPIP was applied to these static fALFF maps, it successfully predicted baseline clinical phenotypes in low burden phenotype, correlating significantly with baseline chronic pain scores (r = 0.33, p = 0.04). In contrast, neither the brain-only NPS (r = 0.22, p = 0.17) nor the SIIPS (r = −0.01, p = 0.95) showed a significant association with baseline pain (Figure 5A; Table S3). Crucially, this prediction was not a fixed trait: following treatment (Figure 5B), longitudinal changes in predicted pain (ΔCsPIP) derived from fALFF difference maps were highly correlated with the actual analgesic effects in the treatment group (ΔPain Rating; r = 0.30, p = 0.04; Figure 5C), but did not predict responses in the sham group (r = −0.06, p = 0.74; Figure S3). By contrast, both the NPS (r = 0.11, p = 0.44) and SIIPS (r = 0.17, p = 0.23) failed to capture treatment-related pain changes in the treatment group.

Maladaptive temporal dynamics underlie static spectral abnormalities

While the fALFF analysis confirmed that the pain network is hyperactive on average, it relies on temporal integration, thereby obscuring the dynamic nature of brain network configurations. We hypothesized that the observed elevation in static spectral power might stem from a temporal bias—specifically, the corticospinal pathway being transiently locked into specific pro-nociceptive states.24,25 To deconstruct this, we employed a hidden Markov model (HMM) on the CsPIP-defined voxels (Figure 5D) to parse the resting-state data into discrete, recurring brain states. All available resting-state data from the baseline experimental group and the sham group were included in this analysis (n = 83).

This dynamic analysis (optimal K = 5, Figure 5E) revealed a clear dissociation between states in how strongly they expressed the task-trained CsPIP and how these state-wise predictions related to clinical pain. For each state, we averaged the voxel-wise BOLD signal across all TRs assigned to that state and applied CsPIP to the resulting state-specific mean map to obtain a predicted pain score. A pro-nociceptive state (state 5) showed CsPIP predictions that were positively correlated with baseline pain severity (r = 0.22, p = 0.048; Figure 5F, green dots). In contrast, an antinociceptive state (state 1) showed CsPIP predictions and a significant negative correlation with pain scores (r = −0.30, p = 0.011; Figure 5F, purple dots). These findings provide a mechanistic bridge between our static and dynamic results: chronic pain severity appears to be shaped by the balance between recurrent engagement of a pro-nociceptive state and an antinociceptive state over time (Figures 5G and 5H).

Discussion

Our findings establish that the corticospinal activity patterns synergistically encode subjective pain intensity, advancing our understanding of neuraxial pain processing beyond traditional brain-centric models. The CsPIP, derived through LASSO-PCR-based decoding of simultaneous corticospinal fMRI signals, reveals that cervical spinal cord dynamics significantly enhances the predictive accuracy of pain biomarkers. These results not only highlight the importance of spinal circuits in pain processing but also support the discovery of more comprehensive pain biomarkers that better capture the complexity of pain perception.

This study evaluated whether corticospinal features contribute to pain intensity perception. Our results demonstrate that cervical spinal cord activation, particularly in the dorsal horn, plays a critical role in pain processing. Concurrent with canonical pain matrix regions (e.g., insula, anterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, and somatosensory cortices),2 the cervical spinal cord demonstrated distinct pain intensity coding patterns, with both positive and negative weights observed at the same vertebral level. The dorsal horn’s distinct laminae and ascending pathways integrate excitatory and inhibitory interneurons, enabling precise sensory encoding and plasticity crucial for nociceptive processing.14,26 Corticospinal tract projections from the somatosensory cortex to both the ventral and dorsal horns further highlight the spinal cord’s active role in modulating early nociceptive and ascending pain signals.27 Notably, the observed negative BOLD responses in the ventral horn support a dynamic, bidirectional pain signaling process involving both facilitatory and inhibitory mechanisms at the spinal level.14,26,27,28 These findings suggest that pain intensity is not solely encoded by brain regions but is a dynamic interaction between supraspinal and spinal circuits. As the first site of nociceptive integration, the spinal cord is not merely a relay station but a critical participant in pain modulation. The integration of spinal signals into predictive models of pain intensity reflects a more accurate representation of the neural underpinnings of pain perception.15 This reinforces the idea that the full neuroaxis, comprising both the brain and spinal cord, should be considered in the model of pain.

Our results highlight the relative advantages of combining brain and spinal signals over using brain-centric biomarkers. While the NPS effectively predicts pain using cortical activity, it struggles to capture the full complexity of pain intensity variations. Conversely, the CsPIP demonstrated higher predictive accuracy, reinforcing the necessity of integrating spinal cord data into pain models. Furthermore, the CsPIP incorporates motor-related regions (M1) linked to pain sensitivity and emotional regulation,29 a dimension absent in the NPS.3 This inclusion aligns with clinical applications where M1 is standardly targeted in TMS for pain relief.20 Despite the CsPIP’s robust overall performance, inherent intersubject variability in neural pain responses influences predictive accuracy.5 For instance, while the model excelled at predicting high pain, its accuracy for low pain intensity was marginally significant. This likely reflects a training bias toward higher-intensity patterns and the subtle, highly variable nature of low-pain neural signals.5

Clinically, the CsPIP’s responsiveness to TENS-induced analgesia positions it as an objective biomarker for treatment efficacy, complementing subjective ratings. Similar to brain-only fMRI signatures tracking opioid analgesia,3 our combined model tracked analgesic gains specific to active TENS. This suggests that monitoring CsPIP changes can verify in real-time whether neuromodulation therapies engage their intended mechanisms. Mechanistically, TENS alleviates pain by interrupting dorsal horn nociceptive signaling, while activating supraspinal descending inhibitory pathways.30,31,32 This integrated spinal-supraspinal dynamic is crucial, as stronger functional coupling between the dorsal horn and PAG correlates with higher pain ratings.33 The specific correlation between CsPIP changes and active TENS analgesia—absent in sham or contralateral controls—confirms that our model directly indexes this spinal gating and descending modulation. Unlike brain-only models, the CsPIP directly captures the spinal gating mechanism, a critical component that supraspinal signatures cannot independently resolve. Intriguingly, while the CsPIP captured localized active-TENS analgesia, the SIIPS correlated more strongly with acute pain reduction. Unlike signatures optimized for primary nociception, the SIIPS is heavily weighted toward extranociceptive regions associated with expectations, context, and top-down pain modulation.21 Because experimental TENS recruits substantial expectancy and placebo components alongside physiological gating,34,35 it is plausible that the SIIPS preferentially reflects this broader cognitive-affective appraisal of analgesia. However, the CsPIP demonstrated robustness in tracking clinical phenotypes and longitudinal treatment responses, where pathophysiology involves persistent spinal sensitization and impaired descending control.14,36 This suggests that while SIIPS effectively reflects the momentary psychological appraisal of relief, the CsPIP provides a more stable readout of the physiological state of the corticospinal axis, making it suited for quantifying mechanistically grounded clinical outcomes. Collectively, while cognitive influences and corticospinal modulation co-shape relief, objectively evaluating chronic treatments requires quantifying actual physiological gating. By verifying authentic spinal inhibitory circuit engagement rather than transient expectancy effects, the CsPIP emerges as a crucial biomarker for precision pain management. A robust CsPIP response to non-invasive TENS indicates a well-functioning descending modulatory system, identifying promising candidates for pathway-specific invasive interventions like spinal cord or peripheral nerve stimulation.1,28

Beyond these empirical findings, the CsPIP can be naturally embedded within existing theoretical frameworks of pain. The corticospinal architecture of the pattern, with prominent weights in medial prefrontal and cingulate cortices, PAG, and cervical dorsal horn segments, aligns closely with classical models of descending pain modulation.2,10,28,37 In these models, cortico-PAG-brainstem feedback loops adjust the gain of nociceptive transmission at the dorsal horn, dynamically shaping the impact of spinal input on perceived pain. Our results suggest that CsPIP expression provides a systems-level readout of this descending control, capturing how cortical and brainstem signals jointly regulate spinal nociceptive encoding. From a predictive coding perspective, the CsPIP can be interpreted as a neuraxial implementation of hierarchical inference: supraspinal components reflect descending predictions and their precision, whereas spinal components index precision-weighted prediction errors arising from dorsal horn processing. The sensitivity of the CsPIP to TENS-induced analgesia is, therefore, consistent with the idea that neuromodulation may act by altering descending predictions and the gain of spinal prediction-error signals, rather than simply attenuating peripheral input.

Beyond experimental neuromodulation in healthy participants, our clinical resting-state findings position CsPIP within the broader landscape of imaging-based pain biomarkers. Whole-brain signatures such as the connectivity-based tonic pain signature have shown impressive sensitivity and specificity for experimentally induced tonic pain and demonstrated generalizability to several forms of clinical pain across multiple cohorts.4 Likewise, the NPS has been validated across dozens of experimental datasets and shown to decrease with effective nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory treatment in knee osteoarthritis, supporting its use as a pharmacodynamic marker of nociceptive pain.12 Complementary approaches using machine learning on resting-state functional connectivity can classify chronic pain patients from pain-free controls above chance, indicating that intrinsic network architecture carries clinically relevant information.13 However, these prior biomarkers are predominantly cortical, typically omit spinal signals, and only a subset have been systematically tested for longitudinal treatment sensitivity in well-characterized patient cohorts. By contrast, CsPIP is trained on simultaneous corticospinal responses to acute nociceptive stimulation, yet its resting-state expression in chronic pain patients predicted baseline symptom severity and tracked treatment-induced pain relief more robustly than the cortical NPS. This suggests that a neuraxial representational format, learned under controlled nociceptive input, can capture clinically meaningful fluctuations in ongoing pain when applied to intrinsic corticospinal activity and highlights the added value of explicitly modeling spinal contributions relative to brain-only signatures. Notably, this predictive relationship was not uniform across the cohort. Unsupervised clustering based on PHQ-9 and IBS-SSS identified a high-symptom subgroup and a low-symptom subgroup, and CsPIP was primarily predictive in the latter. This pattern suggests that the biomarker may be most sensitive to pain-related neurophysiological variation in patients with a lower affective-symptom burden, whereas in more psychologically burdened patients, ongoing pain may be shaped more strongly by affective or cognitive factors not fully captured by a nociception-derived corticospinal signature.

Specifically, both somatic and visceral pain engage the classical pain matrix (e.g., dACC, anterior insula, and thalamus) to encode the unpleasantness and salience of the experience.1,2 This shared supraspinal mechanism is further supported by our finding that the brain-only NPS marginally significantly predicted baseline IBS pain severity, and additionally, the SIIPS1 was able to predict future pain. This confirms that the cortical encoding of suffering is indeed common across modalities. However, it is crucial to note that the CsPIP demonstrated superior predictive accuracy at baseline and, unlike the NPS, successfully tracked longitudinal treatment effects. This dissociation suggests that while the cortical backbone encodes the presence of pain, the integration of spinal features provided by CsPIP appears necessary to capture the broader magnitude of the pathology and its dynamic resolution.14,37 Notably, we observed a dissociation in predictive performance between the models during chronic pain treatment. While the cortical-only NPS exhibited a positive, albeit non-significant, trend in tracking treatment-induced pain relief, the neuraxial CsPIP demonstrated a robust and significant correlation with clinical outcomes. This contrast is mechanistically revealing. The positive trend in the NPS suggests that the cortical representation of nociception was indeed attenuated following treatment, reflecting the sensory outcome of relief. However, its failure to reach statistical significance likely stems from an inability to capture the specific reversal of spinal sensitization—a hallmark of chronic pain pathology.14,38 At the spinal level, this likely reflects viscerosomatic convergence, where visceral and somatic afferents converge onto the same wide-dynamic-range neurons in the dorsal horn, forming a shared ascending pathway for intensity coding.26,28 At the supraspinal level, both modalities engage the classical pain matrix to encode the salience and unpleasantness of the experience.1,2 In chronic conditions like IBS, the CsPIP likely captures the maladaptive hyperexcitability and central sensitization within this shared neuraxial system,14 rather than specific peripheral inputs. In contrast, any observed changes in the sham group are likely mediated by non-specific placebo effects, which lack the systematic biological signatures required for our model to make accurate predictions.

Our results also help reconcile static and dynamic resting-state abnormalities that have been proposed as biomarkers of chronic pain. Spectral studies have shown that alterations in low-frequency BOLD fluctuations, particularly within limbic-striatal regions such as the nucleus accumbens, can serve as both risk and state markers for the transition from acute to chronic pain.23 More generally, reviews of resting-state biomarkers emphasize that changes in local activity (e.g., fALFF) and large-scale network connectivity are robust features across chronic pain syndromes, yet they are often interpreted in isolation from task-derived nociceptive signatures and without explicit reference to neuraxial mechanisms.10,11 In parallel, HMM-based analyses of magnetoencephalography and resting-state fMRI data have demonstrated that chronic pain is associated with abnormal occupancy and transition patterns between large-scale functional states, consistent with the idea that pain involves maladaptive temporal dynamics rather than a single static network configuration.39 By constraining our HMM to CsPIP-defined corticospinal voxels and quantifying both static spectral CsPIP expression and state-wise CsPIP expression, we bridge these lines of work: we identify a pro-nociceptive state and an antinociceptive state. This neuraxial, state-resolved perspective suggests that chronic pain may reflect both an elevated tonic load in nociceptive circuits and a temporal bias toward remaining in high-pain states and failing to access antinociceptive states—features that are, in principle, targetable by state-informed neuromodulatory or pharmacological interventions.

The CsPIP’s corticospinal architecture holds promise for personalized pain management. While the brain biomarker has been applied to chronic pain,4,12 its brain-centric design may overlook maladaptive spinal plasticity (e.g., dorsal horn hyperexcitability in neuropathic pain14,38). This has direct relevance for chronic pain pathologies, where maladaptive spinal plasticity may amplify peripheral input indiscriminately.37 For instance, neuropathic pain conditions like postherpetic neuralgia are characterized by dorsal horn hyperexcitability driven by maladaptive synaptic plasticity.40 Importantly, activation of corticospinal pathway is associated with mechanical allodynia in neuropathic mice.27 The CsPIP’s ability to quantify both spinal (e.g., dorsal horn) and supraspinal (e.g., ACC and M1) contributions could revolutionize subtype classification. For example, a patient presenting with elevated dorsal horn weights and reduced ventral inhibition might be prioritized for spinal cord stimulation, which enhances GABAergic transmission at the dorsal horn,41,42 whereas another with dominant corticospinal covariation (e.g., dACC-spinal connectivity) may respond better to TMS targeting descending inhibitory pathways.20 This approach has the potential to address the limitations of the “trial-and-error” strategy in pain management, where over 40% of patients remain unresponsive to first-line therapies, a challenge largely driven by mechanistic mismatch.43 However, further research is needed to refine this model, particularly by extending and validating it in larger and more diverse clinical populations, including neuropathic and other chronic pain conditions where pain processing pathways may be altered.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. One limitation of the current study is the relatively small sample size in each dataset, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Although we performed rigorous cross-validation and external validation, the model’s performance could be influenced by sample-specific characteristics. Future studies leveraging large-scale population datasets, such as the UK Biobank,44 are warranted to validate these results and explore how different clinical phenotypes—including chronic pain or acute injury—might modulate these neural signatures. Additionally, despite the significant predictive accuracy, the model’s performance in low-pain-intensity conditions remains suboptimal. This may be due to the challenges in detecting neural representations of low pain intensity, which are likely less distinct and more variable. Future studies could explore how task design and experimental conditions might influence model accuracy, particularly in low-pain contexts. Finally, although CsPIP was trained and extensively validated in healthy participants, our clinical resting-state sample was modest in size and restricted to a single chronic pain condition. Future studies with larger, diagnostically diverse cohorts and different treatment modalities will be important to determine the generalizability of CsPIP across pain syndromes and to establish its utility for patient stratification and prognosis.

In conclusion, our study presents a multivariate corticospinal predictive model of pain intensity that integrates spinal cord signals with canonical pain matrix regions. By demonstrating the critical role of the spinal cord in pain processing, our results challenge traditional brain-centric approaches and provide a more comprehensive framework for understanding pain perception.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Study 1 initially recruited 95 healthy university students (all right-handed, without chronic pain conditions, psychiatric disorders, or recent analgesic use). Three participants were excluded from the final analysis due to technical malfunctions during data acquisition, yielding a final cohort of 92 participants (50 females; mean age = 21.9 ± 3.23 years). Of the 92 eligible participants who completed the core neuroimaging protocol, a randomly selected subset formed the Study 1-TENS that evaluated neuromodulation effects. Using a computer-generated random sequence, 61 individuals were allocated to active TENS (n = 30) or sham stimulation (n = 31). Study 2 initially recruited 40 healthy university students (all right-handed, with no history of chronic pain conditions, psychiatric disorders, or recent medication use). One participant was excluded from the final analysis due to a technical issue during data collection, resulting in a final sample of 39 participants (20 females; mean age = 23.05 ± 3.48 years). Study 3 included fifty healthy university students (25 females; mean age = 23.74 ± 2.64 years), all right-handed and free of neurological or psychiatric disorders by self-report. Study 4 initially recruited Sixty healthy university students. However, one participant failed to complete the full fMRI session, and another was excluded because head movement exceeded 3 mm. The final analytic sample therefore comprised 58 participants (30 females; mean age = 23.16 ± 2.61 years). All included individuals self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, and they were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. For Study 5, eighty-five patients with IBS were recruited from three centers in China: Dongfang Hospital, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine; Shaanxi Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine; and Xi’an Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Two patients were excluded due to the low imaging quality. Ultimately, 50 patients in the active electroacupuncture group (19 females; mean age = 30.12 ± 6.78 years) and 33 patients in the sham acupuncture group (8 females; mean age = 22.91 ± 4.46 years) successfully completed the study. Consecutive outpatients presenting with symptoms suggestive of IBS were screened by experienced clinicians. Eligible participants were untreated IBS patients who met the Rome I–IV diagnostic criteria,45 as determined by detailed clinical history, physical examination, and standard gastroenterological work-up. Following the initial clinical symptom assessment and baseline corticospinal fMRI scan, patients were randomly assigned to either the active electroacupuncture group or the sham acupuncture control group. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) evidence of severe organic intestinal disease on prior investigations, including colonoscopy, barium meal radiography, abdominal ultrasound, or other relevant clinical examinations; (2) presence of one or more features, such as unexplained rectal bleeding, anemia, abdominal mass, ascites, fever, or unintentional weight loss; (3) history of severe cardiac, cerebrovascular, rheumatologic/autoimmune, or endocrine disorders; (4) unstable mental state or comorbid psychiatric disorder requiring immediate intervention; or (5) pregnancy or breastfeeding. All included patients were free of ongoing pain-specific pharmacological or neuromodulatory treatments at enrollment. Study 1 was conducted at the Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, whereas Study 2, Study 3 and Study 4 were conducted at the Brain Imaging Platform of the Tsinghua Laboratory of Brain and Intelligence. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, with all procedures conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided written informed consent after passing MRI safety screening and received 100 CNY per hour remuneration upon study completion. Data regarding ancestry, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status were not collected, as they were not primary demographic variables of interest for the current neuroimaging predictive modeling. Additionally, the influence of sex and gender on the results of this study was not formally evaluated due to the primary focus on developing a generalizable corticospinal predictive model across the entire cohort. The absence of sex- and gender-based analyses may limit the generalizability of our findings to specific populations, which warrants further investigation in future adequately powered studies.

Method details

Experimental design

The experimental protocol of Study 1 comprised three phases: 1) Pre-test phase involving demographic data collection and MRI compatibility assessment; 2) Calibration phase featuring quantitative sensory testing (QST) to establish individualized thermal pain thresholds averaged across four trials, accompanied by standardized practice trials; 3) Experimental phase integrating functional neuroimaging with thermal pain assessment (Figure S4A). Thermal nociceptive stimuli were administered using a CHEPS thermode (27 mm diameter; Pathway System, Medoc Ltd., Ramat Yishai, Israel) positioned over the C5-C6 dermatomes of both volar forearms. Pressure uniformity was maintained through adjustable nylon strap tensioning. The experimental paradigm consisted of two counterbalanced stimulation blocks, each delivering 15 thermal stimuli per forearm using a pseudorandomized sequence. Stimulus profiles initiated from a 32°C baseline, reaching target temperatures at 40°C/s ramp rates, maintained for 3-s plateau phases before returning to baseline. Individualized temperature calibration implemented the method of limits: starting from 32°C with 0.5°C increments until three consecutive Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain intensity ratings reached 7/10 (anchors: 0 = no sensation, 4 = pain threshold, 10 = unbearable pain). In the formal experiment, pain ratings were recorded 12 s after the application of a heat pain stimulus at the target temperature, assessing both pain intensity and discomfort via 0–10 NRS (discomfort scale anchors: 0 = no discomfort, 10 = extreme discomfort). Each 5-s rating interface was followed by jittered inter-trial intervals (12–15 s) to mitigate temporal expectation effects. All experimental procedures were implemented using E-Prime 2.0.

Following neuroimaging-based pain assessment, a neuromodulation block was integrated within the same experimental session to evaluate the analgesic efficacy of TENS in the Study 1-TENS subcohort. Specifically, 61 participants were systematically recruited from the Study 1 cohort and randomly assigned via a computer-based randomization algorithm to either an active TENS group (n = 30) or a sham stimulation group (n = 31). TENS was administered using a multichannel electrical stimulator (SXC-4A, Beijing Sanxia Technology Co., Ltd., China). Two electrode pairs (diameter: 16 mm; inter-electrode distance: 3 cm) were positioned over the C5–C6 dermatome on the ventrolateral aspect of the left forearm (treatment side). Stimulation consisted of constant-current square-wave pulses delivered at 100 Hz frequency with 2000 μs pulse width in the active TENS group. Stimulation intensity was determined through sensory threshold titration: commencing at 2 mA and incrementally increased by 0.5 mA steps until participants reported “strong, non-painful tingling.” The final intensity (6.04 ± 1.44 mA, mean ± SD) elicited robust paresthesia without pain or muscle contraction. Sham group participants received identical electrode placement but no actual current flow, with investigators simulating device operation. This investigator-masked single-blind study maintained participant blinding regarding treatment allocation. To minimize placebo effects and confounding variables (e.g., expectancy), investigators: (a) Avoided disclosing the analgesic research objectives, (b) Did not characterize stimulation as therapeutic intervention, (c) Employed identical participant instructions across groups. All participants received standardized instructions: “During the next 30 min, electrical stimulation will be continuously applied to your left forearm. Please close your eyes and focus on perceiving the stimulation.” Participants remained seated quietly with attention directed to the stimulation site throughout the 30-min intervention. neuroimaging-based pain assessment was conducted immediately post-intervention using identical protocols as those employed during baseline neuroimaging assessments.

The protocol of Study 2 (Figure S4B) involved a sequential pre-test and experimental phases designed to examine neural and behavioral responses to calibrated pain and itch stimuli across varying intensities. During the pre-test, participants’ pain thresholds were assessed using a CHEPS thermode (Medoc Ltd.), with personalized thresholds determined by five repeated measurements (threshold: NRS = 1; low-pain: NRS = 3; high-pain: NRS = 7). For low-itch stimuli, a 0.05 mL solution of 1% histamine dihydrochloride was applied via skin prick testing to the volar aspect of the right forearm, followed by placement of a histamine-impregnated patch (2 × 2 cm, 0.1 mL of 1% histamine). High-itch stimuli were induced using a 25°C cooling stimulus administered via an ATS probe (Medoc Ltd.) to the histamine-treated site. The experimental phase utilized a pseudorandomized, counterbalanced block design, incorporating low- and high-intensity pain and itch conditions. Pain stimuli were delivered through the CHEPS thermode (27 mm contact area) at calibrated temperatures (low-pain: 42°C–45°C; high-pain: 47°C–49°C), with a 40°C/s ramp from a 32°C baseline, followed by a 10-s plateau. Itch stimuli were produced through the aforementioned histamine-cold coupling, with low-itch intensity driven by histamine sensitization, while high-itch intensity was enhanced by concurrent 25°C cooling. Each block comprised 15 trials per condition (i.e., 30 trials for either pain or itch), with randomized inter-trial intervals (8–10 s) to minimize anticipatory bias. Post-stimulus ratings for intensity were obtained using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (“no sensation”) to 10 (“maximal pain/itch”), presented for 4 s after each trial. To ensure metric consistency with the training dataset, the subjective ratings in Study 2 were linearly rescaled to align with the effective nociceptive range utilized during model development. Pain and itch stimuli were applied to overlapping regions within the C6 dermatome, marked by methylene blue. To prevent cross-modality interference, MRI scanning sessions for pain and itch conditions were separated by a minimum of 60 min to ensure washout of any residual sensory or inflammatory effects. Skin integrity was checked between sessions, and residual histamine was neutralized using 0.9% saline compresses prior to further stimulation.

In Study 3, electrical stimulation was delivered to the right volar forearm, 10 cm proximal to the wrist, via surface electrodes. Stimuli consisted of trains of rectangular pulses (100 Hz, pulse width 2,000 μs) with a total duration of 5 s per trial. Before the main task, participants were familiarized with the stimulation and used a 0–10 numerical rating scale to evaluate their sensory and pain experience, where 0 indicated “no sensation”, 1 “slight touch”, 2 “moderate touch”, 3 “clear touch”, 4 “pain onset”, and 10 “unbearable pain”; ratings from 4 to 10 indexed increasing levels of pain intensity. For each participant, an individually calibrated stimulation intensity was determined at the right forearm site by gradually adjusting the current and asking the participant to rate each stimulus. The pain threshold was defined as the mean current amplitude that produced the same pain rating on at least three separate trials. In the formal pain task, participants received 15 electrical stimuli at the individually calibrated intensity. The inter-stimulus interval was 5 s. After each 5-s stimulus train, participants reported their perceived pain intensity using the same 0–10 numerical rating scale. Stimulus presentation and behavioral data collection were controlled using E-Prime 3.0.

During simultaneous corticospinal fMRI, each participant completed a 32-trial pain empathy task adapted from Zhou et al.46 in Study 4. Sixteen trials depicted a confederate receiving a painful electric shock videos (“pain” condition) and sixteen depicted a painless contact video (“no-pain” condition). At trial onset, a lightning-bolt cue was displayed for 1 s: a light-colored icon signaled an upcoming painful stimulus, whereas a dark icon signaled a painless one. A variable fixation cross followed for 0.5–1.5 s (jittered, uniform distribution). The video then showed the recipient’s hand as the stimulus was delivered (2 s). Immediately afterward, participants had up to 5 s to rate their own affective response on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 (“no feeling at all”) to 100 (“extremely bad”). All 32 videos were presented once in pseudorandom order, with no more than two trials of the same condition appearing consecutively. Total task duration was approximately 6 min, and the sequence was programmed to synchronize precisely with fMRI volume acquisition.

In Study 5, patients in both groups were scheduled to receive a course of 18 intervention sessions (active or sham), each lasting 20 min, delivered three times per week over a 6-week period (ideally on non-consecutive days). Participants and clinical outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation; however, given the manual nature of the intervention, the acupuncturists were not blinded. All treatments were administered by licensed acupuncturists with at least 5 years of clinical experience. Prior to patient enrollment, all acupuncturists participated in a standardized training workshop that covered acupoint localization, needle insertion depth, stimulation procedures, and documentation, to ensure protocol adherence and inter-operator consistency. In the active electroacupuncture group, the treatment protocol targeted bilateral Zusanli (ST36), selected based on prior work indicating potential analgesic effects of stimulation at this acupoint in chronic pain conditions.47,48 Sterile, single-use disposable needles (Huatuo; length 25–40 mm; diameter 0.25 mm) were inserted perpendicularly at ST36 to an individualized depth sufficient to reach the muscular layer. Immediately after insertion, manual stimulation (alternating twirling, lifting–thrusting, and slight pressing) was applied for approximately 30 s to elicit a typical deqi sensation (described by patients as soreness, numbness, distension, or heaviness). Following deqi, the needles were connected to an electroacupuncture stimulator to deliver continuous electrical stimulation for the remainder of the 20-min session; stimulation intensity was adjusted to produce a clear but tolerable sensation without pain or muscle spasm. In the sham acupuncture group, patients received sham acupuncture at non-acupoints (sham acupoints) using validated blunt-tip placebo needles. These needles were inserted into adhesive pads rather than penetrating the skin. The procedures and other treatment settings were kept consistent with the active electroacupuncture group, and the needles were extracted after 20 minutes for each treatment. Simultaneous corticospinal fMRI scans were acquired twice for each patient, once before the first electroacupuncture session (baseline) and once after completion of the 18-session treatment course. Prior to each scanning session, patients completed a series of clinical questionnaires, including the IBS Symptom Severity Scale (IBS-SSS), IBS Quality of Life (IBS-QoL), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Fear of Pain Questionnaire (FPQ), Pain Anxiety Symptoms Scale (PASS), and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS). Patients also recorded daily abdominal pain scores, with pre- and post-treatment ratings calculated as the averages from the week preceding the baseline scan and the final week of treatment, respectively.

MRI acquisition

All corticospinal imaging data were collected on 3T Siemens Prisma scanners with 64-channel head/neck coils. Participants maintained head stability through customized positioning. Cervical spine elevation using foam padding under the head and lateral support pads minimized motion artifacts. Additional restraint was achieved through a neck stabilization collar while hearing protection ensured participant comfort. The protocol specifically prohibited head movement and swallowing during acquisitions.

Our fMRI acquisition strategy employed a customized Siemens SMS-EPI protocol for simultaneous brain-cervical cord imaging. Key parameters included: Expanded 192 × 192 mm2 FOV with 70 axial slices (4 mm thickness) spanning C1-C7 vertebrae and cerebral regions; Targeted 3D shimming orthogonal to the imaging plane for brainstem-cervical field homogeneity; PA phase encoding configuration with 80° flip angle, 100% phase FOV, and 1.5 × 1.5 mm2 spatial resolution. Imaging acceleration employed multiband GRAPPA (acceleration factor = 2) with interleaved slice acquisition, achieving optimized TR/TE = 2680/27 ms. B0 field inhomogeneity correction utilized dual phase-encoded reference scans (TR/TE = 7500/87 ms) matching the fMRI geometry through 2D SMS-EPI acquisitions. This distortion correction methodology has been previously validated.49 Physiological monitoring integrated Siemens PMU with 400Hz sampling of respiratory and cardiac signals. These recordings generated noise covariates for subsequent fMRI analysis. The high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained via 3D MPRAGE (1 mm3 isotropic) with sagittal orientation and the following parameters: TR/TI = 2300/900 ms, TE = 2.28 ms, 192 × 288 × 288 mm3 FOV, 8° flip angle.

MRI preprocessing

All raw MRI scans were initially stored as individual DICOM files for each imaging sequence. These files were converted into NIFTI format using dcm2niix (version 1.0.20230411). Data preprocessing was accomplished through a pipeline incorporating deepbet, the Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT; version 6.1), FMRIB Software Library (FSL; version 6.0.5), and custom Python scripts (Python 3.9.12). Quality control was systematically performed after each step to ensure accuracy.

Structural MRI preprocessing

For each participant’s cortico-spinal structural MRI, we first recorded the upper z axis coordinate of C1 by visual inspection. The images were then divided into their respective brain and spinal cord portions. Cranial stripping for the 3D brain images was performed with deepbet, whereas SCT was employed to segment the spinal cord and produce binary masks from the 3D spinal cord scans. The segmentation was checked for errors and corrected manually as necessary. Intervertebral discs were marked visually to facilitate straightening of the cord, followed by applying an affine transformation to align these structural images with the standard vertebral levels. A non-rigid transformation was then used to register the images to the PAM50 T1-weighted template.

Functional MRI preprocessing

For each participant’s corticospinal fMRI data, we began by preprocessing the recorded respiratory and cardiac signals using custom Python scripts. Specifically, the scanning time course was synchronized with respiratory and cardiac signals to generate trigger files, then modeled using FSL PNM (Physiological Noise Modeling). Assuming quasi-periodicity of physiological signals, PNM applies a low-order Fourier series to estimate respiration- and heartbeat-related phases. Additional higher-order Fourier terms and interactions between the respiratory and cardiac cycles were included, yielding 32 noise regressors in total. We removed global physiological noise using custom Python code and reconstructed the data in NIFTI format through the Python Nibabel package. To address distortions in the fMRI scans, X- and Y-dimension distortion correction was performed using two reversed-phase-encoded B0 field maps and the FSL topup tool. Next, slice timing correction was subsequently applied using FSL. We then labeled the upper z-coordinate of C1 and separated the brain from the spinal cord in these fMRI images for additional preprocessing steps.

For the brain fMRI portion, images were smoothed with a 5 mm full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel to enhance signal-to-noise ratio, and a high-pass filter (100-s cutoff) was used to remove low-frequency signal fluctuations. Ultimately, the functional scans were aligned to each participant’s structural space via ANTsPy, followed by rigid, affine, and nonlinear registrations to the standard MNI brain template (2 mm resolution). In the spinal cord fMRI images, SCT was used to perform motion correction within a cylindrical mask automatically drawn along the central axis of the spinal cord, thereby excluding regions outside the vertebral canal. Spatial smoothing was carried out in the x and y directions using a 2D Gaussian kernel (3 mm FWHM). To account for noise in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), we computed an additional regressor based on the upper 10% variance in the CSF voxel time series, which was subsequently incorporated in first-level analyses. After segmenting the spinal cord mask with SCT, any errors were manually amended. Finally, spinal cord fMRI data underwent a two-step registration within SCT: first, from the individual structural space to the PAM50 template, and then from the functional space to the individual structural space.

Model development

This study developed multivariate corticospinal patterns for pain intensity prediction using brain and spinal COPEs images acquired during left- and right-hand thermal pain stimulation in Study 1 participants. The dataset was partitioned into training and test subsets at a 2:1 ratio with fixed random seed initialization. The COPE images from the same subject’s left and right hand treated as a single group to ensure that neither COPEs from any participant were split across training, testing, and cross-validation. A LASSO-PCR algorithm implemented in scikit-learn was trained to predict pain intensity, with model performance evaluated through participant-stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Hyperparameters remained fixed (scikit-learn’s default Lasso alpha = 1, all PCA components retained). During each cross-validation iteration in training subset, algorithms were trained on standardized data from approximately 90% of participants and tested on the remaining subset using identical scaling parameters, iterating until all folds served as test data. For the brain, feature extraction was confined to voxels within a pre-specified template of group-level univariate activation regions. By contrast, no a priori feature-selection mask was applied to the spinal cord data, since the spinal-cord voxel count is orders of magnitude smaller. Prediction accuracy was measured via Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (R2), and root-mean-square error (RMSE) between predicted and observed values. To identify neuroanatomical voxels contributing consistently to prediction, bootstrap resampling (10000 iterations) was applied to generate LASSO-PCR models from randomly sampled datasets with replacement. For visualization purposes, voxel-wise z-scores and p-values were derived from coefficient distributions, thresholded at z > 2.3 and cluster-wise correction q < 0.05 using whole-brain weight maps (False positive rate correction for spinal weight maps). Moreover, a null distribution was established through 10000 value-permuted cross-validation trials to confirm statistical significance. For each permutation, pain intensity values were shuffled during model training while keeping the imaging data structure intact. Permutation-derived p-values were calculated as (C+1)/(N+1), where C denotes permutations with correlation coefficients exceeding or equaling the true value, and N represents total permutations. This rigorous validation ensured robustness against overfitting and confirmed suprathreshold predictive performance relative to chance.

To rigorously evaluate whether the relationship between corticospinal activity and pain intensity involves non-linear dynamics not captured by the linear framework, we additionally trained two non-linear multivariate models: SVR with an RBF kernel and RF regression. To ensure a direct and unbiased comparison with the linear CsPIP model, these non-linear algorithms were trained on the identical set of standardized corticospinal features and subjected to the same rigorous data partitioning scheme. Specifically, we employed a nested cross-validation approach where model hyperparameters (e.g., the penalty parameter C and epsilon for SVR; the number of trees and maximum depth for Random Forest) were optimized strictly within the training folds to prevent data leakage, prior to final evaluation on the independent hold-out test sets.

Region-wise pattern similarity analysis

To identify an electrical pain–specific multivariate pattern, we re-trained a LASSO–PCR model on the Study 4 dataset using the same procedure as for CsPIP. Briefly, preprocessed corticospinal activation maps from all electrical pain trials were vectorized and z-scored across voxels within participant. Principal component analysis was first used to reduce dimensionality of the voxel space. Regression weights predicting trial-wise pain ratings were then estimated using LASSO. The resulting coefficients were projected back into voxel space to yield a weight map for electrical pain. To quantify spatial overlap between CsPIP and the electrical pain model, we computed pattern similarity within a 275-region atlas comprising cortical, subcortical, cerebellar, brainstem and spinal cord territories based on the Brainnetome parcellation and PAM50. For each region, we extracted the voxelwise weights from CsPIP and from the electrical pain pattern, vectorized them, and calculated Pearson correlation coefficients as an index of pattern similarity. To control for multiple comparisons across regions, we applied Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/275). Regions showing significant similarity after correction were interpreted as parcels in which heat and electrical pain shared a common multivariate representation.

Single-trial prediction and calibration

We aimed to test whether the CsPIP could effectively predict individual trial-by-trial subjective pain levels in the external validation heat/itch dataset (Study 2). To this end, we performed a single-trial analysis, in which we constructed a first-level GLM design matrix with separate regressors for each stimulus presentation. For each subject, all trials were retained for subsequent analyses. Single-trial voxel-wise activation maps were then obtained by estimating the beta image associated with each regressor.

Next, we calculated CsPIP pattern expressions for the single-trial activation maps by taking the dot product of the vectorized activation images with the fixed CsPIP weight map. For each participant, we correlated the resulting pattern expressions with the corresponding trial-wise ratings separately for each experimental condition (e.g., high/low pain, high/low itch). Statistical significance of trial-wise prediction was evaluated at the individual level using Spearman correlation between predicted and observed ratings. Group-level effects were assessed on Fisher z-transformed correlation coefficients using one-sample tests against zero and paired tests to compare conditions (e.g., pain versus itch). In Study 2, we used the CsPIP derived from the discovery heat-pain cohort without any re-training, such that all trial-wise predictions in these datasets were fully independent of model estimation.

In addition, to formally test whether the strength of CsPIP–rating coupling differed between modalities, we fitted a linear mixed-effects models with single-trial ratings as the dependent variable. To facilitate model convergence and interpretation, the continuous predictor was within-subject standardized (Z scored), and the categorical predictor was effect-coded. The model included fixed effects of CsPIP prediction (continuous), condition (categorical), and their interaction. To account for between-subject variability in baseline ratings and sensitivities to both predictors, we specified a comprehensive random-effects structure, including random intercepts for participants as well as random slopes for both main effects.

For calibration analyses, we examined whether the magnitude of CsPIP predictions corresponded to the absolute level of reported pain at the single-trial level. Within each modality and dataset, trials were binned into k quantiles (k = 7, corresponding to the seven-integer pain-rating levels from 4 to 10 on our scale) according to CsPIP predictions. For each bin, we computed the mean predicted value and the mean observed rating and plotted these against each other to obtain empirical calibration curves separately for pain and itch. We then fitted simple linear regression models of observed rating on predicted value within each modality, extracting the slope and intercept as indices of calibration (ideal calibration: slope ≈1, intercept ≈0).

Comparison with established biomarkers

To benchmark the predictive utility of CsPIP against brain-only markers, we evaluated the pattern expression of the NPS3 and the SIIPS.21 Prior to analysis, the weight maps for both signatures were resampled to match the 2mm isotropic resolution of our preprocessed functional data using FSL’s FLIRT. For each individual fMRI activation map, a scalar pattern expression score was derived by calculating the voxel-wise spatial dot product between the signature weights and the neural response maps. This procedure ensures that comparisons between CsPIP and existing signatures are conducted within a consistent computational framework, allowing for a direct assessment of whether the integration of spinal signals enhances pain decoding beyond established cortical representations.

Phenotype clustering and subgroup analysis

To identify distinct clinical phenotypes, K-means clustering (k = 2) was applied to baseline PHQ-9 and IBS-SSS scores from 81 patients (two of the initial 83 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires). Scores were Z score standardized prior to analysis. Based on the resulting centroids, patients were partitioned into high burden (n = 40) and low burden (n = 41) subtypes. The corresponding raw baseline scores for the high burden and low burden subtypes were 11.03 ± 3.93 vs. 5.78 ± 3.26 for PHQ-9 and 289.12 ± 69.66 vs. 172.17 ± 51.36 for IBS-SSS, respectively. To determine whether the predictive power of baseline brain activity on clinical pain varied by phenotype, Pearson correlations were used to evaluate the relationship between baseline fALFF-based scores and average daily abdominal pain scores within each subgroup. Because robust predictive performance was specifically observed in the low burden cohort, our primary analysis and visualization (Figure 5A) focus on this subgroup.

Resting-state fMRI and clinical tracking

To validate the generalization of the task-trained CSPIP to resting-state data, we therefore utilized the fALFF as a static proxy for the tonic intensity of intrinsic neural excitability. Unlike raw time courses, fALFF captures the spatial topography of spontaneous signal power, providing a stable activation-like map compatible with the amplitude-based weights of the predictive model. Preprocessing followed standard pipelines mentioned above and our recent work.49 The preprocessed time series for each voxel were transformed into the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to obtain the power spectrum. We focused our analysis on the 0.01–0.13 Hz frequency bands, which are known to contain the majority of neuronally relevant BOLD fluctuations.11,23 fALFF was calculated as the ratio of the power sum within this specific low-frequency range to the sum of power across the entire detectable frequency range. This fractional approach effectively suppresses non-specific signal components derived from physiological noise (e.g., cardiac and respiratory pulsations) that typically manifest in higher frequency bands. The resulting fALFF maps were standardized and dot-producted with the CSPIP weight map to generate a static predicted pain score for each patient at baseline and post-treatment.

Baseline CSPIP, NPS, and SIIPS expression scores were correlated with clinical phenotypes. To assess sensitivity to longitudinal pain relief, we computed Δ-maps for each subject as the pre-treatment minus post-treatment fALFF difference. Pattern change scores (ΔCSPIP, ΔNPS, and ΔSIIPS) were derived by applying the same pattern-expression operation to these Δ-maps. For the formal clinical analyses in Study 5, the CsPIP, NPS, and SIIPS1 were applied in its complete, unmodified form.22,50 Analgesic response (ΔPain) was defined as the change in self-reported pain intensity (pre minus post). To ensure comparability across different models, the model responses were Z score standardized. Associations between ΔCSPIP and ΔPain were quantified using Pearson correlations.

Hidden Markov modeling of dynamic states

PCA preprocessing and dimensionality reduction

Dynamic analyses based directly on voxel-wise time courses are high-dimensional and can be dominated by global fluctuations and noise. We therefore adopted principal component analysis (PCA) to capture spatiotemporal patterns. This is particularly relevant for a corticospinal pain network, where abnormal propagation of nociceptive activity between spinal cord and cortex may be crucial. For the dynamic analysis, we restricted all computations to voxels within the CsPIP mask, including both supraspinal and spinal gray-matter voxels. For each subject and session, we extracted the preprocessed BOLD time series from these voxels and z-scored each voxel across time. We performed group-level PCA by temporally concatenating the time series across all subjects at baseline. We retained the first 20 components following prior fMRI research,51 which together explained the majority of variance in the corticospinal signal while providing a tractable feature space for Hidden Markov modeling.

Hidden Markov modeling

Resting-state fMRI is increasingly understood as reflecting rapid transitions between quasi-stationary network configurations rather than a single static connectivity pattern. HMMs explicitly model such data as a sequence of discrete latent states, each characterized by a multivariate distribution over observations and connected by probabilistic transitions. When applied to rs-fMRI, HMMs have been shown to reveal recurrent whole-brain states whose dwell times and transition probabilities are behaviorally relevant and heritable.25

Compared to sliding-window approaches, HMMs do not require specifying an arbitrary window length, can capture fast state transitions, and provide a principled description of state occupancy and switching structure. Because chronic pain is hypothesized to involve maladaptive persistence in pro-nociceptive network configurations and impaired access to antinociceptive ones, an HMM is well suited to testing whether the corticospinal network becomes locked into particular states at rest. We adapted this logic to resting-state pain by training a common state model across individuals and studying between-subject variation in state occupancy as a marker of pain severity.

HMM specification and training

Dynamic state estimation was performed on the baseline data from all 83 patients in the clinical dataset (including both the treatment and sham groups) using the GaussianHMM implementation from the hmmlearn Python package (version 0.3.3; https://hmmlearn.readthedocs.io). For each subject, the observation matrix consisted of the 20-dimensional PCA component time series at baseline. To obtain a group-level state space while respecting subject boundaries, time series were temporally concatenated across participants and passed to GaussianHMM together with a vector of segment lengths specifying the duration of each participant’s scan. We modeled each hidden state with a multivariate Gaussian distribution with diagonal covariance matrix. The initial state distribution and transition matrix were initialized to be uniform. Model parameters were estimated via expectation–maximization (EM) with a maximum of 1000 iterations and a convergence tolerance of 1e−4 on the change in log likelihood. A fixed random seed was utilized to ensure the reproducibility of the state estimation.

Selection of the number of states

To determine the optimal number of discrete corticospinal states, we fit a series of HMMs with the number of states K ranging from 2 to 15. For each K, we used a cross-validated log likelihood procedure adapted from Nau et al.24 Specifically, participants were split into two equally sized folds. An HMM was trained on the concatenated PCA time series of fold A and evaluated (without further parameter updates) on fold B by computing the log likelihood of the held-out data. The optimal number of states was defined as the K that maximized the mean cross-validated log likelihood across folds; this procedure selected K = 5, which was used in all subsequent analyses (Figure 5E). A final 5-state HMM was then fit to the concatenated baseline data from all participants (using the same initialization scheme but without cross-validation), yielding state-specific means and covariances in the 20-dimensional PCA space, along with a common transition matrix.

State time series and occupancy metrics

For each participant, we inferred the most probable state sequence over time using the Viterbi algorithm applied to that participant’s PCA time series under the group-level HMM. In addition to the temporal metric, we derived state-specific spatial patterns at the voxel level. For each subject and state k, we identified all TRs assigned to state k by the Viterbi decoding and averaged the reconstructed data over these TRs within the CsPIP voxel mask (cortex, brainstem and spinal cord). This yielded a state-specific mean activation map for each subject.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9.12, SciPy 1.13.1, antspyx 0.4.2, Nibabel 5.2.1, pingouin 0.5.4, scikit-learn 1.3.0, FSL 6.0.5, SCT 6.1, and hmmlearn 0.3.3. To evaluate predictive performance, associations between predicted and ground-truth values were generally assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Furthermore, the feature importance of the CsPIP model was robustly estimated using a bootstrapping test with 10,000 iterations. For Study 2, Spearman’s rank correlation was utilized to evaluate within-subject predictive performance for pain and itch conditions, and direct statistical comparisons between these two conditions were conducted using paired Student’s t-tests. For comparisons involving multiple conditions or time points (e.g., pre-versus post-treatment interactions, as shown in Figures 4B and 4D), an ANOVA was conducted. Subsequent post-hoc analyses were performed using the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. For clinical data analysis, an HMM was implemented to parse the time-series data into five distinct latent states. The comprehensive statistical details of all experiments, including the specific statistical tests used, exact p values, and correlation coefficients, can be found in the Results section, the figure legends, and the associated tables. Unless otherwise specified, n represents the exact number of human participants included in each analysis, as detailed in the Experimental Model and Study Participant Details section and the respective results paragraphs. Dispersion and precision measures are reported as mean ± SD in the text, or as 95% CI and SEM where specified in the figure legends. Significance was defined as p < 0.05, unless multiple comparison corrections (e.g., Bonferroni, TFCE) were explicitly applied. ∗ denotes p < 0.05; ∗∗ denotes p < 0.01; ∗∗∗ denotes p < 0.001; ns denotes not significant.

fMRI statistical analysis

First-level analysis

Brain and spinal cord fMRI data were independently analyzed using General Linear Model (GLM). Both models integrated pain stimulus events, rating block periods, and their temporal derivatives as explanatory variables (EVs). For spinal cord data, region-specific noise correction was implemented by extracting the highest-variance decile of CSF voxels per slice as nuisance regressors. Following first-level GLM estimation, spinal cord contrast parameter estimates (COPEs) for pain stimuli were normalized to PAM50 standard space using precomputed distortion field maps prior to group-level analysis.

Group-level analysis

For brain and spinal cord data, we implemented a multistage analytical approach tailored to neuroanatomical characteristics. Whole-brain examinations utilized FMRIB’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects (FLAME) with cluster-wise correction (Z > 3.1, p < 0.05). However, identifying activation in the brainstem poses particular challenges due to its heightened sensitivity to physiological noise and artifacts, its small volume, and its relative distance from the signal detectors within the head coil. Moreover, the use of FWE correction based on Gaussian random field theory may be less appropriate for small cylindrical structures such as the spinal cord, potentially increasing the risk of false negatives. Therefore, the analysis of spinal cord was employed nonparametric permutation-based inference using FSL’s randomize tool, incorporating 10,000 iterations with threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE, p < 0.05). We focused on the C6 segment in the PAM50 spinal gray matter template. For experimental conditions involving unilateral and bilateral hand pain stimulation, one-sample t-tests generated population-level activation maps through contrast-weighted parameter estimates.
