Racial and ethnic disparities in the cause of death for clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yingwei Xie, Yishan Zhang, Shuang Li, Li Chen, Yuexin Liu, Wenlian Xie, Zaosong Zheng, Hao Ping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The trends and contributors of racial and ethnic disparities in causes of death among patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma remain unclear. We analyse SEER data (2000-2019) and find that Black patients have the highest 5-year cumulative incidence of death (22.4% vs. 21.7% in whites). Despite a decreasing trend in mortality, the disparities persist between Black and white people (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.09–1.22) and between AIAN and white people (1.25, 1.10-1.43). Disparities in death from cardiovascular disease between Black and white people increase over time. Stage at diagnosis, receipt of surgery, income, and geographic factors partially explain the observed disparities in mortality patterns. This study identifies specific, measurable contributors to mortality disparities, suggesting that interventions targeting earlier detection, equitable treatment, and socioeconomic barriers are needed.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is among the most prevalent malignant tumors of the urinary system. The most common pathological type of RCC is clear cell RCC (ccRCC), which accounts for approximately 70% of cases and is characterized by high malignancy and a poor prognosis1. According to recent cancer statistics, kidney and renal pelvic cancer rank as the 6th most common cancers in men and 9th in women in terms of estimated new cases and deaths in the United States2. Notably, the mortality rate of RCC has been decreasing in the USA, with an annual decrease of about 2% between 2016 and 20202. However, whether this decline in mortality is equally seen in patients of all racial and ethnic origins warrants additional research.

Indeed, racial and ethnic disparities have been reported in several tumors3–8, with disparities in the outcome of RCC being the most prominent9–11. According to several epidemiological studies, the 5-year survival rate for ccRCC is 74% for White people, 55% for Black people, and 78% for Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders12,13. The survival rate of White people with RCC is higher than that of Black people with RCC9,10,14. This lower survival rate in Black people cannot be explained by a lower life expectancy in this population, since Black people are often diagnosed at a younger age, which should indicate a better prognosis15. Studies attribute the survival disadvantage of Black people to difficult access to surgical treatment, such as nephrectomy16,17. However, other scholars believe that differential access to nephrectomy cannot explain the racial disparities in survival18,19. As such, the reasons for these disparities are not completely understood. Moreover, racial and ethnic disparities may differ depending on the subtype of RCC. The proportion of subtypes varies among different racial and ethnic groups. For example, the proportion of papillary or chromophobe RCC is higher in Black people than in White people, and the prognosis of papillary or chromophobe RCC is significantly better than that of ccRCC20–23. Previous studies have found that, compared with White people, Black people have a 16.3% higher risk of death for all types of RCC, and a 10.8% higher risk for the ccRCC subtype12. These findings suggest that subtype analysis for RCC could help understand racial and ethnic disparities in RCC mortality. Moreover, characterizing mortality trends and patterns among patients with ccRCC from underrepresented racial and ethnic minority groups is essential to design targeted strategies that advance health equity across populations.

Here, we show the long-term trends and determinants of racial and ethnic disparities in ccRCC, which accounts for the majority of RCC cases and exhibits distinct molecular drivers linked to aggressive progression24. Using population-level data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, we quantify the cumulative incidence of ccRCC-specific death, analyze temporal shifts in racial and ethnic disparities, and identify modifiable factors contributing to these inequities. These findings provide actionable insights for developing targeted interventions to mitigate inequities in RCC outcomes.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included a total of 92,121 people diagnosed with ccRCC from the SEER database. Of these, 1072 (1.2%) were American Indian and Alaska Native (AIAN), 5939 (6.4%) were Asian and Pacific Islander (API), 5737 (6.2%) were Black, 16,960 (18.4%) were Hispanic, and 62,417 (67.8%) were White (Supplementary Dataset 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 60.4 years, ranging from 57.9 years in Hispanic people to 61.2 years in White people. Overall, 27,973 (30.3%) people died, of whom 15,555 (16.9%) died from ccRCC. The death rate from all causes was 32.2% (1845/5737) in Black people and 32.0% (19,953/62,417) in White people. AIAN and White people had the lowest localized cancer rates at diagnosis. The highest surgery rate was approximately 93.8% (in API people) and the lowest was 90.4% (in AIAN people). The proportion of people from a high-income county was the highest in API people (56.5%) and lowest in Black people (19.3%; Supplementary Dataset 1).

The trend in the cumulative incidence of death

Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 1 present the cumulative incidence of all-cause and cause-specific death between 2000 and 2019. The highest 5-year cumulative incidence of all-cause death was seen in Black people (22.4%) and the lowest in API people (18.5%) (Supplementary Dataset 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). This trend was consistent with the 3-year and 10-year cumulative incidence of death (Supplementary Dataset 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). The highest 5-year cumulative incidence of death from ccRCC was seen in White people (13.9%) and the lowest in AIAN people (11.8%; Supplementary Dataset 2). During the 2000–2014 period, the 5-year cumulative incidence of all-cause deaths decreased in all racial and ethnic groups except in AIAN people (who experienced fluctuating mortality), and with the highest declines were noted in Black people (absolute change 11.7%). This decrease was primarily attributed to the continued decline in mortality from ccRCC. The relative racial and ethnic disparities

Analysis of factors linked to racial and ethnic disparities

Covariate selection was informed by prior knowledge through directed acyclic graph (DAG) construction (Supplementary Fig. 5). All included covariates underwent evaluation for multicollinearity and intercorrelations (Supplementary Fig. 6). Multivariable regression analysis showed that the stage of diagnosis, tumor grade, surgery, and county income were significantly associated with the risk of all-cause and cause-specific death (Supplementary Table 3). There was a negative association between county income level and the risk of all-cause and cause-specific death. Racial and ethnic disparities were prominent: Black people showed the lowest proportions of patients with metastatic/poor-grade tumors, but the highest proportions of patients without surgery, while API groups had the highest proportion of patients living in high-income counties (Supplementary Figs. 7–10). Adjusted analyses demonstrated intensified racial and ethnic disparities between Black/AIAN and White people in the patients with local/regional disease and the patients living in high-income counties and rural areas, whereas the racial and ethnic disparities between API/Hispanic and White people peaked in the patients living in middle-income counties (Supplementary Dataset 4).

We further quantified the proportional mediation of these factors on racial and ethnic disparities in all-cause and cause-specific mortality using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. The proportion of disparity explained by individual factor adjustments is detailed in Supplementary Table 4. After adjusting for stage at diagnosis, the HR for Black–White disparities increased (from 1.15 to 1.26), while the API–White HR decreased (from 0.84 to 0.82), indicating more frequent late-stage diagnosis among White patients. Based on Supplementary Table 4 findings, sequential adjustment for multiple factors revealed that differences in tumor stage, surgery, location, and income collectively explained 56.0% of the AIAN–White disparity (HR reduction from 1.25 to 1.10). Surgery and income differences explained 33.0% of the Black–White disparity (HR decrease from 1.15 to 1.10). Discussion

Using a public dataset, our study confirms racial and ethnic disparities in ccRCC mortality in the USA. We present the cumulative incidence of death and cause of death distribution in patients diagnosed with ccRCC in the USA between 2000 and 2019. To our knowledge, this study presents the most comprehensive and long-term analysis of racial and ethnic disparities among patients diagnosed with ccRCC. Our findings reveal that, despite an overall decline in ccRCC mortality, persistent survival gaps exist between Black and AIAN patients compared with White patients. Disease stage, surgery, income, and geographic location emerged as factors contributing to these differential outcomes. These findings substantially advance our understanding of health inequities and suggest the need for interventions tailored to different racial and ethnic groups and for the promotion of optimal resource allocation. They also have implications for future research directions, which should consider exploring the interaction between socioeconomic factors and biological mechanisms.

Furthermore, we observed a progressive decline in cumulative mortality in patients with ccRCC, which corroborates previously published findings2,25. On the one hand, the decline in death from ccRCC may be partly attributed to the increased proportion of localized RCC14. On the other hand, it could be attributed to vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors and checkpoint inhibitors, which are used in cases of advanced RCC and significantly influence the survival of these patients26–28. Additionally, the decline in CVD mortality seems to be responsible for the decline in total mortality in patients with ccRCC. Previous results from the general population in the USA have also shown a dramatic decline in CVD mortality over the past 20 years29,30. This decline is largely attributable to rapid advances in CVD prevention and treatment, including reduction in major risk factors such as smoking, treatment and control of hypertension, and widespread use of statins30,31.

Although previous studies have documented racial and ethnic disparities in RCC, most of these studies are based on survival32–34. However, mortality is a better indicator of anti-cancer progression than morbidity and survival, since these are more susceptible to bias from changes in testing practices35. Despite an overall decline in ccRCC mortality, racial and ethnic mortality gradients persist. Black and AIAN patients experience significantly higher mortality rates than White patients, whereas API patients tend to have better outcomes. Although Black/AIAN populations are often diagnosed earlier (potentially because of the higher prevalence of risk factors like smoking and hypertension in these populations)9,14,33, this advantage does not translate into survival benefits10,11,16. Healthcare access inequity seems to be a key driver of these disparities: lower surgical rates in Black and AIAN groups account for ~20% of the disparities between Black and White people, a finding that aligns with documented systemic biases (e.g., physician implicit bias, cultural distrust)36–38. Additionally, income disparities seem to exacerbate these inequities in two ways: by limiting access to advanced diagnostics in lower-income groups and by promoting risk behaviors (e.g., smoking) through chronic stress39. Notably, the widening outcome gaps in high-income areas suggest that conventional socioeconomic metrics do not adequately capture structural discrimination. This highlights the need for novel “race-space vulnerability” assessment frameworks to better understand these disparities.

Previous studies have also found that the causes of racial and ethnic disparities include differences in the quality of care received9,16, patient attitudes and beliefs regarding treatment decisions9,16, comorbidities, and stressful life events related to socioeconomic status9,14,40,41. Evidence has shown that structural racism is a root cause of racial health inequities42. Structural racism refers to the totality of ways in which societies foster racial discrimination through mutually reinforcing inequitable systems that in turn reinforce discriminatory beliefs, values, and distribution of resources, which together affect the risk of adverse health outcomes and perpetuate racial group inequity43. Therefore, addressing the inequalities faced by racial and ethnic minorities in the survival of ccRCC requires starting from the structural, cross-cutting, and internal barriers they face and promoting fairness in multiple aspects, such as education, culture, policies, and equal opportunities.

Our study analyzed data for the 20 years preceding the COVID-19 pandemic. During the COVID-19 pandemic, various health inequalities were specifically pronounced, disproportionately affecting racial and ethnic minority groups44–46. Among patients with cancer, Black patients were more likely to contract COVID-19 than White patients47–49. Although the effect of the COVID-19 epidemic on ccRCC was not investigated, our analysis of the causes of death suggests that the COVID-19 epidemic may further exacerbate racial and ethnic disparities in patients with ccRCC.

This study has limitations. First, we could not obtain more specific data for treatment options, including the type of surgery and the use of targeted drugs and immune checkpoint inhibitor drugs. This limitation impaired our capacity to explore true explanations of racial and ethnic associated variations in treatment interventions. Second, we could not obtain information on body-mass index and comorbidities, which could not be explored as potential factors affecting racial and ethnic disparities. Third, the lack of individual-level socioeconomic data, including insurance status, household income, and cultural level, hindered our exploration of socioeconomic contributors to differential outcomes across populations. Fourth, a potential misclassification of causes of death by population-based cancer registries cannot be ruled out50,51. However, we excluded patients from studies with unknown causes of death to minimize this misclassification. Fifth, there is poor representation and intra-racial/ethnic heterogeneity in the included population. Although we included data from 12 states, there may be poor representation of some racial/ethnic minorities, such as AIAN, due to the small number of people included. In addition, there is also a large heterogeneity within the API and Hispanic populations. As a holistic analysis, equity may be overlooked for some disadvantaged racial/ethnic groups. Finally, we only quantified racial and ethnic disparities in ccRCC mortality in the USA. Notably, ccRCC is a global disease with increasing epidemiological importance, necessitating a global perspective. This study presents a fundamental step in future research to elucidate the effect of racial and ethnic groups on the prognosis of ccRCC.

In conclusion, this study provides the most comprehensive analysis of racial and ethnic disparities in both all-cause and cause-specific mortality for ccRCC. Our findings reveal that despite overall mortality reductions, racial disparities have persisted and, in some cases, widened—particularly in deaths due to CVD. Notably, disparities in non-cancer mortality have intensified over time, which highlights the need to expand survivorship care beyond cancer treatment. Through multiple Cox proportional hazards regressions, we demonstrate that socioeconomic and treatment-related factors, particularly differential access to nephrectomy, geographic location, and income, explain a substantial portion of these mortality gaps. However, structural inequities persist beyond these mediators, underscoring the need for interventions that address systemic barriers to equitable cancer care. Our study’s integration of multi-level socioeconomic and geographic gradients provides a refined framework for understanding racial disparities in ccRCC survival and calls for urgent policy and clinical interventions targeting modifiable social determinants of health. Future research should further investigate the post-pandemic evolution of outcome differences and the interaction between social and biological determinants in shaping ccRCC outcomes.

Methods

Study design, data sources, and variables

This retrospective, observational study adhered to the STROBE observational research reporting guidelines. We obtained ethical approval (TREC2024-KYS070) for this study from the institutional review board of the Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, and written informed consent was waived, given the data from a public database. We used data from the SEER database. We included people with their first primary ccRCC (histological code: ICD-O-3 code: 8310/3) diagnosed between 2000 and 2019. Data were retrieved from 12 registries (Connecticut, Hawaii, Iowa, San Francisco-Oakland SMSA, Utah, New Mexico, Seattle [Puget Sound], San Jose-Monterey, Rural Georgia, Los Angeles, Alaska Natives, and Atlanta [Metropolitan]) (November 2021 submission), covering approximately 12.24% of the US population (based on the 2010 census).

We included the following variables in the database: race and ethnicity, year of diagnosis, age at diagnosis, sex, stage at diagnosis, tumor grade, cancer-directed surgery status, chemotherapy status, radiotherapy status, median household income, and location. We reported race and ethnicity in five mutually exclusive categories according to the SEER original coding standard of race/ethnicity: Non-Hispanic AIAN, Non-Hispanic API, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Non-Hispanic White. Information on patient sex was obtained from the SEER registry data, which is based on medical records and relevant administrative documents. The final cohort comprised 34,526 females and 57,595 males. We defined localized/regional stage cancer as non-metastatic cancer and distant-stage cancer as metastatic cancer52. The income level was defined as the median household income at the county level where the population was located. Based on the SEER Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, we categorized geographic locations into urban and rural areas.

Given the retrospective, population-based design utilizing pre-existing, de-identified registry data, blinding was neither applicable nor feasible. All variables were objectively defined and extracted from standardized registry fields, minimizing subjective bias. As the study involved analysis of pre-collected data, no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Outcomes

People were followed up from the date of cancer diagnosis until death or until censored on December 31, 2019. The causes of death were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases editions (ICD), ninth (ICD-9) for the years 1995–98 and tenth (ICD-10) for 1999–2019. We classified the causes of death into all-cause, ccRCC-specific, and other causes. Other-cause deaths included deaths due to CVD, other (non-ccRCC) cancers, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, and other specific causes (Supplementary Table 5).

Statistics and reproducibility

We categorized the year of diagnosis into four periods: 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. We used the Fine and Gray model to calculate the cumulative incidence of death at 3 years, 5 years, and 10 years of follow-up, accounting for competing risks of death53. We used Join-Point regression to analyze temporal trends in the cumulative incidence of death. This methodology employs an iterative algorithm to identify statistically significant inflection points (joinpoints) in time-series data. Model selection was conducted through Monte Carlo permutation testing (n = 4499 iterations), systematically comparing nested models with k versus k + 1 joinpoints. Two-tailed p values were calculated to quantify the probability of observing additional joinpoints under the null hypothesis of no temporal trend alteration. Given that the White population was the majority population in the USA, we selected White people as the reference group. We quantified absolute racial and ethnic disparities using the absolute rate difference for the 5-year cumulative incidence of death. We quantified the relative disparities using adjusted HRs with the cause-specific hazard regression model, adjusting for age as a continuous variable54. We added a multiplication interaction term between racial and ethnic groups and diagnostic period in a regression model to test whether racial and ethnic disparities changed over time.

We utilized DAGs to identify potential covariates55, addressed multicollinearity using variance inflation factor analysis, assessed linear relationships via Pearson correlation, and subsequently employed a series of univariable and multivariable cause-specific hazard regression models to evaluate potential associations between covariates and all-cause/cause-specific mortality. For hierarchically structured data, cluster-robust standard errors were implemented to account for inter-cluster heterogeneity, with parallel sensitivity analyses conducted using frailty models to verify model robustness. We performed a multiple Cox proportional hazards regression to assess the relative impact of each covariate on racial and ethnic disparities in both all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality56. The baseline model was defined as race and ethnicity plus age. The impact of each covariate on racial and ethnic disparities was initially tested in the baseline model, with covariates added one by one to the model. We ranked covariates by their magnitude of attenuation on racial and ethnic disparities (quantified by the decrease in HR when added to the model). These covariates were then sequentially incorporated into a series of multivariable models, ordered by their HR attenuation strength. The resulting HR reduction was used to calculate the proportion of disparity explained by covariates57. The false discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed for multiple tests. All statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.2.0). All two-sided p values lower than 0.05 were considered significant.
