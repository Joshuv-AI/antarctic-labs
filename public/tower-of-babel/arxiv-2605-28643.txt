GraphLit: Learning Text-Enriched Dynamic Character Network Representations for Literary Study
Gaspard Michel, Elena V. Epure, Romain Hennequin, Christophe Cerisara, Mirella Lapata

Abstract
Methods to represent literary texts as graphs or sequences of graphs mainly focus on representing character interactions, and often overlook another crucial aspect: the textual context in which characters interact. We introduce Dynamic Heterogeneous Character Networks (DHCNs), which organize long novels into temporally localized heterogeneous graphs that align characters with their textual contexts. We extract around 20,000 DHCNs from Project Gutenberg, and propose GraphLit, a self-supervised learning framework that learns rich literary representations through a masked graph autoencoder objective. Across a wide-range of 12 character-related tasks, GraphLit improves over text-only and graph-only baselines, particularly on tasks requiring contextual understanding. Finally, we demonstrate the applicability of DHCNs and GraphLit for literary analysis by studying the link between narrative non-linearity and dynamic social features. 1 1 1 Code and data available at https://github.com/gasmichel/GraphLit

A longstanding debate in literary criticism concerns whether characters should be analyzed as narrative functions or as human-like figures with psychological, social, and affective dimensions
Woloch 2003
.
Formalist approaches, such as Propp’s, define characters through the roles they fulfill in narrative structures, such as hero or villain
Propp 1968
. In contrast, for Barthes, characters emerge through evolving textual signifiers, including names, appearances, and patterns of speech
Barthes 1970
, while Zunshine emphasizes how readers interpret characters as social agents with beliefs, intentions, actions, and emotions
Zunshine 2003
.

These divergent views have also been reflected in computational methods to model fictional characters.
For instance, aligned with
Zunshine 2003
, methods for extracting Character Networks that represent relationships among characters as interconnected nodes and links have been proposed
Labatut and Bost 2019
.
However, because they do not incorporate literary content, they cannot explicitly capture
signifiers used to describe characters
.

In contrast, text-based methods
Michel et al. 2024
;
Yang and Anderson 2024
;
Bourgois et al. 2026
do not integrate social dynamics inherent to character networks.
This compromises the ability to explicitly model important aspects such as the prominence in Woloch’s
character-space
—or how much narrative space a character is taking with respect to the others
Moretti 2011
.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have also been explored as a means of generating character representations, including embeddings
Xu et al. 2024
, textual descriptions
Gurung and Lapata 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
, roles
Stammbach et al. 2022
, or event participation
Zhang et al. 2026
.
However, LLMs are known to exhibit poor generalization capabilities when applied to previously unseen novels
Chang et al. 2023
and are often evaluated privately on copyrighted novels that cannot be shared for reproducibility
Gurung and Lapata 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
.
These limitations motivate models that can be trained and evaluated on reproducible public-domain corpora while explicitly representing both textual and relational information.

In this work, we propose
GraphLit
, a self-supervised framework for learning literary representations by organizing long novels into temporally localized heterogeneous graphs that align characters with the textual contexts in which they appear, which we call
Dynamic Heterogeneous Character Networks
(DHCNs).
Using DHCNs extracted from a large corpus of approximately 20,000 novels drawn from the Project Gutenberg library, GraphLit extends the Heterogeneous Graph Masked Autoencoder framework (HGMAE)
Tian et al. 2023
to derive representations of narrative units such as characters, text blocks, and entire books.
Figure
1
summarizes the pipeline.

To our knowledge,
Inoue et al. 2022
is the only prior hybrid approach that combines (static) character networks with textual signifiers through a set of typed dependency relations based on the work of
Bamman et al. 2014
.
In contrast, GraphLit models characters through evolving textual contexts over narrative time, and incorporates social relationships through character network structures.
Learned representations improve over text-only and graph-only baselines across several character- and book-level tasks
Inoue et al. 2022
, and yield substantial gains when incorporated into a quotation attribution model
Michel et al. 2024
.
Through ablations, we show that the main source of information in DHCNs is the relationship between characters and the textual contexts in which they appear, while including explicit character relationships provides smaller, task-dependent improvements, especially on socially grounded tasks such as role prediction and quotation attribution.

We further show the applicability of DHCNs and
GraphLit
to the field of Digital Humanities by analysing narrative non-linearity in novels.
Narrative non-linearity corresponds to various types of discrepancies, such as a shift across time, space, diegetic levels, or character perspectives.
While prior works have shown that semantic discrepancy strongly correlates with non-linearity
Toubia et al. 2021
;
Piper and Toubia 2023
, our analysis of topological measures derived from DHCNs suggests that non-linearity also correlates with social discrepancies, well-captured by GraphLit representations.

To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose DHCNs that extend Dynamic Character Networks (DCNs) with textual information. Then, we introduce
GraphLit
, a self-supervised learning framework that learns expressive representations from DHCNs built on a large corpus of Project Gutenberg novels.

2.

Across character-level and quotation attribution benchmarks, GraphLit improves over text-only and graph-only baselines, especially on tasks requiring
contextual
understanding. Ablations show that character-segment grounding accounts for much of the downstream performance, while explicit character-character relations and global character objectives provide task-dependent gains.

3.

We illustrate the applicability of GraphLit to Digital Humanities by showing that
narrative non-linearity
also correlates with dynamics of social relationships among characters.

2
Related Work

Characters as Social Beings

Character Networks (CNs) model relationships between characters
Labatut and Bost 2019
, and have been extensively applied to literary analysis
Moretti 2011
.
Common variants include co-occurrence networks, where characters are connected when they appear in the same short text segment
Bonato et al. 2016
, and dialogue networks, where links are derived from conversational interactions
Elson et al. 2010
.
Graph measures over these networks support literary analysis: for example,
centrality
can identify prominent characters
Masías et al. 2017
, while high
transitivity
may indicate the presence of multiple social groups, as observed in Shakespeare’s plays
Stiller et al. 2003
.

Static networks are, however, limited in their ability to capture narrative time.
Dynamic networks address this limitation by encoding the order of events, thereby better reflecting the story and character dynamics
Agarwal et al. 2012
;
Prado et al. 2016
, and have been applied to plot understanding
Bost et al. 2018
and automatic summarization
Bost et al. 2019
.
Still, both static and dynamic character networks remain limited as they do not encode rich textual information, which restricts their use in representation learning and their ability to associate characters with textual signifiers, such as names or appearances
Barthes 1970
.

Characters through Textual Signifiers

Another line of work represents characters through the textual context that creates the storyworld, such as words, sentences and dialogues.
Characters are not only defined by narrative function or their social roles; they are also given real or imagined attributes that reinforce readers’ social cognition
Zunshine 2003
.
Prior works have modelled characters through latent personas
Bamman et al. 2014
, dialogues
Li et al. 2023
;
Michel et al. 2024
and assertions or events associated with them
Inoue et al. 2022
.
Such representations take the form of latent embeddings, which are difficult to interpret from a human perspective.
Thus, recent studies have introduced theory-grounded ontologies that map characters to interpretable narratological classes such as
sociological

Bourgois et al. 2026
or
interiority

Mian et al. 2026
.

LLMs offer a promising direction by generating rich character profiles on entire novels
Yuan et al. 2024
or short stories
Gurung and Lapata 2024
, but it remains unclear whether LLM-based representations generalize to novels unseen during pre-training
Chang et al. 2023
.
Besides, recent works suggest that LLMs have trouble understanding important narrative concepts such as ordering
Wang et al. 2024a
and relationships
Hamilton et al. 2026
,
limiting their reliability when analysing large corpora of fictional works.

Graph Representation Learning

has emerged as a powerful
paradigm for analysing structured data represented as graphs across various fields.
Despite the prominent literature on CNs, only few works have applied graph representation learning to literary networks.

Perri et al. 2022
found character embeddings derived with Graph Neural Networks from CNs to be largely superior to word2vec representations when trying to classify characters in Tolkien’s Legendarium.

Lee and Jung 2020
encode substructures of dynamic character networks with the Doc2Vec algorithm, followed by a training paradigm that pools character representations to learn story-level embeddings.

GraphLit
differs from prior approaches by learning representations on DHCNs.
The DHCN formulation encodes characters through evolving textual contexts over narrative time, while also incorporating explicit social relationships through CN structures.
GraphLit uses a large amount of publicly available works of fiction, offering a reproducible framework to learn rich representations.
Besides, GraphLit extends the analysis beyond characters to other important narrative concepts such as chapters or books in a single framework.

Methodology

The process of converting novels into DHCNs relies on independent steps depicted in Figure
1
.
First, we apply standard preprocessing such as NER followed by Character Name Clustering (CNC) and DHCN Extraction
Labatut and Bost 2019
;
Amalvy et al. 2024
.
For CNC, we follow
Amalvy et al. 2025
to extract named mentions of characters, and regroup them by unique characters.
We chose not to use coreference resolution because of its computational cost and noisy predictions
Martinelli et al. 2025
.
We provide further details and discuss this choice in Appendix
A
.

Notations

For a book
B

c\in\mathcal{C}
and its associated mention clusters
M_{c}=\{m_{1,c},\dots,m_{n,c}\}

We divide each book
B

\mathcal{T}

with each block having a fixed size.
For a block
t\in\mathcal{T}

LCN

G_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}=(V_{t}^{\mathcal{C}},E_{t}^{\mathcal{C}})
.
A vertex
v\in V_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}

c\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
c

t

and an edge
e=(c,c^{\prime})\in E_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
is built between characters
c
and
c^{\prime}
if there exists at least two mentions
m_{c}
and
m_{c^{\prime}}
that co-occur in a restrictive window of 20 words.

For an entire book
B

DCN
as a sequence of disconnected LCNs:
\{G^{\mathcal{C}}_{t},\;t\in\mathcal{T}\}

Labatut and Bost 2019
.
The same character
c\in\mathcal{C}
can appear in several LCNs, as shown in Figure
1
, and therefore occur multiple times in DCNs.

In the context of representation learning, standard static and dynamic CNs are limited as they do not explicitly encode the textual contexts in which characters appear.
We thus extend DCNs by adding textual information through the introduction of a new node type:
segment
nodes.
Formally, we split each block
t\in\mathcal{T}
using a paragraph break into
n
smaller segments
V^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}=\{s_{t,1},\dots\,s_{t,n}\}
, encoded with textual encoders.
Based on this augmentation, we add a new link-type to the graph:
character-segment
, connecting a
character

c
and a
segment
node
s_{i}
,
(v_{c},v_{s_{i}})\in E^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
when
c
is mentioned in
s_{i}
, where
E^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
is the set of such edges at block
t
.

We now define the Heterogeneous Character Network (
HCN
) as
H=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},T_{\mathcal{V}},T_{\mathcal{E}},X^{\mathcal{C}},X^{\mathcal{S}})
where
\mathcal{V}=V^{\mathcal{C}}\cup V^{\mathcal{S}}
,
\mathcal{E}=E^{\mathcal{C}}\cup E^{\mathcal{S}}
,
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of node types (
character
or
segment
),
T_{\mathcal{E}}
is the set of edge types (
character-character
or
character-segment
), and
X^{\mathcal{C}}
and
X^{\mathcal{S}}
are initial node attributes.
Many real-world graphs are attributed with initial node and edge features that help describe them, such as atom types in molecular graphs.
In our case, we leverage any textual encoder
\Phi
to compute initial representations of the textual information contained in
segments
(
X^{\mathcal{S}}
) and

X^{\mathcal{C}}

3.2
Representation Learning with DHCN

In this section, we describe the training framework of GraphLit.
We use a modified version of HGMAE
Tian et al. 2023
, a self-supervised learning framework which attempts to extract information from the graph data itself.
During training, HGMAE corrupts graphs by applying several masks to the edges and initial node attributes, and learns to reconstruct the original graphs from the corrupted ones.
At inference time, we skip the masking and directly input the original graphs.

Masking Strategy

Formally, given an input graph
H
, and its initial edges
\mathcal{E}
, we apply a random mask with fixed rate
\tau
to
character-character
edges
E^{\mathcal{C}}
and
character-segment
edges
E^{\mathcal{S}}
.
We denote as
\tilde{E}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\tilde{E}^{\mathcal{S}}
the masked edges that are not seen during training.
For node attributes, we sample a masking rate
\delta(i)
from a linear schedule with respect to epoch
i
, followed by randomly selecting a subset of nodes
\tilde{{V}}^{{type}}\subset V^{{type}}
according to the rate
\delta(i)
for both types of nodes.
We then replace initial representations with:

{\tilde{X}}_{v}=\begin{cases}[M]&if $v\in\tilde{V}$\\
X_{v}&{if} $v\notin\tilde{V}$\end{cases}

(1)

where
[M]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{qwen}}
is a learnable mask token.

GraphLit Backbone

We start by projecting initial node attributes (
X^{\mathcal{C}}
and
X^{\mathcal{S}}
), applying different layers depending on the node type, as shown in Figure
3(a)
.
For
X^{\mathcal{C}}
, we simply project textual representations with a linear layer
Lin
.

For
X^{\mathcal{S}}
, though, the heterogeneous graph formulation has a limitation: it does not explicitly encode their order because of the permutation invariance property of graphs.
Yet, the autoregressive nature of texts is a fundamental property that must be preserved to ensure coherence.
To address this, for an HCN at block
t
, we input the ordered sequence of segment attributes
\{X_{t,s_{1}},\dots X_{t,s_{n}}\}
to a small Transformer Decoder (
T
)
Vaswani et al. 2017
.
This allows the projected segment embeddings to capture information from preceding segments within its block.

Projected node attributes are then passed to an Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT) backbone with
L
layers
Hu et al. 2020
, that refines node representations
\mathbf{h}^{(L)}_{c}
and
\mathbf{h}_{s}^{(L)}
by aggregating messages from their neighbours. Details are provided in Appendix
B
.
We input entire sequences of HCNs:
\{H_{t},\;t=t_{1},\dots t_{n}\}
to the GraphLit backbone, processing entire novels in a single forward pass.
The final node representations
\{(\mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{(L)},\mathbf{h}_{u,t}^{(L)}),v\in V^{\mathcal{C}}_{t},u\in V^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\}
are then aggregated to build
global representations
of characters
c
, blocks
t
and the entire book
B
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{c}

\displaystyle=\text{POOL}_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{(L)},v\in V^{\mathcal{C}}_{t},\;v=c,\;t\in\mathcal{T})

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{t}

\displaystyle=\text{POOL}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{(L)},\mathbf{h}_{u,t}^{(L)},\;v\in V^{\mathcal{C}}_{t},u\in V^{\mathcal{S}}_{t})

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{B}

\displaystyle=\text{POOL}_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathbf{h}_{v,t}^{(L)},\mathbf{h}_{u,t}^{(L)},\;v\in V^{\mathcal{C}}_{t},u\in V^{\mathcal{S}}_{t},\;t\in\mathcal{T})

(4)

where
\text{POOL}_{\phi_{1}}
is a multi-head attention layer, and
\text{POOL}_{\lambda_{j}}
are heterogeneous multi-head attention layers, described in Appendix
C
.
We hypothesize that segment and character nodes should contribute differently to global representations.
Thus, attention layers allow to weight the importance of a specific character and/or segment in the final pooled representation.
Figure
3(b)
summarizes this process.

Block Ordering

In the DHCN formulation, each local HCN in the sequence
\{H_{t},\;t=t_{1},\dots t_{n}\}
, is a disconnected graph, with no access to information from other blocks.
Similar to the order of segments within a block, the order in which blocks occur in the original narrative is an important signal for story understanding.
While explicit information can be introduced directly in the DHCNs creation, we instead follow a self-supervised paradigm, and choose to incorporate ordering signal through an ordering loss.
Formally, we train a neural network to provide a scalar score for each block representation, augmented with global information from book embeddings
g_{t}=f_{\phi}([\mathbf{H}_{t}\;|\;\mathbf{H}_{B}])

where
f_{\phi}
is a Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP).
Then, we maximise the likelihood of the true ordering of
N
blocks with a ListMLE loss
Xia et al. 2008
:

\mathcal{L_{\text{ord}}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{N}(g_{t}-\log\sum_{j=t}^{N}\exp({g_{j})})

Graph Reconstruction

Following HGMAE, we train GraphLit to reconstruct corrupted DHCNs, as shown in Figure
3
.
Edge reconstruction is framed as a binary link prediction task
over masked character-character and character-segment edges with subsampled negatives.
Link prediction is done by augmenting local character representation with the corresponding global information:
\mathbf{\hat{h}}_{c}^{(L)}=\mathbf{h}_{c}^{(L)}+\mathbf{H}_{c}
, followed by scoring with a shallow Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP) applied to the concatenation of node embeddings.
We denote as
\mathcal{L}_{cc}
and
\mathcal{L}_{cs}
the type-dependent link-prediction losses.
This formulation follows standard definitions in Graph Autoencoders
Kipf and Welling 2016
.
For attribute reconstruction, we feed final node representations along with masked edges to a small decoder, set as a single-layer HGT model, and learn to reconstruct initial masked attributes with a scaled cosine loss
Tian et al. 2023
for both characters (
\mathcal{L}_{attr}^{\mathcal{C}}
) and segments (
\mathcal{L}_{attr}^{\mathcal{S}}
).
Appendix
D
provides a detailed explanation.

Global Link Prediction

To build global character embeddings that encode book-level information, we add a global link prediction task.
We sample global positive and negative interactions between characters
within the same novels
and
across different novels
, and learn to dissociate characters with an additional negative log-likelihood loss
\mathcal{L}_{global}
applied to global character embeddings
\mathbf{H}_{c}
in a similar way as other link prediction tasks.
The final loss applied to GraphLit is the sum of the ordering, attribute and edge reconstruction losses.

4
Experiments

4.1
Training

We train GraphLit on DHCNs extracted from a corpus of novels from Project Gutenberg.
We initially use 9,000 books from
Kim and Skiena 2022
, to which we add 11,000 different novels, and use similar validation and test splits.
For CN extraction, we use a co-occurrence window of 20 tokens and remove characters that are mentioned less than 10 times to avoid the long tail of minor characters.
We build HCNs with segments of approximately 100 tokens, and create DCNs with blocks of 1500 tokens, which have been proven to be coherent units when analysing narrative non-linearity
Piper and Toubia 2023
.
We also evaluate GraphLit trained on DHCNs where blocks are built at the chapter level and report results in Appendix
H
.
DHCNs’ descriptive statistics can be found in Appendix
E
.

We use a fixed edge masking rate of
50\%
and an initial attribute masking rate of
50\%
, progressively increasing by intervals of
0.005
at each epoch.
We set the hidden dimension of the model to
256
, number of segment-encoder Transformer layers to
4
with
4
attention heads and the number of HGT layers to
L=3
, resulting in an approximately 8.2 million parameters model.
We train GraphLit for 50 epochs with a batch size of 64 novels, and report full hyperparameters in Appendix
F
.

4.2
Evaluation

Character Embedding Benchmark

We evaluate GraphLit embeddings with linear probing on 11 character-related tasks taken from the Character Embedding Benchmark (CEB)
Inoue et al. 2022
, spanning
character
-,
contextual
- and
book
-level tasks, described in Appendix
G
.
We automatically map characters present in CEB to the characters we extracted during CNC.
However, the CEB pipeline differs from ours, which sometimes leads to ambiguous character identities or failing to match a CEB character.
We thus keep only data points where characters are unambiguously identified, resulting in 60 to 90% of data points depending on the task.
Besides, we include two different contextual tasks: character identification from masked character descriptions (
maskd)

Brahman et al. 2021
and
sentence description
extracted from BookWorm
Papoudakis et al. 2024
.
For training, we use a setup similar to CEB, which includes k-fold cross validation.
All experimental and dataset details are described in Appendix
G
.

Quotation Attribution

We follow
Michel et al. 2024
and evaluate character representations on the task of quotation attribution which tasks to find the speaker of an utterance given its surrounding context.
We use PDNC
Vishnubhotla et al. 2022
, a large dataset of around 40,000 manually annotated quotations drawn from canonical novels, from which we manually map PDNC characters to DHCNs characters.
We use all character mentions (created during DHCNs extraction) in a context window of 200 tokens as candidate mentions, and build mention representations by concatenating the associated character embeddings with fine-tuned contextual mention representations.
Details are provided in Appendix
H
.

Baselines

We compare against a text-only baseline that averages all
Qwen3-4b-Embeddings
from segments
s
in which
c
was mentioned, and a graph-only baseline that trains a GraphLit variant on DCNs.
We also include GraphLit trained on DHCNs variants: graphs where

GraphLit improves on Context tasks.

Across CEB task types, GraphLit notably yields large improvements on Context tasks.
While Qwen3 Embeddings DNC struggle on description and QA tasks, GraphLit trained with DHCNs, Static CNs and No CN shows strong gains, suggesting that learned representations encode richer information from localized context than Qwen3 embeddings.
This improvement is further revealed on quotation attribution, a contextual task by nature.
Here, GraphLit largely improves over the Qwen and DCN baselines, especially on quotations where the speaker is not explicit.

Character-segment grounding matters more than social relations

Surprisingly, our ablations show that GraphLit trained with removed character interactions (No CN) is competitive against standard DHCN, reaching slightly higher average accuracy on book and Context tasks.

This counter-intuitive result echoes the debate among literary critics, where our proposed framework focuses rather on the textual signifiers of characters than a story’s social structure.

Detection of Narrative Non-Linearity

Non-linearity, a discrepancy that occur in a story when the next logical step is replaced by a shift of focus in a different direction, is a notion that has always been present in human narration.

Recently,
Toubia et al. 2021
proposed
circuitousness
, an embedding-based measure that captures non-linearity when used with Word2Vec embeddings
Piper and Toubia 2023
.
It is defined as the sum of observed euclidean distances between consecutive chunk embeddings compared to the minimal distance that could be travelled by following the shortest path with respect to the distance between chunk embeddings.
Then, highly circuitous books are narratives that feature an ordering of events that does not match the expected order of events.
In our analysis, we instead project each block with
Qwen3-Embeddings-4b
, and contrast the analysis by comparing circuitousness calculated with GraphLit block embeddings
\mathbf{H}_{t}
, in the full DHCN and No CN formulations.

To analyse the extent to which narrative non-linearity is also a social phenomenon, we compute
topological features
from local CNs which characterize social relationships among characters:

transitivity
,
connected components
and
ratio of new characters
, and analyse their impact on circuitousness.
A high
transitivity
and number of
connected components
implies the presence of separated social groups, while a high
ratio of new characters
from one block to another implies a change of character focalization in the narrative.
Given the sequence of character-networks
G_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
, we compute the associated sequence of features, followed by calculating two book-level statistics: 1) features values averaged over chunks and 2) feature autocorrelation at lag 1, that informs how each feature is evolving from one block to another, thus providing a global measure of social discrepancy over time.

Quantitative Study

Following
Piper and Toubia 2023
, we analyse the impact of our measures of social discrepancy on circuitousness using a linear regression, where we control for text length, number of characters, and a dummy variable that takes a value of 1 if the novel was seen at training time.
We use a set of 6084 books from which we gather genre information using metadata from Project Gutenberg, and ensure that no books are tagged as Romance because of their stylistic outlier status

Wilkens 2016
.
We standardize log-circuitousness calculated from Qwen and GraphLit embeddings across books, and use it as the dependent variable in the regression.

We present regression results in Table
2
and discuss per-genre results in Appendix
I
.
Interestingly, most of our social measures significantly impact narrative non-linearity (with one exception: the average number of components).
As expected, stories with higher average social transitivity or larger average alternation between characters tend to be more non-linear.
In contrast, when social patterns repeat consistently across story blocks (high autocorrelation), the narrative tends to be more linear.
The regression coefficients indicate that GraphLit embeddings trained with DHCN account more for social measures than Qwen embeddings and DHCN without CN structure, especially in the case of autocorrelations which measures social discrepancy.
Thus, using different embeddings shows different pictures: while Qwen embeddings favour semantic (dis)similarities, GraphLits’ embeddings integrate social discrepancies at a higher rate.
This is corroborated when looking at the most circuitous books (DHCNs): these are often compilations of works, featuring distinct plot-lines and social structures.

6
Conclusion

We introduced GraphLit, a framework for learning rich literary representations from character-text graphs.
GraphLit draws on our proposed Dynamic Heterogeneous Character Networks (DHCNs), which ground characters in their localized context by adding textual information to Character Networks through the addition of
segment
nodes.
Our experiments show that GraphLit learns character representations which are particularly informative when used for
contextual
tasks such as Character Identification or Quotation Attribution.
Interestingly, we show that downstream gains can mostly be attributed to how characters are grounded in their context, rather than signal from their evolving social relations.
Finally, we propose a case study of GraphLit and DHCNs, showing that narrative non-linearity is also correlated with how character relationships evolve over time, a phenomenon that DHCNs capture more strongly.

7
Limitations

Our work bears several limitations.
First, as with all character network methods, DHCNs identify and group mention of characters through an imperfect Character Name Clustering (CNC) step.
Thus, for some novels, DHCNs might fail to link crucial mentions of characters together, which can hinder downstream analysis on some characters.
While coreference resolution is often used in the extraction, we chose not to use it because it is a noisy and computationally intensive process.
However, we note that extending GraphLit to coreference-based character networks is a promising direction for future work.

Besides, our graph extraction process relies on important hyperparameters that affect the resulting graph structure: the co-occurence window and minimal number of mentions for character-network extraction, and the block size and segment size for textual information.
While we did look at how block size impacts downstream performance, we think that measuring the effect of a change in these parameters in both the final structures as well as downstream performance is an interesting future work.

Our ablations of GraphLit components focus on modifying the graph structure, global loss, block sizes, text-encoder model for initial attributes, and attention-pooling for global representations.
However, we acknowledge that other ablations would be particularly interesting to understand the effect of each component, such as ablating the ordering loss, the Transformer block, or replacing the HGT backbone with a simple homogeneous GNN backbone.
Although these ablations would allow a better understanding of GraphLit components, we note that our main contribution relies on the formulation of DHCNs, and the proposition of an architecture that learns rich representations from them.
We leave finding the best architecture to future work.

Another limitation is the scope of our evaluation. While CEB provides various
character
-related tasks, it does not encompass how character representations can help on broader downstream tasks, such as description generation, entity linking or long-document understanding.
While we use quotation attribution as an example of such downstream tasks, it would be beneficial to extend the analysis to other downstream tasks.
We note that the applicability of character representations working jointly with Large Language Models is a promising direction for generalization to unseen tasks.

Finally, our case-study on narrative non-linearity mainly includes a quantitative analysis of correlation between DHCNs topological measures and circuitousness.
Although our analysis is extended in Appendix
I
(for example we discuss that GraphLit most circuitous books are either volumes or compilations of different works, which are by nature non-linear), a complete qualitative study done with literary scholars would be a promising direction for future work.

Appendix A
Character Name Clustering

Following standard procedure of character network extraction
Labatut and Bost 2019
, we first use NER on the entire book and only keep mentions that are tagged as character mentions (
PER
tag).
Then, we follow
Amalvy et al. 2025
and perform Character Name Clustering (CNC) to regroup
PER
mentions referring to the same character together.
Formally, for each book, we define a graph where each vertex is a character mention inferred by NER, followed by applying a set of inclusion and restriction heuristics on connections between vertices.
We chose not to use coreference resolution as additional clustering information, namely because coreference resolution on full books is a noisy and computation intensive process
Martinelli et al. 2025
.
We note that perfectly identifying all characters of a fictional work is a very challenging task that has been investigated in prior works
Vala et al. 2015
;
Amalvy et al. 2025
, and it is thus expected that discovered mention clusters might not fully reflect the reality of all characters.

The full CNC process is described below.
For each vertex in the graph, we infer its gender based on a priority list: 1) if the mention contains a title, we infer the gender based on it, 2) we use a list of around 8000 common English names with their associated gender, and assign a gender to a name if it is non-ambiguous
3
3

3

https://www.cs.cmu.edu/Groups/AI/areas/nlp/corpora/names/
and 3) we inspect each occurrence of the vertex and calculate the proportion of gendered pronouns (
his
,
himself, her, herself
) and assign a gender based on a majority vote.
Then, we add an edge between vertices following a set of rules based on name variations, and additionally use a gazetteer of around 1000 names and their variations (
e.g.
Tim and Timmy) to link vertices.
Finally, we use heuristics to remove unlikely connections: 1) if the two names have a different inferred gender, 2) if the two names share a common surname but have a different first name and 3) if the two names contain a different honorific (
e.g.
“Mister” and “Mr.”).
The standard procedure then proceeds to remove all edges belonging to the shortest path between prohibited pairs
Vala et al. 2015
.
We also include an additional heuristic that removes isolated vertices (
i.e.
mentions that are not connected to any other vertices) in cases where these isolated vertices can be attributed to multiple connected clusters.
This heuristics targets the removal of isolated last names that occur when two characters have the same last name and an inferred mention is this sole last name (
e.g.
“Bennet” which could be attributed to both “Mr. Bennet” and “Elizabeth Bennet”).

Appendix B
Background on Heterogeneous Graph Transformer

As most Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT)
Hu et al. 2020
processes graph
data through a cascade of Message Passing layers, which are neural networks that aggregate neighbouring information to update a node representation.
Compared to standard GNNs, HGT processes heterogeneous graphs
H=(V,E,T,X)
where
V
is the set of nodes,
E
the set of edges,
T
the set of relation types for all edges and
X
are initial attributes.
As we have shown, Heterogeneous Character Networks only support two types of nodes (
character
and
segments)
as well as two types of relations (
character-character
and
character-segment
).
However, HGT operationalises attention in the general framework of heterogeneous graphs.
In particular, it introduces multi-head Heterogeneous Mutual Attention to capture the difference between distribution types of nodes and edges, when aggregating neighbouring information.
Formally, let
e=(u,v)
be any edge connecting any node
u
to any node
v
. Then, an attention score is calculated for all edges with a multi-head attention layer that accounts for 1) the type of the source node
u
, 2) the type of the target node
v
and 3) the type of the edge
(u,v)
.
These attention scores are then combined with a
message
from a source node
u
to a target node
v
, that also accounts for different types.
Then, for a target node
v
, its updated representation is calculated as the aggregation of all messages from its neighbours
u\in\mathcal{N}{(v)}
weighted by the associated attention scores.

Appendix G
Character Embedding Benchmark

The Character Embedding Benchmark (CEB)
Inoue et al. 2022
is a benchmark suite designed to evaluate fixed-length representations of fictional characters in novels.
It is derived from 17,275 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg.
The benchmark is intended to test whether character embeddings encode information about individual character attributes, local narrative context, and book-level metadata.
We describe below the different tasks presented in CEB.

Gender
is a binary classification task in which the model predicts whether a character is male or female. Gold labels are assigned heuristically from the majority of male or female pronouns coreferent with the character. A character is labelled male if male pronouns outnumber female pronouns by at least 10%, and female under the converse condition. The dataset originally contains 5,000 examples from which we could recover 4,216 data points.

Role
asks the model to identify a character’s role, such as
schoolmaster
or
aristocrat
, from four candidate roles.
Gold roles are extracted from two reference books on English literature, with dependency parsing used to retain head nouns. This task originally contains 484 examples, from which we recover 418 data points.

Protagonist
is a binary task that asks whether a character is the protagonist of a book. The most frequently mentioned character in a book is used as an approximate gold-standard protagonist. This task originally contains 5,000 examples, from which we recover 4,315 data points.

Identity
is a binary pairwise task in which the model determines whether two characters from different books are the same entity. Positive examples are constructed from characters that share the same full name and author. This task originally contains 5,000 examples, from which we recover 4000 examples.

Speaker
presents an explicit quotation of at least 50 words and asks the model to identify which character uttered it among 4 candidates. This task originally contains 2,879 examples, from which we recover 1490 data points.

Character Identification
presents a description of a character where all mentions of the character are replaced by a [MASK] token, and asks the model to identify which characters among 4 candidates it corresponds to. We use descriptions from LiSCU
Brahman et al. 2021
to which we could map original characters to our DHCNs characters. We build the candidate subsets by always choosing the characters that are the most mentioned in the story, which we believe can be strong distractors.
It contains 893 examples.

Sentence description
presents a single sentence description of a character (
e.g.
“A simple but honest man”), in which we ensure that it does not contain any named mention of the character it refers to. The model need to identify which characters among 4 candidates the description corresponds to. We use descriptions from BookWorm
Papoudakis et al. 2024
, mapping and building candidates using the same process as above.
It contains 1,987 examples.

Question Answering
presents a character-related question and asks the model to select the correct character answer from four candidates. The questions are drawn from NarrativeQA
Kociský et al. 2017
and character-related annotations from
Angelidis et al. 2019
. This task originally contains 587 examples, from which we recover 345 data points.

Author
is a binary pairwise task in which the model predicts whether two characters, drawn from different books, come from books by the same author. This task originally contains 5,000 examples, from which we recover 3,566 data points.

Book
is a binary pairwise task in which the model predicts whether two characters come from the same book. This task originally contains 5,000 examples, from which we recover 3,543 data points.

Genre
asks whether a character belongs to a book with a given genre label. Since Project Gutenberg books may have multiple subject labels, the benchmark defines 11 binary classification tasks from frequent Project Gutenberg metadata subjects: 19th century, adventure stories, detective and mystery stories, fiction, historical fiction, humorous stories, juvenile fiction, love stories, science fiction, short stories, and western stories. The reported score averages across these binary genre tasks. The genre task originally contains 44,152 examples, from which we recover 37,332 data points.

Evaluation Setup

For evaluation, we use 5-fold cross validation for tasks with more than 2000 instances, and 10-fold cross validation for tasks with less.
For binary tasks, we train a single linear layer on top of frozen embeddings.
For pairwise tasks (such as
book
or
author
), we use the CEB setup and train a linear classifier on top of element-wise product and differences
[c_{1}\odot c_{2};|c_{1}-c_{2}|]
.
For scoring tasks (such as
role
and
qa
), we project the query to the dimension of candidate embeddings with a linear layer, and build scores by computing a dot product between the two.
As all contextual tasks require text-understanding, we project textual queries with
all-MiniLM-L12-v2
as done in CEB.
We train each probe for 20 epochs with a learning rate of
0.001
, using test-accuracy at the last epoch.
We fix the cross-validation seed and train linear probes 5 times, reporting the cross-validation accuracy averaged across model seeds.

Appendix H
Additional GraphLit Results and Ablations

H.1
Block Ordering

As GraphLit variants have been trained on the task of block ordering, we provide in Table
5
ordering results of the test-split containing around 1700 novels unseen during training.
In particular, these books are novels from the test split of
Kim and Skiena 2022
, and contain between 5 to 50 chapters.
In particular, chapter ordering has been shown to be a very challenging task which requires dedicated models, and where LLMs tend to fail drastically
Chen et al. 2026
.
We report standard rank correlation metrics between the order predicted by GraphLit and the ground truth order, as done in
Kim and Skiena 2022
.
These metrics include Kendal’s Tau, Spearman Rho and Rouge-S.
They all evaluate to which extent the predicted order correlates with the ground truth order, and are less strict than metrics such as position accuracy.

GraphLit models understand narrative order

Considering how hard the task is (the average number of chunks per novel is about
40
), Kendall’s Tau and Spearman Rho values indicate that GraphLit—although not being perfect—has a great understanding of how the narrative is unfolding.
In contrast, Dynamic Character Networks, although trained for ordering, completely fail at capturing narrative order, suggesting that social relationships are not sufficient to fully understand it.
Thus, DHCNs dynamic character-segment grounding allows to better encode narrative order than pure dynamic character networks.
Interestingly, DHCNs with Static CNs seem to better capture narrative order than standard DHCNs, which might suggest that the structure encoded by the former is a more adequate modelling of how stories unfold.

At the chapter level, GraphLit works better than dedicated models

Through its self-supervised learning framework, GraphLit achieves better results than prior models from
Kim and Skiena 2022
dedicated to the task of chapter ordering. Among others, these models include several fine-tuned Roberta models, while GraphLit only uses less than 10 million parameters. However, we note that initial node attributes play an important role in the success of the task, as shown by the performance drop when using E5 embeddings.

H.2
CEB

We provide in Table
4
additional experimental results on CEB.
We focus on two ablations: 1) GraphLit trained without global link prediction and 2) GraphLit where Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Attention Pooling are replaced by simply averaging node embeddings across the sets
I(c)
,
I(t)
and
I(B)
.
We also provide results for two variations: 1) we replace blocks of 1500 tokens with blocks created at the chapter level and 2) we concatenate global book embeddings with global character embeddings before applying the probes.

Global Link Prediction helps on book-tasks

Training GraphLit without the global link prediction tasks particularly hurts the
author
and
book
tasks (from
84.1\%
to
75.7\%
on average)
Thus, our formulation seems well designed to help character embeddings encode book-level information, which is what it was designed for.

Attention Pooling does not help

Results from replacing attention pooling shows that it performs on-par on contextual tasks, slightly underperforms for character-level task but improves on book-related tasks.
Thus, attention pooling does seem like a determinant in GraphLit good contextual performance, but rather how it processes character-segment information.

Chapter-level DHCNs improve book-level task

Interestingly, training GraphLit with blocks at the chapter level provides better results on all author tasks.
Indeed, chapter organization is likely to be an authorial stylistic factor that can help distinguish among books.
Thus, character representations from DHCNs at the chapter level seem to better encode this authorial information.

Book Embeddings capture Authorial patterns

When using both character and book embeddings, we see an expected large increase on book-level tasks.
Thus, book embeddings seem to better capture surface-level information such as authorial style and genre definitions.

H.3
Quotation Attribution

Quotation attribution has been shown to be a challenging task, requiring dedicated pipelines
Vishnubhotla et al. 2023
.
It has recently been shown that augmenting dedicated models with embeddings of characters provides significant gains
Michel et al. 2024
.
We thus follow this setup by using our learned character embeddings as additional information to perform quotation attribution on the Project Dialogism Novel Corpus
Vishnubhotla et al. 2022
, which contains around 40,000 quotations drawn from 27 novels.
However, we had to exclude a book from our evaluation setup (“Howard Ends” written by E. M. Forster) as it is not part of Project Gutenberg.

Training Setup

Compared to the setup of
Michel et al. 2024
, we change the following: 1) we use ModernBERT rather than SpanBERT to extract quotation and mention contextual representations, 2) we do not use coreference resolution to form candidate mentions, and instead form candidate mentions by using named mentions extracted during DHCN creation, 3) we use a larger contextual window of 200 tokens before and after the target quotation, and do not replace other quotations in the context with special tokens.
The latter point is, in fact, possible thanks to ModernBERT that extends maximum sequence length to a value larger than 512 tokens.

We found that in some cases, a percentage of quotations were
unanswerable
in the sense that no available candidate mentions referring to the true speaker is available in the provided context.
Thus, we apply the following modification to the BookNLP+ architecture: for any training quotation, we consider the set of all contextual mentions augmented with characters that are not present in context.
For a character
c
which is not mentioned in context, we build mention representation by concatenating representations from a
[start]
and
[end]
learnable tokens.
Then, we simply concatenate the mention representation with a character embedding
\mathbf{H}_{c}
to form a candidate representation that still contains character-level information.
Thus, the model can still execute predictions when it thinks that no candidate mentions in context are referring to the speaker.
Finally, we create contextualized representations for candidate by concatenating its representation with the target quotation representation (which is itself a concatenation of its start and end token embeddings).
Candidate embeddings are fed to a 2-layer MLP to provide a scalar score.

For training, we maximize the likelihood of all candidates referring to the ground-truth speaker.
We use a batch size of 64 quotations with learning rate
7\times 10^{-5}
.

Evaluation Setup

We follow
Michel et al. 2024
and perform 5-fold cross-validation with dedicated splits, from which we removed the book “Howards End”.
For the UAR
Scene
baseline, we use the original code to build stylistic character embeddings from explicit quotations, and then use them as global character representations
\mathbf{H}_{c}
.
Since the ModernBERT baseline does not use additional character information, we train it without the modifications applied to the candidate set mentioned above.

Ablations

For GraphLit variants, instead of using only the available global character representation, we also include the segment representation from which the quote was taken from as well as the local character representation derived from the block the quote was taken from (for Static CN, we do not use such local character representation as they are similar to the global representation).
In case where a character was not mentioned in the block, we instead use the nearest available local character representation.
We present in Table
6
additional results, where we vary the amount of information provided to the ModernBERT model to assess the utility of each type of representation.
We see that the
Global
character representation accounts for most of the downstream performance, followed by the
local
character representation.
Interestingly, the best performance on non-explicit quotations is achieved when using both local and global embeddings, while the best performance on explicit quotations is achieved when combining the segment and global representations.
These results suggest that although the global representation is more informative overall, segment and local character representations encode different information types that can be leveraged to solve different kinds of tasks.

Appendix I
Detecting Narrative Non-Linearity

As mentioned, circuitousness calculates the ratio between the distance travelled from an observed ordering of events and the minimum distance that could be travelled when taking the shortest path between events.
It is calculated as the following: suppose an ordered sequence of
T
block embeddings
\mathbf{H}_{t}
.
First the euclidean distance between two succeeding blocks is computed
d_{t}=||\mathbf{H}_{t+1}-\mathbf{H}_{t}||
, allowing to calculate
Speed

=\frac{1}{T-1}{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}d_{t}}
.
Lower speed indicates that the events featured in any given sequence of text tend to be more related (where the notion of similarity is entirely defined by the representation space
\mathbf{H}_{t}
).
Then,
Distance
is defined as the minimal distance required to cover all text blocks in the narrative, holding the first and last points fixed.
Finally, the circuitousness for a book
B
is calculated as
circ(B)=\frac{Speed(B)}{Distance(B)}
.

Circuitousness has been shown to be lower for non-fiction books relative to fiction books, where the former are usually more linear in structure
Piper and Toubia 2023
.
Since we only focus on fiction novels, we are rather interested in looking at differences across genre when computing circuitousness with varying embeddings.
We use 8 genre tags extracted from Project Gutenberg:
Science-Fiction
,
Adventure Fiction
,
Humorous stories
,
Detective stories
,
Westerns
,
Historical fiction
,
Fantasy
and
Juvenile
.
We show in Table
7
the resulting regression coefficients per genre, along with their associated confidence interval, in order to interpret how genres differ in terms of linear storytelling.
We take the
Detective
genre as comparison point by dropping its genre label.

When using Qwen embeddings, we recover the analysis from
Piper and Toubia 2023
where detective stories are the most circuitous, as shown by the negative signs for all other genre.
However, the picture is drastically different when using GraphLit embeddings.
Interestingly, the difference in genre are less pronounced: only Juvenile fiction is statistically more circuitous than detective stories for both embeddding types, while NO CN embeddings also show higher circuitousness for humourous stories.
Besides, although the impact of being part of the training is significant for both types, it is relatively small (recall that the dependent variable is the standardized log-circuitousness ; a value of
-0.189
indicates that books part of the training data have, on average, a log circuitousness
-0.189
below the mean.
Together with the fact that topological measures of social dynamics have a strong impact on non-linearity as captured by these embeddings, this suggests that DHCNs and No CN networks learn a different kind of non-linearity than standard semantic measures, which is not a mere training artifact.
As part of a verification, we found that the most circuitous books for both embeddings were often volumes or compilations of different works, thus often establishing new characters and settings.
