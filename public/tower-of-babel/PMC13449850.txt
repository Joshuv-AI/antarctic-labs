Training needs and self-rated competence in pressure injury prevention among clinical nurses: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Yihong Cai, Tingting Jian, Yuanjing Li, Qiumei Liu, Tingting Gu, Siyi Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure injury prevention relies heavily on nurses’ ability to recognize risk, implement preventive measures and respond promptly to early skin changes. Before developing large-scale training programs, it is important to understand nurses’ current self-rated competence and their learning needs. This study assessed clinical nurses’ self-rated knowledge, attitudes, self-reported practices and training needs related to pressure injury prevention in a multicenter sample.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted among 997 clinical nurses from 34 hospitals. Data were collected using an online questionnaire covering demographic characteristics, self-rated pressure injury-related knowledge, attitudes, self-reported preventive practices and training needs. Domain scores were standardized to a 0–100 scale. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize item-level findings and training needs. Subgroup comparisons were performed to examine differences in knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) domain scores across professional and institutional groups. Exploratory multivariable linear regression was used to examine factors associated with self-reported practice scores.ResultsThe mean standardized scores for self-rated knowledge, attitude and self-reported practice were 91.24 ± 10.87, 95.05 ± 9.38 and 77.44 ± 20.23, respectively. Attitude scores were high, while self-reported practice scores were lower and more widely distributed. Areas requiring further attention included dressing use, stage-specific management, pressure-relief strategies, consultation with wound, ostomy and continence specialist teams, and wound photo documentation. Nurses working in hospitals with pressure injury specialist teams reported higher KAP domain scores. In exploratory multivariable analysis, hospital specialist team status was associated with higher self-reported practice scores after adjustment. Training needs were high, with hands-on bedside instruction, online video learning and continuing education lectures being the most preferred approaches.ConclusionsThis multicenter survey showed that nurses generally held positive attitudes toward pressure injury prevention, but gaps remained in self-rated knowledge and self-reported practice, especially in areas requiring clinical judgment, documentation and hands-on skills. Background

Pressure injury (PI) remains a common but largely preventable problem in hospital care, especially among patients with limited mobility, older adults, postoperative patients and those receiving intensive care. It can lead to pain, infection, delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalization and increased healthcare costs. Because PI prevention is closely linked to nursing quality and patient safety, hospitals need to ensure that frontline nurses are familiar with current prevention guidance and are able to apply it in routine care [1].

Nurses are involved in nearly every stage of PI prevention. Their work includes risk assessment, skin inspection, repositioning, nutritional screening, documentation, patient and caregiver education, and early referral when specialist support is needed. These responsibilities make nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and daily preventive practices central to the success of PI prevention programs [2]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis also showed that nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices remain central indicators in PI prevention research [3]. Yet in routine clinical settings, gaps may persist between what nurses know, what they believe is important and what they are able to carry out consistently at the bedside.

Continuing education is one way to reduce these gaps, but training resources are not always distributed evenly across hospitals or departments [4]. Face-to-face sessions may be difficult for frontline nurses to attend because of rotating shifts, staffing pressure and clinical workload [5]. Similar concerns have been reported among intensive care nurses, whose PI prevention knowledge may vary across clinical settings [6]. Barriers to PI prevention may include knowledge gaps, workload, documentation habits and limited access to local support systems [7]. Previous research has also suggested that nurses’ knowledge levels may influence their attitudes toward PI prevention [8]. Evidence from medical device-related PI prevention further suggests that gaps may persist between knowledge, attitudes and practice behaviors in clinical nursing [9]. At the same time, pressure injury prevention requires both updated theoretical knowledge and practical skills, such as dressing selection, pressure-relief planning, documentation and timely consultation with wound-care specialists. Before developing training programs, it is therefore useful to identify the specific areas where nurses feel less confident and the types of training they prefer. Previous studies have examined nurses’ knowledge and attitudes toward PI prevention, and several reviews have suggested that educational interventions can improve PI-related knowledge and practice. However, less attention has been given to the combined assessment of baseline knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) profiles and training needs among clinical nurses across multiple hospitals. Such information is important for designing education that is not only guideline-based, but also responsive to nurses’ actual work demands and perceived learning priorities.

This study surveyed 997 clinical nurses from 34 hospitals to assess their self-rated PI-related knowledge, attitudes, self-reported preventive practices and training needs. The aim was to describe baseline patterns of perceived competence, areas requiring further educational support, and preferred training methods and content, thereby providing empirical information to inform more targeted PI prevention education programs.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a multicenter cross-sectional survey of clinical nurses’ self-rated competence and training needs related to PI prevention. A total of 997 clinical nurses from 34 hospitals were included. The participating hospitals were recruited through the regional nursing quality control network, which supported questionnaire distribution and coordination across sites. Nurses worked in departments where PI prevention and management are commonly encountered, including internal medicine, surgery and intensive care units.

Eligible participants were required to hold a valid nursing license and have at least one year of clinical nursing experience. Nurses who were on leave during the survey period or who did not complete the questionnaire were excluded. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were submitted through an online questionnaire platform.

Questionnaire development and content

The questionnaire used in this study was specifically developed to assess clinical nurses’ self-rated knowledge, attitudes, self-reported practices, and training needs regarding pressure injury prevention. Item generation was guided by the 2019 international guideline for pressure injury prevention and treatment. The pressure injury-related knowledge items were informed by the original Pressure Ulcer Knowledge Assessment Tool (PUKAT) [10]. The updated PUKAT 2.0 also provided a useful reference for organizing knowledge domains related to PI prevention [11]. Attitude items were informed by the Attitude towards Pressure Ulcer Prevention instrument (APuP) [12]. These sources helped define the main content areas, including pressure injury staging, risk factors, risk assessment, prevention strategies, pressure redistribution, dressing use, and attitudes toward prevention.

The initial item pool was further reviewed and refined by a seven-member nursing expert panel. The panel included five wound, ostomy and continence (WOC) specialist nurses, three nursing managers and one nursing quality-control staff member, with some members serving in more than one role. Regarding professional title, the panel included one chief nurse, two associate chief nurses, and four supervisor nurses. The experts reviewed each item for relevance, clarity, clinical applicability, and redundancy. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made to item wording, response options, and item order. Although the above sources and expert discussions provided the framework for item development, the final questionnaire was not a direct translation or formal adaptation of any single existing instrument.

Before the formal survey, the questionnaire was pilot-tested among 30 clinical nurses to assess comprehensibility, response burden, and the clarity of item wording. Minor wording revisions were made according to the pilot feedback. Content validity was assessed through expert review, yielding a scale-level content validity index of 0.91 [13]. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha based on the 997 valid responses in the formal survey. The coefficients were 0.83 for self-rated knowledge, 0.87 for attitude, and 0.80 for self-reported practice, with an overall coefficient of 0.85. Although content validity and internal consistency were examined, the questionnaire should be regarded as a study-specific instrument. More extensive psychometric evaluation, including construct validity testing, factor structure assessment, test–retest reliability and criterion-related validity, was not conducted in this study. The complete English version of the questionnaire is available as Supplementary File 1.

The questionnaire included four sections: demographic information, PI-related self-rated knowledge, attitudes toward PI prevention, self-reported preventive practices and training needs. Demographic, professional and institutional variables included sex, professional title, education level, years of nursing experience, department, participating institution category, whether the hospital had a pressure injury specialist team, and whether the participant was a WOC specialist nurse or WOC team member.

The self-rated knowledge domain assessed nurses’ perceived familiarity with PI definition and staging, mechanisms, risk factors, assessment tools, high-risk populations, prevention measures, stage-specific management, pressure-relief strategies and dressing use. These items measured nurses’ self-rated familiarity with PI-related topics rather than objective test performance. The attitude domain assessed views on timely risk assessment, nutritional screening, patient and caregiver education, prevention planning, multidisciplinary collaboration, treatment knowledge, pressure-relief measures, dressing use and regular training. The practice domain assessed nurses’ self-reported frequency of selected preventive behaviors, including risk assessment for newly admitted patients, reporting, health education, risk-factor analysis, prevention planning, dietary guidance, reassessment when patient condition changed, specialist consultation and wound photo documentation.

The training needs section asked nurses to select preferred training methods and PI-related training topics of interest. Training method options included hands-on bedside instruction, continuing education lectures, online video learning, workshops and other approaches. Training content options included PI definition and staging, risk assessment, assessment tool application, PI prevention, stage-specific treatment and nursing care, wound assessment and classification, wound case collection and documentation, wound cleansing and debridement, infection management, dressing selection and application, and patient and family education.

Scoring and data collection

Each item in the questionnaire was scored according to the respondents’ self-assessment. Knowledge items were rated on a five-point scale: “Completely know” (5), “Partly know” (4), “Uncertain” (3), “Almost do not know” (2), and “Do not know at all” (1). Attitude items were rated on a five-point Likert scale: “Strongly agree” (5), “Agree” (4), “Generally agree” (3), “Disagree” (2), and “Strongly disagree” (1). Practice items were rated according to frequency: “Always” (5), “Often” (4), “Sometimes” (3), “Rarely” (2), and “Never” (1). Total scores for the knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) domains were calculated by summing the relevant items and converting the raw scores to a 0–100 scale. Higher scores indicated greater self-rated knowledge, more positive attitudes, or more frequently reported preventive practice.

Data were collected through an online survey platform, and the reporting of online survey procedures was informed by the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) [14]. The questionnaire link was distributed by nursing department coordinators and ward-level communication groups in the participating hospitals. Because the link was forwarded through department- and ward-level online groups, the exact number of nurses who received the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained before questionnaire completion. Required questions were set as mandatory in the online platform. The platform recorded IP addresses and restricted repeated submissions from the same IP address. After data export, the research team checked the dataset for completeness and duplicate IP addresses. All 997 valid questionnaires were submitted from distinct IP addresses and were included in the final analysis. Demographic and institutional variables, including professional title, education level, years of nursing experience, wound, ostomy and continence specialist nurse status, wound, ostomy and continence team membership, and the presence of a pressure injury specialist team in the hospital, were recorded for subgroup analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Aggregate domain scores for self-rated knowledge, attitude and self-reported practice were calculated by summing the relevant items and converting the raw scores to a standardized 0–100 scale.

For item-level interpretation, knowledge items were summarized according to the proportion of nurses reporting that they “completely knew” or “partly knew” each topic. Attitude items were summarized according to the proportion selecting “strongly agree” or “agree”, and practice items according to the proportion reporting “always” or “often”. Training needs were summarized using frequencies and percentages for each selected training method and topic.

Subgroup comparisons of standardized KAP domain scores were conducted according to professional title, education level, years of nursing experience, participating institution category, WOC specialist nurse status, WOC team membership, and whether the hospital had a PI specialist team. Mann–Whitney U tests were used for two-group comparisons, and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for multi-group comparisons because the domain scores showed ceiling effects and non-normal distributions.

Exploratory multivariable linear regression was performed to examine associations between selected professional and institutional variables and self-reported practice scores. Candidate variables included self-rated knowledge score, attitude score, professional title, education level, years of nursing experience, participating institution category, WOC specialist nurse status, WOC team membership, and hospital PI specialist team status. Because the standardized domain scores showed ceiling effects and non-normal distributions, regression coefficients were reported with heteroscedasticity-consistent robust standard errors, 95% confidence intervals and p values. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because of the cross-sectional design, the regression model was used for exploratory association analysis only and was not intended to establish causal relationships or predictive performance.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Jiangjin Hospital (Jiangjin Central Hospital) and was granted an exemption from full ethical approval. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained before questionnaire completion. All responses were anonymized before analysis. Further details are provided in the Declarations section.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 997 clinical nurses from 34 hospitals completed the survey. Of these, 520 nurses (52.16%) were from a tertiary general hospital, and 477 (47.84%) were from non-tertiary member institutions of the regional nursing quality control network. Most participants were female (n = 977, 97.99%). Regarding professional title, 245 nurses (24.57%) were nurses, 363 (36.41%) were senior nurses, 359 (36.01%) were supervisor nurses and 30 (3.01%) were associate chief nurses. Most participants had a bachelor’s degree (n = 640, 64.19%) or a college diploma (n = 326, 32.70%). More than half had 5–20 years of nursing experience. In addition, 774 nurses (77.63%) reported that their hospital had a pressure injury specialist team, while 69 nurses (6.92%) were wound, ostomy and continence specialist nurses. Aggregate domain scores for self-rated knowledge, attitudes and self-reported practices

The aggregate domain scores provided an overall picture of nurses’ perceived competence in pressure injury prevention. On the standardized 0–100 scale, the mean self-rated knowledge score was 91.24 ± 10.87, the mean attitude score was 95.05 ± 9.38, and the mean self-reported practice score was 77.44 ± 20.23. Attitude scores were the highest and showed a narrow distribution, suggesting that most nurses recognized the importance of pressure injury prevention. In contrast, self-reported practice scores were lower and more widely distributed, indicating greater variation in how consistently preventive behaviors were reported in routine work. Self-rated knowledge of pressure injury prevention

Consistent with the high aggregate self-rated knowledge score, nurses reported relatively high familiarity with several basic PI prevention topics. More than 95% of participants reported that they completely or partly knew the definition and staging of PI, its mechanisms, major risk factors, high-risk populations and basic preventive measures. However, familiarity was less consistent for topics requiring more specific clinical judgment. Compared with general prevention concepts, lower proportions of nurses reported complete familiarity with assessment tools, stage-specific management, pressure-relief plans and dressing use. Dressing use showed the greatest room for improvement, with only 509 nurses (51.05%) reporting that they were completely familiar with the use of commonly used dressings in PI care. These findings suggest that nurses were generally aware of PI prevention principles, but more detailed clinical decision-making topics remained important targets for training. Attitudes toward pressure injury prevention

Participants showed strongly positive attitudes toward PI prevention. Most nurses agreed or strongly agreed that timely risk assessment, nutritional screening, patient and caregiver education, preventive planning and multidisciplinary collaboration were necessary. For example, 849 nurses (85.16%) strongly agreed that timely and accurate PI risk assessment for newly admitted patients was necessary, and the same proportion strongly agreed that patient family members and caregivers should receive PI-related education. Positive responses were also common for nutritional screening, preventive care planning, pressure-relief strategies, dressing use and regular training. These findings indicate that nurses generally recognized the importance of PI prevention and the need for continued learning.

Self-reported preventive practices

Self-reported practice patterns were more variable than attitudes. A majority of nurses reported that they always or often conducted risk assessment for newly admitted patients and provided health education to high-risk patients. However, several practice behaviors were less consistently reported. Only 367 nurses (36.81%) reported that they always analyzed intrinsic and extrinsic factors contributing to PI, and 399 nurses (40.02%) reported that they always developed prevention plans for bedridden patients. Specialist consultation and wound photo documentation also showed room for improvement. For difficult PI cases, 343 nurses (34.40%) reported that they always invited wound, ostomy and continence specialist teams for consultation. For wound photo documentation, 338 nurses (33.90%) reported that they always collected wound photographs for treatment comparison. These findings suggest that reported preventive practices may be less consistent in areas requiring active clinical judgment, documentation and specialist coordination. Training needs and preferred educational approaches

Training needs were high across the sample. The most frequently selected training method was hands-on bedside instruction, chosen by 911 nurses (91.37%). Online video learning was also highly preferred, selected by 793 nurses (79.54%), followed by continuing education lectures (n = 730, 73.22%) and workshops (n = 421, 42.23%). This pattern suggests that nurses valued flexible online learning but still expressed a strong need for practical, skill-oriented teaching.

Nurses also expressed interest in a broad range of PI-related training topics. The most frequently selected topics included stage-specific PI treatment and nursing care (n = 887, 88.97%), dressing selection and application (n = 826, 82.85%), wound cleansing and debridement (n = 819, 82.15%), PI assessment tools and application (n = 817, 81.95%), infection wound management (n = 811, 81.34%) and PI prevention (n = 808, 81.04%). More than three quarters of nurses also selected patient and family education, PI risk assessment, wound assessment and classification, PI definition and staging, and wound case collection and documentation. Subgroup comparisons of KAP domain scores

Subgroup analyses showed that KAP domain scores varied across selected educational, professional and institutional characteristics. In these exploratory subgroup comparisons, differences were most apparent according to participating institution category, institutional PI specialist team status and WOC-related professional roles. Nurses from the tertiary general hospital reported higher self-rated knowledge, attitude and self-reported practice scores than those from non-tertiary member institutions. Nurses working in hospitals with a PI specialist team also reported higher scores across the three domains. In unadjusted subgroup comparisons, WOC specialist nurses and WOC team members reported higher KAP domain scores than non-WOC nurses. Education level was associated with attitude and self-reported practice scores, with nurses holding a bachelor’s degree or above reporting slightly higher scores. In contrast, differences by professional title and years of nursing experience were less pronounced. Detailed subgroup comparisons are shown in Table 6.

Exploratory analysis of factors associated with self-reported practice scores

An exploratory multivariable linear regression model was used to examine factors associated with self-reported practice scores. After adjustment for professional, educational and institutional variables, higher self-rated knowledge and attitude scores were associated with higher self-reported practice scores. Participating institution category and hospital PI specialist team status were also associated with self-reported practice scores. Nurses from the tertiary general hospital and those working in hospitals with a PI specialist team reported higher practice scores after adjustment. Compared with nurses with 1–5 years of experience, those with 5–10 years of experience had slightly lower self-reported practice scores. Professional title, education level, WOC specialist nurse status and WOC team membership were not consistently associated with self-reported practice scores in the adjusted model. The model explained 46.3% of the variance in self-reported practice scores. Detailed regression results are shown in Table 7. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations. The cross-sectional design and limited institutional-level contextual data did not allow causal inference regarding the effects of hospital type or specialist team infrastructure.

Discussion

Principal findings

This multicenter cross-sectional survey examined clinical nurses’ self-rated knowledge, attitudes, self-reported practices, and training needs related to pressure injury (PI) prevention. The study adds to previous KAP research by combining aggregate domain scores, item-level findings, training needs, and professional and institutional subgroup analyses in a large sample of nurses from 34 hospitals. The main finding was not simply that nurses were aware of PI prevention. Rather, the results showed a more nuanced pattern: nurses reported highly positive attitudes toward PI prevention, while self-rated knowledge and self-reported practices were less consistent in several clinically important areas.

On the standardized 0–100 scale, attitude scores were the highest, whereas self-reported practice scores were lower and more widely distributed. This difference suggests that most nurses recognized the importance of PI prevention, but some preventive behaviors were not reported with the same consistency in routine clinical work. The item-level results further supported this interpretation. Nurses reported high familiarity with basic PI concepts, but lower complete familiarity with dressing use, stage-specific management, pressure-relief strategies, and assessment tools. Similarly, self-reported practices such as risk-factor analysis, individualized prevention planning, WOC specialist consultation, and wound photo documentation were less consistently reported. These findings may help identify practical targets for future nurse education, particularly in areas that require clinical judgment, documentation and coordination with specialist support.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous multicenter and cross-sectional studies showing that PI prevention knowledge, attitudes and practices vary across clinical settings and that implementation barriers often remain despite generally positive attitudes toward prevention [4, 5, 7]. Compared with studies that mainly assessed knowledge or attitudes, the present study adds a training-needs perspective by linking lower-confidence content areas with nurses’ preferred educational approaches. The identified gaps in dressing selection, stage-specific management, pressure-relief planning, specialist consultation and wound documentation suggest that future training should move beyond general awareness and focus more directly on clinical decision-making and local workflow support.

The high self-rated knowledge and attitude scores should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Because knowledge, attitudes and practices were assessed using the same self-report questionnaire, the results may have been influenced by social desirability bias, self-perception bias and common-method bias. Nurses may have overestimated their familiarity with PI prevention topics or reported more favorable preventive behaviors than those performed consistently in routine bedside care. Therefore, the findings reflect perceived competence and reported behaviors rather than objective knowledge, observed clinical performance, documentation quality or patient outcomes.

Self-rated knowledge and areas requiring further education

The high overall self-rated knowledge score may indicate that many nurses perceived PI prevention as a familiar topic in routine clinical care. This perceived familiarity may reflect the increasing emphasis on PI prevention in nursing quality management, hospital accreditation and patient safety programs. However, because knowledge was self-rated rather than objectively tested, this finding should be interpreted as perceived familiarity rather than confirmed knowledge competence. Self-rated familiarity was also not evenly distributed across all content areas. Nurses were more confident with general concepts, such as PI definition, staging, risk factors and high-risk populations, but less confident with topics that require more detailed clinical judgment.

Dressing use was the most notable example. Only about half of the nurses reported complete familiarity with commonly used dressings in PI care. This finding is clinically relevant because dressing selection is not merely a theoretical topic. It requires assessment of wound stage, exudate level, surrounding skin condition, infection risk, patient comfort, and available clinical resources. Similar challenges may also apply to stage-specific management and pressure-relief planning. These areas are difficult to improve through general lectures alone. They require case-based teaching, wound image interpretation, bedside demonstration, and opportunities to discuss complex cases with wound-care specialists. Simulation-based approaches, including virtual reality training, may also be useful for novice nurses when direct exposure to complex wounds is limited.

The gap between positive attitudes and self-reported practices

One of the most informative findings was the gap between positive attitudes and less consistent self-reported practices. Most nurses strongly agreed that PI risk assessment, nutritional screening, patient and caregiver education, prevention planning, multidisciplinary collaboration, and regular training were necessary. However, fewer nurses reported always performing some practice behaviors that require active judgment, documentation, or specialist coordination.

This pattern suggests that positive attitudes alone may not be sufficient for consistently reported prevention practice. Because practice was assessed by self-report, however, this finding should not be interpreted as direct evidence of bedside performance. Nurses may agree with prevention principles but still face barriers when applying them at the bedside. These barriers may include workload, time pressure, limited access to pressure-relieving equipment, uncertainty in dressing selection, lack of clear consultation pathways, or insufficient feedback on documentation quality. Some tasks, such as collecting wound photographs or inviting WOC specialist teams for difficult cases, may also depend on local workflow, institutional policy, and the availability of specialist support. Therefore, education should not only strengthen awareness, but also support nurses in applying prevention principles through practical and repeatable clinical actions.

Professional and institutional differences in KAP scores

The subgroup and regression analyses provided further insight into the factors associated with perceived competence and self-reported practice. Nurses from the tertiary general hospital and those working in hospitals with a PI specialist team reported higher KAP domain scores. In the adjusted exploratory model, participating institution category and the presence of a PI specialist team were associated with higher self-reported practice scores. This finding is clinically meaningful but should be interpreted as an exploratory association. Hospitals with PI specialist teams may also have more mature prevention infrastructures, clearer consultation pathways, stronger documentation requirements, regular audit and feedback mechanisms, or more frequent staff training. These factors could contribute to higher self-reported practice scores, but the present cross-sectional survey was not designed to determine which organizational processes were responsible. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that multicomponent PI prevention programs can improve prevention processes in adult intensive care settings [15].

WOC specialist nurses and WOC team members also reported higher scores in subgroup comparisons. After adjustment, however, WOC-related professional roles were not consistently associated with self-reported practice scores. This suggests that the observed differences may be partly explained by higher self-rated knowledge, more positive attitudes, and institutional support. In other words, individual specialist status alone may not fully explain practice patterns. Organizational structures, including institutional level and the availability of PI specialist teams, may therefore be relevant to more frequently reported PI prevention practices.

Education level was associated with attitude and self-reported practice scores in subgroup comparisons, but its independent association was less clear after adjustment. Differences by professional title and years of nursing experience were also less pronounced than expected. These results suggest that training needs should not be determined only by seniority or formal title. Instead, hospitals may need to assess actual learning needs, local workflows, and access to specialist resources when designing PI prevention education.

Training needs and implications for nursing education

The training needs identified in this study were both strong and practical. Hands-on bedside instruction was the most preferred training method, followed by online video learning and continuing education lectures. This pattern indicates that nurses value flexible learning, but they also need direct practical guidance, which is consistent with the broader role of continuing professional development in nursing practice [16]. Online learning can make theoretical content accessible and repeatable, especially for nurses working shifts, and e-learning programs have been reported to improve nurses’ knowledge and practice in pressure injury management [17]. More broadly, evidence from health professions education suggests that internet-based learning can improve knowledge outcomes when appropriately designed [18]. Bedside teaching, workshops, and case discussions are better suited to skill development and clinical decision-making, while structured online education may support knowledge acquisition and attitude improvement when it is designed around active learning [19].

The preferred training topics were also highly practice-oriented. Nurses most frequently selected stage-specific PI treatment and nursing care, dressing selection and application, wound cleansing and debridement, assessment tool application, infection management, PI prevention, and patient education. These choices are consistent with the lower-scoring knowledge and practice areas observed in the survey. Mobile learning tools may also help learners revisit key PI prevention content outside traditional classroom settings [20]. Together, the findings are compatible with a blended training approach, which is also consistent with evidence supporting blended learning in nursing skills education [21]. Basic guideline content can be delivered through online modules, while complex topics such as dressing use, wound assessment, consultation pathways, and documentation can be taught through bedside guidance, wound images, case-based discussion, and feedback from WOC or PI specialist teams.

This approach may be particularly useful in multicenter nursing quality networks. Standardized online resources can help reduce variation in basic knowledge across hospitals, while local specialist teams can adapt training to department-specific needs. For early-career nurses, training may focus on risk identification, prevention planning, documentation, and basic wound assessment. For senior nurses and specialist team members, training may emphasize difficult case management, quality indicators, consultation coordination, and leadership in PI prevention.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It included a multicenter sample of 997 nurses from 34 hospitals and assessed self-rated knowledge, attitudes, self-reported practices, and training needs within the same survey. The study also combined aggregate domain scores with item-level analyses, allowing both an overall view of perceived competence and the identification of potential educational targets. In addition, subgroup and regression analyses helped explore how professional and institutional factors were associated with self-reported practice.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the questionnaire was a study-specific instrument developed for this survey. Although expert review, content validity and internal consistency were assessed, more extensive psychometric testing, including construct validity, factor structure, test–retest reliability and criterion-related validity, was not performed. In addition, the knowledge domain measured self-rated familiarity rather than objective knowledge. Nurses’ perceived familiarity with PI-related topics may therefore not fully reflect their actual knowledge or clinical decision-making ability.

Second, practice was self-reported and was not verified through direct observation, chart review, wound documentation audit or patient outcome indicators. Recall bias, social desirability bias and self-perception bias may therefore have influenced the findings. Because knowledge, attitudes and practices were collected using the same questionnaire at the same time point, common-method bias may also have contributed to the observed associations among the KAP domains.

Third, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. The associations observed between self-rated knowledge, attitudes, institutional specialist team status and self-reported practice scores should be interpreted as exploratory relationships rather than evidence that any individual or institutional factor caused better practice.

Fourth, only limited institutional-level contextual information was available. This study collected data on participating institution category, the reported presence of a PI specialist team, and WOC-related professional roles. However, more detailed information on the structure and function of specialist teams, consultation pathways, documentation systems, audit and feedback mechanisms, staffing, equipment availability, local prevention protocols and training frequency was not collected. Therefore, the institutional comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory associations and may not fully capture the complexity of local PI prevention systems.

Fifth, although the study included nurses from 34 hospitals, the participating hospitals were recruited through a regional nursing quality control network. The findings may not be fully generalizable to all regions or healthcare systems. Finally, because the survey link was distributed through hospital- and ward-level online groups, the exact number of nurses who received the invitation could not be determined, and a formal response rate could not be calculated.

Future studies should combine self-reported surveys with objective knowledge tests, bedside observation, documentation audits, evaluation of consultation and feedback processes, and PI incidence or other patient outcome data. Longitudinal or interventional designs would also be useful to evaluate whether needs-based, blended education can improve observed clinical practice and patient outcomes, and to examine how specific components of PI prevention infrastructure, such as specialist consultation pathways, bedside feedback, documentation systems, audit mechanisms, prevention protocols and equipment availability, influence nursing practice.

Conclusions

This multicenter cross-sectional survey found that clinical nurses generally held positive attitudes toward PI prevention, but gaps remained in self-rated knowledge and self-reported practice. The most important areas requiring further educational support included dressing use, stage-specific management, pressure-relief planning, risk-factor analysis, WOC specialist consultation, and wound photo documentation. Nurses from the tertiary general hospital and those working in hospitals with PI specialist teams reported higher self-reported practice scores. These exploratory associations suggest that institutional context and specialist support warrant further investigation, particularly in relation to consultation pathways, documentation systems, feedback mechanisms and local prevention infrastructure.

Nurses expressed strong demand for practical and flexible training, especially hands-on bedside instruction, online video learning, continuing education lectures, and clinically focused topics such as stage-specific care, dressing selection, wound cleansing, infection management, risk assessment, and patient education. Future PI prevention education may need to be more targeted, needs-based and practice-oriented. A blended model combining online learning, case-based discussion, bedside guidance and specialist feedback may help guide the application of PI prevention principles in routine care, but its effects on observed practice, documentation quality and patient outcomes should be evaluated in future studies.
