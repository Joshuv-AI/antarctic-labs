Bacterial and parasitic etiologies of diarrhea and the emerging role of Aeromonas spp. among children aged under 5 years in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a case-control study
Authors: Sitotaw Kolato Koche, Berhanu Yitayew, Takele Teklu, Bethlehem Admasu Yayina, Marechign Yimer, Ashenafi Alemu Wami, Abel Abera Negash, Adane Mihret
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Aeromonas spp. were the most frequently detected bacterial pathogens among children aged under 5 years with diarrhea in Addis Ababa.•Aeromonas spp. and Giardia lamblia showed strong independent associations with diarrheal disease.•Aeromonas spp. accounted for the highest attributable fraction among identified enteric pathogens.•Shared sanitation facilities, large household size, and pet ownership increased the risk of childhood diarrhea.•Findings support Aeromonas spp. as an emerging enteric pathogen in Ethiopian children.

Introduction

Diarrheal diseases remain a significant global health concern, particularly, for children under 5 years of age (under 5) [1]. Despite a two-thirds reduction in diarrhea-related deaths since 2000, diarrheal diseases continue to be a major contributor to child mortality [1]. Worldwide, diarrheal diseases cause approximately 1.6 million child deaths per year, surpassing malaria and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome combined. In addition, it is the main cause of malnutrition, stunting, and delayed brain growth, particularly, in low- and middle-income countries [2].

Recent studies have highlighted the persistent burden of diarrheal diseases among children under 5 in sub-Saharan Africa [3,4]. The prevalence of diarrheal diseases in sub-Saharan Africa is approximately 18.44% [4]. Evidence indicates that child morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries arise from the complex interaction of behavioral, socioeconomic, and environmental factors [5,6]. Addressing these issues requires intersectoral collaboration, focusing on poverty alleviation, improving living conditions, optimizing child nutrition, and ensuring access to clean water [3].

Diarrheal diseases represent a persistent public health challenge in Ethiopia, especially among children under 5, with a pooled prevalence of 22% [7]. According to a recent study, the prevalence of diarrheal diseases among children under 5 in Addis Ababa is 11.9% [8]. Studies have identified key determinants for diarrheal diseases among children under 5 in Ethiopia, including a lack of latrines, lower maternal education, lower socioeconomic status, poor handwashing practices, improper waste disposal, lack of proper sanitation services, and the presence of multiple children in a household [[6], [7], [8]]. In addition, etiologic studies have identified bacterial pathogens, including Campylobacter, Shigella, and Salmonella species, as well as parasitic agents, such as Giardia lamblia and Entamoeba histolytica, and viral pathogens, such as Rotavirus, from diarrheal cases in children [[9], [10], [11]]. Although Aeromonas species have not been specifically reported in Ethiopian studies, research from other regions has shown their potential as enteric pathogens, particularly, in children under 5 [12].

Evidence on the burden, risk factors, and microbiological etiology of diarrheal diseases is important for informing targeted interventions and supporting progress toward the Sustainable Development Goal of reducing childhood mortality [13]. However, the data are scarce in areas with high morbidity and mortality among children under 5, such as the study area. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the risk factors associated with diarrheal cases among children under 5, identify common etiologic agents, and compare their prevalence in cases and controls among selected health centers in Addis Ababa.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in four purposively selected health centers in Addis Ababa from October 2022 to April 2023. The selection of facilities was guided by data from the Health Management Information System, which was obtained from the Addis Ababa Health Bureau. The health centers with the highest case flow of diarrhea among children under 5 during the previous year were selected.

Study design

A health facility–based unmatched case-control study was conducted in four selected health facilities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Source and study population

All children attending the study site health center for different purposes during the study period composed the source population. The patients were children under 5 attending health centers for the treatment of diarrhea and did not receive antibiotic treatment within 2 weeks. The controls were children attending the selected health facility for causes other than diarrhea.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using Epi-Info version 7 (StatCalc) for an unmatched case-control study. The calculation assumed an exposure prevalence of 27% among controls, based on national estimates of household sanitation coverage in Ethiopia [14]. A 95% confidence interval (CI), 80% statistical power, and a case-to-control ratio of 1:1 were used. Under these assumptions, the study was powered to detect a minimum odds ratio (OR) of 2.0. Accordingly, 326 children under 5 (163 cases and 163 controls) were included in the study. Children with diarrhea brought to one of the centers were enrolled in the study, along with one unmatched control in the same setting, using a systematic random sampling method.

Participant enrollment and data collection

Children were enrolled in the study after their caregivers provided consent for their participation. Sociodemographic, environmental, and behavioral data were collected from the children’s caregivers using a standardized questionnaire. Clinical and anthropometric data were extracted from the medical records. The questionnaire was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into the local language (Amharic). The questionnaire was initially pretested at the All-Africa Leprosy, Tuberculosis, Rehabilitation and Training Center (ALERT) Comprehensive Specialized Hospital pediatric ward. The understanding of the guardians was checked, and confusing and ambiguous questions were improved.

Specimen collection and processing

Stool samples were collected by caretakers using a sterile stool cup. Portions of the stool samples were transferred to Cary-Blair transport medium using cotton swabs and stored at 4°C before being transported to the Armauer Hansen Research Institute laboratory within 48 hours for bacteriological analysis. The remaining stool was examined for intestinal parasites at the health center laboratories using direct saline wet mounts, with microscopic examination performed at 10× and 40× magnifications to identify ova, cysts, and trophozoites.

Isolation and identification of bacterial pathogens

For bacteriological examination, each stool sample from Cary-Blair media was inoculated into thiosulfate citrate bile salt sucrose (TCBS) agar media, Salmonella Shigella (SS) agar, and MacConkey agar (Oxoid). For the isolation of Aeromonas species, the sample was inoculated into TCBS media. After being aerobically incubated at 37°C for 24 hours, the plates with greenish, yellowish, and yellowish-black growth were subcultured on nutrient agar media and incubated at 37°C for 18-24 hours. The oxidase test was conducted on overnight-grown colonies. Oxidase-positive isolates from TCBS were presumed to be Aeromonas or Vibrio species. Phenotypic identification was performed using conventional biochemical tests and standard identification keys, with supplementary confirmation by Advanced Bacterial Identification Software (ABIS) online. Aeromonas spp. were identified as Gram-negative, oxidase-positive, motile, glucose-fermenting rods that were indole-positive, nitrate-reducing, urease-negative, O/129-resistant, and string test–negative.

Overnight-grown colonies from MacConkey agar were collected and differentiated based on colony characteristics and lactose fermentation. The mixed colonies on the MacConkey plate were subcultured on a new MacConkey plate and incubated. Non–lactose fermenters were subjected to conventional biochemical tests for the phenotypic identification of pathogens. Pure fermenters that appeared pink on MacConkey agar were subcultured on sorbitol MacConkey agar. The colorless colonies on the sorbitol MacConkey were presumed to be enterohemorrhagic E. coli. Colorless colonies and colonies with black centers on SS agar were collected and subjected to biochemical analysis for identification of Salmonella and Shigella species.

Data quality control

Data quality was assured by careful design of the data collection tools, appropriate recruitment of children, and adequate training and follow-up of the data collectors. Data collection was conducted under intensive supervision by the principal investigator. The quality of the data was ensured by following standard procedures in each step of the work. The quality of the media was checked by incubating 10% of the prepared plates at 37°C for 18-24 hours, and the batches of plates in which colonies grew after incubation were discarded. The ability of the culture media to support bacterial growth was verified using E. coli American Type Culture Collection 25922 as a reference strain.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata Version 14. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with P ≤ 0.05 were statistically significant. The attributable fraction among the exposed (AFE) was calculated as 1 − (1 ÷ OR), where OR represents the case-control odds ratio for the pathogen. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was calculated as AFE% for a given pathogen × prevalence of a given pathogen among cases.

Associated risk factors

Binomial logistic regression analysis identified three variables significantly associated with diarrhea. Children under 5 from households with a family size of five and more were almost twice more likely to experience diarrhea than those from households with a family size of four and less (adjusted OR [AOR] = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.05-3.36; P = 0.033) (Table 1).

Children from households that used toilets shared with other households had higher odds of diarrhea than those from households with private toilets (AOR = 3.72; 95% CI: 1.75-7.91; P = 0.001). In addition, children from households owning pet animals were approximately two times more likely to experience diarrhea than were those from households with no pets (AOR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.06-2.96; P = 0.028) (Table 2).

Parasitic and bacterial pathogens associated with diarrhea

Pathogens were more frequently detected in stool samples of cases than controls (39.3% vs 18.4%, P < 0.005). Aeromonas species were the most frequently detected bacteria, identified in 14.4% (47 of 326) of samples. The majority of Aeromonas species were recovered from cases, 91% (43 of 47), compared with controls 9% (four of 47). Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (O157:H7) was detected in 19 (57.6%) control participants and 14 (42.4%) cases. Salmonella species were isolated from 14 (4.3%) stool samples, of which eight (57.1%) were from cases and six (42.9%) from controls. Among the 326 stool samples screened for parasites, 63 (19.3%) were positive for three different types of parasites, of which 44 (69.8%) were from cases and 19 (30.2%) were from controls. Entamoeba histolytica/dispar (n = 45, 13.8%) was most frequently detected among both cases (n = 30, 66.7%) and controls (n = 15, 33.3%). Giardia lamblia (n = 18, 5.5%) was identified more frequently in cases (n = 15, 83.3%) than in controls (n = 3, 16.7%). Only one (0.3%) trophozoite stage of Hymnolepsis nana was identified from the control (Figure 1). After adjusting for the presence of bacterial and parasitic pathogens, the logistic regression analysis indicated a significant detection rate for Aeromonas species (OR = 16.86; 95% CI: 5.48-48.68) and Giardia lamblia (OR = 7.47; 95% CI: 2.07-26.87) (Figure 1).

Attributable fraction analysis of the pathogens causing diarrhea

For the bacteria and parasites that were significantly associated with diarrhea, we conducted AFE and PAF analyses. Aeromonas species had the highest AFE of 94.06% (94.06% of the diarrheal cases positive for Aeromonas spp. were attributable to Aeromonas). Giardia lamblia had the second-highest AFE at 86.61% (Figure 2). To determine the percentage of cases linked to each pathogenic bacteria and parasite, the PAF was calculated. Aeromonas species also had the highest PAF at 13.56%; that is, the proportion of all cases attributable to Aeromonas spp. was 13.56%. Discussion

Our study revealed a greater likelihood of detection of bacterial and parasitic enteric pathogens among cases than controls (39.3% vs 18.4%, P < 0.005). Multiple studies have demonstrated a greater prevalence of enteric pathogens in children with diarrhea than in asymptomatic controls. Studies from Ethiopia (41%) and Egypt (46%) reported greater detection of enteropathogens from cases than from controls [11,15]. Furthermore, a multiple-country African study revealed bacterial pathogens in 93% of cases vs 72% of controls [16]. These findings indicate the complex relationship between enteric pathogen carriage and diarrhea in low-income settings.

Our study also revealed that Aeromonas species were the most prevalent (n = 47, 14.5%) among diarrheal cases (91%, 43 of 47) and significantly associated with diarrhea (OR = 16.86; 95% CI: 5.48-48.68). This finding is in agreement with other reports from Australia (0.56%), Latvia (3.7%), and Iran (3.1%), which reported the detection of Aeromonas species from diarrheal outbreaks in the pediatric population [[17], [18], [19]]. Identification of Aeromonas isolates was limited to the genus level. Although TCBS agar supported preliminary isolation, the absence of Aeromonas-specific selective media and advanced confirmatory resources prevented reliable species-level differentiation. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to report Aeromonas species as a cause of diarrhea among children in Ethiopia. This might be due to under-detection and underreporting of the pathogen from diarrheal conditions attributable to diagnostic limitations and surveillance gaps. Other bacterial entropathogens detected were enterohemorrhagic E. coli (O157:H7), Salmonella species, and Shigella species. However, there was no significant difference among the groups in terms of detection.

Among parasitic pathogens, Entamoeba histolytica/dispar, Giardia lamblia, and H. nana were detected in both groups in our study. Giardia lamblia was the most frequently isolated parasite from cases than from controls and significantly associated with diarrhea in our study (OR = 7.47; 95% CI: 2.07-26.87). This finding is in agreement with a study in Baghdad that detected G. lamblia (10.72%) and E. histolytica/dispar (2.92%) from diarrhea in children [20]. Our findings also agree with reports from Gambella [11] and Mizan Tepi, southern Ethiopia [21]. There was no significant difference among the groups for the other parasitic pathogens. These findings highlight the importance of accurate detection methods and consideration of environmental factors in understanding and controlling intestinal parasite infections.

The AFE and PAF were adjusted for pathogens significantly associated with diarrhea to estimate the proportion of diarrhea that could be prevented by tackling the exposure. The PAFs for Aeromonas species and Giardia lamblia were 13.56% and 4.78%, respectively. The PAF is usually interpreted as a percentage of disease risk that might be removed from the population if exposure (in our case, Aeromonas species and Giardia lamblia) is stopped. For people concerned with public health prevention policy, PAF is useful, especially when addressing exposures that may be changed with the right preventive and therapeutic measures [22]. A similar high PAF for Aeromonas species was reported in Asia in a global enteric multicenter study [13], and the dominance of Giardia lamblia was reported in other parts of Ethiopia [11,21].

In our study, children from households with five children or more were more vulnerable to diarrhea than those living in households with small families. Children from large families may live in conditions with inadequate environmental and personal hygiene, as well as improper food handling procedures. This finding is in agreement with reports from rural areas of Wama Hagelo District, western Ethiopia [23].

Toilet use was associated with diarrheal illnesses in our study. Families using shared toilets had a greater risk of diarrhea than did families using private toilets. Using a private restroom might indicate hygienic circumstances. Similar results have been reported from west Ethiopia; northwest Ethiopia; and Jigjiga, Somali [[23], [24], [25]]. This highlights the importance of improving water, sanitation, and hygiene conditions to reduce diarrhea among Ethiopian children.

Our study also revealed that children from households with pet animals are more likely to develop diarrheal diseases. This finding is in agreement with other reports from rural Ethiopia that reported children living in households where animals shared living spaces had higher odds of contracting diarrheal diseases [26]. Another report from Uganda reported that children in households with poultry farms had a significantly higher incidence of diarrhea [27]. In addition, in Ecuador, children who interact with domestic animals showed a higher risk of zoonotic enteric pathogen colonization [22]. A study from Bangladesh has also reported that children exposed to animal feces from compounds have higher odds of diarrhea [28]. Young children are often exposed to pet-borne microorganisms because of limited sanitation standards and increased direct physical contact with the animals’ surroundings, such as the floor and carpets. This highlights the importance of proper animal handling, hygiene practices, and fecal management in reducing diarrheal diseases among children in households with pets and livestock.

Limitations of the study

This study explored common parasite and bacterial etiologies in the study area; however, it has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The study did not include viral or protozoal etiologies. The seasonal variation in enteric infection was also not determined by the present study. Furthermore, culture-based detection of Aeromonas is known to underestimate its true occurrence when non-selective media are used. In the absence of these approaches, Aeromonas colonies may be overlooked or misidentified, particularly, in mixed enteric flora. Therefore, the prevalence reported in this study likely represents a conservative estimate of the true burden, underscoring the need for selective culture methods in routine surveillance and research. In addition, this study might not be representative of the overall burden of diarrhea in Addis Ababa and the rest of Ethiopia.

Conclusions

This study explored common pathogens and risk factors associated with diarrhea among children under 5 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Aeromonas species, enterohemorrhagic E. coli (O157:H7), Salmonella species, and Shigella species were common bacterial pathogens, whereas Entamoeba histolytica/dispar, Giardia lamblia, and H. nana were the common parasites detected. The likelihood of detection of bacterial and parasitic pathogens was higher among cases than among controls, with Aeromonas species and Giardia lamblia showing a significant association with diarrhea. The PAFs for Aeromonas species and Giardia lamblia were 13.56% and 4.78%, respectively. Family size, toilet use, and the presence of pets were significantly associated with diarrhea among children under 5 in our study. Future studies covering other potential parasitic, bacterial, and viral enteric pathogens, with their seasonal variation, are needed to provide more insights into the dynamics of diarrheal diseases among children. Moreover, a high prevalence of Aeromonas observed in this study suggests that future investigations may benefit from a One Health framework incorporating human, animal, and environmental samples to better understand transmission pathways.
