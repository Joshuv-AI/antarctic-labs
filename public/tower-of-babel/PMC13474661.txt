Trends and disparities for co-existent pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular-related deaths: a retrospective 1999–2020 analysis of CDC WONDER data
Authors: Rohit B Ponnada, Atman K Shah, Atharva M Bhagwat, Bhanuteja Pujari, Chien-Jung Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease are among the leading causes of death in the U.S. and may co-occur with compounding effects. This study assesses trends in mortality rates for comorbid pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease, along with sociodemographic disparities to better inform public health and policy efforts.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using CDC WONDER data between 1999 and 2020. Mortality for pancreatic cancer was assessed using ICD-10 code C25 with I00-I99 for any cardiovascular-related death. Joinpoint regression software was used to assess mortality trends overall and across strata of race, sex, and urbanization status. Age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 1,000,000 was examined on a year-by-year and demographic basis by assessing annual percent change (APC) with confidence intervals and p-values.ResultsBetween 1999 and 2020, 189,463 deaths were associated with both cardiovascular death and pancreatic cancer. Overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 1,000,000 increased from 26.164 in 1999 to 31.478 in 2020 (APC = 0.85%, CI = 0.72–0.98%, p < 10⁻⁶). Males consistently had higher mortality rates than females, with both groups showing significant increases (APCmale=0.86%, APCfemale=0.94%, p < 10⁻⁶). White individuals had higher absolute mortality rates, whereas Black/African-American individuals demonstrated a greater relative increase over time (APC = 1.38% vs. 1.10%; p < 10⁻⁶ for both). Metropolitan residents had consistently lower mortality rates than non-metropolitan residents, but metropolitan residents experienced a sharper increase (APCmetro=0.84%, APCnon−metro=0.63%, p < 10⁻⁶).ConclusionsPancreatic cancer-associated cardiovascular mortality has consistently increased in the United States from 1999 to 2020. There were notable demographic disparities which could provide insight towards targeted interventions for long-term mortality decrease.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is infamously known for its lethal malignancy, with a five-year survival rate of less than 10% despite improvements in screening and therapy [1]. Specifically, there were 510,992 new cases with 467,409 deaths in the year 2022 that can be attributed to pancreatic cancer [2]. It is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death by 2030 [3]. Simultaneously, cardiovascular disease (CVD) has an equally burdensome impact on the United States, remaining the leading cause of mortality in the US. The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) attributable to cardiovascular disease (CVD) was 22.3 per 100,000 [4]. The high mortality burden associated with both pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease suggests that their coexistence may contribute disproportionately to overall mortality. While prior studies explored the burden of CVD in oncology populations broadly, there remains a gap in understanding the intersection of cardiovascular pathology and pancreatic cancer specifically. Pathophysiologically, pancreatic cancer induces systemic inflammation and a hypercoagulable state, thereby increasing the risk of thromboembolic events and heart failure [5,6]. Furthermore, cardiotoxicity from chemotherapeutic agents and the physiological stress of malignancy may worsen or reveal underlying cardiovascular conditions [7]. The combined burdens of cancer and CVD can lead to poorer overall outcomes due to delays in treatment and a decrease in tolerance to adjuvant therapies. Emerging data also suggests that certain social determinants of health factors like race, income, and geographic region also contribute to disparities in both CVD and pancreatic cancer outcomes [8,9].

Despite this, there exists a paucity of literature examining long-term mortality trends in patients with coexistent pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular-related deaths at the national level. Moreover, there exists a gap in understanding the extent to which demographic disparities affect outcomes in this population. Therefore, to further quantify this association, this study analyzes national mortality data from the CDC WONDER database to assess demographic trends associated with concurrent pancreatic cancer and any cardiovascular-related death.

Methods

Data source and study population

Mortality data was obtained and analyzed from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) platform, using the Multiple Cause of Death database covering the years 1999 through 2020. These files include death certificate records compiled by the National Center for Health Statistics. Each record lists both the underlying and contributing causes of death. For this analysis, we included individuals whose deaths were attributed to both pancreatic cancer (ICD-10 code: C25) and cardiovascular conditions (ICD-10 code: I00–I99).

Variables and outcomes

We evaluated overall mortality trends using the age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR), calculated per 1,000,000 persons annually. Mortality rates were stratified by demographic characteristics, including sex, race, and degree of urbanization, to identify patterns in AAMR from 1999 to 2020.

Statistical analysis

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated using the direct standardization method based on the 2000 U.S. standard population, as implemented in CDC WONDER. The AAMR was calculated using the formula: AAMR = Σ ((di/ni)*wi) where di represents the number of deaths in age group i, ni represents the population in age group i, and wi represents the corresponding weight derived from the 2000 Standard Million Population for the United States. The weights (wi) represent the proportion of the population in each age group based on the 2000 U.S. Standard Million Population, which distributes 1,000,000 individuals across predefined age strata (e.g., 5–14, 15–24, 25–34 years), allowing each age-specific mortality rate to be proportionally weighted for standardized comparisons across populations. AAMRs were computed independently for each subgroup (e.g., sex, race, urbanization) and therefore are not additive across groups. Joinpoint regression analysis (version 5.4.0; National Cancer Institute) was used to evaluate changes in mortality trends over time. Annual Percent Change (APC) values and 95% confidence intervals were generated for each trend segment. Significance was set at α = 0.05 for both overall and stratified analyses.

Results

Baseline mortality trends for pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease

To contextualize trends in comorbid pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease, it is important to first examine mortality trends for each condition individually. It is important to note that there was an increasing mortality trend with both conditions together, greater than that observed for cardiovascular disease alone but lower than that observed for pancreatic cancer alone. Additionally, it is also important to note that the subgroup-specific AAMRs (e.g., by sex or race) were calculated independently and are not intended to sum to the overall AAMR. From 1999 to 2020, pancreatic cancer alone demonstrated a significant increase in AAMR, with an annual percent change (APC) of 2.44% (95% CI: 2.36 to 2.52; p < 10⁻⁶). In contrast, cardiovascular disease alone showed a slight, non-significant decline over the same period (APC: -0.37%; 95% CI: -0.99 to 0.28; p = 0.23). These findings raise the possibility of a meaningful clinical overlap when both diseases are present, setting the stage for the trends observed in the combined analysis (Table 1).

The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 1,000,000 showed a modest increase from 26.164 in 1999 to 31.478 in 2020, which yielded an annual percent change (APC) of 0.85% (95% CI: 0.72 to 0.98; p < 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 1; Table 3). Distinct trends were also noticed within this 20-year span. Specifically, between the year 1999 and 2004, the data revealed a statistically significant decrease with an APC of -0.91% (95% CI: -2.46 to -0.29; p = 0.018), followed by an increase between 2004 and 2007 (APC: 1.42%; 95% CI: 0.29 to 2.18; p = 0.016), a relatively stable period between 2007 and 2015 (APC: -0.06%; 95% CI: -1.34 to 1.36; p = 0.479), and a more pronounced increase between 2015 and 2020 (APC: 3.81%; 95% CI: 2.07 to 5.15; p < 10⁻⁶) (Table 1).

Stratified by gender for comorbid pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease

The AAMR for cardiovascular deaths with comorbid pancreatic cancer showed an evident disparity between gender, with males consistently experiencing higher mortality rates than females. Among males, the AAMR rose from 29.432 in 1999 to 35.789 in 2020 (Table 3), corresponding to an overall APC of 0.86% (95% CI: 0.72 to 1.02; p < 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 2; Table 1). When broken down further by time, males demonstrated an APC of -0.90% (95% CI: -2.47 to 0.08; p = 0.07) from 1999 to 2003, followed by an increase from 2003 to 2015 (APC: 0.35%; 95% CI: 0.11 to 1.48; p = 0.02), and a steeper increase from 2015 to 2020 (APC: 3.56%; 95% CI: 2.78 to 5.17; p = 0.0004).

In contrast, females had a lower AAMR throughout the period while still increasing from 23.359 in 1999 to 28.046 in 2020 (Table 3), with an overall APC of 0.94% (95% CI: 0.77 to 1.10; p < 10⁻⁶). Further trend analysis showed a relatively stable trend between 1999 and 2015 (APC: -0.04%; 95% CI: -0.26 to 0.16; p = 0.65), followed by a significant rise from 2015 to 2020 (APC: 4.15%; 95% CI: 3.10 to 5.72; p < 10⁻⁶) (Table 1).

Stratified by race for comorbid pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease

The population was stratified into the Asian/Pacific Islander, Black/African-American, and White individuals. White individuals consistently showed the highest rates throughout the study period with an AAMR increase from 25.524 in 1999 to 31.984 in 2020 (Table 4), yielding an overall annual percent change (APC) of 1.10% (95% CI: 1.02 to 1.17; p < 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 3; Table 1). This trend for White individuals can be further divided into two periods: between 1999 and 2015, the APC was 0.52% (95% CI: 0.42 to 0.61; p < 10⁻⁶), followed by an APC of 2.97% between the years 2015 and 2020 (95% CI: 2.46 to 3.72; p < 10⁻⁶).

Black/African-American individuals experienced lower AAMR compared to White individuals but with notable changes over time. Their rates went from 22.145 in 1999 to 29.451 in 2020 (Table 4), which yielded an overall APC of 1.38% (95% CI: 1.28 to 1.46; p < 10⁻⁶). Despite an overall increase along the 20 years, a relatively stable trend was observed between 1999 and 2004 (APC: -0.01%; 95% CI: -0.92 to 0.56; p = 0.87), followed by progressively increasing trends in subsequent periods, including APCs of 1.19%, 2.28%, and 4.61% in later intervals, reflecting an accelerating relative increase over time that exceeded that observed in White populations in recent years, despite persistently lower absolute mortality rates (Table 1).

Lastly, Asian or Pacific Islander individuals showed the lowest mortality rates. Their AAMR rates went from 19.033 in 1999 to 23.324 in 2020 (Table 4), with an overall APC of 0.69% (95% CI: 0.33 to 1.38; p = 0.0004). These trends should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively small mortality counts within this subgroup (Fig. 3; Table 1).

Stratified by urbanization status for comorbid pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease

The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for cardiovascular deaths also varied based on metropolitan versus non-metropolitan status. In metropolitan areas, the AAMR increased from 25.964 in 1999 to 31.291 in 2020 (Table 5), corresponding to an overall annual percent change (APC) of 0.84% (95% CI: 0.74 to 0.96; p < 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 4; Table 1). The growth was more modest between 1999 and 2003 (APC: -0.74%; 95% CI: -2.09 to 0.03; p = 0.056), followed by an increase between 2003 and 2015 (APC: 0.63%; 95% CI: 0.44 to 0.96; p = 0.0016), and a more pronounced increase from 2015 to 2020 (APC: 2.65%; 95% CI: 2.10 to 3.73; p < 10⁻⁶).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the individual trends of mortality attributable to pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) before examining their combined impact. This framework provided a comprehensive view of their coexistence on mortality outcomes. When considering pancreatic cancer alone, mortality significantly increased over the study period from 1999 to 2020, with an annual percent change (APC) of 2.44% (p < 0.001). Comparatively, cardiovascular disease alone had a slight, non-significant decrease in mortality (APC: -0.37%; p = 0.23). When pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease were analyzed jointly, mortality increased over time, contrasting with the slight decline observed for cardiovascular disease alone. We saw an overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) increase from 26.164 per 1,000,000 in 1999 to 31.478 per 1,000,000 in 2020, with a modest but significant APC of 0.85% (p < 0.001). An acceleration of the mortality rates occurred from 2015 to 2020 (APC: 3.81%; p < 0.001).

A notable inflection in trend occurred around 2015, after which mortality increased more rapidly. Several factors likely contributed to this trend. First, there have been significant advancements in clinical therapies that have improved pancreatic cancer survival, but this may place patients at increased risk for cardiovascular-associated mortality due to prolonged disease duration. Second, the modern era has brought enhanced recognition and reporting of cardiovascular disease in oncology populations, which likely also contributed to this increase. Lastly, changes in ICD coding practices and death certificate reporting may also partially explain this observed acceleration. Overall, these changes support the possibility that the interaction between pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease is becoming increasingly clinically significant over time.

These findings suggest a potentially additive or interacting burden between pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease that may contribute to worsened cardiovascular outcomes in this population. The effects seen may be attributed to an immunological response caused by pancreatic cancer that facilitates the release of proinflammatory cytokines like IL-1 IL-6, and TNF-α [10, 11, 12]. These cytokines are known contributors to chronic inflammation [4,5] that has been linked as a central driver in cardiovascular pathogenesis such as accelerated atherosclerosis and thrombosis [10]. As a result, this persistent inflammatory state could accelerate cardiovascular pathology and increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, potentially contributing to the observed rise in mortality. This biological interplay may be a topic for future studies to better understand the importance of integrated clinical approaches or pharmacology when these conditions are seen together.

In our subgroup analysis, males had consistently higher mortality rates, increasing from 29.432 in 1999 to 35.789 per 1,000,000 in 2020 (APC = 0.86%, p < 0.001), compared to females, whose rates increased from 23.359 to 28.046 per 1,000,000 over the same period (APC = 0.94%, p < 0.001). This disparity may reflect the differences in lifestyle behaviors as males have higher smoking prevalence and alcohol consumptions [13,14]. Hormonal factors can also play a role as estrogen in females have protective cardiovascular effects and could have contributed to this observed gender gap [15].

Racial disparities were observed, with Black/African-American individuals experiencing the greatest relative increase in mortality over time (22.145 to 29.451 per 1,000,000; APC = 1.38%, p < 0.001), followed by Whites (25.524 to 31.984 per 1,000,000; APC = 1.10%, p < 0.001). Asian/Pacific Islanders demonstrated the smallest relative increase (19.033 to 23.324 per 1,000,000; APC = 0.69%, p = 0.0004). Broader literature has demonstrated higher absolute rates of incidence and mortality from CVD or pancreatic cancer among Black/African-American populations attributed to numerous sociodemographic factors [16,17,18]. Moreover, underlying factors such as healthcare access, payment ability, and other social determinants may contribute to decreased screening and thereby reporting of conditions such as CVD among Black/African-American populations [4,19]. Our findings demonstrating a greater relative increase among Black populations are consistent with prior literature describing disproportionate disease burden among historically marginalized groups, though further investigation is needed to better characterize the underlying drivers of these disparities.

The data demonstrate an urban-rural divide with non-metropolitan areas exhibiting a higher mortality rate (27.286 to 31.298 per 1,000,000; APC = 0.63%, p < 0.001) compared to metropolitan areas (25.964 to 31.291 per 1,000,000; APC = 0.84%, p < 0.001). Accessibility to care in rural areas is often diminished in comparison to urban areas and may potentially explain the divide seen in the data [21]. Rural residents often travel greater distances for healthcare as well as face limited access to specialized medical services that may be needed in the case of pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease [20,21]. While non-metropolitan areas continue to exhibit higher overall mortality rates, the more rapid increase observed in metropolitan populations suggests a narrowing urban–rural divide, highlighting the need for close monitoring of trends in urban settings. This rise in metropolitan mortality may reflect increasing prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, shifting population demographics, and improved detection and reporting in urban centers, all of which may contribute to a growing disease burden [22].

Previous literature has extensively described the increasing incidence and burden of pancreatic cancer throughout the years, along with potential risk factors that may help explain this increase [23,24,25]. While studies have described the increase in the co-existence of cardiovascular disease among patients with various cancers, limited work has demonstrated the exacerbated increase among those with pancreatic cancer [24,26]. Across the general United States population, Truitte, et al. reported an increase in co-morbid cancer and CVD from 0.93% to 1.36% in more recent years between 2009 and 2020 [26]. Merza, et al. demonstrated hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and coagulopathies to be significant comorbidities among patients with pancreatic cancer between 2004 and 2014 [27]. Similarly, Ramai, et al. observed major gastrointestinal cancers to have a significantly increased risk of mortality from cardiac pathologies compared to noncardiac [28]. Our findings corroborate these data, further providing a temporal relationship between the co-existence of CVD with pancreatic cancer demonstrating an overall upward trend over time. Moreover, our findings uncover disparities among these comorbid mortalities, suggesting potential areas of early intervention by recognizing populations that may be more vulnerable to developing CVD with progressive pancreatic cancer.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is its retrospective nature and utilization of data available within the CDC WONDER database. Mortality data within CDC WONDER is compiled from numerous statistics-reporting sources managed by the Department of Health and Human Services, and as such, the availability of data is predicated upon accurate timely reporting of deaths to subsidiary agencies and bureaus within the United States. Factors underpinning limited healthcare access and disparities in healthcare utilization may thereby limit the availability of data for marginalized and minoritized populations. As such, data for Black/African-American and rural populations may be notably underreported, consequently resulting in an underrepresentation of these populations’ trends in comorbid deaths.

Conclusion

The compounding burden of pancreatic cancer and cardiovascular disease has risen steadily over time, with notable demographic and geographic disparities that highlight the urgent need for integrated care strategies and targeted interventions. These findings underscore the importance of prioritizing early recognition and equitable access to healthcare to mitigate excess mortality in at-risk populations.
