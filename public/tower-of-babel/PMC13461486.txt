Case Report: Recurrent spontaneous coronary artery dissection involving the left main
Authors: Matko Spicijarić, Lana Vivoda, Tomislav Jakljević, David Gobić, Zlatko Čubranić, Zoran Bakotić, Vjekoslav Tomulić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) is an increasingly recognised cause of acute coronary syndrome, particularly in previously healthy women under 60 years of age. Despite growing awareness, SCAD remains difficult to diagnose and treat because of its variable presentation and the risk of recurrence. This report aims to illustrate the complexity of recurrent SCAD (R-SCAD), particularly when it occurs in the peripartum period and involves the left main (LM) coronary artery, and to highlight the importance of a personalised, multidisciplinary approach. A 47-year-old woman presented with R-SCAD. Her first episode occurred four years earlier, two months postpartum, when she was admitted with an anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Coronary angiography showed SCAD of the mid left anterior descending artery (LAD). A percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was attempted, including stent placement in the LAD; however, the procedure failed because the true lumen of the LAD could not be identified, and an iatrogenic dissection of the LAD ostium was observed. The patient underwent emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Four years later, she was readmitted due to inferior STEMI. Angiography revealed R-SCAD of the LM and LAD, with additional involvement of the left circumflex artery (LCx). Optical coherence tomography (OCT)-guided PCI of the LM and LAD was performed. The patient was discharged in stable condition, with plans for long-term follow-up and testing for possible underlying disease. This case highlights the recurrent and challenging nature of SCAD, especially when it presents with peri- and postpartum onset and involves the LM. The clinical significance of this paper is that it emphasises the need for tailored management, careful evaluation of invasive procedures, the adoption of advanced interventional techniques and imaging, and multidisciplinary follow-up. R-SCAD should prompt evaluation for systemic arteriopathies or connective tissue disorders. Recognising these complexities is vital to improving outcomes and preventing future cardiovascular events.

Introduction

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) was, until recently, considered a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome (ACS); however, recent studies show that SCAD accounts for up to 4% of all ACS cases in the general population and approximately 35% of ACS cases in women under 60 years of age (1, 2). Women make up about 90% of SCAD cases and typically present at a mean age of 44–53 years (3–6). According to available registry data, recurrent SCAD (R-SCAD) appears to be a rare event. Based on the Australia–New Zealand registry and the French DISCO registry, the incidence of R-SCAD was 3.6% and 3.3% at median follow-up of 21 and 12 months, respectively (5, 6). A recently published meta-analysis of 4,587 patients with SCAD demonstrated recurrence rates of 15.5% for major adverse cardiovascular events, 10.0% for myocardial infarction (MI), and 7.3% for R-SCAD (7).

SCAD is a non-atherosclerotic, non-traumatic event characterised by the formation of an intramural haematoma within the tunica media of an epicardial coronary artery (8). As the haematoma expands, it separates the intima and narrows or blocks the true lumen, with or without an intimal tear, ultimately causing acute myocardial ischaemia. Several factors are linked to SCAD onset and are reported in up to two-thirds of patients. Extreme emotional stress is more commonly reported among women, whereas extreme physical stress is more frequent in men (9). Other triggers include pregnancy, retching or vomiting, cocaine use, and cancer treatment (10). Given that R-SCAD still occurs in some patients despite close follow-up, therapy and comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, it is clear that an important knowledge gap remains.

Case report

A 47-year-old woman was admitted to local general hospital with chest pain radiating to her left arm. The symptoms had begun two days earlier and had worsened on the day of admission.

Medical records showed that four years earlier, she had been hospitalised two months postnatally with ACS secondary to a SCAD in the mid-apical left anterior descending artery (LAD) (Figure 1A). Prior to hospitalization, the patient was completely healthy, with no history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, or alcohol consumption. Additionally, the family history was negative for coronary artery disease, arteriopathies, and connective tissue diseases. The attending physicians proceeded with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), which was preceded by administration of acetylsalicylic acid 300 mg and ticagrelor 180 mg. Despite repeated attempts, the coronary wire could not be advanced into the true lumen of the apical LAD (Figure 1B), and the procedure was stopped and the wire removed. The patient reported increasing chest pain. A control angiogram revealed catheter-induced dissection at the origin of the LAD, and the patient was clinically deteriorating (Figure 1C). Further attempts to cross the distal lesions were unsuccessful, and the operator decided to seal the proximal dissection entry point. Two stents were implanted in the proximal and mid LAD, each over a differently positioned coronary wire (Figure 1D). Due to impaired flow in the distal LAD, the patient was referred to a tertiary surgical centre, where emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to LAD grafting was performed. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and clopidogrel was continued for six months, after which clopidogrel was discontinued. The remaining chronic therapy included standard medication used in secondary prevention: bisoprolol 1.25 mg, atorvastatin 10 mg, and ezetimibe 10 mg. The patient remained symptom-free in subsequent years and reported good exercise tolerance prior to the onset of the index event.

This time, the patient was diagnosed with ACS with inferior ST-segment elevation (STEMI), and urgent coronary angiography was performed. Prior to angiography, acetylsalicylic acid 300 mg was administered. The angiogram showed a dissection of the left main coronary artery (LM) extending into the LAD (Figure 2A). The left circumflex artery (LCx) had reduced distal flow (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction flow grade - TIMI 2) due to dynamic obstruction of the distal LCx lumen (Figure 2A), resulting in ECG changes. Flow through both previously stented areas was preserved, and it was now clear that one of the previously implanted stents was in the false lumen and extending to the septal branch. The right coronary artery (RCA) was hypoplastic and without significant disease (Figure 2B). The LIMA graft to the LAD was occluded (Figure 2C).

The patient was transferred to tertiary referral hospital. On arrival at the emergency department, an electrocardiogram showed small Q waves in the inferolateral leads, lateral T-wave inversions, and conduction disturbances consistent with right bundle branch block. The working diagnosis was ACS with transient ST-elevation, with Killip class I status. The troponin I value upon admission was 8,126 ng/L, while the following morning it rose to 12,780 ng/L. Repeat angiography via the right femoral approach was performed upon admission and revealed a significant dissection in the proximal LAD and LM, along with spontaneous resolution of the previously noted LCx stenosis (Figure 3A). Two previously placed stents were visible: one in the false lumen of the proximal LAD and another in the true lumen of the proximal/mid LAD (Figure 3B). A guidewire was successfully advanced into the LAD, and a second guidewire was positioned in the distal LCx. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging of the LM–LAD segment demonstrated underexpansion of the previously implanted LAD stent. Additionally, an LM intramural haematoma and a dissection involving the origin of the LAD, with a small thrombus, were observed (Figure 3C and Figure 3D). The measured minimal lumen area of the underexpanded stent was 5.23 mm2, the dissection length was 5.1 mm, and the length of the intramural haematoma was 10.2 mm. Balloon dilation using a 3.75 × 15 mm non-compliant (NC) balloon was performed within the mid-LAD stent, followed by implantation of two new drug-eluting stents extending to the LM ostium (4.0 × 29 mm and 4.0 × 29 mm). Proximal optimisation technique (POT) was performed in the LM using an NC balloon (6.0 × 8 mm). Control OCT confirmed good expansion of all implanted LAD and LM stents and maintained flow through the LCx. The final coronary angiogram demonstrated patent stents with an optimal angiographic result (Figure 3E).

On the third day, the patient developed pain in the right femoral region. CT angiography of the peripheral arteries demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm at the junction of the right external iliac artery (EIA) and the common femoral artery (CFA), with an active contrast extravasation into a large ipsilateral retroperitoneal haematoma. Angiography via a contralateral femoral approach revealed extravasation above the previous puncture site. A balloon-expandable stent-graft was deployed, successfully controlling the bleeding. The patient's subsequent clinical course was uneventful. DAPT was initiated immediately prior to the start of the PCI and was briefly paused 48 h later due to the aforementioned haemorrhage and bleeding risk.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed akinesia of the apical interventricular septum and inferior wall, with aneurysm formation in these segments, which were thinned and scarred. Mild diastolic dysfunction was present, with a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. On the CT performed for the puncture-site complication, basal bilateral pleural effusions and compressive atelectasis of the adjacent lung parenchyma were observed. Given her history of R-SCADs, a potential underlying connective tissue disorder was suspected, prompting referral for further evaluation.

She was transferred to local general hospital for ongoing care. On discharge, the patient was haemodynamically stable, normotensive, and without chest pain. Her discharge medications included DAPT (acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg and ticagrelor 2 × 90 mg) and anti-ischaemic therapy: bisoprolol 2.5 mg, ramipril 2.5 mg, and atorvastatin 80 mg. The planned duration of DAPT was 12 months, due to the complexity of the intervention.

Follow-up coronary angiography three months later showed patent stents in the LM and LAD, with no evidence of new stenoses or dissections (Figure 4). The patient was asymptomatic, denying any anginal symptoms or other cardiac complaints, and demonstrated compliance with the medication regimen. Meanwhile, the results of the rheumatological–immunological evaluation have been received and showed no significant abnormalities. Family history and anthropometric data did not suggest a connective tissue disorder or arteriopathy, nor did the immunological tests, which were within normal limits (antinuclear antibodies (ANA), extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) panel (including anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB, anti-Sm, anti-RNP, anti-Jo-1), anti–double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (anti-CCP), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA; p-ANCA and c-ANCA), complement levels (C3 and C4), and antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant)). CT angiography of the aorta and its branches, as well as imaging of the renal arteries, was normal. Genetic testing was not undertaken; however, it will be thoroughly considered at the upcoming follow-up appointment, subject to patient consent.

Discussion

SCAD is a non-atherosclerotic, non-iatrogenic process characterised by separation of the layers of the coronary artery wall, with formation of an intramural haematoma and/or a false lumen, leading to reduced coronary flow and clinical presentation as ST-elevation or non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI or NSTEMI), and, less often, sudden cardiac death (4, 9, 10).

The pathophysiology of SCAD is not yet fully understood; however, intimal rupture and bleeding from the vasa vasorum are most often identified as the primary mechanisms underlying intramural haematoma formation (4, 8). SCAD most often affects the LAD, whereas involvement of the LM is less common but tends to be associated with a more severe clinical course (11).

SCAD is particularly common in women under 60 and during the peripartum period, where it accounts for up to 43% of ACS cases (2, 12). Although SCAD is often linked to the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, their presence does not rule out the diagnosis, especially in younger patients (9). The disease likely results from an interaction between predisposing factors, such as female sex, pregnancy, and fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), and trigger events, such as emotional and physical stress (4). Peripartum SCAD has distinct angiographic and clinical features compared with non-peripartum SCAD. It more commonly affects the proximal segments of the coronary arteries, including the LM and proximal LAD, and tends to present as STEMI, with higher rates of haemodynamic instability and a greater need for invasive management, such as percutaneous or surgical revascularisation (13, 14). LM involvement is recognised as a key predictor of a more severe course and poorer outcomes in SCAD patients (14).

The case closely aligns with these observations, as the first SCAD episode occurred early postnatally and involved the LAD. The initial PCI was complicated by iatrogenic LM dissection, necessitating emergency surgical revascularisation. In light of the complication encountered, the appropriateness of PCI remains debatable. Nevertheless, given the persistent pain and ischaemia, the operator opted for intervention. The complexity is further underscored by a recurrence years later, again involving the LM, demonstrating that even after initial stabilisation, SCAD can relapse and pose clinical challenges. This pattern supports recommendations emphasising long-term follow-up, personalised treatment strategies, and consideration of underlying systemic arteriopathy in patients with R-SCAD episodes (4).

R-SCAD is defined as a de novo dissection that is distinct from the extension of the index lesion, accompanied by ACS symptoms and biomarker elevation (9, 15). Since up to 7.3% of patients experience this event, comprehensive workup and monitoring are warranted (7). Although the exact causes of R-SCAD remain elusive, avoiding potential triggers such as emotional exertion and stress is strongly advised, while optimal management of arterial hypertension appears to be the most promising secondary prevention strategy (9).

The therapeutic approach to SCAD remains a clinical challenge, and optimal management must be individualised according to clinical presentation, angiographic findings, and haemodynamic status. According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, conservative management is preferred for haemodynamically stable patients without significant coronary flow obstruction, given the high rate of spontaneous healing of dissections reported in prospective registries—over 70% of cases during follow-up (9, 16, 17).

In contrast, involvement of the LM, persistent ischaemia, haemodynamic instability, or progression of the dissection are clear indications for invasive management, including PCI or CABG (4, 16). LM-SCAD is particularly associated with a more severe clinical course and poorer prognosis, often prompting early revascularisation (14). Due to the difficulty in identifying the true lumen, the extent of the dissection, or vessel occlusion, the success rate of PCI is less than 50% (18). Therefore, intravascular imaging, such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or OCT, is recommended during every intervention. OCT is more commonly used because of its superior spatial resolution compared with IVUS, which facilitates easier differentiation between the true and false lumina, more accurate assessment of vessel diameter, and better optimisation of stent expansion (19).

Persistent ischaemia and chest pain in the first presentation, and LM involvement in the second, were the primary indications for PCI. A Danish group reported a remarkably similar case of a 51-year-old woman who had previously presented with SCAD involving the mid- LAD at the ages of 39 and 48 years (20). As her symptoms resolved spontaneously on both occasions, she was managed conservatively. At the age of 51 years, she presented again with cardiac arrest secondary to SCAD involving the LM and LAD. Successful PCI of the LM and LAD was performed with the support of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). However, despite successful revascularisation, the patient died several days later due to multiorgan failure. As her sister had also experienced SCAD, genetic testing was performed; however, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified. Another published case describes a 41-year-old woman who had initially presented two years earlier with an inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) caused by SCAD of the second obtuse marginal branch (21). As coronary flow was preserved and the patient was haemodynamically stable, PCI was not performed. She was discharged on aspirin 81 mg daily, atorvastatin 40 mg daily, and metoprolol succinate 25 mg daily. Her medical history was notable for obesity and hypertension, while her family history was unremarkable. Two years later, she presented with a non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) caused by R-SCAD involving the distal RCA and proximal LAD. Once again, the patient remained haemodynamically stable with preserved distal coronary flow, and PCI was deferred. CT angiography of the head and neck, as well as aortography, revealed no abnormalities. In addition to her previous medical therapy, clopidogrel and amlodipine were initiated. In the two aforementioned cases, the use of intravascular imaging for diagnosis and treatment is not reported. That represents a very important aspect and added value of our case report. In both described cases, no underlying causes of SCAD were identified, which, as in our case, also points to an existing knowledge gap.

Given the recurrence of SCAD and involvement of proximal segments, including the LM, it is reasonable to consider an underlying systemic arteriopathy. This clinical pattern indicates generalised arterial wall vulnerability, consistent with recent guidelines and expert consensus, which emphasise the importance of extended aetiological evaluation in patients with recurrent, atypical, or haemodynamically significant SCAD (4, 16). The literature shows that SCAD is most often associated with FMD, which is identified in a significant number of patients during systemic vascular screening, often without prior clinical signs (8, 22). In addition to FMD, SCAD is linked to other inherited and acquired connective tissue disorders, such as vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome and Marfan syndrome, as well as other systemic arteriopathies, particularly in patients with early disease onset or multiple dissection episodes (22). A meta-analysis including more than 14 studies and over 4,200 patients with SCAD concluded that hypertension and FMD were associated with an increased risk of R-SCAD, whereas beta-blocker therapy was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence (23).

The case highlights the complexity and recurrent nature of SCAD, particularly in patients with peripartum onset and LM involvement. Experiencing SCAD twice in a short period underscores the need for a personalised treatment approach, including careful consideration of indications for invasive procedures, use of advanced interventional techniques, and intravascular imaging. Multidisciplinary long-term follow-up is essential to minimise the risk of future cardiovascular events. Given the recurrence and involvement of proximal coronary segments, assessment for underlying systemic arteriopathy or connective tissue disorders is necessary, further emphasising the importance of long-term aetiological assessment and monitoring.

Conclusion

This case illustrates both the challenges and potential benefits of invasive management in carefully selected patients with complex R-SCAD. It also highlights the need for a multidisciplinary approach to help reduce the existing knowledge gap in the current treatment of SCAD and its recurrence.
