Valorization of teak wood biomass into nanobiochar for environmental remediation
Authors: Deshraj Singh Thakur, Santosh Narayan Chadar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The release of hazardous pollutants into aquatic systems has increased due to rapid industrialization, making the development of effective and sustainable remediation techniques necessary. Although biochar made from biomass has drawn interest as an inexpensive adsorbent, its limited adsorption capacity and surface reactivity limit its practical use. Systematic research on underutilized biomass feedstocks, especially teak wood, and their structure-property connections is still lacking, even though nanobiochar offers better performance. Here, we describe the production of nanobiochar obtained from teak wood as a novel nanohybrid adsorbent, which is made via mechanical size reduction after regulated high-temperature pyrolysis. A highly porous architecture, richer functional groups, and improved surface reactivity are revealed by thorough analysis (BET, FTIR, XRD, SEM–EDX, and zeta potential). Through cooperative processes including surface complexation, electrostatic interactions, and pore filling, these characteristics facilitate the effective adsorption of heavy metal ions and organic contaminants. The proposed material has the potential to be an affordable, sustainable, and scalable solution for biomass valorization and water remediation due to its high removal efficiency and operational adaptability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56198-x.

Introduction

Heavy metal and persistent organic pollution contamination of aquatic environments has become a serious global concern due to rapid urbanization and industry. Because of their high toxicity, persistence, and potential for bioaccumulation, these pollutants represent major risks to human health and ecological systems. Conventional wastewater treatment methods that are commonly employed include chemical precipitation, ion exchange, and membrane-based separation. However, their poor efficiency at trace contamination concentrations, high energy demand, and secondary waste formation sometimes limit their practical applicability. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop cleanup methods that are economical, efficient, and sustainable.

Adsorption has become the most widely used and versatile technique for treating wastewater due to its high efficacy, ease of application, and capacity to adjust to complex aqueous systems. The physicochemical interactions that regulate the adsorption process include pore diffusion, ion exchange, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and surface complexation. Both organic and inorganic contaminants can be effectively removed using these methods throughout a wide concentration range. According to recent studies, adsorption is particularly well-suited for sustainable water treatment applications since it not only ensures high removal efficiency but also offers advantages like reduced sludge generation, operating flexibility, and potential for adsorbent renewal1–6. The removal behavior in environmental remediation varies significantly depending on the kind of contaminants, such as heavy metals, organic pollutants, and gaseous species. Ion exchange, electrostatic attraction, and surface complexation are the primary mechanisms that remove heavy metals, whereas hydrophobic contacts, π–π interactions, and pore-filling mechanisms typically adsorb organic contaminants. Conversely, materials with high surface reactivity and specialized pore designs are required for effective adsorption and catalytic transformation of gaseous pollutants. Many adsorbent materials have been thoroughly studied for these uses, including membrane-based systems, biochar, activated carbon, and ion-exchange resins. Excellent selectivity and separation efficiency are offered by resins and membranes, but they are often associated with high running costs, regeneration issues, and fouling issues. On the other hand, carbon-based substances such as biochar provide an inexpensive, sustainable, and versatile alternative due to their tunable surface chemistry, porosity structure, and environmental friendliness. These differences demonstrate how important it is to develop adsorbent materials with certain physicochemical properties in order to effectively target specific pollutant groups.

Recent advances in adsorption research have further demonstrated that the performance of adsorbents is significantly influenced by the availability of active sites, pore architecture, and surface chemistry. Because of their high adsorption affinity and tunable physicochemical properties, engineered carbon-based materials in particular have shown remarkable promise. For instance, surface functionalization and hierarchical porosity are crucial for enhancing adsorption kinetics and capacity for complex pollutant systems, according to recent research published in the Journal of Hazardous Materials7. Similarly, studies conducted in the Chemosphere have demonstrated that improved carbonaceous nanoparticles exhibit superior adsorption performance due to the synergistic impacts of surface functional groups and nanoscale structural characteristics, which significantly improve contaminant interactions and mass transfer efficiency8.

Biochar, a carbon-rich material created by thermochemically converting biomass in an oxygen-limited environment, has garnered significant attention as a sustainable adsorbent for environmental remediation9. It is a potential option for the removal of pollutants due to its inherent qualities, which include porous structure, surface functional groups, and chemical stability. However, virgin biochar’s adsorption efficiency under a variety of environmental circumstances is frequently hampered by its comparatively small surface area, limited pore accessibility, and lack of active sites10. Previous research has also shown that surface heterogeneity and functional group distribution, which control pollutant–adsorbent interactions at the molecular level, have a significant impact on adsorption efficiency11.

To overcome these limitations, biochar has undergone extensive modification and functionalization. Increasing surface area, pore volume, and functional group density by physical and chemical activation methods improves adsorption effectiveness12,13. Numerous active sites, including redox-active centers and Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, are introduced by chemical treatments with acids, alkalis, or metal oxides and are crucial for pollutant binding processes14–16. Furthermore, it has been shown that the addition of functional moieties and nanoparticles to the biochar matrix significantly boosts adsorption capacity through synergistic effects such as improved surface reactivity and increased active site formation17.

Nanobiochar, which is created by combining nanotechnology with biochar, outperforms traditional biochar in terms of adsorption. Nanobiochar’s higher specific surface area, improved pore accessibility, and increased availability of functional groups, due to its nanoscale size, all improve adsorption kinetics and capacity18. Because of better diffusion paths and stronger surface contacts, recent research has shown that nanostructured biochar systems have higher removal efficiencies for both organic pollutants and heavy metals19–23. Moreover, sophisticated nanohybrid materials with specific adsorption capabilities can be developed by incorporating metal oxides and other functional nanoparticles into the carbon matrix24.

The structural and functional characteristics of materials made from biochar are greatly influenced by the choice of biomass precursor. Changes in lignocellulosic composition, mineral content, and intrinsic porosity directly affect surface area, pore structure, and functional group distribution25,26. This makes teak wood biomass, a rich but underutilized lignocellulosic waste of the sawmilling, forestry, and furniture industries, a feasible feedstock for the production of high-performance adsorbents. Its robust carbon structure and relatively high lignin concentration provide the perfect basis for the production of robust and highly porous nanobiochar materials. There are very few systematic studies on nanobiochar generated from teak wood, particularly when it comes to adsorption-driven wastewater treatment, despite the notable advancements in biochar-based materials. Because most prior research has focused on easily accessible biomass sources, there is a significant knowledge vacuum about the structure-property-performance relationship of nanomaterials made from teak.

Novelty of this study:

This work presents a unique nanohybrid adsorbent made of nanobiochar generated from teak wood that combines superior nanoscale engineering, surface functionalization, and sustainable biomass valorization. The produced material has improved physicochemical characteristics, such as a well-defined micro-mesoporous architecture and enriched oxygen-containing functional groups, which work in concert to improve adsorption performance, in contrast to traditional biochars. This work offers a sustainable method for turning lignocellulosic waste into value-added nanomaterials for environmental remediation applications, in addition to advancing the design of high-efficiency, low-cost adsorbents.

Hypothesis:

Nanobiochar derived from teak wood biomass is expected to exhibit much better adsorption capacity and removal efficiency for both organic pollutants and heavy metals when compared to traditional biochar and commercial adsorbents. Both a high density of oxygen-containing surface functional groups that facilitate a variety of adsorption mechanisms, including electrostatic interactions, surface complexation, π–π interactions, and hydrogen bonding, and a hierarchical micro–mesoporous architecture that encourages rapid diffusion and accessibility of contaminants are expected to contribute to this enhancement. Furthermore, the nanoscale structure is anticipated to significantly increase specific surface area and active site availability, enhancing equilibrium capacity and adsorption kinetics. Furthermore, it is expected that the synergistic interplay between pore structure and surface chemistry will improve selectivity toward a spectrum of contaminants under a variety of environmental conditions. Therefore, it is proposed that this material function as an incredibly efficient, sustainable, and affordable nanohybrid adsorbent, offering a workable alternative for complex water and wastewater remediation applications while also promoting biomass valorization and circular resource utilization.

Materials and methods

Raw material preparation

Teak wood biomass waste that was gathered from neighboring sawmills was cleaned with distilled water to get rid of surface impurities and clinging dust before being oven-dried for 24 h at 105 °C. A 100-mesh screen was used to sift the finely ground dried biomass, resulting in a consistent particle size that was appropriate for thermal treatment. The raw biomass powder underwent a chemical pretreatment with strong hydrochloric acid (HCl) to eliminate metal ions, inorganic minerals, and ash-forming components before being loaded into the muffle furnace. In order to oxidize the surface and improve chemical reactivity by adding oxygen-containing functional groups, concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃) was then applied to the acid-washed biomass. Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) was used to neutralize excess acid residues, and the item was then repeatedly rinsed with distilled water until the pH was neutral. Following chemical treatment, the biomass was oven-dried and sealed in airtight containers, making it ready for direct loading into the muffle furnace for regulated pyrolysis27,28.

Biochar production

A stainless-steel tubular reactor (SS-310 grade) was filled with the chemically prepared and oven-dried teak wood biomass, which was then sealed to preserve an oxygen-limited environment. Pyrolysis was carried out for five hours at temperatures between 800 and 900 °C in an inert tubular furnace. The elevated temperature range used in this study was purposefully chosen to enable enhanced carbonization, promote structural ordering, and induce partial graphitization, all of which are essential for the development of high-performance nanobiochar. Conventional biochar production is typically carried out at moderate temperatures (500–600 °C). Efficient devolatilization, a rise in fixed carbon content, and the development of aromatic carbon networks are all made possible by high-temperature pyrolysis, which greatly enhances the material’s physicochemical characteristics. Increased specific surface area and well-defined micro-mesoporous structures, which are crucial for optimizing adsorption capacity and promoting quick mass transfer during contaminant removal, are especially encouraged by such conditions.

Additionally, higher temperatures lead to better structural stability and the creation of thermodynamically stable graphitic domains, which improve surface reactivity and electron density distribution. In order to prevent excessive pore collapse and guarantee enough activation of the carbon matrix, the heating rate and ultimate temperature were carefully adjusted to balance pore development and structural integrity. Therefore, this regulated high-temperature treatment is beneficial for creating nanostructured biochar with improved durability under operating circumstances and greater adsorption capability. After pyrolysis, the furnace was allowed to naturally cool to ambient temperature under inert conditions to prevent oxidation. The resulting biochar was collected and stored in sealed containers to prevent moisture absorption and preserve surface functionality29,30.

Nanobiochar synthesis

To convert bulk biochar to nanoscale particles, a planetary ball mill (Pulverisette 7) was used. Milling was done in dry conditions for three hours at a rotational speed of 350 rpm. As grinding media, zirconia balls of one millimeter in diameter were employed. The zirconia milling jar was filled with 14 balls totaling 37 g in mass and 3.7 g of biochar, ensuring a 10:1 ball-to-powder weight ratio. The milling operation was stopped for 10 min every 30 min of running time in order to regulate temperature rise during operation. Particle size reduction was significantly aided by the combined action of shear and collision forces during planetary motion. Upon milling, the resulting powdered nanobiochar was meticulously gathered and stored in airtight containers for subsequent examination31,32.

Characterization techniques

The physicochemical characteristics of the produced nanobiochar were described using an extensive range of analytical methods. Surface morphology, microstructural features, and elemental composition and distribution were clarified by combining energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy with scanning electron microscopy33. X-ray diffraction analysis was used to determine the crystalline structure and phase components using Cu Kα radiation across an appropriate 2θ range. Surface functional groups and chemical bonds were identified using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, which operates in the 4000–400 cm-¹ wavenumber range. Adsorption performance was measured by evaluating nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms recorded at 77 K using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda model for pore volume and size distribution and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method for specific surface area. Fixed carbon, ash, moisture, volatile matter, and elemental concentrations were measured by proximate and ultimate analysis34. Particle size distribution and polydispersity index were calculated using dynamic light scattering, confirming the homogeneity and nanoscale dimensions. Lastly, Zetasizer Nano ZS zeta potential measurements assessed aqueous dispersion behavior, isoelectric point, surface charge, and colloidal stability35.

Environmental application studies

Heavy metal ions, such as Pb(II), Cd(II), Hg(II), and As(V), were chosen as model adsorbates in this investigation because of their high toxicity, persistence, and non-biodegradability, making them priority pollutants in aquatic ecosystems. These metals are frequently released from industrial processes like mining, electroplating, and battery production. Once in water bodies, they can build up in living things through biomagnification, which can cause serious health problems like kidney damage, neurological disorders, and carcinogenicity even at trace levels. Heavy metal ions were chosen as typical contaminants in this investigation because their removal from wastewater continues to be a major environmental concern36.

The adsorption capability of teak wood nanobiochar displays a significant affinity for these dangerous metal ions, as seen in Fig. 1, highlighting its potential for environmental rehabilitation. The reported removal efficiencies (Pb²+ 89%, Cd²+ 85%, Hg²+ 82%, and As₄+ 78%) demonstrate that a well-developed porous structure and numerous active sites allow for efficient metal uptake. Due to variations in speciation and adsorption behavior, As(V) has a much lower removal effectiveness, but Pb(II) has a relatively higher removal efficiency because of its advantageous ionic characteristics and stronger interaction with surface functional groups. These findings support the effectiveness of nanobiochar as a flexible adsorbent for heavy metal-contaminated water systems37.

The impact of operational conditions on adsorption efficiency is also depicted in Fig. 3. The pH-dependent behavior indicates that maximum adsorption occurs in the moderately acidic range (pH 5–6), where favorable surface charge conditions and less competition from H+ ions improve electrostatic attraction between metal ions and the adsorbent surface. Increasing the adsorbent dosage improves removal efficiency because there are more active sites available; however, saturation effects cause the advantage to fade beyond an optimal level. Strong metal–surface interactions are indicated by partial saturation of adsorption sites at higher initial metal ion concentrations, which reduces the percentage removal while increasing adsorption capacity. Furthermore, the rise in adsorption efficiency with temperature points to an endothermic process that is fueled by increased metal ion diffusion into the porous matrix and the activation of new adsorption sites. Kinetic and equilibrium studies support these conclusions by demonstrating that chemisorption is the main mechanism and that pseudo-second-order kinetics govern the adsorption process38. Compatibility with the Freundlich and Langmuir isotherm models suggests that heterogeneous surface contacts and monolayer adsorption coexist39,40. Thermodynamic properties indicate that the process is endothermic and spontaneous, with higher entropy signifying greater disorder at the solid-liquid interface41,42. Among other surface characterization data, zeta potential and BET/BJH tests demonstrate the material’s highly porous structure and attractive surface charge properties, enabling efficient adsorption through a combination of surface complexation and electrostatic mechanisms43,44. All things considered, these results show that nanobiochar made from teak wood is an extremely effective, environmentally safe, and sustainable nanohybrid adsorbent for removing harmful heavy metal ions from aqueous systems. Its potential for use in cutting-edge wastewater treatment and environmental remediation technologies is highlighted by its great adsorption performance, advantageous physicochemical qualities, and environmental compatibility45–47.

Results and discussion

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of teak wood-derived nanobiochar produced at 800–900 °C shows a Type IV isotherm with a noticeable hysteresis loop at higher relative pressures (P/P₂ > 0.4), indicating the presence of a well-developed hierarchical pore structure. Figure 4. The analysis was carried out using the Quantachrome NOVA Series, which includes the NOVA 2200e and 4200e. The creation of a highly porous carbon framework was confirmed by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area, which was estimated within the linear relative pressure range of 0.05–0.30 and found to be around 298 m² g-¹. While capillary condensation within mesopores causes a sharp increase in adsorption at higher relative pressures, micropore filling from extensive devolatilization and carbon matrix rearrangement during high-temperature pyrolysis causes the initial slow uptake of nitrogen at low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.1). The material has a combination micro–mesoporous architecture, according to the IUPAC classification, where mesopores promote improved mass transfer and adsorbate diffusion while micropores contribute to high specific surface area and adsorption capacity. Adsorption-based environmental remediation applications benefit greatly from this hierarchical pore structure, which is a feature of nanostructured biochar produced at high temperatures48.

Morphological characterization (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy images, Nova NanoSEM 450, provide comprehensive information on the microstructural characteristics and surface appearance of the produced nanobiochar Fig. 5. These images reveal the significant structural disturbance caused by high-energy milling, milling. They show extremely asymmetrical, angular, and shattered particles with sharp edges and uneven arrangements. A porous architecture with roughness, unevenness, and diversity is produced by the surfaces’ many fissures, microcracks, and interparticle gaps. Significant nanoparticle aggregation is caused by the strong van der Waals forces and high surface energy of these carbon-based nanostructures. Since most of the particles are tiny, size reduction and the creation of nanostructured biochar are made easier.

Fig. 5Synthesized nanobiochar micrographs taken using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at various magnifications.

Synthesized nanobiochar micrographs taken using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at various magnifications.

The original lignocellulosic carbon matrix may have been partially preserved in layered and flaky textures, although at greater magnifications, shattered edges exhibit increased surface reactivity. The interstitial spaces and porous network give more surface area, which promotes mass transfer and adsorbate diffusion. Because they provide a plethora of active sites for heavy metal sequestration through ion exchange, surface complexation, electrostatic attraction, and pore filling, these morphological characteristics are very beneficial for adsorption processes. Its excellent potential for environmental cleanup is demonstrated by its considerable surface heterogeneity and coarse topography, which further enhance adsorbent–metal ion interactions.

Elemental composition analysis (EDX)

The SEM analysis reveals a porous structure with rough, flake-like morphology and interparticle voids, which are advantageous for surface-dependent adsorption applications Fig. 6. The heterogeneous surface texture enhances the availability of active sites and facilitates efficient interaction with adsorbates. The corresponding EDS spectra confirm the elemental composition of the material, showing dominant peaks for carbon (C) and oxygen (O), along with a minor but distinct presence of iron (Fe), Fig. 7. Quantitative EDS analysis (Table 1) indicates that carbon is the predominant element (89.64 wt% and 94.45 at%), confirming the carbon-rich nature of the nanobiochar, while oxygen (5.68 wt%) corresponds to oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface. The presence of iron (4.68 wt%) can be attributed to the intrinsic mineral content of teak wood biomass, as trace metal ions are naturally absorbed from soil during plant growth and remain embedded within the lignocellulosic matrix. During high-temperature pyrolysis, these inorganic constituents are retained and become incorporated into the carbon structure in the form of stable mineral phases. Additionally, minor contributions from processing conditions or reactor materials cannot be entirely excluded. The elemental mapping further demonstrates a uniform distribution of C, O, and Fe across the surface, indicating that iron species are well-dispersed within the carbon matrix without significant aggregation. This homogeneous distribution suggests that the retained iron may also contribute to additional active sites, potentially enhancing adsorption performance. Overall, the SEM–EDS results confirm the formation of a structurally uniform, chemically stable, and functionally active nanobiochar suitable for environmental remediation applications.

Crystallographic structure analysis (XRD)

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern obtained using D8 ADVANCE reveals that the teak wood-derived nanobiochar possesses a predominantly amorphous carbon framework with limited crystalline ordering, which is typical of lignocellulosic biomass-derived carbon materials Fig. 8. A broad diffraction band observed in the range of 20°–30° (2θ) is attributed to disordered aromatic carbon structures, indicating incomplete graphitization and the presence of turbostratic carbon domains. The peak centered at approximately 26.5° corresponds to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon, suggesting partial stacking of graphene-like layers and short-range structural organization. Weak diffraction features observed in the higher angle region (~ 33°–48°) indicate the presence of minor crystalline phases. In light of the EDX results, which predominantly confirm carbon, oxygen, and trace iron, these reflections are more reasonably attributed to iron-containing mineral phases (e.g., Fe-based oxides or residues) rather than calcium- or silica-based compounds. The reflection near 29.3° is relatively weak and cannot be definitively assigned to crystalline calcite in the absence of supporting elemental evidence; thus, it is interpreted as originating from residual inorganic species formed during pyrolysis. Such observations are consistent with previous reports, where biomass-derived biochar exhibits an amorphous carbon matrix embedded with trace mineral phases dependent on feedstock composition and thermal treatment conditions48,49.

Fig. 8X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of Nanobiochar made from teak wood displaying crystalline mineral phases and amorphous carbon properties.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of Nanobiochar made from teak wood displaying crystalline mineral phases and amorphous carbon properties.

The overall absence of sharp and intense diffraction peaks confirms the largely amorphous nature of the material, which is advantageous for adsorption applications due to the increased density of structural defects and accessible active sites. The coexistence of a disordered carbon matrix with uniformly dispersed trace mineral phases contributes to enhanced surface reactivity and facilitates multiple adsorption mechanisms, including electrostatic interactions and surface complexation. Overall, the XRD analysis confirms that the synthesized nanobiochar consists of a predominantly amorphous carbon structure with minor iron-associated crystalline domains, consistent with EDX observations. This structural configuration is highly favorable for environmental remediation applications, as it provides a balance between adsorption efficiency and structural stability50.

Functional group analysis (FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared spectra recorded in the 4000–500 cm-¹ range using (ALPHA II) confirm that the produced biochar or nanobiochar possesses several surface functional groups Fig. 9. The broad absorption band located between 3893 and 3561 cm-¹, which is indicative of hydrophilic surface properties, is caused by O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups in phenols, alcohols, and adsorbed water. To support the creation of condensed aromatic frameworks caused by pyrolysis, a small peak at around 3110 cm-¹ shows aromatic C–H stretching. Asymmetric stretching vibrations of CO2, which are frequently seen in carbon-rich materials due to carbonate impurities or ambient CO2 adsorption, are shown by absorptions at 2355 and 2315 cm-¹. At 1690 cm-¹, a noticeable band with peaks at 1866 and 1832 cm-¹ shows carbonyl C = O stretching. While the bands at 1513 and 1459 cm-¹ represent aromatic skeletal vibrations and –CH₂ deformation, respectively, the 1647 cm-¹ feature displays aromatic C = C stretching vibrations, indicating graphitic domain expansion. These oxygen-bearing functional groups and aromatic carbon structures enhance the material’s adsorptive performance through surface complexation, π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces, confirming its suitability for environmental remediation and adsorption processes.

Surface charge analysis (zeta potential) and particle size

The mobility distribution graph and EOS plot display the findings of the zeta potential investigation, which was utilized to evaluate the synthesized material’s surface charge characteristics and colloidal stability using Zetasizer Nano ZS Fig. 10. The material’s negative zeta potential value of − 11.28 mV, which indicates the presence of negatively charged functional groups on the particle surface, is compatible with oxygen-containing moieties discovered in compositional analysis. The rather narrow mobility distribution around this value suggests a relatively homogeneous distribution of surface charges. The particles’ negative electrophoretic mobility (− 8.799 × 10-⁵ cm²/V·s) further supports their anionic nature in an aqueous medium. Although the magnitude of the zeta potential indicates moderate colloidal stability, it is sufficient to prevent rapid aggregation under the measurement conditions. This zeta potential was calculated using the Smoluchowski model, which is appropriate for aqueous systems, and the conductivity value (0.0823 mS/cm) reflects the ionic strength of the dispersion medium. Overall, the zeta potential results highlight the synthesized material’s negatively charged surface and adequate dispersion stability, which is advantageous for applications requiring interfacial interactions and adsorption in aqueous environments.

The measurement data, zeta potential distribution, and electrophoretic mobility map of the generated material.

The synthesised material’s particle size characteristics were examined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) Fig. 11. In addition to cumulant analysis, the results are displayed in terms of intensity, volume, and number distributions. The several peaks in the intensity distribution imply a polydisperse system with main particle populations in the nanometer range, along with some larger aggregates. Cumulant analysis demonstrates a broad size range and moderate aggregation in the aqueous medium, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 1610.8 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.802. The volume and number distribution plots also demonstrate that while smaller particles predominate in terms of particle count, larger particles significantly contribute to the total scattering intensity due to their higher scattering efficiency. The diffusion coefficient of 3.054 × 10-¹ cm² s-¹ supports the observed particle mobility behavior in water. These results suggest that the synthetic material is composed of particles with a degree of agglomeration, ranging from nanoscale to submicron, which is characteristic of materials based on carbon and in line with the surface charge properties observed in zeta potential analysis.

Adsorption performance and mechanism

Heavy metal ion adsorption onto nanobiochar produced from teak wood is regulated by the synergistic relationship between surface chemistry and hierarchical pore structure, as illustrated in Fig. 13. The postulated adsorption mechanism is supported by the physicochemical characterization data. FTIR examination confirms the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and carbonyl (C = O), which are the primary active sites for metal ion binding. Further evidence for this comes from the zeta potential study, which demonstrates a negatively charged surface at operating pH values, favoring cation adsorption. These functional groups deprotonate in slightly acidic to nearly neutral environments, producing negatively charged sites that promote electrostatic attraction toward cationic species like Pb²+, Cd²+, and Hg²+.

Ion-exchange mechanisms, in which metal ions replace exchangeable cations (such as H+, Na+, and Ca²+) initially linked with the biochar surface, play a substantial role in metal uptake in addition to electrostatic interactions. Additionally, metal ions and oxygen donor atoms found in functional groups coordinate to create stable inner-sphere complexes by surface complexation. According to BET analysis, the high specific surface area and micro-mesoporous structure promote effective metal ion migration into internal pores, improving adsorption by pore filling and physical trapping.

Therefore, the combined evidence from FTIR (functional groups), BET (porosity and surface area), and zeta potential (surface charge behavior) supports the validity of the proposed mechanism, showing that adsorption is controlled by several concurrent processes rather than a single dominant pathway52.

Important operational factors also affect the adsorption performance. Maximum adsorption takes place in the pH 5–6 range, where favorable surface charge conditions and less proton competition strengthen electrostatic interactions, according to the influence of solution pH. While near-neutral circumstances encourage deprotonation and improved metal binding, excessive protonation of functional groups restricts adsorption at lower pH values. Due to the availability of more active sites, increasing the adsorbent dosage increases removal efficiency; nevertheless, site saturation and potential particle aggregation cause the improvement to become marginal beyond an ideal dosage. Another important factor is the starting concentration of metal ions; greater concentrations cause partial saturation of adsorption sites, which lowers the percentage removal even while adsorption capacity increases. Furthermore, an endothermic process is indicated by the observed rise in adsorption effectiveness with temperature, which suggests increased metal ion diffusion into the porous matrix and activation of more adsorption sites at higher temperatures. The high adsorption effectiveness of nanobiochar for heavy metal removal is mostly due to a combination of surface complexation, ion exchange, electrostatic attraction, and pore-filling mechanisms that control the adsorption mechanism.

Environmental significance

Teak wood biomass is valued for its use in nanobiochar, which lowers environmental pollution, promotes sustainable waste management, and offers an affordable substitute for commercial adsorbents. Furthermore, nanobiochar’s stable carbon structure promotes long-term carbon sequestration, which is consistent with methods for mitigating climate change.

Conclusion

The current work shows that teak wood biomass may be efficiently converted into nanobiochar with improved physicochemical qualities that are appropriate for use in environmental remediation. The produced nanobiochar’s effective adsorption of heavy metal ions from aqueous solutions was facilitated by its well-defined micro-mesoporous structure, abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups, and comparatively large specific surface area.

The adsorption performance was strongly influenced by key operational parameters, including solution pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial metal ion concentration. Interestingly, the material performed best in moderately acidic environments, which are typical of industrial wastewater streams. These results demonstrate the promise of nanobiochar made from teak wood as an affordable, sustainable, and eco-friendly adsorbent for water filtration. Beyond these encouraging findings, however, a number of crucial research areas need to be addressed to facilitate real-world applications. The performance of nanobiochar in actual wastewater systems with complex matrices and competing ions should be the main focus of future research. To evaluate economic viability, thorough studies on regeneration efficiency, reusability across several cycles, and long-term structural stability are also crucial. To create continuous-flow and column-based treatment systems that mimic actual industrial settings, more research is also required. To better understand adsorption routes, advanced mechanistic insights using methods like XPS, FTIR (post-adsorption), and modeling methodologies should be investigated. Furthermore, adsorption efficiency and scalability might be further improved by integration with cutting-edge technologies, including magnetic modification, composite synthesis, and machine learning-based optimization. All things considered, this study establishes teak wood biomass as a sustainable precursor for the synthesis of nanobiochar and lays the groundwork for future developments in the creation of high-performance adsorbents for extensive environmental remediation.
