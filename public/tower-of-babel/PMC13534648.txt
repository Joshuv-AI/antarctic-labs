Joint full waveform inversion and source localization of virtual and actual passive source seismic data
Authors: Xujia Shang, Liguo Han, Pan Zhang, Wensha Huang, Donghao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Full waveform inversion (FWI) using passive seismic data offers potential advantages for obtaining deep subsurface structural information. However, its application depends on a reliable background model and accurate source locations. Here, we introduce a joint inversion framework that uses virtual and actual passive seismic data to achieve velocity modeling and source localization. This method first retrieves the broadband virtual reflection responses at the surface by seismic interferometry. Then, the virtual responses are used as the basis for multiscale FWI to recover the subsurface velocity structures without knowledge of the actual passive source locations. Finally, using the inversion result as the initial model, alternately perform reverse-time wavefield scanning and FWI with actual passive-source data, enabling simultaneous inversion for source locations and velocity information and compensating for deep illumination. Numerical examples under various conditions of inhomogeneous passive-source distribution and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) show that the proposed method can robustly recover the subsurface velocity structures using only passive seismic data, even when the initial model is rough, while accurately locating passive seismic events.

Introduction

Full waveform inversion reconstructs a velocity parameter model that describes the internal structures of the Earth by simulating the propagation of seismic waves and fitting observed information1,2. It is a core technology in modern seismic imaging. The sources that generate seismic waves can be divided into two categories: active sources and passive sources. An active source generally refers to a highly controllable artificial seismic source on the surface. Under ideal conditions, active source FWI can accurately and gradually map the subsurface velocity structures from shallow to deep3. However, when a reliable initial model is unavailable, active source FWI tends to accumulate errors in the shallow part of the model, resulting in insufficient updating of deep structures and distortion and blurring in the description of complex structures.

Passive sources refer to naturally occurring or industrially induced random sources that typically exist within the Earth’s interior4,5. Therefore, passive actual-source FWI, which relies on direct observed data, provides more extensive illumination within the medium compared to active source FWI. However, as a crucial initial condition for synthesizing the wavefield, source location parameters cannot be directly observed in a passive seismic survey6,7. Accurate source localization is critical for passive actual-source FWI and contributes to understanding subsurface processes such as rock fracturing or hydrocarbon migration8. A variety of methods have been proposed for source localization9–11. Among them, reverse time extrapolation methods based on the wave equation offer high accuracy but rely on a precise velocity model. It leads to a strong coupling relationship between velocity inversion and source localization in passive actual-source FWI, with errors propagating between the two and ultimately affecting the inversion results12. In addition, passive seismic data contains surface-related multiples, which can easily increase the non-linearity of passive actual-source FWI13. Therefore, passive actual-source FWI is typically only considered for constructing a low-wavenumber background using the transmitted first-arrival wavefield to provide an initial model for high-precision active source FWI.

As a data-driven method, seismic interferometry can retrieve virtual reflection responses located on the observation plane from actual seismic data without the need to understand medium parameters and source information, and image subsurface structures like an active seismic survey14–17. This study utilizes this feature to propose a joint FWI method for virtual and actual sources, targeting passive seismic data. This method first retrieves broadband virtual reflection responses using seismic interferometry by full-field multidimensional deconvolution (MDD) and recovers the main velocity structures without a reliable initial model or knowledge of the source locations. We refer to this process as passive virtual-source FWI. Subsequently, using the passive virtual-source FWI result as the initial model, we alternately perform passive actual-source FWI and source localization using reverse-time wavefield scanning. The internal direct illumination provided by actual passive seismic events is utilized to enhance the deep velocity inversion effect and optimize source localization accuracy. Using this method, we can simultaneously achieve high-precision velocity inversion and source localization using only passive seismic data.

Methods

Passive virtual source FWI

The first step in joint inversion is to recover the main velocity structures using passive virtual-source FWI. This process requires utilizing the virtual responses retrieved from seismic interferometry as observed data for inversion. Seismic interferometry can be divided into explicit cross-correlation and implicit deconvolution methods. Recent studies have shown that virtual responses retrieved using seismic interferometry by MDD are more suitable for FWI18,19. This study uses the full-field MDD method to retrieve the virtual responses located on the observation plane20. Passive actual source FWI

Passive actual source FWI is the second step in the joint inversion framework. It involves synthesizing internal seismic wavefields that are jointly represented by velocity parameters and source parameters. Source parameters include the spatial location and time function of the source. This study focuses on source space localization, and the source time function can be inverted and estimated using methods commonly used in active source FWI23,24. Here, we assume that the source time functions are known. Source localization by reverse-time scanning

The passive source position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{x}{\prime\:}}_{S}$$\end{document} is determined by reverse-time scanning. The process consists of two steps. First, the observed passive seismic data are propagated in reverse time from the receiver locations to the model space, and the boundary condition is defined as8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{b}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{R},t\right)={u}_{obs}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{R},{\mathbf{x}}_{S},T-t\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{b}$$\end{document} represents the reverse time wavefield. Subsequently, scan the maximum energy focus point in the reverse-time wavefields along the time dimension and consider it the source location. When the velocity model is basically correct, the reverse time wavefields will focus near the true passive source location.

Joint FWI workflow

The joint inversion proposed in this study is achieved by connecting passive virtual-source FWI and passive actual-source FWI in series. Through gradually increasing the dominant frequency of the self-defined source function, we can conduct multiscale passive virtual-source FWI to improve stability25. Based on the passive virtual-source FWI result, reverse-time scanning will be used to estimate the initial source locations and conduct passive actual-source FWI, then the source localization will be corrected using the updated velocity model. This alternating process will be repeated until the iteration termination requirements are met. This study adopts a hybrid source inversion strategy to reduce computational costs and suppress crosstalk noise26. For the inversion stage of each frequency band, we divide it into several sub-stages. At the end of iterations in the previous sub-stage, we randomly reselect a subset of seismic data and stack them into hybrid source observed data for inversion in the next sub-stage. Results

In this section, we demonstrate the performance of the proposed joint inversion of virtual and actual passive sources using synthetic numerical examples on the rescaled Marmousi2 model and a metal ore body model. We test the influence of different passive-source distributions and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels on inversion results. We use the Ricker wavelet as the passive-source time function to simulate transient passive sources. The top boundary of the models is set as a free surface. The seismic data are synthesized using the time-domain finite-difference method based on the first-order velocity-stress wave equation.

Marmousi2 model test

The true velocity model is shown in Fig. 2a. We add a low-velocity layer at depth to compare the performance of different methods in recovering deep structures. The model has a total width of 12 km and depth of 2.8 km, with a grid spacing of 20 m in both horizontal and vertical directions. The dominant frequency of the passive-source wavelets varies randomly between 6 Hz and 12 Hz. A total of 201 vertical-component receivers are evenly deployed on the free surface at an interval of 40 m, covering a lateral range from 2 km to 10 km. Note that this range also defines the effective domain for velocity inversion.

Tests on ideal passive-source distribution

As shown in Figs. 3a and 200 passive sources are randomly distributed within the entire model domain, representing a nearly ideal scenario. The initial model is a linear velocity model, as shown in Fig. 2b. We first conduct passive actual-source FWI to recover the velocity model. It should be noted that in passive actual-source FWI, source localization is updated by reverse-time scanning every 10 iterations of velocity inversion. Figure 4a shows a randomly selected observed record, and we can identify transmission responses and complex scattered-wave information. A multi-scale inversion strategy is adopted, successively using passive-source data with cutoff frequencies of 2 Hz, 4 Hz, 6 Hz, 8 Hz, 12 Hz, 16 Hz, and 24 Hz. The obtained inversion result is shown in Fig. 5a. We can see that standalone passive actual-source FWI recovers partial structures but yields significant errors in imaging deep formations. This is because reverse-time wavefield scanning starting from a rough initial model fails to correctly focus source locations, leading to misaligned internal illumination and velocity artifacts in the synthetic wavefield.

Next, we recover the velocity model using the proposed joint inversion method. Figure 4b shows the virtual response at x = 4.4 km. To reduce interference, we mute the direct wave arrivals. We can identify both refraction and reflection events in the virtual response, which help recover the low-wavenumber background and the high-wavenumber details in the velocity inversion, respectively. We conduct multi-scale passive virtual-source FWI by sequentially using Ricker wavelets with dominant frequencies of 1 Hz, 2 Hz, 3 Hz, 4 Hz, and 6 Hz as the source functions. The initial model remains the linear model shown in Fig. 2b. Figure 5b shows the passive virtual-source FWI result. The deterministic source location and broadband characteristics of the virtual response enable passive virtual-source FWI to recover the main velocity structures with higher accuracy than standalone passive actual-source FWI. However, some velocity distortions caused by retrieval errors in the virtual response still exist. Based on the passive virtual-source FWI result, we obtain the final joint inversion result (Fig. 5c) by alternately conducting passive actual-source FWI and source localization based on reverse-time wavefield scanning. The results demonstrate that the internal illumination provided by passive actual-source FWI corrects several artifacts in the passive virtual-source FWI result and further improves the inversion precision of deep structures.

Figures 6a–c show the 20 randomly selected passive events located by reverse-time wavefield scanning, based on the three inversion results shown in Figs. 5a–c, respectively. Under the ideal passive-source distribution, all three models can achieve relatively accurate source localization. In comparison, however, the localization results derived from the passive actual-source FWI model show larger errors. In the joint inversion, the source localization (Fig. 6b) based on the passive virtual-source FWI result is already close to the true source locations. It enables passive actual-source FWI within the joint inversion framework to effectively refine velocity inversion results through internal illumination while driving source localization (Fig. 6c) to converge toward the true positions.

Tests on inhomogeneous passive-source distributions

In practice, passive sources are rarely distributed extensively over a large spatial domain. It is therefore essential to consider the influence of inhomogeneous passive-source distributions on velocity inversion. Here, we design two inhomogeneous passive-source distribution scenarios. The source distribution for the first scenario is shown in Fig. 3b, which mimics the case where passive sources cluster in shallow and deep low-velocity reservoirs. The result of passive actual-source FWI is shown in Fig. 5d. We can see that the inversion result yields relatively high resolution at and near the reservoirs, but inversion artifacts appear in other areas. The retrieved virtual response is shown in Fig. 4c. Benefiting from the insensitivity of full-field MDD to source distribution, no obvious spurious interference events in the virtual response. Figure 5e shows the obtained passive virtual-source FWI result. The lateral recovery of the inversion result is relatively consistent. We also note that more inversion errors are introduced compared with the ideal case. It is attributed to increased retrieval errors in the virtual response due to the inhomogeneous passive-source distribution. Using Fig. 5e as the initial model and conducting passive actual-source FWI, the obtained final joint inversion result (Fig. 5f) further improves the accuracy of the inverted model. Figures 6d–f compare the passive-source localization results obtained from the inversion models shown in Figs. 5d–f. The located passive sources based on the joint inversion result show better agreement with the true source locations.

The second inhomogeneous distribution we design is that passive sources are only distributed in the deep region on the left side of the model, simulating a one-sided illumination scenario. The source distribution is shown in Fig. 3c. The passive actual-source FWI result is shown in Fig. 5g. We can see that passive actual-source FWI performs well on the left side of the model, where passive sources are concentrated, but produces artifacts in other regions with sparse source illumination and fails to indicate the location of deep reservoirs. The retrieved virtual response is shown in Fig. 4d. We can see that one-sided illumination of passive sources weakens the reflected energy from structures on the right side of the model in the virtual response. The passive virtual-source FWI result is shown in Fig. 5h. We can see that the passive virtual-source FWI result also suffers from imbalanced model recovery caused by one-sided illumination of passive sources. However, compared with passive actual-source FWI, passive virtual-source FWI recovers more structures on the right side and in the deep part of the model, and also avoids inversion artifacts in the shallow section. The joint inversion result obtained is shown in Fig. 5i. It is clear that the joint inversion accurately recovers the shallow fault structures and the fold structures on the right side, and successfully reveals the deep low-velocity reservoirs. Nevertheless, we note that the joint inversion result in Fig. 5i is inferior to those in the previous two cases, indicating that joint inversion cannot fully eliminate the adverse effects of one-sided passive-source illumination. Even so, the velocity modeling accuracy of the joint inversion is still significantly better than that of standalone passive actual-source FWI and passive virtual-source FWI, as also verified by the source localization comparison shown in Figs. 6g–i.

In this section, we take the ideal passive-source distribution as an example to demonstrate the performance of the proposed method using noisy passive seismic data. We inject the Gaussian random noise into the passive seismic data to reduce the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the observed dataset. Discussion

Inverting velocity parameters using directly observed seismic data is a conventional idea in FWI. However, the strong coupling between source location and velocity parameters increases the inversion instability for passive seismic. The proposed joint inversion framework provides a new approach for passive source FWI. The independence of seismic interferometry to source parameters helps avoid the challenge of accurate source localization when the initial model is crude. passive virtual-source FWI utilizes virtual responses to recover a reliable velocity structure, which enables passive actual-source FWI to start from a position closer to the true solution space and correct source localization errors during the inversion process.

Passive virtual-source FWI plays a critical role in joint inversion. Two main factors affect the inversion result: virtual responses retrieval and objective function. The wavefield properties of the virtual responses determine the content of the passive virtual-source FWI fit. At the same time, the objective function decides whether the passive virtual-source FWI can correctly map the velocity model using these properties. Analyzing the sensitivity of passive virtual-source FWI to different wavefield components in virtual responses can provide a better understanding of this process. More robust and advanced objective functions can also help improve the inversion effect, providing a more reliable initial model for joint inversion. Furthermore, improving the stability of virtual response retrieval is also an important task in future studies.

In this study, we assume the source time functions of passive seismic data are known in the numerical tests. In practice, however, the source time functions need to be estimated in addition, and their accuracy is also critical for passive actual-source FWI. A promising approach is to use the passive virtual-source FWI result as the background velocity model and to invert the source time function using least-squares optimization. In addition, adopting convolution-type objective functions or similar wavelet-independent methods provides an effective way to reduce the impact of source time function errors.

The joint inversion framework proposed in this paper is based on 2D acoustic media, but can be extended to 3D media and elastic media. For the 3D case, the high computational cost of full-field MDD will become an obstacle. A feasible strategy is to construct an initial solution using cross-correlation seismic interferometry with lower computational cost, and then approximate the retrieval effect of full-field MDD through least-squares inversion. In addition, deep learning-based prediction of retrieved virtual responses is also a promising approach. For elastic media, mutual interferometry of multi-component observed data can retrieve virtual responses for additional source and receiver components, thereby benefiting the targeted inversion of P- and S-wave velocities. However, extracting the first arrivals of P-waves and S-waves from complex observed wavefields remains a major challenge. Fast and accurate P/S wavefield decomposition will be a powerful tool to address this issue.

Conclusion

We proposed a joint inversion method for passive virtual and actual-source seismic data. This method first uses the broadband virtual responses retrieved by seismic interferometry to recover a main velocity structure using passive virtual-source FWI. Then, it uses passive actual-source FWI in series to further refine the inversion result and correct source localization using internal illumination. The results of numerical experiments show that this method can accurately invert the velocity structures and locate passive events when the initial model is rough and the passive source locations are unknown. The proposed method remains robust even under inhomogeneous passive-source distributions and when the observed data are contaminated by random noise.
