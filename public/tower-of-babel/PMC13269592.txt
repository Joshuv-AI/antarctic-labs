Phytogenic synthesis and characterization of silver nanoparticles from Crataegus songarica aqueous extract for comprehensive biological evaluation
Authors: Faisal Zaman, Samin Jan, Nasir Assad, Muhammad Nauman Khan, Dawit Kifle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe need for sustainable synthesis of bioactive silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) has driven interest in plant-mediated nanotechnology. The synthesis of silver nanoparticles using the aqueous extract of Crataegus songarica K. Koch was investigated in this study. To determine the formation, size, surface morphology, and composition of the synthesized AgNPs, various techniques were used, such as UV-Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, SEM, EDX, and DLS, to validate the formation of AgNPs. A typical absorption peak (447 nm) in the UV-Vis spectrum indicated the formation of spherical AgNPs, and FTIR analysis revealed the presence of phenolic compounds in the reduction and stabilization of nanoparticles (NPs). XRD was used to determine that the crystallite size was 28.01 nm on average to ensure that the AgNPs were crystalline. SEM images showed clearly defined rounded particles with a size ranging from 20 to 60 nm, and EDX analysis revealed the presence of metallic silver. The produced AgNPs exhibited high biological activity, as demonstrated by their strong antibacterial effect against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, with the largest linear fungal growth inhibition observed against Staphylococcus epidermidis. In addition, AgNPs were effective as antifungal agents against Candida albicans and Aspergillus niger and as dose-dependent anticancer agents against HepG2 cancer cells. As demonstrated in their antioxidant tests, the DPPH and FRAP assays showed good radical scavenging and ferric ion reduction potentials, which are similar to those of ascorbic acid. This study demonstrates that AgNPs mediated by C. songarica are a promising bioactive product that is safe for use in biomedical research and has potential in antimicrobial, anticancer, and antioxidant therapies.Keywords: Antioxidant activity, Antibacterial activity, Antifungal activity, Crataegus songarica, Silver nanoparticles, Green synthesis

Introduction

Since the early 21st century, nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative field with wide-ranging applications across medicine, materials science, and environmental engineering. This revolutionary science operates at a scale of 1 to 100 nanometers (nm), driving advancements across various fields and reshaping industries [1, 2]. At such small sizes, the physical, chemical, and biological properties of materials differ fundamentally from those of bulk materials. Quantum effects coupled with a high surface area-to-volume ratio govern the behavior of nanomaterials, bestowing upon them unique and often enhanced properties at the nanoscale [3]. This extraordinary characteristic has opened up new ideas in materials science, resulting in revolutionary applications in various fields, such as electronics, catalysis, environmental science, and medicine [4, 5].

Among the myriad nanomaterials, metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) have received unparalleled scientific attention because of their manipulable optoelectronic, catalytic, and antimicrobial properties [6, 7]. These properties depend not only on the metal itself but also on the size, shape, composition, and surface chemistry of the nanoparticles (NPs) [8, 9]. As an example, surface plasmon resonance of gold NPs (spheres, rods, cubes) can be precisely manipulated with the ability to control the shape, and thus they are invaluable in sensing, imaging, and photothermal therapy [10, 11]. Similarly, titanium dioxide (TiO2) and zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs have been extensively utilized because of their powerful photocatalytic capacity in wastewater treatment and capacity to deliver broad-spectrum UV protection in cosmetics [12].

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are one of the most widely investigated and industrially applied nanomaterials among other MNPs [13]. Silver has long been known as an antimicrobial agent; however, its nano-preparation has contributed significantly to improving its effectiveness and expanding its uses [14]. AgNPs have complex bioactivities, which involve a combination of several processes, such as the clumping of microbial respiratory enzymes and DNA replication, enabled by the slow accumulation of bactericidal Ag+ ions [15]. Moreover, they cause oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and directly damaging the cell membrane [16].

The strong antimicrobial characteristics of AgNPs have made them indispensable in many biomedical and consumer-level applications. They are part and parcel of dressings of wounds, catheters, and orthopedic implants to avoid infection [15, 17]. Their application to textile fabrics has resulted in odor-resistant fabrics, and in food packaging materials, they increase the shelf life by preventing the growth of spoilage microorganisms [18, 19]. In addition to their antimicrobial properties, AgNPs have a promising future in diagnostics as biosensors, therapeutics as drug delivery vehicles, and in oncology as targeted therapy agents for cancer [20, 21].

The synthesis of AgNPs is typically carried out using three main approaches: physical, chemical, and biological methods [22–24]. Traditional approaches to reduce it, such as the use of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) or citrate, are more yield- and size-controlled but often use hazardous substances, produce toxic by-products, and consume a large amount of energy [25]. Physical techniques, such as laser ablation and evaporation-condensation, do not involve solvent contamination; however, they require advanced equipment and are expensive for large-scale production [26]. The disadvantages of these classic methods in terms of environmental conditions and the economy have triggered a shift in the paradigm to a more sustainable and environmentally friendly synthesis protocol [27].

The adoption of biological organisms, such as plants, algae, fungi, and bacteria, has become a better alternative (green synthesis) or photosynthesis [28–30]. Of these, there is the benefit of plant-mediated synthesis. Plants are easy to access, inexpensive, and safe [31, 32]. Their extracts are an excellent source of bioactive metabolites, including flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, phenolic acids, and reducing sugars, which serve as dual-action reductants of Ag+ ions to form Ag0 andcapg the newly formed NPs to stabilize them [33]. The technique of one-pot synthesis is straightforward, fast, and can be carried out at ambient temperature and pressure, which has a significant impact on reducing the ecological footprint [34]. In addition, the phytochemicals that coat the NPs tend to provide other biological properties, increasing their therapeutic advantages [35, 36].

The Crataegus (hawthorn) genus, which belongs to the Rosaceae family, has gained popularity worldwide owing to its medicinal benefits, especially for cardiovascular health [37]. The genus Crataegus is a good source of bioactive compounds, such as oligomeric procyanidins, flavonoids (e.g., hyperoside, vitexin), phenolic acids, and triterpenoids, which have strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, and antimicrobial properties [38–40]. The species Crataegus songarica K. Koch is native to Central Asia and has a traditional history of use in folk medicine in the region [41]. Although its more widespread counterparts, such as C. monogyna and C. laevigata, have been well studied, C. songarica has been relatively little studied, representing a major gap in the literature. Early phytochemical studies have indicated that C. songarica has a potent array of reducing and stabilizing agents, which qualifies it as a good candidate for the green production of AgNPs [42]. The excellent flavonoid and phenolic properties not only assure the high efficiency of AgNP synthesis using NPs but also indicate that the resultant C. songarica-capped AgNPs (Cs-AgNPs) may have superior and complementary biological activities.

Previous studies on plant-mediated AgNPs have demonstrated significant biological activities; however, variability in plant sources and synthesis conditions necessitates further investigation into species-specific effects. Although the potential of green-synthesized AgNPs is well documented and the bioactivity of the Crataegus genus is established, the use of C. songarica for the facile production of AgNPs and the subsequent extensive analysis of their biological capabilities is a new and interesting study. The literature on plant-based AgNPs describes a restricted number of activities, and in many cases, it covers only antimicrobial or antioxidant activities. Another less extensively studied topic is a biological profile that is holistic and includes antibiofilm, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory potentialities. The emerging discipline of green nanotechnology exploits the abundant and diversified phytochemical content of plants as a nontoxic and sustainable nanomaterial source. Although the genus Crataegus is famous for its medicinal use, little is known about the potential of C. songarica K. Koch as a bioreagent in the fabrication of functional AgNPs.

To address this gap, this study presents a green and efficient approach for the synthesis of AgNPs using an aqueous extract of C. songarica. The synthesized NPs were comprehensively characterized to confirm their formation, stability, and physicochemical properties. Their biological potential was systematically evaluated, including antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, and cytotoxic activities. Additionally, the study explores the therapeutic relevance of these nanoparticles in relation to with inflammation.

Materials and methods

Materials

The C. songarica, commonly known as Jand Proar or Pendak, were obtained in the Ojhor Valley, Chitral, Pakistan, in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province of Pakistan, collected from privately owned land belonging to one of the authors, and identified by Dr. Muhammad Nauman Khan, and a voucher specimen was deposited under the number (12821(ICP)) in the herbarium of the, Department of Botany, Islamia College Peshawar, Peshawar, Pakistan for future reference. The species is not protected and is not listed as endangered. Therefore, no specific permission or license was required for data collection. All procedures complied with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. All chemicals were of analytical grade. Silver nitrate (AgNO3), used as a precursor salt, was acquired from Merck, Germany. n-Hexane was purchased locally, and deionized water (DI) was used in all experimental processes. The MTT assays were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and the DMEM and the Penicillin-Streptomycin solutions (100X) were obtained from Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA).

Extraction of aqueous extract

The flowers of C. songarica were thoroughly cleaned with tap water, followed by deionized water, to remove surface contaminants and particulate matter. The cleaned sample was then divided into small fragments and shade-dried for 12 days, as per the standard procedures of Assad et al. and Tooba et al. [43, 44]. The dried plant material was then crushed into a fine powder using a mechanical grinder. To prepare the aqueous extract, a suspension of the powdered biomass with 200 mL of deionized water (10 g of biomass) was prepared and stirred continuously at room temperature (5 h). Filtration using a Whatman filter paper (No. 42) was used to purify the resulting mixture. The filtrate was subsequently transferred into a Petri dish and allowed to dry in an air-drying oven at 45 °C overnight. The solid extract was stored in Eppendorf tubes until further use in the synthesis experiments.

Synthesis of AgNPs

Silver nanoparticle synthesis was triggered by the addition of a 1.0 mM aqueous solution of silver nitrate (17.0 mg/100 mL) to an aqueous suspension of the powdered flowers (22.2 mg/100 mL) and subjecting the mixture to diffused sunlight over a period of 10 min. Visual interaction was observed and recorded as a color change, as represented in Scheme 1, which is typical of AgNPs, to identify the creation of AgNPs, according to a modified Rastogi and Arunachalam methodology [45–47].

Characterization

The process of the photo-reduction of silver ions to metallic AgNPs was observed with the help of the UV-Visible spectroscopy (UV-1700 Pharma Spec Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and scans were taken in the 800 –200 nm interval. Periodic measurements were made at 60 min intervals to monitor the formation of typical surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption bands. Fourier-Transform Infrared spectroscopy (Shimadzu FTIR8400S spectrometer) of by KBr pellets technique (Scanning range 4000 –500 cm− 1) at a 4 cm− 1 frequency of the functional groups of the pure plant extract and the synthesized AgNPs were compared using a Fourier-transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) technique [48, 49]. An X-ray diffractometer (Jeol JDX-3532) with a monochromatic X-ray source was used to determine the crystallographic properties of the synthesized AgNPs. The diffraction patterns were observed in the 2θ range of 10° to 80° with 40 kV and 15 mA. The morphology and elemental composition of the AgNPs produced using the polar extract were further determined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Nova NanoSEM 450) with a scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) detector. To analyze the sample, a droplet of the NPs suspension was placed on a grid that had been coated with carbon, and elemental mapping was performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 5 mm. A Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS, using a He 633 nm laser, was used to determine the hydrodynamic particle size. A 0.2 μm Whatman filter was used to filter the NPs suspension before measurement to remove any particulate impurities. The filtrate was then transferred to a 10 mm path length solvent-resistant cuvette. All samples were pre-equilibrated at 25 °C for 2 min and then analyzed. Data interpretation was performed using the Zetasizer software (Malvern Instruments) to present the particle size distribution as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Antibacterial activity

The well-diffusion technique was used to determine the antibacterial activity of the synthesized AgNPs, according to Assad et al. (2025), with some modifications [43, 50]. Briefly, Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) plates were prepared, and bacterial suspensions of Escherichia coli (ATCC 10536), Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC 13932), and Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCC 12228) were inoculated onto the plates. Each plate contained four wells, each made with a sterile cork borer. The wells were filled with (a) positive control (erythromycin) 40 µg/mL, (b) synthesized AgNPs, (c) aqueous extract of C. songarica, and (d) distilled water (negative control). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was determined in millimeters (mm). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

The broth microdilution method was used to determine the MIC of the synthesized AgNPs. Bacterial strains were cultured in Mueller-Hinton broth until they reached the stationary stage. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL. AgNPs of different concentrations (10–80 µg/mL) were introduced into test tubes, and the bacterial cultures were incubated with the NPs for 24 h at 37 °C. The lowest concentration of AgNPs that prevented microbial growth (measured by turbidity) was documented as the MIC. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Antifungal activity

The efficacy of green-synthesized C. songarica-mediated AgNPs in antifungal activity was tested against four pathogenic fungi of clinical importance: Candida albicans (ATCC 10231), Aspergillus niger (ATCC 16404), Cryptococcus neoformans (ATCC 208821), and Trichophyton rubrum (ATCC 28188) using test-tube macrodilution/slant method. The fungi were grown on a selective fungal growth medium, Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA), and incubated at 28 °C.

Fungal inoculums were made by picking off spores of A. niger and T. rubrum, and cells of yeast of C. albicans and C. neoformans off fresh cultures. To ensure uniform inoculum density in all groups of the experiment, the spore and cell suspensions were further readjusted to a 1 × 10− 6 CFU/mL McFarland standard [43].

For the antifungal test, 5 mL of Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB) was transferred into sterile test tubes, and 30 µg/mL AgNPs produced using C. songarica extract were added. In control tubes, sterile distilled water (DW) was added to keep the volume constant. A 100 µL aliquot of the fungal suspension was inoculated into each tube. Terbinafine (30 µg/mL) was used as the positive control, and tubes with broth and inoculum were used as the negative control. The test tubes were tilted to develop a slant and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h to encourage aeration and maximum exposure to the surface.

The antifungal activity was determined through the visual comparisons of fungal growth in the experimental and positive and negative tubes. The level of fungal inhibition was determined by the level of growth retardation in the AgNP-treated tubes relative to the controls.

Anticancer activity

The cytotoxic potential of the C. songarica-mediated green-synthesized AgNPs was evaluated using a standard MTT [(3–4,5-dimethylthiazol- 2-yl)- 2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay [51]. A density of 1 × 106 human hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) cells was seeded in 96-well microtiter plates and incubated overnight under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). The cells were then subjected to different doses of AgNPs (10, 20, 50, 100, 150, and 200 µg/mL) for 24 h. A negative control was prepared using untreated cells in full medium, and a positive control of toxicity was prepared using paclitaxel (Taxol) at a concentration of 200 µg/mL.

After treatment, 20 L of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to the wells, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow for the formation of formazan crystals. The supernatant was then aspirated, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance at 540 nm with a reference wavelength of 690 nm was measured using a microplate ELISA reader. Antioxidant activity

DPPH

The antioxidant ability of the AgNPs made with the aid of C. songarica was determined with the help of the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picryl-hydrazyl) free radical scavenging assay. Various AgNPs concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL) were prepared and investigated for their capacity to neutralize DPPH radicals.

One milliliter of 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol was added to each sample, and the reaction volume was brought to 2 mL by adding methanol. The solutions were well mixed and allowed to darken at room temperature for 30 min to enable the NPs to react with the DPPH radicals. The incubation period was followed by the measurement of absorbance of the samples with a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer at 517 nm [52].

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

The Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) was conducted in accordance with the adaptation of the procedure reported by Assad et al. (2024). The reaction medium was prepared by combining 2.5 mL of 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of 1% potassium ferricyanide solution. The reaction mixture was mixed with different concentrations of AgNPs (100–500 µg/mL) and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min to allow reduction.

The incubation was stopped by pouring 2.5 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, and the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the solid contents. The supernatant was obtained, and 2.5 mL of distilled water (DW) and a few drops of 0.1% ferric chloride solution were added. The emergence of the Prussian blue complex indicated that the AgNPs had reduced ferric ions.

The absorbance of the reaction mixture at 700 nm was measured using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer. The antioxidant activity of the AgNPs was measured by relating the absorbance values of the samples to a standard curve plotted using known concentrations of ferrous sulfate [53].

Results and discussion

Optical analysis

It was visually verified that phytogenic synthesis of AgNPs was performed using C. songarica aqueous extract as a catalyst, in which a significant change in the color of the reaction mixture occurred after 10 min of incubation, and the reaction changed its color to deep brown instead of pale yellow (Fig. 1). The color change indicates the excitation of surface plasmon vibrations, which is a typical phenomenon in the reduction of Ag+ ions to metallic Ag0 NPs. The UV–Vis spectrum of the green-synthesized AgNPs exhibits a distinct surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at approximately 447 nm, confirming NPs formation. In contrast, the plant extract shows strong absorption in the UV region (around 300–350 nm) with no characteristic SPR band. This shift and emergence of the SPR peak indicate the reduction of Ag⁺ ions and stabilization of NPs by phytochemicals present in the extract. The broad nature of the AgNP peak suggests a degree of polydispersity in particle size distribution. This characteristic of spherical AgNPs and is in line with previously reported green syntheses with plant polyphenols and flavonoids as reducing agents (e.g. [54, 55]), . The sharp plasmon band indicates that the particles are uniformly formed, and the lack of secondary peaks proves the low level of anisotropy. The high-efficiency bioreduction within a short reaction time is a positive indication that phytochemicals in C. songarica, especially phenolics and flavonoids, are the electron donors and stabilizing ligands in NPs growth and nucleation.

FTIR analysis

The analysis of AgNPs mediated by C. songarica using FTIR would be able to give useful information on the interactions between the plant extract and the synthesized NPs and the functional groups that reduce and stabilize the NPs.

Some characteristic peaks in the FTIR spectrum of the C. songarica extract (in grey) suggest the presence of bioactive compounds, mostly phenolic groups and flavonoids, as shown in Fig. 2. The wide band at 3730 cm− 1 is the O–H stretch that is characteristic of phenolic compounds found in the plant extract. The N–H stretching vibrations at 3452 cm− 1 and 3272 cm− 1 produced peaks, indicating the presence of amines or amino acids in the extract. These reducing groups may have a significant influence on the reduction of silver ions (Ag+) to metallic silver (Ag0).

FTIR peaks of C. songarica and C. songarica-mediated AgNPs

When C. songarica-mediated AgNPs were formed, the FTIR spectrum (red) indicated that the spectrum was changed considerably, with a new peak observed at 491 cm− 1, indicating the presence of an Ag-N bond. The peak indicates the effective formation of NPs and reflects the reaction between silver ions and phytochemicals from the plant extract. The peaks at 2754 cm− 1, 2210 cm− 1, and 2087 cm− 1 imply the presence of carbonyl and phenolic groups that stabilize the NPs. One possible function of these groups is to act as capping agents, preventing NP aggregation and keeping them stable in aqueous solutions. Phenolic and flavonoid functional groups identified by FTIR likely acted as both reducing and capping agents, facilitating electron transfer during Ag⁺ reduction and stabilizing the NPs through surface adsorption.

The change in the C–H stretching peak between the plant extract (3282 cm− 1) and AgNPs spectrum (2754 cm− 1) is another indication of the interaction of the organic compounds present in the plant with the surface of the NPs. The C = O and C = C stretching bands in the AgNPs spectrum also indicate that flavonoids and polyphenols in the extract have a dual role: they not only reduce silver ions to metallic silver but also stabilize the NPs by capping their surface.

FTIR data indicate that AgNPs prepared with C. songarica are stabilized by the phytochemicals in the plant extract, especially the phenolic, flavonoid, and amines. These compounds reduce silver ions, stabilize NPs to prevent aggregation, and increase dispersibility in solution. The antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities of the phytochemicals are likely due to their interaction with the surface of AgNPs.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the AgNPs mediated by C. songarica provided important data on the crystallinity and phase structure of the NPs. The XRD pattern presented in Fig. 3 has sharp peaks at 2θ values of 38.1, 44.3, and 64.5, which are associated with the (111), (200), and (220) planes of the FCC structure of silver (JCPDS 04-0783). These peaks are in line with the crystalline properties of AgNPs, which proves that AgNPs were successfully produced.

XRD Graph of Crataegus songarica-mediated AgNPs

SEM analysis

The values of the diffraction peaks were very high, implying the high crystallinity of the AgNPs, which contributed to their chemical stability and biological activity at various magnifications (Fig. 4A–C). The SEM images revealed that the NPs were predominantly spherical to nearly spherical and uniformly distributed on the surface, although some minor clumps could occasionally be observed, probably as a result of the drying and coating procedures in the preparation of the sample. When the magnification was further increased (60,000×), the NPs were well resolved with smooth surfaces, indicating that the phytochemicals present in the C. songarica extract successfully coated and stabilized them. The graph of the size distribution of the particles (Fig. 4D) revealed that the average size of the NPs was 20 to 60 nm, which is consistent with the general properties of the green-synthesized AgNPs. Other AgNPs prepared using Crataegus species and polyphenols have exhibited similar size distributions (Rahman et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023), which, together with the previous argument, suggests that phenolic hydroxyl and carbonyl functional groups contribute significantly to nucleation and growth control. The absence of bulky, irregular clumps also indicates that the biomolecules of vegetable origin were effective stabilizers and assisted in preserving consistency throughout the synthesis.

EDX analysis

The elemental composition of the biosynthesized AgNPs was verified using energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy (Fig. 5). A sharp, clear silver (Ag) signal at approximately 3 keV validated that the sample was largely composed of metallic silver. The weight-based (atomic composition-based) analysis revealed that the proportion of silver constituted 38.51 (26.33), which means the successful production of silver ions (Ag+) to elemental silver (Ag0). Plant-based compounds in the C. songarica extract contained carbon (C), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P), which were adsorbed onto the NPs surface and served as natural capping and stabilizing agents. Sodium (Na), silicon (Si), and chlorine (Cl) were present in small amounts, probably as part of the plant matrix or residual reagents. Simultaneous presence of organic components (C, N, O) and metallic silver justifies the dual role of the plant compounds in reducing the silver ions and stabilizing the particles, which is a characteristic prerequisite of the green NPs synthesis [56, 57]. This elemental distribution is in accordance with the SEM results, which indicated separate and stabilized NPs, and also supports the UV-Vis results, which indicated the successful synthesis of AgNPs.

Zeta sizer analysis

DLS analysis was employed to evaluate the hydrodynamic size and distribution of the biosynthesized AgNPs (Fig. 6). The mean size of the particles was 54 nm, which is consistent with the one estimated based on SEM images. The single sharp peak in the distribution indicates that the population size of the NPs was largely homogeneous with little polydispersivity. This shows that the C. songarica extract was an efficient part in regulating the formation and growth of the NPs during synthesis. A smaller secondary peak at larger diameters was also observed, which was probably attributed to the occasional aggregation of the NPs, which was a frequent phenomenon in the plant-based synthesis of these NPs owing to the residual plant compounds and the hydrogen bonding between the capped NPs. All in all, the DLS values, combined with the results of SEM and EDX, confirm the effective fabrication of small, plant-stabilized AgNPs with stable dispersion characteristics that may be used in bio-applications [58, 59].

Zeta potential analysis

To assess the surface charge and stability of the colloidal suspension of the biosynthesized AgNPs, Zeta potential analysis was performed (Fig. 7). The zeta potential of the NPs was − 39 mV, indicating a very stable colloidal system owing to the strong repulsion of similarly charged particles by repulsive electrostatic forces. Such a negative surface charge can be associated with the interactions of the negatively charged plant-based compounds, such as phenolic acids, flavonoids, and carboxylate-containing molecules of the C. songarica extract, which adsorb on the surfaces of the NPs during the synthesis and stabilization process. Overall, zeta potentials of more than ± 30 mV are considered to be good colloidal stability indicators since they are used to stop aggregation of particles, which otherwise causes repulsion [60]. The obtained value proves that the biomolecules of the plant extract not only played the role of reducing agents but also acted as effective capping materials, which facilitated the preservation of the size and dispersion of the NPs. These findings are in good agreement with the DLS results and contribute to the effective green production of stable, bioactive AgNPs.

The AgNPs showed notably strong antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, which is in line with earlier studies suggesting that AgNPs tend to be more effective against Gram-positive strains due to their more penetrable cell wall structure. The phytochemicals in the C. songarica extract, especially flavonoids and phenolic acids, may also contribute to this enhanced antibacterial effect, possibly by stabilizing the NPs and working in synergy to boost antimicrobial action.

The main mechanisms of the antibacterial effect of AgNPs include several synergistic modes of action such as membrane damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, and release of Ag⁺ ions [61]. These ions bind to thiol groups in proteins and DNA, causing enzyme dysfunction and DNA replication loss. Improved activity in Gram-positive bacteria might be due to an altered cell wall structure, allowing better NPs access [17, 62].

The broth dilution technique was used to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of AgNPs. The MICs of S. epidermidis, L. monocytogenes, and E. coli were 30 µg/mL, 30 µg/mL, and 40 µg/mL, respectively, meaning that S. epidermidis and L. monocytogenes are more susceptible to the NPs as compared to E. coli. These results agree with the zone of inhibition (ZOI) results, in which Gram-positive bacteria had bigger zones than the Gram-negative E. coli. Such differences in MIC values can probably be explained by the structural disparities between the two types of bacteria, in which gram-positive bacteria possess a thicker peptidoglycan layer that NPs can easily permeate, whereas the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria acts as a barrier, preventing the entry of NPs [63].

AgNPs have a complicated antibacterial mechanism and act through multiple pathways. They have a small size and large surface area, which allows them to interact directly with bacterial cell membranes, damaging the membrane and releasing internal cellular components. Moreover, the silver ions emitted by the NPs can attach to bacterial proteins and DNA, interfering with major metabolic processes and preventing replication [64]. Another way in which AgNPs can cause oxidative stress is through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to additional cell damage [65]. The experimental findings prove that the biosynthesized AgNPs have broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, particularly against gram-positive bacteria such as S. epidermidis and L. monocytogenes. The green synthesis protocols with C. songarica extract are environmentally friendly, efficient, and produce stable AgNPs with significant antimicrobial effects. These MIC values also indicate their efficacy at relatively low doses, which is why they could be used in the fight against bacterial infections. The action mechanisms of NPs, such as membrane disruption, ROS generation, and metabolic interference, make them potential agents for pharmaceutical and biomedical applications.

Antifungal activity

The antifungal properties of the synthesized AgNPs were tested against four medically important fungal strains: Candida albicans (ATCC 10231), Aspergillus niger (ATCC 16404), Cryptococcus neoformans (ATCC 208821), and Trichophyton rubrum (ATCC 28188) as shown in Table 3. The highest inhibition was observed against Cryptococcus neoformans (91.0 ± 1.0 mm), followed by Aspergillus niger (89.0 ± 1.0 mm), Candida albicans (88.0 ± 1.0 mm), and trichophyton rubrum (86.0 ± 1.0 mm). AgNPs also demonstrated broad-spectrum antifungal activity, with inhibition zones ranging from 81.0 to 86.0 mm as shown in Fig. 10. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences between the treatments for all strains (p < 0.05). For example, in T. rubrum, a significant difference was observed between Terbinafine and AgNPs (F₁,₄ = 24.0, p = 0.0080), with terbinafine showing superior inhibition. Homogeneity of variance was confirmed by Bartlett’s test, and Tukey’s HSD test validated the pairwise differences.

The increased antifungal effects of AgNPs can be explained by the fact that they are small in size, and have a large surface area-to-volume ratio, which enhances close contact with fungal cell membranes [66]. AgNPs likely interfere with the cell walls and cell membranes of fungi and liberate silver ions, leading to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS cause oxidative stress, which degrades proteins, lipids, and DNA, eventually causing cell death [67]. The C. songarica extract was necessary for the synthesis of AgNPs; it converted silver ions into metallic NPs and stabilized them. The antifungal effect was probably improved by the presence of polyphenols and flavonoids in the extract that reacted with the NPs and increased antimicrobial potential [68].

The green-synthesized AgNPs obtained using C. songarica extract exhibited excellent antifungal activity and are a good candidate for antifungal treatment. These findings indicate that AgNPs are an effective alternative to traditional antifungal drugs, as fungal resistance to existing drugs is becoming increasingly problematic.

Anticancer activity

The cytotoxicity of AgNPs prepared with C. songarica extract was determined using the HepG2 liver cancer cell line and the MTT assay, which is a method used to determine cell viability through mitochondrial activity. The MTT assays were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and the DMEM and the Penicillin-Streptomycin solutions (100X) were obtained from Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). The viability of HepG2 cells was evaluated by exposing them to different concentrations of AgNPs (10, 20, 50, 100, 150, and 200 µg/mL) and measuring the absorbance at 540 nm. The results are presented in Table 4. The viability of the cells decreased in a concentration-dependent manner, as indicated in Table 4. At the maximum concentration (200 µg/mL), the viability decreased to 42.16%, indicating strong cytotoxic activity. Reduced concentrations also resulted in a progressive decrease in viability, which was 77.35% at 10 µg/mL, 69.40% at 20 µg/mL, 62.17% at 50 µg/mL, and 47.95% at 150 µg/mL. These findings indicate that AgNPs produced using C. songarica have strong cytotoxic activities on HepG2 cells, with the maximum level of toxicity observed at 200 µg/mL. The IC50 value of Cs-AgNPs against HepG2 cells was calculated from the concentration-dependent cell viability data. Based on interpolation of the dose–response curve, the IC50 value was approximately 135.8 µg/mL, indicating that Cs-AgNPs exhibited notable cytotoxic potential against HepG2 liver cancer cells. This confirms the dose-dependent anticancer activity of the synthesized NPs. The cytotoxicity pattern was dose-responsive, indicating that these AgNPs have potential as anticancer agents. Their high cytotoxic properties and potential as anticancer agents are due to their ability to decrease cell viability by over 50% at 200 µg/mL.

There are probably multifaceted cytotoxic mechanisms involved. AgNPs produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which cause oxidative stress and are harmful to important cellular components, such as membranes, proteins, and DNA. Their small size and high surface area promote cell membrane interactions, which causes cellular entry and the release of silver ions (Ag+). These ions disrupt metabolism and initiate apoptosis [69]. AgNPs demonstrated significant cytotoxic properties even at lower concentrations (10–100 µg/mL), and hence, therapeutic effects when administered at moderate levels. This is in line with other investigations conducted on plant-derived AgNPs, which, in most cases, exhibit selective killing of cancerous cells but not normal cells. The phytochemicals present in the C. songarica extract may have contributed to the increased stability, bioavailability, and targeting of NPs to cancer cells. In summary, AgNPs produced with the aid of C. songarica have significant potential in cancer therapy. Their cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells, together with the possible selectivity and low toxicity in normal cells, has placed them as a possible alternative to standard chemotherapy. More studies however on mechanisms, in vivo effectiveness and safety are needed to bring about the full potential of therapies [69].

Antioxidant activity

DPPH assay

To measure the capability of AgNPs to neutralize DPPH radicals, a DPPH free radical scavenging assay was performed. The antioxidant activity was determined at 50, 100, 200, 250, and 300 µg/mL. Figure 11A demonstrates that the antioxidant activity improved with an increase in AgNP concentration. The NPs at 50 µg/mL suppressed 22.2% of DPPH radicals, which increased to 63.83% at 300 µg/mL. As a comparison, ascorbic acid, a well-known antioxidant, was inhibited by 31.34% at 50 µg/mL and 69.02 at 300 µg/mL. According to the DPPH outcomes, AgNPs prepared using C. songarica extract have a significant radical scavenging capacity with an increasing antioxidant activity dependency on the dose. AgNPs obtained 63.83% inhibition, which is very similar to that of ascorbic acid (69.02%), at the highest concentration (300 µg/mL). The IC50 value for DPPH radical scavenging activity of Cs-AgNPs was estimated to be approximately 216.9 µg/mL, based on the dose-dependent inhibition response. This value indicates that the synthesized AgNPs possess considerable free radical scavenging potential. Although slightly weaker than ascorbic acid, the high free radical scavenging effect indicates that these NPs would be useful in reducing oxidative stress. In particular, in the states associated with oxidative damage (inflammation, cancer, aging, and others), antioxidants preserve cells by protecting them against harm [70]. The apparent enhancement of the antioxidant activity with an increase in concentration also indicates that the small size and surface characteristics of the AgNPs enhance their interaction with free radicals, as reported for other plant-based AgNPs [71, 72]. Thus, these NPs can be considered promising targets of the future researches on the use of therapeutic antioxidants.

FRAP assay

The antioxidant capacity of AgNPs was also determined using the FRAP assay, in which AgNPs were tested at concentrations between 50 and 300 µg/mL. Figure 11B indicates that AgNPs exert a dose-dependent effect on the reduction of ferric ions. The AgNPs at 50 µg/mL had an inhibition of 32.96, and the increase was 62.59 at 300 µg/mL. This was similar to the case of ascorbic acid, with 43.33% inhibition at 50 µg/mL and 64.07% inhibition at 300 µg/mL. The FRAP test confirmed that AgNPs are effective antioxidants and can reduce ferric ions (Fe3+ to Fe2+). AgNPs were able to inhibit the activity of 62.59% at the highest level of 300 µg/mL, which is a bit lower than the performance of ascorbic acid (64.07%). For the FRAP assay, the concentration required to achieve 50% reducing activity was estimated as approximately 193.8 µg/mL. This result further supports the antioxidant potential of Cs-AgNPs through ferric ion reducing capacity. This finding underscores the diminishing effect of AgNPs, which is a major measure of antioxidant power, because the AgNPs can contemplate electrons to ferric ions and, therefore, transform them into their ferrous form. The observed comparable activity between AgNPs and ascorbic acid also indicates that AgNPs could possess comparable antioxidant activity to that of ascorbic acid, which is widely used in many pharmaceutical and health-related products because of its redox properties [73]. This is also supported by the FRAP results, which suggest that the size and surface chemistry of the AgNPs, which are determined by the phytochemicals present in the C. songarica extract, play a significant role in the antioxidant activity of the AgNPs. These results have been reported by other researchers who found AgNPs mediated by plants with similar antioxidant properties as synthetic antioxidants. Thus, AgNPs mediated by C. songarica have become a viable substitute of conventional antioxidants, and they can find their applications in the preservation of food, skincare, and pharmaceutical preparations.

Conclusion

This study establishes the aqueous extract of Crataegus songarica as an effective system for the green synthesis of AgNPs. The synthesized AgNPs were fully characterized with respect to size, shape, crystalline structure, and surface characteristics. These NPs exhibited strong antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, and antioxidant activities, as well as dose-dependent cytotoxicity, indicating their potential as multipurpose biomedical agents. The green synthesis procedure that builds on the available phytochemicals found in C. songarica has provided a sustainable and environmentally friendly process for producing NPs and boosted the bioactivity of the produced NPs. The data indicate that C. songarica-based AgNPs can be a feasible alternative to conventional antimicrobial and anticancer therapy. However, these findings are limited to in vitro conditions, and the absence of studies on normal cell lines restricts conclusions regarding biocompatibility and selectivity. Hence, further research on the mechanism of action, in vivo studies, and toxicity analysis is warranted to support their biomedical applications. This study suggests that C. songarica is a potential bioresource for the green preparation of NPs and provides a basis for future multidisciplinary studies.
