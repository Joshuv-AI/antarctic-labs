No measurement induced phase transition in the entanglement dynamics of monitored non-interacting one-dimensional fermions in a disordered or quasiperiodic potential
Can Yin (殷灿) ∗, Bo Fan (范波) ∗, Antonio M. García-García

Abstract
We show that the entanglement entropy (EE) of one-dimensional (1d) non-interacting fermions with U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry in the presence of a disordered or quasi-periodic potential in which the occupation number is being monitored by homodyne or projective protocols is always in an area-law phase so no measurement induced phase transition (MIPT) occurs. The reason for the previously claimed MIPT in these systems was a finite size effect related to the fact that the maximum lattice size L ∼ 500 L\sim 500 was of the order of the correlation length. By increasing the system size up to L ≤ 18000 L\leq 18000 , employing Graphics Processing Unit (GPU), and performing a careful finite size scaling analysis, we find that the critical monitoring strength is consistent with zero so no MIPT occurs. For the disordered case, these numerical results are fully supported by an analytical calculation based on mapping the problem onto a nonlinear sigma model (NLSM) that confirms the absence of the MIPT for any monitoring or disorder strength. The effect of disorder is captured by a change of symmetry, from BDI to AIII, which results in an enhanced correlation length in the weak disorder limit and, by an effective monitoring strength that increase linearly with disorder.

Disorder has a profound impact on the dynamics of quantum mechanical systems. Quantum coherence effects in one (1d) and two dimensions (2d) induce Anderson localization
Anderson 1958
;
Abrahams et al. 1979
for any strength of the disordered potential. In this insulating phase, eigenstates are exponentially localized and spectral correlations are described by Poisson statistics.
In three and higher dimensions an Anderson metal-insulator transition
Schreiber and Grussbach 1991
;
Rodriguez et al. 2011a
;
García-García and Cuevas 2007
;
Rodriguez et al. 2011b
;
García-García 2008
occurs at a finite disorder strength. The metallic phase is characterized by states delocalized in space and level statistics described by random matrix theory.
Similarly, a metal-insulator transition takes place
Harper 1955
;
Aubry and André 1980
;
Kohmoto et al. 1983
in a 1d system in the presence of a quasiperiodic potential. Disorder has also a strong impact on the entanglement properties. For instance, the entanglement entropy (EE) of the ground state of 1d non-interacting fermions verifies an area law if disorder is present while it scales logarithmically
Burmistrov et al. 2017
;
Gioev and Klich 2006
with the system size in the clean limit.

In recent years, motivated by both

;

Although initially research was focused on single particle and systems with few levels, there is growing interest in the effect of monitoring in the more realistic case of many-body quantum systems which brings new challenges and opportunities. One of the most interesting, and more intensively investigated problems, is that of the entanglement dynamics and, more specifically, the conditions for the existence of measurement induced phase transitions

;
Koh et al. 2023

A natural question to ask is whether the presence of a disordered or quasiperiodic potential, which as mentioned earlier, dramatically changes the dynamics in the non-monitoring limit, has a strong effect in the entanglement dynamics of the monitored system. We note that quantum coherence effects leading to Anderson localization are quite fragile so they are likely destroyed or heavily suppressed in the presence of monitoring.
This question was recently addressed in the case of 1d free fermions in a disordered
Szyniszewski et al. 2023
and a quasiperiodic
Matsubara et al. 2025
potential. In both cases, it was found that a MIPT occur at a finite strength of the potential. This is somehow surprising since, as mentioned earlier, no MIPT occurs in the clean case for any finite monitoring strength. One would expect that, as in the disordered no-monitoring case
Gioev and Klich 2006
;
Burmistrov et al. 2017
, disorder reduces, not increases, the scaling of the EE with system size.
In this paper, we show that indeed, the presence of a quasiperiodic potential or a random potential does not change the scaling of the EE with respect to the clean case. Therefore, for any finite monitoring or disorder strength, the scaling of the EE with system size, for sufficiently long times, is area law so there is no MIPT. The transition claimed in Refs.
Szyniszewski et al. 2023
;
Matsubara et al. 2025
was a finite size effect related to the fact that the max lattice size was only
L\sim 500
while the correlation length could be much larger in the limit of weak monitoring and disorder strength. As a result, the observed deviations from the area law in this limit were due to the fact that the system size was smaller than the correlation length. In order to gather conclusive evidence of the absence of a MIPT in this setting, we will see it is necessary to reach
L\leq 18000
and perform a careful finite size scaling analysis. For the disordered case, the absence of a MIPT is demonstrated analytically by mapping the problem onto a NLSM and using field theory techniques. We find that due to a change in symmetry with respect to the clean case, the correlation length in the limit of weak disorder is larger. The explicit solution of the renormalization-group equation reveal that, in weak disorder limit, the correlation length increases with the disorder strength. We start with an introduction of the model and the monitoring protocols.

The model.-
We consider the one-dimensional free fermionic chain with on-diagonal potential
V_{i}
,

H=J\sum_{i=1}^{L}[c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}+c_{i+1}^{\dagger}c_{i}]+\sum_{i=1}^{L}V_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i},

(1)

where
c_{i}
and
c_{i}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators for fermions at site
i=1,2,\cdots L
and we set
J=1
. The filling rate is
N/L=1/2
, with
N
the number of fermions, and the initial state is
|\psi\rangle_{0}=|010101\cdots\rangle
with
0
,
1
the site occupation numbers. We consider two potentials
V_{i}
:

(1) Disordered potential:
V_{i}
is the random potential extracted from a box distribution between
[-W,W]
. We impose periodic boundary conditions
c_{i}=c_{i+L},c_{i}^{\dagger}=c_{i+L}^{\dagger}
.

(2) Quasiperiodic potential:
V_{i}=V\cos({2\pi i\over\tau}+\theta)
, where
V
is the strength of the quasiperiodic potential, and we consider the golden mean
\tau=(\sqrt{5}+1)/2
. We fix
\theta=0
for simplicity. Since
\tau
is a irrational value, we choose the system sizes
L
from the Fibonacci number. This allows the quasiperiodic potential to be compatible with the periodic boundary conditions.

We consider projective (PM) and homodyne measurement protocols, the latter usually termed quantum state diffusion (QSD), see Appendix
S1
for further details, for the quasi-periodic and disordered potential respectively. The reason for that choice is to match the protocol employed in previous literature
Matsubara et al. 2025
;
Szyniszewski et al. 2023
. For both protocols, the observable being measured is the occupation number
n_{i}=c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}
.
In the PM protocol, projective measurements of the occupation number of individual sites, chosen randomly, occur at random times whose frequency is governed by a Poisson distribution depending on a parameter
\gamma
. The outcome of this measurement results in the projection of the wavefunction to one eigenstate of
n_{i}
with eigenvalue either
0
or
1
. The probability of each of these two outcomes is given by the Born’s rule,
\langle n_{i}\rangle
and
1-\langle n_{i}\rangle
for the filled and unfilled state at site
i
, respectively, and therefore depends on the state before this measurement event. By contrast, in the QSD protocol, all sites are weakly measured at each time step. The state evolution is governed by a stochastic equation with a Gaussian noise of zero average and variance
\gamma dt
, where
dt
is the time step.

Since the full system is still quadratic even in the presence of monitoring,
the application of the Wick’s theorem enables us to express any observable in terms of the
L\times L
correlation matrix
D
given by
D_{ij}(t)\equiv\langle\psi(t)|c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}|\psi(t)\rangle
, where the explicit expression of the time dependence
|\psi(t)\rangle
for each protocol is in Appendix
S1
.
The EE is defined as
S_{A}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\rho(t)\ln(\rho(t)))
. Until stated otherwise, the
subsystem is
A=\{1,2,\cdots L/2\}
,
\rho(t)=|\psi(t)\rangle\langle\psi(t)|
is the density matrix and the EE is expressed as
S_{A}(t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}\left(\lambda_{i}(t)\ln(\lambda_{i}(t))+(1-\lambda_{i}(t))\ln(1-\lambda_{i}(t))\right)
,
where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
D_{i_{A}j_{A}}
with
i_{A}\in A,j_{A}\in A
.
The scaling with system size of the EE after the saturation time is an indicator of a MIPT.

However, it is more convenient
Klich and Levitov 2009
;
Poboiko et al. 2023
to expand the EE as a series of cumulants of the occupation number. Perturbatively, it was found
Klich and Levitov 2009
;
Poboiko et al. 2023
that it is only necessary to consider the second cumulant proportional to the density-density correlation function
C(x-y,t)
,

S_{A}(t)\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\int_{0}^{L/2}dx\int_{0}^{L/2}dy\,C(x-y,t).

(2)

For computational purposes, we use
C(r,t)=\overline{C(|x-y|=r,t)}
where the overline denotes an average over all pairs of
(x,y)
satisfying
r=|x-y|
, and also over quantum trajectories, namely, different realizations of the
|\psi(t)\rangle
resulting from the monitored dynamics. In a lattice,
C_{ij}(t)
, with
i,j
denoting site indices, is obtained from the correlation matrix
D_{ij}(t)
using Wick’s theorem,

C_{ij}(t)=-D_{ij}(t)D_{ji}(t)\qquad C(r,t)=\overline{C_{ij}(t)},

(3)

where we only consider the case
i\neq j
and
|i-j|=r
. We will focus on long times
t\geq L/2
where the EE has reached its saturation value. We have checked that up to an overall prefactor, Eq. (
2
) is still accurate
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Poboiko et al. 2024
beyond the perturbative regime
\gamma\ll 1
.

Numerical Entanglement dynamics-

Numerically, we compute
C(r,t)
in Eq. (
3
) for
t\geq L/2
, that from now on we denote
C(r)
, so that EE has already reached its saturation value. For the QSD protocol corresponding to the disordered potential, we implement the bulk of our numerical operations on the GPU (device) using C++ and CUDA platforms, reserving the CPU (host) for only lightweight tasks that do not justify additional kernel launches. The PM protocol corresponding to the quasi-periodic potential relies on MATLAB’s GPUArray framework to treat the GPU as an accelerator of the CPU workload, which is similar to CUDA when no fine-grained control over memory allocation or thread organization is needed.

We now proceed with the calculation of the correlation function
C(r)
for the disorder potential, with the mentioned QSD protocol. Specifically, we will focus on the correlation length
l_{\rm cor}
extracted from its expected exponential decay
C(r)\propto\exp(-r/l_{\rm cor})
. Our main goal is to provide sufficient numerical evidence to demonstrate the absence of an MIPT in a disordered quantum monitoring system. Later, this numerical evidence will be supported by an explicit analytical calculation.
As mentioned earlier, it was shown in Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Fava et al. 2024
that, for
V_{i}=0
, the entanglement dynamics after saturation can be mapped to a NLSM in class BDI. The NLSM prediction for the density-density correlation function
C(r)

Poboiko et al. 2025b
;
Fava et al. 2024
is,

C(r,t\to\infty)\sim\left\{\begin{aligned} &\exp(-r/l_{\mathrm{cor}}),&&r\gg l_{\mathrm{cor}},\\
&1/r^{2},&&r\ll l_{\mathrm{cor}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

The asymptotic exponential decay implies that EE is in the area-law phase for any
\gamma>0
. Importantly, the correlation length
Poboiko et al. 2025b
;
Fava et al. 2024
in the clean case,

l_{\rm cor}\sim\frac{1}{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{2\gamma}\right),\text{BDI, No disorder}\\

(5)

that belongs to the BDI
Poboiko et al. 2025b
;
Fava et al. 2024
universality class, increases exponentially with
1/\gamma
. Therefore, for
\gamma\ll 1
, it is challenging to confirm numerically the area-law phase because for weak monitoring
l_{\rm cor}\gg L
, so only the power-law decay, related to a subarea/critical-law phase, is observed.
However, the latter is a finite size artifact. A similar problem occurs in the 2d Anderson model
MacKinnon and Kramer 1983
;
Eilmes et al. 1998
where it is necessary to reach
L^{2}\sim 100\times 100
to show the analogue result that, for any disorder, all eigenstates are exponentially localized.

In the present case, we first extract the correlation length from the exponential decrease of density-density correlation function
C(r)
in Eq.
4
, as shown in the inset of Fig.
1

L\gg l_{\rm cor}
. In the clean case,
l_{\rm cor}
depends exponentially on the inverse monitoring strength

according to Eq.
5

We note that in the presence of disorder, the symmetry is AIII instead of BDI

V_{i}=0
(See Supplemental Materials).
Therefore, due to the disordered potential

in Eq. (

l_{\rm cor}\sim\frac{1}{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{\gamma}\right),\text{AIII}

(6)

so there is a factor two difference in the correlation length exponent with respect to the clean limit Eq. (
5
). We shall see that in the weak disorder limit,
w>0,w\to 0
, this expression is obtained after the mapping of the entanglement dynamics onto a NLSM. Details of the numerical procedure are found in the section

Analytical Entanglement Dynamics.-

Finally, we proceed with the analytical demonstration of the lack of the MIPT in the disordered case and the calculation of the correlation length. The mapping into a NLSM and the resulting one loop renormalization group (RG) analysis are carried out in the End Matter and sections
S3
and
S4
of the Supplemental Material. Here, we only detail the main results.
The one loop RG equation is given by

\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log l}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}+O(1/D),

(8)

where
D
is the diffusive coefficient and
l
is the RG length scale. Since the right hand side is still negative, the EE is in the area law phase for any finite
W
and
\gamma
.
Its solution gives the correlation length as a function of
W
and
\gamma
,

l_{\mathrm{cor}}\sim l_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{\gamma+w}\right)

(9)

where
l_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\gamma+w}
is the initial RG length scale, and
w=W^{2}/3
.
Therefore, disorder modifies the clean correlation length Eq.
5
in two respects:

(1). It changes the symmetry class, from BDI to AIII, so the correlation length, given by Eq. (
6
), for weak disorder is larger than in the clean limit.

(2). It shifts the monitoring strength from
\gamma
to
\gamma+w
which reduces the correlation length.

We note that Eq. (
9
) is in apparent contradiction with the numerical results for weak disorder where, for weak disorder
w/\gamma\lesssim 1
,
l_{\mathrm{cor}}
increases monotonically as
w
increases. However, it explains the flattening and decrease of the correlation length for stronger disorder. As mentioned earlier, we believe that the discrepancy is a consequence of the fact that effectively, the change of symmetry from BDI to AIII is gradual because, for very week disorder, the typical disorder is larger than the correlation length and therefore the system does not feel the effect of disorder.
More explicitly, the RG flow starts from
(l_{0},D(l_{0}))
and effectively stops at
(l_{\mathrm{cor}},D(l_{\mathrm{cor}}))
, where
D(l_{\mathrm{col}})\approx 0
which we used to determine
l_{\rm cor}
. Whether this RG flow will belong to the BDI class (clean) or to the by AIII class (disorder) will depend on the existence of a disorder length scale
l_{\mathrm{w}}
and its relation to
l_{0}
and
l_{\mathrm{c}or}
. At least qualitatively, as a rough approximation to a RG analysis, we could set
l_{\mathrm{w}}\propto 1/W^{2}\propto 1/w
to be the disorder mean free path around the center of the band since we are effectively at infinite temperature.

For
l<l_{\rm cor}<l_{\mathrm{w}}
, the RG flow is governed by the BDI equation whereas for
l<l_{\mathrm{w}}<l_{\mathrm{cor}}
, it crosses over the AIII RG flow Eq. (
8
). As we further increase
W
,
l_{\mathrm{w}}\lesssim l_{0}
for
w/\gamma\sim O(1)
and the symmetry is AIII and Eq. (
9
) is applicable up to subleading disorder corrections.

Conclusions.-

We have studied the entanglement dynamics of monitored 1d non-interacting
U(1)
fermions in the presence of a disordered or a quasiperiodic potential using quantum state diffusion and projective measurement protocols respectively. In both cases, we have shown that the EE after saturation does not scale with the system size for any finite monitoring rate independently on the strength of the disordered or quasiperiodic potential, so no MIPT occurs in the system. Our conclusion is supported by both analytical results based on the mapping of the problem onto a NLSM and large scale (
L\leq 18000
) simulations, more than ten times larger than in previous literature, based on a CUDA/C++/MATLAB implementation of the numerical algorithm for the calculation of the entanglement dynamics combined with a finite size scaling analysis.

In the case of a disordered potential, the absence of the MIPT is also shown by mapping the entanglement dynamics beyond saturation onto a non-linear sigma model and using field theory techniques. Another outcome of this analytical approach, supported by the numerical results, is that, in the limit of weak disorder and weak monitoring
w\ll\gamma\ll 1
, the correlation length increases with disorder and exponentially as the monitoring strength decreases. It is also larger than in the clean limit because the symmetry is different which justifies the need of reaching
L>10000
to demonstrate numerically the lack of a MIPT. In addition, when both disorder and monitoring are weak, while the disorder strength remains comparable to the monitoring rate,
\frac{w}{\gamma}\sim O(1)
, the correlation length decreases as the disorder strength increases.

I
End Matter

In this end matter we address the symmetries of the system, the mapping of the entanglement dynamics to a NLSM and the subsequent RG flow analysis that leads to an analytical expression of the correlation length as a function of disorder and monitoring strength in the weak coupling limit.

Symmetry differences between the disordered, or quasiperiodic, and the clean limit.-
In the limit of no disorder, the clean Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
) with
V_{i}=0
has both time-reversal (TRS) and chiral (CS) symmetry. The addition of a disordered potential
V_{i}
makes it impossible to separate the system into two sublattices so that the CS symmetry is explicitly broken.
In the doubled Keldysh basis, before any averaging, the Lagrangian operator
\hat{L}
takes a chiral form with two off-diagonal blocks, and this chiral symmetry is preserved under averaging over quantum trajectories. In the disordered case, the TRS of the Hamiltonian does not lead to a corresponding symmetry constraint on the Lagrangian
\hat{L}
, so the Lagrangian belongs to the chiral symmetry class AIII. In contrast, in the clean case, the combination of TRS and CS imposes a reality condition on the Lagrangian
\hat{L}
, thereby changing the symmetry class from AIII to BDI.
The explicit form of symmetry operators are discussed in Sec.
S4
of the Supplemental Material.

Mapping of the late-time entanglement dynamics to a NLSM.-

In the Keldysh path-integral formulation, the fermionic fields are defined in a doubled basis that encodes the two branches,
+
and
-
, of the Keldysh contour. It was shown in Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2023
that the entanglement properties can be formulated in an
R
-replicated theory, with the
R\to 1
limit obtained from the cumulant expansion of the particle density in full-counting statistics (FCS)
Klich and Levitov 2009
. Using a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation, the Grassmann action can be mapped to an effective bosonic action whose field variable takes values in
U(R)
and
U(2R)/Sp(2R)
, for the disordered and clean cases, respectively.

In Secs.
S3
and
S4
of the Supplemental Material, we systematically develop the effective Keldysh field theory for the QSD protocol in the presence of disorder. We take the disorder to obey a Gaussian distribution with variance
w
.
We first write down the Keldysh action
S[\Psi,\bar{\Psi}]
, where
\Psi
and
\bar{\Psi}
are Grassmann fermionic fields, for the master equation of the replicated density matrix
\overline{\rho^{\otimes R}}
. Here, in the field-theory formulation, the order of the averages
\overline{(\cdots)}
over disorder and quantum trajectories does not affect the resulting effective action. Although the static disorder is correlated in the temporal direction, its contribution factorizes due to the Gaussian nature of the system, and the disorder and monitoring distributions are independent.
We then map the fermionic action to the bosonic field theory
S[Q]
through a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation. Next, we perform the saddle-point analysis and identify the saddle-manifold
Q\in SU(R)
.

We find the soft-mode fluctuations around the saddle are governed by SU(R) NLSM,

S[U]=\mathrm{Tr}\left(-D\sum_{\mu=\tilde{x},\tilde{t}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U)\right)

(10)

where the field
U=\exp(i\Phi)\in SU(R),i\Phi\in\mathfrak{su}(R)
,
\tilde{t}=v_{*}t,\tilde{x}=x,v_{*}=\sqrt{2}J
are the rescaled coordinate so that the temporal and spatial derivatives carry the same dimension.
D
is the diffusive coefficient
D=\frac{J}{2\sqrt{2}(\gamma+w)}
. Besides the NLSM action in Eq.
10
, an additional mass-like term appears in the
\omega\neq 0
sector. After applying the approximation in which the nonzero slow modes in
(0,\Lambda_{k}]
are discarded, with
\Lambda_{k}
denoting the Wilsonian momentum shell, this term becomes a fast massive contribution that decouples from the soft modes considered here, as detailed in Sec.
S4
.

At the tree level, as in the standard Anderson-localization problem,
D
is marginal at the critical dimension two, so that
\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log l}=0
.
On the one-loop level, as detailed in Sec.
S4
, the RG flow of the diffusion coefficient
D
in Eq. (
8
) has the same exponential form as the clean case, but differs by a factor 2 difference, yields the solution Eq.
9
.

We note that although some of the disorder induced effects enhance entanglement, and therefore increases the correlation length, they cannot drive the system out of the area-law phase. The factor 2 change in Eq.
9
increases the magnitude of the right-hand side implies that the diffusion coefficient
D(l)
reaches the strong-coupling regime,
D(l)\leq 1
but at a larger correlation length
l_{\mathrm{col}}
. To observe a transition, however, one would expect the opposite trend, that the magnitude of the right-hand side should decrease and eventually change sign. The enhancement of monitoring rate
\gamma\to\gamma+w
in the diffusive coefficient
D
put the system more to the area-law phase.
Numerically, the absence of an MIPT has been verified in Figs.
2
and
3
through large-scale simulations, where the extrapolated critical point
\gamma_{c}
remains consistent with zero.
We therefore conclude that the entanglement dynamics of disordered fermions in one-dimension do not undergo a MIPT because for any finite strength of disorder and monitoring, the system is in the area-law phase.

The free Hamiltonian

The imaginary monitoring strength, in the level of Kraus operator, breaks TRS. However, although the on-diagonal monitoring randomness also breaks CS, the non-Hermitian chiral symmetry CS

\displaystyle+G^{A}_{\Lambda}(\omega,k)G^{R}_{\Lambda}(\omega+\Omega,k+q)\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left(P_{-}a^{x}(q,\Omega)P_{+}a^{x}(-q,-\Omega)\right)\Big]=\frac{D_{x}}{4}\mathrm{Tr}\left([\Lambda,a^{x}]^{2}\right)

where by
v_{k+q}\approx v_{q}
and
G^{A/R}_{\Lambda}(\omega+q,k+\Omega)\approx G^{A/R}_{\Lambda}(\omega,k)
,

D_{x}\approx\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}v_{k}^{2}G_{R}(\omega,k)G_{A}(\omega,k)=\int\frac{4J^{2}\cos^{2}kdk}{2\pi}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\left(\omega-\xi_{k}+i\frac{\gamma+w}{2}\right)\left(\omega-\xi_{k}-i\frac{\gamma+w}{2}\right)}=\frac{2J^{2}}{\gamma+w}

We then transform back to the
\pm(\Psi,\Psi^{\dagger})
basis in order to simplify the
a^{x,t}
terms, where
R=e^{i\Phi\sigma^{z}/2}
and
\Lambda=\sigma^{x}\otimes\mathbf{1}
. In this basis,
R=\begin{pmatrix}U^{1/2}&0\\
0&U^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}
, with
U=\exp(i\Phi)\in SU(R)
, so that
a_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}U^{-1/2}\partial_{t}U^{1/2}&0\\
0&U^{1/2}\partial_{t}U^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}
,
\mu\in(x,t)
. It is then straightforward to verify that

[\sigma_{x},a_{\mu}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&U^{\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{\mu}U^{-\frac{1}{2}}-U^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{\mu}U^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
U^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{\mu}U^{\frac{1}{2}}-U^{\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{\mu}U^{-\frac{1}{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}

where
\mu=x,t
. Then
U^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\frac{1}{2}})-U^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{-\frac{1}{2}})=U^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\frac{1}{2}})+(\partial_{\mu}U^{\frac{1}{2}})U^{-\frac{1}{2}}=U^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{\mu}U)U^{-\frac{1}{2}}
so that,

[\sigma_{x},a_{\mu}]=-2\mathrm{Tr}\left(U^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{\mu}U)U^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}=-2\mathrm{Tr}\left(U^{-1}(\partial_{\mu}U)U^{-1}(\partial_{\mu}U)\right)^{2}=2\mathrm{Tr}\left((\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U)\right)

where we have used
\partial_{\mu}(U^{-1})=-U^{-1}(\partial_{\mu}U)U^{-1}
and the unitary condition
U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}
. The second order expansion of spatial derivation follows the previous proof of full manifold. As a result, we recover the standard second-order derivative (space-time) NLSM in
SU(R)
for the
\mathrm{Tr}\log(\cdots)
term,

S_{0}[U]=-\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg(\frac{1}{4(\gamma+w)}(\partial_{t}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{t}U)+\frac{J^{2}}{2(\gamma+w)}(\partial_{x}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{x}U)\Bigg)\qquad U=e^{i\Phi}\in SU(R)

(S32)

where the spatial and temporal coefficients differ by a factor of
2J^{2}
, which originates from

\overline{v^{2}}\equiv\frac{D_{x}}{D_{t}}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}v_{k}^{2}=4J^{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\cos^{2}(k)=2J^{2}

By dimensional analysis,
[D_{t}]=\frac{T}{L}
and
[D_{x}]=\frac{L}{T}
, so it is natural to rescale the temporal and spatial coefficients by a quantity with the dimension of a velocity,
L/T
. We therefore introduce the characteristic velocity scale
v_{*}\equiv\sqrt{\overline{v^{2}}}
to cast the action into a more uniform form,

S_{0}[U]=-\mathrm{Tr}\Bigg(\frac{1}{v_{*}}D(\partial_{t}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{t}U)+v_{*}D(\partial_{x}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{x}U)\Bigg)\qquad D=\frac{J}{2\sqrt{2}(\gamma+w)}

(S33)

where we can define the rescaled coordinates
\tilde{t}=v_{*}t,\tilde{x}=x
to express the action into standard NLSM form
\sum_{\mu=\tilde{x},\tilde{t}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U)
.
For the remaining terms in the action, namely Eqs. (
S23
) and (
S22
), we work in the original
\pm
basis, where
Q=R\Lambda R^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&U\\
U^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}
. One then directly verifies that
\{Q,\sigma^{x}\}=0
, and hence
\mathrm{Tr}(Q^{2})=-\mathrm{Tr}((\sigma^{x}Q)^{2})
. As a result, Eq.
S22
reduces to,

S_{int}^{0}[Q]=-\frac{\gamma+w}{8}\mathrm{Tr}(Q^{0,2})=-\frac{\gamma+w}{4}U^{0}U^{{\dagger},0}

When there is no static disorder projection into zero-frequency modes, the mass term satisfies
UU^{\dagger}=1
by unitarity and therefore does not contribute to the action. In the disorder case, however, there is a sharp distinction between the
\omega=0
and
\omega\neq 0
sectors, and the difference
\Delta S[U]
yields a nontrivial contribution from the nonzero-frequency modes,

\Delta S[U]=-\frac{(\gamma+w)w}{4\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{\perp}U^{\perp,{\dagger}})

(S34)

so that the full action
S[U]=S_{0}[U]+\Delta S[U]
,

S[U]=\mathrm{Tr}\left(-D\sum_{\mu=\tilde{x},\tilde{t}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U)-\mu U^{\perp}U^{\perp,{\dagger}}\right)

(S35)

where
\mu=\frac{(\gamma+w)w}{4\gamma v_{*}}=\frac{(\gamma+w)w}{4\sqrt{2}J\gamma}
, and the extra
v_{*}^{-1}
factor arises from rescaling the time variable
t\to\tilde{t}
so that the temporal and spatial derivatives carry the same scaling dimension. Now we split the fast and slow modes,

U=U_{f}U_{0}\qquad U_{f}=\exp(i\Phi_{f})

where the unitarity is preserved for both slow and fast modes
U_{0}
and
U_{f}
. For
\Delta S[U]
components of non-zero frequency components, we make the following approximation:

U^{\perp}=\hat{P}^{\perp}(U)=\hat{P}^{\perp}(U_{f}U_{0})=\hat{P}^{\perp}\left((1+i\Phi^{f}+O((\Phi^{f})^{2}))U_{0}\right)\approx i\hat{P}^{\perp}(\Phi^{f}U_{0})\approx i\Phi^{f}U_{0}

(S36)

where
\hat{P}^{\perp}
is the projector projecting into the non-zero frequency sector. We neglect higher
O((\Phi^{f})^{2}))
term and the contribution of the momentum
(0,\Lambda/b)
for the slow-field
U_{0}
. The latter approximation is justified because the saddle is chosen in the zero-frequency sector, while the interval
(0,\Lambda/b)
does not belong to the Wilsonian ultraviolet shell. Therefore, this interval carries neither the retained infrared degrees of freedom nor the ultraviolet shell modes integrated out at each RG step; instead, its effect is absorbed into local mass-like renormalizations and does not survive as an explicit running degree of freedom in the RG flow.
As a result,
U^{\perp}U^{\perp,{\dagger}}=\Phi_{f}U_{0}U_{0}^{\dagger}\Phi_{f}^{\dagger}=\Phi_{f}\Phi_{f}^{\dagger}
, which contributes to a massive term in the fast propagator, which should be decoupled and neglected in the following analysis. Finally we are only left of the NLSM term,

S[U]=\mathrm{Tr}\left(-D\sum_{\mu=\tilde{x},\tilde{t}}(\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U)\right)

(S37)

Then we perform the renormalization procedure,

\displaystyle S[U_{f}U_{0}]

\displaystyle=-D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0}^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})\right]-D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{f}^{\dagger})(\partial_{\mu}U_{f})\right]

\displaystyle-D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0}^{\dagger})\,U_{f}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}U_{f})\,U_{0}\right]-D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[U_{0}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}U_{f}^{\dagger})\,U_{f}(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})\right]

By using
U_{0}(\partial_{\mu}U_{0}^{\dagger})=-(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}
,

(\partial_{\mu}U_{f}^{\dagger})U_{f}=-U_{f}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}U_{f})
, the last two fast-slow correlated term merge into
2D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}U_{f}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}U_{f})\right]
. Then we try to integrate out the fast
\Phi_{f}
field, we first perform the expansion of
W_{\mu}\equiv-iU_{f}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\mu}U_{f})
:

W_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}-\frac{i}{2}\left[\Phi_{f},\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}\right]-\frac{1}{6}\left[\Phi_{f},\left[\Phi_{f},\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}\right]\right]+O(\Phi_{f}^{4}).

and we expand the non-zero interaction vertex in
S_{0}
of three leading orders,

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=2iD\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}\,\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}\right],\quad V_{2}=D\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}\,[\Phi_{f},\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}]\right],

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{iD}{3}\sum_{\mu}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\partial_{\mu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}\,[\Phi_{f},[\Phi_{f},\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}]]\right].

where in Wilsonian RG, we consider a thin shell
b=e^{dl}\sim 1+dl
in range
[\Lambda_{k}/b,\Lambda_{k}]
,
\Lambda_{k}
is the momentum scale, so that
\Phi_{f}\sim O(dl)
. The leading order corrections for

\displaystyle e^{S[U]}

\displaystyle=e^{S[U_{0}]}\left\langle e^{(V_{1}+V_{2}+V_{3}+\cdots)}\right\rangle_{f},

(S38)

\displaystyle S[U]

\displaystyle=S[U_{0}]+\langle V_{1}\rangle_{f}+\langle V_{2}\rangle_{f}+\langle V_{3}\rangle_{f}+\cdots-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{1}^{2}\rangle_{f,c}-\langle V_{1}V_{2}\rangle_{f,c}-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f,c}+\cdots.

where the lower index
f
means average over fast modes,
c

For the first linear term
\langle V_{1}\rangle_{f}

\langle V_{2}\rangle_{f}

\langle V_{3}\rangle_{f}

\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f,c}=\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f}

To compute the vertex, we first expand by
\mathfrak{su}(R)

\Phi_{f}=\Phi_{f}^{a}T^{a}
,
a=1,2,\cdots R^{2}-1

T^{a}

[T^{a},T^{b}]=if^{abc}T_{c}\quad\mathrm{Tr}\left(T^{a}T^{b}\right)=\delta^{ab}

where
f^{abc}

\mathfrak{su}(R)

V_{2}

V_{2}=iD\sum_{\mu}f_{abc}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\partial_{\mu}U_{0}U_{0}^{\dagger}T^{c}\Phi_{f}^{a}\partial_{\mu}\Phi_{f}^{b}\right)

so that the vertex,
\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f}

\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f}=-D^{2}\sum_{\mu,\nu}f_{abc}f_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\mathrm{Tr}_{1}\mathrm{Tr}_{2}\left(X_{\mu,1}^{c}X_{\nu,2}^{c^{\prime}}\left\langle\Phi_{1}^{a}\partial_{\mu,1}\Phi_{1}^{b}\,\Phi_{2}^{a^{\prime}}\partial_{\nu,2}\Phi_{2}^{b^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{f}\right)

where
X_{\nu}=(\partial_{\nu}U_{0})U_{0}^{\dagger}

1,2

f_{abc}f_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\delta^{aa^{\prime}}\delta^{bb^{\prime}}=f_{abc}f_{abc^{\prime}}=2R\delta_{cc^{\prime}},\quad f_{abc}f_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\delta^{ab^{\prime}}\delta^{ba^{\prime}}=f_{abc}f_{bac^{\prime}}=-2R\delta_{cc^{\prime}},

\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f}=-2RD^{2}\sum_{\mu,\nu,c}\mathrm{Tr}_{1}\mathrm{Tr}_{2}\Bigg[X_{\mu,1}^{c}X_{\nu,2}^{c}\Big(G_{f}\partial_{\mu,1}\partial_{\nu,2}G_{f}-(\partial_{\mu,1}G_{f})(\partial_{\nu,2}G_{f})\Big)\Bigg]

(S39)

where
G_{f}

To proceed, we define
r=(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})

q=(\omega,q)

r

K_{\mu\nu}(\rho)=G_{f}(\rho)\partial_{\mu,1}\partial_{\nu,2}G_{f}(\rho)-(\partial_{\mu,1}G_{f}(\rho))(\partial_{\nu,2}G_{f}(\rho))

in Eq.
S39

\partial_{\mu,1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{\mu}},\partial_{\nu,2}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{\nu}}

\mathcal{F}[K_{\mu\nu}]=2\int_{\text{shell}}\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}G_{f}(p)^{2}=\delta_{\mu\nu}\int_{\text{shell}}\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}p^{2}G_{f}(p)^{2}

X_{\mu,1}^{c}X_{\nu,2}^{c}

r_{c}=\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{2}

dr_{1}dr_{2}=dr_{c}d\rho

X_{\mu}^{c}\left(r_{c}+\frac{\rho}{2}\right)X_{\nu}^{c}\left(r_{c}-\frac{\rho}{2}\right)

\rho

X_{\mu}^{c}(r_{c})X_{\nu}^{c}(r_{c})+O(\rho\partial X)

\displaystyle\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle_{f}

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{RD^{2}}{2\pi}\sum_{\mu}\int d^{2}r_{c}\mathrm{Tr}\left(X_{\mu}^{c}(r_{c})X_{\mu}^{c}(r_{c})\right)\int_{\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\Lambda}\frac{p^{3}dp}{(Dp^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{R}{2\pi}dl\sum_{\mu}\int d^{2}r_{c}\mathrm{Tr}\left(X_{\mu}^{c}(r_{c})X_{\mu}^{c}(r_{c})\right)

We then explicitly take the summation over
r_{c}

\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda e^{-dl}

\log(\Lambda^{\prime})-\log(\Lambda)=d\log(\Lambda)=-dl

D

D(l+dl)-D(l)=-\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log\Lambda}
,

\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log\Lambda}=\frac{R}{4\pi}+O(1/D).

(S40)

Then we define the RG length scale
\tilde{l}=\Lambda^{-1}
(we have freedom to fix the equality),
d\log(\tilde{l})=-d\log(\Lambda)

\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log\tilde{l}}=-\frac{R}{4\pi}

\tilde{l}_{cor}
as the coefficient
D(l)

O(1)

D(\tilde{l}_{cor})\equiv D_{c}

D_{0}-D_{c}=\frac{R}{4\pi}\int_{\tilde{l}_{0}}^{\tilde{l}_{cor}}\frac{d\tilde{l}}{\tilde{l}}

(S41)

where we evolve from
\tilde{l}_{0}

D(\tilde{l}_{0})\equiv D_{0}=\frac{J}{2\sqrt{2}(\gamma+w)}
. The RG flow is terminated at the crossover scale between the perturbative and nonperturbative regimes. As follows from Eq.
S40
, the running coupling
D(\tilde{l})
decreases with increasing length scale
\tilde{l}
and becomes small near the crossover. In the weak-perturbation regime, the leading dependence of
\tilde{l}
is governed by the bare coupling
D_{0}
, so that, for the present estimate, one may set the crossover value
D_{c}=0
.
To determine the initial length scale
l_{0}
, we note the dressed Green function in Eq.
S29
is formally analogous to a single-particle Green function with an imaginary self-energy. We use this analogy only as a convenient way to identify the microscopic broadening scale
\tau_{0}=\frac{1}{\gamma+w}
and the associated crossover length,

\tilde{l}_{0}=v_{*}\tau_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2}J}{\gamma+w}

where in the present measurement-heated many-body setting, these quantities should not be interpreted literally as the quasiparticle relaxation time and mean free path near a Fermi surface. Instead, they characterize the short-distance and short-time crossover beyond which the coarse-grained diffusive description and the nonlinear sigma model become applicable. We finally take
R\to 1
limit, and the solution of Eq.
S41
leads to,

\tilde{l}_{cor}=\frac{\sqrt{2}J}{\gamma+w}\exp\left(\frac{J\sqrt{2}\pi}{\gamma+w}\right)

(S42)

which is the expected solution of AIII NLSM with the diffusive coefficient proportional to
(\gamma+w)^{-1}
.
