1 Executive Summary
 The purpose of the project was to observe and quantify temporal variation in snow albedo and
snow characteristics across the Arctic coastal landscape as winter transitioned into spring and
snowmelt occurred, both on tundra and sea ice. This
transition is bounded by fully snow-covered landscapes
with broadband albedos of approximately 0.8 and snow-
free landscapes with albedos of 0.15 (tundra or ponded sea
ice). For these landscapes, we monitored the spring surface
characteristics and radiative properties nearly daily at three
locations on or near the Department of Energy ARM North
Slope of Alaska (NSA) User Facility in Utqiagvik, Alaska:
the central NSA Facility (hereafter called ARM), one near
NSA-E12 (BEO), and one on the sea ice of Elson Lagoon
(ICE) during three melt seasons (2019, 2022, and 2024).
The field campaign component of this award was named
SALVO (Snow ALbedo EVOlution). Typical field seasons
began in mid-April and lasted until mid-June. Main
measurements included snow depth (at 1-m intervals),
broadband albedo (at 5-m intervals), spectral albedo (at 5-
m intervals), and multiple digital images. Orthomosaics

Both tundra and sea ice landscapes exhibit considerable multi-scale heterogeneity, characterized
by the wavelength (or correlation distance) of landscape features. For the tundra, this consists of
tussocks (l ~ 1 m), polygons (l ~ 10 m), and rolling beach hills (l ~ 100 m). For the non-
deformed sea ice, 1 to 100-m wavelengths were present due to freeze-up dynamic stresses, while
10-m heterogeneity developed during the melt as ponds formed.

The first SALVO field season (2019) examined how meltwater influences the timing of the spring
albedo transition, either accelerating or delaying it. While the primary predictor of when a patch
of tundra or sea ice became snow-free was pre-melt snow depth, areas where meltwater was
channeled beneath and through the snow often experienced faster melting and earlier snow-free
exposure of tundra or ice. The exceptions were locations where early-spring meltwater seeped into
deep, cold snow at the ground interface, forming superimposed ice. Landscape features, such as
micro- to macro-topography, greatly affected pre-melt snow depth, the formation of superimposed
ice, and how meltwater influenced the rate of albedo change. The effect of meltwater on speeding
up or delaying the albedo transition—through both snow melt and erosion—appears comparable
to the impact of advected and latent heat from bare tundra patches, which has been studied in more
than six other studies. This finding about meltwater is new.
The second and third SALVO field seasons (2022, 2024) focused on directly linking albedo
measurements to specific landscape features. Improved instrumentation and protocols enabled
direct comparisons of broadband albedos with tussock-level landscape features, mapped both
photographically and through probing and excavation. This work clearly demonstrated that albedo
can be accurately modeled as a linear combination of surface features, such as the percentage of
exposed tundra versus snow. The aging of snow, which has been extensively studied, reduced
snow albedo by less than 15%, while rapid spring melt and snow removal reduced it by more than
six times, highlighting the importance of accurately modeling melt rates during the transition.
Later, the focus shifted to identifying the most effective methods for obtaining the pre-melt snow
distribution. While several physically based snow-evolution models are now available, significant
uncertainties in winter precipitation and sublimation regimes make their outputs less reliable.
Conversely, we found that, at least for a large area (>20 km) around the NSA, the distribution of
snow cover is consistent over time. In essence, the detailed distribution pattern of one year
resembles that of the next, simplifying the modeling of albedo transition and enabling more
accurate surface energy budget calculations.

2.2 Meltwater
Not surprisingly, water flowing beneath and through the snow affected melt rates and,
consequently, the timing at which a specific location transitioned from snow-covered to snow-free.
This has been documented in Water Resources Research (Pinzner et al., 2024). We identified seven
snow-water mechanisms that can either increase or decrease melt rates, all primarily controlled by
local topography and substrate type (Figure 3 shows examples). These mechanisms are critical
because the fastest changes in albedo occur during the peak of water-snow melt interactions. Their
influence is as significant, if not more so, than warm air advection from exposed patches near the
snow. Modeling these complex processes remains a challenge.

2.3 Deriving Fractional Snow Cover Maps
Broadband albedo measurements were taken at 5-m intervals along the 200-m SALVO transects
using a Kipp and Zonen (K&Z) albedometer; nadir images were captured at 6-m above each point.
Using aerial and ground-based imagery, digital elevation models (DEMs) and orthomosaic maps
with 5-cm (or better) resolution were produced for selected locations. Together, these products
were used to develop time series of the snow-covered fraction (SCF), which is closely related to
local-to-regional albedo. Binary images (snow-on/snow-off) were generated from the high-
resolution images. Albedo values were assigned based on surface type (ice, tundra, wet tundra,
new snow, old snow) to compute broadband albedo from landscape features. As a check on this
photogrammetric albedo modeling, nadir images were acquired at 6-m above each location where
broadband albedo had been measured using a Kipp and Zonen (K&Z) albedometer. The center
point of the K&Z measurement was aligned with the image, to which pure (tundra, new snow, old
snow, sea ice, etc.) was assigned, and the computed albedo was compared to the measurement
value (Figure 4). This was done using nearly 1000 nadir images, and the results clearly show that
albedo is a linear function of the surface material beneath the instrument, providing confidence in
the larger-scale albedo computations.

2.4 Spectral Albedos

We collected hundreds of spectral albedos, their corresponding surface conditions and types, and
nadir images. We also conducted a series of field sensitivity tests of the standard measurement
protocols for the use of a spectral albedometer. These showed several areas where great care is
required when collecting spectral field data of this sort. First, because the instrument's collection
head must be held steady upward, rotated, and then downward to measure the downwelling-to-
upwelling energy ratio, it is nearly impossible to obtain reliable measurements under intermittent
cloud cover. Second, the clothing of the operator (color, etc.) matters as it reflects off the snow
under the instrument head. Finally, settling time (e.g., the time required for the collector to stabilize
after the initial photon spike) also matters. A review of past efforts to collect spectral albedo over
snow and sea ice (Light et al., 2021) showed that spectral signals across many types of ice and
snow were similar, with only pond ice varying substantially, and that all this variation occurred in
the visible spectrum.

2.5 Snow Patterns and Climatic Convergence
Using three years of snow-depth mapping from the SALVO project and augmenting it with records
from the NSF SnowNet and CALM projects, we examined similarities and differences in snow
distribution and melt patterns over nearly 20 years. These patterns, if they can be shown to repeat,
are the secret to modeling the complexity of the spring albedo transition. If the patterns are
invariant, or nearly so, from year to year, then the melt sequence and the transition from high to
low albedo as the snow disappears can be predicted from simple 1D melt-surface energy
computations. The results to date indicate very high repeatability, at least in the SALVO field area.
This arises from the fact that wind (always present in winter), not the snowfall, is the prime agent
of the snow distribution. A manuscript is in preparation to present these findings to the community.
