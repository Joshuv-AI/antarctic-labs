Periosteum-enhanced triple-pass Krackow stitch provides higher ultimate load than conventional Krackow methods in a bovine patellar tendon model: a controlled biomechanical study
Authors: Hamit Çağlayan Kahraman, Mehmet Demirel, Cem Yıldırım, Reşit Karaköse, Sude Gücük, Süreyya Ergün Bozdağ, Yavuz Kocabey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn all-inside anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction, grafts with free tendon ends require bilateral suturing to suspensory fixation devices during preparation. Despite prior descriptions of periosteal augmentation techniques and biomechanical analyses of different suture configurations, no study has specifically examined the biomechanical properties of a periosteum-augmented graft sutured with the Krackow technique. To address the uncertainty regarding the biomechanical superiority of suture configurations, three graft preparation techniques were biomechanically compared.MethodsBovine patellar tendons were mounted with the patellar end secured in the upper grip and the free tibial end fixed in the lower grip after preparation with one of three suture configurations: a periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow stitch (Group 1), a standard triple-pass Krackow stitch (Group 2), or a quintuple-pass Krackow stitch (Group 3). Each configuration comprised 10 specimens. All constructs were subjected to a cyclic loading protocol followed by monotonic axial load-to-failure testing, consisting of a 5 N preload, 500 loading cycles between 50 and 200 N at 1 Hz, and final load-to-failure testing performed at a constant failure displacement rate of 20 mm/min. Outcome variables were first cycle elongation, last cycle elongation, cyclic elongation, failure displacement, ultimate load to failure, and stiffness. Groups were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with Dunn’s test and Bonferroni adjustment for post hoc pairwise comparisons; statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.ResultsA significant group effect was found for the ultimate load to failure. Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc analysis indicated that Group 1 was superior to Group 2 (p_adj = 0.004) and Group 3 (p_adj = 0.001), with no difference between Groups 2 and 3 (p_adj = 1.000). No differences were found for first-cycle elongation, last-cycle elongation, cyclic elongation, failure displacement, and stiffness.ConclusionWithin the limits of this time-zero bovine patellar tendon model, periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow suturing provided the most favorable ultimate load profile, whereas increasing the number of Krackow passes alone did not provide a measurable biomechanical advantage.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, ACL reconstruction, All-inside, Biomechanics, Patellar tendon graft

Introduction

Graft fixation strength and minimal elongation under cyclic loading are critical to early stability and long-term success of all-inside anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACL-R) [1, 2]. The choice of suture technique during graft preparation in ACL-R may result in significant biomechanical differences among constructs, although these differences may not necessarily translate into clinically meaningful effects [3, 4]. In all-inside ACL reconstruction, hamstring grafts are typically folded and passed through the suspensory device as a loop, whereas grafts such as the patellar tendon require being sutured to the suspension apparatus at both ends [5, 6]. Locking tendon-grasping stitches such as the Krackow stitch typically show greater ultimate load and less construct elongation than non-locking whipstitch variants in bench testing, though configuration matters [7]. Although Krackow sutures are widely adopted in tendon repair due to their favorable biomechanical properties, including reduced gap formation and better preservation of tendon architecture compared with the whipstitch, the biomechanical impact of technique modifications—such as varying the number of throws or incorporating periosteal augmentation—remains insufficiently defined [8, 9].

Previous research suggests that when suturing a tendon, adding more suture passes may increase strength but potentially increase trauma to the tendon [10]. Recent biomechanical studies using suspensory fixation systems have yielded mixed results regarding the importance of suture configuration [4, 11]. Primary fixation stability is critical to protect an ACL graft during early rehabilitation; inadequate fixation or excessive time-zero elongation predisposes the graft to failure and potential revision. Recently, periosteal wrapping of hamstring autografts has been associated with improved graft maturation and less tunnel widening on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after ACL reconstruction, suggesting potentially favorable biological and mechanical effects [12]. Despite prior descriptions of periosteal augmentation techniques and biomechanical analyses of different suture configurations, no study has specifically examined the biomechanical properties of a periosteum-augmented graft sutured with the Krackow technique [8, 13]. In the present study, healing processes were not evaluated; therefore, any effect of periosteal augmentation on later graft healing or integration can only be considered speculative. Although previous follow-up data have reported promising findings regarding healing [12], such effects were not assessed in the current experimental model. Accordingly, any potential biological contribution should be interpreted with caution and requires direct evaluation in future in vivo studies.

To date, no study has directly compared a periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow stitch with standard multiple-pass Krackow techniques. Although numerous tendon-preparation suture configurations have been described for all-inside ACL-R—and a recent study described a harvesting technique for the periosteum–patellar tendon–bone autograft [14]—the comparative superiority of these techniques remains unclear [15]. Therefore, validated loading protocols comprising cyclic loading and load-to-failure testing were used to biomechanically compare three suture techniques for soft tissue graft preparation, such as the patellar tendon.

We hypothesized that there would be a significant difference in biomechanical performance among the suture techniques, with the periosteum-augmented triple-pass configuration demonstrating superior performance.

Materials and Methods

Based on previous biomechanical studies demonstrating that bovine tendons serve as suitable surrogates for human tendons due to their significant structural and mechanical similarity, all procedures—including graft preparation, suturing, and mechanical testing—were conducted under standardized laboratory conditions utilizing fresh-frozen bovine patellar tendons [16, 17]. The experimental protocol was structured to replicate graft fixation within a simplified simulated suspensory fixation model.

Specimen preparation

Thirty fresh-frozen bovine patellar tendons with attached patellae (age < 6 months) were harvested and stored at − 20 °C. Prior to biomechanical testing, the embedded bones were thawed overnight at room temperature, and the tendon specimens were subsequently prepared two hours before testing. Throughout all handling, preparation, and testing procedures, the samples were continuously kept moist with isotonic saline solution to preserve tissue hydration and mechanical integrity.

The trimmed tendon specimens were standardized to a uniform cross-sectional width of 10 mm and a length of 8 cm, and a 2 cm distal segment was designated as the suture application zone. Following current bone patellar tendon bone graft preparation concepts, patellar tendon specimens were prepared with a standardized width of 10 mm [18]. The specimens were then randomly assigned to three experimental groups (n = 10 per group) using a simple randomization method.

Suturing configurations

In all groups, the Krackow stitch technique was applied using USP No. 2 high-strength braided polyethylene suture (Fiberwire®, Arthrex, Naples, USA). All sutures were performed by a single experienced orthopedic surgeon (H.C.K.) to ensure consistency. The suture was performed along a 2-cm marked segment adjacent to the patellar attachment site of each tendon. Stitch spacing was standardized at 5 mm between passes using a surgical ruler, and uniform suture tension was applied across all constructs.

Group 1 (Periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow stitch): A 2 cm periosteal strip harvested from the patella was circumferentially wrapped around the end of the tendon. Three Krackow passes were placed through both the tendon and the periosteum to enhance suture–tendon integration (Fig. 1).

Biomechanical test set-up

Biomechanical testing

All biomechanical tests were performed using a servohydraulic materials testing machine (MTS 858 Mini Bionix II, MTS Systems Corporation, Eden Prairie, MN, USA) at the Strength of Materials and Biomechanics Laboratory of Istanbul Technical University.

During biomechanical testing, each specimen was mounted with the patellar end secured in the upper grip, whereas the free tendon end detached from the tibial tubercle was fixed in the lower grip. To minimize slack and fixation artifacts, a cyclic preconditioning protocol was applied: 10 loading cycles between 10 and 50 N at a frequency of 1 Hz. This protocol was designed to evaluate submaximal suture stability and to determine whether cyclic loading within this range was sufficient to detect early construct loosening [19]. The ultimate mechanical performance of the graft construct was subsequently assessed using load-to-failure testing [20]. Force data were continuously recorded using a 100 kg-capacity load cell (ESIT SPA, Istanbul, Türkiye; Serial No: 824), with resolution and accuracy defined according to manufacturer specifications (± 0.5% of full scale). Elongation was simultaneously measured via a linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) transducer (MTS, Serial No: 10188729), offering high-resolution displacement measurements (0.01 mm) in accordance with manufacturer-reported accuracy. Raw data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and MATLAB R2018a (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Elongation was defined as the displacement measured by the LVDT at the peak load of each loading cycle, with first-cycle elongation recorded at the peak load of the initial cycle, last-cycle elongation at the peak load of the final cycle, and cyclic elongation calculated as the difference between these two measurements. Statistical analysis

A priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on ultimate load-to-failure as the primary outcome measure. Based on an anticipated large effect size (f = 0.40), with α = 0.05 and 80% power, the required minimum sample size was 10 specimens per group. Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) using non-parametric methods due to non-normal data distribution and small, equal group sizes (n = 10 per group). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared among groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When the omnibus Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant group effect, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. For selected pairwise comparisons, Mann–Whitney U tests were additionally used to confirm between-group differences. Effect sizes for omnibus comparisons were reported as epsilon-squared (ε²), while pairwise effect sizes were calculated using Cliff’s delta (δ). Categorical variables, including rupture location distribution, were analyzed using the chi-square test, with effect size reported as Cramér’s V. Rupture location distribution was additionally evaluated using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test because expected cell counts in the 3 × 3 contingency table were below the thresholds required for reliable asymptotic chi-square inference. Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Ultimate load to failure differed significantly among the three groups (Kruskal–Wallis H(2) = 15.44, p < 0.001, ε² = 0.50). Values are reported as median [IQR]: Group 1, 761 [651–898] N; Group 2, 522 [391–551] N; Group 3, 397 [355–544] N (Fig. 5).

Ultimate load to failure by suture technique. (G1: Group 1, G2: Group 2, G3: Group 3)

Post-hoc Dunn–Bonferroni comparisons demonstrated that the periosteum-augmented configuration achieved significantly higher ultimate load to failure than both the Group 2 (p_adj < 0.004) and Group 3 (p_adj = 0.001), whereas no difference was observed between the two non-augmented Krackow configurations (p_adj = 1.00). Corresponding pairwise effect sizes (Cliff’s δ) indicated large effects for comparisons involving group 1 (δ = 0.88–0.90) and a negligible effect between the group 2 and group 3 (δ = 0.12).

Construct stiffness did not differ significantly among groups (H(2) = 3.31, p = 0.192, ε²=0.05). Median [IQR] stiffness values were 53.5 [47.0–57.0] N/mm for the group 1, 34.9 [31.6–46.0] N/mm for the group 2, and 44.1 [27.7–54.5] N/mm for the group 3.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that the periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow stitches significantly increased ultimate load to failure than the two non-augmented Krackow configurations, whereas no significant differences were found in first-cycle elongation, last-cycle elongation, cyclic elongation, failure displacement, or stiffness. Accordingly, the mechanical advantage observed in the augmented construct appears to be related primarily to load-bearing capacity rather than to elongation-related behavior under cyclic loading.

These findings are broadly consistent with prior biomechanical studies showing that suture configuration can influence graft construct performance, although the magnitude and pattern of these effects vary across models and preparation techniques [7, 21]. Previous reports have demonstrated that locking tendon-grasping constructs such as the Krackow stitch generally provide favorable fixation strength compared with simpler non-locking configurations. In the present study, however, increasing the number of Krackow passes from three to five did not confer a measurable biomechanical advantage, whereas periosteal augmentation combined with a triple-pass configuration resulted in the highest ultimate load to failure. This suggests that construct modification may be more relevant than simply increasing suture passes within the same basic stitch design.

No between-group differences were observed in cyclic elongation, and median values in all groups remained below the commonly cited 3-mm threshold for clinical laxity [22]. Thus, the advantage of the periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow stitch in the present model was related primarily to higher ultimate load to failure rather than to reduced elongation during cyclic loading.

Periosteal augmentation may reduce the risk of distal suture cut-through by increasing the suture–tendon contact area and redistributing local stress concentrations along the graft, thereby altering the failure pattern rather than uniformly reducing all failure modes. In contrast, increasing the number of suture passes alone, as in the quintuple-pass Krackow configuration, appears to redistribute load toward the proximal suture–loop interface, which may explain the higher proportion of upper-end failures observed in this group. These findings suggest that both periosteal augmentation and increased suture passes may influence load transfer characteristics, although the underlying mechanical mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study. The so-called “envelope” or “belt” effect associated with periosteal augmentation seems to preferentially protect the tendon–suture interface from cut-through, whereas additional needle passes may increase local stiffness without providing the same protective distribution of stress and with the biological potential of the periosteum [12, 23]. In this study, healing or biological integration was not assessed. Rather, periosteal augmentation was associated with improved time-zero biomechanical performance, as reflected by the higher ultimate load to failure. Any potential biological contribution of the periosteum should therefore be considered speculative and requires further in vivo investigation.

Previous biomechanical studies on graft failure have shown that the same suturing technique may yield different rupture area patterns when applied to different tendon types [24, 25]. In addition, several studies have compared different suturing configurations within a single graft type [26–28]. This view is further supported by previous evidence indicating that increasing suture complexity or the number of suture strands does not invariably improve biomechanical performance [29]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study to date has investigated the rupture area of varying the number of suture passes within the same suturing technique and tendon type during graft preparation, as examined in the present study. In this context, the observed differences in rupture area distribution may provide additional insight into construct behavior; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Because local stress distribution, contact area, and tissue–suture interaction were not directly measured, any mechanistic explanation remains hypothesis-generating rather than demonstrative.

Alternative graft-preparation stitches, including locking loop constructs, have also been reported as viable options [30]. However, such techniques may require specialized and potentially more costly suture materials.

The present findings should also be interpreted in the context of the simplified simulated suspensory fixation model used in this study. While the steel-pin setup enabled a controlled comparison of the tested suture configurations, it did not replicate important in vivo elements such as adjustable-loop devices, cortical buttons, or graft–bone tunnel interaction. As a result, the observed failure patterns and stiffness values likely reflect the isolated behavior of the tendon–suture construct rather than the full mechanical response of a clinically implanted graft. Accordingly, the findings should be regarded as comparative time-zero biomechanical data rather than a direct representation of in vivo fixation performance.

The clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted within the limits of the present model. This was an ex vivo, time-zero biomechanical study performed in bovine patellar tendons, and the results therefore reflect initial construct behavior rather than biological healing, graft incorporation, or long-term in vivo performance. In addition, ultimate load-to-failure testing evaluates mechanical reserve under supraphysiologic loading conditions and does not directly reproduce the forces encountered during routine postoperative knee function.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the biomechanical analysis was performed using an ex vivo, time-zero testing model, which does not account for biological healing, graft incorporation, or remodeling processes that occur in vivo. Consequently, the results reflect initial mechanical behavior only and cannot be extrapolated to long-term clinical performance. Second, ultimate load-to-failure testing was conducted at load levels that exceed physiological forces encountered during normal knee function; therefore, differences observed in load-to-failure values represent mechanical reserve rather than conditions directly experienced in clinical settings. Third, the sample size was relatively small, although it was determined a priori and is consistent with comparable biomechanical studies; nonetheless, subtle differences between constructs may have been underpowered.

Conclusion

Within the limits of this time-zero bovine patellar tendon model, periosteum-augmented triple-pass Krackow suturing provided the most favorable ultimate load profile, whereas increasing the number of Krackow passes alone did not provide a measurable biomechanical advantage.
