Distant lymph node metastasis in head and neck cancer: incidence, predictors, and survival outcomes in a population-based cohort
Authors: Jianlin Yue, Dayu Liu, Ruijie Sun, Huidong Zhang, Suyuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistant lymph node metastasis (DLM) is rare in head and neck cancer (HNC), occurring in only 0.76% of cases, underscoring the importance of understanding its clinical behavior. However, population-based data on the incidence, predictors, and prognostic implications of DLM relative to other metastatic patterns remain limited. This study aimed to determine the incidence of DLM at diagnosis, identify its independent predictors, and evaluate the impact of DLM and its treatment on survival in a population-based cohort of patients with HNC.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database [2016–2022] and identified 42,908 patients with HNC. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine factors associated with DLM at diagnosis. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Cancer-specific mortality was assessed using a competing-risks analysis, while treatment effects within the DLM-positive cohort were studied using a 3-month landmark analysis to reduce immortal-time bias.ResultsDLM was present at diagnosis in 325 patients (0.76%). Independent prognostic factors were age 60 years or older, hypopharyngeal tumor primary site, advanced tumor (T) and node (N) stage, neuroendocrine carcinoma, and melanoma (all P<0.05). Model discrimination was good [area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) =0.835]. Patients with DLM had significantly shorter median OS than those without distant metastasis (8.0 vs. 78.0 months, P<0.001). Among the DLM-positive population, isolated nodal disease correlated with longer median OS than DLM in the presence of visceral spread (12.0 vs. 6.0 months; P<0.001), and this benefit persisted in a competing-risks analysis for cancer-specific death. In the landmark analysis, combined chemoradiotherapy remained associated with improved survival compared with no active treatment [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) =0.41, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.26–0.63; P<0.001].ConclusionsThese findings suggest that subclassifying metastatic disease could enhance risk stratification and guide more personalized treatment strategies.Keywords: Head and neck, cancer, lymph node, metastasis, oligometastasis

Highlight box

Key findings

• In a population-based cohort of 42,908 patients with head and neck cancer (HNC), distant lymph node metastasis (DLM) was present at diagnosis in 325 patients (0.76%). Independent predictors included age ≥60 years, hypopharyngeal primary site, poor differentiation, advanced tumor (T) and node (N) stage, neuroendocrine carcinoma, and melanoma. DLM was associated with markedly shorter overall survival (OS) than no distant metastasis (8.0 vs. 78.0 months). Among DLM-positive patients, isolated nodal disease carried better OS than DLM with concurrent visceral metastases (12.0 vs. 6.0 months), and combined chemoradiotherapy was associated with improved survival versus no active treatment.

What is known and what is new?

• DLM is recognized as an infrequent but biologically distinct pattern of spread in HNC, separate from visceral metastasis. This study provides population-based estimates of DLM incidence and its independent predictors, and shows that isolated DLM carries a more favorable prognosis than DLM with visceral metastases—indicating that metastatic pattern conveys prognostic information beyond the current binary M1 classification.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support further subclassification of M1 disease and selective treatment intensification in patients with isolated DLM. The results are hypothesis-generating; prospective validation is needed before staging criteria or treatment algorithms are revised.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) remains a major global health problem, with more than 900,000 new cases and over 400,000 deaths reported annually (1). Prognosis is largely determined by the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification, in which distant metastasis defines the most advanced stage of disease (2).

HNC often spreads to nearby lymph nodes, although distant lymph node involvement is less frequent and is more biologically diverse (3,4). Most discussions of metastatic disease focus on hematogenous spread to the lungs, bones, or liver; however, HNC can also disseminate through broader lymphatic basins such as the mediastinal or axillary nodes (5). The current American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system classifies these patterns as M1 disease (6). That approach is useful, but it may obscure clinically important heterogeneity between metastatic subgroups. Multiple studies have indicated that distant lymph node metastasis (DLM) may exhibit distinct behavior compared to visceral metastasis and, in certain contexts, may be more responsive to intensive treatment (7,8). This point is particularly significant in nasopharyngeal cancer because metastatic load and patterns already influence prognostic considerations (9).

Nevertheless, population-based studies specifically addressing DLM remain scarce. The prevalence of DLM at diagnosis, the clinicopathological characteristics associated with its presence, and its prognostic implications relative to other metastatic patterns remain incompletely defined. We therefore used the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to examine three questions: the incidence of DLM at presentation, the independent predictors of DLM, and the impact of DLM and its treatment patterns on survival in a contemporary population-based cohort. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1133/rc).

Methods

Data source and study population

Data were obtained from the SEER 18 registries database for the period 2016–2022 using SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.41). We included patients with malignant tumors of the head and neck identified by ICD-O-3 site codes C00.0–C14.8 and C30.0–C32.9. Cases were excluded if information on stage, histology, grade, primary site, marital status, or distant metastasis status was missing. In total, 100 patients were excluded due to missing data on one or more of these variables (Table S1 for a complete breakdown of exclusion criteria and counts). The final analytic cohort comprised 42,908 patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Complete-case analysis was employed; given that the excluded proportion was small (<0.2% of the total screened population), we consider this unlikely to have meaningfully biased the principal findings, although we acknowledge this as a limitation.

Variable definitions

The primary outcome was DLM at diagnosis. DLM was defined using information collected from the SEER variable “Mets at DX-Distant LN (2016+),” which codes for distant lymph nodes separately from other metastatic sites. Other covariates consisted of age at diagnosis, sex, marital status, primary site, histology and grade, T classification (according to the eighth edition of the AJCC TNM classification), N classification (same edition), tumor number, and initial treatment types (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). Age was categorized as younger than 60 years vs. 60 years or older. Marital status was classified as married, divorced, single (never married), widowed, or separated.

Statistical analysis

The Chi-squared test was used to assess differences in categorical variables between DLM-positive and DLM-negative groups. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of DLM at diagnosis. Variables exhibiting P<0.10 in univariable screening were incorporated into the multivariable model, with results presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess model discrimination, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess calibration.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Kaplan-Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test. The Fine-Gray method was used to assess cancer-specific mortality, treating non-cancer deaths as competing events.

To evaluate treatment associations within the DLM-positive cohort, we applied a 3-month landmark analysis to mitigate immortal time bias. Patients who died or were lost to follow-up before the landmark were excluded; treatment was modeled from that time point onward. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were then fitted, adjusting for age, sex, primary site, T classification, N classification, and treatment modality. All tests were two-sided; P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in SPSS version 27.0 and R version 4.5.2 (cmprsk and survival packages).

Results

Patient characteristics and incidence of DLM

The inclusion criteria identified 42,908 patients diagnosed with HNC. DLM was present at diagnosis in 325 patients, yielding an incidence of 0.76%. Among DLM-positive patients, 171 had isolated DLM (without concurrent visceral metastasis), 154 had DLM in combination with visceral metastases (DLM + other site), and 1,021 patients in the broader M1 group had visceral metastases without DLM (other site metastases only). Baseline clinicodemographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. Compared with patients without DLM, those with DLM were more often male, unmarried, and classified as having advanced T and N disease. DLM was most frequently reported as a site-specific proportion for hypopharyngeal (2.57%) and nasopharyngeal (2.54%) primaries. In terms of histology, DLM was most frequent by proportion in melanoma (9.09%) and neuroendocrine carcinoma (4.49%).

Predictors of DLM at diagnosis

In multivariable logistic regression (Table 2, Figure 1), age ≥60 years was independently associated with DLM (aOR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.14–1.92; P=0.003). Widowed marital status was also independently associated (aOR 1.46, 95% CI: 1.02–2.09; P=0.04). Among primary sites, hypopharyngeal tumors had elevated odds of DLM compared with oral cavity tumors (aOR 2.54, 95% CI: 1.18–5.46; P=0.02). Strong histologic predictors included neuroendocrine carcinoma (aOR 3.46, 95% CI: 1.56–7.67; P=0.002) and melanoma (aOR 20.52, 95% CI: 2.24–187.57; P=0.007). DLM was also independently associated with poor differentiation, T3 and T4 classification, and advanced nodal disease—most strongly with nodal stage (aOR =5.13 for N1, 8.79 for N2, and 8.08 for N3 vs. N0; all P<0.001). The model discriminated well (AUC 0.835, 95% CI: 0.811–0.859) and calibrated acceptably (Hosmer-Lemeshow P=0.88; Figure 2).

Survival analyses

DLM was strongly associated with inferior OS. The median OS was 8.0 months for DLM-positive patients vs. 78.0 months for those without distant metastasis (log-rank P<0.001). The hazard ratio (HR) for mortality in DLM-positive patients was 3.5 (95% CI: 2.8–4.4), indicating a significantly increased risk of death.

Within the DLM-positive cohort, a significant prognostic gradient was observed according to metastatic burden. Patients with isolated DLM (n=171) achieved a median OS of 12.0 months (95% CI: 8.8–15.2), compared with 6.0 months (95% CI: 4.4–7.6) in patients with both DLM and concurrent visceral metastases (n=154; P<0.001). Survival for isolated DLM was numerically higher than for non-nodal M1 disease (other visceral metastases only; 12.0 vs. 10.0 months), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.12) (Figure 3).

In the DLM-positive group, treatment modality was associated with survival outcome. Patients treated with combined chemoradiotherapy achieved the longest median OS at 17 months; those receiving chemotherapy alone had a median OS of 12 months; those receiving radiotherapy alone had a median OS of 7 months; and patients without active treatment exhibited the shortest survival (median OS 3 months). Log-rank testing identified significant disparities between the no-treatment group and the remaining treatment groups; however, pairwise differences among the active treatment groups did not reach statistical significance.

Competing-risks analysis

At 12 months, the cumulative incidence of cancer-specific mortality was 58.2% in patients with isolated DLM and 78.5% in those with DLM plus visceral metastases (P<0.001; Table S2). This confirms that the apparent survival benefit in isolated DLM reflects reduced cancer-specific mortality rather than differences in competing non-cancer deaths.

Treatment modality and landmark analysis

To mitigate immortal time bias in treatment comparisons, a 3-month landmark analysis was conducted. Forty-eight patients were excluded because they died before the landmark, leaving 277 patients for treatment-outcome analyses. In this landmark cohort, the median OS for patients receiving combined chemoradiotherapy was 17.0 months (95% CI: 13.8–20.2). Patients receiving no active treatment had a median OS of 12.0 months (95% CI: 9.1–14.9); those receiving chemotherapy alone had a median OS of 7.0 months (95% CI: 4.9–9.1); and those receiving radiotherapy alone had a median OS of 4.0 months (95% CI: 2.8–5.2). Note that these landmark-adjusted estimates differ from the unadjusted estimates reported in the preceding survival analysis section, reflecting the effect of excluding early deaths occurring within the first 3 months and the restriction of follow-up to landmark survivors—a methodological feature inherent to the landmark design. In a multivariable Cox analysis, combined chemoradiotherapy was independently associated with improved survival compared with no active treatment (HR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.26–0.63; P<0.001).

Discussion

Principal findings and clinical relevance

This population-based study demonstrates that DLM at presentation is rare in HNC, occurring in fewer than 1% of newly diagnosed patients. The central finding is not merely rarity but heterogeneity: patients with isolated DLM exhibited significantly superior outcomes compared to those with concurrent nodal and visceral metastatic disease, a trend that persisted in the competing-risks analysis. These results indicate that DLM should not be considered biologically synonymous with extensive visceral dissemination.

The survival gradient described here is broadly consistent with the original concept of oligometastases postulated by Hellman and Weichselbaum (10) and subsequently updated in contemporary oncologic practice (11,12). Within this framework, limited metastatic burden may represent an intermediate biological state between localized and widely disseminated disease. Our results do not prove that isolated DLM constitutes an oligometastatic state in every case, but they do support the view that metastatic pattern carries prognostic information that is lost when all M1 disease is collapsed into a single category.

Incidence, heterogeneity, and staging implications

The observed incidence of DLM provides population-level support for earlier, smaller reports describing distant nodal spread as a distinct but infrequent event in HNC (3,8). More importantly, the present findings suggest that isolated DLM may deserve separate clinical consideration. The current AJCC system appropriately classifies distant nodal disease as M1, yet the survival differences observed here suggest that further subclassification could improve risk stratification. A more nuanced metastatic taxonomy, similar in spirit to burden-based refinements proposed for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (13), may eventually help identify patients most likely to benefit from aggressive multimodal treatment.

That said, these data alone are insufficient to justify immediate staging revision. The proposal is best viewed as hypothesis-generating. Prospective validation and better characterization of metastatic burden, treatment intent, and disease biology are required before formal subclassification can be recommended.

Biological mechanisms and pathogenetic hypotheses

The strong association between advanced nodal stage and DLM is noteworthy. The magnitude of the effect for N2 and N3 disease suggests that lymphatic tumor burden may be central to the development of distant nodal spread. One plausible explanation is retrograde or aberrant lymphatic dissemination following disruption of normal lymphatic drainage pathways, an idea discussed in prior work on axillary and noncervical nodal spread (14,15). An alternative explanation is hematogenous dissemination followed by colonization of distant lymph nodes. The present study cannot distinguish between these mechanisms, but the data favor a link between extensive regional nodal disease and subsequent distant nodal involvement.

The relatively better outcomes observed in isolated DLM may reflect biological differences beyond anatomy. Tumor-intrinsic molecular features, host immune competence, or both may contribute. The lymph node microenvironment is a highly immunologically active compartment, containing resident dendritic cells, macrophages, and lymphocytes that can mediate both immune surveillance and immune evasion (16). In HNC, the tumor immune microenvironment is recognized as a critical determinant of metastatic dissemination and treatment response. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells, and exhausted CD8+ effector cells collectively promote an immunosuppressive milieu that may facilitate nodal colonization, as reviewed recently by Litsou (17). Furthermore, viral oncogenesis—particularly human papillomavirus (HPV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)—exerts substantial influence on immune escape and the overall metastatic biology of HNC, as comprehensively discussed by Litsou (18). These viral pathways may partly account for the heterogeneous metastatic patterns observed across HNC subsites and the relatively distinct behavior of DLM in virally associated tumors such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma (9). Future studies combining clinical annotation with genomic and immune profiling may address whether isolated DLM represents a biologically distinct metastatic phenotype (19-21).

Therapeutic implications and future directions

The treatment analysis suggests that active multimodal therapy may be associated with longer survival in selected patients with DLM. Even after a landmark design to mitigate immortal time bias, combined chemoradiotherapy remained associated with improved outcomes. This observation must be interpreted cautiously because residual confounding is unavoidable in registry-based comparisons: patients selected for combined treatment are likely to differ from untreated patients in performance status, comorbidity burden, metastatic extent, and clinician-assessed fitness.

Nevertheless, the direction and magnitude of association are clinically meaningful. Rather than advocating a nihilistic approach to all DLM-positive disease, these results support selective intensification of treatment, especially when DLM is isolated. Ablative local therapy for limited disease burden plays an increasingly important role in contemporary metastatic management (11), and systemic therapy for recurrent or metastatic HNC now routinely includes immune checkpoint inhibition (19). Whether these approaches are particularly effective in distant nodal disease remains unknown, but this is a reasonable question for future prospective investigation.

Strengths and limitations

The principal strength of this study is the use of a large, population-based cohort, which improves generalizability and enables investigation of a rare metastatic pattern that is difficult to characterize in single-center series. The statistical framework is a further strength: competing-risks methods provided a more specific assessment of cancer-related mortality, and the landmark analysis reduced one important source of bias in the treatment comparison.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, SEER lacks important biological and clinical variables such as HPV/p16 status, performance status, smoking history, comorbidity measures, and treatment intent. Second, treatment data in SEER are coarse and do not capture systemic regimen details, radiation dose or technique, surgical margins, or use of immunotherapy. Third, the definition of isolated DLM depends on the sensitivity of baseline staging studies, and occult visceral metastases may have led to misclassification. Fourth, as with all retrospective observational analyses, residual confounding cannot be eliminated; the treatment findings should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Fifth, the proposed nomogram was not subjected to internal validation (e.g., bootstrapping or cross-validation) or external validation in an independent cohort; its clinical utility is therefore preliminary and should be interpreted with caution until validated prospectively. As such, the nomogram should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating tool to aid prognostic stratification rather than a decision-making instrument for routine clinical practice.

Conclusions

At diagnosis, distant lymph node involvement in HNC is uncommon but is not clinically homogeneous. Patients with isolated DLM had improved OS compared with those with combined nodal and visceral metastatic disease, indicating that metastatic pattern provides prognostic information beyond binary M1 classification. Advanced regional nodal disease was the strongest predictor of DLM, reinforcing the clinical link between extensive cervical nodal burden and distant nodal spread. Although these findings do not yet warrant immediate staging revision, they support more refined prognostic stratification and justify prospective evaluation of intensified multimodal treatment strategies for carefully selected patients with isolated DLM.
