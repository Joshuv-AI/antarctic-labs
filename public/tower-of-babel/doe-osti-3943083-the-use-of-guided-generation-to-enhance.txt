LDRD PROJECT TITLE: The use of Guided Generation to Enhance Extraction of
Information from Technical Research Papers
PROJECT TEAM MEMBERS: Travis Bauer (PI), Phoebe Yeoh

ABSTRACT:

This project studied whether "guided generation"—forcing a Large Language Model (LLM) to answer
a question using a structured format—helps extract information from technical research literature
better than letting it answer free form. Finding answers in technical literature has historically meant
keyword search plus manual review; LLMs are changing that, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG), which grounds an LLM's answers in retrieved source passages, is an alternative that is explored
in the AI community1. Using a corpus built from the International Symposium for Testing and Failure
Analysis (ISTFA) proceedings2, we generated answers under ten conditions—a no-retrieval baseline,
two synthetic degraded conditions for validating the judge, five RAG retrieval conditions, the guided
generation condition, and “pondering,” a two-turn condition. We tested these with two completion
models (Mistral-Small 24B and GPT-OSS 120B) and two embedding backends (a local Model2Vec
embedder and Google's EmbeddingGemma). All answers (~431 per configuration) were scored 0–10
by a fixed LLM-as-judge, which we first validated: it ranks deliberately degraded answers
monotonically (No-RAG baseline > Mostly Right > False), confirming it scores correctness rather than
fluency. Retrieval reliably improved quality over the no-RAG baseline, with most gains within two to
three documents. Critically, guided generation did not materially help and actively harmed the smaller
model (−1.9 to −2.3 points versus plain RAG), while remaining neutral on the larger model; a gentler
prompting variant was neutral throughout. The cheaper local embedder matched EmbeddingGemma
on median quality from two documents onward. Across the two models and two embedders tested, the
evidence argues for simple, shallow RAG with a capable model rather than elaborate guided
generation or multi-turn response—especially for small, locally deployed models.

body of specialized literature, exemplified by the proceedings of the International Symposium for
Testing and Failure Analysis (ISTFA). For new practitioners (and even experienced ones) who are less

familiar with the literature, it is likely that they will develop ideas that have already been tried. A
common problem is taking advantage of the extensive literature. At the time of this R&D, large
language models had become capable general question-answering engines, and retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) had emerged as a popular technique for this type of use case: paper passages are
embedded, indexed in a vector database, retrieved at query time by semantic similarity, and supplied to
the model as context in response to a particular question. In parallel, the AI community was exploring
different ways of forcing the output of an LLM into specific formats. This had the potential to keep
LLMs focused on the type of information the user wants and to make it less likely that they drift into
irrelevant information. The question we wanted to ask was whether guided generation could improve
the performance of this type of question answering use case.

Technical Hurdles. In the course of conducting this research, a number of technical hurdles had to be
addressed to make RAG practical, both for the users who tested our prototypes and for the research
study itself. Relying on server-based models for embedding documents is time-consuming. Installing
the code necessary to do fully local embeddings can be unreliable. So we also had to explore different
ways to embed documents quickly. We were also surprised at how proficient the models were at
answering questions with no documents retrieved from the literature. We expected that without RAG
retrieval that the models would score poorly because of the technical nature of the material. But
especially the large model had a surprisingly high no-RAG baseline.

Questions addressed. The LDRD's central hypothesis was that guided generation will make it easier
for an LLM to focus on specific aspects of papers, compared to allowing free-form responses. To test it
rigorously we answered three questions: (1) Is the automated LLM-as-judge trustworthy enough to
score thousands of answers? (2) Does retrieval help, and how many retrieval documents are needed?
(3) Does guided-generation improve on plain RAG when the number of retrieved shingles is held
constant? We examined two kinds of guiding: strict guided generation, which forces a structured
response to a question, and pondering, a gentler two-stage analyze-then-answer. We also compared a
fast local static embedder against a stronger neural embedder to quantify the cost/quality trade-off.

Experimental design in brief. The corpus was built from the ISTFA proceedings (2,821 PDFs
ingested). A pre-generated benchmark of question/reference-answer pairs drawn from the same
literature drove the evaluation, yielding roughly 431 scored questions per configuration (429 for
EmbeddingGemma+GPT-OSS and 426 for local-m2v+GPT-OSS, reflecting a small number of
guided-generation output failures). For every question we generated ten answers—a no-retrieval

Baseline; two synthetic degraded conditions (False, an intentionally wrong answer, and Mostly Right,
one correct plus one plausible-but-wrong sentence) used only to validate the judge; five plain-RAG
depths (RAG1, RAG2, RAG3, RAG5, RAG6, retrieving 1–6 documents); and the two guided-
prompting variants (Guided Generation and Pondering, both held to the same five retrieved shingles as
RAG5 so that only the generation strategy varied). We ran all four combinations of two completion
models—Mistral-Small (24B) and GPT-OSS (120B)—and two embedding backends—a lightweight
local Model2Vec static embedder and Google's EmbeddingGemma served via Ollama. A single fixed
judge (GPT-OSS:120B) scored every answer 0–10 against the reference, so all scores are comparable
across models and embedders.

Result 1 — the judge is trustworthy. See Figure 1. Across every embedder/model combination, mean
judge scores fall monotonically across three conditions of deliberately decreasing correctness: No-RAG
baseline (mean 5.10–6.01) > Mostly Right (3.65–3.91) > False (0.39–1.26 out of 10), with
intentionally false answers near the floor. This shows that fluency does not fool the judge; it scores
correctness, which we used as a justification for using it for the rest of the study. It is worth saying that
"LLM-as-judge" is now a commonly accepted method for evaluating the output of systems like these
and is itself being formally studied.3

Result 2 — retrieval helps, shallow depth suffices. Retrieval reliably raised quality above the no-RAG
baseline for every configuration. Most of the gain arrived within the first two to three documents and
plateaued thereafter (differences between five and six documents are not statistically significant in any
configuration). For the cheaper Model2Vec embedder the first document alone was not reliably better
than no retrieval; the significant gain appeared from two documents onward, whereas
EmbeddingGemma already helped at one. See Figure 2. For Mistral-Small with EmbeddingGemma,
the mean rose from 5.11 (no-RAG baseline) to 6.29 at two documents and 6.76 at five; for GPT-OSS it
rose from 6.00 to 6.97 and 7.39. The median rose more sharply than the mean (Mistral-Small 5�8,
GPT-OSS 8�9), indicating that a tail of low-scoring answers depresses the mean while the typical
answer benefits more than the average suggests. Notably, the no-RAG baseline was already high (mean
�5.1 for Mistral-Small, �6.0 for GPT-OSS), reflecting substantial relevant knowledge about failure
analysis in modern models.

Result 3 — a simpler embedder can be good enough, given extra retrieval. EmbeddingGemma led at
every retrieval depth by mean, but by median the two embedders were largely tied from two or three
documents onward (8/10 on Mistral-Small; 9/10 on GPT-OSS from three documents), differing
mainly at a lower number of retrieved shingles. This makes the far less computationally intensive
Model2Vec static embedder a favorable trade-off whenever speed matters. For this study and as we are
deploying the prototype on desktops, this difference was material because it makes it practical to index
a large number of documents on a desktop.

Result 4 — guided generation performance improvement depends on model capacity (the headline
result). See Figure 3. Holding retrieval constant at five documents so that only the prompting and
generation strategy varied, guided generation significantly hurt performance when using the small
model (Mistral-Small 24B): −1.90 ± 0.37 points with Model2Vec and −2.28 ± 0.37 with

EmbeddingGemma (both 95% confidence intervals); in absolute terms its mean fell to 4.06 and 4.48
versus 5.96 and 6.76 for RAG5. On the larger GPT-OSS 120B model the effect reversed and was
small: a modest gain of +0.44 ± 0.26 with Model2Vec (95% CI) and a non-statistically significant
+0.19 ± 0.22 with EmbeddingGemma (CI). It is possible that because the fixed judge is itself GPT-
OSS:120B, it may favor GPT-OSS's own guided-generation output (which posts the highest scores in
the study). However, we would have expected that to have affected the scores generated when testing
LLM-as-judge as well. Also, other published research has shown that even using the same LLM in
judging and performance modes can still leave to performance improvement.4

We believe the reason for the difference is that forcing the output into a specific format constrains the
model, forcing the token generation down pre-determined paths that might not have been generated
otherwise, from which smaller models are not able to recover. Pondering showed no more than a small
effect on any configuration (−0.24 to +0.32 points), statistically distinguishable from zero on two of the
four configurations but minor in size throughout. This was surprising. We’d have expected more
performance improvement when the LLM had an extra turn to consider the retrieved shingles.

Bottom line. We reject the guiding hypothesis for the models and data tested. Guided generation is not
free: it degrades a deployable 24B model by roughly two points and never materially helps. The
evidence argues for simple, shallow RAG with a capable completion model, and it demonstrates that a
useful, fully local RAG system over a technical corpus is both achievable and performant.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:

Overall design and evolution. The project proceeded through in multiple stages: (1) configuring locally
hosted AI model servers or configuring access to an existing one—one serving a completion model for
answering and judging, another serving an embedding model for indexing. For the GPT-OSS model,
an internal OpenAI compatible endpoint was used. The Model2Vec embedder requires no hosting.
Mistral-Small and EmbeddingGemma was hosted on a local Ollama instance. (2) developing an
automatically generated question/answer benchmark from the corpus; (3) applying that benchmark
across an expanding set of answer-generation conditions; and (4) running the full experiment and
analyzing results. The AI analytic pipeline software TalkPipe1 was used for building the pipelines for
the study. Early exploration surfaced a key methodological insight: because general-purpose LLMs
already carry substantial parametric knowledge of topics like IC delayering, general questions can be
answered from parametric memory without engaging the retrieved shingles.

Corpus and benchmark. The retrieval corpus was built from the ISTFA proceedings. In the initial
ingestion, 2,821 PDFs across all available years were parsed into roughly 15,430 text chunks. For the
final study, PDF documents were parsed with TalkPipe's native listFiles + readFile segments (pypdf-
backed), filtered to remove trivial fragments (content length > 50 characters), split into ~500-character
chunks (splitText), and expanded into overlapping three-word shingles (shingleText, shingle size 3,
overlap 1) before embedding. A pre-generated benchmark of question/reference-answer pairs was
created from the same literature by prompting an LLM to produce quiz-style Q&A from passages; after
retaining only questions shorter than 500 characters (i.e., dropping over-long prompts), roughly 431
questions were scored per configuration (429 for EmbeddingGemma+GPT-OSS and 426 for

1 TalkPipe is Sandia open source software and is available at https://github.com/sandialabs/talkpipe

Model2Vec+GPT-OSS, reflecting a small number of guided-generation output failures described
under Data hygiene below).

Embedding backends. Two vector databases were built over identical text. The Model2Vec static
embedder (potion-retrieval-32M) is extremely fast and CPU-friendly. The second uses Google's
EmbeddingGemma served through a local Ollama instance. Both were stored as local LanceDB vector
databases.

Completion models. Answers were generated by two chat models: Mistral-Small (24B) and GPT-OSS
(120B), the latter served through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. GPT-OSS:120B also served as the
fixed judge for all conditions so that scores are comparable across models and embedders.

Scoring. The judge compares each answer to the reference on a 0–10 scale: 10 = equivalent in
meaning; 7–9 = shares key points; 4–6 = same perspective, different points; 1–3 = largely different; 0 =
contradictory. We report the mean with a 95% confidence interval (1.96 × standard error of the mean)
and the median, which is more robust to the heavy low-score tail. Strategy effects are reported relative
to plain RAG at the same retrieval depth. Because this is a repeated-measures design—every condition
is scored on the same set of questions—those differences are computed question by question (paired):
we form each question's strategy-minus-RAG5 difference and report the paired mean with a 95%
confidence interval (1.96 × standard error of the paired differences). The paired test accounts for the
positive correlation between a question's scores across conditions, so its intervals are tighter than—and

preferred to—the difference of independent means. Point estimates are identical under either test; only
the confidence intervals differ. Under the paired analysis the two large negative effects on the small
model remain significant, and two of the small strategy effects that would read as neutral under an
independent-samples test become statistically distinguishable from zero (though minor in size).

Data hygiene. Two forms of cleanup were applied. First, on a small number of questions the Guided
Generation condition produced output that failed to validate against its required {analysis, answer}
schema—in practice, truncated/unterminated JSON consistent with a generation-length or streaming
cutoff rather than a structurally wrong response; the pipeline logged the failure and dropped that
question's entire answer set, which is why the two GPT-OSS runs carry 429 (EmbeddingGemma) and
426 (local-m2v) scored questions rather than 431 (both Mistral-Small runs completed all 431). Second,
the smaller model occasionally "leaked" the intent of the Mostly Right prompt by appending a note
identifying which sentence it had made incorrect (e.g., "(Incorrect: …)"). Such self-labeling hands the
judge an unfair shortcut, so those rows were excluded before analysis: 15 of 431 for local-m2v+Mistral-
Small (3.5%), 20 of 431 for EmbeddingGemma+Mistral-Small (4.6%), 1 of 426 for local-m2v+GPT-
OSS (0.2%), and 0 of 429 for EmbeddingGemma+GPT-OSS (Addendum, "Excluded Artifact
Answers" slide). Third, individual judge calls that returned no parseable score were dropped by the
evaluation pipeline; this is independent of the two mechanisms above and affects single (question ×
condition) cells rather than whole questions. One such drop occurred in this study—the Pondering
condition of EmbeddingGemma+Mistral-Small is computed from 430 rather than 431 answers—so
per-condition sample sizes can differ by one from the question counts above.

Implementation. The entire pipeline was implemented on the open-source talkpipe framework using
its ChatterLang scripting. Separate scripts were writtein for creating the benchmark, creating the
vector databases, generating answers, and evaluating the answers. This design makes the experiment
fully reproducible on new data and is the basis for the software artifacts described below. Full pipeline
diagrams, the per-condition results table, and verbatim example answers appear in the Addendum.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:

The LLM-as-judge is reliable (validation of method). Before drawing any conclusions from thousands
of automated scores, we confirmed the evaluator discriminates quality. Across all embedder/model
combinations, mean judge scores fall monotonically across three conditions of deliberately decreasing
correctness: No-RAG baseline > Mostly Right > False. Intentionally false answers land near the floor
(mean 0.39–1.26 out of 10), and the partially-correct condition sits between the two by mean (�3.7–
3.9); by median it lands closer to the low end in some configurations (medians of 2–5 against no-RAG
baselines of 5–8), as expected when a single wrong sentence is penalized heavily. A worked example
makes this concrete: for a question on context-dependent design rules, the correct no-RAG baseline
scored 9, the fabricated answer scored 0, and a single wrong sentence pulled the "mostly right" answer
to the middle—even though all three were fluent and on topic. Fluency does not fool the judge; it
scores on correctness (Addendum, "LLM-as-Judge Diagnostic" and "Example Diagnostic
Question/Answer" slides). This, along with its general adoption in the AI community, justifies it as the
evaluation methodology for this study.

Retrieval improves answer quality. Plotting quality against the number of retrieved shingles, with the
no-RAG baseline as the zero-document anchor, retrieval helps for every model and embedder. Most
improvement is captured within the first two to three documents; returns then plateau, with no
significant change between five and six documents in any configuration. The depth at which retrieval
first helps is embedder-dependent: with the cheaper local-m2v embedder a single document is not
reliably better than no retrieval (the gain becomes significant only from two documents), whereas
EmbeddingGemma helps significantly at one. The median rises more sharply than the mean—for
Mistral-Small the median jumps from 5 (no-RAG baseline) to 8, and for GPT-OSS from 8 to 9—
indicating that a tail of low-scoring answers depresses the mean and that the typical answer benefits
from retrieval more than the average suggests. A notable and somewhat surprising observation is how
high the no-RAG baseline already is (mean �5.1 for Mistral-Small, �6.0 for GPT-OSS), reflecting
substantial relevant failure analysis knowledge in modern models—consistent with the early-project
discovery that these models can answer many microelectronics questions from memory alone.

Guided prompting depends on model capacity (the headline result). Holding retrieval constant at five
documents so that only the prompting strategy varies, guided generation significantly hurts the small
model (Mistral-Small 24B): −1.90 ± 0.37 points with local-m2v and −2.28 ± 0.37 with
EmbeddingGemma (both 95% CIs exclude zero). On the large model (GPT-OSS 120B) it reverses to a
small effect: a modest gain of +0.44 ± 0.26 with local-m2v (95% CI excludes zero) and a neutral +0.19 ±
0.22 with EmbeddingGemma (CI overlaps zero). Pondering shows at most a small effect on any of the

four configurations (−0.24 to +0.32 points; all paired 95% CIs). In other words, once retrieval is held
constant, neither guided strategy reliably improves answers—and the more elaborate one is actively
counterproductive for the smaller model. This directly answers the LDRD's motivating hypothesis in
the negative for the models and data tested.

A cheaper embedder is often good enough. All four model/embedder combinations were run, so the
embedder comparison is now available on both completion models. On each, EmbeddingGemma leads
at every retrieval depth by mean. By median, however, the two embedders are largely tied from two
documents onward—8/10 on Mistral-Small, and 9/10 on GPT-OSS from five documents—differing
mainly at a single retrieved document. EmbeddingGemma's advantage is thus concentrated in the
average rather than the typical case, so the far cheaper local-m2v static embedder is a favorable trade-off
whenever cost or latency matters and typical-case quality is the target (Addendum, "Embedder
Comparison" slide).

How this fits prior work and how the data were processed. TODO:The findings align with the broader
RAG literature that shallow retrieval often captures most of the achievable gain, and they extend it
with a clear, quantified caution that structured-generation strategies validated on large models can
*backfire* in practice.

Limitations. All four model/embedder combinations were run, so the embedder comparison and the
prompting-strategy effect are each observed on both completion models; the study is nonetheless
confined to two models and two embedders on a single corpus, and it compares embedding-based
retrieval only against no retrieval—no keyword search was run. In the prototype at the time of this
writing, keyword/RAG capabilities are included and an agentic approach is being tested. The
benchmark question/reference-answer pairs were generated by an LLM from the same corpus and
were not fully reviewed by a failure-analysis subject-matter expert, although they were spot checked.
Finally, correctness here is a 0–10 judge score; it does not capture the operational cost of a wrong
answer in failure analysis, where a plausible-but-wrong mechanism could misdirect a destructive,

single-shot diagnostic step. A confident, fluent, hallucinated failure mechanism is a distinct risk from
the gross fabrication the judge was validated against, and is not measured here.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:

Immediate next steps. Three concrete follow-on activities are planned: (1) write a magazine article for
Electronic Device Failure Analysis (EDFA) summarizing the practitioner-facing findings; (2) finish
extending the question-answering software to be agent-based, allowing multi-step retrieval and
reasoning rather than single-shot RAG; and (3) incorporate the testing/benchmarking software as a
capability for and internal LLM/agentic AI evaluation program, so that new document collections and
models can be evaluated with the same rigorous, judge-validated methodology.

Publications and presentations. The work is documented in this SAND report and the accompanying
presentation deck. The planned EDFA magazine article is the primary anticipated publication,
targeting the failure-analysis practitioner community that owns the ISTFA corpus and is the natural
consumer of these results. We will additionally look for opportunities to present the material at
relevant workshops.

Software artifacts and technology delivered. The repository containing the scripts and results from this
study will be kept, preserving a reproducible record of the evaluation. Building on it, a software
prototype will be developed for running the evaluation in a more turnkey fashion on new data. Two
deployable products embody the lessons learned. Talkpipe Vault2 (see Figure 6) is open-source
software used by this project: a locally hosted RAG solution designed to give users maximum control
over data by keeping everything local; it can be configured for multiple LLM endpoints and
embedding models, and by default uses a local completion endpoint, a locally hosted Model2Vec
embedder, and a local LanceDB vector database—precisely the "simple, shallow, local" configuration
the study found most effective. Berth (see Figure 7) addresses deployment: bringing a RAG system to a
user's desktop is hard because of complex, multi-parameter command-line setup, so Berth provides an
"app store" experience for deploying container-based applications directly to desktops. Berth was
originally written to make the Talkpipe Vault prototype easier to deploy for this particular project, but

Potential new R&D deriving from what was learned. The negative result on guided generation
redirects future effort away from elaborate single-shot prompting and toward agentic retrieval, where a
model can iteratively query the corpus. The reusable, judge-validated benchmarking harness lowers
the cost of these follow-on studies substantially.

Impact and path forward for NNSA and DOE. The most actionable finding for the mission is that a
RAG system built from local components—no cloud dependency, data and models on the analyst's
own hardware—can deliver useful, performant answers over a demanding technical corpus, which is
essential where data sensitivity forbids external services or requires their use to be minimized. (In this
study GPT-OSS answering and all judging ran through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the
deployed Talkpipe Vault configuration is fully local, and the fully-local claim applies to that
deployment target rather than to the evaluation harness.) The study further shows that expensive
components are not always necessary: a lightweight local embedder matches a stronger neural one on
typical-case quality, and elaborate prompting is counterproductive on the small models that are
realistically deployable on local hardware.

Intellectual property. The primary IP developed is the talkpipe-based evaluation pipeline, positioned
for internal reuse and external community contribution rather than restrictive protection.

CONCLUSION:

This LDRD set out to test whether guided generation helps a large language model focus on the right
information from technical research literature when answering questions, using the ISTFA
microelectronics failure-analysis corpus as a demanding test bed. The answer, for the models and data
tested, is no. First we validated the LLM-as-judge methodology using deliberately degraded answers,
showing that correctness rather than fluency was judged. We then established three conclusions. First,
plain RAG is the workhorse: retrieval reliably lifts answer quality above a no-RAG baseline for every
model and embedder, and a handful of documents (two to three) captures the bulk of the achievable
gain, with returns plateauing beyond that. (The no-RAG baseline was itself high—these models answer
many microelectronics questions from memory—so the corpus provides less additional information
than we originally assumed). Second, guided generation, which forces structured reasoning before
answering, degrades the smaller deployable model (Mistral-Small 24B) by roughly two points (−1.9 to
−2.3) and never significantly helps; it is not materially helpful on the much larger GPT-OSS 120B
model. The "pondering" strategy is essentially neutral. This capacity dependence is a practical
warning: a technique that looks safe on a large model can backfire on the smaller model one actually
runs in production. Third, embedding choice is a tunable cost lever—a lightweight local Model2Vec
embedder matches a stronger embedder (EmbeddingGemma) on median quality from two documents
onward, so teams can trade a little average quality for large gains in speed, cost, and data locality.
Taken together, the evidence argues for simple, shallow RAG with a capable completion model rather
than elaborate guided-generation prompting, especially when the deployed model is small and must
run locally. Beyond the scientific finding, the project informed deployable tooling that embodies these
lessons—Talkpipe Vault (a locally hosted, configurable RAG solution defaulting to local components)
and Berth (an under-development "app store" for desktop deployment)—plus a reusable, judge-
validated benchmarking harness for Sandia so the evaluation can be repeated on new corpora, models,
or embedders. Next steps include an EDFA magazine article and extension of the Q&A software to an
agent-based architecture. For NNSA and DOE, the work provides a defensible, cost-aware, local-first
RAG architecture and the methodology to keep it validated.

Term Definition
Embedding / Embedder An embedding is a numerical representation of text used to
 compare semantic similarity. An embedder is the model that
 creates those numerical representations.
Guided Generation In this report, a structured generation strategy that requires the
 model to produce output in a specified format, such as separate

International Symposium for The conference whose proceedings form the technical literature

(ISTFA)
Large Language Model (LLM) An AI model trained on large text corpora to generate,

 answers against reference answers, using a defined scoring
