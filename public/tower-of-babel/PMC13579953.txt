Computed tomography findings and severity scores in Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia: a retrospective study of 69 cases with clinical correlation
Authors: Yanyan Li, Hongyi Zhu, Ge Li, Ying Cao, Zijie Zhan, Quan Zhou, Chao Zheng, Fan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlamydia psittaci pneumonia (CPP) is a rare but potentially severe zoonotic disease.This study aimed to characterize the computed tomography(CT)findings of CPP and evaluate the utility of CT severity scores for predicting intensive care unit(ICU) admission.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients diagnosed with CPP between January 2022 and September 2025.Patients were divided into ICU and non-ICU groups based on disease severity and ICU admission.Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and radiological data were collected.Two radiologists independently reviewed CT images to record imaging features and calculate two scores: the chest CT score(CTS, range 0–25 based on lobar involvement)and the chest CT severity score(CTSS, range 0–40 based on 20 lung segments).Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic(ROC)curve analyses were performed to identify predictors of ICU admission.We further compared the predictive performance of CTS with that of the CURB − 65 (confusion, urea, respiratory rate, blood pressure, age ≥ 65 years) score, a widely - utilized clinical severity assessment tool for community - acquired pneumonia. The CURB − 65 score was calculated for each patient upon admission.ResultsA total of 69 patients were included(45 male,24 female; mean age 61.6 ± 12.5years).A history of poultry or bird exposure was reported in 65 patients(94.2%).The ICU group comprised 22 patients(31.9%).Among survivors(n = 65),follow-up showed complete clinical recovery, and repeat CT imaging demonstrated complete resolution or marked improvement of interstitial abnormalities in those with available follow-up scans, supporting the reversible acute nature of these changes.In the ICU group, both the CTS (9.9 ± 6.4 vs. 5.9 ± 3.4, P = 0.011) and CTSS (13.5 ± 9.0 vs. 9.0 ± 4.8, P = 0.035) were significantly elevated. ROC analysis indicated that the area under the curve was 0.710 for CTS (cut - off 7.5; sensitivity 63.6%; specificity 76.6%) and 0.657 for CTSS. The CURB − 65 score yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.633 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.476–0.790, P = 0.076). The DeLong test showed no statistically significant difference between the AUCs of the CTS and CURB − 65 (P = 0.452). Notably, only the CTS reached statistical significance in predicting intensive care unit (ICU) admission, implying that computed tomography (CT) - based severity assessment might offer prognostic information that cannot be captured solely by clinical score.ConclusionThis study provides a detailed imaging characterization of CPP.CT severity scores, particularly the CTS, are independently associated with ICU admission and may serve as adjunctive tools for early risk stratification.The reversible nature of interstitial changes on follow-up supports their acute inflammatory origin.Compared with CURB-65,the CTS offered a numerically higher AUC and provided significant prognostic information where clinical scoring alone did not.

Introduction

Psittacosis is a zoonotic disease caused by Chlamydia psittaci, a Gram- negative intracellular bacterium transmitted primarily through direct contact with poultry or birds or inhalation of respiratory secretions [1]. Clinical manifestations range from mild influenza-like symptoms to fulminant severe pneumonia with multiple organ failure [2]. Human - to - human transmission is rare [3]. During the COVID − 19 pandemic, a family outbreak was reported [4]. Existing data indicate that Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia (CPP) accounts for approximately 1% of community-acquired pneumonia cases and is associated with a high incidence of severe pneumonia, reaching up to 7.5% [5]. Early recognition and prompt intervention are critically important.

With the advancement of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, an increasing number of CPP cases has been documented [6, 7]. A 2022 multicenter study in China revealed that C. psittaci, detected by metagenomic sequencing (mNGS) accounted for 6.8% of pneumonia cases, suggesting a substantial public health concern [8]. Another study reported that severe pneumonia attributable to this pathogen constituted up to 8% of severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) cases [9].Although mNGS and targeted NGS(tNGS)are efficient and accurate, they remain costly and often require invasive sampling, which limits their widespread use [10–12]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need for early identification of CPP using accessible diagnostic tools and accumulated experience.

Radiological technologies, particularly computed tomography (CT), have become valuable for assessing pulmonary infections.CT scoring systems, initially developed for COVID-19,enable semiquantitative assessment of disease extent and have been correlated with clinical severity [13–15]. However, the application of CT scoring in CPP has not been reported.Moreover, given the rarity of CPP, systematic descriptions of its imaging features are scarce.This study therefore had two main objectives: (1)to characterize the CT findings of CPP in a large cohort to enrich knowledge of this rare disease, and(2)to evaluate the association of CT severity score(CTS and CTSS)with ICU admission, thereby providing an objective tool for early risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

We conducted a retrospective review of patients diagnosed with Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia (CPP) at Changde Hospital, Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University between January 2022 and September 2025. Diagnosis was confirmed by next-generation sequencing (NGS; either mNGS or tNGS) performed on respiratory specimens—including bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, sputum, or endotrachea aspirate—which identified C. psittaci as the causative pathogen.Patients were excluded if chest computed tomography(CT)images were unavailable or of insufficient quality.The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. Clinical data encompassing demographic characteristics, presenting symptoms, laboratory results, comorbidities, and outcomes(including intensive care unit [ICU] admission, length of hospital stay, and mortality) were collected from electronic medical record.Follow-up data were obtained for all surviving patients, documenting clinical recovery and available repeat CT imaging.Based on ICU admission during hospitalization, patients were categorized into ICU and non-ICU groups.The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (Approval No.2026-041-01), and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

CT examinations

All patients were examined using a 64 -detector row spiral computed tomography scanner(Siemens Healthineers, Germany). Scanning was performed with subjects in the supine position and the upper limbs elevated above the cranial region.The acquisition extended from the thoracic inlet to a level below the costophrenic angle at the end of deep inspiration.Transverse, sagittal, and coronal images were reconstructed with a slice thickness ranging from 0.625 mm to 1.5 mm. Image display was configured using both mediastinal window settings(width:400 HU; level:30 HU) and lung window settings (width:1500 HU; level:−600 HU).

Image analysis

CT images were independently reviewed by two radiologists (one with > 10 years of experience and one with 3 years of experience) and one pulmonologist (> 10 years of experience). All reviewers were blinded to clinical outcomes. CT scores (CTS and CTSS) were calculated by the two radiologists independently. Disagreements were resolved by consensus among all three reviewers.The following features were recorded: distribution(unilateral/bilateral, single/multiple lobes) and specific signs(ground-glass opacity [GGO]), consolidation, air bronchogram, interstitial lung abnormalities, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, reversed halo sign, tree-in-bud sign, etc.),defined according to the Fleischner Society glossary [16].

Two semiquantitative scoring systems were applied:

Chest CT score (CTS)

Each of the five lobes was scored from 0 to 5 based on the percentage of parenchymal involvement(0:0%;1:<5%;2:5–25%;3:26–50%;4:51–75%;5:>75%). The total CTS ranged from 0 to 25 [17].

Chest CT severity score (CTSS)

Twenty lung segments(the 18 standard segments, with the left upper lobe divided into apical and posterior segments and the left lower lobe divided into anterior medial basal and basal segments)were evaluated.Each segment received 0 points for 0% involvement,1 points for < 50% involvement, and 2 points for ≥ 50% involvement.The total CTSS ranged from 0 to 40 [18].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of ICU admission, adjusting for age, height, and weight.Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis assessed the predictive performance of CT scores and CURB-65.Correlations between CT scores and laboratory parameters were evaluated using Spearman or Pearson methods.The DeLong test was used to compare the areas under the ROC curves(AUCs) of different scoring systems.Statistical significance was defined as a P - value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Amherst, NY, USA).

Results

Clinical characteristics

Among the 74 patients suspected of CPP,5 were excluded because of missing CT images, leaving 69 patients(45 male,24 female; mean age 61.6 ± 12.5 years). A history of poultry or bird exposure was reported in 65 patients (94.2%). The ICU group comprised 22 patients (31.9%). Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the two groups, highlighting variables that differed significantly between ICU and non-ICU patients.The length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the ICU group (11.5 ± 6.8 days vs. 9.0 ± 3.3 days, P = 0.042).Dyspnea, chest tightness and disturbance of consciousness were more frequent in the ICU group(P < 0.05).Respiratory failure, sepsis, metabolic acidosis, anemia and renal insufficiency were significantly more common in the ICU group(P < 0.05). Hypertension was also more prevalent in the ICU group(P = 0.018).Four patients(18.2%) in the ICU group died; there were no deaths in the non-ICU group (P = 0.008).No significant differences were observed in other clinical manifestations(cough, expectoration, headache, dizziness, etc.)or comorbidities between the two groups.

CT imaging features

Unilateral involvement was observed in 45 patients (65.2%),while bilateral involvement was identified in 24 patients (34.8%). Single-lobe involvement occurred in 27 cases (39.1%), and multiple -lobe involvement was present in 42 cases(60.9%).

No statistically significant differences in lesion distribution were found between the two groups (Table 3). The most frequent CT findings were GGO (95.7%),consolidation(92.8%),air bronchogram(89.9%), and interstitial lung abnormalities(91.3%) (Fig. 2). Pleural effusion was observed in 55.1% of patients. The reversed halo sign was seen in 4 patients(5.8%),the tree-in-bud sign in 3(4.3%),and cavitation in 7(10.1%);the latter was mostly related to prior non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection. Notably, among the 65 survivors, follow-up showed complete clinical recovery, and repeat CT imaging demonstrated complete resolution or marked improvement of interstitial abnormalities in those with available follow-up scans, supporting the reversible acute nature of these changes.

CT scoring

The CTS and CTSS were significantly higher in the ICU group(Tables 4 and 5). The total CTS was 9.9 ± 6.4 in the ICU group compared with 5.9 ± 3.4 in the non-ICU group(P = 0.011).Significant differences were observed in the right upper lobe (2.5 ± 2.3 vs. 1.1 ± 1.6, P = 0.003), and left upper lobe (2.0 ± 1.9 vs. 0.9 ± 1.5, P = 0.010). The total CTSS was 13.5 ± 9.0 versus 9.0 ± 4.8 (P = 0.035).

Significant positive correlations were identified between the CTS and white blood cell count (r = 0.266, P = 0.027) and neutrophil count (r = 0.351, P = 0.003), whereas negative correlations were observed with lymphocyte count (r = -0.346, P = 0.004) and hemoglobin (r = -0.317, P = 0.008) (Table 6).

Correlation between CTS and laboratory parameters

Significant positive correlations were identified between the CTS and white blood cell count (r = 0.266, P = 0.027) and neutrophil count (r = 0.351, P = 0.003), whereas negative correlations were observed with lymphocyte count (r = -0.346, P = 0.004) and hemoglobin (r = -0.317, P = 0.008) (Table 6). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Fig. 3) demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.710 (95% CI: 0.574–0.847, P = 0.005) for the CTS and 0.657 (95% CI: 0.513–0.800, P = 0.037) for the CTSS. The optimal cutoff value for the CTS was 7.5, yielding a sensitivity of 63.6% and a specificity of 76.6% (Table 7). Multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, height, and weight, confirmed that both the CTS (OR: 1.258, 95% CI: 1.048–1.510, P = 0.014) and the CTSS (OR: 1.148, 95% CI: 1.022–1.289, P = 0.020) were independent predictors of ICU admission (Table 8).

Comparison with the CURB-65 score

To further evaluate the clinical utility of computed tomography (CT)-based severity assessment, we compared the predictive performance of the CT severity score with that of the CURB-65 score (Table 9), a widely adopted clinical scoring system for community-acquired pneumonia.The AUC for the CURB-65 score was 0.633 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.476–0.790; P = 0.076), compared with 0.710 (95% CI: 0.574–0.847; P = 0.005) for the CTS.The DeLong test demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the two AUCs (p = 0.452). Notably, only the CT-based severity score achieved statistical significance in predicting ICU admission, suggesting that CT-based severity assessment may provide prognostic information not captured by clinical scoring systems alone.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of computed tomograph(CT) findings and severity scores in 69 patients with microbiologically confirmed CPP, constituting one of the largest single-center cohorts reported to date. Our findings offer insights into two key areas: (1) enriching the imaging characterization of this rare disease, and (2) demonstrating the independent association of CT severity scores with ICU admission, which suggests their potential utility in early risk stratification.

The computed tomography(CT) features identified—including ground-glass opacities, consolidation, air bronchograms, and interstitial lung abnormalities—generally align with those reported in earlier, smaller series [19–21]. However, these radiographic findings are not pathogen-specific and exhibit considerable overlap with other bacterial and viral pneumonias. Nevertheless, given the rarity of CPP, the systematic documentation of these imaging patterns in a large cohort constitutes a valuable reference for clinicians.Notably, the interstitial changes, which were observed in over 90% of our patient cohort, demonstrated complete resolution or significant improvement on follow-up imaging among survivors. This finding confirms their acute inflammatory etiology, as opposed to representing underlying chronic interstitial lung disease. This observation addresses a potential concern regarding pre-existing pulmonary pathology and supports the interpretation of these imaging findings as part of the spectrum of acute CPP.

In alignment with prior investigations [22], laboratory parameters including neutrophil percentage, procalcitonin, lactate dehydrogenase, and D-dimer demonstrated marked elevation in the ICU cohort, indicative of a heightened inflammatory response and coagulation activation. CPP manifested as either unilateral or bilateral pulmonary involvement. Multi-lobar and multi-segmental involvement patterns exhibited a significantly higher prevalence in the ICU group, consistent with established literature [23–25]. Nevertheless, similar to CT imaging findings, these parameters reflect systemic disease severity rather than being pathognomonic for CPP.

Beyond descriptive imaging analyses, this study implements semiquantitative computed tomography (CT) scoring systems—specifically the CT score (CTS) and CT severity score (CTSS)—for the evaluation of CPP. Both scoring systems exhibited significantly elevated values in patients who required intensive care unit (ICU) admission and persisted as independent prognostic indicators following adjustment for potential confounding variables. The CTS demonstrated moderate discriminative capacity, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.710. A cutoff value of 7.5 demonstrated a sensitivity of 63.6% and a specificity of 76.6%. Although the sensitivity remains modest, these metrics can function as an objective adjunct to clinical judgment, facilitating the identification of patients at elevated risk of severe disease who may benefit from intensified monitoring or early referral to intensive care. The predictive utility of CT scores in CPP corroborates their established role in COVID-19 and other pneumonias [15, 16, 26], reflecting the general principle that greater parenchymal involvement correlates with worse outcomes.

Notably, the CURB-65 score, a standard clinical severity tool for community-acquired pneumonia, did not achieve statistical significance for predicting ICU admission in our cohort(AUC 0.633,P = 0.076),whereas the CTS did(AUC 0.710,P = 0.005).Although the difference between the two AUCs was not statistically significant(DeLong test, P = 0.405),the CTS offered a numerically higher AUC and provided a significant prognostic signal where clinical scoring alone did not.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged.First, the single-center design and modest sample size(n = 69)limit statistical power and generalizability: multi-center validation is needed.Second, although all cases were confirmed by NGS, the possibility of concurrent infections cannot be completely excluded, which may have influenced imaging findings and outcomes.Third, the lack of pre-infection baseline CT precludes definitive exclusion of pre-existing chronic lung disease; however, the complete resolution of imaging abnormalities in survivors supports their acute nature.Fourth, manual CT scoring is inherently subjective, and inter-observer variability may exist.Future studies could employ artificial intelligence-assisted quantifacation to improve reproducibility.Fifth, the optimal CTS cutoff derived from our cohort requires external validation before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

This study provides a detailed imaging characterization of CPP and demonstrates that CT severity scores, particularly the CTS, are independently associated with ICU admission.While CT findings alone are not diagnostic for CPP, they contribute to the recognition of this rare infection and can serve as an adjunctive tool for early severity assessment and clinical decision-making.The reversible nature of interstitial changes on follow-up supports their acute inflammatory origin.Compared with CURB-65,the CTS offered a numerically higher AUC and provided significant prognostic information where clinical scoring alone did not.
