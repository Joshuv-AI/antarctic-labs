Evolutionary game-theoretic modeling of electricity market dynamics for elastic load optimization
Authors: R. Vimal Prakash, R. Rengaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modern electricity markets are increasingly shaped by the proliferation of distributed energy resources, dynamic pricing schemes, and elastic load behavior. Existing approaches lack behavioural adaptivity and scalability under dynamic market conditions. This study introduces a novel Evolutionary Adaptive Genetic Algorithm (EAGA) framework designed to model strategic interactions among heterogeneous actors—residential consumers, prosumers, aggregators, and utility providers—engaged in real-time energy consumption decisions. The framework synergizes Replicator Dynamics, Evolutionary Algorithms, and multi-agent simulation to enable agents to evolve optimal load-shifting or consumption strategies based on payoff feedback, grid constraints, and peer influence. The model incorporates real-time electricity price signals, consumption elasticity, and local grid constraints, creating a behaviorally realistic and adaptive DR environment. Compared to WOA, PSO, BAT, and DE, EAGA demonstrates faster convergence and superior optimization performance, achieving a final fitness score of 1.40 at 500 iterations, where the fitness function represents a composite objective of energy cost, peak load, and grid stability, formulated as a minimization problem (lower values indicate better performance), outperforming WOA (1.42), BAT (1.58), PSO (1.60), and DE (1.60). Key operational benefits include 27.0% energy cost reduction, 23.1% peak load reduction, 57.5% increase in renewable energy utilization, and a 25.6% improvement in load shifting accuracy. Moreover, the curtailment rate is reduced by 54.7%, and grid stability is enhanced through lower voltage and frequency deviations. The proposed EAGA model presents a scalable and adaptive solution for enhancing smart grid resilience and economic performance. It holds direct relevance for automated demand-side management systems, tariff mechanism design, and regulatory energy policy development in decentralized and prosumer-dominated energy markets.

Introduction

The electricity market has changed dramatically in the last ten years due to technological breakthroughs, regulation and increasing concerns about the environment1. The conventional one-way electricity distribution systems are being replaced by more interactive and decentralized systems2. The most significant trends are the proliferation of smart meters that enable real-time monitoring and control of energy consumption and the growing inclusion of distributed energy resources (DERs) including rooftop solar panels, wind turbines and home-manufactured energy storage systems3,4. Along with these innovations, the use of dynamic electricity pricing has also taken place, where the price of electricity fluctuates according to the real-time supply and demand. Such developments have made possible a more responsive and efficient energy system, the foundation of DR programs5. Demand Response is the term used to describe the strategies and technologies that allow consumers to modify their electricity usage patterns as a response to price signals, grid constraints, or incentives6. It plays a very important role in today power system as it is used to enhance grid resilience, reduce peak demand, and is used in integration of intermittent renewable energy sources7.

However, the practical implementation of DR has challenges related to its potential. Most of the models that are put in place today in DR and load optimization are not dynamic and lack the ability to adapt to the rapidly changing market conditions8,9. These models have the disadvantage of assuming that user behavior is predetermined, failing to take into account peer influence, and failing to adapt to consumer preference and capability differences. This renders the load balancing quite inefficient particularly during instability of prices or absence of supply and demand10. The conventional optimization approaches, such as linear programming or heuristic rules, are also not adaptable to capture the strategic, dynamic nature of the energy consumption behaviours11,12. The motivation behind this research is the need to have a behaviourally realistic and adaptive optimisation paradigm that is able to capture interactions between different participants in the market, enable real-time updates of strategies, and coexist with real-world dynamic pricing mechanisms. Such a model is required to enable smarter, robust, and user-responsive electricity markets.

Recent innovations and its limits

Recent innovations in smart grid systems have introduced new approaches such as multi-agent modelling, machine learning and game-theoretic approaches to enhance demand response and load optimization13. Specifically, evolutionary game theory enables dynamical adaptability of strategies by heterogeneous players14. However, most of the models still possess such constraints as simplistic behavioural assumptions, failure to adjust in real-time, and lack of compatibility with real-world pricing information15. In addition, the majority of the techniques are founded on centralized optimization, which may be not very scalable in a decentralized environment16. These constraints support the significance of a scalable, adaptive, behavior-aware framework, which can be deployed to effectively operate under dynamic and heterogeneous electricity market conditions.

Research gap

Despite the fact that there has been a lot of research on the optimization of the electricity market, the existing models suffer in terms of limited applicability since they employ a combination of static assumptions, centralized control, and simplistic agent behavior in dynamic, decentralized market with high demand elasticity17. Many studies do not consider the adaptive decision-making processes of heterogeneous agents and do not model the dynamics of strategy interactions with time18. Furthermore, the current demand response and price models are not combined with evolutionary learning and real-time market dynamics19. There are no scalable, behavior-based models that can realistically model and optimize elastic load behavior in modern electricity markets using game-theoretic updates in the strategies with evolutionary algorithms.

Research motivation

Increasing infiltration of distributed resources, dynamic pricing, and elastic demand demands intelligent, responsive load control of electricity markets. The changing strategies and realistic consumer behavior are not taken into account in historical models. The proposed work is driven by the need to have a scalable, behavior-aware optimization platform that can dynamically respond to market dynamics and support better demand-side engagement.

Research significance

The proposed work proposes an Evolutionary Game-Theoretic model that represents the strategic interaction of the various participants in the electricity market. It can be incorporated with replicator dynamics and evolutionary algorithms to provide a solid foundation to the optimization of elastic load behavior. It increases the economic efficiency, supports real-time decision-making and offers feasible applications in dynamic tariff planning, automated demand response and policy planning in intelligent grid.

Related works

Lee and Kim suggest an evolutionary game theory combined multi-agent scheduling scheme that is associated with particle swarm optimization to optimize charging/discharging of PV-ESS, EV pricing, and demand response incentives under voltage management. Agents such as PV systems, EVs, and loads adjust strategies using behavior functions to minimize operating costs under DSO control. A voltage-stabilizing reward mechanism is included in the process of cooperation. Applying the EGT-PSO model to the IEEE 33-bus system, optimal energy scheduling with a cost-effective approach has been attained with the voltage kept within the limits. Weaknesses are computational complexity and the requirement to model agents accurately to achieve real-time scalability and adaptability to dynamic grid environments17.

Shen et al. propose a tripartite evolutionary game to study the strategic game between government, power grid companies, and electricity users in demand-side management. The model considers asymmetrical decision-making and the evolving incentives to examine dynamic equilibriums under different policy scenarios. The results indicate that well-designed incentive schemes can encourage cooperation and stabilize DSM adoption by balancing the interests of the various stakeholders. The findings highlight the importance of adaptive behaviours in increasing energy efficiency and the effectiveness of policies. The limitations are the assumptions of stakeholder rationality and simplified interaction structures that can limit applicability in more complex or heterogeneous real-world DSM environments18.

Alfaverh et al. present a model of peer-to-peer (P2P) energy sharing that incorporates a Home Energy Management System (HEMS) of smart homes with rooftop solar, electric vehicles and battery storage. This is a dynamic pricing process mechanism, which involves supply–demand and retail price ratio to make the process incentive compatible and fair. The proposed scheme is compared with the existing ones SDR, MMR and BS in simulations on a 100-household Microgrid. Outcomes indicate a 15–25% reduction in cost of energy and fair distribution of revenue among peers. Physical constraints of the distribution network are also taken into account in the study. Constraints include the need to accurately have real-time data and infrastructure preparedness on the model20.

Dolanyi et al. present a Mathematical Program with Neural Network Constraint (MPNNC) to deal with the optimal bidding problem in electricity markets based on non-convex and partially known constraints. The technique is used to replace the low-level equilibrium problem with a trained neural network surrogate, and thus perform efficient optimization. It is used in the load shedding strategy of a strategic load-serving entity and compared with conventional MPEC formulations. In convex and non-convex environments, MPNNC performs as well as MPEC when having full market knowledge. Practical viability of case studies employing Belgian spot market data is evidenced Limitations are model dependence on the accuracy of the surrogate and generalization in different market conditions21.

Malehmirchegini and Farzaneh have developed a mathematical model that uses microeconomic theory of customer to optimize the welfare of risk-averse, price-sensitive customers (CUs) in Tokyo wholesale electricity market. The model also uses expected utility functions and a price elasticity matrix of demand (PEMD) to reflect different consumer response strategies in Demand Response Programs (DPRs). The SARIMA model is used to carry out hourly demand forecasting with a MAPE error of 0.94% on the TEPCO data. The implementation of TOU and RTP schemes produces peak demand reductions of 10.7 and 7.3% , respectively, in flexible CUs, and 7.7 and 1.6% peak reduction and consumption decrease in curtailable loads. Limitations include model reliance on forecast accuracy and static elasticity assumptions22.

Kaur and Gabrijelcic introduce an unsupervised learning method to group electricity consumers using smart meter data in terms of consumption patterns and peak demand times. The statistical and behavioural feature extraction techniques are used to reduce noise and dimensionality in the high-dimensional time series data. The Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) is tested on a real-life data of 5,038 households in Slovenia. The model successfully determines the consumption trends based on the time of day, weekdays, variability, and seasonal aspects, which were confirmed by visual analysis and the internal index scoring. Weaknesses involve sensitivity to feature selection and clustering parameters, which can influence interpretability and scalability to various regions23.

Lu and Zhang formulate a non-cooperative Stackelberg game-theoretical model to optimize dynamic electricity pricing given both grid load variations and dissatisfaction of users. The consumption behavior of users is classified and two utility functions are developed, one of which is the profit of the power supplier and another of the dissatisfaction of the consumer. The NSGA-II algorithm is used to balance the conflicting objectives in the model. When applied to a practical example, the strategy illustrates the enhanced load management and user satisfaction among various groups of consumers. Robustness is verified by sensitivity analysis. Its drawbacks are that it requires proper classification of users and computational complexity of NSGA-II in large-scale settings24.

Lee and Zhang suggest a controlled peer-to-peer transactive energy market to coordinate the distributed energy resources without adverse emergent behaviours like peak load spikes. The prosumers provide supply or demand curves to the utilities to quantify and dispatch flexibility on a surcharge basis. The model is verified within a high-fidelity simulation of electrified residential buildings in New York State in the month of January, when solar generation and heating demand are out of phase. The results show that the peak load and ramp rates have been reduced by almost 50% and that the local energy usage is increased. The drawbacks are the fact that it depends on proper bidding behavior and is scalable to various climatic and regulatory zones25.

Krayem et al. use a game-theoretic repeated game to examine the peer-to-peer electricity trading using solar in the shortage-prone urban environment of Beirut. The existing study assesses the rooftop solar installation and the building-to-building interactions of the trading in a decentralized microgrid system supported by blockchain. According to the simulation, the solar energy will be able to supply up to 25% of the city electricity based on roof coverage. Nevertheless, the peer-to-peer trading is rather insignificant because the city is characterized by the deficit of energy, which means that most of the buildings will prefer self-consumption. The drawbacks involve the scalability issues in data-poor conditions and the fact that there is little trading potential in environments where supply is highly constrained, making decentralized models of trading economically infeasible26.

Xie et al. employ evolutionary game on complex networks in the study of electricity retailer’s prices in different trading scenarios. The model describes the market share behavior, information exchange and competitive interactions among the retailers through a market share function. The size of the network, the volume of contracts, the rate of switching consumers, and the level of overconfidence of retailers are the key factors that influence the decision of pricing. The simulation findings show that these parameters have an immense influence on the equilibrium of price strategy and therefore there is a need to keep these parameters at optimal levels to maximize retailer profits and market stability. Limitations include assumptions of network structure and behavioural parameters, which can influence applicability to varied retail market conditions27.

Habib et al. suggest an IWOA-based PID tuning of AVR systems, which yields better transient response, stability, and robustness than classical ZN/CC and other AI approaches; but the method is not applicable to market-driven load elasticity, or multi-agent interactions28. Abbas et al. propose a BiCo multi-objective framework of DG allocation and network reconfiguration with simultaneous optimization that results in a substantial cost, voltage profile, and loss reduction, but the scaling to real-time market dynamics and behavioural uncertainties is not studied so far 29. Ali et al. create an evolutionary optimization of large scale integration of RES and BESS and show that it improves costs, emission and reliability significantly; however, the model does not explicitly model strategic players, and adaptive demand-side responses in the form of a game30.

The related works reviewed in Table 1 provide a diverse methodological foundation for modeling and optimizing electricity markets, informing the proposed EAGA framework. Strategic interactions are well represented in game-theoretic models like those of Xie et al.27 and Shen et al.18, but both tend to make simplified assumptions about agent heterogeneity and network structures. The performance of the optimization-based frameworks, such as MPNNC and BiCo models, is good in terms of cost and operational efficiency, but they are not adaptive in behavior and responsive in real-time. Likewise, Machine Learning methods like clustering and neural surrogates offer good predictive insights, but they lack the explicit use of strategic decision-making among market players. Moreover, P2P and transactive energy models focus on decentralized coordination but have problems in scalability, dependence on infrastructure, and are not very effective in supply-constrained settings. Together, these studies indicate a gap in the literature, in which no single framework is capable of jointly modeling adaptive agent behavior, price dynamics in a market, network constraints and scalable optimization under uncertainty. Through this analysis, there is evident gap in research in terms of integrating behavioural evolution and optimization in a decentralized multi-agent environment. To overcome this shortcoming, the suggested framework presents a single Evolutionary Adaptive Game-Theoretic Algorithm, a hybrid of replicator dynamics to evolve the strategies and an adaptive genetic algorithm to optimize the strategies. Proposed evolutionary game-theoretic framework for load optimization

The proposed research methodology presents an Evolutionary Adaptive Genetic Algorithm to optimize the selection of strategies in a smart grid simulation environment. In this method, the process starts with gathering real-time data of the electricity market, such as voltage and frequency measurements on a 24-h period. The data will go through preprocessing, in which missing data will be processed, and the data will be normalized to standard operating limits. After this, an agent strategy population is initialized. The strategies are coded in chromosomes that describe potential actions in stabilizing grid. Genetic parameters like population size, crossover rate, mutation rate and penalty weights of voltage and frequency deviations are set to control the process of evolution. The algorithm will subsequently get into an iterative process in which each of the strategies is tested depending on how it can reduce operational deviations. Tournament selection, a form of selection, determines the fittest individuals to crossover. The genetic information of chosen parents is combined to produce offspring strategies, and mutation adds diversity to avoid premature convergence. Elitism is used to maintain the best-performing strategies so that high-quality solutions are kept in the later generations. The algorithm further optimizes the population until the termination conditions are satisfied including a specific number of iterations or convergence of the strategy performance. The last process is to extract optimized strategy profiles, which offers an optimistic and robust way of maintaining voltage and frequency stability in smart grids. The dataset that is used in this work was acquired via a publicly available Kaggle dataset called Smart Grid Electricity Marketing Dataset31. The data set offers the hourly readings of electricity consumption over a 30 days period, which shows the real world smart grid dynamics. It covers environmental factors (temperature, humidity), time factors (weekend, holiday), consumer segmentation, price signals, past demand, grid frequency and voltage, energy mix. The variable of interest is the category of demand (Low, Medium, High). This highly structured and rich data can be used to model the behavior of the agents responding to the dynamic market signals, which facilitates the validation of the proposed game-theoretic framework of optimization. Evolutionary adaptive game-theoretic framework

The proposed Evolutionary Adaptive Game-theoretic Algorithm is a new framework that combines evolutionary game-theoretic modeling and adaptive genetic algorithm to simulate and optimize the behaviour of multiple agents in the market including consumers, aggregators and utility providers in a dynamic electricity market. Every agent is a rational agent in a strategic setting dictated by the pricing signals and grid limitations and the behavior of peers. The evolutionary game-theoretic core represents an adaptive agent interaction game and payoff, whereas the genetic algorithmic layer is an adaptive evolution of agent strategies over generations using selection, crossover and mutation based on fitness payoff-based utility. This combined approach enables the system to find behaviourally complete, cost-optimal, and grid-compatible equilibria when there is uncertainty and heterogeneous constraints of actors.

Figure 2 illustrates the flow of the Evolutionary Adaptive Game-theoretic Algorithm that integrates the Genetic Algorithm in an optimized way to enable the smart grids to operate on load scheduling. It begins with loading the input data, price, voltage, frequency and then setting a population of agent strategies, which are represented in the form of chromosomes. The fitness of individual chromosome is determined in comparison with the scheduling efficiency. In the event that termination criteria is not met, the selection, crossover, mutation and elitism are employed to generate a new population. Nash Equilibrium no strategy deviation is a strategy that is verified to be Nash Equilibrium by all the agents. The outcome of convergence of the system is the optimization of the strategies, which are cost efficient, require flattening of demands and grid reliability at strategic evolution.Fig. 2EAGA framework.

Multi-agent identification and load profile mapping

Electricity markets are increasingly becoming complex due to the existence of distributed energy resources (DERs), dynamic demand-side participation and highly volatile pricing schemes. A good model must indicate the strategic decisions of different market actors that vary their behavior over time because of economic and grid-based incentives. To address this, it is possible to model electricity market as a multi-agent evolutionary game, whereby the agent’s consumers, aggregators, utilities interact repeatedly over a simulated environment. Replicator dynamics modify the strategy of each agent and cost-performance feedback is used to apply evolutionary pressure. The aim is to reach and converge to a Nash Equilibrium, in which no agent has any interest in unilaterally switching its strategy. The three main types of agents proposed in the system are the Consumers (C), Aggregators (A) and Utility Providers (U).

Consumers are individual energy users who are grouped into Residential (RC), Commercial (CC) and Industrial (IC) consumers based on demand elasticity and usage patterns. Aggregators organize communities of consumers, using schedules and incentives to maximize local energy use. They are intermediaries between consumers and utility providers. The Utility Providers are focal centers that control energy production, grid control, and real-time pricing and implement demand-response measures and the overall system stability considering the dynamic grid restrictions.

Evolutionary game-theoretic framework

In electricity marketing, the decision of consumers in smart grid is dynamic and depends on price signals, grid constraints and social behaviours. Evolutionary Game Theory (EGT) offers a realistic model to capture these dynamic interactions among the agents- consumers, aggregators and utilities without perfect rationality or perfect information. In contrast to classical game theory, EGT is a model in which strategies can evolve, by imitation and selection through payoffs. Agents are trained on the previous results and the effective actions take the centre stage whereas the ineffective ones fade away. This reflects natural selection and therefore the EGT is an appropriate way to model adaptive decentralized decision-making in complex energy systems where strategies and conditions are constantly changing. The replicator dynamics is a fundamental process of the evolutionary game theory, which describes the evolution of the strategies of agents in terms of relative payoffs or fitness. Replicator dynamics is used to determine the evolution of consumer, aggregator, and utility strategies in the context of this study, which is an electricity market modeled as a multi-agent evolutionary game. The main idea is that strategies with a higher performance lead to reduced costs of energy and a more stable grid, as well as increased utility and a decreased frequency of suboptimal strategies. The Nash Equilibrium (NE) plays a role in the electricity market modeled as a multi-agent system, in the sense that it establishes stable strategy profiles of the agents-consumers, aggregators and utility providers-such that no single agent can gain more by unilateral switching to an alternative strategy, when the strategies of other agents remain fixed. In this case, both agents are supposed to be rational and make decisions that result in minimal personal cost taking into account grid conditions and the impact of peers. The strategies will be driven towards a Nash Equilibrium, as the system evolves with replicator dynamics, and no more incentive is left to deviate. Nash Equilibrium is observed in the strategy stabilization part of simulation. Following numerous evolutionary iterations of the updates and strategy changes through replicator dynamics, the system arrives at a stable frequency of strategies and locally optimal payoffs of the selected strategies. This is a Nash Equilibrium state, where Consumers remain with their favourite actions (consume, shift, curtail) since additional variations would either raise the cost or diminish the comfort. Optimization using adaptive genetic algorithm

The proposed study employs the Adaptive Genetic Algorithm as an optimization technique to develop the most optimal 24-h electricity consumption plans of agents-consumers, aggregators and utility providers- under different conditions in the grid. Each chromosome is coded with a strategy vector to be applied daily, and will include choices like consume, shift, or cut load at each hour of the day. The fitness of strategies is determined as a composite score that encompasses minimization of energy cost, reduction in peak demand load flattening and contribution to grid stability voltages and frequency compliance. The strategies that have a higher fitness score are chosen at each generation. Crossover is the recombination of the parts of two good chromosomes in the offspring so that good traits are inherited. Mutation introduces random changes to the selected genes hourly actions and therefore explores new regions of the solution space and does not prematurely converge. This evolutionary process is repeated through many iterations, successively driving the population of strategies toward equilibrium points of optimal grid performance and economic advantage. The GA framework is especially useful in this context since the strategy space of the agents in the electricity market is large, complex, and nonlinear, which allows coordinating the load shaping and participating in the grid in a cost-effective way across a large variety of agent profiles with just game-theoretic adaptation.

Multi-agent simulation environment

The proposed methodology will use a multi-agent simulation framework to simulate the dynamics of decentralized decision-making and interaction among heterogeneous agents within the smart grid electricity market. This type of simulation is used with discrete time steps (e.g., 24-h periods), and enables continuous learning, strategy development and adaptation between consumers, aggregators and utility providers. The simulation is based on 24-h time steps, which is similar to the 1-h resolution of the Smart Grid Electricity Marketing Dataset, which includes hourly electricity demand, price signals, environmental data (temperature, humidity), grid data (voltage level, frequency), and consumer behaviour. This hourly model must give the time resolution of consumption patterns and grid response. The simulation is executed as a series of episodes, each of which is a 24-h time period, to observe how strategies converge, agent learning systems and adaptive behaviour over time. The simulation has three basic types of agents, namely consumers (residential, commercial, and industrial), aggregators and utility providers. The ultimate users are the consumers who decide when and how to consume and shift or cut their energy consumption. Aggregators coordinate consumer groups and coordinate their collective actions by providing incentive or strategic advice to smooth or balance load curves. Grid operators are utility providers, which establish price signals on an hourly basis, observe stability and respond to variable demand patterns. Each agent is initially assigned a strategy coded as a 24 h chromosome vector, which is based on a past record and is applied in the genetic algorithm functions. Instead of interacting directly, agents act upon one another indirectly through the common environment-especially the real-time grid state variables and dynamic pricing. As an example, when a residential consumer responds to price signals by switching consumption to off-peak periods, such an action lowers individual cost and changes aggregate demand. Moreover, the use of multi-agents allows the delegation of decisions to individual agents, alleviating computation load on central controllers.

The proposed Evolutionary Adaptive Genetic Algorithm offers a generalized framework that combines the evolutionary game-theoretic modeling with the adaptive genetic optimization of the dynamic energy management through the establishment of a two-way interaction between the strategic decision-making and the evolutionary search. In contrast to traditional methods that consider these paradigms separately, the current models of evolutionary game theory are mostly based on equilibrium analysis via replicator dynamics, but not adaptive search to improve strategy quality, whereas traditional models of genetic algorithms are better at scheduling but not strategic interaction among heterogeneous agents. To minimize these shortcomings, the suggested framework includes a coupled game-optimization mechanism where replicator dynamics determine the evolution of strategies, and the genetic algorithm optimizes strategies based on feedback of fitness, allowing simultaneous learning and optimization; a dynamic fitness adaptation scheme that incorporates real-time pricing, user discomfort, and grid constraints to ensure responsiveness to changing system conditions; and a multi-agent co-evolution mechanism that models decentralized interactions among consumers. All these combined characteristics make the proposed EAGA framework superior to the current hybrid and standalone methods in terms of flexibility, realism and optimization ability.

Figure 3 shows the proposed methodology workflow. This study introduces an adaptive and data-driven framework to optimise the increasing complexity of electricity demand management in smart grids by combining evolutionary game theory and a genetic algorithm-based optimization method. The framework simulates the strategic behaviour between heterogeneous participants in the market, which consist of residential, commercial and industrial consumers, aggregators and utility providers, to optimize elastic loads under real-time pricing and grid constraints. The smart grid is envisioned as a multi-agent system, with each agent trying to minimize its own electricity cost, in reaction to external influences like dynamic pricing, availability of renewable energy sources, voltage levels, and grid frequency. Agents are participants of a non-cooperative game, where they choose between consumption, load-shifting or curtailment strategies, according to adaptive incentives. The evolutionary game-theoretic approach employs replicator dynamics to model the evolution of strategies, to the extent that successful behaviours those that incur less cost or are more grid-compatible–spread with time. To optimize strategic optimization, a unique EAGA is introduced in the study. In this case, the 24-h energy profile of each agent is coded as a chromosome. With the help of GA operators such as selection, crossover, and mutation, the system evolves iteratively the optimal strategies minimizing the peak demand and the cost of energy and not compromising the comfort of the consumers. The model is verified against the real-world Smart Grid Electricity Marketing Dataset in a time-stepped multi-agent simulation with the following parameters timestamp, temperature, humidity, price signal, consumer type, voltage, and share of renewable energy. Agents learn to adjust their strategies due to the environmental conditions and the actions of their peers through the process of iterative simulation. The system would reach a Nash-like equilibrium with a better distribution of demand, better grid stability and lower operational costs- proving the efficacy of the proposed framework in dealing with elastic load in dynamic electricity markets.Fig. 3Flow chart.

The sensitivity analysis of the proposed EAGA confirms that it is strong with the key parameters that are: mutation rate (μ), crossover probability (Pc) and population size (N). Equally, Pc close to 0.8, will ensure that there is effective recombination and faster convergence, extreme values will not help. The N further improves the quality and stability of the solutions and N = 1000 provides the best trade-off prior to the solution beginning to decrease. Discussion

The proposed EAGA presents an adaptive load scheduling scheme that is highly effective in the cost-efficiency, grid reliability, and system responsiveness of smart grid settings. The framework allows agents to dynamically improve their strategies and thus reach a dynamic equilibrium instead of a static optimization solution by combining Evolutionary Game Theory with Genetic Algorithms. This decentralized form of learning enables the agents to react efficiently to system constraints, changing energy availability and real time price signals and enhances the overall decision intelligence within multi-agent environments. The main performance indicators such as average cost of energy, peak load, curtailment rate and the use of renewable energy show significant increase following optimization. Comparative analysis ensures that EAGA always performs better than benchmark algorithms like PSO, BAT, DE and WOA in terms of convergence speed and quality of final solution. Specifically, the strategy can minimize the daily energy bill by a factor of 26.7% and the accuracy of load shifting is enhanced, together with consumer contentment, suggesting a trade-off between optimization and user utility. Less curtailment also signifies superior demand-renewable generation co-ordination, which improves grid sustainability. Moreover, the distributed nature of the framework enables scalability to large-scale smart grid applications and flexibility. Overall, EAGA provides a robust, efficient and future-ready solution to integrated energy management that will contribute to the realization of both economic optimization and environmental sustainability in the modern power systems.

Despite the fact that the proposed EAGA framework has been doing well, there are some assumptions behind the framework that should be identified. The model assumes rational behavior of agents, the consumers and aggregators are optimum in reacting to the dynamic pricing signals but in reality, the process of decision making by the user may be influenced by behavioral uncertainty and unresponsiveness. Another assumption of the model is that there is perfect or near perfect exchange of information among the agents, but in real smart grid environments, there may be delays in the exchange of information, incomplete or inaccurate information, or measurement noise. Furthermore, it needs a well-established communication infrastructure to provide real-time coordination and data sharing, which not all large-scale or heterogeneous grid systems can afford. These can affect the convergence stability and the overall optimization performance. Modeling of uncertainties to eliminate these limitations, communication-sensitive methods and realistic behavior modeling is a major research direction in the future work.

Conclusion and future scope

The proposed work suggested a new hybrid optimization scheme using the Evolutionary Game Theory and a Genetic Algorithm called EAGA-to address the challenges of energy cost, load balancing, and utilization of renewable energy in smart grid. The EAGA model can achieve a stable equilibrium that is dynamic and cost-effective by allowing agents to change their strategies dynamically based on pricing signals, availability of resources and the behaviours of peers. The simulation outcomes showed that EAGA was highly effective in minimizing average daily energy costs, minimizing peak loads and maximizing renewable energy involvement as compared to conventional optimization algorithms including PSO, BAT, DE and WOA. The ablation study also confirmed that the synergy between EGT and GA as well as the update of adaptive strategies was essential to the better performance of the model. Notably, the suggested framework is scalable and robust to large scale, decentralized smart grid applications, where the agent heterogeneity and energy demands introduce significant complexity. EAGA is not based on fixed heuristics as the rule-based systems, which means that it is less susceptible to changes in consumer profiles and energy pricing schemes.

Future Work will further develop this study by incorporating real data on consumption and multi-objective optimization, which includes emission reduction and battery storage management. Adaptively can be further improved by integrating reinforcement learning agents in the online strategy adjustment and decision-making in real-time. Further, the introduction of vehicle-to-grid (V2G) and peer-to-peer energy trading models into the EAGA framework will give a more comprehensive energy management system. Deployment of the framework on cloud-based platforms is also intended to support large-scale simulations and enable real-time smart grid implementation.
