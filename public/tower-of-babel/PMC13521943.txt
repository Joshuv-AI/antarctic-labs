Pure gaseous-born aerogels from solid solutions via vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization
Authors: Chin-Yun Lee, Shu-Man Hu, Theresia Cecylia Ramli, Hui-Hsuan Wang, Jane Christy, Chi-Yen Tsao, Yu-Ming Chang, Hung-Jui Ting, Chih-Yu Wu, Nai-Chen Cheng, Yu-Chih Chiang, Hsien-Yeh Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProspective aerogels are envisioned to exhibit process adaptability, molecular functionality, and biological compatibility. Here, we show a purely gaseous-born fabrication process based on vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization to produce prospective aerogels and conformal coating with complex surface topologies and geometries. The proposed fabrication mechanisms enable rational processability and direct integration of a wide range of organic and inorganic additive molecules, including zinc-based metal–organic framework (MOF) aerogels, multicomponent aerogels, cell-laden aerogels, and gas-laden aerogels. The fabricated cell-laden aerogels maintain excellent cell viability, stem cell spheroid formation, differentiation abilities, and coculture compartmentalization, while the gas-laden aerogels exhibit tunable gas adsorption and release behavior. The gaseous fabrication process is environmentally friendly, produces no harmful byproducts and uses water-based solutions. The fabricated aerogels are characterized by an ultralight of approximately 0.004 g/cm3 in density, ranging from 90% to 99% in porosity, and a high surface area of approximately 1300 m2/g and above.Subject terms: Metal-organic frameworks, Gels and hydrogels, Gels and hydrogels

Introduction

Aerogels are a fascinating class of materials distinguished by their ultra-lightweight structure, extremely high porosity, and large specific surface area1–3. These aerogel materials have also been found to be useful commercial products in thermal insulation4–6, energy storage7,8, aerospace and defense materials9,10, and biotechnological engineering11–13. Since Kistler first demonstrated aerogels by replacing the liquid phase of a wet gel with air through drying techniques14, extensive efforts have been devoted to developing silicone, carbon, or their combination into functionalized derivatives with tailored properties15–18. Despite these advances, the fabrication of aerogels remains predominantly rooted in solution-based sol–gel chemistry, followed by multistep drying and post-processing procedures. However, current obstacles for these aerogels include the limited material types available and the reliance on liquid-phase processing introduces solvent-related challenges such as phase separation, capillary stress, and limited compatibility with complex functional additives19–21. The existing aerogels are synthesized as bulk materials and require subsequent postengineering steps to enable integration with substrates, devices, or hierarchical architectures. Additionally, the liquid-mediated routes significantly restrict the incorporation of sensitive components such as biomolecules, living cells, or volatile functional species, which are often incompatible with solvents, elevated temperatures, or prolonged drying processes. With the urgent drive to modern technologies and materials science needs, the developments in these ultralight materials are envisioned with adaptability and multifunctionality innovations across various fields.

In this report, the concept of a fully gaseous-born, drying process to fabricate functional aerogel materials is introduced, and a mechanism involving vapor-phase sublimation and deposition is exploited for the fabrication process. A solid solution template composed of volatile and nonvolatile functional additives in the continuous phase, and gaseous aeropockets/bubbles of nanometer to micron size are suspended in the discontinuous phase. Notably, any recipe of interesting combinations of functional components in both the continuous phase and the discontinuous phase is prepared to form a solid solution template, e.g., additives that promote the abovementioned functional activities in terms of physical and biochemical properties and combinations including sensitive biomolecules and living cells. These solid solutions, subsequently, readily allow for later processing by vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization into the proposed ultralight aerogel materials. The molecular dynamics within the vapor system are intricately governed under mild thermodynamic conditions at 200 mTorr and 0 °C, resulting in control of both the physical structures and chemical properties of the aerogel products. With the ability to assess the rates of sublimation and/or the solid solution concentrations, the structures and properties of the final aerogel products are customizable. The thermodynamic principles and the deductions of the relation equations during the vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization mechanism are included in the Supplementary Information. The fabricated organic aerogels exhibit an ultralight density of approximately 0.004 g/cm3, a porosity ranging from 90% to 99%, and a surface area from approximately 400 to 1300 m2/g, which are significantly outperforming conventional sol–gel-derived cryogels and aerogels17,22,23. Besides, the herein reported aerogel fabrication technique and the resulting aerogel products represent (1) a specific class of aerogel systems that are customizable in terms of their physical and chemical functionalities that can be fabricated in one vapor phase step from solid solutions; (2) distinct and yet fundamental fabrication mechanisms that enable access to and compatibility with theoretically asymmetric, secondary, tertiary and more diverse aerogel structures and functionalities; (3) preloading accommodation of living cells and biomolecules with compatibility in cell culture and coculture; and (4) accessibility to the controlled release and/or adsorption of gaseous molecules that exploit the voidage structures of aeropockets with the proposed fabrication process and aerogels.

Results

Solid solutions of pure water with highly concentrated nano- to microsized bubbles are generated through pressurized microchannels; theoretically, these miniaturized bubbles in water are termed milky water by methods such as microchannel devices and ultrasonic waves24–29. Unlike conventional bubbles, the small size and high surface-area-to-volume ratio, longer residence time, reduced buoyancy, self-pressurization, and extensive gas‒liquid interface are characteristic of these miniaturized bubbles30–34. To maintain a homogeneous distribution of bubbles throughout the solid solution, a liquid-nitrogen-assisted supercooling process was introduced to suppress the effects of unsteady-state mass transport, such as bubble phase separation and the eventual evaporation of depleted bubbles35–37. As shown in Fig. 1b, c, this approach preserved the solid solution in an unsteady-state mass balance state prior to further processing. Subsequently, the solid solution was subjected to thermodynamic conditions of approximately 200 mTorr and 0 °C. These conditions induced distinct molecular responses among the components of the solid solution, giving rise to three characteristics of the mechanism: (i) vapor sublimation in the contentious phase of the ice crystal, i.e., water molecules are sublimated from the solid phase to the vapor phase, and mass transport of vapor escaping the solid system, occurs; (ii) rapid vapor escape in the discontinuous phase of void components, i.e., gaseous molecules in the pockets/bubbles can also escape from the solid solution at a rapid speed due to a drastic pressure change from 760 Torr to 200 mTorr, leaving discontinuous vacant spaces in the solid solutions prior to sublimation; and (iii) vapor deposition of the vaporized precursor of [2,2]paracyclophane is introduced to the processing system, and poly-p-xylylenes and derivatives are deposited and polymerized in three dimensions to replicate and occupy the volumetric space of continuous-phase ice crystals; accordingly, polymerization occurs simultaneously with the encapsulation of discontinuous void pockets and assembly into porous polymeric structures. The mechanism continued with vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization of (i) and (iii) to construct the proposed ultrahigh-porosity organic aerogel products. A schematic illustration of the fabrication process is shown in Fig. 1a. An in situ gas mass analyzer was used for monitoring during the vapor-phase fabrication process, and as indicated in Fig. 1e, characteristic peaks at 18 amu and 28 amu were well detected, indicating water and air molecules for vapor sublimation for mechanisms (i) and (ii). Peaks at 104 amu and 139 amu were recorded for the intermediate monomers of quinodimethanes and chloro-para-quinodimethanes, revealing a step of the ongoing vapor deposition process and mechanism (iii) that would thus polymerize to form poly-p-xylylenes and derivatives. Furthermore, the gas mass analyzer also recorded real-time water, oxygen, and nitrogen signals, as revealed in Fig. 1f, and a decreasing trend was detected for water molecules, indicating depletion of the solid solution sublimation. Two increasing trends were detected for accumulated oxygen and nitrogen gas release from the solid solution. Finally, with analytical tools such as mercury porosimetry, the fabricated organic aerogel products were determined to have a density of approximately 0.004 g/cm³, a porosity between 90% and 99%, and a high surface area from approximately 400 to 1300 m²/g. The mechanical property was also measured with the corresponding stress–strain curve from dynamic mechanical testing, which further confirmed the mechanical stability of the aerogels, demonstrating a Young’s modulus of 182 MPa. Compared with previously reported systems38–41, the organic aerogel developed here exhibits comparable mechanical performance, underscoring the intrinsic robustness of the vapor‑deposited architecture. Transformation of a dense polymer into a three‑dimensional network yields a continuous load‑bearing skeleton whose stiffness is governed by network topology in addition to the polymer’s intrinsic modulus, challenging the conventional perception of aerogels as mechanically fragile. The highly porous yet conformal structure enables efficient load transfer while suppressing stress localization, with uniform walls and well‑connected junctions preserving structural integrity under compression. Complementary thermal analysis, conducted using a steady-state method based on Fourier’s law of heat conduction, determined a low thermal conductivity of approximately 0.05 W/m·K. With existing shaping techniques during the supercooling process, e.g., molding, sculpting, layered and multiplied cooling procedures42–44, solid solutions with a variety of geometric form factors were generated in this study, and by utilizing the described fabrication mechanism via vapor sublimation and deposition reaction processes, aerogels with replica architectures were thus produced, as demonstrated in Fig. 1g–i. Graphitization under the same low-pressure conditions and at an elevated temperature of 800 °C can further convert organic aerogels into inorganic carbon aerogels in situ, as shown in Fig. 1j, and an increased thermal conductivity ranging from 0.08 to 0.10 W/m·K was measured for such a fabricated carbon aerogel. The interior and three-dimensional structures of the organic aerogel were further analyzed by a combination of microcomputed tomography (micro-CT), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and laser confocal microscopy techniques. Rather homogeneous void distributions were discovered in dissections (Fig. 1k) and in topology, density, and phase contrast analyses (Fig. 1l). A voidage of 97.6 ± 1.4% was confirmed by the simulated micro-CT calculations, revealing a highly interconnected porous network spanning the entire bulk of the aerogel. The resultant data provided compelling evidence for the anticipated high porosity and ultralow density achieved in the fabricated aerogels. These structural attributes were quantitatively in accordance with both mercury porosimetry and nitrogen physisorption measurements, collectively revealing a desirable hierarchical pore structure and a high specific surface area. As shown in Fig. 1d, the fabricated ultralightweight aerogels with a devised shape were weighed on down feather fibers without causing deformation of these down feathers. More characterization data for the fabricated aerogels are included in the Supplementary Information in Figs. S1–S6. As summarized in Table 1, these capabilities clearly distinguish the present gaseous-born aerogels from conventional silica, carbon, metal, and organic aerogels. The microscopic images of these variant aerogels, fabricated based on the reported process, are also included in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S7.

With the fabrication of more attractive multicomponent aerogels via the proposed vapor sublimation and deposition reaction process, solutions were prepared to contain a functional soluble solute molecule and/or nonsoluble additive in suspension to form the same supercooled solid solutions. Notably, the same continuous phase contains these solutes and additives, keeping the gaseous voids in the discontinuous phase. The experiment revealed a Zn-based metal organic framework (MOF) solution prepared in a hydrothermal solution, which formed an accordant solid solution. Compared with the aforementioned methods for the fabrication of organic aerogels from a pure water/bubble solid solution, the MOF solid solution presented an interesting yet complicated mechanism of (i) vapor sublimation and deposition. As the ice crystallization front propagated, MOF molecules were rejected from the pure ice crystals and concentrated within the continuous phase of the solid solution. The resulting freezing interface imposed steric stabilization, limiting both random diffusion and directed molecular motion45–48. Consequently, the MOF molecules remained essentially stationary and exhibited negligible mass transport under the applied conditions. Therefore, even in the presence of an unsteady-state mass transfer configuration, transport processes remained confined to the molecular scale49–51. The MOF solid solution exhibited the same sublimation behavior that occurred for the water molecules, leaving the nonsublimated solutes of the MOF in the solid solution. On the other hand, similar vapor polymerization occurred between quinodimethanes and resulted in poly-p-xylylene polymers in the same replica space of water molecules and the same encapsulation of the void pockets in the solid solution system, verifying mechanisms (ii) and (iii). An ultralight MOF aerogel was thus produced with a measured density of 0.01 g/cm³, a surface area of approximately 400–1300 m²/g, and a porosity of 98%. The 3D structure of the overall MOF aerogel shown in Fig. 2a was analyzed by micro-CT, and the phase contrast signals revealed that a secondary MOF structure was constructed within the primary structure of the aerogel. This MOF aerogel was observed using SEM, as shown in Fig. 2b, which revealed well-preserved interconnected pores and scattered MOF molecules. The interconnected pore structure played a crucial role in maintaining the low-density properties of the material while providing support for functionality. These MOFs were dispersed uniformly within the scaffold, ensuring consistent performance across the material. In addition, a focused ion beam (FIB) was employed for further observation of the MOF aerogel. The scanning of the FIB through the central MOF provided insights into the internal structure. To analyze the composition, EDS elemental mapping was conducted to confirm the presence of carbon and zinc from the aerogel polymer and MOF molecules, respectively. More XRD characterization data for the fabricated MOF aerogels are included in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S8. The functional and nonvolatile solutes in solid solutions and thus the fabrication of multicomponent aerogels via the proposed process and mechanism were extended to access multiple functional solutes/suspensions and the localization of these functional entities in layered and/or anisotropic configurations. In the experiment and for ease of analytical detection, examples of a solid solution were prepared via multiple supercooling procedures to stabilize multiple components of zinc, iron, and cerium oxides into devised and layered compartments in the solid solution. Theoretically, controlling the geometries and the number of functional entities of interest is not limited to the demonstrated examples. The solid solutions produced in such a multifunctional and compartmentalized fashion were subsequently subjected to the proposed vapor sublimation and deposition process and mechanisms of (i), (ii), and (iii) to finally produce a state-of-the-art multifunctional, ultralight, anisotropic aerogel. The image recorded by EDS elemental analysis in Fig. 2c shows the detected signals of zinc, iron, and cerium and confirms the anticipated layered distributions in the fabricated anisotropic aerogels. For the functionalities, relevant examples include first using high-density copper and zirconium dioxide particles as the selected functional entities. With the proposed multifunctionalization fabrication technique, by suspending and distributing copper and zirconium dioxide in a solid solution and in the continuous phase, the subsequent process and mechanism of (i), (ii), and (iii) produce homogeneous distributions of copper and zirconium dioxide in secondary and tertiary structures within the primary structure of poly-p-xylylene, which are generated as copper/zirconium dioxide aerogels, as shown in Fig. 2d. An enhanced performance in terms of absorbing and reducing sound reflection and penetration was shown, with an average of 20 dB in the tested single-frequency range of 5, 8, 10, and 13 kHz on a consumer-graded graphic card system, and the results are statistically summarized in Fig. 2e. The enhancement in acoustic noise reduction is believed to be due to the combined properties, including ultrahigh porosity, high surface area, and discontinuous aeropocket structure, and metal-induced high areal density, of the copper/zirconium dioxide aerogels fabricated herein. A conformal coating advantage similar to the nature of the chemical vapor deposition52,53 process was inherited by the fabricated functional aerogels, and the application of copper/zirconium dioxide aerogels to high-frequency inductor components was also shown. More details of the acoustic test experiments are included in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S9. A second example involves the use of two entities, a highly conductive graphite particle and a temperature-responsive poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), in solid solutions for vapor sublimation and deposition to fabricate heat-conductive aerogels. Similar secondary and tertiary structures are anticipated for these graphite particles and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) in the fabricated aerogels. In the test, an average temperature reduction of approximately 12.6 ± 1.7 °C was discovered, and the enhanced thermal conductivity was attributed to the high intrinsic thermal conductivity of the graphite particles and parallel thermal-induced poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) shrinkage to calendar the overall structure of the aerogel, which is summarized in Fig. 2f, with the corresponding control sample shown in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S10. Similar conformal coatings of the thermally conductive aerogels were demonstrated on a complex heat-generating component during the tests, as shown in Fig. 2g. Details of the thermal dissipation tests are also included in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S11.

When the use of biomolecules for functional entities and aerogels in biotechnological applications is discussed, the essential biocompatibility, more complicated cell-interface, and cell‒cell interactions of the constructed aerogel materials are important. To address these topics, biomolecules and preloaded living cells were prepared in solid solutions for vapor sublimation and deposition to produce bio-component aerogels that were composed of sensitive growth factors and living cells. The micro-CT analysis was performed and scanned the fabricated bio-component aerogels, and a 3D phase contrast image was computed showing similar formation of a secondary structure composed of biomolecular and cellular entities within the primary structure of the aerogels, as shown in Fig. 3a. Notably, concerns of potential cell-lethal conditions of low pressure and supercooling temperatures were suppressed by an oil-in-water protection technique54–56 in solid solutions, and because only a water solution instead of harmful solvents was used, the resultant poly-p-xylylene polymer was agreed upon by the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) class VI and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The results of cell culture on the preloaded adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) aerogel clearly revealed the ability of the aerogel to form confluent ADSC spheroids (Fig. 3b), which are known for their ability to contribute to highly compatible biointerfaces and microenvironments57,58. In addition, upregulated ADSC stemness was also confirmed in Fig. 3b. Furthermore, a paired combination of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) growth factor and living pheochromocytoma 12 (PC12) cells was used with the proposed fabrication mechanism to fabricate nervous aerogels. The neurogenesis activities of cultured PC12 cells were evaluated, and the extended morphology of growing neurites, as well as the high levels of the differentiation markers Nestin and Tuj-1, confirmed the compatibility and neurogenesis activities of the nervous aerogels (Fig. 3c). To establish this foundation, a synergistic coculture aerogel was produced by the proposed fabrication process, and coculture experiments were performed successfully with simultaneous induction and independent osteogenesis and angiogenesis on the same coculture aerogel construct composed of osteoblasts (MG-63) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and the paired growth factors BMP-2 and VEGF. As shown in Fig. 3d, the results revealed that the formation of classic tube networks enhanced CD31 signals in vascularized HUVECs and indicated strong angiogenic activity; while on the other hand and in the same coculture aerogel sample, detections of high collagen type-I signals in MG-63 corresponded to elevated osteogenesis activities. A confluent cells growth pattern was discovered in three dimensions by a laser confocal microscopic image that revealed a detection depth of 5 µm in z-direction, as shown in Fig. 3e. Statistical analysis for the segment and branch lengths of the HUVECs’ microvascular network morphology and the number of calcified nodules in MG-63, were both verified, for the proposed physiological activities of angiogenesis and osteogenesis in synergy in these coculture aerogels. This cell-loaded coculture aerogel exhibited an extra-low density of 0.03 g/cm³, high porosity above 90%, and a variable and similar surface area ranging from 400 to 1300 m²/g, as confirmed by a combined analysis with micro-CT and laser confocal microscopy. At the time of this study, preloaded cell technologies were not available for existing aerogel materials and were not compatible with the conventional aerogel fabrication process. Additional fabrication of bacterial aerogels was attempted, and additional data from cell culture for the bio-component aerogels are included in the Supplementary Information in Figs. S12–S14.

Finally, the diffusion behaviors and utilities of the gaseous pockets were analyzed for the proposed aerogels. A gas-stabilized emulsion technique, i.e., the selection of gas bubbles in an oil emulsion through microchannels, was used in the proposed fabrication mechanism (ii), in contrast to previous examples in which pure air was used in microchannels. The gas-stabilized emulsions/suspensions were prepared into solid solutions for vapor sublimation and deposition, and, thus, ultimately, for the fabrication of a gas-stabilized aerogel, of which the aerogel can choose the gas ingredients in its aeropockets. During the aforementioned fabrication mechanisms (i), (ii), and (iii), the discontinuous phase of gaseous pockets in (ii) is now not escaping due to the oil emulsion barriers. For the vapor sublimation and deposition mechanism of (i) and (iii), the nonvolatile oil is stable during the process, and the polymerized poly-p-xylylenes are deposited on the interfaces of oil/water54,59, encapsulating not only the void spaces but also the gas bubbles. A scanned and computed 3D structural image by micro-CT of such a gas-stabilized aerogel was displayed in Fig. 4a, and the structures in continuous and discontinuous phases were illustrated. To specifically address the fabrications, the noble gas argon (Ar) was exploited with a similar pressurized stream in the microchannels and was dispersed in the coumarin 6-stained oil-in-water emulsions, thereby generating a solid solution. An Ar aerogel was fabricated on the basis of the proposed vapor phase mechanisms (i), (ii), and (ii) from such a solid solution, and the fluorescence images in Fig. 4b verified the dispersed Ar in the coumarin 6-stained oil, the fabricated Ar aerogel with anticipated emulsion structures from the discontinuous phase in the solid solutions and the stored Ar in the aeropockets, which is believed to be due to the excessive surface areas of the aerogel structures, supported by the widely agreed-upon effects of van der Waals interactions60,61 and induced dipole interactions62 to retain gaseous Ar adsorption. Similarly, a carbon dioxide (CO2) aerogel was fabricated with the same vapor phase mechanism and similar staining technique but with the use of oil red O. The different emission color assisted in differentiating the results of the gaseous components used. Despite the similar adsorption mechanisms of van der Waals interactions and capillary condensation, hydrogen bonds and dipole interactions63,64 contributed to prolonged CO2 retention and adsorption in the aerogels, as shown in the fluorescence images in Fig. 4b. Experiments were further performed to fabricate a naphthalene aerogel, the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) signals of which are detectable in Fig. 4c, identifying stored naphthalene gas in the fabricated aerogels. Finally, aerogels composed of selected Ar and CO2 gas bubbles were fabricated, and their controllable pore sizes were adjusted during fabrication. Depending on the pore connectivity and tortuosity, this can lead to either rapid equilibration with the surroundings or sustained controlled release. In addition, the porosity of the aerogel influenced the diffusion rate; higher porosities with smaller pore sizes impeded gas flow, thus enhancing retention, whereas lower porosity environments promoted greater gas mobility and faster equilibration with the surroundings. The degree of pore connectivity further played a crucial role in determining transport efficiency. A well-interconnected network facilitated continuous gas movement, ensuring more uniform and predictable release. In contrast, isolated or partially blocked pores may act as traps, delaying gas release and creating localized concentration gradients. The data recorded by the real-time residual gas analyzer (RGA) to release gases from the fabricated aerogels with varied pore sizes were analyzed, and the releasing profiles for Ar and CO2 gases were compared in Fig. 4d. A controlled release of these accommodated Ar and CO2 gases were witnessed by giving prolonged durations before releasing from the aeropockets in smaller pore-sized aerogels that was also verified by the aforementioned adsorption mechanism. It was also observed that the more polar CO₂ gas exhibited a longer retention time than Ar in the fabricated aerogels. Theoretically, the structural characteristics and molecular interactions in aeropockets can be customized and tuned to ensure precise gas storage and release within these gas-stabilized aerogels. Finally, tamed adsorption and accommodation in asymmetry were used to distribute multiple gaseous components of Ar and CO2 in the gaseous pockets of a multipocket cargo aerogel, and a selected 1:10 ratio was used in the experiments. As displayed in Fig. 4e, the fluorescence microscopy images confirmed the distributions, and the analytical results of the fluorescence signals also verified the deployed 1:10 ratios of Ar and CO₂ during the fabrication of the resulting multipocket cargo aerogels. Additional data demonstrating the accommodation of the gas-stabilized aerogels are provided in the Supplementary Information in Fig. S15.

A class of aerogels was developed via a vapor sublimation and deposition process through mechanisms from solid solutions. Functional entities and utilities are not limited to the examples in the current study, and vapor-deposited species with either organic or inorganic compounds have been reported under similar thermodynamic conditions and are theoretically exploitable by the proposed fabrication process to produce corresponding aerogels and their functional derivatives. Gaseous pockets of aerogels are interesting in terms of their ability to adsorb and release materials and certainly deserve more attention for development; unlimited applications are expected to exploit existing gas compounds of interest in combination with the proposed aerogel technology. Despite the biochemical functions and sophisticated structural supercooling procedures available, prospective aerogels with alignment and/or directional interior structures are additionally feasible for fabrication and are envisioned to synergize with the reported aerogel technology.

Methods

Vapor sublimation and deposition fabrication of aerogels

Solid solutions with highly concentrated bubbles were prepared using custom-built and pressurized microchannels, which were solidified through a supercooling process with liquid nitrogen. The geometric template method employed negative molds of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Dow Corning, USA) created following established protocols. The solid solutions containing functional additives included MOFs synthesized from 0.075 g of zinc acetate dihydrate (98%, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) and 0.225 g of 2-methylimidazole (99%, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) with a solvothermal method, as well as 3 wt% zinc oxide (99.7%, Emperor, Taiwan), 3 wt% iron oxide (95%, Emperor, Taiwan), and 3 wt% cerium oxide (99.95%, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). The vapor sublimation and deposition process was carried out by a handmade system comprising three sections: a sublimation zone, a pyrolysis furnace, and a deposition chamber, in which the resulting solid solutions were placed under controlled conditions of 200 mTorr and 0 °C. The commercial precursor dichloro-[2,2]-paracyclophane (92%, Galentis, Italy) was sublimated at approximately 100 °C, and a carrier gas of 25 sccm argon, regulated by a mass flow controller (MKS Instruments, USA), was used to transport the vaporized precursor to the pyrolysis zone, where the temperature was maintained at 670 °C. Within this region, dichloro-[2,2]-paracyclophane underwent pyrolysis to generate para-xylylene diradicals (quinodimethanes), which subsequently polymerized onto the sublimating solid solutions in the deposition chamber. The deposition rate was maintained at approximately 0.4 Å/s and monitored using an in situ quartz crystal microbalance (Sycon Instruments, USA), resulting in the corresponding functional aerogel structures. The total fabrication time was primarily governed by the dimensions and composition of the solid solution. Micrometer-sized samples with lower mass and thinner geometries typically required approximately 10 min to complete the sublimation-deposition process, whereas larger, centimeter-scale samples necessitated extended processing times of up to 2 h to ensure complete sublimation and uniform polymer deposition throughout the entire structure. Additionally, thermal graphitization was carried out on the synthesized aerogels by placing the samples in a high-temperature furnace. The heating protocol involved an initial ramp-up to 100 °C to eliminate residual moisture, followed by a continuous temperature increase at a controlled rate of 5 °C/min until reaching 800 °C. Once the target temperature was reached, the samples were held isothermally for 1 h to complete the graphitization process. This treatment effectively removes volatile components, resulting in the formation of carbon-rich structures, referred to as carbon aerogels.

Characterizations

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was performed using a Nova NanoSEM system (FEI, USA) with a primary energy of 10 keV and a chamber pressure of 5 × 10⁻⁶ Torr. To ensure high-quality imaging, the samples were first dried overnight in an oven and then sputter-coated with a thin layer of platinum or gold to improve conductivity and reduce charging effects. In addition, chemical composition analysis was carried out using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) integrated into the SEM system. This technique was employed to map the distribution of key elements, including carbon, zinc, iron, and cerium, originating from the functional additives. Moreover, the internal microstructure of the functional aerogels was investigated through cross-sectional imaging using a focused ion beam (FIB) system (FEI, USA). The integrated SEM was operated at an accelerating voltage of 2 kV to minimize beam-induced damage to the sample. To measure the surface morphology of the prepared aerogels, a 3D profile of the area of interest was taken with a VK-9500 laser confocal 3D profile microscope (Keyence, Japan), and the surface roughness and 3D profile images were constructed by a Keyence analyzer (version VK-H1-A9). Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) was also used to evaluate the internal structure and morphology of the aerogels. Scanning was performed with a Bruker SkyScan 1272 high-resolution micro-CT system (Bruker, Germany) at a pixel size of 2.5 μm, operating at 40 kV, 250 μA, and 10 W output. Image reconstruction was carried out using GPU-Nrecon software (version 1.7.1.0), with corrections applied for ring artifacts and beam hardening. Structural analysis was conducted using CTAn software (version 1.20.8), whereas 3D visualizations were generated with CTVox software (version 3.3.1). Additionally, fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany), with image capture facilitated by Leica confocal software (version 1.9.0). The resulting 3D fluorescence images were then reconstructed and analyzed using Imaris software (version 9.7). On the other hand, the vapor composition was monitored in real time using an in situ residual gas analyzer (RGA, Hiden Analytical, UK). Measurements were performed under ultrahigh vacuum conditions at 10⁻⁸ Torr with an ionization energy of 70 eV and an emission current of 20 μA, covering a mass range of 1–150 amu. The signals were used to assess relative gas release behavior and diffusion kinetics under identical experimental conditions, allowing meaningful comparison of gas retention trends across different pore architectures. The pore size distribution of the aerogels was analyzed using a mercury intrusion porosimeter (Micromeritics, USA), which calculates the pore characteristics on the basis of the relationship between the high surface tension of mercury and the pressure required to force the mercury into the pores of the material. The instrument operated across a wide pressure range, with measurements reported from 0.10 to 60,000 psia. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms and pore size distributions were also determined at 77 K using an ASAP 2020 analyzer (Micromeritics, USA). Prior to the measurement, all samples were degassed at room temperature for at least 6 h to remove adsorbed impurities. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was utilized to calculate the specific surface area, while the pore size distribution was determined by applying the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method to the desorption branch of the isotherm. The mechanical properties were assessed through stress–strain measurements conducted on a mechanical testing instrument (ElectroForce, USA). Stress was calculated as applied load/original cross-section area, and engineering strain was calculated by dividing the change in length by the original gauge length. The Young’s modulus was precisely determined by the slope of the visually identified initial linear elastic region of the resulting stress–strain curve. The thermal conductivity of the aerogel was determined using a steady-state method based on Fourier’s law of heat conduction. A constant heat flux was applied to one side of the material, and after achieving a steady-state temperature profile, the resulting temperature difference across the sample thickness was measured to calculate the thermal conductivity. Furthermore, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted with a Spectrum 100 FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) fitted with a liquid nitrogen-cooled MCT detector. To analyze the aerogels, an advanced grazing angle specular reflectance accessory (PIKE Technologies, USA) was utilized. The spectra were acquired within the wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹, with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and a total of 128 scans. Raman spectroscopy was performed using an inVia confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw, UK) equipped with a helium–neon laser (wavelength of 633 nm). The laser power was controlled at 14.1 mW, and each spectrum was acquired with 30 accumulations and evaluated using Renishaw WiRE 5.0 software. Crystal structure identification was conducted using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). The diffraction patterns were recorded in the 2θ range from 5° to 60° with a step size of 0.02° and a continuous scanning speed of 20° min⁻¹.

Cell culture

Human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs, ATCC® PCS-500-011™) and rat adrenal pheochromocytoma PC12 cells (ATCC® CRL-1721™) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, BCRC No. HUV001) and human osteosarcoma-derived MG-63 cells (BCRC No. 60279) were obtained from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC, Taiwan). ADSCs and MG-63 cells were cultured in MEM-α (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution (Biological Industries, Israel). PC12 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 10% heat-inactivated horse serum (Biological Industries, Israel), and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution. HUVECs were grown in M199 medium (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) supplemented with 25 U/mL heparin (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA), 30 μg/mL endothelial cell growth supplement (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA), 10% FBS, and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic solution. All the cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and 95% air. The media were changed every 3 to 4 days. The cells were seeded at an approximate density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per sample unless otherwise specified. The fabricated aerogel samples were directly cultured in either their respective growth media or in lineage-specific induction media to assess various types of differentiation. For the cell coculture experiments, the labeled cells were then separately introduced into designated compartments of the aerogels at initial seeding densities of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/cm² for HUVECs and 0.5 × 10⁵ cells/cm² for MG-63 cells. In parallel, ADSCs were coencapsulated with HUVECs at a 1:1 ratio to promote and stabilize tubular network formation within the engineered scaffold regions. The cocultured constructs were maintained in a hybrid culture medium composed of equal volumes of MEM-α and M199 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution, 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate, 100 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid, 0.1 μM dexamethasone, 25 U/mL heparin, and 30 μg/mL endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) to support subsequent functional evaluation.

Analysis of cellular activities

Fluorescent dyes, including rhodamine-phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), were applied to stain F-actin, while 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for nuclear staining during ADSC spheroid formation. The expression of the pluripotency-associated genes Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog in ADSCs cultured on the tested surfaces was analyzed for stemness confirmation. For neurogenic differentiation studies, PC12 cells were subjected to electrical stimulation at an amplitude of 100 mV/cm for 1 h per day over a period of 5 days. The cells were immunofluorescence stained for neural-specific markers to evaluate their differentiation status with the following primary antibodies: Anti-Nestin antibody [Rat-401]–Neural Stem Cell Marker (Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab6142, used at 1:500) and Anti-beta III Tubulin antibody–Neuronal Marker (Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab18207, used at 1:500). In addition, the angiogenic potential of HUVECs was investigated after 10 days of culture. Immunofluorescence staining was carried out with the following primary antibodies: Anti-CD31 antibody (Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab28364, used at 1:100) and Anti-Collagen I antibody [COL-1] (Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab6308, used at 1:200). The fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies were Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) preadsorbed (Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab150081, used at 1:200) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (whole molecule)–TRITC antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat. No. T5393, used at 1:200). The resulting fluorescence images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ2 (beta version) to assess marker expression and vascular network formation, including branching number and segment length. Image contrast adjustment and overlaying were performed using Adobe Photoshop (version 19.0).

Statistical analysis

All the quantitative data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) of at least three independent experiments unless otherwise stated. Statistical evaluations were carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 7.0, GraphPad Software, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21.0). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were applied, whereas one-way ANOVA followed by least significant difference (LSD) post hoc tests was used for multiple group comparisons. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
