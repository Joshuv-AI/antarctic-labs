The quintessential high-risk profile: advanced management strategies for placenta accreta spectrum in patients with advanced maternal age and IVF conception

Background

Recent reports suggest that Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) incidence varies widely by population and healthcare setting, with published estimates ranging from approximately 1 in 533 to 1 in 2500 pregnancies. This variability reflects differences in cesarean section rates, access to prenatal care, and diagnostic criteria across populations [1–3]. Contemporary data from tertiary care settings reinforce the central role of uterine scarring within this high-risk profile. A recent cross-sectional study from Türkiye analyzing 68,944 deliveries reported that prior cesarean section was the most common indication for cesarean delivery (56.2% of cases) and was significantly associated with maternal age > 35 years and adverse perinatal outcomes [4]. These findings underscore how escalating operative delivery trends continue to fuel the growing global burden of placenta accreta spectrum disorders and highlight prior uterine surgery as the cornerstone risk factor within this high-risk profile [4]. The rising incidence of PAS has emerged as a critical challenge in modern obstetrics, driven primarily by two powerful demographic trends: the increasing prevalence of childbearing at Advanced Maternal Age (AMA) and the growing utilization of In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) [1–3].

Advancing maternal age is a well-established risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including placenta accreta spectrum. A large population-based cohort study by Wu et al. demonstrated that the risk of abnormal placentation increases with each additional year of maternal age (OR 1.13 per year; 95% CI 1.089–1.194; p < 0.0001), with women aged 35–39 years having a 2.2-fold increased risk and those ≥ 40 years a 3.6-fold increased risk compared to women under 35 years [1]. This finding has been corroborated by subsequent systematic reviews and meta-analyses [2, 3]. Concurrently, IVF conception serves as an established and independent risk factor for PAS, substantially elevating risk above that of spontaneously conceived pregnancies [2, 3].

PAS encompasses a dangerous pathological spectrum of abnormal placental adherence, ranging from accreta (adhesion to myometrium) to increta (myometrial invasion) and percreta (penetration to adjacent organs). These conditions are strongly associated with catastrophic peripartum hemorrhage, unplanned hysterectomy, and severe maternal morbidity, necessitating comprehensive prenatal surveillance and coordinated, multidisciplinary management to optimize outcomes [2, 3].

When AMA, IVF conception, and prior uterine scar converge, we hypothesize that they create a compounded high-risk clinical profile. As visually conceptualized in Fig. 1, each element may contribute to and amplifies the others’ effects: a prior hysterotomy scar provides the anatomical locus of defective decidua basalis, creating the substrate for invasion [5–7]; IVF is associated with fundamental disruptions in endometrial decidualization, adhesion molecule expression, and placental development from the earliest stages of implantation [8, 9]; and AMA introduces systemic vascular endothelial dysfunction and local uterine changes, including structural pathology and impaired receptivity, that may create a permissive environment for abnormal placentation [10]. The hypothesis is that the convergence of these three pathways is associated with deep, uninhibited trophoblastic invasion that defines PAS.

This article presents a case-based narrative review. Importantly, given the limited observational nature of the available evidence, all conclusions and recommendations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical mandates. We provide a critical analysis of a paradigmatic case of catastrophic hemorrhage in a patient embodying this high-risk profile and synthesize a comprehensive body of contemporary evidence to contextualize the findings. The detailed evidence synthesis is presented in seven supplementary tables (Supplementary Tables S1–S7), which summarize the literature on epidemiology, pathophysiology, prenatal diagnosis, multidisciplinary care, hemostatic strategies, outcomes, and emerging technologies. We suggest considerations for a structured clinical pathway, acknowledging that these derive from limited evidence and require prospective validation.

Methods

Study design

This article presents a case-based narrative review. This format combines a detailed index case presentation with a comprehensive synthesis of contemporary and historical literature to contextualize the findings and develop hypothesis-generating considerations for managing this high-risk population.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for articles published between January 2005 and December 2025. The start date of 2005 was chosen to capture modern-era studies on PAS following the widespread adoption of the 2003 consensus criteria for placenta accreta and the significant rise in cesarean delivery rates in the early 2000s. The end date of December 2025 includes the most recent evidence available up to the time of manuscript submission, including in-press articles and early online publications.

Search terms included combinations of: “placenta accreta spectrum,” “PAS,” “placenta percreta,” “placenta increta,” “placenta accreta,” “morbidly adherent placenta,” “advanced maternal age,” “AMA,” “maternal age,” “in vitro fertilization,” “IVF,” “assisted reproductive technology,” “ART,” “embryo transfer,” “frozen embryo transfer,” “FET,” “multidisciplinary care,” “multidisciplinary team,” “patient blood management,” “PBM,” “peripartum hemorrhage,” “postpartum hemorrhage,” “PPH,” “cesarean hysterectomy,” “arterial balloon occlusion,” “tranexamic acid,” “cell salvage,” “prenatal diagnosis,” “ultrasound,” “magnetic resonance imaging,” and “MRI.” These terms were used in various combinations using Boolean operators (AND, OR).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: English language publications, human studies, original research (cohort studies, case-control studies, randomized controlled trials), systematic reviews and meta-analyses, clinical practice guidelines from major obstetric and gynecologic societies, studies specifically addressing PAS, AMA, or IVF or combinations thereof, and landmark historical studies providing foundational context.

Exclusion criteria included: case reports (except where historically significant or providing unique insights), opinion pieces, editorials, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, non-English language publications, and studies not reporting original data or not relevant to the research question.

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles were screened for relevance by two independent reviewers (S.W. and S.Z.). Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then assessed against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (X.X.). Data from included studies were extracted into standardized evidence tables (Supplementary Tables S1-S7). The reference lists of included articles were manually screened for additional relevant publications.

Evidence synthesis

A total of 184 articles were ultimately included in this review, spanning from modern-era studies in 2005 to contemporary evidence through 2025. The evidence was synthesized narratively, with key findings organized into seven thematic areas as presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S7: Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the epidemiology and pathophysiology of the AMA/IVF/PAS risk profile; Supplementary Table S2 covers prenatal diagnosis and imaging modalities; Supplementary Table S3 describes multidisciplinary care structure and team composition; Supplementary Table S4 presents surgical and hemostatic strategies; Supplementary Table S5 addresses special considerations for patients with coexisting risk factors (timing, delivery, outcomes); Supplementary Table S6 details outcomes and economics; and Supplementary Table S7 explores emerging technologies and future directions. Given that most included studies are observational (Level II-III evidence), all synthesized findings are presented as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive conclusions.

Case presentation

Index case finding — The following description is specific to our single patient and does not derive from the literature synthesis.

A 54-year-old gravida 4, para 2 (living children 2) presented via emergency air transport at 34 + 1 weeks gestation with acute, painless vaginal hemorrhage exceeding 1,000 mL. Her dichorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy resulted from IVF with donor oocytes via a single Frozen Embryo Transfer (FET) cycle after prolonged infertility. Her obstetric history included two prior low transverse cesarean deliveries. Notably, her gynecologic history was significant for multiple diagnostic dilatation and curettage (D&C) procedures performed for abnormal uterine bleeding and infertility workup prior to her successful IVF conception.

Prenatal course and diagnostic evaluation

Antenatal care included low-dose aspirin (100 mg daily) initiated at 12 weeks for preeclampsia prophylaxis in the setting of advanced maternal age and IVF conception, which are established risk factors for preeclampsia. Prophylactic enoxaparin (4000 IU daily) was prescribed from 28 weeks due to reduced mobility from the complete placenta previa, which increases thromboembolic risk, combined with the patient’s advanced maternal age and IVF-conceived pregnancy, both recognized risk factors for venous thromboembolism in pregnancy. Serial ultrasounds confirmed a complete placenta previa overlying the prior uterine scar. A dedicated PAS ultrasound at 28 weeks revealed key sonographic features: loss of the retroplacental clear zone, multiple large placental lacunae, and myometrial thinning to less than 1 mm at the bladder interface. A subsequent MRI at 32 weeks confirmed the diagnosis of placenta percreta with invasion into the posterior bladder wall (Fig. 2).

Concurrent fetal ultrasound findings included an isolated short femur length (< 10th percentile) and polyhydramnios.These findings raised concern for possible placental insufficiency or underlying fetal growth restriction, prompting enhanced fetal surveillance including weekly biophysical profiles and umbilical artery Doppler assessments, which remained reassuring throughout the third trimester. A formal multidisciplinary team consultation at 30 weeks established a plan for scheduled delivery at 34 weeks and 3 days of gestation, with prophylactic bilateral common iliac artery balloon catheters to be placed by interventional radiology prior to the cesarean section. However, the patient presented with an emergency two days prior to the planned admission.

Acute presentation and multidisciplinary response

On arrival, the patient was tachycardic and hypotensive. The institutional “Code Crimson” protocol for suspected PAS was immediately activated. Because this was a planned admission with the multidisciplinary team and interventional radiology already prepared, bilateral common iliac artery balloon occlusion catheters were placed prophylactically under general anesthesia as per the pre-existing plan, prior to the start of the surgery. A midline vertical laparotomy confirmed extensive placenta percreta with invasion into the posterior bladder wall and dense adhesive disease involving the anterior abdominal wall and omentum. A classical uterine incision was made to deliver both viable neonates. Given the extent of invasion and hemodynamic instability, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with a classical cesarean hysterectomy and partial cystectomy.

Hemostatic management and outcomes

A multimodal hemostatic strategy was employed: tranexamic acid (1 g IV) was administered at cord clamping along with carbetocin (100 µg) and methylergometrine (0.2 mg IM); intermittent arterial balloon occlusion was utilized for temporary vascular control; intraoperative cell salvage with a leukocyte depletion filter was initiated; and resuscitation was guided by a massive transfusion protocol. Despite these prophylactic measures, the patient’s estimated blood loss was 4,500 mL, reflecting the severity of the placental invasion and the complexity of the surgery. The hemorrhage was progressive and difficult to control intraoperatively, ultimately necessitating hysterectomy. The patient remained hemodynamically labile throughout the procedure, requiring ongoing resuscitation.

The patient’s preoperative hemoglobin was 11.2 g/dL. Intraoperative cell salvage processed and returned 850 mL of autologous blood (approximately equivalent to 3–4 units of PRBCs). The patient received 2 units of leukocyte-reduced packed red blood cells. Additionally, Fibrinogen levels were monitored throughout the case and remained above 2.0 g/L; therefore, no fibrinogen concentrate was administered. Postoperative day 1 hemoglobin was 9.8 g/dL, and day 3 hemoglobin was 10.1 g/dL. The massive transfusion protocol was activated but discontinued after initial resuscitation due to hemodynamic stability achieved with the combination of cell salvage and limited allogeneic transfusion. The patient was extubated 8 h postoperatively, transferred from the ICU on postoperative day 2, and discharged home on postoperative day 10 following treatment for a superficial surgical site infection. Pathologic examination of the hysterectomy specimen confirmed placenta percreta with chorionic villi penetrating the full thickness of the myometrium and extending into the serosa, with direct invasion into the resected portion of the posterior bladder wall.

Neonatal outcomes

Both neonates required NICU admission. The first neonate (2050 g) had Apgar scores of 9-9-10 but developed respiratory distress, pathological jaundice (peak total bilirubin 104.0 µmol/L), and was found to have a possible left ventricular ependymal cyst on cranial ultrasound, necessitating long-term neurological follow-up. Both infants were discharged in stable condition after 10 days with multidisciplinary follow-up plans.

Discussion

In this section, findings specific to our index case are presented first, followed by synthesized evidence from the published literature (summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S7).

Potential compounded pathophysiology and risk stratification

Index case finding

Our patient’s profile AMA (age 54), IVF-conceived twin gestation, two prior cesarean deliveries, and multiple D&C procedures represents a convergence of risk factors. The severity of her presentation (placenta percreta with bladder invasion, estimated blood loss 4,500 mL) suggests, but does not prove, a compounded effect.

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Table S1 )

Based on available observational studies (Level II-III evidence, summarized in Supplementary Table S1), we hypothesize that these risk factors have cumulative associations [11–35]. IVF increases the risk for PAS approximately five-fold (OR 5.03, 95% CI: 3.34, 7.56) [36]. This risk appears profoundly amplified by a history of prior uterine surgery. Specifically, hysteroscopic adhesiolysis is associated with a 7.72-fold increase in risk (OR 7.72, 95% CI: 4.10-14.53), while endometrial ablation demonstrates a 20-fold elevation (OR 20.26, 95% CI: 17.15–23.93) [37]. AMA is an independent factor within this risk profile. Among women diagnosed with PAS, AMA is associated with a three-fold increased risk of requiring hysterectomy (OR 3.0, p = 0.045) [38]. However, these are observational associations, not proven causal mechanisms. Supplementary Table S1 provides a comprehensive summary of the key studies supporting these associations.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we hypothesize that the convergence of AMA, IVF, and a prior uterine scar creates a compounding risk profile. The interacting pathways of vascular/uterine senescence, impaired decidualization, and anatomical decidual deficiency may overwhelm normal implantation defenses, facilitating deep trophoblastic invasion. Consequently, identification of these coexisting risk factors might warrant classification as “highest possible risk” in clinical practice, but this recommendation is hypothesis-generating and requires prospective validation.

Prenatal diagnosis and protocolized care considerations

Index case finding

In our patient, MRI at 32 weeks successfully confirmed placenta percreta with bladder invasion (Fig. 2), which guided surgical planning. Despite this, emergency presentation occurred two days before scheduled delivery, highlighting the unpredictable nature of this condition even with prenatal diagnosis.

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Tables S2–S3)

Based on the literature (summarized in Supplementary Table S2), we suggest that effective management begins with prenatal diagnosis [36, 39–58]. Ultrasound findings of placenta previa over a prior scar in an AMA/IVF patient may trigger MRI evaluation, which offers superior anatomical detail for assessing invasion depth [59]. However, caution is warranted: one study reports that MRI sensitivity for antenatal PAS diagnosis was only 22.2% in IVF patients compared to 94.7% in spontaneous conceptions, though this finding derives from a single retrospective study (Level II-2 evidence) and requires validation [60]. Supplementary Table S2 details the diagnostic performance characteristics of various imaging modalities, including ultrasound and MRI, for PAS detection.

The literature (summarized in Supplementary Table S3 ) suggests that planned delivery at a center with a dedicated multidisciplinary team may improve outcomes [30, 32, 61–73]. Implementation of a dedicated multidisciplinary PAS program has been associated with reduced ICU admissions (from 53.7% to 19.2%) and decreased transfusion rates (from 85.4% to 53.9%) [74]. Structured protocols have been associated with increased antenatal PAS detection rates (from 23.5% to 90.9%) [75]. These are observational findings; causality has not been established. Supplementary Table S3 provides detailed information on multidisciplinary team composition, structure, and reported outcomes from various PAS programs.

Hemostatic strategies — evidence and controversies

Index case finding

Our patient received a multimodal hemostatic strategy including tranexamic acid, intraoperative cell salvage (850 mL returned), and prophylactic common iliac artery balloon occlusion. Despite these interventions, estimated blood loss was 4,500 mL, and hysterectomy was required. This single case cannot determine the efficacy of any individual intervention.

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Table S4 )

The literature on hemostatic strategies (summarized in Supplementary Table S4) shows conflicting evidence regarding prophylactic arterial balloon occlusion [2, 20, 32, 46, 53, 76–96]. A randomized controlled trial found no reduction in estimated blood loss or transfusion requirements compared to standard care, with reversible catheter-related complications in 15.4% of patients [97]. A recent meta-analysis of randomized trials demonstrated increased red cell transfusion requirements with internal iliac balloon occlusion (weighted mean difference 1.04 units; 95% CI 0.52–1.55) [98]. Conversely, observational studies have reported reduced blood loss with aortic or common iliac balloon occlusion, particularly in placenta percreta [99, 100]. Given the low quality of evidence (Level II-III) and high heterogeneity, routine use cannot be recommended [98]. Prophylactic arterial balloon occlusion should be considered an adjunct for selected high-risk cases within experienced multidisciplinary teams, with patient counseling about its experimental nature. Supplementary Table S4 comprehensively reviews the available evidence for balloon occlusion, tranexamic acid, cell salvage, and other hemostatic interventions.

Evidence for other hemostatic strategies (also detailed in Supplementary Table S4): Systematic use of tranexamic acid and intraoperative cell salvage is supported by moderate-quality evidence [2, 20, 32, 46, 53, 76–96]. Comprehensive Patient Blood Management protocols have been associated with reduced transfusion requirements in observational studies [73, 86].

Special considerations for AMA/IVF/PAS patients

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Tables S5–S6)

Supplementary Table S5 summarizes evidence on special considerations for patients with coexisting AMA, IVF, and PAS risk factors. The literature suggests that this high-risk profile is associated with more invasive phenotypes, often necessitating modified protocols including planned delivery at 34–35 weeks gestation [3, 31, 32, 46, 65, 68, 73, 95, 101–110]. While the standard of care remains planned cesarean hysterectomy with the placenta left in situ to mitigate catastrophic hemorrhage risk [59, 111], emerging uterine-preserving techniques show promise in selected cases [111]. Readers are directed to Supplementary Table S5 for detailed data on delivery timing, surgical approaches, and outcomes specific to patients with these coexisting risk factors.

Supplementary Table S6 quantifies the economic burden of PAS, which incurs significantly higher transfusion volumes and ICU utilization [2, 3, 31, 32, 106–109, 112–117]. Prenatal diagnosis is associated with a significant reduction in transfused red blood cell units (mean difference: -1.96 units) and intraoperative blood loss (mean difference: -0.65) [118]. Implementation of multidisciplinary PAS models has been associated with reduced ICU admissions (from 53.7% to 19.2%) and total costs by 33% [74, 118]. Supplementary Table S6 provides detailed cost and outcome data from major studies. However, dedicated cost-effectiveness analyses for the AMA/IVF/PAS subpopulation are lacking.

Neonatal considerations

Index case finding

Both neonates in our case required NICU admission. The first developed respiratory distress, pathological jaundice, and was found to have a possible left ventricular ependymal cyst on cranial ultrasound. The relationship between this cyst and maternal PAS or IVF conception is uncertain; the current literature does not establish a causal link [108].

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Tables S5–S6)

Late preterm infants (34–36 weeks) face higher NICU admission rates (36% vs. 2% in term infants) and increased respiratory morbidity [119, 120]. Emerging cohort data demonstrate higher composite adverse neonatal outcomes in PAS pregnancies (33.6% vs. 18.7%), with lower 5-minute Apgar scores and more frequent NICU admission [121]. Meta-analytic data show that emergency deliveries in suspected PAS substantially increase the odds of NICU admission (OR 2.5) and adverse composite neonatal outcomes (OR 2.6) [122]. These findings suggest, but do not prove, that structured neonatal care pathways may be beneficial in PAS pregnancies. Additional neonatal outcome data can be found in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

Future directions: integrating innovation

Literature synthesis (Supplementary Table S7)

As summarized in Supplementary Table S7 [123–177], emerging technologies with direct relevance to PAS include AI-enhanced ultrasound and MRI for automated detection of PAS features [123–134], machine learning algorithms for risk stratification [135–139, 149–152, 156, 157], radiomics analysis of placental texture to predict invasion depth [124–128, 136–138, 146–148, 159, 160, 168–173], and computer-aided diagnosis systems [129–135, 139–145, 155, 174–177]. A recent meta-analysis of MRI-based radiomics demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for PAS, with sensitivity of 87% (76–93%) and specificity of 92% (89–94%) [135].

The integration of AI-assisted diagnostic tools into clinical workflows, guided by structured frameworks like the one proposed in Fig. 3, holds potential for improving early detection, surgical planning, and personalized care pathways for this high-risk population [140, 145, 164–167], 174– [59]. Supplementary Table S7 provides a comprehensive overview of emerging technologies, including their reported diagnostic performance and current stage of development. However, as with all recommendations in this review, these technologies require prospective validation before clinical implementation.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that affect the interpretation and generalizability of our findings. First, the index case represents an extreme phenotype a 54-year-old patient with twin gestation, placenta percreta with bladder invasion, and catastrophic hemorrhage (4,500 mL EBL) and such extreme presentations are rare; therefore, the management strategies described may not be generalizable to all patients with PAS, particularly those with less severe disease, younger age, singleton gestation, or different clinical profiles. Second, this is a single case report with a narrative literature review, not a prospective cohort study, randomized controlled trial, or systematic review with meta-analysis of individual patient data, and the evidence summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S7 is predominantly observational (Level II-III); consequently, all conclusions and recommendations are hypothesis-generating only, and causality cannot be inferred from the presented evidence. Third, the supporting evidence for most interventions is derived from observational studies (Level II-III evidence) with high heterogeneity and potential risk of bias — for example, data on prophylactic arterial balloon occlusion are conflicting, with randomized trials showing no benefit and observational studies suggesting benefit, and the proposed “compounded pathophysiology” is based on epidemiological associations rather than direct mechanistic data; therefore, readers are cautioned against overinterpreting these findings as definitive. Fourth, our recommended clinical pathway (Fig. 3) has not been prospectively validated, as the suggested algorithm represents a synthesis of available evidence (Supplementary Tables S1–S7) and clinical experience from a single tertiary center, leaving its effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness in other settings unknown. Fifth, the patient was managed at a high-resource tertiary care center with 24/7 access to interventional radiology, specialized anesthesia, cell salvage, and massive transfusion protocols, resources that are not available in all healthcare settings; therefore, our recommendation for MRI in all AMA/IVF/previa cases should be considered “where available” rather than mandatory, particularly in resource-limited settings where cost-effectiveness must be carefully evaluated. Sixth, much of the evidence synthesized in this review (Supplementary Tables S1–S7) comes from heterogeneous populations and may not specifically apply to the AMA/IVF/prior scar subpopulation, as dedicated prospective studies in this specific high-risk group are lacking. Readers are therefore cautioned against overgeneralizing our findings and recommendations to all patients with these coexisting risk factors without consideration of individual patient characteristics, local resources, and evolving evidence.

Hypothesis-generating considerations for clinical practice

Based on our single case analysis and synthesis of limited observational evidence (summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S7), we offer the following as hypothesis-generating considerations rather than clinical mandates, recognizing that these suggestions require prospective validation before widespread adoption. First, regarding screening and referral, clinicians may consider dedicated PAS ultrasound at 18–20 weeks for patients with advanced maternal age, IVF conception, and a prior uterine scar, and where available, referral to a regional PAS center of excellence could be considered. Second, regarding centralized, protocolized care, where resources permit, delivery at designated Level IV centers with 24/7 multidisciplinary availability may be considered, governed by institutional checklists for preoperative planning. Third, regarding a proactive hemostasis bundle, as part of comprehensive patient blood management (PBM), clinicians may consider preoperative anemia correction, intraoperative cell salvage, tranexamic acid, and goal-directed transfusion therapy [178]; however, the role of prophylactic arterial balloon occlusion remains controversial, and if used, patients should be counseled about its experimental nature and potential risks [98, 179]. Fourth, we suggest the development of specialized registries to generate higher-quality evidence, specifically prospective registries tracking outcomes for the AMA/IVF/prior uterine scar subpopulation, with prioritization of cost-effectiveness analyses and integration of emerging technologies (e.g., AI-assisted imaging, summarized in Supplementary Table S7) [178].

Conclusions

In this hypothesis-generating case-based narrative review, we suggest that the patient with Advanced Maternal Age, IVF conception, and suspected Placenta Accreta Spectrum represents one of the most formidable challenges in contemporary obstetrics. Based on limited observational evidence (summarized in Supplementary Tables S1–S7), we hypothesize that the convergence of these specific risk factors may create a compounded risk profile that might benefit from coordinated, preemptive care. However, all conclusions are hypothesis-generating only, derive from a single case and Level II-III evidence, and require prospective validation.

The management principles outlined here derive from an extreme case and must be applied judiciously based on individual patient characteristics and institutional resources, and evolving evidence. We suggest that recognizing this potential pathophysiology, implementing structured management protocols centered on multidisciplinary expertise and comprehensive hemorrhage control, and developing standardized referral pathways may improve outcomes for these highest-risk pregnancies. This represents an area for continued research and quality improvement in maternal healthcare systems.
