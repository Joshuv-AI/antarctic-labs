Association between obstructive sleep apnea and perinatal outcomes in a cohort of Japanese pregnant women: a prospective observational study
Authors: Manaka Moro, Jun Hosomichi, Takumi Suzuki, Yu Takenouchi, Naoyuki Miyasaka, Meiyo Tamaoka, Yasunari Miyazaki, Chikako Morioka, Manabu Sugie, Mari Hayata, Jun Aida, Takashi Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is common during pregnancy, and maternal intermittent hypoxemia has been suggested to affect fetal growth. However, diagnosis-based risk assessment and research in East Asian pregnant women remain insufficient. In this study, we aimed to clarify the prevalence of sleep-disordered breathing in Japanese pregnant women and to examine its association with neonatal outcomes.MethodsWe included 185 pregnant women aged ≥ 20 years and their newborns. A questionnaire was administered, and sleep evaluation was performed using a portable monitoring device (WatchPAT) at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation. OSA severity was classified, based on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), as mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), and severe (AHI ≥ 30). Neonatal physical growth was evaluated at birth and compared between the OSA (AHI ≥ 5) and non-OSA groups.ResultsThe prevalence of OSA (AHI ≥ 5) was 15.7%, comprising 23 mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), 5 moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), and 1 severe (AHI ≥ 30) cases. Pre-pregnancy weight and body mass index (BMI) were significantly higher in the OSA group than in the non-OSA group, whereas the rate of BMI change during pregnancy was significantly lower. Regarding neonatal outcomes, no significant differences in physical growth or gestational age at delivery were observed between the two groups.ConclusionThis study provides insights into the phenotype of pregnancy-related OSA in a lean Japanese population, where OSA may develop at lower BMI levels than those reported in Western cohorts.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent sleep-disordered breathing condition characterized by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in apnea and hypopnea [1]. These recurrent events may lead to intermittent hypoxemia [2].

During pregnancy, the prevalence of OSA increases substantially, particularly in the third trimester [2–5], because of physiological changes such as hormonal fluctuations, increased blood volume, reduced lung volumes, fluid shifts during sleep, and anatomical alterations that promote upper airway collapsibility [2, 4]. Pregnancy complications, such as gestational diabetes mellitus and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, are associated with adverse perinatal outcomes [6, 7]. Emerging evidence suggests that OSA is closely linked to these comorbidities, potentially sharing underlying mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation [8, 9], and may further exacerbate risks to the fetus. Prior studies have suggested that maternal intermittent hypoxemia associated with OSA may adversely influence fetal growth and pregnancy outcomes [2, 8]. However, evidence regarding the association between maternal OSA and neonatal outcomes remains inconsistent [10]. Within the framework of the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis, intrauterine environmental factors, including maternal sleep disturbances and associated comorbidities, have lasting effects on offspring health [11, 12].

Despite the growing awareness of sleep-disordered breathing during pregnancy, data on OSA among Asian populations, particularly in Japan, remain scarce. Recent studies have investigated OSA in Korean pregnant women: they were either limited to symptom-based screening questionnaires [13] or focused specifically on overweight women [14]. Anthropometric characteristics of Japanese women differ markedly from those in Western populations. For instance, the mean body mass index (BMI) of adult Japanese women is ~ 22 kg/m² [15], which is significantly lower than ~ 30 kg/m² observed in adult women in the United States [16]. Furthermore, while the prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) among women aged 20–39 years in the United States is ~ 40% [17], the prevalence of underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) among young Japanese women was 20.7% in 2019 [18], highlighting the unique issue of thinness in this population. Given that such distinct anthropometric features differ significantly between Western and East Asian populations, findings from Western studies or high-risk cohorts may not be directly applicable to Japanese women. Specifically, the prevalence, clinical characteristics, and potential associations with perinatal outcomes have not been fully investigated in this population using objective sleep testing. Notably, OSA can occur even in non-obese individuals. In such patients, factors other than obesity, including craniofacial anatomy, contribute to the pathogenesis of OSA [19].

Therefore, the aims of this study were (1) to clarify the prevalence and clinical characteristics of OSA among Japanese pregnant women, and (2) to examine its association with perinatal outcomes, including maternal physiological parameters and neonatal anthropometric measurements. We hypothesized that OSA would be prevalent even in non-obese Japanese pregnant women and would be associated with adverse perinatal outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Institute of Science, Tokyo Hospital (Japan). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Science Tokyo (Approval Number: G2020-037; March 23, 2021) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

We recruited Japanese pregnant women aged ≥ 20 years who were scheduled to deliver at our institution. A total of 260 women were initially recruited between May 27, 2021, and July 29, 2024. Participants underwent sleep evaluations at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation. We included 185 participants in the final analysis. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) lack of informed consent; (2) measurement failure due to device error; (3) multiple examinations for the same individual; (4) multiple pregnancies; (5) sleep evaluation conducted at < 28 weeks of gestation; (6) emergency hospitalization or inability to undergo the examination; or (7) unavailability of neonatal data at the start of the analysis.

Sleep evaluation

Sleep-disordered breathing was assessed using a validated portable sleep monitoring device (WatchPAT; Philips, Murrysville, PA) worn overnight at home at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation. Although polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing OSA, overnight monitoring in a sleep laboratory poses practical and ethical challenges during late pregnancy, including physical discomfort and sleep disruption. Therefore, we employed home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) using the WatchPAT, which offers a feasible, less invasive alternative with favorable acceptable agreement with PSG during pregnancy [20].

The apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) is a key metric used for identifying OSA and for quantifying its severity [21]. Sleep parameters and respiratory events, including hypopneas, were automatically analyzed using the proprietary algorithm of the WatchPAT system. The respiratory event indices assessed included the AHI, respiratory disturbance index (RDI), and oxygen desaturation index (ODI). The AHI was defined as the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. The RDI was defined as the AHI plus respiratory effort-related arousals. The ODI was defined as the number of oxygen desaturation events ≥ 4% per hour. Consistent with established clinical thresholds, OSA was defined as an AHI ≥ 5 events/h.

Maternal and neonatal data collection

Maternal characteristics, including age, pre-pregnancy weight and BMI, weight and BMI at the time of measurement, and percentage change in BMI from pre-pregnancy were collected. Heart rate parameters and sleep position variables were also recorded. Neonatal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, birth weight, birth length, head circumference, chest circumference, and placental weight.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28.0.1.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Participants were categorized into the OSA (AHI ≥ 5) and non-OSA (AHI < 5) groups. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Group comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. All p-values were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Ethics approval

The study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Science Tokyo (Approval Number: G2020-037; date: March 23, 2021). Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 185 pregnant women were included in the final analysis (non-OSA, n = 156; OSA, n = 29). The gestational age at sleep assessment did not differ between the groups (median, non-OSA: 32.43 weeks [IQR, 30.14–34.43] vs. OSA: 31.57 weeks [IQR, 29.93–34.43]; P = 0.707). Maternal age was significantly higher in the OSA group (non-OSA: 34.81 ± 0.39 vs. OSA: 36.69 ± 0.63 years; P = 0.015) (Table 1).

The prevalence of OSA, defined as an AHI ≥ 5, was 15.7% (29/185) in this cohort. During sleep assessment, 156 participants (84.3%) had an AHI < 5, whereas 23 (12.4%) had mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), 5 (2.7%) moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), and 1 (0.5%) severe (AHI ≥ 30) OSA (Fig. 1).

Maternal characteristics

Anthropometric indices, including pre-pregnancy weight, pre-pregnancy BMI, weight at measurement, and BMI at measurement, were all significantly higher in the OSA group (P < 0.01) than in the non-OSA group. Conversely, percentage change in BMI from pre-pregnancy during pregnancy was significantly lower in the OSA group (P < 0.05). Regarding physiological parameters, the mean heart rate and maximum heart rate were significantly higher in the OSA group (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) (Table 2).

Sleep study variables

Respiratory parameters indicated a significantly higher respiratory burden in the OSA group. The AHI, supine AHI, RDI, and ODI were all significantly higher in the OSA group than in the non-OSA group (P < 0.01). Regarding oxygenation, the mean and minimum peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were significantly lower, and the number of desaturation events per hour was significantly higher in the OSA group (P < 0.01). Snoring was also significantly higher in the OSA group (P < 0.01) (Table 3). No significant differences in sleep position parameters (time spent in supine, prone, or lateral positions) or sleep quality indices, including sleep latency, sleep efficiency, and awakening count, were observed between the groups (Table 4).

Discussion

In this prospective study of Japanese pregnant women, the prevalence of OSA was 15.7%. Although maternal physiological characteristics—specifically age, BMI, and heart rate parameters—differed significantly between the OSA and non-OSA groups, neonatal anthropometric outcomes were largely comparable. In this cohort, maternal OSA was not associated with differences in gestational age at delivery or adverse perinatal outcomes. Nevertheless, these findings provide important insights into the clinical characteristics of OSA in Asian pregnant women, particularly within a relatively lean Japanese population.

The observed prevalence of 15.7% falls within the range reported in previous international studies [22], confirming that OSA is a common condition even in the Japanese population. However, direct comparisons with recent studies in similar ethnic populations reveal variations likely attributable to differences in inclusion criteria and assessment methods. Ryu et al. reported an OSA prevalence of 27.5% in Korean pregnant women [14], and Ghesquière et al. reported 43.3% in a French cohort [23]. Unlike our study, which included a general cohort with a lower mean BMI, these studies specifically targeted overweight (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m²) or obese (BMI > 35 kg/m²) populations. Japanese women typically have a lower BMI before and during pregnancy than do women in other developed countries, with a notably high thinness prevalence [18]. The lower prevalence of OSA in our study compared to that in these high-risk cohorts likely reflects the difference in demographic characteristics, underscoring the strong influence of maternal BMI on OSA prevalence. Additionally, Ko et al. reported a prevalence of 32.2% in a general population of Korean pregnant women [13]. The difference in figures may be explained by the diagnostic methodology employed; while their study used a symptom-based screening questionnaire, our study relied on objective physiological monitoring using the WatchPAT device.

Interestingly, Japanese women generally have a lower BMI than do women in Western populations; however, OSA was prevalent. This supports reports in Asian populations indicating that craniofacial skeletal anatomy—such as relative upper airway restriction—constitutes an important anatomical risk factor for OSA in addition to obesity [19]. However, in our cohort, the OSA group had significantly higher pre-pregnancy BMI and weight gain indices, reaffirming that excess weight remains a major modifiable risk factor.

Regarding perinatal outcomes, no significant differences in gestational age at delivery or in anthropometric measures such as birth weight, length, head circumference, chest circumference, or placental weight were observed between the OSA and non-OSA groups. This is contrary to the findings of Ryu et al. [14], identifying OSA as a risk factor for preeclampsia in an overweight cohort, often accompanied by preterm delivery, and that of Ding et al. [24] who conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, where moderate-to-severe sleep-disordered breathing during pregnancy was associated with increased risks of gestational diabetes and other adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight. Our results are more consistent with those of Ko et al. [13], who found no significant differences in adverse outcomes such as small for gestational age or preeclampsia. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that adverse perinatal outcomes may be more pronounced in populations with higher BMI or more severe OSA, whereas such associations may be less evident in a general cohort with predominantly mild OSA.

The absence of significant adverse outcomes in our study can be attributed to two main factors. First, the severity of OSA in our population was predominantly mild (79.3%). Mild intermittent hypoxia may not be severe enough to compromise placental blood flow to the extent of causing fetal growth restriction or severe hypertensive disorders. Second, all participants received regular standard prenatal care, which may have mitigated potential adverse effects through appropriate weight management and monitoring.

However, the lack of difference in birth size does not necessarily imply the absence of intrauterine effects. We observed significantly higher mean and maximum heart rates in the OSA group, suggesting sustained sympathetic nervous system activation. From the perspective of the DOHaD hypothesis [11], even subtle changes in the intrauterine environment, such as maternal sympathetic activation and intermittent hypoxemia, could program fetal physiology [8, 12]. Recent literature suggests that offspring neurodevelopment may be affected by maternal OSA even when no clear differences are observed in somatic growth [25]. However, the current sample size may not be sufficient to detect small differences in neonatal anthropometric outcomes, including birth weight. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences should be interpreted with caution. In addition, long-term follow-up is crucial to investigate potential impacts on neurodevelopment and cognitive function, which may not be apparent at birth.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the participants were recruited from a single center, which limits generalizability and statistical power. Second, sleep was evaluated using WatchPAT. While this validated device minimizes patient burden in late pregnancy, its measurements may not fully align with the gold standard, PSG, and limitations in respiratory event detection and severity classification may have introduced some measurement error in our OSA assessment [20]. Third, detailed information on potential confounders, such as socioeconomic status, was not fully adjusted for. Finally, the small number of severe OSA cases prevented us from assessing dose–response relationships.

In conclusion, OSA occurred in ~ 16% of Japanese pregnant women and was associated with distinct maternal characteristics, including higher BMI and sympathetic activation. Pregnancy-related OSA among Japanese pregnant women was not clearly associated with adverse perinatal outcomes; nevertheless, continued clinical vigilance is warranted. Future research with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up is needed to elucidate the full spectrum of mother-to-child health transmission, particularly regarding offspring neurodevelopment.
