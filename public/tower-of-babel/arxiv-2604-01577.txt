Thinking While Listening: Fast–Slow Recurrence for Long-Horizon Sequential Modeling
Shota Takashiro, Masanori Koyama, Takeru Miyato, Yusuke Iwasawa, Yutaka Matsuo, Kohei Hayashi

Abstract
We extend the recent latent recurrent modeling to sequential input streams. By interleaving fast, recurrent latent updates with self-organizational ability between slow observation updates, our method facilitates the learning of stable internal structures that evolve alongside the input. This mechanism allows the model to maintain coherent and clustered representations over long horizons, improving out-of-distribution generalization in reinforcement learning and algorithmic tasks compared to sequential baselines such as LSTM, state space models, and Transformer variants.



1
Introduction

Humans are capable of reasoning while simultaneously processing external inputs, allowing them to cope with long-horizon streams of variable lengths
(
1
;
19
)
.
In such settings, an agent must maintain and update an internal state that summarizes past information while processing new observations, selectively extracting task-relevant signals and retaining them until future inputs resolve their relevance.
Such a state-tracking type computation is central to navigation, decision-making, and prediction in nonstationary environments
(
25
;
43
;
20
)
.
However, learning to reliably maintain such an internal state over long horizons is a well-known challenge for recurrent neural networks
(
2
;
34
)
and it remains a major bottleneck in modern neural sequential models such as Transformers
(
42
;
30
;
39
)
.

To explore this endeavor, we draw on recent advances in latent recurrent modeling
(
12
;
17
;
15
;
44
;
32
;
7
;
26
)
.
These methods iteratively apply a recurrent block in latent space to a fixed input, learning an internal evolution of the state whose repeated application leads to improved performance.
While they are often motivated as a mechanism to scale the test-time compute, we particularly want to exploit its ability to iteratively refine and organize the information in the latent space.
This ability is supported empirically by recent works like
(
32
;
7
)
as well as by theoretical results showing that recurrent modules can dynamically cluster information
(
28
;
13
;
14
)
.
These results suggest latent recurrent models as a promising computational core for “tracking the evolving state of both real and abstract worlds”
(
29
)
.
However, the approaches we introduced above lack an explicit mechanism to scalably integrate
evolving
external inputs into the dynamics of the internal representation.
In direct applications of these approaches, the latent state is reinitialized at the beginning of each loop that processes incoming input, and prior computation is preserved by appending latent summaries to the external context.

The goal of this work is to explore the extension of the recent latent recurrent model to evolving sequential inputs, so that new observations can be continuously incorporated into the latent representation of fixed size.
We achieve this by reformulating the latent recurrent system as a pair of coupled dynamical processes: a
fast
-evolving latent reasoning process and a
slow
-evolving observation process.
We refer to this architecture as Fast Slow Recurrent Model (FSRM).
More concretely, without resetting the latent state, we run a continuous latent recurrence that is conditioned on external observations, which evolve on a slower timescale.

We study this construction through benchmark tasks that fundamentally require

Our strategy enables continuous integration of new observations while preserving the benefits of recurrent latent computation.

We also compare our approach against various competitive baselines, including SSMs and Transformers, on other benchmarks including reinforcement learning tasks.
Finally, we provide qualitative visualizations illustrating how our architectural design transforms incoming information in a structured and traceable manner.

2
Preliminary: Latent Recurrent Model

Our method is inspired by models that conduct reasoning in the latent space by iterating a recurrent block
(
15
;
12
;
32
)
.
Given a specified number of recurrent iterations
T
at training time, this class of approaches takes an observation
O
as input together with an initial noise tensor
\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
, where
K
is the number of latent tokens and
D
is the model dimension.
The model then iteratively updates the latent state
\mathbf{X}(t)
by initializing
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}
and applying

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t+1)=R\left(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathrm{Encoder}(O)\right)

(1)

for
t=0,1,\dots,T-1.

This produces
\mathbf{X}(T)
, a latent conclusion of depth
T
. We use
\mathrm{Decoder}(\mathbf{X}(T))
for inference.

This system can be applied autoregressively; for example, one may set
\mathrm{Decoder}(\mathbf{X}(T))
to serve as the next observation
O
in a subsequent round of reasoning.
When applied autoregressively at turn index
s
, models in this family typically use the accumulated set of the latent tokens
\mathbf{X}^{s}(T)
, or its decoded form, as contextual input, and reinitialize
\mathbf{X}^{s}(0)
at every turn
(
15
;
17
)
.
A naive extension of this strategy to an evolving observation sequence
O_{s}
is to concatenate
\{\mathbf{X}^{s}(T)\}_{s}
with
\{O_{s}\}_{s}
as context, which causes the context length to grow with increasing
s
.
This introduces one source of long-horizon failure, namely the difficulty associated with the use of excessively long contexts
(
30
;
38
)
.
In the next section, we extend the latent recurrent model more directly so that the reasoning proceeds continuously while
listening
to an evolving stream of observations.

3
Fast–Slow Coupling of Latent Recurrence with the Observational Process

Our goal is to explore a system that can continuously integrate the evolving external signals into the latent reasoning process.
To this end, we modify latent recurrent reasoning by considering a coupled dynamical system in which the observational process and the reasoning process evolve on the same timeline but at different speeds.
In our model, the reasoning process proceeds
T
times faster than the observation process, so that
T
also represents the number of latent reasoning iterations performed per observation.

Let
t
denote the fast time scale, and let
s=\lfloor t/T\rfloor
denote the slow time scale indexing the observations, with observations given by
O_{s}
.
Let
\mathbf{C}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
denote the encoded observation process and
\mathbf{X}(t)
denote the latent process of thought, operating in the same latent space as
\mathbf{C}(t)
.
The discretized dynamics of our model are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{C}(t)&=\mathrm{Encoder}(O_{s})\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }s=\lfloor t/T\rfloor\in\{0,\dots,S-1\},\\[6.0pt]
\mathbf{X}(t+1)&=\Pi\left(\mathbf{X}(t)+\gamma F\left(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{C}(t);\theta\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for }t\in\{0,\dots,ST-1\},\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
F
is a neural network parameterized by
\theta
and shared across timesteps,
\gamma>0
is the step size, and
\Pi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}/\|\mathbf{x}\|
is a normalization operator.
When the observation sequence has length
S
, the recurrent module performs a total of
ST
latent updates while receiving
S
encoded observation signals.
Importantly, this differs from standard latent recurrent reasoning in that the time axis of the observation process is aligned with that of the fast latent reasoning, whose state transitions persist over time.

The latent state is initialized as
\mathbf{X}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
at the beginning of each episode and is
never reset
until the episode ends; instead, it continuously moves forward in time and integrates the slow evolution of
\mathbf{C}(t)
into its dynamics, even when the observational process evolves independently of
\mathbf{X}(t)
.
See Figure
2
for visualization of our scheme.

We train the parameters
\theta
and
\gamma
as well as the encoder and decoder for various tasks that use the sequential output of
\mathbf{X}(t)
for inference.
For the prediction task on the observation sequence,
O_{s+1}
is predicted by
\mathrm{Decoder}(\mathbf{X}(sT))
.

3.1
The Choice of
F

For the choice of
F
in our experiment, we explored a mechanism related to the architecture with self-organization properties
(
28
;
14
;
13
)
.
In particular, we used the approach of
32
with attention-type update as follows:

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C})_{i}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\Big(J(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C})_{i}\Big)

(3)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is the
i
-th latent token in
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}-(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}
is the projection of the vector onto the tangent of the unit sphere,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}
is an anti-symmetric matrix, and each
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)
is normalized so that the latent tokens remain on the unit sphere for every
t
.
Following
32
, our
J
is an attention-based function that mixes latent tokens
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
while integrating
\mathbf{C}
in an additive manner.
Please see Appendix
A.1
for details.
We also observe that a variant where
J
is implemented as a vanilla Transformer block works competitively as well, but the model based on
32
performed most stably (Appendix
C.3
).

Energy-like scalar

This model also provides a scalar quantity that is useful for tracing the behavior of the latent updates.
The update can be interpreted as a dynamical system of interacting latent tokens evolving toward self-organization, and there exists a tractable, energy-like scalar that represents the degree of self-organization of the neuronal population
(
28
)
; see Appendix
A.2
.
In the experimental section, we will demonstrate that our model learns to dynamically align the interpretable structural properties of the system with this energy state.

4
Related Work

Recent studies have given birth to many models that utilize recurrent mechanisms in the latent space.
Unlike approaches based on chain-of-thought
(
45
;
27
)
,
12
argue that such latent recurrent approaches do not require specialized training data, can work with small context windows, and can capture types of reasoning that are not easily represented in the output space, such as words and symbols.

12
, similar to
32
, propose the framework of applying a recurrent module to a randomly initialized latent signal while injecting the conditional input.

9
formulates a relevant mechanism through energy minimization in EBMs, and
15
provides transformer-based instantiations.
Unlike our approach, when we apply these approaches to sequential inputs, these methods decode each output, and the conditional context grows in length with the sequence.
The same applies to
48
and
11
as well.
Coconut
(
18
)
, on the other hand, does not decode the output and conducts all computation of the sequence in the latent space.
However, this approach also makes the conditional context grow in the latent space.
Our approach carries over the latent variable over sequences of any length.
Contexts are compressed and organized into a latent variable of fixed size through recurrence.

As a recurrent method that conducts all computations in the latent space of fixed dimension, we shall mention Perceiver
(
23
)
, which proposes an RNN-style latent update to incorporate sequential signals.
While related to our approach, this approach is not equipped with a recurrent reasoning loop that proceeds faster than the observations.
Adaptive Computation Time
(
16
)
is also closely related to our approach, as it separates the fast-loop RNN from the slow input dynamics.
However, it does not apply a recurrent mechanism with a self-organization property like a transformer
(
13
)
, and the latent at the end of the fast loop is not directly carried over to the next loop.
Instead, the initial latent of the next loop is produced by the ensemble of the trajectories in the previous fast loop.
Universal Transformer
(
8
)
uses recurrent self-attention, but without input injection.

(
35
)
is more related to our approach, except that they use SSM to model the fast process, and introduce another auxiliary variable in the fast process that is initialized at every loop.

We shall also mention HRM
(
44
)
, which is inspired by biological
findings
(
33
;
22
;
47
)
, as well as its extension, TRM
(
24
)
.
These methods also introduce a system of coupled fast and slow dynamics.
However, unlike our approach, these methods do not consider temporally varying input sequences, and their reasoning is conducted on a stationary input.
Also, CTM
(
7
)
applies fast recurrence in autoregressive time segments, and is capable of handling the temporally varying input sequences.
CTM frames their architecture as a dynamical system of interacting neurons (latents).
Unlike our approach, they explicitly define the synchronization strength between each pair of neurons that is integrated over time, and produce the output from the synchronization pattern itself as opposed to our direct readout from the latents.

5
Experiments

We conducted a series of experiments to assess our model’s ability to dynamically integrate sequential inputs into its recurrent process in support of correct inference.
We selected tasks that fundamentally require the model to iteratively and selectively identify task-relevant information from an evolving input sequence, and to consolidate it by continuously integrating this information into the latent state.

We first present results on the egocentric maze and Dyck tasks, both of which provide supervision in the form of correct answers. In these settings, the model can retrospectively infer which parts of the input are irrelevant to the correct underlying structure of the data.
We additionally consider more challenging reinforcement learning tasks on MiniGrid. In these online settings, the model cannot directly compute the correct regret or value signal from immediate rewards, and therefore must learn indirectly how to integrate episodic information as it explores the environment.

Hyperparameter

For the maze and Dyck tasks, we first identified a common set of training hyperparameters—specifically the number of epochs, batch size, and learning rate—for which validation performance converged across all baselines. After fixing these shared settings, we further tuned model-specific hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate and hidden dimensionality) independently for each baseline in order to maximize validation performance.
For the MiniGrid experiments, we adopted the same hyperparameters for all baselines as in
40
, while tuning the hyperparameters of our proposed method separately.

and

5.1
Egocentric Maze

The goal of this task is to infer the shortest path from the start to the end, while receiving a stream of egocentric observations from a navigator exploring the maze.
In this task, the information corresponding to dead-end paths is naturally task-irrelevant.
In order for the model to be able to generalize its inference capability to OOD mazes of large size, the model must learn to efficiently integrate the task-relevant information and iteratively consolidate its hypothesis about the shortest path.

More specifically, the model in this task is given a sequence of local, cropped
7\times 7
neighborhood images
O_{s}
corresponding to the egocentric observations of a navigator agent that explores the maze following the right-hand rule (the model has no control over the navigator).
The navigator streams egocentric observations until the goal is reached.
The decoded output of the model is a sequence of movements (
up, down, left, right, pause
) corresponding to the estimated shortest path.
Depending on the geometry of the maze, the length of the observation sequence
S
naturally varies across samples.
As a supervisory signal to be compared against the decoded output, we provide the model, at the end of each episode, with the true shortest path for the maze, represented in the same format.
We evaluate accuracy using a binary exact-match criterion on the entire predicted action sequence, after removing all
pause
tokens from both the prediction and the ground-truth shortest path.

The mazes are generated randomly.
During training, they are of size

Results

Our model significantly outperforms all baselines on the maze task. As shown in Figure
4
, it achieves near-perfect accuracy on the
19\times 19
(ID) mazes and, crucially, demonstrates strong generalization to unseen
39\times 39
mazes with approximately
60\%

Effect of fast-loop iterations

The number of fast inner-loop iterations (
T
) controls how many recurrent updates occur between successive observations (Eq.
2
).
To investigate whether the model benefits from longer recurrence, we conducted experiments with varying values of
T
while keeping all other components fixed.
As shown in Table
1
, OOD accuracy improves monotonically as
T
increases, with gains saturating beyond
T=8
.

Effect of weight sharing (recurrence) in the fast loop

Our fast module applies the same update rule repeatedly for
T
inner steps, i.e., it is
recurrent
or
weight-shared
across fast iterations.
To investigate the importance of this recurrent structure, we replaced it with an
unrolled
residual stack of
T
distinct layers, while keeping the overall fast–slow interface unchanged.
The results (Table
1
)
show that weight sharing is crucial for OOD generalization: with the same inner-loop budget (
T{=}5
), the non-recurrent 5-layer stack reduces OOD accuracy from approximately
0.6
to approximately
0.5
.
Increasing depth without sharing does not recover this performance: a 10-layer unshared stack further degrades OOD accuracy to approximately
0.45
, even after adjusting the learning rate.
These results support our design choice of a recurrent fast module.

Comparison to LLMs

To relate this benchmark to current foundation models, we also compared our model against three frontier LLMs on the same Dyck-
(30,5)
task (see Figure
1
in the introduction).
For the evaluation of our method in this comparative study, we used the model trained with the seed that attained the highest OOD accuracy at length 2,560 in producing Figure

In contrast to our model, which is trained only on raw Dyck strings of length

Despite their advantage in parameter scale and access to the explicit algorithm, their token-wise accuracy deteriorates as sequence length increases, whereas our model maintains accuracy above

Emergent structure in layerwise energies and latents

Interestingly, through learning the prediction task, our model discovers latent reasoning that aligns with the structural organization of the Dyck language.
Recall that for the recurrence used in our experiments (Section
3.1
), there exists an energy-like scalar that reflects the organizational level of the state.
We show the evolution of this energy in Figure
6
, and the evolution of the latent in Figure
7
.
The energy of
X^{(1)}
spikes upon the arrival of a new token and then stabilizes at a level that depends on the bracket type and whether the current token is opening or closing.
In contrast, the energy of
X^{(2)}
is largely insensitive to bracket identity and is instead organized by stack
depth
.
This qualitative shift in energy organization indicates that the second fast process abstracts away from token identity and operates at a higher structural level.
PCA projections of the latents for both
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
support this interpretation: the state trajectories form a crisp, spring-shaped manifold indexed by stack depth that remains coherent over long horizons.
This structured organization in the learned latent space is similar to the report in

(
48
)
that the looped model tends to produce interpretable reasoning traces.
This behavior stands in stark contrast to Mamba (Figure
7(c)
).

We next evaluate our model on challenging partially observable reinforcement learning tasks from the MiniGrid environment
(

.
We consider three MiniGrid tasks: DoorKey, MultiRoom, and LavaCrossing (see Figure

In these navigation tasks, DoorKey requires the agent to pick up a key, open a door, and then reach the goal in this order.
MultiRoom requires the agent to open multiple doors sequentially to reach the goal.
LavaCrossing requires the agent to reach the goal while avoiding the lava.
In all tasks, the reward is provided only at the end of a successful episode (i.e., sparse rewards).

Most importantly, because these tasks do not provide a ground-truth supervisory sequence of actions, it is more difficult for the model to retrospectively infer which information in the episode is worth incorporating into its reasoning and consolidating into intermediate conclusions.

At each slow step, the agent receives an egocentric
7\times 7
observation with object/color/door-state channels.
The agent then selects an action from the set
a_{s}\in\{\texttt{left},\allowbreak\texttt{right},\allowbreak\texttt{forward},\allowbreak\texttt{toggle},\allowbreak\texttt{pickup},\allowbreak\texttt{drop},\allowbreak\texttt{done}\}
.

Setup

We designed our evaluation protocol to test the model’s generalization capabilities in a zero-shot transfer setting.
The agent is trained exclusively on the simplest ID configuration and is subsequently evaluated, without any further training, on more complex and previously unseen OOD environments, which are typically larger than the ID environments (see Table
4
in the Appendix for details).
We compare our model against a few competitive sequence-modeling baselines, including LSTM, Mamba-2, and Transformer-XL.
Results are averaged over five random seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation.

All models are trained end-to-end using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO;
36
).
For our model, the agent’s encoder (sensory module) first processes the input observations through a series of convolutional layers, followed by our recurrent core (Eq.
2
).
Please see Appendix
B.4
for more details.

Figure

shows the success rates across all MiniGrid tasks. Our model performs comparably to or better than the baselines across all tested environments. This suggests that the recurrent mechanism of
thinking while listening
is effective in these complex sequential decision-making problems as well.
Also, we again observe a clear emergent structure in the latent space of our learned model.
We find that fluctuations in the energy value reflect the agent’s level of “frustration”, for example, when the agent’s vision changes beyond a certain threshold (Figure

6
Conclusion

We proposed a recurrent model that integrates sequential observations into recurrent modeling. Across the tasks that require long horizon information of task-relevant information while tending to incoming observation of varying relevance, our approach consistently outperformed strong baselines, including an LSTM, Mamba-2, and Transformer-XL, and it demonstrated competitive generalization and decision-making in dynamic environments.
This suggests the coupling of fast recurrent latent dynamics with slowly evolving observation dynamics as a promising mechanism for sequential inference over long-horizon.

Limitations and future work

Despite strong performance on controlled benchmarks, our study has several limitations.
First, experiments are limited to relatively small-scale synthetic and grid-based domains, and we have not yet validated the approach on larger real-world tasks such as language, video, or robotics.
Second, the proposed fast-slow architecture incurs computational overhead due to its double-loop structure, resulting in lower GPU efficiency and slower inference than standard baselines (Appendix, Figure
17
); improving efficiency via recursion-friendly parallelization methods (e.g.,
(
5
)
) is an important future direction.
Finally, we do note that there is a sensitivity to the random initialization, as observed in Figure
8
.
This suggests gradient instability from repeated layer reuse, as observed in RNNs
(
34
)
, and motivates further architectural and optimization improvements.

21

However, because there are multiple valid choices for the type of the next open bracket, the task is not deterministic when formulated as pure next-token prediction.
To eliminate this source of indeterminacy in accuracy evaluation, we restrict the predictor to output only the closing bracket.

For the comparison with production LLMs on Dyck-(30,5), we queried each model via its public API using a common prompt that describes the stack‑based algorithm (Table
7
).
Following
3
, we designed the prompt so that it clearly separates (i) a natural‑language specification of the ground‑truth stack algorithm from (ii) explicit execution instructions that tell the model to simulate this algorithm step‑by‑step and output only the prediction string.
No additional fine‑tuning was performed; all models were evaluated in a pure inference setting. We set the temperature to 0 if the API accepts it, so that the model’s behavior is more deterministic.

Modern LLMs can adapt their test‑time compute by varying the length of their internal “reasoning tokens”, but commercial APIs strictly enforce a maximum on the total number of output tokens (reasoning plus final answer). As of November 2025, the documented upper limits for our models are 32k tokens for Claude Opus 4.5 and 64k tokens for both Gemini 3.0 Pro and GPT‑5.1. For each provider, we first empirically determined the longest Dyck input length for which the prompt, the model’s reasoning tokens, and the final prediction string all remain within this limit. We then generated 100 Dyck sequences at this length and measured token‑level prediction accuracy. Use of external tools such as Python interpreters or code execution APIs was explicitly disabled so that each model had to execute the algorithm internally.

For our proposed recurrent model, we used the same two‑layer Dyck architecture as in the main experiment (Section
5.2
) and selected the checkpoint that achieved the best accuracy at the maximum length of 2,560. No additional training or hyperparameter tuning was performed when extending the evaluation to longer sequences.

C.4
Inference cost

Unlike SSMs and LSTMs, our proposed model performs
T
forward passes for each observation.
To quantify the resulting inference speed, we measured wall-clock time on the MiniGrid RL task.
All models were implemented in PyTorch without using optimizations such as
torch.compile
,
and timing was recorded on a single NVIDIA GH200 core.
The results are shown in Figure
17
.
With the default choice
T=5
, our method is roughly three times slower than the Mamba and
Transformer baselines.
We note that this computational overhead is partly due to software constraints: competing models
rely on highly optimized CUDA kernels and mature library implementations, whereas our current
implementation lacks comparable low-level optimizations.
We therefore expect that specialized kernels and recursion-friendly parallelization strategies could
substantially reduce this gap without changing the model architecture.

Figure 17
:
Forward computation wall-clock time per batch in the Minigrid task.
For the proposed method, the computation time increases linearly with the number of fast inner loops
T
compared with the baselines.

C.5
Parameter size vs OOD performance

As summarized in Table
3
, our proposed model is roughly an order of magnitude smaller in parameter count than common sequence models. This is because the recurrent core is highly parameter-efficient, and because our implementation does not yet benefit from the kernel-level optimizations available for architectures such as LSTMs or SSMs; scaling up the model size would therefore lead to comparatively higher computational cost despite the smaller parameter count.
A natural concern is therefore whether the observed generalization gains could be attributed to reduced overfitting due to the smaller model size.

We scale the channel width of our model to {64, 128, 256} and train on the maze task using the same data and protocol.
Unless otherwise noted, all training hyperparameters follow Section
5.1
(see Appendix
B.1
).
For this ablation, we set the batch size to
128
and adopt a width-scaling learning rate

\text{lr}=\sqrt{64/\text{channels}}\times 10^{-3}
.

Results

Table
5
reports OOD accuracy (mean
\pm
std over 3 seeds).
The
256
-channel variant attains the best OOD performance, suggesting that increasing capacity does
not
necessarily lead to overfitting in this setting.
These results indicate that our OOD gains cannot be explained solely by a smaller model size.
