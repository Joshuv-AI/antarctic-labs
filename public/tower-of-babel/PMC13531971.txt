Correlation between lumbar disc herniation nerve root compression and clinical symptoms: a study using lumbosacral plexus magnetic resonance neurography combined with T2 mapping
Authors: Rushuang Yang, Yanwen Sun, Ruiqing Shi, Ying Liu, Na Zhao, Xuesong Zhang, Xiaona Li, Zhigang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLumbar disc herniation (LDH) is common, often leading to lumbosacral nerve damage in affected patients. This study aims to assess the correlation between nerve root compression and clinical symptoms of LDH using magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) combined with T2 mapping.MethodsThis prospective study included patients with unilateral LDH-related nerve root compression. All participants underwent 3.0T MRI with 3D SHINKEI and T2 mapping sequences. Clinical symptoms were evaluated using ODI and VAS scores. Measure T2 values of the lumbosacral plexus (LSP) using T2 mapping and calculate the difference in T2 values between the compressed and uncompressed sides. MRN image results and symptoms were analyzed with Fisher’s exact test. Paired samples t-test compared T2 values of compressed and uncompressed groups. Correlation between T2 differences and ODI/VAS scores was examined. A two-sample t-test assessed T2 differences in postganglionic segments of MRN high and non-high signal groups. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.ResultsThere was a significant correlation between MRN image results and clinical symptoms, with MRN visual observation showing 91.7% sensitivity and 50.0% specificity. T2 values of the postganglionic segment on the compressed side and the uncompressed side were 76.04 ± 12.82 ms and 68.91 ± 12.24 ms. The difference in T2 values between compressed nerves and uncompressed segments correlated positively with leg numbness VAS scores (r = 0.35, P = 0.045). T2 values differed significantly between high and non-high signal groups (t = -2.57, P = 0.015).ConclusionMRN and quantitative data from T2 mapping can help in assessing nerve edema and provide a basis for clinical management.Keywords: Lumbar disc herniation, Lumbar radiculopathy, Lumbosacral plexus, Magnetic resonance neurography, Magnetic resonance imaging, T2 mapping

Background

Lumbosacral disc herniation (LDH) is a very common disease, with an estimated 4.03 million people worldwide suffering from symptomatic disc degeneration [1, 2]. Mechanical compression such as annulus fibrosus rupture and nucleus pulposus protrusion of lumbar intervertebral disc leads to compression or injury of the nerve roots of the corresponding lumbosacral plexus (LSP), resulting in clinical symptoms such as low back pain, leg pain, and leg numbness.

Magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) of nerve plexus, as one of the important means to assist in the diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy [3–7], can better show the anatomical course and shape of lumbosacral plexus [8]. It plays an increasingly important role in the diagnosis of peripheral nerve diseases. Compared with conventional lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), MRN can be used to observe the nerve root involvement of the corresponding segments caused by LDH, and the edema of the compressed nerve can be observed. Animal experiments have shown that in rats with lumbosacral nerve root pain, the corresponding level of dorsal root ganglion will show signs of swelling to a certain extent [9, 10].

Although MRN of lumbosacral plexus can better show the course of nerve root and whether there is edema or not, there are still a few cases that can’t well explain its clinical symptoms. The intervention of quantitative imaging is expected to solve this problem and can reduce the deviation of visual observation. At present, the commonly used quantitative methods for nerves mainly include T2 mapping and Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI). T2 mapping is often considered more stable than DTI for detecting peripheral nerve injury, as DTI, though highly sensitive [11, 12], may be affected by variations in devices and imaging parameters [13]. T2 mapping is a quantitative analysis technique that uses spin-echo sequences to collect signal intensities at multiple echo times (TE) under the same repetition time (TR), ultimately deriving the transverse relaxation time (T2 value) of tissues, reflecting changes in tissue free water content. The T2 value is positively correlated with the water content of the tissue, LDH leads to an increase in the T2 value of the nerve root on the compressed side, and the color scale on the T2 mapping pseudo-color image is correspondingly higher [14, 15].

In recent years, T2 mapping has been increasingly used to assess peripheral nerve diseases. The objective of this study was to visually visually assess the involvement of the nerve roots in patients with LDH by MRN and quantitatively assessed the T2 values of the involved nerve roots by T2 mapping and analyzed the correlation with clinical symptoms. The aim of this study was to evaluate the correlation between nerve root compression and clinical symptoms of lumbar disc herniation by lumbosacral plexus MRN combined with T2mapping.

Methods

This prospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval no. W2024-082-1) and was supported by the Medical Science Research Project Program of Hebei Province (grant no. 20240020).

Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Study population

We collected clinically diagnosed LDH patients from our hospital between October 2023 and May 2024. Inclusion criteria: single intervertebral disc herniation and unilateral ganglion compression confirmed by lumbar MRI, along with varying degrees of low back pain and/or limb radiation pain. Exclusion criteria: (1) lumbar trauma, tuberculosis, tumors, or other immune diseases; (2) history of lumbar surgery; (3) MRI contraindications, including claustrophobia, metal implants, and cochlear implants.

Clinical symptom assessment

All patients presented with unilateral nerve root compression caused by lumbar disc herniation (LDH). Prior to MRI examination, symptom severity was evaluated using a 0–10 point Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for three domains: low back pain, leg pain, and lower limb numbness. The VAS scores were categorized as follows: 0 (no symptoms), 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), and 7–10 (severe). The oswestry disability index (ODI), scored out of 100, was used to evaluate pain, daily self-care, lifting, walking, sitting, standing, sleep, sexuality, social activities, and travel ability.

MR imaging techniques

Lumbosacral plexus MRN and T2 mapping scans were performed using a 3.0T MR system (Philips Ingenia CX, Netherlands) with a 32-channel body coil. MRN sequences were applied to all patients, covering at least the L2-S2 nerve roots of the plexus. Scanning sequences included coronal 3D SHINKEI (TR 2200 ms, TE 170 ms, FOV 300 mm × 352 mm, acquisition time 8 min 50 s), axial 3D T2-weighted image driven equilibrium (3D-T2WI-DRIVE) (TR 2000 ms, TE 120 ms, FOV 181 mm × 202 mm, acquisition time 2 min), and coronal T2 mapping (TR 1800 ms, TE 13/26/39/52/65/78 ms, FOV 200 × 200 mm, 6 echoes, acquisition time 13 min 30 s).

MRN evaluation of the LSP

Two experienced musculoskeletal radiologists assessed nerve root involvement in LDH, categorizing nerves based on compression on the affected side versus no compression on the contralateral side. They evaluated LDH types (central, paracentral, lateral, extreme lateral) on axial 3D-T2WI-DRIVE images and analyzed signal changes in nerve roots on coronal 3D SHINKEI sequences, identifying abnormal T2 signals compared to unaffected roots. Subjects were classified into high signal and non-high signal groups based on LSP nerve root signal abnormalities observed in MR neurography images.

Image processing

Both the acquired 3D SHINKEI images and T2 mapping images were transferred to the Philips post-processing workstation, where the system automatically reconstructed the transverse relaxation time T2 mapping maps for each pixel and fused the 3D SHINKEI sequences with the T2 mapping maps. Sagittal fusion images were used to outline regions of interest (ROIs) along nerve cross-sections: at the ganglion, 1 cm pre-ganglionic, and 1 cm post-ganglionic levels [16]. ROIs covered entire cross-sectional areas to minimize peripheral vessel interference. T2 values were measured at these levels, averaged, and differences between compressed and contralateral uncompressed nerve roots were calculated from these T2 values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous data were assessed for normality; normally distributed data were presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range.

Categorical data were reported as n (%). Significance was set at P < 0.05. The following statistical analyses were conducted:

Correlation between findings from MRN imaging and clinical symptoms.

Correlation between findings from MRN imaging and clinical symptoms.

The L3-S1 LSP was evaluated using 3D SHINKEI sequence images, comparing T2 signal changes between the compressed and contralateral sides at the same level.

Nerve roots on the compressed side were classified into high signal and non-high signal groups. Clinical symptoms consistent with unilateral nerve root compression were categorized as positive, while those that did not meet this criterion were classified as negative. The relationship between nerve roots and clinical symptoms was analyzed, producing a four-cell data table, and statistical analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test.

2)Correlation analysis of T2 values with clinical symptoms and MRN image findings.

Correlation analysis of T2 values with clinical symptoms and MRN image findings.

T2 values of preganglionic, ganglionic, and postganglionic segments of nerve roots in both compressed and uncompressed groups were measured, with paired-sample t-tests applied to each group. The difference in T2 values was calculated by subtracting the compressed side from the uncompressed side. Correlation analyses were conducted between the T2 value differences of ganglionic, preganglionic, and postganglionic segments and ODI, low back pain, leg pain, and leg numbness VAS scores. A two-sample t-test compared the T2 value differences in postganglionic segments between MRN high signal and non-high signal groups.

Results

A total of 34 patients (20 males, 14 females) with LDH were enrolled, with a mean age of 59.6 ± 10.8 years. The characteristics of the patients are as follows: 16 (47.1%) cases had paracentral disc herniation, 9 (26.5%) cases had lateral herniation, and 9 (26.5%) cases had extreme lateral herniation. Among the compressed LSP nerve roots, there were 4 (11.8%) L3, 8 (23.5%) L4, 19 (55.9%) L5, and 3 (8.8%) S1 nerve roots. The ODI scores averaged 53.6 ± 20.9, and VAS scores were: low back pain 4.0 (3.0, 6.0), leg pain 7.0 (3.0, 8.0), leg numbness 4.0 (1.5, 6.3), expressed as median (interquartile range).

All cases had clinical symptoms of unilateral nerve compression caused by LDH. Analysis of T2 values of nerve roots in the compressed and uncompressed groups

In the compressed group, T2 values for the preganglionic segment were 88.26 ± 18.07 ms, and 87.58 ± 17.82 ms in the uncompressed group, showing no significant difference (P = 0.851). For the ganglionic segments, T2 values were 91.17 ± 14.11 ms (compressed) and 87.49 ± 11.40 ms (uncompressed), also with no significant difference (P = 0.069). Discussion

This study aimed to assess the correlation between nerve root compression and clinical symptoms of lumbar disc herniation (LDH) using magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) combined with T2 mapping. Our results demonstrated a significantly higher T2 value difference in compressed nerve roots in the MRN high-signal group compared to the non-high signal group, with visual MRN assessments aligning with quantitative T2 mapping findings. Clinical evaluation revealed a positive correlation between T2 value differences in the postganglionic segment and leg numbness (r = 0.35), though no significant correlations were found with other symptoms.

Lumbar disc herniation can compress the lumbosacral spinal nerve roots, leading to nerve root oedema. There are three possible reasons for this: firstly, the compression of the nerve roots leads to an increase in the permeability of the capillaries in the nerves, resulting in oedema of the nerve roots [17]; secondly, the increase in the pressure of the fluid in the nerve cells affects the nutrient delivery of the nerve roots [18]; Thirdly, unlike peripheral nerves, spinal nerves lack the barrier function of perineurium and epineurium, making them more susceptible to damage [19]. Compared with conventional MRI of the lumbar spine, lumbosacral plexus MRN can clearly visualize the anatomical location and morphology of the LSP, accurately assess nerve root compression in patients with LDH, and effectively observe nerve root oedema.

Oedema of the compressed nerve root is manifested on MRN of LSP as increased T2 signal and swelling and thickening of the nerve morphology. In 81.5% of the patients enrolled in our current study, the T2 high signal of the compressed lumbosacral nerve root correlated with the clinical symptoms of its nerve root. The increased T2 signal of the compressed nerve root can be assessed visually by MRN images of the LSP, which can determine the nerve root responsible for the symptoms and provide a basis for treatment by the clinician. Similar results were found in a study by Nico Sollmann [20]. However, sometimes there is not always a perfect correspondence between MRN imaging and clinical symptoms in patients with LDH.

In the present study, 5 (18.5%) subjects with high signal on MRN of the compressed nerve root compared with the contralateral uncompressed nerve root did not show clinical symptoms corresponding to the compressed nerve root, and 2 (28.6%) subjects with clinical symptoms of unilateral LSP compression did not have high T2 signal of the compressed nerve root on MRN images. There are still some limitations of lumbosacral plexus MRN, and the results may be influenced by the imaging physician’s diagnostic experience with peripheral nerve MRN and the degree of nerve root oedema. It is difficult to obtain a positive test result for nerve root oedema in patients with prolonged nerve root compression or mild oedema. Quantitative MRI techniques such as T2 mapping are expected to address these challenges to some extent by identifying nerve root oedema and determining the extent of oedema from the resulting data.

Currently, the primary quantitative assessment methods for lumbosacral plexus nerve injuries include diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and T2 mapping. Bruno et al. demonstrated that both DTI and T2 mapping provide valuable assessment of nerve injuries in both acute and subacute phases. Their study further evaluated the correlation between clinical parameters (EMG) and quantitative imaging metrics including fractional anisotropy (FA), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), and T2 mapping values [21]. Chen et al. confirmed the relationship between lumbosacral plexus nerve compression and T2 values in their T2 mapping study, though their work did not incorporate clinical symptom assessment [13]. Sato et al. utilized T2 ratio to establish a correlation between lumbosacral plexus abnormalities and clinical symptoms, particularly leg pain [22]. In the present study, we applied T2 mapping as a quantitative magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) technique to more objectively evaluate vasogenic edema in nerves and investigate its correlation with clinical symptoms. Additionally, by employing T2 value differences to represent the relative degree of T2 elevation, we achieved improved objectivity and accuracy in our results.

The T2 value of the nerve root can be obtained by T2 mapping sequence. The study of Sollmann [20] showed that the T2 value of nerve root on the affected side of patients with lumbosacral nerve root syndrome increased, and T2 value can be used as an objective index to evaluate lumbosacral nerve herniation. In the study, that the T2 value of the postganglionic nerve root on the compressed side was higher than that on the uncompressed side, suggesting that there was edema in the postganglionic nerve root on the compressed side of the patients with LDH. The higher the T2 value, the worse the degree of edema in the nerve root.

The most common clinical symptom in patients with LDH is lumbosacral radicular pain, which can cause pain, numbness and paresthesia in the innervated area [23]. Clinical symptom assessment is essential for the diagnosis of lumbosacral nerve injury due to the lack of surgical pathological evidence. Many researchers have used the ODI score and the VAS score to assess clinical symptoms. The ODI has high validity and reliability and is widely used throughout the world, making it the gold standard in spinal surgery [24–27]. Koivunen et al. confirmed that the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) provides a reliable assessment of disability severity.

Furthermore, their study demonstrated that telephone follow-up offers a practical alternative for monitoring patients’ clinical symptoms [28, 29]. The VAS score is mainly used to assess pain intensity and LDH disease stage [30, 31]. It is widely used with high acceptability and requires no specialized training [32]. It has been validated for assessing both low back pain and leg pain, and has also been employed in patients with low back pain accompanied by leg pain [31, 33, 34]. Wu et al. reported that the change in DTI parameters had a significant positive correlation with ODI score [35]. Additionally, Sato et al.‘s study compared T2 value and T2 ratio correlations with ODI scores and VAS scores, finding that T2 ratio better reflects the clinical symptoms of LDH patients experiencing leg pain than T2 value does [36].

These findings suggest that while edema from nerve compression is a consequence of the condition, it may not be the primary factor causing symptoms like pain.

There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, the sample size is small, which may introduce bias into the results. Therefore, future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our findings. Secondly, the T2 mapping sequence scanning time is too long, and some patients cannot cooperate well. Lastly, there is a need for further exploration of quantitative imaging methods that strongly correlate with clinical symptoms, thereby establishing a solid foundation for clinical treatment.

Conclusions

MRN images accurately depict the compression of nerve roots and their edema caused by lumbar disc herniation, reflecting the symptoms in most cases. T2 high signals observed in MRN images from compressed nerve roots have high sensitivity and moderate specificity for clinical symptom detection. T2 values measured by T2 mapping sequences in compressed nerve roots are significantly higher compared to the uncompressed side. The degree of T2 value elevation (difference in T2 values) shows weak or no correlation with clinical symptom scores. Therefore, MRN and quantitative data from T2 mapping play an important role in determining nerve root compression edema, and provide a basis for clinical treatment.
