The role of ECM mechanics in cancer mechanotransduction through unraveling the molecular machinery of integrins, FAK, and YAP signaling
Authors: Nourhan Hassan, Burkhard Greve, Martin Götte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanotransduction, the process by which cells convert mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals, is fundamental for maintaining tissue homeostasis and regulating physiological functions. The extracellular matrix (ECM) serves as a critical mediator of mechanotransduction, with its mechanical properties influencing cellular behaviour and function through complex molecular machinery. This comprehensive review examines the role of ECM mechanics in disease mechanotransduction, focusing on the molecular machinery of integrins, focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and YAP signaling pathways. We explore the structure and composition of the ECM, including detailed analysis of key components such as collagens, elastin, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, hyaluronic acid and matrix metalloproteinases. The review elucidates how integrins function as key mediators of mechanotransduction, the role of FAK in signal transduction, and the mechanosensitive functions of YAP/TAZ signaling. We examine the intricate crosstalk between these mechanotransduction pathways and their dysregulation in cancer. Finally, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies targeting mechanotransduction pathways and the challenges and opportunities for translating mechanotransduction research into clinical interventions. Understanding these complex mechanotransduction networks is crucial for developing novel therapeutic approaches to treat diseases characterized by altered tissue mechanics and dysregulated cellular responses to mechanical cues.

Introduction

Mechanotransduction is the result of molecular force transmission, in which cells convert stimuli by mechanical forces into biochemical signals, which is fundamental for maintaining tissue homeostasis and regulating physiological functions in response to varying mechanical environments [1, 2]. This remarkable cellular capability enables organisms to adapt to their physical surroundings and maintain structural integrity across diverse biological systems. For instance, sustained tension in skeletal muscle induces hypertrophy, enhancing muscle strength and functional capacity [3]. In the cardiovascular system, arterial walls thicken under elevated blood pressure to accommodate increased shear stress [4–6]. Similarly, bone remodelling relies on mechanical stress to increase bone mass and resist greater loads, highlighting the critical role of mechanical forces in skeletal homeostasis [7]. Lung function depends on alveolar cell stretching during respiratory cycles [8], while kidney function is modulated by the distension of renal epithelium in response to fluid dynamics [9].

Beyond tissue-specific roles, mechanotransduction is crucial in broader aspects of cell biology, including cell proliferation, differentiation and stem cell fate determination. Mechanical signals such as tissue stiffness guide embryogenesis and govern tissue architecture and mass in adult cells [10]. These processes enable cells to sense and adapt to their physical environment, responding to substrate stiffness, shear stress, compression and other mechanical cues [11]. Given its fundamental importance, defects in mechanotransduction are linked to a wide range of pathologies, making it a critical area of study for understanding disease mechanisms and developing therapeutic interventions [1].

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic network of proteins and polysaccharides that provides structural support and anchorage to cells and serves as a critical mediator of mechanotransduction [12, 13]. This complex three-dimensional scaffold not only provides physical support but also actively participates in cellular communication through mechanical cues. The ECM influences cellular behaviour and function through its mechanical properties, including stiffness, viscoelasticity, and structural organization [14]. In healthy tissues, mechanotransduction regulates essential cellular activities such as differentiation, proliferation, migration and apoptosis, thereby orchestrating the complex processes required for tissue development, maintenance, and repair [1]. ECM stiffness further guides stem cell differentiation, with cells preferentially differentiating into tissue types that match the stiffness of their substrate, a mechanism crucial for tissue regeneration and organ size regulation [15, 16].

Conversely, dysregulated mechanotransduction is implicated in the onset and progression of chronic conditions such as cancer and fibrosis [17, 18]. The relationship between mechanotransduction and these conditions highlights the need to understand how mechanical forces influence disease pathogenesis [17]. In cancer, tumour cells not only respond to but also modify their mechanical surroundings, creating a pathological feedback loop. For example, tumour cells often overproduce ECM components such as collagen, increasing stromal stiffness and creating a mechanically altered microenvironment [19, 20]. This elevated stiffness promotes tumour cell proliferation and disrupts epithelial structure, facilitating the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and enhancing cancer invasion and metastasis [21]. Tumour stiffening further drives cellular motility, enabling more effective metastasis [22]. Upon colonizing new sites, tumour cells continue secreting ECM materials, perpetuating stromal stiffening and supporting unchecked proliferation and motility in a vicious cycle [23].

Understanding the molecular mechanisms of mechanotransduction in both healthy and diseased states offers critical insights into therapeutic targets for managing chronic diseases [1, 17]. The complexity of these mechanisms involves multiple interconnected pathways and molecular players that work in concert to translate mechanical stimuli into appropriate cellular responses. This intricate network includes both passive and active mechanical inputs that collectively determine cellular fate and function [24, 25].

Mechanotransduction involves both passive and active mechanical inputs that work synergistically to regulate cellular behaviour [26, 27]. Passive inputs originate from ECM properties such as stiffness and viscoelasticity, establishing baseline mechanical stress levels within the cell and providing a constant mechanical context [28, 29]. These passive forces create a mechanical “set point” that influences cellular behaviour even in the absence of external perturbations. Active inputs, by contrast, stem from external forces such as tension, compression and shear stress that directly act on the cell and can rapidly alter cellular responses [30, 31]. These active forces can modulate the baseline stress established by passive inputs, resulting in dynamic changes in cellular function mediated through mechanosensitive protein signalling cascades [27, 30]. This dynamic interplay between passive and active mechanical forces is fundamental to how cells perceive and respond to their mechanical environment, ultimately shaping diverse biological processes from development to disease progression [32, 33].

Mechanisms of biological information transfer in ECM mechanotransduction

The ECM plays a dual role as both a structural scaffold and a reservoir of biochemical and mechanical cues that regulate cellular behaviour [34]. As a critical mediator of mechanotransduction, the ECM enables cells to convert mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals through sophisticated molecular mechanisms. Mechanotransduction within the ECM can be categorized into three main pathways: cellular mechanotransduction, bidirectional cell-ECM signaling and extracellular mechanotransduction. Each pathway underscores the intricate interplay between cells and their microenvironment in maintaining mechanical homeostasis [18, 28].

Cellular mechanotransduction

Cellular mechanotransduction establishes a direct link between the intracellular environment and the ECM through specialized transmembrane proteins, primarily integrins [35]. These heterodimeric receptors serve as molecular bridges, anchoring cells to ECM components such as collagen and fibronectin while simultaneously connecting to the actin cytoskeleton through a complex network of adaptor proteins [13, 36]. This physical connection enables the transmission of mechanical forces from the extracellular environment directly into the cell interior, where they can influence cellular behaviour and gene expression [37, 38].

Mechanical cues, such as matrix stiffness or tensile forces, induce conformational changes in integrins, triggering the activation of downstream signalling pathways mediated by proteins including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) [1, 39]. These signalling cascades convert mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals, enabling cellular responses that maintain tissue homeostasis and adapt to changing mechanical environments. The sophistication of this system allows cells to distinguish between different types of mechanical stimuli and respond appropriately to each [40, 41].

Integrins and their associated proteins establish a dynamic feedback loop in which ECM mechanical resistance influences intracellular signalling and, conversely, cellular forces modify the ECM structure and properties [1, 42, 43]. This bidirectional communication ensures that cells can both respond to and actively shape their mechanical environment, fostering dynamic cellular adaptations that are essential for tissue development, maintenance and repair.

Bidirectional cell-ECM communication

The ECM and cells engage in sophisticated reciprocal signalling to maintain mechanical equilibrium and tissue functionality [14, 44]. This bidirectional communication represents one of the most elegant examples of biological feedback systems, where mechanical and biochemical signals are integrated to maintain tissue homeostasis. Cells sense ECM stiffness through integrin-mediated interactions, which regulate fundamental processes such as proliferation, differentiation and migration [45, 46]. The ability of cells to “read” the mechanical property of their environment allows them to make appropriate decisions about their behaviour and fate.

Conversely, cells actively remodel the ECM by secreting enzymes, growth factors and structural proteins that modify its composition and mechanical properties [47, 48]. This active remodelling capability enables cells to create microenvironments that support their specific functional requirements. The bidirectional communication between cells and the ECM encodes mechanical “memory” into the matrix, creating a persistent record of previous mechanical experiences that can influence the behaviour of adjacent cells and sustain long-term homeostasis [48–50].

Dysregulation of this feedback loop contributes to pathological states, such as cancer, where ECM stiffening accelerates tumour progression, therapy resistance [51] and metastasis through a self-reinforcing cycle of mechanical and biochemical alterations [48–50]. Understanding these feedback mechanisms is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies that can interrupt pathological cycles and restore normal tissue function.

Extracellular mechanotransduction

The ECM serves not only as a structural substrate for cellular attachment but also as an active integrator of mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals through force-sensitive proteins embedded within the matrix itself [1, 52]. This represents a sophisticated level of mechanotransduction that occurs independently of direct cellular involvement, highlighting the dynamic nature of the ECM as a signalling platform.

For example, tension in collagen fibres protects them from degradation by matrix metalloproteinases, preserving ECM integrity during processes including wound healing and tissue remodelling [53–55]. This mechanical protection mechanism ensures that load-bearing structures remain intact under physiological stress while allowing for controlled remodelling when mechanical forces are reduced. Additionally, mechanical activation of latent transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) exemplifies how the ECM stores and releases biochemical signals in response to mechanical cues [56–58]. This mechanism allows the ECM to serve as a mechanical sensor that can release bioactive molecules when tissue mechanics are altered, providing a direct link between mechanical and biochemical signalling.

These mechanisms underscore the ECM’s role as a dynamic regulator of mechanotransduction, crucial for tissue remodelling and homeostasis. The ability of the ECM to both sense and respond to mechanical forces makes it a central player in maintaining tissue function and adapting to changing mechanical demands.

ECM structure and composition

The extracellular matrix represents one of the most complex and dynamic biological structures, comprising a sophisticated network of proteins, glycoproteins, proteoglycans and polysaccharides that collectively determine tissue mechanical properties and cellular behaviour [13, 18]. Understanding the structure and composition of the ECM is fundamental to comprehending how alterations in ECM composition and stiffness contribute to disease progression. The ECM is not merely a passive scaffold but an active participant in cellular communication, providing both structural support and regulatory signals that influence cell fate and function [13].

The mechanical properties of the ECM are determined by the relative abundance, organization and cross-linking of its constituent molecules [59, 60]. In mammals, the ECM comprises approximately 300 proteins, 200 glycoproteins and 30 proteoglycans, creating a complex three-dimensional network that varies significantly between tissues [13]. This diversity allows different tissues to achieve the specific mechanical properties required for their unique functions, from the flexibility of skin to the rigidity of bone.

Alterations in ECM composition and stiffness are central to many disease processes. In cancer, increased collagen deposition and cross-linking lead to tissue stiffening that promotes tumour progression [61, 62]. In fibrotic diseases, excessive ECM accumulation results in organ dysfunction and failure [63]. In cardiovascular disease, changes in arterial ECM composition contribute to vessel stiffening and hypertension [64, 65]. Key ECM components and their roles in mechanotransduction

ECM components

Collagens: the structural foundation

Collagen represents the most abundant protein in the human body and serves as the primary structural component of the ECM, providing tissues with tensile strength and mechanical stability [77]. The collagen family comprises over 25 different types, each with specialized functions and tissue distributions that reflect the diverse mechanical requirements of different organs and tissues [78]. The most common types include fibrillar collagens such as type I and type III, which form the backbone of most connective tissues.

Collagen organization and cross-linking profoundly influence tissue mechanics and cellular responses through multiple mechanisms [79, 80]. The hierarchical structure of collagen, from individual molecules to fibrils to fibres, allows for sophisticated mechanical properties that can be fine-tuned through various post-translational modifications [80]. Collagen molecules are approximately 300 nm long and 1.5 nm in diameter, and they assemble into fibrils ranging from 20 to 100 nm in diameter, which then bundle together to form fibres that can reach 0.5–20 µm in diameter [80, 81].

The mechanical properties of collagens are further modulated by cross-linking, which occurs through both enzymatic and nonenzymatic processes. Enzymatic cross-linking involves lysyl oxidase and lysyl hydroxylase, which create aldol condensation products and pyridinium crosslinks that increase collagen stability and tensile strength [82–84]. Nonenzymatic cross-linking, particularly through advanced glycation end products (AGEs), increases with age and disease, contributing to tissue stiffening and altered cellular responses [85, 86].

Specific collagen types play distinct roles in disease pathogenesis, particularly in cancer invasion and fibrosis [87, 88]. Type I collagen, the most abundant collagen in the body, provides structural integrity to skin, bone, tendons, and many other tissues. In cancer, increased type I collagen deposition creates a stiff microenvironment that promotes tumour cell invasion and metastasis [89, 90]. The alignment and density of type I collagen fibres can create “highways” that facilitate cancer cell migration, while increased cross-linking makes the matrix more resistant to degradation [91].

Type III collagen, which is more flexible than type I, is often found in tissues that require elasticity, such as blood vessels and skin. In fibrotic diseases, the ratio of type I to type III collagen is often altered, with increased type I collagen contributing to tissue stiffening and loss of function [92]. Type IV collagen is a major component of basement membranes and plays crucial roles in maintaining epithelial and endothelial barrier function. Alterations in type IV collagen are associated with cancer metastasis, as tumour cells must breach basement membranes to invade surrounding tissues [93].

The mechanical properties of collagen are also influenced by its interaction with other ECM components. Collagen fibrillogenesis is aided by associations with fibronectin, type V collagen and proteoglycans such as decorin and biglycan [14, 94]. These interactions not only affect the mechanical properties of the resulting matrix but also influence how cells interact with and respond to the collagen network.

Elastin: providing tissue elasticity and resilience

Elastin is a key ECM protein responsible for tissue elasticity and resilience, providing tissues with the ability to stretch and recoil without permanent deformation [95]. This remarkable protein is essential for the proper function of tissues that undergo repeated mechanical deformation, such as skin, lungs and blood vessels. Elastin’s unique properties arise from its highly cross-linked structure and hydrophobic amino acid composition, which create a rubber-like network that can extend up to 150% of its original length without failure [96–98].

The role of elastin in maintaining tissue integrity extends beyond simple mechanical support. Elastin provides reversible deformation under mechanical stress, allowing tissues to return to their original shape after the removal of applied forces [95]. This property is crucial for organs that must maintain their structure while accommodating dynamic mechanical loads. In the cardiovascular system, elastin in arterial walls allow vessels to expand during systole and recoil during diastole, contributing to the maintenance of diastolic pressure and efficient blood flow [69].

Elastin is deposited primarily during development and early adulthood, with very limited turnover in healthy adult tissues. This stability is both an advantage and a vulnerability, as elastin provides long-term mechanical memory but cannot be easily replaced when damaged [95, 99]. The half-life of elastin in human arteries is estimated to be 50–70 years, making it one of the most stable proteins in the body [96].

Alterations in elastin content or organization contribute significantly to disease states characterized by altered tissue mechanics, particularly fibrosis. In pulmonary fibrosis, the normal elastin network is disrupted and replaced by excessive collagen deposition, leading to loss of lung elasticity and impaired respiratory function [100, 101]. The destruction of elastin by elastases released during inflammatory processes contributes to the irreversible nature of many fibrotic diseases [100].

In cardiovascular disease, elastin degradation and fragmentation contribute to arterial stiffening, a major risk factor for hypertension and cardiovascular events [69]. Age-related changes in elastin, including increased cross-linking and fragmentation, contribute to the progressive stiffening of arteries that occurs with ageing [102, 103]. These changes not only affect vascular function but also influence cardiac workload and overall cardiovascular health.

The loss of elastin integrity also affects cellular behaviour and mechanotransduction. Cells cultured on elastin-rich substrates exhibit different morphologies, migration patterns and gene expression profiles compared with cells on rigid substrates [1, 46]. The disruption of elastin networks in disease states can therefore have profound effects on cellular behaviour and tissue function beyond the immediate mechanical consequences.

Large ECM glycoproteins: mediating cell-ECM interactions

Glycoproteins represent a diverse class of ECM components that play crucial roles in mediating cell–ECM interactions and modulating cellular responses to mechanical cues [18, 94]. The major ECM glycoproteins include fibronectin, laminin and thrombospondin, each with distinct functions and binding specificities that contribute to the complexity of cell–matrix interactions [18, 104, 105].

Fibronectin is perhaps the most well-studied ECM glycoprotein, serving as a key mediator of cell adhesion, migration and matrix assembly [106, 107]. This large, multidomain protein contains binding sites for multiple integrins, particularly α5β1 and αvβ3, as well as binding domains for other ECM components such as collagen and heparin [108, 109]. Fibronectin exists in multiple isoforms generated by alternative splicing, allowing for tissue-specific functions and responses to different physiological conditions [110, 111].

The role of fibronectin in mechanotransduction extends beyond simple cell adhesion. Fibronectin can undergo conformational changes in response to mechanical forces, exposing cryptic binding sites and altering its interactions with cellular receptors [43, 106, 112]. This mechanosensitive behaviour allows fibronectin to serve as a molecular switch that can modulate cellular responses based on the mechanical environment. In cancer, altered fibronectin expression and organization contribute to tumour progression by promoting cell migration and invasion [28, 113].

Laminin is the major glycoprotein component of basement membranes, providing structural support and serving as a barrier between different tissue compartments [114, 115]. Different laminin isoforms are expressed in tissue-specific patterns, with each isoform having distinct binding specificities for integrins and other receptors such as Syndecan-1 [116, 117]. Moreover, the different domains of laminins allow for multiple interactions with ECM constituents of the basement membrane, thereby contributing to the formation of a crosslinked ECM network [28, 117]. Laminins play crucial roles in maintaining epithelial and endothelial barrier function, and alterations in laminin expression or structure are associated with various diseases, including cancer metastasis and muscular dystrophies [118–120].

The mechanotransduction properties of laminin differ significantly from those of fibronectin. While fibronectin generally promotes cell spreading and the formation of large focal adhesions, laminin tends to promote more dynamic cell–matrix interactions with smaller, more transient adhesions [121, 122]. This difference has important implications for cellular behaviour, as cells on laminin substrates often exhibit enhanced migration but reduced contractility compared with cells on fibronectin [123, 124].

Thrombospondin represents another important class of ECM glycoproteins with complex roles in mechanotransduction and disease [18, 125]. Thrombospondins can interact with multiple cellular receptors and ECM components, serving as modulators of cell–matrix interactions rather than primary structural components [126, 127]. In cancer, thrombospondin-1 has been identified as both a tumour suppressor and a promoter of metastasis, depending on the cellular context and disease stage [128, 129].

The contribution of specific glycoproteins to disease progression has made them attractive targets for therapeutic intervention. Strategies targeting fibronectin–integrin interactions have shown promise in cancer therapy, while approaches aimed at restoring normal laminin function are being explored for treating basement membrane disorders [130, 131]. Understanding the mechanotransduction properties of these glycoproteins is essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Proteoglycans: maintaining ECM hydration and viscoelastic properties

Proteoglycans represent a unique class of glycosylated ECM components characterized by a core protein with covalently attached glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains [132, 133]. These complex molecules play essential roles in maintaining ECM hydration, viscoelastic properties, and cellular signaling, making them critical regulators of tissue mechanics and cell behaviour [134, 135]. The diversity of proteoglycans, with over 30 different types identified in mammals, reflects their specialized functions in different tissues and physiological contexts [133].

The role of proteoglycans in maintaining ECM hydration stems from the highly negatively charged nature of their GAG chains, which attract and bind water molecules [136]. This property is particularly important in cartilage, where the proteoglycan aggrecan can bind up to 50 times its weight in water, providing the tissue with its characteristic resistance to compression [137, 138]. The hydration properties of proteoglycans also contribute to the viscoelastic behaviour of tissues, allowing them to exhibit both elastic and viscous responses to mechanical loading [136].

Alterations in proteoglycan expression or structure significantly influence cell–ECM interactions and mechanotransduction pathways. Changes in proteoglycan composition can alter the mechanical properties of tissues, affecting how cells sense and respond to their environment [18, 136, 139]. In osteoarthritis, the loss of proteoglycans from cartilage leads to tissue softening and altered cellular responses that contribute to disease progression [140, 141].

Specific proteoglycans have been implicated in disease-associated mechanotransduction processes, with syndecans and glypicans receiving particular attention [136, 142]. Syndecans are transmembrane proteoglycans that serve as coreceptors for various growth factors and ECM components, modulating cellular responses to both biochemical and mechanical stimuli [143, 144]. The syndecan family includes four members (syndecan-1 through syndecan-4), each with distinct expression patterns and functions [145, 146].

Syndecan-4 has been particularly well-studied in the context of mechanotransduction, as it localizes to focal adhesions and can modulate integrin signaling [144]. Changes in syndecan-4 expression have been associated with various diseases, including cancer, where it can promote tumour progression by enhancing cell migration and invasion [147, 148]. The ability of syndecans to bind multiple ligands simultaneously makes them important integrators of complex signaling networks [149].

Glypicans represent another important family of proteoglycans that are attached to the cell surface via glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors [150, 151]. Unlike syndecans, glypicans are located primarily in membrane microdomains and can be released from the cell surface by phospholipases [133, 152]. Glypicans play important roles in developmental signaling pathways, particularly those involving Wnt and Hedgehog signaling [150, 153].

The mechanotransduction functions of proteoglycans extend beyond their structural roles. Many proteoglycans can undergo conformational changes in response to mechanical forces, altering their interactions with other ECM components and cellular receptors [18, 136]. This mechanosensitive behaviour allows proteoglycans to serve as molecular sensors that can translate mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals [131].

Hyaluronic acid: regulating cell behaviour and tissue hydration

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a unique nonproteinaceous ECM component that plays crucial roles in tissue hydration, lubrication and cellular regulation [75, 136, 154]. Unlike other ECM components, HA is not covalently attached to a protein core but exists as a free polysaccharide chain of negatively charged repetitive disaccharide units that can reach enormous molecular weights, sometimes exceeding several million daltons [155, 156]. This remarkable molecule can bind up to 1000 times its weight in water, making it a critical determinant of tissue hydration and mechanical properties [75, 156].

The role of hyaluronic acid in tissue hydration extends beyond simple water retention. HA creates a hydrated, gel-like matrix that provides tissues with unique viscoelastic properties, allowing them to resist compression while maintaining flexibility [157, 158]. This is particularly important in tissues such as cartilage and the vitreous humour of the eye, where HA contributes to the tissue’s ability to withstand mechanical loads while maintaining transparency and function [157, 159, 160].

Hyaluronic acid regulates cell behaviour through interactions with specific cell surface receptors, particularly CD44 and RHAMM (Receptor for Hyaluronan-Mediated Motility) [161, 162]. These interactions can influence fundamental cellular processes, including migration, proliferation and differentiation [162]. The molecular weight of HA is a critical determinant of its biological activity, with high molecular weight HA generally promoting tissue homeostasis and antiinflammatory responses, while low molecular weight HA fragments can promote inflammation and tissue remodelling [76, 158].

The mechanotransduction properties of HA are closely linked to its ability to form complex networks with other ECM components. HA can interact with proteoglycans such as aggrecan and versican, forming large aggregates that contribute to tissue mechanical properties [46, 104, 136]. These interactions are stabilized by link proteins that prevent the dissociation of HA-proteoglycan complexes under mechanical stress [163, 164].

The role of hyaluronic acid in disease contexts, particularly cancer progression and tissue inflammation, has received considerable attention [154]. In cancer, HA accumulation in the tumour microenvironment can promote tumour progression through multiple mechanisms. High levels of HA can increase interstitial fluid pressure, facilitating cancer cell invasion and metastasis [165, 166]. HA can also promote angiogenesis and immune evasion, contributing to tumour growth and progression [166]. Moreover, HA is also implicated in stem cell function and modulation of the cancer stem cell phenotype, linked to therapeutic resistance [167].

In inflammatory diseases, the balance between HA synthesis and degradation is often disrupted, leading to altered tissue mechanics and cellular responses [76, 168]. Inflammatory mediators can upregulate HA synthesis while also promoting its degradation, creating a dynamic environment that can either promote healing or contribute to chronic inflammation [76, 169, 170]. The size distribution of HA fragments in inflamed tissues can influence the inflammatory response, with smaller fragments generally promoting pro-inflammatory responses [76, 171].

The therapeutic potential of HA has led to its use in various clinical applications, from joint injections for osteoarthritis to wound healing applications [172]. Understanding the mechanotransduction properties of HA and its role in disease processes is essential for optimizing these therapeutic approaches and developing new HA-based interventions.

Proteases and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs): dynamic ECM remodelling

The dynamic nature of the ECM requires sophisticated mechanisms for controlled degradation and remodelling, processes that are primarily mediated by proteases, with matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) playing the most prominent role [173]. These enzymes are essential for normal physiological processes such as development, wound healing and tissue remodelling, but their dysregulation contributes significantly to pathological conditions including cancer metastasis and tissue fibrosis [48, 174].

Matrix metalloproteinases represent the main family of enzymes involved in ECM degradation, with 23 human MMPs identified to date [174–176]. These zinc-dependent endopeptidases are capable of cleaving virtually all ECM components, making them powerful regulators of tissue structure and function [60]. MMPs are produced as inactive zymogens and require activation through proteolytic cleavage or chemical modification, providing multiple levels of regulation for their activity [176, 177].

The structure of MMPs includes several conserved domains that contribute to their function and regulation. All MMPs contain a signal peptide for secretion, a propeptide domain with a cysteine switch motif that maintains the enzyme in an inactive state and a catalytic domain containing the zinc-binding site [176, 177]. Most MMPs also contain a hemopexin-like domain that contributes to substrate specificity and protein–protein interactions [178]. Some MMPs, known as membrane-type MMPs (MT-MMPs), are anchored to the cell surface and can activate other MMPs, creating proteolytic cascades [179]. These proteases can induce proteolytic cleavage of cell surface (signaling) receptors in a process called ectodomain shedding, affecting cell-ECM interactions and cell signaling [180].

The regulation of MMP activity occurs at multiple levels, including transcriptional control, post-translational modification, compartmentalization and inhibition by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) [181]. This multi-layered regulation is essential because uncontrolled MMP activity can lead to excessive tissue destruction and disease. The balance between MMP activity and TIMP inhibition is particularly important in maintaining tissue homeostasis [182, 183].

Dysregulation of protease activity contributes to ECM remodelling in diseases such as cancer metastasis and tissue fibrosis through several mechanisms. In cancer, increased MMP expression and activity facilitate tumour cell invasion by degrading basement membranes and ECM barriers [48, 184, 185]. MMPs can also release growth factors and bioactive molecules sequestered in the ECM, promoting tumour growth and angiogenesis [186, 187]. The ability of cancer cells to upregulate specific MMPs, such as MMP-2 and MMP-9, is associated with increased metastatic potential [188].

In fibrotic diseases, the balance between ECM synthesis and degradation is disrupted, leading to excessive matrix accumulation [188, 189]. While increased collagen synthesis is a major contributor to fibrosis, altered MMP activity also plays important roles. In some cases, MMP activity is reduced, leading to impaired matrix turnover and accumulation of abnormal ECM [54, 190]. In other cases, specific MMPs may be upregulated but unable to effectively degrade the highly cross-linked collagen that characterizes fibrotic tissues [55, 190].

Carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, including heparanase and hyaluronidases, represent another important class of proteases involved in ECM remodelling and mechanotransduction [184]. Heparanase cleaves heparan sulfate chains on proteoglycans, affecting the structure and function of the glycocalyx and basement membranes [191, 192]. This enzyme plays important roles in cancer metastasis, inflammation and angiogenesis by modulating the availability of growth factors and the integrity of tissue barriers [193, 194].

Hyaluronidases degrade hyaluronic acid, affecting tissue hydration and the mechanical properties of the ECM [195, 196]. These enzymes are important in processes such as fertilization, where they help sperm penetrate the cumulus cell layer, and in pathological conditions such as cancer, where they can facilitate tumour cell invasion [162, 197]. The products of hyaluronic acid degradation, particularly low molecular weight fragments, can have pro-inflammatory effects that contribute to disease progression [76, 171].

Therapeutic strategies targeting protease activity for modulating ECM remodelling in chronic disorders have shown considerable promise [174]. MMP inhibitors were among the first targeted therapies developed for cancer treatment, although early clinical trials were disappointing owing to lack of specificity and side effects [198]. More recent approaches have focused on developing selective inhibitors that target specific MMPs or specific aspects of MMP function [199].

In fibrotic diseases, therapeutic strategies have included both MMP inhibition and MMP activation, depending on the specific disease context [200, 201]. For example, in liver fibrosis, promoting the activity of MMPs that can degrade fibrotic matrix has shown therapeutic benefit [190]. Conversely, in diseases characterized by excessive matrix degradation, such as emphysema, MMP inhibition may be beneficial [202].

The development of more sophisticated therapeutic approaches requires a better understanding of the specific roles of different proteases in disease processes and the development of more selective inhibitors or activators. Recent advances in drug delivery systems have also opened new possibilities for targeting proteases in specific tissues or cellular compartments [203].

Molecular machinery in mechanotransduction

The conversion of mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals requires sophisticated molecular machinery that can sense, transmit and amplify mechanical forces [204–206]. Integrins serve as the primary mechanosensitive receptors, functioning as transmembrane bridges that connect the ECM to the intracellular cytoskeleton [207, 208]. These heterodimeric proteins are uniquely positioned to sense changes in ECM mechanics and transmit this information to the cell interior through conformational changes and clustering events [18]. The ability of integrins to undergo force-induced conformational changes allows them to function as molecular force sensors that can distinguish between different mechanical environments [209].

Focal adhesion kinase represents a critical signaling node that integrates mechanical and biochemical signals at sites of cell-ECM adhesion [207, 210–212]. These nonreceptor protein kinase is activated in response to integrin engagement and mechanical force, initiating signaling cascades that regulate fundamental cellular processes including migration, proliferation and survival [207, 210, 212]. The strategic positioning of FAK at focal adhesion allows it to serve as a molecular switch that can rapidly respond to changes in the mechanical environment [207, 212].

YAP and TAZ function as mechanosensitive transcriptional coactivators that translate mechanical signals into changes in gene expression [11]. These proteins can shuttle between the cytoplasm and nucleus in response to mechanical cues, allowing cells to mount transcriptional responses to changes in their mechanical environment [213].

Additional proteins contribute to the mechanotransduction machinery, including vinculin, which stabilizes focal adhesion and reinforces integrin–cytoskeleton bonds under mechanical stress [214]. Talins serve as critical linker proteins that connect integrins to the actin cytoskeleton and can undergo force-induced conformational changes that regulate signaling [215]. Rho GTPases regulate cytoskeletal dynamics and contractility, providing a mechanism for cells to actively generate and respond to mechanical forces [209]. Mechanosensitive ion channels contribute to rapid cellular responses to mechanical stimuli, allowing for immediate changes in cellular signaling and homeostasis [216].

Understanding these mechanotransduction pathways is crucial for identifying therapeutic targets to manage chronic disorders characterized by altered tissue mechanics. By exploring the molecular machinery of mechanotransduction, including integrins, FAK and YAP, researchers gain insights into how mechanical forces influence disease progression, enabling the development of innovative treatments to improve outcomes for patients with mechanically driven diseases.

Integrins: key mediators of mechanotransduction

Integrins represent the primary interface between cells and the extracellular matrix, serving as sophisticated mechanosensitive receptors that are essential for mediating adhesion, migration, proliferation, differentiation and survival [207, 208]. These transmembrane proteins function as mechanosensors, converting ECM mechanical stresses into intracellular biochemical signals through a process that involves conformational changes, clustering and the recruitment of signalling molecules [43]. By integrating mechanical and biochemical inputs, integrins regulate cellular behaviour and adaptation to environmental changes, playing a pivotal role in cellular mechanotransduction and tissue homeostasis [37, 217].

The structural organization of integrins as heterodimeric receptors composed of α- and β-subunits provides the molecular basis for their mechanosensitive properties [13, 43]. With 18 α-subunits and 8 β-subunits forming at least 24 distinct α–β combinations, integrins exhibit remarkable specificity for different ECM ligands, allowing cells to respond appropriately to diverse mechanical environments [218, 219]. This specificity is achieved through distinct binding preferences: collagen-binding integrins such as α1β1 and α2β1, laminin-binding integrins including α3β1 and α6β1 and fibronectin-binding integrins such as α5β1 and αvβ3 [207, 220–223].

The molecular architecture of integrins includes a large ectodomain that extends into the extracellular space, membrane-spanning helices that anchor the protein in the cell membrane and short cytoplasmic tails that connect to the intracellular signalling machinery [207, 215]. Ligand-binding sites are located either at the interface between α- and β-subunits or within a specialized αI domain, depending on the specific integrin type [224]. This structural organization allows integrins to undergo conformational changes in response to mechanical forces, switching between low-affinity and high-affinity binding states [225, 226].

The cytoplasmic domains of integrins, despite their relatively small size, serve as critical platforms for the assembly of complex signalling networks [149]. These domains interact with adaptor molecules such as talin and kindlin, which function as activators of integrin signalling, as well as inhibitory proteins such as filamin [227–230]. The dynamic nature of these interactions allows for rapid modulation of integrin-mediated signalling in response to changing mechanical conditions [231].

Integrin activation involves sophisticated conformational changes that transition integrins from a bent, low-affinity state to an extended, high-affinity ligand-binding state [232]. This activation process can be triggered by inside-out signalling from the cell or by outside-in signalling from the ECM, providing multiple mechanisms for regulating integrin function [207]. The conformational changes associated with integrin activation expose binding sites for cytoplasmic proteins and alter the mechanical properties of the integrin-ECM connection [225].

Activated integrins dynamically link to the actomyosin system through a complex network of adaptor proteins, forming what has been termed a “molecular clutch” those bridges ECM-bound integrins with the force-generating cytoskeleton [232]. This connection allows for the transmission of forces in both directions: from the ECM to the cytoskeleton and from the cytoskeleton to the ECM. The strength and dynamics of this connection can be modulated by mechanical forces, providing a mechanism for force-dependent regulation of cellular behaviour [121, 225].

The interaction between integrins and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) represents a critical node in mechanotransduction signalling [233, 234]. FAK can interact with integrins either directly or through adaptor proteins such as vinculin and paxillin, initiating downstream signalling pathways that influence cellular processes including migration, proliferation and survival [235, 236]. The recruitment and activation of FAK at integrin-containing adhesions is enhanced by mechanical force, providing a mechanism for force-dependent signal amplification [214].

The mechanosensitive properties of integrins extend beyond simple force transmission to include the ability to discriminate between different types of mechanical stimuli [52]. Integrins can respond to substrate stiffness, applied forces, and ECM topography, allowing cells to extract detailed information about their mechanical environment. This mechanosensitive discrimination is achieved through the cooperative effects of multiple integrin-ECM bonds and the dynamic nature of integrin clustering [35, 237].

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK): central signalling hubs

Focal adhesion kinase is a nonreceptor protein tyrosine kinase that serves as a critical integrator of mechanotransduction signals, bridging mechanical stimuli from the ECM with intracellular signalling pathways [212, 234]. Located at focal adhesion sites – dynamic protein complexes that anchor cells to the ECM – FAK regulates fundamental cellular processes including migration, proliferation, survival and differentiation, thereby maintaining tissue homeostasis and contributing to pathological processes such as cancer and fibrosis [238].

The strategic positioning of FAK at focal adhesions makes it uniquely suited to function as a mechanosensitive signalling hub. FAK activation occurs through autophosphorylation at tyrosine 397 upon integrin binding to ECM components such as collagen, fibronectin or laminin [238, 239]. This initial phosphorylation event creates a binding site for Src family kinases, leading to the formation of a FAK-Src signalling complex that can phosphorylate additional tyrosine residues on FAK and other focal adhesion proteins [234, 240].

The activation of FAK is enhanced by mechanical forces, providing a direct link between ECM mechanics and intracellular signalling [241]. Mechanical tension applied to focal adhesions increases FAK autophosphorylation and kinase activity, demonstrating the mechanosensitive nature of this signalling pathway [242]. This force-dependent activation allows cells to respond proportionally to the mechanical properties of their environment [212].

Activated FAK forms complexes with numerous adaptor proteins, including paxillin, talin and vinculin, facilitating cytoskeletal reorganization and focal adhesion maturation [243]. These protein–protein interactions are dynamic and can be modulated by mechanical forces, allowing for rapid adaptation to changing mechanical conditions [231]. The scaffolding function of FAK extends beyond simple protein recruitment to include the spatial organization of signalling complexes that can integrate multiple inputs [244].

FAK serves as a critical bridge between mechanical signals from integrins and downstream signalling pathways, including RhoA/ROCK, PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways [234]. The RhoA/ROCK pathway regulates cytoskeletal contractility and focal adhesion dynamics, providing a mechanism for cells to actively generate and respond to mechanical forces [245, 246]. The PI3K/AKT pathway promotes cell survival and proliferation, while MAPK pathways regulate gene expression and cellular differentiation [247, 248].

YAP/TAZ signaling: mechanosensitive transcriptional regulation

Yes-associated protein (YAP), a 65 kDa transcription factor present in all mammalian cells, was initially identified as a binding partner of the proto-oncogene Yes, hence its name [249]. The discovery of YAP’s mechanosensitive properties emerged from parallel studies in Drosophila that identified the Hippo signaling pathway, which regulates Yorkie, the Drosophila homolog of YAP [250, 251]. This evolutionary conservation highlights the fundamental importance of YAP/TAZ signaling in mechanotransduction across species.

The Hippo pathway in Drosophila consists of a kinase cascade including Hippo, Salvador and Warts, which regulates Yorkie through phosphorylation, acting as a potent inhibitor of cell proliferation and tissue growth [251]. Mutations or deletions in any of these genes result in uncontrolled cell proliferation and organ enlargement, demonstrating the critical role of this pathway in growth control [252, 253]. The identification of mammalian homologs – MST1/2 (Hippo), Sav (Salvador) and LATS1/2 (Warts) – confirmed the existence of a similar mechanism in mammals [253].

In mammals, disruption of the Hippo pathway leads to excessive cell proliferation and organ overgrowth due to unregulated YAP activity, while YAP deletion results in reduced cell proliferation and underdeveloped organs [254]. Transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) serves as a paralog of YAP in vertebrates, performing analogous functions in mechanotransduction and growth control [249, 255]. Although the Hippo gene is unique to Drosophila, the term “Hippo pathway” is widely used to describe the conserved mammalian signaling pathway regulating YAP/TAZ activity [249].

The Hippo pathway regulates key cellular processes, including proliferation, cell size, migration and angiogenesis, making it a central regulator of tissue homeostasis [251–253]. The regulatory cascade begins with the NF2 kinase protein and involves serine/threonine kinases MST1/2 and LATS1/2, which activate adapter proteins SAV1 and MOB1 [256, 257]. This pathway suppresses YAP and TAZ activity by phosphorylating and sequestering them in the cytoplasm, thereby preventing nuclear transcription and growth-promoting gene expression [249, 254].

YAP and TAZ function as pivotal regulators of cellular mechanotransduction, sensing mechanical cues such as ECM stiffness and cell–cell contact and relaying them to modulate transcriptional programs that control cell proliferation, differentiation and transformation [258]. The mechanosensitive nature of YAP/TAZ allows cells to adjust their behaviour on the basis of the mechanical properties of their environment, making these proteins central to mechanotransduction [259].

When localized to the nucleus, YAP and TAZ act as transcriptional coactivators that can promote processes including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), malignancy and metastasis [260, 261]. Nuclear YAP/TAZ activation is associated with other signaling pathways, including PI3K-AKT and Wnt signaling, particularly under conditions of increased ECM stiffness, altered cell adhesion and disrupted mechanical homeostasis [250, 262]. The integration of these pathways allows for sophisticated responses to complex mechanical environments [263].

YAP/TAZ activity is modulated by both Hippo-dependent and Hippo-independent pathways in response to mechanical stimuli [11]. Hippo-independent regulation includes direct effects of cytoskeletal tension and focal adhesion dynamics on YAP/TAZ localization. This dual regulation provides multiple mechanisms for fine-tuning YAP/TAZ activity in response to different types of mechanical stimuli [264].

In the vascular system, YAP/TAZ plays critical roles in endothelial and smooth muscle cell mechanotransduction [265]. Loss of YAP/TAZ in vascular smooth muscle cells results in aneurysm formation in mice, highlighting their importance in maintaining vascular integrity under mechanical stress [265, 266]. The mechanosensitive regulation of YAP/TAZ in endothelial cells contributes to vascular remodelling in response to changes in blood flow and pressure [204].

The mechanotransduction functions of YAP/TAZ extend to the regulation of stem cell fate and tissue regeneration [258, 267]. Mechanical cues transmitted through YAP/TAZ signalling can influence stem cell differentiation, with stiff substrates generally promoting osteogenic differentiation and soft substrates favouring adipogenic differentiation [267]. This mechanosensitive regulation of stem cell fate has important implications for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [254].

Crosstalk between mechanotransduction pathways

The complexity of mechanotransduction arises not only from the individual components of the signalling machinery but also from the intricate crosstalk between different mechanotransduction pathways. The interplay between ECM stiffness, integrin signalling, FAK activation and YAP/TAZ activity creates a sophisticated network that can integrate multiple mechanical and biochemical inputs to generate appropriate cellular responses [28]. Understanding this crosstalk is essential for comprehending how mechanical forces influence disease progression and for developing effective therapeutic interventions.

The relationship between ECM stiffness and integrin signalling represents a fundamental aspect of mechanotransduction crosstalk. Changes in ECM stiffness directly affect integrin clustering and activation, which in turn influences the recruitment and activation of downstream signalling molecules [14, 35]. Stiff substrates promote the formation of large, stable focal adhesions with high levels of integrin clustering, while soft substrates favour smaller, more dynamic adhesions [268]. This stiffness-dependent regulation of integrin signalling provides a mechanism for cells to sense and respond to the mechanical properties of their environment [269].

The crosstalk between integrin signalling and FAK activation is particularly well-characterized and represents a critical node in mechanotransduction networks [239]. Integrin engagement and clustering promote FAK autophosphorylation and kinase activity, while FAK signalling can reciprocally regulate integrin function through effects on focal adhesion dynamics [35, 209]. This bidirectional communication creates a positive feedback loop that can amplify mechanical signals and promote stable cell-ECM adhesions.

FAK serves as a central hub that integrates signals from multiple mechanotransduction pathways [212, 235]. In addition to its interactions with integrins, FAK can be regulated by mechanical forces transmitted through the cytoskeleton, providing a mechanism for integrating different types of mechanical stimuli [210]. FAK signalling can also influence YAP/TAZ activity through effects on cytoskeletal tension and focal adhesion dynamics [234, 239].

The crosstalk between FAK and YAP/TAZ signalling represents a particularly important aspect of mechanotransduction networks. FAK-mediated signalling can promote YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation through effects on cytoskeletal contractility and focal adhesion maturation. Conversely, YAP/TAZ can regulate the expression of genes encoding focal adhesion proteins, creating a feedback loop that can amplify mechanotransduction signals [270].

YAP/TAZ signalling integrates inputs from multiple mechanotransduction pathways, including those mediated by integrins, cadherins and mechanosensitive ion channels [271]. This integration allows YAP/TAZ to function as a master regulator of cellular responses to mechanical stimuli. The ability of YAP/TAZ to translate mechanical signals into transcriptional responses provides a mechanism for long-term cellular adaptation to mechanical environments [204, 250].

The crosstalk between mechanotransduction pathways is particularly evident in disease contexts, where dysregulation of one pathway can have cascading effects on others [272, 273] (Fig. 2). In cancer, increased ECM stiffness promotes integrin clustering and FAK activation, which in turn promotes YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation and the expression of genes that further modify the ECM [242, 261]. Strategies that simultaneously target multiple components of mechanotransduction networks may be more effective than single-target approaches [1, 25]. Understanding the crosstalk between pathways is also important for predicting and managing potential side effects of mechanotransduction-targeted therapies.

Mechanotransduction in cancer

Cancer represents one of the most compelling examples of how dysregulated mechanotransduction can drive pathological processes and disease progression [1, 28]. The mechanical properties of tumours and their microenvironments play crucial roles in virtually every aspect of cancer biology, from initial transformation and tumour growth to invasion, metastasis and therapeutic resistance [274]. Understanding the complex interplay between mechanical forces and cancer biology has emerged as a critical frontier in cancer research, offering new insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets (Fig. 2).

Tumour mechanics and the cancer microenvironment

The mechanical landscape of cancer is fundamentally altered compared with healthy tissues, creating a pathological environment that promotes tumour progression through multiple mechanisms [275]. Tumours are characteristically stiffer than their surrounding normal tissues, with this increased stiffness serving both as a consequence of cancer progression and as a driver of further malignant behaviour [274]. This mechanical transformation of the tumour microenvironment (TME) involves complex interactions between cancer cells, stromal cells, immune cells and the extracellular matrix [274, 275].

The increased stiffness of tumour tissues results from multiple factors, including excessive collagen deposition, collagen crosslinking and altered ECM organization [61, 62]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a central role in this process by producing large quantities of collagen and other ECM components, while also secreting enzymes that promote collagen crosslinking [276, 277]. This creates a mechanically altered microenvironment that can be detected clinically through imaging techniques and is often used as a diagnostic marker for malignancy [275].

The mechanical properties of tumours are not uniform throughout the tissue, creating heterogeneous mechanical landscapes that influence cancer cell behaviour in complex ways [278]. Regions of high stiffness can promote proliferation and survival signalling, while areas of intermediate stiffness may favour invasion and migration. This mechanical heterogeneity contributes to the phenotypic diversity observed within tumours and may influence treatment responses [28, 278].

Interstitial fluid pressure represents another critical mechanical factor in the tumour microenvironment. Tumours often exhibit elevated interstitial fluid pressure owing to leaky vasculature, poor lymphatic drainage and the physical constraints imposed by the stiff tumour matrix [279, 280]. This elevated pressure can impair drug delivery, promote invasion along pressure gradients and contribute to hypoxic conditions that drive aggressive cancer phenotypes [279].

Mechanotransduction pathways in cancer progression

Cancer cells exploit normal mechanotransduction pathways to promote their survival, proliferation and dissemination [281]. The integrin–FAK–YAP/TAZ axis represents a central mechanotransduction network that is frequently dysregulated in cancer [17, 281]. Integrins, which normally mediate cell-ECM adhesion and force transmission, become overexpressed or altered in many cancers, leading to enhanced mechanosensing and survival signalling [131, 209, 233].

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) serves as a critical mechanosensitive signalling hub in cancer cells. FAK activation in response to mechanical stimuli promotes multiple pro-tumorigenic processes, including cell survival, proliferation, migration and invasion. The mechanosensitive nature of FAK allows cancer cells to respond to the altered mechanical environment of tumours by activating pathways that support malignant behaviour [238, 242, 282].

YAP/TAZ signalling represents perhaps the most important mechanotransduction pathway in cancer biology. These transcriptional coactivators are activated by mechanical stimuli and promote the expression of genes involved in proliferation, survival, and stemness. In many cancers, YAP/TAZ are constitutively active owing to the stiff tumour microenvironment, driving continuous pro-tumorigenic signalling [250, 260, 261, 283].

The Hippo pathway, which normally regulates YAP/TAZ activity, is frequently disrupted in cancer. This disruption can result from mutations in Hippo pathway components, mechanical activation due to tumour stiffness or other oncogenic signals. The loss of normal Hippo pathway regulation allows YAP/TAZ to drive cancer progression through multiple mechanisms [253, 254, 256].

The mechanical properties of cancer cells and their microenvironment play crucial roles in invasion and metastasis [274] (Fig. 3). During local invasion, cancer cells encounter the mechanically altered tumour stroma, which can both facilitate and impede their movement [281]. Aligned collagen fibres, often found in tumour stroma, can serve as “highways” for cancer cell migration, providing directional cues and mechanical support [286–288]. The stiffness of the surrounding matrix influences the mode of cancer cell migration, with stiffer environments generally favouring mesenchymal-type migration [286, 289, 290].

The process of intravasation requires cancer cells to breach the basement membrane and endothelial barriers, processes that are mechanically demanding [291]. Cancer cells must generate sufficient force to disrupt these barriers while also being deformable enough to squeeze through small openings. The mechanical properties of cancer cells, including their stiffness and deformability, are critical determinants of intravasation efficiency [292, 293].

Once in circulation, cancer cells face the mechanical stresses of blood flow, including shear stress and compressive forces [294]. Circulating tumour cells (CTCs) must be mechanically robust enough to survive these stresses while remaining deformable enough to arrest in capillaries (Fig. 3) [295]. The mechanical properties of CTCs are increasingly recognized as important biomarkers for metastatic potential [294, 296].

Extravasation presents another mechanical challenge, requiring cancer cells to adhere to and penetrate the endothelial barrier [297]. The mechanical interactions between cancer cells and endothelial cells are complex, involving both adhesive forces and the generation of contractile forces that can disrupt endothelial integrity [298]. The mechanical properties of the target organ also influence extravasation efficiency and subsequent colonization [297].

Mechanotransduction in cancer stem cells and tumour heterogeneity

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a subpopulation of cancer cells with enhanced tumorigenic potential and resistance to therapy [299, 300]. The mechanical microenvironment plays important roles in regulating CSC behaviour and maintaining stemness [274]. Mechanical cues can influence CSC self-renewal, differentiation and dormancy through mechanotransduction pathways [28, 301]. While several ECM constituents (e.g., HA, Sdc-1) have been shown to modulate the CSC phenotype, the contribution of ECM stiffness to these phenotypes is so far only superficially explored [302, 303].

The stiffness of the CSC niche is a critical factor in maintaining stemness [304, 305]. Intermediate stiffness environments often promote stemness, while very soft or very stiff environments may drive differentiation [46]. This mechanical regulation of stemness has important implications for cancer therapy, as CSCs are often responsible for treatment resistance and tumour recurrence [299, 305].

YAP/TAZ signalling is particularly important in CSC biology, as these pathways promote stemness and self-renewal [306]. The mechanosensitive nature of YAP/TAZ allows CSCs to respond to mechanical cues in their niche, maintaining their undifferentiated state in appropriate mechanical environments [11]. Targeting mechanotransduction pathways in CSCs represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

The mechanical heterogeneity of tumours contributes to cancer cell heterogeneity by creating diverse mechanical niches that select for different cancer cell phenotypes [278]. This mechanical selection pressure can drive the evolution of cancer cell populations with distinct mechanical properties and behaviours [307, 308]. Understanding this mechanical basis of tumour heterogeneity is important for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Mechanotransduction and cancer drug resistance

The mechanical properties of tumours and cancer cells play important roles in drug resistance, representing a significant challenge for cancer therapy [309, 310]. Mechanical factors can influence drug resistance through multiple mechanisms, including altered drug delivery, activation of survival pathways and selection for resistant cell populations [311, 312].

The elevated interstitial fluid pressure in tumours can impair drug delivery by reducing the pressure gradient that drives drug penetration into tissues [313]. Additionally, the dense and crosslinked ECM in tumours can act as a physical barrier to drug diffusion [314, 315]. These mechanical barriers to drug delivery contribute to the poor efficacy of many cancer therapies [316].

Mechanotransduction pathways themselves can promote drug resistance by activating survival signalling [1]. For example, FAK activation in response to mechanical stimuli can promote resistance to apoptosis and enhance DNA repair mechanisms [317, 318]. Similarly, YAP/TAZ activation can drive the expression of drug efflux pumps and anti-apoptotic proteins [319].

The mechanical properties of cancer cells can also influence their sensitivity to therapy. Stiffer cancer cells may be more resistant to mechanical disruption by certain therapies, while softer cells may be more susceptible to treatments that target cell mechanics [51, 285, 320]. Understanding these mechanical determinants of drug sensitivity could inform the development of personalized treatment strategies.

Mechanical stress can also induce adaptive responses in cancer cells that promote resistance. For example, compressive stress can activate autophagy and metabolic reprogramming that enhance cell survival under stress conditions [321, 322]. These mechanically induced adaptive responses represent important mechanisms of therapy resistance.

Mechanotransduction in cancer immunology

The mechanical properties of tumours significantly influence immune cell function and the effectiveness of immunotherapy [323]. The stiff tumour microenvironment can impair immune cell infiltration and function, contributing to immune evasion [324, 325]. Understanding these mechanical aspects of cancer immunology is important for improving immunotherapy outcomes.

T-cell activation and function are mechanically regulated, with the stiffness of the antigen-presenting surface influencing T-cell responses [326]. In the stiff tumour microenvironment, T-cells may receive suboptimal mechanical signals that impair their activation and effector function [327, 328]. This mechanical immunosuppression represents an important mechanism of immune evasion [328].

The mechanical properties of cancer cells themselves can influence their interactions with immune cells [285]. Softer cancer cells may be more susceptible to immune cell-mediated killing, while stiffer cells may be more resistant [329]. The mechanical phenotype of cancer cells may therefore serve as a biomarker for immunotherapy responsiveness.

Mechanotransduction pathways in immune cells are also important for their function in the tumour microenvironment [1]. For example, macrophage polarization can be influenced by mechanical cues, with different mechanical environments promoting either pro-tumorigenic or anti-tumorigenic macrophage phenotypes [308, 330, 331]. Targeting these mechanotransduction pathways in immune cells could enhance immunotherapy efficacy.

Future directions and clinical translation

The future of mechanotransduction therapeutics lies in developing more sophisticated approaches that can address the complexity of these signalling networks [1, 332, 333]. This includes the development of multi-target inhibitors that can simultaneously modulate multiple mechanotransduction pathways [1].

Personalized medicine approaches that take into account the specific mechanical environment and mechanotransduction pathway activity in individual patients represent another important direction [334, 335]. This would require the development of diagnostic tools that can assess tissue mechanics and mechanotransduction pathway activity in clinical settings.

The integration of mechanotransduction-targeted therapies with other treatment modalities, including immunotherapy and regenerative medicine, offers exciting possibilities. For example, modulating mechanotransduction pathways might enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies by altering the mechanical environment of tumours [323, 336].

The development of mechanotransduction-targeted therapies will also benefit from advances in our understanding of the fundamental mechanisms of mechanotransduction. Continued research into the molecular basis of mechanotransduction will likely reveal new therapeutic targets and strategies.

The field of cancer mechanobiology is rapidly evolving, with new discoveries continuously expanding our understanding of how mechanical forces influence cancer biology. Several emerging areas of research hold particular promise for clinical translation. This makes these pathways attractive therapeutic targets [337]. However, developing effective mechanotransduction-targeted therapies requires careful consideration of the complex and context-dependent roles of these pathways [332]. Several therapeutic strategies are being explored, ranging from direct pathway inhibition to mechanical modulation of the tumour microenvironment [338, 339].

Small molecule inhibitors targeting key mechanotransduction proteins have shown promise in preclinical studies [333, 340]. FAK inhibitors, for example, have demonstrated efficacy in multiple cancer models and are currently being evaluated in clinical trials [341]. Targeting the mechanical properties of the tumour microenvironment represents another promising approach. Strategies to reduce tumour stiffness, such as collagen crosslinking inhibitors or ECM-degrading enzymes, could potentially improve drug delivery and reduce mechanotransduction-driven cancer progression [62, 336]. However, these approaches must be carefully designed to avoid promoting invasion and metastasis.

Biomaterial-based approaches offer unique opportunities to modulate mechanotransduction in cancer [203, 314, 316]. Drug delivery systems that respond to mechanical cues could provide targeted therapy to mechanically altered tumour regions [203, 316]. Additionally, biomaterials designed to normalize the mechanical properties of the tumour microenvironment could potentially reverse pathological mechanotransduction signalling [342].

Combination therapies that target both mechanotransduction pathways and conventional cancer pathways may be particularly effective [51, 337, 343]. For example, combining FAK inhibitors with chemotherapy or immunotherapy could potentially overcome resistance mechanisms and improve treatment outcomes [317, 341]. The development of such combination strategies requires a deep understanding of the interactions between mechanotransduction and other cancer pathways.

On the other hand, the development of mechanical biomarkers for cancer diagnosis, prognosis and treatment selection represents an important frontier [344, 345]. Techniques for measuring tissue stiffness, cancer cell mechanical properties and mechanotransduction pathway activity could provide valuable clinical information [329]. These mechanical biomarkers could complement existing molecular biomarkers to provide a more comprehensive assessment of cancer biology.

Advanced imaging techniques that can visualize mechanical properties and forces in living tissues are being developed. These techniques could provide real-time information about tumour mechanics and mechanotransduction activity, enabling more precise therapeutic targeting [275, 346]. The integration of mechanical imaging with other imaging modalities could provide unprecedented insights into cancer biology.

The application of artificial intelligence and machine learning to cancer mechanobiology data is opening new possibilities for understanding complex mechanotransduction networks [347–349]. These computational approaches could help identify new therapeutic targets and predict treatment responses based on mechanical properties [347, 348]. The integration of mechanical and molecular data through machine learning could lead to more effective personalized treatment strategies [349].

Understanding the role of mechanotransduction in cancer represents a critical frontier that promises to transform our approach to cancer diagnosis, treatment and prevention. As our knowledge of these mechanisms continues to expand, we can expect to see the development of innovative therapeutic strategies that target the mechanical aspects of cancer biology. The successful translation of these discoveries into clinical practice will require continued collaboration between basic scientists, engineers, and clinicians.

Challenges and opportunities in clinical translation

The translation of mechanotransduction research into clinical interventions faces several significant challenges. One major challenge is the complexity of mechanotransduction networks, which involve multiple interconnected pathways that can compensate for each other when one pathway is inhibited [355, 356]. This complexity makes it difficult to predict the effects of targeting specific components of the mechanotransduction machinery.

Another challenge is the dual role of many mechanotransduction pathways in both normal physiology and disease [1, 357]. Integrins [232, 358], FAK [210, 234] and YAP/TAZ [258, 359] all play important roles in normal tissue homeostasis, making it challenging to develop therapies that selectively target their pathological functions. This has led to interest in developing context-specific inhibitors that are active only in disease environments [360–362].

The heterogeneity of mechanical environments within tissues and between different disease states presents additional challenges [363]. The mechanical properties of tissues can vary significantly between different regions and can change over the course of disease progression [336]. This heterogeneity makes it difficult to develop one-size-fits-all therapeutic approaches.

Conclusions

Mechanotransduction plays a central role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and regulating a wide range of physiological and pathological processes. The intricate interplay between the extracellular matrix (ECM) and cellular mechanotransduction pathways, including integrins, focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and YAP/TAZ signalling, underscores the dynamic relationship between cells and their mechanical environment. ECM stiffness, composition and remodelling are pivotal factors in regulating cellular behaviour, influencing processes such as cell proliferation, migration, differentiation and survival. While these mechanisms are essential for normal physiological functions, their dysregulation contributes to the onset and progression of chronic diseases, including cancer, fibrosis and metabolic disorders.

In cancer, the mechanotransduction pathways driven by ECM stiffness, integrin-FAK signalling and YAP/TAZ activity create a tumour microenvironment (TME) conducive to tumour progression, immune evasion and therapy resistance. Similarly, in fibrotic diseases, YAP/TAZ activation and integrin-mediated mechanotransduction drive fibroblast differentiation and excessive ECM production, leading to progressive tissue scarring. These findings reveal the dual nature of mechanotransduction, where its physiological functions become detrimental when dysregulated.

Understanding the crosstalk between ECM mechanics and mechanotransduction pathways has highlighted the complexity and adaptability of cellular responses to mechanical stimuli. Advances in this field have provided new insights into the molecular machinery of mechanotransduction, offering potential therapeutic strategies to disrupt pathological feedback loops. Targeting ECM remodelling enzymes, modulating integrin and FAK signalling or inhibiting YAP/TAZ activity are promising approaches for managing diseases characterized by aberrant mechanotransduction.

Future research should focus on translating these mechanistic insights into clinical applications, exploring combination therapies that integrate mechanotransduction-targeting strategies with conventional treatments. Emerging technologies, such as 3D culture models and real-time biomechanical sensors, provide opportunities to study the dynamic behaviour of cells in physiologically relevant conditions, enabling the development of more effective interventions.

By addressing the mechanotransduction pathways at the intersection of physiology and pathology, we can better understand the biomechanical underpinnings of disease progression and design innovative therapeutic approaches. Ultimately, targeting the ECM–mechanotransduction axis holds immense potential to improve patient outcomes in diseases such as cancer, fibrosis and beyond, advancing the frontier of precision medicine.
