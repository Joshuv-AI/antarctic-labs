Quantized Online LQR
Barron Han, Victoria Kostina, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
We study online linear–quadratic regulation (LQR) with unknown dynamics under communication rate constraints. Classical networked control quantizes the plant state at every time step, requiring 𝒪 ⁡ ( T ) \mathcal{O}(T) total bits while injecting persistent quantization noise that limits control performance. We consider a setting where the plant observes its state locally and can estimate system dynamics via ordinary least squares, while a remote controller possesses knowledge of the control cost. Rather than quantizing the raw state, the plant transmits learned dynamics estimates over a rate-limited uplink, and the controller returns the optimal control policy so that the plant can compute actions locally using its superior state knowledge. We first prove a fundamental information-theoretic lower bound: any scheme achieving 𝒪 ⁡ ( T α ) \mathcal{O}(T^{\alpha}) regret for α ∈ [ 1 / 2 , 1 ) \alpha\in[1/2,1) compared to the optimal infinite horizon LQR controller that knows the true system dynamics must transmit at least Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) \Omega(\log T) bits. We then design the Quantized Certainty Equivalent (QCE-LQR) algorithm, which matches this bound. The resulting regret bound contains inflation factors Q slow ​ ( ϱ ) Q_{\mathrm{slow}}(\varrho) and Q fast ​ ( ϱ ) Q_{\mathrm{fast}}(\varrho) that vanish as the codebook resolution increases, smoothly recovering the unquantized baseline regret. Numerical experiments on four benchmark systems—from a scalar unstable plant to a 24-parameter Boeing 747 lateral model—confirm that a variant of QCE-LQR achieves regret comparable to an unquantized certainty equivalent controller over a horizon of T = 10,000 T=10{,}000 steps.



I
Introduction

The online LQR problem is a canonical model for analyzing the regret guarantees of data-driven adaptive control algorithms
[
1
,
2
]
. Practical deployments face tight limits on communication bandwidth and onboard computation, motivating theoretical results that quantify performance–communication tradeoffs and algorithms that provably operate under rate constraints.

The online LQR problem without communication constraints has been well studied
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
. Over a horizon of
T
, the optimal regret relative to the infinite-horizon LQR controller with known dynamics is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
(where
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
suppresses logarithmic factors). This rate is achieved by standard
\varepsilon
-greedy exploration schemes when a stabilizing safety controller is available
[
2
]
, and remains achievable without such a controller via more sophisticated strategies
[
3
]
.

The offline LQR problem with communication constraints has also been of recent interest. The fundamental tradeoff between LQR cost and communication rate is characterized in
[
4
]
. Joint source-channel codes for LQR over noisy communication channels have been studied in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Crucially, these classical networked control schemes quantize the
state

x_{t}
at every time step, requiring
\mathcal{O}(T)
total bits over a horizon of
T
. Moreover, continuous state-quantization noise is injected into the feedback loop at each step, which fundamentally limits the achievable regret when the dynamics are unknown.

These works also assume the linearized dynamics are known a priori, which is often unrealistic. Most real-world systems are inherently non-linear, and their corresponding linear models are obtained by computing the local Jacobian around a nominal operating point. As a system transitions between different operating regimes—due to changes in velocity, varying payload distributions, or environmental wear—this local approximation drifts, causing the effective system parameters to vary with the changing operating conditions. This motivates a data-driven approach that learns the linearized dynamics online. Furthermore, unlike
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, we assume the actuator has direct access to the system state and can apply control inputs without quantization, so that communication is required only for transmitting the control
policy
.

An alternative model-free problem setting considers designing the optimal control policy without explicitly learning the system dynamics. For this problem,
[
8
]
devise the Adaptively Quantized Gradient Descent (AQGD) algorithm based on
[
9
]
to learn LQR controllers over rate-limited channels. Their main result shows that, above a finite bit-rate threshold, AQGD converges exponentially fast to the globally optimal policy with no degradation of the convergence rate relative to the unquantized setting. However, their framework studies convergence to the optimal
policy
rather than
online regret
, and requires the agent to observe multiple independent trajectories of the system to form policy gradient estimates.

In this work, we study online LQR with control policy quantization, motivated by a fundamental
information asymmetry
between the system and the controller: the system observes its state locally and can form accurate dynamics estimates via ordinary least squares (OLS), while the controller possesses knowledge of the control cost. To address this asymmetry, the system transmits its dynamics estimates to the controller, which returns the optimal control
policy

K_{t}
—not the control action
u_{t}
—computed from those estimates and the cost objective. The system then applies
u_{t}=K_{t}x_{t}
locally, exploiting its superior knowledge of the state,
x_{t}
. Unlike classical networked control
[
7
,
4
]
, the controller transmits the policy rather than the action and therefore does not need access to the system state. This extends naturally to tracking problems, where the controller transmits the target state alongside the policy gain, yielding an affine control law that still requires no knowledge of the current state. In the offline setting where the linear system dynamics are known, this is trivial: the controller computes the optimal gain
K
once and transmits it at
t=1
, requiring only
\mathcal{O}(1)
bits. In the online setting, the controller cannot compute the optimal
K
since the dynamics are unknown, and exploration noise must be injected to accelerate learning
[
2
,
1
]
. The goal is to recover the optimal
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret scaling.

A naive approach of uniformly quantizing the OLS dynamics estimates fails to preserve the optimal
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret for two reasons.
First
, the OLS estimation error of the linearized system is highly anisotropic: different parameter subspaces converge at different polynomial rates
[
1
]
, so a single-scale quantizer is forced to track the slowest rate and incurs unnecessary dimensional overhead in the dominant regret term.
Second
, before these asymptotic rates take effect, the transient OLS error cannot be tightly bounded, so any fixed-radius codebook risks overflow. Both challenges necessitate an adaptive, mixed-scale quantization scheme.

In this paper, we study online LQR with rate-limited feedback and establish sharp limits on the communication needed to retain optimal regret. We first prove a fundamental information-theoretic lower bound: any scheme achieving
\mathcal{O}(T^{\alpha})
regret for
\alpha\in[1/2,1)
must transmit at least
\Omega(\log T)
bits, even if the true dynamics are known by the system. We then construct the
Quantized Certainty Equivalent (QCE-LQR)
algorithm, which matches this lower bound in its dependence on the horizon
T
. QCE-LQR is a rate-limited variant of the
\varepsilon
-greedy exploration certainty equivalent policy from
[
1
]
, modified to compress the evolving sequence of dynamics estimates into an information-theoretically optimal
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
bit stream without degrading the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret scaling.

We present the following informal theorem that combines our main results in Theorems
1
and
2

(Informal)

In the quantized online LQR setting for horizon
T

B(T)=\Theta(\log T)
is necessary and sufficient to achieve
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

(Fundamental Regret-Communication Trade-off)

IV-A

Algorithm Overview

Our main algorithm, Quantized Certainty Equivalent LQR (
QCE-LQR
) (Algorithm
1
), is a rate-limited adaptation of the
\varepsilon
-greedy scheme from
[
1
]
. The algorithm takes as input a stabilizing controller
K_{0}
and proceeds in doubling epochs
k
of length
\tau_{k}=2^{k}
. During an initial burn-in period, the plant uses
K_{0}
with Gaussian excitation to explore the state space. Once the plant’s local, unquantized OLS estimate achieves sufficient statistical reliability at epoch
k_{\mathrm{safe}}
, it transmits this initial estimate to the controller using an absolute Elias Gamma encoding. This establishes a shared, reliable baseline model
(\tilde{A}_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}},\tilde{B}_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}})
between the plant and the controller. From this baseline, both tracking sides natively compute a known
safe set

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{safe}}
designed to guarantee closed-loop stability for any contained parameter.

In the post-safe phase (
k>k_{\mathrm{safe}}
), the plant relies on differential quantization. At the end of each epoch
k
, it computes an updated OLS estimate

\hat{\theta}_{k}:=\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A}_{k},\hat{B}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}

(28)

and sends a
quantized innovations update
relative to the previously shared estimate. Writing
\tilde{\theta}_{k}:=\mathrm{vec}(\tilde{A}_{k},\tilde{B}_{k})
for the shared decoded parameter, both sides maintain it via

\tilde{\theta}_{k}\;=\;\tilde{\theta}_{k-1}+s_{k}c_{q_{k}},

(29)

where
q_{k}
is the transmitted codeword index corresponding to a vector
c_{q_{k}}
from a fixed codebook
\mathcal{C}
(a
\varrho
-net of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
), and
s_{k}
is a scalar radius. Given the sequence of shared parameters, the controller projects onto the known safe set:

\check{\theta}_{k}:=\mathrm{vec}(\check{A}_{k},\check{B}_{k})=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{safe}}}(\tilde{\theta}_{k}),

(30)

which ensures closed-loop stability with high probability. It then synthesizes the certainty-equivalent controller

\tilde{K}_{k}:=K_{\infty}(\check{A}_{k},\check{B}_{k})

(31)

and transmits it back to the plant over the unconstrained downlink. The plant applies this policy with decaying exploration noise:

u_{t}=\tilde{K}_{k}x_{t}+\sigma_{k}g_{t},\qquad\sigma_{k}^{2}:=\min\{1,\,\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\,\tau_{k}^{-1/2}\},

(32)

where
g_{t}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d_{u}})
is isotropic Gaussian excitation and
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}>0
is an exploration constant computed at epoch

, specified in Lemma
1
(Section

Because the codebook

IV-B

Quantization Rule: Adaptive Scale Protocol

The key challenge in designing the quantization schedule is that the OLS estimation error is
highly anisotropic
. As shown in Corollary
2
in Section
IV-F
, below, after a bounded period
\tau_{\mathrm{ls}}
, the error decays as
\tau^{-1/4}
for the
d_{x}d_{u}
-dimensional subspace (the “slow” rate) but as
\tau^{-1/2}
for the
d_{x}^{2}
-dimensional subspace (the “fast” rate). A standard single-scale tracking quantizer would set its radius to
s_{k}\propto\tau_{k}^{-1/4}
, forcing the slow rate to dominate and coupling the
d_{x}^{2}
scaling into the dominant
\sqrt{T}
regret term.

To avoid this, we define the
ideal base schedule

s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}=c_{\mathrm{slow}}\,\tau_{k}^{-1/4}+c_{\mathrm{fast}}\,\tau_{k}^{-1/2},

(33)

where
c_{\mathrm{slow}}
and
c_{\mathrm{fast}}
are constants computed at epoch
k_{\mathrm{safe}}
in Section
IV-G
, below. The key design principle is that
c_{\mathrm{slow}}
and
c_{\mathrm{fast}}
are calibrated to match the dimension-dependent scaling of the OLS estimation error (Corollary
2
in Section
IV-F
, below):
c_{\mathrm{slow}}
isolates the
d_{x}d_{u}
-dimensional slow-rate component, while
c_{\mathrm{fast}}
isolates the
d_{x}^{2}
-dimensional fast-rate component. Because the quantization error
\varrho s_{k}
inherits the same two-scale decay as the underlying estimation error, it adds no order-wise overhead to the total parameter error. While the quantizer remains an isotropic
\varrho
-net of the unit ball, this tighter envelope ensures the
d_{x}^{2}
contribution is quarantined into the lower-order
\log T
regret term.

After the safe epoch
k_{\mathrm{safe}}
, the plant projects the OLS estimate onto the safe set,

\bar{\theta}_{k}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{safe}}}(\hat{\theta}_{k}),

(34)

and computes the innovation

\Delta_{k}:=\bar{\theta}_{k}-\tilde{\theta}_{k-1}.

(35)

Because the asymptotic OLS bounds only hold after a mixing time
\tau_{\mathrm{ls}}
(Corollary
2
in Section
IV-F
, below), early innovations may exceed
s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}
. The plant therefore dynamically computes a multiplier

m_{k}=\max\!\left\{1,\;\left\lceil\frac{\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}}{s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}}\right\rceil\right\},

(36)

and sets the actual scale
s_{k}=m_{k}\cdot s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}
. By construction,
\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}\leq s_{k}
, making quantizer overflow impossible. The multiplier
m_{k}
is transmitted via Elias Gamma coding at a cost of
2\lfloor\log_{2}m_{k}\rfloor+1
bits. The plant then quantizes using the
\varrho
-net:

q_{k}\in\arg\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\|\Delta_{k}-s_{k}c\|_{2},\qquad\|\tilde{\theta}_{k}-\bar{\theta}_{k}\|_{2}\leq\varrho s_{k}.

(37)

K_{0}
with Gaussian excitation,

g_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)

from the received indices,

IV-D

Correctness of Quantized Perturbations

Let

(44)

The following lemma verifies that the quantized controller

inherits the essential properties of the optimal controller

[
1
]

(1)
links per-epoch excess cost to parameter estimation error, driving the main

(2)
ensures the closed-loop Riccati solution does not blow up, which is needed for the self-normalized OLS confidence sets to remain valid;

(5)

(6)

that balances fast OLS convergence against exploration cost, achieving the optimal

.

(Correctness of Quantized Perturbations)

3.

4.

\|A_{\mathrm{cl},k}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\infty}}\lesssim\|A_{\mathrm{cl},\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\infty}}\lesssim\|P_{\star}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{3/2}.

\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\asymp\sqrt{d_{x}}\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{9/2}\Psi_{B}\sqrt{\log\frac{\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}{\delta}}.

IV-E

Regret Bound Sketch

The regret decomposes into two phases: pre-safe burn-in, and post-safe tracking.

Pre-safe regret.
Before the safe trigger fires, the algorithm executes
K_{0}
identically to the unquantized setting of
[
1
]
. Because the trigger depends only on the OLS confidence and the quantization error is subsequently constrained to be of similar order, the safe epoch occurs at a bounded stopping time
\tau_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{safe}}
(
22
). The accumulated pre-safe regret is

\begin{split}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}}-1}c(x_{t},u_{t})&\lesssim d^{2}\Psi_{B}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{0}\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{10}(1+\left\lVert K_{0}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2})\\
&\quad\times\log\!\tfrac{\Psi_{B}^{2}J_{0}}{\delta}\,\log\!\tfrac{1}{\delta},\end{split}

(48)

which matches the unquantized pre-safe bound of
[
1
, Lem. 5.5]
.

Post-safe regret.
Following
[
1
, Lem. 5.2]
, the post-safe regret decomposes as

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=\tau_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}}}^{T}\big(c(x_{t},u_{t})-J_{\infty}\big)\\
&\qquad\lesssim\sum_{k=k_{\mathrm{safe}}}^{k_{\mathrm{fin}}}\tau_{k}(J_{k}-J_{\infty})\\
&\qquad\quad+\sqrt{T}\big(d_{u}\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\Psi_{B}^{2}\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\sqrt{d\log\!\tfrac{1}{\delta}}\,\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{4}\big)\\
&\qquad\quad+\text{lower-order terms}.\end{split}

(49)

The perturbation bound gives

J_{k}-J_{\infty}\lesssim\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{8}\left\lVert\tilde{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{\star}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(50)

which splits into an unquantized OLS error and a quantization reconstruction error
\varrho^{2}s_{k}^{2}
. Using the OLS error bounds and the ideal base schedule (
33
), the quantization-induced regret separates as

\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{8}\,\varrho^{2}C_{\varrho}^{2}\big(c_{\mathrm{slow}}^{2}\sqrt{T}+c_{\mathrm{fast}}^{2}\log T\big),

(51)

recovering the inflation factors
Q_{\mathrm{slow}}(\varrho)
and
Q_{\mathrm{fast}}(\varrho)
. The exploration variance
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}
appears in two competing terms in (
49
): the direct exploration cost
d_{u}\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\Psi_{B}^{2}\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{T}
and, through the OLS estimation cost, a
1/\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}
term. Balancing these yields
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}\asymp\sqrt{d_{x}}\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{9/2}\Psi_{B}\sqrt{\log(\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}/\delta)}
and recovers the optimal dominant coefficient
d_{u}\sqrt{d_{x}}\,\Psi_{B}^{3}\,\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{11/2}\sqrt{\log(\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}/\delta)}\cdot\sqrt{T}
.

IV-H

Quantization Error Analysis

Standard differential quantizers set a fixed codebook radius
s_{k}
and assume the parameter innovation
\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}
will always land within it. In our setting, the two-scale base schedule (
33
) is calibrated to the asymptotic OLS error rate (Corollary
2
), which only holds for epochs
k>k_{\mathrm{ls}}
. During the transient window between
k_{\mathrm{safe}}
(when differential tracking begins) and
k_{\mathrm{ls}}
(when the asymptotic OLS rate bounds take effect), the estimation error may significantly exceed
s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}
, causing the innovation
\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}
to overflow the base codebook radius. Enlarging the codebook to cover the worst-case transient error would force the per-epoch bit cost to scale with
\Omega(T)
, exceeding the logarithmic budget. Adaptive quantizer scaling resolves this via the dynamic multiplier
m_{k}
, which expands the effective quantization radius on-the-fly to absorb any transient innovation excess. The key mathematical insight is that once the asymptotic OLS regime begins, the interaction between the shrinking estimation error and the codebook dynamics forms a
contraction
:
m_{k}
collapses back to
\mathcal{O}(1)
within a constant number of epochs with high probability, achieving overflow immunity during the transient phase without incurring an asymptotic bit-rate penalty.

What remains is to show that: (i) the multiplier
m_{k}
contracts to
\mathcal{O}(1)
, ensuring
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
bit rate, and (ii) the random scale
s_{k}=m_{k}s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}
does not inflate the regret beyond the ideal base schedule.

Lemma 6

(Contraction of the Adaptive Multiplier)
.

Let
\varrho<1/\sqrt{2}
and
\beta:=\varrho\sqrt{2}<1
. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ls}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{safe}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{bound}}
, for all epochs
k>k_{\mathrm{ls}}
(where the asymptotic OLS bound (
55
) holds), the multiplier satisfies

m_{k}<1+\beta(m_{k-1}-1)+1.

(64)

Consequently,
\limsup_{k\to\infty}m_{k}\leq\frac{2-\beta}{1-\beta}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

We establish an envelope recurrence
s_{k}\leq\varrho s_{k-1}+U_{k}
(where
U_{k}
bounds the unquantized estimation errors), allowing us to bound the total quantization-induced regret. The formal statement and proof of this bound are deferred to Appendix
D
.

IV-I

Pre-Safe Period Regret

Because our algorithm executes the baseline stabilizing controller
K_{0}
before the safety trigger, and this trigger occurs with high probability at a bounded stopping time, the system behavior during this phase is identical to the unquantized setting. Consequently, we can upper-bound the regret accumulated during the pre-safe period using the same method as Lemma 5.5 in

Coordinate-Wise Scalar Quantization

The theoretical analysis uses a
\varrho
-net codebook on the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{d_{s}-1}
to quantize the correction
\Delta_{k}=\bar{\theta}_{k}-\tilde{\theta}_{k-1}
, transmitting the direction via a codebook index and the scale via Elias Gamma coding. While this achieves optimal rate–distortion scaling, it is difficult to implement in practice.

We instead quantize each coordinate of
\Delta_{k}
independently using a uniform scalar quantizer with step size
1/\sqrt{\tau_{k}}
. This matches the
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{-1/2})
“fast” OLS estimation rate (Corollary
2
), which in practice governs the convergence of all parameter coordinates—including the
d_{x}d_{u}
subspace whose worst-case theoretical rate is
\tau^{-1/4}
. Coordinate
i
is encoded as a sign bit plus the Elias Gamma encoding of the integer index
\lceil|\Delta_{k,i}|\sqrt{\tau_{k}}\,\rceil
. Unlike the theorem’s two-scale vector quantizer, this simplification is not accompanied by the same worst-case guarantee: using the fast rate instead of the conservative two-scale schedule (
33
) may inflate the worst-case bit budget from
\mathcal{O}(d_{s}\log T)
to
\mathcal{O}(d_{s}\log^{2}T)
. For the four benchmarked systems considered here, however, the realized communication cost remains close to
\mathcal{O}(d_{s}\log T)
since OLS converges at the fast
\tau_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
rate across all dimensions in these simulations.

VI
Conclusion

We have established that
\Theta(\log T)
bits of communication are both necessary and sufficient to achieve the optimal
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret in online LQR with unknown dynamics and a rate-limited plant-to-controller link. By transmitting learned models instead of raw states, communication only needs to track the estimator’s innovation, which shrinks as learning converges, breaking the
\mathcal{O}(T)
bandwidth barrier of classical state-quantization approaches.

Summary of Contributions

1.

Converse (Theorem
1
).
Any scheme achieving worst-case expected regret
\mathcal{O}(T^{\alpha})
for
\alpha\in[1/2,1)
must transmit at least
\Omega(d_{u}d_{x}(1-\alpha)\log T)
bits by time
T
, even if the true dynamics are known at the system. This establishes a fundamental information-theoretic floor on the communication cost of near-optimal adaptive control.

2.

Achievability (Theorem
2
).
The QCE-LQR algorithm attains
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret using only
\mathcal{O}((d_{x}^{2}+d_{x}d_{u})\log T)
total bits. The algorithm employs a two-scale adaptive quantization protocol that separately tracks the slow (
\tau_{k}^{-1/4}
) and fast (
\tau_{k}^{-1/2}
) decay rates of the OLS estimation error, combined with an absolute initialization via Elias Gamma coding that guarantees zero overflow risk.

3.

Explicit Quantization–Regret Tradeoff.
The regret bound in Theorem
2
exposes the precise cost of quantization through the inflation factors
Q_{\mathrm{slow}}(\varrho)
and
Q_{\mathrm{fast}}(\varrho)
. As the codebook resolution increases (
\varrho\to 0
), these factors vanish, smoothly recovering the exact unquantized Simchowitz & Foster baseline.

4.

Dimension matching and explicit tradeoff.
The two-scale schedule quarantines the
d_{x}^{2}
scaling into the lower-order
\log T
term, preserving the optimal
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d_{x}d_{u}^{2}T})
dimension dependence. The quantization penalty is fully captured by
Q_{\mathrm{slow}}(\varrho)
and
Q_{\mathrm{fast}}(\varrho)
, which vanish as
\varrho\to 0
, making the bandwidth–regret tradeoff explicit.

Next Steps

Closing the dimensional gap.
Our bounds are tight in the horizon
T
but a gap remains in the dimension-dependent constant multiplying
\log T
. The converse establishes a lower bound of
\Omega(d_{u}d_{x}\log T)
bits, counting only the
d_{u}d_{x}
degrees of freedom in the gain
K\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{x}}
, since the proof technique relies on a mutual information bound between
K_{\infty}(A,B)
and
\hat{K}
, while the achievability requires
\mathcal{O}((d_{x}^{2}+d_{x}d_{u})\log T)
bits for the full estimate
\hat{\theta}=\mathrm{vec}(\hat{A},\hat{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}^{2}+d_{x}d_{u}}
. The additional
d_{x}^{2}\log T
term arises because the controller needs
\hat{A}
to solve the DARE; in our architecture these parameters cannot be eliminated from the uplink. Closing this gap by strengthening the converse to
\Omega((d_{x}^{2}+d_{x}d_{u})\log T)
remains open.

Symmetric channel extension.
Our main results assume an asymmetric bi-directional channel (rate-limited uplink, unconstrained downlink). The analysis extends to a fully symmetric rate-limited channel in which the controller must also quantize the policy
\tilde{K}_{k}
. The policy innovation
\Delta K_{k}:=\tilde{K}_{k}-\tilde{K}_{k-1}
inherits the same
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{k}^{-1/4})
decay rate as the dynamics estimates, so the controller can quantize
\Delta K_{k}
using the identical two-scale schedule and Elias Gamma protocol. The resulting Frobenius-norm error can be bounded using the same techniques. A rate of
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
downlink bits preserves the optimal
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1
(Converse)

Proof.

As outlined in Section
III
, this proof proceeds in four main steps. First, we construct a hard subclass of systems
\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}
parametrized by the optimal controller gain
K
. Second, we establish a regret identity demonstrating that sub-linear regret bounds the mean-squared error of estimating
K
. Third, we use the controller’s sequence of actions to construct a concrete estimator
\widehat{K}
for the hidden gain. Finally, we place a uniform prior on the hard class and apply information-theoretic inequalities to show that transmitting enough bits to form this estimator requires
\Omega(\log T)
bits.

We may assume
T\geq 4
, since finitely many smaller horizons can be absorbed into the constant
C
.

Step 1: Constructing a Hard Subclass.

We begin by defining a localized hypercube of optimal control gains.
Set

a:=\frac{r}{\sqrt{d_{u}d_{x}}},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{r}:=[-a,a]^{d_{u}\times d_{x}},

(69)

so that every
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
satisfies
\|K\|_{F}\leq r
.

Choose

c>1+\frac{r^{2}\lambda_{\max}(R_{u})}{\lambda_{\min}(R_{x})}

(70)

and set

P:=cR_{x}\succ 0.

(71)

For each
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
, define

M_{K}:=(c-1)R_{x}-K^{\top}R_{u}K.

(72)

Since
\|K\|_{F}\leq r
,

K^{\top}R_{u}K\preceq\lambda_{\max}(R_{u})\|K\|_{F}^{2}I_{d_{x}}\preceq r^{2}\lambda_{\max}(R_{u})I_{d_{x}},

(73)

so our choice of
c
implies
M_{K}\succ 0
. Now define

\Phi_{K}:=P^{-1/2}M_{K}^{1/2},\qquad B_{K}:=-P^{-1}\Phi_{K}^{-\top}K^{\top}R_{u},\qquad A_{K}:=\Phi_{K}-B_{K}K.

(74)

Then

\displaystyle A_{K}+B_{K}K

\displaystyle=\Phi_{K},

(75)

\displaystyle\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}

\displaystyle=M_{K}=(c-1)R_{x}-K^{\top}R_{u}K,

(76)

\displaystyle B_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}

\displaystyle=-R_{u}K.

(77)

Hence

R_{x}+K^{\top}R_{u}K+\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}=P.

(78)

Also,
\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}\preceq(c-1)R_{x}=\frac{c-1}{c}P
,
so
\rho(\Phi_{K})<1
. Therefore
(A_{K},B_{K})
is stabilizable.

Let

\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}:=\{(A_{K},B_{K}):\ K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}\}.

(79)

We now show that
K_{\infty}(A_{K},B_{K})=K
for every
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
, so in particular
\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}\subset\Theta
.

Step 2: The Regret Identity.

Next, we relate the expected regret to the mean-squared-error of the controller’s sequence of actions. Fix
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
and consider the corresponding system
(A_{K},B_{K})
. Define

V(x):=x^{\top}Px,\qquad S_{K}:=R_{u}+B_{K}^{\top}PB_{K}.

(80)

Then
S_{K}\succ 0
and
S_{K}\succeq R_{u}\succeq\lambda_{\min}(R_{u})I_{d_{u}}
.
For arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
,

A_{K}x+B_{K}u=\Phi_{K}x+B_{K}(u-Kx).

(81)

Therefore

\displaystyle V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u)

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}x+2x^{\top}\Phi_{K}^{\top}PB_{K}(u-Kx)+(u-Kx)^{\top}B_{K}^{\top}PB_{K}(u-Kx).

(82)

Consider

\displaystyle x^{\top}R_{x}x+u^{\top}R_{u}u+V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u)-V(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=x^{\top}\bigl(R_{x}+\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}-P\bigr)x+u^{\top}R_{u}u-2x^{\top}K^{\top}R_{u}(u-Kx)+(u-Kx)^{\top}B_{K}^{\top}PB_{K}(u-Kx)

\displaystyle\qquad=x^{\top}\bigl(R_{x}+K^{\top}R_{u}K+\Phi_{K}^{\top}P\Phi_{K}-P\bigr)x+(u-Kx)^{\top}S_{K}(u-Kx)

\displaystyle\qquad=(u-Kx)^{\top}S_{K}(u-Kx).

(83)

The first equality substitutes (
82
) for
V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u)
and applies (
77
) to the cross term. The second expands
u^{\top}R_{u}u=x^{\top}K^{\top}R_{u}Kx+2x^{\top}K^{\top}R_{u}(u-Kx)+(u-Kx)^{\top}R_{u}(u-Kx)
: the
\pm 2x^{\top}K^{\top}R_{u}(u-Kx)
terms cancel and
R_{u}+B_{K}^{\top}PB_{K}=S_{K}
(
80
). The third applies (
78
).
If
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}I_{d_{x}})
, then

\mathbb{E}[V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u+w)]=V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u)+\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(P)
,
so with
J_{P}:=\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(P)
we have the one-step identity

x^{\top}R_{x}x+u^{\top}R_{u}u+\mathbb{E}\!\left[V(A_{K}x+B_{K}u+w)\right]-V(x)-J_{P}=(u-Kx)^{\top}S_{K}(u-Kx).

(84)

Since
S_{K}\succ 0
, the unique minimizer is
u=Kx
. Hence

K_{\infty}(A_{K},B_{K})=K
and
J_{\infty}(A_{K},B_{K})=J_{P}
,
confirming
\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}\subset\Theta
.

Fix
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
and run any scheme achieving the regret bound (
14
) on
(A_{K},B_{K})
. Write

\mathrm{Regret}_{T}^{K}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}-TJ_{P},\qquad\epsilon_{t}^{K}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(u_{t}-Kx_{t})^{\top}S_{K}(u_{t}-Kx_{t})\right].

(85)

Summing (
84
) from
t=1
to
T
with

gives

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon_{t}^{K}=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}_{T}^{K}]+\mathbb{E}[V(x_{T+1})].

(86)

Since
P=cR_{x}
by (
71
), we have
x^{\top}R_{x}x=\frac{1}{c}V(x)
exactly. Identity (
84
) therefore implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{c-1}{c}\right)\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t})]+J_{P}+\epsilon_{t}^{K}.

(87)

Iterating from
V(x_{1})=0
yields

\mathbb{E}[V(x_{T+1})]\leq cJ_{P}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{c-1}{c}\right)^{\!T-t}\epsilon_{t}^{K}
,
and combining with (
86

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\left(\frac{c-1}{c}\right)^{\!T-t}\right)\epsilon_{t}^{K}\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}_{T}^{K}]+cJ_{P}.

(88)

Let
m:=\lfloor T/2\rfloor
.
For every
t\in\{2,\dots,m\}
we have
T-t\geq 1
, hence
1-\left(\frac{c-1}{c}\right)^{T-t}\geq 1-\frac{c-1}{c}=\frac{1}{c}
.
Therefore (
88

\frac{1}{c}\sum_{t=2}^{m}\epsilon_{t}^{K}\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}_{T}^{K}]+cJ_{P}.

(89)

Since
\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}\subset\Theta
, the assumed regret bound gives
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}_{T}^{K}]\leq C_{1}T^{\alpha}
for all
K\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
.

Fix

and let
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
denote the sigma-field generated by
(x_{1},u_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1},u_{t-1})
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{t-1}
be the enlargement of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
by all encoder/controller randomization used before

is chosen. By the timing convention in Section
II

is generated from the past state-action history, so

is
\mathcal{G}_{t-1}

Writing

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}^{K}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_{t}^{\top}M_{t}x_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{Tr}(M_{t}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(M_{t}\,\mathbb{E}[x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\mid\mathcal{G}_{t-1}]\right)\right]

(92)

Step 4: Information-Theoretic Lower Bound.

Finally, we apply the data-processing inequality to bound the mutual information between the communication transcript and the true system gain. Place the prior
K\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{r})

\Theta_{\mathrm{hard}}

\Pi_{T}

(93)

Since

is uniform on

h(K)=d_{u}d_{x}\log_{2}(2a)=d_{u}d_{x}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2r}{\sqrt{d_{u}d_{x}}}\right)

By the Gaussian maximum-entropy bound under a second-moment constraint and (

(94)

Therefore

\displaystyle B(T)+1

\displaystyle\geq h(K)-h(K\mid Z,\widehat{K})

\displaystyle\geq d_{u}d_{x}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2r}{\sqrt{d_{u}d_{x}}}\right)-\frac{d_{u}d_{x}}{2}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2\pi e}{d_{u}d_{x}}\,C_{\mathrm{est}}T^{\alpha-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{u}d_{x}}{2}(1-\alpha)\log_{2}T+d_{u}d_{x}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2r}{\sqrt{d_{u}d_{x}}}\right)-\frac{d_{u}d_{x}}{2}\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{2\pi e}{d_{u}d_{x}}\,C_{\mathrm{est}}\right).

(95)

This gives

for the finite constant

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
2
: Communication Bound

Proof.

Let
K_{T}:=\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil+1
be the number of doubling epochs intersecting
[1,T]
. Since the algorithm communicates only at epoch boundaries, every uplink message is counted once per epoch. We account for four message types.

Codeword indices.

The codebook
\mathcal{C}
is a
\varrho
-net of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
. The standard volumetric covering bound gives
|\mathcal{C}|\leq(1+2/\varrho)^{d_{s}}
. Each index
q_{k}
costs at most

\log_{2}|\mathcal{C}|\leq d_{s}\log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{2}{\varrho}\right)

bits, and over at most
K_{T}
post-safe epochs the total is

B_{q}(T)\leq d_{s}\log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{2}{\varrho}\right)K_{T}.

(107)

Adaptive multipliers.

Algorithm
1
sends
m_{k}
via Elias Gamma coding at cost
2\lfloor\log_{2}m_{k}\rfloor+1
bits. By Lemma
6
, for
k>k_{\mathrm{ls}}
,

m_{k}<2+\beta(m_{k-1}-1),\qquad\beta:=\varrho\sqrt{2}<1,

so
\limsup_{k}m_{k}\leq m_{\infty}:=(2-\beta)/(1-\beta)
. Define
M_{\varrho}:=\lceil m_{\infty}\rceil

m_{k}-m_{\infty}<\beta(m_{k-1}-m_{\infty})
, after at most
h=\mathcal{O}(\log_{+}(m_{k_{\mathrm{ls}}}-m_{\infty}))
additional epochs,
m_{k}\leq M_{\varrho}
for all subsequent

. Therefore all but finitely many multiplier messages cost at most

b_{\varrho}:=2\lfloor\log_{2}M_{\varrho}\rfloor+1

bits each, giving

B_{m}(T)\leq b_{\varrho}K_{T}+\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}m_{k_{\mathrm{ls}}}).

(108)

Since
m_{k}
decays geometrically over

epochs, the sum of the Elias encoding costs
\mathcal{O}(\log m_{k})
forms an arithmetic progression bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}m_{k_{\mathrm{ls}}})

m_{k_{\mathrm{ls}}}
scales polynomially with
\tau_{\mathrm{ls}}
, this, along with the pre-contraction expansion phase, adds a finite, one-time transient overhead of

111
). Under the theorem choice
\delta=1/T
, we have
\tau_{\mathrm{ls}}=\mathcal{O}(\log T)
, so this overhead is at most
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}\!\log T)
.

Pre-safe flags.

Algorithm
1
sends a 1-bit safe flag during the pre-safe phase. Thus
B_{\mathrm{flag}}\leq k_{\mathrm{safe}}
. Since
\tau_{k}=2^{k}
, Lemma
7

k_{\mathrm{safe}}\leq 1+\log_{2}\tau_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\log\!\left(d(1+\left\lVert K_{0}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2})\lVert P_{\infty}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{10}\log\frac{\Psi_{B}^{2}J_{0}}{\delta}\right)\right),

(109)

which is a one-time overhead.

Absolute initialization.

AbsoluteInit
sends the exponent
E
and the signed integers

via Elias Gamma coding. Both sides compute

from the shared decoded center (

and by Lemma

Each

with

, so Elias coding gives

(110)

where

. On
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{safe}}
,
\hat{\theta}_{k_{\mathrm{safe}}}
is within
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}})
of

, so this is

Total.

Combining all four components:

Lemma 8

(Regret Convolution Bound)
.

For any non-negative sequence
U_{k}
satisfying

(112)

Remark.
By the definition of
m_{k}

s_{k}\leq\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}+s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}

\|\Delta_{k}\|_{2}\leq E_{k}+E_{k-1}+\varrho s_{k-1}
where

. This gives the envelope recurrence
s_{k}\leq\varrho s_{k-1}+U_{k}
with
U_{k}:=E_{k}+E_{k-1}+s_{k}^{\mathrm{base}}

Proof of Lemma
8
.

We split
\varrho^{k-j}=(\varrho\sqrt{2})^{(k-j)/2}\cdot(\varrho/\sqrt{2})^{(k-j)/2}
and apply Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to

by

\sum_{k}\tau_{k}s_{k}^{2}\leq C_{\varrho}\sum_{k}\sum_{j\leq k}2^{k}\left(\frac{\varrho}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{\!k-j}U_{j}^{2}.

Setting
