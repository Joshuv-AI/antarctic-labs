Beyond descendants: integrable observables for cohomological field theories
Xavier Blot, Danilo Lewański, Sergey Shadrin

Abstract
. We introduce the concept of integrable observables and propose them as alternatives to the standard Witten’s psi classes (a.k.a. descendants in 2 ​ D 2D quantum gravity) to be coupled with cohomological field theories and their generalisations. The main property of integrable observables is that they retain the integrability properties. We present three examples of integrable observables. The first two recover the Dubrovin–Zhang and double ramification hierarchies, while revealing new structural features in this framework. The third, a new example, builds on recently established properties of the so-called Π \mathbb{\Pi} -class, extending them and placing this class naturally within the theory of integrable systems. Notably, our integrable observables framework yields a proof that the new Π \mathbb{\Pi} -hierarchies are Miura equivalent both to the Dubrovin–Zhang hierarchies and to the double ramification hierarchies. A new very short proof of Witten’s conjecture is also provided.



0.
Preface: history and context

In 1991 Witten
[
53
]
conjectured that the generating function of the intersection numbers of
\psi
-classes on the moduli spaces of curves
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
solves the Korteweg-de Vries integrable hierarchy of evolutionary PDEs (for history and a new proof, see Sec.
6.1
). This started an intensive study of the interaction of different mathematics avatars of topological string theory, such as quantum singularity theory and Gromov-Witten theory (formalized as cohomological field theories, or CohFTs, introduced in
[
39
]
), with integrable systems.

0.1.
Dubrovin-Zhang theory

Dubrovin and Zhang came up in
[
24
]
with a classification project based on the theory of Frobenius manifolds, introduced by Dubrovin in
[
26
]
. Their approach was fully developed it in
[
25
]
proposing a formal system of axioms that should identify a unique bi-Hamiltonian tau-symmetric dispersive integrable system associated to an input Frobenius manifold, whose dispersionless limit is the principal hierarchy of Dubrovin
[
26
]
. They proved uniqueness, but not existence, which was fully proved much later in
[
43
]
. An excellent concise survey of their approach and important intermediate steps is available in
[
46
, Sec. 1]
.

Dubrovin and Zhang also identified in
[
25
]
a particular tau function of their (back then conjectural) integrable systems with the Givental formula
[
32
]
canonically associated to a calibrated Frobenius manifold. By Teleman’s classification
[
52
]
, there is a unique semi-simple homogeneous cohomological field theory associated to a given Frobenius manifold, with Givental’s formula giving this partition function coupled to
\psi
-classes. This linked Dubrovin-Zhang theory to the realm of cohomological field theories.

A natural question back then (besides the existence of the Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies, which was reduced to the polynomiality property in
[
25
]
, although left as an open problem) was whether the Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies can also be defined for a wider class of CohFTs. Indeed, according to
[
52
]
the homogeneous semi-simple CohFTs in the realm of all CohFTs form just isolated points, or, in some cases, finite dimensional subvarieties inside an infinite dimensional space.

To resolve this issue, the Hamiltonian tau-symmetric integrable systems for the partition function for general semi-simple CohFTs was constructed in
[
16
,
17
]
. By the uniqueness result of Dubrovin and Zhang, this also settled the existence problem for all structures conjectured in
[
25
]
, with the exception of existence of the second Hamiltonian structure. Indeed, the second Hamiltonian structure does demand homogeneity and is out of reach of any method that doesn’t employ it.

It is important to stress the difference between the axiomatic approach of Dubrovin-Zhang and all subsequent developments, that we discuss here and below. The axiomatic approach of
[
25
,
43
]
works in a much more restrictive setting: it in fact requires homogeneity. On the other hand, it only requires
genus zero
data, providing a remarkable full scale alternative to the Givental-Teleman theory, with an extra advantage that it remains entirely in the realm of integrable systems. Moreover, it offers a large variety of extra tools to control the higher genus data, just in terms of the genus zero input (see e.g.
[
50
]
). All other approaches, in particular the one of
[
16
,
17
]
, demand to take as input the CohFT (or some of its generalised versions, as we discuss below) in
all genera
.

The construction of
[
16
,
17
]
is based on the so-called tautological relations proposed in Eguchi-Xiong
[
27
]
. Thus, it can be applied to non-semi-simple CohFTs, and more generally to the so-called partial CohFTs
[
42
]
, although in these cases the methods of
op. cit.
do not allow to establish the polynomiality of the equations, of the Hamiltonians, and of the Poisson bracket. A different but equivalent construction was proposed in
[
21
]
, where these properties were reduced to a system of different tautological relations, later proved in
[
12
]
building up on the key insights in
[
10
]
.

In short, one can summarise Dubrovin-Zhang theory as follows:

•

The approach of
[
25
,
43
]
works only for semi-simple homogenenous CohFTs, but uses only genus zero data as input. Because of the homogeneity assumption, it does provide bi-Hamiltonian structures.

•

The approach of
[
16
,
17
]
works for semi-simple CohFTs without assumption of homogeneity, but it requires as input all genera data, lacks bi-Hamiltonian structure, and the methods of proofs in these papers are not working outside the realm of semi-simple CohFTs (although the construction itself can be extended to general partial CohFTs).

•

The approach of
[
21
,
10
,
12
]
works for general partial CohFTs. It does use as input all genera data, it lacks bi-Hamiltonian structure, but it ultimately gives the most general result in this context.

In all these cases we call the constructed integrable systems the
Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies
.

0.2.
Buryak’s double ramification hierarchies

Buryak proposed in
[
22
]
a drastically different construction of a Hamiltonian integrable system associated to a CohFT, based on the so-called double ramification (DR) cycles, which are explicit cohomology classes on the moduli space of stable curves. These integrable systems are usually called the
DR hierarchies
in the literature. The construction of Buryak might be seen as inspired by symplectic field theory context
[
28
]
, and it works for any CohFT, without any restrictions. It was noticed in
[
18
]
that it can be applied to partial CohFTs as well, also without any further assumption.

Buryak conjectured that DR hierarchies are Miura equivalent to the Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies. This conjecture was known under the name of DR/DZ equivalence. Initially the conjecture was posed in
[
22
]
in the semi-simple case, but it was further developed to a much more precise statement that also identified tau structures and gave a concrete path towards a proof in
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
21
]
. In particular, the latter reference formulated it as a conjecture for partial CohFTs (alongside the conjecture of the existence of the Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies for partial CohFTs, as we mentioned above).

The DR/DZ equivalence was proved for semi-simple CohFTs in
[
10
]
, and for a general partial CohFT it was reduced in
op. cit.
to the so-called master relation, which was then proved in
[
12
]
.

0.3.
Beyond Hamiltonian structure

A natural question, first studied in the dispersionless limit, addresses which axioms of the Frobenius manifold (designed to classify Dubrovin’s principal hierarchies, which are bi-Hamiltonian and tau symmetric) can be dropped without affecting integrability. In
[
45
]
Dubrovin’s construction was generalized to a correspondence between the so-called
flat F-manifolds
(known in the literature outside integrable systems under the name
hypercommutative algebras with a unit

[
30
]
) and integrable systems of conservation laws. This correspondence delivered a rich variety of new structural results, examples, and conjectures, see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
46
]
.

It was noticed in
[
18
]
that Buryak’s DR construction can be applied to produce integrable systems of conservation laws from the so-called F-CohFTs, the higher genera analog of flat F-manifolds. This approach was studied in detail in
[
3
]
. In
[
21
]
the Dubrovin-Zhang theory for general partial CohFTs was also proposed for F-CohFTs and the existence (polynomiality) of the corresponding system of conservation laws and equivalence to the DR construction of conservation laws were reduced to a system of tautological relations proved subsequently in
[
10
,
12
]
.

Note that the approach of
[
21
]
uses
all genera
data input. A proposal on how to restore the original Dubrovin-Zhang axiomatic approach in the context of flat F-manifolds, that would employ purely
genus zero
data, was recently developed in
[
46
]
.

0.4.
The
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
class

The study of the tau function of the DR hierarchy, and in particular its quantum analog
[
14
,
8
,
9
]
led the first two authors of the present paper to realize that many correlators can be alternatively described by the intersection numbers with the so-called
\<
h>
-classes, which is a particular family of specializations of the
\Omega
-classes, or Chiodo classes, whose Chern characters were introduced and computed in
[
23
]
, and it was considered as and proved to be a CohFT in
[
41
]
. See e.g.
[
31
]
for a summary on their properties. In the case of the
classical
(as opposed to quantum, which we do not discuss in this paper) correlators one has to multiply the
\<
h>
classes by the top Chern class of the Hodge bundle, and the resulting classes are called
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
-classes in
[
11
]
.

In Dubrovin-Zhang theory (both for partial CohFTs and F-CohFTs) the partition functions are always described in terms of intersections with
\psi
-classes. In the DR approach to integrability it was shown that the partition functions can be described via the so-called
{\mathbb{A}}
-classes
[
14
,
15
,
21
]
. It was noticed in
[
7
,
11
]
that the
{\mathbb{A}}
-classes interact with
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
classes in exactly the same way as it was conjectured to interact with
\psi
-classes, and in fact this similarity was the main source of ideas that led to the proofs of existence of the Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies and their equivalence to the DR hierarchies in the most general situation in
[
10
,
12
]
.

A natural question is whether the
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
classes carry integrability intrinsically. In the present paper we answer this question in the affirmative by revisiting the notion of coupling to gravity, traditionally understood as intersection with
\psi
-classes, and introducing the concept of
integrable observables
. This framework places
\psi
-,
{\mathbb{A}}
-, and
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
-classes on equal footing as integrable observables, and provides a uniform mechanism that recovers the Dubrovin–Zhang and double ramification hierarchies, while also yielding a new class of integrable systems, that deserves further investigation per sé. It would be also interesting to know whether integrable observables different from
\psi
-classes, as e.g.
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
-classes, have some physics meaning arising from
2D
quantum gravity.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Cohomological field theories and their generalisations

There are several natural algebraic structures on the collection of the moduli spaces of curves
\{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}\}
,
g\geq 0
,
n\geq 0
,
2g-2+n>0
, that are relevant for the connection to integrable systems: a modular operad, a cyclic operad graded by genus, or, once we distinguish one of the marked point and consider the spaces
\{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\}
, an operad graded by genus. It is not the exhaustive list, one can also consider the most natural structure of properad or wheeled prop, but they are not connected to fundamental types of integrability.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}
equipped with a non-degenerate bilinear symmetric map
\eta\colon V^{\otimes 2}\to\mathbb{C}
. Then
\{\mathrm{Hom}(V^{\otimes n},\mathbb{C})\}
can be equipped with a structure of a cyclic operad, or a modular operad, and the representations of
\{H_{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}),\mathbb{C}\}
in
\{\mathrm{Hom}(V^{\otimes n},\mathbb{C})\}
are called
partial cohomological field theories (P-CohFTs)
in the case of genus graded cyclic operad structure or
cohomological field theories (CohFTs)
in the case of modular operad structure.

The latter one was proposed by Kontsevich and Manin in
[
39
]
as an axiomatic way to capture the key properties of Gromov-Witten theories. It is the most studied object in this context, but it turns out that for connections to integrability the less restrictive and more general concept of partial cohomological field theory is already sufficient.

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
, not necessarily finite dimensional.
\{\mathrm{Hom}(V^{\otimes n},V)\}
is canonically equipped with a structure of an operad and the representations of
\{H_{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}),\mathbb{C}\}
in
\{\mathrm{Hom}(V^{\otimes n},V)\}
are called
F cohomological field theories (F-CohFTs)
.

This paper focuses on universal integrability properties of P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs, and the main goal of this paper is to break the tradition that goes back to Witten
[
53
]
to couple cohomological field theories to gravity using, as the fundamental system of observables, the so-called
\psi
-classes (gravitational descendants).

Before we proceed, a few remarks and conventions are in order. First of all, in the context of operadic structures it is natural to consider
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs (represented in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
vector spaces), and it does lead to non-trivial integrable systems as well. But we omit this more general framework for simplicity, in particular, we assume that we only take even classes on moduli spaces.
Second, we solely use P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs in the rest of the text, but each time we state anything for P-CohFTs, it does work also for usual CohFTs. Third, we focus on P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs equipped with a
unit
that we introduce below.

1.2.
Precise definitions of P-CohFT and F-CohFT with a unit

Let
V
be a finite dimentional vector space with a chosen vector
e\in V
and a non-degenerate bilinear symmetric map
\eta\in\mathrm{Hom}(V^{\otimes 2},\mathbb{C})
.
A (
V
-valued)
P-CohFT
is a system of linear maps of vector spaces

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\colon V^{\otimes n}\to H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n},\mathbb{C})

satisfying the following conditions:

commutativity:

\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-equivariant with respect to the action on
V^{\otimes n}
by permutation of the factors and the action on
H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n},\mathbb{C})
induced by the automorphisms of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
caused by permutation of labels of the marked points.

flat unit:

Let
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
be the forgetful morphisms, that is, the map that forgets the last marked point and stabilizes the curve. Then
\pi^{*}\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}=\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n+1}(e)
, where
e
is fed as the last argument. Moreover,
\mathfrak{pc}_{0,3}(e)=\eta
.

factorization:

Let
\mathrm{gl}\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1}+g_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}}
be the gluing morphisms, that is, the map that creates a nodal curve out of two curves by identifying into a node their last marked points and identifying the labels on the first and the second component with the ordered subsets
I_{1},I_{2}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n_{1}+n_{2}\}
,
|I_{1}|=n_{1}
,
|I_{2}|=n_{2}
. Then

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{gl}^{*}\mathfrak{pc}_{g_{1}+g_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}v_{i}\Big)=

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha\beta}\mathfrak{pc}_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i\in I_{1}}v_{i}\otimes e_{\alpha}\Big)\otimes\mathfrak{pc}_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i\in I_{2}}v_{i}\otimes e_{\beta}\Big),

where by
\vec{\bigotimes}
we denote the ordered tensor product with an index that runs over an ordered set, and
\eta^{\alpha\beta}e_{\alpha}\otimes e_{\beta}\in V^{\otimes 2}
is a bivector
\eta^{-1}
dual to
\eta
expressed in the basis of
V=\langle e_{1}=e,e_{2},\dots,e_{N}\rangle
. Here and below we always assume the summation over repeated Greek indices.

Remark 1.1
.

In order to define a more traditional concept of CohFT one has to enhance the factorization property with one more condition. Let
\mathrm{gl^{\prime}}\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g-1,n+2}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
that creates a nodal curve of the arithmetic genus one higher by identifying the
(n+1)
-st and
(n+2)
-nd marked point into a node. Then a P-CohFT is a CohFT if we additionally require that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{gl^{\prime}}^{*}\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\Big)=\eta^{\alpha\beta}\mathfrak{pc}_{g-1,n+2}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\otimes e_{\alpha}\otimes e_{\beta}\Big).

Examples of P-CohFTs include Gromov-Witten theories of target varieties, Witten’s
r
-spin classes and more general classes coming from quantum singularity theory of Fan-Jarvis-Ruan, etc. (which all are also examples of CohFTs). The authentic examples of P-CohFTs that are not CohFTs come, for instance, from the study of quantum singularity theory with symmetries, see
[
42
]
where this concept was first introduced.

Let
V
be a vector space with a chosen vector
e\in V
. It is now convenient to assume that the marked points of moduli spaces
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}
are labeled from
0
to
n
. A (
V
-valued)
F-CohFT

[
3
,
4
]
is a system of linear maps of vector spaces

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\colon V^{\otimes n}\to V\otimes H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1},\mathbb{C})

satisfying the following conditions:

commutativity:

\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-equivariant with respect to the action on
V^{\otimes n}
by permutation of the factors and the action on
H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1},\mathbb{C})
induced by the automorphisms of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}
caused by permutation of the labels
\{1,\dots,n\}
of the marked points (that is, the zeroth marked point is left intact).

flat unit:

Let
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}
be the forgetful morphisms, that is, the map that forgets the last marked point and stabilizes the curve. Then
\pi^{*}\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}=\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n+1}(e)
, where
e
is fed as the last argument. Moreover,
\mathfrak{fc}_{0,2}(e)=\mathrm{Id}_{V}
.

factorization:

Let
\mathrm{gl}\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1},n_{1}+2}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1}+g_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}+1}
be the gluing morphisms, that is, the map that creates a nodal curve out of two curves by identifying into a node the last marked point on the first curve with the zeroth marked point on the second curve and identifying the remaining non-zero labels on the first component and all non-zero labels on the second component with the ordered subsets
I_{1},I_{2}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n_{1}+n_{2}\}
,
|I_{1}|=n_{1}
,
|I_{2}|=n_{2}
. Then

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{gl}^{*}\mathfrak{fc}_{g_{1}+g_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}v_{i}\Big)=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{fc}_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i\in I_{1}}v_{i}\otimes\mathfrak{fc}_{g_{2},n_{2}}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i\in I_{2}}v_{i}\Big)\Big),

where we assume that the substitution of
\mathfrak{fc}_{g_{2},n_{2}}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i\in I_{2}}v_{i}\otimes e_{\beta}\Big)
as an argument in
\mathfrak{fc}_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}
acts as a right module with respect to the tensor multiplication by classes in
H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},1+n_{2}},\mathbb{C})
, that is, the right hand side takes values in
V\otimes H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1},1+n_{1}+1},\mathbb{C})\otimes H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},1+n_{2}},\mathbb{C})
.

In genus
0
this concept is reduced to the so-called flat F manifolds, or, in the formal setup and without unit, to hypercommutative algebras
[
45
,
30
]
.
Examples of F-CohFTs can be obtained, for instance, by forgetting part of the structure of given P-CohFTs. It is a canonical construction, and it reads

(1.6)

\displaystyle\eta\bigg(v_{0},\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\Big)\bigg)=\bigg[\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n+1}(\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\otimes v_{0}\Big)\bigg]^{\circlearrowright},

where
[\cdots]^{\circlearrowright}
is the operation of relabeling marked points
that makes the
last marked point the zeroth one, which is needed to match the convention we used in the definition of F-CohFT.

Remark 1.2
.

Both P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs can be considered with an infinite-dimensional target space
V
(for P-CohFTs one might need to replace the bilinear symmetric map
\eta
with a symmetric
2
-tensor in the basic setup), and there are meaningful examples, cf.
[
19
]
. However, to simplify the presentation of the basic constructions below one might always assume that
V
is finite dimensional. Moreover, we shall always work with a fixed basis
V=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\rangle
,
N=\dim V
, and assume that
e=e_{1}
.

1.3.
Tautological classes and coupling to gravity

The P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs can provide physically relevant information once one integrates the corresponding cohomology classes over arbitrary given cycles on the moduli spaces of curves. The structure of the cohomology of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
over
\mathbb{C}
is quite involved (even in the case one is interested just in the even degree part of it), and one typically restricts attention to the cycles that are Poincaré dual to the so-called
tautological classes
.

The cohomological tautological rings
RH^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
are defined as the minimal system of subalgebras of
H^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n},\mathbb{Q})
that is closed under the push-forwards with respect to all natural maps between the moduli spaces of curves, namely, the projections
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, the gluing maps
\mathrm{gl}\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1},n_{1}+1}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},n_{2}+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1}+g_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}}
, and the gluing maps
\mathrm{gl^{\prime}}\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g-1,n+2}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
.

Of particular importance are the so-called
\psi
-classes,
\psi_{i}\in RH^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
,
i=1,\dots,n
, defined as follows:
\psi_{i}
is the first Chern classes of the line bundle over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
whose fiber at a point in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
represented by a stable curve
C
with the marked points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in C
is given by
T^{*}_{x_{i}}C
.

Remark 1.3
.

It is often convenient to consider the Chow version of the tautological rings denoted by
R^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
. In these cases we use the Chow grading which is half of the cohomological grading, e.g.,
\psi_{i}\in R^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
.

The partition function that stores the information about the correlators of a given
(V,\eta)
-valued P-CohFT is defined as

(1.7)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\exp\big(\epsilon^{-2}F\big)=\exp\bigg(\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2g-2}F_{g}(\{t^{\alpha,d}\})\bigg)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\bigg(\sum_{g,n}\frac{\epsilon^{2g-2}}{n!}\sum_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}=0}^{\infty}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\mathfrak{pc}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{i}}\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{d_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\alpha_{i},d_{i}}\bigg)

where
e_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, form a basis of
V=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\rangle
,
\epsilon
and
t^{\alpha,d}
,
d\geq 0
, are formal variables.

The particular choice of
\psi
-classes as the key “observables” to form a partition function
Z
has, beyond its motivation from theoretical physics, at least three deep mathematical reasons:

•

If
\{\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\}
is a CohFT (to be a P-CohFT is not enough for this property), then the full collection of integrals
\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\mathfrak{pc}\big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{i}}\big)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{d_{i}}
determines uniquely and with explicit formulas all integrals of the shape
\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\mathfrak{pc}\big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{i}}\big)\cdot\mathfrak{a}
, where
\mathfrak{a}\in R^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is an arbitrary tautological class.

•

If
\{\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\}
is a CohFT with extra properties (it must be homogeneous), then it is expected that
Z
satisfies the so-called Virasoro constraints, that is,
Z
is annihilated by a system of differental operators of a particular shape that form a half of the Virasoro algebra. It is proved in some situations, but remains an open conjecture in general.

•

With no extra assumptions
on a P-CohFT
\{\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\}
,
Z
appears to be a tau function of a Hamiltonian integrable systems whose dispersionless limit is the so-called principal hierarchy, which is a Hamiltonian integrable system of hydrodynamic type
[
21
,
10
,
12
]
, see Section
3
for more details.

The third property (
integrability
) is especially remarkable since it does not require any extra assumptions on a P-CohFT and it allows to fully embed the study of P-CohFTs into the realm of Hamiltonian dispersive deformations of hydrodynamic integrable systems. In fact, it is a manifestation that virtually all problems in enumerative geometry possess strong integrability properties that can be used to universally resolve them.

It is also the only property that survives when one considers a
V
-valued F-CohFTs. More precisely, in this case one can assemble the generating series for the correlators into the so-called vector potential of an F-CohFT, which is indeed a system of formal vector fields on the loop space of
V^{*}
. For clarity, let us assume that
V
is finite dimensional with a fixed basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}
, and let
\{e^{1},\dots,e^{N}\}
be the dual basis. Then define

(1.8)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{g,n}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}=0}^{\infty}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,1+n}}e^{\alpha_{0}}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{i}}\Big)\bigg)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{d_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\alpha_{i},d_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha_{0},0}}

This vector field serves as a vector potential related in a particular universal way (mimicking the properties of a tau function) to a topological solution of an integrable system of conservation laws.

We focus on the integrability property and the main message of this paper is that in this regard the
\psi
-classes are not as exceptional as it might seem from the Witten’s theory of topological gravity, and we formulate general conditions and provide examples of two more systems of classes that turn partition functions of P-CohFTs into tau functions of Hamiltonian integrable systems and vector potentials of F-CohFTs into integrable systems of conservation laws.

1.4.
Integrable observables

The goal of this section is to formulate a list of formal requirements for a collection of classes in
R^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
that allows to replace the monomials of
\psi
-classes in the discussion above retaining the integrability properties of P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs. The definition below is motivated by a practical guide to construction of integrable systems from P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs that we explain in the next sections.

Definition 1.4

(Integrable observables)
.

We call a collection of polynomial-valued classes

(1.9)

\displaystyle O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in R^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}],

g\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
,
2g-2+n>0
,
integrable observables
if it satifies the following list of properties:

(1)

String and dilaton equations:
Let
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
be the map that forgets the last marked point. Then

(1.10)

\displaystyle\pi_{*}O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)=\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}+b\left(2g-2+n\right)\right)O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})+O(b^{2}).

Here the string equation is obtained by taking the constant term in
b
of this relation and the dilaton equation is obtained by extracting the coefficient of
b^{1}
. We also call this property the
push-forward property
.

(2)

Genus 0 normalization:

(1.11)

\displaystyle O_{0,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}\psi_{i}}.

(3)

Homogeneity:

(1.12)

\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in R^{\sum d_{i}}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}),

where
\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}
denotes the operator that extracts the coefficient of the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}
from the polynomial next to it.

(4)

Leveled rooted tree relations LRT-
1
and LRT-
2
:
these are some complicated properties of the classes that involve the sum over stable rooted trees with a level structure. We introduce them in Sec.
1.5
, see Def.
1.9
.

Both leveled rooted tree relations LRT-
1
and LRT-
2
define integrable observables, but the properties of the associated integrable systems are going to be different. When we need to specify what type of leveled rooted tree relations is used, we refer to
type
1
integrable observables
or
type
2
integrable observables
, respectively.

The definition might look complicated at first glance, especially the leveled rooted tree relations that are still to be defined, but it is motivated by the fact that it properly stores the universal integrability properties. In particular, we have the following.

Theorem 1

(Precise version: Proposition
3.2
)
.

(1) For any integrable observables
\{O_{g,n}\}
, and any F-CohFT
\{\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\}
, the vector field

(1.13)

\displaystyle{}^{O}\!X

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{g,n}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}=0}^{\infty}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}}e^{\alpha_{0}}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{i}}\Big)\bigg)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}O_{g,n+1}(0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\alpha_{i},d_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\alpha_{0},0}}

the integrable system is independent of the choice the integrable observable.

(2) If the integrable observables are of type

.

(3) If the integrable observable are of type
2

Theorem 2

(Precise version: Proposition

)
.

(1) For any integrable observables
\{O_{g,n}\}
, and any P-CohFT
\{\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\}

\displaystyle{}^{O}\!Z=\exp\bigg(\frac{{}^{O}\!F}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\alpha_{i},d_{i}}\bigg)

\epsilon\to 0
the integrable system is independent of the choice the integrable observable.

1.5.1.
Basic notation for trees

Let

•

•

•

H_{e}(T)\coloneqq H(T)\setminus L(T)

•

H_{-}(T)\subset H(T)

T

directed towards the root at the vertices where they are attached,

•

For a

1.5.3.
Levels

We enhance the structure of a stable rooted tree to the so-called leveled stable rooted tree (of genus

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{lvl}}(l)\coloneqq\sum_{v\in V(T),\ell(v)\leq l}g(v).

Of course,

It is also geometrically very natural to consider a full version of the leveled rooted tree relations. For
g,n,m\geq 0
, such that
2g-2+n+m>0
, the
full system of leveled rooted tree relations
reads:

(1.35)

LRT-m:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}{}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq 2g-2+m,\qquad m\geq 2;

(1.36)

LRT-1:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{0}\right]}{}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{1}-{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2g-1;

(1.37)

LRT-0:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left({}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{0}-{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2g-2.

However, among these relations only the type

and type

. We will show in Lemma

that LRT-

implies LRT-0.
The leveled rooted tree relations for

, whose consequence are discussed Sec.

Remark 1.12
.

There is also a different type of relations that we call
master relations
and introduce below. Their special cases can serve as an alternative for the leveled rooted tree relations in the definition of integrable observables. We discuss this alternative in Sec.
2
.

1.6.
Old and new
examples of integrable observables

In this section, we present three examples of integrable observables.
The first one is the classical Witten’s
\psi
-classes arranged into generating
n
-point functions. This example is so far the only one fully studied in the literature. The second example is the
{\mathbb{A}}
-class that leads to the DR hierarchy but in an unconventional way, and thus needs a revision and proofs of its key properties. The third example is new and constitutes the main geometric result of this paper.

The original Witten approach with

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\coloneqq{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}\psi_{i}}}.

Theorem 3
.

The classes
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
are integrable observables of type
1
and of type
2

The corresponding integrable systems associated to F-CohFTs and P-CohFTs by Theorems

The DR integrable systems, used as canonical reference points in Theorems

Let us also present a genuinely new example of integrable observables. More precisely, the classes themselves are not new, but their interpretation and usage as integrable observables is a new result.
To this end, we recall the definition of the so-called
\Omega

2.
Leveled rooted tree relations and master relations

2.1.
Full system of leveled rooted tree relations

As we mentioned in Remark
1.11
, it is natural to consider the leveled rooted tree relations of type
m
(LRT-
m
), which we recall here for convenience:

(2.1)

LRT-m:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}{}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq 2g-2+m,\qquad m\geq 2;

(2.2)

LRT-1:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{0}\right]}{}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{1}-{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2g-1;

(2.3)

LRT-0:

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left({}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{0}-{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2g-2.

The type
1
relation has the following direct corollary:

Lemma 2.1

(LRT-
1
implies LRT-0)
.

If
\{O_{g,n}\}
satisfy the leveled rooted tree relations of type
1
, then for any
g\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
,
2g+n>0
, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left({}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{0}-{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\right)\leq 2g-2.

Proof.

Apply the push-forward that forgets the last marked point in (
1.32
) and divide by
\mathbf{a}
(the latter factor emerges in the push-forward of
\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{0}\right]}{}^{O}\!B_{g,n}^{1}
by the string equation for
\{O_{g,n}\}
, and this matches the definition of
{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}
).
∎

Denote by
\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}m\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{0}\right]}\text{LRT-}m
,
m\geq 2
.
Then Lemma
2.1
has the following extension:

Lemma 2.2
.

\pi_{*}(\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}m)=\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}(m-1)
for
m\geq 3
and
\pi_{*}(\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}2)=0
.

This lemma is an easy observation proved in
[
7
]
for classes
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
. Since the proof only uses the combinatorics of leveled graphs and the string equation, it can be universally stated for any
\{O_{g,n}\}
satisfying the string equation.

Remark 2.3
.

Interestingly enough, we see that the statement of Lemma
2.2
allows to derive
\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}m
from
\text{LRT}_{0}\text{-}m^{\prime}
for
m^{\prime}>m
. However, the way these relations are applied to integrable systems (see Sec.
3
) rather suggests that there should be a way to derive
\text{LRT}\text{-}m
from
\text{LRT}\text{-}2
for any
m\geq 2
. This is totally not obvious from the structure of these relations, however, it was proved in the context of classes
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
in
[
44
]
.

2.2.
The master relations

For any
g\geq 0,n\geq 1
such that
2g-2+n\geq 0
and for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, let

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{\lambda_{g}\DR_{g}\big(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},-\mathbf{a}\big)}{(1+\mathbf{a}\psi_{n+1})}

where
\mathbf{a}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
.
If
T\in\mathrm{SRT}_{g,n,m}
and
v\in V_{nr}(T)
, we introduce

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}(v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{D}}_{g(v),|H(v)|}(a(h_{1}),\dots,a(h_{|H_{+}(v)|}))\in R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g(v),|H(v)|})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}Q.

For
m\geq 1
, consider the following class in
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+m})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}Q
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\qquad\delta_{m,1}\mathbb{D}_{g,n+1}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)+O_{g,n+m}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathrm{SRT}(g,n,m)\\
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(t),\,\ell(T)=1\end{subarray}}\biggl(\prod_{e\in E(T)}a(e)\biggr)(b_{T})_{*}\bigg(O(v_{r})\otimes\bigotimes_{v\in V_{nr}(T)}{\mathbb{D}}(v)\bigg),

recalling that
O(v_{r})
is defined in Eq. (
1.19
).
Note that the Chow degree
d
of
{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{m}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in the variables
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
.

Definition 2.4

(Master relations)
.

For
m\geq 2
, we say that the classes
\{O_{g,n}\}
satisfy the
m
-th master relation
, or M-
m
, if

(2.8)

\displaystyle\deg_{a}{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{m}\leq 2g-2+m.

For
m=1
, we say that the classes
\{O_{g,n}\}
satisfy the
1
-st master relation
, or M-1, if

(2.9)

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\Big(\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{0}\right]}{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1})\Big)\leq 2g-1.

Note that in this case
\deg_{R^{\bullet}}=\deg_{a}
by homogeneity.

2.3.
Connections between relations

We have the following system of logical implications between the sets of relations:

Lemma 2.5
.

\text{M-}1\Leftrightarrow\text{LRT-}1
. Moreover,
\text{M-}1\ \&\ \text{M-}m\Rightarrow\text{LRT-}m
.

The statements of this Lemma are proved in
[
10
,
11
]
in application to
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
and
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
classes, but the proofs use only combinatorics of graphs, properties of double ramification cycles / rubber relative stable maps to the projective line (to treat the
\mathbb{D}_{g,n+1}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)
class in the master relations), and combinatorics of graphs.

This suggests some alternatives for the fourth condition in the definition of integrable observables (Def.
1.4
). Namely, for type
1
integrability to can alternatively demand
\text{M-}1
instead of
\text{LRT-}1
. For type
2
integrability we can demand
\text{M-}1\ \&\ \text{M-}2
. The master relations are easier to prove from the scratch, and indeed, for
\{{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\}
we prove below
\text{M-}1
rather than
\text{LRT-}1
thus establishing the type
1
integrability.

The natural statement that can be uniformly proved for
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
and
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
is yet a bit different, and we formulate it in the next subsection.

2.4.
The geometric master relation

For
m\geq 1
, consider the following class in
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+m})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}Q
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\delta_{m,1}\mathbb{D}_{g,n+1}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)+O_{g,n+m}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-b_{i}\psi_{i+n}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\mathrm{SRT}(g,n,m)\\
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(t),\,\ell(T)=1\end{subarray}}\biggl(\prod_{e\in E(T)}a(e)\biggr)(b_{T})_{*}\bigg(O(v_{r})\otimes\bigotimes_{v\in V_{nr}(T)}{\mathbb{D}}(v)\bigg)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-b_{i}\psi_{i+n}\right).

That is, compared to
{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{m}
we just multiplied the two non-exceptional summands in (
2.7
) by
\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-b_{i}\psi_{i+n})
. In particular, in the case of
\{O_{g,n}={\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle{}^{\mathbb{\Psi}}\Upsilon_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})

does not depend on
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
. For a collection of polynomial valued classes
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
satisfying the homogeneity assumption, we say that is satisfies the
m
-th
geometric master relations
, or GM-
m
, if

(2.12)

\displaystyle\deg_{R^{\bullet}}{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\leq 2g-2+m.

It satisfies geometric master relations if GM-
m
holds for any
m\geq 1
.

This new definition is justified by the following statements and remarks. First, the clear advantage of geometric master relations is that we use the geometrically defined degree
\deg_{R^{\bullet}}
in their formulation, and this does help to prove them in examples.

Lemma 2.7
.

For a collection of polyomial valued classes
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
we have that GM-
m
implies M-
m
, for any
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

For
m=1
, we just take the constant term in
b_{1}
in
{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}^{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1})
in order to obtain
\text{M-}1
. For
m\geq 2
, observe that

(2.13)

\displaystyle{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})={}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}^{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-b_{i}\psi_{i+n}).

Moreover,
\deg_{R^{\bullet}}{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}\geq\deg_{a}{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}
. Thus, the geometric master relation implies

(2.14)

\displaystyle\deg_{a}{}^{O}\Upsilon_{g,n}\leq 2g-2+m,\quad m\geq 2.

The
m
th master relation for
m\geq 2
then follows from Eq. (
2.13
) and the fact that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\deg_{a}\left({}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-b_{i}\psi_{i+n})\right)=\deg_{a}{}^{O}\Xi_{g,n}.

∎

Remark 2.8
.

Note that for
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
the geometric master relations trivially coincide with the master relations.

Proposition 2.9
.

The collections of classes
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
and
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
satisfy the geometric master relations.

As an immediate corollary
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
and
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
satisfy the master relations
\text{M-}m
, which, by the discussion above, implies the integrability of both type
1
and type
2
, once the other necessary properties are established. We prove this proposition for
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
in Sec.
5
as a part of the proof of Thm.
5
, and for
\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
it is proved in
[
12
]
.

Remark 2.10
.

Specializing to
b_{1}=\cdots=b_{m}=0
, the geometric and ordinary master relations are equivalent. In this specialization the statement of Prop.
2.9
for
\{{\mathbb{\Pi}}_{g,n}\}
was conjectured in
[
7
]
and proved in
[
11

.

3.1.
Basic setup for evolutionary equations

The phase space is the formal loop space that can be realized as the spectrum of

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\coloneqq\mathbb{C}[[\epsilon,\{w^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}]][\{w^{\alpha,d}\}_{{\alpha=1,\dots,N;\;d\geq 1}}].

Here

\partial_{x}\colon\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\to\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
is given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{x}\coloneqq\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}w^{\alpha,d+1}{\partial}_{w^{\alpha,d}}.

Define the grading
\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}
on
\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
by
\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}(w^{\alpha,d})\coloneqq d
,
\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}(\epsilon)\coloneqq-1
. Let
(\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]])_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=k}
denote the component of
\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
of homogeneous
\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}
degree
k
.

An
evolutionary equation
is an equation of the form

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}w^{\alpha}=P^{\alpha},

where
P^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=1}
.

We say that
P^{\alpha}
is a
conservation law
if

(3.4)

P^{\alpha}=\partial_{x}Q^{\alpha}

for some
Q^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
called
flux
. Two evolutionary equations
{\partial}_{t^{i}}w^{\alpha}=P^{\alpha}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are
compatible
(or, equivalently, the flows
{\partial}_{t^{i}}
commute), if

(3.5)

{\partial}_{t^{1}}(P_{2}^{\alpha})={\partial}_{t^{2}}(P_{1}^{\alpha}),\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N.

An
integrable system
is a system of mutually compatible evolutionary equations.

It is useful to consider the action action of the group of Miura transformations, which is the semidirect product of two subgroups, the so-called Miura transformations of the first and the second kind.
Miura transformations of the first kind
are the changes of the dependent variables
\tilde{w}^{\alpha}=W^{\alpha}(w^{1},\dots,w^{N})
, where
W^{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N,
are formal power series in
w^{1},\dots,w^{N}
with vanishing constant terms and
\det({\partial}W^{\alpha}/{\partial}w^{\beta})|_{w^{1}=\cdots=w^{N}=0}\not=0
. We will not need them in this paper, due to normalization condition in genus
0
(i.e. at
\epsilon=0
).

Miura transformations of the second kind
(also called Miura transformations close to the identity) are the changes of the dependent variables of the type
\tilde{w}^{\alpha}=w^{\alpha}+\epsilon U^{\alpha}
, where
U^{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=1}
.

Let

(3.6)

\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\coloneqq\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]/{\partial}_{x}\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]

be the associated space of
local functionals
and let

(3.7)

\int\!dx\colon\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\to\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]],\qquad f\mapsto\bar{f}\coloneqq\int fdx

be the projection that sends densities to their functionals (the degree
\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}
descends to
\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
. Let
\delta_{w^{\alpha}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\to\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
be the variational derivatives, defined by

(3.8)

\delta_{w^{\alpha}}\coloneqq\sum_{d\geq 0}(-{\partial}_{x})^{d}\partial_{w^{\alpha,d}}.

A bilinear map
\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\times\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]\to\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]
,

(3.9)

(\bar{f}_{1},\bar{f}_{2})\mapsto[\bar{f}_{1},\bar{f}_{2}]\coloneqq\iint(\delta_{w^{\alpha}}\bar{f}_{1})\{w^{\alpha}(x),w^{\beta}(y)\}(\delta_{w^{\beta}}\bar{f}_{2})dxdy,

where
\{w^{\alpha}(x),w^{\beta}(y)\}=\sum_{d\geq 0}A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}\delta^{(d)}(x-y)
,
A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=1-d}
, gives a Poisson bracket if it satisfies the Jacobi identity.

We say that Eq. (
3.3
) possess a Hamiltonian structure if it can be written as

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}w^{\alpha}=\sum_{d\geq 0}A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}{\partial}_{x}^{d}\delta_{w^{\beta}}\bar{h}

for a Poisson bracket
\sum_{d\geq 0}A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}\delta^{(d)}(x-y)
and a Hamiltonian
\bar{h}\in\mathcal{F}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
. If the Hamiltonians
\bar{h}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, commute with respect to the Poisson bracket, then their flows commute as well, so one can define an integrable system by a Poisson bracket and a system of pairwise commuting Hamiltonians.

We say that a bracket
\{w^{\alpha}(x),w^{\beta}(y)\}=\sum_{d\geq 0}A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}\delta^{(d)}(x-y)
has
hydrodynamical limit
, if at
\epsilon=0
it reduces to

(3.11)

g^{\alpha\beta}(\{w^{*,0}\})\delta^{\prime}(x-y)+\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(\{w^{*,0}\})w^{\gamma,1}\delta(x-y)

for a non-degenerate metric
g_{\alpha,\beta}
. With our normalization condition in genus
0
, we shall always have
g^{\alpha\beta}=\eta^{\alpha\beta}
, where
\eta
is the constant metric defined by the bilinear symmetric map
\eta\colon V^{\otimes 2}\to\mathbb{C}
on the underlying vector space
V
and
\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}=0
.

All integrable systems that we study below have infinite set of commuting flows indexed by
(\alpha,p)
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N
,
p=0,\dots,\infty
, that is, they are of the form

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}w^{\alpha}={\partial}_{x}Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}

in the case of the conservation laws, or, in the case of Hamiltonian systems, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}w^{\alpha}=\sum_{d\geq 0}A^{\alpha\beta}_{d}{\partial}_{x}^{d}\delta_{w^{\beta}}\bar{h}_{\beta,p}.

Here
\{\bar{h}_{\beta,p}\}
are the pairwise commuting Hamiltonians.

In the former case, pick a solution
w^{\alpha}_{{\rm str}}
of Eq. (
3.12
) such that
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{w^{\gamma,q}=\partial^{q}_{t^{1,0}}w^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{str}}}dt^{\beta,p}
is closed. Since the closeness implies exactness, there exists a unique vector (up to constant term)

(3.14)

X^{\alpha}(\{t^{\gamma,q}\})\partial_{t^{\alpha,0}},

called
vector potential
, such that

(3.15)

Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{w^{\gamma,q}=\partial^{q}_{t^{1,0}}w^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{str}}}=\partial_{t^{\beta,p}}X^{\alpha}.

In the coordinates such that
w^{\alpha}=Q^{\alpha}_{1,0}
, we therefore have
w^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{str}}=\partial_{t^{1,0}}X^{\alpha}
. We repeatedly use the following: if
w^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{str}}=\partial_{t^{1,0}}X^{\alpha}
satisfies the string equation (to be explicitly defined below), then the fluxes
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}
are reconstructed as the unique elements of
\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{\partial_{x}}=0}
satisfying Eq. (
3.15
) (see Remark
3.5
below). In that sense we say that the system is controlled by the vector potential
X^{\alpha}(\{t^{\gamma,q}\})\partial_{t^{\alpha,0}}
.

In the Hamiltonian case, there is a notion of tau symmetric integrable system (see
[
13
]
for a comprehensive introduction), in which one can define
tau functions
. For our purposes, what matters is that a choice of tau function
Z
(or rather its logarithm
F(\{t^{\gamma,q}\})=\epsilon^{2}\log Z
) together with a special solution
w^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{str}}=\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}F
controls particular choices of densities and fluxes. More precisely, all densities
h_{\beta,p}
and fluxes
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}
are reconstructed as the unique elements of
\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
satisfying

(3.16)

\displaystyle h_{\beta,p}|_{w^{\gamma,q}={\partial}_{t^{1,0}}^{q}w^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{str}}}

\displaystyle={\partial}_{t^{\beta,p+1}}{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}F

\displaystyle Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{w^{\gamma,q}={\partial}_{t^{1,0}}^{q}w^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{str}}}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}F

as formal power series in the variables
\{t^{\zeta,k}\}
.
There is a subgroup of the group of Miura transformations of second kind that preserves the existence of the tau structure and the dispersionless limit and whose action is extended in a coherent way on all ingredients of the integrable system, the so-called
normal Miura transformations
. They are given by
\tilde{w}^{\alpha}=w^{\alpha}+\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{x}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}G
for any
G\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=-2}
, and we evaluate
{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}G
as an element of
\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=-1}
using the integrable system itself, that is,
{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}G=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}{\partial}_{w^{\gamma,q}}{\partial}_{x}^{q+1}Q^{\gamma}_{\mu,0}
.

3.2.
DR hierarchy of conservation laws

The DR hierarchy, originally inspired by the construction of the quantum hierarchy in symplectic field theory
[
28
]
, was introduced in
[
22
]
for CohFTs.
In
[
18
]
the construction was further extended to the cases of P-CohFTs and F-CohFTs. We still work with the target space of finite dimension
N
, though it is possible to extend the construction to the infinite-dimensional setup, cf. Remark
1.2
.

Let
\{\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\}
be a
V
-valued F-CohFT. Following
[
18
]
, for any
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N
and
p\geq 0
, define the flux
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}\in\mathcal{A}_{u}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
by

(3.17)

\displaystyle Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g,n\geq 0\\
2g+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\psi_{2}^{p}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}\lambda_{g}\DR_{g}(-\mathbf{a},0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n+1}\Big(e_{\beta}\otimes\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big)\bigg).

Here
u^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}
are jet coordinates defined in (
3.1
). These fluxes define the commuting flows by
[
18
, Thm. 5.1]
, that is, the system of conservation laws

(3.18)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}u^{\alpha}={\partial}_{x}Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}

is integrable, and it is called the DR hierarchy associated to an F-CohFT. Note that in particular the flow
{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}
is identified with
{\partial}_{x}
, and we adopt this identification in what follows. The coordinates
u^{\alpha}
used in the definition are called the
natural coordinates
.

This hierarchy can also be described in terms of its vector potential and the F-string solution, which we now introduce. The vector potential is
X^{\DR,\alpha}\partial_{t^{\alpha,0}}
, defined by

(3.19)

\displaystyle X^{\DR,\alpha}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,n\geq 1\\
2g-1+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}}\bigg[\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\bigg]^{\circlearrowright}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big)\bigg),

\displaystyle\alpha=1,\dots,N,

where we recall that
[\cdots]^{\circlearrowright}
is the operation of relabeling marked points
that makes the
last marked point the zeroth one, which is needed to match the convention we used in the definition of F-CohFT. The
F-string solution
is defined by

(3.20)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\text{F-str}}\coloneqq{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}X^{\DR,\alpha},\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N.

This is in fact a special solution of (
3.18
) specified by
u^{\alpha}_{\text{F-str}}|_{t^{*,\geq 1}=0}=t^{\alpha,0}
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, see
[
21
, Thm. 4.9]
in combination with
[
10
, Lemma 2.6]
. It can be expressed in terms of the class
{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n+1}^{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)
as

(3.21)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\text{F-str}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{{g\geq 0,n\geq 1}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\bigg[\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)\bigg]^{\circlearrowright}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n+1}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\otimes e_{1}\Big)\bigg),

\displaystyle\alpha=1,\dots,N.

Then, identifying
u^{\gamma,d}_{\text{F-str}}
with
{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}^{d}u^{\gamma}_{\text{F-str}}
,
\gamma=1,\dots,N
,
d\geq 0
, we can reconstruct
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}
as the unique elements of
\mathcal{A}_{u}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
satisfying

(3.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t^{\beta,p}}X^{\DR,\alpha}=Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{u^{\gamma,q}=u^{\gamma,q}_{\text{F-str}},\,\gamma=1,\dots,N,\,q\geq 0}.

for all
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N
,
p\geq 0
. To this end, one needs to use the string equation and
[
21
, Lemma 4.3]
for identification of fluxes, see also Remark
3.5
below.
We refer to
[
21
, Sec. 4.4.1, 4.4.2]
for a full exposition of this approach.

3.3.
Hamiltonian DR hierarchy

Let
\{\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\}
be a
(V,\eta)
-valued P-CohFT.
We can repeat the construction of the previous section using only part of the structure of P-CohFT, that is, the canonically associated F-CohFT. Then the formulas for the fluxes
[
18
]
can be reproduced as follows. For any
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N
and
p\geq 0
, define the flux
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}\in\mathcal{A}_{u}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
by

(3.23)

\displaystyle Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g,n\geq 0\\
2g+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\psi_{2}^{p}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}\lambda_{g}\DR_{g}(-\mathbf{a},0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\eta^{\alpha\mu}\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n+2}\Big(e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\beta}\otimes\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big).

However, in this case the integrable hierarchy (
3.18
) appears to be Hamiltonian. For any
1\leq\alpha\leq N
and
p\geq-1
, define
h_{\alpha,p}\in\mathcal{A}_{u}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
by

(3.24)

\displaystyle h_{\alpha,p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\eta_{1\mu}Q^{\mu}_{\alpha,p+1}.

Consider the Poisson bracket
\{u^{\alpha},u^{\beta}\}_{\eta^{-1}{\partial}_{x}}\coloneqq\eta^{\alpha\beta}\delta(x-y)
.
The main result of
[
22
]
states that

(3.25)

\{\bar{h}_{\alpha_{1},d_{1}},\bar{h}_{\alpha_{2},d_{2}}\}_{\eta^{-1}{\partial}_{x}}=0

for any
1\leq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\leq N
and
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
. This allows to rewrite the system of conservation laws in natural coordinates (
3.18
) as a Hamiltonian system:

(3.26)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}u^{\alpha}={\partial}_{x}Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}=\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{x}\delta_{u^{\mu}}\bar{h}_{\beta,p}.

In this setting of P-CohFT, the system is proved to be tau-symmetric in
[
13
]
and it can be described in the
normal coordinates

(3.27)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{norm}}\coloneqq\eta^{\alpha\mu}{h}_{\mu,-1}

in terms of the tau function.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that in the context of F-CohFT, the same change of variable holds in the form

(3.28)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{norm}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g,n\geq 0\\
2g+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}\lambda_{g}\DR_{g}(0,-\mathbf{a},a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n+1}\Big(e_{1}\otimes\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big)\bigg).

However, this does not provide normal coordinates in the usual sense, as the system is not tau-symmetric.

The logarithm of the tau function is given by

(3.29)

\displaystyle F^{\DR}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\geq 0,n\geq 1\\
2g-2+n>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big).

Note that, by Lemma
4.1
, we have
{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}F^{\DR}=\eta_{1\mu}X^{\DR,\mu}
, where the latter
X^{\DR,\mu}
are the ones defined for the canonically associated F-CohFT.
The
P-string solution
is defined by

(3.30)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\text{P-str}}\coloneqq\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}F^{\DR},\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,N.

This is in fact a solution of (
3.26
) considered in normal coordinates, see
[
21
, Sec. 4.4.4]
in combination with
[
10
, Lemma 2.6]
. It can be expressed in terms of the class
{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n+1}^{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)
as

(3.31)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}_{\text{P-str}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{{g\geq 0,n\geq 1}}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 0}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}t^{\gamma_{i},d_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{d_{i}}\right]}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\eta^{\alpha\mu}\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n+2}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\otimes e_{\mu}\otimes e_{1}\Big),

\displaystyle\alpha=1,\dots,N.

(note the difference of this formula and Eq. (
3.21
) for
u^{\alpha}_{\text{F-str}}
for the canonically associated F-CohFT).
Then, identifying
u^{\gamma,d}_{\text{P-str}}
with
{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}^{d}u^{\gamma}_{\text{P-str}}
,
\gamma=1,\dots,N
,
d\geq 0
, we can reconstruct
Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}
and
h_{\beta,p}
as the unique elements of
\mathcal{A}_{u}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
satisfying

(3.32)

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha\mu}\partial_{t^{\mu,0}}\partial_{t^{\beta,p}}F^{\DR}=Q^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{u^{\gamma,q}=u^{\gamma,q}_{\text{P-str}},\,\gamma=1,\dots,N,\,q\geq 0};

\displaystyle\partial_{t^{1,0}}\partial_{t^{\beta,p+1}}F^{\DR}=h_{\beta,p}|_{u^{\gamma,q}=u^{\gamma,q}_{\text{P-str}},\,\gamma=1,\dots,N,\,q\geq 0};

for all
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N
,
p\geq 0
.
We refer to
[
21
, Sec. 4.4.4]
for a full exposition of this approach.

3.4.
From F-CohFT to a system of conservation laws

Consider the vector field
{}^{O}\!X={{}^{O}\!X}^{\alpha}\partial_{t^{\alpha,0}}
, defined in Eq. (
1.13
), associated to an F-CohFT
\{\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\}
for an arbitrary system of integrable observables
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
.

The goal is to construct an integrable system of conservation laws with a distinguished solution (the so-called
topological solution
) given by
w_{\mathrm{top}}^{\alpha}\coloneqq{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}{{}^{O}\!X}^{\alpha}
and
w_{\mathrm{top}}^{\alpha,d}\coloneqq{\partial}^{d+1}_{t^{1,0}}{{}^{O}\!X}^{\alpha}
for
d\geq 0
. To this end, we have the following

Proposition 3.2
.

(1) Assume
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
are integrable observables. Then there exists a unique system of fluxes
R^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}\in\mathcal{A}_{w}[[\epsilon]]_{\deg_{{\partial}_{x}}=0}
such that

(3.33)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}{{}^{O}\!X}^{\alpha}=R^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}\big|_{w^{\gamma,q}=w^{\gamma,q}_{\mathrm{top}},\,\gamma=1,\dots,N,\,q\geq 0}.

The system of conservations laws

(3.34)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t^{\beta,p}}w^{\alpha}={\partial}_{x}R^{\alpha}_{\beta,p},\qquad\beta=1,\dots,N,\,p\geq 0,

is integrable and
w_{\mathrm{top}}^{\alpha}
is a solution.

Moreover, in the dispersionless limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the fluxes
R^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}|_{\epsilon=0}
are independent of the choice of the integrable observable.

(2) If
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
are integrable observables of type
1
, then this system of conservation laws is Miura equivalent to the DR hierarchy of conservation laws associated to
\{\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\}
. The Miura transformation is given by

(3.35)

\displaystyle u^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=w^{\alpha}-{\partial}_{x}R^{\alpha},

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\geq 0\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}=2g-1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w^{\gamma_{i},q_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}}\bigg[\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{0}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{q_{i}}\right]}{}^{O}\!B^{1}_{g,n}\bigg]^{\circlearrowright}e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\Big)\bigg).

(3) If
\{O_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}
are integrable observables of type
2
, then we have the following formula:

(3.36)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{\beta,p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\geq 0\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w^{\gamma_{i},q_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\bigg[\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{p}b_{2}^{0}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{q_{i}}\right]}{}^{O}\!B^{2}_{g,n}\bigg]^{\circlearrowright}e^{\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{fc}_{g,n+1}\Big(\vec{\bigotimes}_{i=1}^{n}e_{\gamma_{i}}\otimes e_{\beta}\Big)\bigg).

The proof follows
mutatis mutandis
the proof of
[
21
, Thm. 4.6 and Thm. 4.10]
presented there for
\{O_{g,n}={\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}
, with the further improvements and observations given in
[
7
, Thm. 2.15]
and
[
10
, Lem. 2.6]

Remark 3.7
.

Both type
1
and type
2
integrable observables provide an integrable hierarchy of conservation laws. The ones for type
1
(

to the DR hierarchy of conservation laws, with this Miura transformation being fully explicit (in one of the directions, in the other one has to formally invert it). On the other hand, the conservations laws for type

For the integrable observables

\{{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\}

{\mathbb{A}}

3.7

\displaystyle\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}F^{\DR}=\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{t^{1,0}}{\partial}_{t^{\mu,0}}{{}^{O}\!F}-\eta^{\alpha\mu}{\partial}_{x}\Big({\partial}_{w^{\zeta,k}}R\cdot{\partial}_{x}^{d+1}R^{\zeta}_{\mu,0}\Big)|_{w^{\gamma,d}=w_{\mathrm{top}}^{\gamma,d}},

and thus gives the desired normal Miura transformation explicitly.
∎

3.9.
Interrelations between the obtained hierarchies

As we discussed above, for the integrable observables

\{{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}\}

An immediate corollary of Prop.
3.2
and
3.3
is the following:

Corollary 3.13
.

Consider the Dubrovin-Zhang, the Double Ramification, and
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
- hierarchies.

(1)

For any P-CohFT or F-CohFT, they have the same dispersionless limit.

(2)

For any F-CohFT, the three systems of conservation laws are connected by Miura transformations of second kind.

(3)

For any P-CohFT, the three integrable hierarchies are tau symmetric, Hamiltonian, and all normal Miura equivalent to each other.

Remark 3.14
.

It is important to stress that we don’t have a construction of a direct (normal) Miura transformation between the Dubrovin-Zhang and the
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
-hierarchies. Their (normal) Miura equivalence is a consequence of their relations with the DR hierarchy.

.

]

is used in the proof of Cor. 5.5 in

In our terms here it can be interpreted as the statement that

Proof of Corollary

.

Slightly abusing notation, let

be the maps that forget the last and the next to the last marked points, respectively, and
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
be the map that forgets the last marked point.

Recall that
(\mathbf{a}+b){\mathbb{A}}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)=\pi_{*}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a}+b)\pi_{*}{\mathbb{A}}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)=\pi_{*}\pi_{*}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\pi_{*}\pi^{\prime}_{*}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(\mathbf{a}+(2g-1+n)b)\pi_{*}{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})+O(b^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{a}+(2g-1+n)b){\mathbb{A}}_{g,n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})+O(b^{2}).

This is an equality of two polynomials in

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{a}+(2g-1+n)b)}{\mathbf{a}+b}

6.

6.1.
A new proof of the Witten conjecture

The Witten conjecture
[
53
]
stated that the partition function of the trivial CohFT (that is, in our terms we can say that we have a partial CohFT on a one-dimensional vector space
V=\langle e_{1}\rangle
,
\eta_{1,1}=1
, and
\mathfrak{pc}_{g,n}(e_{1}^{\otimes n})=1\in R^{\bullet}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
for all
g
and
n
) satisfies the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equations.

There are by now many proofs and their variations, where one first uses some geometric idea to compute the intersection numbers of
\psi
-classes on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, and then typically some technique on the side of integrable systems to connect it to the KdV equations or some other equivalent formulations. See
[
54
]
for the most recent instance of a variation of the argument on the side of integrable systems, and
[
2
]
for the most recent proof that does use a new geometric idea. We also refer to both papers for an overview of other existing proofs.

For us it is important to count the original geometric ideas, since it is a more difficult part of each proof. We list them here in the historical order with a reference to the first proof that employed that idea:

(1)

[
40
]
uses the ribbon graph model for moduli spaces;

(2)

[
38
,
48
]
use the ELSV formula for Hurwitz numbers (
[
38
]
is published earlier, but
[
48
]
has appeared earlier as a preprint);

(3)

[
47
]
uses symplectic reduction for Weil-Petersson volumes;

(4)

[
2
]
uses reduction of
\psi
-classes on
\DR
cycles.

The proof we give below uses, as we shall see, a new geometric ingredient, namely, the master relation of
[
12
]
, which is based on two geometric inputs: (1) localization of spin Gromov-Witten theory of the projective line, and (2) the degeneration of the
\DR
cycles over the divisors in the Losev-Manin space. Although the first input sounds close to the ELSV-type formula, which can itself be obtained by localization of usual Gromov-Witten theory of the projective line, cf.
[
48
]
, the geometry behind it and the resulting formulas are drastically different. The second geometric input is close to what is used in
[
2
]
, but it is employed in a completely different way in the argument.

Before turning to the proof, we record the following observation:

Lemma 6.1
.

The Dubrovin-Zhang,
\DR
, and
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
hierarchies coincide for the trivial P-CohFT.

Proof.

The Dubrovin-Zhang and
\DR
hierarchies are proved to coincide in this case in
[
22
]
. The coincidence of the
\DR
and the
{\mathbb{\Pi}}
hierarchies follows from Prop.
3.15
.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

The partition function of the trivial P-CohFT satisfies the KdV equation.

Proof.

We have to compute the flux
R^{1}_{1,1}
for the time
t^{1,1}
in the notation of Eq. (
3.39

\{O_{g,n}\}=\{{\mathbb{\Psi}}_{g,n}\}

(6.1)

\displaystyle R^{1}_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{2g}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\geq 0\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w^{1,q_{i}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Coeff}}\nolimits_{\left[b_{1}^{1}b_{2}^{0}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{q_{i}}\right]}{}^{{\mathbb{\Psi}}}\!B^{2}_{g,n}.

For dimensional reasons, the only contributing graphs in

\displaystyle R^{1}_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(w^{1,0})^{2}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,4}}\psi_{1}^{0}\psi_{2}^{0}\psi_{3}^{1}\psi_{4}^{0}+\epsilon^{2}w^{1,2}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{1,3}}\psi_{1}^{2}\psi_{2}^{1}\psi_{3}^{0}.

(6.3)

\displaystyle R^{1}_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(w^{1,0})^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12}w^{1,2}.
