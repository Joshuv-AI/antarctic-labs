Shared genetic architecture between Alzheimer’s disease and brain morphology
Authors: Xiao Wu, Alexey Shadrin, Gabriëlla A M Blokland, Sinan Guloksuz, Ole A Andreassen, David E J Linden, Dennis van der Meer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA hallmark of Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) is brain tissue atrophy across multiple cortical and subcortical regions. However, studies report no significant genetic correlation between LOAD and brain morphology, indicating that the genetic drivers of neurodegeneration are complex. Resolving this shared genetic architecture is key to uncovering the biological mechanisms linking brain morphology to LOAD.MethodsWe analyzed UK Biobank genotype data (n = 272,513; 57.1 ± 8.0 years; 54.6% female) alongside genome-wide association study summary statistics for LOAD and 89 brain morphological traits. We employed linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), polygenic score (PGS), and Local Analysis of [co]Variant Association (LAVA) to evaluate genetic relationships at the whole-genome and locus level. The extent of polygenic overlap was quantified using bivariate causal mixture modeling (MiXeR), and specific shared loci were identified via conjunctional FDR. Enrichment analyses indicated that these shared genes are involved in biological processes related to cell differentiation, development, and cellular component organization.ConclusionsOur findings provide evidence of extensive polygenic overlap between LOAD and brain morphology, revealing that the absence of global genetic correlation stems from mixed local effect directions. These shared genetic architectures implicate pathways in cellular development and differentiation, providing a molecular framework for understanding the link between brain morphology and LOAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02105-5.Keywords: Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease, Brain morphology, Genetic overlap, Polygenic architecture

Background

Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) is the leading cause of dementia in the elderly, with symptoms typically appearing after the age of 65 and affecting about 50 million people worldwide [1]. The heritability of LOAD has been estimated to be 60%–80% based on twin and family studies [2, 3], indicating a substantial genetic contribution to the disease. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous genetic variants associated with LOAD [4], among which the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ɛ4 allele exerts a particularly strong effect, increasing the risk of LOAD by approximately threefold in individuals with one ɛ4 allele and up to fifteenfold in those with two ɛ4 alleles [5, 6]. Despite these findings, the identified genetic variants collectively explain only a fraction of heritability, leaving much of the genetic architecture of LOAD unclear.

A significant pathological feature of LOAD is brain atrophy, induced by neuronal loss across multiple cortical and subcortical regions. Longitudinal MRI studies have shown that the initial sites of brain atrophy in LOAD patients are typically the medial temporal lobe structures, particularly the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, which are closely linked to the clinical signs and symptoms of memory loss [7]. As the disease progresses, these pathological changes spread to other cortical and subcortical areas, leading to general cognitive deficit and other clinical symptoms [8]. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been instrumental in capturing these atrophic changes. For instance, patients with LOAD exhibit decreased cortical thickness and gray matter volume in the frontal, parietal, and medial temporal lobes. Additionally, there is a notable reduction in volume in the subcortical regions, such as the thalamus and amygdala [9, 10].

Recent GWAS studies have demonstrated that brain morphology measurements derived from structural MRI are highly heritable and associated with numerous genetic variants [11, 12]. Previous studies have indicated that certain GWAS-identified genetic risk variants contributing to LOAD also influence brain neuroimaging traits related to this disease and are associated with brain gene expression [13–15]. Moreover, a higher polygenic risk score of LOAD has been associated with lower hippocampal volume and reduced cortical thickness in regions vulnerable to LOAD [16–18]. These findings may indicate a shared genetic architecture between LOAD and brain morphological traits. However, genetic correlation analyses using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) did not find significant associations between LOAD and brain morphological measures, such as cortical thickness, surface area, and subcortical structure, except for hippocampal volume, which showed a negative correlation with LOAD [12, 19]. The absence of significant correlations is likely in part due to GWAS only capturing common SNP variation, not accounting for epigenetics and other complex, dynamic regulatory mechanisms, leading to ‘missing heritability’ [20]. In addition, both LOAD and brain morphology have highly polygenic architectures, in which shared genetic variants may exert mixed directional effects, potentially obscuring detectable correlations. Specifically, some shared genetic variants may contribute to both traits in the same direction, while others exert opposing effects. In this scenario, the mixed effect directions can cancel out the overall genetic correlation when analyzed at the whole genome-wide level, leading to LDSC being insufficient for investigating such nuanced genetic relationships [21, 22]. Consequently, a knowledge gap persists regarding the genetic overlap between LOAD and brain morphological traits.

In this study, we leveraged GWAS summary statistics from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), GWAS of imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) from the UK Biobank (UKB), as well as individual-level genotype data from UKB, to investigate the complex polygenic relationship between LOAD and brain morphological traits, including 64 global and regional cortical thickness and surface area, and 25 subcortical volume measures. Beyond LDSC, we applied a bivariate causal mixture model (MiXeR) [23], local analysis of [co]variant association (LAVA) [24], and polygenic scores to assess the shared genetic basis for each LOAD—brain trait pair. The specific shared loci were identified by conjunctional FDR (conjFDR) [25]. To understand the underlying molecular mechanisms of the shared loci, we mapped them to genes and conducted gene set enrichment analysis.

Methods

Dataset

GWAS dataset and preprocessing

This study utilized GWAS summary statistics for LOAD and brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs). The LOAD GWAS from the PGC [4] comprised 39,918 cases and 358,140 controls based on a meta-analysis of multiple cohorts, excluding datasets from the UKB and 23andMe. IDP GWAS were obtained from the UKB [26], comprising 31,968 participants [27]. Structural brain measures derived from T1-weighted MRI were used to characterize brain morphology, including total surface area, global mean thickness, regional surface area, and cortical thickness of 31 regions defined by the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas [28], as well as 25 subcortical volume measures (subcortical, ventricular, callosal, and other non-cortical volumes) [29]. These measures are widely used in large-scale imaging genetic studies of LOAD and have been shown to be relevant to its pathophysiology [30, 31], while also demonstrating moderate to high heritability [32]. Given the high phenotypic and genetic correlations between brain measures in the left and right hemispheres [33, 34], we restricted analyses to the left hemisphere for bilateral traits to reduce the burden of multiple testing correction. Detailed descriptions of these 89 brain measures are provided in Table S1 and Fig. S1. All individuals in the LOAD and IDP GWAS datasets were of European ancestry. SNPs within the major histocompatibility complex region (chr6: 25,119,106–33,854,733) were excluded from all subsequent analyses except polygenic score analysis [35], because its complex Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) structure can distort statistical models and downstream results. Strand ambiguous and duplicated SNPs were excluded from all analyses.

UKB individual-level dataset

This study was conducted under UKB application 55392 using data from the July 2023 release. Genotype and clinical data from the UKB cohort were utilized for polygenic score analyses, with brain PGS as predictors and AD as outcome. This cohort comprises approximately 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years at recruitment [36]. Given the high heritability of LOAD, offspring of affected individuals may carry an increased genetic risk, and individuals without a diagnosis may develop the disease later in life. To account for this, a LOAD proxy score was computed for each UKB participant based on their own clinical diagnosis as well as parental LOAD status and age, following approaches used in previous genetic studies [4, 31, 37]. Specifically, participants diagnosed with LOAD according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes F00 and G30 were assigned a score of 2. Each parent diagnosed with LOAD contributed 1 point, whereas an unaffected parent contributed (100 − age)/100 (or age at death, if applicable) to account for the possibility of later disease onset. To minimize the influence of young parental ages, the contribution of an unaffected parent was set to 0.32, corresponding to the maximum population prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease. Parental diagnoses and ages (or ages at death) were self-reported by participants.

We used the UKB v3 imputed genotype data, based on the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) reference panel. Detailed information on the genotyping process has been reported elsewhere [38]. Genetic quality control was performed to ensure high-quality genotype data. We excluded individuals with genetic sex inconsistent with self-reported sex, non-white British ancestry, high genotype missingness (> 0.01), extreme heterozygosity (± 3 SD), or close relatedness (kinship coefficient ≥ 0.0442). Variants were filtered based on duplicates, minor allele frequency < 0.01, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P < 1 × 10− 6, and call rate < 99%. We further excluded all participants who had T1-MRI data available to avoid sample overlap with the IDP GWAS. After quality control, 4,633,528 SNPs and 272,513 participants remained for analysis (mean age = 57.1 ± 8.0 years; 54.6% female). The mean (± SD) LOAD proxy score was 0.593 (± 0.325). Participants were additionally classified into LOAD cases and healthy controls for a sensitivity analysis to examine the association between brain polygenic scores and LOAD diagnosis. In total, 2643 LOAD cases were identified, with a mean age of 64.866 ± 4.052 (50.1% female), and 269,870 controls, with a mean age of 57.000 ± 7.994 (54.6% female).

Global and local genetic overlap analyses

Global genetic correlation analysis

Global genetic correlations between LOAD and 89 brain morphological traits were calculated using LDSC [39]. We used pre-computed LD scores derived from the 1000 Genomes European Project reference population [40]. The analysis was restricted to well-imputed HapMap3 SNPs to minimize bias due to variable imputation quality.

Polygenic score analysis

We derived brain-based PGSs for UKB participants using SBayesRC [41] to evaluate whether genetic variation underlying brain morphology is associated with LOAD liability. SBayesRC is based on a Bayesian regression framework scalable to whole-genome analyses that improves polygenic prediction by incorporating GWAS summary statistics with functional genomic annotations. For each brain trait, joint SNP effect sizes were estimated using GWAS summary statistics harmonized and imputed to an LD reference panel derived from individuals of European ancestry, incorporating functional annotation across ~ 8 million SNPs [42]. The resulting SNP weights were then applied to individual-level genotype data to compute PGSs. We assessed the association between each brain-based PGS and LOAD proxy score using linear regression, adjusting for age, sex, and the first 20 ancestry principal components, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to control for multiple testing across the 89 brain measures.

To assess the robustness across different LOAD phenotype definitions, logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between brain-based PGSs and clinically defined LOAD status using the same set of covariates. We also performed an additional sensitivity analysis, excluding individuals with the main neurological and psychiatric disorders to account for potential confounding effects introduced by these conditions. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary method. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the association between the standardized association statistics (z-scores) from two sensitivity analyses and those from the primary polygenic analysis.

Local genetic correlation analysis

The LAVA method was applied to further explore genetic correlation at the genome-wide level [24]. This method complements LDSC by estimating local genetic correlations (rg) within user-defined genetic regions, enabling detection of the genetic relationships when correlations are localized or vary in direction across regions. Specifically, a univariate test within LAVA was performed to estimate local genetic heritability across 2495 semi-independent loci (~ 1 Mb each). To ensure sufficient local genetic signal for correlation analysis, only loci with significant local heritability (α = 0.05/2495 loci, P value < 2 × 10− 5) were included in the bivariate local genetic correlation analysis for pairs of LOAD and brain measures. For traits with local genetic correlation estimated for multiple heritable regions, FDR correction was applied to account for multiple testing.

Bivariate MiXeR analysis

To evaluate the extent of polygenic overlap between each LOAD-brain trait pair, regardless of genetic correlation, we performed bivariate MiXeR analysis (v1.3) [23] using GWAS summary statistics. MiXeR models the additive genetic effects of two traits as a mixture of four Gaussian components, representing: (1) variants affecting one trait, (2) variants affecting the other trait, (3) variants influencing both traits, and (4) variants influencing neither trait. This approach estimates the number of variants influencing each trait and the number of shared variants between two traits. Additionally, MiXeR calculates the proportion of shared variants with concordant effect direction across both traits. MiXeR assumes that all non-null effect variants follow a single normal distribution. However, due to the substantial influence of the APOE gene region on chromosome 19 in LOAD, we decoupled chromosome 19 from all other chromosomes in the LOAD and brain summary statistics [43]. Separate bivariate MiXeR analyses were performed for chromosome 19 and the remaining chromosomes to account for this unique genetic contribution. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to evaluate the model fit, with a positive AIC value indicating the input data has enough statistical power to support the complex model [44].

Identification of shared loci

ConjFDR analysis was performed to identify shared genetic variants between LOAD and brain morphological traits [35]. ConjFDR, an extension of the conditional FDR (condFDR), pinpoints the specific shared variants between two traits. For each trait pair, conjFDR re-ranks the test statistics of variants for the primary trait by incorporating their association with the second trait. This process is then repeated with the roles of the primary and secondary traits swapped. The conjFDR value for each SNP is defined as the maximum of two condFDR values, providing a conservative estimate of the posterior probability that the SNP effect is null for either of two traits. Following standard practice, we applied a conjFDR threshold of 0.05 as a whole-genome significance in all analyses. To identify independent significant variants and genomic loci, we performed LD-based clumping on the resulting summary statistics using the sumatats.py script. Specifically, independent significant SNPs were defined as those with conjFDR < 0.05 and r2 < 0.6 with any other SNPs. Among these, lead SNPs were defined as independent significant SNPs that were in low LD with each other (r2 < 0.1). Candidate SNPs were defined as those with r2 > 0.6 with at least one independent significant SNP and were used to define the boundaries of the genomic loci. Genomic loci located within 250 kb of each other were then merged. All LD information was calculated using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European reference panel.

To identify distinct loci, shared loci across different LOAD-brain trait pairs were merged if they were genomically overlapped. For each merged locus, the lead SNP was defined as the variant with the smallest conjFDR value. We further assessed the novelty of the shared loci identified in our study for LOAD by comparing them with previously reported LOAD associations in the NHGR-EBI GWAS Catalog [45] and the original LOAD GWAS [4]. A locus was considered novel if: (1) none of the candidate SNPs within the locus had been previously reported as significantly associated with LOAD in the NHGR-EBI GWAS Catalog, (2) all candidate SNPs within the locus were not significant in the original LOAD GWAS (P value > 1 × 10− 5).

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis

In order to investigate the functional relevance of variants identified by conjFDR analysis, we performed functional annotation for the lead SNPs in all shared loci utilizing the FUMA platform [46]. These SNPs were functionally annotated with combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) scores, RegulomeDB scores, and chromatin state information. Moreover, we mapped these lead SNPs to genes using the variant-to-gene pipeline from Open Targets Genetics [47]. This pipeline integrates evidence from four types to assign variants to genes: (1) molecular phenotype quantitative trait loci experiments, (2) chromatin interaction experiments, (3) in silico functional predictions, (4) proximity of the variant to the canonical transcription start site of each gene. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analyses were performed for a subset of unique genes with the smallest conjFDR value selected from all mapped genes. These genes were then assigned a weight based on the smallest -log10(conjFDR) value among their corresponding lead SNPs, reflecting the extent of their association with both LOAD and brain imaging traits. We compared these genes against the Gene Ontology (GO) term [48, 49], and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [50, 51] using an R package Clusterprofiler [52].

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using ENIGMA brain imaging GWAS for 73 brain measures (Table S2) that overlapped with the 89 brain measures included in primary analyses. LDSC, MiXeR, conjFDR, and LAVA analyses were conducted following the same procedures as applied to the IDP GWAS. PGS analyses were omitted, as the ENIGMA brain imaging GWAS include participants from the UKB, which would introduce sample overlap with the UKB target sample. To assess consistency between datasets, estimates of genetic overlap with LOAD were compared between ENIGMA and UKB using Spearman correlation. These estimates include genetic correlations (rg) from LDSC, the number of shared variants across all chromosomes except chromosome 19 estimated by MiXeR, the number of shared loci identified by conjFDR, and the number of significantly correlated regions derived from LAVA.

Results

Polygenic overlap between LOAD and brain morphological traits

The LDSC analysis revealed weak genetic correlations between LOAD and all examined brain morphological traits. The genetic correlation estimates ranged from − 0.141 (standard error (SE) = 0.077, P value = 0.066) for hippocampus volume to 0.219 (SE = 0.118, P value = 0.064) for inferior temporal thickness (Fig. 1A and Table S3). None of these genetic correlations reached statistical significance. The overall pattern is consistent with previous findings, in which only the hippocampus volume showed significant positive genetic correlation with LOAD [12, 19]. Similarly, the linear regression analysis indicated that none of the brain trait-derived PGSs were significantly associated with LOAD proxy score (Fig. 1B and Table S4). Among all brain-based PGSs, the cortical thickness of the entorhinal region demonstrated the strongest positive association with LOAD proxy score (β = 0.040, SE = 0.020, P value = 0.040). In contrast, the volume of 5th ventricle showed the strongest negative association (β = -0.027, SE = 0.134, P value = 0.841). However, these associations were not statistically significant after correction for multiple comparison testing. The logistic regression analyses examining the association between brain-based PGSs and clinically defined LOAD status similarly demonstrated no significant association (Fig. S2 and Table S5). Notably, the association patterns across brain-based PGS were significantly correlated between the linear and logistic analyses (Spearman’s rho = 0.536, P value < 0.001), and remained highly consistent before and after excluding individuals with neurological and psychiatric disorders, as evidenced by a strong correlation between the beta coefficients across the 89 brain measures (Spearman’s rho = 0.816, P value < 0.001). These findings indicate concordance between continuous and case-control LOAD phenotype definitions and support the robustness of our results.

The LAVA local correlations analysis identified significant genetic correlations at the regional level. The strongest pattern was observed between LOAD and cerebellar cortex volume (Fig. 1C), with 37 of the 171 genomic regions showing significant heritability in both traits exhibiting significant correlation. Of these, 17 were positive, and 20 were negative. Similar patterns of mixed correlation directions were observed across all other LOAD–brain trait pairs (Table S6), suggesting that the shared genetic architecture between LOAD and brain traits is region-specific and can have opposing effects depending on the genomic locus.

Bivariate MiXeR further supported the presence of genetic overlap between LOAD and various brain traits by estimating a substantial number of shared genetic variants for each trait pair. For all the chromosomes except chromosome 19, 61 out of 89 brain morphological traits exhibited a good model fit with LOAD, as indicated by positive AIC values (Table S7). Among these brain traits, pallidum volume exhibited the largest number of shared variants with LOAD (1012 out of 8946), as shown in Fig. 1D. The volume of the inferior lateral ventricle exhibited the highest proportion of trait-influencing variants (30.5%, corresponding to 129 out of 423). In turn, LOAD was shared between 10.7% (nucleus accumbens volume: 114 out of 1067 variants) and 94.8% (pallidum volume: 1012 out of 1076 variants) of its trait-influencing variants with brain morphological traits. These shared variants exhibited divergent effect directions, with the proportion of concordant effects varying from 0.131 for hippocampus volume to 0.881 for cortical surface area of the fusiform gyrus (Fig. 1E and Table S7). Polygenic overlap estimates for each LOAD–brain trait pair on chromosome 19 are provided in Table S8.

Identification of shared genetic loci

To investigate the shared genetic basis at the individual variant level, we used conjFDR analysis for each pair of LOAD-brain traits. As illustrated in Fig. 2 and Table S9, conjFDR identified a total of 183 loci with 131 distinct lead SNPs jointly associated with LOAD and brain morphological traits. Specifically, 25 cortical area traits shared 65 loci, 23 cortical thickness traits shared 47 loci, and 20 brain volume traits shared 71 loci with LOAD. The most significant locus was located on chromosome 11 (chr11: 47,229,316–49,128,599, lead SNP: rs10437655, conjFDR = 1.153e-06), associated with LOAD and pallidum volume. After accounting for overlapping loci across trait pairs, we identified 28 loci jointly associated with LOAD and at least two brain morphological traits, resulting in 65 distinct shared loci across all LOAD-brain trait pairs detailed in Table S10.

Functional annotation, gene mapping, and enrichment analysis

The functional annotation for distinct lead SNPs across all shared loci revealed that the majority were in intronic regions (44.3%) and intergenic regions (28.2%), as shown in Fig. 3A. 11.4% of SNPs exhibited a high likelihood of regulatory function, as indicated by a RegulomeDB score of less than three. Approximately 93.9% of the SNPs had a chromatin state score below eight, suggesting these variants are situated in open chromatin regions that are susceptible to regulatory influences. A total of seven SNPs were identified as potentially pathogenic, with a CADD score exceeding 12.37. These SNPs include rs77804065 in MAPT, rs34173062 in SHARPIN, rs11039297 in SLC39A13, rs1136165 in CKB, rs71371993 in FAM171A2, rs28375303 in SSBP4, and rs2836882 in ETS2. Details of the results are provided in Table S9.

To investigate the underlying biological process, we mapped all lead SNPs identified by conjFDR for each pair of LOAD-brain traits to genes. Among 183 shared genes, 80 genes are unique, detailed in Table S11. Of these, 31 genes were shared between LOAD and more than one brain morphological trait. Notably, KANSL1 was shared between LOAD and 31 brain morphological traits. Moreover, three lead SNPs—rs157592, rs148933445, rs118147862—shared between LOAD and nucleus accumbens volume, surface area of lingual gyrus, and precuneus thickness, respectively, are all mapped to the APOE gene. Further functional analysis revealed that these distinct shared genes were significantly enriched in three biological process-related GO terms, namely cell differentiation, cellular developmental process, and regulation of cellular component organization, as shown in Fig. 3B.

Sensitivity analysis

We observed significant positive correlations between the primary genetic overlap metrics derived from the UKB and ENIGMA across most methods (Spearman’s ρ = 0.314–0.409, all P values < 0.05), except for LAVA (Spearman’s ρ = -0.198, P value = 0.093), as shown in Fig. S3. Despite the modest magnitude of these correlations, the consistently positive association across multiple methods indicate that the relative pattern of genetic overlap across brain traits is largely preserved. In addition, 65.8% of traits showed concordant direction of genetic correlation (rg) between datasets, further supporting cross-dataset consistency.

Discussion

It is well established that LOAD is associated with alterations in brain morphology; however, the underlying genetic relationship has remained unclear. In this study, we employed complementary approaches to estimate the genetic relationship between LOAD and brain morphological traits. Many brain morphological traits shared a genetic architecture with LOAD, as evidenced by significant genetic correlations in multiple genomic regions, a substantial polygenic overlap, and the identification of multiple shared loci. These shared genetic variants exhibited mixed directional effects, reflecting the complex nature of the genetic relationship between LOAD and brain morphology. Enrichment analysis of genes associated with the shared loci revealed their involvement in the regulation of the cellular development process, particularly cellular differentiation, as well as the regulation of cellular component organization.

By employing statistical methods that account for mixed effect directions between two traits, we identified a shared genetic architecture between LOAD and brain morphological traits, characterized by mixed variations in effect directions. At the genome-wide level, bivariate MiXeR analysis revealed that all brain morphological traits shared a substantial number of variants with LOAD not only on chromosome 19 but also on the remainder of the genome. Further evidence of this genetic relationship was provided by conjFDR analysis at the variant and locus levels, which identified 77 brain morphological traits sharing one or more loci with LOAD. The extensive genetic overlap between these traits suggests a genetic basis for their phenotypic association. Despite this substantial genetic overlap, global genetic correlations between LOAD and all brain traits were negligible, which aligns with previous studies [12, 19, 34]. Furthermore, no significant associations were found between brain-based PGSs and the LOAD proxy score or clinical diagnosis. The absence of significant findings in LDSC and polygenic score analyses may be explained by the presence of mixed directional effects among shared genetic variants, which can offset one another when aggregated across the genome. This interpretation is supported by the observed concordance rates, which were close to 0.5, suggesting that most shared variants across LOAD-brain trait pairs have a mixture of concordant and discordant effect directions. In addition, epigenetic mechanisms may also contribute to this discrepancy. For example, many identified shared variants are in open chromatin regions with potential regulatory functions, and their effects on gene expression are likely to be influenced by epigenetic states and environmental exposures across the lifespan, which are not captured by GWAS. Moreover, the LAVA analysis identified significant local genetic correlations with opposing directions across different chromosomal regions. These findings indicate that shared variants may influence LOAD and brain morphological traits in both the concordant and discordant effect directions, depending on their genomic context. The mixture of concordant and discordant genetic effects reflects the polygenic pleiotropy underlying both LOAD and brain morphological traits, suggesting a complex interplay of genetic factors that confer either vulnerability or resilience. This pattern was further supported by sensitivity analyses using ENIGMA brain imaging GWAS data. Although the cross-dataset correlations were modest and not all findings were replicated, this is expected given differences in sample size, demographics, and imaging processing pipelines between datasets. Importantly, the consistent positive correlations across multiple methods, together with the high concordance direction of genetic correlations, indicate that the overall pattern of genetic overlap is largely preserved across datasets.

Among the loci shared between LOAD and brain morphological traits, three loci associated with nucleus accumbens volume, surface area of lingual gyrus, and precuneus thickness converged on APOE, highlighting its central role in the shared genetic architecture. APOE encodes apolipoprotein E, a lipid transport protein highly expressed in the brain [53] that is critically involved in neuronal repair and maintenance [54]. Notably, the implicated brain regions, nucleus accumbens, lingual gyrus, and precuneus, have been linked to cognitive and behavioral domains disrupted in LOAD, including reward processing [55], visual memory integration [56], and episodic memory [57]. Structural alterations in these regions have been observed in APOE ɛ4 carriers [58–60], suggesting that APOE-related genetic variation may influence LOAD risk partly through its impact on region-specific brain morphology. Collectively, these findings underscore the influence of APOE on brain morphology and its broader implications for LOAD disease pathology.

Beyond APOE, several additional genes shared between LOAD and brain morphological measures have been previously implicated in LOAD pathogenesis, further supporting the biological relevance of the identified overlap. Notably, loci mapped to BIN1 were associated with global and regional cortical thickness in the occipital and parietal lobes, as well as inferior parietal surface area. As the second strongest genetic risk factor for LOAD [61], BIN1 encodes nucleoplasmic adaptor protein BIN1, which has been implicated in the development of tau pathology [62, 63], a major driver of neurodegeneration in LOAD [64]. Similarly, TNIP1, identified here as a shared locus with hippocampus and amygdala volume, has been implicated in immunity regulation and cellular homeostasis, with emerging evidence suggesting a role in oxidative stress response to vulnerable brain regions in AD [65, 66]. In line with immune-related mechanisms, SPI1 encodes a key transcription factor regulating microglial development and function in the brain [67], which is critical for amyloid and tau clearance [68]. In addition, ACE has been linked to paracentral surface area and amyloid-β degradation [69], and genetic variation in ACE has been associated with increased Aβ accumulation [70]. Taken together, these genes converge on biological processes central to LOAD, including tau pathology, immune and microglial function, oxidative stress, and amyloid metabolism.

The enrichment analysis of all mapped genes revealed significant overrepresentation in three key biological processes: cellular development, cellular differentiation, and regulation of cellular component organization. Notably, the cellular development encompasses cellular differentiation, with neural differentiation being particularly crucial for proper brain development, plasticity, and repair [71]. The evidence from multiple postmortem and animal model studies has shown that in the brains of AD patients, newly generated neurons in the subgranular zone of the hippocampus and subventricular zones of the lateral ventricles fail to differentiate into mature neurons in the key target regions, such as the dentate gyrus, neocortex, and entorhinal cortex [72]. Our finding that genes shared between LOAD and the hippocampus are significantly enriched in these processes supports and provides genetic insights into these previously observed neural differentiation deficits in LOAD. Furthermore, the regulation of cellular component organization includes processes that modulate the formation, arrangement of constitute parts, and disassembly of cell structure, such as cytoskeletal organization, membrane trafficking, and organelle positioning [49]. These processes are relevant to a pathological feature of LOAD: the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau, which forms neurofibrillary tangles. Hyperphosphorylated tau impairs its interaction with tubulin, preventing proper microtubule assembly—an essential component of neuronal cytoskeleton. This disruption leads to microtubule depolymerization, cytoskeletal collapse, and subsequent neuronal dysfunction [73, 74]. Our observed enrichment of this biological process suggests that genetic regulation of cellular architecture may serve as a crucial link between structural brain variation and the progressive neurodegenerative processes in LOAD.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, all GWAS summary statistics were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the applicability of our findings to other populations. Expanding analysis to large non-European populations is essential for improving the generalizability of our findings. Second, certain LOAD–brain trait pairs exhibited negative AIC values in the bivariate MiXeR analysis. This outcome is likely due to the low heritability and high polygenicity of these brain traits, resulting in unreliable Gaussian mixture mode estimates. These findings highlight the need for more powerful brain imaging GWAS to enable precise estimations of polygenic overlap. Third, we note that alternative statistical approaches for estimating local genetic correlation, such as a local version of high-definition likelihood (HDL-L), may offer increased statistical power compared to LAVA [75]. Future studies could systematically evaluate the consistency and potential differences between these methods in the context of LOAD and brain morphological traits. Fourth, the analysis was restricted to brain morphological measures derived solely from the left hemisphere. If genetic influences on brain structure differ across hemispheres, the genetic overlap between LOAD and brain morphology may not be fully captured. Although the consistently high genetic correlation estimations across 78 bilateral brain measures (rg = 0.714-1, P value < 1e-5) indicate that our overall conclusions are unlikely to be distorted, future studies incorporating bilateral brain measures could provide a more comprehensive understanding of potential hemispheric-specific genetic effects.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identified the shared genetic basis between LOAD and brain morphological traits using statistical approaches that account for mixed effect direction between polygenic traits, picking up on overlap missed by previous studies. Genes mapped by the shared loci were involved in cellular development, differentiation, and regulation of cellular component organization. These findings offer molecular genetic insight into the relationship between LOAD and brain traits, which is essential for understanding the biological mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration and identifying potential molecular targets for future therapeutic investigation.
