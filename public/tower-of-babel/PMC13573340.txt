The relationship between rest intolerance and problematic internet use of college students: the mediating role of self-compassion and the moderating role of self-control
Authors: Haixia Zhao, Zimo Yang, Song Han, Bihua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet use has become an essential component of college students’ academic and social lives. Students who experience negative emotions such as anxiety, guilt, or shame during rest periods—commonly referred to as rest intolerance—may be more prone to problematic internet use (PIU). This study aimed to examine the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU among college students, focusing on the mediating role of self-compassion and the moderating role of self-control.MethodsParticipants were 7,163 Chinese college students recruited via convenience sampling, who completed self-report measures assessing demographic characteristics, rest intolerance, PIU, self-compassion, and self-control. A moderated mediation model was conducted to test the hypotheses.ResultsRest intolerance was significantly and positively associated with PIU. Self-compassion was negatively associated with both rest intolerance and PIU, while self-control was negatively associated with rest intolerance. Rest intolerance exerted a significant direct effect on PIU (β = 0.08, CI [0.06, 0.10]) and an indirect effect through self-compassion (β = 0.14, CI [0.12, 0.15]). Furthermore, self-control moderated both the direct and indirect pathways: high self-control attenuated the negative association of rest intolerance on self-compassion and weakened the association of rest intolerance on PIU.ConclusionThis study reveals that self-compassion mediates the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU, and that self-control moderates both the direct and mediated pathways, offering potential guidance for prevention and intervention strategies aimed at reducing the association of rest intolerance on PIU in college students.Keywords: Rest intolerance, Problematic internet use, Self-compassion, Self-control, College students

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of digitalization and information technology, the internet has become deeply embedded in the daily academic and social lives of college students. According to the 56statistical report on China’s Internet development, the Internet penetration rate in China has reached 79.7%, with young adults—the primary demographic of digital users—averaging 30.6 h of online engagement per week [1]. Although the internet brings convenience to college students’ access to information, social connections, and opportunities for self-expression, it also has severe consequences when used in a problematic manner. Problematic internet use (PIU) refers to uncontrolled online behaviors, excessive use, and withdrawal symptoms [2, 3], which negatively affect mental health, academic performance, interpersonal relationships, and social functioning [4–7]. PIU has become increasingly prevalent among college students [6, 8, 9], posing significant challenges to their development. It is therefore essential to investigate the risk factors and underlying pathways of PIU. Recently, “rest intolerance“(i.e., feelings of shame, self-reproach, guilt, and anxiety during relaxation or inactivity), which was associated with psychological stress, emotional distress, and sleep problems [10], has attracted increasing attention in young adults [11, 12]. It has been preliminarily linked to problematic internet behaviors [13, 14]. However, the psychological pathways through which rest intolerance is associated with PIU remain largely unexplored.

According to the I-PACE model, the development of PIU is statistically consistent with the combined presence of relatively stable Person (P) factors (e.g., personality traits), Affective(A) and Cognitive(C) responses to situational triggers (e.g., cue-reactivity, coping, cognitive biases), and Executive (E) functions (e.g., inhibitory control, decision-making) [15]. Within this framework, rest intolerance can be conceptualized as a Person (P) factor which is a relatively stable individual characteristic involving a tendency toward negative affect and maladaptive cognitions during rest [12]. When individuals with high rest intolerance are at rest or leisure, this stable P tendency may give rise to heightened Affective (A) responses (e.g., guilt, shame, anxiety) and maladaptive Cognitive (C) responses (e.g., social comparison, obsessive thinking, biased beliefs about rest as laziness) [12, 15]. Self-compassion represents an adaptive emotion regulation capacity rooted in self-regulation theory [16], and it draws on self-regulatory resources to support deliberate coping and may thereby preserve executive functioning (E), rather than constituting an executive function itself [16]. In contrast, self-control directly corresponds to the inhibitory control component of Execution (E) and is hypothesized to moderate these pathways [17–19].

Despite burgeoning literature identifying various correlates of PIU—including affective states such as anxiety and guilt [20], cognitive factors [4], and negative life events [21, 22]—the specific association of rest intolerance remains conspicuously underexamined. Crucially, there is a dearth of systematic investigation into the psychological processes and boundary conditions involved in the “rest intolerance–PIU” pathway. Consequently, the present study aims to elucidate the direct association between rest intolerance and PIU among Chinese college students. Furthermore, we propose a moderated mediation model to examine how self-compassion mediates this relationship and how self-control acts as a critical moderator. The aim of this study is not only to enrich a contextualized understanding of PIU among college students, but also to provide empirical groundwork for developing targeted interventions, such as cognitive restructuring of rest-related beliefs or self-compassion training, that may facilitate comfortable rest and buffer against PIU risk.

Rest intolerance and problematic internet use

Rest intolerance refers to a multidimensional negative psychological state experienced during periods of rest, characterized by core negative affect(guilt, shame, and anxiety) and interrelated cognitive features such as social comparison (i.e., concern about being surpassed by others), obsessive thinking (i.e., uncontrollable rumination about academic or work-related tasks), and cognitive bias (i.e., the belief that rest is conditional or should be minimized) [11, 12]. Unlike the unidimensional conceptualization of rest-related distress proposed by Koo [11] within a Western cultural framework—which focuses solely on negative affect, and distinct from general negative emotions such as anxiety or depression, the rest intolerance advanced by Wang et al. [12] is a complex psychological phenomenon characterized by the emergence of negative affect and cognition during rest, which is statistically associated with East Asian cultural values. These cognitive-affective states typically arise from individuals’ beliefs that self-worth is contingent upon constant busyness and academic productivity [13]. Compared with Western developed countries, rest intolerance may be more prevalent in East Asian cultures, where diligence and academic engagement are frequently prioritized over leisure, and this tendency is particularly pronounced in Chinese higher education [12, 23]. A previous study has indicated moderate levels of rest intolerance among Chinese college students [13] and has linked rest intolerance to heightened stress, anxiety, emotional distress, and sleep disturbances [10, 11]. Given contemporary societal demands for high achievement and efficiency, college students face multifaceted academic and employment pressures.

According to the I-PACE model, when individuals are at rest, these pressures function as internal triggers associated with negative affective responses(A)-including guilt, shame, and anxiety-accompanied by maladaptive cognitive processes (C) such as self-judgment, rumination, and upward social comparison [15, 19, 24]. These affective–cognitive cycles may place sustained demands on limited self-regulatory resources, progressively compromising executive functions (E) such as inhibitory control and decision-making [15, 18]. As executive resources become depleted, individuals are less able to engage in constructive coping and increasingly adopt maladaptive coping strategies—such as seeking distraction and immediate gratification through social media, short videos, or online gaming—to escape rest-related discomfort [10, 25, 26]. Over time, this maladaptive pattern may elevate the risk of PIU.

Furthermore, rest intolerance has been shown to heighten negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, guilt), diminish well-being, and increase stress levels, outcomes identified as indirect precursors to PIU [27–29]. Consistent with the I-PACE proposition that person–affect–cognition interactions drive maladaptive coping when executive resources are compromised [15, 16], rest intolerance may lead college students to engage in avoidant or compensatory online behaviors to increase the risk of PIU [15, 30]. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis (H1): there is a positive correlation between rest intolerance and PIU.

The mediating role of self-compassion

Self-compassion is defined as a positive self-attitude characterized by treating oneself with self-kindness, a sense of common humanity, and mindful awareness in the face of personal suffering [16, 31, 32]. Based on the I-PACE model, coping strategies may also play an important role in the development of PIU [15]. Self-compassion represents an adaptive emotion regulation capacity that relies on self-regulatory resources to enable deliberate coping, such as mindful self-attention and self-soothing [16, 17]. Rather than constituting an executive function itself, self-compassion supports executive functioning (E) by preserving self-regulatory resources, thereby enabling more effective inhibitory control and deliberate coping [17, 33]. Consistent with this conceptualization, we propose self-compassion as a mediating role in our study. Rest intolerance is fundamentally an experience laden with guilt, shame, anxiety, and harsh self-reproach during rest [12]. These negative self-evaluative emotions narrow attention toward self-criticism and sustain rumination, reducing the likelihood that individuals will respond to their distress with self-kindness and mindful acceptance [16]. Persistent activation of these affective-cognitive processes consumes self-regulatory capacity, thereby impairing execution-level self-compassion and its regulatory functions [10, 17, 33]. Existing research has established that heightened levels of shame undermine self-kindness and acceptance, while also exacerbating self-criticism and rumination; thereby, they diminish overall self-compassion [32, 34]. Avcı et al. reported a significant negative correlation between rest intolerance and self-compassion [10]. The psychological resources necessary for self-compassion are systematically depleted or remain unactivated in individuals with high rest intolerance. Specifically, individuals with high levels of rest intolerance exhibit negative affect and maladaptive cognitions, such as shame and self-blame. They also tend to engage in self-judgment and criticism, often viewing rest as a personal failure. This significantly undermines their self-compassion. Furthermore, in contexts characterized by high achievement pressure, individuals were more likely to conflate their ability to rest with their self-worth; as a result, they experienced intensified shame during rest, further depleting their capacity for self-compassion [11, 12]. Thus, it can be inferred that rest intolerance likely erodes the psychological resources necessary for self-compassion.

Additionally, prior research has established a negative association between self-compassion and both Internet and mobile phone addiction [35]. Further studies indicate that self-compassion is associated with addictive behaviors, along with shaping cognitive styles (e.g., such as reducing negative attention bias) and preserving self-regulatory resources; these patterns are statistically consistent with reduced self-regulatory fatigue [36]. Based on the I-PACE model, college students with persistent rest intolerance are likely to experience negative affect and cognitive biases (e.g., social comparison, intrusive thoughts). This condition not only depletes self-regulatory resources, but also diminishes self-compassion and weakens a key capacity that buffers against maladaptive coping, ultimately increasing the risk of PIU [15, 19]. This study aims to propose the Hypothesis H2: self-compassion mediates the connection between rest intolerance and PIU.

The moderating effect of self-control

Self-control defined as the ability to suppress emotions, thoughts, behaviors, and desires when facing temptations, impulses, or pressures [18, 33, 37]. According to the hot/cool system, self-control can not only suppress impulsive reactions driven by immediate rewards (e.g., avoidance and impulsive acts), but also help individuals reduce impulse behaviors and facilitate adaptive coping strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal and problem-solving) [38, 39]. Individuals with high levels of self-control engage in high levels of cognitive processes, reducing the occurrence of internalization or externalization problems. Conversely, individuals with low self-control are more likely to exhibit behavioral dysregulation and addictive tendencies [40]. Previous research has shown that low self-control is associated with PIU [28, 41] and has also demonstrated a similar buffering moderating effect of self-control in the relationships between negative emotions or cumulative ecological risk and maladaptive online behaviors [42, 43]. Therefore, this study treats self-control as a relatively stable individual difference that regulates the strength of the pathway from risk factors to behavioral outcomes.

Based on the I-PACE model for PIU [15, 19], executive function and inhibitory control (e.g., self-control) may serve as moderating variables in the relationship between susceptibility variables (such as rest intolerance) and PIU. Specifically, individuals with high self-control are more proficient in employing constructive strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal, to regulate negative emotions induced by rest intolerance [28]. By inhibiting the reliance on the internet as an avoidance strategy or compensatory tool, high self-control attenuates the positive association between rest intolerance and PIU. Conversely, individuals characterized by low self-control are more susceptible to self-regulatory resource depletion and impulse control failures; they may resort to excessive internet use to obtain short-term psychological relief, thereby exacerbating the association of rest intolerance on PIU. Collectively, these findings suggest that self-control may serve as a moderator in the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU.

Furthermore, self-control encompasses not only the inhibition of impulsive responses but also heightened sensitivity to personal standards and self-monitoring [33]. Individuals with high self-control typically demonstrate stronger self-monitoring, greater goal consistency, and stricter adherence to internalized standards [44]. When rest is perceived as laziness or insufficient effort [12], these individuals may exhibit increased vigilance regarding their rest behaviors, which in turn can intensify self-criticism and self-judgment, thereby undermining their self-compassion [45]. A study has also shown that maladaptive perfectionism, characterized by harsh self-evaluation when standards are unmet, is positively associated with self-criticism and negatively associated with self-compassion [46]. Such heightened sensitivity to the discrepancies between actual rest behavior and internalized achievement standards is associated with intensified negative affect (e.g., guilt, shame) and cognition during rest. In contrast, those with low self-control may lack sufficient regulatory resources to monitor their behavior against internalized standards, potentially reducing the negative association of rest intolerance on self-compassion. Prior research indicates that self-control may moderate the relationship between negative affect and self-related outcomes, sometimes amplifying rather than buffering negative associations [42, 47]. Therefore, the negative association between rest intolerance and self-compassion may be stronger for individuals with high self-control, supporting the hypothesis that self-control moderates this relationship. Based on these findings, the following hypotheses are proposed:

The current study

The present study aims to investigate the potential ways in which rest intolerance is related to PIU among college students, and to provide a theoretical reference for the prevention and reduction of PIU among college students. Specifically, this research focuses on examining three aspects: (1) whether rest intolerance is significantly positively associated with PIU; (2) whether self-compassion mediates the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU; (3) whether self-control moderates the association between rest intolerance and PIU, as well as between rest intolerance and self-compassion. Methods

Procedure and participants

Convenience sampling was used to recruit participants from a university in Shaanxi province, China. It should be noted that this convenience sampling approach, drawn from a single university in one province, may limit the geographic and demographic representativeness of the sample. The survey material and all procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the corresponding author’s institution. In addition, the present study was conducted in accordance with the principle of voluntary participation. Before the formal survey, each participant was informed about the general aim of the research and obtained electronic informed consent. Participants had the prerogative to withdraw from the study at any juncture, and were prompted to give their explicit consent to participate. All data collectors received standardized training before data collection. The participants were also told that the data were analyzed anonymously. A total of 7244 questionnaires were collected. Based on exclusion criteria—including missing data, abnormal responses, and duplicate submissions—81 questionnaires were discarded, resulting in 7163 valid responses. The effective response rate was 98.9%, which provided a robust dataset that enhanced the reliability and validity of the findings. The valid sample included 4,623 males and 2,540 females, comprising 1,836 only children and 5,327 non-only children. The mean age of participants was 19.44 years (SD = 2.51).

Measures

Rest intolerance scale

The 8-item Rest Intolerance, developed by Wang et al. [12] based on a sample of Chinese college students, was used to assess individuals’ rest intolerance. The scale has 8 items measuring four dimensions: negative feelings (e.g., “When I am resting or having fun, I feel guilty”), social comparison (e.g., “When I’m resting or having fun, I worry about being passed by someone else”), obsessive thinking (e.g., “When I’m resting or having fun, I can’t help but think about studying or working”), and cognitive bias (e.g., “I think I should need to spend more time studying and working rather than resting”). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 means “(strong disagreement)” and 5 means “strong agreement”. Higher scores indicate a higher level of rest intolerance. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale was 0.914 in this study.

Problematic internet use questionnaire

The short version of the Problematic Internet Use Questionnaire, developed by Demetrovics et al. and revised by Demetrovics [48, 49], was used to measure individuals’ PIU. The scale has 6 items measuring three dimensions: obsession (e.g., “How often do you feel tense, irritated, or stressed if you cannot use the Internet for as long as you want to?”), neglect (e.g., “How often do people in your life complain about spending too much time online?”), and control disorder (e.g., “How often do you try to conceal the amount of time spent online?”). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 means “never” and 5 means “always”. Higher scores indicate a higher level of PIU. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale was 0.834 in this study.

Self-compassion scale

The Self-Compassion Scale, developed by Gong et al. and revised by Neff [16, 50], was used to assess individuals’ self-compassion abilities. The scale has 12 items measuring three dimensions: self-kindness (e.g., I’m intolerant and impatient towards those aspects of my personality I don’t like”), common humanity (e.g., “When I fail at something important to me, I tend to feel alone in my failure”), and mindfulness (e.g., “When something upsets me, I try to keep my emotions in balance”). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 means “(almost never)” and 5 means “almost always”. Higher scores indicate a higher level of self-compassion. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale was 0.813 in this study.

Brief self-control scale

The Brief Self-Control Scale, developed by Morean et al. and revised by Luo et al. [51, 52], was used to assess individuals’ self-control abilities. The scale has 7 items measuring two dimensions: self-discipline (e.g., “I am good at resisting temptation”) and impulse control (e.g., “I do things that are fun but bad for me”). The scale was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 means “very much in line” and 5 means “very much out of line”. Higher scores indicate a greater ability to control oneself. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.775 for the self-discipline subscale and 0.757 for the impulse control subscale.

Statistical analysis

We used IBM SPSS 26.0 and PROCESS 4.2 software packages for all data processing and analyses. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to check the common method bias, which was considered present if the first factor accounted for more than 40% of the total variance [53]. Second, preliminary analyses were conducted to show the means, standard deviations, and correlations among the main variables. Third, the mediation effect of self-compassion was tested using PROCESS macro version 4.2 (model 4) as proposed by Hayes [54]. Fourth, the moderated mediation model was tested using Hayes’s PROCESS macro (version 4.2, Model 8). The bootstrapping method was used in the mediation and moderated mediation analyses, using 5000 bootstrap samples and a 95% confidence interval. An effect was considered statistically significant if the confidence interval did not contain zero. The main variables were standardized for reducing multicollinearity before doing the mediation or moderation analyses [55]. Gender was treated as a dummy variable (0 = male, 1 = female) in this study. Gender and age were included as covariates in the mediation and moderated mediation analyses.

Preliminary analyses

Mediation analysis

To examine whether self-compassion mediates the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU, we conducted mediation analysis using Model 4 in the PROCESS macro. The coefficients reported in the following mediation and moderation model (including those in tables and figures) are all standardized.

As shown in Table 2, after controlling for age and gender, the direct path coefficient from rest intolerance to PIU was significant (β = 0.22, p < 0.001). In addition, rest intolerance was negatively associated with self-compassion (β = − 0.34, p < 0.001). When rest intolerance and self-compassion were simultaneously included as predictors of PIU, self-compassion was negatively associated with PIU (β = − 0.40, p < 0.001), and rest intolerance was positively associated with PIU (β = 0.08, p < 0.001). That is, higher rest intolerance was associated with lower self-compassion, which, in turn, was associated with a stronger tendency toward PIU. Moreover, the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method revealed that the indirect effect of rest intolerance on PIU via self-compassion was significant (indirect effect = 0.14, SE = 0.01, CI = [0.12, 0.15]). This indirect effect accounted for 63.64% of the total effect. However, given the modest total effect size (β = 0.22), this proportion should be interpreted with caution. More importantly, the indirect effect (0.14) was larger than the direct effect (0.08), indicating that the mediating role of self-compassion is statistically reliable, albeit with a small-to-medium effect size.

Furthermore, when self-compassion was treated as the dependent variable, as shown in Table 3, after controlling for age and gender, rest intolerance was negatively correlated with self-compassion (β = -0.25, p < 0.001), and the interaction effect of rest intolerance × self-control on self-compassion was also significant (β = -0.05, p < 0.001), indicating that self-control moderated the relationship between rest intolerance and self-compassion. The bootstrap analysis confirmed that the indirect effects of rest intolerance on PIU via self-compassion were significant at both high and low levels of self-control (high level: β = 0.11, SE = 0.01, CI = [0.09, 0.12]; low level: β = 0.07, SE = 0.01, CI = [0.06, 0.08]).

Discussion

This study developed a moderated mediation model to examine the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU, and to explore its underlying pathways. Specifically, self-compassion was tested as a mediating variable, while self-control was expected as a moderating factor. By investigating a sample of Chinese college students, our findings revealed a positive association between rest intolerance and PIU, with self-compassion partially mediating this relationship. Moreover, self-control moderated both the direct relationship between rest intolerance and PIU and the indirect effect by reinforcing the association between rest intolerance and self-compassion. These results provide valuable insights into the complex psychological pathways linking rest intolerance to PIU and offer both theoretical and practical implications for the prevention and intervention of PIU among Chinese college students.

The association between rest intolerance and problematic internet use

Supporting Hypothesis 1, we found rest intolerance was significantly and positively correlated with PIU among college students; specifically, higher levels of rest intolerance was associated with higher levels of PIU. This finding is consistent with the I-PACE model [19], which posits that person-level affective and cognitive vulnerabilities interact with compromised executive functions to promote maladaptive, reward-seeking behaviors [15, 19]. When individuals experience negative affect (e.g., guilt, shame, anxiety) and cognitive biases (e.g., social comparison, rumination, the belief that rest signifies laziness) during rest [11, 12], these negative affect and cognition imposes sustained demands on executive resources (e.g., inhibitory control, attentional regulation), thereby weakening the capacity to inhibit Internet use impulses and increasing PIU risk [10]. Previous research has consistently linked negative affect and cognitive biases to PIU [4–6, 20].

Building on this evidence, the present study identifies rest intolerance as a relevant risk factor for PIU. In high-pressure academic environments, students frequently experience uncomfortable rest due to elevated levels of academic demands and employment pressures. This discomfort, accompanied by negative affect and cognitive distortions, may drive them to use the internet as a maladaptive coping strategy for emotion regulation or attentional disengagement. Accordingly, these findings suggest that educators should pay greater attention to factors that impede comfortable rest and help students regulate, identify, and restructure the irrational belief that“rest is laziness” and cultivate adaptive strategies.

The mediating role of self-compassion

This study found that self-compassion partially mediated the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU among college students, supporting Hypothesis 2. Specifically, rest intolerance exacerbates the risk of PIU among college students by diminishing self-compassion. This finding provides empirical support for the I-PACE model [25, 26, 57], which posits that addictive behaviors emerge from the dynamic interaction of person-level vulnerabilities and diminished executive control and emphasizes how insufficient regulatory resources foster maladaptive coping. Based on self-regulation theory, sustained negative affect and repetitive cognition deplete limited self-regulatory resources [58]. The core features of rest intolerance—guilt, shame, anxiety, and other negative evaluations, along with accompanying obsessive thinking, cognitive bias, and social comparison—impose continuous demands on attentional control, working memory, and inhibitory control, and these demands may in turn be associated with the gradual depletion of self-regulatory resources [10]. Self-compassion is not an automatic response, as Neff [16] posited that self-kindness, mindful awareness, and cognitive reappraisal each require the support of self-regulatory resources. When rest intolerance persistently depletes these resources, individuals lack sufficient cognitive and emotional capacity to generate self-kind responses or maintain a mindful perspective, defaulting instead to habitual self-criticism [58]. Feelings of guilt and shame inherent in rest intolerance further intensify this self-critical tendency, thereby diminishing self-compassion [10].

Consistent with this interpretation, the present data revealed a significant negative association between rest intolerance and self-compassion, corroborating recent findings [10]. Empirical research indicates that low self-compassion is linked to heightened emotional distress and a greater tendency toward avoidant, immediate reward-seeking behaviors [10]. In the context of rest intolerance, diminished self-compassion impairs individuals’ capacity for adaptive emotion regulation, which was in turn related to a greater tendency to use the internet for immediate, low-effort relief—such as social media, gaming, or short videos [32]. Although such avoidance behavior provides transient relief, it reinforces avoidance and maladaptive coping patterns, ultimately increasing susceptibility to PIU [25, 26]. Taken together, this pathway aligns with the I-PACE model: the negative affect and cognition evoked by rest intolerance persistently consume self-regulatory resources, diminishing self-compassion and thereby prompting individuals to adopt PIU as a maladaptive coping strategy. Thus, this study showed that the negative affect and cognition accompanying rest intolerance were associated with sustained demand on self-regulatory resources, which was in turn related to lower self-compassion. As an adaptive emotion regulation capacity that relies on these resources, diminished self-compassion weakens individuals’ ability to cope adaptively with distress, and this depletion was associated with PIU as a compensatory, maladaptive coping strategy. This pattern suggests that low self-compassion may serve as an early warning indicator of PIU.

The moderating effect of self-control

Consistent with Hypothesis 3, the results indicated that self-control moderated both the direct association between rest intolerance and PIU and the first-stage indirect pathway via self-compassion. On the one hand, high self-control weakened the positive association between rest intolerance and PIU, and this finding aligns with the I-PACE model [15, 19], which posits that executive functions such as inhibitory control serve as moderators that can attenuate the translation of affective and cognitive vulnerabilities into addictive behaviors. The pattern also resonates with prior evidence that low self-control is consistently associated with elevated PIU risk [41, 42, 59]. Specifically, self-control buffered the effect of rest intolerance on PIU. When individuals experience rest intolerance(characterized by negative affect, social comparison, and obsessive thinking about rest as unproductive ), those with high self-control are more capable of engaging cognitive reappraisal to reframe rest-related thoughts and inhibiting the impulse to escape into excessive internet use [41, 42, 59]. These regulatory processes may attenuate the emotion-driven pathway to PIU. In contrast, individuals with low self-control may lack sufficient regulatory resources to interrupt the emotion-driven pathway to PIU, making them more susceptible to using the Internet as a compensatory tool for psychological relief. This interpretation is consistent with the limited-resource model of self-control, which suggests that self-regulatory depletion increases vulnerability to addictive behaviors [33].

On the other hand, high self-control simultaneously amplified the negative association between rest intolerance and self-compassion, resulting in a negative-by-negative interaction in predicting self-compassion; this suggests that self-control may not function as an unqualified protective factor across processes. This indicates that high self-control may exacerbate, rather than buffer, the detrimental effect of rest intolerance on self-compassion, and this pattern aligns with—and extends—the protective-attenuating moderation effect previously documented in the literature [60]. Specifically, for individuals high in rest intolerance, perceived self-worth may be threatened. In this context, individuals with high self-control combined with stringent self-imposed demands tend to appraise even minor perceived deviations from “resting comfortably,” “resting appropriately,” or “studying effectively” as personal shortcomings, which in turn heightens internal self-criticism and further undermines self-compassion [16, 32, 61]. Furthermore, the present findings align with the limited-resource model of self-control, positing that diverting self-regulatory resources toward intrapsychic conflict—such as suppressing rest-related thoughts and compelling oneself to study—paradoxically depletes these limited resources [62], thereby eroding individuals’ capacity for self-kindness and self-understanding. Within the Chinese Confucian cultural context, self-control is frequently intertwined with positively valued virtues such as diligence, discipline, perseverance, and a strong emphasis on hard work, whereas rest is often devalued or viewed with suspicion [63]. Consequently, students exhibiting high self-control may internalize cognitive biases that frame rest as indicative of laziness or moral failing, prompting them to prioritize studying or supplementary tutoring even during leisure time to outperform peers, which in turn elicits feelings of shame and guilt [64]. This conflicted psychological state may increase the degree of rest intolerance, ultimately eliciting heightened anxiety and self-devaluation [65], rather than self-kindness. These reactions may undermine self-compassion, thereby increasing the risk of PIU.

Limitations and implications

The research has several limitations. First, the sample in the present study was drawn exclusively from college students in a single province, which may limit the geographic and demographic representativeness of the findings and thereby constrain the generalizability of the results. Future research should adopt multi-center, multi-province sampling strategies to broaden regional coverage and enhance sample diversity across age groups, occupations, and educational backgrounds. Second, this study used a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between rest intolerance and PIU. Furthermore, the mediation proportion (63.64%) should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively large sample size (N = 7163) may have inflated the substantive or practical significance of the indirect effect. Future research should adopt longitudinal and experimental designs, or utilize more sensitive measurement instruments, to better establish causality and evaluate the real-world importance of these pathways. Third, despite using well-established scales, all constructs were assessed through self-report questionnaires, which may inflate relationships due to common method bias. Although Harman’s single-factor test did not indicate a dominant common factor, this approach is widely regarded as a relatively weak diagnostic and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should collect data from multiple sources or at multiple time points to further strengthen the validity of the findings. Fourth, although this study identified the moderating role of self-compassion and the moderating role of self-control, these effects were only examined in the context of the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU. Future studies should explore other individual characteristics, such as personality traits and perceived stress, to gain deeper insights into the complex relationship between rest intolerance and PIU. Finally, from a cross-cultural perspective, the development and manifestation of rest intolerance may be shaped by cultural context. Since this study included only college students from China, it could not examine rest intolerance across cultures or its role in other cultural settings. Future research should test the hypothesis that rest intolerance may be more prevalent in East Asian cultures and explore cross-cultural dimensions to assess whether rest intolerance is a related factor in PIU across different cultural contexts.

Despite these limitations that warrant future study, the present study advances both theoretical understanding and practical applications in several meaningful ways. By demonstrating that self-compassion partially mediates the association between rest intolerance and PIU, it extends self-regulation theories by clarifying the specific resource-depletion pathway through which rest intolerance undermines adaptive coping. Furthermore, the paradoxical moderating role of self-control—attenuating the direct path to PIU while intensifying its negative effect on self-compassion—challenges the assumption that self-control uniformly serves as a protective factor and introduces important boundary conditions to existing self-regulatory and I-PACE models. Practically, these findings highlight the value of compassion-focused interventions (e.g., self-compassion training programs) to mitigate the association between rest intolerance and PIU. They further suggest the need for differentiated strategies: students with low rest intolerance may benefit from self-control skills and emotion regulation techniques, whereas those with high rest intolerance may require cognitive restructuring through Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which is vital for alleviating rest intolerance and thereby helping students reduce PIU-related problems. Integrating such approaches into university mental health curricula and group-based counseling could promote healthier internet use among college students.

Conclusion

Overall, this study examined the relationship between rest intolerance and PIU among college students, focusing on the mediating role of self-compassion and the moderating effect of self-control. The results revealed a significant positive relationship between rest intolerance and PIU, wherein self-compassion partially mediated this association. Furthermore, self-control moderated both the direct path from rest intolerance to PIU and the indirect path via self-compassion. These findings provide new insights into the pathways by which rest intolerance is associated with PIU in college students and aid in identifying effective interventions to reduce the risk of PIU.
