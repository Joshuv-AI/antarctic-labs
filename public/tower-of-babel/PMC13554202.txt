Automated quantification of lens epithelial cell density using deep learning: validation and large-scale clinical application
Authors: Poramaporn Luangprasert, Chutimon Sindhuprama, Piyorod Srisawad, Sipat Triukose, Sirin Nitinawarat, Chaiwat Teekhasaenee, Apichat Tantraworasin, Praewpailin Kaimuk, Yanin Suwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLens epithelial cells (LECs) have a critical role in nutrient transport, ion balance, and the synthesis of essential molecules required to preserve the lens’s transparency. We hypothesize that lens epithelial cell density (LECD) may be correlated with the formation and severity of cataracts. Studying this relationship is limited by current quantification methods. This study aimed to develop an AI-driven model capable of automatically performing epithelial cell (LEC) counts in excised capsules, including density, distribution, and determining factors that affect LECD. We developed an AI-based software that leverages deep learning algorithms to automate the enumeration of LECs from light micrographs of harvested anterior lens capsules. To evaluate the performance and reliability of our AI model, we compared its results against traditional manual cell counting methods. Validation analyses included repeated-measures ANOVA, Bland-Altman analysis, mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and a comparison against inter-observer agreement between two independent expert observers to quantitatively assess agreement between AI-generated cell counts and manual enumeration. Participants were patients with age-related cataracts scheduled for phacoemulsification. Over 43,000 individual cellular targets were analyzed across 20 validation images. The AI-driven software showed excellent agreement with consensus manual counts (98.1% accuracy; MAPE 1.87%, 95% CI 1.27–2.47%; Pearson r = 0.99; ICC 0.994, 95% CI 0.982–0.997), tighter than the agreement between the two expert observers themselves (MAPE 3.74%; ICC 0.972). Introduction

The lens capsule is a transparent basement membrane produced and maintained by a single layer of lens epithelial cells (LECs).1 These cells are metabolically active throughout life, playing a critical role in nutrient transport, ion balance, and the synthesis of essential molecules required to preserve the avascular lens’s transparency.2 The density of these cells, particularly in the central zone, declines with age.1 Consequently, disruption to the LEC layer or its function can lead to a loss of lens transparency, a condition known as a cataract.3 We hypothesize that a quantitative assessment of lens epithelial cell density (LECD) may be correlated with the formation and severity of cataracts. Mechanistically, lens epithelial cells maintain lens homeostasis and antioxidant defense, and their senescence and oxidative-stress–induced loss are widely implicated in cataractogenesis; a reduction in viable epithelial cell density may therefore reflect the cumulative cellular injury that underlies cataract formation. Establishing this density–severity relationship quantitatively has not been feasible at scale, which is the bottleneck the present tool is designed to remove rather than to resolve within this validation study.

However, studying this relationship is hindered by the limitations of current quantification methods. The gold standard, manual cell counting, is a laborious, time-consuming, and inefficient process. More importantly, it is prone to sampling bias, as researchers typically count cells within a small grid area to extrapolate the density for the entire specimen, a method that may not be representative of the overall cellular landscape. This methodological bottleneck creates a demand for a more robust, objective, and efficient analytical tool.

To address these challenges, this study aimed to develop and validate a deep-learning-based model for the automated analysis of LECs. We leveraged an object-detection framework to create a tool that rapidly and accurately quantifies LECD across an entire image. The model’s performance was rigorously evaluated against expert ophthalmologists’ manual counts to assess its accuracy, consistency, and potential to serve as a new standard for cellular analysis in ophthalmic research.

The development of this AI-driven software represents a significant contribution to both clinical research and computational medicine. Firstly, it overcomes methodological barriers. It replaces the slow, subjective, and laborious manual “gold standard” with a high-throughput, objective, and reproducible method, eliminating sampling bias by analyzing the entire specimen. Secondly, it enables large-scale research. The model’s efficiency enables, for the first time, large-scale epidemiological and clinical studies to definitively investigate the link between cellular density and cataract development. Thirdly, it provides deeper analytical insight. By providing detailed spatial data on cell distribution and density, this tool allows researchers to investigate novel topographical biomarkers that are completely inaccessible through traditional counting methods.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study was conducted at Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki; the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital (MURA 2024/103). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This study enrolled consecutive patients with age-related cataracts who were scheduled to undergo phacoemulsification. Exclusion criteria included systemic and ophthalmic diseases other than hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hypertensive retinopathy. Furthermore, patients who were receiving systemic drug treatment (except for antihypertensive drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, and antiplatelet drugs) were excluded from this study. Additionally, no participants had undergone laser or ocular surgery in the included eye. The human tissue experiments complied with the guidelines of the ARVO Best Practices for Using Human Eye Tissue in Research (Nov2021).

All participants underwent complete ophthalmic examinations by glaucoma specialists, including best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, gonioscopy, Goldmann-applanation tonometry, and fully dilated fundoscopic examination.

Lens capsules preparation

All specimens of anterior capsules were harvested during phacoemulsification. Trypan blue was used to stain the anterior capsule for 60 s before irrigation with balanced salt solution, resulting in a transparent capsule that could be clearly identified. However, excessive staining can cause trypan blue precipitation and obscure cell boundaries. Supplemental Fig. 1A (left, middle) shows images of limited and excessive trypan blue staining. Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis was performed through a 3-mm clear corneal incision using a 27-gauge needle with the minimal-touch technique.

The anterior central lens capsule and adherent lens epithelium were carefully collected by pressure on the posterior lip of the temporal corneal incision. Then, the capsule was spread on smooth, non-absorbent paper and immersed in a sterile balanced salt solution. Immediately after harvest, each capsule was gently washed with an irrigating solution to remove the viscoelastic agent; curled or wrinkled layers were gently unfolded using a balanced salt solution. The capsule was immersed in 10% neutral formalin for 20 min, then rinsed into a microtube.

Lens epithelial cell imaging

A single ophthalmologist (PL, with 3 years of experience in clinical ophthalmology) performed all examinations of LECs. A plastic pipette and blunt cannula were used with the minimal-touch technique to place each smooth, single-layer preparation on a glass slide. The glass slide was tilted to rinse away excess solution until the capsule exhibited appropriate moisture (approximately 5–10 min) (Supplemental Fig. 1A, right).

Using a light microscope (Olympus BX61 with DP72 camera and Olympus cellSens Dimension V 1.18 software, Tokyo, Japan), photographs were obtained at 10x objective and 10x eyepiece magnification. Examinations were conducted to determine the integrity and continuity of the epithelial monolayer, as well as local cell reduction, epithelial cell enlargement, and nuclear degeneration. To minimize artifacts, examinations included only the non-peripheral area of the capsule, without any signs of mechanical damage related to surgical trauma.

Tissue and image quality were graded by the examiner as good (Supplemental Fig. 1C) or poor (Supplemental Fig. 1B); poor quality capsules were excluded.

Widefield (666 891 ) images of LECs with good tissue and image quality from 124 eyes (10–20 images per eye) were captured under a light microscope at a resolution of 4,140 3,096 pixels in standard RGB color and saved in TIFF format. A reference micrometer was embedded to serve as a reference scale.

Deep-learning-based software methods

Model development

The task in this study is the detection and counting of individual lens epithelial cells, not pixel-level segmentation of cell boundaries. We therefore selected an object-detection architecture. The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of single-stage detectors is well-matched to this task: target cells are approximately uniform in size, exist as a largely non-overlapping monolayer, present a high-contrast detection problem against the trypan-blue-stained background, and require efficient inference for large research cohorts. The open-source nature of YOLO additionally supports reproducibility by other research groups.

Four YOLO v5 variants (S, M, L, and X) with varying model capacities were systematically trained and evaluated. The YOLO-M variant achieved performance equivalent to the larger v5-L and v5-X variants while requiring substantially less inference time, indicating that the task did not benefit from additional capacity beyond this scale; v5-M was therefore selected. It achieved a peak accuracy of 98.23% on the validation set, with mAP@0.5 of 0.905 and mAP@0.5–0.95 of 0.411. The dataset was partitioned at the patient level to prevent data leakage, ensuring that the training cohort was distinct from the cohorts used for evaluation (Supplemental Table 1) and clinical application (Tables 1 and 2).

Model performance evaluation method and statistical analysis

The performance of the final model was validated by comparing Deep-Learning-based cell counts against a reference standard. This standard was established by two ophthalmologists who independently performed manual cell counts on a random sample of 20 images.

Two observers (OBS1 and OBS2, Mahidol university) independently counted all cells in each image. The number of cells per image was counted and recorded by two observers separately. Lens epithelial cell (LEC) counts in each image were compared with manual counts and with those from deep-learning-based software analysis. The only observer (OBS3, with 15 years of experience in data analysis and software engineering, Chulalongkorn University) who performed the deep-learning-based software analysis method was blinded to the manual counting results.

To evaluate agreement between the model’s predictions and manual counts, we performed the following analyses: (i) a repeated-measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to test for systematic differences in mean counts among the model and the two observers; (ii) per-image percentage accuracy and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) against the consensus (mean of two observers) manual count, with 95% confidence intervals estimated by the t-distribution; (iii) Bland-Altman analysis to characterize bias and 95% limits of agreement; (iv) the Pearson correlation coefficient with 95% confidence interval via Fisher’s z-transformation; and (v) the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way random-effects, absolute agreement, single measurement)4, with 95% confidence intervals estimated by nonparametric bootstrap (20,000 resamples). All pairwise comparisons among the model, OBS1, OBS2, and the consensus are reported (Supplemental Table 2).

Results

Evaluation results

The automated cell counts generated by the deep learning model were highly concordant with the manual reference standard. Agreement was assessed using three complementary analyses, each addressing a distinct aspect of agreement: a repeated-measures ANOVA for systematic bias, a Bland-Altman analysis and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for the magnitude of disagreement, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for overall reliability accounting for both error types.

First, a repeated-measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant difference in mean cell counts among the model and the two observers (F(2,38) = 2.40, P = 0.11; Table 3), indicating the absence of detectable systematic bias. Second, across the 20 validation images — encompassing over 43,000 individually counted cells — the model achieved a mean accuracy of 98.12% (95% CI 97.52% to 98.72%) and a MAPE against the consensus manual count of 1.87% (95% CI 1.27% to 2.47%), with a mean model count of 2,170.2 cells compared with a mean manual count of 2,168.95 cells (Supplemental Table 1). Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated minimal bias (1.25 cells) with 95% limits of agreement from − 101.4 to 103.9 cells (Fig. 2), and the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.99 (P < 0.001). Third, the ICC (two-way random-effects, absolute agreement, single measurement) indicated excellent agreement between the model and the consensus count (ICC 0.994, 95% CI 0.982 to 0.997).

Bland-Altman Analysis.

To place these results in the context of the inherent variability of expert manual counting, we computed the agreement between the two observers and the model’s agreement with each observer individually (Supplemental Table 2). The inter-observer MAPE between OBS1 and OBS2 was 3.74%, with an inter-observer ICC of 0.972 (95% CI 0.923 to 0.988). The model’s agreement with the consensus (MAPE 1.87%, ICC 0.994) was therefore tighter than the agreement between the two human experts, and the lower bound of the model-versus-consensus ICC (0.982) exceeded the inter-observer ICC point estimate (0.972). The model also agreed with each individual observer (MAPE 2.94% versus OBS1; 2.06% versus OBS2) at least as closely as the observers agreed with each other. These findings indicate that the model’s variability is within — and in this validation, below — the irreducible variability of expert manual counting, supporting its use as a reproducible alternative to manual quantification.

Establishing a single absolute ground truth for in situ LEC counts is inherently difficult, since expert ophthalmologists do not achieve perfect cell-by-cell agreement. We therefore used percentage accuracy and MAPE relative to the consensus (average) manual count as the primary agreement metrics, validating the model’s ability to generate LECD values that align with the clinical consensus of human experts.

Applying the AI-driven software in a clinical study – evaluating LECD and its associated factors

The previous section demonstrated a high concordance between AI-driven LEC counting and standard manual assessment by subject matter experts, achieving 98.12% accuracy and a correlation of 0.99. Statistical analysis showed no significant difference between the software’s results and expert manual counts, with agreement comparable to that observed between the expert observers themselves.

Building on this validation, we now demonstrate the tool’s application in a clinical study aimed at evaluating factors associated with LECD in a large-scale dataset. To this end, we retrospectively collected 2,574 high-quality light micrographs of the lens epithelium from harvested anterior lens capsules of 118 patients. This clinical-application cohort was assembled independently of the 124-eye imaging pool used for model development and validation [AUTHOR: please confirm the relationship between the 124 eyes and the 118 patients — e.g., overlap, additional cases, or a separately collected series].

First, we excluded 216 images with incorrect reference scales (178 with 20-µm and 38 with 200-µm) and 207 images containing fewer than 1,000 cells, resulting in 2,151 qualified images. To avoid potential confounders, we then excluded images from 25 patients with systemic and ophthalmic diseases: 161 images from patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), 134 images from patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), and 183 images from patients with true exfoliation syndrome (TEX). Additionally, we selected only one eye per patient for analysis, thereby excluding an additional 86 images. Lastly, we screened out 7 images with insufficiently uniform cell counts based on grid distribution (Supplemental Fig. 2).

In conclusion, the screening pipeline resulted in a final dataset of 1,580 images from 93 patients. The demographic characteristics of this final cohort are summarized in Table 1. The participants included 60 females (64.52%) and 33 males (35.48%). The mean age was 66.72 ± 7.74 years (range: 36–80 years), with the largest proportion of patients (48.39%) aged 61–70 years. The dataset comprised 51 right eyes (54.84%) and 42 left eyes (45.16%). Regarding sample processing, the median interval between tissue harvest and examination was 3 h (IQR: 2–14 h), with the majority of samples (62.37%) processed within 8 h.

Factor analysis

Using multiple linear regression, we analyzed the correlation of three factors with mean cell density: sex, age, and the time interval between harvest and tissue examination. Two of these factors—sex and age—demonstrated a statistically significant correlation with mean cell density (P < 0.05), while the time interval showed no such correlation (Table 2).

Specifically, the analysis revealed a significantly higher LECD in women compared to men (indicated by the negative coefficient for the male group). Additionally, we observed a significant negative correlation with age, indicating that LECD decreases with age. The time interval between harvest and examination did not significantly impact cell density.

The current literature suggests that in vitro quantification of LECs may predict capsular alteration following cataract surgery. Higher LEC counts have been associated with increased proliferative activity, which contributes to capsular phimosis,5 anterior5,6 and posterior7,8 capsular opacification. These situations compromise long-term outcomes of intraocular lens implantation, particularly in cases of preexisting zonular weakness, a premium lens, or a toric lens. Further studies are needed to establish the predictive value of LECD, which informs surgical planning and postoperative care.

Discussion

In this study, we examined LECD in 93 anterior capsules from Asian individuals. Our findings indicate that the mean LECD significantly decreases with age (P < 0.05) and is significantly higher in women than in men. While manual counting remains the standard for determining LECD, it is reliable but labor-intensive and inefficient, underscoring the need for a new automated analysis tool.

To address this, we developed a customized software analysis tool using a deep learning model based on the YOLO framework. Validation was performed on 20 images (from 20 eyes of 20 patients), where statistical analysis (ANOVA) and Bland-Altman plotting confirmed that the software’s reliability and accuracy are comparable to manual counting by ophthalmologists.

Several features of the study design support the adequacy of this validation for its intended purpose. The validation reflects more than 43,000 individual cell-level detections spanning a wide range of cell densities (approximately 1,300 to 3,000 cells per image), so the evidentiary base for a detection-and-counting task is substantially larger than the image count alone would suggest. The resulting agreement estimates were precise, with narrow 95% confidence intervals for both the ICC (0.982 to 0.997) and the MAPE (1.27% to 2.47%), indicating that the cohort was sufficient to characterize agreement with adequate precision for this purpose. Moreover, because the model’s agreement and the inter-observer agreement were estimated from the same images, the finding that the model matches or exceeds inter-observer agreement is internally controlled and robust to cohort size. As inter-observer agreement represents the practical ceiling for agreement against a manual reference standard, these results indicate that the model performs at a level consistent with expert manual counting. The cohort size also reflects the practical reality that the present tool is designed to address: expert manual annotation of 2,000 to 3,000 cells per image at scale is rate-limiting, which is itself the methodological bottleneck that an automated method removes.

This study has several limitations. All images were acquired at a single center using a single staining and imaging protocol in a single patient population, and the validation cohort comprised 20 images; while the precision of the agreement estimates (above) supports adequacy for the methodological purpose of this study, generalizability to other laboratories, staining methods, imaging equipment, populations, and a wider range of clinical specimens has not yet been established and represents an important direction for future work. The manual reference standard, although based on two independent expert observers, is itself subject to inter-observer variability, and no absolute ground truth for in situ lens epithelial cell counts exists. The model was developed as a detection-and-counting tool and does not assess cell viability, morphology, distinguish healthy from injured cells, identify staining artifacts, or handle fractional cells at image edges — capabilities that an expert examiner can provide and that may be relevant to certain research applications. Multi-center external validation, incorporating diverse imaging protocols, larger and more varied image cohorts, and a prospectively defined annotation procedure, is the appropriate next step toward broader applicability.

Planned improvements address the capability gaps identified above. Future development will extend the model beyond detection-and-counting to include cell morphology classification, discrimination of healthy from injured cells, identification of staining artifacts such as trypan blue precipitation, and handling of fractional cells at image edges. We also plan to validate robustness across diverse datasets through multi-center collaboration, benchmark against alternative detection and segmentation architectures, and improve precision through targeted retraining on edge cases. These extensions are designed to broaden the tool from a quantification utility into a more comprehensive analytical platform for lens epithelial assessment.

Newer detection architectures and segmentation-based cell-analysis tools (e.g., U-Net derivatives and transformer-based detectors) have been widely adopted since this work was initiated. We did not benchmark against these alternatives, as the goal of the present study was to validate a feasible deep-learning workflow rather than to optimize architectural choice. Comparative benchmarking represents a natural direction for future work.

Previous studies utilized different protocols to evaluate LECD. Most studies collected capsules from various types of cataractous eyes, whereas some were limited to cortical cataract9 or white mature cataract.10 Guggenmoos-Holzmann et al.11 only included postmortem donors. In the past, we obtained capsulotomy tissue during extracapsular and intracapsular cataract extraction,12–14 but continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis is currently preferred. Fagerholm et al.15 and Hara et al.16 examined unstained capsules; other authors have processed specimens using various solutions,14,17 heat,14 or centrifugation.18 Each research group has used specific tools (e.g., corneal specular microscopy9,19 or Scheimpflug densitometry20 and specific methods to determine LECD (either manual count11,14,19 or various analysis software.9,10,13,18,21 Importantly, most studies included fewer than 70 simple senile cataractous capsules.9,10,12,13,15–18,20,21.

In this study, the mean LECD values in eyes with simple cataract were 3,443.26 ± 260.68 cells/mm2 by deep-learning-based software. These results were consistent with findings by Hara and Hara,16 who reported 3,574 ± 512 cells/mm2 in eyes with senile cataract. Other studies found higher mean cell density numbers.9,13,18,20,21 The lower mean LECD in the present study may have been related to the use of trypan blue. Nanavaty et al.10 reported that the mean cell density in the trypan blue-staining group was 3533 ± 664 cells/mm2; this was significantly lower than the mean of 4235 ± 414 cells/mm2 in the no-staining group (P = 0.01).

While our absolute LECD values are lower than those in historical studies, the relative demographic trends (age and sex correlations) remain consistent with the established literature. This suggests that the tool is highly valid for comparative clinical studies, even when absolute baseline values differ across staining methods.

We found that LECD significantly decreased with advancing age (all P < 0.05). Several studies also reported that LECD decreased with increasing age.9,11,14,16,18–20 However, many studies found no correlation between cell density and age.12,13,15,17,22 Thus far, the effects of age on LECD remain unclear.

Our study showed that LECD was significantly higher in women than in men. This observation was consistent with previous findings.11,14 However, Laspias et al.18 showed that cell density decreased with age in a similar manner in both men and women.

Concerning the interval from harvest to tissue examination, there were no significant differences in LECD values among interval ranges, similar to Fagerholm et al. study.15.

Most of these previous studies were limited by small sample sizes, whereas our study leverages a significantly larger dataset. A notable advantage was the accuracy of the cell count per unit area; for example, the customized software analysis could not count fractional cells at the edges of images (Fig. 1). These limitations are room for improvement in our development milestone.

This study demonstrates that our deep learning model offers significant advantages over manual cell counting, enabling both scale and depth that current practice is not feasible for, and has a clear trajectory for future enhancement.

The tool offers four principal benefits over manual counting.

Efficiency

Reduces image analysis time from over a couple of hours on average to a matter of seconds, enabling high-throughput research.

Accuracy & Consistency

Achieves 98.12% accuracy with no significant statistical difference compared to expert ophthalmologists, with fully reproducible counts on repeated analysis of the same image.

Enhanced Spatial Insights

Provides detailed data on cell location and distribution, enabling the creation of density maps and the analysis of spatial patterns not possible with manual counting.

Scalability

Provides a practical solution for analyzing large-scale imaging datasets that are infeasible to process manually. This could be a particularly useful tool for testing emerging hypotheses regarding LECD in future prospective trials.

Several improvements are planned.

Future improvements will focus on enhancing precision, validating robustness across diverse datasets, benchmarking against a range of novel models, and introducing new features, e.g., cell differentiation.

A limitation is the potential underestimation of LECD from surgical specimens when compared with cadaveric lenses. However, comparative cadaveric tissue was unavailable, and surgical specimens likely provide more realistic pathological alterations in diseased eyes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the mean LECD in simple cataract was 3,443.26 ± 260.68 cells/mm2. Our customized software analysis of LECD showed high accuracy with no significant statistical differences and a strong linear relationship with manual counting in excised capsules, highlighting its potential as a robust tool for LEC detection, offering precision comparable to manual methods. There was a gradual decrease in LECD with advancing age in eyes with simple senile cataract. Furthermore, sex was identified as a factor that affected LECD. Although the customized software analysis provided reliable LEC assessment, further optimization is needed. Finally, additional research is underway to evaluate LECDs in various diseases using the customized software analysis. These findings confirm that the AI-based tool delivers manual-grade precision while enabling rapid, scalable LEC quantification for both clinical and research applications.
