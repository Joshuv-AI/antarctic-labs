Frontal brain injury alters human risky choices in self and other contexts
Authors: Farzad Rostami, Sarvenaz Soltani, Jordan Grafman, Raheleh Heyrani, Aryan Yazdanpanah, Amin Jahanbakhshi, Abdol-Hossein Vahabie, Seyed Vahid Shariat, AmirHussein Abdolalizadeh, Fatemeh Sadat Mirfazeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFrontal lobe integrity is crucial for risky decision-making. We investigated how frontal lesions affect computational mechanisms underlying risk when decisions impact oneself versus others. Twenty patients with frontal damage and 20 matched controls accepted or rejected mixed-outcome gambles for themselves or an anonymous other. Patients accepted more disadvantageous gambles than controls in the self condition and showed greater caution for another person; controls showed no self-other difference. Formal model comparison favored a four-parameter Prospect Theory model. Relative to controls, patients showed near-linear probability weighting, reduced loss aversion, and steeper utility curvature; counterfactual analysis identified reduced loss aversion as the principal driver of increased acceptance of disadvantageous gambles. Parameter differences were consistent across conditions; the behavioral dissociation was not reflected computationally. Lesion overlap revealed heterogeneous frontal damage with common ventral and medial prefrontal involvement. These findings provide a mechanistic account of how frontal damage produces dissociable decision patterns across social contexts.

Introduction

Risky decision-making is a cornerstone of human cognition, influencing choices from personal finances to leadership in social dilemmas. These decisions can be self-focused, affect only the decision-maker, or involve others, where outcomes impact people beyond oneself. This self-other distinction introduces unique complexity because it requires balancing self-interest with other-regarding values through distinct cognitive and neural mechanisms.1,2 Behaviorally, people often make different risk choices for others than they do for themselves. A growing literature demonstrates systematic self-other discrepancies in risk preferences. For instance, one study found that individuals tend to be more risk-averse and loss-averse when choosing on behalf of someone else compared to choosing for themselves.3 In that study, choices for others (especially close others like friends) were made more cautiously and consistently, whereas decisions for oneself allowed slightly more risk-taking. Social distance modulated this effect: when deciding for a friend (a socially closer other), participants’ choices became more risk-neutral and reliable than when deciding for a stranger.3 These results suggest that people feel a greater sense of responsibility or caution with someone else’s outcomes, adjusting their decision strategy accordingly. Importantly, differences also emerge at the level of outcome processing. Electrophysiological evidence indicates that the brain evaluates the results of risky choices differently depending on who is affected. Xu4 reported that negative feedback in a Balloon Analogue Risk Task elicited a larger P300 component (a late positive potential indexing evaluative attention) when participants were risking their own outcome, compared to when they were risking someone else’s outcome. Together, these findings imply that despite deciding more cautiously for others, people allocate more attention or emotional significance to losses incurred for themselves than for others. These studies also underscore that self-versus-other decisions are behaviorally distinct and recruit partially different neural mechanisms. Social-cognitive processes, including those mediated by the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ), intertwine with valuation processes during other-regarding decisions, leading to unique patterns of risk-taking.5,6 Understanding these patterns is particularly important in clinical populations with frontal lobe damage, who may struggle with both decision-making and social cognition.

Damage to the frontal lobes, whether from trauma, stroke, or degenerative disease, often yields profound changes in decision-making, especially in situations involving risk or social judgment. The frontal lobe is essential for executive functions like planning, impulse control, and considering future consequences; thus, lesions in frontal regions can produce disinhibition and impaired judgment. Classic neuropsychological cases have shown that injuries to the ventromedial/orbitofrontal prefrontal cortex (PFC) led to reckless financial choices and insensitivity to future outcomes, even when basic intellect is preserved.7 Clinically, an orbitofrontal syndrome may emerge, characterized by antisocial behaviors, emotional instability, and impulsivity.8 Patients with orbitofrontal damage often exhibit heightened risk-taking, for example, gambling large sums despite losses, alongside a blunted concern for negative consequences. Recent lesion studies confirm that the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) is crucial for regulating risk behavior: individuals with vmPFC lesions tend to bet more money on gambles and have difficulty delaying gratification, reflecting a loss of the normal loss-aversion and self-control mechanisms.9,10,11 They may also fail to adjust their strategy after losses (a behavior sometimes called “risk chasing” or perseveration), further highlighting deficits in feedback-based learning.11 Frontal lobe damage not only affects risk-taking but can also disrupt the social aspects of decision-making. Because the medial and orbitofrontal sectors of the frontal cortex overlap with the social cognition network, patients with frontal injuries often struggle to interpret social information and to empathize with others’ perspectives.

Other prefrontal regions contribute to controlling risk-taking: the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) and lateral prefrontal areas (e.g., dorsolateral and inferior frontal gyrus) are engaged in cognitive control processes such as response inhibition and conflict monitoring.12 These frontal regions help individuals “think twice” before acting, promoting cautious decisions under high stakes. Damage to these control regions undermines these regulatory processes and has been linked to increased impulsivity and maladaptive risky behavior. Individuals with lesions to the dACC or lateral PFC often struggle to inhibit prepotent responses and fail to learn from negative outcomes, resulting in disinhibited or suboptimal choices. Consistent with this, experimentally silencing the dACC in animal models causes more impulsive choices and suboptimal decision-making.13 Emotional and interoceptive inputs are also integrated into choice behavior via the anterior insular cortex. The anterior insular cortex links visceral emotional states (like stress or anxiety) with decision processes, and perturbations to this region can shift risk preferences. For example, experimental manipulations in animals show that stress-related changes in anterior insula activity led to greater risk-seeking, highlighting this region’s importance in adapting decisions under emotional strain.14 In summary, an extended healthy frontal network, including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)/vmPFC for value representation, dACC/lateral PFC for impulse control, and insula for emotional context, works in concert to guide effective risky decision-making under normal conditions, and damage to this network leads to impaired risk-taking decisions. Neuroimaging studies in healthy participants have corroborated this functional architecture, demonstrating that the ventral striatum and PFC encode gain and loss signals during mixed-gamble evaluation,15 that the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) causally contributes to probability weighting,16 and that fronto-limbic circuits mediate loss aversion.17

Consequently, a patient with a frontal lobe lesion may fail to appropriately adjust their risk-taking when making decisions on behalf of another person. For example, such a patient might engage in gambles that a neurologically healthy individual would avoid in situations where someone else’s welfare is at stake.6,18 In summary, frontal lobe lesions provide a unique window into the necessity of frontal circuits for adaptive decision-making. They reveal how damage to specific neural substrates (for value representation, impulse control, or mentalizing) can lead to aberrant risk preferences. However, to date, few studies have directly examined how frontal lobe damage impacts the difference between self-focused and other-focused risky decisions. Addressing this gap can advance both scientific understanding and clinical care. The present study builds on the aforementioned literature by investigating risky decision-making in a cross-sectional study of patients with frontal lobe injuries. Our sample consists of individuals with diverse frontal lesions, including orbitofrontal, frontopolar, frontotemporal, and deep frontoparietal damage, and a demographically matched healthy control group. Focusing exclusively on frontal-damage patients (and excluding those with subcortical-only lesions) allows us to isolate the role of frontal cortical networks in modulating risky choices. We employ a computational modeling approach to capture the nuanced effects of frontal damage on decision behavior. Specifically, we use frameworks from Prospect Theory to quantify each participant’s risk preferences in parametric terms (such as their degree of loss aversion and probability weighting). This modeling approach enables us to examine overall shifts in risk attitude due to frontal injury, the underlying computational mechanisms driving those shifts, and how the resulting behavior may differ across self and other decision contexts.

By analyzing how patients with frontal lobe lesions perform in self-oriented and other-oriented risk scenarios, we aim to shed light on the specific contribution of frontal neural circuits to socially contextualized decision-making. We expect this lesion-based investigation to provide causal evidence complementing prior correlational neuroimaging findings. Understanding these dynamics has important rehabilitation implications. If frontal damage is found to selectively impair certain aspects of decision-making (for example, exaggerating risk-seeking for oneself or failing to adjust caution when others are impacted), such knowledge can guide targeted interventions. Rehabilitation programs for frontal lobe patients might incorporate decision-making training modules that explicitly address these deficits, for instance by coaching patients to pause and consider consequences for others (to counteract impulsivity and egocentric decisions) or using feedback-based exercises to improve learning from negative outcomes. Indeed, behavioral therapy for brain-injured patients often targets issues like disinhibition and impaired social judgment.19 The insights from this study can refine those interventions by pinpointing which cognitive mechanisms to focus on. In sum, by examining risky choice behavior through the dual lenses of social context and frontal lobe integrity, our study seeks to advance theoretical models of decision neuroscience and contribute to better clinical management of patients with frontal lobe damage. Ultimately, deeper knowledge of how the frontal lobes govern risky decisions for self and others may help affected individuals relearn safer decision strategies and improve their autonomy and social functioning after injury.

Results

Model-free behavioral analysis

Participants completed a self-versus-other risky decision-making task in which mixed-outcome gambles displayed gain and loss magnitudes and probabilities (Figure 1). Patients and controls were matched on age, sex, and education; demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Overall, both groups’ gamble acceptance rates increased as the gambles’ expected value (EV) became more favorable, but clear group and context differences emerged (Figure 2). Healthy control participants behaved as expected for mildly risk-averse decision-makers: they rarely accepted gambles with strongly negative EVs and accepted nearly all gambles with highly positive EVs. Furthermore, controls showed no reliable difference between decisions for themselves and for another person, with paired t tests confirming no significant difference in acceptance rates between self and other for either negative-EV or positive-EV gambles (all p > 0.5). In contrast, frontal lesion patients exhibited a striking dissociation in their risk behavior. When deciding for themselves, patients were notably more willing to accept gambles with negative EVs than controls were. For these disadvantageous trials, the patient group’s acceptance rate in self-decisions was significantly higher than the control group’s (patients: 38.81%, controls: 13.81%; two-sample t(38) = 4.14, p = 0.0018, Cohen’s d = 1.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] [12.6%, 37.4%]), indicating that frontal damage was associated with substantially increased maladaptive risk-taking in personal decisions. Conversely, when making choices for another person, patients’ behavior shifted toward conservatism. Their overall acceptance rate was significantly lower for other decisions than for self-decisions (paired t(19) = 2.31, p = 0.038, Cohen’s d = 0.41, 95% CI [0.6, 12.3]), revealing a robust within-patient self-other dissociation. This suggests that frontal damage disrupts not only the evaluation of personal risk but also the modulation of that risk in a social context.

Across all 48 gambles in the self condition, patients accepted a significantly higher proportion of gambles overall compared to controls (patients: 60.83%, controls: 45.83%; t(38) = 3.64, p = 0.0024, Cohen’s d = 1.15, 95% CI [6.7, 23.3]). This elevated acceptance rate was disproportionately concentrated among gambles with negative EV (Figure 2C), confirming that patients were not simply more engaged but were specifically accepting disadvantageous propositions. Consequently, patients’ expected earnings across the task were significantly lower than those of controls (patients: mean EV = 38.28 thousand Tomans, controls: mean EV = 42.74 thousand Tomans; t(38) = −2.32, p = 0.038, Cohen’s d = −0.73, 95% CI [−8.39, −0.53] thousand Tomans), demonstrating that their altered decision-making carried a tangible economic cost. In the other condition, a modest but significant group difference in overall acceptance was also observed (t(38) = 2.12, p = 0.041, Cohen’s d = 0.67, 95% CI [0.004, 0.197]).

To assess model-free choice consistency, we computed a transition-count measure separately for each participant and decision condition. Gambles were sorted in ascending order of objective EV, with gain amount, loss amount, and gain probability used as deterministic secondary sort keys to handle ties consistently. The transition count was defined as the number of times the binary accept/reject response changed between adjacent trials in this EV-ordered sequence. In a 48-trial sequence, there are 47 adjacent pairs, so the metric ranges from 0 (all responses identical) to 47 (alternation on every adjacent pair). An idealized single-threshold EV-monotonic pattern yields 1 transition, and under independent random responding with p(accept) = 0.5, the expected count is 23.5. Because choices in this task depend on gain magnitude, loss magnitude, and gain probability separately, each transformed nonlinearly in the Prospect Theory model; the transition count is a descriptive index of EV-ordered consistency rather than a measure of model fit.

A mixed-design ANOVA (group × decision context) on transition counts revealed a significant main effect of group, F(1, 38) = 6.21, p = 0.017, ηp2 = 0.140, with patients exhibiting more transitions (self: M = 18.20; other: M = 18.20) than controls (self: M = 15.00; other: M = 14.55). Neither the main effect of decision context nor the group × context interaction reached significance (both F < 0.10, p > 0.76). A participant-specific permutation analysis confirmed that observed counts in both groups were significantly below the corresponding random-ordering baselines in all four group-condition cells (all p < 0.01; Table S3), indicating structured rather than random responding. The interpretation of this finding is considered in the discussion.

Model selection and validation

To determine the most parsimonious computational model justified by our data, we compared four nested models of increasing complexity (Table 2). The simplest model (M4, Expected Utility) assumed a single utility curvature parameter (α) and choice temperature (θ), with no loss aversion or probability weighting. Successive models introduced probability weighting (M3), then loss aversion (M2), and finally a separate curvature parameter for losses (M1, the full five-parameter Prospect Theory model). The four-parameter Single-Curvature Prospect Theory model (M2), in which a single curvature parameter α operates symmetrically across gains and losses (α = β), provided the best fit at the group level, achieving the lowest mean Akaike information criterion (AIC; 31.84) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC; 39.33) across all 80 participant-condition datasets (Table 2). M1 and M2 showed essentially equivalent empirical support by AIC (ΔAIC = 0.44), indicating that the additional loss-domain curvature parameter (β) does not provide meaningful explanatory gain; under the principle of parsimony, the simpler M2 is therefore preferred.20 The BIC comparison, which imposes a stronger complexity penalty, favored M2 more decisively (ΔBIC = 2.31). Both M3 and M4 performed substantially worse than M2 (ΔAIC >9.7), confirming that probability weighting and loss aversion are essential model components. We therefore adopted M2 as the primary model for all subsequent analyses.

To verify that M2 parameters could be reliably estimated from 48 trials, we conducted a parameter recovery analysis in which each participant’s fitted parameters were used to generate synthetic choice data that were then re-fitted using the identical procedure. Recovery was good for utility curvature (α: r = 0.80), probability weighting (γ: r = 0.79), and loss aversion (λ: r = 0.77). The softmax temperature showed moderate recovery (θ: r = 0.56), consistent with known estimation challenges for this parameter under limited trial counts.21 Critically, all parameters showed improved recoverability relative to the original five-parameter specification (M1: α: r = 0.59, λ: r = 0.66, γ: r = 0.63), confirming that the model reduction enhanced parameter identifiability (Figure S1).

To further characterize individual-level estimation precision, we computed nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals (500 resamples per participant-condition dataset; 40 datasets per group, i.e., 20 participants × 2 decision contexts). Median 95% CI widths were 0.89 for γ, 0.91 for λ, 1.39 for α, and 18.56 for θ. For γ and λ, the majority of individual bootstrap CIs excluded the opposing group mean, indicating that the group-level differences are resolvable at the individual level for most participant-condition datasets (individual-level CIs are available in the deposited data repository).

Mixed-design ANOVAs (group × decision context) on each M2 parameter revealed three group differences in fitted parameters of differing statistical magnitude (Figures 4C–4F). Probability weighting (γ) showed the largest statistical between-group difference (F(1, 38) = 20.66, p < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.352). Patients exhibited near-linear probability weighting (mean γ ≈ 1.23), in stark contrast to the strongly nonlinear weighting characteristic of controls (mean γ ≈ 0.33). This effect was equally pronounced across both decision contexts (self: t = 3.48, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.10; other: t = 4.19, p = 0.0002, Cohen’s d = 1.33, 95% CI [0.49, 1.41]), with no significant main effect of decision context (p = 0.920) and no group × context interaction (p = 0.687). Healthy controls displayed the classic Prospect Theory pattern: strong overweighting of small probabilities and underweighting of large probabilities, producing an S-shaped weighting function. Patients exhibited a Prelec weighting function22 close to linearity, with mild inverse-S curvature (mean γ = 1.23; Figure 4A). Loss aversion (λ) showed the second-largest statistical group difference (F(1, 38) = 13.96, p = 0.0006, ηp2 = 0.269). Patients exhibited significantly reduced loss aversion (mean λ = 0.81) compared to controls (mean λ = 1.54), and this difference was robust in both the self condition (t = −3.27, p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = −1.03, 95% CI [−1.20, −0.27]) and the other condition (t = −2.72, p = 0.012, Cohen’s d = −0.86, 95% CI [−1.26, −0.17]). No significant main effect of decision context (p = 0.640) or group × context interaction (p = 0.390) was observed. The fitted λ values indicate that the asymmetric weighting of losses relative to equivalent gains—with controls showing the canonical Prospect Theory pattern in which losses are weighted approximately 1.5 times more heavily than equivalent gains—was attenuated in patients, with the patient group mean falling below unity (λ = 0.81). Mechanistically, λ < 1 implies that the loss branch of the value function carries less subjective weight than the gain branch, reversing the asymmetry present in controls (λ = 1.54). As demonstrated in the counterfactual analysis reported in the following paragraph, this is the parameter difference most directly responsible for the increased acceptance of negative-EV gambles in patients.

Utility curvature (α) showed a significant omnibus main effect of group (F(1, 38) = 8.19, p = 0.007, ηp2 = 0.177), with patients exhibiting steeper curvature (mean α = 0.32) than controls (mean α = 0.75). Post hoc comparisons indicated lower α in patients in both the self (t = −2.09, p = 0.044, Cohen’s d = −0.66, 95% CI [−0.86, −0.01]) and other (t = −2.30, p = 0.027, Cohen’s d = −0.73, 95% CI [−0.82, −0.05]) conditions, though these individual comparisons did not survive Bonferroni correction (corrected threshold: p < 0.0125). No significant main effect of context (p = 0.768) or interaction (p = 0.240) was found. This pattern suggests that patients showed greater diminishing sensitivity to outcome magnitudes, though the condition-specific effects should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the multiple-comparison correction (Figure 4B). The choice temperature parameter (θ) did not differ between groups (F(1, 38) = 2.18, p = 0.148, ηp2 = 0.054), with no significant context (p = 0.497) or interaction (p = 0.493) effects. No group × context interactions were significant for any parameter (all p > 0.05), indicating that the group differences in fitted parameters were stable across both self- and other-directed decisions.

The model-based analysis identified three group differences in fitted Prospect Theory parameters, all consistent across self and other conditions. By partial eta-squared, the largest group difference was in probability weighting (γ = 1.23 in patients vs. 0.33 in controls; ηp2 = 0.352, post hoc tests surviving Bonferroni correction in both contexts). Loss aversion was reduced in patients (λ = 0.81 vs. 1.54 in controls; ηp2 = 0.269, post hoc tests surviving correction in both contexts), with the patient mean falling below unity. Utility curvature was steeper in patients (α = 0.32 vs. 0.75 in controls; ηp2 = 0.177, omnibus group effect significant; condition-specific post hoc comparisons did not survive Bonferroni correction). To distinguish the statistical magnitude of these between-group differences from each parameter’s contribution to the behavioral outcome, we performed a counterfactual parameter-substitution analysis on the 21 negative-EV gambles. Starting from the control parameter set, substituting only the patient λ raised mean predicted P(accept) from 0.007 to 0.298 and reversed the subjective value of a canonical negative-EV gamble (40% chance to win, 60% chance to lose at ±3,000 Tomans) from V = −127 to V = +6. Substituting only α produced essentially no change in mean P(accept) (0.003) and reduced the magnitude of V on the canonical gamble to approximately −4. Substituting only γ raised mean P(accept) marginally (to 0.024) and made V on the canonical gamble more negative (−238). The three parameter differences were stable across decision contexts, with no group × context interaction on any parameter. The mechanistic interpretation of these results is developed in the discussion. Discussion

This study demonstrates that frontal lobe lesions are associated with dissociable patterns of risky choice across decision contexts: increased acceptance of negative-EV gambles when deciding for oneself, yet greater caution when deciding on behalf of another person. These behavioral patterns are accounted for by systematic differences in fitted Prospect Theory parameters between the patient and control groups. Formal comparison of four nested models identified a parsimonious four-parameter model (M2) as providing the best account of trial-by-trial choice in both groups. The estimated parameters differed reliably between groups in all three value-relevant components: probability weighting shifted from strongly S-shaped in controls (γ = 0.33) to near-linear with mild inverse-S curvature in patients (γ = 1.23); loss aversion was substantially lower in patients (λ = 0.81) than in controls (λ = 1.54), with the patient group mean falling below unity; and utility curvature was steeper in patients (α = 0.32) than in controls (α = 0.75). These differences were stable across self and other decision contexts, with no group × context interaction on any parameter.

The mapping from these parameter differences to the behavioral outcome is not uniform across the three parameters, and identifying which differences are principally responsible requires decomposing their contributions. The counterfactual substitution analysis reported in the Prospect theory model results section performs this decomposition by replacing each patient mean parameter individually into the control parameter set and tracking the resulting change in predicted acceptance of negative-EV gambles. Substituting only the patient mean λ was sufficient to reverse the subjective value of a representative disadvantageous gamble from V = −127 to V = +6 and to raise predicted mean acceptance from 0.007 to 0.298: when the loss aversion coefficient falls below unity, the loss branch of the subjective value function carries less weight than the gain branch, a reversal that directly reduces the subjective penalty of negative-EV gambles. Substituting only α primarily compressed the absolute scale of subjective values without reliably changing the sign of V across most gamble configurations, producing negligible change in predicted acceptance. Substituting only γ raised predicted acceptance modestly, to 0.024, and made V on the representative gamble more negative rather than less so, because patients’ near-linear weighting function assigns higher decision weights to moderate-to-large loss probabilities than controls’ S-shaped function does (e.g., w(0.60) = 0.65 in patients vs. 0.45 in controls). The γ difference therefore does not contribute to the behavioral outcome by reducing the subjective weight of losses; rather, its contribution is mediated by the interaction between the removal of the S-shaped distortions present in controls and the collapsed value scale and reversed loss asymmetry produced by lower α and λ. The numerical walkthrough in the following paragraph makes this convergence concrete.

The interaction of the three parameter differences accounts for the behavioral pattern in Figure 2C. Consider a representative disadvantageous gamble from the task: a 40% chance to win 3,000 Tomans paired with a 60% chance to lose 3,000 Tomans (EV = −600 Tomans). For a control participant (γ = 0.33, α = 0.75, λ = 1.54), the Prelec function transforms the 40% gain probability to w(0.40) ≈ 0.38 and the 60% loss probability to w(0.60) ≈ 0.45, compressing both moderate probabilities below their objective values. The power utility with α = 0.75 transforms the outcome magnitude as 3,000ˆ0.75 ≈ 405. With λ = 1.54 amplifying the loss branch, the resulting subjective value is V = 0.38 × 405–1.54 × 0.45 × 405 ≈ 154 − 281 = −127. The gamble is evaluated as strongly negative, and the control rejects it with high probability.

For a patient (γ = 1.23, α = 0.32, λ = 0.81), the same gamble is processed under markedly different parameter values. The Prelec function with γ = 1.23 yields w(0.40) ≈ 0.41 and w(0.60) ≈ 0.65. Notably, the patient assigns a higher subjective weight to the 60% loss probability (0.65) than the control does (0.45); the shift from S-shaped to inverse-S weighting increases, not decreases, the weight on moderate-to-large probabilities. However, the extreme compression of the value scale means that 3,000ˆ0.32 ≈ 13, approximately a 30-fold reduction relative to controls. Loss aversion below unity (λ = 0.81) means that the loss branch carries less subjective weight than the gain branch, reversing the asymmetry present in controls. The resulting subjective value is V = 0.41 × 13–0.81 × 0.65 × 13 ≈ 5.3–6.8 = −1.5. The gamble is evaluated as only marginally negative, and the patient accepts it at near-chance probability.

The near-indifference in patients is generated primarily by the reduction of λ below unity, which inverts the loss-gain asymmetry; a counterfactual substitution of the patient λ alone into the control parameter set is sufficient to reverse V on this gamble from negative to positive. The α reduction contributes secondarily, collapsing the absolute scale of subjective values by approximately 30-fold and bringing V close to zero. The γ difference, despite being the largest statistical group effect, does not reduce the weight on loss probabilities; patients in fact weight the 60% loss probability more heavily than controls do, and γ contributes to the net outcome by removing the S-shaped distortions of controls rather than by directly facilitating acceptance. The convergence of these parameter differences explains the behavioral pattern in Figure 2C: patients evaluated most negative-EV gambles as marginally rather than strongly aversive, increasing the probability of acceptance and, as a direct consequence, reducing their expected earnings relative to controls.

These findings converge with and extend emerging evidence on the neural architecture of risky choice. Causal evidence from transcranial magnetic stimulation has implicated the dorsolateral PFC specifically in the generation of nonlinear probability distortions, with stimulation selectively altering probability weighting while leaving other Prospect Theory parameters unchanged.16 Consistent with this account, patients in the present sample exhibited near-linear probability weighting relative to controls. However, given the descriptive nature of the overlap map and the heterogeneity of lesion locations, the lesion data do not permit strong focal localization claims. The robust reduction in loss aversion is consistent with the established role of fronto-limbic circuits, particularly the amygdala and ventromedial PFC, in generating the affective asymmetry between gains and losses.17 The concurrent compression of utility curvature aligns with the known contribution of the OFC to subjective value encoding.7,8,23,24 The ordering of statistical effect sizes in the present data (γ > λ > α by partial eta-squared) indicates that the frontal network contributes to probability weighting and loss aversion, not only to subjective value representation.

Beyond these aggregate group differences, our study revealed a striking behavioral dissociation in how patients behaved across social contexts. They exhibited greater risk-taking when choosing for themselves but became more risk-averse when choosing for another person. This divergence is particularly notable because our control participants showed no such self-other gap, a finding consistent with some studies in healthy populations.3,25 Importantly, this behavioral bifurcation was not accompanied by context-dependent shifts in the fitted computational parameters. Our Prospect Theory modeling under the adopted M2 model revealed no significant group × context interactions on any parameter (α, γ, λ, θ; all p > 0.05), indicating that the group differences in fitted parameters (higher γ, lower λ, and lower α in patients) were stable across both self and other contexts. The tendency toward caution and responsibility commonly observed when participants decide on behalf of another person may have attenuated the behavioral expression of these parameter differences in the other condition, leading to lower acceptance rates. In the self condition, where this contextual tendency does not apply, the same parameter differences were associated with increased acceptance of negative-EV gambles. Notably, our control participants showed stable decision-making across both contexts in terms of both computational parameters and behavior, confirming that healthy frontal function maintains consistent valuation processes regardless of social context. The combination of context-invariant fitted parameter values and context-dependent behavioral outputs in patients suggests that the self-Other behavioral dissociation was driven by context-specific motivational factors modulating how a stable parameter profile translated into observed choices, rather than by context-dependent changes in estimated valuation parameters. Another explanation for this self-other reversal is that decisions for another person engaged a more deliberative, social rule-based cognitive strategy that partially offset the behavioral expression of the patients’ altered parameter profile. A patient might consciously invoke a social norm, such as a “do no harm” principle, and thus systematically reject risky options when acting on behalf of another, even while intuitively or automatically accepting similar gambles for themselves based on their disrupted valuation system. This aligns with the concept of responsibility aversion, where individuals become more cautious when their choices affect others’ outcomes.26,27 Despite their injury, our patients may retain an intellectual understanding of this responsibility, and in the absence of the personal lure of a reward, they may default to an overly cautious strategy.

A complementary explanation lies in the differential affective engagement between contexts. In the self condition, patients may still experience a diminished but present motivational pull from the possibility of winning. In the other condition, this vicarious reward is likely even weaker. Lacking both a strong aversion to loss and personal excitement for gain, patients may simply find gambles for another person insufficiently compelling to warrant acceptance, leading to a conservative pattern of rejection. This interpretation is supported by our finding of reduced sensitivity to outcome magnitudes in patients: if large gains are not strongly motivating for oneself, they are likely even less so when they accrue to an anonymous other. We emphasize that this behavioral pattern, reduced risk-taking for others, should not be interpreted as evidence of prosocial motivation or altruistic concern. Our task did not require participants to sacrifice their own outcomes to benefit another and therefore cannot assess other-regarding preferences. The conservative pattern in the other condition is more parsimoniously explained by the combination of a stable parameter profile (producing near-zero subjective values for most gambles) and the absence of the personal reward motivation that drives acceptance in the self condition.

Our results extend previous research by demonstrating that frontal lobe damage produces behaviorally dissociable patterns of risk-taking across social contexts, rather than a monolithic increase in impulsivity. Prior lesion studies have variously reported that frontal patients can be risk-seeking, apathetic, or show impaired social judgment.6,8,19 By examining decisions in both personal and interpersonal contexts, we help reconcile these observations. Frontal patients can be both risk-seeking and risk-averse: they take excessive risks when only their own welfare is at stake but become highly guarded when responsible for another’s outcome. This behavioral bifurcation underscores that a single computational disruption can manifest as opposing behavioral patterns depending on the motivational context in which decisions are made.

The transition-count analysis provides a model-free, descriptive index of how closely each participant’s choices tracked the EV ordering of the gambles. Patients showed more transitions than controls across the EV-ordered sequence in both decision contexts, indicating less tight alignment between EV ordering and observed choice. Two qualifications are important. First, the index is a strict EV-monotonicity criterion: any pair of gambles for which the participant’s subjective-value ordering departs from the EV ordering contributes an additional transition, so values above 1 are expected under any non-linear weighting of gains, losses, and probabilities. Second, in all group-condition cells, the observed transition counts were significantly below the permutation baseline that preserves individual acceptance rates, which rules out random responding as an account of either group’s behavior.

The elevated transition count in patients does not, on its own, account for the directional pattern of acceptance differences. Independent decision noise would be expected to produce approximately symmetric deviations across positive-EV and negative-EV gambles, whereas patients’ deviations were concentrated on negative-EV gambles (Figure 2C). The non-significant group difference in the softmax temperature parameter is consistent with this picture, though this null result should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the study’s sample size: conditional on each participant’s estimated subjective values, the stochasticity of the choice process did not differ reliably between groups. We therefore interpret the higher transition count in patients as a descriptive correlate of the parameter-level differences reported earlier, rather than as evidence of a separate increase in choice stochasticity.

These findings have practical implications for the clinical management of individuals with frontal damage. The computational profile we identified suggests that such patients may be vulnerable to accepting disadvantageous financial propositions, not from ignorance but from an altered internal valuation system. Clinicians and caregivers should be aware of this vulnerability when patients face consequential decisions.

Our results point to specific targets for cognitive rehabilitation. Metacognitive strategy training could teach patients to pause and explicitly evaluate the probabilities, gains, and losses involved in high-stakes choices, providing a deliberate process that may attenuate the behavioral expression of the parameter differences identified here. Emerging evidence that noninvasive brain stimulation of prefrontal regions can modulate probability weighting16 and feedback-based learning11 offers a potential future avenue for more targeted therapeutic intervention.

The hierarchy of effect sizes we observed (γ > λ > α) generates testable predictions for neuroanatomical dissociation. Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping in larger cohorts (n > 50, as recommended previously28) could test whether near-linear probability weighting maps specifically onto lateral prefrontal and insular damage, reduced loss aversion onto ventromedial-amygdalar circuits, and compressed utility curvature onto orbitofrontal pathology. Damage to dorsomedial PFC, a region involved in social cognition,29,30 might specifically contribute to the abnormal self-other dissociation. More complex paradigms incorporating learning, feedback processing, or naturalistic social contexts would further extend the scope of these findings.

A further avenue for future inquiry concerns whether the present findings generalize to experience-based decision-making contexts. Because our paradigm presented probabilities explicitly, it falls within the description-based tradition; a growing literature demonstrates that probability weighting and risk attitudes differ systematically when probabilities are learned through experiential sampling rather than stated numerically, a phenomenon known as the description-experience gap.31,32 Whether frontal lesions similarly disrupt probability weighting in experiential settings, where feedback-dependent frontal circuits are more heavily engaged, remains an important open question.

Additionally, the other condition in our paradigm involved hypothetical rather than real consequences for the anonymous other person. While this design is consistent with established methodology in the self-other decision-making literature,26 the lack of real stakes for others means that we cannot fully rule out the possibility that the behavioral self-other divergence partly reflects reduced motivational engagement rather than genuine context-dependent modulation of the decision process. We note, however, that the systematic directionality of the behavioral shift (toward conservatism, not toward random responding) and the absence of any group × context interactions in the computational parameters argue against a pure disengagement account. Future studies employing designs with real monetary consequences for other participants would provide stronger evidence for the social-cognitive interpretation of the self-other dissociation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that frontal lobe lesions are associated with systematic differences in fitted Prospect Theory parameters that together account for the altered pattern of risky choice observed in the patient group. Relative to matched controls, patients exhibited near-linear probability weighting, loss aversion below unity, and steeper utility curvature, with counterfactual analysis identifying reduced loss aversion as the principal driver of their increased acceptance of negative-EV gambles. This parameter profile was behaviorally dissociable: patients accepted more disadvantageous gambles when deciding for themselves yet became more conservative when making choices on behalf of another person, a finding that points to context-sensitive rather than globally altered risk processing. These results advance understanding of the frontal lobes’ contribution to both self-directed and other-directed decision-making under risk and carry practical implications for the assessment and rehabilitation of individuals with acquired frontal damage.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size (n = 20 per group), while typical of clinical lesion studies, limits statistical power for detecting smaller effects and precludes examination of individual differences within the patient group. Second, because all patients in our sample sustained traumatic brain injuries, our findings have strong internal consistency but may not generalize to frontal damage arising from other etiologies, such as slow-growing tumors or stroke, which differ in their spatial distribution, temporal onset, and potential for neural reorganization. Whether the parameter-level group differences identified here extend to non-traumatic frontal lesions remains an empirical question for future research. Third, the lesion overlap map was constructed from digital neuroimaging data available for only 14 of the 20 patients; the remaining 6 had lesion locations confirmed via clinical reports but could not be included in the spatial analysis. Moreover, given the heterogeneous anatomical distribution of the lesions and the modest maximal voxel overlap (25%; 3–4 patients out of 14), the overlap map should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of focal structure-function localization. The overlap analysis characterizes lesion geometry in the present sample but does not constitute a voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping analysis. Fourth, the task comprised 48 trials per condition, which, while clinically feasible, limits individual-level parameter precision, as reflected in the bootstrap CI analyses (median CI widths: γ = 0.89, λ = 0.91, α = 1.39). Fifth, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about the temporal evolution of these computational deficits following injury. Sixth, trial-level reaction times were not recorded, precluding analysis of decision speed differences between groups; incorporating reaction time measurement in subsequent implementations of this paradigm would help distinguish impulsive from deliberative decision-making profiles.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

A total of 33 patients with brain injuries and 30 healthy control participants were initially recruited for the study. All patients had documented brain lesions involving frontal cortical regions, and all controls were neurologically healthy. Thirteen patients were excluded due to lesion characteristics (11 had primarily subcortical lesions without significant frontal cortical involvement, and 2 had unclear or diffuse diagnoses), leaving 20 patients with confirmed frontal lobe lesions (16 men, 4 women; mean age = 35.09 years, SD = 11.77; mean education = 10.5 years, SD = 2.8). Lesion confirmation and overlap mapping are described below. Ten control participants were excluded to better match the patient sample (7 had exceptionally high educational attainment beyond a master’s degree, and 3 showed anomalously extreme response patterns with >95% gamble acceptance, indicating potential non-compliance). This yielded a final healthy control sample of 20 individuals (16 men, 4 women; mean age = 37.65 years, SD = 9.06; mean education = 11.2 years, SD = 2.5), well-matched to patients on age (t(38) = −0.76, p = 0.45), education (t(38) = 0.83, p = 0.41), and sex distribution (χ2(1) = 0.00, p = 1; Table 1). All participants provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria for both groups included any history of major psychiatric illness (e.g., psychosis, mania, moderate-to-severe depression, substance abuse) or additional neurological conditions (such as significant stroke outside the lesion area for patients, or any neurologic disorder in controls). The healthy controls were primarily hospital staff. All patients had sustained traumatic brain injuries (TBI) and were in a stable, chronic post-acute phase (mean time since injury = 2.53 ± 2.58 months; Table 1), ensuring that performance reflected stable impairment rather than acute effects. No patient had widespread cognitive impairments preventing task comprehension, as verified by neuropsychological screening. Ethnicity data were not collected.

Method details

Risky decision-making task

We employed a computerized risky choice task adapted from Edelson et al.’s26 paradigm for self-versus-other decision-making, programmed and presented using MATLAB (MathWorks) with Psychtoolbox-3. Each participant completed two blocks of trials: one block making decisions for oneself (“Self” condition) and another block making decisions on behalf of another person (“Other” condition). The order of blocks was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. In each block, the participant encountered a series of 48 independent gambles. On each trial, a mixed-valence lottery was presented, consisting of a potential monetary gain and a potential monetary loss, each paired with a specified probability of occurring. The task display showed the probability of winning versus losing (as a percentage and a graphical bar) and the corresponding gain amount (in positive Tomans) versus loss amount (in negative Tomans). For example, a trial might show a 20% chance to win +5000 Tomans (approximately $0.40 USD) and an 80% chance to lose −1000 Tomans (approximately $0.08 USD). The probabilities of a win were systematically varied across four levels (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%), and outcome magnitudes were drawn from a predefined set of values. Gain amounts were in the range +1000 to +7000 Tomans (approximately $0.08–$0.56 USD, in increments of 2000 Tomans), and loss amounts ranged from −1000 to −5000 Tomans (approximately $0.08–$0.40 USD, increments of 2000 Tomans), similar to the range used by Edelson and colleagues.26 At the time of testing, the exchange rates were approximately 12,500 Tomans per USD. This yielded a range of gamble expected values (EV = p(win)×gain + p(lose)×loss) from substantially negative to substantially positive.

Each trial required a binary choice: the participant decided to either accept the gamble, in which case the outcome (win or loss) would be realized with the given probabilities, or reject the gamble, in which case neither gain nor loss was incurred for that trial (Figure 1). Participants indicated their choice by button press, with no time limit. They were explicitly instructed that in the “Self” block, accepting a gamble meant they would win or lose the stated amounts, whereas in the “Other” block, their choices would affect a hypothetical anonymous other person instead of themselves. Importantly, participants were told that one trial from each block would be randomly selected at the end to count “for real”: the outcome of a randomly chosen self-decision trial would be applied to their bonus payment, and the outcome of a randomly chosen other-decision trial would hypothetically be applied to another person’s bonus. All other trials would not directly affect payment. To ensure incentive compatibility, each participant was given a starting endowment (bankroll) of 15,000 Tomans (approximately $1.20 USD) and informed that their bonus earnings could increase or decrease based on the selected gamble outcomes. They were kept unaware of the outcomes of each gamble during the task to prevent feedback from influencing subsequent choices. In the “Other” condition, they were told to imagine that someone else’s payment would be determined by their decision on the selected trial, and likewise, to imagine that another person’s decision would determine the outcome of one of their own trials. Participants never met the individuals for whom they made decisions (to avoid personal familiarity effects), and all decisions for others were made anonymously. These instructions emphasized the responsibility in other-regarding decisions while maintaining the hypothetical nature of the other person. The task lasted roughly 15–20 min per block. Performance-based compensation was computed after task completion, and all participants received at least a base compensation with the possibility of earning more based on their gamble outcomes.

Lesion confirmation and lesion overlap mapping

All patients had documented frontal lobe lesions confirmed via clinical neuroimaging or clinical reports. A lesion overlap map was constructed from digital neuroimaging data available for 14 of the 20 patients and registered to MNI standard space (Figure 5); the remaining 6 patients had their lesion locations confirmed via clinical reports but could not be included in the spatial lesion overlap map. Lesion masks were prepared and registered to MNI standard space using standard neuroimaging software, including MRIcron, ANTs, and SPM12, as appropriate for the available imaging data. The overlap map revealed a heterogeneous lesion distribution across ventral and medial frontal regions, with the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex largely spared (Figure 5). Areas of maximal overlap (25%; 3–4 of 14 patients) were limited to small clusters in lateral ventral frontal cortex, consistent with lateral orbitofrontal cortex and the pars orbitalis of the inferior frontal gyrus. Some patients also had dorsal frontal involvement. The overlap map was used descriptively to visualize voxel-wise lesion overlap across the available neuroimaging data and does not constitute a voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Model-free behavioral analysis

We first analyzed the risk-taking behavior descriptively by computing the acceptance rate of gambles across different conditions. For each participant, we sorted trials by the gamble’s expected value (EV) and grouped them into deciles (10 bins from most negative to most positive EV) to examine how often participants accepted gambles with negative versus positive expected value. This provided an overall behavioral risk profile, visualized as group-level acceptance curves for each decision context (Figure 2). We statistically tested for differences in mean acceptance rates using both within-subject (Self vs. Other) and between-group (Patient vs. Control) comparisons. Paired t-tests were used to compare each group’s self vs. other acceptance rates (with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons), and two-sample t-tests were used to compare patients vs. controls. We paid particular attention to differences in the negative-EV range, where rejecting is optimal, versus the positive-EV range, where accepting is optimal, to precisely characterize how groups differed in their choices on disadvantageous versus advantageous gambles. The EV-binned acceptance curves presented in Figure 2 serve as descriptive visualizations of the behavioral data; no inferential statistics are derived from the binned values. All inferential analyses are based on trial-level computational modeling, in which the specific gain magnitude, loss magnitude, and win probability of each gamble enter as separate inputs to the Prospect Theory value and weighting functions, thereby avoiding the confound of aggregating across gamble configurations with different component structures but similar expected values.

Computational modeling

To formally quantify individual risk preferences and the underlying cognitive mechanisms, we applied a computational model grounded in Prospect Theory to each participant’s trial-by-trial choice data. We evaluated four nested models of increasing complexity. In the most general model (M1, Full Prospect Theory), the subjective value of a mixed gamble with gain amount g, loss amount l, win probability p, and loss probability (1 − p) is: V = w(p)·gα-λ·w(1-p)·|l|β, where α and β are utility curvature parameters for gains and losses respectively (values <1 indicate diminishing sensitivity), λ is the loss aversion parameter (λ > 1 implies losses loom larger than gains), and w(·) is the Prelec (1998)22 probability weighting function: w(p) = exp⁡(-(-lnp)γ), where γ governs the degree of nonlinear distortion (γ < 1 produces the classic S-shaped overweighting of small probabilities and underweighting of large probabilities; γ = 1 corresponds to linear, undistorted weighting). The probability of accepting the gamble is determined by a softmax function: P(accept) = 1/(1+exp⁡(-θV)), where θ is the inverse temperature governing choice stochasticity. Three simpler nested models were derived by successively constraining parameters: M2 (Single-Curvature Prospect Theory; k = 4) constrains β = α, using a single curvature parameter for both gains and losses; M3 (No Loss Aversion; k = 3) additionally fixes λ = 1; and M4 (Expected Utility; k = 2) further fixes γ = 1, eliminating probability weighting. All four models were fitted separately for each participant in each condition (Self and Other) by maximizing the log likelihood of the trial-by-trial choices via L-BFGS-B optimization with parameter bounds (α, β: [0.01, 2.0]; λ: [0.01, 5.0]; γ: [0.01, 2.0]; θ: [0.01, 20.0]) and 20 random starting points to mitigate local minima. Models were compared using AIC and BIC.20 The winning model was validated through parameter recovery analysis, described in Methods S1 and shown in Figure S1. After model selection, we performed mixed-design ANOVAs (Group × Decision Context) and post-hoc Welch t-tests on each parameter of the winning model to test for group differences and context effects. To control the familywise error rate across the four tested parameters, post-hoc between-group comparisons were evaluated against a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of α = 0.0125 (0.05/4). Omnibus mixed-design ANOVAs were evaluated at the conventional α = 0.05 level. Additional validation and sensitivity analyses, including parameter recovery, bootstrap confidence intervals, exclusion-threshold sensitivity, transition-count permutation analysis, and counterfactual parameter substitution, are reported in the Results, Methods S1, Figure S1, Tables S1–S3, and the deposited data repository where applicable.

Figure-level statistical reporting

Where statistical significance is indicated in figures, the corresponding figure legend defines the statistical test, number of biological units, degrees of freedom, exact p values, and effect sizes. In Figure 4, significance brackets indicate the main effect of Group from mixed-design ANOVAs with Group as a between-subject factor and Decision Context as a within-subject factor. EV-binned curves in Figure 2 are descriptive visualizations; no inferential statistics were computed on the binned values.

Software

The risky choice task was programmed in MATLAB R2023b (MathWorks) using Psychtoolbox-3. Computational modeling, parameter fitting, statistical analyses, and figure generation were implemented using MATLAB R2023b and Python 3.10.x with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib, and seaborn as applicable. Final Python model fitting used maximum-likelihood estimation with scipy.optimize.minimize(method = “L-BFGS-B”). Lesion overlap mapping used standard neuroimaging software, including MRIcron, ANTs, and SPM12. Custom Prospect Theory model-fitting and analysis scripts are deposited at the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/CX73N).
