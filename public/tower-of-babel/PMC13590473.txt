Tasimelteon inhibits glioma proliferation by inducing ferroptosis via the SP1/ACSL4 pathway
Authors: Yi Chen, Jicheng Li, Huilan Yang, Haiyang Liang, Ji Shi, Quan Li, Qiang Liu, Jia Liu, Xuanming Zhang, Huizhe Xu, Haozhe Piao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGliomas are aggressive tumors with poor treatment outcomes. Tasimelteon, an FDA-approved melatonin receptor agonist with favorable blood-brain barrier penetration, has unexplored anti-tumor potential. This study investigated whether Tasimelteon inhibits glioma proliferation through ferroptosis induction.MethodsCell viability, colony formation, and immunofluorescence were employed to assess the effects of Tasimelteon on glioma cells. Ferroptosis was assessed by measuring lipid peroxidation markers (MDA, 4-HNE, BODIPY 581/591 C11) and ROS levels, and mitochondrial damage was detected by TEM. Mechanisms were examined through Western blotting, qPCR, dual-luciferase reporter assays, site-directed mutagenesis and molecular docking. In vivo, LN229-Luc glioma cells were orthotopically injected into mouse brains, followed by Tasimelteon treatment. Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging, and body weight and survival were recorded. Systemic toxicity was evaluated through H&E staining of major organs.ResultsTasimelteon decreased glioma cell viability and colony formation in dose- and time-dependent manners. Mechanistically, Tasimelteon induced ferroptosis, characterized by elevated lipid peroxidation markers (MDA, 4-HNE) and ROS levels, along with mitochondrial damage observed by TEM. This process was accompanied by ACSL4 upregulation. Further investigation revealed that Tasimelteon bound to SP1 at Gln465 and Leu467 residues, stabilizing the protein and enhancing ACSL4 transcription, independent of MT1/MT2 receptor activation. In orthotopic glioma models, Tasimelteon suppressed tumor growth by inducing ferroptosis and prolonged survival without systemic toxicity.ConclusionTasimelteon inhibits glioma growth by inducing ferroptosis via the SP1/ACSL4 pathway, offering a novel therapeutic approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01244-z.Keywords: Tasimelteon, Glioma, Ferroptosis, Lipid peroxidation, ACSL4, SP1

Introduction

Gliomas, originating from neuroglial and neural precursor cells, are the most common primary intracranial tumors [1]. Their annual incidence has risen over the past 30 years [2], with a global rate of approximately 6 per 100,000 people [3]. According to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, gliomas are now classified by integrating histological features with key molecular markers such as IDH mutation and 1p/19q codeletion, highlighting their substantial molecular heterogeneity [4]. Despite treatment advances, the median survival for high-grade gliomas remains low due to complex biology, molecular heterogeneity, and poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration [5]. Although emerging modalities such as immunotherapy [6] and nanomedicine-based drug delivery like engineered RAP-anchored copper-escorting liposomes [7] have been explored in glioblastoma, overall prognosis remains discouraging with a 5-year survival rate below 10% [8].

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine), secreted by the pineal gland, regulates circadian rhythms and diverse physiological functions [9, 10]. Beyond its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory roles, melatonin exhibits tumor-suppressive effects in various cancers, including glioblastoma [11, 12], through diverse mechanisms involving antioxidative, antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, and antiangiogenic actions [13]. However, melatonin’s clinical utility is limited by short half-life (~ 30 min), low blood-brain barrier permeability, and potential endocrine interference [14]. Tasimelteon as a melatonin receptor agonist exhibits higher MT2 selectivity, superior blood-brain barrier permeability, a longer half-life, and minimal pituitary impact on pituitary function compared to melatonin [15–17]. While explored for sleep and neuropsychiatric disorders, its anti-tumor potential remains largely unknown.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death characterized by lipid peroxide accumulation [18, 19], involving mitochondrial shrinkage and increased membrane density [20]. The mechanism is closely linked to iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant systems [21], notably the SLC7A11-GPX4 axis [22–24]. Cancer cells are particularly susceptible to ferroptosis due to their high iron demands [25]. Moreover, the brain’s high concentration of polyunsaturated fatty acids makes gliomas especially sensitive to ferroptosis [26]. While molecules like Erastin and sorafenib induce ferroptosis in brain tumors, triggering this pathway remains a promising research frontier.

This study demonstrates, for the first time, that Tasimelteon inhibits glioma proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, Tasimelteon induces ferroptosis via ACSL4 upregulation, driven by the enhanced binding of transcription factor SP1 to the ACSL4 promoter independently of MT1/MT2 receptor activation. Our findings provide a novel therapeutic perspective for glioma treatment.

Methods

Cells, plasmids, reagents and antibodies

Human glioblastoma LN229, A172 and human microglial HMC cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). LN229 cells harbor TP53 mutation, wild-type PTEN, IDH-wildtype status and homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion; A172 cells retain wild-type TP53 but exhibit PTEN loss/mutation, IDH-wildtype status, and high-molecular-weight EGFR expression [27] with mesenchymal features according to CCLE (https://depmap.org/portal/ccle/) and COSMIC (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic/) database. The patient-derived glioblastoma cells GBM1492 were a gift from Dr. Zhiyou Fang, Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences. SP1 and ACSL4 cDNA were obtained from Miaoling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Mutations of SP1 with Flag tag were constructed by standard molecular cloning procedures. siControl and siACSL4 were designed by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). GoldenTran®-R (Golden Trans Technology, Changchun, China) was used for transfection.

Several chemical inhibitors were employed in this study, including 4-P-PDOT (MCE, HY-100609), BAPTA-AM (MCE, HY-100545), BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Dojindo, L267), Chloroquine (Selleck, S6999), Doxorubicin (Selleck, S1208), Erastin (MCE, HY-15763), Ferrostatin-1 (Selleck, S7243), Liproxstatin-1 (Selleck, S7699), Mdivi-1 (Selleck, S7162), Melatonin (MCE, HY-B0075), Necrostatin-1 (Selleck, S8037), S26131 (MCE, HY-122136), Tasimelteon (Selleck, S4281), Z-VAD-FMK (Selleck, S7023). The following kits and reagents were used for various assays: cAMP ELISA KIT (Elabscience, E-EL-0056), CCK-8 kit (MCE, HY-K0301-3000T), Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK085), SYTOX Green Nucleic Acid Stain (Invitrogen, S7020), and BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012).

For immunoblotting and immunofluorescence, the following antibodies were used: TFRC (Invitrogen, 13-6800), ACSL4 (Abcam, ab155282), DMT1 (Proteintech, 20507-1-AP), FTH1 (CST, 4393 S), NCOA4 (Abcam, ab62495), NRF2 (Proteintech, 16396-1-AP), FSP1 (Proteintech, 20886-1-AP), DHODH (Proteintech, 14877-1-AP), GPX4 (Abcam, ab125066), SLC7A11 (CST, 12691 S), GAPDH (Huabio, ET1601-4), SP1 (Santa Cruz, SC-420), A20 (Santa Cruz, SC-166692), YAP (Novus, NB110-58358), SHP2 (Santa Cruz, SC-7384), Rabbit IgG-HRP (Huabio, HA1001), Mouse IgG-HRP (Huabio, HA1006), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, A-11034), 4-HNE (Abcam, ab46545) and Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580).

Cell viability and colony formation assay

Cell viability was determined by a CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The IC50 value was calculated via nonlinear regression fitting. For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. The colonies were fixed, stained and counted.

SYTOX green nucleic acid staining and immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at 40% confluence and treated with DMSO, 100 µM Tasimelteon, or 200 µM Tasimelteon for 48 h. After washing with HBSS, cells were incubated with SYTOX Green (167 nM) for 30 min in the dark. Bright-field and fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. For immunofluorescence, cells on coverslips in a 12-well plate were treated with the same condition, fixed, permeabilized and incubated with anti-Ki67 or 4-HNE primary antibodies and fluorescent secondary antibodies as described previously [28]. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining

Cells in a 20-mm confocal dish were treated with DMSO, 200 µM Tasimelteon with/without 4 µM Fer-1. BODIPY 581/591 C11 was dissolved in 20 µl DMSO.

Prior to staining, cells were first washed twice with HBSS, followed by incubation with the probe diluted 1:1000 in HBSS at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with HBSS, replenished with pre-warmed HBSS, and immediately subjected to live-cell imaging. Live-cell imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM 900 with Airyscan 2). The oxidized form of BODIPY 581/591 C11 was detected in the green channel (Ex: 488 nm; Em: 510–550 nm), and the non-oxidized form was detected in the red channel (Ex: 561 nm; Em: 600–630 nm). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (Beyotime, C1027) and detected in the DAPI channel.

Apoptosis detection

Cells in a 10-cm dish were treated with DMSO, 100 µM or 200 µM Tasimelteon, or 10 µM Doxorubicin for 48 h, then harvested and resuspended in binding buffer, stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 min. Apoptotic cells were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h.

cAMP assay and intracellular calcium detection

LN229 and A172 cells were pretreated with MT1 receptor antagonist S26131 or MT2 receptor antagonist 4-P-PDOT at 0, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 µM for 2 h, and co-cultured with 40 µM melatonin for 48 h. cAMP concentration was measured by ELISA while Ca2+ level was reflected by fluorescence intensity of Fluo-4 AM measured by flow cytometry.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were washed and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for at least 2 h. Cells were then collected by centrifugation (≤ 3000 rpm, 2 min) and post-fixed with fresh electron microscopy fixative in the dark for 30 min. Samples were rinsed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (3 min each), followed by dehydration, embedding, and polymerization. Ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then examined using a JEOL JEM-1230 TEM at 80 kV.

Quantification of ROS and MDA levels

Cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) in serum-free medium for 30 min. After washing, fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry. For MDA assay, cells were lysed, and absorbance was measured to quantify MDA level according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN). Cells in 6 cm dishes were lysed with 500 µL Trizol per dish. After adding chloroform and shaking, samples were centrifuged to separate phases. The aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol at -80℃. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, dried, dissolved in RNase-free water, and quantified spectrophotometrically. Then, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R323). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711) on a real-time PCR system. Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 1 µL of cDNA template, and 8.2 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences were as follows: ACSL4 (forward: AAGTAGACCAAGCCTTCAG, reverse: GTCCCAGTCCAGGTATTTTTC), SP1 (forward: AGGCTGTGGAAAGTTGTATG, reverse: TGTACGTTTGGGCTCTGTGA), and GAPDH (forward: AATCCCATCACCATCTTCCA, reverse: AAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCC). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal control.

Luciferase assay

The firefly luciferase reporter plasmid was generated by inserting the SP1 binding sequence (− GGGGCGGGCG−) upstream of the promoter region in the pGL3-Basic vector was co-transfected with SP1 overexpression plasmid, and the Renilla luciferase plasmid pRL-TK into LN229 and A172 cells. After 24 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System, and firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase.

In vivo animal experiment

LN229-Luc cells were intracranially injected into the right striatum of nude mice to establish orthotopic glioma models. Mice were randomly assigned to receive PBS or Tasimelteon (50 or 100 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection every three days for 21 days. Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging, and body weight and general health were recorded to assess treatment tolerance. At the end of treatment, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

Western blotting, histological and immunohistochemistry analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and the lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS – PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Thermo, 32106). All the protein samples were processed for immunoblotting analysis as previously described [29].

On the 21st day after tumor inoculation, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys and brains of the nude mice were collected, fixed, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Immunohistochemistry and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were performed to evaluate drug efficacy and toxicity.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0. Two groups were assessed via t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Results

Tasimelteon Inhibits the Proliferation of Glioma Cells

To explore whether Tasimelteon has an inhibitory effect on gliomas, cell viability was detected through the CCK-8 assay. In glioma cell lines LN229, A172 and GBM1492 primary glioma cells, cell viability gradually decreased with increasing doses of Tasimelteon. Furthermore, as treatment duration extended, a progressive decline in cell viability was noted (Fig. 1A-C), indicating that Tasimelteon significantly inhibits glioma cell viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Notably, Tasimelteon’s effect was more profound in glioma cell lines than HMC, hinting at a potential preferential inhibition of tumor cells over normal glial cells (Fig. 1D).

To clarify whether Tasimelteon inhibits glioma cells proliferation, colony formation and Ki67 staining assays were employed. Tasimelteon treatment significantly reduced the number of colonies of A172 and LN229 cells, with higher concentrations producing more pronounced inhibition; In contrast, treatment with Tasimelteon did not significantly alter the colony formation ability of HMC cells. (Fig. 1E and F). Furthermore, treatment with 200 µM Tasimelteon significantly inhibited the Ki67 positive staining rate of LN229 and A172 cells, indicating that Tasimelteon suppressed the proliferative capacity of glioma cells (Fig. 1G-I).

Tasimelteon induces ferroptosis in glioma cells

To ascertain whether Tasimelteon induces cell death, we utilized immunofluorescence to examine nucleic acid staining. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, compared to the DMSO-treated control, the positive staining rate significantly increased in LN229 and A172 cells treated with Tasimelteon for 48 h in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that Tasimelteon promotes glioma cell death. Next, flow cytometry was employed to detect apoptosis (with DOX as a positive control). Treatment of LN229 or A172 cells with 100 µM and 200 µM Tasimelteon did not significantly increase the number of cells in early apoptosis, late apoptosis, or death phases, indicating that Tasimelteon did not induce apoptosis (Fig. 2C and D).

(L, M) The fluorescence intensities of the reduced and oxidized forms of the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe were detected using a confocal microscopy in GBM cells treated with Tasimelteon alone or in combination with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 for 48 h. We then pretreated glioma cells with multiple inhibitors, including ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 (4 µM) and Lip-1 (0.1 µM), necrosis inhibitor Nec-1 (10 µM), apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD (25 µM), autophagy inhibitor CQ-1 (10 µM), and mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 (20 µM). As displayed in Fig. 2E and F, pretreatment with ferroptosis inhibitors weakened the inhibitory effect of Tasimelteon, with Lip-1 showing a particularly significant attenuating effect. In contrast, cell viability remained stable following pretreatment with other inhibitors. These results indicate that ferroptosis, especially excessive lipid peroxidation, is likely a pathway for glioma death caused by Tasimelteon, as Fer-1 prevents membrane lipid damage, while Lip-1 mainly inhibits mitochondrial lipid peroxidation.

Furthermore, DCFH-DA, a ROS probe, showed that Tasimelteon obviously increased intracellular ROS accumulation (Fig. 2G and H). Consistently, the levels of MDA and 4-HNE, two well-established markers of lipid peroxidation, were also markedly elevated in Tasimelteon-treated cells compared to the control group (Fig. 2I and K). To further validate lipid peroxidation in Tasimelteon-treated GBM cells, we employed the lipid hydroperoxide probe BODIPY 581/591 C11. Confocal microscopy revealed a significant increase in green fluorescence intensity in Tasimelteon-treated cells, which was markedly reversed by co-treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, confirming that Tasimelteon promotes ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation (Fig. 2L and M). The results of TEM revealed reduced mitochondrial size and a decreased number of mitochondrial cristae in Tasimelteon-treated cells — characteristic morphological hallmarks of ferroptosis, which were comparable to those observed in the Erastin-treated positive control group (Fig. 2N). Altogether, these data demonstrate that Tasimelteon induces ferroptosis by increasing lipid peroxidation in glioma cells.

ACSL4 participates in the ferroptosis process triggered by Tasimelteon

To investigate the mechanism by which Tasimelteon induces ferroptosis, we examined protein levels related to lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and the antioxidant system in LN229 and A172 cells. Following Tasimelteon treatment, ACSL4 expression showed a remarkable increase in both cell lines (Fig. 3A), while other proteins displayed no significant changes or consistent trends, suggesting that Tasimelteon may have differential regulatory effects on iron metabolism and antioxidant responses across different cell lines. Further experiments showed that the upregulation of ACSL4 by Tasimelteon was both dose-dependent and stable over time (24 h, 48 h, and 72 h; Fig. 3B).

To further elucidate the role of ACSL4 in Tasimelteon-induced ferroptosis, we conducted knockdown experiments using two different siRNAs. While Tasimelteon significantly upregulated ACSL4 in the control groups, ACSL4 knockdown partially impaired the upregulation (Fig. 3C). Crucially, ACSL4 knockdown significantly rescued the decrease in cell viability and colony formation in LN229 and A172 cells exposed to Tasimelteon (Fig. 3D and E). Additionally, cellular MDA and ROS levels were dramatically reduced upon ACSL4 knockdown (Fig. 3F and G). These findings collectively indicate that ACSL4 plays a pivotal role in the Tasimelteon-mediated ferroptosis of glioma cells.

SP1 binding to ACSL4 promoter dominates ferroptosis behavior in glioma cells by Tasimelteon

To explore how Tasimelteon regulates ACSL4, we first determined mRNA levels in treated glioma cells, observing a gradient increase in ACSL4 at the transcriptional level (Fig. 4A). Potential factors (SP1, STAT1, ETS1, RELA) were screened from transcription factor databases (Fig. 4B). Given that ACSL4 can be regulated by several factors such as SP1, A20, SHP2, and YAP [30], we examined their protein levels in LN229 and A172 cells exposed to Tasimelteon. SP1 expression was significantly upregulated, while others showed no consistent trends (Fig. 4C). The JASPAR database predicted that SP1 potentially binds to the ACSL4 promoter (Fig. 4D). To verify this, we constructed a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid (Fluc-insert) by inserting the binding site -GGGGCGGGCG- before the promoter region. Dual-luciferase assays showed that relative fluorescence was significantly higher in Fluc-insert transfected cells, and Tasimelteon treatment further amplified this luciferase activity (Fig. 4E), confirming that SP1 binds to the ACSL4 promoter and that Tasimelteon enhances this binding capability.

To reinforce these findings, we compared the effects of wild-type ACSL4 (ACSL4-WT) and a mutant lacking SP1 binding capability (ACSL4-Mut). ACSL4-Mut significantly lessened the upregulation of ACSL4 by Tasimelteon, further elucidating that Tasimelteon activates ACSL4 transcription by promoting SP1 binding (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, while overexpression of ACSL4-WT reduced colony number and size—an effect further enhanced by Tasimelteon—the ACSL4-Mut failed to exert a similar inhibitory effect (Fig. 4G). Similarly, MDA and ROS levels were elevated in ACSL4-WT overexpressed cells, whereas ACSL4-Mut showed no significant increase (Fig. 4H and I). Overall, these results underscore the critical role of SP1 binding to the ACSL4 promoter in mediating Tasimelteon’s effects, including the suppression of proliferation and induction of lipid peroxidation.

Gln 465 and Leu 467 are crucial for the binding of tasimelteon to SP1

Interestingly, Tasimelteon did not significantly alter SP1 mRNA levels (Fig. 5A), but instead stabilized SP1 protein expression in both LN229 and A172 cells (Fig. 5B). A molecular docking model showed that Tasimelteon could combine with SP1 amino acid residues through hydrogen bonding, with binding energy of -6.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 5C), revealing that Tasimelteon binds to glutamine 465 (Q465) and leucine 467 (L467) of SP1 to stabilize its protein structure and expression.

To test this hypothesis, Flag-tagged point mutations (SP1-Q465N, SP1-L467V) and a double mutation (SP1-Q465N/L467V) were constructed. Upon Tasimelteon treatment, cells transfected with these mutations showed a distinct decrease in SP1 protein stability compared to cells transfected with SP1-wildtype (WT) (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, these three mutations partially inhibited the Tasimelteon-induced upregulation of ACSL4 (Fig. 5E). Correspondingly, lipid peroxidation and ROS levels were suppressed in the mutant groups compared to SP1-WT-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5F and G). Finally, SP1 mutants overexpression markedly weakened the inhibitory effect of Tasimelteon on cell proliferative capacity (Fig. 5H). These results confirm that Gln 465 and Leu 467 of SP1 are crucial for the binding of Tasimelteon and the subsequent induction of ferroptosis-related behaviors in glioma cells.

Tasimelteon triggers ferroptosis in glioma cells without requiring MT1/MT2 activation

Next, we wondered if Tasimelteon-triggered ferroptosis was associated with its receptor’s activation. Activation of MT1 mainly decreases intracellular cAMP levels, whereas MT2 activation causes calcium influx [31]. To determine the dosage of agonists, 40 µM Melatonin-treated LN229 and A172 cells were exposed to inhibitor of MT1-S26131 or MT2-4-P-PDOT with different concentration gradients to detect intracellular cAMP or Ca2+ concentration, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). As shown in Fig. 6C and D, Tasimelteon significantly reduced cAMP content and increased Ca2+ concentration in the two glioma cell lines. Given that Ca2+ influx may cause calcium overload and subsequently induce oxidative stress, we next examined whether the increase in ROS induced by Tasimelteon was Ca2+-dependent. After treatment with the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM (10 µM), ROS levels in 200 µM Tasimelteon-treated glioma cells were not significantly reduced (Figure S1A and S1B), indicating that the oxidative stress triggered by Tasimelteon is likely independent of Ca2+ influx [32]. This phenomenon was reversed with 10 µM S26131, 10 µM 4-P-PDOT and both pretreatment for 2 h, indicating that 200 µM Tasimelteon activated MT1 and MT2 in glioma cells. However, the same treatment did not cause protein level increase in SP1 or ACSL4 in Tasimelteon-treated cells (Fig. 6E). Moreover, the cellular MDA in LN229 or A172 cells exposure to Tasimelteon displayed almost no difference between groups with or without the two inhibitors treatment (Fig. 6F). Similar results were observed in ROS detection (Fig. 6G). Additionally, S26131, 4-P-PDOT, or even a combination of the two failed to rescue the Tasimelteon-induced growth inhibition (Fig. 6H). Taken together, our data suggest that MT1/MT2 was not involved in Tasimelteon-induced ferroptosis.

Tasimelteon suppresses tumor growth in vivo

An orthotopic glioma-bearing nude mice model was established with LN229-Luc cells. Mice were randomly divided into 3 groups and treated with PBS or Tasimelteon at 50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneal injection over 21 days. Tasimelteon significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the control group, with the higher dose (100 mg/kg) showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 7A). H&E staining confirmed substantial reduction in tumor size in the Tasimelteon-treated groups compared to the PBS control (Fig. 7B). In addition, survival curves revealed that Tasimelteon treatment prolonged the lifespan of the glioma-bearing mice, with a more prominent extension observed at the 100 mg/kg dose (Fig. 7C). Weight tracking indicated that Tasimelteon treatment obviously delayed weight loss associated with tumor burden, suggesting a protective effect on the overall health of the mice (Fig. 7D). Noteworthy, Tasimelteon did not cause observable damage to vital organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) from the treated mice, as shown by H&E staining (Fig. 7E). Together, these results suggested that Tasimelteon not only effectively inhibits glioma growth in vivo but also improves survival without causing toxicity.

Tasimelteon causes ferroptosis in glioma-bearing mice

Immunohistochemistry analysis showed that Tasimelteon treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in Ki67 expression, while simultaneously increasing the expression of ACSL4 and SP1 (Fig. 8A-D). Tasimelteon significantly elevated SP1 and ACSL4 levels in tumor tissues from treated mice, supporting the immunohistochemistry findings (Fig. 8E-G). Moreover, biochemical assays revealed that increased MDA and ROS levels in the tumor tissues, both of which are hallmarks of ferroptosis (Fig. 8H and I). Finally, TEM images showed structural changes in the mitochondria of tumor tissue from Tasimelteon-treated mice, including reduced cristae and other signs of mitochondrial damage (Fig. 8J).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive evidence that Tasimelteon, a clinically approved melatonin receptor agonist, exerts potent anti-glioma activity by inducing ferroptosis. Through both in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrate that Tasimelteon significantly inhibits glioma cell proliferation in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, Tasimelteon promotes ferroptotic cell death by enhancing lipid peroxidation and increasing intracellular ROS levels, accompanied by mitochondrial damage characteristic of ferroptosis. Importantly, we identify SP1 as a direct molecular target of Tasimelteon. Tasimelteon stabilizes SP1 protein by binding to specific residues (Gln465 and Leu467), thereby enhancing SP1’s transcriptional activation of ACSL4, a key driver of ferroptosis. These effects occur independently of MT1/MT2 receptor signaling, revealing a receptor-independent mechanism.

Previous studies have shown that melatonin exerts anti-tumor effects through antioxidant activity, modulation of calmodulin signaling, inhibition of angiogenesis, and estrogen receptor blockade [33–35]. However, its clinical application in oncology remains limited by blood-brain barrier permeability, short half-life, and endocrine side effects [36]. Tasimelteon was originally developed for sleep disorders, yet its anti-tumor potential was unexplored [14]. In our study, we demonstrate that Tasimelteon induces ferroptosis in glioma cells by activating the ACSL4 signaling pathway, a mechanism distinct from melatonin’s previously reported anti-proliferative pathways (Fig. 9). Notably, this occurs independently of MT1/MT2 receptor activation, highlighting a novel receptor-independent mechanism of action for melatonin receptor agonists in cancer therapy. Tasimelteon’s improved pharmacokinetics may overcome the translational limitations of melatonin in oncology [16].

Ferroptosis has gained considerable attention as a novel strategy in cancer therapy, particularly due to its distinct mechanism of inducing regulated cell death through iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, which bypasses many of the resistance pathways associated with conventional apoptosis-based treatments [37]. Compounds such as Erastin, RSL3, and FIN56, despite showing strong ferroptotic activity in vitro. However, these ferroptosis inducers are beset by critical pharmacological limitations: Erastin undergoes rapid metabolic degradation and exhibits poor aqueous solubility [38], while RSL3 and FIN56 display suboptimal pharmacokinetic profiles and dose-dependent cytotoxicity, factors that narrow their therapeutic windows and fundamentally limit their potential in combinatorial treatment strategies [39, 40]. Many of these agents have not progressed beyond the preclinical stage, and their application in brain tumors is further restricted by their inability to efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier [41], a critical challenge given the highly selective nature of this anatomical boundary.

In contrast, Tasimelteon is a clinically approved melatonin receptor agonist with an established safety profile validated through extensive clinical trials. Unlike melatonin, Tasimelteon exhibits several improved pharmacological properties, including a longer plasma half-life, enhanced oral absorption, greater receptor selectivity, and a markedly lower propensity for endocrine disruption. It has demonstrated reliable permeability of central nervous system, with the capacity to effectively penetrate both the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the blood-tumor barrier (BTB), making Tasimelteon a viable candidate for targeting brain tumors such as glioma [42, 43]. These advantages position Tasimelteon as a highly practical candidate for repurposing in glioma treatment, where effective, BBB-penetrant, and well-tolerated therapeutic options remain critically limited. Nevertheless, although our findings support the involvement of the SP1/ACSL4 axis in Tasimelteon-induced ferroptosis, the upstream regulatory events linking Tasimelteon treatment to oxidative stress and ferroptotic activation require further clarification. In addition, the broader applicability of this mechanism across different glioma contexts warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

This study is the first to demonstrate the inhibitory effects of Tasimelteon on glioma, offering a novel perspective on its therapeutic potential. Given the urgent need for effective therapies, this research may help overcome current clinical limitations.
