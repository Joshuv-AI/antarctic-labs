The proximal ulna dorsal angle (PUDA): a systematic review
Authors: Julian Okorocha, Fiona Ashton, Flavia Stettler, Joideep Phadnis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe proximal ulna dorsal angulation (PUDA) is an anatomical feature of the ulna pertinent to proximal ulna fracture reduction and implant design. This systematic review primarily aims to establish normative values for the PUDA through synthesis of the existing literature, with the secondary aim of evaluating methodological variability and identifying sources of heterogeneity across studies.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched between January 2000 and November 2024. Eligible studies reported quantitative measurements of PUDA in adults without pathology. Imaging modality, measurement technique, and risk of bias (assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) were extracted and analyzed. Weighted means were calculated and intergroup differences assessed using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey's honestly significant difference.ResultsTwenty-one studies were included, comprising over 2000 ulnae across 3 imaging modalities: computed tomography (CT) (n = 6), lateral radiographs (n = 7), and cadaveric (n = 8). The weighted mean PUDA was 5.8° ± 1.4°. Cadaveric studies reported significantly higher mean (7.8° ± 2.87°), compared to CT (5.2° ± 1.07°) and radiographic (5.1° ± 0.43°) (P < .001). The weighted mean tip-to-apex distance was 53.7 mm ± 17.6 (25.8% ± 5.4% of ulna length). Cadaveric studies reported a significantly higher mean (79.5 mm ± 16.9), compared to CT (49.1 mm ± 6.1) and radiographs (51.8 mm ± 3.5) (P < .001). Substantial variation was observed such as ulna landmark reference points and line measurement technique across modalities.ConclusionThis review identified significant methodological heterogeneity across modalities, with radiological methods demonstrating greater reproducibility. These findings underscore the importance for standardization of measurement methodology, clinical practice, and implant design.Keywords: Proximal ulna, Elbow anatomy, Morphology, Measurement, Angulation, Olecranon

The morphology of the proximal ulna plays a critical role in maintaining elbow joint stability. Failure to appreciate and accurately restore the anatomy of the proximal ulna following fracture can result in impaired elbow biomechanics causing joint instability, arthritis, and functional impairment.5,9,16,19

The morphology of the proximal ulna has been quantified extensively using a variety of parameters, each with its own clinical relevance. Among these, the proximal ulna dorsal angulation (PUDA) is one of the most frequently assessed. The PUDA is an angular measurement which quantifies the dorsal curvature of the proximal ulna.18 In addition, the linear distance from the tip of the olecranon to the apex of the PUDA is often referred to as the tip-to-apex (TTA) distance. The PUDA and TTA distance are intrinsically linked in both anatomical and clinical contexts, making it an important parameter in optimizing surgical precision and implant compatibility.14

The reported PUDA and TTA distance values demonstrate substantial variation across the published literature, reflecting not only the natural anatomical variations but also inconsistencies in how these parameters are defined and measured across studies. The absence of standardized methodology highlights the need for consensus to improve reliability across the literature. An accurate understanding and quantification of these anatomical parameters is essential for informing implant design and guiding the surgical plan to optimize clinical outcomes.

This is the first systematic review of the PUDA, aiming to establish normative values and ranges for the PUDA and TTA distance, evaluate methodological variability to identify the most reliable and clinically applicable measurement techniques, and provide recommendations for standardized measurement with specific reference to surgical planning, implant design, and biomechanical research.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines to identify studies which have undertaken measurements of the proximal ulnar dorsal angulation and tip-apex distance—quantified as either a discrete length or as a percentage of the total ulna length—in order to define the morphology of the proximal ulna.

An ethics application was submitted to the Brighton and Sussex Medical School Research Governance and Ethics Committee and approved without formal board review given the proposed study design. All cadaveric images have been taken under the auspices of the anatomy Human Tissue Authority License held by Brighton and Sussex Medical School with necessary permissions granted. The study was prospectively registered on the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42024610045.

Search strategy

A search of the literature was conducted across 3 electronic databases—MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane library—covering the period January 1, 2000–November 1, 2024. The search strategy combined the relevant keywords and Medical Subject Heading using Boolean operators, including terms relating to the proximal ulna (“Proximal Ulna” OR “Olecranon” OR “Coronoid” OR “Radial Notch” OR “Trochlear Notch” OR “Ulna Anatomy”) AND “Morphology” OR “Measurement” OR “Angulation” OR “Torsion.” Unpublished studies and gray literature were not included. Additional manual reference searching of included publications was performed to identify any additional articles for inclusion. Database searches were last performed on November 22, 2024. Results were imported into EndNote reference management software and de-duplicated prior to the screening process.

Study selection

Studies were screened against predefined criteria to ensure relevance. Inclusion criteria are as follows: study of a healthy ulna (no history of trauma, gross pathology, known congenital deformity, or skeletal dysplasia) in those aged 18 years or older; reporting of quantitative measurements of the PUDA. Variations in the terminology describing sagittal plane angulation of the proximal ulna and its position were encountered. Descriptions of the PUDA included anterior angle, maximum anterior angulation, anterior deviation, diaphysis-diaphysis angle, and dorsal apex curve of the ulna. Studies were published between January 1, 2000, and November 1, 2024, in English language or non-English language, which were amenable to translation via the online translation tool Google Translate. Letters to editors, case reports, conferences, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses were excluded.

Titles of all identified journal articles were initially screened for relevance. Abstracts of all remaining studies were then analyzed for reference to the PUDA. Studies with insufficient detail in the abstract proceeded to full-text screening. To minimize selection bias, the search, screening, and final selection were independently performed by 3 reviewers (J.O., F.A., and J.P.). Consensus from all reviewers was deemed necessary for inclusion.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was assessed using an adapted Newcastle-Ottawa scale tailored to anatomical morphological studies. It examines 3 domains: selection bias, comparability, and outcome. Studies with an overall score of 8-9 are considered low risk; scores of 6-7 are medium risk; scores of 5-4 are fair risk; and studies scoring <4 are considered to be at high risk of bias.

Data extraction

For each eligible study, the following information was recorded: authorship; year of publication; country of publication; sample size; and participant demographics, including mean patient age and gender. Methodological details that were extracted included study type (clinical or cadaveric), imaging modality, and measurement technique including the definition of reference landmarks and tangent line placement where reported. Measurements for PUDA—and when reported TTA—were extracted in degrees and millimeters or percentages, respectively. Data were recorded in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics have been used to summarize basic study characteristics, with weighted mean calculated to account for variations in sample size, and results presented with ranges to reflect the full spread of values observed across included studies. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to assess group differences. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 30.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Initial searches identified 1,374 records. Fifty-eight duplicated articles were removed, and further 493 articles were deemed ineligible based on selection criteria. The remaining 824 studies were subject to title and abstract screening. Fifty-seven articles reported on the PUDA/TTA, hence proceeded to full-text screening for eligibility. Twenty-one studies met the inclusion criteria, including 7 studies identified from manual reference searching. Terminology and grouping of measurements

Across the literature, various overlapping terms were used to describe the same parameters. To allow comparability, the following terms have been grouped as follows:

Proximal dorsal ulna angle: included terms such as “anterior angle,” “anterior deviation,” “diaphysis-diaphysis angle,” and “dorsal apex curvature,” all representing dorsal angulation of the ulna in the sagittal plane.

Tip-to-apex distance (TTA distance): included terms such as “PUDA apex distance” and “point of anterior angulation,” all representing distance from the proximal reference point on the olecranon to the PUDA apex.

Grouping these descriptors allowed statistical analysis of data and direct comparison across studies despite variations in terminology and measurement definition.

Discussion

This systematic review consolidates the PUDA and TTA distance values observed in the published literature and summarizes the methodologies in use. We have observed significant inconsistencies in the definitions, methods, and measurement techniques. These variations contribute to substantial heterogeneity in reported values, limiting the ability to compare findings across studies and complicating the establishment of a globally representative PUDA and TTA distance.

Terminologies

Terms such as “anterior angle,”23,25 “anterior deviation,”8 “diaphysis-diaphysis angle,”3 and “dorsal apex curvature”1 were often used interchangeably to describe the PUDA, despite referring to the same anatomical concept.

Across the literature, there were further similarities between the measurements of the TTA distance and the ulna sagittal bow,10,12,22,27 as both relate to a linear distance along the proximal ulna. However, values for the ulna bow were observed to refer to the full length of the natural curvature of the ulna in the sagittal plane, usually measured from a proximal point of the olecranon to the end of the bowed segment of the ulna, rather than the PUDA apex utilized for the TTA distance. The ulna bow values are observed to result in a much longer distance, or higher percentage of total ulna length. In determining study eligibility, we concluded that these terms represented 2 discreet concepts and did not refer to the same anatomical feature. Consequently, studies reporting solely on the sagittal bow were excluded from this review to ensure consistency and clarity in the analysis of PUDA and TTA distance.

Reference points and measurement technique

The chosen points of reference and placement of tangent lines varied notably between studies. All but 1 study1 made their assessment using a linear measurement technique. These linear measurement studies initially defined their chosen proximal reference points and established parameters for identifying the PUDA and its apex before measuring the TTA distance. This sequential approach highlights the intrinsic link between these 2 features of proximal ulna morphology.

The PUDA often manifests as a gradual curvature, rather than a specific point of angulation. Most studies identified the PUDA apex at the point of greatest “depth” of the dorsal curvature. In contrast, Totlis et al25 and Singh et al23 defined the PUDA apex as the “point of anterior angulation,” which appeared closer to the terminal aspect of the dorsal curvature, yielding significantly higher PUDA values (8.5° and 8.1°, respectively) compared to the mean of 5.8°.

The proximal reference points in the measurement of the TTA distance (Figs. 5 and 6) appeared more variable. The most significant observation was the lack of standardization for the “tip of the olecranon” (Fig. 5). Due to natural anatomical variation, the position of the posterior tip of the olecranon is influenced by the dorsal “hook” angle of the olecranon, which ranges from 95 to 115°7,14,25,26; hence, this location may occur more proximally or distally within the olecranon metaphysis. This natural variation is evident when comparing cadaveric ulna depicted in Figures 2 and 3. Consequently, the posterior tip of the olecranon may not serve as an objective reference point. This challenge likely contributed to the need for studies to establish their own objective reference points.

The cadaveric and 3D CT studies exhibited the most variability in their reference points. In cadaveric studies, Totlis et al25 and Singh et al23 used the MPPDS, and Erdem7 used the most posterior point of olecranon, which appeared to be the same reference point (point 2 in Fig. 5). Within the 3D CT scan studies, Yong et al29 and Jarvie et al13 reported using the tip of the olecranon; however, upon examination of the figures, the selected point appeared to be “the most anterior proximal point of the olecranon” (point 4 in Fig. 5). Wang et al26 reported using the tip of the olecranon, but their figures appear to be the anterior tip rather than the posterior tip of the olecranon (point 3 in Fig. 5). Puchwein et al14 employed the “edge point,” which appears comparable to the posterior tip of the olecranon as described in Rouleau et al18 (point 1 in Fig. 5). Grechenig et al8 reported using the tip of the triceps insertion which commonly inserts around the posterior olecranon; hence, this landmark is an even less precise for measurement purposes. All radiographic studies utilized the posterior tip of the olecranon (Fig. 5 [point 1] and Fig. 6A), yielding the lowest standard deviation within the studies.

For the PUDA measurement, the placement of tangent line A parallel to the flat spot6 of the olecranon was consistent across linear measurement studies (Figures 2 and 3). However, the orientation of line A was dependent on the selected proximal reference point. Radiographic studies consistently used the “posterior” tip of the olecranon (Fig. 3). However, 2 cadaveric studies, Totlis et al25 and Singh et al23 used the MPPDS (Fig. 4), which appeared to be a more objective landmark (Fig. 6). This anatomical distinction influenced the PUDA angular measurement as observed in Figure 4.

The placement of tangent Line B for the PUDA measurement was based on 2 techniques as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. When combined with the variable reference landmarks, measuring the PUDA along the posterior border generally produced larger results with higher standard deviation. Similarly, these differences in technique impacted the TTA distance measurement, as the length parallel to the flat spot generally produced longer distances compared to the adjacent length parallel to the long axis (illustrated in Fig. 6, A and B). These technical differences were not always clear in the figures provided within the studies.

Formal reliability assessment was notably underreported, with only 30% of included studies providing quantitative reproducibility data. Where reported, values ranged from moderate (ICC, 0.762)26 to excellent (ICC, 0.934),25 suggesting acceptable measurement reliability in those studies. However, confidence intervals were not consistently reported alongside ICC values, precluding synthesis. These limitations substantially restrict the ability to draw conclusions regarding the reproducibility of PUDA and TTA measurements across the literature.

Influence of modality on observed results

This systematic review demonstrates a weighted mean PUDA value of 5.8 ± 1.4° and a TTA distance of 53.7 mm ± 17.6 mm (or 25.8% ± 5.4% of the ulna length). Notably, the imaging modality appears to significantly influence the absolute values and associated ranges for both parameters, as evidenced by the data presented in Tables IV and VI. The measurements observed in cadaveric studies demonstrated higher PUDA mean values and ranges, with greater standard deviations compared to radiological studies, a difference that was statistically significant. This trend was more pronounced in the measurements of TTA distance, with significant differences observed between all observed modalities.

The variable measurement techniques and selection of reference points within the cadaveric studies are particularly significant within these results. While direct measurement offers real-time assessment and tactile feedback, it is susceptible to imprecision, inconsistent landmark identification, and interference from surface irregularities. Additionally, maintaining a neutral rotational position in cadaveric specimens during measurement is a significant challenge. Specific cadaveric studies3,30 utilized the long axis during their measurement; however, the inability to calculate the centroid introduces error when positioning line B parallel to the long axis. Furthermore, intraobserver and interobserver reliability was only formally assessed in one cadaveric one study,25 limiting evaluation of measurement consistency across the cadaveric studies.

The technique and reference points were more consistent in the radiological studies, particularly within the radiographic studies. However, radiographs are limited to two-dimensional (2D) imaging, preventing evaluation of 3D angulation or torsion. Furthermore, many radiographic studies focused primarily on the proximal third of the ulna, limiting the view of the full curvature, and some studies suffered from poor image resolution or projection errors.

3D CT studies leverage advanced computational tools to determine the centroid of the ulna to establish its longitudinal axis. This accounts for the overall alignment of the ulna, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the ulna 3D geometry without localized irregularities in the posterior border. 3D CT studies identified their reference points using automated mathematical calculations1,13,29 or preselected objective reference points,11,14,26 minimizing measurement variability. While the definition of reference points remains observer-dependent, once established, these points were consistently applied across all ulnae, ensuring greater measurement reproducibility compared to X-ray and cadaveric studies. However, greater cost, limited accessibility in lower-resource settings, and ionizing radiation exposure restrict its routine clinical application, and the added complexity of computational workflows may limit reproducibility outside specialist centers.

Posterior angulation of the ulna

In reviewing the published literature that explores proximal ulna morphology, it becomes clear that dorsal angulation within the proximal ulna cannot be universally assumed and that posterior angulation represents a recognized variant within the normal anatomical spectrum. Grechenig et al8 reported posterior angulation in up to 30% of ulnae in their cadaveric study, while radiographic studies by Rouleau et al18 (4%), Savakkanavar20 (8%), and Wang et al26 (2%) documented lower frequencies of this variant. This finding is further supported by the presence of negative PUDA values in studies such as those by Sağlam et al21 and Rouleau et al.18 It is plausible that studies reporting low PUDA values (eg, 1° or 0°) may have included cases with apex posterior angulation. These findings observed in multiple studies exploring PUDA suggest that posterior angulation represents a less common anatomical variant of a healthy ulnae, rather than a pathological feature. This phenomenon is also well-documented in sagittal bow studies.12,27 These findings underscore the importance of accounting for this morphological variant in both surgical planning and implant design.

Recommendations for future studies and clinical practice

This review has demonstrated that methodological differences in anatomical landmarks and measurement techniques can substantially influence our understanding of normative values for PUDA and TTA distance, with significant clinical implications. In order to standardize terminology, we recommend the term PUDA be the definitive term to describe the sagittal plane angulation of the proximal ulna. Similarly, the TTA should be the term used to define the measurement of the PUDA apex.

We recommend simplification of measurement techniques and reference points. The PUDA should be measured using 2 tangent lines: tangent Line A placed parallel to the flat spot of the olecranon, and tangent Line B placed parallel to the long axis of the ulna (Fig. 2), with the PUDA apex defined at their intersection. A consistent and objective proximal anatomical landmark should be adopted across; we recommend the most proximal anterior point of the olecranon (point 4, Fig. 5). The TTA distance should be measured along the long axis of the ulna as described in Fig. 6D, quantified primarily as a length in millimeters, and calculated secondarily as a percentage of total ulna length to account for individual anatomical variability. Where possible, digital image analysis software should be employed in preference to manual measurement tools such as calipers and goniometers, and 3-dimensional CT imaging with centroid-based computational analysis considered where resources permit.

Future studies should report quantitative reliability measures, including ICC with accompanying confidence intervals, to facilitate synthesis and enable meaningful comparison of measurement reproducibility across the literature.

Radiological studies reported the lowest standard deviations across the literature, with no significant difference in mean PUDA between CT and radiographic studies, although a significant difference was observed for TTA distance. Radiological methods therefore demonstrate greater reproducibility compared to cadaveric studies, which is reassuring for clinical practice as it confirms that both radiographic and CT measurements represent reliable and applicable modalities for quantifying proximal ulna morphology.

The strength of this review lies in its systematic comparison of highly heterogeneous methodologies across the literature, culminating in the proposal of a consensus-based approach for standardized measurement techniques. A comprehensive search strategy was implemented, incorporating diverse studies that may have been overlooked in prior research. This inclusive approach enhances the generalizability of the findings, ensuring a more robust and representative synthesis of the available evidence.

There are some limitations to our review. The substantial methodological variation across included studies, encompassing differences in imaging modality, measurement technique, and reference landmarks, precluded formal meta-analysis. This limits direct comparability between studies and introduces imprecision in the reported weighted mean values. The strict inclusion criteria necessitated the exclusion of studies that did not align with our predefined parameters, potentially omitting relevant studies, introducing selection bias. We only used the most widely accessed databases (MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane), potentially excluding relevant studies not indexed or cited in the references of selected articles. Non-English language studies were included to maximize global representation; however, reliance on Google Translate for interpretation may introduce translation bias. For each included article, only the technique used to assess PUDA and TTA distance was reviewed, and the role of other bony and soft tissue stabilizers was not analyzed, which may have provided further context on the anatomical and biomechanical findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review establishes normative values and ranges for PUDA and TTA distance across over 2000 ulnae for the first time, with implications for fracture fixation, implant design, and elbow arthroplasty. A weighted mean PUDA of 5.8° and TTA distance of 53.7 mm were identified, though these must be interpreted in the context of significant methodological heterogeneity. Radiological methods demonstrated the greatest reproducibility and lowest standard deviations, supporting their preferential use in clinical practice. This review underscores the need for a standardized and reproducible measurement methodology, without which direct comparison of values across studies remains limited.
