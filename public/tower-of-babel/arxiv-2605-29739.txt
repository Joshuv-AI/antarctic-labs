Inverse generalised spin models of answers to questionnaires
Arianna Armanetti, Luca Cecchetti, Paolo Sarti, Diego Garlaschelli, Miguel Ibáñez-Berganza

Abstract
Network psychometrics conceptualises psychological constructs as emergent properties of systems of interacting variables. Energy-based probabilistic models have gained popularity as models of these interactions, but their psychometric application has so far been limited, since most implementations assume binary or ternary responses and rely on limiting inference assumptions. We infer and analyse three generalised spin models of ordinal questionnaire data: the generalised Ising, Blume-Capel (BC), and Blume-Emery-Griffiths (BEG) models. These are maximum-entropy models that accommodate ordinal responses on symmetric Likert-type scales with an arbitrary number of options, allowing for single-site anisotropy (BC, BEG) and bi-quadratic item interactions (BEG). We prove the identifiability and the concavity of the maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters, as well as the gauge invariance of the Ising and BC models. Afterwards, we propose an inference protocol of approximated maximum likelihood, based on the estimation of the likelihood gradients through Monte Carlo sampling. We apply this procedure to eleven psychometric and sociological questionnaire datasets, comparing the inferred spin models against the multivariate Gaussian and three further benchmark probabilistic models. We then assess whether the inferred models reproduce the empirical features of the data in terms of different observables, including principal-component histograms, and histograms of Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances to the mean answer. At the item level, the multi-modality observed in the histograms of principal components is partially captured by the spin models. These traits of polarisation can be understood, in the light of mean-field theory, as coexistence of stable and metastable (finite-size) phases of the spin models. At the subject level, the BEG model systematically outperforms the other models in capturing the distribution of distances to the mean, while all models underestimate the heavy tails of the Mahalanobis distance. Overall, the analysis witnesses the predictive power of the BEG model, able to account better than others for the abundance of outliers and mean responders, and reveals several highly non-linear features of questionnaire data that both Gaussian and spin models fail to account for.



I
Introduction

In conventional psychometric frameworks, observed indicators (e.g., questionnaire items such as “I work hard to accomplish my goals” and “I strive for excellence in everything I do”) are treated as effects of an underlying latent variable (e.g., a personality trait such as conscientiousness), consistent with a cause-and-effect interpretation
28
. Although this approach has dominated psychometrics for much of the past century, alternative perspectives have emerged in recent years. In particular, approaches rooted in network theory conceptualise psychological phenomena as emergent properties of complex systems
93
;
12
;
27
;
25
. From this perspective, a construct is not assumed to be the common cause of its indicators but is instead understood as arising from interactions among them, reflecting a mereological (i.e., part–whole) relation
10
.
Within this framework, a range of psychological constructs can be conceptualised as systems of interacting components. For example, personality may be viewed as a network of affective, cognitive, and behavioural processes
24
;
22
; intelligence as emerging from mutualistic interactions among cognitive abilities during development
94
;
83
; psychopathology as arising from interdependencies among symptoms
26
;
82
;
81
; and attitudes as reflecting interactions among beliefs, feelings, and behaviours toward an object
29
;
30
.
This perspective has motivated the development of formal models that differ from those traditionally used in latent variable approaches, with network models providing one such class of tools
11
. Importantly, conceptual differences between network and latent variable frameworks do not imply a strict separation at the statistical level. A growing body of work has shown that several network models are equivalent to latent variable models—such as item response theory
67
and factor analysis
37
;
89
—under specific conditions. Moreover, network models can recover meaningful structure even when the underlying data-generating process follows a common-cause model
67
;
43
. Thus, although network theory entails specific assumptions about the nature of psychological constructs, the associated statistical models are not tied to a single data-generating mechanism.
To represent interactions in network models, researchers commonly employ Pairwise Markov Random Fields, in which nodes represent variables (e.g., questionnaire items) and edges represent conditional dependencies
11
;
56
. Estimating such networks is a central and nontrivial task
42
;
72
, as it involves determining both the presence and the strength of conditional relationships between variables. This step is critical because commonly used network indices—such as measures of global structure, node centrality, and community organisation—depend directly on the estimated edge strengths
11
. Accordingly, a range of methods has been developed to estimate network structures from psychometric data (see, for instance,
90
;
47
;
38
;
97
;
48
;
49
;
68
;
92
, with ongoing refinements driven by critical evaluations in the literature
16
;
15
;
17
)
.
Among these methods, the Ising model has received increasing attention in the psychometric literature. Originally developed in statistical physics to describe ferromagnetism, it represents a system of discrete elements that can take one of two states, whose configuration is determined by local interactions and external influences
18
. Within this framework, the probability of a given system state is defined by an energy function, and its behaviour can, under certain conditions, be approximated using methods such as mean-field models. In psychometrics, the Ising model has been adopted as a general framework for modelling interactions among dichotomous variables
90
. For example, it can represent binary responses—such as endorsement versus rejection of an item (e.g., pro vs. contra attitudes, coded as
+1
and
-1
)—and examine how these responses interact with one another and with external variables, such as involvement
30
or attention
31
. This approach has been applied to the study of attitudes
29
, political beliefs
13
, polarisation
91
, and choice behaviour
62
. Similarly, in the network approach to psychopathology
82
, the Ising framework is used to model interactions among symptoms, which can be either absent (coded as 0) or present (coded as 1), as well as perturbations induced by external variables such as stress
26
. This framework has been used to account for phenomena such as recovery, chronicity, and resilience
65
.
Despite its usefulness, the standard Ising model is limited by its assumption of binary variables. Most psychological data, however, are collected on ordinal scales, and practices such as dichotomisation can result in information loss and potential bias
52
;
68
. Furthermore, intermediate response options—such as neutral or undecided categories (e.g., “neither agree nor disagree”)—often carry substantive meaning that is not adequately captured by binary representations
92
. One way to address this limitation is to consider extensions of the Ising model developed in the physics literature (see
41
for an accessible overview), such as general-spin formulations that allow for more than two discrete states
79
. These models have only recently been introduced into psychometrics as a framework for modelling responses on visual analogue scales, with accompanying work examining their mean-field behaviour and dynamical properties, including pitchfork bifurcations and hysteresis
98
.
Another extension is the ternary spin Blume–Capel model, originally developed to describe magnetic systems with vacancies
9
;
20
. In addition to the interaction and external field terms of the standard Ising model, the Blume–Capel formulation includes a single-site anisotropy term that modulates the tendency of spins to occupy the zero state, thereby regulating the balance between active (
\pm 1
) and inactive (0) states. In a psychometric context, this property directly addresses a key limitation of the standard Ising model by allowing nodes to occupy a neutral or inactive state, providing a natural representation of neutrality or uncertainty in item responses
92
. It has also been suggested that such models can offer a unified framework for studying different forms of psychopathology, including bipolar and major depressive disorders
92
. In parallel, dedicated software for analysing psychometric data within the Blume–Capel framework is currently under development
96
.
A relatively underexplored extension of the Ising model in network psychometrics is the Blume–Emery–Griffiths (BEG) model
8
. This model builds on the Blume–Capel formulation by retaining the single-site term and introducing an additional biquadratic coupling term that captures intensity associations—that is, the tendency of two nodes to exhibit strong activation simultaneously, irrespective of their sign. Consequently, when applied to psychometric data, the BEG model can account for neutrality and uncertainty while also enabling the modelling of tendencies toward extreme responding
39
.
A final aspect concerns the notions of alignment and energy minimisation in Ising models. In general, the energy of the system is minimised when nodes (i.e., questionnaire items) adopt the same state (e.g., +1 or -1) and are aligned both with one another (i.e., responses tend to be consistent across items) and with external influences acting on the system
41
. This reasoning entails the constraint that interactions between nodes, when present, are predominantly positive, a consideration particularly relevant for network structure estimation, a core aspect of network psychometrics. In practice, this implies that items can be coded consistently with respect to the emergent property of the system. For example, if the item “Artificial Intelligence (AI) will make this world a better place” is coded positively, then “I prefer technologies that do not feature AI” should be reverse-coded to maintain consistency and reduce attitudinal energy
30
, thereby facilitating the emergence of a coherent positive attitude toward AI
86
. Although these assumptions may be reasonable in some domains, such as attitude research, they represent a substantial simplification for more complex systems and are likely to be violated in multi-construct settings, where variables may differ in meaning and direction. Allowing for both positive and negative interactions introduces structural frustration, in which not all pairwise relations can be simultaneously satisfied, resulting in more complex system behaviour but also greater modelling flexibility
70
;
41
.

In light of these considerations, we consider the
generalised Ising, BC and BEG models
as a framework for describing cross-sectional questionnaire data. This approach is designed to accommodate graded responses on an ordinal scale of arbitrary length, as well as tendencies toward neutral and extreme response categories. By relaxing the constraint of predominantly positive pairwise associations between items, the generalised Ising, BC and BEG models are also suited to multi-construct settings, such as personality research. We infer the parameters of such models from different empirical cross-sectional questionnaire datasets spanning diverse psychological constructs, including both unipolar and bipolar scales. We compare the inferred generative models against simpler probabilistic benchmarks and examine their predictive properties, including the statistics of distances to the mean and the emergence of multi-stability.

In particular, we first define the models and prove their identifiability and the concavity of their maximum likelihood inference, we also prove the gauge invariance for the generalised Ising and BC models. Afterwards, we propose a protocol of approximated full likelihood maximisation, based on the estimation of the stochastic gradients of the likelihood by means of Markov Chain Monte Carlo Gibbs sampling, combined with Adaptive Moment Estimation (ADAM) and Persistent Contrastive Divergence (PCD). The description of the models and their properties, the numerical methods, the observables in terms of which we evaluate the inferred distributions (histograms of principal components and of distances to the mean), and the benchmark probabilistic models, is done in Sec.
II
. We then perform an assessment of the convergence of the spin models, an out-of-sample analysis of their relative accuracy, and an analysis of their adherence to the empirical data in Sec.
III
. We draw our conclusions in Sec.
IV
.

Since the full likelihood is unfeasible to compute explicitly, convergence is monitored through three loss functions equal to the squared norms of the gradients in Eqs. (
5
):

\displaystyle L_{\bf h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\Bigl(\left<x_{j}\right>_{\rho_{X}}-\left<x_{j}\right>_{P}\Bigr)^{2},

\displaystyle L_{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq j}\Bigl(\left<x_{i}x_{j}\right>_{\rho_{X}}-\left<x_{i}x_{j}\right>_{P}\Bigr)^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle L_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\Bigl(\left<x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\right>_{\rho_{X}}-\left<x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\right>_{P}\Bigr)^{2}.

These losses tend to diminish, net of stochastic fluctuations, as the algorithm converges toward the moment-matching conditions, as will be explained below. The PCD protocol, ADAM hyper-parameters, and block structure are detailed in Appendix
B
.

In Sec.
III
, we present the comparison between the empirical, training-set histograms
{\sf h}_{o,\rho_{X}}
for a set of observables
o
(defined in Sec.
II.4
), and their equivalent
{\sf h}_{o,Q_{\cal M}}
, where
Q_{\cal M}
are the likelihoods
P_{\cal M}(\cdot|\theta^{*})
of the spin models defined above in Sec.
II.2
, whose parameters
\theta^{*}

are fitted with the PCD algorithm of approximated maximisation of the full likelihood
. The pseudo-likelihood maximisation algorithm is used, in Sec.
III
, only as a guess initial condition
\theta({\tt t}=0)
for the maximisation of the parameters with the PCD algorithm. We stress, however, that we obtain qualitatively equivalent results using the maximum pseudo-likelihood parameters as
\theta^{*}
, instead of the approximated maximum likelihood. The probability distributions of the spin models
P_{\cal M}(\cdot|\theta_{{\rm pl}}^{*})
with parameters
\theta_{\rm pl}^{*}
inferred with maximum pseudo-likelihood are still probabilistic maximum entropy models of the data (since
maximum entropy
is a characteristic of
the functional form of a model
) that, unlike the maximum likelihood models
P_{\cal M}(\cdot|\theta^{*})
, do not reproduce accurately the empirical sufficient statistics. The consistency of our results across maximum likelihood and maximum pseudo-likelihood parameters indicates that the relative advantage of the BEG model with respect to the Ising and BC models, and that of the spin models with respect to the simple models, do not depend much on the details of the maximisation protocol.

II.4
Quantities that we sample from the inferred generative models

Once learned, we compare the probabilistic generative models with the data, through some
quantities
that we estimate both from the empirical histogram
\rho_{X}
, and from the likelihood probability distribution of the learned models
P_{\cal M}(\cdot|\theta_{\cal M})
, through MCMC sampling. We can therefore assess to what extent the spin models, that are constructed in such a way that they reproduce
by construction

some
empirical quantities (the sufficient statistics
\left<o_{\mu}\right>_{P(\cdot|\theta_{\cal M})}=\left<o_{\mu}\right>_{\rho_{X}}
), also reproduce different empirical quantities that are not required to reproduce. This distinction is the central diagnostic of our study: a model that is consistent with its training constraints (the sufficient statistics) may nonetheless fail to capture other structural features of the data, and such failures reveal which physical ingredients are missing from the model. While in the next subsection we describe the sampling protocol, we now describe which are these quantities:

1.

The histogram of Euclidean (squared) distance to the mean. We estimate, according to the data and to all the models the histogram of Euclidean (squared) distances to the sample mean
\bm{\mu}=\left<{\bf x}\right>_{\rho_{X}}
:

d_{{\bf x}}:={||{\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}||_{2}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}(x_{j}-\mu_{j})^{2}

(7)

We therefore compare the histogram of Euclidean distances according to the fitted model
{\sf h}_{d_{{\bf x}},P_{\cal M}}
, with that of the empirical data,
{\sf h}_{d_{{\bf x}},\rho_{X}}
where
{\sf h}_{o,Q}(\cdot)
refers to the histogram of the quantity
o:{\mathbb{S}}^{\otimes M}\to{\mathbb{R}}
over the probability density
Q
:

\displaystyle{\sf h}_{o,Q}(z)=\sum_{{\bf x}}Q({\bf x})\delta(o({{\bf x}})-z).

(8)

We notice that one disposes of the analytical expression for
{\sf h}_{d_{{\bf x}},\nu({\bf x})}
, i.e., for the particular case of the Gaussian model. Such distribution corresponds to that of the sum of
M

\chi^{2}
variables, weighted by the eigenvalues
\bm{\lambda}
of the sample covariance matrix
C
–a distribution of which we know its characteristic function (see Appendix
H
).

2.

We consider as well the histogram of the Mahalanobis distance:

\displaystyle d^{({\rm M})}_{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\dagger}\cdot C^{-1}\cdot({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})

(9)

where
C
is the sample estimator of the covariance of the data
X
. In the language of statistical models, the Mahalanobis distance is the energy (minus the exponent) of the probability density of the multivariate Gaussian exhibiting the same mean and covariance of the data
X
. Unlike the Euclidean distance, the Mahalanobis distance accounts for the correlation structure of the data: it rescales each direction in response space by the corresponding variance, so that directions of high variance count less. A response vector
{\bf x}
that deviates from the mean along a high-variance principal component will have a smaller Mahalanobis distance than one deviating by the same Euclidean amount along a low-variance direction. The two distances are therefore complementary: the Euclidean distance is sensitive to the overall spread, while the Mahalanobis distance is sensitive to the shape of the distribution relative to its covariance structure. Again, one disposes of an analytical expression for
{\sf h}_{d^{({\rm M})}_{{\bf x}},\nu({\bf x})}
in the particular case of the Gaussian model. This is the Gamma distribution with shape parameter
M/2
(see Appendix
H
).

3.

The histogram of each item value, or the
M\times R
matrix encoding the frequency
f_{jq}:={\sf h}_{x_{j},P_{\cal M}}(v_{q})=\left<\delta_{x_{j},v_{q}}\right>_{P_{\cal M}}
of answering the
q
-th value to the
j
-th item, versus the same empirical quantity,
{\sf h}_{x_{j},\rho_{X}}(v_{q})=\left<\delta_{x_{j},v_{q}}\right>_{\rho_{X}}
.

4.

The histogram of principal components, or
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{j},P_{\cal M}}
, where
x^{\prime}_{j}=(U\cdot({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}))_{j}
is the
j
-th Principal Component (PC) of the data, where
U
is the matrix of row-eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix,
C=U^{\dagger}\cdot\Lambda\cdot U
. The PC histograms explore the full shape of the marginal distributions along the directions of maximum variance, going beyond what the covariance matrix alone captures; a model that matches
\left<x_{i}x_{j}\right>
but produces, say, bimodal PC distributions when the data is unimodal (or vice versa) will be visibly detected here.

For completeness, we have verified that the histogram of factor scores
{\sf h}_{f_{j},P_{\cal M}}
yields results consistent with those of the principal-component histograms
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{j},P_{\cal M}}
across all models and datasets; we therefore report only the latter for brevity.

II.5
Sampling strategy from the inferred generative models

Once the parameters of each model
\theta_{\cal M}
have been learned, we sample the quantities from the corresponding probability distributions
P_{\cal M}(\cdot|\theta_{\cal M})
. For this scope,
we employ MCMC Gibbs sampling again.
The MCMC sampling protocol (different from the one employed to estimate the gradient terms in Eqs. (
5
)) consists in
T=10^{7}
Monte Carlo Gibbs sampling sweeps, of which we collect all the consecutive configurations
{\bm{\sigma}}(\tau)
. Importantly, every
\tau_{\rm c}
we reset the configuration
{\bm{\sigma}}(\tau):={\bf x}_{i}
to the vector of answers of a random empirical subject
i
, with
\tau_{\rm c}=10^{4}
. In this way, there are essentially
{\rm int}(T/\tau_{\rm c})
independent Markov chains, each one starting from a random empirical subject. Finally, we estimate the theoretical expectation values
\left<\cdot\right>_{P_{\cal M}}
and histograms of the observables
{\sf h}_{o,P_{\cal M}}
as the sample mean and histograms of the sampled configurations
\{{\bm{\sigma}}(\tau)\}_{\tau=1}^{T}
.

This protocol will also serve as a consistency test of the PCD algorithm, when comparing the consistency between theoretical and empirical sufficient statistics after learning (see Appendix
D
). We observe (see Appendix
D
) that the agreement between empirical and theoretical sufficient statistics
\left<o_{\mu}\right>_{P_{\cal M}}
is consistent with the one found during the training.

III
Results

The results are organised in three groups. Section

concerns the validity of the inference and the out-of-sample comparison between the spin models. Section

III.1
Inference validity and out-of-sample model comparison.

Moment matching.

The maximum-likelihood spin models fitted with PCD approximately reproduce, up to the attained value of the losses
L_{\bf h},L_{J},L_{K}
after the training, the corresponding sufficient statistics. This is illustrated for the
mach
dataset in Figs.
7
and
9
of Appendix
D
. See the equivalent figures for the rest of the datasets in Figs.
14
,
15
of Appendix
J
).

The BEG and BC models present higher out-of-sample pseudo-likelihood than Ising.

The BEG model presents higher out-of-sample scores (higher test-pseudo-likelihood and lower test-
completion-error
, see Appendix
I
) than the Ising and BC models. This suggests that the BEG model is a better model also accounting, in the sense of the accuracy-complexity trade-off, for its higher complexity. This, however, may depend on the quantity in terms of which one performs such a model selection analysis –considering that, as we discuss in Appendix
I
, the likelihood function is not computationally accessible in this case. The main scope of this article is, in any case, not to perform an exhaustive model comparison between these models but, rather, to examine whether the spin models, and which of them, reproduce the observables defined in Sec.
II.4
and evaluated in the training-set. We assess the statistical significance of the histograms evaluated in the training-set reporting, as error-bars in the histogram figures, the Wilson score confidence interval with a p-value
p=0.05
(see Appendix
I
for further details).

Beyond their sufficient statistics, the maximum-likelihood, maximum-entropy models approximately reproduce as well other quantities that they are not required to reproduce by construction. At the item-level, these are the histogram of item responses and the histogram of principal components. At the subject level, these are the histograms of Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances to the mean answer.

III.2
Predictive power of the spin models on item quantities.

Histograms of item responses and principal components.

First, the spin models approximately reproduce the histograms of item responses
{\sf h}_{x_{i}}
and the histograms of item principal components
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{i}}
(see Figs.
16
,
17
in Appendix
J
). Generally speaking, the BEG model tends to reproduce better than the Ising and BC models the histogram of answers, as can be seen by inspection from the histograms of item responses (Fig.
16
in Appendix

Non-Gaussian structure and metastability in the inferred models.

In some of the considered datasets we observe multi-modality in the histograms of the first principal component, a non-linear phenomenon that cannot be explained by the Gaussian model. As we discuss in Appendix
F
, these multi-modal distributions correspond, at least in the naif mean-field picture of the spin models, to the emergence of a second (or a third) metastable phase, characterised by higher local minima of the mean-field variational free energy. We observe multi-modal histograms of
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{1}}
in the Ising-BC models inferred from the datasets
gcbs
,
rwas
, and in the BEG model inferred from the
cfcs
,
dass
,
hsns
,
mach
and
rwas
data (see Fig.
17
in Appendix
J
). For the
rwas
dataset, shown in Fig.
2
, the Ising and BC models exhibit two maxima of
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{1}}
, while the BEG model exhibits three local maxima.
Two qualitatively different situations arise.

In the first, the multi-modality in the model mirrors a genuine multi-modal structure present in the empirical data. In the case of the
gcbs
questionnaire, the BEG model captures multi-modality of the principal components (or factors) that is actually present in the empirical data. This is apparent in the histogram of the first two principal components of the
gcbs
data in Fig.
4
(see also Fig.
17
in Appendix
J
).

The phase-separation-like phenomenon exhibited by the BEG model inferred in the
gcbs
questionnaire is reflected in the presence of a second and a third peak in the histogram of Euclidean distances (see Fig.
4
) that, also in this case, are present as well in the data. Such second and third peaks in
{\sf h_{d_{\bf x}}}
are absent in the Ising and BC models.

Notably, the multi-modal structure of the
gcbs
data in the space of factors is captured by the BEG model but not by the copulas (see Fig.
18
in Appendix
J
), while the second and third peak in the Euclidean distance histogram are reproduced by both the BEG model and the copulas (see Fig.
20
). This suggests that the multi-modal factor structure requires bi-quadratic couplings
K_{ij}
to be reproduced, and is not reducible to the marginal and pairwise structure captured by the copulas.

In the second situation, the multi-modality is present in the inferred model but not in the empirical data –as is the case for
rwas
(Fig.
2
),
cfcs
,
hsns
, and
mach
. Here, the inferred model develops a metastable state that is not reflected in the empirical histogram, indicating that there are essential features of the data eluding an accurate description in terms of the spin models.

Some datasets exhibit histograms that, while not multimodal, present a change in the sign of the curvature
{\rm d}^{2}{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{k}}(z)/{\rm d}z^{2}
–from concave to convex and back to concave– that is incompatible with the Gaussian density
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{j}}(x^{\prime}_{1})=\nu(x^{\prime}_{1};0,\lambda_{j})
. This occurs in the datasets:
mach
(

),

(

),

),

(

),

(

) (see Fig.

in Appendix

of

The non-Gaussian structure identified here in the principal component histograms can be regarded as a weak manifestation of the multi-stability phenomenon described above (see also Appendix
F

Mean-field interpretation of the metastable states.

The multi-stability of the Ising model described above is qualitatively and partially quantitatively predicted by the naif mean-field approximation, as discussed in Appendix
F
. Under the hypotheses of the mean-field approximation and of
weak enough disorder
(or, equivalently, of a sufficiently large spectral gap between the first and second eigenvalues of the coupling matrix
J
,
\epsilon_{1}\gg\epsilon_{2}
), the variational mean-field free energy as a function of the first principal component
\mu^{\prime}_{1}

can develop
(for sufficiently low fields
|{\bf h}|
and sufficiently high interaction strength
\epsilon_{1}
) two local minima, corresponding to the coexistence between a thermodynamically stable and a metastable phase. In this theoretical picture, the first principal component plays the role of the
order parameter
, or the
generalised magnetisation

\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{M}x_{i}U_{1i}
, generalising the ordered magnetisation
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{M}x_{i}/M
in the presence of a weakly disordered coupling matrix. The weak-disorder, naif mean-field approximation therefore predicts that the histogram of
x^{\prime}_{1}
(and not those of
x^{\prime}_{k>1}
) presents two local maxima for those Ising models lying in their low-temperature
phase
(satisfying the discriminant condition
\beta>\beta_{1}
in Eq. (
34
) in Appendix
F
, where
\beta=1
is the inverse temperature of the inferred spin models, and
\beta_{1}
is a transition inverse temperature depending on
R,{\bf h},J
). The analysis in Appendix
F
reveals that the weak-disorder hypotheses leading to the bi-modality of
x^{\prime}_{1}
are at least partially satisfied in those datasets for which the inferred Ising model actually exhibits a double peak in
{\sf h}_{x^{\prime}_{1}}
–despite the inaccuracy of the mean-field approximation to describe such finite-
M
systems with inhomogeneous inferred couplings
J
and fields
\bf h
.

III.3
Predictive power of the spin models on subject quantities: distributions of distances from the mean.

The BEG model captures better than Ising, BC, and simple models the Euclidean distance to the mean.

The BEG model captures better than the Ising and BC models some stylised facts of the histogram of Euclidean distances to the mean,
{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x}}
.
In particular, the BEG model accounts better for the high probability density of subjects close to and far from the mean (and particularly better than Ising for the high probability of subjects close to the mean).
This effect can be seen, for the
dass
and
sd3
questionnaires, in Fig.
1
, although it is not a peculiarity of such datasets –see the same quantity for all the datasets that converged in Fig.
19
in Appendix
J
.

Moreover, the BEG model also reproduces the Euclidean distances to the mean better than the simple models, and than the copulas in particular. The copulas are the simple model that better reproduces some empirical features, as the histograms of data principal components (see Fig.
18
in Appendix
J
).
At least for what concerns the histogram of Euclidean distances, the BEG model reproduces better its salient features with respect to all simple models, including the copulas, which have a comparable number of parameters. This is shown in Figs.
20
and
21
, respectively, in Appendix
J
. The same is less clear, as we will see, for the Mahalanobis distance.

While the psychological interpretation of outlier subjects—whose response patterns deviate significantly from the mean—is well established across various psychological domains (such as attitudes, personality, and emotions), the interpretation of subjects whose responses closely align with the mean remains elusive. Specifically, interpreting such average responses as ’conformity’ or ’social desirability’ may be valid in attitudinal research
44
, but it does not necessarily generalise to other constructs, such as personality. By outperforming alternative models in accounting for both types of response profiles across a wide range of questionnaires, the BEG model demonstrates its potential to provide a unified descriptive framework.

The Mahalanobis distance to the mean is generally not well reproduced by the spin models; among them, only the BEG model partially accounts for this quantity in the
rwas
questionnaire.

Some features of the data are elusive to all the considered models. This is clear for the Mahalanobis distance: all the models underestimate the frequency of empirical subjects that are close to and especially far away from the mean in the sense of the Mahalanobis distance. We show this effect in Fig.
3
for the
sd3
data. Fig.
25
in Appendix
J
shows that this occurs for many, if not for all the considered questionnaires.

In order to assess to what extent the differences between the empirical and inferred Mahalanobis distances are due to the fact that the
L_{J}
losses are not negligible and, therefore, the theoretical covariance matrix may be different from
C
(something that can have an impact especially in the inverse of
C
in Eq.
9

54
), we report as well the comparison between the empirical and theoretical histograms of a (model-dependent) variant of the Mahalanobis distance such that the covariance matrix in Eq.
9
) is replaced by the covariance matrix according to each model.
In this variant,
{\sf h}_{d_{{\bf x}}^{({\rm M})},P}
is the histogram of the quantity
d_{{\bf x}}^{({\rm M})}=(1/2){\bf x}^{\dagger}\cdot\Sigma^{-1}\cdot{\bf x}
, where
\Sigma=\left<{\bf x}{\bf x}^{\dagger}\right>_{P}-\left<{\bf x}\right>_{P}\left<{\bf x}^{\dagger}\right>_{P}
. The results of such analysis (See Fig.
23
in Appendix
J

C
.

Unlike the Euclidean distance, the Mahalanobis distance takes into account the correlation or factor structure of the data which, by construction, is present in psychometric answers. A small deviation from the mean along a principal axis with low associated variance would lead to a small Euclidean distance to the mean, but to a large Mahalanobis distance. As a consequence, this quantity is more subtle and difficult to fit than the Euclidean distance. The systematic failure of all models to reproduce its heavy tails indicates that the empirical data exhibit features that cannot be well captured by any of the considered models, including those with finite discrete support. This observation holds for both the spin and the simple models (see Fig.
24
in Appendix
J
).

For completeness, we report as well the histograms of Mahalanobis distances for the simple models, in Fig.
24
in Appendix
J
.

Closer to the mean outliers in
rwas
.

In contrast to the above general limitation of the spin models, the
rwas
questionnaire presents a proliferation of subjects close to the mean in terms of the Mahalanobis distance, for which, among the spin models, only the BEG model accounts. This effect is shown in Fig.
5
. Among the simple models, only the copulas and its variant
gauss-disc
account for the empirical peak in the histogram near the origin. Fig.
5
indicates that the
gauss
,
cat-ind
, Ising and BC models predict instead a maximum at a much higher value of the Mahalanobis distance from the centre.

Overall, the above analysis suggests that some essential traits of the questionnaire data –such as the histograms of principal components and of Mahalanobis distance to the mean– are elusive not only to the Gaussian model but also to the spin models. Other traits of the data are, instead, reproduced only by the BEG model, as the abundance of subjects close to the mean according to both distances, and the multi-modal structure of the
gcbs
data in the principal component space.
Interestingly, this last phenomenon corresponds, in the language of spin models, to (the finite-size equivalent of) the onset of a secondary, metastable minimum of the variational free energy, as naif mean-field theory predicts. Polarisation and multi-modality in questionnaire data can therefore be understood, at least in some cases, as (the finite-size counterpart of) metastability emerging in statistical-physical models, while this phenomenon cannot be captured by the Gaussian model (hence, by Factor Analysis), nor by any of the other models analysed here (cat-ind, copulas, gauss-disc, see
gcbs
data in Fig.
18
in Appendix
J
). The low-temperature phase behaviour of spin models may, however, not always correctly describe non-linear traits of questionnaire data. In fact, when forced to reproduce the strong item-item correlations of some questionnaires, the inferred spin models may exhibit features of metastability that are absent in the data, as illustrated in Fig.
2

IV
Conclusions and perspectives

We have studied the generalised Ising, Blume-Capel (BC) and Blume-Emery-Griffiths (BEG) spin models as inverse probabilistic models of questionnaire data, fitting them to eleven psychometric and sociological questionnaires and comparing their ability to reproduce a range of observables beyond their training constraints. The main findings of this work can be summarised as follows.

First, we have inverted the spin models with an algorithm of approximated full likelihood maximisation, based on a stochastic Markov Chain Monte Carlo estimation of the gradients, and with no need of regularisation (that is, however, probably needed to fit questionnaires with too low number of subjects per item, subject to the curse-of-dimensionality).
The full likelihood maximisation is necessary in order to achieve moment-matching (Appendix
D
), since Pseudo-likelihood maximisation yields parameters whose theoretical moments do not match the empirical ones (Appendix
E
). Methodologically, and to the best of our knowledge, this represents an advancement in the literature of inverse spin models of real questionnaire data, from the points of view of the algorithm of full likelihood maximisation
96
;
40
;
68
, of the inference of generalised spin models of general value of the spin
68
, and of the study of the BEG model, which has received less attention in the literature
41
.

These improvements also translate into a theoretical gain in terms of adherence of the model to the data. The BEG model outperforms Ising and BC in out-of-sample predictive accuracy (Appendix
I
, Tables
3
–
4
). This suggests that the biquadratic couplings
K_{ij}
, penalising or favouring couples of response intensities both of which are very small or very large, actually capture genuine structure in questionnaire data beyond the description capacity of bilinear interactions. Interestingly, thanks to this extra information, the BEG model faithfully reproduce the histogram of Euclidean distances to the empirical mean
{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x}}
, better than the Ising, BC, or simple models, for all considered datasets. In particular, it accounts for the empirical over-representation of subjects close to and far from the mean.

Nonetheless, there are features of the empirical data that remain elusive not only to the spin models, but to the Gaussian, categorical-independent, Gauss-discrete and copulas models. The empirical data exhibit heavier tails in the Mahalanobis sense than any of the considered discrete-support models can predict, suggesting that questionnaire responses could encode a higher-order structure of correlations beyond the reach of the present models.

Summing up, we conclude that questionnaire data present non-Gaussian properties at multiple levels, and features that elude an accurate description not only by Gaussian models, but also by discrete-support spin models with bilinear and biquadratic interactions.
Our results open several directions for future investigation, which we now outline.

We have shown that the double peak in the first principal component distribution of at least one questionnaire can be understood as the coexistence of stable and metastable phases of the (BEG) model inferred from such data. However, in some cases, the coexistence of stable/metastable states, typical of the low-temperature phase of such models, may be an artefactual feature, absent in the data. In order for inverse spin models to be better representations of survey answers, it may be necessary
to avoid the discontinuous, first-order phase transition
, for example, by fitting the Ising model with zero external fields from de-meaned data, since this system does not exhibit a first-order phase transition. This constitutes a possible direction for future developments.

A second natural completion is a systematic assessment of the validity of mean-field theory for the inferred models in Appendix
F
, and a comparison of its predictions for histograms, phase transitions and critical temperatures against MCMC sampling. In particular, the extension of the mean-field argument to the BEG model, needed to account for the three-maxima structure observed in
hsns
,
mach
and
rwas
, will be presented elsewhere.

The multi-stability identified in Sec.
III
raises the question of the nature of the low-temperature phases of the inferred finite-size models. An in-depth analysis –via overlap histograms and spectral properties of the overlap matrix– would make possible an analysis beyond the simple metastability picture emerging from the weak-disorder approximation of Appendix
F
. If the Gibbs measure of the (finite-size) distributions
P(\cdot|\theta)
exhibited more than two well-separated local maxima, this would provide a new, interaction-based descriptive paradigm for questionnaire data, not only beyond the factor paradigm but also beyond simple metastability in Ising-BC-BEG models. Rather than reducing the response space to a small number of orthogonal factors (as in factor analysis), the spin models would describe in such new paradigm a multiplicity of energetically preferred response profiles or emerging “clusters”, reminiscent of replica-symmetry-breaking phenomena in disordered spin systems
69
. This picture naturally emerges when the weak disorder approximation
\epsilon_{1}\gg\epsilon_{2}
in Appendix
F
breaks down. The variational free energy
F
in Eq.
31
of Appendix
F
presents, in this case, non-negligible fourth-order interaction terms of the form
\mu^{\prime}_{i_{1}}\mu^{\prime}_{i_{2}}\mu^{\prime}_{i_{3}}\mu^{\prime}_{i_{4}}
with random, disordered coefficients, which can induce a proliferation of local minima of
F
whose number grows rapidly with
M

14
;
45
;
69
.

The systematic failure of all considered models to reproduce the heavy tails of the Mahalanobis distance motivates the interest for models beyond the energy-based models considered here. Possible extensions include spin models with higher-order interactions
32
, mixtures of spin models, or latent variable models with discrete support.

Finally, several aspects of the learning algorithms may deserve further investigation: the influence of
\ell_{2}
regularisation on the inferred parameters; the sensitivity of the results to the ADAM algorithm and the PCD block protocol; and a comparison with alternative inference methods such as naive mean-field inversion
73
or message-passing approaches. These analyses would clarify the practical scope of the present methodology and guide its application to new datasets.

Protocol of Contrastive Divergence learning

The PCD algorithm approximates the model averages
\left<\cdot\right>_{P({\bf x}|\theta_{\tt t})}
in Eqs. (
5
) at each gradient step
\tt t
by running
n_{\rm c}
independent MCMC Gibbs sampling chains
78
;
85
;
7
of length
\tau_{\rm PCD}
sweeps. In each Gibbs sweep, all
M
spins are updated sequentially: spin
i
is sampled from its conditional distribution
P_{{\cal M},i}(x_{i}|{\bf x}_{\setminus i},\theta({\tt t}))
defined in Eq. (
10b
), which requires summing only
R
terms and is therefore computationally cheap even when the full partition function
Z_{\theta}\sim R^{M}
is intractable for large
M
. The ensemble average is then estimated as the empirical mean over the
n_{\rm c}\times\tau_{\rm PCD}
sampled configurations:

\displaystyle\left<o({\bf x})\right>_{P({\bf x}|\theta_{\tt t})}\approx\frac{1}{n_{\rm c}\,\tau_{\rm PCD}}\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\rm c}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{\tau_{\rm PCD}}o\!\left({\bm{\sigma}}({\tt t},n,\tau)\right).

(12)

The algorithm is
persistent
(PCD
87
): rather than re-thermalising
n_{\rm c}
fresh chains from scratch at each step — which would require long burn-in periods and greatly increase the computational cost — the final configuration of chain
n
at step
\tt t
is used as the initial configuration at step
\tt t+1
:

\displaystyle{\bm{\sigma}}({\tt t},\tau=0,n):={\bm{\sigma}}({\tt t}-1,\tau_{\rm PCD},n).

(13)

Simulating
n_{\rm c}
parallel copies rather than a single chain of
n_{\rm c}\tau_{\rm PCD}
sweeps is advantageous for two reasons. First, it is statistically more efficient: the
n_{\rm c}
copies provide approximately independent gradient estimates, whereas the configurations of a single long chain are correlated on the timescale
\tau_{o}
(see Appendix
C
).
Second, when the inferred model exhibits a multi-basin free-energy landscape — as we show occurs for several questionnaires — the parallel copies can simultaneously explore different basins, providing a more representative estimate of the model averages and preventing the gradient from being dominated by a single energy minimum.

Every
{\tt t}_{\rm i}
gradient steps, all
n_{\rm c}
chains are reset to independently drawn empirical configurations
{\bf x}_{s}
. In Appendix
C
we show that the inter-reset interval
{\tt t}_{\rm i}
\times\tau_{\rm PCD}
is at least two orders of magnitude larger than the longest autocorrelation time
\tau_{x^{\prime}_{1}}
of the slowest observable, so that the reset does not interfere with the persistent exploration of the chains within a block.

2

of
{\tt n}_{\rm i}

are adjusted to achieve progressive refinement. At the beginning of each block, the
n_{\rm c}
persistent Markov chains are re-initialised to independent, randomly drawn empirical configurations
{\bf x}_{s}

n

Correlation time analysis

We have estimated the correlation time of the MCMC induced by the Gibbs sampling algorithm on the maxent models with the values of the parameters
\theta^{*}
inferred with the maximum likelihood prescription. This is done to exclude the possibility that the correlation time is of the same order of the number of MCMC sweeps from which we sample the theoretical PC and distance histograms presented in this article. In order for our estimation to be reliable,
\tt T
should be much larger than such a correlation time. Indeed, one should not talk about about a single correlation time, but about a correlation time for each observable
o
of interest. When sampling from
\tt T

T

effective number of MCMC sweeps

;
85

whose parameters

Appendix G
Identifiability, gauge invariance and strict concavity of the log-likelihood

This appendix establishes three interrelated mathematical properties of the spin models used in this paper:
gauge invariance, identifiability and strict convexity
of the log-likelihood.

We first introduce gauge transformations (induced by translations of the spin values) and their effect on the parameters (§
G.1
), we define gauge invariance as invariance under these transformations, then recall the notion of model identifiability and its connection to the strict concavity of the log-likelihood (§
G.2
–
G.3
). Finally, we verify explicitly that these three properties - gauge invariance, identifiability and strict concavity of the log-likelihood- hold for the Ising, BC and BEG models (§
G.4
–
G.5
).
More precisely, gauge invariance for the BEG model requires including symmetric mixed cubic terms, while identifiability holds for all three models whenever
R\geq 3
(and already for
R=2
in the Ising case).

Notation.
A statistical model
\mathcal{M}
is specified by a parameter space
\Theta
and a likelihood function
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot|\theta),\,\theta\in\Theta,
on a finite state space
\mathbb{X}
.
For the spin models considered here,
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
k
is the number of maximum entropy constraints, and
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}^{M}
if
N=1
, while
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}^{N\times M}
for a sample of
N
subjects, where
\mathbb{S}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{R}\}
is the common set of
R
spin values for each item. We also write
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{M})\in\mathbb{S}^{M}
for the response vector of a single subject, while
X=({\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N})
will be the matrix corresponding to a sample of
N
subjects, with
{\bf x}_{i}
denoting response vectors of subjects in the sample. There will be cases when we want to talk about the case of one or more subjects simultaneously; we will then just write
{\bf x}
for a state in
\mathbb{X}
, whether it be the response vector of a single subject or the matrix corresponding to a sample of subjects.

G.1
Gauge transformations and gauge invariance

Given a shift vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, let
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=\mathbb{X}+a
denote the translated state space. We say that the pair
\xi=(a,f)
defines a
gauge transformation
from
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
(on
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
) to
\mathcal{M}
(on
\mathbb{X}
) if
f:\Theta^{\prime}\to\Theta
is invertible and

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{X}+a}({\bf x}+a\,|\,\theta^{\prime})=P_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,f(\theta^{\prime}))\qquad\forall\,{\bf x}\in\mathbb{X},\;\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}.

(36)

Since both
f
and the translation
{\bf x}\mapsto{\bf x}+a
are invertible, if
\xi=(a,f)
is a gauge transformation and
\theta^{*}
maximises
\log P_{\mathcal{M}}({\bf x}^{*}|\cdot)
for some fixed state
{\bf x}^{*}
, then
\tilde{\theta}=f^{-1}(\theta^{*})
maximises
\log P_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}({\bf x}^{*}+a|\cdot)
. Maximum-likelihood estimators in
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are thus related by
\xi
, and one can freely work in whichever gauge is most convenient.

Taking logarithms,
\xi
defines a gauge transformation if and only if

\displaystyle H_{\mathbb{X}+a}({\bf x}+a\,|\,\theta)=H_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,f_{a}(\theta))+c(\theta,a),

where
c
is state-independent and
f_{a}(\theta)=\theta+g(\theta,a)
is invertible.

We say that
gauge invariance
or
invariance under gauge transformations
holds for a certain class of models
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, with
\mathcal{M}_{a}
being defined on
\mathbb{X}+a
, if Eq.
G.1
holds for any
a
, where the Hamiltonians in both sides have the same functional form (e.g., that of the Ising model or the BC model).

G.2
Identifiability

A statistical model is
identifiable
if
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}
implies
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot|\theta)\neq P_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot|\theta^{\prime})
.
For a maximum-entropy model with Hamiltonian
H_{\mathcal{M}}({\bf x}|\theta)=\sum_{\mu}\theta_{\mu}C_{\mu}({\bf x})
, two parameter vectors give the same distribution if and only if
H({\bf x}|\theta)-H({\bf x}|\theta^{\prime})=c
is constant over all states
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{X}
. Since in this case the Hamiltonian is linear in
\theta
,
this is equal to
H({\bf x}|\theta-\theta^{\prime})=c
, so identifiability is equivalent to:
H({\bf x}|\theta)=c
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{X}
implies
\theta=0
- and, thus, also
c=0
.
We observe that
identifiability is invariant under gauge transformations
in the following sense: if a class of models is gauge-invariant, then the model is identifiable when considered on a state space
\mathbb{X}
if and only if it is identifiable when considered on the state space
\mathbb{X}+a
, due to the invertibility of the gauge transformations.

Finally, for ease of notation, in this section we will follow a different kind of convention than the one used in the rest of this article: here, the BEG model (and the Ising and BC models, too, once we set
K_{ij}=0
or
K_{ij}=0,\qquad J_{ii}=0
) will have Hamiltonian
H({\bf x}|K,J,h)=\sum_{i<j}K_{ij}x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i}J_{ii}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}h_{i}x_{i}=0
.

Reduction to the single-subject case.

The full-data log-likelihood factorises as

\displaystyle\log P_{\mathcal{M}}(X|\theta)=-\sum_{s=1}^{N}H_{\mathcal{M}}({\bf x}_{s}|\theta)-N\log Z_{\mathcal{M}}=-\sum_{\mu}\theta_{\mu}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{N}C_{\mu}({\bf x}_{s})\Bigr)-N\log Z_{\mathcal{M}},

(37)

so the
N
-subject model is itself a maximum-entropy model with constraints
\sum_{s}C_{\mu}({\bf x}_{s})
.

Let us see that in order to verify both identifiability and gauge invariance for a
maximum entropy model
with the full-data likelihood
on
N>1
subjects, it suffices to check them for
N=1
.

For what concerns gauge invariance, a transformation
\xi=(a,f)
on single-subject states extends to
N
subjects by applying the same translation to each subject: if
X=({\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N})
, we have
X+a=({\bf x}_{1}+a,\ldots,{\bf x}_{N}+a)
, while
f
is the same as in the
N=1
case.

For what concerns identifiability, if gauge invariance holds for our (class of) model(s), then, due to invariance of identifiability (both for
N
and for just one subject) under gauge transformations, we can suppose to have already shifted the spin values so that
0\in\mathbb{S}
. Suppose the single-subject model is identifiable, and suppose
P_{\mathcal{M}}(X|\theta)=c
for all samples
X
of
N
subjects. In particular, this equality holds when the sample
X
is such that
{\bf x}_{s}=0
for all
s>1
. Then
H_{\mathcal{M}}(X|\theta)=H_{\mathcal{M}}({\bf x}_{1}|\theta)
, and the condition
H_{\mathcal{M}}(X|\theta)=c
reduces to
H_{\mathcal{M}}({\bf x}_{1}|\theta)=c
for all
{\bf x}_{1}
, and this, since the model on one subject is identifiable by hypothesis, implies
\theta=0
and
c=0
.

G.3
Strict concavity of the log-likelihood

As shown in
(
95
, pp. 62–63)
, a maximum-entropy model is identifiable in the sense of the definition in subsection
G.2
if and only if the Hessian of the log-likelihood is negative definite for all states and parameters (the term that is used in
(
95
, pp. 40,62–63)
for an identifiable model is a
model having a minimal representation
). Identifiability therefore implies that the log-likelihood is
strictly concave
with at most one local (hence global) maximum. As a consequence, any gradient-ascent algorithm that maintains an ascent direction which is a sufficiently good approximation of the gradient of the log-likelihood is guaranteed to converge to the unique maximum-likelihood estimator, and to always strictly increase the log-likelihood at each step, for sufficiently small learning rates. We note that the discussion in the previous two subsections will allow us to establish for our maximum entropy models identifiability - and so, strict concavity of the log-likelihood - both in the case of
N=1
and of
N>1
subjects.

G.4
Assessment of gauge invariance for the Ising, the BC and the BEG models

Keeping in mind the reduction to the case of
N=1
subjects in the previous subsections, in this and in the next subsection we will just consider the case of one subject.

The key ingredient is the binomial identity:
(z+c)^{l}=z^{l}+(\text{lower-degree terms in }z)
, so the leading monomial in each spin variable is preserved under translation while only the lower-order parameters are shifted.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{S}^{M}
.

G.4.1
Ising model

\displaystyle H_{\mathbb{X}+a}({\bf x}+a\,|\,J,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}\Bigl(h_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}a_{j}\Bigr)x_{i}+\mathrm{const}(J,h,a)

\displaystyle=H_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,J,\;h+g(J,a))+c(J,h,a),

where
g_{i}(J,a)=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}a_{j}
. Since
J
is preserved,
f_{a}
is invertible with
f_{a}^{-1}(J,h)=(J,\;h-g(J,a))
. So, the Ising model is invariant under gauge transformations.

G.4.2
BC model

\displaystyle H_{\mathbb{X}+a}({\bf x}+a\,|\,J,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}J_{ii}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}\Bigl(h_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}a_{j}+2J_{ii}a_{i}\Bigr)x_{i}+\mathrm{const}(J,h,a)

\displaystyle=H_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,J,\;h+g(J,a))+c(J,h,a),

with
g_{i}(J,a)=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}a_{j}+2J_{ii}a_{i}
, and
f_{a}^{-1}(J,h)=(J,\;h-g(J,a))
as before. Therefore, also the BC model is invariant under gauge transformations.

G.4.3
BEG model

For the BEG model, a uniform translation
a_{i}=\alpha
generates cross-terms of the form
x_{i}^{2}x_{j}
and
x_{j}^{2}x_{i}
(
i\neq j
), which are absent from the BEG Hamiltonian. Gauge invariance therefore does not hold within the plain BEG model; it does hold - if we restrict just to tanslations of the form just discussed - for the
extended BEG model
obtained by adding symmetric mixed cubic couplings
S_{ij}
:

\displaystyle H_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,K,S,J,h)=\sum_{i<j}K_{ij}x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}S_{ij}(x_{i}^{2}x_{j}+x_{j}^{2}x_{i})+\sum_{i}J_{ii}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}h_{i}x_{i}.

The plain BEG model is the special case
S_{ij}=0
. For a uniform shift
a_{i}=\alpha
, the translated Hamiltonian satisfies
H_{\mathbb{X}+\alpha}({\bf x}+\alpha\,|\,K,S,J,h)=H_{\mathbb{X}}({\bf x}\,|\,K,\,S+g^{(S)},\,J+g^{(J)},\,h+g^{(h)})+c(K,S,J,h,\alpha)
, where
K
is preserved and the shifts
g^{(S)},g^{(J)},g^{(h)}
are functions of
(K,S,J,h,\alpha)
. We omit the expression of the transformed Hamiltonian, which is analogous to the Ising and BC cases, though more cumbersome.
Since
K
is preserved, the map
(K,S,J,h)\mapsto(K,S,J,h)+g(K,S,J,h,\alpha)
is invertible, establishing gauge invariance (under a uniform shift) for the extended BEG model.

Note that since we must admit the mixed, symmetric cubic terms, indeed gauge invariance does not hold for the plain BEG model.

Note also that here we chose to restrict to uniform
a_{i}=\alpha
in order to obtain
symmetric
couplings
S_{i,j}
and to obtain a minimal model invariant under gauge containing the original, plain BEG model.

This result has a practical consequence: for the Ising and BC models one can freely re-centre the spin values (useful, e.g., to ensure
0\in\mathbb{S}
for the identifiability proofs in the following subsection), while for the BEG model the spin values should be chosen symmetric around zero from the start, as done throughout this paper; in fact, the choice to use the BEG model is
explicit, and not natural
- in the sense that the plain BEG model is not gauge invariant, so the version of it with centred spins is not retrievable via gauge transformations by the version of it with spins taking values in
\{1,\dots,R\}
, and viceversa - but it is the only choice which can ensure we can avoid considering
\binom{M}{2}
additional parameters
S_{i,j}
, which would increase the complexity of the model and increase, with many of the questionnaires under consideration, the risk of overfitting the data or, worse, of having the number of parameters
n_{\mathcal{M}}>N
.

G.5
Assessment of identifiability for the Ising, the BC and the BEG models

The strategy is the same in all three cases. By gauge invariance (§
G.1
) and invariance of identifiability under gauge transformations (where we use the restricted type of gauge invariance with a uniform
a
in the case of the extended BEG model, as will be seen) we may assume
0\in\mathbb{S}
, so that setting
{\bf x}=0
in the condition
H({\bf x}|\theta)=c
immediately forces
c=0
. We then isolate each parameter in turn by
activating
a certain set of spins, i.e. setting them to a (possibly nonzero) value and leaving all others at zero.

G.5.1
Ising model

We must show that
H({\bf x}|J,h)=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}h_{i}x_{i}=0
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{X}
implies
(J,h)=0
.

•

Activate
one
spin: set
x_{i}=v\neq 0
,
x_{j}=0
for
j\neq i
. The Hamiltonian reduces to
h_{i}v=0
, giving
h_{i}=0
for every
i
.

•

Activate
two
spins: set
x_{i}=x_{j}=v\neq 0
,
x_{l}=0
for
l\neq i,j
. The Hamiltonian reduces to
J_{ij}v^{2}=0
, giving
J_{ij}=0
for every
i<j
.

The Ising model is therefore identifiable for all
R\geq 2
.

G.5.2
BC model

We must show that
H({\bf x}|J,h)=\sum_{i}J_{ii}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum_{i}h_{i}x_{i}=0
for all
{\bf x}
implies
(J,h)=0
.

•

Activate
one
spin (
R\geq 3
): set
x_{i}=v\neq 0
,
x_{j}=0
for
j\neq i
. The Hamiltonian gives
J_{ii}v^{2}+h_{i}v=0
, i.e.,
J_{ii}v+h_{i}=0
. Since
R\geq 3
there exist two distinct nonzero values
v\neq w
in
\mathbb{S}
; applying the same equation to
w
and subtracting gives
J_{ii}(v-w)=0
, so
J_{ii}=0
and hence
h_{i}=0
for every
i
.

•

Once
J_{ii}=h_{i}=0
, the Hamiltonian reduces to the Ising form
\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=0
, which by the Ising argument forces
J_{ij}=0
.

BC is therefore identifiable for
R\geq 3
.

For
R=2
, BC is
not
identifiable: by gauge invariance one can centre the values to
\mathbb{S}=\{-q,q\}
, but then
x_{i}^{2}=q^{2}
is constant and the diagonal terms
J_{ii}x_{i}^{2}
become a state-independent additive constant, so the model reduces to an overparametrisation of the Ising model.

G.5.3
Extended BEG model and plain BEG model

Let
R\geq 3
. We prove identifiability for the
extended
BEG model (which includes the cubic couplings
S_{ij}
); since the plain BEG model is nested within it, identifiability of the plain model follows.

We must show that
H({\bf x}|K,S,J,h)=0
for all
{\bf x}
implies
(K,S,J,h)=0
. As in the BC case, activating one spin at a time gives
J_{ii}=h_{i}=0
for all
i
.

Case
R\geq 4
.
Activate two spins: set
x_{i}=x_{j}=v\neq 0
,
x_{l}=0
for
l\neq i,j
. The Hamiltonian gives

\displaystyle K_{ij}v^{4}+2S_{ij}v^{3}+J_{ij}v^{2}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{ij}v^{2}+2S_{ij}v+J_{ij}=0.

This is a degree-2 polynomial in
v
that must vanish for at least three distinct nonzero values (since
R\geq 4
). A polynomial of degree
\leq 2
with more than two roots is identically zero, so
K_{ij}=S_{ij}=J_{ij}=0
for every
i<j
.

Case
R=3
.
Write
\mathbb{S}=\{-q_{1},0,q_{2}\}
with
q_{1},q_{2}>0
. Activating pairs
(x_{i},x_{j})
at the three possible nonzero combinations yields:

\displaystyle\mathrm{(1)}

\displaystyle\quad K_{ij}q_{2}^{2}+2S_{ij}q_{2}+J_{ij}=0

\displaystyle(x_{i}=x_{j}=q_{2})

\displaystyle\mathrm{(2)}

\displaystyle\quad K_{ij}q_{1}^{2}-2S_{ij}q_{1}+J_{ij}=0

\displaystyle(x_{i}=x_{j}=-q_{1})

\displaystyle\mathrm{(3)}

\displaystyle\quad-K_{ij}q_{1}q_{2}+S_{ij}(q_{2}-q_{1})+J_{ij}=0

\displaystyle(x_{i}=-q_{1},\,x_{j}=q_{2}).

Three unknowns, three equations. The combination
(1)+(2)-2\times(3)
cancels
S_{ij}
and
J_{ij}
, giving
(q_{1}+q_{2})^{2}K_{ij}=0
, hence
\mathbf{K_{ij}=0}
. With
K_{ij}=0
, the combination
(1)-(2)
gives
2(q_{1}+q_{2})S_{ij}=0
, hence
\mathbf{S_{ij}=0}
. Substituting back into (1) gives
\mathbf{J_{ij}=0}
.

The extended BEG model (and, thus, the plain BEG model) is therefore identifiable for both
R=3
and
R\geq 4
.

For
R=2
, an argument analogous to the BC case shows that both the extended and the plain BEG models reduce to an overparametrisation of the Ising model and are not identifiable.

Appendix H
Analytical distributions of distances for the Gaussian model

We derive here the analytical expressions for the histograms of the Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances to the mean,
{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x},\nu}
and
{\sf h}_{d^{({\rm M})}_{\bf x},\nu}
, in the particular case of the Gaussian model
\nu({\bf x})={\cal N}({\bm{\mu}},C)
, referenced in Sec.
II.4
.

Let
{\bf y}={\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}
and let
C=U^{\dagger}\Lambda U
be the eigendecomposition of the sample covariance matrix, with
\Lambda={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})
and
U
orthogonal. The principal components
x^{\prime}_{j}=(U{\bf y})_{j}
are independent and satisfy
x^{\prime}_{j}\sim{\cal N}(0,\lambda_{j})
. Since
U
is orthogonal,
\|{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}=\|U{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j}x_{j}^{\prime 2}
, so that the Euclidean distance reads:

\displaystyle d_{\bf x}=\|{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}^{\prime 2}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}z_{j}^{2},

(38)

where
z_{j}=x^{\prime}_{j}/\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
are independent standard Gaussian variables. The variable
d_{\bf x}
is therefore a
weighted sum of independent
\chi^{2}_{1}
variables
. For such distributions, closed-form expressions of the probability density generally do not exist, but the characteristic function is known exactly:

\displaystyle\varphi_{d_{\bf x}}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\!\left[e^{it\,d_{\bf x}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(1-2i\lambda_{j}t\right)^{-1/2}.

(39)

The probability density
{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x},\nu}
is recovered from Eq. (
39
) via the Gil-Pelaez inversion formula
46
:

\displaystyle{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x},\nu}(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm Re}\!\left[e^{-itu}\,\varphi_{d_{\bf x}}(t)\right]{\rm d}t,\qquad u>0.

(40)

In the isotropic case
\lambda_{j}=\bar{\lambda}
for all
j
, Eq. (
39
) reduces to
(1-2i\bar{\lambda}t)^{-M/2}
, the characteristic function of
\bar{\lambda}\chi^{2}_{M}
, and
{\sf h}_{d_{\bf x},\nu}
is the Gamma density with shape
M/2
and scale
2\bar{\lambda}
.

For the Mahalanobis distance, let
z_{j}=x^{\prime}_{j}/\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
i.i.d., so that:

\displaystyle d^{({\rm M})}_{\bf x}=\frac{1}{2}{\bf y}^{\dagger}C^{-1}{\bf y}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}z_{j}^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2}Q,\qquad Q\sim\chi^{2}_{M}.

(41)

The probability density of
W=Q/2
follows from the
\chi^{2}_{M}
density by change of variable:

\displaystyle{\sf h}_{d^{({\rm M})}_{\bf x},\nu}(w)=e^{-w}\frac{w^{\frac{M}{2}-1}\,}{\Gamma(\frac{M}{2})},\qquad w>0.

(42)

This is the
{\rm Gamma}(M/2,\,1)
distribution: shape parameter
\alpha=M/2
, scale parameter
\theta=1
(equivalently, rate
\beta=1
).

Appendix I
Out-of-sample analysis

We present the out-of-sample (test) pseudo-likelihoods of the maxent models for all the considered questionnaires in Table
3
, as well as the test completion errors-1 in Table
4
.
The completion error-1 of a model with inferred parameters
\theta^{*}
on a test subject with response vector
{\bf x}
is defined as the mean absolute deviation between each observed answer
x_{i}
and its conditional expectation given all other answers,

\varepsilon_{1}({\bf x})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|x_{i}-\langle x_{i}\rangle_{P_{i}(x_{i}|{\bf x}_{\setminus i},\,\theta^{*})}\right|,

(43)

where
P_{i}(x_{i}|{\bf x}_{\setminus i},\theta^{*})
is the single-spin conditional distribution (Eq.
10b
) and
M
is the number of items. The conditional mean
\langle x_{i}\rangle_{P_{i}}
is thus the model’s best prediction of question
i
given the subject’s answers to all other questions. The quantity reported in Table
4
is the average of
\varepsilon_{1}({\bf x})
over all
N_{\text{te}}
test subjects.

The statistical significance of the empirical histograms shown in the results is assessed via confidence intervals on the bin frequencies. Each bin contains
k
counts out of
n
total observations, giving an empirical frequency
p=k/n
. We use the Wilson score interval
99
, which provides a confidence interval
[p_{-},p_{+}]
at significance level
\alpha
without assuming normality and without requiring
np\gg 1
:

p_{\pm}=\frac{p+\dfrac{z^{2}}{2n}\pm\dfrac{z}{2n}\sqrt{4np(1-p)+z^{2}}}{1+\dfrac{z^{2}}{n}},

(44)

where
z=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
is the quantile of the standard normal distribution and
\alpha=0.05
throughout. Alternative methods — the Agresti-Coull interval and non-parametric bootstrap resampling of the histograms — were implemented and yield consistent results, but are not used in the figures.
