 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report summarizes the status of the long-term creep testing and progress in the mechanical-property
evaluation of long-term-aged Alloy 709 (UNS S31025) at the US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in FY 2026. The draft 100,000 h Alloy 709 Code Case was submitted
to the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Code Committee for approval and is currently
under ballot. The ongoing long-term creep and thermal aging test programs remain critical for supporting
future Code Cases aimed at extending the design life to 300,000 and 500,000 h.

The ongoing long-term creep rupture testing at ORNL includes both base metal and weldments and is
intended to support the development of the 300,000- and 500,000-hour Alloy 709 ASME Code Cases.
The current long-term Code Case testing program includes tests initiated in FY 2025 and FY 2026 to
accommodate ORNL’s creep laboratory relocation schedule. The creep lab relocation is approaching
completion, and the remaining four long-term creep tests on Alloy 709 weld will start in the first quarter
of FY 2027. Based on extrapolation of the short-term creep rupture data used to develop the 100,000-hour
Code Case, these tests are projected to have rupture lives exceeding 35,000 h. To date, the longest
accumulated creep exposure is approximately 52,000 h.

Thermal aging tests of Alloy 709 are being conducted at ORNL at six temperatures ranging from 550°C
to 927°C. This report summarizes the evaluation of aging times up to 10,000 h, whereas longer-term
exposures remain in progress. Room-temperature (RT) tensile results showed the largest reductions in
yield strength and ultimate tensile strength after aging near 760°C and 927°C. RT ductility showed the
most pronounced reductions after aging between 650 and 816°C. In contrast, tensile properties measured
at the aging temperatures showed only minor changes relative to the unaged Alloy 709 baseline materials,
with no clear progressive reduction with aging time. Tensile results from subsize tensile tests conducted at
650°C, 700°C, 850°C, 875°C, and 950°C, with exposure times of up to approximately 30,000 h, were also
summarized and used as supporting data in the development of the 100,000-hour Code Case.

The Charpy V-notch impact energy also decreased with aging time, and the lowest impact energies
occurred near 760°C for all three heats. The preliminary low-cycle fatigue and creep-fatigue results
indicate that 3,000 h of aging at 650°C does not cause a distinct reduction in fatigue life under the tested
conditions.

These results expand the long-term aging database for Alloy 709 and provide supporting data for the
development of the Alloy 709 long-term Code Cases.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Advanced nuclear reactor concepts require structural materials capable of withstanding high temperatures,
mechanical stresses, corrosive environments, and irradiation. To address this materials challenge, the US
Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Office of Nuclear Energy (NE) Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART)
Program selected Alloy 709 (UNS S31025), an advanced austenitic stainless steel, for qualification as a
new Class A construction material in the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and
Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC), Section III, Division 5 (ASME, 2025a).The qualification strategy uses a
phased approach to support design lifetimes of 100,000, 300,000, and ultimately 500,000 h. This
approach requires substantial long-term design data that support the assessment of the material
performance over the intended service conditions (Sham & Natesan, 2017).

In FY 2026, the 100,000-hour Code Case was submitted to the ASME Code Committee for approval and
is currently under ballot. Development of the long-term Code Cases and generation of supporting

 1

materials design data for 300,000 and 500,000 h design lives will be completed under DOE-NE’s
Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program. The long-term Code Cases
provide the design basis for extended component lifetimes, enabling reactor designers and vendors to
optimize safety margins and reduce maintenance and replacement costs. For long-term service, Alloy 709
must maintain adequate strength, ductility, fatigue resistance, and creep resistance under sustained
thermal and mechanical loading. The qualification program therefore focuses on generating mechanical-
property data to characterize long-term behavior, establish creep strength, and quantify the effects of
aging temperature and exposure time for development of the design parameters needed for extended
service.

This report summarizes the status of the long-term creep testing during FY 2026. This report also presents
progress in the mechanical testing of long-term-aged Alloy 709 at DOE’s Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL). The resulting data expand the Alloy 709 long-term testing database and support the
development of the Code Cases beyond 100,000 h. The results also provide information for assessing the
suitability of Alloy 709 for extended service in advanced nuclear reactor systems.

3.1 ALLOY 709 THERMAL AGING MATRIX

(1,020°F, 1,200°F, 1,400°F, 1,500°F, 1,600°F, and 1,700°F) at ORNL. Additionally, the Idaho National

3 summarizes the current long-term aging plan and mechanical-testing status. Aging exposures of up to
10,000 h have been completed, and longer-term exposures of up to and beyond 100,000 h are ongoing,
with their respective accumulated hours listed in the table.

In FY 2026, tensile tests at room temperature (RT) and corresponding aging temperatures, as well as RT
Charpy impact tests, have been completed for all three heats after 3,000 and 10,000 h of aging at ORNL.

Heat numbers 529900 and 530843 aged at 650°C for 3,000 h. Besides full sets of aged Alloy 709

3.2 TENSILE TESTING OF AGED ALLOY 709

Standard round-bar tensile specimens were machined from the aged Alloy 709 plates, with the gauge
length parallel to the rolling direction. The specimens had a diameter of 0.25 in. and gauge lengths of 1.25
in. for full sets of materials listed in Table 3 or 1.0 in. for materials aged for 5,000 h. Additional subsize

 4

tensile specimens were machined from the tab sections of creep-tested specimens to extend the aging-time
range evaluated in this report. These specimens had a rectangular gauge section of 0.08 × 0.08 in. and a
gauge length of 0.3 in.

RT tensile tests followed ASTM E8 (ASTM E8/E8M, 2025), and elevated-temperature (ET) tensile tests
at the materials’ respective aging temperatures followed ASTM E21 (ASTM E21, 2020). An
electromechanical testing machine was used for all tests. A three-heating-zone furnace attached to the
testing system provided temperature control during ET testing. The initial strain rate was set at 3 × 10−4
s−1 for RT tests and 1.17 × 10−4 s−1 for ET tests for yield-strength (YS) determination. After yielding, the
strain rate was increased to 1 × 10−3 s−1 when the specimen strain reached approximately 2%. This strain
rate was maintained until tensile fracture.

Figure 1 presents the RT tensile results of Heat number 529900 after aging at different temperatures for
150 to 10,000 h. Figure 1(a) shows that the YS and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) gradually decrease
with increasing aging time at temperatures above 650°C. The largest strength reductions occur after aging
near 760°C and at 927°C. In contrast, aging at lower temperatures of 550°C and 650°C results in limited
strength degradation owing to better precipitate stability and limited grain-boundary segregation. Figure
1(b) shows the corresponding changes in total elongation (TE) and reduction of area (RoA). The TE
decreases after long-term aging, particularly for aging temperatures between 650°C and 816°C. This trend
indicates a significant loss of ductility in the medium aging-temperature range. The ductility loss may
result from the formation of unfavorable precipitates or precipitate–matrix interactions that limit plastic
deformation. The RoA exhibits a similar overall trend, although the data show greater scatter than the TE
results. This scatter may reflect the measurement uncertainty of fractured cross sections associated with
the small specimen geometry. Nevertheless, the largest RoA reductions also occur after aging between
650°C and 816°C. Notably, even though there is an obvious reduction in YS and UTS, the values remain
above the minimum requirements of ASTM standards, as labeled by the green dashed lines in Figure 1.

3.3 CHARPY V-NOTCH IMPACT TESTING

Full-size Charpy V-notch (CVN) specimens measuring 0.4 × 0.4 × 2.2 in. were machined with their
longitudinal axis parallel to the rolling direction. The notch was machined with the opening in the
transverse direction. Impact tests were conducted at RT to evaluate the impact energy of the aged material
for all three heats. The tests followed ASTM E23 (ASTM E23, 2025). At least three specimens were
tested for each aging condition to establish the impact-energy results.

Figure 5 presents the RT impact testing results for Heat number 529900 as a function of aging
temperature for various aging times. The impact energy generally decreases with increasing aging time,
which indicates a progressive loss of RT impact toughness during long-term thermal exposure. The
largest reduction occurs for specimens aged near 760°C, at which the CVN energy reaches its lowest
value for all aging times. This pronounced decrease is consistent with the RT tensile results, which show
substantial reductions in TE after aging in the intermediate-temperature range. The similar behavior of
CVN energy and TE at RT suggests that aging-related microstructural changes reduce both impact
toughness and tensile ductility.

 9

 Figure 5. Charpy impact testing results for Heat number 529900 as a function of aging temperature for
 various aging times.

Figure 6 compares the CVN impact testing results for the three Alloy 709 heats after 3,000 and 10,000 h
of aging. All three heats show similar temperature-dependent trends, with the most pronounced impact-
energy reduction occurring near 760°C. However, Heat-1 retains higher impact energy than Heats-2 and 3
at all aging temperatures and for both aging times. In addition, the Heat-1 results show little additional
reduction between 3,000 and 10,000 h. This behavior suggests that the toughness degradation in Heat-1
may approach saturation by 3,000 h, whereas the other two heats continue to show greater degradation
with longer aging time.

3.4 FRACTURE SURFACES

The fracture-surface observations provide fractographic evidence to support the tensile and Charpy
impact results presented in Sections 3.2 and 3.3. RT tensile testing showed the most pronounced ductility
reduction after aging near 760°C, whereas the Charpy results showed the lowest impact energy at the
same aging temperature. The fracture features described in this section help explain these property
changes by identifying differences in deformation and fracture mechanisms. All fracture-surface
observations in this section were obtained from Heat number 529900.

Figure 7 shows the side surfaces and fracture surfaces of RT tensile specimens aged for 3,000 h at 760°C
and 927°C. The side-surface images show that the specimen aged at 760°C experienced less necking
before fracture. This limited plastic deformation is consistent with the reduced TE and RoA measured
after aging at 760°C. The fracture surface of the specimen aged at 760°C contains predominantly brittle
intergranular facets, which indicate preferential crack propagation along grain boundaries. In comparison,
the specimen aged at 927°C shows a mixed fracture mode consisting of intergranular regions and ductile
dimples. The presence of dimpled regions indicates that the material aged at 927°C retained greater local
plastic deformation before fracture. These observations are consistent with the tensile results, which
showed that aging at 760°C caused the most pronounced reduction in RT tensile ductility.

3.5 LOW-CYCLE FATIGUE AND CREEP-FATIGUE TESTING

Low-cycle fatigue (LCF) and CF tests used standard cylindrical specimens with a nominal gauge diameter
of 0.25 in. and a gauge length of 0.75 in. All specimens were machined from the aged materials at
approximately quarter-thickness locations. The specimens were oriented parallel to the rolling direction.
The LCF tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM E606 (ASTM E606/E606M, 2021). The tests
used fully reversed, strain-controlled loading with a strain ratio of R = −1 and a total strain range of 1%.
A triangular waveform was applied at a constant strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1. The CF tests followed ASTM
E2714 (ASTM E2714, 2020) and used the same fully reversed strain-controlled loading condition and
strain rate. However, the CF waveform included a tensile hold for 1,800 s at the peak tensile strain. All
tests were performed in air using servo-hydraulic test frames equipped with high-temperature furnaces.
High-temperature extensometers with a 0.5 in. gauge length provided strain measurement and control.
Before cyclic loading, each specimen was held at the test temperature (650°C) for 3 h to achieve thermal
stabilization. The fatigue life was defined as the cycle corresponding to a 20% reduction in the ratio of
maximum-to-minimum stress. When a specimen fractured before reaching this criterion, the observed
fracture cycle was reported as the failure life.

The preliminary LCF and CF results presented in Figure 9 indicate that thermal aging has a limited effect
on fatigue life at 650°C. The results for specimens aged for 3,000 h were compared with corresponding
PT materials tested at the same temperature and total strain range. The fatigue lives of the aged material
generally fall near the lower bound of the scatter band for the PT data. However, the aged specimens do
not show a distinct or systematic reduction in fatigue life relative to the PT material. These results suggest

 12

that the microstructural changes introduced by aging have not caused a substantial degradation in the LCF
or CF resistance of Alloy 709 within the tested conditions.

3.6 MICROSTRUCTURE EVOLUTION

More detailed results on the microstructural evolution from this study are presented in the previous study
(Fan & Wang, 2026), and a brief summary is provided in the following paragraphs. Samples for optical
microscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were prepared using standard metallographic
sectioning, mounting, polishing, and Glyceregia etching procedures. Microstructures were examined
using a Keyence optical microscope and a TESCAN MIRA3 field-emission scanning electron microscope
(FE-SEM) equipped with backscattered electron (BSE) and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
detectors. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)/scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)
specimens were prepared by mechanical thinning followed by twin-jet electropolishing, and analyses
were performed using a Thermo Fisher Spectra 300 TEM/STEM with EDS. Bright-field scanning
transmission electron microscopy (BF-STEM) imaging and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED)
were used to characterize fine microstructural features and phase crystallography.

TEM analyses were performed to examine microstructural evolution using the SA condition as the
baseline and specimens from Heat number 529900 for consistency. In the SA condition, Figure 10(a)
shows a relatively clean face-centered cubic (FCC) austenitic matrix containing planar slip bands and
dispersed Nb-rich MX particles with a cube-to-cube orientation relationship to the matrix, as confirmed
by SAED. In contrast, the PT condition exhibits a more complex microstructure: Figure 10(b) shows
nanosized Nb-rich precipitates forming along planar slip bands and dislocations, whereas Figure 10(c)
reveals MX particles surrounded by an M23C6 carbide shell, with SAED confirming the shell structure.
Chemical mapping in Figure 10(d) further distinguishes the precipitates, showing Nb-, Al-, and N-rich
MX cores and Cr- and Mo-rich M23C6 shells. Overall, the PT condition demonstrates precipitation-
induced microstructural refinement.

 13

 Figure 10. BF-STEM images and EDS maps of Alloy 709 in the SA and PT conditions. (a) SA specimen, with
 SAED as the inset. (b) Nb-rich precipitates in the PT specimen. (c) MX particle with an M23C6 shell in the PT
 specimen, with SAED embedded. (d) EDS maps of the MX + M23C6 particle.

Because Charpy impact energy and tensile ductility decreased most at 760°C, TEM was used to
characterize corresponding microstructural evolution in Alloy 709 during aging.

• At 3,000 h (Figure 11[a] and Figure 11[b]), the microstructure shows a high density of oriented
 dislocations that act as nucleation sites for Nb-rich precipitates. MX particles remain enclosed by
 M23C6 shells, but the MX cores appear smaller than in the PT condition, indicating the onset of core
 dissolution.
• At 5,000 h (Figure 11[c] and Figure 11[d]), dislocation density decreases, although planar slip bands
 remain. Nb-rich precipitates along dislocations become more abundant, whereas the MX cores shrink
 further, suggesting continued dissolution as alloying elements redistribute to more stable sites.
• At 10,000 h (Figure 11[e] and Figure 11[f]), dislocation density is further reduced by thermal
 recovery, but Nb-rich precipitates are even more concentrated along the remaining dislocations. The
 MX cores become significantly smaller, with fine precipitates appearing inside the M23C6 shells,
 consistent with progressive dissolution and elemental redistribution.

TEM was performed on Alloy 709 specimens aged at 927°C. The results in Figure 12 reveal distinct
microstructural changes, demonstrating noticeably different behaviors compared with those aged at
760°C.

• After 3,000 h (Figure 12[a] and Figure 12[b]), long dislocations are present but without the strong
 preferred orientation seen at 760°C, indicating greater dislocation mobility and rearrangement at the
 higher temperature. Nb-rich nanosized precipitates along dislocations are rare at 927°C, suggesting
 much lower precipitation activity than in the 760°C aged specimens. The MX + M23C6 core–shell
 particles remain similar to the PT condition, showing little evidence of MX core dissolution after
 3,000 h.
• After 10,000 h (Figure 12[c] and Figure 12[d]), dislocation density decreases further because of
 thermal recovery, and planar slip bands become less distinct. Although some Nb-rich precipitates are
 still observed, their density remains far lower than in specimens aged at 760°C for the same aging
 time. Even after 10,000 h at 927°C, the MX + M23C6 core–shell particles appear largely unchanged,
 indicating stable core–shell structures and no significant MX core dissolution at this temperature.

         Figure 12. BF-STEM images of Alloy 709 aged at 927°C for (a, b) 3,000 and (c, d) 10,000 h.

 16

The observed differences between the microstructural evolution at 760°C and 927°C can be explained by
the balance of diffusion kinetics, dislocation recovery, and thermodynamic stability at the two aging
temperatures. At 760°C, moderate diffusion and favorable thermodynamics promote Nb-rich nano-
precipitation along dislocations and gradual dissolution of MX cores, leading to clear evolution of the
MX + M23C6 particles. In contrast, at 927°C, higher atomic mobility accelerates dislocation recovery and
reduces available nucleation sites, whereas increased solubility lowers the driving force for Nb
precipitation. As a result, far fewer Nb-rich precipitates form, and the MX + M23C6 particles remain
largely stable.

3.7 SURFACE OXIDATION AND DECARBURIZATION

Figure 13 illustrates the progression of surface oxidation and carbide depletion in Alloy 709 aged for
3,000 h at different temperatures. Both the surface oxide layer and the carbide-depleted zone (CDZ)
thicken progressively with increasing aging temperature. Only minor surface degradation is observed at
lower temperatures (e.g., 550°C), whereas much thicker oxide and CDZ layers develop at 871°C and
927°C. This behavior reflects accelerated oxidation and carbon diffusion at higher temperatures, which
promote surface degradation and carbide dissolution.

In addition, the EDS maps for the specimen aged at 760°C for 3,000 h (Figure 14) show a Cr-, Mn-, N-,
and O-rich surface oxide with Fe depletion, whereas the CDZ is depleted in Cr and Mn and exhibits Mo
and Cr segregation along underlying grain boundaries. Such grain-boundary segregation was not observed
after aging for 3,000 h at 550°C and 650°C.

 4. STATUS OF LONG-TERM CREEP TESTING ON ALLOY 709

The creep rupture data package supporting the draft 100,000-hour Alloy 709 Code Case under the DOE-
NE Fast Reactor Program (FRP) included 131 rupture data points for base metal and 25 for cross-weld
specimens. Base-metal creep rupture testing was conducted at ORNL, INL, and Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL), whereas all cross-weld rupture tests were performed at ORNL. Each laboratory
conducted the experiments in accordance with ASME NQA-1 or an equivalent laboratory quality
assurance program. Interim results from each laboratory have been documented in technical reports

 19

published between 2018 and 2025 and are publicly available through OSTI. In the 100,000-hour Code
Case data package, the longest creep rupture life was 36,595 h for base metal and 31,001 h for cross-weld
specimens. An ORNL report (Wang et al., 2026) provides a summary of the complete experimental data
package used to support the Code Case.

Long-term creep testing on both base metal and welds is planned only at ORNL. However, at the time the
draft 100,000-hour Code Case was submitted in FY 2026, a number of base-metal tests in the near-term
Code Case plan were still in progress and had not yet ruptured, with several already exceeding their
originally projected rupture times. Because these tests had already accumulated years of exposure, they
were not terminated and instead have continued under monitoring at minimal cost until rupture under the
FRP. The resulting data can be used to inform the ASME balloting process for the 100,000-hour Code
Case and to support future AMMT submissions for the 300,000-hour and 500,000-hour Code Cases. For
completeness, this report also includes the eight ongoing tests at INL, the four ongoing tests at ANL, and
four additional ANL tests that ruptured after submission of the draft 100,000-hour Code Case.

The current long-term Code Case testing matrix at ORNL includes tests initiated in FY 2025 and FY
2026. The test schedule was planned to accommodate the relocation of ORNL’s creep laboratory, which
temporarily affected the timing of new test starts. With the creep laboratory relocation now approaching
completion, the program is transitioning back to full testing capability. The remaining four long-term
creep tests on Alloy 709 weldments are scheduled to be initiated in the first quarter of FY 2027,
completing the currently planned test matrix for the long-term creep Code Case testing program.

Based on extrapolation of the short-term creep rupture data used in developing the 100,000-hour Code
Case, the ongoing long-term tests are expected to produce rupture lives greater than 35,000 h. These tests
are therefore critical for extending the creep database into the longer time regime needed to validate
extrapolation methods and reduce uncertainty in the higher-duration Code Case assessments. To date, the
longest accumulated creep exposure in the program is approximately 52,000 h, and continued progress is
being made in generating the long-term performance data required for Alloy 709 Code qualification.

For extension of the design life to 300,000 h and 500,000 h, the time-dependent allowable stresses
required for use in ASME Section III, Division 5, are based on extrapolation of creep test data. Data
obtained from creep testing are needed to establish the time-dependent allowable stresses used in the
design Code, including time to 1% total strain, time to 1% creep strain, minimum creep rate, time to onset
of tertiary creep, and creep-rupture life. To support these determinations, the complete creep curves are
recorded throughout each test.

Figure 15 compiles all creep rupture test results for Alloy 709, including the 100,000-hour Code Case
base-metal and cross-weld rupture data, the Larson–Miller (LM) relation for the 100,000-hour Code Case
base metal with C = 17, and the ongoing long-term creep rupture tests on base metal and welds. Five tests
that have ruptured, indicated by arrows, show good agreement with the LM relation established from the
previous 100,000-hour Code Case base-metal data.

The ongoing long-term base-metal test matrix provides good coverage from low to high stress levels and
across a temperature range of 550°C–1,000°C, with the tests evenly distributed among three heats of
Alloy 709. This matrix is considered sufficient to support the long-term Code Case effort.

Of the six long-term cross-weld tests, one has ruptured and five are still in progress. With the addition of
four more planned weld tests, the weld test matrix is also considered sufficient to support the long-term
Code Case effort.

 5. FUTURE WORK

Future work should focus on developing and executing a strategic long-term qualification plan for Alloy
709 to support progressive extension of the Code Case to 300,000 h and ultimately a 500,000 h design life
and to enable broader industrial deployment.

• Leverage the existing long-term testing program for plate product form. The existing long-term creep,
 thermal aging, and cross-weld testing program for Alloy 709 plate is on track to generate sufficient
 data to provide a strong technical basis for extending the Code Case design life to 300,000 h and
 500,000 h for Alloy 709 plate product form. The existing test matrix has been reviewed and
 confirmed the remaining cross-weld rupture tests needed to support these design-life extensions and
 to optimize the use of ongoing long-term testing.
• Update the thermal aging evaluation schedule. Based on the thermal aging data presented in this
 report, it appears technically justified to eliminate the intermediate property evaluation between
 10,000 and 30,000 h and proceed directly to the 30,000 h evaluation. This approach would reduce
 testing time and resources while maintaining an adequate basis for assessing long-term property
 stability.

 21

• Expand qualification to other product forms. Future work should extend the long-term qualification
 strategy beyond plate to other Alloy 709 product forms needed for industrial deployment. Existing
 data and gaps for each product form should be evaluated, and targeted long-term creep, thermal
 aging, and cross-weld testing should be incorporated into an integrated qualification plan.

In FY 2027, these efforts will be integrated into a strategic, information-based plan for future structural
material qualification, establishing an accelerated and technically defensible pathway for long-term Alloy
709 Code Case development. The plan will identify the data gaps, qualification priorities, and
recommended testing strategies needed to support 300,000 h and 500,000 h design-life applications and
guide future industrial deployment.

 6. SUMMARY

This report summarizes the current status of long-term creep and thermal aging testing for Alloy 709 in
support of extending the design life to 300,000 h and 500,000 h in ASME Section III, Division 5, for
advanced high-temperature reactor applications.

Mechanical-property evaluations of three commercial heats of Alloy 709 following thermal aging at
temperatures from 550°C to 927°C for exposures of up to 31,001 h have been completed. The RT tensile
results show reductions in UTS and YS after aging at 650°C and above, but they still remain above the
specified RT minimum for this material. Tensile tests performed at the corresponding aging temperatures
showed relatively smaller changes in strength. The largest ductility losses and Charpy impact energy
occurring over the intermediate-temperature range of 650°C to 816°C for both RT and tensile at aging
temperatures, although TE and RoA remained above 30%. Based on the aging results obtained to date, the
data support eliminating the intermediate property evaluation between 10,000 and 30,000 h and
proceeding directly to the 30,000 h evaluation.

The existing long-term creep testing program on Alloy 709 plate and cross-weld specimens is on track to
generate sufficient data to provide a strong technical basis for extending the Code Case design life to
300,000 h and ultimately 500,000 h for the plate product form. Building on this foundation, future work
should develop a strategic and technically defensible pathway for long-term Alloy 709 Code Case
development, including evaluation of other product forms and identification of the data and testing
needed to support future structural material qualification and industrial deployment.
