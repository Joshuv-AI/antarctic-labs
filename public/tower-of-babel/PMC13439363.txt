N-Bordered Rylene Arches via Programmable Curved π-Extension
Authors: Kai Chen, Zuoyu Li, Jiangtao Chan, Xuan Jin, Ming-Wei Wang, Xu Wen, Zixin Liu, Guogang Liu, Wei Jiang, Zhaohui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curved molecular carbons have attracted considerable interest owing to their distinctive properties relative to planar analogues and their structural relevance to diverse nonplanar carbon allotropes. Aromatic ribbons with continuously and directionally curved π-surfaces, predicted as key building blocks for circular carbon architectures, remain challenging synthetic targets, with only a few examples reported to date. Herein, we report the synthesis and properties of arch-shaped nitrogen-bordered nanoribbons featuring rylene backbones fused with five-membered rings at the armchair edges, achieved through a programmable curved π-extension strategy. X-ray crystallography reveals well-defined arched topologies and continuously curved π-surfaces, while variable-temperature proton nuclear magnetic resonance experiments and theoretical calculations demonstrate high arch-to-arch inversion barriers. The resulting quaterrylene arch exhibits an enhanced fluorescence quantum yield in solution and forms host–guest complexes with fullerene C60 in both solution and the solid state. This work provides a foundation for the synthesis of long-sought-after carbon nanobelts.

Introduction

Rylenes, due to their chemical structures resembling ultra-narrow armchair-edged graphene nanoribbons, have long attracted substantial interest from the scientific community and emerged as a vibrant research focus in recent years. Pioneering work by Scholl, Clar, and Müllen not only established the chemical foundation for rylene derivatives1–6, but also paved the way for their extensive applications in organic electronics, photonics, and spintronics7–9. The one-dimensional (1D) longitudinal conjugated extension of rylenes is of profound scientific significance, as it enables precise tailoring of the characteristic electronic structures of these π-systems10. While synthetic challenges remain, well-established strategies to realize this 1D extension have been extensively developed in both solution-phase and on-surface synthesis11–14. In sharp contrast, the controlled bending of rylenes into armchair-edged carbon nanotubes with near-minimum diameters represents a far more formidable challenge that pushes the limits of modern synthetic chemistry. In particular, Vögtle belts, topologically defined as radially peri-fused tubular rylene arrays, have remained among the most sought-after synthetic targets in chemistry ever since their conceptualization in 198315.

The synthesis of belt-shaped aromatic systems is plagued by two inherent and unavoidable challenges, namely the rapidly escalating strain energy (ΔHstr) accumulated during the construction of curved conjugated carbon-rich frameworks, and the persistent difficulty in controlling molecular orientation throughout the macrocyclization process16,17. While single- and double-stranded macrocycles for these target structures have been successfully synthesized, their late-stage aromatization to afford fully conjugated aromatic belts has not yet been realized18–20. This critical bottleneck arises primarily from thermodynamically unfavorable ring-closure steps and kinetically dominant competing rearrangement pathways during the final aromatization21,22. Despite these long-standing challenges, seminal advances have been made toward the construction of segments of curved aromatic belts. Pioneering work by Bodwell laid the critical groundwork for the field, demonstrating that highly strained carbon nanotube segments can be efficiently accessed via a valence isomerization/dehydrogenation (VID) reaction23. More recently, Shinokubo and coworkers reported the synthesis of end-to-end bent perylene bisimide cyclophanes through a double sulfur extrusion reaction24. These contributions firmly established that significant curvature can be rationally introduced into aromatic systems through precise macrocyclic strain management. However, both of these established synthetic routes toward Vögtle belts inherently require a demanding macrocyclization step, a transformation that has thus far remained synthetically intractable. In comparison, the modular stitching methodology, which employs pre-aromatic conjugated scaffolds, offers a powerful complementary strategy to circumvent the traditional synthetic bottlenecks mentioned above (Fig. 1). As the core building block of rylene chemistry, perylene functionalization not only serves as the cornerstone for scalable synthesis of oligomeric fused rylenes and atomically precise armchair graphene nanoribbons (AGNRs), but also has a decisive influence on the topological structures and electronic and photophysical properties of these π-conjugated systems13,28,29. Recent advances have enabled the rational construction of curved perylene derivatives by fusing pentagonal rings at both bay positions, wherein the perylene core undergoes a distinct planar-to-bowl transition driven by strain-induced curvature, accompanied by a decrease in carbon-heteroatom bond lengths30–36. However, longitudinal π-extension of these curved perylene motifs only affords nonplanar rylene ribbons with dynamic wavy conformations, which are limited to linear growth modes and exhibit only low global curvature, making them unsuitable as precursors for tubular aromatic belts34,36. To address this intrinsic limitation, a unique rigid curved scaffold was theoretically proposed by Siegel and Baldridge in 1999, and their calculations revealed that [5]canastane adopts a well-defined, directionally curved skeleton with bay positions fully annulated by saturated carbons (Fig. 2a)37. The computed high inversion barrier of 44.5 kcal mol−1 further indicated the excellent configurational stability of its curved geometry. Most notably, Siegel predicted that extension of the canastane motif would enable the construction of circular aromatic belts and carbon nanocoils without incurring additional strain. Nevertheless, the experimental realization of this design has remained a long-standing challenge, as incorporating multiple pentagonal rings into such a confined curved framework generates extremely high strain energy that is difficult to overcome via conventional synthetic methods. In this work, we address this challenge through rational molecular engineering and report the facile synthesis and comprehensive characterization of curved quaterrylene diimides 9 (Fig. 2b). Owing to the fused pentagonal rings annulated at the rylene edges, the molecule adopts a rigid arch geometry with a continuously and directionally curved π-surface and exhibits a high barrier to planarization. Photophysical studies reveal that quaterrylene 9 displays intense fluorescence emission with a quantum yield of up to 79%, which is significantly higher than that of its planar quaterrylene diimide analogues. Furthermore, the arch-shaped scaffold enables stable host–guest complexation with C60 both in solution and in the solid state. Results and discussion

Design and Synthesis

To elucidate the effect of pentagon-annulation on the curvature of the aforementioned curved motif, we first optimized the geometry of hexapentagon-annulated quaterrylene (6X-QR, where X is a carbon atom or a heteroatom) using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Subsequent comparative DFT studies of heteroatom-annulated derivatives (X = C, O, N, B, P, Si, Se) revealed that the degree of curvature38 increases with decreasing covalent radius of the (hetero)atoms (Fig. 2a). Accordingly, nitrogen-annulated systems emerged as the optimal candidates for constructing these curved graphene nanoribbons, as they not only afford sufficient curvature, but also offer excellent synthetic tractability and widely tunable electronic properties.

From a synthetic perspective, fusing multiple pentagonal rings into higher rylene diimides via chloro-substituted precursors is extremely challenging, owing to the predictably high ring strain and the resulting low reaction efficiency. Furthermore, previous attempts to synthesize double N-annulated perylenes from precursors bearing pre-installed N-substituents yielded no target products (Supplementary Fig. 1a)39. Likewise, our efforts to prepare the target N-bordered higher rylene diimides from dinitroperylene and tetranitroterrylene derivatives under standard Cadogan conditions (PPh₃, o-DCB, 160 °C) were unsuccessful (Supplementary Fig. 1b and 1c). These results collectively demonstrate that direct one-step conversion of planar rylene precursors to curved frameworks requires surmounting a prohibitively high strain barrier, highlighting the intrinsic challenge of installing multiple pyrrole rings into highly strained, electron-deficient rylene cores. We therefore reasoned that using inherently curved or preorganized fragments would lower the energetic penalty for constructing highly curved higher rylene derivatives.

On this basis, we propose a programmable curved π-extension strategy that enables the structural evolution of the targeted curved quaterrylene diimides from a prebuilt molecular bowl (2). As illustrated in Fig. 2b, this strategy involves three key sequential steps: (A) longitudinal extension of the molecular bowl to expand the π-conjugated framework, (B) post-functionalization of the resulting extended bowl, and (C) geometric transformation via sequential N-annulations to afford the final N-bordered rylene arch structures. Benefiting from the prebuilt curvature of the bowl core (2, ΔHstr = 66 kcal mol−1), the additional strain energy required for multiple pyrrole-ring formation is significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 17), enabling the highly efficient Cadogan cyclization that was unsuccessful for planar precursors. To ensure sufficient stability and solution processability of the resulting curved π-systems, electron-deficient aryl (ArF) and imide substituents were rationally introduced into the molecules. Furthermore, the resulting derivatives could undergo further post-functionalization via the Hunsdiecker reaction, a transformation well-established in classical perylene chemistry²⁹ that potentially provides a robust synthetic handle for subsequent iterative π-extension toward Vögtle belts.

As shown in Fig. 2b, the synthetic routes commenced with tetrachloroperylene (1), a known compound previously reported by our group35. In this work, double N-annulated perylene (2) was prepared on a 10 g scale, followed by installation of electron-withdrawing 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl groups onto the periphery35. Compared with previously reported synthetic protocols for double N-annulated perylenes34,35, this scalable preparation renders bowl-shaped 2 a robust and readily accessible building block for the construction of intricate curved architectures. Driven by the electron-deficient nature of the perylene core, dibromination of 2 proceeded at 50 °C using NBS as the brominating agent, and subsequent Miyaura borylation reaction afforded the corresponding diboronate esters 3. It is worth noting that dibromination of 2 yielded a mixture of two regioisomers (bromination at the 1,6- and 1,7-positions), affording 3 as an isomeric mixture, as verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 21). Gratifyingly, this isomeric mixture could be directly employed in the subsequent transformation without tedious separation, as both regioisomers displayed comparable reactivity in the subsequent coupling reaction. Notably, intermediate 3 underwent efficient one-pot tandem Suzuki coupling and C − H transformation with dibromonaphthalene diester, furnishing the extended quaterrylene bowl (4) in 55% yield (Step A), following a strategy developed by Würthner and coworkers40. With the ester groups serving as robust protecting groups, selective nitration of 4 was achieved to give tetranitro intermediate 6 in 99% yield (Step B). As outlined in our synthetic design, the subsequent N-annulations required to construct the curved arch backbone were accomplished via the Cadogan reaction of 6, affording compound 7 in 52% yield (Step C). Compound 7 exhibits excellent solubility in a wide range of common organic solvents (including dichloromethane, toluene, THF, ethyl acetate, methanol, DMF, and DMSO), which can be attributed to its curved backbone that suppresses tight intermolecular π-π stacking, as well as the polar NH groups. For ease of subsequent synthetic manipulation, the NH groups were N-butylated to afford intermediate 8. Finally, sequential hydrolysis of the ester groups and imidization delivered the target curved quaterrylene diimides 9 in 66% yield over two steps. Similarly, compound 5 was prepared from compound 4. The molecular structures of all new compounds 3 − 9 were fully characterized by NMR spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 21−41).

X-ray Crystallographic and Conformational Analysis

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis unambiguously confirmed the solid-state structures of 7 and 9@C60. As illustrated in Figs. 3a and 3d, both compounds adopt the expected continuously and directionally curved geometries, featuring a quaterrylene core bordered by six pyrrole rings. The curved backbone of these molecules originates from the intrinsic ring strain imposed by the fused pentagonal rings, with the total strain energies calculated to be 176.2 and 193.0 kcal mol−1 for 7 and 9, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 17). To quantitatively assess the curvature evolution enabled by our programmable curved π-extension (PCE) strategy, we extracted the central angles (θ) and π-orbital axis vector (POAV) angles of the non-planar π-surfaces from the crystal structures of bowl-shaped precursor 235 and arch-shaped 7, as defined in prior literature38,41. The θ values of 2 and 7 were measured to be 41° and 149° from the crystal data, which are in close proximity to DFT-calculated values (Supplementary Fig. 11a and 11c). This marked increase in the central angle directly demonstrates the transition from a slightly bent bowl to a highly curved arch framework via our PCE strategy (Fig. 3a). Further analysis of the local curvature via POAV angles revealed that 2 and 7 exhibit maximum POAV values of 7.1° and 9.2°, respectively. For both molecules, the smaller POAV angles (2.6° − 4.4° for 2, 3.0° − 7.1° for 7) are located at the rim, while the larger values (5.7° − 7.1° for 2, 7.6° − 9.2° for 7) are concentrated at the hub of the π-framework (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7). This distribution confirms the positive Gaussian curvature of these curved π-surfaces. Notably, the maximum POAV angle of 7 exceeds that of the classic bowl-shaped aromatics corannulene (8.1°) and sumanene (8.7°)42,43. Geometrically, the quaterrylene arch is fundamentally distinct from conventional bowl-shaped aromatics, with a span-to-width ratio of up to 1.6. For compound 7, the arch span at the rim (defined as the distance between the opposite peri-carbon atoms) was measured to be 10.6 to 10.8 Å, the arch width (defined as the distance between the two nitrogen atoms at the arch base) was 6.44 Å, and the arch depth (defined as the vertical distance from the plane of the peripheral rim carbons to the centroid of the central hub benzene ring) was 4.58 Å. For the final target compound 9, DFT calculations revealed that it adopts an arched geometry and central angle is similar to those of 7 in its free state (Supplementary Fig. 11c and 11d), but the crystal structure exhibits remarkable conformational adaptability upon host–guest complexation with C60 (Figs. 3d and 3f), indicating that the conformational changes of 9 are primarily driven by shape complementarity during C60 encapsulation. Compound 7 crystallizes in the non-centrosymmetric orthorhombic space group Fdd2. The formation of this non-centrosymmetric lattice can be attributed to the asymmetric intermolecular N-H···O = C hydrogen bonds between orthogonally packed dimer units (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Specifically, the hydrogen bonds between the N-H donors and carbonyl oxygen acceptors exhibit N···O distances of 2.98 − 3.01 Å. Beyond the discrete hydrogen-bonded dimers, intermolecular π-π stacking interactions are observed between adjacent dimeric units, with distances of 3.35 − 3.47 Å. These intermolecular interactions drive the assembly of the hydrogen-bonded dimers into a three-dimensional supramolecular framework with cage-like cavities (Supplementary Fig. 5). For the co-crystal of compound 9 with C60, the crystal packing is jointly governed by two types of intermolecular π-π stacking interactions: (1) convex-convex π-π stacking between the arch-shaped backbones of adjacent 9 molecules, which drives the formation of wavy one-dimensional supramolecular arrays along the crystallographic c axis; (2) concave-convex host–guest π-π stacking between the curved cavity of 9 and the spherical surface of C60, which leads to the formation of regularly stacked columns along the crystallographic a axis (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The conformational inversion behavior of these curved π-systems was investigated via VT 1H NMR spectroscopy and DFT calculations. For computational simplicity, the N-substituents were omitted in all calculations, which were performed at the B3LYP/6-31 G(d) level of theory for all compounds (5, 7 and 9) to enable a consistent comparison of their dynamic evolution from bowl-shaped to arch-shaped backbones. For the quaterrylene bowl (5), DFT calculations reveal that its inversion proceeds via a planar transition state with a bowl-to-bowl inversion barrier of 16.5 kcal mol−1 (Supplementary Fig. 15). The relatively low inversion barrier of 5 indicates that it undergoes rapid conformational inversion at room temperature. Notably, the VT 1H NMR spectra of the final target arch 9 showed no appreciable line broadening or signal splitting over the examined temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 20). This result confirms that 9 has a much higher arch-to-arch inversion barrier that prevents rapid conformational exchange on the NMR timescale. Further DFT calculations reveal that the inversion of 9 follows a distinct stepwise pathway via two S-shaped transition states, with high inversion barriers of 42.2 and 54.4 kcal mol−1 for TS1 and TS2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the programmable curved π-extension (PCE) strategy not only enables the progressive increase of molecular curvature from bowl to arch, but also fundamentally modulates the dynamic behavior of the π-framework. This strategy transforms the rapidly inverting dynamic bowl (5) into the configurationally rigid arch (9) with an exceptionally high inversion barrier.

Photophysical and Electrochemical Properties

To elucidate the effect of N-annulation and backbone curvature on the photophysical properties of the curved π-systems, we measured the absorption and fluorescence emission spectra of the reference quaterrylene diimides (QDI, Supplementary Fig. 2), 5 and 9 in dilute toluene solutions. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, a progressive blue shift of the absorption maximum was observed as the number of fused pyrrole rings increases. The absorption spectra of QDI44, 5 and 9 exhibit distinct maxima at 758 nm (ε = 1.43 × 105 M−1 cm−1), 697 nm (ε = 1.00 × 105 M−1 cm−1) and 630 nm (ε = 0.71 × 105 M−1 cm−1), respectively. The optical bandgaps (Eg,opt) were estimated to be 1.55, 1.69 and 1.80 eV for QDI, 5 and 9 according to Eg,opt = 1240/λonset. Notably, an absorption shoulder emerged at 655 nm for 9. Time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations revealed that this shoulder is attributed to the S0 → S1 excitation, which is dominated by the HOMO → LUMO transition (99% contribution, Supplementary Fig. 14). This assignment was further corroborated by the gradual decrease in the intensity of this peak upon titration with C60 (vide infra), as the host–guest complexation perturbs this electronic transition. The steady-state fluorescence spectra showed that the emission maxima for 5 and 9 were centered at 725 and 687 nm, respectively. Compound 5 exhibits two well-resolved vibronic peaks in the near-infrared (NIR) region (660 − 875 nm), while 9 displays broadened emission bands with dual shoulders spanning 600 − 875 nm, indicative of altered vibronic coupling induced by the highly curved backbone and additional N-annulation. The electrochemical properties of compounds 5 and 9 were investigated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) methods. As shown in Fig. 4c, both compounds exhibited one reversible two-electron reduction wave and two well-defined reversible single-electron oxidation waves, as further confirmed by DPV measurements. For compound 5, the oxidative half-wave potentials were determined to be E1/2ox1 = 0.38 V and E1/2ox2 = 0.74 V, while the reductive half-wave potential was E1/2re1 = −1.30 V. In comparison, compound 9 with two additional fused pyrrole rings displayed significant negative shifts in both oxidation and reduction potentials, with E1/2ox1 = 0.18 V, E1/2ox2 = 0.40 V and E1/2re1 = −1.63 V. This trend indicates that the electron-donating pyrrole N-annulation elevates the energy levels of both the HOMO and LUMO, enhancing the electron-rich character of the π-conjugated backbone. The electrochemical HOMO and LUMO energy levels were calculated using the empirical formula ELUMO = -(4.8 + E1/2re1) eV and EHOMO = -(4.8 + E1/2ox1) eV, giving ELUMO values of −3.50 eV for 5 and −3.17 eV for 9, and EHOMO values of −5.18 eV for 5 and −4.98 eV for 9. Both the HOMO and LUMO levels are elevated relative to those of the planar reference QDI44 (Fig. 4d). Accordingly, the electrochemical energy gaps of 5 and 9 were calculated to be 1.68 and 1.81 eV, respectively, which are in excellent agreement with their optical bandgaps.

Aromaticity

The aromatic properties of the curved π-systems were first investigated via 1H NMR spectroscopy. The aromatic regions of the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 5 and 9 showed well-resolved proton signals (Fig. 5a), which were fully assigned using 2D COSY, NOESY, and HMBC spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 18 and 19). All protons on the quaterrylene backbones resonate in the low-field region (δ > 7.9 ppm). Notably, protons a, d and e of 9 exhibit a significant downfield shift relative to those of 5, indicative of an enhanced deshielding effect upon additional N-annulation. To gain deeper insight into the aromaticity evolution from QDI to 5 and 9, we performed nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS) and anisotropy of the induced current density (AICD) calculations (Figs. 5b-5g)45. For the reference planar QDI, the NICS(1)zz scan revealed highly negative values for rings A and C (average: −19.03 and −15.90 ppm), indicating their strong aromatic character. In contrast, rings B and D showed positive NICS(1)zz values, consistent with their antiaromatic features. The AICD plot further verified the localized aromaticity of QDI, with a 10π-electron diamagnetic ring current confined within each individual naphthalene subunit. For compound 5, the NICS(1)zz value of ring C becomes more negative, indicating enhanced aromaticity. The fused pyrrole rings exhibit positive NICS(1)zz values, and the double N-annulated perylene subunit forms an 18π-electron (4n + 2) conjugated pathway that satisfies Hückel’s rule of aromaticity. The AICD plot of 5 clearly shows a clockwise diamagnetic ring current with 18π electrons delocalized over the double N-annulated perylene subunit, demonstrating that N-annulation effectively expands the aromatic domain. For arch-shaped 9 with four additional fused pyrrole rings, the NICS(1)zz value of ring A decreases further to a more negative value, corresponding to a significant enhancement of aromaticity. The AICD plot of 9 reveals a continuous clockwise diamagnetic ring current with 34π (4n + 2) electrons delocalized along the entire periphery of the curved conjugated system, which incorporates the lone pair electrons of all six nitrogen atoms (Fig. 5g). Supramolecular Interactions with Fullerene C60

The electron-rich, curved π-surface of the quaterrylene arch 9 is expected to enable efficient concave-convex host–guest interactions with electron-deficient fullerene C60. To verify this hypothesis, we systematically investigated the supramolecular complexation behavior between 9 and C60 both in solution and in the solid state. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, UV-vis absorption titration of 9 with C60 in toluene revealed a well-defined isosbestic point, supporting the formation of a host–guest complex. To quantify the binding affinity, we further performed fluorescence titration experiments by monitoring the fluorescence intensity changes of 9 at 685 and 745 nm upon gradual addition of C60. Significant fluorescence quenching was observed, indicating efficient energy transfer between 9 and C60. Nonlinear fitting of the titration data was consistent with a 1:1 binding model, giving an association constant of 6.97 × 105 M−1 (Supplementary Figs. 8d and 10). Remarkably, this binding affinity is more than one order of magnitude higher than the values previously reported for perylene bowls and giant molecular bowl hosts34,35,46–49, which can be ascribed to the large curved π-surface and shape complementarity between the arch-shaped cavity of 9 and the spherical C60 molecule. For comparison, we summarize the association constants of representative bowl-shaped aromatic hosts binding to C60. These reported values include 3.21 × 104 M−1 for double N-annulated perylene34, 1.54 × 104 M−1 for molecular bowl/saddle hybrids35, 1.27 × 104 M−1 for trichalcogenasupersumanenes46, 1.06 × 104 M−1 for triazasupersumanene47, 3.04 × 104 M−1 for trisilasupersumanene48 and 8.28 × 102 M−1 for 3N-HBC49.

The solid-state structure of the 1:1 host–guest complex 9@C60 was unambiguously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction, which is fully consistent with the binding stoichiometry determined in solution (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1). In the crystal lattice, the spherical C60 guest is closely encapsulated within the concave cavity of the arch-shaped host 9, with the two components interacting via complementary concave-convex π-π stacking. Intriguingly, a comparison between the crystal structure of 7 and the 9@C60 co-crystal suggests that the quaterrylene arch framework undergoes significant conformational adaptation upon C60 binding. As DFT calculations suggest that the terminal substituents do not affect the intrinsic curvature of the arch backbone, we used the crystal structure of 7 as a reference for the free-state conformation of 9. The central angle (θ) of the arch decreases markedly from 149° in the unbound state (7) to 120° in the 9@C60 complex (Figs. 3d and 3f), which enables optimal shape complementarity between the curved cavity of 9 and the spherical surface of C60. The shortest host–guest contact distance between C60 and 9 is as short as 3.19 Å, demonstrating the presence of strong π-π interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4). In terms of crystal packing, adjacent C60 molecules and adjacent molecules of 9 form convex-convex intermolecular contacts of 3.30 Å, leading to the formation of alternating arrays of C60 and 9 along the crystallographic a axis (Supplementary Fig. 6). Along the crystallographic c axis, molecules of 9 are arranged in an antiparallel fashion to generate wavy arrays, which are stabilized by convex-convex π-π interactions with interplanar distances of 3.17-3.35 Å. Notably, this close-packed, long-range ordered π-stacking motif may provide favorable pathways for intermolecular electronic coupling, suggesting potential relevance to organic electronic materials.

In summary, we present a programmable curved π-extension strategy that enables the controlled structural evolution from a prebuilt molecular bowl to continuously curved, directionally arched π-skeletons. The key breakthrough of this strategy lies in the successful construction of N-bordered quaterrylene motifs via Cadogan cyclization, which is a transformation previously unachievable using planar rylene precursors. X-ray crystallographic analysis unambiguously confirms the highly curved arch geometry of the target molecules, which feature positive Gaussian curvature and a continuous arched topology. Notably, the quaterrylene arch exhibits a high arch-to-arch inversion barrier, as verified by VT 1H NMR spectroscopy and DFT calculations. Moreover, the curved quaterrylene arch exhibits a high fluorescence quantum yield (Φf) of up to 79%. The electron-rich, curved π-surface also enables efficient host–guest complexation with C60 and supports the formation of well-defined, tightly packed supramolecular assemblies. These topologically unique, rylene-based molecular carbons might provide a modular platform for the construction of complex curved π-systems, particularly the long-sought-after Vögtle belts. Future work in our laboratory will focus on the functionalization and subsequent “stitching” of these arch units to realize this challenging synthetic target.

Synthesis of compound 1

A 5 L flask was charged with 3,4,9,10-tetrabromo-1,6,7,12-tetrachloroperylene (60 g, 85 mmol), Pd(OAc)2 (1.9 g, 8.5 mmol), PPh3 (8.9 g, 34 mmol), K2CO3 (94 g, 680 mmol) and n-butanol (3.8 L) and the mixture was stirred at 90 °C for 12 h under argon.
