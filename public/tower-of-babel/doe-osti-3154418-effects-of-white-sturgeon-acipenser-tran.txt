1.0 Executive Summary

This report summarizes research conducted by the University of California Davis (UC Davis) in
partnership with Natel Energy under DOE Award Number DE-EE0011089, issued through the Water
Power Technologies Office (WPTO) Funding Opportunity Announcement "Advancing Fish Passage and
Protection Technologies" (DE-FOA-0002801), conducted from January 2024 through December 2025.
The project compared a novel Restoration Hydropower Turbine (RHT) runner design featuring thick,
forward-swept blades against a conventional thin-bladed propeller-type runner and characterized both
the lethal and sublethal effects of RHT passage on juvenile White Sturgeon (Acipenser transmontanus).
Sturgeons are among the most imperiled vertebrate groups on Earth, with 68% of species classified as
critically endangered, and their downstream migrations place juvenile fish at direct risk of injury and
mortality from hydropower turbines. Despite this conservation urgency, no prior physiological study of
turbine passage had been conducted on any sturgeon species, making this project a significant first
contribution to the scientific literature on fish-turbine interactions.
The research was organized around four experimental objectives. First, acute survival and injury rates
were compared across three passage treatments: RHT runner, conventional thin-bladed runner, and a
runner-less control, at five peripheral tip speeds ranging from 15.0 to 27.6 m/s. Second, sublethal
impacts on growth and delayed mortality were assessed by monitoring individually marked White
Sturgeon for five weeks following passage through the RHT at high (27.7 m/s) and low (15.0 m/s) tip
speeds, including cohorts exposed to a repeat passage event three days after the first. Third, behavioral
effects of turbine passage were evaluated by recording fish movement and opercular breathing rate
(OBR) before and after passage using video analysis. Fourth, physiological stress markers including
blood cortisol, glucose, and lactate were measured at six timepoints (0, 20 min, 1, 3, 6, and 24 hours)
post-passage to characterize the magnitude and duration of the stress response. All live-fish trials were
conducted at Natel Energy's test facility in Alameda, CA, using juvenile White Sturgeon spawned and
reared at UC Davis. The test turbine had a runner diameter of 0.55 m; the blade length-to-thickness ratio
(L/t) of approximately 1.5 is characteristic of an RHT-type design, and at minimum equivalent to, and
likely conservative relative to, larger-scale hydropower scenarios facing migrating juvenile sturgeon.
The results demonstrated that the RHT runner design substantially improved acute survival outcomes.
Acute survival through the RHT exceeded 99% across all speeds tested, while the conventional runner
caused significant White Sturgeon mortality and injury attributable to direct blade strikes. High-speed
video analysis confirmed that the thick, swept blade geometry of the RHT deflected rather than struck
fish, reducing both the frequency and severity of blade contact at operational speeds representative of
the existing U.S. hydropower fleet. Simulations of cumulative passage based on observed per-passage
mortality rates indicated that White Sturgeon could safely pass an RHT-equipped facility repeatedly
during migration, whereas equivalent passage through a conventional turbine would produce substantial
population-level mortality.
Sublethal assessments indicated that RHT passage did not produce biologically meaningful adverse
effects beyond those associated with experimental handling. White Sturgeon that passed through the
RHT grew at rates comparable to control fish (0.67 vs. 0.75-0.76 g day⁻¹), an approximately 11%
reduction that was modest relative to known ecological drivers of growth such as temperature or food
availability. All fish survived the five-week post-passage monitoring period across all treatment groups.
Behavioral observations showed a short-term reduction in swimming activity that resolved within three
days and was not associated with the RHT runner specifically, indicating it was attributable to handling

 7

stress rather than blade interaction. Blood glucose and lactate concentrations were elevated post-passage
but returned to baseline within 24 hours and did not differ between RHT-passed and runner-less control
fish, confirming that the RHT runner imposed no additional physiological stress beyond the handling
inherent in any turbine passage event. Cortisol results were too variable for statistical inference.
Collectively, these findings represent the first empirical physiological evaluation of hydropower turbine
passage effects on any sturgeon species and provide strong quantitative evidence that RHT-type runner
designs can achieve high fish survival at operational hydropower speeds. From a public benefit
perspective, this research matters because sturgeon are a commonly threatened and endangered taxa,
many populations of which may be directly impacted by the hydropower dams that generate renewable
electricity across the United States. Demonstrating that a turbine runner can be redesigned to let juvenile
sturgeon pass safely (without meaningful effects on their growth, behavior, or stress physiology) shows
that clean energy production and fish conservation are not mutually exclusive goals. Economically,
RHT-type runners are engineered to operate at the same rotational speeds as conventional turbines
already in use, meaning they can in principle be installed as direct replacements within existing
hydropower infrastructure rather than requiring costly facility-wide redesigns. This retrofit compatibility
is a key practical advantage over previous fish-friendly turbine concepts that required slower operating
speeds and therefore larger, more expensive generators. By providing regulators, hydropower operators,
and policymakers with credible, quantitative fish passage data, this project can also help streamline the
environmental permitting process for hydropower facilities, potentially reducing the time and cost of
relicensing. Project data will be archived on Dryad for open access. One manuscript documenting the
acute survival and injury results was submitted to the Journal of Ecohydraulics in January 2025 and is
currently in press; a second manuscript on sublethal effects is in preparation for submission in 2026.

2.0 Introduction

Hydropower is the largest source of renewable energy globally and is expected to play a vital role in the
transition to net-zero carbon emissions (Bilgili et al. 2018; IEA 2021). Hydropower offers substantial
environmental benefits, such as low CO₂ emissions (NREL 2021) and flexibility to balance intermittent
renewables (Stark and Brinkman 2023), yet it also presents challenges due to its impact on river
structures, flow patterns, and habitat connectivity (He et al. 2024). Turbine passage represents a
persistent point-source of fish mortality which can effectively fragment populations and erode
population numbers. These impacts may be most prominent on long-lived, migratory fish species such
as sturgeons where the risks associated with passage can compound via repeated interactions over time
(Zhang et al. 2023).

Acipenseriformes (sturgeons and paddlefishes) are an order of ancient (>200 million-year-old
evolutionary lineage) fishes found throughout the Northern Hemisphere and are the most imperiled
vertebrate taxon in the world (Pikitch et al. 2005). Of the 27 species, two are extinct, and 17 of the
remaining 25 (68%) are classified as critically endangered, where critically endangered is defined as
facing an extremely high risk of extinction in the wild (IUCN 2024). Conservation of sturgeon species is
a global effort, and primary causes of population decline include overharvest, habitat degradation, and
barriers to migration (Rochard et al. 1990). Sturgeons are late to mature (some species requiring up to 30
years), long-lived (capable of exceeding 100 years of age), iteroparous, and migratory, with all species
spawning in freshwater and engaging in anadromous or potamodromous migrations (Billard & Lecointre
2001).

 8

North American sturgeon species include White Sturgeon (Acipenser transmontanus) and Green
Sturgeon (A. medirostris) in the western part of the continent, Pallid Sturgeon (Scaphirhynchus albus)
and Shovelnose Sturgeon (S. platorynchus) in the Mississippi and Missouri Rivers, Lake Sturgeon (A.
fulvescens) in the Great Lakes drainage, and Atlantic Sturgeon (A. oxyrinchus) and Shortnose Sturgeon
(A. brevirostrum) which occupy rivers along the East Coast. All these sturgeon species exhibit migratory
behavior and have populations that are listed as endangered or threatened by state or federal agencies.
Among North American sturgeons, only the Hudson Bay drainage subpopulation of lake sturgeon is
classified as “least concern” on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List.
Remaining species are listed as threatened (Gulf Sturgeon and the Gulf of Maine Distinct Population
Segment (DPS) of Atlantic Sturgeon), endangered (Shortnose, Green [Southern DPS], Atlantic [several
DPSs], White Sturgeon [several DPSs], Pallid and Alabama Sturgeon) or are protected due to similarity
of appearance (Shovelnose Sturgeon). Many of these declining populations inhabit rivers fragmented by
hydropower dams (Figure 1). The most widespread and persistent threat to sturgeon populations are
hydropower developments and operations (Rochard et al. 1990, Huang & Li 2024).

Throughout their life-history, sturgeon are likely to encounter hydropower facilities during downstream
movements, and passage through turbines can result in severe injury or mortality. The distribution of
sturgeons substantially overlaps with hydropower developments (Figure 1), and sturgeon species can
travel thousands of kilometers between adult feeding grounds and spawning habitats (Auer 1996).
Typical physical barriers installed at turbine intakes, such as bar racks with 50–125 mm spacing, may
prevent adults from entering the turbines, yet developing larvae and juveniles are typically not protected
(Parsley et al. 2007, Poletto et al. 2014). These larval and juvenile sturgeon are most likely to follow the
bulk river flow, which often passes through turbines (Cooke et al. 2020). Within turbines, fish are
exposed to physical stressors such as rapid decompression, cavitation, fluid shear, turbulence, and blade
strike (Čada 2001, Pracheil et al. 2016), which can result in injury, reduced fitness, or mortality. The
impact of these stressors varies by species, age, and size (Coutant & Whitney 2000, Brown et al. 2014).
In general, larger fish face a higher risk of blade strikes and more severe injuries from strikes (Coutant
& Whitney 2000). If entrained in turbines, adult sturgeons are especially vulnerable to severe injury and
mortality from blade strike due to their large size. Finally, the long lives and migration distances of
sturgeon mean that individual fish may pass multiple turbines throughout their lives. As efforts to
facilitate upstream passage expand (Cooke et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2023), the compounding risks of
repeated downstream passage will require greater efforts to prevent injury and mortality associated with
turbine passage.

Methods exist to prevent fish entrainment into turbine intakes but are limited in applicability. Bar racks
designed to keep large debris out of turbines may also prevent large fish from entrainment, but their use
is not universal. At larger facilities, constructing and maintaining fine screens that can withstand high
hydraulic loads is challenging, and keeping them free of debris requires significant effort and operations
and maintenance costs. Additionally, large, powerful turbines are less vulnerable to damage or jamming
from debris, so they are often operated without bar racks. Similarly, fine screens (15-20 mm bar
spacing), which are specifically designed to exclude fish from turbine entrainment, cannot be installed at
all hydropower facilities. For turbines with increasingly large flow capacity, the installation, operation,
and maintenance of exclusion screens becomes prohibitive due to the greater amounts and size of
excluded debris. Furthermore, exclusion screens are not fully effective at preventing the entrainment of
small- and medium-sized fish. For instance, Knott et al. (2023) found that 9% of fish (Common Barbel
[Barbus barbus], Brown Trout [Salmo trutta], European Perch [Perca fluviatilis], and Huchen [Hucho
hucho]) ≥15 cm in length were able to pass through 15 mm exclusion screens, while 18% were able to
pass through 20 mm screens; additionally, they found that screens with 15 to 20 mm bar spacing were
completely ineffective at excluding small-bodied species (Cottus gobio, Gobio gobio) with maximum
total lengths below 20 cm. If designed incorrectly, fine exclusion screens have been observed to directly
injure or kill fish that are impinged on or forced through the screen when flow velocities exceed their
swimming ability (Calles et al. 2010, Rytwinski et al. 2017). On the west coast of the United States,
screening criteria at hydropower facilities are designed to protect salmonids (National Marine Fisheries
Service 2023), yet effects of these screens on other species, including threatened and endangered ones,
remains understudied. Species with reduced swimming capabilities compared to salmonids, such as
sturgeon (Peake et al. 1997), may be at greater risk of injury and impingement from screens designed
primarily for salmonids. For instance, green sturgeon have been shown to be particularly prone to
contact and impingement on screens (Poletto et al. 2014). Given the difficulty of effectively preventing
fish from entering turbine intakes, it is important to adapt turbines to minimize harm to entrained fish.

 10

Various aspects of hydropower plant and turbine design affect fish survival outcomes. Strike speed may
be a determinant of blade strike severity, with typical blade peripheral speeds (speed at a blade’s
outermost radius) ranging from 20 to 35 m/s. Turbines designed specifically for high fish survival have
historically been designed to operate at reduced peripheral speeds (5.8-22.0 m/s) to minimize the blade
strike severity (Mueller et al. 2022, Cook et al. 2003). However, low-speed turbines can be difficult to
implement within existing hydropower infrastructure due to higher torque requirements on the
generator, which can increase generator size and cost, as well as a larger runner diameter and overall
turbine footprint. These costs and complexities make turbine replacement with lower-speed units at
existing hydropower facilities exceptionally challenging. Consequently, most “fish-friendly” turbines
have been installed at new hydropower developments or for niche applications. Turbines capable of
passing fish safely while operating at high speeds would offer an option for in-situ runner replacement at
existing hydropower sites where fish survival is a concern.

Beyond direct mortality and physical injury, turbine passage may impose a range of sublethal effects on
fish that compromise individual performance and long-term survival even in the absence of observable
wounds. The study of sublethal stress responses in fish has a well-established history, with researchers
using a suite of physiological and behavioral indicators to characterize the severity and duration of a
stress event (Wendelaar Bonga 1997;Watson et al. 2020, Colotelo et a. 2017).

Growth rate provides an integrative measure of sublethal impact over time, as energetic resources
diverted toward stress recovery or injury repair are unavailable for somatic growth. Even modest
reductions in growth rate can have consequences as smaller fish are more likely to be predated upon
(Baird et al. 2020), more likely to lose in interactions with competitors for food or habitat (Ward et al.
2006), and may be less capable of completing important life-history events (e.g., migration, Chaparro-
Pedraza and de Roos 2021).

Sublethal effects of turbine passage may also manifest through changes in behavior that are ecologically
consequential even when fish survive the immediate passage event. Changes in locomotor activity, such
as reductions in swimming distance or exploratory behavior, have been used as behavioral endpoints for
sublethal stress assessment across a range of taxa and stressor types (Bell et al. 2010, Melvin and Wilson
2013). From a fitness perspective, behavioral changes matter because a fish exiting a turbine tailrace in a
disoriented or suppressed state may be more vulnerable to the elevated predator densities commonly
observed in tailrace environments (Coutant and Whitney 2000).

When fish experience an acute stressor, the hypothalamic-pituitary-interrenal (HPI) axis is activated,
triggering the release of cortisol into the bloodstream. Cortisol, a primary glucocorticoid, mobilizes
glucose as an energy substrate to meet the demands of the stress response, and when anaerobic
metabolism is engaged, lactate accumulates in the blood as a byproduct (Barton and Iwama 1991;
Wendelaar Bonga 1997). Together, elevated plasma cortisol, glucose, and lactate are widely used as
indicators of acute stress severity (Martinez-Porchas et al. 2009), with faster return to baseline
concentrations interpreted as evidence of a less severe or shorter-duration stress event (Sopinka et al.
2016, Mclean et al. 2016). These blood-based stress markers have been applied to evaluate fish
responses to fishway passage (Mesa et al. 2013), and handling and transport stress (Barton and Iwama
1991), but to the authors' knowledge have not previously been applied to fish undergoing hydropower
turbine passage.

 11

Together, these complementary metrics, growth, behavioral responses, and blood stress markers, provide
a comprehensive picture of whether turbine passage imposes costs on fish that extend beyond the
immediate passage event, a question of particular relevance for endangered species such as sturgeon
where every individual's survival and reproductive contribution is consequential.

This project directly advances the objectives of the Department of Energy’s Water Power Technologies
Office (WPTO) Funding Opportunity Announcement “Advancing Fish Passage and Protection
Technologies” by generating rigorous, empirical data on the biological performance of a novel
hydropower turbine design (the RHT). Through controlled laboratory testing, the project quantified both
lethal and sublethal effects of turbine passage on White Sturgeon, producing detailed survival, injury,
and behavioral datasets. The results demonstrate a substantial reduction in fish injury and mortality
compared to conventional turbines. By advancing the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of the RHT,
the project provides critical verification data needed to move the RHT design toward prototype
deployment and market adoption. To that end, we discuss how the RHT approach may scale to larger
turbines and pumping infrastructure.

The RHT turbine exemplifies how engineering innovation can simultaneously reduce environmental
impacts, lower long-term mitigation costs, and expand the range of viable options available for
hydropower licensing and relicensing. By enabling safer downstream fish passage, this project
contributes to DOE Hydropower Program’s strategic goals of developing effective multispecies passage
solutions, improving the environmental performance of existing hydropower assets, and supporting a
resilient energy system.

3. Project Description
We sought to assess four different aspects of turbine impact on sturgeon passage. The first quantified the
effect of blade design on rates of immediate injury and mortality, the second looked at sublethal impacts
on fish growth and delayed mortality (>48 h), the third assessed the impact of turbine passage on fish
behavior, and the final measured stress markers in the blood. All trials were conducted in collaboration
with Natel Energy, who aided in designing the hydropower turbines and facilitating the passage trials at
their test facility in Alameda, CA.

Two turbine runners were designed by Natel Energy and tested to evaluate fish passage safety under
realistic hydropower conditions. The first, an innovative RHT-type runner developed by Natel Energy,
featured two thick, forward-swept blades designed to minimize fish strikes. The second was a
conventional three-bladed design with thin, backward-curved edges typical of standard Kaplan turbines.
Both runners were tested at five speeds representative of common hydropower operations, with flow
rates, power outputs and strike probabilities carefully matched to ensure fair comparison. This
comparative analysis allowed for a direct evaluation of the effect of blade shape on injury and mortality
outcomes under equivalent hydraulic conditions. For measures of sublethal impacts (growth, behavior
and blood stress) we only examined the impact of the RHT-type runner and at two rotational velocities
(low: 15.0 m/s, high 27.7 m/s).

 3.1 Conventional vs. RHT Turbine Rates of Injury and Mortality
In the first set of experiments, we directly compared rates of injury and immediate (<48 h) mortality
among fish exposed to turbine passage through either the RHT or a conventional thin-bladed turbine, or
a control (no turbine) passage procedure as outlined in SOPO task 3.0 (appendix 13.2). The goal was
twofold, 1) to document the rates of injury and mortality associated with passage through a thin-bladed
turbine runner representative of the conventional hydropower fleet, and 2) determine whether the thick
and slanted blade design of the RHT would improve survival relative to the thin-bladed conventional
turbine. Observations of fish passage through controlled turbine environments are rare, and such data
was not previously available for any sturgeon species. We conducted fish passage trials over a range of
turbine speeds to see how blade velocity influences rates of injury or mortality. A diagram of the
experimental design is shown in Figure 2.

 3.2 Sublethal Impacts on Growth
The second set of experiments explored delayed mortality and sublethal impacts on sturgeon growth due
to passage through the RHT as outlined in SOPO subtask 4.1 (appendix 13.2). Sublethal impacts to
growth can have effects on the long-term outcomes for an individual. Smaller fish are more likely to be
predated upon (Baird et al. 2020), more likely to lose in interactions with competitors for food or habitat
(Ward et al. 2006), and may be less capable of completing important life-history events (e.g., migration,
Chaparro-Pedraza and de Roos 2021).

 14

These experiments involved exposing fish to a passage event and then monitoring them for several
weeks to assess growth rate and to track any instances of delayed mortality. Passage events were
conducted either through the RHT turbine or through a Control pipeline with no exposure to runner
blades. They were also conducted at one of two Passage Velocities: High (27.6 m/s peripheral speed or
976 rpm) or Low (15.0 m/s peripheral speed or 521 rpm). Additionally, two Treatment (RHT and
Control) groups were exposed to two passage events, separated by 3 days, to investigate whether
sublethal impacts of passage compound with repeated exposure. Finally, two treatment groups had no
exposure to passage (RHT or Control) and were maintained in tanks at Natel Energy and the Center for
Aquatic Biology and Aquaculture as additional Non-Passage Controls. A graphical representation of this
set of experiments can be found in Figure 3. We also observed instances of injuries on these fish to
assess whether injury rate differed across treatment conditions (i.e., Passage Velocity, Repeated Passage,
Turbine Condition). If fish exposed to both RHT and control treatments had comparable growth rates,
this would indicate that the RHT did not sublethally impact growth. Likewise, if fish exposed to high or
low velocities exhibited comparable growth rates, we would conclude that Passage Velocity did not
impact growth. Finally, should those fish which were exposed to two passage events have comparable
growth rates to those which only experienced one, then we would conclude that repeated passage did not
compound the sublethal impacts on growth associated with passage.

 3.3 Sublethal Impacts on Behavior
Our third set of experiments sought to explore sublethal impacts of turbine passage on fish behavior as
outlined in SOPO subtask 4.2 (appendix 13.2). Behavior is the mechanism by which fish interact with
their world and respond to challenges. Behavioral changes such as reduced swimming activity or altered

 15

reaction to novel stimuli can impact a fish’s ability to avoid predation, forage for food, or engage in
important life-history events (e.g. migration and spawning).

For this set of experiments, we recorded individual fish behaviors before and after a passage event.
During each of these recording periods, we exposed the fish to a novel object (LEGO construction,
Appendix X). From each video, we measured the distance the fish traveled and estimated its breathing
rate. Treatment groups were similar to those used in the second suite of experiments, with passage
events being conducted with either the RHT or a control exposure, at either high or low velocity. We
also repeated the passage event with the cohort of fish exposed to the high velocity three days after their
first passage event to assess whether fish became habituated to the stressor after a prior exposure. A
graphical representation of this experiment can be found in Figure 4. As each fish was repeatedly
measured for its behavior at several timepoints (before and after introduction of a novel object, or before
and after a passage event) we evaluated the sublethal impact of each treatment (turbine type, passage
velocity, number of passage events) via the change in each behavioral response variable (fish movement,
breaths per minute). For fish movement specifically, a decrease in distance traveled after the passage
event would indicate that the fish had altered its behavior to be less exploratory, a change that could
have consequences for finding food resources (Sneddon et al. 20016). We also quantified the opercular
breathing rate (OBR) of fish during each recording period. Changes in OBR or ventilation rate have
been used as a behavioral indicator of stress with fish commonly changing their OBR in response to a
stressor (Bell et al. 2010).

 3.4 Passage Impacts on Stress Response

 16

Our fourth set of experiments measured markers of stress in the blood of sturgeon exposed to different
passage conditions as outlined in SOPO subtask 4.3 (appendix 13.2). The stress response of fish is a
widely studied aspect of fish physiology, and the concentration and duration of stress markers in the
blood can be used as an indicator of the severity of a stress event (Barton and Iwama 1991). We looked
at three blood stress markers: cortisol, lactate and glucose. In fishes following a stressful event, the
hypothalamus-pituitary-interrenal (HPI) axis is stimulated to release cortisol into the blood. Cortisol, a
primary glucocorticoid, signals mobilization of glucose to be used as fuel to respond to increased
energetic needs (Barton and Iwama 1991). In addition, when anaerobic metabolism is triggered due to
oxygen limitation in tissues, lactate begins to accumulate in the blood as a byproduct (Cech et al. 1996).
These three metrics measured together can help interpret differences in the level of stress an event or
treatment elicits within individuals. Repeated measurements through time are designed to assess the
magnitude and duration of the stress response, as well as the time it takes for an individual to recover to
baseline levels.

For this set of experiments, we exposed groups (n=5) of fish to a passage event (Control or RHT
passage, High or Low speed, Single passage or Repeat passages). Groups were kept separate so the
blood of individual fish could be sampled at set timepoints (0.0, 0.33, 1.0, 3.0, 6.0, 24.0 h, n = 10-15 per
timepoint) after passage. We also sampled fish who were not exposed to a passage event directly from
the holding tank (Non-Passage Controls, N = 126). Blood samples were analyzed for the concentration
of our three blood stress markers as outlined in SOPO task 5.0 (appendix 13.2). Typically, conditions
which lead to lower concentrations of circulating stress markers and faster return to baseline
concentrations are indicative of a less-severe stress response. A graphical representation of this
experiment can be found in Figure 5.

4. Methods
.1 Test Facility and Animal Husbandry
4.1.1. Turbine Testing Facility

Turbine passage testing occurred at the Natel Energy hydraulic turbine and fish passage testing facility
in Alameda, California (Figure 6). The testing facility consisted of a closed-loop recirculating system
with a 76,000 L sump tank, pipeline, 336 kW pump, and 55 cm diameter turbine. The turbine generator
was connected to a variable frequency drive allowing for variable speed control. Net head across the
turbine was measured with a Rosemount 3051C differential pressure sensor (-25.4 to 25.4 mH₂O ±
0.04%) and flow rate was measured with a Krohne Enviromag 2000 electromagnetic flow meter ( -12 to
12 m/s ± 0.2%).

In a standard test, a group of five fish were released into the pipeline via an injector, which consisted of
a pressure canister connected to an air compressor line and a 10 cm diameter transparent pipe with a
knife gate valve (Figure 2c) (Watson et al. 2022). After increasing the pressure in the injector canister to
match that of the turbine testing pipeline, the valve was opened and fish were released into the pipeline.
For treatment releases, the injector was inserted into the pipeline 8.7 m upstream of the turbine, and for
control releases, it was inserted 5.5 m downstream of the turbine. The inner diameter of the pipeline

 18

upstream of the turbine was 0.56 m, increasing to 0.71 m just downstream of the turbine. When
assembled, the transparent injector outlet pipe extended into the center of the flow cross-section. The
control injector was removed from the pipeline during treatment releases, to eliminate the risk of
treatment fish colliding with it. The average flow velocity in the pipeline increased proportionally with
flow according to the operating condition, ranging from 2.4 m/s to 4.7 m/s.

The runner was encased in a transparent acrylic housing to allow for observation of the fish passage
through the runner during turbine operation. Two high-speed video cameras, one monochrome (Chronos
1.4, 16 GB, with Canon EF 8–15-mm f/4L Fisheye lens) and one colour (Chronos 1.4, 32 GB, with
Computar 8-mm f/1.4 prime lens), were placed on opposite sides of the housing and captured footage at
1280x1024 pixel resolution and 1069 fps. The region was illuminated externally through the transparent
housing with two Godox SL150W II LED Video lights (150 watts).

To enable extraction of the fish from the pipeline, a conical wedgewire screen was installed downstream
of the turbine and control injectors (Figure 6). The included half-angle of this conical screen was
approximately 7.9° to the flow and was constructed of 2 mm stainless steel wedgewire. The downstream
end of the screen led to a 150 mm inner diameter, 3 m long flexible hose terminating in a 200 mm
diameter diffuser, which released the fish into a circular, 1.83 m diameter galvanized steel recapture tub.
Water and fish were released tangentially along the wall of the cylindrical recapture tub. An elongated
rectangular cutout screened with soft fabric mesh (3 mm hole size) allowed flow to exit the recapture tub
into the sump tank.

4.1.2. Runner specifications

Two 55 cm diameter runners were designed by Natel Energy and manufactured for the turbine passage
trials (Table 1). The first runner was an innovative design, with two blades of approximately 100 mm
leading edge thickness emerging from the hub with a forward-sweeping slant toward the maximum
radius (Figure 7a). These geometric characteristics are representative of the RHT design concept. The
second runner was a conventional design, consisting of three fixed blades with a leading edge thickness

 20

of approximately 6 mm (Figure 7b). The blade profile and axial outline of the conventional blades were
derived from traditional Göttingen airfoil geometries (Adolph 1965). In contrast to the RHT-type runner,
the conventional blade’s leading edge curved slightly backward at the maximum radius, which is typical
of most fixed- and variable-pitch Kaplan runners. These runners were designed in fulfillment of SOPO
(appendix 13.2) Task 1.0.

For the comparison between the conventional turbine runner and RHT runner (Section 4.2 & 5.1) tests
were conducted at five speeds—521, 625, 729, 833, and 957 rpm—corresponding to 15.0, 18.0, 21.0,
24.0, and 27.6 m/s peripheral speed. These speeds were selected to cover the peripheral speed range of
most conventional hydropower turbines. Flow rate and power were closely matched for both runners
across all tested speeds (Table 2). The five operating points were selected to preserve kinematic
similitude; i.e., the shaft speed increased with the square root of net head such that the fluid streamlines
were identical, with velocities proportional by a constant scale factor, maintaining the shape and
direction of the flow. For assessments of sublethal impacts (Sections 4.3 & 5.2), only the RHT runner
was used and only at two velocities, a low (15.0 m/s) and a high (27.6 m/s).

4.1.3. Fish Holding and Handling

White Sturgeon were sourced from The Fishery, Inc. aquaculture facility located in Galt, California and
were transported (42 miles) as one day post-hatch larvae to UC Davis’ Center for Aquatic Biology and
Aquaculture (CABA) in Davis, California on April 11, 2024. At CABA, the sturgeon were reared in 850

fed ad libitum of a commercial pellet diet (Rangen, Skretting) before being transported to Natel Energy
hydraulic turbine testing and aquaculture facility. The two sets of trials occurred over two separate days,
two weeks apart: July 29, 2024, for the RHT-type runner and August 13, 2024, for the conventional
runner. Individuals were selected such that the fork length distribution of the fish passed through both

 4.2 Conventional vs. RHT Passage Comparison
4.2.1 Passage Methodology

Experimental procedures were identical for RHT and conventional turbine trials. Control groups were
utilized to account for handling, release, and recapture effects. The control groups underwent identical
handling to treatment groups, except that they were released downstream of the operating turbine. The
RHT trial consisted of 36 passage events (N = 172 fish): 12 control passages (n = 60) and 24 treatment
passages (n=112). The conventional trial also consisted of 36 passage events (N = 180 fish): 13 control
passages (n= 65) and 23 treatment passages (n=115). Each passage event consisted of a group of five
fish, except for one RHT treatment passage with only two fish. All groups underwent preexposure
evaluations, release and recapture, postexposure evaluations, and a 48 h post-passage holding period.

Prior to each passage event, a group of White Sturgeon was gently transferred using a dip net from the
holding tank into a 19 L bucket. Each fish was briefly removed from the bucket and individually
examined for injuries. Preexisting injuries included frayed fins, small fin tears, and abrasions. Since
individual fish were not tagged, the type and location of preexisting injuries were recorded for the entire
group.

Due to the possibility of severe injury associated with turbine passage, all White Sturgeon (treatments
and control) were anesthetized prior to being placed in the injector chamber. Each group of fish was
anesthetized in a 350 mg/L MS-222 solution buffered with 420 mg/L NaHCO₃ and 6.0 g/L NaCl
(InstantOcean™). Fish remained in the anesthetic until loss of equilibrium and their unresponsiveness to
light physical stimulus indicated that they had reached stage IV surgical anesthesia (Summerfelt and
Smith 1990).

Once anesthetized, White Sturgeon were placed into the water-filled injector chamber, and chamber
pressure was increased via air compressor slowly (over a period of 15 to 30 s) to approximately 103 kPa
(10.5 m of head) above atmospheric pressure. The injector valve was then opened quickly (<1 s),
allowing the pressurized air to push the water and fish into the pipeline, and closed after all fish were
observed entering the pipeline (Watson et al. 2022). After passing through the pipeline (including the

 23

turbine, for treatment groups) the fish were extracted from the outlet pipe flow via the conical screen and
recovered from the recapture tub with a dip net. The duration between removal from anesthetic to
recapture at the outlet was kept below one minute to ensure that the fish would remain anesthetized
during turbine passage.

After recapture at the outlet, the White Sturgeon were returned to a buffered MS-222 solution (350 mg/L
MS-222) to re-anesthetize. Immediately upon losing equilibrium, each fish was transferred to a lower
concentration of buffered MS-222 (100 mg/L) to maintain their anesthetized state while they were
inspected for injury. Fish that presented with mortal injuries were immediately euthanized with an
overdose of buffered MS-222 solution (500 mg/L).

Once anesthetized or euthanized, each fish was imaged, weighed (+/- 0.1 g), measured (+/- 0.1 cm), and
evaluated for injuries. Photographs were taken using a Canon Rebel Ti (EOS Rebel T2i) camera with 35
mm lens. The camera was mounted on a tripod above the fish, which was laid on a flat surface. Each
fish was imaged dorsally, then inverted and placed on supports for a ventral image. A visual inspection
was conducted to identify and note injuries across all external body parts (Table 4). Injuries were not
rated for severity. No internal injury assessments were conducted post passage. Postexposure injury
evaluations were performed by a single evaluator experienced in White Sturgeon injury identification.
Injuries were classified as amputations, hemorrhages, scale/scute loss, fin tears and gouges, or dermal
lesions as described in (Mueller et al. 2017).

After injury assessment, all live fish remaining from each group were placed into a 19 L bucket placed
inside one of two partially filled 1136 L circular holding tanks. Each bucket was equipped with a secure
lid and an air and water supply (8 L/hr), as well as holes in the sides to allow water to exit. Each group
of fish was assigned to its own bucket (36 buckets total). Any mortalities occurring within the 48 h hold
period were recorded and removed. Since fish were not tagged, 48 h mortalities were identified within
their group and matched with their post-passage injury assessment based on fork length and other
distinguishing features. After the 48 h holding period, all fish were euthanized with an overdose of
buffered MS-222 (500 mg/L).

4.2.2 Impingements

White Sturgeon were occasionally impinged on the conical screen downstream of the turbine. This
screen was designed to reduce impingement risk by minimizing the ratio of the normal component of
flow velocity to the screen to the parallel component; however, approximately 4.8% (17 out of 352;
RHT: n = 6, control: n = 6, conventional: n = 5) of the fish across all trials were impinged. Since all fish
were anesthetized, impingement likelihood was assumed to be unrelated to swimming ability or injury
status. Impinged fish, identified by their failure to exit the pipeline until turbine shutdown, experienced
being pinned against the conical screen (an injury mechanism distinct from turbine passage). They were
therefore excluded from the injury and survival rate analyses. These impinged fish were immediately
euthanized with an overdose of buffered MS-222 (500 mg/L).

4.2.3 Data Analysis

Conditions for controls were nearly identical between RHT and conventional trials, with the only
difference being a slightly higher flow rate during the RHT trial (see Table 2). As no mortalities were

 24

observed among any control passage fish, we combined control trials from the RHT and conventional
testing and analyzed them as a single group, referred to hereafter as “controls.” Similarly, for injury
analysis a preliminary analysis estimated the ratio of injury rates between the two control varieties (RHT
vs. conventional) as 0.81 (95% CI 0.35 - 1.46). This interval includes 1, therefore the rates of injuries
between these two control types is considered non-significant and further analysis considers them a
single control group.

4.2.3.1 Survival Modeling

White Sturgeon survival rates (both immediate and after 48 h) were assessed using a generalized linear
mixed-effect model assuming a Bernoulli distribution with a logit link function implemented in a
Bayesian framework. Models were constructed using R (v 4.3.2) and the packages brms (Bürkner 2017;
Bürkner 2018) and emmeans (Lenth 2020) and visualized using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). Fish survival
was the response variable and the predictor variables included turbine runner type (categorical), runner
peripheral speed (continuous), fish length (continuous), and a random intercept attributed to the passage
group. Priors used were weakly regularizing but uninformed. Fish that were immediately euthanized
post-passage due to serious injury, such as complete or partial severing, were classified as immediate
mortalities. Any mortalities that occurred within the 48 h postexposure hold period were classified as 48
h mortalities. Model-fit was evaluated using leave-one-out criterion (LOO scores) and the model which
exhibited the lowest LOO score was selected (model summaries in Supplemental Table 1 & 2).
Significance was assigned when the 95% credible interval (95% CI) of the posterior distribution did not
include 0.

4.2.3.2 Injury Rates

Each White Sturgeon received a cumulative injury score. For example, a fish with a torn pelvic fin, a
torn pectoral fin, and a scrape on its rostrum would receive an injury count of 3. Injuries were not scored
differently based on severity due to inherent subjectivity; all injuries including mortal injuries like
severing received an injury value of 1. Injuries recorded pre-passage were attempted to be matched with
injuries recorded post-passage within the 5-fish group. For example, if one fish in a group was recorded
with a split pectoral fin prior to passage, and one fish was recorded with the same injury after passage,
then it was assumed those were the same fish and the pre-passage injury count was subtracted from the
post-passage count.

The injury rate analysis focused on identifying differences among the trial types (control, RHT, or
conventional) as well as any differences across turbine peripheral speeds. The analysis was performed
with generalized linear mixed-effects models using a Poisson distribution including fixed effects of fish
length, turbine runner type, and turbine peripheral speed as both a linear and quadratic predictor. A
random intercept for each passage group was also included in the analysis.

4.2.3.3 High-Speed Video Analysis

Each group that passed through the RHT-type or conventional runner was recorded from two
synchronized high-speed camera angles, one on each side of the runner, to maximize coverage. Videos
were manually reviewed to identify individual fish, which were then matched across both views. In
some cases, if a fish moved out of one camera’s field of view, the other camera could be used to

 25

complete its passage record. Fish were categorized as “struck” if visible contact with a runner blade was
observed. In the RHT trials only, it was sometimes difficult to determine whether a fish made contact or
simply passed very close to a blade. Some fish passages through the RHT-type runner were classified as
“deflected around the blade” if they appeared to be directly in the path of the blade but were visibly
deflected without contact. Due to the subtlety of these interactions, and the limitations of the camera
angles, some deflections may have gone unrecorded, meaning the reported number of “deflections
around the blade” likely underestimates the true value.

4.2.3.4 Simulation of Repeated Passages

To estimate the effect of repeated turbine passages on survival we used our lowest LOO-scoring 48h
survival model to simulate passing through one of four different passage conditions: control passage
with no turbine exposure, RHT-type runner passage, conventional runner passage, and an optimized
conventional runner passage. This ‘optimized conventional turbine’ condition was added to represent the
survival of repeated passage under theoretical conditions optimized for fish survival. For each passage
event, each simulated fish was assigned a randomly generated runner peripheral speed between 15 m/s
and 27.6 m/s. For the ‘optimized conventional turbine’ condition, the randomized runner peripheral
speed was limited to between 19.0 m/s and 22.0 m/s, velocities which yield mean survival probability
greater than 0.80 based upon the best fitting model. We used a simulated fish’s assigned velocity and
passage treatment to predict its likelihood of survival. Then, if a randomly generated number was
smaller than a given fish's predicted survival likelihood, the simulated fish was marked as surviving
passage; otherwise, it was deemed to have died during passage. To assess the impact of repeated
passage, the simulation was rerun on the dataset of all simulated fish that survived passage to represent a
second passage event. This process was iterated to simulate a total of five sequential passage events.
Each iteration of the simulation modeled 100 fish per passage condition. We conducted 10,000
interactions for a total of 4,000,000 simulated fish (1,000,000 per passage treatment).

 4.3 Sublethal Impacts of RHT Passage
Assessments of the sublethal impacts of turbine passage were investigated using the RHT runner and a
control passage. We assessed potential sub-lethal impacts (growth, behavioral and blood stress markers)
among White Sturgeon exposed to different velocities and after a single or repeated passage (described
in greater detail below). We did not assess sub-lethal impacts among fish which passed the conventional,
thin-bladed turbine runner described in Section 4.2. We elected to forgo assessing sub-lethal effects of
passage past the conventional turbine runner as we wanted fish to be conscious for properly assessing
blood stress markers and behavioral changes. It was known from research on blade thickness that the
thin-bladed turbine runner posed could severely injury our passage fish (Amaral et al. 2020), which
would preclude us from effectively measuring sub-lethal impacts and necessitate exposing greater
numbers of fish to injury to achieve proper statistical power. We posit that any sub-lethal effects
experienced by fish passing the RHT would be at most equal to those of fish passing conventional
turbines, if not reduced given the differences in rates of injuries (Section 5.1.3).

4.3.1 Growth Measurements

 26

White Sturgeon (n = 160) were selected for size and good condition (118.9 ± 3.7 mm, target 120mm)
and then subsequently VIE tagged ventrally posterior to the anus. Color and location of the tags (e.g. left
vs. right, forward, or back) were to uniquely identify each fish. Fish were anesthetized with MS-222
(100 mg/l) and allowed to recover for seven days (Long et al. 2024, Kozlowski et al. 2017). During the
tagging procedure, fish were weighed (± 0.01 g) and measured for fork and total length (± 0.1 cm). Prior
to a passage event fish were housed in a 6’ tank with an attending 1000 gallon partially recirculating
aquaculture system.

An arbitrary selection of tagged White Sturgeon (n = 140) were trucked to Natel Energy Inc. two days
prior to their initial passage trial. Fish were not provided feed during their time at Natel. The 20
remaining fish stayed at CABA and were kept on feed to establish a baseline growth rate of unperturbed
fish. This process fulfilled SOPO subtask 2.1 and 2.2 (appendix 13.2)

For testing, White Sturgeon were chosen arbitrarily and placed into an injector and transported (~30m)
to the test loop. Fish were then either loaded into a control or test injector which was then pressurized
(as described above section 4.2.1) and subsequently released into the flow of water. Fish then passed
downstream and were removed from the water column using a conical wire-mesh screen which
deposited fish into the recovery tank. Fish were then quickly netted and placed into a recovery bucket
that was carried over to a station where fish were weighed, measured, and their VIE Tags recorded.
Additionally, fish were inspected for physical damage to their fins, eyes, scutes, gills, and epidermis.
Any active bleeding was noted.

After three days, an arbitrarily selected subset of fish exposed to turbine passage (n = 20) or control
passage (n = 20) at high speed were retested. Their test treatment (turbine vs. control) was the same as
their initial passage. Post-passage fish were assessed for external injuries (e.g., fin tears, skin scrapes,
bruising etc.). Injuries were scored as a Boolean value for statistical models.

All fish returned to the Center for Aquatic Biology and Aquaculture (CABA) after 6 days of rearing and
passage trials at Natel Energy. White Sturgeon were arbitrarily split into four rearing tanks (96 cm
diameter, 235 L, n = 39-40) with fish placed into tanks without awareness of their VIE code or treatment
condition. Fish were weighed and measured for five weeks post passage.

Four fish (of 140 fish-passage-events, 2.8%) were impinged upon the conical wire-mesh screen. In these
instances, the hydraulic pressure in the test loop would be reduced until the fish was dislodged and
flushed out of the system. Among fish used in growth trials, impinged fish were identified using their
VIE codes and then returned to a post-recovery tank. Fish that were impinged were not used in repeated
passage trials, but their growth was continually measured. Impingement resulted in the mortality of one
fish approximately 6 hours after passage, so no growth data was gathered on this fish.

4.3.1.2 Injury Analysis

After each passage event, we assessed the fish for any instances of external injury. These data were used
to assess whether different passage conditions (RHT vs. control passage, high vs. low velocity, single vs.
repeated passage) led to different rates of injury and whether injuries were associated with growth rate.
We evaluated injury as a categorical variable (injured vs. non-injured) with any detected injury leading a

 27

fish to be considered injured. This analysis was limited to only fish which underwent passage trails (n =
136). Due to the unbalanced distribution of injuries among treatment groups, we used separate linear
models to assess whether injury rates were influenced by velocity, turbine type, or repeated passage.

4.3.1.3 Statistical Analysis

We assessed the impact of turbine passage on growth by calculating a linear growth rate (g day-1) for
each fish using measurements acquired after completing its passage trials and being returned to CABA.
155 of the 160 original fish were used in our growth analysis, two were removed due to mortality post-
passage, and three became impinged within the test apparatus.

We used Bayesian linear models to assess differences in growth rate between passage types and non-
passage controls. We transformed growth rate into z-scores to improve model fitting, growth rate is
reported below as untransformed. Model predictors were categorical and included passage velocity (low
or high), turbine exposure (turbine or no turbine) and number of passages (single or double) whether the
fish was a non-passage control and whether the fish was brought to the Natel test facility or maintained
at CABA. Additional interactions between turbine exposure and velocity and turbine exposure and
number of passages were included to reflect our partially-nested treatments. Additional random effects
for the specific tank the fish were reared at CABA, as well as the turbine passage cohort. Models were
implemented using the package brms (Bürkner 2017; Bürkner 2018) posteriors were compared using
emmeans (Lenth 2020) and visualized using ggplot2 and tidybayes (Wickham 2016). Significance was
assigned if the 89% credible interval of the posterior distribution of pairwise comparisons did not
overlap 0. Models used uniformed and weakly regularizing priors.

We constructed a simple single-predictor linear model regressing growth rate against presence/absence
of injuries to test whether fish with injuries grew at a different rate than those with recorded injuries.
This model used a Gaussian distribution for growth rate (standardized to Z-scores) and weakly
regularizing priors. Separately we investigated whether rates of injuries were influenced by the speed,
passage type and number of passages that a fish experienced. These models used injury
(presence/absence) as the response variable, modeled using a Bernoulli distribution and weakly
regularizing priors. Each model assessed only single predictors (passage type, velocity, repeated
passage). For the models investigating turbine type and velocity we only used injury data on fish
observed after their first passage event so as to not confound results with the impacts of a second
passage attempt. For the model investigating the repeated passage, we used each fish only once, with
each fish’s injury status assigned a treatment value reflecting when it was censused (after the first round
[n = 59], after the second round [n = 39], or after the first round but contemporaneously with 2nd round
[n = 18]). This approach allows us to compare rates of injury between the first and second passage
events while controlling for the effect that the 3-days between passage events may have on the
detectability of prior injuries. These statistical methods were employed to fulfill SOPO subtask 6.1
(appendix 13.2)

4.3.2 White Sturgeon Behavior

4.3.2.1 White Sturgeon Passage and Behavior Trials

 28

White Sturgeon (n = 80) were size selected (11.8 ± 0.6 cm) and then subsequently VIE tagged ventrally
behind the anus. Fish were anesthetized with buffered MS-222 [100mg/L] and recovered for eight days.
During the tagging procedure, fish were weighed and measured for fork and total length. Fish (n = 80)
were trucked to Natel Energy two days prior to their passage trial, as outlined in SOPO subtask 2.1 and
2.2 (appendix 13.2)

After two days of recovery at Natel Energy, we conducted the initial behavior trials. Behavior trials
consisted of a 20-minute recording prior to passage, followed by another 20-minute recording post-
passage (Figure 4). Each 20-minute behavioral recording included the introduction of a novel object
(Lego brick constructions) after the first 10 minutes. Fish never encountered the same novel object
among their behavioral trials. Behavioral trials included just a single fish, but five fish would be trialed
simultaneously and be injected into the test loop together.

White Sturgeon were gently netted from their rearing tank and transported via bucket to the behavioral
setup. Fish were quickly handled in order to determine their VIE code and then placed into the
behavioral tanks. After their 20-minute recording, fish were gently netted and placed into the injector
(with water and aerator) and brought to the turbine test stand for either a Control or RHT passage at high
(27.6 m/s) or low (15.0 m/s) turbine speed.

After passage, White Sturgeon were collected from the recovery tank, placed into a bucket, and brought
back to the behavioral system. Fish were then briefly handled to read their VIE codes and placed into the
same behavioral arena as their pre-passage recording. After their second recording, fish were caught
with a net and weighed and measured prior to being placed into recovery tanks for a subsequent passage
event or humanely euthanized using a buffered solution of MS-222 [500 mg/L].

After 3 days, White Sturgeon exposed to the high speed passage treatment underwent a second passage
event. The treatment of their second passage (Control or RHT) was the same as their initial passage
event. Methodology for fish undergoing the second passage event was identical to that of the initial
passage event with the exception that the behavioral arena was not preserved between passage events,
and neither was the passage cohort of 5 fish.

4.3.2.2 Description of Behavior Arena

Each behavioral arena consisted of a 55-gallon white Nalgene cylindrical tank (91.4 cm height, 55.9 cm
diameter) containing a shelter structure made of white 4” PVC pipe cut in half lengthwise and cut to 15
centimeters long. Arenas were filmed from above using Dragon Touch 4K cameras (Vision 3, Model
PG90) at an approximate height of 91 cm. Arenas were placed atop light tables illuminated with LED
lights (30 Watt, Model # SHOP/3X1/840/HD/4) which served to backlight the videos. During
recordings, arenas were covered with translucent sheeting to prevent external disturbances. Arenas were
filled to a depth of 10 cm. Water in each arena was refreshed between individual fish with water from a
temperature-controlled (20 °C) basin.

Video Analysis:

 29

Recordings were cut to length for analysis (10 min) in ETHOVISION (Noldus ™) using the software
ffmpeg (Tomar 2006). Each video was split into a pre- and post-novel object recording. The following
video description codes will be used within this document:

 1. Pre-passage, pre-novel object = PrePass_PreNO
 2. Pre-passage, post novel object = PrePass_PostNO
 3. Post-passage, pre-novel object = PostPass_PreNO
 4. Post-passage, post-novel object = PostPass_PostNO

4.3.2.3 Behavior Video Processing & Data Acquisition (Ethovision methods)

Videos were clipped into their respective behavioral treatments, resulting in four 10-minute videos per
fish (PrePass_PreNO, PrePass_PostNO, PostPass_PreNO, and PostPass_PostNO). Videos were
processed using Ethovision Software (Noldus, Ethovision XT17) to quantify behavior of each video
clip. Videos were analysed at 5 frames per second (fps). The first 30 seconds were not analyzed to
account for habituation to the behavioral chamber and the addition of the novel object. Ethovision data
was then exported and post-processed in R (v 4.3.2) to calculate total distance each fish traveled per clip
from distance traveled per frame.

Additionally, from each PreNO and PostNO recording three non-overlapping 10 s recordings were
randomly sampled (N = 1,228). These recordings were used for the measurement of the opercular
breathing rate (OBR). The number of tidal opercular movements was recorded during each recording
and then averaged. OBR videos were viewed by one of two trained observers, with a subset of 50 videos
being analyzed by both. OBR videos were only analyzed if breathing could be observed for the entire
duration of the 10 s video, and if the fish was positioned in such a manner as to make the counting of
breaths unambiguous. We sought to count breathing in at least three OBR videos for each combination
of fish, passage states (PrePass or PostPass), and presence or absence of the novel object (PreNO or
PostNO). Due to fish location (e.g., in the shelter) or angle relative to the camera, some videos could not
be analyzed.

4.3.2.4 Statistical Methods

Our experimental design allowed for testing multiple questions regarding how turbine passage
influences fish behavior. In the following section we describe each question and our modeling approach
to evaluating each question in completion of SOPO subtask 6.2 (appendix 13.2).

Question 1: How does behavior change after a passage event?

To assess whether the exploratory behavior of White Sturgeon changes directly after a passage event we
assessed the change in exploratory behavior among fish before and after their first passage, prior to
novel objects being introduced. We quantified this change in behavior by dividing the total distance
traveled after passage (PostPass_PreNO) by total distance traveled before passage (PrePass_PreNo). If
the ratio is below 1, it indicates a reduction in exploratory behavior, whereas a value above 1 indicates
an increase in exploratory behavior. If the group values are not different than 1, this shows no change in
exploratory behavior. We assigned significance to the effect of passage (pre v.s post) if the ratio was
found to be different from 1 (p <0.05). This ratio is the response variable in our models of treatment

 30

effects, a log10 transformation was chosen to improve normality of this response variable. We analyzed
the change in activity using a linear mixed-effects model from the lme4 package in R (Bates et al. 2015).
Significance values were achieved using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). Fixed effects
included two categorical variables: Treatment (Control vs. RHT) and Turbine Velocity (Low vs. High)
as well as a random effect of which behavioral chamber they were assigned. Post-hoc pairwise
comparisons among fixed effects were completed using the emmeans package (Lenth 2020),
significance was assigned if the difference between two groups was found to be different from 0 (p <
0.05).

Question 2: Does a passage event have an effect on their response to a novel object?

We calculated a White Sturgeon’s response to a novel object (Novel Object Difference, NOD) by
dividing the total distance traveled after the novel object was introduced (PostNO) by the total distance
traveled before the novel (PreNO) object was introduced. To assess whether the White Sturgeon’s
response to a novel object changes after passage events, we compared the pre-passage NOD
(PrePass_PostNO/PrePass_PreNO) to the post-passage NOD (PostPass_PostNO/PostPass_PreNO). A
ratio below 1 indicates a reduction in exploratory behavior, above 1 indicates an increase in exploratory
behavior, and a value of 1 indicates no change in exploratory behavior. We assigned significance to the
effect of novel objects (pre vs. post) if the ratio was found to be different from 1 (p <0.05). This ratio is
the response variable in our models of treatment effects. A square root transformation was chosen to
improve normality. We analyzed the data using a linear mixed effects model from the lme4 package in R
(Bates et al. 2015). Significance values were achieved using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al.
2017). Fixed effects include three categorical variables: Passage Stage (Pre vs. Post), Treatment (Control
vs. RHT) and Turbine Velocity (Low vs. High) as well as a random effect of which behavioral chamber
they were assigned. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons among fixed effects were completed using the
emmeans package (Lenth 2020), significance was assigned if the difference between two groups was
found to be different from 0 (p < 0.05).

Question 3: Does the Post-Passage NOD change between the 1st and 2nd passage event?

To assess whether the response of White Sturgeon to a novel object changes after the first passage event,
we assessed the change in exploratory behavior (NOD) among fish after their first passage and after
their second passage 3 days later. If fish are able to recover, we would expect the two NOD values to be
the same. If fish do not recover within 3 days, we would expect the NOD to be lower after the second
passage than first passage. We assigned significance to the effect of novel objects (pre vs. post) if the
ratio was found to be different from 1 (p <0.05). This ratio is the response variable in our models of
treatment effects. A square root transformation was chosen to improve normality. We analyzed the data
using a linear mixed effects model from the lme4 package in R (Bates et al. 2015). Significance values
were achieved using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). Fixed effects include two
categorical variables: Repeated Passage (First vs. Second) and Treatment (Control vs. RHT), as well as a
random effect of VIE code to account for individual fish being tested twice. Post-hoc pairwise
comparisons among fixed effects were completed using the emmeans package (Lenth 2020),
significance was assigned if the difference between two groups was found to be different from 0 (p <
0.05).

Question 4: Does the Pre-Passage NOD change between the 1st and 2nd passage event?

 31

To assess whether the exploratory behavior of White Sturgeon recovered from a passage event we
assessed the change in exploratory behavior (NOD) among fish prior to their first passage and prior to
their second passage 3 days later. If fish do recover within 3 days, we would expect the pre-passage
NOD to be the same prior to their first or second passage event. If fish do not recover we would expect
the NOD for fish prior to their 2nd passage event to be different from their NOD prior to their 1st
passage event. We assigned significance to the effect of novel objects (pre v.s post) if the ratio was
found to be different from 1 (p <0.05). This ratio is the response variable in our models of treatment
effects. A square root transformation was chosen to improve normality. We analyzed the data using a
linear mixed effects model from the lme4 package in R (Bates et al. 2015). Significance values were
achieved using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). Fixed effects include two categorical
variables: Repeated Passage (First vs. Second) and Treatment (Control vs. RHT), as well as a random
effect of VIE code to account for individual fish being tested twice. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons
among fixed effects were completed using the emmeans package (Lenth 2020), significance was
assigned if the difference between two groups was found to be different from 0 (p < 0.05).

4.3.2.5 OBR Analysis

We sought to address the following questions in regards to effects on opercular breathing rate (OBR).

 1. How do OBR change with a passage event?

 a. Does the runner tip speed influence the OBR?

 b. Does the presence of an RHT lead to higher or lower OBR than the control passage?

 2. Do White Sturgeon adjust their OBR in response to a novel object?

 a. Does the novel object response in OBR change regarding passage conditions?

 3. Do White Sturgeon exhibit the same OBR response after a 2nd passage?

To assess the impact of our passage conditions on opercular breathing rate (OBR), we conducted two
modeling efforts. Each model was a generalized linear mixed effect model which used a Poisson
distribution (using a log link function) and random effects for the behavioral arena, the observer who
counted the breaths and the individual fish. We used Bayesian models with weakly regularizing priors
and the package emmeans (Lenth 2020) to estimate the marginal mean OBR for each treatment group.
We assigned significance if the 89% credible interval of the model-estimated ratio of two treatment
means did not overlap 1.

To answer questions 1 and 2, the model assessed only the OBR of fish experiencing their first passage
event (n = 810). This model included fixed effects for turbine velocity, runner type (RHT or control
passage), the presence or absence of the novel object, and whether the count occurred before or after the
passage event.

 32

To address question 3, we limited the data to only fish exposed to high velocity passage (n = 418): there
was no low velocity treatment which underwent a repeated passage event). This model included fixed
predictors of passage number (first or second), passage type (RHT or control) and whether the OBR was
counted before or after the passage event. We only included OBR measurements taken prior to the
presence of the novel object.

4.3.3 White Sturgeon Stress Response

4.3.3.1 White Sturgeon passage

A selection of White Sturgeon (n = 600, FL: 16.1 ± 1.1cm) were trucked to Natel Energy two days prior
to their initial passage trial. Fish were not provided feed during their time at Natel. For testing, fish were
chosen randomly from their rearing tank and placed into an injector and transported (~30 m) to the
turbine test stand (see description above section 4.2.1). Fish were not anesthetized Fish were then either
loaded into a control or test injector which was then pressurized (103 kpa above atmospheric) and
subsequently released into the flow of water. Fish then passed downstream and were removed from the
water column using a conical wire-mesh screen which deposited fish into the recovery tank. Fish were
then quickly netted and placed into a recovery bucket that was carried over to either the blood draw
station or to a dedicated recovery bucket in a tank. Recovery buckets are equipped with flow through
water and an airstone to maintain water quality until their sample time.

After three days, a selected subset of White Sturgeon exposed to turbine passage (n = 90) or control
passage (n = 90) at high speed were retested. Their test treatment (turbine vs. control) was the same as
their initial passage. A group of fish (n = 84) were sampled as representing baseline conditions, sampled
directly from the holding tanks in cohorts of 5 (one cohort of 4). These fish were sampled throughout
each day during which we conducted passage trials for stress fish.

4.3.3.2 Blood Sampling and analysis

White Sturgeon were processed after passage at one of six timepoints: 0 h, 0.33 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h.
An additional group of baseline fish (n=84) were sampled throughout each trial day. Of these fish, a
subset passed either the RHT (n = 19) or control passage (n = 15) three days prior to sampling to serve
as a baseline for fish undergoing repeated passages.

On the day of their sampling, these baseline fish did not interact with the turbine test stand and were
simply netted from the holding tank. Immediately following euthanasia via buffered MS-222 (500mg/l),
blood (~500 uL) was collected from the caudal vein using a heparinized needle and syringe (23G and
1ml, respectively). Whole blood was centrifuged for 3 min at 2,000 rpm after which plasma was
separated from the pellet and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at -80C for future
analysis.

Plasma cortisol concentrations were measured using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s
instructions (Salivary Cortisol Immunoassay, Salimentrics, LLC). Briefly, standards and diluted samples
(25 ul) were added to each well in duplicate. Enzyme conjugate was added, and the plate was mixed
then incubated for 1 hr. The plate was then washed 4 times and TBM Substrate Solution was added. The
plate was again mixed followed by another incubation period (30 min). Finally, a stop solution was

 33

added. The plate was mixed and read in a spectrophotometer plate reader at 450 nm within 10 min of
adding the stop solution. Cortisol concentrations (µg dl -1 ) were calculated with a four-parameter
sigmoid standard curve and corrected for dilution factor and intra-assay variation <10%.
The concentrations of glucose and lactate were measured from 65-μl subsamples with an ABL90 FLEX
blood gas analyzer (Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark).

4.3.3.3 Statistical Methods

Blood stress markers were analyzed using a generalized additive model (GAM) which allowed for
fitting the nonlinear relationships between these stress markers and sampling timepoint. For each blood
stress marker, two models were constructed, one which modeled the impact of passage type (RHT or
control passage) and velocity (low or high velocity) and the other which modeled the impact of passage
type and whether the passage was the first or second passage event a fish had experienced. Models were
implemented in a Bayesian framework using the brms package (Bürkner 2017; Bürkner 2018), and aside
from the predictors listed above, all models included a smoothing term for time post-passage (minutes).
In some models, time was transformed either by taking the square root or natural log, which has the
effect of increasing model flexibility at earlier timepoints where we sampled most frequently. Model
selection was performed using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO), which estimates out-of-sample
predictive accuracy to identify the model with the best generalization performance.

Given the nonlinear behavior of our models through time, predictors (e.g., passage type, velocity,
passage number) can be significant during only specific periods of time. For example, two treatments
may exhibit the same concentration at 24 h, but have different concentrations of glucose at the 6 h mark.
To visualize these regions of significant difference, we used the posterior distributions of our models to
quantify the estimated difference in stress marker concentration between treatment pairs (e.g., high vs.
low speed or RHT vs. control passage). If the 89% credible interval of the difference in model-estimated
posterior distribution did not overlap 0, then treatments were considered to be significant. Similarly, we
estimated when stress marker concentrations returned to baseline levels by assessing when the 89%
credible interval of the difference between the model-estimated posterior distribution of stress marker
and the distribution of that stress maker among baseline fish overlapped 0, thereby indicating that the
mean value of passage fish and baseline fish would no longer be considered significantly different. All
statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 or later (R Core Team) and fulfill SOPO subtask
6.3 (appendix 13.2)
.

5. Results
5.1 Conventional vs. RHT Passage Comparison

 • The RHT design was much safer for White Sturgeon than the conventional turbine.
 Immediate survival was greater than 99% for fish passing the RHT, matching survival among
 fish tested under control conditions without blades.

 34

 • The conventional turbine caused high mortality among White Sturgeon. Depending on
 speed, between 40–80% of fish survived passage through the conventional turbine, with many
 deaths caused by direct blade strikes.

 • Injuries were less severe with the RHT. Only one sturgeon died from RHT-related injuries
 within 48 hours, whereas more than half of the injuries from conventional turbines were
 immediately fatal.

 • Repeated passage makes the difference in turbine survival more clear. Simulations showed
 that after five turbine passages, survival was >96% for fish passing the RHT, but dropped below
 20% for fish exposed to conventional turbines.

 • High-speed cameras confirmed fewer dangerous strikes with the RHT. Video footage
 showed White Sturgeon were much less likely to be hit by RHT blades (21.7% struck), and some
 fish were even deflected safely away without contact, a benefit not observed with conventional
       blades (57.9% struck).

 • Dataset for Conventional vs. RHT Passage experimental suite can be found on Datadryad.org
       (DOI: 10.5061/dryad.8kprr4z0g)

5.1.1 Survival Results

The White Sturgeon were of similar size among the RHT, conventional, and control trials. Fork length

5.1.2 Survival modeling

A linear model using a Bernoulli distribution (logit link) was fit to assess the influence of runner
peripheral speed on immediate survival rate. The Bernoulli distribution was selected because survival
outcomes were recorded as binary responses (1 = survived, 0 = mortality), making it the appropriate
distribution for modeling individual-level probability of survival. Model selection was performed using
a leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation approach, which provides robust out-of-sample predictive
performance estimates and is well-suited for comparing models of varying complexity without
overfitting. The best-fitting mode included a categorical predictor for passage type (RHT, conventional,
or control) and a continuous quadratic predictor for speed as well as the random effect for passage
group. Passage type and peripheral speed did not interact. Two other models exhibited non-significantly
different LOO scores, one included an additional predictor of fish length and the other included an
interaction between peripheral speed and runner type. In both cases, the additional terms were not found
to be significant. Runner peripheral speed was found to have a significant effect on immediate survival
for the conventional runner (Figure 8a). Survival rates were lowest at the extreme velocities tested:
55.8%, [95% CI: 33.3, 77.3] at 15.0 m/s and 53.5% [32.6, 74.6] at 27.6 m/s. In contrast, survival peaked
at 84.7% [74.6, 94.4] when the runner operated at 21.0 m/s. Immediate survival was 100% in both the
RHT and control trials, so there was no predicted relationship between survival and runner peripheral
speed.

Similar models were constructed to estimate the impact of peripheral speed on 48 h mortality. The
lowest LOO-score 48 h mortality model had the same structure as the lowest LOO-score immediate
mortality model: a categorical predictor for passage type and a continuous quadratic term for peripheral
speed. Alternate, low-scoring models included fish length or an interaction between velocity and
passage type. In both cases, these predictors did not have a significant effect on model estimates.
Control fish exhibited no mortality and thus there was no predicted relationship with peripheral speed.
The model found that probabilities of survival were not significantly different between the RHT and
control trials, with the lowest rates of survival for either treatment at a velocity of 27.6 m/s (98.1% [95%
CI: 94.4, 100] and 98.9% [96.2, 100] respectively), while the conventional runner elicited reduced
survival ranging from 40.5% [20.4, 60.8] at 27.6 m/s to 80.5% [69.0, 91.4] at 21 m/s (Figure 8b).

5.1.3 Injury rates

The injuries observed in this study were primarily associated with blade strike. The dominant injury
associated with the conventional runner was severing (27%, 30 of 110 passed fish). No severing was
observed in the RHT trial or during control passage events.

Injury likelihood, as with mortality, was highest for the conventional turbine. The best-fitting model
(lowest LOO-score) included fish passage treatment (RHT, conventional, or control) and peripheral
velocity predictor modelled quadratically. Comparably well-fitting models included an interaction of

 38

passage treatment and velocity, or as velocity modeled linearly. In these alternate models, velocity was
always a significant predictor, although the interaction terms were not.

White Sturgeon which passed the conventional runner had greater total rates of injury than either the
control or RHT passages, and only the conventional runner produced mortal injuries (Figure 9). In the
conventional trial, 30% (33 of 110) of fish suffered an immediate mortal injury. Based upon the
counting system, fish undergoing the control passage received a modeled estimated 0.30 [95% CI: 0.19,
0.43] injuries per fish which are attributable to handling or non-turbine related passage injuries (e.g.,
interaction with the wedgewire screen). Fish that passed through the conventional runner had an average
of 0.68 [0.48, 0.91] injuries per fish and of the fish which suffered any injury, 54% (36 of 67 injured
fish) led to immediate mortality. Fish which passed the RHT had an average of 0.45 [0.30, 0.62] injuries
per fish and none led to immediate mortality or required subsequent euthanasia. Across trials, pre-
passage injury rates were low averaging 0.01, 0.04, and 0.02 injuries per fish for control, RHT, and
conventional trials, respectively. Pre-passage injuries consisted of small splits or fraying in the caudal,
pectoral, or pelvic fins.

Turbine velocity was associated with the count of White Sturgeon injuries, with a significantly positive
first-order term and a non-significant but positive second order term, indicating that injuries became
more common as turbine velocity increased. At the lowest peripheral speeds (15.0 m/s) mean injuries
per fish were estimated to be 0.46 [95% CI: 0.29, 0.64] and at the highest peripheral speed (27.6 m/s)
injuries per fish were estimated at 0.84 [0.57-1.10].

White Sturgeon that experienced mortality within 48 h had a range of injuries and injury counts
immediately post-passage. The one 48 h mortality from the RHT trial received an injury count of 4,
which included several injuries to fins, including a split dorsal fin, split caudal fin, hemorrhaging at the
base of pelvic fins, and a split pelvic fin. In the conventional trial, there were six total 48 h mortalities
split evenly between the 21.0 m/s and 27.6 m/s velocity tests. One of these fish received an injury count
of 3 (hemorrhaging in both pelvic fins, detached scutes), two received counts of 1 (hemorrhaging ventral
scutes and hemorrhaging at the base of anal fins), and two received injury counts of 0.

5.1.4 High-speed video analysis

High-speed video footage of turbine passage was captured for all treatment trials. The duration of time
that each White Sturgeon spent passing through the runner region was approximately 0.2 s. Because of
blind spots in the field of view of the two cameras, some fish were able to pass through the runner
unobserved. The proportion of fish that were visible in the video footage was similar for both trials:
82.1% (92 of 112 fish) and 82.6% (95 of 115 fish) for the RHT and conventional trials, respectively. Of
the fish visible, a smaller proportion were observed being struck by a blade of the RHT-type runner
(21.7%, 20 of 92 fish) than the conventional runner (57.9%, 55 of 95 fish). An additional 8.7% of visible
fish (8 of 92 fish) in the RHT trial were observed entering the runner in the direct path of a blade, but
were deflected away without contacting the blade (see Table 5). In contrast, all fish observed in the
direct path of a conventional runner blade were subsequently struck.

Approximately half of the strikes observed in the conventional runner footage were obviously lethal,
with 51% (28 of 55) of visible strikes resulting in immediate mortality. These “clearly mortal” strikes
were categorized as either immediate severing (as illustrated in Figure 10) or “dragging”, in which the
White Sturgeon was caught on the leading edge of the blade and dragged at high speed for numerous
runner rotations. Of the 28 clearly mortal visible strikes, 25 were the immediate severing type, and 3
were the dragging type. All dragging type strikes occurred close to the hub and resulted in axial
disruption of the notochord, although the fish remained intact, due to the high velocities the fish
experienced while being dragged by the blade. Strikes that resulted in severing more commonly
occurred closer to the blade tip. Strikes that occurred closer to the tail region of the fish were less likely
to be mortal.

5.1.5 Simulation of repeated passages

It is possible that a White Sturgeon may pass hydropower turbines repeatedly throughout their life (e.g.,
one dam passed annually during migration, several dams passed in sequence, or frequent exposure
during tidal hydropower). We conducted a simple mathematical simulation to estimate the probability of
survival that could be expected after repeated passage events. We found that the RHT-type turbine
runner exhibited simulated single-passage survival (99.4% [95% CI: 98, 100]) very similar to the control
passage survival (99.7% [99, 100]. For both the Conventional Runner and Optimized Conventional
Runner (where turbine velocity was constricted to a range which yielded survival ≥ 80%) treatments,
survival was significantly lower after a single passage (70.0% [62, 80] and 79.3% [72, 87] respectively)
as compared to the RHT-type turbine. After five simulated passage events survival of the conventional
turbine was 16.8% [10, 24] and 31.4% [23, 41] for the conventional turbine operated at ‘optimized’
velocities, whereas survival among simulated fish which passed the RHT or control passage was 96.9%
[94, 100] or 98.3% [96, 100] respectively (Figure 11).

 5.2 Sublethal Impacts of RHT Passage
 In the following section are reported the results of experiments investigating the sub-lethal effects of
 White Sturgeon passage past the RHT. These trials did not assess the impact of the conventional,
 thin-bladed turbine (see explanation in section 4.3). Sub-lethal effects included impacts to growth
 (section 5.2.1) fish behavior (section 5.2.2) and impacts on circulating levels of stress markers
 (section 5.2.3). Datasets for the sublethal effects of RHT passage can be found on Datadryad.org
    (DOI: 10.5061/dryad.p8cz8wb5v)

5.2.1 Impacts on White Sturgeon Growth

 • White Sturgeon generally kept growing after passage. Sturgeon that passed through the
 turbine grew at similar rates to control fish, with only small reductions in growth observed.

 44

 • Survival remained very high. All White Sturgeon that survived the passage trial also survived
 through the five-week growth period, indicating no delayed mortality effects.

 • Injury risk increased with repeated passage. A single turbine passage did not significantly
 increase injury rates compared to controls, but White Sturgeon that went through turbines twice
 were much more likely to be injured.

5.2.1.1 Passage Effects on Fish Growth

Impacts of passage treatment on the growth rate of White Sturgeon in the subsequent weeks following
passage testing were non-significant, with no passage treatment group exhibiting growth rates different
from non-passage controls (Figure 12: A). However, when modeled collectively passage past the RHT
did lead to a slight, but significant reduction in fish growth relative to both non-passage treatments (Δ -
0.14 g day -1 [-0.27 to -0.02 g day 89% credible interval]) and passage control treatments (Δ -0.11 g day
-1
 [-0.19 to -0.02 g day 89% credible interval]) (Figure 12: B). We found no significant impact of
passage velocity or repeated passage on the growth rates of fish. However, among the low velocity
group, we found that the effect of turbine passage was significant, leading to a statistically significant
reduction in growth rate (Δ -0.14 g day -1 [-0.26 to -0.03 89% C.I.]). All fish which survived passage
trials survived the subsequent 5-week growth period. One fish belonging to the CABA control group
was euthanized due to weight loss after 3 weeks.

5.2.1.2 Injury Analysis Results

White Sturgeon with documented external injuries after their passage trials grew less quickly relative to
their uninjured counterparts (Δ -0.05 g day-1 [-0.13 to 0.01 89% C.I.]), although this association was

did not find any statistically significant difference in the likelihood of injury associated with the RHT
over the control passage. Likewise, we did not find any effect of passage velocity on injury rates with
28% of high velocity fish (17 of 87) and 21% (7 of 32) low velocity fish experiencing an injury.

5.2.2 Impacts on White Sturgeon Behavior

 • White Sturgeon reduced their activity after passage (Question #1). Regardless of turbine
 presence or turbine velocity, fish significantly reduced their activity after passage.

 • Passage did not have an effect on the White Sturgeon’s response to a novel object [NOD]
 (Question #2). The fish’s overall response to introduction of a novel object was to slightly
 increase activity, yet no significant difference in response to the novel object was found between
 treatment variables.

 • Repeated passage may have had an effect on a White Sturgeon’s response to a novel object
 [NOD] (Question #3). While the model identified a significant effect of repeat passage on post-
 passage NOD, post-hoc pairwise comparisons only found this in the Control passage treatment,
 rather than for both RHT and Control passage treatments. This indicates that the passage event
 (handling, transport, trial) may have an effect on NOD, yet the addition of a turbine did not yield
 the same results.

 47

 • White Sturgeon recovered behaviorally from the first passage event within 3 days
 (Question #4). Pre-passage NOD did not significantly differ between first and second passage
 events spaced three days apart.

 • White Sturgeon breathing rate was relatively unchanged by turbine passage (Question #5).
 This lack of change in the opercular breathing rate (OBR) paired with a significant decline in fish
 movement post-passage indicates that passage through the system induced a behavioral change.

5.2.2.5 Question 5. How does fish opercular breathing rate (OBR) change with treatment conditions?

On the whole, we found that the opercular breathing rate (OBR) of juvenile White Sturgeon was
relatively unchanged by our treatment conditions. Notably, the passage treatment (RHT-type or control
passage) did not elicit a significant response under any treatment category (e.g., high or low speed,
before or after the novel object) (Figure 18). Furthermore, for a given treatment condition (e.g., RHT-
type low speed) the OBR was not significantly affected by the passage event itself. The only exception
was among the RHT-type runner at high speed after the novel object had been introduced, we found that
the OBR increased by 2.2 [0.3-4.0 (89% credible interval) breaths per 10 seconds] relative to OBR
measured before passage and after the introduction of the novel object. Finally, we found that the
velocity of passage had nearly no influence on the OBR with the exception of the control passage,
during the pre-novel object period. For this treatment set, low velocity passages led to OBR ~2 breaths
per 10 seconds higher than the high velocity treatments. These few significant results were on the edge
of detection and would not be considered significant if using a 95% credible interval.

5.2.3 Impacts on Stress Response

 • Turbine type did not affect physiological stress response. Blood cortisol, glucose, and lactate
 levels were similar between fish passing the RHT and those in control conditions, indicating the
 RHT does not impose additional physiological stress relative to conventional passage.

 53

 • Higher passage velocity elevated blood glucose, but not lactate. Fish exposed to high-speed
 passage showed greater circulating glucose concentrations than those at low velocity, though
 lactate levels were unaffected by speed — suggesting a moderate, velocity-dependent metabolic
 response.

 • Repeated passage reduced the stress response. Fish on their second turbine passage showed
 lower glucose and lactate concentrations than on their first, and returned to baseline levels more
 quickly, suggesting physiological habituation or acclimation with repeated exposure.

 • Cortisol data were too variable to yield firm conclusions. Cortisol levels varied widely across
 individuals and could not be explained by treatment, velocity, or sampling conditions, limiting
 the strength of inference from that marker alone.

We measured the circulating levels of plasma cortisol, glucose and lactate at specified timepoints
following turbine passage (immediately, 20 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h post-passage) to assess whether
the RHT elicited increased stress response relative to the control passage, and whether passage velocity
or repeated passage impacted the stress response of White Sturgeon.

5.2.3.1 Cortisol Results

Cortisol concentrations were highly variable with some individuals exhibiting levels near 0 while others
had cortisol values exceeding 180 ug/dL. This variation challenged our ability to effectively model these
data as the uneven variation and non-normal residuals violate modeling assumptions and therefore
weaken any inference. Variation in the data could not be attributed to the date or time of the passage
trial, date or time of the sampling, date of sample processing, specific processing plate, or any specific
treatment group or condition. This widespread variation contradicts our hypotheses of a methodological
or experimental cause to the variation (e.g., samples were mishandled on a specific day, or mistakes in
the processing of cortisol assays) and therefore we are unable to draw strong conclusions from the blood
cortisol dataset. We found no consistent significant effects of turbine type (RHT or Control) or passage
velocity (Low vs. High). It does appear that sturgeon exhibited a lower cortisol concentration during
their second passage. This result was significant at the 50% credible interval level, but not at the 89%
credible interval (Figure 20).

6. Discussion
Hydropower provides reliable, low-carbon energy, but may produce multiple negative impacts on
aquatic ecosystems. Improved downstream migration success for aquatic fauna through hydropower
facilities is essential to reconcile the life-history needs of aquatic species with the energy demands of
human society. To date, research efforts have focused both on documenting and understanding the
impacts of hydropower turbines on fish (Pracheil et al. 2016; Dadswell et al. 2018; Algera et al. 2020;
Mueller et al. 2022; Huang and Li 2024) as well as designing turbines to reduce injury and mortality risk
for entrained fish (Cook et al. 2003; Lagarrigue and Frey 2011; Watson et al. 2022; Watson et al. 2023).
The objective of this study was to understand the effect of turbine runner design on injury and mortality
outcomes for White Sturgeon across a range of operationally relevant runner peripheral speeds, flow
rates and head pressures. Two representative runner designs were directly compared for their fish
passage performance: the RHT-type runner, which was deliberately designed to minimize fish mortality,
and a conventional runner, which was designed to represent a typical propeller-type blade profile
common within the existing global hydropower fleet. Analysis of immediate and 48 h survival rates, as
well as qualitative and quantitative assessment of high-speed video revealed substantial differences in
survival outcomes for fish subjected to turbine passage through the two runners.

 6.1 Differences in Acute Mortality and Injury among Turbine Types
6.1.1 Survival results

The rates of White Sturgeon survival observed through the conventional runner in the present study are
consistent with those observed for large fish passing through turbines around the world. Across our test
velocities (15.0 – 27.6 m/s), passage past a conventional turbine yielded 48 h survival rates between
42% to 78%. Dadswell and Rulifson (1994) reviewed rates of survival for several anadromous fishes
passing a low-head tidal turbine and found the results to be species specific, but with an overall mean of
62.3 ± 25.9%. These results are supported by a systematic review of 206 datasets on hydropower
induced fish mortality and injury (Algera et al. 2020). Notably, their results indicate that turbines are the
dominant source of immediate mortality among fishes, with Francis-style turbines being more dangerous
than Kaplan-style turbines.

Among passage trials using the conventional runner, survival was low across all speeds but was
significantly higher at the middle speed (21 m/s). High-speed video analysis also showed a lower strike
occurrence at this middle speed than the other speeds tested. Given that all combinations of turbine
speed and head were kinematically similar (i.e., the ratio of speed at which the flow moves axially
downstream to the speed at which the blades move circumferentially is constant), it is not clear why the
middle speed resulted in a higher survival and a lower strike occurrence rate than other speeds for the
conventional runner trials.

6.1.2 High-speed video

Quantitative analysis of high-speed video footage showed that the RHT-type runner produced both
fewer and less severe strikes compared to the conventional runner. While the conventional runner had
50% more blades than the RHT-type runner (3 blades versus 2), 167% more blade strikes were observed

 61

in the conventional trial (57.9% of fish struck) than the RHT trial (21.7% of fish struck). This result
directly contradicts the hypothesis that increased blade thickness necessarily results in higher strike
probability for fish, which has been proposed in some analyses of blade strike risk for turbine and pump
design (Yang et al. 2023). Instead, there appears to be a deflection effect near the leading edge of the
thick blades that reduces strike probability under the tested conditions, as hypothesized in several prior
works (Solomon 1988; Franke et al. 1997; Turnpenny 1998). Approximately one third of fish (8 of 28)
that were observed entering the RHT-type runner in the direct path of the blade were visibly deflected
away from it without contacting the blade at all. In contrast, all fish that were observed entering the
runner in the direct path of the conventional runner blades were clearly struck. Not only were strikes less
frequent in the RHT trial, but the strikes that did occur were also less severe than those that occurred in
the conventional runner. Nearly half (25 of 55) of all fish that were observed being struck by the
conventional runner blades were immediately severed, even at the lowest peripheral speed tested (15.0
m/s).

6.1.3 Research relevancy to fish passage of deployment scale turbines

This study investigated the effects of turbine passage on small juvenile sturgeon passing through a small,
model-scale turbine. Provided the assumption that the impact of a blade-strike is a function of the ratio
of fish length to blade thickness (EPRI 2008; EPRI 2011; Amaral et al. 2020), similar conclusions may
be drawn for large sturgeon passing through proportionally large turbines. We used fork length to
turbine diameter as a proxy for fork length to blade thickness, because blade thickness will scale
proportionally with turbine diameter for a given runner design. The ratio of sturgeon fork length to
turbine diameter in this study was 0.23 to 0.36. While limited knowledge exists of large sturgeon
passage through hydropower turbines, one published example is the Annapolis Royal Generating
Station, a tidal power hydropower facility in Nova Scotia with a single 7.6 m diameter Straflo turbine
(Dadswell et al. 2018). Since the project’s commissioning in 1985, a total of 21 dead adult Atlantic
sturgeon (ranging from 146 to 203 cm fork length) with clear turbine-related injuries have been
observed floating or on shorelines seaward of the hydropower project, which is likely an underestimate
since dead sturgeon tend to sink. The range of fork length to turbine diameter for the fatally injured
sturgeon at this site is 0.19 to 0.27, similar to the present study. The peripheral speed of the Annapolis
turbine runner is 19.9 m/s, which is also within the operating conditions tested in this study. Crucially,
many of these large individuals exhibited similar severing injuries to the juvenile sturgeon (Figure 25).

Compared to other evaluations of turbines designed for improved fish survival, the speeds tested in this
study were relatively high, up to 27.6 m/s at the blade periphery. Among similar turbine passage
evaluations (Table 6), only the minimum gap runner (MGR) has been evaluated at comparable or higher
speeds, up to 34.8 m/s peripheral speed (Dauble et al. 2007). These high-speed units also stand out as
retrofits for existing hydropower turbines, as opposed to new construction with highly different civil
works and generator requirements compared to conventional hydropower. The survival rate (>99%) of
juvenile White Sturgeon through the model-scale RHT-type runner exceeds that of the 7.1 diameter
MGR unit (96% survival) at the Bonneville Dam (Normandeau Associates Inc. et al. 2000), the 7.7m
AHT unit (98% survival) at the Wanapum dam (Dauble et al. 2007) and the 7.0 m unit (98% survival) at
the Ice Harbor Dam (Heisey et al. 2019). This is notable, as the fish tested passing these large turbines
were similar in length to the juvenile White Sturgeon we studied (balloon-tagged juvenile chinook
salmon, mean fork length 16.6 cm at Bonneville Dam [Normandeau Associates Inc. et al. 2000], 16.9
cm at Wanapum [Dauble et al. 2007], 13.0 cm at Ice Harbor Dam [Heisey et al. 2019]), but much
smaller relative to the turbine diameter. This suggests that RHT-type runners could be a viable retrofit
option for enabling very high fish survival rates at similar high-speed large hydropower facilities and for
proportionally much larger fish.

When turbines and pumps are operated at peripheral speeds above those tested for fish impacts, survival
rates tend to be lower than expected. Research on the Fairbanks Nijhuis Axial Flow Pump demonstrated
much lower than expected survival rates for common bream (Abramis brama, 24%) and common roach
(Rutilus rutilus, 70%) in field testing (Bruneel et al. 2024). Expectations for survival were based on a
smaller-scale study conducted at a single peripheral speed of 13.9 m/s (Vriese 2009), whereas the field
tests operated at higher speeds of 14.7 and 17.3 m/s. Turbines and pumps designed for high fish survival
should be evaluated across the range of speeds relevant to real-world operations. In the present study,
the RHT-type runner maintained a high rate of survival across the range of peripheral speeds (15.0 to
27.6 m/s) providing a broad range of deployment speeds over which fish survival is expected to be high.
Unexpectedly the conventional runner had lower survival at our lowest and highest (15.0 % 27.6 m/s)
peripheral tip speeds, and higher survival at a middle speed (21.0 m/s). This highlights that the
relationship between rotational speed and survival outcome may not be linear and multiple velocities
should be tested when assessing fish safety.

6.1.4 Simulated Impacts of Repeated Passage

Sturgeon are iteroparous, and thus their life histories require repeated spawning migrations over years
(Rochard et al. 1990), typically necessitating movement into large river systems. The sympatry of
sturgeon species with hydropower (Figure 1), and their life-history reliance on repeated, bi-directional
movement within the river system ensures repeated interaction with hydropower installments. As we
have demonstrated, these interactions can prove acutely lethal to sturgeon in the laboratory and in a field
setting (e.g. Dadswell et al. 2018). We simulated the impact of repeated passage to explore how a
population of fish can be rapidly winnowed via repeated or sequential passage events. As an extant
example, the Annapolis Royal Generating Station in Nova Scotia captures the large tidal flux of the Bay
of Fundy twice per day. Each time the tide rises, there is the opportunity for an Atlantic sturgeon to
become trapped above the dam and subsequently pass through the turbine as the tide recedes. In this
case, a fish could conceivably pass through a given turbine twice a day. Our simulation results indicate
that when using a conventional runner only two to three passages are required to reduce the passed
population to 50% of its original number. We believe our simulation to be reasonable as work by
Dadswell et al. 2018 studying the Annapolis Royal Generating Station demonstrated its impact on
American Shad (A. sapidissima), Striped Nass (Morone saxatilis), and Atlantic Sturgeon population size
and structure. After hydropower operations commenced, populations of these fish became younger,
smaller and contained fewer repeating spawners. These population-level impacts are consistent with
strong, size-dependent selective pressure, such as repeated passage past conventional hydropower
turbines. Given the long lives of sturgeon (>40 years) the default expectation should be that if upstream
movement is facilitated (e.g. tidally, sturgeon passage structures, etc.), then repeated downstream
passage will occur. If safe downstream passage is not facilitated, then the high rates of injury and
mortality will serve as lethal barriers to migration, thereby decimating populations and fragmenting
habitats.

6.1.5 Maximum Safe Operating Procedure

As described above, we found that the injury and mortality rates of White Sturgeon exposed to the RHT-
type turbine was not statistically different from control passage at any velocity. The thin-bladed
conventional turbine however was unsafe at all measured speeds (peripheral tip velocity ranging from
15.0 to 27.6 m/s). This is further exemplified by our simulation where even under operationally
optimized conditions the thin-bladed conventional turbine led to a rapid decline in surviving fish. We
expected that at low speeds (e.g. 15.0 m/s) the rates of mortality and injury would be reduced, however
our results indicated that velocities in the middle of the measured range (e.g. 21.0 m/s) led to greater
rates of survival. Further research should investigate this phenomenon as it may indicate some
hydrodynamical process that increases fish survival. The RHT-type runner did not show a similar
relationship with runner tip velocity with near-complete survival across the full range of tested speeds.

 6.2 Sublethal Effects of RHT-Type Passage
Sublethal effects of turbine passage on sturgeon are poorly understood. We hypothesized that sublethal
injuries incurred during passage would lead to affected physiological traits important to fish fitness (e.g.,
effects on feeding and growth, changes in fish behavior, or stress responses). These response traits were
selected to provide different vantages on the sub-lethal effects of turbine passage. Sublethal stressors in

 64

fish can have a range of negative consequences, including reduced reproductive fitness, increased
energy expenditure, and impaired immune function (Baktoft et al. 2020; Ammar et al. 2020; Watson et
al. 2020). Note, we did not investigate rates of sublethal impacts among fish that passed a conventional
thin-bladed turbine runner. We posit that sublethal effects of turbine passage are correlated to lethal
effects, at least directionally if not in magnitude or frequency (i.e., greater rates of serious injury or
mortality lead to more severe or numerous sublethal impacts). Under this assumption, the high rates of
injury and mortality caused by passage past the conventional runner would be expected to yield greater
sublethal effects than RHT passage. Therefore, we assume that the sublethal impacts discussed below
would at least be equivalent among fish passing a conventional turbine blade, if not more severe.

6.2.1 Growth & Injury Discussion

Fish growth rate is commonly interpreted as a measure of fish health and suitability of habitat conditions
(Sneddon et al. 2016). As a holistic physiological trait it combines multiple physiological and ecological
processes (e.g., metabolic efficiency, food abundance and risk of foraging, energetic costs of disease).
Therefore, reductions of a fish’s growth rate can be indicative of physiological limitations (e.g., lack of
food or foraging, energetic demands of injury or infection, etc.). We assessed the growth rate of White
Sturgeon that underwent our RHT passage treatments for 5 weeks post-passage to determine whether
there was any effect on growth rate attributable to passage, and whether passage velocity, turbine type or
repeated passages influenced this sub-lethal effect.

The thick, swept blades of the RHT runner appears to have minimal effects on the growth of juvenile
White Sturgeon under the passage conditions tested (velocities between 15 and 27.9 m/s and a fish
length to blade thickness ratio of ~1.5). All fish which successfully passed the turbine survived the 5-
week post-trial period. We did find a slight decrease in the growth rate among fish which passed the
RHT opposed to those fish which underwent the control passage, or experienced no passage event at
all (0.67 vs. 0.75 vs. 0.76 g day-1 respectively) indicating that interaction with a turbine may yield a
small, negative effect on fish growth. The model-estimated reduction in growth rate was ~11% of the
mean growth rate of fish which did not pass the RHT. This reduction in growth can be considered
modest relative to other ecological drivers of growth rate. For comparison, a 3°C decrease in rearing
temperature (19 vs. 16°C) led to a 60% reduction in growth rate among green sturgeon (Poletto et al.
2018), and 60% reduction in feed led to nearly an 80% reduction in growth rate (Poletto et al. 2018).
Work on White Sturgeon found that a 25% reduction in food led to a 20% reduction in fish growth, and
a 50% reduction in food led to a 49% reduction in fish growth (Hung et al. 1989). As sturgeon
commonly migrate to reach greater food resources (Auer et al. 1996), it could be that increasing fish
passage efficiency to habitats of greater productivity could counteract the small negative effects of
passage.

We hypothesized that passage-related effects on growth may be attributable to increased rates of injury
under the assumption that recovery and repair from injuries requires energetic resources that would
otherwise be utilized for somatic growth (Griffen et al. 2023, Firkus et al. 2023). However, we did not
detect a difference in the rates of injuries among fish passing the RHT vs. the control passage, nor did
the repeated passage fish which experienced two passage events show slower growth rates than their
single-passage counterparts. We did find that fish which suffered an injury grew slower -0.05 g day-1,
although this effect was not significant at the 89% threshold. We were only able to detect visible,
external injuries (e.g. torn fins, bruising etc.) and any undetectable internal injuries could cause reduced

 65

growth but obscure our ability to detect a clear effect. Despite this limitation, we did not detect any
difference in external injury rates between turbine and control passages, indicating that most observed
injuries could be attributed to the passage apparatus and not the RHT, a result in keeping with the
turbines design which sought to distribute blade strike impact across a thick, swept, surface area and
avoid strikes entirely by displacing fish with a cushion of water. Further developments in improving the
passage of fish through hydropower systems such as more streamlined piping or reductions in pressure
differentials could further alleviate the impacts of passage observed in the present study.

6.2.2 Impacts on White Sturgeon Behavior

Behavior is often the initial mode through which organisms interact with the wider environment.
Behaviors like foraging for food, seeking shelter to avoid predation, or migrating to safer or more
productive habitats are essential to the successful recruitment of fish into a population. Fish behavior can
often be impacted even though no physical injury occurred, for example a fish may reduce foraging or
movement activity after chemically or visually detecting a predator in the environment (Baird et al.
2019, Bell et al. 2010). Such behavioral modifications can lead to fitness consequences (Watson et al.
2020). Therefore, a hypothetical fish could pass a hydropower turbine uninjured and still exhibit
detrimental behavioral changes. For example, post passage a fish may cease eating, or fail to respond to
predator cues, either of these behavioral changes could lead to the fish’s death and consequently be a
negative externality of the hydropower turbine (Colotelo et al. 2017). We sought to assess whether fish
which passed the hydropower turbines altered their behavior in response to the RHT (or Control
Passage), turbine velocity and repeated passage. Fish were exposed to a novel object to see if their
exploratory behavior changes with turbine passage.

The results from our growth study indicate that White Sturgeon readily returned to feed after passage of
the RHT, as fish in all passage treatment groups continued to grow post exposure. The post-passage fish
did exhibit a clear reduction in movement, which could reduce foraging. We were unable to document
how long this change in behavior would last beyond our 20-minute behavioral observation period but
fish had recovered when observed again three days later providing an upper bound on the duration of
behavioral impact associated with turbine passage.

Predator response impacts may be more relevant, as the tailrace of hydropower installations is a
common predator hotspot (Coutant and Whitney 2000, Reiman et al. 1991) where disoriented fish are
deposited post passage. The reduction in movement post passage may make White Sturgeon which have
passed a hydropower turbine more likely to be predated upon as they are less likely to leave the high-
risk tailrace area. We did not detect any effect associated with RHT vs. the non-turbine Control Passage,
so the behavioral effect is more likely due to the stress and discombobulation associated with
hydropower passage, rather than direct interaction with the runner (e.g. blade strike). The RHT does not
appear to increase or compound this effect. Furthermore, injured or severed fish, as may be observed by
fish passing a conventional turbine are more likely to be predated upon and the subsidy of easy prey may
amplify the risk of predation by attracting additional predators (Coutant and Whitney 2000, Schilt 2007,
Rieman et al. 1991). Future research on the ecological impacts of fish-safe turbines could investigate
whether the installation of RHT-type turbines reduces the predation risk by shrinking the abundance of
waiting predators due to fish passing in better condition. Given the observed impact of a passage event
on fish mobility and the duration of the sturgeon stress response (blood glucose and lactate

 66

concentrations), additional passage improvements may be achieved by depositing passed fish into
sheltered habitats where they can recover (up to 24hrs) prior to returning to the river.

We found that passage of any kind (RHT vs. Control, High vs. Low speed) did not have an effect on a
fish’s opercular breathing rate (OBR). In a prior study studying stressors on sturgeon, a reduction in
OBR (or ventilation rate) was observed (McLean et al. 2016), however in this study the stressor included
an exhaustive component (e.g., chasing to simulate fishery capture), whereas in the present study
sturgeon were not forced to swim. This difference in methodology, and presumably the final aerobic
state of the fish may account for this difference in response. Notably while the OBR of sturgeon did not
change with passage, their overall movement declined. This mismatch between OBR and fish movement
implies that post passage, fish were experiencing a stress response and engaging in a “freeze” behavior
wherein their aerobic resources were maintained (similar OBR) but their activity was reduced (lower
movement).

6.2.3 Sublethal Stress Markers

We quantified the concentration of glucose, lactate and cortisol in the blood of White Sturgeon at
specific timepoints after a passage event. While the cortisol results were too variable for inference, our
measures of glucose and lactate concentrations do indicate that the fish elicited a stress response to both
the RHT or Control passage, exhibiting a typical increase in stress markers followed by a subsequent
return to baseline levels. Our results indicate that the presence of the RHT runner did not alter the
timecourse or overall concentration of stress markers relative to the Control Passage, indicating that the
RHT runner likely does not cause additional stress to fish and the response we detected was likely the
result of the fish handling itself. We did find that repeated passage reduced the concentration of
circulating glucose, which could be due to differences in fasting time between fish on their first passage
trial (~2 days fasted) vs. their second passage trial (~5 days fasted). It could also be an indication that the
stress response was not as severe after a second exposure to passage.

For both glucose and lactate, circulating levels returned to baseline concentrations within 24 hours after
the passage event. Paired with our indistinguishable differences in stress markers between fish exposed
to the RHT and those exposed to the control passage, these data indicate that sturgeon passage past an
RHT runner does not lead to an increased or persistent stress response greater than passage through a
runner-less control pipe. It is possible that the stress response of the fish was maximally engaged by the
handling and transport associated with either the RHT or the Control Passage, in this case we would be
unable to detect an RHT-associated change in the stress response.

We did not measure the stress response of fish exposed to the thin-bladed conventional turbine (due to
anesthetization requirements) so we cannot definitively say that the stress response of fish passing the
conventional turbine would be greater or more persistent than that of fish passing the RHT. However,
research by Griffin et al. (2024) on Striped Bass (Morone saxatilis) found that stress indicators increased
with duration of angling pressure and severity of hooking injuries, and research by Weirup et al. (2021)
on hatchery reared Rainbow Trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) found that levels of circulating cortisol were
positively correlated with number and severity of fin injuries, indicating that number and severity of
injuries are a likely cause of fish stress responses. Given that the conventional turbine led to a greater
number of injuries we would predict that impact of the conventional runner on a sturgeon’s stress
physiology would be at least equivalent, if not more stressful than the RHT passage.

 6.3 Additional considerations for sturgeon passage
This study focused on effects of downstream passage through turbines, but there are other potential
negative impacts of hydropower on sturgeon populations. The design of upstream fish passage facilities
is a complex biological and engineering challenge, especially when aiming to accommodate a diverse
range of migratory fish species and life stages. Upstream passage facilities have been shown to be highly
effective at passing adult salmonids (Keefer et al. 2021), so many early attempts to pass other species
relied on, unsuccessfully, adapting designs of facilities that worked for salmonid passage (Parsley et al.
2007). More recently, studies have focused on identifying characteristics of effective sturgeon upstream
passage facilities. Cooke et al. (2020) describe a variety of upstream fish passage facilities that have
been studied with regards to passing sturgeon with varying levels of success. Equal upstream and
downstream migration rates are highly important. Jager et al. (2001) simulated White Sturgeon
populations in fragmented rivers and found that extinction risk remained high for populations linked by
high downstream but low upstream migration rates. Thus, as the technology around downstream passage
develops, there is continued need for research to improve upstream passage.

The effects of habitat fragmentation on sturgeon populations have been documented globally (Haxton
and Cano 2016; Friedrich et al. 2019; Steel et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2025) with consequences to genetic
diversity and overall population abundance. Research on lake sturgeon from the Black Sturgeon River
highlights the impact of dams on habitat quality. Prior to the installation of the Camp 43 Dam, this
population had access to both the natural spawning habitat in the upper watershed of the Black Sturgeon
River as well as a highly productive habitat in Black Bay, Lake Superior for rearing and growth. Post
installation, the now-bifurcated lake sturgeon population does not have access to both habitat types. This
has resulted in the decline of the upper river population and indicators of detrimental genetic impacts on
both the upper and lower river populations (Wilson et al. 2022). Facilitating fish movement can alleviate
these impacts as fish gain access to historical habitat types and expanded genetic diversity.

Novel runner designs that improve fish survival and thereby reduce habitat fragmentation represent an
important reconciliation between the needs of society for power and water management, and the use of
the riverscape by resident species. In this context, hydropower runners, like RHT-types, can be designed
to provide an effective additional pathway for sturgeon to safely migrate downstream through
hydropower facilities. Improving the survivability of fish through hydropower turbines will remove an
important barrier to downstream migration. To fully reconnect populations and habitats, improving
upstream passage of hydropower barriers is necessary as well (Cooke et al. 2020). The deployment of
technologies capable of achieving high fish survival across a variety of species and life stages for
downstream passage will hopefully motivate the development of methods for effective upstream passage
as well.

7. Conclusions and Recommendations

 • The RHT turbine meaningfully improves fish safety. Compared to conventional thin-bladed
 runners, the RHT design reduced both injury and mortality rates in White Sturgeon, supporting
 broader adoption of fish-friendly turbine designs at hydropower installations.

 68

 • Conventional turbines pose serious risks to fish. Thin-bladed turbine runners caused high rates
 of injury and mortality among White Sturgeon, reinforcing the need to evaluate and modernize
 the existing hydropower fleet.
 • Sub-lethal effects of the RHT were minor and limited. The RHT was associated with slightly
 slower fish growth, but produced no observable changes in fish behavior or physiological stress
 response.
 • Findings likely extend beyond sturgeon. Studies on American Eel and Alewife produced
 consistent results, suggesting RHT-type turbines may reduce fish injury across a range of species
 and body shapes.
 • Field-based injury and mortality data remain scarce. Very little research has documented
 real-world injury and mortality rates at operational hydropower installations, representing a
 critical gap for future study.

This research sought to determine whether fish-friendly turbine runners (e.g. the RHT) offer
improvements to fish safety relative to conventional turbines. The results of this work indicate that
adoption of RHT-type turbines will likely lead to reduced rates of fish injury and mortality. We assessed
the rates of acute mortality and injury as well as sublethal physiological and behavioral effects
associated with passage of White Sturgeon past a novel turbine runner design to assess whether it offers
benefits to fish safety. We documented that conventional thin-bladed turbine runners cause high rates of
White Sturgeon mortality and injury and that a runner design with thickened, swept runner blades
alleviate both the rates of injury and mortality. Additionally, our experiments indicate that the RHT-type
runner may produce small, sub-lethal effects, in the form of slower fish growth, but we did not find any
RHT-associated changes in either fish behavior or physiological stress response.

We recommend several options for future research investigating the improvement of fish passage past
hydropower turbine installations seeking to reconcile the economic operation of hydropower
installations and the health and connectivity of riparian ecosystems. Additional work studying other
native fish species (e.g. salmonids, lampreys) could inform whether the results found here on sturgeon
are representative of fish with alternative body shapes. To that end, work by Watson et al. (2022, 2023)
studying American Eels or alewives are consistent with the results of the present study, indicating the
utility of an RHT-type turbine in preventing fish injury across a suite of taxa. Given the clear rates of
injury and mortality attributable to passage of conventional thin-bladed turbines future research could
seek to better determine the in-field rates of injury and mortality attributable to the current hydropower
fleet. Currently there is very limited research on the rates of injury and observed mortality of operational
hydropower installations (Dadswell et al. 1994, 2018, Mueller et al. 2022). Finally, turbines are similar
in their form and function to water pumps which are used to deliver water to agriculture, aqueducts, or in
pumped-hydro power storage. Research studying how to improve the fish safety of water pumping
operations may benefit from advances in hydropower turbines and vice versa.

8. Project Impacts

 8.1 Scientific Contributions
This project provided one of the most detailed experimental evaluations to date of how turbine blade
design influences White Sturgeon (N = 1,192 total fish) survival, injury, physiology and behavior during

 69

hydropower passage. Across a suite of experiments we assessed turbine type (RHT vs. conventional
thin-bladed runner), passage velocity (15.0 to 27.6 m sec-1 peripheral tip speed) and the effect of
repeated passage exposures. Through use of high-speed videography, physiological assays, and post-
passage growth monitoring, the study supports the conclusion that runner geometry strongly affects fish
outcomes. The novel “RHT-type” turbine design significantly reduced blade strikes and fatal injuries
compared to conventional designs, achieving greater than 99% survival in controlled tests. These
findings advance our scientific understanding of fish–turbine interactions and provide a foundation for
designing more sustainable hydropower systems that align energy generation with aquatic conservation.

 8.2 Advancements in Fish Passage and Protection Technologies
The research demonstrated clear performance advantages of the RHT-type turbine, a next-generation
runner design engineered to improve fish passage safety without sacrificing operational efficiency.
Compared to conventional turbine blades, the RHT’s thick, swept geometry deflected fish substantially
reduced the frequency and severity of blade impacts, even at high rotational speeds. Our results indicate
that RHT-type turbines do not cause serious sub-lethal impacts beyond those associated with turbine-
less hydropower passage (i.e. high-speed passage through a pipe). In doing so, we highlight that some
sub-lethal impacts of hydropower passage past the RHT turbine (e.g. changes in behavior) are likely
attributable primarily to uncharacterized aspects of hydropower passage (e.g. pressure changes,
interactions with diversion screens or passage pipes) and not blade strikes. Technological improvements
to these aspects may further improve downstream passage of fish. To that end, these results confirmed
that engineering refinements to turbine runners may yield major biological benefits, extending safe
passage to species such as sturgeon that are especially vulnerable to hydropower impacts. The project
thus provides a validated design pathway for modernizing the hydropower fleet with fish-safe
technologies.

 8.3 Potential Impacts on Policy, Permitting, and Industry Practice
By generating quantitative, transparent data on fish survival and sublethal responses, this project
produced actionable evidence that can inform future hydropower licensing, environmental compliance,
and technology certification. The demonstrated safety of the RHT-type runner suggests that hydropower
facilities can significantly reduce biological risk while maintaining high energy performance, offering a
viable retrofit option for existing infrastructure. These findings can help streamline permitting processes
by providing regulators and stakeholders with credible, science-based benchmarks for fish passage
performance. More broadly, the project supports national goals of expanding low-carbon hydropower
while safeguarding aquatic ecosystems illustrating how targeted engineering innovation can reconcile
energy production with ecological resilience.

Summary: The Natel Energy project team will design two suitable scale model test runners, one with

Task 3.0: Propeller Test

Summary: To fulfill Objective 2 and to determine the relative efficiency of the RHT as opposed to a
thin-bladed conventional Turbine.

 Subtask 3.1: Conduct acute mortality velocity tests with thin-bladed conventional
 Turbine Blade and RHT

 Summary: Small cohorts (n = 20) of anesthetized fish will pass through either a thin-bladed
 conventional turbine or RHT turbine at multiple velocities. Post-passage fish will be observed for
 mortality, several physical trauma, and recovery. This data will determine the maximum fish-
 safe operational velocity of each turbine type.

 SMART Milestone 3.1.1: Conduct velocity passage on juvenile sturgeon at multiple
 velocity steps (n > 4, 20 sturgeon per step per turbine type). Use resulting data to
 determine the maximum upper ‘safe’ velocity for two turbine types and identify test
 velocities for subsequent growth, behavioral, and stress biomarkers using the RHT. This
 work will take place over several days in Month 9 of the project.

 Completed, see section 4.2 for methods and 5.1 for results.

Task 4.0: Assessing Sub-Lethal Physiological Effects

 Summary: To fulfill Objective 3 we will expose juvenile White Sturgeon to passage through
 either the RHT or a control pipeline. We will then quantify the growth rate among fish that pass
 through the turbine at high (>20 m/s) or low (<20 m/s) speeds and make one or two passages.
 We will also assess individual changes in fish behavior for fish that pass through the RHT.
 Finally, we will collect blood samples from fish at regular time intervals to determine the effect
 of RHT passage on the physiological stress markers.

 Subtask 4.1: Conduct passage experiments for assessing impacts on Growth.

 Summary: Fish marked with visible implant elastomer (VIE) tags will make one or two passes
 through the RHT and at either a high or low velocity. Fish will then be measured weekly for 3
 weeks post-passage to assess the impacts of passage on sustained fish growth.

Task 5.0: Conducting blood plasma stress marker analysis

Summary: To complete Objective 4 we will analyze the frozen blood plasma samples collected as part
of Objective 3 (Task 5.3). Blood plasma samples will be assayed for concentrations of three stress
biomarkers; cortisol, lactate, and glucose. Concentrations of lactate and glucose will be measured using
an ABL90 Flex blood gas analyzer (Radiometer, USA). Using a separate aliquot of each blood plasma
sample we will determine the cortisol concentration using commercially made enzyme-linked
immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Neogen Corp., USA). We anticipate measuring the stress markers
from 1200 tested fish.
