The Reverse Telescoping Coordinate System for Positive Definite Matrices: Geometry, Computation, and Generative Modeling
Anindya Bhadra

Abstract
We design a new unconstrained coordinate system where a p × p p\times p symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix 𝚯 \boldsymbol{\Theta} is represented by a reverse telescoping map 𝚯 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) = ℛ ​ 𝒯 ​ ( 𝐱 ) \boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{RT}(\mathbf{x}) , with 𝐱 = ( v , 𝐝 , 𝐫 ) ∈ ℝ × ℝ ( p − 1 ) × ℝ p ⁡ ( p − 1 ) / 2 \mathbf{x}=(v,\mathbf{d},\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p(p-1)/2} , representing respectively the log volume or log determinant; and the shape, as encoded by log relative diagonal scales and partial covariances among the nodes. This construction results in important properties not available in other charts, e.g., matrix logarithm, such as Jacobian depending on only the log-determinant. A useful feature of our construction is 𝐱 {\bf x} contains a lossless symbolic representation of both the matrix and its inverse. Many important computations involving a matrix and its inverse can be performed in 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 2 ) \mathcal{O}(p^{2}) in the transformed domain, while it is the rendering of results in matrix forms (on demand) that must incur an 𝒪 ⁡ ( p 3 ) \mathcal{O}(p^{3}) cost. Moreover, two unit-determinant matrices in the transformed domain can be joined by a straight line with pathwise unit determinant. For generative modeling, this allows designing a split volume–shape flow model trained by conditional flow matching for transporting the shape over the unit-determinant path, with a separate one-dimensional flow for transporting the volume or the determinant. The forbidding SPD constraint , tamed thus into a powerful guiding force, leads to the surprising insight that it is in some sense easier to design a volume-normalized shape flow for SPD compared to the unconstrained ℝ p × p \mathbb{R}^{p\times p} , with no intrinsic notion of volume to aid normalization, unlike the determinant of SPD matrices. We apply our construction for up to p = 200 p=200 in generative modeling of SPD matrices on a difficult synthetic bimodal target, and in generating brain connectivity networks by models trained on fMRI data; as well as in intrinsic diffusion on the SPD manifold.



Keywords:
Brain connectivity; Fisher–Rao metric; fMRI; Intrinsic diffusion; Laplace–Beltrami operator; Riemannian conditional flow matching.

1
Introduction

Symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix-valued data often arise from
connectivity analysis
, e.g., in diffusion tensor imaging
(
Tournier et al., 2011
;
Lope-Piedrafita, 2018
, see, e.g.,)
or in the study of brain functional connectivity networks
(
Smith et al., 2013a
;
Smith et al., 2013b
;
Wei et al., 2024
, see, e.g.,)
, among others, where the
covariance or partial covariance structure
is the primary object of interest, or is indeed the primary data, rather than the raw node-wise signals. Similarly, multivariate financial market data often display correlation patterns among component stocks
(
Mantegna, 1999
;
Laloux et al., 1999
)
. While model-based analysis of such data, e.g., learning a population covariance or precision matrix given some training samples using a parametric or nonparametric model is more classical
(
Pourahmadi, 2011
, see, e.g.,)
, a key recent focus area of increasing importance is
generative modeling
of such manifold-valued data
(see
Loaiza-Ganem et al., 2024
, for a recent survey)
. That is, given only some samples from some underlying unknown SPD-valued data distribution, could one generate data with similar features, but not identical to, the training set? Given that SPD matrices form a Riemannian manifold
(
Amari, 2016
, Section 1.1.2.E)
, a principled analysis of such data, either in a model-based or a generative framework, typically requires an unconstrained coordinate system or
chart
that respects the SPD geometry. Among these, some of the most popular ones are Cholesky, matrix log and spectral or eigenvalue charts
(
Pennec, 2020
)
. The overall contributions of this work stem from the construction of a new coordinate system based on recursive Schur updates to remove the SPD constraint, that we term the
reverse telescoping
(RT) coordinates. The single-most distinguishing feature of this new chart is the isolation of the Jacobian into an intrinsic scalar coordinate encoding the log determinant of an SPD matrix, that, to our knowledge, does not occur under other commonly used charts. This leads to several nontrivial consequences, both geometric and algorithmic. Among the most important geometric consequences are a stage-local structure of the Fisher–Rao metric
(
Rao, 1945
)
in the RT coordinates, a block diagonal inverse metric tensor, and that the Fisher–Rao volume form is a constant multiple of the Euclidean volume in normalized volume–shape RT coordinates. Among the most prominent algorithmic consequences are a separation of volume (encoded by the determinant) and shape (encoded by partial covariances and relative diagonal scales) of a matrix for learning a conditional flow matching model that allows these models to scale up to 200-dimensional matrices, while geometric obstacles become challenging for alternative methods using other charts at more than 50 dimensions. Similarly, the RT coordinates allow defining intrinsic diffusion on the SPD manifold with the same ease as the Euclidean space, with no additional bookkeeping cost for repeated evaluation of the metric tensor or chart Jacobian. Given the breadth of the current work, we provide the requisite background on the geometric and algorithmic aspects of generative SPD-modeling in the rest of this section, before summarizing our key contributions.

1.1
The SPD Manifold and Existing Coordinate Charts

A main challenge with modeling manifold-valued data, compared to real-valued data, lies in having to respect the manifold constraint or geometry. For the SPD manifold, various coordinate systems or charts exist that allow unconstrained modeling of such data, while admitting a diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, the space of
p\times p
SPD matrices, and
\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
, the space of reals with the same dimension as the number of free elements in the matrix. For example, for
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, the Cholesky decomposition allows one to write
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{L}
is lower triangular with positive diagonal entries. Thus, for
i,j=1,\ldots,p;\;i\leq j,
if one defines
\mathbf{z}=\{l_{ij},\log l_{jj}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
, then one obtains a diffeomorphism under the log-Cholesky chart between
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
and unconstrained modeling of real-valued
\mathbf{z}
is possible
(
Pourahmadi, 1999
;
Lin, 2019
)
. Other charts that also allow unconstrained modeling include the matrix logarithm and log-spectral charts.

However, a key challenge under any chart is to relate its properties with the SPD space under the pullback. Several properties depend on the Jacobian of transformation, and this is where the difficulties begin to appear. For example, under the Cholesky chart mentioned above, one has:

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{L}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=2^{p}\prod_{j=1}^{p}l_{jj}^{p-j+1},

(1)

using standard calculations. One may immediately notice the unequal weighting of the diagonal
l_{ii}
terms. Thus, the change that is induced for a perturbation in
l_{11}
is vastly different from a perturbation in
l_{pp}
, i.e., the Jacobian is highly anisotropic and not compressible into a single native scalar chart coordinate. Similar issues arise with other existing charts. Table
1
gives a summary of the commonly used charts and their respective Jacobians. Here
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, constants depending only on
p
are suppressed,
and all Jacobians are with respect to Lebesgue measure on the symmetric matrix
entries. The formulas show that, standard charts either
weight coordinates anisotropically or couple all eigenvalues globally. Consequently, in standard charts the determinant, shape, and reference volume are globally entangled at the coordinate level, making likelihood evaluation, volume-normalized transport, and intrinsic volume preservation dependent on nontrivial Jacobian bookkeeping rather than a single native coordinate correction.

1.2
Existing Generative Approaches for SPD-valued Data

Generative modeling is a fast-paced world, and any cursory survey of influential methods is bound to be incomplete. Nevertheless, among the most influential approaches historically, the field had moved from early approaches based on Boltzmann machines or their variants
(
Ackley et al., 1985
;
Salakhutdinov and Hinton, 2009
)
, to variational autoencoders or VAEs
(
Kingma and Welling, 2013
)
, generative adversarial networks or GANs
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
)
, score-based diffusion models
(
Song et al., 2021
)
and flow-matching approaches
(
Lipman et al., 2023
)
; among many others.

However, the outlook is more sobering under manifold-valued data
(
Loaiza-Ganem et al., 2024
)
, which can be attributed to geometric constraints. For general Riemannian manifolds, score matching
(
De Bortoli et al., 2022
)
or flow matching
(
Chen and Lipman, 2024
)
approaches exist, that essentially replace the Euclidean loss by a Riemannian metric, but their scalability is nowhere close to unconstrained modeling.

Methods specialized to SPD manifolds have also appeared in the recent generative literature.
Li et al., 2024
design a score-based denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) for SPD-valued data.
Collas et al., 2025
show that a conditional flow matching (CFM) under an appropriate pullback can successfully be used for generative modeling for SPD-valued data.
Tan et al., 2025
use manifold-aware Wasserstein GANs for generating SPD-valued brain functional connectivity data. Scalability under geometric constraints remains a barrier for all methods.

1.3
Summary of Our Contributions and Connections to Prior Works

Our main contribution, interpreted at its broadest, is a new unconstrained coordinate system that we term the
reverse telescoping coordinates
, admitting a diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
. The proposed coordinate system has its origin in the telescoping block decomposition of
Bhadra et al., 2024
that was developed for computing evidence of marginal likelihood under Gaussian graphical models, and was used by
Gao et al., 2025
for designing faster posterior sampling algorithms for such models. Some of the properties we present, such as unit Jacobian under a suitable parameterization, were noted by
Gao et al., 2025
. However, these works are solely concerned with posterior sampling of Bayesian graphical models, and neither work explored the reverse telescoping decomposition in its full generality to design an unconstrained coordinate system for SPD matrices itself. Our specific contributions could be summarized as follows.

1.3.1
Geometric Contributions

1.

We demonstrate in Section
2
that in addition to being unconstrained, the RT coordinates admit an extremely simple form of the Jacobian, depending only on the determinant of an SPD matrix. Moreover, under a suitable reparameterization, the Jacobian is unity. Thus, all tasks involving a change of variables and Jacobian bookkeeping, e.g., evaluating or tracking the likelihood of SPD-valued data, become far simpler compared to all other commonly used charts. Specifically, for a map between
\mathbf{x}=(v,\mathbf{d},\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p(p-1)/2}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
, we show that:

\displaystyle J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp(v),

(2)

where the scalar
v
is the log-determinant of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Contrasting (
2
) with (
1
) is revealing, as it shows the Cholesky anisotropy problem can be effectively resolved by a coordinate change. Comparisons with other charts in Table
1
lead to the same insight.

2.

A further useful feature is that the same encoded object
{\bf x}
decodes losslessly to both
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}
, so that likelihoods, quadratic forms, conditional quantities, and inverse-space computations can often be performed without repeatedly rendering or inverting dense matrices.

3.

A direct consequence is the blockwise partitioning of the Fisher–Rao metric by freezing the past that does not introduce a global coupling. We contrast this explicitly with Fisher–Rao metric Cholesky coordinates in Section
3
. Another critical consequence is that under a suitable reparameterization, the Fisher–Rao volume density in normalized RT coordinates becomes a constant multiple of the ambient Euclidean volume density, so that there is no volume distortion.

4.

The Laplace–Beltrami operator and the intrinsic coordinates are explicitly derived in Section
4
that make designing an intrinsic diffusion as simple as a Euclidean diffusion.

1.3.2
Computational Contributions and Applications to Generative Modeling

1.

A critical property of the RT coordinates is the separation of volume and shape, that allows us to connect any two unit-determinant SPD matrices in the RT space by a straight line with pathwise unit-determinant. In Section
5
, we utilize this property to design a split volume–shape flow model that can be trained by conditional flow matching. The volume–shape separation allows us to scale our approach to
p=200
in generative SPD models, and an application to brain imaging data is described in Section
8
.

2.

Similarly, as a geometric consequence of the identified intrinsic coordinates, we also design an intrinsic Langevin diffusion model as described in Lemma
4.1
for a given target law, which is useful in Bayesian inverse problems for sampling from a target posterior, and is a counterpart to the generative application described above.

3.

For a
p\times p
matrix, the encoding and decoding steps cost
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. However, we show that once encoded, many commonly used operations involving SPD matrices, e.g., recurring computation of quadratic forms needed for density evaluation, can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
complexity. In particular, under spectral control, we obtain in Section
6
explicit upper and lower bounds on the Fisher–Rao distance computable at
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
, whereas exact computation remains
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
.

2.4
Leveraging Sparsity Under Decomposability

If the sparsity graph of an SPD matrix is decomposable and the variables are encoded in a perfect elimination ordering, then the RT recursion is zero-fill: the Schur-complement update at each step is a rank-one outer-product update supported on the higher-neighbor set of the eliminated vertex, which is a clique under a perfect elimination ordering. Hence no structural nonzeros are created outside the graph. This follows by the proof of Theorem 9.1 of
Vandenberghe and Andersen, 2015
, applied to rank-one Schur updates of RT, instead of rank-one Cholesky updates. This yields computational savings under chordal sparsity for the encoder and decoder. However, under arbitrary non-decomposable graphs, fill-ins will generally be created under rank-one adjustments.

2.5
Jacobians of Transformation

Note that although it is certainly possible to describe the encoding and decoding maps
\mathbf{x}\leftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
directly, we introduce the intermediate object
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\Theta}}
to understand its properties of our construction better. To begin, we note from
Gao et al., 2025
that a nontrivial consequence of our construction for
j=p-1,\ldots,1
is that we have
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}
is a function of
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})
, of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}),

(6)

for some nonlinear map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{(p-j+1)\times j}\to\mathbb{R}^{j}
,
where by convention
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
. As a consequence it also holds that:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\gamma(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j+1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}),

(7)

for another nonlinear map
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{(p-j+1)\times j}\to\mathbb{R}^{j}
. This linear shift implies:

J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})=1,

for all
j=1,\ldots,p
, where the case
j=p
is trivial. As a consequence,

J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j:p\times j:p}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j:p\times j:p})=\prod_{k=j}^{p}J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p})=1,

for all
j=1,\ldots,p
and globally,

J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=1,

as well. Thus, there is volume preservation of the densities under both marginalization and conditioning. To compute probabilities involving the first
j
variables, one simply drops variables
j+1,\ldots,p
. Sequential addition of variables does not introduce any Jacobian factors in the telescoping chart. This observation was leveraged gainfully by
Gao et al., 2025
for developing posterior sampling mechanism in certain Bayesian graphical models without explicit Jacobian corrections, but no further implications as a fully general coordinate system were explored.

The above property also means that for the purpose of density evaluation on the space of SPD matrices
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, one can directly evaluate them using
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
, which has unrestricted off-diagonal entries and positive diagonal entries (representing the Schur pivots
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}
), but no SPD constraints. One may also directly evaluate any densities in terms of
\mathbf{x}
. Thus, the Jacobian of transformations satisfy
J(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=J(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=J(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})=J((v,\mathbf{d})\mapsto\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\theta}_{11},\ldots,\tilde{\theta}_{pp}))
, since
\mathbf{r}_{j}
is a direct copy of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}
. Let
s_{j}=\log\tilde{\theta}_{jj}=d_{j}+v/p
. Then, by the chain rule:

J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\left|\mathrm{det}\frac{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}{\partial{\bf x}}\right|=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\partial\tilde{\theta}_{jj}}{\partial s_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\exp(s_{j})=\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp(v),

(8)

i.e., the Jacobian is determined by single scalar volume coordinate. These properties are considerably simpler compared to Cholesky, matrix logarithm or spectral charts, where there is global entanglement of shape and scale (see Table
1
). The RT chart isolates all Lebesgue-volume distortion into the scalar log-determinant coordinate. The Jacobian of transformation for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}
can similarly be computed solely as a function of
v
as:

\displaystyle J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})J(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\exp(v)\{\mathrm{det}({\boldsymbol{\Theta}})\}^{-(p+1)}=\exp(-pv).

(9)

The conditional Jacobians also have a simple form:

\displaystyle J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},\ldots\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j-1})

\displaystyle=J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\ldots\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j-1})=\{\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{(j-1)\times(j-1)})\}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{-(j+1)}=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}\tilde{\theta}_{kk}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{-(j+1)}.

(10)

Supplementary Section
S.1
gives a derivation of (
10
). Combining the expressions in (
10
) also yields:

J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{j\times j}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j\times j})=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j}\tilde{\theta}_{kk}\right)^{-(j+1)}.

These expressions are useful for calculating the densities of any functions of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
after decoding the inverse; jointly, marginally and conditionally. The following clarification is now warranted.

Remark 1

(Rank-one Schur versus rank-one Cholesky updates and Jacobian implications)
.

At this point, it is instructive to briefly highlight the subtle differences between the recursive rank-one Schur updates of (
3
) and rank-one Cholesky updates in
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
parameterization:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\leftarrow\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top}}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{RT/rank-one Schur update}};\qquad\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\leftarrow\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+d_{jj}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\bullet j}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&d_{jj}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\bullet j}\\
d_{jj}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&d_{jj}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{rank-one Cholesky update}}.

(11)

These two rank-one updates are shown side-by-side in (
11
), and the critical difference is the scaling of the off-diagonal terms by the diagonals for the rank-one Cholesky updates at each
j
that leads to a global volume–shape entanglement, and the loss of the Jacobian isolation into a single intrinsic scalar coordinate. The RT recursion leaves the off-diagonals unscaled by the diagonals and avoids this entanglement. This is what leads to
J(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=1
and
J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp(v)
under RT, but
J((\mathbf{L},\mathbf{D})\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}d_{jj}^{p-j}
under Cholesky in Table
1
. Thus, the Cholesky Jacobian is affected under a permutation of the nodes, due to unequal exponents for
d_{jj}
, but the RT Jacobian is unaffected, since
v
is invariant under node permutations, despite both Cholesky and RT being triangular maps
(
Marzouk et al., 2017
)
.

2.5.1
Applications of the Jacobian

1.

Exact Likelihood Evaluation.
Given a target density
\pi(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
(e.g., Wishart), the density in RT-space is available in closed form via the change-of-variables formula:
\log\pi_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})=\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\mathcal{RT}({\bf x}))+v
. This enables exact likelihood estimation without numerical Jacobians.

2.

Integration over the SPD Manifold.
Under the RT transform, integrals over the SPD manifold transform into Euclidean integrals:

\int_{\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}}f(\boldsymbol{\Theta})d\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mathcal{RT}({\bf x}))\exp(v)d{\bf x}.

(12)

This is particularly useful for computing normalization constants in Bayesian inverse problems.

3.

Marginalization and Conditioning.
Marginalization and conditioning contribute unit Jacobian for moving between
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
, facilitating computations.

3
Fisher–Rao Geometry in the Reverse Telescoping Coordinates

The Fisher–Rao metric on the SPD manifold
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
is defined by the line element
ds_{FR}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left((\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Theta})^{2}\right)

(see
Bhatia, 2007
, Equation (6.1), modulo factor
1/2
scaling convention)
, which is also the standard affine invariant Riemannian metric and the unique Riemannian metric that is invariant under the sufficient statistics
(
Chentsov, 1982
)
. We demonstrate that the Reverse Telescoping (RT) chart provides a natural coordinate system for this metric, leading to a recursive, simplified form. First, note from (
3
) that the differential
d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}
with respect to the
j
-th column coordinates
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj})
is:

d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top}}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{2}}d\tilde{\theta}_{jj}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}}(d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top})&d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}\\
d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&d\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\end{bmatrix}.

The following proposition gives the form of the Fisher–Rao metric.

Proposition 3.1

(Fisher–Rao metric in RT coordinates)
.

The Fisher–Rao metric in RT coordinates admits the additive partitioning:

ds^{2}_{FR}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(ds_{j})^{2}}_{\text{Volume / Diagonal pivots}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=2}^{p}\exp(-s_{j})\,(D\mathbf{r}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\,D\mathbf{r}_{j}}_{\text{Shape (Recursive)}}.

(13)

where,

s_{j}=d_{j}+v/p=\log\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\;\mathbf{r}_{j}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},\;D\mathbf{r}_{j}=d\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j}ds_{j}.

See Supplementary Section
S.2
for a proof of Proposition
3.1
. A critical property of this decomposition is that there is no differential term across
j
. This suggests using the following local whitening where the
past
term
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}
is frozen, and one sets:

d\mathbf{z}_{j}=\exp(-s_{j}/2)\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}D\mathbf{r}_{j},

so that,

\displaystyle d\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\top}d\mathbf{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp(-s_{j})\,(D\mathbf{r}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1})^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}\,D\mathbf{r}_{j}=\exp(-s_{j})\,(D\mathbf{r}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}^{-1}\,D\mathbf{r}_{j}=\exp(-s_{j})\,(D\mathbf{r}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\,D\mathbf{r}_{j},

and (
13
) yields:

ds^{2}_{FR}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(ds_{j})^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{p}d\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\top}d\mathbf{z}_{j},

i.e.,
(\mathbf{z}_{j},s_{j})
constitute local orthonormal frames. Further, given
\mathbf{L}_{j-1}
and
D{\bf r}_{j}
, one can solve for
d\mathbf{z}_{j}
by solving a triangular system with complexity
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
.

We now contrast this to the Fisher–Rao metric in Cholesky coordinates. We prove the following result, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.3
.

Proposition 3.2

(Fisher–Rao metric in Cholesky coordinates)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
and write its lower-Cholesky factorization as:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{p-1}&0\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{p}^{\top}&\alpha_{p}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\alpha_{p}>0,

and more generally, for each
j=1,\dots,p
,

\mathbf{L}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}&0\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}&\alpha_{j}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top},\qquad\alpha_{j}>0,

with the convention that
\mathbf{L}_{1}=(\alpha_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}=(\alpha_{1}^{2})
. Then the Fisher–Rao line element
ds_{FR}^{2}=\sum_{j}ds_{FR,j}^{2}
admits the recursion:

\displaystyle ds_{FR,j}^{2}=ds_{FR,j-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}\bigl\|d\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1}\bigr\|^{2}+2\left(\frac{d\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{2}.

(14)

Comparing the expressions of
D{\bf r}_{j}
of (
13
), which depends only on the current column, with the differential term
(d\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1})
of (
14
), which couples columns
j
with all previous columns in the differential term through
\mathbf{L}_{j-1}
, reveals one of the strongest advantages of RT chart. For example, consider the calculation of natural gradients
(
Amari, 1998
)
. Let
\nabla_{\bf x}\mathcal{L}
denote the Euclidean gradient of an objective

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}({\bf x}))
in RT coordinates. The FR-natural gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\bf x}\mathcal{L}

is defined by:

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\bf x}\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{FR}}({\bf x})^{-1}\nabla_{\bf x}\mathcal{L},

where
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{FR}}({\bf x})
is the FR metric tensor. The lack of coupling across
j
under the RT coordinates suggests a natural
splitting
or coordinate-wise computation of gradient over
{\bf x}_{j}
with the past columns
1,\ldots,j-1
held fixed.
In particular from (
13
), for each
j\geq 2
, the slice
(s_{j},{\bf r}_{j})
has metric block:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{FR}}(s_{j},{\bf r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+e^{-s_{j}}{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}r_{j}&-e^{-s_{j}}{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\\[2.84526pt]
-e^{-s_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}{\bf r}_{j}&e^{-s_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\end{bmatrix},

(15)

whose inverse is:

\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{FR}}^{-1}(s_{j},{\bf r}_{j})=\begin{bmatrix}2&2{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\\[2.84526pt]
2{\bf r}_{j}&e^{s_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+2{\bf r}_{j}{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, if
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(s_{1},\dots,s_{p},{\bf r}_{2},\dots,{\bf r}_{p})
is a smooth objective, then the exact slice-wise Fisher natural gradient at level
j\geq 2
is

\widetilde{\partial_{s_{j}}\mathcal{L}}=2\,\partial_{s_{j}}\mathcal{L}+2\,{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\nabla_{{\bf r}_{j}}\mathcal{L},

and,

\widetilde{\nabla_{{\bf r}_{j}}\mathcal{L}}=2\,{\bf r}_{j}\,\partial_{s_{j}}\mathcal{L}+\bigl(e^{s_{j}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+2{\bf r}_{j}{\bf r}_{j}^{\top}\bigr)\nabla_{{\bf r}_{j}}\mathcal{L},

where the per-slice updates of
(s_{j},{\bf r}_{j})
given the current
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}
, cost
\mathcal{O}(j^{2})
, so that updating over all slices cost
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathcal{O}(j^{2})=\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
.

However, this splitting strategy fails for the Cholesky coordinates, due to the global coupling between columns
j
with all previous blocks in the differential term, as can be seen from (
14
). To our knowledge, among the standard global SPD parameterizations (e.g. Cholesky/modified Cholesky, spectral or log-based charts), RT is unusual in that its exact Fisher–Rao metric is recursively stage-local: each slice admits a local inverse metric depending only on the current slice variables and the frozen past state, so the exact per-slice natural gradient is
\mathcal{O}(j^{2})
, whereas a full sweep remains
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. By contrast, Cholesky-type charts can be recursively parameterized but not recursively decoupled at the differential level
(
Lin, 2019
, Section 3)
.

The Fisher–Rao volume form also admits a particularly simple representation, as shown in the next proposition, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.4
.

Proposition 3.3

(Fisher–Rao volume form)
.

The pulled-back Fisher–Rao volume density is:

d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}:=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{FR}}({\bf s},{\bf r})}\,d{\bf s}\,d{\bf r}=2^{-p/2}e^{-(p-1)v/2}\,dv\,d{\bf d}\,d{\bf r}=2^{-p/2}(\mathrm{det}\boldsymbol{\Theta})^{-(p+1)/2}\,d\boldsymbol{\Theta}.

A remarkable feature of this result is that the volume density depends only on the single scalar
v=\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
and is insensitive to
({\bf d},{\bf r})
. In this way, there is true separation of the determinant of a matrix, encoded by
v
and the shape of the matrix, encoded by
({\bf d},{\bf r})
, under the RT coordinates. Once again a comparison could be drawn with the Cholesky coordinates, where the anisotropic contributions of the diagonal
l_{jj}
terms prevent separating the volume into a single native chart coordinate. A further useful corollary is that if one defines
{\bf r}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\exp(-v/p){\bf r}
, then:

d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}=2^{-p/2}dvd{\bf d}d{\bf r}_{\mathrm{norm}}.

In Section
5
, we will comment further on the implication of this property in designing a split volume–shape flow model, which will be shown to preserve Fisher volume, not just Euclidean volume. The explicit volume density forms may be useful in at least two more ways. First, it provides a closed-form reference measure for Fisher–Rao geometric priors on SPD. Second, it suggests that determinant and shape may be modeled separately even at the level of intrinsic volumes. The final expression in Proposition
3.3
can be seen to coincide with Jeffreys’ prior on the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
for multivariate normal model
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Theta})

(
Jeffreys, 1946
)
, as expected.

4
The Laplace–Beltrami Operator and Intrinsic Diffusion on SPD

We present results on some second order properties that are useful for designing intrinsic diffusions on the SPD manifold. In order to do this, first it is helpful to define the following parameterization:

{\bf y}=(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\bigl(v,d_{2},\dots,d_{p},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{p}\bigr),

where,
(v,{\bf d})
are the same as in
{\bf x}=(v,{\bf d},{\bf r})
coordinates, and,

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}:=e^{-(d_{j}+v/p)}{\bf r}_{j}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}_{\bullet j}}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}},\qquad j=2,\dots,p.

The key reason for the reparameterization is that it allows us to avoid mixed derivatives involving
(d_{j},{\bf r}_{j})
in the subsequent development of this section. For a Riemannian metric
\mathbf{G}
, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is:

\Delta_{FR}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\mathbf{G}}}\partial_{i}\Bigl(\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\mathbf{G}}\,\mathbf{G}^{ij}\partial_{j}f\Bigr).

We have the following result, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.5
. Along with the desired Laplace–Beltrami operator, it also gives an alternative form of the Fisher–Rao metric.

Proposition 4.1

(Fisher–Rao metric and the Laplace–Beltrami operator in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates)
.

The Fisher–Rao line element of (
13
) takes the form:

ds_{FR}^{2}=\frac{1}{2p}dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{p}e^{d_{j}+v/p}(d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},

(16)

giving a block diagonal inverse metric tensor:

\mathbf{G}_{FR}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}\!\Bigl(2p,\;2{\bf P},\;e^{-(d_{2}+v/p)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},\;\ldots,\;e^{-(d_{p}+v/p)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p-1}\Bigr),

(17)

where
{\bf P}:=\mathbf{I}_{p-1}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
. Moreover, the corresponding Laplace–Beltrami operator is:

\Delta_{FR}f=2p\,\partial_{vv}f+2\sum_{i,j=2}^{p}\Bigl(\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)\partial_{d_{i}d_{j}}f+\sum_{j=2}^{p}(2j-p-1)\,\partial_{d_{j}}f+\sum_{j=2}^{p}e^{-(d_{j}+v/p)}{\rm tr}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}{\bf H}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}f\bigr),

(18)

where
{\bf H}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}f
denotes the Hessian of
f
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
and
\delta_{ij}=\mathbf{1}(i=j)
.

Remark 2
.

Under our convention
ds_{FR}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left((\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Theta})^{2}\right)
, the SPD manifold is known to exhibit sectional curvature
-1\leq K\leq 0

(see
Thanwerdas and Pennec, 2023
, Table 5, with
\alpha=1/2,\beta=0
)
, which is made explicit in coordinates by (
16
). If
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is held fixed, the metric is flat in
(v,{\bf d})
and attains
K=0
. Conversely, fixing all coordinates except
(d_{2},\beta_{2})
gives
ds^{2}=dd_{2}^{2}+Ce^{2d_{2}}d\beta_{2}^{2}
for some constant
C>0
, which is the Poincaré upper-half-plane metric (with change of variable
y=e^{-d_{2}})
and hence attains
K=-1
.

The block diagonal structure of
\mathbf{G}^{-1}_{FR}
in (
17
) has a direct probabilistic interpretation under a model
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Theta})
. Marginalization over slices
j+1,\ldots,p
corresponds to dropping the corresponding blocks, contributing no metric distortion. Conditioning on slices
1,\ldots,j-1
is restricted to the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
block
\exp(-(d_{j}+v/p))\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}
, whose geometry is completely determined by the frozen past state
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}
. Thus, the RT coordinates simultaneously simplify both marginal and conditional Fisher–Rao geometry, a property not shared by Cholesky or spectral charts where off-diagonal metric terms couple the conditional and marginal geometries. An immediate corollary of Proposition
4.1
is the following, with proof in Supplementary Section
S.6
.

Corollary 4.1

(Action of
\Delta_{FR}
on
v
,
{\bf d}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
)
.

Under the notation of Proposition
4.1
, the Laplace–Beltrami operator satisfies:

\displaystyle\Delta_{FR}v

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Delta_{FR}{\bf d}_{j}

\displaystyle=2j-p-1,\qquad j=2,\dots,p,

\displaystyle\Delta_{FR}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad j=2,\dots,p,\ \ k=1,\dots,j-1.

Equivalently,
\Delta_{FR}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=0
componentwise for every
j\geq 2
.

Corollary
4.1
shows that the global volume coordinate
v=\log\mathrm{det}\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and the normalized successive regression coordinates
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
are harmonic for the Fisher geometry, whereas the determinant-free pivot coordinates
{\bf d}_{j}
have constant Laplacian. Thus, the coordinates
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
are adapted to the Fisher geometry in a particularly simple way:
v
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are harmonic coordinates, while the
{\bf d}
-coordinates are affine with constant drift. Nevertheless, we remark here that although the
{\bf y}=(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates may be particularly well-suited for studying the intrinsic geometry under the Fisher–Rao metric, it may not be the best choice for other problems, e.g. density evaluation, as in these coordinates, one loses the remarkably simple Jacobian property
J({\bf x}\to\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp(v)
that holds in the
{\bf x}=(v,{\bf d},{\bf r})
coordinates.

Nevertheless, with the intrinsic coordinates thus identified, the natural next step is to specify the intrinsic diffusions in these coordinates. We have the following result, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.7
.

Lemma 4.1

(Intrinsic diffusion on SPD in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates)
.

Let
\mathbf{B}=(B_{v},B_{\bf d},B_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{p}})
be any smooth drift field in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates. Let
\kappa_{j}=2j-p-1
with
\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\{\kappa_{j}\}_{j=2}^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
,
{\bf Q}_{j-1}(x)\,{\bf Q}_{j-1}(x)^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}(x)
and
{\bf P}=\mathbf{I}_{p-1}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, as before. Then the Itô diffusion,

\displaystyle dv_{t}

\displaystyle=B_{v}(X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2p}\,dW_{t}^{(v)},

(19)

\displaystyle d\mathbf{d}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(B_{{\bf d}}(X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}\Bigr)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,{\bf P}^{1/2}\,d{\bf W}_{t}^{(d)},

(20)

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,t}

\displaystyle=B_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(X_{t})\,dt+e^{-(d_{j,t}+v_{t}/p)/2}\,{\bf Q}_{j-1}(X_{t})\,d{\bf W}_{t}^{(j)},\qquad j=2,\dots,p,

(21)

where
W^{(v)}
,
{\bf W}^{(d)}
, and
{\bf W}^{(j)}
are independent standard Brownian motions of dimensions
1
,
p-1
, and
j-1
, respectively, has generator:

Lf=\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla f+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{FR}f.

(22)

In particular, if
\pi
is a smooth positive density with respect to Fisher volume
d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}
, then the intrinsic Langevin diffusion with invariant measure
\pi\,d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}
is obtained by taking

\mathbf{B}=\frac{1}{2}\,G_{FR}^{-1}\nabla\log\pi.

Equivalently, in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates one may write,

\displaystyle B_{v}

\displaystyle=p\,\partial_{v}\log\pi,

(23)

\displaystyle B_{d}

\displaystyle={\bf P}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{d}}\log\pi,

(24)

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,e^{-(d_{j}+v/p)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}\log\pi,\qquad j=2,\dots,p.

(25)

Remark 3
.

Lemma
4.1
shows that the intrinsic Brownian motion separates into:

1.

Ordinary Brownian motion in the log-determinant coordinate
v
,

2.

Brownian motion with constant drift on the unit-determinant hyperplane in the
{\bf d}
-coordinates,

3.

Drift-free anisotropic Brownian motions in the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
-coordinates, with covariance determined recursively by the frozen-past block
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}
.

Similar observations are true for the Langevin diffusion as well. This makes it simple to design intrinsic diffusion, without any explicit accounting for the path Jacobian via numerical calculations. We proceed to discuss two concrete applications of the geometric properties discussed so far in the next two sections.

5
Application 1: Generative Modeling of SPD-valued Data by Linear Euclidean Split Volume–Shape Flow

Given two SPD matrices
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B}
, let
{\bf x}_{A}=(v_{A},{\bf d}_{A},{\bf r}_{A})=\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{A})
and
{\bf x}_{B}=(v_{B},{\bf d}_{B},{\bf r}_{B})=\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})
be the mapped coordinates via the encoder. Consider the straight line path joining them:

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t){\bf x}_{A}+t{\bf x}_{B},\qquad t\in[0,1].

(26)

Since the domain of
{\bf x}
is unconstrained, this defines a valid SPD path, so that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(t)=\mathcal{RT}({\bf x}(t))\in\mathcal{M}^{p}_{+}
uniformly in
t
with
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}(t))=(\mathrm{det}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{A})^{1-t}(\mathrm{det}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})^{t}
, since
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp(v)
. Thus, along the path we have:

\frac{d}{dt}\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{t})=\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})-\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{A}),

i.e., log determinant changes linearly along the path. In particular, If
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{A})=\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})=c
, then the entire straight line path joining these two has constant determinant, and suggests designing a flow or diffusion model that maintains a
constant determinant
over the entire flow path.

We leverage this observation to design a transport mechanism for SPD matrices
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
that separates global volume from the internal geometry. This is achieved by first normalizing the matrix to the unit-determinant manifold before mapping it to the unconstrained RT-coordinate system.
Given an SPD matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}({\bf x})=\mathcal{RT}({\bf x})
for
{\bf x}=(v,{\bf d},{\bf r})
with volume
v=\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
, we define the normalized matrix:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\exp(-v/p){\boldsymbol{\Theta}},

(27)

so that
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{norm}})=1.
The normalized matrix
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is then encoded via Algorithm
3
(a) into
(0,\mathbf{d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})
, where
\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p(p-1)/2}
is related to
\mathbf{r}
as:

\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\exp(-v/p){\bf r}.

(28)

This allows us to design separate flows for
v
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\mathbf{d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})
, so that
{\bf x}=(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})
always decodes to a unit-determinant matrix, and
v
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v
can be transported triangularly. The decoded unit-determinant matrix is rescaled at the end of the transport via the transported volume to match the target determinant. The Jacobian of transformation is easily computed as:

J((v,{\rm d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=J((v,{\rm d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})\mapsto{\bf x})J({\bf x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\exp\left(\frac{v}{p}\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\right)\exp(v)=\exp\left(\frac{p+1}{2}v\right),

and similarly,

J((v,{\rm d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})\mapsto\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\exp\left(-\frac{p+1}{2}v\right).

Using (
28
) in Proposition
3.3
further yields:

d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}=2^{-p/2}\,dv\,d{\bf d}\,d\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}}=2^{-p/2}\,dv\,d\boldsymbol{\xi},

suggesting the Fisher–Rao volume density is flat in the
(v,\boldsymbol{\xi})
coordinates, motivating our flow design.
Given a base distribution
p_{0}(\mathbf{{\bf x}})
and target
p_{1}(\mathbf{{\bf x}})
, we minimize the flow matching objective:

\mathcal{L}_{FM}=\mathbb{E}_{t,q_{t}(\mathbf{{\bf x}})}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}(\mathbf{{\bf x}})-\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{t}(\mathbf{{\bf x}}_{0})\|^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}(\mathbf{{\bf x}}_{0})
is a probability path. Because the domain of
\mathbf{{\bf x}}
is the entire Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}
, a straight-line path
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}(\mathbf{{\bf x}}_{0})=(1-t)\mathbf{{\bf x}}_{0}+t\mathbf{{\bf x}}_{1}
always maps to valid SPD matrices under decoding and is the optimal path that minimizes transport cost (with respect to the flat
\mathcal{X}
space, not geodesically). The target velocity for this path is simply
({\bf x}_{1}-{\bf x}_{0})
. We train the unit-shape and volume models separately via conditional flow matching
(
Lipman et al., 2023
;
Albergo et al., 2025
)
. Given an SPD sample
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}
from the target, we volume-normalize via (
27
), we compute the pair
(v_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1})
and sample
v_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
The training loss for
v
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(v)=\mathbb{E}_{t,v_{0},v_{1}}\left\|{u}(v_{t},t)-(v_{1}-v_{0})\right\|^{2},

(29)

where
v_{t}=(1-t)v_{0}+tv_{1}
, and sampling proceeds as
dv_{t}/dt=\mathbf{u}_{v}(v_{t},t)
.
We then sample noise
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
and a random time
t\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)
. The training loss for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v=v_{1}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mid v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},v_{1}}\left\|\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},t;v_{1})-(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})\right\|^{2},

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}
. The probability flow ODE in the coordinate space, conditional on a
v
sample is:
{d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}}/{dt}=\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},v,t)
.
The SPD sample reconstructions are as follows.

1.

Reconstruction of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. The reconstruction follows
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\exp(v/p)\mathcal{RT}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
, by mapping
{\bf x}_{t}=(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})
into
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
via (
5
), followed by Algorithm
3
(b) to recover
\mathcal{RT}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
.

2.

Reconstruction of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. The inverse is recovered via
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\exp(-v/p)\mathcal{RT}_{inv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
, by mapping
{\bf x}=(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})
into
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
via (
5
), followed by Algorithm
6
(b) to recover
\mathcal{RT}_{inv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
.

If
\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v)=\pi({\bf d},\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{norm}}\mid v)
is the terminal density of
\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v
and
\pi(v)
is the terminal marginal density of
v
learned by the flow, then the joint terminal density for the SPD matrix and its inverse are:

\pi(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\pi(v)\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v)\exp(-(p+1)v/2),\quad\pi(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\pi(v)\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v)\exp((p+1)v/2).

This confirms that the RT-chart allows for joint, marginal, and conditional density evaluations of both
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
without complicated Jacobian bookkeeping during the transport process, where the transport of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
stays on the unit determinant (
v=0
) path throughout. This can be contrasted with flow matching under log-Cholesky or log-Euclidean pullback
(
Collas et al., 2025
)
, where straight line paths are possible, but volume–shape split and Jacobian bookkeeping for density evaluation could be nontrivial.

5.1
Solenoidal Parameterization of the Split Volume–Shape Velocity Field

Although the Euclidean flow matching of the previous section is simple, and preserves unit-determinant throughout the flow path (if the matrix is normalized before encoding), it is not automatically volume preserving. However, the RT coordinates also make it far simpler to implement a volume-preserving flow compared to most other charts, and we now discuss this. As before, let
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{norm}})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
denote the unit-determinant coordinate vector of dimension
K=p(p+1)/2-1
. To strictly enforce the incompressibility of the shape component for the flow matching objectives in (
29
)–(
30
), we parameterize the shape and volume velocity fields
\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)
and
{u}(v,t)
separately, where the shape network is indexed by volume.

Specifically, we utilize a Hamiltonian flow model to enforce the incompressible condition:

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)=0.

Instead of directly predicting
\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)
, we utilize a neural network parameterized by
\psi
to learn a scalar potential (Hamiltonian)
H_{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The velocity field is then defined as the skew-symmetric gradient of the potential:

\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}H_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v),

(31)

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\{\gamma_{ij}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is a constant skew-symmetric matrix, satisfying
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\top}=-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. We choose to model
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
via the block-diagonal matrix composed of
2\times 2
symplectic blocks:

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\text{diag}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix},\dots,\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}\right).

(32)

If
K
is odd, we append a one-dimensional zero block. By the properties of skew-symmetric operators, the divergence vanishes identically for any twice-differentiable potential:

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)=\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}H_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v))=\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}H_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}}=0.

since
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is skew-symmetric and the Hessian matrix is symmetric. This parameterization ensures that the probability path
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t}(0,\boldsymbol{\xi})
remains on the unit-determinant manifold throughout the transport, and the entropy in
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is strictly invariant along trajectories. The flow matching objective in (
30
) is then minimized with respect to the parameters
\psi
of the potential function
H_{\psi}
.

For the scalar velocity field
{u}(v,t)
we use a simple multi-layer perceptron with time features. All determinant transport is isolated in
v
.

5.2
Isentropic Properties of the Hamiltonian Flow Model

We investigate the properties of the Hamiltonian flow model, as specified in Section
5.1
, and show that it is entropy preserving in a precise sense, and with respect to both the Euclidean volume as well as Fisher–Rao volume. Our main result is the following, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.8
.

Proposition 5.1

(Ambient and Fisher–Rao entropy of the flow model)
.

Let
q_{t}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})
be a time-dependent density in the normalized RT coordinates
{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}}=(v,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, with
\boldsymbol{\xi}=({\bf d},{\bf r}_{\mathrm{norm}})
, evolving under the continuity equation

\partial_{t}q_{t}+\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}}}\cdot(q_{t}{\bf u})=0,

with velocity field of the form:
{\bf u}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}},t)=(u(v,t),\,{\bf u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v))

where
u(v,t)\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\bf u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Assume further that the shape field is Hamiltonian in the shape coordinates, so that:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\bf u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v)\equiv 0.

Then the following hold.

(i)

If

H_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(q_{t}):=-\int q_{t}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})\log\!\left(\frac{q_{t}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})}{\exp((p+1)v/2)}\right)d{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}},

denotes the entropy relative to the ambient SPD volume
d\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, then,

\frac{d}{dt}H_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\partial_{v}u(v,t)+\frac{p+1}{2}u(v,t)\right].

(ii)

If

H_{FR}(q_{t}):=-\int q_{t}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})\log\!\left(\frac{q_{t}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})}{2^{-p/2}}\right)d{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}},

denotes the entropy relative to the Fisher–Rao volume
d\mathrm{Vol}_{FR}
, then,

\frac{d}{dt}H_{FR}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\partial_{v}u(v,t)\right].

A consequence is that under both notions of entropy, the Hamiltonian shape component contributes no entropy production, which is carried only by the determinant drift
u(v,t)
. As a result, if
u(v,t)\equiv 0
, then Proposition
5.1
yields:

\frac{d}{dt}H_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(q_{t})=\frac{d}{dt}H_{FR}(q_{t})=0.

Hence, a pure Hamiltonian shape flow is simultaneously entropy-preserving (or isentropic) with respect to both the ambient volume and the Fisher–Rao volume. This has important implications for our design, as a Hamiltonian shape flow for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid v
on a unit determinant manifold ensures the neural network’s capacity is spent entirely on learning the solenoidal reconfiguration of the shape coordinates, without altering the manifold-valued entropy.

6
Application 2: Fisher–Rao Distance Bounds Computable in
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
on the SPD Manifold

As another computational application, we show that, under a uniform spectral bound on the SPD matrix, the Fisher–Rao metric in the
(v,{\rm d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates is sandwiched between two fixed quadratic forms. This yields an explicit ellipsoidal surrogate for Fisher balls computable in
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
, whereas exact FR distance calculations remain
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
. Recall from (
16
) that in the
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates the Fisher–Rao line element is:

ds_{FR}^{2}=\frac{1}{2p}\,dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{p}e^{d_{j}+v/p}\,({\rm d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}.

Then we have the following result, with a proof in Supplementary Section
S.9
.

Proposition 6.1

(Two-sided ellipsoidal bounds under spectral control)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
be a region in the
(v,{\rm d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates such that for every
{\bf y}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
,

mI_{p}\preceq\Theta({\bf y})\preceq MI_{p}

(33)

for some constants
0<m\leq M<\infty
. Then, throughout
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, the Fisher–Rao metric satisfies the two-sided bound:

\frac{1}{2p}\,dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\frac{m}{M}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|d\beta_{j}\|^{2}\;\leq\;ds_{FR}^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2p}\,dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\frac{M}{m}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|d\beta_{j}\|^{2}.

(34)

Proposition
6.1
implies that, if

{\bf y}_{0},{\bf y}_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and the straight coordinate segment

\gamma(t)={\bf y}_{0}+t({\bf y}_{1}-{\bf y}_{0}),\qquad t\in[0,1],

remains in
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, then the restricted Fisher–Rao distance

d_{FR,\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\bf y}_{0},{\bf y}_{1}):=\inf_{\gamma\subset\boldsymbol{\Omega}}L_{FR}(\gamma)

satisfies:

\frac{(v_{1}-v_{0})^{2}}{2p}+\frac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{d}^{(1)}-\mathbf{d}^{(0)}\|^{2}+\frac{m}{M}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{(0)}\|^{2}\leq d^{2}_{FR,\boldsymbol{\Omega}}({\bf y}_{0},{\bf y}_{1})\leq\frac{(v_{1}-v_{0})^{2}}{2p}+\frac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{d}^{(1)}-\mathbf{d}^{(0)}\|^{2}+\frac{M}{m}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{(0)}\|^{2}.

(35)

In particular, on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, the restricted Fisher–Rao distance is
bi-Lipschitz equivalent to an explicit quadratic norm in the

(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates. If the spectral bound holds globally on
the full admissible domain, then the same statement holds with

d_{FR,\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
replaced by the unrestricted distance
d_{FR}
. Numerical results exploring the tightness of the bounds over varying values of
c=m/M\in(0,1]
are presented in Section
7.2
. The following remarks clarify some implications.

Remark 4

(Computational complexity)
.

Evaluating these bounds cost
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
. If
c
is a global constant on the region of interest, then RT coordinates make Fisher geometry globally equivalent to a weighted Euclidean geometry on
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
with distortion depending only on
c
. This turns many geometric, optimization and analytic problems on SPD under Fisher geometry to Euclidean questions, up to fixed constants. The penalty is separable in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and the Gaussian quadratic form is
{\bf y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}{\bf y}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}e^{s_{j}}(y_{j}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{\top}y_{1:j-1})^{2}
and
\log\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}s_{j}
. Thus, the per iteration complexity of Gaussian likelihood evaluation is also
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
. If
c=m/M\in(0,1]
is uniform on the search region, the exact Fisher metric and the RT quadratic metric are uniformly equivalent in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, with distortion controlled only by
c
.

Remark 5

(Ellipsoidal surrogate for Fisher balls and optimization implications)
.

Proposition
6.1
shows that, on any spectrally controlled region
\Omega
, Fisher balls are trapped between two explicit ellipsoids in the
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
coordinates. In particular,

\{{\bf x}:\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}\|_{Q_{+}}\leq\rho\}\subseteq\{{\bf x}:d_{FR}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0})\leq\rho\}\subseteq\{{\bf x}:\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}\|_{Q_{-}}\leq\rho\},

where
Q_{+}
and
Q_{-}
are the upper and lower distances. Thus, although the exact Fisher distance is curved and state-dependent, it admits a conservative quadratic surrogate in these coordinates. Suppose one wishes to solve a Fisher-regularized optimization problem of the form:

\min_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\;F({\bf x})+\lambda\,d_{FR}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0})^{2}.

On a spectrally controlled region
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, Proposition
6.1
shows that the Fisher penalty can be replaced by explicit quadratic lower and upper surrogates:

\lambda\,\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}\|_{Q_{-}}^{2}\;\leq\;\lambda\,d_{FR}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0})^{2}\;\leq\;\lambda\,\|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}\|_{Q_{+}}^{2}.

This does
not
identify the exact Fisher regularization with a Euclidean quadratic penalty. Rather, it provides a uniform quadratic envelope on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which is often sufficient for trust-region design, inner proximal iterations, or conservative local models.

)
using the learned flows and starting from a Gaussian base by successively sampling

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=u(v,t),\qquad v_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle={\bf u}(\boldsymbol{\xi},t;v),\qquad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}).

The standardized volume variable is transported from a standard Gaussian base

9
Conclusions

We propose a new unconstrained coordinate system for SPD matrices that demonstrates nontrivial demarcations from existing unconstrained charts such as matrix log, Cholesky or spectral charts. The most salient feature is the isolation of the determinant and the Jacobian in a single scalar coordinate, which is a demarcation from other existing charts. This leads to several nontrivial geometric and algorithmic consequences that we thoroughly explore in the rest of the paper, including intrinsic diffusion and split volume–shape generative flow models.

The present paper explores the RT coordinates and their implications from several angles, but of course, it is not possible to explore or even anticipate all consequences in a single paper. Nevertheless, some areas bear mentioning. For generative modeling, we explore approaches based on CFM due to their speed and flexibility. Nevertheless, score-based diffusion models may be preferable for generative tasks if sample diversity in CFM becomes a concern. Similar to intrinsic sampling from a target distribution described in Section
7.3
, we expect the RT chart to enable intrinsic diffusion-based generative modeling in a manner far easier than most other charts. On the other hand, one may also explore sharper geometric consequences, such as Levi–Civita connections or curvature computation in RT coordinates. We leave these areas open for future investigations.

S.1
Derivation of (
10
)

We seek the conditional Jacobian of the
j
-th step, representing the change in the new precision variables
(\mathbf{v}_{j},\sigma_{jj})
with respect to the
j
-th column coordinates
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj})
. Below, we abbreviate
\mathbf{u}_{j}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}
and
d_{j}=\tilde{\theta}_{jj}
.
From (
3
), the
j
-th block is defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u_{j}}}{d_{j}}\right),

\displaystyle\sigma_{jj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}}{d_{j}^{2}}.

The Jacobian matrix
J_{j}
for this conditional mapping is:

J_{j}=\frac{\partial(\mathbf{v}_{j},\sigma_{jj})}{\partial(\mathbf{u}_{j},d_{j})}=\begin{bmatrix}-d_{j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}&d_{j}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}\\
2d_{j}^{-2}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}&-d_{j}^{-2}-2d_{j}^{-3}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Using the Schur determinant formula:
\mathrm{det}\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}=\mathrm{det}(A)\mathrm{det}(D-CA^{-1}B)
, we compute:

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}(-d_{j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})=(-d_{j}^{-1})^{j-1}\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(D-CA^{-1}B)

\displaystyle=(-d_{j}^{-2}-2d_{j}^{-3}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j})-(2d_{j}^{-2}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})(-d_{j}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}^{-1})(d_{j}^{-2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j})

\displaystyle=-d_{j}^{-2}-2d_{j}^{-3}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}+2d_{j}^{-3}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\mathbf{u}_{j}=-d_{j}^{-2}.

The conditional Jacobian magnitude is:

|J_{j}|=\left|(-d_{j}^{-1})^{j-1}\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})(-d_{j}^{-2})\right|=d_{j}^{-(j+1)}\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}).

Since
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})
is the reciprocal of the determinant of the
j-1
covariance sub-block, it is expressed as the product of the preceding pivots:

\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}\tilde{\theta}_{kk}\right)^{-1}.

Substituting this into
|J_{j}|
, we obtain the final closed-form expression:

\left|\frac{\partial(\mathbf{v}_{j},\sigma_{jj})}{\partial(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j},\tilde{\theta}_{jj})}\right|=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}\tilde{\theta}_{kk}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{-(j+1)}.

S.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

The Fisher–Rao (FR) metric on the SPD manifold
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{+}
is defined by the line element:

ds^{2}_{\mathrm{FR}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{(\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Theta})^{2}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,d\boldsymbol{\Theta})^{2}\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}
.
We define the
incremental
telescoping contribution of level
j
to the FR line element by:

ds^{2}_{\mathrm{FR},j}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\;:=\;\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}\,d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\big)^{2}\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\,d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}\big)^{2}\right\},

so that
ds^{2}_{\mathrm{FR}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}ds^{2}_{\mathrm{FR},j}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
.
To prove the result, we define:

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}:=\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\bullet j}}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}}.

and recall the block partition formula from (
3
) for
j=2,\dots,p
:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}\\
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}^{\top}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\end{bmatrix},\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}&-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}\\
-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j}\end{bmatrix}.

We abbreviate:

a:=\tilde{\theta}_{jj},\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}:=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\bullet j},\qquad{\bf S}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}^{-1},

so that,

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+a\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}&a\boldsymbol{\beta}\\
a\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}&a\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}&-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\\
-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}&a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{bmatrix}.

Throughout the derivation below, the predecessor block
(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1})
is held fixed while differentiating the new coordinates
(\boldsymbol{\beta},a)
.
Differentiating the block representation gives:

d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}d(a\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})&d(a\boldsymbol{\beta})\\
d(a\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})&da\end{bmatrix}.

Using the chain rule,

d(a\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})=\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\,da+a\,(d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}),\qquad d(a\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}\,da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Thus,

d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\,da+a(d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})&\boldsymbol{\beta}\,da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}&da\end{bmatrix}.

We further simplify notations by writing:

d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&{\bf b}\\
{\bf b}^{\top}&da\end{bmatrix},

where,

\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a(d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}),\qquad{\bf b}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\,da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Then,

\mathbf{M}_{j}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}\mathbf{A}-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}{\bf b}^{\top}&{\bf S}{\bf b}-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}da\\
-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\mathbf{A}+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})b^{\top}&-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}{\bf b}+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})da\end{bmatrix}.

We simplify each block separately.

1.

For the upper-left block,

\displaystyle{\bf S}\mathbf{A}-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}{\bf b}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+a{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\Bigr]-\Bigl[{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}.

2.

For the upper-right block,

\displaystyle{\bf S}{\bf b}-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}da

\displaystyle={\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\beta}da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta})-{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}da

\displaystyle=a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}.

3.

For the lower-left block,

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\mathbf{A}+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}){\bf b}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\Bigl[{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+a{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\Bigr]+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da-a\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}-a(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}

\displaystyle\qquad+a^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}da+d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+a(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}

\displaystyle=d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}}{a}da-a(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}.

4.

Finally, for the lower-right block,

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}{\bf b}+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})da

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\beta}da+a\,d\boldsymbol{\beta})+(a^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})da

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})da-a\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta}+a^{-1}da+(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\beta})da

\displaystyle=\frac{da}{a}-a\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Therefore,

\mathbf{M}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}&a{\bf S}\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\\
d\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}+\dfrac{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}}{a}da-a(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta})\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}&\dfrac{da}{a}-a\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}{\bf S}d\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{bmatrix}.

We proceed to compute the trace of the square. Introduce the abbreviations:

Proof of Proposition
3.2

Recall that:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top},\qquad d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=d\mathbf{L}_{j}\,\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}+\mathbf{L}_{j}\,d\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}.

Hence,

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-1}(d\mathbf{L}_{j}\,\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}+L_{j}\,d\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top})=\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-\top}(\mathbf{A}_{j}+\mathbf{A}_{j}^{\top})\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{A}_{j}=\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j}
. By cyclicity of trace,

\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j})^{2}\bigr)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\mathbf{A}_{j}+\mathbf{A}_{j}^{\top})^{2}\bigr),

and so,

ds_{FR,j}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\mathbf{A}_{j}+\mathbf{A}_{j}^{\top})^{2}\bigr).

We now compute
\mathbf{A}_{j}
recursively. Since,

\mathbf{L}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}&0\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}&\alpha_{j}\end{bmatrix},

its inverse is:

\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}&0\\
-\alpha_{j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}&\alpha_{j}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Also,

d\mathbf{L}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1}&0\\
d\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}&d\alpha_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

\mathbf{A}_{j}=\mathbf{L}_{j}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1}&0\\
-\alpha_{j}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1}+\alpha_{j}^{-1}d\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}&\alpha_{j}^{-1}d\alpha_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Writing:

\mathbf{A}_{j-1}:=\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1},\qquad c_{j}:=\frac{d\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}},\qquad{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}:=\alpha_{j}^{-1}(d\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j-1}^{-1}d\mathbf{L}_{j-1}),

we have the compact block form:

\mathbf{A}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{j-1}&0\\
{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}&c_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence,

\mathbf{A}_{j}+\mathbf{A}_{j}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}_{j-1}&{\bf b}_{j}\\
{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}&2c_{j}\end{bmatrix},\qquad{\bf S}_{j-1}:=\mathbf{A}_{j-1}+\mathbf{A}_{j-1}^{\top}.

\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}_{j-1}&{\bf b}_{j}\\
{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}&2c_{j}\end{bmatrix}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}_{j-1}^{2}+{\bf b}_{j}{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}&{\bf S}_{j-1}{\bf b}_{j}+2c_{j}{\bf b}_{j}\\
{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}{\bf S}_{j-1}+2c_{j}{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}&{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}{\bf b}_{j}+4c_{j}^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Taking traces gives,

\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\mathbf{A}_{j}+\mathbf{A}_{j}^{\top})^{2}\bigr)=\operatorname{tr}({\bf S}_{j-1}^{2})+2{\bf b}_{j}^{\top}{\bf b}_{j}+4c_{j}^{2}.

Therefore,

ds_{FR,j}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}({\bf S}_{j-1}^{2})+\|{\bf b}_{j}\|^{2}+2c_{j}^{2}=ds_{FR,j-1}^{2}+\|{\bf b}_{j}\|^{2}+2\left(\frac{d\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{2}.

This proves the recursive formula.

For

Fix

Now,

. By the RT Jacobian formula, for the ambient SPD volume, the reference density is:

m_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})=\exp((p+1)v/2),\qquad\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}}}\log m_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=((p+1)/2,0,\ldots,0).

Thus,

\displaystyle{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}\log m_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p+1}{2}u(v,t).

(S.12)

Using (
S.11
) and (
S.12
) into (
S.10
),

\frac{d}{dt}H_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}[\partial_{v}u(v,t)+\frac{p+1}{2}u(v,t)],

which proves Part (i).

For the Fisher–Rao volume, the reference density is:

m_{FR}({\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}})=2^{-p/2},\qquad\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}}}\log m_{FR}=\left(0,\ldots,0\right).

Therefore,

{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{norm}}}\log m_{FR}=0,

and so,

\frac{d}{dt}H_{FR}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\partial_{v}u(v,t)\right].

This proves Part (ii).

S.9
Proof of Proposition
6.1

Recall the notation:

s_{j}=\log\tilde{\theta}_{jj}=d_{j}+\frac{v}{p},\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}_{\bullet j}}{\tilde{\theta}_{jj}},

and recall from (
16
) that the Fisher–Rao line element in
(v,{\bf d},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is:

ds^{2}_{FR}=\frac{1}{2p}dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{p}e^{s_{j}}(d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}.

Thus it remains only to bound the last term. Recall from (
3
) that at stage
j
one has:

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}+\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{\top}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\\
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{\top}&\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}=(\tilde{\theta}_{11}),

and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}^{-1}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}
is obtained recursively from
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
by rank-one Schur complements, the spectral bound:

mI_{p}\preceq\boldsymbol{\Theta}\preceq MI_{p},

implies, recursively, that:

mI_{j}\preceq\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}\preceq MI_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,p.

To see this, indeed, if,

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix},

satisfies
mI\preceq A\preceq MI
, then the Schur complement:

A/A_{22}=A_{11}-A_{12}A_{22}^{-1}A_{21},

satisfies the same bounds. The lower bound follows from the variational identity:

x^{\top}(A/A_{22})x=\min_{z}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
z\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
z\end{pmatrix}\geq m\|x\|^{2},

while the upper bound follows from:

A/A_{22}\preceq A_{11}\preceq MI.

Applying this argument repeatedly along the reverse telescoping recursion gives the displayed
bounds for all
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j}
. Consequently,

mI_{j-1}\preceq\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}\preceq MI_{j-1},\quad\quad\frac{1}{M}I_{j-1}\preceq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j-1}^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{m}I_{j-1}.

Moreover, since
\tilde{\theta}_{jj}
is itself the final Schur pivot at stage
j
, the same Schur-residual bounds give:

m\leq\tilde{\theta}_{jj}\leq M.

Therefore, for any increment
{\bf u}_{j}=d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
,

\frac{m}{M}\|{\bf u}_{j}\|^{2}\leq e^{s_{j}}{\bf u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j-1}{\bf u}_{j}\leq\frac{M}{m}\|{\bf u}_{j}\|^{2}.

Substituting
{\bf u}_{j}=d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
into the Fisher–Rao line element yields:

\frac{1}{2p}dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\frac{m}{M}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\|^{2}\leq ds^{2}_{FR}\leq\frac{1}{2p}dv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(dd_{j})^{2}+\frac{M}{m}\sum_{j=2}^{p}\|d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\|^{2},

which proves (
34
).
