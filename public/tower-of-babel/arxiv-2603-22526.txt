Statistical Flux Freezing with Magnetic Path-lines in Turbulence
Amir Jafari

Abstract
The classical Alfvén flux–freezing theorem states that, in ideal magnetohydrodynamics, magnetic flux is transported by the flow and magnetic field lines remain frozen into the plasma. In turbulent plasmas, however, the velocity and magnetic fields are spatially rough, invalidating the regularity assumptions underlying the classical theorem. Previous work has shown that Lagrangian trajectories in such rough flows can become nonunique in the limit of small magnetic diffusivity, leading to stochastic formulations of magnetic flux freezing based on magnetic field lines. Field lines, however, are instantaneous geometric objects that do not possess a natural time evolution and do not preserve any identity in turbulent flows. Recent work has proposed an alternative description based on magnetic path-lines , which are time-parametrized trajectories generated by an effective transport field constructed from the Eulerian magnetic field. This formulation provides dynamical objects in spacetime and has been shown to recover known reconnection rates and to define magnetic topology change within a mathematically well-defined framework. In this work we use path-lines to formulate a statistical flux-freezing theorem in turbulent plasmas. Introducing a stochastic regularization of the path-line equation 𝐗 ˙ ​ ( t ) = 𝐁 ​ ( 𝐗 ​ ( t ) , t ) \dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}(t),t) , we study the zero–diffusivity limit of the resulting stochastic dynamics. We show that if backward pair dispersion persists in this limit, the transition kernel of the stochastic path-line dynamics cannot collapse to a Dirac measure. Magnetic path-lines therefore remain intrinsically stochastic in the ideal limit, implying that magnetic flux is conserved only in the statistical sense over ensembles of backward-advected path-line surfaces. This yields a statistical formulation of Alfvén’s theorem in terms of magnetic path-lines and shows that the classical deterministic form of flux freezing cannot hold in sufficiently rough turbulent magnetic fields. While closely related in physical content to earlier stochastic flux-freezing approaches based on magnetic field lines, the path-line formulation provides time-evolving dynamical trajectories that retain identity in spacetime and offer a simpler and more transparent framework for analyzing stochastic magnetic transport.



I
Introduction

The classical Alfvén flux–freezing theorem
Alfvén 1942
in

ideal
magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), describing the transport of
magnetic flux by the flow, has played a central role in plasma physics.
However, the presumed
ideal
regime is extremely sensitive to
initial conditions and can quickly give rise to turbulence. The
classical derivation of flux freezing relies on implicit regularity
assumptions for the velocity and magnetic fields. In particular, it
assumes that Lagrangian trajectories of the flow are uniquely defined.
In turbulence this assumption can easily fail, as the velocity field
may become only Hölder continuous with exponent
h=1/3<1
, well
below the Lipschitz regularity required by the Picard–Lindelöf
theorem for uniqueness of trajectories
Sreenivasan 1984
;
Eyink 2018
.
This lack of regularity is closely related to the Onsager
conjecture
Onsager 1945
;
Eyink and Sreenivasan 2006
;
Eyink 2018
concerning
anomalous dissipation in turbulent flows.

When the advecting field is sufficiently rough, Lagrangian trajectories
can become non-unique in the limit of vanishing viscosity or
diffusivity. This phenomenon is known as
spontaneous
stochasticity

Bernard et al. 1998
. Instead of converging to a single
deterministic trajectory, the vanishing–noise limit can produce a
probability distribution of possible particle histories. In the context
of magnetized turbulence, this behavior has important implications for
magnetic transport and flux freezing
Eyink 2011
. Most existing
stochastic formulations of flux freezing are expressed in terms of
magnetic field lines
Lazarian et al. 2020
.

Field lines, however, are geometric objects defined only at a fixed
instant of time and require an auxiliary curve parameter. As a result
they do not naturally form dynamical objects evolving in time.
Especially in turbulent plasmas, field lines do not preserve any
identity as the magnetic field evolves
Lazarian et al. 2020
.

In this paper we adopt a different viewpoint and formulate flux
freezing in terms of
magnetic path-lines
, as proposed
in
Jafari 2025a
. These are trajectories
\mathbf{X}(t)

generated by an effective transport field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)

through the dynamical equation

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}(t),t).

(1)

Here
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is interpreted as an effective
transport field constructed from the Eulerian magnetic field and
representing the propagation of magnetic structures in spacetime. In
practice this transport field is obtained through the path-line
construction introduced in
Jafari 2025a
, which associates
time–parametrized trajectories to the evolution of magnetic
structures in spacetime. Essentially, this construction assigns to
each spacetime point a local transport direction determined by the
evolution of the magnetic field, yielding an effective vector field
that generates the magnetic path-line trajectories. This
construction replaces the instantaneous geometric notion of magnetic
field lines by genuine dynamical trajectories in spacetime.

In the path-line construction, the effective transport field is obtained from the spacetime evolution of the magnetic field through a coarse-grained representation that associates to each spacetime point a local transport direction for magnetic structures. For the purposes of the present analysis we treat

{\mathbf{B}}({\mathbf{x}},t)
as a given divergence-free transport field satisfying the structural assumptions stated below. The details of this construction are not required for the present
analysis, except for the structural properties (boundedness,
measurability, and divergence-free condition). The crucial point is that, unlike field lines, these curves
are naturally parametrized by time and therefore constitute genuine
dynamical objects even in turbulent flows. This makes it possible to
analyze magnetic transport using the tools of Lagrangian dynamics and
stochastic processes
Jafari 2025a
;
Jafari 2025b
.

The path–line viewpoint provides a useful framework for analyzing
magnetic reconnection. In this formulation the separation of nearby
path-lines naturally measures the rate at which magnetic structures
diverge in spacetime. In particular, the rate of separation of
path-lines is consistent with reconnection rates predicted by models
based on field-line diffusion. In laminar flows this reproduces the
Sweet–Parker reconnection rate

v_{\rm rec}\sim\frac{v_{A}}{\sqrt{S}},

where
v_{A}
is the Alfvén speed and
S
is the Lundquist number

Parker 1957
;
Sweet 1958
. In turbulent flows, path-line approach leads to the same stochastic reconnection
rates traditionally obtained using field-line
wandering,

v_{\rm rec}\sim v_{A}\,M_{A}^{2},

where
M_{A}
is the Alfvénic Mach number

Lazarian and Vishniac 1999
;
Eyink et al. 2013
;
Lazarian et al. 2015
;
Lalescu et al. 2015
;
Lazarian et al. 2020
.
This formalism therefore captures the same underlying
stochastic transport physics that appears in field-line formulations,
while providing a dynamical trajectory framework in spacetime.

The path-line viewpoint also leads naturally to a dynamical–systems
interpretation of magnetic topology. For a magnetic field coarse–grained
on an inertial scale
l
, the coupled system

d\mathbf{X}/dt=\mathbf{B}_{l}(\mathbf{X},t)
together with the
coarse–grained induction equation defines a regular dynamical
system on the phase space
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B}_{l})
. The rate of
magnetic topology change can then be identified with the compression
rate of phase–space volume generated by this dynamical
system
Jafari 2025a
. For smooth coarse–grained fields this rate
can be expressed as the instantaneous trace of the Jacobian of the
flow, whose time–averaged value is related to the sum of Lyapunov
exponents. In turbulent flows the characteristic compression rate
scales as the eddy turnover rate at scale
l
,

\tau_{T}^{-1}\sim\delta u_{l}/l
, and therefore diverges as
l\to 0
.
This provides a dynamical bridge between magnetic topology change and
the emergence of spontaneous stochasticity at small scales, associated
with unbounded Lyapunov growth rates (see Appendix
A
).

We will not pursue these dynamical considerations about reconnection
and topology change in the present work. Instead we focus on flux
freezing itself and analyze it directly in terms of path-lines. To
investigate the behavior of these trajectories in rough (turbulent)
transport fields, we introduce a stochastic regularization of
Eq. (
1
) and examine the limit of vanishing magnetic
diffusivity. The central question is whether the regularized stochastic
trajectories collapse to a single deterministic path-line as the noise
amplitude tends to zero, or whether a finite spread of possible
histories persists.

A useful diagnostic of this behavior is backward pair dispersion.
Consider two trajectories that arrive at the same spacetime point

(\mathbf{x},t)
and are integrated backward in time. If the dynamics
were deterministic in the ideal limit, the two trajectories would
coincide at all earlier times. Persistent separation of such
trajectories therefore signals nonuniqueness of path histories and
provides a natural indicator of spontaneous stochasticity.

Our analysis shows that persistent pair dispersion prevents the
stochastic dynamics from collapsing to a unique deterministic
trajectory. Magnetic path-lines remain intrinsically stochastic even
in the ideal limit. Instead of being described by a single trajectory,
the motion of magnetic structures must therefore be represented
statistically by probability measures on path histories.

Physically, this result implies that the classical Alfvén
flux–freezing picture can break down in sufficiently rough turbulent
magnetic fields. In such regimes magnetic transport cannot be
represented by a unique deterministic mapping of path-lines, but only
by a stochastic ensemble of possible trajectories. The path-line
formulation developed in
Jafari 2025a
thus provides a natural
Lagrangian framework for describing stochastic magnetic transport in
turbulent plasmas.

We then formulate a stochastic version of Alfvén flux freezing in the
present path-line framework. For the stochastic flow generated by the
regularized path-line dynamics, magnetic flux is conserved only in the
ensemble sense over random backward-advected surfaces. If
S_{t}
is a
surface at time
t
and
S_{t_{0}}^{\omega}
denotes its random backward
image under the stochastic path-line flow, then the magnetic flux
satisfies the stochastic Alfvén relation

\Phi_{t}=\mathbb{E}\,\Phi_{t_{0}}^{\omega},

where
\Phi_{t}=\int_{S_{t}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot d\mathbf{A}
is the
flux through
S_{t}
,
\Phi_{t_{0}}^{\omega}
is the flux of the initial
field through the random surface
S_{t_{0}}^{\omega}
, and

\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes expectation over realizations of the
stochastic path-line flow. Thus magnetic flux is frozen not into a
single deterministic family of path-lines but only in the statistical
sense over the ensemble of stochastic backward surfaces generated by
the regularized path-line dynamics (see Appendix
B
).

The present approach is closely related to earlier stochastic
flux–freezing theories
Eyink 2011
;
Eyink et al. 2013
;
Lalescu et al. 2015
,
but differs in the choice of fundamental objects. Previous formulations
focus on magnetic field lines, whereas here the primary objects are
time–parametrized magnetic path-lines
Jafari 2025a
. This
perspective leads to a simpler dynamical description and clarifies how
spontaneous stochasticity manifests itself in the ideal limit. The
purpose of the present work is not to replace stochastic flux-freezing
theories, but to provide an alternative Lagrangian formulation based on
dynamical path-lines rather than instantaneous field-line geometry.

II
Stochastic Path-lines and Flux Freezing

We now develop the stochastic path-line formulation underlying the
preceding discussion. Our aim is to replace deterministic magnetic
path-lines by a regularized stochastic dynamics and then analyze the
zero–noise limit in a form directly analogous to stochastic
formulations of turbulent transport.

We consider trajectories defined by

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}(t),t),

(2)

where
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is a divergence–free vector field on

\mathbb{R}^{3}
;
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
. In the present framework we assume that
{\mathbf{B}}({\mathbf{x}},t)
is divergence-free, consistent with the path-line construction in
Jafari 2025a
.

Here
\mathbf{B}
is interpreted as an
effective transport field

constructed from the Eulerian magnetic field and generating

magnetic path-lines
. These trajectories evolve under

\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
rather than describing instantaneous
magnetic field lines, which are curves tangent to the magnetic field at
fixed time. The field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is not the
instantaneous magnetic field itself but a derived transport direction
associated with the spacetime evolution of magnetic structures. Its
construction from the Eulerian magnetic field is described in detail in
Ref.
Jafari 2025a
. In the present work we treat

\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
as a given transport field and focus on the
stochastic properties of the resulting trajectory dynamics.

The path-line equation

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}(t),t)

describes trajectories generated by a time-dependent transport field
whose evolution is governed by the underlying MHD dynamics. In general form this evolution may be written as

\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\bm{\mathcal{E}},

where
\bm{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{x},t)
denotes the electric
field or, more generally, the effective electromotive force governing
the magnetic dynamics. The path-line equation therefore describes
trajectories generated by a time-dependent vector field whose evolution
is determined by the underlying MHD dynamics
Jafari 2025a
.

A related class of trajectory dynamics arises in the Elsässer
formulation of incompressible MHD. Introducing the Elsässer fields

\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{B}
, one may define
trajectories generated by

\dot{\mathbf{X}}^{\pm}=\mathbf{z}^{\pm}(\mathbf{X}^{\pm},t)
. These
curves represent Alfvénic transport directions associated with
counter-propagating fluctuations. The stochastic path-line formalism
developed below can be generalized to such Elsässer trajectories. For
completeness this extension is outlined in Appendix
D
.

The analysis here isolates the kinematic properties of the path-line
equation in rough transport fields and analyzes the stochastic
regularization of this trajectory dynamics independently of the
specific dynamical closure for
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
.

If
\mathbf{B}
is Lipschitz in space, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem
guarantees existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
2
).
However, turbulent vector fields may violate this regularity
condition. For example, Kolmogorov scaling suggests spatial Hölder
continuity with exponent
1/3
, which lies below the Lipschitz
threshold. Such roughness is closely related to the Onsager conjecture
on anomalous dissipation and can lead to nonuniqueness of Lagrangian
trajectories. The divergence–free condition implies that the
advection operator
\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla
is anti–adjoint in
L^{2}

and that deterministic flows preserve volume whenever they exist.

II.1
Stochastic regularization and transport

To regularize the dynamics we introduce a small stochastic perturbation.
For
\kappa>0
consider the backward stochastic differential equation
obtained by adding a diffusive perturbation to the path-line dynamics,

d\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa}=-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa},s)\,ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,d\mathbf{W}_{s},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x},

(3)

where
\mathbf{W}_{s}
is standard three–dimensional Brownian motion.
The minus sign reflects the backward–in–time integration used to
trace trajectories arriving at the final spacetime point

(\mathbf{x},t)
. The time variable
s
runs backward from the final time
t
toward
earlier times, while the backward Kolmogorov equation describes the
dependence of the conditional expectation on the terminal time
t
.

Because the noise is additive, the Itô and Stratonovich formulations
coincide. The parameter
\kappa
plays the role of an effective
Lagrangian diffusivity. In the MHD setting this stochastic
regularization corresponds physically to the magnetic resistivity

\eta
appearing in the induction equation, but it is convenient here
to denote the regularizing diffusivity by
\kappa
. The limit

\kappa\to 0
therefore corresponds to the high magnetic Reynolds
number regime
R_{m}\to\infty
.

The regularized dynamics induces a natural backward transport
representation. For a smooth observable
f
, define

u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa})\mid\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x}\right],\qquad\tau<t.

(4)

The conditional expectation is understood through the transition
kernel
p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}|t,\mathbf{x})
of the diffusion
process. Equivalently, in terms of the transition kernel

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}|t,\mathbf{x})
,

u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int f(\mathbf{y})\,p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}|t,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{y}.

(5)

Standard backward Kolmogorov theory then gives

\partial_{t}u^{\kappa}=\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla u^{\kappa}-\kappa\Delta u^{\kappa},\qquad u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=f(\mathbf{x}).

(6)

Thus stochastic magnetic path-lines transport observables according to
an advection–diffusion equation with drift
\mathbf{B}
and
diffusivity
\kappa
, posed as a backward parabolic problem.
Physically, this equation describes the stochastic spreading of
backward path-line histories generated by the effective transport
field.

II.2
Pair dispersion and noncollapse

The crucial question is whether the stochastic regularization
collapses to a single deterministic path-line as
\kappa\to 0
.
To test this, consider two trajectories

\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa,1}
and
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa,2}
arriving at
the same final spacetime point
(\mathbf{x},t)
. If the zero–noise
limit were deterministic, then these two backward trajectories would
converge to the same point at any earlier time
\tau<t
.

Persistent pair dispersion rules out this possibility.
If two trajectories conditioned to arrive at the same final point
retain a finite probability of separation at earlier times, the
zero–noise limit cannot collapse to a single deterministic path-line. Here
\mathbf{X}^{\kappa,1}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\kappa,2}
denote two
independent realizations of the conditioned stochastic dynamics,
equivalently two independent samples from the transition kernel

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot\mid t,\mathbf{x})
.

Theorem II.1

(Noncollapse of the zero–noise limit)
.

Let
p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot|t,\mathbf{x})
denote the backward transition
kernel associated with the stochastic dynamics (
3
), and let

\mathbf{X}^{\kappa,1}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\kappa,2}
be two independent
samples drawn from this conditional distribution. Assume that the family

\{p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot|t,\mathbf{x})\}_{\kappa>0}
is tight on

\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Suppose there exist
\tau<t
,
r_{*}>0
, and
p_{*}>0
such
that

\limsup_{\kappa\to 0}\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|\geq r_{*}\big)\geq p_{*}.

(7)

Such persistent pair dispersion is expected in turbulent flows with
rough advecting fields, where backward trajectories separate according
to Richardson–type scaling (see e.g.
Bernard et al. 1998
;
Eyink 2011
). Then the transition kernel

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot|t,\mathbf{x})
cannot converge weakly to a Dirac
measure as
\kappa\to 0
:

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot|t,\mathbf{x})\nRightarrow\delta_{\mathbf{a}_{*}}.

(8)

Hence the ideal-limit path-line dynamics remains intrinsically
stochastic and cannot be represented by a single deterministic
trajectory.

Proof sketch.

Assume instead that the kernel converges to a Dirac mass

\delta_{\mathbf{a}_{*}}
. Then each marginal converges in probability to
the same point
\mathbf{a}_{*}
at time
\tau
. It follows that the pair
separation

|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|

converges to zero in probability, contradicting the lower bound
(
7
). Therefore the zero–noise transition kernel cannot
collapse to a deterministic point. A detailed proof is given in
Appendix
C
.

Note that this theorem is conditional: it does not establish the existence of persistent pair dispersion for the path-line dynamics itself, but shows that whenever such dispersion occurs the stochastic regularization cannot collapse to a deterministic trajectory. In turbulent flows with rough advecting fields such persistent backward dispersion is widely expected on physical grounds and is consistent with Richardson-type separation observed in turbulent transport.

II.3
Stochastic path-line freezing

Theorem
II.1
implies that persistent pair dispersion prevents
collapse of stochastic magnetic path-lines to unique deterministic
curves in the ideal limit. Instead, the limiting dynamics must be
described statistically by probability measures on path space.
We refer to this phenomenon as
stochastic path-line freezing
.

Corollary II.2

(Stochastic path-line freezing)
.

Whenever the pair–dispersion condition (
7
) holds, magnetic
path-lines are not frozen as unique deterministic curves. Instead the
dynamics induces an ensemble of admissible path histories in the
zero–noise limit.

This stochasticity provides a kinematic Lagrangian mechanism by which
magnetic connectivity and transport can evolve in highly conducting
turbulent flows. A full dynamical theory of reconnection, however,
requires coupling to the induction equation and energy balance.

II.4
Statistical flux freezing

The noncollapse result established above shows that deterministic
path-line freezing fails in rough turbulent fields. The natural
replacement is a statistical version of flux freezing based on the
stochastic path-line flow generated by the regularized dynamics.

To state this precisely one must distinguish between the effective
transport field that generates the path-lines and the magnetic field
whose flux is transported by that stochastic flow. Let
\mathbf{B}_{l}

denote the effective transport field generating the stochastic
path-lines, and let
\mathbf{B}
denote the magnetic field transported
by that flow. For each realization of the stochastic dynamics, a
smooth surface
S_{t}
at time
t
is mapped backward to a random
preimage surface

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(S_{t})

at an earlier time
t_{0}
. Magnetic transport is then described not by
a single deterministic preimage, but by an ensemble of
backward-advected surfaces.

The resulting stochastic Alfvén theorem takes the form

\int_{S_{t}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot d\mathbf{A}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(S_{t})}\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{a})\cdot d\mathbf{A}\right],

(9)

where
\mathbf{B}_{0}
is the initial magnetic field and

\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes expectation over realizations of the
stochastic path-line flow generated by
\mathbf{B}_{l}
. Thus magnetic
flux through a surface at time
t
equals the ensemble average of the
initial flux through its random backward images.

Equation (
9
) expresses flux conservation under the stochastic path-line flow in the same sense as stochastic flux-freezing relations derived previously for velocity-driven transport: the deterministic Lundquist relation is recovered only if the stochastic flow collapses to a single trajectory in the ideal limit. Eq. (
9
) is the path-line analogue of the
stochastic flux-freezing relation previously obtained in formulations
based on fluid trajectories and magnetic field lines. The difference
here is that the random transport is generated directly by the
effective magnetic path-line field rather than by the plasma velocity.
For each fixed regularization scale the stochastic flow is well
defined, and the deterministic Lundquist stretching relation is
replaced by its stochastic counterpart. In the zero-noise limit, if
the stochastic flow collapses to a unique map,
Eq. (
9
) reduces to classical deterministic flux
freezing; if the flow remains nontrivial, as in spontaneous
stochasticity, flux freezing survives only in the ensemble sense.
A complete derivation is given in Appendix
B
.

III
Discussion

The formulation developed in the present work approaches flux freezing
from a different perspective than previous treatments. Instead of
using magnetic field lines as the primary objects, we consider
time–parametrized magnetic path-lines generated by an effective
transport field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
. Because these curves
evolve in time, they can be analyzed using the standard tools of
Lagrangian dynamics and stochastic processes.

\color
black
In ideal incompressible MHD with negligible bulk flow
(
\mathbf{u}\approx 0
and
\rho=\mathrm{const}
), the equation

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{B}
coincides with propagation of Alfvénic
disturbances at the Alfvén speed, so the path-lines describe genuine
signal transport along the magnetic field. In general non-ideal,
turbulent, or advecting plasmas (
\mathbf{u}\neq 0
), these curves
should not be interpreted as trajectories of material carriers nor as
exact characteristics of the full MHD system (whose propagation
directions are given by the Elsässer velocities

\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{B}
). Rather, they represent geometrically
preferred pathways along which magnetic tension communicates
disturbances and along which magnetic structure reorganizes in
spacetime. Their physical significance is therefore operational: the
stochastic divergence of nearby path-lines quantifies the rate at
which magnetic configurations decorrelate. In particular, this
dispersion reproduces both laminar Sweet–Parker reconnection and the
turbulent Lazarian–Vishniac scaling, analogously to Richardson
dispersion in stochastic flux-freezing theories. In fact, the coarse-grained induction equation implies that the filtered Eulerian field
{\bf B}_{\ell}
does not evolve as if it were simply frozen into
{\bf u}_{\ell}
, nor necessarily as if it propagated purely with
{\bf u}_{\ell}\pm{\bf B}_{\ell}
.

Within this framework the key diagnostic of deterministic behavior is
the collapse of backward trajectories arriving at the same spacetime
point. If the classical flux–freezing picture were valid, two such
trajectories would coincide at earlier times in the limit of
vanishing diffusivity. The analysis presented above shows that this
collapse fails whenever backward pair dispersion persists. In that
situation the transition kernel of the stochastic path-line dynamics
cannot converge to a Dirac measure, and the ideal-limit transport
process remains intrinsically stochastic.

As a consequence, magnetic transport cannot in general be described by
a unique deterministic mapping of path-lines in sufficiently rough
turbulent magnetic fields. Instead, magnetic transport must be
described statistically through probability distributions on path
histories. In this setting the classical deterministic form of the
Alfvén flux–freezing theorem breaks down. Nevertheless, a
statistical form of flux freezing survives: magnetic flux is conserved
in the ensemble sense over random surfaces obtained by backward
advection along the stochastic path-line flow (see Sec.
II
and
Appendix
B
).

This statistical flux-freezing law arises naturally from the
stochastic path-line formulation. When backward trajectories fail to
collapse, the preimage of a surface under the path-line flow becomes a
random ensemble of surfaces. Magnetic flux is therefore not preserved
along a single deterministic mapping, but only in the mean over the
ensemble of stochastic path-line histories.

The mechanism underlying this behavior is spontaneous stochasticity.
Persistent pair dispersion prevents the stochastic regularization of
the path-line equation from collapsing to a single deterministic
trajectory in the zero–diffusivity limit. The resulting stochastic
transport of magnetic structures provides a natural Lagrangian
mechanism by which magnetic connectivity can evolve even when the
microscopic nonideal effects are arbitrarily small.

\color
black
Importantly, this evolution should not be understood as motion of
individual field lines or flux tubes. The stochastic path-lines encode
transport of magnetic
pattern
rather than transport of a
localized physical object. In this sense they are analogous to
quasiparticles such as phonons or photons in field theory: asking
“what exactly moves” along these trajectories is no more meaningful
than assigning a precise classical trajectory to a photon. The curves
instead represent propagation of correlations and constraints in the
field, i.e. trajectories of Alfvénic causal influence.

The stochastic path-line picture also has implications for magnetic
reconnection. Classical reconnection theories often assume that
magnetic connectivity evolves through localized nonideal effects,
while the large-scale magnetic field remains approximately frozen
into the flow. In turbulent plasmas, however, the stochastic
transport implied by spontaneous stochasticity allows magnetic
connectivity to evolve through turbulent dispersion of magnetic
structures. Recent work based on the path-line formulation has
derived expressions for rates of magnetic connectivity change in
turbulent flows
Jafari 2025a
. The present results provide a
complementary foundation for such approaches by establishing that the
underlying path-line dynamics may remain intrinsically stochastic in
the ideal limit.

\color
black
Within this viewpoint, reconnection is associated with rapid
reorganization of magnetic pattern rather than with the identity of
individual field lines. Exponential separation of nearby path-lines
quantifies the rate at which magnetic configurations decorrelate,
while loss of continuity of the bundle flow corresponds to genuine
topological change.

A quantitative theory of reconnection rates requires coupling the
present path-line framework to the full induction equation and the
dynamics of the magnetic and velocity fields. The analysis presented
here establishes the kinematic ingredient of this picture by showing
that the ideal-limit path-line dynamics can remain intrinsically
stochastic.

The persistent pair-dispersion condition appearing in the theorem can
in principle be tested in numerical simulations of MHD turbulence.
Given a time sequence of magnetic-field data, one first constructs the
effective transport field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
following the
procedure described in Ref.
Jafari 2025a
. Stochastic path-line
trajectories satisfying the regularized equation (
3
) can then
be integrated backward from a common spacetime point
(\mathbf{x},t)

for multiple realizations of the noise. The distribution of backward
pair separations

|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|

provides a direct estimate of the transition kernel

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot|t,\mathbf{x})
. If the probability of separation
larger than some fixed scale
r_{*}
remains nonzero as
\kappa\to 0
,
the noncollapse condition of Theorem
II.1
is realized.

The path-line viewpoint also highlights the difference between
time-evolving trajectories and instantaneous geometric objects.
Magnetic field lines are defined only at a fixed time and require an
auxiliary curve parameter, whereas path-lines are ordinary dynamical
trajectories in spacetime. Treating these trajectories as the basic
objects provides a direct way to analyze stochastic transport
mechanisms arising from rough advecting fields.

\color
black
Mathematically, the construction defines a closed dynamical system on
the tangent bundle with base coordinates
\mathbf{x}
and fiber
coordinates
\mathbf{B}
, since
\dot{\mathbf{B}}
is determined by
the induction equation. This base–fiber structure admits a natural
Riemannian geometry given by the Sasaki metric, providing a canonical
notion of distance, continuity, and topology for evolving magnetic
configurations.

It is also important to emphasize that the present analysis is purely
kinematic. It establishes the noncollapse of path-line dynamics under
persistent pair dispersion, but does not determine reconnection rates
or energy conversion. Addressing those questions requires coupling the
stochastic path-line description to the induction equation and to the
dynamics of the magnetic and velocity fields. In such an extension the
effective transport field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
would evolve
consistently with the MHD equations, allowing the stochastic
path-line dynamics to be embedded in a fully dynamical model.

Finally, closely related trajectory dynamics arises in the Elsässer
formulation of incompressible MHD, where the natural advecting fields
are the Elsässer variables
\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{B}
.
Trajectories defined by

\dot{\mathbf{X}}^{\pm}=\mathbf{z}^{\pm}(\mathbf{X}^{\pm},t)

describe propagation directions associated with counter-propagating
Alfvénic fluctuations. In incompressible MHD turbulence these fields
represent the fundamental carriers of nonlinear energy transfer in the
inertial range. Extending the stochastic path-line framework to such
Elsässer trajectories suggests that spontaneous stochasticity may
arise more generally in transport processes associated with rough MHD
advecting fields.

Appendix A
Topology Change and Spontaneous Stochasticity

In this Appendix we sketch the derivation of the rate of topology
change based on the path–line formalism proposed
in
Jafari 2025a
.

The path–line formulation naturally leads to a dynamical–systems
interpretation of magnetic transport. At a coarse–graining scale
l

we consider the smoothed magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{l}(\mathbf{x},t)

obtained by averaging the Eulerian magnetic field over spatial scales

\sim l
; for details of possible coarse–graining schemes
see
Jafari 2025b
and references therein. The corresponding
coarse–grained induction equation can be written schematically as

\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}_{l}=\nabla\times(\mathbf{u}_{l}\times\mathbf{B}_{l}-\mathbf{R}_{l}),

where
\mathbf{R}_{l}
denotes the turbulent electromotive force arising
from subscale fluctuations. For any fixed
l>0
the coarse–grained
field
\mathbf{B}_{l}
is smooth and Lipschitz continuous, so the
trajectory equation

d\mathbf{X}/dt=\mathbf{B}_{l}(\mathbf{X},t)
defines a regular
dynamical system.

Combining the trajectory equation with the coarse–grained induction
equation yields a coupled dynamical system on the phase space

(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B}_{l})
,

\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt}=\mathbf{B}_{l}(\mathbf{X},t),\qquad\frac{d\mathbf{B}_{l}}{dt}=(\nabla\times\bm{\mathcal{E}})_{l},

where
\bm{\mathcal{E}}
denotes the effective electromotive
force. Introducing the phase–space variable

Z=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B}_{l})
, this system may be written compactly as

dZ/dt=F(Z,t)
. The rate of magnetic topology change introduced
in
Jafari 2025a
is defined as the compression rate of phase–space
volume generated by this flow. For a dynamical system
dZ/dt=F(Z,t)

the instantaneous change of an infinitesimal phase–space volume
V
is

\frac{d}{dt}\ln V=\nabla\!\cdot F.

Thus the topology–change rate corresponds to the divergence of the
phase–space flow associated with the coupled

(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{B}_{l})
dynamics.

When the flow is locally linearized, the divergence can be written as
the trace of the Jacobian matrix of the dynamical system,

\nabla\!\cdot F=\mathrm{tr}\,J_{l},\qquad J_{l}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z}.

In ergodic dynamical systems the long–time average of this quantity is
related to the sum of Lyapunov exponents,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{tr}\,J_{l}(t)\,dt=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}
are the Lyapunov exponents of the flow. In this way
the topology–change rate may be interpreted as the instantaneous
phase–space compression rate of the coarse–grained dynamical system,
whose time–averaged value is connected to the Lyapunov spectrum.

In MHD turbulence, the dominant nonlinear contribution to the
coarse–grained induction equation arises from the turbulent
electromotive force
\mathbf{R}_{l}
. Standard inertial–range estimates
give
\mathbf{R}_{l}\sim\delta\mathbf{u}(l)\,\delta\mathbf{B}(l)
, where

\delta\mathbf{u}(l)
and
\delta\mathbf{B}(l)
denote velocity and
magnetic increments across scale
l
. The curl entering the induction
equation therefore scales as

|\nabla\times\mathbf{R}_{l}|\sim\frac{|\mathbf{R}_{l}|}{l}\sim\frac{\delta u(l)\,\delta B(l)}{l}.

Estimating the sensitivity of this induction term with respect to

\mathbf{B}_{l}
gives the characteristic phase–space compression rate

\mathrm{tr}\,J_{l}\sim\frac{\delta u(l)}{l}.

Thus the topology–change rate at scale
l
is of order

\tau_{T}^{-1}(l)\sim\frac{\delta u(l)}{l},

which corresponds to the eddy turnover rate at that scale.

In turbulent flows the velocity increments obey inertial–range
scaling laws. Kolmogorov theory gives
\delta u(l)\sim(\varepsilon l)^{1/3}
, leading to
\tau_{T}^{-1}(l)\sim\varepsilon^{1/3}l^{-2/3}
.
This scaling is consistent with Onsager’s
argument
Onsager 1945
;
Eyink and Sreenivasan 2006
;
Eyink 2018
that finite
energy dissipation in the limit of vanishing viscosity requires
turbulent velocity fields to be at most Hölder continuous with
exponent
h\leq 1/3
(dissipative anomaly). Such Hölder singularity
implies that the instability rate
\delta u(l)/l
diverges as

l\to 0
, providing a natural dynamical route to spontaneous
stochasticity.

For any fixed coarse–graining scale
l
the field
\mathbf{B}_{l}
is
regular and the corresponding Lyapunov exponents remain finite.
However, as the coarse–graining scale decreases the effective
instability rate grows without bound,

\tau_{T}^{-1}(l)\rightarrow\infty\qquad\text{as}\qquad l\rightarrow 0.

This divergence reflects the loss of Lipschitz regularity of the
underlying turbulent magnetic field. In the limit of vanishing
coarse–graining scale the effective Lyapunov exponents become
unbounded and the deterministic trajectory description breaks down,
leading to nonunique path histories and persistent stochasticity.

From this viewpoint spontaneous stochasticity may be understood as the
singular limit of the coarse–grained dynamical system when the
smoothing scale tends to zero. At finite
l
the flow possesses
well–defined Lyapunov growth rates and a finite phase–space
compression rate
\mathrm{tr}\,J_{l}
, while in the limit
l\to 0
these
instability rates diverge, producing intrinsic stochasticity of the
Lagrangian dynamics and breakdown of classical flux freezing in rough
turbulent magnetic fields.

Appendix B
Statistical Flux Freezing via Magnetic Path-Lines

In this appendix we derive a stochastic flux-freezing relation in
terms of magnetic path-lines. To avoid notational confusion, we denote
by
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)
the divergence-free transport field
generating the path-lines, and by
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x},t)
the
divergence-free vector field transported by that flow. Here

\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{H}
are conceptually distinct:

\mathbf{V}
generates the stochastic flow of trajectories, while

\mathbf{H}
denotes a vector field transported by that flow.

The derivation below establishes a general kinematic result: whenever a
divergence-free vector field
\mathbf{H}
satisfies the transport
equation

\partial_{t}\mathbf{H}=\nabla\times(\mathbf{V}\times\mathbf{H})+\kappa\Delta\mathbf{H},

the flux of
\mathbf{H}
through surfaces evolves according to a
stochastic flux-freezing relation associated with the stochastic flow
generated by
\mathbf{V}
. This statement does not require the
transport field
\mathbf{V}
to coincide with the magnetic field or
with the fluid velocity.

In the application relevant to the present work the transported field
is the magnetic field itself,
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{B}
, while the
transport field is the effective path-line field

\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{B}_{l}
. These trajectories represent the
propagation directions of magnetic structures in spacetime, as
introduced in Ref.
Jafari 2025a
. The purpose of the present
derivation is therefore not to introduce a new dynamical induction
equation in which the magnetic field is advected by
\mathbf{B}_{l}
,
but rather to establish the stochastic flux relation associated with
the stochastic path-line flow generated by this effective transport
field. For comparison, the stochastic flux-freezing theorem of
Eyink
Eyink 2011
is recovered from the same derivation by taking

\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{u}
, the fluid velocity.

Assume that
\mathbf{V}\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[t_{0},t_{f}])
with

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}=0
, and let

\mathbf{H}_{0}\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfy

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H}_{0}=0
.
Under these assumptions, for each fixed
\kappa>0
the backward SDE
introduced below generates a stochastic flow of
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms,
so that the Jacobian matrices and surface pullback used in the
derivation are well defined. For
\kappa>0
consider the induction
equation driven by
\mathbf{V}
,

\partial_{t}\mathbf{H}=\nabla\times(\mathbf{V}\times\mathbf{H})+\kappa\Delta\mathbf{H},

(10)

with
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H}=0
and

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x},t_{0})=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
. Using

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H}=0
, this may be written
equivalently as

\partial_{t}\mathbf{H}+(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{V}+\kappa\Delta\mathbf{H}.

(11)

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{x})
denote the backward
stochastic flow generated by
\mathbf{V}
, defined for
t_{0}\leq s\leq t

by

\hat{d}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{V}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{x}),s\big)\,ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,\hat{d}\mathbf{W}_{s},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t,t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x},

(12)

where
\hat{d}
denotes the backward Itô differential. Because the
drift field
\mathbf{V}
is divergence-free and the noise is additive,
the stochastic flow generated by (
12
) is
volume-preserving almost surely. For each fixed realization of the
Brownian motion, the map

\mathbf{x}\mapsto\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{x})
is a

C^{1}
diffeomorphism, with inverse

\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{s,t}=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{s,t}^{-1}
.

Write

\mathbf{J}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a})=\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a})

for the Jacobian matrix of the inverse flow. Differentiating the flow
equation with respect to
\mathbf{a}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{J}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a})=\nabla\mathbf{V}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a}),t\big)\,\mathbf{J}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a}),\qquad\mathbf{J}_{s,s}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{I}.

(13)

Since
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V}=0
, Liouville’s formula implies

\det\mathbf{J}_{s,t}(\mathbf{a})=1.

(14)

Define the random vector field

\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{a})\Big|_{\mathbf{a}=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{x})}.

(15)

Equivalently,

\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{a}),t\big)=\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{a}).

(16)

A direct backward Itô–Wentzell calculation shows that

\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}
satisfies the stochastic transport equation

\hat{d}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}+\Big[(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}-(\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{V}-\kappa\Delta\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}\Big]dt+\sqrt{2\kappa}(\hat{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}=0,

(17)

with initial condition

\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},t_{0})=\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
.

Taking expectations and using

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}]=0

gives

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}+(\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{E}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}=(\mathbb{E}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{V}+\kappa\Delta\mathbb{E}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{H}},

which coincides with (
11
). By uniqueness of
solutions of (
10
), it follows that

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{a})\Big|_{\mathbf{a}=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{x})}\right].

(18)

Equation (
18
) is the stochastic Lundquist formula in
path-line form.

We now derive the corresponding stochastic Alfvén theorem. Let

S
be an oriented smooth surface at time
t
, and let

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(S)
denote its random backward image
under the stochastic path-line flow. If

d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{x}}
and
d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{a}}
are the
oriented area elements on
S
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(S)
,
respectively, then the standard area transformation formula gives

d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{x}}=\operatorname{cof}\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}(\mathbf{a})\,d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{a}},

where
\operatorname{cof}\mathbf{J}
denotes the cofactor matrix.
Because of (
14
), incompressibility implies

\operatorname{cof}\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}=\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}^{-T}.

Hence

\big(\mathbf{J}_{t_{0},t}\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)\cdot d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}_{0}\cdot d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{a}}.

Using this identity in (
18
) yields

\int_{S}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t_{0},t}(S)}\mathbf{H}_{0}(\mathbf{a})\cdot d\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{a}}\right].

(19)

Equation (
19
) is the stochastic Alfvén theorem in
path-line form: the flux through a surface at time
t
equals the
ensemble average of the initial flux through the random surfaces
obtained by stochastic backward advection along path-lines.

For the path-line formulation of the present paper one takes

\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{B}_{l}
, the effective transport field defining the
coarse-grained magnetic path-lines. In that case
(
18
) and (
19
) give the statistical
flux-freezing theorem associated with the stochastic path-line flow.

For each fixed coarse–graining scale
l>0
, the field
\mathbf{B}_{l}

is smooth and the stochastic flow is well defined. If, in the
zero-noise or zero-scale limit, the stochastic path-line flow
collapses to a unique deterministic map, then
(
19
) reduces to classical deterministic flux freezing.
If instead the zero-noise limit remains nontrivial, as in spontaneous
stochasticity, deterministic flux freezing fails while the statistical
relation (
19
) survives. Thus magnetic flux is frozen not
into a single deterministic family of path-lines, but only in the
ensemble sense over stochastic path histories.

Appendix C
Stochastic path-lines

In this appendix we summarize the mathematical assumptions and provide
the detailed arguments underlying the stochastic path-line formulation
used in the main text. We work in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Throughout this appendix we assume that the effective transport field

\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfies the following properties on the
spacetime domain considered:

(i)
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is bounded and measurable in

(\mathbf{x},t)
;

(ii)
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is divergence–free,

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
;

(iii) for each
\kappa>0
the stochastic differential equation
(
21
) admits a weak Markov solution with transition kernel

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}|t,\mathbf{x})

possessing the
usual continuity properties associated with diffusion processes.
(iv) the family of conditioned path measures

\mathrm{Law}(\mathbf{X}_{\cdot}^{\kappa}\mid\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x})

is tight on the path space
C([\tau,t];\mathbb{R}^{3})
equipped with the
uniform topology.

Such tightness is typical for diffusion processes with bounded
coefficients and is physically justified here by the boundedness of
the effective transport field
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
in turbulent
flows over finite domains or inertial ranges. Under these conditions
the stochastic trajectories remain confined and subsequential weak
limits of the path measures exist. Weak convergence is understood in the usual topology on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and conditioning on the terminal point is defined through the
corresponding regular conditional probabilities.

C.1
Deterministic path-line dynamics

We consider trajectories defined by

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}(t),t),

(20)

where
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)
is a divergence–free vector field on

\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0.

The analysis below does not require the detailed construction
of the transport field B(x,t). Instead only the structural properties
listed above are used, namely boundedness, measurability, and the
divergence–free condition. These assumptions ensure that the
stochastic differential equation (
21
) defines a diffusion
process with well-posed transition kernels and that the backward
Kolmogorov representation used below is valid.

If
\mathbf{B}
is Lipschitz in space, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem
guarantees existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
20
).
However, turbulent vector fields may violate this regularity
condition. For example, Kolmogorov scaling suggests spatial Hölder
continuity with exponent
1/3
, which lies below the Lipschitz
threshold. Such roughness is closely related to the Onsager conjecture
on anomalous dissipation and can lead to nonuniqueness of Lagrangian
trajectories.

The divergence–free condition implies that the advection operator

\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla
is anti–adjoint in
L^{2}
and that, for sufficiently regular fields, the associated deterministic flow is
volume preserving.

C.2
Stochastic regularization

To regularize the dynamics we introduce a small stochastic perturbation.
For
\kappa>0
consider the backward stochastic differential equation

d\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa}=-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa},s)\,ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,d\mathbf{W}_{s},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x},

(21)

where
\mathbf{W}_{s}
is standard three–dimensional Brownian motion.
The minus sign reflects the backward-in-time integration used to trace
trajectories that arrive at the final spacetime point
(\mathbf{x},t)
.

Because the noise is additive, the Itô and Stratonovich formulations
coincide; we adopt the Itô interpretation.

The parameter
\kappa
plays the role of an effective Lagrangian
diffusivity. In the MHD setting it corresponds physically to the
magnetic resistivity
\eta
, but it is convenient here to denote the
regularizing diffusivity by
\kappa
. The limit
\kappa\to 0

corresponds to the high magnetic Reynolds number regime
R_{m}\to\infty
.

Equation (
21
) defines a time–inhomogeneous diffusion
process with drift
-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},s)
and diffusivity

\kappa
, whose generator is

L_{s}\phi(\mathbf{x})=-\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},s)\cdot\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x})+\kappa\Delta\phi(\mathbf{x}),

(22)

for smooth test functions
\phi
.

C.3
Stochastic transport representation

For
f\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
define

u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int f(\mathbf{y})\,p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}\,|\,t,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{y}.

(23)

Proposition C.1

(Backward Kolmogorov equation)
.

The function
u^{\kappa}
defined in (
23
) satisfies

\partial_{t}u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\nabla u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t)-\kappa\Delta u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t),\;u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=f(\mathbf{x}).

(24)

Proof.

The process (
21
) is a time–inhomogeneous Markov diffusion
with generator (
22
). By backward Kolmogorov theory
for time–dependent diffusions the semigroup identity

-\partial_{t}P_{t,\tau}^{\kappa}f=L_{t}P_{t,\tau}^{\kappa}f

holds for
f\in C_{b}^{2}
.

Since

P_{t,\tau}^{\kappa}f(\mathbf{x})=\int f(\mathbf{y})\,p^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}\,|\,t,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{y}=u^{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},t),

substituting the generator (
22
) yields

-\partial_{t}u^{\kappa}=-\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla u^{\kappa}+\kappa\Delta u^{\kappa},

which rearranges to (
24
). The terminal condition follows
from (
23
).
∎

Equation (
24
) is an advection–diffusion equation with drift

\mathbf{B}
and diffusivity
\kappa
, posed as a backward parabolic
problem.

C.4
Pair dispersion

Consider two independent realizations of (
21
), conditioned
to arrive at the same spacetime point
(\mathbf{x},t)
:

\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa,1},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{s}^{\kappa,2}.

Independence of the two Brownian noises is convenient but not
essential; the argument below requires only convergence of the
individual marginals.

C.5
Noncollapse of the zero–noise limit

Theorem C.2

(Non–Dirac zero–noise limit)
.

Suppose there exist
\tau<t
,
r_{*}>0
, and
p_{*}>0
such that

\limsup_{\kappa\to 0}\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|\geq r_{*}\big)\geq p_{*}.

(25)

Then the transition kernel cannot converge weakly to a Dirac measure:

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot\,|\,t,\mathbf{x})\nRightarrow\delta_{\mathbf{a}_{*}}.

Here weak convergence refers to convergence of probability measures on

\mathbb{R}^{3}
against bounded continuous test functions.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that

p^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot\,|\,t,\mathbf{x})\Rightarrow\delta_{\mathbf{a}_{*}}.

Convergence in distribution to
\delta_{\mathbf{a}_{*}}
implies
convergence in probability to
\mathbf{a}_{*}
. Thus

\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}\to\mathbf{a}_{*},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}\to\mathbf{a}_{*},

in probability.

Consequently

|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|\to 0\quad\text{in probability}.

This implies

\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,1}-\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa,2}|\geq r_{*})\to 0,

contradicting (
25
). Hence the transition kernel cannot
converge to a Dirac measure.
∎

C.6
Stochastic path-line freezing

By tightness, subsequences of the conditioned path–space laws

\mathrm{Law}(\mathbf{X}_{\cdot}^{\kappa}\mid\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x})

converge weakly to probability measures
\mu_{\mathbf{x},t}
on the
path space
C([\tau,t];\mathbb{R}^{3})
. These limiting measures
describe the statistical ensemble of admissible backward path
histories.

Corollary C.3

(Stochastic path-line freezing)
.

Whenever the pair–dispersion condition (
25
) holds,
magnetic path-lines are not frozen as unique deterministic curves.
Instead the dynamics induces a probability measure
\mu_{\mathbf{x},t}

on path space describing an ensemble of admissible trajectories.

This stochasticity provides a kinematic mechanism by which magnetic
transport can evolve statistically in highly conducting turbulent
flows. A full dynamical theory requires coupling the present
description to the induction equation and energy balance.

C.7
Remarks

Quantitative noncollapse.

If one can establish a uniform lower bound

\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}_{\tau}^{\kappa}|\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\kappa}=\mathbf{x})\geq c>0,

then the distribution of backward endpoints retains a finite spread
independent of
\kappa
in the zero–noise limit. Physically this means
that magnetic structures arriving at the same spacetime point

(\mathbf{x},t)
can originate from a finite spatial region at earlier
times, rather than from a single deterministic trajectory.

Two–particle formulation.

Condition (
25
) can equivalently be expressed using the
two–particle transition kernel

p_{2}^{\kappa}(\tau,\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\,|\,t,\mathbf{x}).

Noncollapse corresponds to the failure of

p_{2}^{\kappa}(\tau,\cdot,\cdot\,|\,t,\mathbf{x})\Rightarrow\delta_{(\mathbf{a}_{*},\mathbf{a}_{*})}.

Path–space topology.

Weak limits of path measures are taken in the uniform topology
on
C([\tau,t];\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Appendix D
Elsässer path-line formulation

The stochastic path-line formalism developed in the main text and
Appendix
C
can be extended to trajectory dynamics generated
by the Elsässer fields of incompressible magnetohydrodynamics.
Introducing the Elsässer variables

\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{B},

the incompressible MHD equations may be written in Elsässer form as

\partial_{t}\mathbf{z}^{\pm}+(\mathbf{z}^{\mp}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=-\nabla P_{*}+\nu\Delta\mathbf{z}^{\pm},

with
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=0
. Here
P_{*}
denotes the total
pressure, including both fluid and magnetic contributions, which
enforces the incompressibility constraint.

In analogy with the magnetic path-line equation

\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{X},t)
, one may consider the
Elsässer trajectory equations

\dot{\mathbf{X}}^{\pm}(t)=\mathbf{z}^{\pm}(\mathbf{X}^{\pm}(t),t).

These trajectories represent propagation directions associated with
counter-propagating Alfvénic fluctuations. When the Elsässer fields
are spatially rough, the deterministic trajectory equations may fail
to possess unique solutions. Note that these trajectories should not be interpreted as characteristics of the
Elsässer equations, which involve cross-advection by the opposite
field, but simply as Lagrangian curves generated by the Elsässer
vector fields themselves.

A stochastic regularization can therefore be introduced:

d\mathbf{X}^{\pm,\kappa}_{s}=-\mathbf{z}^{\pm}(\mathbf{X}^{\pm,\kappa}_{s},s)\,ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,d\mathbf{W}_{s}.

The same arguments used in Section

apply to this system. In particular, if backward pair dispersion
persists in the zero-noise limit, the transition kernels of the
stochastic Elsässer dynamics cannot collapse to Dirac measures.
Consequently the Elsässer trajectory dynamics remains intrinsically
stochastic in the ideal limit.

This extension indicates that spontaneous stochasticity is not tied
specifically to magnetic path-lines but can arise more generally in
trajectory dynamics generated by rough advecting fields appearing in
the MHD system. In particular, stochastic transport may arise in
multiple MHD propagation channels associated with the Elsässer fields.
