‘Not the basic ABC of how you control a blood vessel’: perceptions of UK Defence Medical Services personnel toward live tissue training for the surgical management of trauma
Authors: C S Swain, M G Harjani, G Helgesson, R F Rickard, K Karlgren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction‘Live tissue training’ (LTT) is simulation using live anaesthetized animals as human patient substitutes. The educational benefits specific to LTT are unclear. This study aimed to explore the perceptions and attitudes of UK Defence Medical Services (DMS) personnel toward the use of LTT in the surgical management of trauma.MethodsFive virtual focus groups were conducted, with 20 regular and reservist DMS personnel representing surgical specialties, anesthesia, emergency medicine and primary care. Participants discussed their experiences of LTT and the impact on their professional development. Systematic text condensation was used to thematically analyze transcribed data.ResultsSix themes were constructed, titled: (1) LTT: Who should do it and when; (2) conducting LTT as a military team; (3) psychomotor learning; (4) affective learning; (5) fidelity; (6) ethics and justification. Differences in perspectives were noted for prehospital and secondary care learners - LTT is used to individually train primary care personnel and military medics in basic, initial trauma management skills; secondary care personnel consider LTT a ‘finishing school’ for experienced clinicians to practice rare and high-risk procedures, within a military team setting.ConclusionDMS personnel report positively about LTT, consider it to be realistic (higher fidelity) simulation training, and have educational and moral rationales to justify its use. The educational attributes of LTT appear to be context-specific and dependent on DMS learner role. This knowledge should influence the design and delivery of military trauma simulation involving live animals, allowing for replacement, reduction and refinement in their use for training different cohorts of learners.Level of evidence6 – qualitative research.

Introduction

The US military has recently announced that they will stop shooting live pigs and goats as part of medics’ battlefield training, although further unclarified uses of animals within wartime preparations will continue.1 So-called ‘live tissue training’ (LTT), the use of live anaesthetized animals to simulate human trauma casualties, has long been used by military medical populations to practice skills required for operational patient care. In addition to combat medics, military surgical teams have also trained using live animals.24 However, as alternative simulation modalities have developed, live animal use in simulation has steadily diminished, complying with a moral obligation to the principles of the 3Rs of humane animal research5—replacement, reduction and refinement.

In previous qualitative studies, US and Canadian combat medics considered LTT to have unique attributes, judging it irreplaceable for some aspects of training.68 They perceived that animals should be used above, or at least alongside, other simulation modalities,610 despite demonstrated equivalence in skills training.10 11 This finding is also supported by a study exploring the experiences of Australian Defence Force physicians12 and US military surgeons.4

Personnel within the United Kingdom (UK) Defence Medical Services (DMS)—combat medics and surgical teams—participate in LTT, although training has historically been conducted overseas with North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (NATO) partner nations. Many DMS personnel work and train within the National Health Service (NHS), but unlike other countries, LTT does not feature within UK civilian specialist medical training. Many nations report insufficient training and experience to maintain the skills required to manage traumatically injured casualties in deployed environments.1315 Increasing global instability has driven interest in generating trauma surgical capacity in military and civilian contexts.16 17 Only 27% of DMS surgeons feel they have sufficient exposure to penetrating trauma in their civilian practice13 and in response to a perceived lack of specific opportunity to learn to control major hemorrhage, the desire for LTT has been raised also by UK civilian surgical trainees.18

Despite a historic belief in the educational value, empirical evidence in support of LTT is equivocal. The perspectives of UK DMS personnel may richly contribute to the discussion regarding the use of LTT to learn about the surgical management of trauma. This study aimed to explore the perceptions and attitudes of this population with experience of both military and civilian contexts and simulation training with and without LTT. Information gained from this study might inform an understanding of how LTT can be appropriately and logically replaced, reduced and refined in military medical training, particularly in the presence of alternative simulators.

Methods

Context and participants

This is a qualitative study, conducted in a critical realist paradigm, which used focus group interviews to explore the perceptions of the study population. Participants were recruited from the DMS. Information about the study was disseminated electronically, inviting all full-time and reservist consultants and specialty trainees in surgery (general, vascular, trauma and orthopedic, burns and plastic surgery), anesthesia and emergency medicine to participate. These specialty groups were chosen based on historical DMS involvement with LTT, and the potential for likely future exposure to training using live animals. Purposive sampling was used to recruit personnel who had deployed operational experience working with specific military units known to use LTT as part of their predeployment training.

Study participation was voluntary and no incentives were offered or given. All participants were provided with information about the purpose of the study, informed their involvement would be anonymized, and provided written consent in advance.

Data collection

Focus group interviews lasting 45–60 min were conducted virtually across five dates in October and November 2022, moderated by one author (CS). Participants were allocated to a focus group interview according to their availability. A semistructured flexible approach was taken, using an interview guide, and initiated with the question: ‘Can you share any experiences of LTT and the impact that training had on your professional development’? Areas of interest were explored when raised by the participants within the conversation, or by posing questions at an appropriate time. Topics included: learning across different educational domains; specific attributes of LTT that may support learning; possible outcome measures; translation between military and civilian practice and ethical implications of animals as simulators. Audio recordings were subsequently transcribed using voice recognition software and transcriptions independently confirmed by two authors (CS and MH).

Data analysis

The four-step process of systematic text condensation19 was used to analyze discussions. Systematic text condensation as an analytical method was chosen as a transparent and collaborative approach to thematic analysis. Additionally, while use of both descriptive quotations and text condensation allows the participants’ voices to be present within the analysis, the latter better assists in maintaining the anonymity of individual participants. Text condensates should not be considered as quotations attributable to any one participant, but represent a collective narrative.

Researchers CS, MH and KK independently familiarized themselves with the data, reviewing transcripts to identify recurring topics. Through iterative discussion of possible codes and groups, preliminary themes were determined to guide further analysis (step 1). Transcribed data were systematically searched by CS for ‘meaning units’ (text fragments containing information relating to the research question). Codes were assigned to meaning units, with the core meaning and defining features of these codes evaluated throughout the process. Codes with similar or associated meanings formed subgroups within preliminary themes (step 2). The content of meaning units within a subgroup was then amalgamated, reducing the data to a text condensate, maintaining the original expressions used by participants where possible. Limited changes to text (to reduce use of military vernacular, or improve readability and comprehension) were denoted with the use of square brackets around the word or phrase. Where many participants voiced a similar phrase, the most representative example was chosen to include within the condensate (step 3). CS, MH and KK collectively reviewed the condensates, synthesizing the content to produce a descriptive story conceptualizing each theme, ensuring the synthesis was validated by reflecting the original context (step 4). Preliminary analytic results were critically discussed, modified and agreed by all authors.

Results

Twenty DMS personnel participated in five focus group interviews with Royal Navy, British Army and Royal Air Force represented, including both full-time military employees and reservists (n=3). All participants, except one, were male. The majority were consultants or senior specialty trainees, including 11 surgeons (five general, three vascular, two orthopedic, one plastic/reconstructive), 2 emergency medicine (EM) physicians, 2 anesthetists and 4 general practitioners (GP). There was a single non-physician represented, who had prior experience of LTT as a combat medic and now worked as a paramedic.

Seventeen participants had previously participated in LTT, as a learner or an educator in either a military or civilian setting. Educators had mostly been involved in teaching combat medics about the prehospital management of trauma. The described contexts varied from learners practicing a specified skill (ie, insertion of a surgical airway or chest drain), to training to perform an operation in a simulated operating theater, to large-scale military exercises with casualties simulated by anaesthetized pigs, injured in combat and transferred from Role 1 to Role 2 field hospital for teams to perform damage control surgery.

242 min of recorded data were transcribed and analyzed. Six main themes were identified and given descriptive labels. Early in the analytic process, it was identified that some perspectives about the educational use of LTT were discussed differently by military personnel: prehospital (GPs and medics—referred to as primary care) and hospital-based specialist learners (surgeons and anesthetists—referred to as secondary care), with EM physicians contributing perspectives to both cohorts. Text condensates illustrate the multivocal narratives of a subgroup within a theme (tables 1–6), with some subgroups noted to reflect the view of one cohort only.

Findings and analysis

The six themes are titled: (1) LTT: who should do it and when; (2) conducting LTT as a military team; (3) psychomotor learning; (4) affective learning; (5) fidelity; (6) ethics and justification. Each theme is interpreted within this section and should be read in conjunction with the associated table reporting the participants’ collective narrative.

LTT: who should do it and when

DMS personnel recognize a requirement to address a lack of clinical experience in managing trauma. LTT is one method to achieve this, but the type of learner who should participate in the training and when to conduct it is unclear (table 1). Secondary care personnel obtain their specialty training within a civilian NHS environment. Opportunities to regularly manage traumatic injuries are dependent on factors such as job role and location. In contrast, medics and GPs, required to provide prehospital trauma care in a deployed environment, often have no equivalent clinical practice and relatively low exposure to human trauma patients. Regardless of professional specialization, there is a known gap between civilian and military trauma management and an individual needs to demonstrate competence in their deployed role. This was previously addressed by conducting LTT during predeployment training, allowing medics and GPs to revise their skills and secondary care personnel to practice and reflect how their civilian-learned experiences can be appropriately transferred to a military environment. As a predeployment tool, LTT was felt to be beneficial, closely associated with findings within the theme conducting LTT as a military team (table 2).

There was no clear consensus about how often LTT should be conducted, although it was expressed that combat medics ‘who have done it a few times get bored’. Adequate frequency of training is likely to be highly individualized, depending on factors including prior experiences and current exposure to managing trauma. For secondary care personnel, specifically surgeons, there was discussion around when would be the most appropriate time within their professional training to attend an LTT course. Considering this alongside Psychomotor Learning subthemes attributed to secondary care personnel (table 3), educational value, especially for surgeons, may be more significant if LTT is performed at a later stage of professional training, or when qualified as a consultant.

Conducting LTT as a military team

Participants reported a key aspect of LTT was interprofessionalism, that is, conducting the training as a team and rehearsing early phases of the operational patient care pathway. The majority of participants felt this produced a wider range of educational opportunities, contributing to different aspects of learning such as situational awareness, communication and decision-making processes. Learning objectives and training outcomes should ideally be predetermined and address both individual and collective goals. One participant raised a potential benefit from ‘an interoperability point of view’, due to DMS personnel conducting LTT with other nations.

Psychomotor learning

While psychomotor learning was important for all participants, the value of it is attributed differently by prehospital and secondary care personnel (table 3). Prehospital learners use LTT to train in the management of basic trauma skills, gaining experiences using bespoke equipment. In contrast, secondary care personnel considered that LTT should be used for rare and high-risk procedures and considered ‘a finishing school’, where the technical aspects of performing damage control surgery are combined with other skills. Implicit within psychomotor learning is a requirement for the application of knowledge, although cognitive learning was not a usual or valued feature of LTT described by these participants.

What bridged these two distinct cohorts of learners was the importance of the tactility of the animal as part of their learning experience, although the ‘knife-to-skin’ experience is interpreted differently. For military medics, LTT may be a genuine initial learning experience as they obtain new sensory information, gain tacit knowledge of what living tissue feels like and how it behaves as they perform specific procedures. For other learners, especially physicians with less experience with invasive procedures, or, for example, dealing with major hemorrhage (‘warm blood bleeding onto your hands’) this experience may allow them to use or adapt their skills in different circumstances. For certain learners, specifically surgeons, there was a desire for the familiarity of living tissue to enhance practical learning, a theme that might be more aligned with their perception of realism (see Fidelity theme).

Affective learning

DMS personnel felt being able to perform under pressure, and in front of colleagues, was a beneficial learning experience (table 4). This performance related to making a clinical decision while in a stressful situation and subsequently being able to carry out the appropriate task successfully, training ‘psychological memory, as well as the muscle memory’. By doing this, learners feel LTT increases both individual and team confidence and competence in managing trauma.

Prehospital learners reported aiming for a type of desensitization from the training, managing or ‘getting over the emotional response’ to what they are experiencing. They reported that this builds psychological resilience for when they are required to apply the skills of basic trauma management during combat. Considering secondary care personnel already have an embodied understanding of what trauma casualties look and feel like, and are using LTT to train in the management of more complex trauma skills (inferring they have significant clinical experience to build on), ‘the sights, the smells, the sounds’ are not going to be new.

Fidelity

Fidelity is a term often used in simulation as a synonym for realism. LTT was described as high (or higher) fidelity training—meaning more realistic—on multiple occasions throughout the focus group interviews. The text condensates for this theme (table 5) illustrate key aspects of fidelity highlighted as being important for DMS personnel. Live animals were compared with other simulator modalities, including cadavers and non-organic surgical models, but it was felt that aspects such as appropriate and responsive physiology and the presence of blood and active bleeding could not yet be mimicked convincingly by these modalities. Although the anatomy of a pig does not accurately simulate the anatomy of a human, the presence of ‘real, correct physiology’ was more significant in training for trauma management.

Ethics and justification

The general ethical principle embodied by the participants aligned with ‘the ends justify the means’, a utilitarian view. The text condensates in table 6 illustrate the various rationales that DMS personnel use to defend LTT: the amount of animals used to train are minimized; training opportunities from each animal are maximized for the greatest number of learners; animals are treated humanely with veterinary involvement in their care; and, as pigs specifically are raised for human consumption, LTT was likely to be more humane than alternative methods of slaughter.

There was recognition that LTT is often conducted covertly, due to ‘previous bad publicity’, which could contribute to an associated stigma. Regardless, the participants overwhelmingly felt the requirement to save human lives, and the ability to ‘get it right when it really matters’ was of critical importance, justifying their own, and wider military participation in LTT.

Discussion

Live animal simulation training is used in surgical training across the world, but animal activist organizations have often focused on the training and actions of military personnel. Our study contributes to the literature by being the first to report the perspectives of Defence Medical Services personnel and to provide more information regarding physicians’ use of LTT alongside military combat medics.

LTT was described as ‘a good experience’, ‘brilliant’, ‘incomparable’ and ‘the best training I’ve ever had’. Justification of the training, both on a personal and an organizational level, was noted. Many of the features raised (meat eating, treatment of the animals, hypothetical discussions about training on humans or animals) have been reported in other studies.12 20 However, some individuals raised the question whether LTT is required at all, with the main concern that regardless of LTT being considered higher fidelity training, the educational value remains less than learning obtained from real-life clinical trauma experience with actual human patients. Notably, these people had previous significant operational experience or had trained in a high-volume civilian major trauma center; this is not practical for all DMS personnel and does not address the ongoing requirement to train to manage military-specific trauma casualties.

Our themes psychomotor learning, affective learning and fidelity support findings from previous qualitative studies of military cohorts.9 12 A more realistic stress response has been reported as a positive feature of LTT across previous studies of combat medics,610 along with ‘an emotional psychological component’ potentially unique to LTT, highlighted as of key value to DMS personnel.

Fidelity is the extent to which the appearance and behavior of a simulation emulates reality. It encompasses three dimensions of realism: physical, conceptual and psychological/emotional.21 22 During LTT, bleeding leads to adverse physiology, which is recognizable to the learner without the need for cues from a facilitator. Cueing is the use of triggers or prompts that either progress a simulation toward the learning objectives, or assist the learner in interpreting the simulated reality.22 Fewer external cues and faculty input arguably means LTT confers a high degree of conceptual fidelity, which is central in developing and practicing clinical reasoning skills. Using a live animal in a simulated event allows learners to make a clinically-oriented decision based on the injury pattern and physiology, practice a skill and receive feedback on their actions in real time, without direct faculty involvement, encouraging reflection during educational activity.

Psychological fidelity is associated with learner engagement and their experience within the simulation; higher fidelity in this domain elicits learners’ emotions and beliefs.22 Live animals are perceived to be the most engaging simulator modality7; the ability to work on a live animal/patient is considered the most significant strength of LTT by the majority of military personnel8 perhaps fostering investment during the simulation, which other modalities do not, especially when employed within a realistic team-based educational environment. According to our participants, the requirement to act, and ability to receive ‘live real time feedback’, influences investment. In fact, unlike other simulated learning events, LTT could be interpreted as a real clinical experience, although with an animal rather than a human patient. If so, it would confer a high degree of psychological fidelity, evidenced by participants’ statements such as ‘you’re no longer acting’ and ‘you have to keep the model alive’. Our findings indicate that high perception of fidelity in both conceptual and psychological domains is sufficient for learners to forgive the inaccuracies of animal anatomy. This is not problematic, providing learners have sufficient experience to understand the critical differences and are applying their knowledge of human anatomy to the pig, rather than learning for the first time.

Military prehospital personnel, specifically combat medics, are using LTT to train in technical skills, managing trauma at the point of wounding. These skills may be learned using alternative modalities, such as deceased tissue or non-animate models,23 although US combat medics report that live animals are the best modality to learn them.7 In addition to psychomotor skills, medics are also gaining tacit knowledge about tissue tactility and bleeding, managing stress and building psychological resilience, experiences which may not be captured well, or at all, in synthetic models or cadavers.6 This is in lieu of being able to access this learning via alternative means. There is an argument therefore, that it would be more appropriate for medics to be exposed to human trauma patients in a safe, supportive clinical environment to learn these lessons.

Some participants questioned how much additional benefit is obtained from the animal being alive, especially when the training focus is learning psychomotor skills with lower risk of bleeding (eg, cricothyroidotomy, finger thoracostomy). For simple procedures, live animals add little to the educational experience of surgeons beyond their daily practice.12 Arguably, for all specialist physicians LTT should, therefore, be reserved for rare, and high-risk procedures that involve major hemorrhage, and ideally be conducted as team-based training. Live tissue should only be used after other skill acquisition7—animals should be considered ‘the culmination… a finishing school’ to bridge the gap between other simulator training and real clinical practice, only when required. To maximize learning opportunities using a single animal, medic learners could be included soon after the animal has died to obtain the tactile feeling of real tissue and practice their skills.

What remains unclear is both the frequency of participation in LTT for different professions, and the most appropriate time in one’s training to conduct it. It was suggested that boredom was displayed by military personnel completing the same training multiple times; another opinion expressed was that ‘as a consultant learner you learn a great deal from a small volume of high-fidelity learning experiences’ and that it was a course one may go to once every five years or less. Obtaining objective evidence for the effectiveness of trauma surgery simulation courses is challenging, partly due to a lack of performance metrics.24 It is known that cognitive and psychomotor skill knowledge decay in the months after undergoing training,25 26 with complex tasks and practical skills decaying more quickly,25 particularly for novices with minimal opportunity to practice after training is completed.26 This would support the idea that LTT may be of greatest benefit immediately prior to deploying, with the aim of targeting training to specific personnel on each iteration to prevent boredom or stagnation of learning objectives. This is probably easier to achieve for secondary care personnel undertaking complex surgical scenarios in comparison to prehospital personnel who have a more formulaic approach to military trauma care.

Limitations

The participants represent a sample of voluntary DMS personnel and may not be representative of the whole organization. In particular, the views of military combat medics have been extrapolated from comments by those who have been involved in training them. It is possible that their lived experience of LTT differs from what has been reported in this article. Unfortunately despite concerted efforts, it was not possible to recruit these personnel to directly contribute to this study.

Additionally, this study population was almost entirely male; there are gender-associated differences in how animals are perceived, with females typically demonstrating greater concern for animal welfare and being less agreeable toward animal use in general.27 Although the DMS population does have a significantly higher proportion of males within the sampled specialties, no explicit effort was made to achieve participant gender representation.

It has been recognized that military populations may be positively biased towards LTT due to its historical use, and perceived success of the training in contributing to improved mortality rates in combat.9 This study specifically explored the DMS, based in the UK where LTT is severely restricted, and an opportunistic feature of medical training. Differences in training structure, cultural perceptions of live animal usage and accessibility to LTT by military personnel of other nations may mean some findings are not transferable beyond the UK.

Focus group interviews rely on facilitated discussion to generate data. Particularly in relation to this topic and study population, social desirability bias could be problematic. During interview moderation, the exploratory nature of the study was continually reiterated with a conscious effort made to use neutral language throughout during questioning. Power differentials can also contribute to bias.28 It should be recognized that the lead researcher (CS) who moderated the focus groups is a surgeon and DMS employee. Notably, she holds an equal or lower rank to the majority of the participants. This was beneficial in terms of reassuring participants of confidentiality, promoting familiarity as a member of the population and being able to manage the group dynamic, but may have contributed to issues experienced when recruiting lower ranked combat medics. The researchers’ interest was on DMS perceptions of education use of LTT. Through the inclusion of two researchers with no military background in the analysis phase, the likelihood of findings being biased by military social desirability should have reduced further.

Finally, although this research reports results of a specific population, the fact that many DMS personnel work within the civilian NHS may mean their insights could be considered more generalizable; there is evidence of beneficial reciprocal learning between military and civilian surgeons.29

Conclusion

This study aimed to report the perceptions and attitudes of DMS personnel toward LTT. Notably, perceptions of the educational value of LTT vary by different cohorts of learners. Although the participants report positively about LTT, consider it to be realistic (higher fidelity) simulation training, and have rationales to justify its usage, the data indicates possible opportunities to refine and reduce the use of live animals, specifically in relation to prehospital trauma care. This supports the US military’s recent announcement to stop LTT use for combat medics.

The information gleaned from this study can assist pedagogical design of simulations for military medical personnel with specific learning objectives. DMS personnel want to rehearse their skills in managing trauma in a stressful environment in order to gain competence and feel more confident in the clinical provision of trauma care. Instructors should consider the type of learners who are participating in the training and tailor the content of the educational experience. Multiple LTT experiences may not be beneficial for learners with significant trauma experience; this is a potential topic for further research. Simulation training using live animals should not be standardized, and each use should be considered for its appropriateness, with efforts made to continue enacting the moral obligation of the 3Rs.
