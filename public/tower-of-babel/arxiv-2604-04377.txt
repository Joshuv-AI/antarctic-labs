String Representation in Suffixient Set Size Space
Hiroki Shibata, Hideo Bannai

Abstract
Repetitiveness measures quantify how much repetitive structure a string contains and serve as parameters for compressed representations and indexing data structures. We study the measure χ \chi , defined as the size of the smallest suffixient set. Although χ \chi has been studied extensively, its reachability, whether every string w w admits a string representation of size O ⁡ ( χ ⁡ ( w ) ) O(\chi(w)) words, has remained an important open problem. We answer this question affirmatively by presenting the first such representation scheme. Our construction is based on a new model, the substring equation system (SES), and we show that every string admits an SES of size O ⁡ ( χ ⁡ ( w ) ) O(\chi(w)) .



1
Introduction

Repetitiveness measures aim to quantify how much repetitive structure a string contains.
They offer a unified way to compare strings and to reason about the space usage of compressed representations.
Beyond repetitiveness measures that arise directly from compression techniques, such as LZ-style parsing
[
16
]
and the run-length encoded Burrows–Wheeler transform
[
2
]
, repetitiveness measures that are not derived from any specific compression technique have been proposed, notably string attractors
[
8
]
and normalized substring complexity
[
10
]
.
These measures are widely used to analyze the size of existing compressed representations and indices, and they provide convenient parameters for stating space bounds for both representations and indexing data structures
[
14
]
.

Reachability
is a central notion for assessing the power of a repetitiveness measure.
A repetitiveness measure
f
is
reachable
if there exists a representation scheme that, for every string
w
, encodes
w
in
O(f(w))
machine words, i.e.,
O(f(w)\log|w|)
bits.
If
f
is reachable, then it can be used directly as a compressed representation.
If
f
is not reachable, then
f
will strictly lower-bound the space of any universal representation scheme for some (infinite) subset of strings.

Recently,
suffixient sets

[
5
]
have been proposed as a repetitiveness measure not derived from any particular compression scheme.
They were first introduced to support efficient random access and related queries together with compressed indexing in small space, and have since been studied in the context of space-efficient string indexes
[
3
]
.
Owing to a one-to-one correspondence with super-maximal right extensions, the size of the smallest suffixient set, denoted by
\chi
, can be computed in linear time in the text length, and it has attracted attention as an easily computable repetitiveness measure
[
4
,
13
]
.

Existing results already place
\chi
among the central repetitiveness measures
[
13
]
. For example, it is known that
\chi(w)\leq 2r(w)
for every string
w
, where
r
is the number of runs in the Burrows–Wheeler transform
[
2
]
, while there exist strings for which
\chi
is asymptotically smaller than more classical parsing measures such as the LZ factorization
[
16
]
size
z
and the lex-parse
[
12
]
size
v
.
At the same time, it is not known whether every string
w
admits a bidirectional macro scheme (BMS)
[
15
]
of size
O(\chi(w))
.
Since BMS is a general framework that subsumes many existing schemes, compression based on suffixient sets might lead to new compression methods that cannot be expressed as BMS.
Moreover,
\chi
upper bounds the smallest string attractor size
\gamma
, whose own reachability is a long-standing open problem, so proving
\chi
unreachable would immediately imply that
\gamma
is unreachable as well.
For this reason, Navarro et al.
[
13
]
state the conjecture: “We conjecture, instead, that
\chi
is not reachable, proving which would imply that
\gamma
is also unreachable, a long-time open question.”

In this paper, we disprove their conjecture by presenting the first string representation scheme that represents any string in
O(\chi(w))
words.
Our
O(\chi(w))
representation can be computed in
O(|w|)
time, and since there exist instance where
\chi(w)=o(z(w))
and

\chi(w)=o(v(w))

[
13
]
,
our result advances the frontier of linearly computable compression.
To this end, we introduce a general framework for representing strings by a
substring equation system
(SES).
Our construction is an instance of SES, and we show that every string admits an SES of size
O(\chi(w))
.
Since any bidirectional macro scheme can be transformed into an SES of the same size, SES provides a unifying framework for several standard compression schemes that are known to be expressible as BMS.

Preliminaries

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet of size
\sigma
.
An element of
\Sigma
is called a character.
A string
w
of length
|w|=n
over the alphabet
\Sigma
is a sequence
w[1]\cdots w[n]
of characters where
w[i]\in\Sigma
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

For any two strings
x
and
y
, we denote by
xy=x[1]\cdots x[|x|]y[1]\cdots y[|y|]
the concatenation of
x
and
y
.
For any string
x
, we denote by
x^{R}=x[|x|]\cdots x[1]
its reversal.
If
w=xyz
holds for some strings
x,y,z\in\Sigma^{*}
, then
x
,
y
, and
z
are called a prefix, a substring, and a suffix of
w
, respectively.
For
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, we denote by
w[i..j]=w[i]\cdots w[j]
the substring of
w
from position
i
to
j
,
and
w[i..j)=w[i..j-1]
. For convenience
w[i..j]
is the empty string when
j<i
.
We will assume that the string
w
is terminated by an end of string symbol denoted by
\$
, which does not occur elsewhere in the string.
For every string
w
, we denote by
\sigma(w)
the number of distinct characters appearing in
w
, including the special character
\$
.

For any
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
c\in\Sigma
,

xc
is a
right extension
in
w
if

xc
is a substring of
w
and for some

c^{\prime}\neq c
,
xc^{\prime}
is also a substring of
w
.
A right extension in
w
is
super-maximal
if it is not a proper suffix of another right extension in
w
.
The set of all right extensions and super-maximal right extensions in
w
is denoted by
\mathit{RE}(w)
and
\mathit{SRE}(w)
, respectively.

Existing work establishes a one-to-one correspondence between smallest suffixient sets and super-maximal right extensions
[
3
,
4
]
.
Using the notation of Navarro et al.
[
13
]
, we adopt the following definitions.

Definition 1
.

For a string
w
of length
n
,
a set
S\subseteq[1..n]
is a
suffixient set
for
w
if for every right extension
x\in\mathit{RE}(w)
there exists
j\in S
such that
x
is a suffix of
w[1..j]
.

Definition 2
.

For a string
w
of length
n
,
a set
S\subseteq[1..n]
is a
smallest suffixient set
for
w
if
there exists a bijection
\mathrm{pos}:\mathit{SRE}(w)\rightarrow S
such that every super-maximal right extension
x\in\mathit{SRE}(w)
is a suffix of
w[1..\mathrm{pos}(x)]
.

The repetitiveness measure
\chi
is defined as
\chi(w)=|\mathit{SRE}(w)|
.
By the above definition,
\chi(w)
is exactly the size of a smallest suffixient set for
w
.

1
shows an example of a smallest suffixient set and the corresponding super-maximal right extensions.

w=\texttt{aabbaababa}\$
.
The blue boxes indicate the positions in the smallest suffixient set.
The line segments under the characters indicate the super-maximal right extensions, with the blue segment marking the last character of each extension.

A
bidirectional macro scheme
(BMS) for a string
w
is a representation that partitions
w
into consecutive phrases
f_{1}\cdots f_{k}
.
Each phrase
f_{i}
is either stored explicitly as a character, or represented by a pointer
(p,\ell)
meaning that
f_{i}
equals the substring
w[p..p+\ell-1]
.
Such a scheme induces a transition function
\tau:[1..n]\to[1..n]\cup\{\bot\}
on text positions.
If position
i
is stored explicitly, then
\tau(i)=\bot
.
Otherwise, if
i
is the
t
-th position of a phrase defined by a pointer
(p,\ell)
, then
\tau(i)=p+t-1
.
We call the scheme
valid
if for every position
i\in[1..n]
there exists
k\geq 0
such that
\tau^{k}(i)=\bot
(equivalently, the induced reference relation contains no directed cycle).
We denote by
b(w)
the minimum number of phrases in a valid bidirectional macro scheme for
w
.

A
string attractor
for a string
w
is a set
\Gamma\subseteq[1..n]
such that every substring
x
of
w
has an occurrence
w[i..j]=x
with
i\leq p\leq j
for some position
p\in\Gamma
.
We denote by
\gamma(w)
the minimum size of a string attractor for
w
.

3
Substring Equation System

A
substring equation system
specifies constraints of two types on an unknown string
w
of length
n
: substring equalities of the form
w[i..i+\ell-1]=w[j..j+\ell-1]
, and character assignments of the form
w[k]=c
.
There exists an
O(n)
-time algorithm that decides satisfiability and uniqueness of a substring equation system and, if the system is satisfiable, outputs a string
w\in\Sigma^{n}
satisfying all constraints
[
7
]
.
When the satisfying string is unique, the system can be viewed as a compact representation of the string.
We formalize the substring equation system as follows.

Definition 3

(Substring equation system (SES))
.

An instance of a
substring equation system
for a string
w\in\Sigma^{n}
is a triple
(n,\mathsf{Eq},\mathsf{Ch})
, where
n
is the length of
w
,
\mathsf{Eq}
is a finite set of substring-equality constraints, and
\mathsf{Ch}
is a finite set of character-assignment constraints.
Each element of
\mathsf{Eq}
is a triple
(i,j,\ell)
with
1\leq i,j\leq n
and
1\leq\ell\leq n-\max\{i,j\}+1
, representing the equation
w[i..i+\ell-1]=w[j..j+\ell-1]
.
Each element of
\mathsf{Ch}
is a pair
(k,c)
with
1\leq k\leq n
and
c\in\Sigma
, representing the equation
w[k]=c
.
We say that
(n,\mathsf{Eq},\mathsf{Ch})

represents

w
if
w
satisfies all constraints in
\mathsf{Eq}
and
\mathsf{Ch}
and
w
is the unique string in
\Sigma^{n}
satisfying them.
The
size
of the system is defined as
|\mathsf{Eq}|+|\mathsf{Ch}|
.

Note that without loss of generality, we can assume that
\ell\geq 2
for any
(i,j,\ell)\in\mathsf{Eq}
.

Analogously to other compression-based repetitiveness measures, we define
s(w)
as the minimum size of an SES that represents
w
.

SES can be seen as a flexible way of expressing copy–paste constraints between substrings.
In this sense, they generalize classical directed copy–paste representations such as bidirectional macro schemes (BMS).
BMS first fixes a parsing into phrases and gives each copied phrase one directed pointer to a source interval, whereas SES specifies only substring-equality constraints.
Accordingly, SES does not require an explicit partitioning of the string into phrases, and its constraints are stated as undirected equalities between substrings.
In particular, every valid BMS can be converted into an SES of the same size.

Theorem 4
.

For every string
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and every valid bidirectional macro scheme for
w
with
k
phrases,
there exists a substring equation system (SES) of size
k
that represents
w
.

Proof.

Let
f_{1}\cdots f_{k}
be a valid bidirectional macro scheme for
w
.
For each phrase
f_{i}
, let
[a_{i}..b_{i}]
be its interval in
w
.
If
f_{i}
is stored explicitly as a character
c
, then we add the character constraint
(a_{i},c)
to
\mathsf{Ch}
.
Otherwise
f_{i}
is represented by a pointer
(p,\ell)
with
\ell=b_{i}-a_{i}+1
, meaning that

w[a_{i}..b_{i}]=w[p..p+\ell-1]
; in this case we add the substring-equality constraint
(a_{i},p,\ell)
to
\mathsf{Eq}
.
Let
(n,\mathsf{Eq},\mathsf{Ch})
be the resulting SES.
By construction,
w
satisfies all constraints and
the size of the SES is
|\mathsf{Eq}|+|\mathsf{Ch}|=k
.

It remains to show that the string satisfying all constraints is unique.
Consider the transition function
\tau
induced by the macro scheme.
Every substring-equality constraint
(a_{t},p,\ell)
implies that for each
0\leq q<\ell
we have
\tau(a_{t}+q)=p+q
,
while every character constraint
(a_{t},c)
corresponds to
\tau(a_{t})=\bot
.
Because the macro scheme is valid, the directed graph on positions induced by
\tau
is acyclic and every position reaches some position with
\tau=\bot
.
Take any topological order of this graph.
In this order, each position either has an explicit character constraint fixing its value, or it is constrained to be equal to a position that appears earlier in the order.
Thus, by induction along the order, the character at every position is uniquely determined.
Hence,
(n,\mathsf{Eq},\mathsf{Ch})
represents
w
.
∎

The inequality
s(w)\leq b(w)
follows immediately from Theorem
4
.
Conversely, it is currently unclear whether every SES can be transformed into a bidirectional macro scheme of comparable size.

To recapitulate,
SES defines an undirected referencing graph where the nodes are the positions in the string, and edges are explicit equivalences
specified by the equations.
On the other hand, the edges of BMS are directed,
where each position (that is not an explicit phrase)
has an edge pointing to a source position.
We may also consider a directed version of SES, where
each “equation” is directed as in BMS.
It is easy to see that smallest size
s^{\prime}(w)
of such a directed SES that represents a string
w
is within a constant factor of
s(w)
:
any directed SES can trivially be made undirected, and
any undirected SES can be made directed by making two directed “equations” per undirected equation.

A directed SES can be viewed as a relaxed version of BMS where:
1) phrases need not be a partition of the original string, but may overlap,
implying that a position may have multiple sources as long as they are consistent,
and
2) there can by cycles in the referencing graph, as long as
there is a directed spanning forest where positions corresponding to elements
in
\mathsf{Ch}
are roots.
We conjecture that SES, and therefore such relaxed version of BMS
is strictly more powerful than the standard BMS,
i.e., there exists an infinite family of strings
\{w_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that

s(w_{n})=o(b(w_{n}))
as
n\to\infty
.

On the other hand, it can be shown that
s(w)
is lower bounded by
\gamma(w)
for every string
w
, within a constant factor.

Theorem 5
.

For every string
w
,
\gamma(w)\leq 4s(w)
.

Proof.

Given any (including a smallest) SES that represents a string
w
,
consider the set of positions
S
consisting of
the positions corresponding to each element of
\mathsf{Ch}
, and
the four endpoints of the two substrings for each substring equation in
\mathsf{Eq}
.
By construction,
|S|=4|\mathsf{Eq}|+|\mathsf{Ch}|
.
We claim that
S
is a string attractor of
w
, which implies
\gamma(w)\leq 4s(w)
.

Consider an occurrence of a substring
x
of
w
, that does not
contain any position in
S
. Next, consider all occurrences of

x
that are implied by the substring equations,
i.e., the closure of occurrences of
x
obtained by substring equations
that contain the corresponding occurrences of
x
.
If none of these occurrences contain a position in
S
,
this implies that none of the symbols in all the copies
of
x
can be recovered, contradicting that the SES represents
w
.
Therefore, at least one occurrence of
x
must contain a position in
S
, and therefore,
S
is a string attractor of
w
.
∎

It can also be seen that there is a separation between
\gamma
and
s
via the Thue–Morse words defined
as

t_{n}=\mu^{n}(\texttt{a})
for
n\geq 0
,
where
\mu
is
the morphism
\mu(\texttt{a})=\texttt{ab}
,
\mu(\texttt{b})=\texttt{ba}
.

Theorem 6
.

For the Thue–Morse words
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
,

\gamma(t_{n})=o(s(t_{n}))
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since it is known that
\gamma(t_{n})\leq 4
for any
n\geq 0

[
11
]
, it suffices to show

s(t_{n})=\omega(1)
. This follows from
Theorem

7
below.
Note that since
|t_{n}|=2^{n}
, the gap is a log factor with respect to the length of the strings.
∎

Theorem 7
.

s(t_{n})=\Theta(n)
.

Since it is known that
b(t_{n})=\Theta(n)

[
1
]
,

s(t_{n})=O(n)
follows from
Theorem

4
.
To show
s(t_{n})=\Omega(n)
, the proof is similar to that for showing

b(t_{n})=\Omega(n)
of
[
1
]
.
Here, we will not focus on obtaining the exact lower bound as in
[
1
]
,
simplifying the case analyses.
For any SES
E_{w}=(n,\mathsf{Eq},\mathsf{Ch})
for
w
,
define its
weighted size

s^{\prime}(E_{w})=8|\mathsf{Eq}|+|\mathsf{Ch}|
.
Given a smallest SES
E_{w}^{*}=(|w|,\mathsf{Eq}^{*},\mathsf{Ch}^{*})
for
w
,

s^{\prime}(E_{w}^{*})=8|\mathsf{Eq}^{*}|+|\mathsf{Ch}^{*}|\leq 8(|\mathsf{Eq}^{*}|+|\mathsf{Ch}^{*}|)=8s(w)
.
Therefore, the smallest weighted size
s^{\prime}(w)
of an SES for
w
satisfies

s^{\prime}(w)=\min_{E_{w}}s^{\prime}(E_{w})\leq s^{\prime}(E_{w}^{*})\leq 8s(w)
,
and it suffices to show
s^{\prime}(t_{n})=\Omega(n)
in order to show
s(t_{n})=\Omega(n)
.

The
parity
of a non-negative integer
i
is
i\bmod 2
.
We say that a substring equation
(i,j,\ell)
is
parity preserving
, if
i\equiv j\pmod{2}
.
We say that an occurrence
t_{n}[i..i+\ell)=x
of
x
is
image aligned

if
i
is odd and
\ell
is even,
i.e.,
x=\mu(t_{n-1}[i^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+\ell/2))
for
i^{\prime}=\lceil i/2\rceil
.
For the special case of
\ell=2
, we say that a consecutive pair of positions
(i,i+1)

image aligned
, if
i
is odd.

Lemma 8

(Lemma 4 in
[
1
]
)
.

For any substring
w\not\in\{\texttt{aba},\texttt{bab},\texttt{ab},\texttt{ba},\texttt{a},\texttt{b}\}

of
t_{n}
, the parities of all occurrences of
w
in
t_{n}
are the same.

The following lemma immediately implies that
s^{\prime}(t_{n})=\Omega(n)
.

Lemma 9
.

For any
n\geq 1
, and any SES

E_{n}=(|t_{n}|,\mathsf{Eq}_{n},\mathsf{Ch}_{n})
for
t_{n}
,
there exists an SES

E_{n-1}=(|t_{n-1}|,\mathsf{Eq}_{n-1},\mathsf{Ch}_{n-1})
for
t_{n-1}

satisfying

s^{\prime}(E_{n-1})<s^{\prime}(E_{n})
.

Proof.

We first construct an SES

E^{\prime}_{n}=(|t_{n}|,\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime})
from
E_{n}
,
s.t.
s^{\prime}(E^{\prime}_{n})\leq s^{\prime}(E_{n})

with the additional constraint that
all elements in
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
consist of pairs of consecutive positions that are image aligned,
and all substrings of substring equations in
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
are image aligned and of length at least
4
.
In other words, for any
1\leq k\leq 2^{n-1}
,

(2k-1,t_{n}[2k-1])\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}\iff(2k,t_{n}[2k])\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
,
and any element in
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
, can be written as
(2i-1,2j-1,2\ell)
for some
i,j\geq 1
and
\ell\geq 2
.
Such constraints allow us to construct an SES

E_{n-1}=(|t_{n-1}|,\mathsf{Eq}_{n-1},\mathsf{Ch}_{n-1})
for
t_{n-1}

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Eq}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\{(i,j,\ell)\mid(2i-1,2j-1,2\ell)\in\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}\},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ch}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\{(k,\mu^{-1}(t_{n}[2k-1..2k]))\mid(2k-1,t_{n}[2k-1]),(2k,t_{n}[2k])\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}\}.

(2)

It is clear that
s^{\prime}(E_{n-1})<s^{\prime}(E^{\prime}_{n})
since
|\mathsf{Eq}_{n-1}|=|\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}|
,
and
2|\mathsf{Ch}_{n-1}|=|\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}|>0
.
The validity of
E_{n-1}
is clear from the validity of
E^{\prime}_{n}
, since
consecutive image aligned position pairs are always
a pair of explicit character assignments or will reference consecutive image aligned position pairs via the substring equations.

E^{\prime}_{n}
can be constructed by the following steps:

1.

Make
E_{n}^{\prime}
a duplicate of
E_{n}
with the exception that
all parity preserving substring equations of
\mathsf{Eq}_{n}

are extended to be image aligned, i.e.,
if
(i,j,\ell)\in\mathsf{Eq}
with
i\equiv j\pmod{2}
,
then
(2\lceil i/2\rceil-1,2\lceil j/2\rceil-1,2\lceil\ell/2\rceil)\in\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
.
This does not change the (weighted) size of the SES, and also does not affect the validity of the SES since for any
i,j
,
t_{n}[2i-1]=t_{n}[2j-1]\iff t_{n}[2i]=t_{n}[2j]
and thus only valid constraints are added.

2.

Next, we focus on elements of
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
that are not consecutive image aligned position pairs.
Suppose
(2k-1,t_{n}[2k-1])\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
but
(2k,t_{n}[2k])\not\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.
We consider replacing
(2k-1,t_{n}[2k-1])
with
other elements, so that any reference to this position
2k-1
is still
resolvable
,
i.e., there exists some path from
2k-1
via the substring equations to a different position with a character assignment.
To do this, we utilize the resolvability of the position
2k
,
and follow the referencing graph from
2k
.
Since in Step
1
, all parity preserving substring equations are image aligned, any parity preserving substring equation connecting
2k
and
2k^{\prime\prime}
for some
k^{\prime\prime}
will connect
2k-1
and
2k^{\prime\prime}-1
.
If
2k
and
2k^{\prime}
are connected
via some sequence of parity preserving substring equations,
it follows that
2k-1
is connected
2k^{\prime}-1
via the same sequence.
Since
2k
is resolvable,
for at least one such
k^{\prime}
where
2k^{\prime}
is connected to
2k
via a sequence of parity preserving substring equations, it holds that either
(1)
(2k^{\prime},t_{n}[2k^{\prime}])\in\mathsf{Ch^{\prime}}
, or
(2)
2k^{\prime}
is involved in a non-parity preserving substring equation.

For the first case, we simply insert
(2k^{\prime}-1,t_{n}[2k^{\prime}-1])
into
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
, making
2k^{\prime}-1
and
2k^{\prime}
an image aligned position pair in
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.
In the latter case,
we insert into
\mathsf{Ch^{\prime}}
,
all image aligned position pairs that has an overlap with the substrings in the equation.
By
Lemma

8
, any non-parity preserving substring equation must be
between substrings of length at most
3
,
and since any such substring can be covered by at most

4
character assignments consisting of at most
2
pairs of image aligned pairs,
both sides of the equation can be replaced by at most

8
character assignments consisting of at most
4
pairs of image aligned pairs.
More precisely, if
(i,j,\ell)\in\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
is a non-parity preserving substring equation with
i\leq 2k^{\prime}<i+\ell
,
remove it from
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}

and add the elements
\{(p,t_{n}[p])\mid p\in[2\lceil i/2\rceil-1,2\lceil i/2\rceil-1+2\lceil\ell/2\rceil)\cup[2\lceil j/2\rceil-1,2\lceil j/2\rceil-1+2\lceil\ell/2\rceil)\}
to
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.

Replacing substring equations with character assignments does not affect the validity of the SES, and it is clear that
2k-1
is now resolvable.

The case for
(2k-1,t_{n}[2k-1])\not\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
but
(2k,t_{n}[2k])\in\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
is symmetric.

3.

After replacing, in the previous step, all character assignments that do not constitute
image aligned position pairs with ones that do,
we remove from
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
,
all remaining non-parity preserving substring equations
as well as length-
2
image aligned parity preserving substring equations,
by again replacing each of them with at most
8
character assignments (consisting of at most
4
image aligned position pairs), adding them to
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.

It is clear from the above arguments that
E^{\prime}_{n}
is a valid SES and now satisfies the required additional constraints on
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.

Finally, we analyze
s^{\prime}(E^{\prime}_{n})
.
In Step
2
, at least one character assignment is deleted from
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
,
and either: another is added to
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
, or,
a substring equation is further deleted from
\mathsf{Eq}^{\prime}
,
replaced by at most
8
character assignments added to
\mathsf{Ch}^{\prime}
.
In Step
3
,
again, each substring equation, if deleted, is replaced by at most
8

character assignments.
Therefore, by definition of
s^{\prime}
,

s^{\prime}(E^{\prime}_{n})\leq s^{\prime}(E_{n})
holds,
thus implying
s^{\prime}(E_{n-1})<s^{\prime}(E^{\prime}_{n})\leq s^{\prime}(E_{n})
,
finishing the proof.
∎

4
String Representation Based on Suffixient Sets

In this section, we propose the first string representation scheme that uses
O(\chi(w))
machine words for every string
w
.

Our strategy proceeds in three steps.
We start by defining a position-equivalence relation based on super-maximal right extensions, which are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of a smallest suffixient set.
We then show that the resulting equivalence classes correspond to the distinct characters in the text, and thus their number is
\sigma(w)\leq\chi(w)
.
Finally, we encode the relation using
O(\chi(w))
substring-equality constraints and
\sigma(w)
character assignments, obtaining an SES of total size
O(\chi(w))
representing
w
.

We first define an equivalence relation
\equiv_{\chi}
over positions
[1..n]
of
w\in\Sigma^{n}
as follows:

Definition 10

(position equivalence by suffixient sets)
.

For any pair of super-maximal right extensions

yxc
and
zxc^{\prime}
where
y,z\in\Sigma^{*}
,
c,c^{\prime}\in\Sigma
, and
x\in\Sigma^{+}
is the longest common suffix of
yx
and
zx
,
let
w[i..i+|x|-1]
be the occurrence of
x
in the leftmost occurrence of
yxc
in
w
,
and
let
w[i^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+|x|-1]
be the occurrence of
x
in the leftmost occurrence of
zxc^{\prime}
in
w
.
Then,
i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k<|x|
.

Intuitively, Definition
10
declares positions equivalent when they lie in the common-suffix part of the leftmost occurrences of two super-maximal right extensions, ignoring the final extending character.
Note that in the above definition, we have chosen the leftmost occurrence of each super-maximal right extension to simplify the exposition, but the choice can be arbitrary.

Lemma 11
.

If
u=w[i..i+|u|-1]
is a right extension with a unique occurrence,
then there exists
1\leq j\leq i
such that
w[j..i+|u|-1]
is a super-maximal right extension.

Proof.

Let
j
be the smallest value such that
w[j..i+|u|-1]=xu

is a right extension.
If
xu
is not a super-maximal right extension,
there must be some other right extension
v=yxu
having
xu
as a proper suffix.
Since
xu
is the longest right extension having an occurrence ending at
i+|u|-1
,
v
cannot have an occurrence ending at
i+|u|-1
, implying another occurrence of
v
– and thus of
xu
– elsewhere.
However, this contradicts the assumption that the occurrence of
u
is unique.
∎

Lemma 12
.

If
u
is a right extension,
then there exists a super-maximal right extension containing
u
as a suffix.

Proof.

Straightforward from definition of super-maximal right extensions.
∎

Lemma 13
.

For any repeating substring
u
of
w
and any pair of occurrences

u=w[i..i+|u|-1]=w[i^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+|u|-1]
,

i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k<|u|
.

Proof.

Proof by induction on the length of
u
in decreasing order. Let
u
be a longest repeating substring of
w
.
Then
w[i+|u|]\neq w[i^{\prime}+|u|]
, and both
w[i..i+|u|]
and
w[i^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+|u|]
are right extensions that have a unique occurrence in
w
.

It follows from
Lemma

11
that for some
1\leq j\leq i,1\leq j^{\prime}\leq i^{\prime}
,
w[j..i+|u|]
and
w[j^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+|u|]
are super-maximal right extensions.
Since their occurrences are unique, they are leftmost occurrences.
Therefore, it holds that
i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k<|u|
by
Definition

10
.

Now, suppose that the statement holds for any repeating substring longer than
u
.
Let
uc=w[i..i+|u|]
and
uc^{\prime}=w[i^{\prime}..i^{\prime}+|u|]
.
If
c=c^{\prime}
, then
uc=uc^{\prime}
is a repeating substring longer than
u
and thus

i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k\leq|u|
holds from the induction hypothesis.
Otherwise,
c\neq c^{\prime}
and thus
uc
and
uc^{\prime}
are right extensions.
If the occurrence of both
uc
and
uc^{\prime}
are unique, then
Lemma

11
again implies that

uc
and
uc^{\prime}
respectively occur as suffixes of uniquely occurring super-maximal right extensions (which are leftmost occurrences) and therefore

i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k<|u|
by
Definition

10
.

If
uc
is a repeating substring, then by the induction hypothesis the following holds: for any occurrence
uc=w[i^{\prime\prime}..i^{\prime\prime}+|u|]
, we have
i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime\prime}+k
for all
0\leq k\leq|u|
.
In particular, this applies to the occurrence of
uc
in the leftmost occurrence of a super-maximal right extension that has
uc
as a suffix, whose existence follows from
Lemma

12
.
The same argument applies to
uc^{\prime}
. Therefore, by
Definition

10
and transitivity,

i+k\equiv_{\chi}i^{\prime}+k
holds for all
0\leq k<|u|
.
∎

As a result, the following statement holds immediately by applying
Lemma

13
to substrings of length
1
.

Corollary 14
.

For any string
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and any
i,j\in[1..n]
, we have
i\equiv_{\chi}j\iff w[i]=w[j]
.
In particular, the equivalence classes of
\equiv_{\chi}
are in one-to-one correspondence with the distinct characters of
w
, and thus the number of classes is
\sigma(w)
.

We are now ready to prove that
\chi
is reachable.

Theorem 15
.

For any string
w
, there exists an
O(\chi(w))
-word representation (an SES of size
O(\chi(w))
,
and hence
s(w)\in O(\chi(w))
) which can be computed in linear time assuming a linearly sortable alphabet.

Proof.

We first show that
\equiv_{\chi}
can be represented by an
O(\chi(w))
size SES for
w
.

By Corollary
14
, the equivalence classes of
\equiv_{\chi}
coincide with the character classes of
w
.
Thus, it suffices to store (i) the equivalence relation
\equiv_{\chi}
itself and (ii) a mapping from each equivalence class to its character, which can be done by storing one representative position (e.g., the leftmost occurrence) for each character.
Since
\sigma(w)\leq\chi(w)
, this mapping takes
O(\chi(w))
words, so it remains to show that
\equiv_{\chi}
can be represented in
O(\chi(w))
space.

The equivalence relation can be encoded as a reverse compacted trie of the following set of strings:

\{x^{R}\mid xc\in\mathit{SRE}(w),\,c\in\Sigma\}
,
where each node corresponding to
x^{R}
stores the leftmost occurrences of the super-maximal right extensions

\{xc\mid xc\in\mathit{SRE}(w),\,c\in\Sigma\}
, and each edge is labeled by its length.
Figure
2
shows an example of this trie representation.
It is clear that the size of the trie is
O(\chi)
.
For any two super-maximal right extensions
yxc
and
zxc^{\prime}
,
the positions corresponding to the respective occurrences of
x
in
the leftmost occurrences of
yxc
and
zxc^{\prime}
can be determined from the
information in the nodes, and the lengths of the paths.
Thus, the equivalence relation can be retrieved.

We next show how to encode this trie as an SES.
Fix an arbitrary ordering of children at each trie node, and consider the resulting depth-first traversal order according to this child ordering.
In that order, list all super-maximal right extensions by visiting each node and collecting the extensions associated with that node.
For each consecutive pair in the list, we add one substring-equality constraint as in Definition
10
, using the common-suffix substring induced by the lowest common ancestor of the corresponding nodes.
This produces exactly
\chi(w)-1
substring-equality constraints.
We also add
\sigma(w)
character-assignment constraints, one for each distinct character.
Figure
3
shows an example of the resulting SES.

It remains to argue that these constraints induce the same position equivalence as
\equiv_{\chi}
.
Take any two entries in the list and consider the common suffix
x
determined by their lowest common ancestor.
Among entries between them in list order, every adjacent pair yields a constraint whose associated common suffix has
x
as a suffix.
Therefore, by applying transitivity along this chain of adjacent pairs, the induced position-equivalence relation makes corresponding positions in the two occurrences of
x
equivalent.
In other words, corresponding positions in the two occurrences of
x
belong to the same equivalence class.
Hence the SES implies all equalities of
\equiv_{\chi}
.
Thus, we obtain an SES of size
\chi(w)-1+\sigma(w)\in O(\chi(w))
that represents
w
.

Finally, we analyze the time complexity for constructing the SES representation.

\mathit{SRE}
can be computed in linear time
[
4
]
.
Furthermore, the reverse compacted trie is
essentially a sub-tree of the suffix tree of
w^{R}\$
,
where the suffix tree is truncated and pruned
below the positions in the suffix tree corresponding to
x^{R}
with
xc\in\mathit{SRE}(w)
for some
x
and
c
.
The suffix tree can be computed in linear time
[
6
]
,
all the positions of
x^{R}
in the suffix tree
can be identified in linear time (e.g. Lemma 7.2 of
[
9
]
),
and the pruning can be done in linear time by a simple depth-first traversal.
The conversion from the reverse compacted trie to an SES is also another depth-first traversal and can be done in linear time.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proved the reachability of
\chi
by presenting the first string representation scheme that encodes any string in
O(\chi(w))
words.
To establish this result, we focused on substring equation systems (SES) and showed that every string admits an SES of size
O(\chi(w))
.
An important direction for future work is to clarify the exact gap between BMS and equality-based representations. Two natural open questions are whether there exist strings with
s(w)\in o(b(w))
and whether there exist strings with
\chi(w)\in o(b(w))
.
In particular, establishing the existence of strings with
s(w)\in o(b(w))
would show that SES is a genuinely stronger compression scheme than BMS, and would further underscore the importance of studying SES.
