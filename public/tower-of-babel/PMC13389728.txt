The triple-hit hypothesis: exploring pulmonary e-cigarette, PM2.5 and viral polyexposure
Authors: Hilma Clemén, Sangeetha Ramu, Lena Uller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposure to e-cigarette aerosols, particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) and respiratory viruses rarely occurs in isolation, but rather in complex polyexposure contexts during daily life. On a cellular and molecular level, vaping induces a distinct lipid-laden macrophage phenotype, alters pulmonary neutrophilic infiltration and impairs epithelial differentiation and ciliary function. In contrast, PM2.5 alters the macrophage polarisation equilibrium, temporally favouring an acute pro-inflammatory phenotype and a subsequent chronic tissue-remodelling phenotype, while also driving an oxidative inflammatory epithelial milieu. Both exposures thus impair antiviral defences and enhance susceptibility to respiratory viral infections such as influenza A, rhinovirus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2. The triple-hit hypothesis proposes that concurrent exposure to vaping aerosols, PM2.5 and respiratory viruses may exert additive or synergistic effects on chronic airway inflammation. PM2.5 may amplify vaping-induced macrophage lipid accumulation, thereby reducing the macrophage clearance capacity. Decreased efferocytosis and autophagy may further exacerbate inflammation by increasing secondary necrosis from apoptotic cells and debris. This persistent inflammatory state coupled with epithelial injury and impaired antiviral responses may increase the risk of infection and accelerate the development or progression of chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma and COPD. These insights highlight the crucial need for polyexposure models to accurately reflect real-world environmental and behavioural exposures and evaluate their impact on respiratory health and disease exacerbations. Understanding this exposure triad is also crucial for refining exposure guidelines, updating risk assessments and implementing preventive strategies.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases encompass a spectrum of diseases affecting the lungs and airways, including COPD and asthma [1]. With a global prevalence of 6.7% and approximately 4 million premature deaths annually, chronic respiratory diseases represent the fourth leading cause of mortality worldwide [2]. Well-established risk factors include respiratory viral infections and exposure to fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of ≤2.5 μm (PM2.5), both of which drive airway inflammation and structural remodelling of the respiratory tract [3–8].

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) have attracted considerable popularity as a smoking cessation tool and are often perceived as less harmful than combustible cigarettes. While switching to e-cigarettes may confer relative respiratory benefit, accumulating evidence indicate that “vaping” exerts substantial effects on the lungs [9–12]. Indeed, e-cigarette aerosols elicit pathophysiological responses overlapping with those induced by respiratory viral infections and PM2.5, including epithelial injury, dysregulated innate immunity and altered antimicrobial mechanisms [13–16]. Importantly, PM2.5 and e-cigarette aerosols vary in composition depending on environmental conditions and user preferences, respectively. This contributes to heterogeneous biological effects and fragmented evidence due to variability in devices, e-liquids, user practices, sampling sites and experimental models [17–20].

A critical but unexplored angle is that vaping rarely occurs in isolation. Rather, individuals are simultaneously exposed to air pollutants, pathogens and occupational or household inhalants creating complex exposure environments. Recent evidence links vaping to increased incidence and disease severity of viral respiratory infections including influenza A (IAV), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and rhinovirus (RV) infection [21–24]. These reciprocal amplifications propose a risk of self-propagating cellular injury, inflammation and resolution characteristic of chronic respiratory disease development and progression. Additionally, only one study to date has investigated the combined effects of vaping and PM2.5 [17], raising concern that current literature underestimates the true respiratory risk of vaping in the reality of daily polyexposure. Therefore, this review aims to critically evaluate the mechanistic impact of vaping on the respiratory system and explore how these effects may interact with, or aggravate, those associated with respiratory viral infections and PM2.5 exposure.

Methods

To identify original peer-reviewed manuscripts pertaining the effects of e-cigarettes on respiratory health, respiratory infections and the implications of PM inhalation, the PubMed database was searched using combinations of specific keywords. Search terms included e-cigarette, vaping, PM2.5, particulate matter, respiratory infection, influenza, COVID-19, rhinovirus, bronchial epithelial cells and small airway epithelial cells. To be able to evaluate current knowledge gaps, searches were limited to 2015 onwards and restricted to publications in English. As the only currently available publication investigating the effects of e-cigarette and rhinovirus co-exposure was published in 2014, the publication was exempted from the search restrictions.

E-cigarettes: devices, constituents and aerosol chemistry

E-cigarettes are electronic nicotine delivery systems that aerosolise “e-liquids” for inhalation. A standard device comprises a cartridge reservoir, a battery-powered atomiser that heats the e-liquid and a mouthpiece [25]. These devices are highly customisable with user-modifiable design, power input, e-liquid composition and nicotine content [26], generating substantial heterogeneity that complicates experimental reproducibility and the study of vaping-related health effects.

While e-cigarette aerosols contain lower levels of toxicants found in cigarette smoke, they introduce a distinct array of other chemicals [27]. E-liquids typically contain propylene glycol (PG) and vegetable glycerine (VG) as solvents for flavouring agents and nicotine [28]. Heating alters their chemical composition, producing potentially harmful compounds including varying alkyl and polypropylene glycol chains, formaldehyde and acrolein [27, 29, 30]. The PG/VG ratio influences both user experience and nicotine delivery; higher PG content enhances “throat hit” and facilitates efficient nicotine absorption due to its higher volatility and lower boiling point, whereas higher VG content generates denser exhaled “clouds” [26, 28, 31]. Flavouring agents further add to this complexity, with 15 600 flavours described as of 2017 [32]. Although generally recognised as safe food additives, no safety assessments were performed for chronic inhalation prior to e-cigarette commercialisation in the mid-2000s [33]. Several flavour chemicals are also established sensitisers capable of inducing immune-mediated hypersensitivity, raising concerns that frequent inhalation may trigger an immunological cascade contributing to the initiation or progression of chronic respiratory diseases [33]. Lastly, vitamin E acetate (VEA) can be added to thicken e-liquids, although it is only approved for ingestion and dermatological applications [34]. Upon inhalation, VEA is delivered directly to the airway epithelium where it accumulates in droplets within epithelial cells and interfere with the kinetics of the pulmonary surfactant system [35]. VEA is also associated with e-cigarette or vaping use-associated lung injury (EVALI), where it can be detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of most hospitalised patients [36].

Pulmonary effects of e-cigarette aerosols

Vaping and the pulmonary epithelium

The bronchial epithelium

The respiratory epithelium consists of diverse cell types, including ciliated, secretory and type I/II alveolar cells (ATI/II) that play functionally distinct roles in pulmonary homeostasis [37]. E-cigarette exposure induces structural and functional epithelial alterations as users display emphysema-like alterations and pulmonary parenchyma changes, while long-term daily exposure in mice results in increased respiratory resistance and airspace enlargement [14, 38]. Although findings on transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) are inconsistent [13, 21, 39–41], multiple studies consistently report enhanced oxidative stress and upregulation of pro-inflammatory markers [13, 14, 21, 39–44].

At the cellular level, vaping drives transformation of bronchial epithelial cells, reduces metabolic activity and produces distinct proteomic, transcriptomic and epigenomic profiles [13, 14, 44–48]. Specifically, e-cigarette aerosols disrupt epithelial differentiation by decreasing the expression of the ciliogenesis regulator forkhead box J1 (FOXJ1) and impairing ciliary structure and motility through post-translational microtubule modifications without reducing overall cilium coverage [13, 38, 49, 50]. These changes may hinder mucociliary clearance, although current reports are inconsistent and conflicting [13, 16, 38, 39, 46]. Vaping also modifies epithelial composition by increasing the proportion of club cells while reducing the secretoglobin family 1A member (SCGB1A1)+ subset essential for immune function [13]. This imbalance may compromise epithelial integrity and amplify airway inflammation, thereby heightening susceptibility to respiratory injury. Barrier function is further compromised as depicted by increased plasma membrane rigidity, impaired monolayer integrity, destabilisation of apical junctional complexes and increased epithelial apoptosis [13, 38, 40, 41, 46]. Finally, bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells release collagen 1 and fibronectin in a concentration-dependent manner in response to e-cigarettes, leading to the formation of mesh-like fibrils at the ciliary shafts [51, 52].

The alveolar epithelium

The size of the inhaled aerosol particles ranges from 10 nm to 2.5 μm [53, 54]. This large span is attributed to user preferences and device settings, where higher power is associated with larger particles, higher nicotine yield and greater e-liquid consumption [53, 55, 56]. Higher VG content also produces larger particles [55, 56]. Computational predictions estimate that 7–31% of particles deposit in the bronchioalveolar regions [55, 56]. Despite these insights, knowledge of small airway epithelial cell responses is limited. Current evidence suggests that vaping increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production without affecting baseline TEER [25, 39], but comprehensive studies of differentiation, barrier function and remodelling are lacking. Importantly, heterogeneity of bronchial and alveolar models in response to vaping further suggests that distinct lung areas react differently [57]. Vaping and innate immunity

Alveolar macrophages are specialised sentinel cells of the innate immune system residing in the respiratory tract. They modulate pulmonary homeostasis by defending against pathogens and pollutants, removing apoptotic cells and supporting surfactant turnover [58]. Alveolar macrophages from e-cigarette users contain unique cytoplasmic bodies independent of nicotine or flavour exposure, reflecting disrupted surfactant metabolism and impaired lipid efflux linked to reduced expression of the phospholipid efflux transporters ATP-binding cassette A1 (ABCA1) and ABCG1 [15, 34, 45, 47, 59]. Consistently, BALF from C57BL/6 mice exposed to e-cigarette aerosols shows accumulation of phospholipids, particularly those bearing two saturated acyl chains and cholesterol esters [47]. This phenotype depicts the lipid-laden macrophages found in patients hospitalised for EVALI and shares features with foam cells commonly seen in fibrotic lung diseases [15, 59, 60], yet it remains unclear whether vaping induces classical foam cells or a distinct, novel macrophage phenotype.

Functionally, e-cigarette exposure alters alveolar macrophage motility and immune function. Intravital imaging reveals increased probing behaviour, while squeezing into different alveoli is reduced due to cell division cycle 42 (CDC42) dysfunction. This impaired interalveolar migration compromises the ability to mount an effective immune response [15]. Exposure to e-cigarette vapour also decrease phagocytic capacity and expression of the phagocytosis receptor class A scavenger receptor 1 (SR-A1) [29, 61–63]. Nicotine further exacerbates macrophage dysfunction by binding to nicotinic receptors on the alveolar macrophages, which elevates cytoplasmic calcium levels, leading to protease release and inhibition of autophagy via NFκB activation [61, 64–66]. Furthermore, e-cigarette-exposed alveolar macrophages display reduced efferocytosis of apoptotic airway cells through decreased cluster of differentiation (CD)36 and CD44 efferocytosis receptors [67]. This inability to effectively clear dead cells and debris cause persistent inflammation resulting in secondary neutrophil necrosis and increased pulmonary protease burden [68].

Macrophage polarisation is generally shifted toward pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes with reduced M2 populations coupled with a marked increase in ROS and pro-inflammatory mediators including interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-9 detected in bronchial brushings and BALF, although conflicting evidence suggests that M1 suppression occurs under certain conditions [14, 15, 29, 65]. This inflammatory state is also associated with increased macrophage recruitment in murine models [38]. Alveolar macrophages also release the neutrophil chemoattractant IL-8 in response to vaping, although clinical evidence of neutrophil infiltration is inconclusive [16, 62, 69]. Regardless, neutrophils exposed to e-cigarette extract display reduced chemotaxis, phagocytosis and NETosis capacities [70]. Neutrophilic proteins such as neutrophil elastase, myeloperoxidase and various MMPs are consistently increased in response to vaping aerosols and this imbalanced protease/antiprotease activity can increase neutrophilic damage of bystander cells [16, 65, 68]. Neutrophilic inflammation is further amplified by defective alveolar macrophage pathogen clearance and increased granule content in sputum [15, 16, 71].

Dendritic cells (DCs) act as an important link between innate and adaptive immunity by capturing pathogens and presenting antigens to T-cells [72]. In response to e-cigarette extract, DCs display reduced expression of human leukocyte antigen DR isotype and major histocompatibility complex-II, with varying effects on CD86 expression depending on the flavouring [33, 73]. DCs also display an upregulation of molecules involved in DC activation and immunogenicity [73]. Nicotine further disrupts DC function by downregulating molecules associated with DC maturation and T-cell stimulation, suggesting an alternative route of e-cigarette-mediated DC effects [74]. Collectively, vaping induces a distinct innate immune profile marked by defective lipid-laden macrophages, aberrant neutrophilic responses and reduced antigen-presenting capacity of DCs (figure 2).

Interactions between vaping and antiviral defence

Impaired antiviral immunity, particularly deficient interferon (IFN) responses and dysregulated innate immune signalling, is a hallmark of both COPD and asthma contributing to increased susceptibility and exacerbation risk of respiratory viral infections [75, 76]. Although comprehensive evidence describing the effects of vaping on antiviral immunity is lacking, vaping-induced epithelial damage and immune dysregulation suggest compromised antiviral defences.

Viral infections are predominant triggers of chronic respiratory disease exacerbations [24, 77]. IAV, SARS-CoV-2 and rhinovirus serve as exemplar viruses due to their epidemiological significance and well-characterised biology; these pathogens are among the most studied respiratory viruses responsible for widespread infections and substantial public health burden across age groups. Although other viruses such as respiratory syncytial virus are clinically important, the substantial evidence base for IAV, SARS-CoV-2 and rhinovirus supports their use as exemplars for exploring interactions with vaping or PM2.5.

IAV infection underlies most annual epidemics and primarily targets the tracheobronchial epithelium [8]. It is associated with epithelial pro-inflammatory cytokine production, NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and IFN-stimulated mediators, as well as epithelial apoptosis, pyroptosis and necroptosis [8, 78, 79]. In murine models and human precision-cut lung slices, e-cigarette exposure impairs host responses to IAV by amplifying pro-inflammatory signalling while diminishing antiviral efficacy [21, 22, 47, 80]. Functionally, this translates into increased IAV-induced cytokine production, reduced viral clearance and enhanced epithelial barrier permeability [47, 81]. Collectively, these findings suggest that e-cigarettes generate a unique innate immunity profile, where the magnitude and direction of these effects depend on aerosol composition, nicotine content and experimental context [82].

Concerns have also emerged regarding susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. SARS-CoV-2 is the virus behind the coronavirus 2019 pandemic and targets secretory and ciliated epithelial cells [8, 83]. Infection is associated with epithelial apoptosis, distinct reduction of TEER, distorted cilia with associated shrinkage and intracellular accumulation of plaques and pleomorphic double-membraned vesicles as well as robust inflammatory responses [83, 84]. Retrospective investigations suggest an increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis among e-cigarette users [23]. Mechanistically, vaping increases angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)2 expression (the receptor for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein) on bronchial epithelial cells and in BALF, potentially heightening vulnerability to infection, although findings are inconsistent [82, 85–88]. ACE2 upregulation correlates with suppression of several immune pathways including IL receptor and IFN-γ signalling in murine models [89]. Notably, these changes exhibit sex specificity, where ACE2 expression increases in male BALB/c and C57BL/6J mice, but remains unaffected in females [90, 91]. Vaping has also been linked to increased transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) expression (a protease required for viral entry), although findings remain conflicting [82, 86, 88].

Lastly, rhinoviruses primarily infect ciliated epithelial cells of the upper and central airways and typically cause mild respiratory illness associated with pro-inflammatory mediator release, IFN responses, epithelial shedding and mucus production [92, 93]. In severe cases, airway remodelling may occur [93]. Although rhinovirus is the most common airway infection and is associated with asthma and COPD exacerbations [24, 92, 93], little is known about the interactions between e-cigarette exposure and rhinovirus infection. Tracheobronchial epithelial cells exposed to e-liquid have been shown to increase viral load and IL-6 production [24]. Similar increases in viral titres accompanied by reduced IFN- and pro-inflammatory responses can also be seen during cigarette smoke and rhinovirus co-exposure [77]. These findings raise the possibility that e-cigarette aerosols may similarly impair epithelial antiviral defences upon rhinovirus co-exposure, although direct experimental evidence is currently lacking.

Pulmonary effects of PM exposure

PM is a heterogeneous mixture of airborne particles of varying sizes and compositions, commonly classified as coarse particles (PM10), PM2.5 and ultrafine particles (PM0.1) [94]. The health effects of these particles largely depend on their size and ability to penetrate the respiratory tract. Specifically, PM10 predominantly accumulates in the upper airways, where it contributes to mucosal irritation and exacerbation of respiratory conditions, including asthma and chronic bronchitis. In contrast, PM2.5 reaches the distal airways and alveoli, where it induces oxidative stress and inflammation and contributes to the development and exacerbation of chronic respiratory diseases. PM0.1 particles can penetrate even deeper, potentially entering the circulation and triggering systemic effects such as hypertension and autonomic dysfunction [7, 95–97]. Pollutants such as PM10 and nitrogen oxides tend to exhibit great spatial variability due to mixed source contributions and may act partly as proxies for traffic-related pollution in epidemiological studies [98]. In contrast, PM2.5 is widely used as a key indicator of ambient air pollution, is more consistently monitored across different settings and is of particular relevance to respiratory health and COPD pathophysiology [7, 95, 99]. Therefore, this review focuses on PM2.5.

PM2.5 and the pulmonary epithelium

The bronchial epithelium

PM2.5 comprises a complex assortment of inorganic and organic compounds, metals and carbonaceous particles that can penetrate deep into the lungs due to their small size [7, 100]. Its chemical composition differs substantially between industrial, urban and rural sampling sites and is further influenced by meteorological factors, yet sustained exposure is consistently associated with increased risk of COPD and asthma development and exacerbations where it accounts for up to 41% of COPD risk [3, 7, 20, 101–106]. In murine models, PM2.5 induces macrophage and neutrophil infiltration, alveolar wall thickening, epithelial hyperplasia, alveolar stenosis, collagen deposition and fibrotic remodelling [107–111]. Mucus-regulatory markers including IL-13, transforming growth factor (TGF)-α, epidermal growth factor and mucin 5AC are also upregulated, linking exposure to mucus overproduction [112, 113].

At the epithelial level, PM2.5 is internalised and accumulate near mitochondria, driving a pro-inflammatory milieu characterised by NFκB-dependent induction of IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α release, suppressed IFN responses, toll-like receptor 2/4 (TLR2/4) signalling and upregulation of NLRP3 inflammasome components [101–104, 107, 111, 114–120]. Barrier integrity is compromised through downregulation of the tight-junction proteins zonula occludens-1, e-cadherin and occludin, alongside indirect effects mediated via macrophage-derived MMP-12 release [101, 115, 121]. PM2.5 exposure also drives epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), apoptosis, cell cycle arrest and dysregulated autophagy [122–125]. At a metabolic level, PM2.5 exposure increases glycolysis and glycolytic capacity and reserve, indicating a shift of the metabolic phenotype from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis [126].

Oxidative stress represents another central mechanism, with PM2.5-induced increased ROS generation from NADPH oxidase and mitochondria coupled to impaired antioxidant defences [113, 114, 120, 123, 127, 128]. This oxidative imbalance can amplify inflammatory cascades that augment epithelial dysfunction and may synergise with dysfunctional autophagy to further amplify epithelial injury [114, 129]. Parallel epigenetic alterations including genome-wide DNA hypomethylation and H3K27ac histone modifications further reprogramme inflammatory and stress-response pathways [111, 118, 130]. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore oxidative stress as a pivotal mediator connecting PM2.5 exposure to epithelial dysfunction and inflammatory amplification.

The alveolar epithelium

In the alveolar epithelium, PM2.5 deposition contributes to emphysematous changes and damaged alveolar structures [7, 131]. One explanation for this is enhanced epithelial apoptosis and necroptosis as well as reduced e-cadherin expression [132, 133]. Similar to bronchial epithelial cells, alveolar epithelial cells can internalise PM2.5 [133]. This is accompanied by broad intracellular modifications, including enhanced oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage and release of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and IL-18 [132, 134–137]. PM2.5 also enhances the production of EMT markers such as vimentin [133]. This is mirrored at the transcriptomic and methylomic level, where enhanced markers of EMT and senescence have been observed [136]. Additionally, this remodelling phenotype is further aggravated by a lowered ATI/II ratio attributable to ATII proliferation and inhibited ATI-to-ATII transdifferentiation [131]. PM2.5 and innate immunity

PM2.5 exposure increases pulmonary immune cell infiltration in murine models and drives dynamic alterations to innate immunity [112, 138]. Alveolar macrophages display distinct temporal phenotypic alterations, where acute exposure promotes intracellular particle accumulation, glycolytic reprogramming and a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterised by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and release of IL-1β, IL-18 and TNF-α amplified by exosome- and microRNA-mediated crosstalk [108, 109, 117, 127, 139–143]. In contrast, prolonged exposure drives M2 macrophage polarisation, elevates IL-4/signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (Akt) activity and upregulates MMP-12 consistent with tissue remodelling in COPD [101, 109, 112, 139]. PM2.5 can also induce trained immunity via H3K27ac epigenetic modifications, leading to robust inflammatory responses upon secondary insults [144]. PM2.5 further disrupts AM function by increasing autophagic membrane accumulation and compromising the lysosomal acidic microenvironment, leading to reduced autophagy and phagocytosis [145]. PM2.5 also drives AM metabolic reprogramming toward abnormal glycolysis promoting histone lactylation which induces the expression of pro-fibrotic mediators driving EMT in murine models [110].

Neutrophil count is typically increased in response to PM2.5 and pulmonary neutrophil accumulation is a common feature of PM2.5-induced lung injury [108, 146–148]. This neutrophil infiltration is not only attributable to increased chemokine production by macrophages and epithelial cells, but is also associated with increased ROS seen in both human and rodent models [107–111, 146]. Reduced expression of anti-apoptosis proteins such as B-cell lymphoma-2 further exacerbates neutrophil accumulation and persistence [147].

Finally, DCs can internalise PM2.5, which is associated with increased metabolic activity, reduced pro-inflammatory signalling and decreased endocytic and phagocytic capacity [149–151]. Whether this is accompanied by altered antigen presenting capabilities is unclear. While some find impaired antigen presentation, others report increased antigen presentation, priming of naïve T-cells towards a Th17-like phenotype and induction of cytotoxic T-cell expansion [151–153]. Given the critical role of DCs in bridging innate and adaptive immunity [72], careful evaluations of these discrepancies are required to fully understand the implications of air pollution on the immune system. Interactions between PM2.5 and antiviral response

PM2.5 alters respiratory antiviral defences through oxidative stress, inflammatory skewing and direct impairment of innate immune mechanisms. Specifically, PM2.5 suppresses type I/III IFN responses, dysregulates macrophage and DC function and increases epithelial susceptibility to viral entry [154, 155]. These effects arise from particle composition and exposure patterns and have important implications for infectious disease-risk in polluted regions and for public health interventions [19, 20]. Epidemiological and experimental data consistently link PM2.5 exposure to impaired IFN responses and increased incidence and severity of respiratory viral infections such as IAV, rhinovirus and SARS-CoV-2 [156–166]. Indeed, PM2.5 exposure is positively correlated to SARS-CoV-2 infection and post-infection manifestations [156–160]. While some murine studies describe increased ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression in alveolar cells and fibroblasts [167, 168], the applicability to humans remain unclear.

In contrast, higher PM2.5 levels are positively associated with IAV incidence [161–165]. Pre-exposure of bronchial epithelial cells to PM2.5 enhances viral susceptibility by increasing pro-inflammatory cytokine release such as IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α and decreasing IFN responses [18, 113, 116, 117, 130, 169]. Furthermore, TLR4 activation by PM2.5 is suggested to be partially involved in the enhanced IAV infection [116]. Pre-exposure of bronchial epithelial cells to PM2.5 also induce cellular damage and altered autophagy, with transcriptomic analysis suggesting the involvement of pleiotropic genes and pathways promoting IAV infection [117, 169].

Although interactions between PM2.5 and rhinovirus infection remain largely unexplored, emerging evidence suggests potential interference with antiviral defence pathways. Studies involving co-exposure to larger particulate fractions demonstrate a negative correlation between particle size and rhinovirus-induced mRNA expression in bronchial epithelial cells, indicative of an impaired IFN response [166]. Similarly, exposure to diesel exhaust particulates promotes epithelial upregulation of pattern recognition receptors, viral entry receptors and pro-inflammatory cytokines in nasal epithelial cultures [170]. In parallel, reductions in AM phagocytic activity and attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokine release from fibroblasts have been reported following particulate exposure [171, 172]. Collectively, these findings imply that PM2.5 may dysregulate innate immune signalling and cellular antiviral responses during rhinovirus infection. However, a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of PM2.5 rhinovirus co-exposure and its implications for respiratory pathophysiology remains critically lacking.

PM2.5 and chronic fibrotic responses

Chronic PM2.5 exposure promotes pulmonary fibrogenesis through persistent epithelial injury, aberrant tissue repair and altered immune responses. In both serum and BALF of Wistar rats, levels of TGF-β, several growth factors including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) as well as tissue-inhibitor of MMPs 1, 2, 9 and 13 are elevated in response to PM2.5 exposure [112]. In fibroblasts, PM2.5 increases transcription of collagen type 1α1 chain, α-smooth muscle actin and fibronectin, indicating initiation of myofibroblast differentiation. Bronchial epithelial cells also release TGF-β following exposure [143], demonstrating that epithelial crosstalk contributes to fibrogenesis.

The fibrotic potential of PM2.5 is further linked to its chemical composition. Metals, heavy metals and other toxic nonmetallic compounds consistently detected in PM2.5 can directly trigger pro-fibrotic signalling cascades [114, 173, 174]. In bronchial epithelial cells, these components stimulate TGF-β and PDGF production, thereby reinforcing epithelial-derived mediators during fibrotic remodelling [174]. Collectively, these PM2.5-driven mechanisms contribute to extracellular matrix deposition and progressive structural remodelling characteristic of chronic respiratory diseases.

The triple-hit hypothesis: vaping, PM2.5 and viral infections

Vaping exposure does not occur in isolation, but rather alongside other environmental pollutants. As vaping shares mechanistic overlap with PM2.5, it is plausible that co-exposure produces an additive or synergistic effect amplifying airway exposure beyond what is observed with single exposure. Moreover, respiratory infections are a major cause of chronic respiratory disease exacerbations [24, 77], suggesting that co-exposure may accelerate infection severity and chronic respiratory disease progression. It is hence reasonable to assume that polyexposure to vaping aerosols, PM2.5 and respiratory viral infections converge at several signalling axes that may aggravate the epithelial dysfunction, immune dysregulation and antiviral imbalance induced by each stressor individually (figure 5), although critical thresholds governing the transition from adaptive responses to pathological injury and remodelling remain undefined.

Together, these insults interact synergistically through three central pathological processes: 1) inflammatory milieu and oxidative stress, 2) impaired mucociliary and immune defences leading to reduced pathogen clearance; and 3) barrier dysfunction with enhanced inflammatory mediator release. The cumulative effect results in epithelial damage, chronic inflammation and airway remodelling, ultimately contributing to worsened outcomes such as exacerbations of COPD and asthma. Effects on pulmonary innate immunity

To date, only one study has examined the effects of dual e-cigarette and PM2.5 exposure, reporting decreased cellular viability and increased inflammatory mediators including ROS, cytokines, NF-κB and NLRP3 expression in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages [17]. Indeed, as PM2.5 promotes time-dependent shifts between pro-inflammatory and remodelling phenotypes in alveolar macrophages [109, 139] and vaping induces lipid-laden macrophages with defective clearance and altered M1/M2 equilibrium [14, 15, 45, 47], inhaled aerosols or particulates impose convergent stress on innate immunity with distinct cellular signatures. As PM2.5 increases macrophage lipid deposition via TLR4 and CD36, aggravates mitochondrial damage and accelerates apoptosis [173, 175], it may exacerbate lipid accumulation in alveolar macrophages exposed to vape aerosols. This altered macrophage function could then impede viral clearance and perpetuate inflammation. Increased ROS from co-exposure may further enhance viral susceptibility, as oxidative stress reduces rhinovirus pattern recognition receptors, attenuates IFN responses and disrupts redox balance in airway epithelial cells. This suggests a self-perpetuating cycle in which viral exacerbations impair antiviral defence, promoting further inflammation [176].

Alveolar macrophages exposed to e-cigarettes show reduced efferocytosis, while PM2.5 exposure impairs autophagy [67, 68, 145]. As autophagy limits viral replication and efferocytosis clears dying cells noninflammatorily, their disruption increases viral persistence, secondary necrosis and sustained inflammation [68, 177, 178]. This is further amplified by SARS-CoV-2- and IAV-induced neutrophil recruitment, the latter of which is associated with IAV-induced epithelial necrosis [8, 108, 179]. Elevated IL-6, IL-8 and MMPs from both exposures drive a neutrophilic phenotype characteristic of COPD, where neutrophil accumulation and degranulation contribute to tissue damage [34, 68]. Hence, a complex loop of secondary necrosis due to impaired clearance of apoptotic neutrophils and attraction of circulating neutrophils due to secondary necrosis may be initiated.

In terms of the pulmonary epithelium, IAV, SARS-CoV-2 or rhinovirus infection could further exacerbate the inflammatory milieu through cytopathic injury, epithelial integrity disruption and excessive cytokine release [8, 78, 79, 83, 84, 92, 93]. Additionally, IAV infection is characterised by extensive epithelial sloughing [180, 181]. This pro-inflammatory state may be further exacerbated by the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome formation induced by SARS-CoV-2, rhinovirus or PM2.5 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and bronchial epithelial cells, associated with disease severity and lethality [107, 108, 142, 143, 182, 183]. PM2.5 exposure further intensifies epithelial injury, oxidative stress, mucus production and inflammation in COPD-derived cells [120, 184, 185], emphasising its aggravating effect on compromised lungs.

Effects on antiviral responses and susceptibility to infection

In addition to impaired epithelial barrier function and dysregulated immune signalling, reduced or delayed IFN responses in bronchial epithelial cells from asthmatics or COPD patients has been associated with increased viral titres [186]. Although SARS-CoV-2 and IAV infection induce distinct inflammatory profiles, the decreased antiviral response associated with both PM2.5 and e-cigarette use greatly enhances the susceptibility of IAV and SARS-CoV-2 and induces a prolonged inflammatory state [23, 103, 156–160, 187–190]. Additionally, severe and critical SARS-CoV-2 patients also display reduced IFN signalling [179], further perturbing the antiviral response. Impaired mucociliary function induced by both e-cigarettes and PM2.5 in addition to PM2.5-associated mucus hypersecretion further increases the risk of infection by increasing the contact time of viruses and epithelial cells [191, 192]. This greatly increases the risk of exacerbations of already-existing chronic respiratory diseases as well as increasing the risk of chronic respiratory disease diagnosis later in life [5, 6, 24, 77]. This enhanced frequency of exacerbations in combination with decreased antiviral responses induced by vaping and PM2.5 also suggests increased tissue damage and deferred resolution during each exacerbation, although a comprehensive understanding of polyexposure on the effects of viral-induced tissue damage, resolution and susceptibility to secondary pathogens remains elusive. Additionally, there is a clear knowledge gap regarding the intersection between epithelial and immune signalling networks including the magnitude, timing and cellular sources of mediator release and their crosstalk with antiviral pathways.

At the cellular level, e-cigarette exposure amplifies the barrier dysfunction, cytokine release and reduced IFN response of IAV infection in small airway epithelial cells, human precision-cut lung slices and murine models [21, 22, 47, 81]. Similarly, PM2.5 increases the number of IAV-infected bronchial epithelial cells, reduces IFN responses and increases IL-6 and IL-8 secretion [116, 130, 169]. As the effects of PM2.5 and IAV co-exposure are not simply additive, pleiotropic pathways are hypothesised to be involved in this promotive effect [169]. Increased IL-8 secretion from lung epithelial cells has also been associated with worse obstruction in COPD [193], highlighting a role of viral-induced cytokine secretion in PM2.5- or vaping-induced lung injury. Lungs damaged by COPD also display increased IAV-infected cell numbers, indicating increased viral susceptibility of compromised lungs that could be further enhanced by PM2.5 [164]. As for SARS-CoV-2, PM2.5 and e-cigarette vapour may both upregulate ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression to facilitate viral entry and replication [82, 85–88, 167, 168]. The characteristic cytokine overproduction of SARS-CoV-2 infection may then further aggravate immune cell infiltration and associated damage induced by PM2.5 and/or vaping [84]. Finally, epigenetic alterations following both PM2.5 and e-cigarette exposure also proposes a risk of sustained impairment of viral defences beyond exposure cessation [48, 111, 118, 130]. Together, this highlights a multifaceted interplay between vaping, PM2.5 exposure and respiratory viral infections, forming the basis of the triple-hit hypothesis.

Effects on lung remodelling and clinical manifestations

PM2.5 contributes to airway fibrosis via TGF-β, MMPs and fibronectin release, leading to apical collagenous deposition [112, 143]. Long-term exposure to heavy metals found in PM2.5 are also able to induce a profibrotic phenotype of fibroblasts and apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells [100], highlighting the risk of similar responses from heavy metals derived from the inside of the vape pod. Aggravated protease/antiprotease imbalance seen by both vaping and PM2.5 exposure also indicates an increased risk of airway remodelling and fibrosis development [68, 184]. However, it is currently unknown whether repeated polyexposure accelerates this airway remodelling, fibrosis and emphysema, thereby propagating the progression of chronic respiratory diseases.

Further, acrolein and the wide range of other chemical compounds found in inhaled e-liquid aerosols poses a risk of neurogenic inflammation as evident by classic symptoms noted by e-cigarette users including cough, dyspnoea, wheezing and mucus hyperproduction [194]. Similarly, PM2.5, IAV, rhinovirus and COVID-19 have been implicated in sensory nerve interactions and sympathetic activation [195, 196]. As the respiratory nervous system is highly connected to chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma, COPD and pulmonary fibrosis [196], polyexposure proposes a risk of additive or enhanced symptoms of neurogenic inflammation induced by each individual stressor.

Integrated perspectives of the triple-hit hypothesis

The triple-hit hypothesis predicts how sequential or concurrent exposures may lead to heightened sensitivity to other exposures. These reciprocal amplifications may drive a self-propagating loop of cellular injury, inflammation and failed resolution characteristic of chronic respiratory diseases. From a public health perspective, the triple-hit hypothesis underscores the inadequacy of current single-exposure models to predict real-world outcomes. Populations in urban and industrial regions may face increased respiratory risks as PM2.5 pollution and viral transmission coincide with rising e-cigarette use. While evidence of direct spatial overlap is limited, sociodemographics of vaping use in Europe, Asia and Africa reveals significant cigarette use in large cities, while ambient PM2.5 levels tend to be increased in highly populated areas, suggesting co-localisation of these exposures in urban and industrial areas [197–200].

Hence, integrative experimental models and longitudinal cohort studies capturing polyexposure interaction effects are critically needed. Importantly, the extent to which current experimental models systematically underestimate pulmonary injury relative to real-world exposure contexts must be determined to identify in vitro, in vivo and human challenge models that best capture the temporal sequencing effects of polyexposure. Moreover, variability in PM2.5 and e-cigarette vapour composition, environmental context such as humidity and viral seasonality and host susceptibility probably modulate disease severity under polyexposure. It is therefore of urgent importance to incorporate these factors into study design and risk assessment. Additionally, further longitudinal and population-based research is required to establish definitive associations between vaping and susceptibility to a broader range of respiratory viruses. Similarly, future research should also incorporate other environmental pollutions to provide a more comprehensive and realistic interpretation of PM as a factor in polyexposure.

Finally, the link between cigarette smoking and COPD required decades to establish due to the slow progression of cigarette-induced lung injury and lag of multiple decades between smoking initiation and development of pathology [34]. Therefore, understanding the triad of vaping, PM2.5 and respiratory viral infections is crucial for refining risk assessment frameworks, exposure guidelines and preventive strategies targeting respiratory health.

Conclusions

Despite rapid advances in vaping and PM2.5 research, major knowledge gaps remain regarding their combined effects in the context of respiratory viral infections. Polyexposure represents the exposure norm rather than the exception and the triple-hit hypothesis addresses this complexity by proposing additive or synergistic interactions that may heighten viral susceptibility and accelerate chronic respiratory disease progression. This framework underscores the urgent need for integrative models comparable to real-world exposure contexts, as validating the comparability of the triple-hit hypothesis may prove pivotal for both mechanistic insight and preventive public policies.

Questions for future research

Growing evidence highlights the individual effects of e-cigarette aerosols, PM2.5 and viral infections on lung inflammation and immune dysregulation. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the pulmonary effects of e-cigarette aerosols, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and respiratory viral infections individually, the biological consequences of their combined exposure remain largely undefined. Addressing these gaps will be critical for advancing research on multi-hit environmental insults and their contribution to lung inflammation and disease.
