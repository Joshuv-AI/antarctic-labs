Deep mutational scanning reveals the antibody escape and infectivity landscape of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron JN.1 and XEC receptor-binding domains
Authors: Chengwei Shao, Lisa Yang, Chuyao Xiao, Yumei Huang, Xiangxi Wang, Ling Chen, Pan Liu, Si Chen, Mingwei Wei, Siyue Jia, Lunbiao Cui, Yiyue Ge, Ming Xu, Xiaoyan Jia, Jingxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSARS-CoV-2 continuously accumulates mutations in the spike receptor-binding domain (RBD), affecting both viral infectivity and antibody evasion. Systematic characterization of RBD mutations is therefore essential for understanding viral adaptation under immune pressure and predicting evolutionary trajectories. In this study, we employed a two-step, non-replicating pseudovirus deep mutational scanning (DMS) platform to measure the effects of all single amino acid substitutions in the RBD of Omicron variant JN.1 and its descendant lineage XEC within a full-length spike background. To identify representative antibodies for escape profiling, we first evaluated six RBD-targeting monoclonal antibodies against JN.1 and XEC pseudoviruses. Only BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA retained substantial neutralizing activity and were selected for subsequent escape mapping. The results showed that most single RBD amino acid mutations did not significantly enhance pseudovirus cellular invasion. Among mutations that are functionally retaining and confer marked escape from either antibody, most high escape substitutions cluster within the receptor-binding motif (RBM) and receptor-binding ridge. Furthermore, BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA each exhibited distinct, antibody-specific escape sites, demonstrating that different epitope preferences exert unique selective pressures within the same viral lineage. Overall, this pseudovirus-based DMS analysis elucidates the molecular mechanisms of immune escape and fitness for the JN.1 and XEC lineages. Our findings provide critical insights for forecasting SARS-CoV-2 evolution under population immunity and offer guidance for assessing emerging variants, selecting vaccine strains, and optimizing therapeutic antibodies.

Introduction

The spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 is a key molecule for viral invasion of host cells and a core target for neutralizing antibodies [1]. Since the outbreak at the end of 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has rapidly and continuously accumulated mutations throughout its spike protein in an attempt to evade natural infection and the immunity induced by vaccination [1,2]. Compared with the ancestral strain (Wuhan-Hu-1, WT) and the early representative variants BA.2 and XBB.1.5 of Omicron, a new subvariant of BA.2, BA.2.86, has attracted widespread attention since its first discovery [3]. The spike protein of the BA.2.86 variant carries a high number of amino acid mutations compared to previous lineages, specifically harbouring 58 mutations relative to the ancestral strain, 34 relative to BA.2, and 36 relative to XBB.1.5 (Figure 1(a)). Notably, BA.2.86 also introduced the 483del mutation-the first deletion identified within the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein [3]. The subsequent JN.1 variant, which descends from BA.2.86, carries an additional L455S mutation. This change confers a significant advantage in transmission and immune evasion, contributing to JN.1’s rapid global spread and current dominance as the circulating strain [3]. Building upon this lineage, the more recent XEC variant retains the mutation profile of BA.2.86 and JN.1 while accumulating two further spike substitutions: T22N and F59S (Figure 1(a)) [4]. These newly acquired key-site mutations within the RBD significantly reshape the antigenic profile of the virus. These changes have progressively eroded the neutralizing efficacy of immunity derived from prior infection or vaccination. Furthermore, they have rendered most monoclonal antibody therapies developed during the early stages of the pandemic virtually ineffective against current variants [5]. Therefore, a systematic characterization of how RBD mutations affect viral infectivity and antibody escape in representative strains like JN.1 and its descendant XEC is essential. Such analysis is critical for understanding their immunological properties, evaluating the effectiveness of existing RBD-targeting countermeasures, and forecasting future evolutionary paths. Within the spike protein, not all regions contribute equally to viral entry and immune recognition. Among its multiple functional domains, the RBD plays a uniquely central role because it directly mediates the interaction with the host receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), which is crucial for viral entry into host cells [6]. Meanwhile, the RBD is also the primary target of antibodies induced by natural infection and vaccination, making it a focal point of humoral immune pressure [7]. Therefore, amino acid substitutions within the RBD are subject to dual and competing selective pressures: the need to maintain sufficient receptor-binding affinity and structural integrity for efficient cell entry, and the need to evade antibody recognition [8]. This dual pressure makes the RBD a key region for balancing invasion-related functions and immune escape benefits [8]. Given this context, a systematic analysis of RBD mutations within the full-length spike protein serves to identify high-risk sites at single-residue resolution and provides the necessary framework for understanding the adaptive changes and immune escape mechanisms of evolving Omicron lineages like JN.1 and its descendant, XEC [1,2,5–9].

Deep mutational scanning (DMS) is a powerful technique for assessing the functional impact of numerous amino acid substitutions in a protein, which allows for the evaluation of mutations before they emerge or spread widely [7]. Early applications of DMS to SARS-CoV-2 primarily utilized yeast surface display systems to systematically characterize how RBD mutations affect spike protein expression and ACE2 binding affinity. These studies provided foundational, biochemical insights into changes in receptor recognition [6,7]. More recently, the strategy has been extended by integrating DMS with pseudovirus-based neutralization assays. This approach involves constructing mutant libraries spanning the RBD or full-length spike to profile antibody and serum escape within a more physiologically relevant viral entry context [1,2]. By doing so, these studies directly link individual amino acid substitutions to changes in spike-mediated infectivity and neutralization sensitivity, offering a more functional understanding of immune evasion [1,2]. To date, most studies are based on early Omicron variants, whose mutant libraries cover only a limited number of sites and amino acid substitutions, and the representative neutralizing antibodies included in the evaluation are mainly early developed antibodies, and their numbers are limited [1,2,6–8]. For variants after the XBB lineage, especially JN.1 and its descendant subclades, there is currently a lack of systematic DMS data that simultaneously assess viral infectivity and sensitivity to next-generation monoclonal neutralizing antibodies [5–8]. This gap limits our ability to fully characterize the antigenic consequences of newly accumulated RBD mutations and to assess the effectiveness of current next-generation antibodies.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the impact of RBD amino acid substitutions on viral invasion efficiency in the context of the full-length spike protein, thereby mapping the infection phenotypes of JN.1 and XEC. In addition, we evaluated the ability of these mutants to escape from the RBD-targeting neutralizing monoclonal antibodies BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA (representing humoral and mucosal immunity, respectively) using a high-throughput approach. This analysis revealed key sites associated with viral entry and immune escape in JN.1 and its descendant lineages.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

HEK-293T cells (Vazyme) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 4.5 g/L D-glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (NEWZERUM, FBS-UE500) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15070063) referred to as complete DMEM medium. HEK-293-ACE2 cell line (Vazyme) was cultured in HEK293 basal medium (OPM-293 CD05 Medium, 81075-001) with a defined chemical composition. A stable HEK-293T cell line expressing rtTA (HEK-293T-rtTA), required for DMS library maintenance and TRE3G promoter activation, was generated according to the method described by Dadonaite et al. [1]. HEK-293T-rtTA cells and HEK-293T cells were cultured in the same complete DMEM medium. All cells were cultured at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2, passaged every 2–3 days.

Design of lentiviral vector backbone

The detailed information on the lentiviral vector backbone has been reported by Dadonaite et al. before [1]. Briefly, the lentiviral backbone is a replication defective type, and can only assemble infectious viral particles in cells when the helper plasmids Gag/Pol, Tat1b and Rev1b provide the structure and replication protein. The target gene (spike protein) is placed under the TRE3G inducible promoter, and its transcription depends on the reverse tetracycline transcription activator (rtTA). Adding doxycycline (MCE, HY-N0565) to HEK-293T-rtTA cells can activate TRE3G and start the transcription of the spike protein gene, achieving strict Tet-On control. In addition, the vector also contains ZsGreen and puromycin resistance genes driven by the CMV promoter. ZsGreen is used as a reporter gene to facilitate monitoring of infection levels, while puromycin resistance is used for drug screening to obtain a stable integrated cell population.

Spike protein gene synthesis and modification

The spike gene of the JN.1 variant used in this study was synthesized, quality-controlled, and sequence-verified by GenScript (China). The spike gene of the XEC variant was engineered from the JN.1 spike by introducing two mutations at the key positions T22N and F59S. Two pairs of overlap-extension PCR primers were designed to introduce these substitutions. The XEC spike gene was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the JN.1 spike gene template. A linearized vector backbone of approximately 8.6 kb was obtained by amplification using high-fidelity polymerase (2× Phanta Max Master Mix, Vazyme P515-03), and the template was then digested with DpnI (abclonal, RK21109) to remove methylated template. The spike protein gene fragment containing the target mutation (XEC spike protein) was amplified by standard PCR using primers containing homologous arms, and was assembled with the linearized vector at a 3:1 molar ratio using 2× MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (ABclonal, RK21020). The assembly products were transformed into Stbl3 competent cells (ALpaLifeBio, KTSM110L), and the positive clones were screened using ampicillin plates (100 μg/mL). The correct insertion of the target gene and the integrity of the vector were ultimately confirmed by next-generation sequencing (NGS).

Design of DMS library

Using Wuhan-Hu-1 as the reference strain for residue numbering, the RBD in the JN.1 spike protein spans positions R319 to F541. Because the amino acid sequences within the RBD are identical between JN.1 and XEC, a shared mutant library can be used. Using the JN.1 spike gene as the template, we generated a saturation mutant library encompassing the full 666 bp RBD sequence. Considering the short read-length limitations of NGS, the RBD was divided into four fragments: three fragments of 168 bp (56 aa) each and one fragment of 162 bp (54 aa). Each fragment had 25 bp homologous arms added to both ends, and these were assembled into single-fragment libraries. The libraries were designed using a single-codon saturation strategy, in which each codon was substituted with the other 63 possible codons (including synonymous and nonsense/stop codons). Therefore, for a 168 bp fragment, the library theoretically contains 56 × 63 = 3,528 mutants, along with one WT sequence as a control. All mutagenic oligonucleotides were synthesized by NEOPRIMARIES (BGI, China) and were used to introduce mutations into both the JN.1 and XEC spike genes.

Design of the plasmid library for DMS

Plasmid libraries for DMS were generated by seamless cloning of the library fragments into the lentiviral vector backbone. To ensure library complexity, at least 10-fold coverage was required during cloning, resulting in ≥35,280 independent bacterial clones carrying mutants. To this end, the ligation products were transformed into Stbl3 competent cells, and 2 μL and 10 μL aliquots of the transformation culture were plated on LB agar plates containing ampicillin, followed by incubation at 30°C for 21 h. The overall library complexity was estimated using colony counts, while the remaining transformation culture was expanded in 120 mL of LB medium. Subsequently, the integrity of the plasmid vector backbone was verified by sequencing using a Tn5 transposase-mediated library construction strategy (see below: NGS Sample Processing). The coverage and uniformity of mutant distribution were analyzed by amplicon sequencing (see below: NGS Sample Processing), thus confirming that the library quality met experimental requirements.

Production of the DMS cell library

The procedure for preparing a cell-storage spike protein DMS library has been described in detail previously [1]. In brief, the above-mentioned DMS plasmid library, helper plasmids HDM-Hgpm2 (Addgene #204152), HDM-Tat1b (Addgene #204154) and pRC-CMV-Rev1b (Addgene #204153) and VSV-G expression plasmid (Addgene #204156) were used to transfect two 100 mm culture dishes of HEK-293T cells with jetOPTIMUS transfection reagent (Polyplus, 117-15) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, producing a VSV-G pseudotyped lentiviral pool whose genomes carry mutated spike sequences. HEK-293T-rtTA cells were then infected with VSV-G pseudotyped lentiviral at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) to ensure that each cell was infected with at most one viral particle. After infection, HEK-293T-rtTA cells that successfully integrated the lentiviral backbone were screened with puromycin until all surviving cells expressed ZsGreen fluorescent protein by fluorescence microscope. At this point, all the cell-stored libraries were stored in liquid nitrogen for later use, Additionally, a portion of the stored library cells are used for amplicon sequencing analysis to assess the coverage and uniformity of the mutant library.

Measuring pseudovirus infection effects using DMS library

The methods for measuring the infectivity effects of each library variant on target cells were performed as previously reported [1]. In brief, using the same batch of HEK-293T-rtTA cell-stored libraries, approximately 1 × 106 transduction units of spike pseudovirus library and approximately 3 × 106 transduction units of VSV-G pseudovirus library were prepared to infect target cells. HEK-293-ACE2 cells were used as target cells for spike pseudovirus library infection. HEK-293T cells that did not express any ACE2 were used for VSV-G pseudovirus library infection to avoid infection on the spike protein that may be present on the surface of these VSV-G pseudoviruses. All spike pseudovirus library and VSV-G pseudovirus library infection assays (including antibody escape assays described below) were performed with an additional 5 µg/ml polybrene (Beyotime, C0351). The viral genome was recovered from cells using the genome extraction kit (Vazyme, DC112) at 24 h post-infection, following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the composition and enrichment of the mutant library were analyzed by amplicon sequencing.

Antibody escape assays

The six RBD-targeting monoclonal antibodies used for escape assay were BD55-1205, 719-14 sIgA, LY-CoV1404, COV2-2130, VIR-7831, and BRII-196. BD55-1205 is a broadly neutralizing IgG antibody derived from a WT-infected individual and encoded by IGHV3-66. It has been reported to neutralize multiple existing and potentially emerging Omicron sublineages, including BA.5, XBB.1.5, HK.3, JN.1, and KP.3. Structural studies have shown that BD55-1205 is a typical Class 1 antibody that binds to the top of the RBD, with an antibody footprint that substantially overlaps the ACE2-binding interface, and exhibits a pronounced ACE2 receptor mimicry feature, meaning that it recognizes the viral receptor-binding region in ACE2 [5]. 719-14 sIgA is a secretory IgA antibody derived from the nasal mucosa. Previous competition-binding assays have shown that it can compete with RBD antibodies from different classes, specifically those targeting Class 2 and Class 3 epitopes, suggesting that its recognition range may cover broader conformational epitopes. In addition, its polymeric structure confers higher avidity and stronger cross-neutralizing capacity, allowing it to maintain effective binding and neutralization even when some contact residues are altered. It has been shown to neutralize several representative Omicron sublineages, including BA.5, XBB.1.5, JN.1, and KP.3 [10]. LY-CoV1404 (MCE, HY-P99103), COV2-2130 (MCE, HY-P99604), VIR-7831 (MCE, HY-P99340), and BRII-196 (MCE, HY-P99435) were all purchased commercially available monoclonal antibodies. Specifically, LY-CoV1404 (Bebtelovimab) is a human IgG1-λ2 antibody against SARS-CoV-2 [11]. BRII-196 (Amubarvimab) is a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to two regions in the spike protein: non-competitive epitopes spanning amino acids 453–505 in the RBM and amino acid residues 403–421 in the RBD core region [12]. LY-CoV1404 and BRII-196 primarily block ACE2 binding by occupying the RBD receptor-binding interface [13–15]. LY-CoV1404 potently neutralizes early circulating strains and Omicron subvariants BA.1, BA.2, and BA.4/5 [16]. In contrast, while BRII-196 effectively neutralizes the ancestral strain and early VOCs like Delta, its activity is significantly reduced against Omicron BA.1, BA.2, and BA.4/5 [17,18]. COV2-2130 (Cilgavimab, AZD-1061) is a human monoclonal antibody targeting SARS-CoV-2, possessing strong neutralizing activity and human ACE2 blocking activity, and is also a component of the monoclonal antibody combination AZD7442 [19,20]. COV2-2130 targets overlapping, relatively conserved epitopes on the outer RBD, maintaining good neutralization against early strains (ancestral, Alpha, Delta) but showing markedly reduced efficacy against Omicron BA.1, BA.2, and BA.4/5 [16,21]. VIR-7831 (Sotrovimab) is a human IgG1κ coronavirus monoclonal antibody developed based on S309, with a long half-life and high bioavailability in the respiratory mucosa [22]. VIR-7831 targets a conserved glycopeptide epitope near the N343 glycosylation site, retaining activity against Omicron BA.1 but exhibiting significantly reduced neutralization against BA.2 [16,23].

The selection experiment under antibody pressure included four groups: spike pseudovirus + antibody, spike pseudovirus-antibody, VSV-G pseudovirus + antibody, and VSV-G pseudovirus-antibody. The antibody in the antibody-treated groups was diluted according to the IC80 results of the standard pseudovirus neutralization assays. The diluted antibody was mixed with the pseudovirus suspension at a 1:1 volume ratio and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 × 106 HEK-293-ACE2 or HEK-293T cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates and infected with the antibody-pseudovirus mixtures. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2, cells were then collected, genomic DNA was extracted, and full-length spike PCR amplification and Illumina amplicon library preparation were performed for sequencing analysis. The group without antibodies was infected with the virus in the same conditions, and the treatment time and method were the same as above.

NGS sample processing

We employed two complementary sequencing approaches: Tn5-based random fragmentation sequencing and targeted amplicon sequencing. Tn5 sequencing was used to assess the integrity of the full-length lentiviral plasmid backbone, ensuring no large deletions or unexpected mutations were introduced during cloning. For this, DNA was fragmented using Tn5 transposase, barcoded, and amplified by PCR to construct sequencing libraries, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Products were purified with magnetic beads (abclonal, RK20257) and quantified for Illumina sequencing. Separately, to evaluate library coverage and mutation uniformity, amplicon sequencing libraries were prepared via two rounds of PCR using KAPA HiFi HotStart DNA polymerase (KAPA, KK2502). The first round amplified the target RBD regions using primers containing partial Illumina adapters. The second round added full dual indexes (p7 and p5) for sample multiplexing. All PCR products were purified with magnetic beads at a 1.8x ratio and sequenced on an Illumina platform.

Pseudovirus neutralization assay

First, all viruses were titrated to standardize the viral input between assays. Antibodies were serially diluted 4-fold in 96-well plates, with two replicates per sample, starting at 10 µg/mL. Then, pseudovirus diluted to 2 × 104 TCID50/mL was added at a 1:1 ratio, and the pseudovirus-antibody mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, HEK-293-ACE2 cells were added at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for approximately 48 h. Luciferase activity was quantified using a luciferase assay kit (Beyotime, RG042S) following the manufacturer’s instructions and using SoftMax Pro v.7.0.2 (Molecular Devices) software. Neutralization curves were fitted using a non-linear five-parameter dose–response model in GraphPad Prism v9.2, from which IC50 and IC80 values were derived.

NGS data processing

For bioinformatic analysis, sequencing data were processed through two pipelines. For the Tn5 sequencing data, used to verify plasmid backbone integrity, reads were aligned to the reference sequence using BWA (v0.7.17) [24], with subsequent file conversion and sorting handled by Samtools (v1.9) and visualization performed in IGV (v2.8.0) [25,26]. For the amplicon sequencing data, from which variant frequencies were quantified, paired-end reads were first merged into single sequences using bbmerge (v38.84), adapter and primer sequences were trimmed with Cutadapt (v3.5), and finally, identical sequences were clustered and counted using Starcode (v1.3) to generate count tables for each unique variant [27–29].

Calculation of the functional effect of mutation

To assess the impact of spike mutations on infectivity, the functional score for each variant based on the sequencing frequency of VSV-G pseudovirus and spike pseudovirus infections in HEK-293T/HEK-293-ACE2 cells. The sequencing frequency was calculated as the variant read count divided by the sum of variant and WT reads in the library. To avoid issues with logarithms of zero counts, a small constant was added to all counts (pseudocount = 10−8), maintaining consistency across all samples. The functional score (log2 enrichment ratio) was then calculated using the following formula: score (υ)=log2( fυ,Spike fυ,VSV-G) where fv,Spike and fv,VSV-G are the sequencing frequencies of variant v in spike pseudovirus and VSV-G pseudovirus libraries respectively in HEK-293T/HEK-293-ACE2 cells. The score(v) calculated from this formula represents the raw functional score for each mutation. To improve the biological interpretability of the functional score, we further corrected the raw values by using the median score of synonymous mutations as a neutral baseline. Specifically, the median score of synonymous mutations was subtracted from all raw functional scores, thereby centreing the distribution of synonymous mutations around zero. This correction removes the systematic bias introduced by using VSV-G as a reference and enables functional effects to be interpreted relative to an internal neutral control (synonymous mutations). After this correction, the sign of the functional score reflects the effect relative to the neutral baseline: values below zero indicate that the mutation impairs spike-mediated entry relative to the neutral level, values above zero indicate functionally retaining or enhancing, and values close to zero indicate minimal functional impact.

To screen variants that escape capture with specific monoclonal antibodies, the non-neutralizing ratio/escape probability pv(c) for each variant at various antibody concentrations c: pυ(c)= fυ,Spike,c/fυ,VSV−G,c fυ,Spike,0/fυ,VSV−G,0 where the fv,Spike,c and fv,VSV-G,c represent the sequencing frequencies of variant v in the spike pseudovirus libraries and VSV-G pseudovirus libraries at antibody concentration c, respectively in HEK-293T/HEK-293-ACE2 cells. The fv,Spike,0 and fv,VSV-G,0 are the sequencing frequencies of the same variant in the respective virus libraries without antibody (control condition). To avoid meaningless of zero counts, a small constant was added to all counts (pseudocount = 10−8), and this was consistent across all samples to calculate the values based on the pv(c) formula. Then, normalized all mutations pv(c) by dividing by the global maximum, thereby constraining all mutations pv(c) to a range between 0 and 1. The normalized pv(c) values were used for two types of visualization analyses. At the site level, the escape score is calculated by summing all normalized pv(c) values at a given site, thereby quantifying the overall escape contribution of that site: Si=∑υ∈i pυ(c)max[pυ(c)] Si denotes the total escape score at site i, and v∈i represents all amino acid mutations located at that site.

At the site-amino acid substitution level, the mean escape score for each substitution at a given site was first calculated, and then normalized by the global maximum site-level escape magnitude (the maximum summed escape score across all sites). The escape score reflects the relative contribution of each amino acid substitution and enable comparability across sites: Hi,a=mean( pi,a(c)max[pυ(c)])maxi(∑amean( pi,a(c)max[pυ(c)])) Hi,a denotes the letter height of amino acid substitution “a” at site “i”. The numerator represents the mean normalized escape score for the same site and the same amino acid substitution, while the denominator represents the maximum total escape intensity observed across all sites.

Results

Genotype–phenotype linked pseudovirus DMS platform and mutation library construction

We applied a pseudotyped lentiviral DMS framework to systematically characterize the functional effects of spike mutations in the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sublineages JN.1 and XEC. This platform employs a two-step workflow to package pseudovirus spike RBD mutation libraries for both variants, enabling high-throughput sequencing of spike-mediated phenotypes while maintaining strict genotype-phenotype linkage.

Because the RBD sequences of JN.1 and XEC are identical, we designed a shared saturation mutagenesis library for both variants. The library was constructed based on Wuhan-Hu-1 numbering and targeted the RBD region (residues 319–541), covering all possible single amino acid substitutions. To achieve comprehensive single-codon saturation mutagenesis, the RBD was divided into four segments (three 56-amino-acid segments and one 54-amino-acid segment). For each amino acid position, a library containing all 63 non-wild-type codons was designed, ensuring coverage of all 20 amino acid substitutions and stop codons, while incorporating synonymous codon redundancy to control for codon-specific effects. Consequently, for each 56-amino-acid segment, 3,528 unique mutant DNA constructs (56 positions × 63 variants per position), along with one wild-type control were synthesized. This defined single-codon substitution design enables precise mapping of observed phenotypes to specific amino acid changes at individual residue positions.

High-throughput sequencing analysis of the RBD mutation library fragments showed that, at a sequencing depth of approximately 10⁶ reads, the vast majority of designed single-site mutations were successfully detected (>99%), indicating high library coverage. The frequency distribution across amino acid positions and substitution types was generally uniform, with only minor enrichment observed at a small number of positions, likely due to oligonucleotide synthesis bias. Overall, the frequencies of single-codon variants were predominantly on the order of 10−⁴. The cumulative mutation frequency at most residue sites was approximately 0.02, with only a few sites reaching higher values (up to 0.06). When aggregated across all positions, the total frequency of most substitution types remained below 0.1, with only a small subset approaching 0.15 (Figure S1(a)). These results indicate that the constructed RBD saturation mutagenesis library achieved high coverage and near-saturation in the current sequencing depth.

Following library construction, the mutated RBD sequences were cloned into full-length spike backbones and control of the TRE3GS inducible promoter (Figure 1(b)), delivered into HEK-293T-rtTA cells by VSV-G pseudotyped lentiviral transduction. After puromycin selection, each cell stably expressed a single spike variant. Prior to pseudovirus production, we evaluated the quality of the integrated libraries in HEK-293T-rtTA cells. Sequencing analysis revealed that, consistent with the library fragment results, nearly all designed single-site mutations were retained in both the JN.1 and XEC libraries. The distribution of mutation frequencies across positions and substitution types remained highly consistent, indicating that no substantial compositional shift occurred during cloning, viral transduction, or cell selection. The frequency of single-codon variants in the intracellular libraries remained predominantly at the 10−⁴ level, and the cumulative mutation frequencies across sites and substitution types closely mirrored those observed in the initial library fragments (Figure S1(b and c)).

Subsequently, doxycycline was used to induce the expression of mutated spike, resulting in the production of pseudoviruses displaying the corresponding spike variants on their surface. This strategy ensures a one-to-one correspondence between the spike protein presented on the pseudovirus and its packaged genome, thereby enabling high-throughput and accurate measurement of the functional phenotypes of individual mutations (Figure 1(c)).

Effects of RBD mutations on the spike-mediated pseudovirus infection of the JN.1 and XEC variants

To assess cell entry competence by pseudovirus, we collected target cells after pseudovirus infection and sequenced the integrated mutation library. The relative abundance changes of each mutant were quantified to assess the effect of spike protein mutations on pseudovirus infection. We first compared the composition of amino acid mutation categories in three contexts: the initial library within HEK-293T-rtTA cells, the library recovered after VSV-G pseudovirus infection, and the library recovered after spike pseudovirus infection. This comparison revealed that, relative to the initial intracellular library, VSV-G–mediated and spike-mediated pseudoviruses result in distinct changes in mutant abundance and differences in library composition after cell entry. Consistent trends were observed both in individual replicates (Figure S2(a)) and in the combined data (Figure S2(b)). After the pseudovirus infection, the VSV-G pseudovirus library retained a mutation type distribution similar to the initial intracellular library, with missense mutations remaining predominant. In contrast, the spike pseudovirus library was overwhelmingly dominated by wild-type sequences, showing significant reductions in the proportions of both missense and nonsense mutations. This stark difference indicates that the spike-mediated entry process exerts strong functional selection against many mutations, whereas VSV-G-mediated entry, which bypasses spike function, largely preserves the initial mutant distribution.

To quantify the impact of spike mutations on cell entry competence, we calculated a functional score for each variant by comparing its relative abundance after spike pseudovirus infection to its abundance after VSV-G pseudovirus infection. Because these functional scores are derived from competitive experiments within their respective mutant libraries, direct comparison between the JN.1 and XEC variant scores could be confounded by systematic biases arising from differences in the underlying composition of mutation types (synonymous, missense, nonsense) between their two libraries. To account for this, we further evaluated and compared the mutation type distributions in the spike protein libraries and VSV-G libraries for both the JN.1 and XEC variants. We calculated the proportion of mutation types in the spike and VSV-G libraries against the JN.1 (Figure S3(a)) and XEC (Figure S3(b)) variant templates. The results showed that both the spike and VSV-G libraries were dominated by missense mutations. Among the missense mutations in the spike library, approximately 90.7% for JN.1 and 90.2% for XEC, synonymous mutations accounted for approximately 7.7% and 7.6%, and nonsense mutations accounted for 1.6% and 2.2%. The corresponding VSV-G libraries also showed very similar proportions in JN.1 and XEC, showing approximately 90.5% for JN.1 and XEC, approximately 4.7% for synonymous mutations, and approximately 4.8% for nonsense mutations. Therefore, in the spike protein library, the overall proportion of the three mutation types in JN.1 is similar to that in XEC, despite the different template backgrounds. Similarly, in the VSV-G library, the composition of mutation types in JN.1 and XEC remains consistent across different template backgrounds. This suggests that the subsequent observed functional differences are unlikely to be due to systematic biases in the composition of mutation types.

The functional scores for JN.1 and XEC spike RBD mutations reflects their expected effects on spike-mediated entry. In the absence of baseline correction, the median functional scores of synonymous mutations in the spike RBD of JN.1 and XEC were −2.896 and −2.796, respectively, indicating a systematic negative shift (Figure S4(a and b)). To improve the biological interpretability of the functional scores, we corrected the raw functional scores by using the median of synonymous mutations as a neutral baseline, aligning their distribution to zero. This correction enables a more accurate representation of the effects of RBD mutations on spike-mediated pseudovirus infection (Figure 2). After correction, the sign of the functional score reflects the effect relative to the neutral baseline: positive functional scores indicate functionally retaining, whereas negative functional scores indicate functional impairment. Importantly, the corrected scores no longer represent absolute differences relative to VSV-G–mediated entry, but instead remove the systematic bias introduced by the reference system, allowing direct comparison of variant effects under a unified internal baseline. As expected, nonsense mutations showed strongly deleterious effects on viral entry. The median functional scores of nonsense mutations in the spike RBD of JN.1 and XEC were −11.102 and −11.157 after correction (−13.998 and −13.953 before correction), indicating strong depletion of these variants in cells following entry selection, reflecting severe impairment of spike-mediated entry. Missense mutations exhibited a broader distribution. However, most had functional scores below the neutral baseline, with medians of −1.914 and −2.118 (−4.810 and −4.914 before correction) for JN.1 and XEC, respectively. This suggests that most amino acid substitutions reduce spike-mediated entry to varying degrees, while only a small fraction confer improved entry efficiency relative to synonymous mutations (Figure 2(a and b)). Heatmap analysis at the site level further showed that, in both JN.1 and XEC backgrounds, most spike RBD mutations do not enhance spike-mediated pseudovirus infection relative to the template variant and that a substantial proportion fail to retain basic spike function. Only a limited number of sites (e.g. residues 445 and 481) exhibited mutations that are functionally retaining (Figure 2(d and e)). Without correction, the heatmaps displayed a consistent negative shift across all sites (Figure S4(d and e)). To determine whether this systematic shift affects the relative relationships among mutations, we compared the correlations between JN.1 and XEC functional scores before (Figure S4(c)) and after (Figure 2(c)) correction. The correlations were highly consistent, indicating that the correction introduces only a global shift without altering the relative ranking of variant effects. Accordingly, the functional differences and spatial distribution patterns across sites remain unchanged, and functionally retaining or enhancing mutations are still concentrated in the same localized regions. Further analysis showed a strong overall correlation between JN.1 and XEC functional scores (Figure 2(c)). In addition, functional scores were highly reproducible across experimental replicates (Figure S5). At the structural level, mutations with functional effects comparable to the template RBD exhibited a markedly non-uniform distribution across sites. In the JN.1 background, mutations that are stably and reliably functionally retaining at near-neutral or above-neutral levels are enriched in the 430–450 and 470–490 regions, which are located in the flexible loop regions on the RBD surface and in adjacent areas of the receptor-binding motif (RBM, residues 438–506). Within these regions, multiple amino acid substitutions were functionally retaining near-neutral levels, and some sites (e.g. 445, 478, and 481) displayed pronounced positive signals indicative of enhanced function. In contrast, although mutations with near-neutral functional scores were broadly distributed within the core β-sheet regions (residues 330–436 and 507–527), these mutations were generally weakly functionally retaining compared to those in the RBM. The distribution pattern in XEC was largely similar to that in JN.1 but showed a slightly reduced overall extent of functionally retaining mutations. Notably, additional regions with pronounced positive signals (490–505 and 515–522) were observed in JN.1 but not in XEC, suggesting that JN.1 exhibits greater tolerance to amino acid substitutions at the C-terminal edge of the RBD and adjacent transitional regions.

Overall, the two variants exhibit a highly consistent spatial pattern of functional constraint, with functionally retaining mutations primarily localized to the RBM and surrounding loop regions. Importantly, no extended continuous regions with strong positive signals were observed in either variant, indicating that functionally retaining mutations within the RBD is governed by discrete key sites rather than extended structural domains and remains broadly conserved across different genetic backgrounds.

Antibody escape mapping based on DMS

To evaluate the neutralization profiles of JN.1 and XEC before constructing escape maps, we first compared the following six RBD-targeting antibodies: BD55-1205, 719-14 sIgA, LY-CoV1404, COV2-2130, VIR-7831, and BRII-196. The results of pseudovirus neutralization detection showed that, except for BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA, the other antibodies completely lost their neutralizing activity against JN.1 (Figure 3(a)) and XEC (Figure 3(b)) variants. Previous studies have reported that LY-CoV1404, COV2-2130, and VIR-7831 lost their neutralizing activity against BA.2.86, while BRII-196 lost its neutralizing activity against XBB. Since JN.1 is a sublineage derived from BA.2.86 through further cumulative mutations, and its RBD mutation degree is no less than that of the XBB variant, it is reasonable to infer from an immunoevolutionary perspective that these monoclonal antibodies will not regain activity against the JN.1 variant, but will only exhibit worse neutralizing activity and level [16,23,30,31]. The relative contributions of mutated amino acids to neutralization escape in the BD55-1205 escape variant showed overall similar but slightly distinct hotspot patterns in the JN.1 and XEC RBD (Figure 4(b and e)). In both JN.1 and XEC, sites driven by single amino acid substitutions, such as 443E, 456R, 470E, and 474G, constituted the prominent escape hotspots (escape score (relative values) > 0.5). In addition, single amino acid substitutions at sites such as 433G, 434L, 441C, 457L, and 471T also showed high escape contributions (escape score (relative values) approximately 0.5). Notably, within the 455–478 region, except for site 464, the escape signals were primarily dominated by single amino acid substitutions. Especially in the two consecutive regions of 456-458 and 470–474, these single amino acid substitutions contributed distinguishable to antibody escape, the vast majority of escape scores (relative values) exceed 0.25, forming relatively concentrated regional escape hotspots. Multiple amino acid mutations exhibiting escape effects were detected in the 377–430 and 481–529 regions. However, except for 420Y, 493K, and 493R, the escape scores (relative values) for most mutations in these two regions are less than 0.1, indicating the relatively low escape levels. In addition, the types of low-escape amino acid substitutions (escape score (relative values) < 0.25) differed between the JN.1 and XEC backgrounds. In addition, slight differences were observed in the types of low-escape amino acid substitutions between the JN.1 and XEC. For example, escape mutations at sites 472, 476, 491, and 492 were detected only in JN.1 but not in XEC.

From a structural perspective, mapping the escape mutations of BD55-1205 onto defined RBD antibody epitope classes revealed that the majority of escape sites are situated within regions commonly recognized by Class 1/2 antibody-recognized regions that typically overlap with the RBM and the ACE2 binding interface (Figure 4(c and f)), particularly at positions 420, 433–443, 456–474, and 493, which are spatially proximal within the folded RBD. This suggests that BD55-1205 primarily recognizes a relatively narrow epitope that overlaps with the ACE2 binding interface. Furthermore, several low-level escape mutations (escape score (relative values) < 0.25) also accumulated within the 445–455 and 498–505 regions. In contrast, regions distant from the RBM exhibited only sparse and low-magnitude escape signals (escape score (relative values) < 0.1), consistent with stronger structural or functional constraints.

In both lineages, the 719-14 sIgA antibody exhibited an escape pattern primarily centred around residues 346 and 493, with the strongest signal intensity at position 346, while position 493 consistently maintained the second highest signal intensity (Figure 5(a and d)). Multiple escape sites were observed in the 433–443 and 456–476 regions in both lineage variants. The low-value magnification panel (escape score < 0.1) showed that both lineages exhibited a continuous distribution of low-amplitude escape signals (escape score < 0.1) within the 445–481 segment. However, subtle differences were observed in the low-level escape patterns (escape score < 0.1) within the 395–430 region: in JN.1, low-level signals were mainly concentrated at position 414, whereas in XEC they peaked at position 423.

Under the selective pressure of the 719-14 sIgA antibody, the relative contributions of mutated amino acids to immune escape showed generally similar distribution characteristics in both the JN.1 and XEC lineages, but there were distinguishable differences in the relative intensity at some key sites (Figure 5(b and e)). In both lineages, the 346S mutation constituted the most largest escape hotspot, while 443E and 493K also showed prominent escape contributions (escape score (relative values) > 0.5) second only to 346S, with a noticeable difference in the relative escape intensity of 493K between JN.1 and XEC. In the 465–478 region, multiple sites in both lineages showed escape effects driven by single amino acid substitutions, with 470E, 471T, 474G, and 476E all exhibiting moderate-intensity (escape scores are around 0.5) escape signals. Furthermore, the 433–443 and 456–464 segments constituted relatively concentrated escape regions in both lineages, but the escape contributions of sites such as 434L, 437A, 457L, and 464S were inconsistent between the two lineages. In contrast, the amino acid mutations detected in the 379–430 and 510–538 regions showed overall lower escape effects (escape score (relative values) < 0.1), and the types of amino acid substitutions were not entirely consistent between the two lineages.

Mapping the escape sites of JN.1 and XEC RBD against 719-14 sIgA onto the RBD structure showed that, similar to BD55-1205, many of the major escape hotspots (escape score > 0.1) for 719-14 sIgA are located within the canonical Class 1/2 regions. However, its escape profile extends further to site 346, a site corresponding to the Class 3 epitope region (Figure 5(c and f)). Specifically, beyond the strongest and second strongest escape sites at 346 and 493, multiple escape sites cluster within the 433–443 and 456–476 regions. These constitute a relatively broad conformational epitope spanning both the RBM and its peripheral areas. Conversely, the structurally conserved and constrained core β-sheet region of the RBD exhibits virtually no strong escape mutations.

In order to validate the DMS results for BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA, we cloned three sets of representative escape mutations into the JN.1 and XEC variant, respectively, and performed standard pseudovirus neutralization assays for each mutant (Figure S6). In the presence of BD55-1205, mutants carrying D420Y, R457L, or Q493K showed a rightward shift of the neutralization curves, such that at the same antibody concentrations they failed to reach neutralization levels comparable to the corresponding JN.1 or XEC. In the presence of 719-14 sIgA, mutants carrying T346S, R457L, or Q493K likewise exhibited reduced neutralizing efficacy, with some mutants failing to achieve complete neutralization even at the highest antibody concentration. Overall, these validation neutralization results are highly consistent with the escape effects predicted by DMS.

Discussion

This study constructed a DMS platform for the RBD of JN.1 and XEC variants based on a non-replicating two-step pseudovirus system. We systematically evaluated the impact of RBD mutations on pseudovirus infection ability in antibody-free conditions, as well as the escape profile under pressure from two representative neutralizing monoclonal antibody BD55-1205 and mucosal monoclonal antibody 719-14 sIgA. Our results demonstrate that the DMS for the RBD of both variants can simultaneously resolve two key fitness related phenotypes, pseudovirus infection and antibody neutralization escape, within a single experimental framework. This provides an experimental framework for understanding the functional characteristics of JN.1 and XEC at the RBD and their adaptive changes under existing antibody pressure, significantly improving the ability to interpret the functional impact of newly emerging RBD mutations compared to relying solely on sequence monitoring.

In the absence of baseline correction, the functional scores exhibited an overall negative shift, which mainly results from the baseline bias introduced by using VSV-G pseudovirus as a reference. Because VSV-G-mediated entry efficiency is substantially higher than that mediated by spike, even synonymous mutations appear as negative functional scores in this system. This indicates that introducing an internal neutral reference based on synonymous mutations is necessary to eliminate systematic bias and improve the accuracy of functional interpretation. After correction using the median of synonymous mutations as the neutral baseline, the DMS results showed that the functional scores of most RBD mutations fall below the neutral level, indicating that single amino acid substitutions generally reduce spike-mediated entry efficiency, and only a part of mutations are functionally retaining at near-neutral or slightly above-neutral levels. This overall distribution suggests that the RBD of JN.1 and XEC are already in a relatively optimized functional state, with limited tolerance to further amino acid changes.

Although mutations with near-neutral functional scores are distributed across the entire RBD, such apparent neutrality may in part be influenced by experimental noise and sequencing errors. Therefore, interpretation of these functional scores should consider both their spatial distribution patterns and signal intensity. Nevertheless, from a structural perspective, the distribution of functional score is consistent with the conformational features of the RBD. The core β-sheet region, which serves as a key structural scaffold maintaining overall folding stability and the ACE2-binding conformation, imposes structural constraints on amino acid substitutions. Although mutations in this region are broadly distributed and may appear functionally retaining at near-neutral levels, their overall signal intensity is relatively weak. This suggests that the region is not completely intolerant to mutations but permits only low-magnitude functional perturbations, while being unable to support stable functionally retaining or enhanced effects. In contrast, although the RBM region does not exhibit as broad a distribution of near-neutral functionally retaining mutations as the core β-sheet region, the signals of functionally retaining mutations within the RBM are more concentrated and of higher magnitude, with some sites showing clearly positive effects above the neutral level. This is consistent with the higher conformational plasticity of the RBM, which allows it to accommodate a certain degree of amino acid variation.

In addition, some variants were present at extremely low abundance or even undetectable in sequencing libraries following cellular infection. This may be explained by two main factors: first, certain amino acid substitutions may severely impair spike-mediated entry efficiency. Second, during pooled library pseudovirus infection, variants with higher entry competence compete for limited target cells, leading to depletion of functionally impaired variants below the detection limit. Furthermore, technical factors during library construction and sequencing may also contribute to the underrepresentation of certain variants in the initial library. Consistent with previous DMS and structural studies, our results show that most single amino acid substitutions in the RBD of JN.1 and XEC are subject to functional constraints, indicating that the functional sequence space of RBD across different lineages is highly restricted [7,32–35]. Because single-site mutational landscapes cannot capture epistatic interactions, substitutions that are deleterious on their own may be compensated by additional mutations during natural evolution [8,36]. Therefore, the limited functionally retaining entry capacity observed for spike single amino acid substitutions suggests that meaningful functional optimization is more likely driven by cooperative effects of multiple mutations rather than by individual substitutions alone.

Most antibodies lost activity against the JN.1 and XEC, whereas BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA retained neutralizing activity, suggesting that under the selective pressure imposed by these two antibodies, it is not easy for the virus to achieve effective escape through RBD mutations alone. This indicates that both antibodies possess structural features that confer resistance to antigenic drift, although the underlying mechanisms are not identical. BD55-1205 is a broadly neutralizing antibody targeting a Class 1 epitope. Its binding interface substantially overlaps with the ACE2-binding site, allowing it to recognize the RBD in a receptor-like manner rather than relying primarily on highly variable surface side chains. As a result, viral escape through mutations at key residues often requires a concurrent reduction in ACE2 binding, this mechanism confers upon the antibody a high barrier to viral escape and sustained broad-spectrum activity [5]. In contrast, the persistent activity of 719-14 sIgA is more likely attributable to its broader epitope coverage and the intrinsic structural advantages of secretory IgA. Previous studies have shown that 719-14 competes with both Class 2 and Class 3 antibodies, suggesting that its recognition pattern is not restricted to a single narrow epitope but instead spans more complex and cross-class conformational regions of the RBD. In addition, 719-14 exists in a polymeric secretory IgA form, which confers higher avidity and multivalent binding advantages compared with its monomeric IgG or monomeric IgA. Therefore, even when some contact residues on the viral RBD are altered, it may still maintain effective binding and neutralization [10]. Together, these findings suggest that the persistent activity of BD55-1205 is mainly associated with the high escape cost imposed by its ACE2 mimicry, whereas the persistent activity of 719-14 sIgA is more likely derived from its broader epitope coverage and the multivalent structural advantages of sIgA.

Consistent with this structural basis, the viral RBD escape maps generated under BD55-1205 and 719-14 sIgA selection pressure showed that high-escape sites (escape score > 0.1) for both antibodies were mainly enriched in regions commonly recognized by Class 1 and Class 2 antibodies. These regions usually lie within or overlap the RBM and strongly overlap with the ACE2-binding interface, indicating that both antibodies are closely associated with ACE2-competitive epitopes. For each antibody, the major escape hotspots (escape score > 0.1) were largely conserved between the JN.1 and XEC, suggesting that the key epitope recognition features remain highly consistent across these two closely related sublineages. Comparing the escape profiles corresponding to the two antibodies, the major viral escape hotspots (escape score > 0.1) were also broadly similar, indicating substantial overlap in the viral escape pathways associated with IgA and IgG under the current antigenic background. However, several representative differences in escape sites exist between the two selective pressures: under BD55-1205 pressure, the virus exhibited a more prominent escape peak (escape score approximately 1.0) at residue 420, whereas under 719-14 sIgA pressure, the highest escape peak emerged at residue 346. Because residue 346 corresponds to a reported Class 3 epitope region, this finding suggests that the recognition range of 719-14 sIgA may extend beyond the typical RBM-associated epitopes into peripheral conformational regions, implying that mucosal IgA may correspond to a more dispersed and more strongly conformation-dependent viral escape pattern than systemic IgG [10].

Further analysis showed that the vast majority of high-escape sites were located at or near the ACE2-binding interface, indicating that under current immune pressure, escape prone mutations are concentrated in key exposed regions involved in both antibody recognition and receptor binding. Because these residues also contribute to ACE2 binding, amino acid substitutions that weaken antibody binding often also reduce receptor recognition efficiency to varying degrees. This explains why escape mutations in our study were accompanied by reduced entry competence, reflecting a clear evolutionary trade-off between immune escape and the maintenance of receptor-binding capacity, consistent with previous large-scale DMS studies [6,7]. Meanwhile, the major escape hotspots (escape score > 0.1) were mainly located in the structurally flexible outer loops of the RBM and adjacent regions, overlapping with immunodominant regions targeted by multiple human monoclonal antibodies and polyclonal sera [8,37–42]. In contrast, the core β-sheet region of the RBD, including part of the region commonly recognized by Class 4 antibodies, rarely harboured high-level escape mutations (escape score > 0.1). This indicates that these regions are less permissive to escape substitutions because they are subject to stronger structural and functional constraints required to maintain RBD folding stability and the ACE2-binding conformation. This result is also consistent with previous DMS analyses [6,7]. Notably, some of the high-escape sites (escape score > 0.1) identified in this study have already appeared during the real-world circulation of JN.1-derived variants and show features of convergent evolution. Recent studies have shown that JN.1 descendant lineages, such as KP.2, KP.3, and KP.3.1.1, have accumulated additional spike mutations associated with immune escape, such as R346T, F456L, and Q493E, during their transmission. Among these, R346T and F456L have been demonstrated to independently reduce the neutralizing titres of breakthrough infection sera, and their combined presence can further enhance immune escape. Antigenic cartography has shown that these variants are clearly separated from JN.1, suggesting further antigenic evolution [43]. Significantly, residues 346, 456, and 493 were all key escape sites (escape score > 0.1) in the monoclonal antibody escape maps generated in this study. Residue 346 formed a prominent escape hotspot under 719-14 sIgA pressure, residue 456 and its neighbouring region showed continuous escape signals in the serum escape maps, and residue 493 represented a strong escape site with both antibody pressures. It should be noted that the DMS results in this study were obtained under defined monoclonal antibody pressure and therefore do not directly correspond to the specific amino acid substitutions that are ultimately selected and fixed during natural transmission. The emergence of a specific substitution in the real world is usually shaped by multiple factors, including ACE2-binding capacity, viral replication fitness, epistatic interactions within the genetic background, and the immune landscape of the population. Nevertheless, the consistency between the escape sites identified by DMS and the recurrently mutated sites observed in subsequent circulating variants suggests that these sites or regions may be more prone to mutation under population immune pressure and may contribute to the antigenic evolution of JN.1 and its descendant lineages. On the other hand, although XEC does not contain additional RBD mutations compared with JN.1, it carries two additional mutations, T22N and F59S, in the N-terminal domain (NTD). Despite the absence of further RBD changes, previous studies have suggested that these NTD-associated alterations may indirectly reduce the neutralizing activity of multiple classes of RBD-targeting antibodies, especially ACE2-competitive antibodies, by affecting spike conformational dynamics or through allosteric effects, rather than merely altering NTD antigenicity itself [4]. This suggests that future antigenic evolution of SARS-CoV-2 may not be limited to RBD mutations, but may also involve coordinated changes between the RBD and NTD to further optimize immune escape and transmission fitness.

This study has several limitations. First, the DMS based on pseudovirus infection only reflects changes in genotype frequency as a measure of spike-mediated entry and escape. It does not capture other key fitness phenotypes, such as replication efficiency, tropism, or transmissibility. Therefore, the functional scores in this study primarily characterize the effects of RBD mutations on viral entry and immune escape, and do not represent full replication of live virus or in vivo infectivity and immune evasion. Second, we only scanned mutations in the RBD of JN.1 and XEC spikes, omitting other domains like NTD and S2. Therefore, we could not assess fitness changes involving full-spike conformation, intracellular signalling, or non-structural proteins. Third, as we tested single amino acid substitutions, the results only show direct effects in a given background. Natural evolution often involves multi-site mutations and epistasis, so some detrimental single substitutions could be compensated or become beneficial in combination. Hence, our map should be seen as defining single-site acceptability, not predicting all combinatorial mutation pathways. Fourth, co-infection with mixed pseudovirus libraries may introduce competition biases. Without antibody pressure, more efficient variants may be enriched, while mildly impaired ones may be underrepresented. Additionally, bottlenecks in sequencing depth, and library synthesis may also cause some mutations to fall below detection, preventing reliable scoring. This could narrow the observed effect distribution and limit assessment of extreme or rare mutations. Therefore, at the technical level, bias can be further reduced, and uniformity enhanced by optimizing library construction strategies, such as improving oligonucleotide synthesis and amplification conditions. Fifth, although we discussed the circulation of some immune evasion associated mutations in JN.1 and its descendant lineages by published studies and publicly available surveillance information, the present study primarily focuses on this work was to establish a DMS platform for the JN.1 and XEC variants and to characterize their antibody escape and spike-mediated entry competence profiles. Therefore, the systematic dedicated integration with real-world epidemiological surveillance data is beyond the scope of the current work. Nevertheless, in our follow-up work, we have initiated comparative analyses between the escape sites identified by DMS and mutations observed in real-world circulating variants using SARS-CoV-2 surveillance data collected during 2024–2025. This work is currently ongoing.

Overall, our pseudovirus DMS platform closely reflects the impact of RBD mutations on viral function during spike-mediated cell entry, especially showing advantages in polyclonal or complex immune environments. This DMS study on JN.1 and XEC RBD identify a series of key escape mutation sites under representative monoclonal antibody pressure, and offers guidance for the antigenicity assessment of emerging variants, vaccine strain selection, and therapeutic antibody optimization.
