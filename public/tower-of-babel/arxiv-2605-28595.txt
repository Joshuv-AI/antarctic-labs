Abstract
. The twisted Alexander polynomials of a space, associated to a linear representation σ \sigma of the fundamental group, are non-abelian refinements of the classical Alexander polynomial from knot theory. In this paper, we show that they arise naturally from a new family of invariants—the twisted homology jump loci—which extend the rank-one characteristic varieties to higher-rank local systems. Using the tropical geometry of these twisted loci, we obtain sharper upper bounds for the Bieri–Neumann–Strebel–Renz (BNSR) Σ \Sigma -invariants. For compact orientable 3 3 -manifolds with toroidal or empty boundary, we use a theorem of Friedl–Vidussi to show that the closure of the union of these twisted tropical bound is sharp: it recovers the fibered faces of the Thurston norm ball exactly, a result that fails without twisting. For compact Kähler manifolds, we prove that the Σ 1 \Sigma^{1} -invariant of π 1 ​ ( X ) \pi_{1}(X) is controlled by the orbifold fibrations of X X for any representation σ \sigma , and that the twisted Alexander polynomial Δ σ ​ ( X ) \Delta^{\sigma}(X) must equal 0 0 or 1 1 . Both results provide obstructions to geometric realizability that are strictly stronger than their classical untwisted counterparts.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
BNSR invariants

A landmark group-theoretic invariant was introduced in 1987 by Bieri, Neumann,
and Strebel
[
6
]
—now known as the BNS invariant. This invariant generalizes
an earlier notion developed by Bieri and Strebel
[
8
,
9
]
for metabelian groups.
Subsequently, Bieri and Renz
[
7
]
extended the invariant to higher degrees, while
Farber, Geoghegan, and Schütz
[
20
]
adapted it from groups to topological spaces.
These extensions are collectively referred to as the Bieri–Neumann–Strebel–Renz (BNSR)
invariants, also known as
\Sigma
-invariants, which capture geometric finiteness properties
of groups and spaces.

Computing the BNSR invariants is notoriously challenging. Even in degree one, explicit descriptions
exist only for limited families of groups, including metabelian groups
[
8
,
9
,
5
]
,
one‑relator groups
[
10
]
, right‑angled Artin groups
[
28
]
, Kähler groups
[
15
]
,
and pure braid groups
[
25
]
. To address this difficulty, Papadima–Suciu

[
30
]
and Suciu
[
36
]
initiated a program to construct
upper bounds for the BNSR invariants via homology jump loci, which are more
computationally accessible.

1.2.
Twisted homology jump loci and twisted Alexander polynomial

The homology jump loci of a CW complex are defined using homology with coefficients in
rank‑one local systems. These loci naturally extend the notion of singular homology by
capturing how the homology varies as the local system ranges over a character variety.
Moreover, they are intimately related to Alexander‑type invariants, most notably the
Alexander polynomial from knot theory.

Papadima and Suciu
[
30
]
first obtained an upper bound for the
BNSR invariants by means of the exponential tangent cones of the homology jump loci.
Subsequently, Suciu
[
36
]
refined this bound using the
tropical variety associated to the homology jump loci with complex coefficients. A crucial
advantage of the tropical approach is that the tropical variety detects positive‑dimensional
translated components of these jump loci that do not pass through the origin, whereas the
exponential tangent cone only captures the analytic germ at the origin of the jump loci.
More recently, Liu and Liu
[
26
]
further improved the bound by
employing the tropical variety associated to the homology jump loci with coefficients in
arbitrary fields as well as with integer coefficients. For metabelian groups, this bound
at degree one coincides with the bound obtained by Bieri, Groves, and Strebel
[
8
,
9
,
5
]

and is therefore sharp. In general, however, these bounds are not sharp.

In knot theory, when the Alexander polynomial fails to resolve a given problem, a common
strategy is to consider non‑abelian invariants obtained by twisting with a linear representation
of the fundamental group—these are known as twisted Alexander polynomials. Inspired by
this idea, we introduce a twisted version of the homology jump loci, which generalizes the
twisted Alexander polynomials of knots. Using these twisted jump loci, we obtain sharper
tropical upper bounds for the BNSR invariants. Conversely, for spaces or groups whose
BNSR invariants are already understood—such as Kähler groups—this relationship
allows us to derive non‑trivial constraints on their twisted jump loci and twisted Alexander
polynomials.

1.3.
Main results

Let
X
be a connected finite CW-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
In this paper,
we always assume that
\dim H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})>0
.
Let
\mathrm{S}(G)
denote the unit sphere in
\Hom(G;\mathbb{R})\cong H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
,
and for a field
\Bbbk
with a possibly trivial valuation
\upsilon
,
let
\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))\subset H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})

denote the tropicalization of the
q
-th twisted homology jump loci
associated to a representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)

(see Section
2
for precise definitions).

The central result of the paper is the following tropical bound,
which strengthens the untwisted bounds of
[
36
,
26
]
by
allowing the local system to range over representations of any rank.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
5.5
)
.

Let
X
be a connected finite CW-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Fix a field
\Bbbk
with a rational valuation

\upsilon
(i.e.,
\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
), possibly trivial.
Let
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
be a representation whose matrix
entries have valuations uniformly bounded below and whose determinant
satisfies
\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
for all
g\in G
;
let
L_{\sigma}
denote the corresponding local system on
X
.
Then

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\big(\!\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))\big)^{\mathrm{c}}.

An explicit example where the twisted bound is strictly sharper than the
untwisted one is given in Example
5.8
.

The key new ingredient in the proof is a module-theoretic observation
(Lemma
5.1
): the completed tensor product
\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
,
associated to the twisted representation
\sigma\otimes\rho
,
carries a natural left module structure over the Novikov–Sikorav
completion
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}G}_{-\chi}
, provided a uniform lower bound
on the valuations of the matrix entries of
\sigma
holds and

\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
for all
g\in G
.
This
valuation bound
(
16
) is what allows
higher-rank local systems to enter the Novikov–Sikorav framework.
Lemma
5.3
supplies concrete conditions under
which this hypothesis is satisfied, including the cases when
\sigma

has finite image or is defined over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Once this module structure is in place, the proof of
Theorem
1.1
proceeds along the
lines of
[
30
, Thm. 10.1]
and
[
36
, Thm. 6.4]
,
via a Künneth spectral sequence argument.

Corollary 1.2

(Corollary
5.6
)
.

Let
X
be a connected finite CW-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
For any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\bigl(\!\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\bigr)\bigr)^{\mathrm{c}}.

Here
\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})
denotes the twisted homology jump ideal over

\mathbb{Z}
(see Section
2
). Integrality of the matrix entries
of
\sigma
automatically satisfies the valuation hypothesis of
Theorem
1.1
for all fields
simultaneously, so the
\mathbb{Z}
-tropical variety encodes all

1.4.
Applications

The main theorem has two geometric applications: to compact Kähler
manifolds (Section
6
) and to compact orientable

3
-manifolds (Section
7
). In both settings,
the twisted tropical bound yields results inaccessible to
the untwisted theory.

Kähler groups

A finitely presented group
G
is called a
Kähler group

if
G\cong\pi_{1}(X)
for some compact Kähler manifold
X
.
For Kähler groups, the BNS invariant
\Sigma^{1}(G)
is completely
described by Delzant’s theorem
[
15
]
in terms of hyperbolic
orbifold fibrations of
X
. Combining Theorem
1.1

with Delzant’s theorem yields the following result, proved in Section
6
.

Corollary 1.3

(Corollary
6.7
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
For any algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
with trivial valuation
and any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
,

\Sigma^{1}(G;\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\Bigl(\Trop_{\Bbbk}\bigl(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})\bigr)\Bigr)^{\!\mathrm{c}},

or equivalently,
\Trop_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma}))
is contained in the
union of the images
\im(f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R}))
over
all hyperbolic orbifold fibrations
f\colon X\to C_{g}
.

This strengthens results of Suciu
[
36
]
and
Liu–Liu
[
26
]
, which treated the case
\sigma=\id
.
A further application of Theorem
1.1

via Fox calculus on orbifold fundamental groups
gives the following new obstruction to Kählerianity.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem
6.8
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Then for any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)

over any field
\Bbbk
, the first twisted Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{\sigma}(X)
is either
0
or
1
.

This strengthens
[
17
, Thm. 4.3(3)]
, which
established the same dichotomy for the
untwisted

Alexander polynomial
\Delta(G)
(i.e.,
\sigma=\id
,
r=1
).
The improvement is genuine: Example
6.13

exhibits groups
G
for which
\Delta(G)=1
but
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)\neq 1

for a suitable finite-image representation
\sigma
, so that
Theorem
1.4
detects non-Kählerianity
where the untwisted result does not.

3
-manifold groups

For a compact, connected, orientable
3
-manifold
M
with
toroidal or empty boundary, the BNS invariant
\Sigma^{1}(\pi_{1}(M))

is the projection of the open fibered faces of the Thurston norm
ball
[
6
, Thm. E]
. Using a theorem of Friedl–Vidussi
[
22
]
,
which ensures that every non-fibered class is detected by the
twisted Alexander polynomial for some finite-image representation,
we show in Section
7
that the closure of the union of all finite-image twisted tropical bounds
recovers
\Sigma^{1}
exactly.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorem
7.2
)
.

Let
M
be a compact, connected, orientable
3
-manifold with
toroidal or empty boundary, and set
G=\pi_{1}(M)
. Then

\Sigma^{1}(G)=\mathrm{S}\Bigg(\overline{\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)}\Bigg)^{\mathrm{c}},

where the union runs over all representations

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
with finite image.

This shows that the inclusion of
Theorem
1.1

could deduce an equality for
3
-manifold groups, a sharpness result
that fails for the untwisted bound: untwisted characteristic
varieties suffice only for fibered classes, while twisted
local systems from finite covers are essential for detecting
non-fibered ones.

Further directions

The
3
-manifold result naturally raises the question of which
other spaces admit a sharp twisted tropical description of
their BNS invariants.

Question 1.6
.

For which spaces
X
and degrees
q\geq 1
do we have

(1)

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{S}\bigg(\overline{\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}\big(\mathcal{J}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\big)}\bigg)^{\mathrm{c}},

where
\sigma
runs over all representations

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
?
In particular, does (
1
) hold when

?

The inclusion
\supseteq
holds in general by

1.2
and the fact that
\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is open, and
Theorem
1.5
gives an affirmative
answer for
3
-manifold groups with

. For right-angled
Artin groups and nilmanifolds, equality already holds with the
trivial representation alone
[

]
, so no twisting is
needed in those cases. Whether infinite-image integral

.

2.1.
Twisted homology jump loci

Let
X
be a connected, finite-type CW-complex, with fundamental
group
\pi_{1}(X)=G
. Fix a field
\Bbbk
, and let
\Hom(G,\GL_{r}(\Bbbk))
be
the
\Bbbk
-algebraic variety of rank
r
representations of
G
over
\Bbbk
.
Each representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
corresponds to a rank
r

local system
L_{\sigma}
on
X
. The degree
i
, rank
r
homology jump
loci of
X
(over
\Bbbk
) are the subvarieties

(2)

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)[r]=\big\{\sigma\in\Hom(G,\GL_{r}(\Bbbk))\mid H_{i}(X,L_{\sigma})\neq 0\big\}\,.

In particular, the rank-one jump loci
\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)\coloneqq\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)[1]

are subvarieties of
\Hom(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
, the commutative,

\Bbbk
-algebraic group of
\Bbbk
-valued, multiplicative characters of
G
.
If
\Bbbk\subset\mathbb{K}
is a field extension, then
\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)[r]
are the

\Bbbk
-points on
\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\mathbb{K})[r]
, while
\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\mathbb{K})[r]
is obtained from

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)[r]
by extension of the base field.

Now fix a local system
L_{\sigma}
on
X
over
\Bbbk
of rank
r\geq 1
.
Observe that if
\rho\colon G\to\Bbbk^{\times}
is a character, then the
local system
L_{\sigma}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho}
corresponds to
the representation
\sigma\otimes\rho\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
that scales each
matrix
\sigma(g)
by the scalar
\rho(g)
. This defines an action of the
character group
\Hom(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
on the representation variety

\Hom(G,\GL_{r}(\Bbbk))
. We are interested in the orbit of
L_{\sigma}

for this action, in particular, its intersection with the subvariety

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)[r]
defined in (
2
). The
twisted
homology jump loci
of
X
with coefficients in
a local system
L_{\sigma}
(in degree
i
) are the jump loci for
homology with coefficients in rank-one local systems, twisted by
L_{\sigma}
,

(3)

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{\sigma})=\big\{\rho\in\Hom(G,\Bbbk^{\times})\mid H_{i}(X;L_{\sigma}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho})\neq 0\big\}\,.

We will denote by
\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})=\bigcup_{i\leq q}\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{\sigma})

the union of the jump loci in degrees up to
q
.

Note that when
L_{\sigma}
is the constant
\Bbbk
-sheaf,

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{\sigma})=\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,\Bbbk)
.
Moreover, if
L_{\sigma}
is a rank-one local system, then

\sigma
is a character in
\Hom(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
. Hence

L_{\sigma}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho}=L_{\sigma\cdot\rho}
, and so

(4)

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{\sigma\cdot\rho})=\rho^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{\sigma}).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two connected, finite-type CW-complexes.
Consider two local systems
L_{\sigma_{1}}
and
L_{\sigma_{2}}
on
X_{1}

and
X_{2}
both over a field
\Bbbk
, respectively. Then we have

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X_{1}\times X_{2},L_{\sigma_{1}}\boxtimes L_{\sigma_{2}})=\bigcup_{s+t=i}\mathcal{V}^{s}(X_{1},L_{\sigma_{1}})\times\mathcal{V}^{t}(X_{2},L_{\sigma_{2}}).

Here
L_{\sigma_{1}}\boxtimes L_{\sigma_{2}}=p_{1}^{-1}L_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes p_{2}^{-1}L_{\sigma_{2}}
with
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
being the projection
maps from
X_{1}\times X_{2}
to
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

Given a character
\rho=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\in\Hom(\pi_{1}(X_{1}\times X_{2}),\Bbbk^{\times})=\Hom(\pi_{1}(X_{1}),\Bbbk^{\times})\times\Hom(\pi_{1}(X_{2}),\Bbbk^{\times})
, by the Künneth formula
(the
\Tor

\Bbbk
is a field), we have

H_{i}(X_{1}\times X_{2},(L_{\sigma_{1}}\boxtimes L_{\sigma_{2}})\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho})\cong\bigoplus_{s+t=i}H_{s}(X_{1},L_{\sigma_{1}}\otimes L_{\rho_{1}})\otimes_{\Bbbk}H_{t}(X_{2},L_{\sigma_{2}}\otimes L_{\rho_{2}}).

The claim follows.

Equations for twisted homology jump loci

), namely,
the formula relating free rank to generic fiber dimension.
(Although
[
17
]
uses the Fox-calculus formula for the Alexander
polynomial, the proof there relies only on the identification of the
characteristic variety with the support of
H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;\Bbbk[A])

and the fact that the zero locus of
\Delta^{\phi,\sigma}(X)

equals the union of codimension one components of the support of
H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;\Bbbk[A])

which holds equally for Definition
2.2

It remains to prove that the free rank of
H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;\Bbbk[A])

equals
m
if and only if
\dim_{\Bbbk}H_{1}(X,L_{\sigma}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\phi^{*}L_{\lambda})=m

for generic
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.
When
\rank A=1
, the ring
\Bbbk[A]\cong\Bbbk[t^{\pm 1}]
is a PID,
so the chain complex
C_{*}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;\Bbbk)
decomposes up to
chain homotopy as a direct sum of complexes of the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Bbbk[t^{\pm 1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Bbbk[t^{\pm 1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

or

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Bbbk[t^{\pm 1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

with

. The free rank of

equals
the number

of summands of the second type. Specializing at

(i.e., applying

),
each second-type summand contributes

Since there are finitely many first-type summands,

∎

We now describe a twisted analogue of the classical Crowell exact
sequence from Fox calculus.

Lemma 2.4
.

For a finitely presented group
G=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mid r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\rangle

and a representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(R)
, the boundary map

\partial_{2}^{\sigma}
of the twisted chain complex fits into a short exact sequence

(8)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;R[A])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{\phi}^{\sigma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\im(\partial_{1}^{\sigma})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}

where
A_{\phi}^{\sigma}\coloneqq\coker(\partial_{2}^{\sigma})
is the

twisted Alexander module
.

Proof.

Both

and
A_{\phi}^{\sigma}=C_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}/\im(\partial_{2}^{\sigma})
are quotients
of
C_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}
by
\im(\partial_{2}^{\sigma})
. The natural inclusion

H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}\hookrightarrow A_{\phi}^{\sigma}
is therefore well-defined,
with cokernel
C_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}/\ker(\partial_{1}^{\sigma})\cong\im(\partial_{1}^{\sigma})

∎

Remark 2.5
.

By the analogue of
[
17
, Prop. 2.2]
for

,

V\!\left(\Fitt_{0}\bigl(H_{1}^{\phi,\sigma}(X;R[A])\bigr)\right)=V\bigl(\Fitt_{r}(A_{\phi}^{\sigma})\bigr)

outside finitely many characters in

, namely,
the support of

, which in the

The minors of size

Hence the polynomial

2.4.
Finite covers and twisted jump loci

Finite-index subgroups
N\leq G
yield finite-rank local systems
defined over
\mathbb{Z}
: the action of
G
on the left cosets of
N

gives a representation
G\to S_{r}
, where
r=[G\colon N]
.

The most geometric instance is a connected finite cover

\pi\colon Y\to X
, with
G=\pi_{1}(X)
,
N=\pi_{1}(Y)
, and
r=[G\colon N]
.
The pushforward
L_{\pi}\coloneqq\pi_{*}R_{Y}
is a rank-
r

R
-local system
on
X
induced by this coset action. When
N\trianglelefteq G
—equivalently,
when
Y
is a regular cover—
L_{\pi}
is associated to the regular
representation of
G/N
.

The next proposition records a formula for computing the homology
groups
H_{i}(Y,\Bbbk)
from the twisted homology jump loci.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\pi\colon Y\to X
be a connected finite regular cover of a connected
finite CW-complex
X
. Set
\pi_{1}(Y)=N
and
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Assume that
\ch(\Bbbk)\nmid r=|G/N|
and
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed.
Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
be the irreducible
\Bbbk
-representations of the
finite group
G/N
, with
L_{1}
the trivial representation and
s
the
number of conjugacy classes of
G/N
.

(1)

For all
i\geq 0
, there is a decomposition

(9)

\displaystyle H_{i}(Y;\Bbbk)

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{1\leq j\leq s}H_{i}(X;L_{j})^{\oplus\rank L_{j}}

\displaystyle\cong H_{i}(X;\Bbbk)\oplus\bigoplus_{2\leq j\leq s}H_{i}(X;L_{j})^{\oplus\rank L_{j}}.

(2)

The homomorphism
\pi^{*}\colon\Hom(\pi_{1}(X),\Bbbk^{\times})\to\Hom(\pi_{1}(Y),\Bbbk^{\times})
satisfies

(10)

(\pi^{*})^{-1}\big(\mathcal{V}^{i}(Y,\Bbbk)\big)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{j})

for all
i\geq 0
. That is, a character

\rho\in\Hom(\pi_{1}(X),\Bbbk^{\times})
has the property that

\pi^{*}\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{i}(Y,\Bbbk)
if and only if

\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{j})
for some
1\leq j\leq s
.

Proof.

Since
\ch(\Bbbk)\nmid r=|G/N|
, by Maschke’s theorem,

L_{\pi}
is a semi-simple local system.
Moreover, since
L_{\pi}
corresponds to the regular representation
of the finite group
G/N
, we have
L_{\pi}\cong\bigoplus_{1\leq j\leq s}L_{j}^{\oplus\rank L_{j}}
by the
decomposition theorem for regular representations of
finite groups. Part (
1
) follows.

For part (
2
), let
L_{\rho}
be any rank-one

\Bbbk
-local system on
X
. By the projection formula,

(11)

H_{i}(Y;\pi^{*}L_{\rho})\cong H_{i}(X;L_{\pi}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho})\cong\bigoplus_{1\leq j\leq s}H_{i}(X;L_{j}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho})^{\oplus\rank L_{j}}.

Hence
\pi^{*}\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{i}(Y,\Bbbk)
, i.e.,

H_{i}(Y;\pi^{*}L_{\rho})\neq 0
, if and only if

H_{i}(X;L_{j}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho})\neq 0
for some
j
,
which is equivalent to
\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L_{j})

for some
1\leq j\leq s
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Call two irreducible representations
L
and
L^{\prime}
of
G/N

equivalent
if
L\cong L^{\prime}\otimes_{\Bbbk}L_{\rho}
for some
rank-one representation
L_{\rho}
of
G/N
; in which case

\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L)=\rho^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{V}^{i}(X,L^{\prime})
.
Since
\Bbbk^{\times}
is abelian, every rank-one representation of

G/N
factors through
\ab(G/N)
, so the equivalence classes
are orbits of
\Hom(\ab(G/N),\Bbbk^{\times})
acting on the
irreducible representations of
G/N
.

3.
Tropical varieties

In this section, we focus on tropical geometry, where the base ring
R

is a field
\Bbbk
or
\mathbb{Z}
. The reader may refer to the monograph
[
27
]

for the required background on tropical geometry.

3.1.
Tropical variety over a valued field

Let
\Bbbk
be a fixed field endowed with a possibly trivial valuation

\upsilon\colon\Bbbk^{\times}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\upsilon(0)=\infty
.
Consider the group
H=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Fixing a basis, we may identify
the group ring
\Bbbk H
with the Laurent polynomial ring

\Bbbk[x_{1}^{{\pm 1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{{\pm 1}}]
.

Definition 3.1
.

For an ideal
I\subseteq\Bbbk[x_{1}^{{\pm 1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{{\pm 1}}]
,
we recall the following two ways to define the
tropical variety of
I
.

(1)

Fix
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any nonzero polynomial

f=\sum\limits_{\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}a_{\textbf{u}}x^{\textbf{u}}\in\Bbbk[x_{1}^{{\pm 1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{{\pm 1}}]
, the initial form

\init_{\textbf{w},\upsilon}(f)
is the sum of all terms in
f

where

\min\limits_{{\textbf{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left\{\upsilon(a_{{\bf{u}}})+{\bf{u}}\cdot{\textbf{w}}\mid a_{\textbf{u}}\neq 0\right\}

is achieved. The initial
ideal
\init_{\textbf{w},\upsilon}(I)
is the ideal generated by

\init_{\textbf{w},\upsilon}(f)
, where
f
runs over
I
. Set

\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)\coloneqq\{\textbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\init_{\textbf{w},\upsilon}(I)\neq\Bbbk[x_{1}^{{\pm 1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{{\pm 1}}]\}.

(2)

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field extending
\Bbbk

such that the extension of
\upsilon
to
\mathbb{K}
is nontrivial, still denoted

\upsilon
. Such a field always exists and the specific choice of
\mathbb{K}
is
not important, as long as it is algebraically closed with a nontrivial
valuation (see
[
27
, Thm. 3.2.4 and Rem. 3.2.5]
).
Denote by
V(I)\subset(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}
the zero locus of
I\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathbb{K}
.
The tropical variety of
I
is then defined as

\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)\coloneqq\overline{\{(\upsilon(x_{1}),\ldots,\upsilon(x_{n}))\mid(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in V(I)\}},

the closure in the Euclidean topology of the image of
V(I)

under the coordinatewise valuation map
(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The fundamental theorem of tropical algebraic geometry in

[
27
, Thm. 3.2.3]
shows the equivalence of the above two definitions.
Moreover,
\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)
only depends on
\sqrt{I}
.
If
\sqrt{I}=\bigcap\limits_{j=1}^{q}P_{j}
, where
\{P_{j}\}_{j=1}^{q}

are all minimal prime ideals containing
I
, then

\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)=\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}\left(\!\sqrt{I}\,\right)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(P_{j}).

We recall the following structure theorem
[
27
, Thm. 3.3.8]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
I
be a prime ideal in
\Bbbk[x_{1}^{{\pm 1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{{\pm 1}}]
.
Then
\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})
-rational polyhedral complex,
pure of dimension
\dim V(I)
.

When
H
has nontrivial torsion, the character variety

\Hom(H,\mathbb{K}^{\times})
is a finite disjoint union
\coprod(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}
,
whose components are translates of the identity component

(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}
by torsion characters; since torsion characters
have valuation
0
, these translations are invisible to
tropicalization. Writing
V_{1},\ldots,V_{q}
for the connected
components of
V(I)
, each
V_{j}
may be identified with a
subset of
(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}
via such a translation, and

\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I)\coloneqq\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}\overline{\{(\upsilon(x_{1}),\ldots,\upsilon(x_{n}))\mid(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in V_{j}\}}.

3.2.
Tropical varieties over rings

In this subsection, we follow
[
27
, Sec. 1.6]
to define
the tropical variety over a commutative Noetherian ring
R
.
We refer to
[
26
, Sec. 3]
and
[
5
]
for more details.

Let
H
be a finitely generated abelian group, not necessarily free.
Fix an additive character
\chi\in\Hom(H;\mathbb{R})
.
For any nonzero
f=\sum_{h\in H}a_{h}h\in RH
, we denote by
\deg_{\chi}(f)

the minimal value of
\chi(h)
with
a_{h}\neq 0
, and call it the
\chi
-degree of
f
.
The initial form
\init_{\chi}(f)
is the sum of all nonzero terms
a_{h}h
in
f

such that
\chi(h)=\deg_{\chi}(f)
. For an ideal
I\subseteq RH
, its initial ideal with
respect to
\chi
is defined as

\init_{\chi}(I)\coloneqq\langle\init_{\chi}(f)\mid f\in I\rangle.

Following
[
27
, Sec. 1.6]
and Definition
3.1
(
1
),
one can define the tropical variety over
R
as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

The
tropical variety over
R
of an ideal
I\subseteq RH

is the following set

\Trop_{R}(I)\coloneqq\{\chi\in\Hom(H;\mathbb{R})\mid\init_{\chi}(I)\neq RH\}.

Note that for the zero character
\init_{0}I=I
.
Hence
0\in\Trop_{R}(I)
if and only if
I
is a proper ideal in
RH
,
which implies that
\Trop_{R}(I)=\emptyset
if and only if
I=RH
.
From now on we always assume that
I
is a
proper
ideal.
Moreover, by definition if
\chi\in\Trop_{R}(I)
, then
r\cdot\chi\in\Trop_{R}(I)

for any positive real number
r
. Therefore,
\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{R}(I))

carries the same information as
\Trop_{R}(I)
. Here
\mathrm{S}(V)

denotes the intersection of the unit sphere in
H^{1}(H,\mathbb{R})

with a subset
V\subset H^{1}(H,\mathbb{R})
.

When
R
is a field
\Bbbk
, the tropical variety
\Trop_{\Bbbk}(I)
defined in
Definition
3.3
is indeed
\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon_{0}}(I)

with the trivial valuation
\upsilon_{0}
on
\Bbbk
defined in
Definition
3.1
. When
R=\mathbb{Z}
, the tropical
variety
\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(I)
can be understood using the
tropical varieties over various fields, as follows.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
26
, Prop. 3.9]
)
.

Let
I
be an ideal in
\mathbb{Z}H
. Then
\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(I)=\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(\sqrt{I})
.
Moreover,

\mathrm{S}\big(\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(I)\big)=\mathrm{S}\Big(\Trop_{\mathbb{Q},\upsilon_{0}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})\cup\bigcup\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\Trop_{\mathbb{Q},\upsilon_{p}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})\cup\Trop_{\mathbb{F}_{p},\hat{\upsilon}_{p}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p})\big)\Big),

where
\mathcal{P}
is a finite set of primes (depending on
I
), and
in the union we are taking the trivial valuation
\upsilon_{0}
on
\mathbb{Q}
,
the
p
-adic valuation
\upsilon_{p}
on
\mathbb{Q}
and the trivial valuation

\hat{\upsilon}_{p}
on
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, respectively.

Remark 3.5
.

In general, we have

\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(I)\neq\Trop_{\mathbb{Q},\upsilon_{0}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})\cup\bigcup\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\Trop_{\mathbb{Q},\upsilon_{p}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})\cup\Trop_{\mathbb{F}_{p},\hat{\upsilon}_{p}}(I\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{p})\big),

although Proposition
3.4
shows that
the two sets coincide after projection onto the unit sphere.

3.3.
Tropical varieties of twisted jump loci

Let
X
be a connected finite CW complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
and
b_{1}(X)>0
.
Set
H=\ab(G)
. Let
\sigma\colon\pi_{1}(X)\to\GL_{r}(R)
be a representation
with
R
being
\mathbb{Z}
or a field
\Bbbk
.
Set
\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\coloneqq\bigcap_{0\leq i\leq q}\mathcal{J}_{i}(X,L_{\sigma})
,
which is an ideal in
R[H]
. If

, we get the
tropical variety
\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))
.
If
R=\Bbbk
is a field, then
\Bbbk
is a field extension of
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
In the first case, by Ostrowski’s theorem, every nontrivial valuation
on
\mathbb{Q}
is equivalent to the
p
-adic valuation
\upsilon_{p}
for some
prime

, see
[
23
, Thm. 3.1.4]
; in addition, one may also take
the trivial valuation
\upsilon_{0}
on
\Bbbk
.
In the latter case (
\Bbbk
a field extension of

),
the only valuation on

is the trivial valuation
\hat{\upsilon}_{p}
.
Moreover, if

, Proposition

shows
that one can compute
\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})))
by the
projections of three types of tropical varieties over fields with the above

Remark 3.6
.

With notation as in Proposition
2.6
and
Remark
2.7
: since

is finite, every

of

has finite order, so

\upsilon(\rho)=0
for any valuation

on

.
Equivalent representations therefore have the same tropicalization,
and

where

runs over one representative from each equivalence class
of irreducible
\Bbbk
-representations of

.

4.
Bieri–Neumann–Strebel–Renz invariants

In this section, we review the definition of the
\Sigma
-invariants
of a group
G
and, more generally, of a space
X
, following
the approach from
[
4
,
20
,
30
,
34
,
36
]
.

4.1.
The
\Sigma
-invariants of a chain complex

Let
C=(C_{i},\partial_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a chain complex over a ring
R
,
and let
q
be a positive integer. We say
C
is of
finite
q
-type

if there is a chain complex
C^{\prime}
of finitely generated, projective
(left)
R
-modules and a chain map
C^{\prime}\to C
inducing isomorphisms

H_{i}(C^{\prime})\to H_{i}(C)
for
i<q
and an epimorphism

H_{q}(C^{\prime})\to H_{q}(C)
. For a free chain complex
C
, this is equivalent
to being chain-homotopy equivalent to a free chain complex
D

for which
D_{i}
is finitely generated for all
i\leq q
.

Now let
G
be a finitely generated group. The character sphere

(12)

\mathrm{S}(G)\coloneqq(\Hom(G,\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\})/\mathbb{R}^{+},

is the set of nonzero homomorphisms
G\to\mathbb{R}
modulo
homothety. To simplify notation, we will usually denote both
a nonzero homomorphism
\chi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
and its equivalence
class,
[\chi]\in\mathrm{S}(G)
, by the same symbol,
\chi
. The character
sphere may be identified with the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
in the
real vector space
\Hom(G,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n=b_{1}(G)
.

Given a nonzero homomorphism
\chi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, the set

G_{\chi}\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid\chi(g)\geq 0\}

is a submonoid of
G
, which depends only on

[\chi]\in\mathrm{S}(G)
. The monoid ring

\mathbb{Z}{G}_{\chi}
is a subring of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}{G}
;
thus, any
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module naturally acquires the structure of a

\mathbb{Z}{G}_{\chi}
-module, by restriction of scalars.

Definition 4.1

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
C
be a chain complex over
\mathbb{Z}{G}
. For each
integer
q\geq 0
, the
q
-th
Bieri–Neumann–Strebel–Renz invariant
of
C

is the set

(13)

\Sigma^{q}(C)=\big\{\chi\in\mathrm{S}(G)\mid\text{$C$ is of finite $q$-type over $\mathbb{Z}{G}_{\chi}$}\big\}\,.

Suppose now that
N\triangleleft G
is a normal
subgroup such that the quotient group
G/N
is abelian. Let

\mathrm{S}(G,N)
be the set of homomorphisms
\chi\in\mathrm{S}(G)

for which
N\leq\ker(\chi)
. Then
\mathrm{S}(G,N)

is a great subsphere of
\mathrm{S}(G)
, obtained by intersecting
the unit sphere
\mathrm{S}(G)\subset H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})

with the image of the linear map

\kappa^{*}\colon H^{1}(G/N,\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
,
where
\kappa\colon G\twoheadrightarrow G/N
is the canonical projection.
Finally, let
C
be a chain complex of free
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-modules, with

C_{i}
finitely generated for
i\leq q
, and let
N
be a
normal subgroup of
G
such that
G/N
is abelian.
Then, as shown in
[
20
]
,
C
is of finite
q
-type when
restricted to
\mathbb{Z}{N}
if and only if
\mathrm{S}(G,N)\subset\Sigma^{q}(C)
.

4.2.
The
\Sigma
-invariants of a CW-complex

Let
X
be a connected CW-complex with
finite
1
-skeleton, and let
G=\pi_{1}(X,x_{0})
be its fundamental
group. A choice of classifying map
X\to K(G,1)
yields
an induced isomorphism,
H^{1}(G;\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.80835pt}{$\scriptstyle\simeq$}}\,}H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
,
which identifies the respective unit spheres,

\mathrm{S}(G)
and
\mathrm{S}(X)
. The cell structure on
X
lifts to a
cell structure on the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
, invariant under the
action of
G
by deck transformations.
Thus, the cellular chain complex
C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})

is a chain complex of free
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-modules.

Definition 4.2
.

For each
q>0
, the
q
-th
Bieri–Neumann–Strebel–Renz invariant

of
X
is the subset of
\mathrm{S}(X)
given by

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\Sigma^{q}(C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z}))
.

We will denote by
\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{\mathrm{c}}
the complement
of
\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
in
\mathrm{S}(X)
. It is shown in
[
20
]
that

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is an open subset of
\mathrm{S}(X)
, which depends
only on the homotopy type of
X
.

4.3.
The
\Sigma
-invariants of a group

Let
G
be a finitely generated group, and let
\Cay(G)
be the Cayley
graph associated to a fixed finite generating set. The invariant
\Sigma^{1}(G)

of Bieri, Neumann, and Strebel
[
6
]
is the set of homomorphisms

\chi\in\mathrm{S}(G)
for which the induced subgraph of
\Cay(G)
on vertex
set
G_{\chi}
is connected. The BNS set is an open subset of
\mathrm{S}(G)
,
which does not depend on the choice of finite generating set for
G
.
The rational points on
\mathrm{S}(G)
correspond to epimorphisms

\chi\colon G\to\mathbb{Z}
; the kernel of
\chi
is
finitely generated if and only if both
\chi
and
-\chi
belong to
\Sigma^{1}(G)
.
Additionally, the complements of the
\Sigma
-invariants enjoy
the following naturality property.

Proposition 4.3

(
[
6
]
)
.

Suppose
\varphi\colon G\twoheadrightarrow Q
is a
surjective group homomorphism. Then the induced embedding,

\varphi^{*}\colon\mathrm{S}(Q)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{S}(G)
, restricts to an injective map
between the complements of the respective BNS-invariants,

\varphi^{*}\colon\Sigma^{1}(Q)^{\mathrm{c}}\hookrightarrow\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}
.

The BNS invariant was generalized by Bieri and Renz
[
7
]
,
as follows. For a
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module
M
, define

\Sigma^{q}(G,M)\coloneqq\Sigma^{q}(F_{\bullet})
, where

F_{\bullet}\to M
is a projective
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-resolution of
M
.
In particular, this yields invariants
\Sigma^{q}(G,\mathbb{Z})
,
where
\mathbb{Z}
is viewed as a trivial
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module;
clearly,
\Sigma^{q}(G,\mathbb{Z})=\Sigma^{q}(K(G,1),\mathbb{Z})
.
As noted in
[
7
, §1.3]
,
\Sigma^{1}(G)=-\Sigma^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
.

4.4.
Novikov–Sikorav completion

Let
G
be a group.
The
Novikov–Sikorav completion
of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}{G}

with respect to a homomorphism
\chi\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
consists of
all formal sums
\sum n_{g}g\in\mathbb{Z}^{G}
,
having the property that, for each
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the set

(14)

\big\{g\in G\mid\text{$n_{g}\neq 0$ and $\chi(g)\geq c$}\big\}

is finite, see
[
29
,
32
,
19
]
.
Equivalently, an element
\alpha\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi}

can be represented as a formal sum

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}n_{i}g_{i}
with
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}

such that the sequence of
values
(\chi(g_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing and tends to
-\infty
., i.e.,

\chi(g_{1})\geq\chi(g_{2})\geq\chi(g_{3})\geq\cdots
,
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}\chi(g_{i})=-\infty
.
With the usual addition and with multiplication defined by

(\sum n_{g}g)\cdot(\sum m_{h}h)=\sum(n_{g}m_{h})gh
,
the Novikov–Sikorav completion,
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi}
, is a ring
containing
\mathbb{Z}{G}
as a subring. Consequently,
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi}

carries a natural structure of left
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module (we will also view
it as a right
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi}
-module).
For instance, if

G=\mathbb{Z}=\langle t\rangle
and
\chi(t)=1
, then

\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi}=\mathbb{Z}[[t^{-1}]][t]=\{\sum_{i\leq k}n_{i}t^{i}\mid\text{$n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}$, for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$}\}
.

Moreover, the Novikov–Sikorav completion enjoys the following
functoriality property. Let
\phi\colon G\to K
be a homomorphism, and
let
\bar{\phi}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{G}\to\mathbb{Z}^{K}
be its linear extension to
formal sums. If
\chi\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
is a character, then

\bar{\phi}
restricts to a morphism of topological rings
between the corresponding completions,
\hat{\phi}\colon\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi\circ\phi}\to\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{K}}_{\chi}
.

4.5.
Novikov–Sikorav homology

In his thesis
[
32
]
, J.-Cl. Sikorav reinterpreted the BNS invariant
of a finitely generated group
G
in terms of Novikov homology.
This interpretation was extended to all BNSR invariants
by Bieri
[
4
]
, and later to the BNSR invariants of
CW-complexes by Farber, Geoghegan, and Schütz
[
20
]
.

Let
X
be a connected CW-complex and let
G=\pi_{1}(X)
.
Recall that the homology groups of
X
with coefficients in a left

\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module
M
are given by

H_{i}(X;M)\coloneqq H_{i}(C_{*}(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}{G}}M)
,
where
\widetilde{X}
is the universal cover of
X
and
C_{*}(\widetilde{X},\mathbb{Z})

is its cellular chain complex, viewed as a right
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module via the
cellular action of
G
on the chains of
\widetilde{X}
.
We will use in an essential way the following theorem, which expresses the
BNSR invariants of
X
as the vanishing loci for homology with
coefficients in the Novikov–Sikorav completions of
G
.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
20
]
)
.

If
X
is a connected CW-complex with finite
q
-skeleton, then

(15)

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})=\big\{\chi\in\mathrm{S}(X)\mid H_{i}(X;\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi})=0,\>\text{for all $i\leq q$}\big\}\,.

In particular, the BNS set
\Sigma^{1}(G)=-\Sigma^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
consists
of those characters
\chi\in\mathrm{S}(G)
for which both
H_{0}(G,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi})

and
H_{1}(G,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{\chi})
vanish.

Remark 4.5
.

There were several steps along the way where we had to make a choice
of sign in the various definitions of BNS invariants. We refer
to
[
36
, Remark 5.7]
for a detailed explanation.

5.
An improved bound for the BNSR-invariants

Throughout this section,
\Bbbk
denotes a field with a possibly
trivial valuation
\upsilon\colon\Bbbk^{\times}\to\mathbb{R}
.
There is then an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
extending
\Bbbk

such that the extension of
\upsilon
to
\mathbb{K}
is nontrivial
(still denoted
\upsilon
): if
\upsilon
is nontrivial, take

\mathbb{K}=\overline{\Bbbk}
with an extended valuation; if
\upsilon

is trivial, take
\mathbb{K}
to be the field of Puiseux series

\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\overline{\Bbbk}(\!(t^{1/n})\!)
when
\ch(\Bbbk)=0
,
or
\overline{\Bbbk}(\!(t^{\mathbb{Q}})\!)
otherwise; see
[
27
]
.

5.1.
Valuations and characters

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Fix a representation

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
.
For a multiplicative character
\rho\colon G\to\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, set

\chi=\upsilon\circ\det\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)
, where
\det

is the determinant function. Then we obtain
a commuting diagram,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\GL_{r}(\mathbb{K})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{K}^{\times}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma\otimes\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\det}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\upsilon}

Let
\hat{\mathbb{K}}
be the topological completion of
\mathbb{K}
with respect
to the absolute value defined by the valuation
\upsilon
. Then
\hat{\mathbb{K}}

is a field, and the map to the completion,
\iota\colon\mathbb{K}\hookrightarrow\hat{\mathbb{K}}
,
is a field extension.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume:

(i)

There exists
d\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
g\in G
and
1\leq i,j\leq r
,

(16)

\upsilon([\sigma(g)]_{ij})\geq d.

(ii)

\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
for all
g\in G
.

Then:

(1)

\chi=r(\upsilon\circ\rho)
;

(2)

\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
is a left
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}G}_{-\chi}
-module via
\sigma\otimes\rho
.

Proof.

For any
g\in G
,

\chi(g)=\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)\otimes\rho(g)))=\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))+r\,\upsilon(\rho(g))=r\,(\upsilon\circ\rho)(g),

since
\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
. This proves (
1
).

For (
2
), let
\sum n_{i}g_{i}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}G}_{-\chi}
and

e_{k}=(0,\cdots,0,1,0,\cdots,0)\in\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
the
k
-th standard basis vector.
The
j
-th coordinate of
(\sigma\otimes\rho)(\cdot)e_{k}
is

\sum_{i}n_{i}\,\rho(g_{i})\,[\sigma(g_{i})]_{kj}.

Using
\upsilon(n_{i})\geq 0
, the bound on matrix entries, and (
1
), we obtain

\upsilon(n_{i}\rho(g_{i})[\sigma(g_{i})]_{kj})\geq\tfrac{1}{r}\chi(g_{i})+d.

Since
\chi(g_{i})\to+\infty
in the Novikov completion,

\upsilon(n_{i}\rho(g_{i})[\sigma(g_{i})]_{kj})\to+\infty
and the terms tend to
0

in
\hat{\mathbb{K}}
, proving convergence. For a general vector
e\in\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
,
each coordinate is a finite linear combination of series of the standard
basis vectors already considered.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The lower bound (
16
) is essential for the convergence
argument in Lemma
5.1
. It ensures that the growth of

\chi(g_{i})\to+\infty
forces the terms
n_{i}\,\rho(g_{i})\,[\sigma(g_{i})]_{kj}

to tend to zero in
\hat{\mathbb{K}}
.

Without such a bound, the
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}G}_{-\chi}
-module structure on

\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
can fail. For instance, let
G=\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with the
p
-adic
valuation
\upsilon_{p}
, and consider the representation

\sigma\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}),\qquad 1\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&p^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\det(\sigma(n))=1
for all
n
, but

\upsilon_{p}(p^{n})=n\to+\infty,\qquad\upsilon_{p}(p^{-n})=-n\to-\infty,

so (
16
) fails. In this situation, the negative drift in the
valuations of the matrix entries cancels the positive drift coming from

\chi(g_{i})\to+\infty
, and the series defining the module action need not
converge.

Note also that this example does not satisfy any of the following conditions in
Lemma
5.3
, and hence falls outside the
range of applicability of Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Each of the following conditions ensures that the hypotheses of
Lemma
5.1
are satisfied.

(a)

\upsilon
is trivial on
\Bbbk
.

(b)

\im(\sigma)
is finite.

(c)

\sigma(G)\subseteq\GL_{r}(\mathcal{O}_{\Bbbk})
, where

\mathcal{O}_{\Bbbk}=\{\lambda\in\Bbbk\mid\upsilon(\lambda)\geq 0\}
is the valuation ring of
\Bbbk
.

Proof.

We check that each condition implies the two hypotheses of
Lemma
5.1
.

Case
(a)
.

Since
\upsilon
is trivial on
\Bbbk
, we have

\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
for all
g\in G
.
Moreover, all nonzero matrix entries satisfy
\upsilon([\sigma(g)]_{ij})\geq 0
,
so the boundedness condition holds.

Case
(b)
.

Since
\im(\sigma)
is finite, all matrix entries lie in a finite subset of
\Bbbk
,
hence there exists
d\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\upsilon([\sigma(g)]_{ij})\geq d

for all
g,i,j
.
Moreover, each
\sigma(g)
has finite order, hence so does

\det(\sigma(g))
, and therefore
\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
.

Case
(c)
.

If
\sigma(G)\subseteq\GL_{r}(\mathcal{O}_{\Bbbk})
, then for all
g
:

\upsilon([\sigma(g)]_{ij})\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\det(\sigma(g))\in\mathcal{O}_{\Bbbk}^{\times}.

Then
\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))\geq 0
together with

\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g^{-1})))\geq 0
implies
\upsilon(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
.
Thus both hypotheses of Lemma
5.1
are satisfied directly,
with
d=0
.
∎

5.2.
A key homological criterion

Let
X
be a connected, finite-type CW-complex, and let
G=\pi_{1}(X)
.
As before, let
\rho\colon G\to\mathbb{K}^{\times}
and

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
be two group homomorphisms,
and set
\chi=r\cdot(\upsilon\circ\rho)\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

With notation as above, assume that
\upsilon
and
\sigma
satisfy one of the
conditions in Lemma
5.3
.
If
\chi
is nonzero and
H_{i}(X;L_{\sigma}\otimes L_{\rho})\neq 0

for some
0\leq i\leq q
, then
\chi\not\in\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

The proof is along the same lines as that of
[
30
, Thm. 10.1]

and
[
36
, Thm. 6.4]
; for completeness, we give full details.

Consider the representation
\sigma\otimes\rho\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{K})

associated with the local system
L_{\sigma}\otimes L_{\rho}
.
By Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.3
,
the completion
\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}
is a left
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}
-module, which
we denote by
\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\sigma\otimes\rho}
. Restricting scalars via the
inclusion
\mathbb{Z}{G}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}
yields the
\mathbb{Z}{G}
-module

\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\iota\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)}
, defined by
the representation
\iota\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\hat{\mathbb{K}})
.

For a ring
R
, a bounded below chain complex of flat right
R
-modules

K_{*}
, and a left
R
-module
M
, there is a (right half-plane, boundedly
converging) Künneth spectral sequence,

(17)

E^{2}_{ij}=\Tor^{R}_{i}(H_{j}(K),M)\Rightarrow H_{i+j}(K\otimes_{R}M)\,,

see
[
38
, Thm. 5.6.4]
.
We will apply this spectral sequence to the ring
R=\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}
,
the chain complex of free
R
-modules

K_{*}=C_{*}\big(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{Z}\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}{G}}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}
,
and the
R
-module
M=\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\sigma\otimes\rho}
.

Assume now that
\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})
, and suppose
\chi

belongs to
\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. By Theorem
4.4
, this condition
implies the vanishing of
H_{j}(X,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi})
for all
j\leq q
;
that is,
H_{j}(K_{*})=0
for
j\leq q
. Therefore,
E^{2}_{ij}=0
for
j\leq q
.
Noting that

(18)

K_{*}\otimes_{R}M=C_{*}\big(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{Z}\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}{G}}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}\otimes_{\widehat{\mathbb{Z}{G}}_{-\chi}}\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\sigma\otimes\rho}=C_{*}\big(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{Z}\big)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}{G}}\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\iota\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)}\,,

we infer from (
17
) that

H_{i+j}\big(X;\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\iota\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)}\big)=0
for
j\leq q
, and so

H_{j}\big(X;\hat{\mathbb{K}}^{r}_{\iota\circ(\sigma\otimes\rho)}\big)=0
for
j\leq q
. From the
definition of the characteristic varieties (over the field
\hat{\mathbb{K}}
), this is equivalent to

\iota\circ\rho\notin\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\hat{\mathbb{K}})
.
It follows that

\rho\notin\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})
, which contradicts our hypothesis on
\rho
.
Therefore,
\chi\notin\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and we are done.
∎

5.3.
Main results

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1.1

from the Introduction. The key input is the Novikov module structure
established in Lemma
5.1
, together with the valuation
conditions ensured by Lemma
5.3
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
X
be a connected finite CW-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Fix a field
\Bbbk
with a rational valuation

\upsilon
(i.e.,
\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
), possibly trivial.
Let
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
be a representation
and let
L_{\sigma}
denote the corresponding local system on
X
.
Consider the twisted jump loci

\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\subset H^{1}(X;\Bbbk^{\times})

and let
\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))\subset H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
be its tropicalization with respect
to the valuation
\upsilon
. Assume that
\upsilon
and
\sigma
satisfy one of the
valuation conditions from
Lemma
5.3
. Then

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\big(\!\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))\big)^{\mathrm{c}}.

Proof.

By the assumption
\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
, we can assume that

\upsilon(\mathbb{K}^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
. Consider a
\mathbb{K}
-valued, multiplicative
character
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(X)\to\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. Composing the valuation map

\upsilon\colon\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\mathbb{Q}
with the homomorphism

\rho
, we obtain an additive character,

\chi=r\cdot(\upsilon\circ\rho)\colon\pi_{1}(X)\to\mathbb{Q}
,
which may be viewed as an element of
H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Q})
, that is,
a rational point in
H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, if
\chi
is nonzero, then
\chi

determines a rational point on
\mathrm{S}(X)
.
Since we work after applying
\mathrm{S}(-)
, multiplication by the positive
scalar
r
does not change the corresponding point of the character sphere.

By the assumption
\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
and

[
31
, Thm. 7.11]
, any nonzero rational point

\chi\in\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))

is of the form
r\cdot(\upsilon\circ\rho)
,
for some
\rho\in\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\times_{\Bbbk}\mathbb{K}
.
Since
\upsilon
and
\sigma
satisfy one of the
valuation conditions from
Lemma
5.3
,
Theorem
5.4
implies that
\chi

belongs to
\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Note that by the assumption that
\upsilon(\Bbbk^{\times})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}

and Theorem
3.2
,
the set of rational points are dense in

\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})))
. Moreover,

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{\mathrm{c}}
is a closed subset of
\mathrm{S}(X)
. Thus,

\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(\mathcal{V}^{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})))\subseteq\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{\mathrm{c}}
.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
X
be a connected finite CW-complex with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
For any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
with

\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})\coloneqq\bigcap_{0\leq i\leq q}\mathcal{J}_{i}(X,L_{\sigma})
,
we have

\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\big(\!\Trop_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}))\big)^{\mathrm{c}}.

Proof.

Set
I=\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma})
.
In each of the three coefficient/valuation settings below considered
in Proposition
3.4
, at least one
of the conditions in Lemma
5.3
holds:

•

I\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
with
\upsilon_{0}
;

•

I\otimes\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
with
\upsilon_{p}
;

•

I\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p}=\mathcal{J}_{\leq q}(X,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{p})
with
\hat{\upsilon}_{p}
.

For
\upsilon_{0}
and
\hat{\upsilon}_{p}
, condition
(a)
holds.

For
\upsilon_{p}
, condition
(c)
holds since

\sigma(G)\subseteq\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
implies

\upsilon_{p}([\sigma(g)]_{ij})\geq 0
and

\det(\sigma(g))=\pm 1
, hence
\upsilon_{p}(\det(\sigma(g)))=0
.

By Lemma
5.3
, the hypotheses of
Lemma
5.1
are satisfied in each case.
Hence Theorem
5.5
applies, giving

\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{\Bbbk,\upsilon}(I\otimes\Bbbk))\subseteq\Sigma^{q}(X,\mathbb{Z})^{\mathrm{c}}

for each pair
(\Bbbk,\upsilon)
above.
The result follows from Proposition
3.4
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

The assumption
\im(\sigma)\subset\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
in
Corollary
5.6
is weaker than
requiring
\im(\sigma)
to be finite: the integrality of
the matrix entries ensures the valuation conditions from
Lemma
5.3
for all three types of valuations
simultaneously. However, in the applications to

3
-manifold groups (Theorem
7.2
),
the representations produced by the Friedl–Vidussi theorem
do have finite image. Whether infinite-image integral representations can
yield sharper upper bounds for
\Sigma^{q}(G,\mathbb{Z})
than
finite-image ones is an open problem directly
relevant to Question
1.6
.

5.4.
An example

We give an example where one can identify the sets
\Sigma^{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
by
means of the twisted jump loci, but not the ordinary jump loci.

Example 5.8
.

Consider the one-relator group
G=\langle x_{1},x_{2}\mid R\rangle
, where

R=x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}.

As an application of his algorithm, Brown
[
10
]
showed that

\Sigma^{1}(G)
consists of two open arcs on the unit circle
S^{1}=\mathrm{S}(G)
,
joining the points
(-1,0)
to
(0,-1)
and
(0,-1)
to

(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}})
,
respectively; see Figure
1(f)
.

Computing Fox derivatives gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{R}}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle=-x_{1}^{-1}(1-x_{2}^{-1})-[x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1}]x_{2}x_{1}^{-1}(1-x_{2}^{-1})+[x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1}][x_{2},x_{1}^{-1}](1-x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}^{-1})-

\displaystyle\qquad[x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1}][x_{2},x_{1}^{-1}][x_{1},x_{2}^{-1}]x_{1}^{-1}(1-x_{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{R}}{\partial x_{2}}

6.
Kähler groups

In this section we apply the general results of the
preceding sections to compact Kähler manifolds and
their fundamental groups. A finitely presented group
G

is called a
Kähler group
if it is isomorphic
to
\pi_{1}(X)
for some compact Kähler manifold
X
.

The key feature of Kähler groups that we exploit
is that both their first characteristic variety
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})

and their BNS invariant
\Sigma^{1}(G)
are completely
determined by the
hyperbolic orbifold fibrations

of
X
— that is, surjective holomorphic maps from
X

onto orbifold curves with negative orbifold Euler
characteristic. These fibrations are finite in number
up to equivalence
[
14
, Thm. 2]
, and their
cohomological footprints tile both invariants, with
one subtle difference: certain genus-
1
fibrations
with some multiple fibers contribute to

\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}
but are invisible in
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
.

In §
6.1
we set up the algebraic
foundations, computing the characteristic varieties
of compact orbifold groups
\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},\bm{\mu})

via Fox calculus. This includes both the twisted version
needed for Alexander polynomial computations and the
untwisted formula (
19
), which feeds into
the structure theorem for
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
.
In §
6.2
we state and compare
the two main structure theorems: Theorem
6.4

for
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
, due to Beauville, Arapura, Campana and
others, and Theorem
6.5
for
\Sigma^{1}(G)
,
due to Delzant. We also derive a corollary
(Corollary
6.7
) relating the tropicalization
of twisted characteristic varieties to the BNS invariant.
In §
6.3
we prove
Theorem
6.8
, one of the main results
of the paper: the first twisted Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{\sigma}(X)
of a compact Kähler manifold is
always
0
or
1
, providing a new obstruction to
Kählerianity that goes beyond the untwisted DPS
obstruction
[
17
]
. In §
6.4
, we give some
examples where Theorem
1.4
detects non-Kählerianity
and the untwisted result does not.

6.1.
Compact orbifold groups

Let
C_{g}
be a compact Riemann surface of genus

g\geqslant 1
and let
s\geqslant 0
be an integer. If
s>0
, fix points

\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\}
in
C_{g}
and assign to these points an integer weight
vector
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{s})
with all
\mu_{i}\geqslant 2
.
The orbifold fundamental group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},{\bm{\mu}})

associated to these data has presentation

\Gamma=\Bigl\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{g},y_{1},\ldots,y_{g},z_{1},\ldots,z_{s}\bigm|\prod_{i=1}^{g}[x_{i},y_{i}]\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{s}z_{j}=1,\ z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}=1\text{ for all }1\leqslant j\leqslant s\Bigr\rangle.

Next we compute the first twisted Alexander polynomial of a compact orbifold group.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},\bm{\mu})
, and fix an algebraically
closed field
\Bbbk
. Let
\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)

be a representation, sending generators
x_{i},y_{i},z_{j}
to matrices

X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{j}
, respectively, such that

\prod_{i=1}^{g}[X_{i},Y_{i}]\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{s}Z_{j}=I_{r},\qquad Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}=I_{r}\quad(1\leq j\leq s).

Then the following hold:

(1)

If
g\geq 2
, or if the matrix
1+Z_{j}+\cdots+Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}-1}

is not invertible for some
j
, then
\Delta^{\sigma}(\Gamma)=0
.

(2)

If
g=1
and the matrices
1+Z_{j}+\cdots+Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}-1}

are invertible for all
1\leq j\leq s
, then
\Delta^{\sigma}(\Gamma)=1
.

In particular, the invertibility condition holds whenever

Z_{j}=I_{r}
and
\ch(\Bbbk)\nmid\mu_{j}
.

Proof.

Consider the compact orbifold group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},{\bm{\mu}})

as above, and denote by
X=K(\Gamma,1)
its classifying space. By Fox calculus,
the boundary map
\partial_{2}
in
C_{*}(\widetilde{X};\Bbbk)
is given by the

(2g+s)\times(s+1)
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{z_{1}^{\mu_{1}}-1}{z_{1}-1}&0&\cdots&0&(\prod_{j=1}^{s}z_{j})^{-1}\\
0&\frac{z_{2}^{\mu_{2}}-1}{z_{2}-1}&\cdots&0&(\prod_{j=2}^{s}z_{j})^{-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\frac{z_{s}^{\mu_{s}}-1}{z_{s}-1}&z_{s}^{-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1-x_{1}y_{1}x_{1}^{-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&x_{1}-[x_{1},y_{1}]\\
0&0&\cdots&0&[x_{1},y_{1}](1-x_{2}y_{2}x_{2}^{-1})\\
0&0&\cdots&0&[x_{1},y_{1}](x_{2}-[x_{2},y_{2}])\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\prod_{k=1}^{g-1}[x_{k},y_{k}](1-x_{g}y_{g}x_{g}^{-1})\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\prod_{k=1}^{g-1}[x_{k},y_{k}](x_{g}-[x_{g},y_{g}])\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Here we adopt the notation
\frac{z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-1}{z_{j}-1}\coloneqq 1+z_{j}+\cdots+z_{j}^{\mu_{j}-1}
.

Consider a character
\rho\in\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
,
the connected component of
\Hom(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})

containing the constant sheaf, which sends
x_{i}\to\rho_{i}
,

y_{i}\to\rho_{g+i}
, and
z_{j}\to 1
.
Then
\partial_{2}^{\sigma\otimes\rho}
has the following

(2g+s)\times(s+1)
matrix:

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{Z_{1}^{\mu_{1}}-I_{r}}{Z_{1}-I_{r}}&0&\cdots&0&(\prod_{j=1}^{s}Z_{j})^{-1}\\
0&\frac{Z_{2}^{\mu_{2}}-I_{r}}{Z_{2}-I_{r}}&\cdots&0&(\prod_{j=2}^{s}Z_{j})^{-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\frac{Z_{s}^{\mu_{s}}-I_{r}}{Z_{s}-I_{r}}&Z_{s}^{-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&I_{r}-\rho_{g+1}\cdot X_{1}Y_{1}X_{1}^{-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\rho_{1}\cdot X_{1}-[X_{1},Y_{1}]\\
0&0&\cdots&0&[X_{1},Y_{1}](I_{r}-\rho_{g+2}\cdot X_{2}Y_{2}X_{2}^{-1})\\
0&0&\cdots&0&[X_{1},Y_{1}](\rho_{2}\cdot X_{2}-[X_{2},Y_{2}])\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\prod_{k=1}^{g-1}[X_{k},Y_{k}](I_{r}-\rho_{2g}\cdot X_{g}Y_{g}X_{g}^{-1})\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\prod_{k=1}^{g-1}[X_{k},Y_{k}](\rho_{g}\cdot X_{g}-[X_{g},Y_{g}])\end{pmatrix},

where
\frac{Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-I_{r}}{Z_{j}-I_{r}}\coloneqq 1+Z_{j}+\cdots+Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}-1}
.

Assume that for some
1\leq i\leq g
, one of the following holds:

•

\rho_{i}
is not an eigenvalue of
[X_{i},Y_{i}]X_{i}^{-1}
and
X_{i}^{-1}
; or

•

\rho_{g+i}^{-1}
is not an eigenvalue
of
X_{i}Y_{i}X_{i}^{-1}
and
Y_{i}
.

Then

\rank\partial_{2}^{\sigma\otimes\rho}=r+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\rank\tfrac{Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-I_{r}}{Z_{j}-I_{r}}

and
\rank\partial_{1}^{\sigma\otimes\rho}=r
.
Putting all together, we get that

\displaystyle\dim H_{1}(\Gamma,L_{\sigma}\otimes L_{\rho})

\displaystyle=r(2g+s)-\rank\partial_{1}^{\sigma\otimes\rho}-\rank\partial_{2}^{\sigma\otimes\rho}

\displaystyle=r(2g-2+s)-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\rank\tfrac{Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-I_{r}}{Z_{j}-I_{r}}.

Hence if either
g\geq 2
or
\frac{Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-I_{r}}{Z_{j}-I_{r}}
is
not of full rank for some
j
, then

\mathcal{V}_{1}(\Gamma,L_{\sigma})\cap\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})=\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times}).

In particular, the first Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{\sigma}(\Gamma)=0
in this case.
On the other hand, if
g=1
and
\frac{Z_{j}^{\mu_{j}}-I_{r}}{Z_{j}-I_{r}}

has full rank for all
1\leq j\leq s
, then

\mathcal{V}_{1}(\Gamma,L_{\sigma})\cap\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
consists
of only finitely many points. In particular, the first Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{\sigma}(\Gamma)=1
in this case.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

As a special case of the above computation (taking
r=1

and
\sigma=\id
), we obtain an explicit description of
the first characteristic variety of the compact orbifold group.
Identify
\widehat{\Gamma}=\widehat{\Gamma}^{0}\times\widehat{A}
,
where
\widehat{\Gamma}^{\circ}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2g}

is the identity component and
A=\Tors(\Gamma_{\ab})

is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{\mu_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{\mu_{s}}/\langle(1,\dots,1)\rangle
.
Then

(19)

\mathcal{V}^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})=\begin{cases}\widehat{\Gamma}&\text{if $g\geq 2$},\\[2.0pt]
\big(\widehat{\Gamma}\setminus\widehat{\Gamma}^{0}\big)\cup\{\mathbf{1}\}&\text{if $g=1$ and $\left|A\right|=\dfrac{\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}}{\lcm(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s})}>1$},\\[2.0pt]
\{\mathbf{1}\}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

see
[
2
, Prop. 3.11]
and
[
35
, §9.1]
.
In particular,
\mathcal{V}^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})
has a positive-dimensional
component through
1
if and only if
g\geq 2
, and has
torsion-translated positive-dimensional components if and
only if
\left|A\right|>1
.

6.2.
Characteristic varieties and BNS invariants
of Kähler manifolds

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold; its fundamental
group
G=\pi_{1}(X)
is called a
Kähler group
.
The structure of both
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
and
\Sigma^{1}(G)
is
controlled by the orbifold fibrations of
X
, as described
in the two theorems below.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
. A holomorphic map

f\colon X\to C_{g}
is called an orbifold fibration if
f
is surjective with connected
fibers onto a Riemann surface
C_{g}
with genus
g\geqslant 1
. Assume that
f

has multiple fibers over the points
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{s}\}
in
C_{g}
and let
\mu_{j}

denote the multiplicity of the multiple fiber
f^{*}(q_{j})
(the
\gcd
of the coefficients
of the divisor
f^{*}q_{j}
).
Such an orbifold fibration is denoted by
f\colon X\to(C_{g},{\bm{\mu}})
,
where
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s})
. It is called
hyperbolic
if
the orbifold Euler characteristic of the base curve,

\chi(C_{g},{\bm{\mu}})\coloneqq 2-2g-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\mu_{j}}\Bigr)

is strictly negative.

Two orbifold fibrations
f\colon X\to(C_{g},{\bm{\mu}})
and

f^{\prime}\colon X\to(C_{g^{\prime}},{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime})
are equivalent
if there is a biholomorphic map
h\colon C_{g}\to C_{g^{\prime}}
which
sends marked points to marked
points while preserving multiplicities. A compact Kähler
manifold
X
admits only finitely many equivalence classes
of hyperbolic orbifold fibrations, see e.g.
[
14
, Thm. 2]
.

The structure of
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
for a compact Kähler
manifold
X
is described by the following result, which
predates and is used in the proof of Theorem
6.5
.

Theorem 6.4

(
[
3
,
1
,
11
,
16
,
14
,
12
,
2
,
18
,
24
]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold. Then

(20)

\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})=\bigcup_{f}f^{\#}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g_{f}},\bm{\mu}_{f}),\mathbb{C})\big)\cup Z,

where
Z
is a finite set of torsion characters, and the
union runs over the finite set of equivalence classes of
orbifold fibrations
f\colon X\to(C_{g_{f}},\bm{\mu}_{f})

satisfying either
g_{f}\geq 2
, or
g_{f}=1
and
\left|A_{f}\right|>1

(where
A_{f}
denotes the torsion part of the abelianization
of
\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g_{f}},\bm{\mu}_{f})
).

Using Simpson’s results
[
33
, Thm. 1]
, Delzant gave a complete
description of
\Sigma^{1}(G)
for
G
a Kähler group in
[
15
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Theorem 6.5

(
[
15
, Thm. 1.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
. Then we have

\Sigma^{1}(G;\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{S}\bigg(\!\bigcup_{f}\im(f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R}))\bigg)^{\mathrm{c}},

where the union runs over all equivalence classes of
hyperbolic orbifold fibrations

f\colon X\to C_{g}
. In particular, it is a finite union.
Moreover,
\Sigma^{1}(G;\mathbb{Z})=\emptyset
if and only if there exists a
hyperbolic orbifold fibration
f\colon X\to C_{g}
such that

f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism.

Remark 6.6
.

Comparing Theorem
6.4
with
Theorem
6.5
, one sees that the two
formulas involve the same orbifold fibrations, with
one exception: a hyperbolic orbifold fibration

f\colon X\to(C_{1},\bm{\mu})
with
g_{f}=1
and

\left|A_{f}\right|=1
contributes to
\Sigma^{1}(\pi_{1}(X))^{\mathrm{c}}

via Theorem
6.5
,
but does
not
contribute a positive-dimensional
component to
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
.

The reason is visible from formula (
19
): when
g=1

and
\left|A\right|=1
, we have
\mathcal{V}^{1}(\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{1},\bm{\mu}),\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}
,
and the pullback
(f)^{\#}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{1},\bm{\mu}),\mathbb{C}))

contributes nothing positive-dimensional to
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,\mathbb{C})
.

However, as shown by Liu–Liu
[
26
]
, the discrepancy
disappears when one considers characteristic varieties
over a field
\Bbbk
of characteristic
p>0
with
p\mid\mu_{j}
for some
j
:
in that case by the computation in the proof of Proposition
6.1

the fibration
f
does contribute a positive-dimensional
component to
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X;\Bbbk)
.
This shows that the union over all fields
\Bbbk

(including those of characteristic
p>0
)
of the tropicalizations
\Trop_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X;\Bbbk))

recovers the full
\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}
,
consistently with the formula of
[
26
]
.

Based on Delzant’s theorem and Theorem
5.5
,
we obtain the following corollary, which strengthens results
from
[
36
]
and
[
26
]
.

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
For any algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
with trivial valuation
and any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
,

\Sigma^{1}(G;\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathrm{S}\!\left(\Trop_{\Bbbk}\bigl(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})\bigr)\right)^{\!\mathrm{c}}.

Equivalently, by Theorem
6.5
,

\Trop_{\Bbbk}\bigl(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})\bigr)\subseteq\bigcup_{f}\im\bigl(f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})\bigr),

where the union runs over all equivalence classes of
hyperbolic orbifold fibrations
f\colon X\to C_{g}
.

Proof.

Since
\upsilon
is trivial,
\Trop_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma}))
is a cone over

\mathrm{S}(\Trop_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})))
. The claim then follows from
Theorems
5.5
and
6.5
.
∎

6.3.
Twisted Alexander polynomials of Kähler manifolds

The next result generalizes
[
17
, Thm. 4.3(3)]
,
giving a new obstruction for a finitely presented group being realizable
as the fundamental group of a compact Kähler manifold.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Then for any representation
\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\Bbbk)
over some field
\Bbbk
,
the first twisted Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{\sigma}(X)
of the pair
(X,\sigma)

is either
0
or
1
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can always assume that
\Bbbk
is
algebraically closed. Next we assume that

\bigcup_{f}\im(f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H^{1}(X,\mathbb{R}))\neq H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R}).

By Hodge theory and the fact that the left side is a finite union,
its codimension is at least
2
in
H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
.
Therefore,
\codim\Trop_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma}))\geq 2
by Corollary
6.7
.
Since tropicalization preserves dimensions due to
Theorem
3.2
, we have the variety
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})

has codimension at least
2
in
\Hom(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
. The zero locus of
the twisted Alexander polynomial corresponds to the codimension one
components of
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})
. Hence, the first twisted Alexander
polynomial
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)=1
in this case.

Next, suppose that

\bigcup_{f}\im(f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R}))=H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R}).

By Theorem
6.5
, this happens if and only if
there exists a hyperbolic orbifold fibration
f\colon X\to(C_{g},\bm{\mu})

such that
f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism.
The map
f
induces a short exact sequence of groups,

where
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},\bm{\mu})
and
K=\ker(f_{\#})

is a finitely generated group due to
[
13
, Lem. 3]
.
Moreover,
f_{\#}
induces an isomorphism between the maximal
free abelian quotients of
G
and
\Gamma
; in particular,

\Hom^{0}(G,\Bbbk^{\times})\cong\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
.

Given a character
\rho\in\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
,
let

denote the rank-one local system on
G

obtained by pullback along

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{0}(\Gamma;H_{1}(K;L_{\sigma})\otimes L_{\rho})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(G;L_{\sigma}\otimes f^{*}L_{\rho})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}(\Gamma;H_{0}(K;L_{\sigma})\otimes L_{\rho})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}

Note that
(L_{\sigma}\otimes f^{*}L_{\rho})|_{K}=(L_{\sigma})|_{K}
,
so that
H_{*}(K;L_{\sigma})
is independent of
\rho
. We consider
two sub-cases.

Sub-case (i):
H_{0}(K;L_{\sigma})=0
.

Then (
21
) gives a surjection from

H_{0}(\Gamma;H_{1}(K;L_{\sigma})\otimes L_{\rho})

onto
H_{1}(G;L_{\sigma}\otimes f^{*}L_{\rho})
.

We claim that
H_{1}(K;L_{\sigma})
is a finite-dimensional
\Bbbk
-vector space.
Since
K
is finitely generated by
[
13
, Lem. 3]
, say by
s
elements

g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
, the partial free resolution

\Bbbk[K]^{s}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}\Bbbk[K]\to\Bbbk\to 0
gives, upon tensoring with

V\coloneqq(L_{\sigma})|_{K}\cong\Bbbk^{r}
, that
H_{1}(K;L_{\sigma})
is a quotient
of
\ker(d_{1}\otimes V)\subset V^{s}\cong\Bbbk^{rs}
, hence finite-dimensional.

Consequently,
H_{0}(\Gamma;H_{1}(K;L_{\sigma})\otimes L_{\rho})\neq 0

for at most finitely many
\rho\in\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
.
Hence
\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})\cap\Hom^{0}(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
is a finite set.
Since
b_{1}(G)=2g\geq 2
, this finite set has no codimension-one
components in
\Hom^{0}(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
, and so
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)=1
.

Sub-case (ii):
H_{0}(K;L_{\sigma})\neq 0
.

Let
L^{\prime}
denote the local system on
\Gamma
with stalk

H_{0}(K;L_{\sigma})
.
By (
21
) and the computations in Section
6.1
,
applied to the orbifold group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},\bm{\mu})

with
g\geq 1
and the local system
L^{\prime}
, one of the
following holds:

(a)

H_{1}(\Gamma;L^{\prime}\otimes L_{\rho})\neq 0
for all

\rho\in\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
, then by (
21
) so is

H_{1}(G;L_{\sigma}\otimes f^{*}L_{\rho})
, hence
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)=0
in this case; or

(b)

H_{1}(\Gamma;L^{\prime}\otimes L_{\rho})=0
for all but
finitely many
\rho
, in which case (combining with
the finiteness of the left-hand term in (
21
))

\mathcal{V}^{1}(X,L_{\sigma})\cap\Hom^{0}(G,\Bbbk^{\times})
is again
finite, and so
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)=1
.

In all cases,
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)
is either
0
or
1

(up to units), completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler manifold with
\pi_{1}(X)=G
.
Consider
\sigma=\id\colon G\to\GL_{1}(\Bbbk)
the trivial representation
over some field. If
b_{1}(X)\geq 4
, then

\Delta^{\sigma}(X)=0
if and only if there exists a
hyperbolic orbifold fibration
f\colon X\to C_{g}
such that

f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism;
otherwise
\Delta^{\sigma}(X)=1
.
In particular, it does not depend on
\ch(\Bbbk)
.

Proof.

Following the proof of Theorem
6.8
,
we may assume that there exists a hyperbolic orbifold fibration

f\colon X\to(C_{g},\bm{\mu})
such that

f^{*}\colon H^{1}(C_{g};\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism
(otherwise
\Delta^{\sigma}(X)=1
already, regardless of
\sigma
).

Since
\sigma=\id
is the trivial rank-one representation,

(L_{\sigma})|_{K}=\Bbbk_{K}
is the constant sheaf on
K
, so

H_{0}(K;\,L_{\sigma})=\Bbbk\neq 0
. Thus sub-case (i)
of the proof of Theorem
6.8
does not occur,
and we are in sub-case (ii), with
L^{\prime}=H_{0}(K;\Bbbk)=\Bbbk

the trivial local system on
\Gamma=\pi_{1}^{\orb}(C_{g},\bm{\mu})
.

It remains to decide between sub-cases (ii)(a) and (ii)(b).
For the trivial local system
L^{\prime}=\Bbbk
,

H_{1}(\Gamma;\,L^{\prime}\otimes L_{\rho})=H_{1}(\Gamma;\,L_{\rho})
. Since
f^{*}
is an isomorphism,

b_{1}(X)=b_{1}(C_{g})=2g
, so the condition
b_{1}(X)\geq 4

forces
g\geq 2
. By formula (
19
),

H_{1}(\Gamma;\,L_{\rho})\neq 0
for
all

\rho\in\Hom^{0}(\Gamma,\Bbbk^{\times})
, placing us in sub-case (ii)(a).
Hence
\Delta^{\sigma}(G)=0
, and this conclusion is independent
of
\ch(\Bbbk)
since formula (
19
) holds over any
algebraically closed field.
∎

6.4.
Examples

In this subsection, we construct two examples from the weighted right angled Artin groups,
which is a natural generalization of the right-angled Artin groups.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
\Gamma_{\ell}=(V,E,\ell)
be an edge-weighted finite simple graph, with
vertex set
V
, edge set
E
and an edge weight function
\ell\colon E\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
The weighted right-angled Artin group associated to
\Gamma_{\ell}
is the group

G_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
generated by the vertices
a\in V
, with a defining relation

[a_{i},a_{j}]^{\ell(e)}=1

for each edge
e=\{a_{i},a_{j}\}
in
E
(here
[a_{i},a_{j}]=a_{i}a_{j}a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}^{-1}
).
If
\ell(e)=1
for all
e\in E
, then
G_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
is the classical right-angled Artin group.

Such weighted right angled Artin groups are studied in
[
26
]
. In particular,
the Kähler weighted right angled Artin groups are classified in loc. cit..

Remark 6.11
.

For an edge with weight
m
in
G_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
connecting vertices

a
and
b
, we have the relation
[a,b]^{m}=1
in
G_{\Gamma_{\ell}}
.
The abelianization of the Fox derivative of this relation gives

\begin{pmatrix}m(1-b)&m(a-1)\end{pmatrix}^{T},

which is a column in the Alexander matrix. Such column vanishes if we
use field coefficient
\Bbbk
and
\mathrm{char}(\Bbbk)
divides
m
.
On the other hand, if we use complex coefficients, this column
is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}1-b&a-1\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

In particular, if
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a complete graph with
n
-vertices,
we have that
\mathcal{V}^{1}(G_{\Gamma_{\ell}},\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}
.

Example 6.12
.

Consider the group

G=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\mid[a_{1},a_{i}]=1\text{ for }2\leq i\leq 4,\;[a_{i},a_{j}]^{2}=1\text{ for }2\leq i<j\leq 4\rangle.

This is a weighted right-angled Artin group; it is shown in
[
26
]
that
G

is not a Kähler group.

By Remark
6.11
, the Fox-derivative columns contributed
by the weight-
2
relations
[a_{i},a_{j}]^{2}=1
are equivalent over
\mathbb{C}

to those of the corresponding unweighted commutator relations
[a_{i},a_{j}]=1
.
Hence the Alexander matrix of
G
over
\mathbb{C}
coincides with that of

\mathbb{Z}^{4}
, giving
\mathcal{V}^{1}(G,\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}
. Since this variety has no
codimension-one component in
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{4}
,
Proposition
2.3
(
1
)
gives
\Delta^{\id}(G)=1
over
\mathbb{C}
—consistent
with Kählerianity, so the untwisted test over
\mathbb{C}
yields no obstruction.

Over a field
\Bbbk
with
\ch(\Bbbk)=2
, the weight-
2
columns vanish
identically, and the Alexander matrix reduces to that of

\mathbb{Z}\times F_{3}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\mid[a_{1},a_{i}]=1,\,2\leq i\leq 4\rangle
.
The unique codimension-one component of
\mathcal{V}^{1}(G,\Bbbk)
is then
\{t_{1}=1\}
,
so Proposition
2.3
(
1
) gives

\Delta^{\id}(G)=t_{1}-1
over
\Bbbk
. Since
t_{1}-1
is neither
0
nor
1
,
Theorem
6.8
shows that
G
is not a Kähler group.

This example illustrates that Theorem
6.8
can
detect non-Kählerianity via fields of positive characteristic even when
the untwisted test over
\mathbb{C}
fails.

Example 6.13
.

Let
G
be the fundamental group of the complement of the Conway knot
K
.
Then the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{K}(t)=1
and as shown by
Friedl–Kim
[
21
, Sec. 5.2]
,
G
admits a representation

\sigma_{1}\colon G\to\GL_{4}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13})
factoring
through
S_{5}
, for which the twisted Alexander polynomial

\Delta_{K}^{\sigma_{1}}(t)\neq 1
. Here
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13}
is
an algebraically closed field with characteristic
13
. Hence we have

\mathcal{V}^{1}(G,\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}
and
\mathcal{V}^{1}(G,\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13})
consists of roots of

\Delta_{K}^{\sigma}(t)
and
1
.

For any
n\geq 2
, set
G_{2n-1}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{2n-1}\mid[a_{i},a_{j}]^{13}=1\text{ for any }1\leq i<j\leq 2n-1\rangle
.
By Remark
6.11
, we have

\mathcal{V}^{1}(G_{2n-1},\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}
and
\mathcal{V}^{1}(G_{2n-1},\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13})=(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13}^{\times})^{2n-1}
.
Using Proposition
2.1
, we have

\mathcal{V}^{1}(G\times G_{2n-1},\mathbb{C})=\{\mathbf{1}\}.
By Proposition
2.3
,
the first Alexander polynomial over
\mathbb{C}
of the group
G\times G_{2n-1}
is
1
, which
is consistent with Kählerianity in
[
17
, Theorem 4.3(3)]
. So the classical Alexander
polynomial gives no obstruction.

On the other hand, consider the representation
\sigma^{\prime}
of
G\times G_{2n-1}
given
by the composed homomorphism

G\times G_{2n-1}\longrightarrow G\longrightarrow\GL_{4}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13}),

where the first map is the quotient map and the second map is
the representation
\sigma_{1}
. Then by Proposition
2.1
,

\mathcal{V}^{1}(G\times G_{2n-1},L_{\sigma^{\prime}})
is a finite union
of codimension one torus of type
\{t_{1}-\lambda\}
with
\lambda

either a root of
\Delta_{K}^{\sigma_{1}}(t)
or
1
.
Due to Proposition
2.3
,

G\times G_{2n-1}
has non-trivial twisted Alexander polynomial
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{13}
, and thus it is not a Kähler group for any
n\geq 2
.

7.
3
-manifold groups

A
3
-manifold pair is a pair
(M,\phi)
, where
M
is a compact, connected,
orientable,
3
-manifold with toroidal or empty boundary, and
\phi\in\Hom(\pi_{1}(M),\mathbb{Z})
is nontrivial. We say that a
3
-manifold pair
(M,\phi)

fibers over
S^{1}
if there exists a fibration
p\colon M\to S^{1}
such that the
induced map
p_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\pi_{1}(S^{1})\cong\mathbb{Z}
coincides with
\phi
.
We refer to such
\phi
as a fibered class.

The
Thurston norm

\|\phi\|_{T}
of a class

\phi\in H^{1}(M;\mathbb{Z})
is defined as the infimum of
-\chi(S_{0})
,
where
S
runs though all the properly embedded, oriented
surfaces in
M
dual to
\phi
, and
S_{0}
denotes
the result of discarding all components of
S
which
are disks or spheres. In
[
37
]
, Thurston proved that

\|-\|_{T}
defines a seminorm on
H^{1}(M;\mathbb{Z})
, which can
be extended to a continuous seminorm on
H^{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
.

The unit norm ball,
B_{T}=\{\phi\in H^{1}(M;\mathbb{R})\mid\|\phi\|_{T}\leq 1\}
,
is a rational polyhedron with finitely many sides and which is symmetric
in the origin. Moreover, there are facets of
B_{T}
, called the
fibered faces

(coming in antipodal pairs), so that a class
\phi\in H^{1}(M;\mathbb{Z})
fibers if and
only if it lies in the cone over the interior of a fibered face.
In
[
6
, Thm. E]
, Bieri, Neumann, and Strebel showed that
the BNS invariant of
G=\pi_{1}(M)
is the projection onto
\mathrm{S}(G)
of
the open fibered faces of the Thurston norm ball
B_{T}
; in particular,

\Sigma^{1}(G)=-\Sigma^{1}(G)
.

On the other hand, the following results due to Friedl and Vidussi
[
22
]

shows that the non-fibered pair can be detected by the
twisted Alexander polynomial.

Theorem 7.1

(
[
22
, Thm. 1.1]
)
.

Let
(M,\phi)
be a
3
-manifold pair. If
\phi
is non-fibered, there exists
an epimorphism
\alpha\colon G=\pi_{1}(M)\twoheadrightarrow Q
onto a finite group such that,
letting
\sigma\coloneqq\reg\circ\alpha\colon G\to\GL_{|Q|}(\mathbb{Z})
,
where
\reg
denotes the regular representation of
Q
, we have

\Delta^{\phi,\sigma}(M)=0.

As a direct application, we show that the upper bound from
Theorem
5.5
is sharp for a large class
of
3
-manifold groups.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
M
be a compact, connected, orientable,
3
-manifold with toroidal
or empty boundary. Setting
G=\pi_{1}(M)
, we have

(22)

\Sigma^{1}(G)=\mathrm{S}\Bigg(\overline{\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)}\Bigg)^{\mathrm{c}},

where the union runs over all representations

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
with finite image.

Proof.

Theorem
5.5
and Corollary
5.6

give
\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)\subseteq\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}
for any collection of finite-image
representations
\sigma
. Since
\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}
is closed,
taking closures preserves this containment, so

\Sigma^{1}(G)\subseteq\mathrm{S}\Bigg(\overline{\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)}\Bigg)^{\mathrm{c}}.

For the reverse inclusion, it suffices to show that every non-fibered
primitive class
\phi\in H^{1}(M;\mathbb{Z})
lies in

\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)
for some
finite-image representation
\sigma
. Indeed, since
\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}

is a finite union of rational polyhedral cones
[
6
, Thm. E]
,
the primitive integral classes are dense in

\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}\cap S(G)
, and any non-fibered rational class is a
positive multiple of a primitive one. Every point of
\Sigma^{1}(G)^{\mathrm{c}}

is therefore a limit of primitive integral non-fibered classes,
hence belongs to the closure of
\bigcup_{\sigma}\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)

once those classes are shown to lie in the union itself.

Let
\phi\colon G\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a non-fibered primitive class.
By Theorem
7.1
, there exists a representation

\sigma\colon G\to\GL_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
with finite image such that, upon
extending
\sigma
over
\mathbb{C}
, we have
\Delta^{\phi,\sigma}(M)=0
.
The surjection
\phi
induces embeddings

\phi^{*}\colon\Hom(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{*})\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\Hom(G,\mathbb{C}^{*})\ \text{ and }\ \phi^{\#}\colon\Hom(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R})\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow H^{1}(G;\mathbb{R}).

By Proposition
2.3
(
2
),

\Delta^{\phi,\sigma}(M)=0
implies that

\mathbb{C}^{*}\cong\im(\phi^{*})\subset\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})
, and therefore

\im(\phi^{\#})=\mathbb{R}\cdot\phi\subset\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big).\qed

Question 7.3
.

With the notation of Theorem
7.2
, let

F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
be the non-fibered faces of the Thurston norm ball
B_{T}
,
and let
C_{j}
denote the closed cone over
F_{j}
.
For each non-fibered class
\phi\in\operatorname{int}(C_{j})
, the
Friedl–Vidussi theorem provides a finite-image representation

\sigma_{\phi}
such that
\Delta^{\phi,\sigma_{\phi}}(M)=0
.
Can this choice be made uniform on each face, i.e., does there exist,
for each
j
, a single representation
\sigma_{j}
such that

C_{j}\subset\Trop_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{1}(M,L_{\sigma_{j}}\otimes\mathbb{C})\big)?

An affirmative answer would imply that the closure in Theorem
7.2

can be taken over at most
k
representations, one for each non-fibered face
(the union being finite, and each summand closed, no closure is then needed).

Remark 7.4
.

The Friedl–Vidussi argument does not yield a positive answer to
Question
7.3
directly. For each non-fibered class

\phi\in\inter(C_{j})
, their proof produces a finite
quotient
\alpha_{\phi}\colon\pi_{1}(M)\twoheadrightarrow G_{\phi}

by applying a separability argument to a Thurston-norm minimizing
surface dual to
\phi
. Since that surface varies with
\phi
,
the resulting representation
\sigma_{\alpha_{\phi}}
varies as well,
and their method gives no control over whether a single finite-image
representation can certify
\Delta^{\phi,\sigma}(M)=0
for all
integral classes
\phi\in C_{j}
simultaneously.
