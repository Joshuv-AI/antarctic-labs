Prompt sensitivity of large language models in orthodontic patient counseling: a scenario-based experimental study
Authors: Ersin Yıldırım, Esra Tunalı, Şeniz Karaçay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as accessible sources of health information, including orthodontic patient counseling. While previous studies have evaluated the accuracy and reliability of AI-generated responses, the effect of prompt formulation on the safety and quality of orthodontic advice remains unclear. Understanding prompt sensitivity is essential for assessing the real-world reliability of conversational AI systems, as patient queries are typically expressed in diverse linguistic forms.MethodsThis in silico experimental study evaluated prompt sensitivity using 24 standardized orthodontic clinical scenarios. Each scenario was queried using four prompt formulations (brief layperson, detailed patient, professional clinical, and anxiety-driven), resulting in 96 prompts. These were submitted to four LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, and Claude), generating 384 responses. Responses were independently evaluated by two orthodontic experts using a predefined expert scoring rubric across accuracy, safety, completeness, and clarity. Consensus scores were analyzed using the Friedman test for prompt effects and the Kruskal–Wallis test for model comparisons. Unsafe response rates and prompt robustness indices were also calculated.ResultsSafety scores did not differ significantly across prompt formulations (χ²(3) = 3.40, p = 0.334) or between models (H = 0.17, p = 0.982). A total of 5 of 384 responses (1.3%) were classified as unsafe. Prompt robustness analysis demonstrated low variability (mean prompt robustness index = 0.15). Response length differed significantly across prompt types (p = 0.0029), whereas response time did not (p = 0.998). A significant difference in clarity scores was observed across models (p = 0.029), with post hoc analysis indicating higher clarity scores for ChatGPT than Claude (adjusted p = 0.020).ConclusionsLLMs demonstrated consistent and clinically safe performance in orthodontic patient counseling, with minimal sensitivity to prompt formulation. While prompt wording influenced response length, it did not affect clinical reliability. Differences between models were primarily related to clarity rather than content. LLMs may provide stable informational support across diverse patient queries; however, their outputs should remain adjunctive to professional care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09058-z.Keywords: Orthodontics, Artificial intelligence, Large language models, ChatGPT, Patient education, Prompt sensitivity, Digital health

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly emerged as powerful tools for generating, interpreting, and disseminating medical information. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled conversational systems such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot to produce complex and context-aware responses to health-related questions expressed in natural language. These models are trained on extremely large textual corpora and rely on deep neural network architectures capable of generating probabilistic language outputs that often resemble expert-written explanations [1–3]. Consequently, conversational AI systems are increasingly used by patients as easily accessible sources of health information before consulting healthcare professionals, particularly for understanding symptoms, treatment options, and potential self-management strategies [4, 5].

The integration of conversational artificial intelligence into healthcare has generated both opportunities and concerns. On the one hand, LLM-based systems may improve access to medical information and support patient education by providing immediate responses to common health-related questions [6–8]. On the other hand, the reliability of AI-generated medical information remains an important concern. Previous investigations have reported factual inaccuracies, incomplete clinical explanations, and the phenomenon commonly described as “hallucination,” in which language models generate plausible but incorrect information [9, 10]. Because these systems rely on probabilistic language prediction rather than explicit clinical reasoning, the quality and safety of generated responses may vary depending on the wording and context of the input query [11]. Consequently, the possibility that AI-generated responses may contain misleading or potentially unsafe recommendations has been highlighted as a critical limitation of conversational AI systems in healthcare environments [12–14].

In dentistry, the application of artificial intelligence and large language models has become an emerging field of investigation. Recent reviews have highlighted the potential roles of LLMs in dental diagnosis, decision support, education, and patient communication while also emphasizing important limitations related to accuracy, transparency, and clinical reliability [15]. Educational studies evaluating LLM performance in dental training contexts have demonstrated that these models can perform relatively well when responding to theoretical or knowledge-based questions, although limitations remain when addressing complex clinical scenarios requiring contextual interpretation [16].

Orthodontics represents a particularly relevant domain for evaluating AI-generated health information because treatment success strongly depends on patient cooperation, adherence to treatment instructions, and appropriate management of appliance-related issues. Patients undergoing orthodontic therapy frequently encounter situations such as loose brackets, protruding wires, discomfort following appliance activation, dietary restrictions, oral hygiene challenges, and questions regarding treatment duration or retention protocols. Investigations evaluating chatbot responses to orthodontic patient questions have reported that although AI-generated explanations are often understandable and informative, the clinical accuracy and completeness of the information may vary considerably [17].

Further research has evaluated the quality of AI-generated information in specific orthodontic contexts. Studies examining chatbot-generated explanations related to interceptive orthodontic treatment have demonstrated moderate to high levels of informational accuracy while emphasizing the need for professional verification of AI-generated advice [18]. Investigations assessing patient-oriented information produced by conversational AI systems in orthognathic surgery have similarly reported variability in accuracy, readability, and informational completeness across different chatbot platforms [19].

Recent orthodontic studies have shown that LLMs can provide informative responses to pretreatment, treatment-related, and general orthodontic questions; however, variability remains in clinical accuracy, completeness, readability, and safety considerations [20–22].

Similar findings have been reported in traumatic dental injuries and pediatric dentistry, where chatbot-generated information was generally useful but occasionally lacked clinical precision [23, 24].

Despite the rapidly expanding literature on artificial intelligence in dentistry and orthodontics, an important methodological factor remains insufficiently explored: prompt sensitivity. Prompt sensitivity refers to the phenomenon in which relatively small variations in the wording, structure, or contextual framing of a query can significantly influence the output generated by a language model [25]. Experimental studies in natural language processing have demonstrated that prompt formulation can strongly affect model reasoning behaviour, response accuracy, and informational completeness [26]. Techniques such as structured prompting and chain-of-thought prompting have been shown to influence how large language models generate explanations and solve complex reasoning tasks [27]. Comprehensive surveys of prompting strategies further emphasize that prompt design plays a central role in determining the reliability and interpretability of LLM outputs [28]. Recent investigations evaluating prompt sensitivity in medical applications have likewise demonstrated that variations in query structure may influence the performance and interpretation capabilities of large language models in clinical tasks [29].

Variability in AI-generated medical information may have important implications for patient understanding, risk perception, and treatment decision-making. In orthodontics, where patient cooperation and adherence to treatment instructions are essential for successful treatment outcomes, inconsistencies in AI-generated advice may influence patient behaviour and potentially affect clinical safety. Understanding how variations in patient question phrasing influence the reliability of AI-generated orthodontic information is therefore critical for evaluating the real-world applicability of conversational AI systems in orthodontic patient education.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the prompt sensitivity of large language models in orthodontic patient counseling using standardized clinical scenarios. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first systematic investigations examining how variations in prompt formulation influence the safety, accuracy, completeness, and clarity of AI-generated orthodontic advice across standardized clinical scenarios. We hypothesized that (H1) prompt formulation would significantly influence safety scores, (H2) prompt formulation would influence accuracy, completeness, and clarity scores, and (H3) differences would exist among LLM platforms regarding response quality.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a scenario-based in silico experimental study to evaluate the prompt sensitivity of large language models (LLMs) in orthodontic patient counseling. The study assessed how variations in prompt formulation influence the safety, accuracy, completeness, and clarity of AI-generated orthodontic advice.

The study comprised four sequential stages: (1) development of standardized orthodontic clinical scenarios, (2) construction of linguistically distinct prompt formulations, (3) collection of AI-generated responses from selected LLM platforms, and (4) blinded expert evaluation of responses using predefined scoring criteria and statistical analysis. This approach is consistent with general research ethics principles for non-interventional studies without human subject participation [30].

Development of orthodontic clinical scenarios

A total of 24 standardized orthodontic clinical scenarios were developed to represent common patient-facing situations encountered during orthodontic treatment. Scenario development was informed by the literature on orthodontic urgencies and emergencies, treatment interruptions, patient adherence, oral hygiene compliance, elastics cooperation, and patient experience with temporary anchorage devices [31–37]. The scenarios were independently reviewed by the three investigators, all orthodontists with academic and clinical experience, to ensure clinical relevance, realism, and coverage of common orthodontic patient concerns.

To ensure clinical breadth and relevance, scenarios were grouped into four conceptual categories:

The emergency and urgent scenarios were informed by published evidence showing that bracket debonding, archwire irritation, pain, and treatment disruption are among the most frequently encountered orthodontic unscheduled problems [31–33]. The routine counseling and adherence-related scenarios were supported by the literature on orthodontic patient compliance, oral hygiene maintenance, and elastics use [34–36]. The miniscrew-related scenario was additionally grounded in published evidence on patient acceptance and pain experience associated with temporary anchorage devices [37].

Prompt design was informed by prior prompt engineering and natural language processing studies [25–29]. Prompt construction was standardized so that the underlying clinical content remained constant while the linguistic framing changed. This design was chosen because recent orthodontic LLM studies have shown that chatbot outputs may vary depending on wording, framing, and intended audience in patient-education settings [38–40]. Prompt formulations were reviewed by the three orthodontic investigators to ensure clinical relevance, consistency, and linguistic distinction before data collection.

All prompts were preceded by the same instruction sentence: “Please provide general informational guidance only and do not assume the role of a healthcare provider.” This statement was included to standardize model framing across all prompt types and reduce uncontrolled variation related to implicit role assignment. These platforms were selected because they represent contemporary public-facing LLM systems that patients may realistically encounter when seeking orthodontic information online, and comparable model families have been used in recent orthodontic and dental AI comparison studies [38–40].

Responses were generated between 23 March 2026 and 25 March 2026 using the web-based interfaces of ChatGPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google), Microsoft Copilot, and Claude (Anthropic). All models were accessed through publicly available user accounts under identical geographic and login conditions. The exact model versions displayed by the platforms at the time of data collection were recorded. The evaluated models were ChatGPT 5.3, Gemini 3, Microsoft Copilot, and Claude Sonnet 4.6. No API access was used.

Response generation procedure

Each of the 24 scenarios was submitted to each of the four LLM systems using each of the four predefined prompt types. Thus, the full design yielded:

scenarios × 4 prompt types × 4 models = 384 AI-generated responses

To avoid contextual carryover, every scenario–prompt–model combination was entered in a new chat session. No follow-up questions were asked. Only the first generated response was recorded. Responses were copied verbatim without editing. Additionally, response length (word count) and response time (seconds) were recorded as secondary outcomes.

To reduce order effects, the full set of scenario–prompt–model combinations was randomized using a computer-generated randomization list prior to data collection.

Inter-rater agreement

Disagreement occurred in 28 of 384 responses (7.3%), and all disagreements were resolved through discussion resulting in consensus scores. Inter-rater agreement between evaluators was high (weighted Cohen’s kappa = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.83–0.91), indicating strong consistency in scoring.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effect of prompt formulation on the safety, accuracy, completeness, and clarity of AI-generated orthodontic advice using standardized clinical scenarios. Contrary to expectations derived from prompt engineering literature, variations in prompt wording did not significantly influence the safety of responses generated by large language models. Instead, AI-generated orthodontic advice remained stable across different linguistic formulations, with low variability and minimal unsafe output rates. Although not statistically significant, slightly lower safety scores observed with the brief layperson prompt may reflect reduced contextual information available to the model. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the rapidly expanding literature evaluating AI chatbot performance in dentistry and orthodontics. Previous studies have consistently shown that large language models can provide informative and generally accurate responses to orthodontic questions, although with varying degrees of reliability and clinical adequacy [17–24]. As summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, most existing investigations have focused on fixed question sets or general patient queries, typically reporting moderate-to-high accuracy while emphasizing the need for professional verification [38–40, 44, 45]. Prior orthodontic studies have also highlighted variability in accuracy, readability, and reliability across AI systems [19, 21, 22], as well as inconsistencies in risk-related counseling [39]. Similarly, studies in related dental domains suggest that although chatbot-generated responses can support patient education, they may lack sufficient clinical nuance for independent clinical use [23, 24].

A key methodological distinction of the present study is the explicit evaluation of prompt sensitivity. As demonstrated in Supplementary Table S3, most prior studies did not incorporate multiple prompt formulations or systematically assess how linguistic variation influences model outputs. Even scenario-based studies typically relied on a single phrasing per scenario [18], thereby overlooking an important dimension of real-world patient–AI interaction. By integrating multiple prompt types within a standardized framework, the present study provides a more realistic and methodologically robust evaluation of conversational AI behavior.

Despite theoretical evidence suggesting that prompt design can substantially influence model outputs [25–29], the present findings indicate that such effects may be less pronounced in orthodontic counseling contexts. One explanation may be that the clinical scenarios used represent well-defined and commonly encountered situations, allowing language models to generate more standardized responses. In such cases, semantic content may dominate over linguistic variation, leading to consistent outputs regardless of prompt phrasing. This observation is consistent with reports indicating that LLMs perform robustly in structured clinical queries [4, 11, 45].

Another important finding is the low rate of unsafe responses. Across all scenarios, unsafe recommendations were rare, contrasting with concerns in the literature regarding hallucinations and potentially harmful outputs [9, 10, 12]. This may reflect the relatively structured nature of orthodontic counseling scenarios. Nevertheless, even low-frequency unsafe outputs may carry clinical importance, particularly in self-management contexts [39].

The overall findings demonstrate a high level of consistency across multiple evaluation domains, including safety, accuracy, and completeness, which remained stable across both prompt formulations and AI models. This multidimensional robustness suggests that LLM-generated orthodontic advice is largely insensitive to variations in input phrasing.

In contrast, differences were observed in clarity, where model-dependent variation reached statistical significance. Post hoc analysis demonstrated that ChatGPT produced significantly clearer responses than Claude, while no other pairwise differences were identified. This suggests that variability across models is more related to communication style, readability, and information structuring rather than differences in clinical content. From a clinical perspective, this distinction is important, as clarity directly influences patient comprehension and usability of AI-generated information.

Response length analysis further supports this interpretation. Although prompt formulation did not affect safety or clinical correctness, it significantly influenced response length, with professional-style prompts generating longer outputs. This indicates that prompt engineering may primarily affect the verbosity and presentation of responses rather than their clinical reliability.

Taken together, these findings provide a nuanced understanding of LLM behavior in orthodontic patient counseling. While clinical content remains stable across prompts and models, communication characteristics such as clarity and response length may vary. This distinction has important implications for the integration of conversational AI systems into patient education.

The clinical relevance of these findings lies in the observation that patients rarely use standardized language when seeking information. The demonstrated robustness across prompt formulations suggests that LLMs can provide consistent orthodontic guidance across diverse patient queries. However, differences in clarity between models indicate that the choice of AI system may influence how effectively information is communicated.

Several limitations should be considered. Although standardized scenarios were used, real-world patient queries may exhibit greater variability and complexity. Prompt formulations were investigator-developed rather than derived from real patient queries, which may limit ecological validity. An additional factor that may have influenced the observed robustness is the standardized instruction that preceded all prompts (“Please provide general informational guidance only and do not assume the role of a healthcare provider”). Although this approach improved experimental standardization, it may also have encouraged more cautious and conservative responses across models. Consequently, the low rate of unsafe outputs and the limited differences between prompt formulations may partially reflect the influence of this instruction. Real-world patient interactions may not include such guidance, potentially resulting in greater variability in model behavior. The study evaluated only single-turn interactions. Safety issues may emerge during multi-turn conversations involving follow-up questions, clarification requests, or contradictory information. Therefore, the present findings should not be generalized to extended real-world patient–AI interactions. The observed safety performance reflects expert-rated responses within predefined simulated scenarios and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of safety in real-world patient interactions. Expert-based evaluation may introduce subjectivity despite high inter-rater agreement. The scoring rubric was based on expert judgment and, although inter-rater agreement was high, the evaluation inevitably contains subjective elements. Furthermore, the rubric has not been externally validated and therefore reflects expert-rated quality rather than objective clinical outcomes. Model performance reflects a specific time point and may evolve with future updates. Model comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the repeated-measures structure of the dataset may not be fully captured by non-parametric between-group analyses. Response time was evaluated as an exploratory outcome and may have been influenced by external factors such as server load, internet latency, platform-specific infrastructure, and account-level prioritization. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as intrinsic performance differences between LLMs. The study evaluated English-language prompts only; findings may not generalize to multilingual patient interactions. Finally, patient understanding and behavioral outcomes were not evaluated.

Future research should investigate more complex and multilingual scenarios, evaluate patient comprehension, and explore real-world patient–AI interactions to better understand the clinical impact of conversational AI systems in orthodontics.

Conclusion

Large language models demonstrated generally safe, accurate, complete, and clear responses across the standardized orthodontic scenarios evaluated in this study. Variations in prompt formulation had minimal influence on expert-rated response quality, although response length differed significantly among prompt types. While overall performance was consistent across models, differences in clarity were observed, with ChatGPT generating clearer responses than Claude.

These findings suggest that LLMs may provide relatively stable informational support across diverse orthodontic patient queries. However, the results are based on expert-rated responses within simulated clinical scenarios and should not be interpreted as evidence of real-world clinical safety. Accordingly, LLM-generated information should be considered a supplementary educational resource rather than a substitute for professional orthodontic consultation.
