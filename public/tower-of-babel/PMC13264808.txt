Cryoneurolysis of auriculotemporal nerve for treatment of otalgia
Authors: Nikit Patel, Shireen Rabiei, Daniel Moon, Yevgeniya Sergeyenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOtalgia in adults is often secondary to non-otologic pathology, particularly with normal otoscopic examination. Auriculotemporal neuralgia (ATN) is a rare and overlooked cause of secondary otalgia. We present a case of an Army veteran with chronic right otalgia and dizziness following blast-related mild traumatic brain injury who was initially diagnosed with vestibular migraine. Despite significant improvement in headache symptoms with migraine-directed preventive and abortive treatments, otalgia persisted. Auriculotemporal nerve block produced complete but short-lived pain relief, confirming ATN. Cryoneurolysis of the auriculotemporal nerve resulted in complete resolution of otalgia for 2–3 months after first and second treatments. However, transient worsening of tinnitus, ear pressure and dizziness also occurred but eventually returned to baseline. This case highlights cryoneurolysis as a potential therapeutic option for refractory ATN-related otalgia.

Background

Otalgia is ear pain that can be either primary, occurring within the ear itself or secondary, referred pain from another source.1 In a retrospective study of 388 904 009 emergency room visits from 2009 to 2011, otologic problems represented 2.21% of all visits.2 When stratified by age, otologic diagnoses were less common in adults, accounting for 1.01% of all emergency department visits.2 Otologic problems in adults are also more likely to be secondary, with approximately 50% of cases arising from non-otologic pathology.3 Otalgia appears to have a higher prevalence in military service members; in a study of more than 250 patients with military-related blast injuries, 26% reported otalgia.4

The differential diagnosis for otalgia includes mechanical, inflammatory, infectious and neoplastic causes. In adults, a normal otoscopic examination is strongly suggestive of secondary otalgia (SO). The aetiology of SO can be difficult to discern given the complex innervation of the ear and the many possible sources of referred pain. Common causes of SO include temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, dental pathology, pharyngitis, sinusitis and cervical spine arthritis.5 6 Less common causes include injury to the auriculotemporal nerve and vestibular migraine, which can present with ear fullness, tinnitus, dizziness and, rarely, otalgia.7 8

The auriculotemporal nerve is a sensory nerve that originates from the posterior division of the mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve.9 Based on its anatomical course, there are areas that may be more prone to nerve entrapment. The nerve surfaces onto the face from behind the TMJ within the superior surface of the parotid gland. It passes over the posterior portion of the zygoma and divides into superficial temporal branches. The cutaneous branches supply the tragus and part of the adjacent auricle of the ear and the posterior part of the temple.10 Given the nerve’s proximity to the lateral pterygoid muscle and TMJ in the infratemporal fossa, spasm of the lateral pterygoid or even chewing can cause irritation of the nerve.

The auriculotemporal nerve can become entrapped or irritated, leading to auriculotemporal neuralgia (ATN) and causing otalgia. ATN is characterised by unilateral pain in the periauricular and temporal region. Some report 0.23–0.4% of the patients in tertiary headache centres presented with ATN.11 12 However, the true prevalence and incidence of ATN remain unclear, with most available data limited to case reports and small case series. As a result, diagnosis of ATN relies heavily on its clinical presentation, which often manifests as attacks of paroxysmal tenderness or pain in the distribution of the auriculotemporal nerve in a unilateral fashion.13 Due to its rarity, guidelines regarding the treatment of ATN are also sparse.

Current treatment options include oral medications and local procedures, such as anaesthetic nerve blocks, the effects of which are often short-lived. Cryoneurolysis is the application of cold temperatures to ablate targeted nerves, oftentimes to provide pain relief.14 15 Unlike traditional anaesthetic nerve blocks, cryoneurolysis provides longer lasting relief by inducing Wallerian degeneration distally with slow axonal regeneration (1–2 mm/day) until conduction is restored.15 While cryoneurolysis has not been previously used to treat otalgia, it has been used successfully for treatment of occipital neuralgia.16 Here we present a case of a patient with SO, suspected to be ATN, treated with cryoneurolysis.

Case presentation

A male Army veteran in his 40s with a history of mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) from a mortar attack presented to an interdisciplinary outpatient mTBI rehabilitation programme with persistent right otalgia, imbalance, vertigo and chronic migraine. Otalgia began after a close-proximity blast in 2007 while on active duty and was associated with significant hearing loss and imbalance. After evaluation by an otolaryngologist, there was concern for persistent ear fistula and he underwent repair in 2009. Hearing and balance subsequently improved, but otalgia persisted. In 2019, he began developing worsening episodes of vertigo, imbalance, tinnitus, headaches and otalgia.

On initial evaluation, patient’s otalgia was 5–6/10 at baseline, worsening with activity, cold weather and sinus infections. He described his dizziness as a sensation of room spinning, occurring three to four times monthly. Regarding chronic migraines, these began after his mTBI in 2007. He had no prior history of migraines. One of his daughters also has migraines, but family history was negative for other headache disorders. At initial evaluation, he had about three bitemporal migraines weekly, with associated photophobia.

Functionally, he was independent for mobility and all activities of daily living, including driving. He was unable to work due to ongoing persistent symptoms.

Investigations

Patient had seen multiple specialists for his symptoms. MRI of the brain and internal auditory canal (IAC) with and without contrast demonstrated normal-appearing IACs including the cochlear apparatus and semicircular canals. On CT scan of the temporal bone, the cochlear apparatus and semicircular canals were grossly normal and cranial nerve VII was intact.

In 2023, patient was re-evaluated by the surgeon who previously performed his right ear fistula repair. Otoscopic examination and flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy were reportedly normal. Since no known otologic pathology was identified, it was felt that his symptoms were suggestive of a migraine process. Neurology follow-up was recommended for ongoing management of migraine.

Differential diagnosis

Working diagnoses for this patient included vestibular migraine and ATN. Although patient’s migraines significantly improved with treatment, right otalgia remained, independent of his migraines. With anaesthetic peripheral nerve block to the auriculotemporal nerve, patient reported immediate relief of right otalgia, which lasted for about 1 week, confirming ATN.

Treatment

Prior to initial evaluation at the multidisciplinary mTBI clinic, patient had trialled multiple preventive and rescue treatments for migraines, including topiramate, propranolol, amitriptyline and sumatriptan (table 1). With all trialled medications, patient either had no improvement in symptoms or was unable to tolerate the medication due to side effects. He was unable to take gabapentin due to allergy. Therefore, the patient started onabotulinumtoxinA injections with PREEMPT (Phase III REsearch Evaluating Migraine Prophylaxis Therapy) protocol17 for preventive treatment of chronic migraines. There was significant improvement in migraine frequency and severity with injections. His migraine frequency decreased from three times per week to three times per month, and there was a decrease in severity as well. For breakthrough migraines, he was initially started on rimegepant 75 mg as needed. Unfortunately, he experienced side effects of tingling around his mouth and worsening of his baseline dizziness. He was therefore switched to ubrogepant 100 mg as needed and had good relief without adverse effects. Although the patient’s migraines decreased in frequency and severity with the combination of botulinum toxin injections and ubrogepant, there was no change in right otalgia.

Right otalgia was initially addressed with peripheral nerve anaesthetic blocks with 1:1 solution of 1% lidocaine and 0.5% bupivacaine to the auriculotemporal nerve. Using two 1 mL syringes, each with a 30-gauge, 0.5-inch needle, 2.0 mL total of anaesthetic solution was injected; 0.5 mL was injected anterior to the tragus, at the proximal part of the nerve and 0.5 mL was injected at each of three sites at the superior nerve branches, using the follow-the-pain method. Peripheral nerve blocks were initially performed weekly for 3 weeks, with good response each time. Patient reported his pain decreased from 6 to 7/10 to 1/10 immediately following the nerve block and relief persisted for about 1 week postprocedure.

Given the positive, but short-lived response to the peripheral nerve blocks, the option of cryoneurolysis was discussed with the patient, with the goal of providing longer-lasting relief. 2 months after the last nerve block, the patient underwent cryoneurolysis of the right auriculotemporal nerve. Ultrasound was used to localise nerve branches and avoid vasculature (figure 1). 1.0 mL of 1% lidocaine was injected for local anaesthetic. Two sites along the nerve were then treated with cryoneurolysis. Using the Iovera system with a trident probe, liquid nitrous oxide was injected at –88° Celsius for 60 s at each site (Pacira BioSciences).

Outcome and follow-up

Patient reported a decrease in right ear pain immediately after completion of cryoneurolysis, stating that his pain had decreased from 7/10 to 4/10. One week postprocedure, he reported complete resolution of right otalgia, rating his pain 0/10. However, he also noted increased right ear pressure and worsened tinnitus, as well as increased dizziness. Effects of cryoneurolysis persisted for 3 months postprocedure, at which time his ear pain returned. Right ear pressure, worsened tinnitus and dizziness returned to baseline at this time.

Given the positive effect of the cryoneurolysis on his pain, the patient elected to undergo repeat cryoneurolysis 6 months later. Once again, 1 week postprocedure, the patient reported complete resolution of right otalgia, rating his pain 0/10. However, he unfortunately noted return of increased right ear pressure and worsening tinnitus and dizziness. Effects of the second round of cryoneurolysis have so far persisted for 2 months postprocedure.

Discussion

This is the first report that we know of describing the use of cryoneurolysis to successfully treat otalgia. Although ATN is rare in the general population, it may be more common in active-duty military service members and veterans given the prevalence of blast exposure and potential for traumatic injury to the auriculotemporal nerve. Specific evidence for the treatment of ATN is limited; treatment is based on recommendations for treatment of other peripheral neuralgias, especially trigeminal neuralgia.

Oral medications used are chosen for their efficacy in treatment of similar conditions. Common options include carbamazepine, a Food and Drug Administration-approved treatment of trigeminal neuralgia, and gabapentin, which is commonly used to treat neuropathic pain. Local anaesthetic nerve blocks are an option for intervention; however, evidence is limited to case series and reports.1820 Additionally, case reports suggest that botulinum toxin injections and pulsed radiofrequency may also provide some pain relief.11 12

Cryoneurolysis is a procedure used for treatment of pain, including occipital neuralgia.16 Like ATN, occipital neuralgia is a craniofacial neuralgia characterised by paroxysmal neuropathic pain and can cause unilateral or bilateral headache.16 Occipital neuralgia may occur secondary to irritation, inflammation, compression or injury to the occipital nerves. In a prospective, single-arm study of 26 patients treated with cryoneurolysis for occipital neuralgia, 88%, 64% and 74% of patients reported clinically significant pain relief immediately after treatment, at Day 7, and at Day 30, respectively.16 35% of patients had up to 2 months of pain relief.16 In this case, we found that cryoneurolysis of the auriculotemporal nerve provided 2–3 months of relief in ATN, similar to studies of patients with occipital neuralgia.

Anatomical variations may influence the success of targeted blocks and cryoneurolysis. Three terminal branches of the auriculotemporal nerve, the anterior auricular branch and the superior and inferior branches, contribute sensory innervation to the external auditory apparatus, including the pinna and external auditory meatus.9 Classically, these branches are described as leaving the main trunk of the auriculotemporal nerve posterior to the mandibular condyle. However, a variant of the auriculotemporal nerve where the anterior auricular branch arises more superiorly, from the posterior of two main superficial temporal trunks, has also been described.9

While this patient experienced improvement in his otalgia, he had unexpected symptoms of worsened tinnitus, increased right ear pressure and dizziness after undergoing the procedure. One possible explanation could be increased sensitivity or inadvertent involvement of terminal nerve branches that were not targeted.21 22 Additionally, the auriculotemporal nerve has branches that supply sensory innervation to the TMJ, and postprocedural irritation may contribute to referred otologic symptoms.9

Elucidating the aetiology of otalgia is challenging due to the broad differential diagnosis. Even when the diagnosis is narrowed, there can be overlap between otalgia and vestibular migraine. This is complicated further when considering ATN as a cause of otalgia with the complex and potentially varied anatomy of the auriculotemporal nerve. Specific guidelines regarding the diagnosis of ATN are lacking, and treatment of referred otalgia can be challenging. Cryoneurolysis may be a good option, but this should be a shared decision with the patient, carefully weighing the potential benefits against the possible side effects. This patient elected to repeat the cryoneurolysis procedure given the significant pain relief he experienced despite the unexpected side effects. While it is an infrequent case presentation, our hope is this case report provides another pain-relieving modality to consider when tailoring patient-centred treatment plans.

Learning points

Auriculotemporal neuralgia is a rare and frequently overlooked cause of secondary otalgia and should be considered when patients present with unilateral periauricular or temporal pain.
