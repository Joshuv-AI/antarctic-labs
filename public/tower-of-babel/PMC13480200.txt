Organizational support and professional identity among emergency medical workers: the mediating role of emotional labor
Authors: Xiao Zhu, Zhi Zeng, Zihe Zhao, Yijuan He, Yan Liu, Zhihua Kuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the relationships and internal mechanisms among professional identity, organizational support, and emotional labor in emergency medical workers.MethodsBetween March and April 2024, we surveyed a convenience sample of 643 emergency medical workers from 54 hospitals across 12 Chinese provinces using the Perceived Organizational Support Scale, Emotional Labor Strategy Scale, and Nursing Career Identity Scale. SPSS 22.0 and AMOS 21.0 software were used to statistically process the data. A structural equation model mediated by emotional labor was constructed.ResultsSignificant gender differences were found in professional identity and emotional labor among emergency medical workers (t = 2.825, -2.115, P < 0.05). Professional identity also differed significantly by professional title (F = 2.749, P < 0.05). Organizational support, professional identity, and emotional labor showed statistically significant differences with years of work experience (F = 3.998, 4.023, 2.681, P < 0.05). There was a significant positive correlation between organizational support, professional identity, and emotional labor among emergency medical workers (r = 0.527, 0.326, and 0.346; P < 0.001). The associations among organizational support, emotional labor, and professional identity were examined in a sample of emergency medical workers. Specifically, deep acting (Estimate = 0.104, P = 0.012) and the expression of naturally felt emotions (Estimate = 0.102, P = 0.011) showed significant indirect associations in the relationship between organizational support and professional identity, while surface acting did not show a significant indirect association (Estimate = -0.001, P = 0.855).ConclusionsBy examining the interrelationships between occupational identity, organizational support, and emotional labor, this study addresses a significant research gap concerning emergency medical workers. The findings offer valuable theoretical insights and practical implications for organizing and managing responses to public health events. The results highlight the importance of organizational support and emotional labor as factors associated with the emotional management skills of emergency medical workers, which may, in turn, be positively associated with their professional identities.Keywords: Emergency medical workers, Organizational support, Professional identity, Emotional labor

Introduction

In recent years, sudden public health events such as epidemics, natural disasters, and accidents have tested the quality and abilities of emergency medical workers (EMWs) [1]. EMWs subjected to high physiological and psychological pressure [2] often experience adverse emotional reactions such as anxiety and depression [3]. Given this context, enhancing the professional identity of EMWs is likely to promote the development of emergency medical teams and improve the quality of medical services. The emotional labor demands on EMWs are distinct from those on other healthcare workers. Unlike in scheduled care settings, EMWs operate under unpredictable surges in patient volume and acuity. This requires rapid, often instantaneous, emotional regulation in response to traumatic events and patient deaths. Such conditions are particularly conducive to resource-depleting strategies like surface acting and pose unique challenges to authentic emotional engagement. Consequently, understanding how organizational support translates into adaptive emotional labor and, in turn, professional identity is critically important for this specific occupational group. Therefore, we considered it valuable to examine the mechanisms associated with the professional identity of EMWs, as the findings may offer insights for hospital managers in proposing, developing, and applying organizational strategies to support such workers in the future.

Organizational support and professional identity

Professional identity, derived from identity achievement theory, refers to an individual’s approval attitude and self-awareness toward the social value of their profession [4]. The professional identity of healthcare professionals contributes to reducing job burnout [5], consolidating career intention, enhancing professional well-being [6], and work engagement [7]. Perceived organizational support—employees’ perception of the extent to which the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being—has been associated with fairness, supervisor support, and organizational rewards [8]. When employees perceive that an organization values them, they are inclined to repay the organization with commitment and loyalty [9].

In healthcare, organizational support is associated with reduced turnover intention [10] and increased job satisfaction [11]. Previous research has shown that occupational support is related to doctors’ professional identity [12]. For example, the degree of professional autonomy granted to doctors is positively correlated with their professional identity [13]. In medical education, providing a supportive environment has been found to be important in developing medical students’ professional identities [14].

Emotional labor as a mediating variable

Emotional labor refers to “the act of managing personal feelings to display observable facial expressions and body language to others in public places” [15]. Current research identifies three distinct emotional labor strategies: surface acting (faking unfelt emotions), deep acting (modifying inner feelings to genuinely experience expected emotions), and expression of naturally felt emotions (spontaneously displaying genuine emotions that align with organizational expectations) [16].

Research has demonstrated the importance of understanding emotional labor in organizations [17]. Healthcare professionals are required to empathize with patients and adjust their own emotions to improve work quality, engaging in high-intensity emotional labor. Emotional dysregulation experienced by healthcare professionals has been linked to job burnout [18], work stress, and intention to leave [19]. Better emotional labor capabilities are associated with clinical empathy [20], job satisfaction [21], and prosocial service behavior. Factors such as job autonomy, leader support, and colleague support in organizational contexts have been shown to relate to emotional labor [22], which in turn is associated with employees’ work engagement [23].

Model and hypotheses of this study

Although substantial literature exists on the relationships between professional identity, organizational support, and emotional labor, most studies have focused on only two of these variables. Few studies have analyzed the mechanisms governing relationships among all three, particularly among EMWs.

This study is grounded in Grandey’s model of emotional labor, which positions emotional labor as a central mediating mechanism linking situational antecedents to individual consequences. The model posits that organizational factors (e.g., job autonomy, supervisor and coworker support) are critical antecedents of emotional labor, and that the outcomes of emotional labor are manifested in employees’ work behaviors and attitudes [24]. This theoretical framework is particularly applicable to emergency medical work, where high-pressure situations place heavy emotional demands on healthcare workers. Consequently, Grandey’s model provides a useful framework for understanding the relationships between organizational resources, emotional labor, and healthcare workers’ well-being and performance.

Building upon this foundation, the present study introduces professional identity as a key outcome variable. For EMWs, a positive professional identity signifies a deep understanding of their unique role within the healthcare system. By incorporating professional identity into the model, this study aims to investigate how emotional labor relates to fundamental cognitive and attitudinal perceptions of their professional role among EMWs.

In summary, within the context of emergency medicine, organizational factors are associated with professional identity through their relationship with emotional labor. Accordingly, this study tested three hypotheses: (H1) organizational support is positively associated with professional identity; (H2) emotional labor mediates this relationship; and (H3) this mediating role differs across the three dimensions of emotional labor (surface acting, deep acting, and naturally felt emotions). Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

We drew the study sample from 54 hospitals in 12 Chinese provinces between March and April 2024. To ensure geographic diversity, we selected four provinces from each of the eastern, central, and western regions of China based on the research team’s accessibility and established collaborative networks. Within each selected province, secondary and tertiary hospitals were invited to participate based on their willingness and the feasibility of data collection. Primary hospitals were excluded as they typically lack emergency departments. Among the participating hospitals, the ratio of secondary to tertiary hospitals was approximately 1:1. The adequacy of the sample size was assessed based on guidelines specific to structural equation modeling (SEM). According to the AMOS output, the proposed mediation model contained 21 freely estimated parameters. Following the recommendations of Kline (2023), who suggests a minimum ratio of 10 to 20 cases per estimated parameter, the current sample size of N = 643 yields a cases-per-parameter ratio of approximately 30.6:1, which exceeds the recommended 20:1 threshold. Therefore, the sample size was deemed more than adequate for obtaining stable and trustworthy SEM solutions.

We designed an online questionnaire that could be completed on mobile phones via the Sojump website (https://www.wjx.cn/). Trained investigators distributed the questionnaire link in hospitals, and medical personnel completed and submitted the questionnaire onsite. A total of 697 questionnaires were collected, of which 54 were deemed invalid owing to patterned responses or consecutive use of the same option. The final valid sample size was 643; the sample structures are presented in Table 1.

At the time of data collection (March–April 2024), the institutional ethics committee had not yet been established. Measures

Nurse’s career identity scale (NCIS)

The Nursing Career Identity Scale was developed by the Nursing Management Teaching and Research Section of the Department of Nursing, School of Medicine, University of Tokyo, Japan. The scale is characterized by concise items and strong operational utility, and has been previously used in countries including Japan and Sweden. Zhao Hong et al. culturally adapted the scale into Chinese through a rigorous process involving translation, back-translation, and cross-cultural adjustment, incorporating discussions with psychology and medicine professionals. This ensured that the final Chinese version is semantically equivalent to the original, linguistically natural, and contextually appropriate for China's healthcare culture, thereby validating its use for measuring professional identity among Chinese healthcare workers [25]. NCIS comprises ten items categorized into three dimensions: sense of consistency, sense of significance, and sense of self-efficacy toward work. Example items for each dimension include, for sense of consistency: “I find my work in healthcare to be a good fit for me.”; for sense of significance: “My work in healthcare is personally fulfilling to me.”; for sense of self-efficacy: “I feel confident in my ability to perform my professional duties.” These items are listed in Appendix A. Participants rated each item using a 5-point Likert scale (5 = “Strongly Agree”; 1 = “Strongly Disagree”). The total score is calculated by summing all responses, resulting in a possible range from 10 to 50, where a higher score indicates a stronger professional identity. The Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.934.

Before the administration of this study, the scale was revised and the factor structure was adjusted through structural equation modeling to improve its fit. The revised scale was then subjected to confirmatory factor analysis using Amos software, with the following results: goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.912, normed fit index (NFI) = 0.946, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.951, incremental fit index (IFI) = 0.951, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.931, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.113. This first-order multi-factor measurement model was significantly superior to a single-factor model, with the following fit indices: GFI = 0.803, NFI = 0.874, CFI = 0.880, IFI = 0.880, TLI = 0.931, RMSEA = 0.170, and Δχ2/Δdf = 129.454 (P < 0.001). This indicates that the three core constructs examined in this study possess good discriminant validity. Regarding convergent validity, the composite reliability (CR) values for the three dimensions were 0.912, 0.908, and 0.884, respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.712, 0.766, and 0.793. For discriminant validity, the square roots of the AVE (√AVE) were 0.849, 0.875, and 0.891, further supporting the distinctiveness of the constructs.

Perceived organizational support scale (POSS)

This study also used the Perceived Organizational Support Scale, developed by Ling et al. [26]. The scale consists of 24 items divided into three dimensions: work support, value recognition, and interest concerns. Example items for each dimension include, for work support: “The hospital provides me with assistance when I encounter problems at work.”; for value recognition: “The hospital takes pride in my accomplishments at work.”; for interest concerns: “The hospital shows concern for my well-being.” These items are listed in Appendix B. Participants rated each item using a 6-point Likert scale (6 = “Strongly Agree”; 1 = “Strongly Disagree”). The total score is calculated by summing all responses, resulting in a possible range from 24 to 144, where a higher score indicates a stronger perception of organizational support. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.974.

The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results for the scale are as follows: GFI = 0.809, NFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.920, IFI = 0.920, TLI = 0.911, and RMSEA = 0.090. This first-order multi-factor measurement model was significantly superior to a single-factor model, with the following fit indices: GFI = 0.642, NFI = 0.827, CFI = 0.840, IFI = 0.840, TLI = 0.825, RMSEA = 0.127, and Δχ2/Δdf = 432.679 (P < 0.001). This indicates that the three core constructs examined in this study possess good discriminant validity. Regarding convergent validity, the composite reliability (CR) values for the three dimensions were 0.971, 0.902, and 0.947, respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.774, 0.606, and 0.7190. For discriminant validity, the square roots of the AVE (√AVE) were 0.880, 0.779, and 0.848, further supporting the distinctiveness of the constructs.

Emotional labor strategy scale (ELSS)

We further adopted the Emotional Labor Strategy Scale developed by Diefendorff et al. [27]. The scale is theoretically grounded in emotional labor strategies and is renowned for its strong psychometric properties and international use. Originally developed for the service industry, it has been successfully applied in Chinese research among healthcare workers. To enhance its contextual relevance for this study, which focuses on emergency departments in China, key adaptations were made to the original items, namely: (1) the general work context was replaced with the physician–patient interaction scenario; (2) the object of emotional labor was changed from “customers” to “patients”; and (3) the required emotional expressions were specified to align with the professional roles of EMWs. The phrasing was also adapted to suit colloquial Chinese. ELSS was adapted for this study by a research team including two psychologists and one emergency physician. To ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence, a translation and back-translation procedure was conducted by two independent bilingual translators. Following the initial translation, cognitive interviews with five emergency medical workers (EMWs) were conducted to assess item clarity and relevance, leading to minor wording adjustments. Content validity was further confirmed by an expert panel comprising two senior EMWs and one psychometrician. As the scale had been previously validated in similar Chinese healthcare populations, a separate pilot test was not conducted. Instead, the psychometric properties of the adapted scale were evaluated using the formal study sample. We acknowledge that the absence of a pilot test specific to the adapted EMW version represents a methodological limitation. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and internal consistency analysis (Cronbach’s α) were performed, with results supporting the scale’s three-dimensional structure and reliability.

Emotional labor was conceptualized as a higher-order construct encompassing three dimensions: surface acting, deep acting, and naturally felt emotions, with a total of 14 items. Example items for each dimension include, for surface acting: “In emergency medical care, l fake a good mood when interacting with patients.”; for deep acting: “I work hard to feel the emotions that l need to show to patients.”; the expression of naturally felt emotions: “The emotions I show patients come naturally.” These items are listed in Appendix C. The questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert rating scale, where 1 indicates “never” and 5 indicates “always”. Higher scores on each item indicate greater emotional labor expended, the total score is resulting in a possible range from 14 to 70, while a higher average score on a particular dimension indicates that the respondent adopted that type of emotional labor more frequently. In the analyses, the higher-order factor was used when examining the overall role of emotional labor, while the three subscales were examined separately to explore the unique contributions of each dimension.

In this study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha value for the scale was 0.843, the CFA results were GFI = 0.921, NFI = 0.923, CFI = 0.933, IFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.911, and RMSEA = 0.096. This first-order multi-factor measurement model was significantly superior to a single-factor model, with the following fit indices: GFI = 0.558, NFI = 0.674, CFI = 0.682, IFI = 0.682, TLI = 0.624, RMSEA = 0.206, and Δχ2/Δdf = 553.507 (P < 0.001). This indicates that the three core constructs examined in this study possess good discriminant validity. Regarding convergent validity, the composite reliability (CR) values for the three dimensions were 0.805, 0.864, and 0.968, respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.509, 0.623, and 0.911. For discriminant validity, the square roots of the AVE (√AVE) were 0.713, 0.789, and 0.954, further supporting the distinctiveness of the constructs.

While all three instruments have demonstrated good reliability and validity in various Chinese populations—including nurses across multiple departments, employees in service industries, and professionals from corporate, hospital, and educational settings—their specific applicability to the emergency medical context remains inadequately explored. Notably, validation studies conducted exclusively with emergency department nurses, or more broadly with EMWs, are exceptionally scarce. In summary, despite their established application foundation and psychometric support in broader Chinese healthcare environments, dedicated validation for the specific context of China’s emergency medical settings is currently either lacking or severely limited.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 22.0; AMOS 21.0). Descriptive statistical analysis was used to assess participants’ basic characteristics. A reliability analysis was conducted to measure scale trustworthiness. An independent samples t-test and a one-way ANOVA were used to evaluate the demographic differences among the various variables. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to explore the relationships among professional identity, organizational support, and emotional labor. Additionally, structural equation modeling was performed using AMOS software, and the mediating role of emotional labor was examined using the bootstrap method. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Common method bias test

Regarding procedural control, the participants responded anonymously throughout the entire process. The questionnaires did not contain Chinese names but instead used English abbreviations to minimize participants’ speculation about the purpose of the measurement. The options for each questionnaire employed differentiated expressions such as levels of agreement, conformity, or impact. The order of items in the online and offline questionnaires was adjusted, and the scoring methods for each questionnaire differed. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. The unrotated exploratory factor analysis revealed nine factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor accounting for 37.634% of the total variance, below the 40% threshold. However, given that this test has limited sensitivity in detecting common method bias [28], the potential influence of such bias cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should employ more rigorous approaches to address this limitation.

Organizational support subdimensions

All three organizational support subdimensions were positively correlated with professional identity. Work support showed the strongest correlation (r = 0.51, P < 0.01), followed by value recognition (r = 0.50, P < 0.01) and interest concerns (r = 0.47, P < 0.01). This indicates that tangible support in the work environment is most closely linked to EMWs’ professional identity.

Professional identity subdimensions

The three professional identity subdimensions were also positively associated with organizational support. Sense of consistency showed the strongest correlation (r = 0.56, P < 0.01), followed by sense of significance (r = 0.48, P < 0.01) and sense of self-efficacy (r = 0.40, P < 0.01). These patterns suggest that organizational factors may be particularly important for EMWs’ perception of alignment between personal and professional values.

Relationships with emotional labor subdimensions

Deep acting and naturally felt emotions were consistently positively correlated with all organizational support and professional identity subdimensions (r ranging from 0.31 to 0.44, all P < 0.01). In contrast, surface acting showed weak or non-significant correlations with most subdimensions (r ranging from 0.06 to 0.16) and was not significantly correlated with professional identity overall (r = 0.08, P = 0.06). These findings align with the mediation model results: adaptive emotional labor strategies are positively associated with both organizational support and professional identity, while surface acting is not.

Mediating role

Based on model fit, bootstrap tests were conducted on the constructed Models 1–4, with 5,000 resamples. The 95% confidence intervals for each effect are detailed in Table 7. Following the mediation effect testing procedure, the results indicated that the total, direct, and indirect effects of Models 1, 3, and 4 were significant. The total effect in Model 2 was significant, while the indirect effect was not significant, with the indirect and direct effects having opposite signs.

Discussion

Before discussing the theoretical and practical implications, it is important to clarify the generalizability of our findings given the convenience sampling strategy. Our sample was drawn from secondary and tertiary hospitals across 12 Chinese provinces with an approximate 1:1 ratio. Therefore, the findings are most directly applicable to EMWs working in relatively well-resourced secondary and tertiary hospitals. However, due to the overrepresentation of nurses (69.5%) relative to physicians (24%) and the 81.5% male composition, the results may better reflect the experiences of male nurses than those of female EMWs or physicians. Generalizations to primary hospitals, which were excluded, or to other healthcare systems should be made with caution.

Model fit considerations

The RMSEA values obtained in this study (0.096–0.113) exceeded the conventional threshold of 0.08 for good fit. While this indicates less than optimal model fit, several considerations justify retaining the proposed theoretical structure. First, RMSEA is known to be sensitive to model complexity and sample size, and with a complex model and a large sample (N = 643), even minor misspecifications can lead to elevated values. Second, other fit indices—specifically CFI (0.938–0.979) and TLI (0.910–0.981)—were within acceptable ranges, suggesting that the model captures the essential covariance structure reasonably well. Third, the target model was theoretically driven by Grandey’s emotional labor framework and demonstrated superior fit compared to plausible alternative models (e.g., full mediation model, direct effects model), supporting its theoretical validity despite the RMSEA values. Consequently, the model was retained on theoretical grounds, and the elevated RMSEA is acknowledged as a limitation in the Limitations section. Future research should consider alternative specifications or collect additional data to further improve model fit.

Gender differences in professional identity and emotional labor

Consistent with previous research, male medical workers tended to report higher levels of professional identity than their female counterparts [5], while female workers reported greater engagement in emotional labor [22, 29]. The effect sizes for these differences, as indicated by Cohen's d (0.286 and −0.193, respectively), were small to moderate. Such small-to-moderate effects are common in behavioral research, reflecting that demographic variables are meaningful but operate among multiple influences on EMWs’ professional identity and emotional labor. The healthcare workforce is predominantly male, with female dominance in the nursing profession. Given this context, it is possible to hypothesize that doctors, who hold a dominant position in the treatment process compared to nurses in a coordinated role, would be likely to have higher levels of professional identity in the medical system. Previous research has suggested that gender differences in socialization may shape emotional labor strategies. For instance, some studies indicate that female employees tend to report higher levels of deep acting compared to their male counterparts [30], possibly reflecting broader patterns in interpersonal engagement.

Differences in organizational support, emotional labor, and professional identity across working years

The findings of this study revealed significant differences in professional identity across working years, which aligns with previous research outcomes [31], indicating that the professional identity of EMWs becomes more entrenched with an increase in working years and accumulation of experience. Specifically, the data suggest that EMWs with 11–15 years and over 20 years of work experience exhibit significantly higher levels of professional identity than those with other ranges of working years. This could be attributed to the substantial improvement in work capabilities and competency after 10 years, positioning such workers in an ascending career phase. Furthermore, after 20 years of service, such EMWs are likely to have become senior figures in the industry, having marked notable career milestones in both work capabilities and accomplishments, thereby contributing to their heightened sense of professional identity.

The relationship between organizational support, emotional labor, and professional identity

Previous studies have indicated that organizational support can enhance employees’ professional identities by fulfilling their emotional needs [32, 33]. These results are in agreement with the present study. When EMWs perceive that their hospital values the significance of their work, acknowledges their contributions, and genuinely cares for their interests, salaries, lives, and so forth, during their prolonged service, they are inclined to expend considerable emotional labor in emergency rescue operations to achieve organizational goals [34, 35].

When EMWs perceive strong support from their hospitals, they exhibit greater identification with their work roles, more extensively align their personal goals and values with their ideal standards, and are more willing to utilize their skills to better accomplish their job responsibilities. Additionally, emotional labor strategies facilitate the maintenance of psychological resource balance and the regulation and establishment of positive doctor-patient relationships, thereby enhancing professional identity [36]. This aspect represents a novel area for medical education in which appropriate guidance and training need to be provided to EMWs to foster these capabilities.

The mediating role of emotional labor

Emotional labor can be categorized into surface acting, deep acting, and the expression of naturally felt emotions. Prior research indicates that these three distinct forms of emotional labor have different associations with workplace outcomes. For instance, surface acting is negatively or non-significantly correlated with positive factors such as work engagement, well-being, and job satisfaction but positively correlated with negative factors such as negative emotions, emotional exhaustion, and turnover intentions. In contrast, deep acting and the expression of naturally felt emotions exhibit opposite patterns [37–39]. This suggests that the more medical workers attempt to align their authentic emotions with those required by medical practice, the more energized, dedicated, and focused they become at their work [40]. These earlier findings corroborate the results obtained in the present work: deep acting and the expression of naturally felt emotional strategies within emotional labor partially mediated the relationship between organizational support and professional identity.

The mediating role found in this study also has practical significance. Deep acting and naturally felt emotions explained 18.6% and 18.2% of the total effect of organizational support on professional identity, respectively. This means that when emergency medical workers perceive greater organizational support, nearly one-fifth of the corresponding increase in their professional identity occurs through their adoption of deeper and more genuine emotional engagement with patients. From a practical perspective, this highlights the value of training programs that encourage deep acting and authentic emotional expression, as these strategies appear to translate supportive work environments into stronger professional commitment. Conversely, the absence of a mediating role for surface acting suggests that simply ‘faking’ emotions does not contribute to this process and may even be counterproductive.

This can be explained by Hobfoll's Conservation of Resources Theory. This theory states that individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect their valued resources, e.g., emotional well-being, authenticity. Stress and burnout occur when these resources are threatened or lost [41]. Surface Acting is a resource-draining process. Deep Acting is a resource-investing and potentially resource-building process [42].

Surface acting involves faking emotions that are not felt and suppressing genuine emotions. This creates an internal state of emotional dissonance—a discrepancy between what one feels and what one displays. This dissonance is psychologically taxing because it requires continuous self-monitoring, effortful Inhibition and fabrication [43]. Because it is purely depleting, surface acting is the mechanism of strain itself, not a mediating process that explains the relationship between the demand and the strain.

In statistical terms, surface acting did not function as a mediator, as the indirect association between organizational support and professional identity through this dimension was not significant. Surface acting is not a coping strategy that explains how a demand leads to strain; it is the strain itself in behavioral form.

Deep acting involves proactively trying to align one’s internal feelings with the required emotions. This process is fundamentally different because it functions as an active coping strategy that reduces dissonance and, in doing so, can build resources rather than deplete them. Therefore, deep acting can explain the relationship between organizational support and professional identity.

The lack of significance for surface acting is an unexpected finding. We hypothesize that this pattern may reflect that EMWs, as a group, rely more on a combination of genuine professional composure and functional detachment than on the simple suppression and faking of emotions. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires further empirical investigation.

EMWs often operate from a foundation of what can be called professional concern or instrumental empathy [44]. Through rigorous training and repeated exposure, EMWs internalize required emotional displays—composure, focus, and empathetic detachment—as core components of their professional identity, making these expressions genuine rather than performative. When emotional regulation is necessary, they predominantly rely on expression of naturally felt emotions to align their inner state with professional demands. By focusing on their critical mission, they authentically generate calmness rather than fabricating it. The “authentic self” and the “professional self” are highly integrated for EMWs.

It is possible that the strategy of functional detachment may be a key factor distinguishing EMWs from other service professions. The high-stakes, procedural nature of their work demands intense cognitive focus on clinical tasks, which may naturally preempt disruptive personal emotions [45]. Surface acting creates emotional dissonance; however functional detachment could avoid the dissonance altogether by minimizing the personal experiencing of the emotion in the first place. If this is the case, the calm EMWs exhibit may not be a performance over panic but rather the outward manifestation of this intense cognitive engagement. Future research is needed to test this hypothesis.

Based on the aforementioned findings, this study argues that enhancing the well-being and service quality of EMWs necessitates that healthcare institutions move beyond traditional skill-based training frameworks and integrate the management of emotional labor into the core of organizational support systems. Hospital management must recognize that fostering deep acting is not about demanding boundless emotional expenditure, but rather about constructing a supportive environment that provides these personnel with essential psychological resources and skills. Accordingly, this study proposes two core intervention recommendations:

Implement Structured Emotional Intelligence Training. Such programs should transcend theoretical instruction and focus on practical skills for transitioning from surface acting to deep acting. Core content should include emotion awareness and labeling, cognitive reappraisal, and emotion regulation strategies.

To counteract the limited long-term effects of one-off training, a dual-track support network integrating mentorship programs and peer support groups should be institutionalized. The mentorship system offers vertical professional guidance and emotional modeling, while peer support provides horizontal, immediate empathy and shared experience. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents inferences of causality, necessitating future longitudinal research to establish causal directions. Additionally, multiple statistical tests were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons, which may inflate the Type I error rate. This issue is particularly relevant given the number of subgroup comparisons reported in Table 2. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Second, due to the convenience-based selection of provinces and hospitals, the sample may not be fully representative of all emergency medical workers in China, and no statistical weighting was applied to adjust for this. The demographic composition of the sample—81.5% male—deviates from the typical gender distribution of EMWs in many regions, suggesting potential sampling bias. Additionally, systematic differences across hospitals in size, staffing, or emergency department structure cannot be ruled out, as detailed hospital-level data were not collected. Therefore, generalizations of the findings should be made with caution.

Third, the reliance on self-report measures may introduce common method bias. While Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first factor accounted for 37.6% of the variance, this test has limited sensitivity and cannot completely rule out bias. Additionally, the RMSEA values (0.096–0.113) exceeded the conventional threshold for good fit (< 0.08), indicating less than optimal model fit. No post-hoc model modifications were made, as such adjustments would require strong theoretical justification. These methodological factors suggest that the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Fourth, the Emotional Labor Strategy Scale was adapted for EMWs, but no pilot testing was conducted. While cognitive interviews and expert reviews were performed to ensure content validity, the absence of a formal pilot test represents a methodological limitation. Future research should conduct pilot testing to further validate the adapted scale. Finally, the cultural context of China's healthcare environment may limit the applicability of the results beyond similar settings, and this contextual boundary should be considered when interpreting the study's implications.

Conclusion

Theoretically, this study demonstrates the mediating role of emotional labor between organizational support and professional identity among EMWs, a role to which surface acting does not positively contribute. However, due to the cross-sectional design, the findings represent associations at a single point in time, and no causal relationships can be inferred. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is needed to establish causal directions. Therefore, fostering supportive environments that promote deep acting and genuine emotional expression may be beneficial for enhancing the professional identity of EMWs.

Based on the observed associations, the following practical recommendations are offered as theoretically grounded suggestions rather than empirically validated interventions. From a practical perspective, efforts to improve the well-being of EMWs and the quality of their services may benefit from moving beyond traditional skill-based training frameworks and integrating emotional labor management into organizational support systems. Hospital management could consider that fostering ‘deep acting’ may be supported by establishing a supportive environment, including structured emotional intelligence training and systematic organizational support mechanisms. The synergy of these approaches has the potential to contribute to the internalization of skills, strengthen organizational belonging, and help ensure that employees’ emotional labor is recognized, validated, and supported. Implementing such measures may help equip EMWs with the psychological resources and skills needed to navigate emotional demands.
