Towards HPV-Based Cervical Cancer Screening in Poland: Lessons From a Landmark Trial
Authors: Artur J Jakimiuk, Marcin Januszewski
Published: 2026

Does primary HPV testing outperform cytology in real-world, population-based settings—and can health systems manage the consequences? The HIPPO trial, the first prospective randomised study of this kind in Eastern Europe, provides a timely and rigorous answer from within Poland's national screening framework.1

The findings are striking. Primary HPV-based screening nearly doubled CIN2+ detection compared with cytology (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.34–2.77), and significantly increased CIN3+ detection (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.16–3.42), confirming the superior sensitivity of HPV testing in a real-world setting. These results align with a 2017 Cochrane review reporting a pooled relative sensitivity of 1.35 (95% CI 1.23–1.48) for CIN2+ detection with HPV DNA testing versus cytology, at a modest cost in specificity.2

Longitudinal randomised trials–including studies from Finland, Australia, and Canada–have confirmed that increased CIN2+ detection in the first HPV screening round translates into a significantly lower risk of CIN2+ in the subsequent round, the biological dividend of earlier disease interception.3, 4, 5, 6 Notably, these studies showed considerable variation in HPV and cytology testing technology, age ranges, and clinical management protocols, yet results were consistent: detection rates of CIN2+ and CIN3+ were at least as high with HPV screening as with cytology across all trials.

The HIPPO study's conservative triage approach–relying on HPV testing alone rather than co-testing–and its integration within the national programme lend it strong external validity. The observed cytological abnormality rate in the cytology arm (6.7%) was more than twice the national average recorded between 2016 and 2020 (2.6%), a discrepancy likely reflecting the quality-controlled trial setting and suggesting that real-world cytology performance outside research conditions may be considerably weaker. This has direct implications for how health systems should interpret comparative performance data.

The study also addresses potential harms with appropriate rigour. The rise in colposcopy referrals and CIN1 detection in the HPV arm underscores the need for well-designed triage algorithms. The authors' evaluation of novel biomarkers–FAM19A4/miR124 methylation and p16/Ki67 immunocytochemistry–as additional triage tools for HPV-positive, cytology-negative women is a meaningful contribution. The data suggest higher CIN2+ detection rates but lower specificity for methylation testing compared with p16/Ki67 in this subgroup, a distinction that becomes particularly relevant as self-sampling programmes expand, since methylation assays are compatible with self-collected material while cytology is not.7,8

From an implementation standpoint, transitioning to HPV-based screening reduces cytotechnicians' workload by 80–90%, but demands investment in validated, high-throughput automated platforms and laboratory centralisation–with European guidelines recommending a minimum threshold of 10,000 tests per laboratory to ensure proficiency. These structural requirements are not trivial, and health systems planning the transition must account for them.

One important limitation deserves attention: the follow-up period is insufficient to observe outcomes in the HPV-positive, twice cytology-negative cohort beyond three years. This is the group in whom long-term surveillance evidence is most needed to guide safe interval extension.

Against this backdrop, the Polish Ministry of Health's decision to introduce HPV-based screening from July 2025 is welcome, but the parallel retention of cytology–leaving the choice of screening test to individual women–is a missed opportunity. Cervical cancer incidence and mortality remain higher in Poland than in many Western European countries,9 and a divided programme risks perpetuating the inequities that organised screening is designed to eliminate.

Several questions remain open. As HPV-based screening is now being implemented, long-term follow-up of both HIPPO arms will be essential to detect any future difference in cervical cancer incidence–the ultimate measure of screening effectiveness. Epidemiological research in vaccinated cohorts should examine whether HPV types beyond the 12 currently classified as Group 1 carcinogens warrant inclusion in primary screening. Finally, planned genotyping analyses from the HIPPO consortium will be important for refining risk-based triage and characterising HPV type distribution in the Polish population.

The HIPPO trial makes a compelling, evidence-based case for replacing cytology with primary HPV testing in Poland and in countries across the region still relying on older screening modalities. The science is clear; the policy must follow.
