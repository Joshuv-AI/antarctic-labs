Mermin’s dielectric function and the f-sum rule
Thomas Chuna, Jan Vorberger, Thomas Gawne, Tobias Dornheim, Michael S. Murillo

Abstract
Mermin’s dielectric function [N.D. Mermin, Phys. Rev. B 1 , 2362 (1970)] is widely assumed to satisfy the f-sum rule because he constrains his ansatz with the continuity equation. However, we identify a moment-closure problem in Mermin’s use of the continuity equation. Further, we show that the Mermin’s model can be derived without invoking continuity. We describe how other approaches such as the “completed Mermin” model of Chuna and Murillo [Phys. Rev. E 111 , 035206 (2025)] remedy this closure issue. We then inspect the f-sum rule for both the original and completed Mermin models and find for the Mermin ansatz that collision frequencies scaling as ω \omega must violate the f-sum rule, whereas constant, real, positive collision frequencies will satisfy it, with the caveat that, in practice, convergence with respect to the upper integration limit ω max \omega_{\max} is sufficiently slow that finite-domain numerical evaluations exhibit apparent violations, regardless of wavenumber q q . We also find that collision frequencies with constant imaginary components cause f-sum rule violations. We conclude that if Mermin’s model is fit to data via optimizing its collision frequency, then the f-sum rule is not inherently satisfied; constraints, though broad, are needed in order to assume the f-sum rule is satisfied. Further, if the f-sum rule is theoretically satisfied, but violations still appear, then these deviations ought to be included in the error estimates.

II.1
Sum rules of density-density correlators

The frequency sum rule is a very commonly calculated sum rule and is intimately tied to the continuity equation, but before we investigate this particular sum rule, let us define sum rules for density-density correlators and show why they are important.

The intermediate scattering function
F(q,t)=\braket{n^{\dagger}(q,t=0)n(q,t)}
is a two-point correlation function describing the density-density correlation of a system. It is related to the dynamic structure factor by the Fourier transform as

\displaystyle F(q,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\ e^{i\omega t}S(q,\omega)

(1)

\displaystyle S(q,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt\ e^{-i\omega t}F(q,t)\ .

(2)

The intermediate scattering function is used in a host of application and plays a central role in linear response theory
Kubo 1966
;
Kubo 1966
;
Boon and Yip 1991
;
Hansen and McDonald 2013
;
Mahan 1990
and, in many fields, is an important point of connection between theoretical and experimental efforts
Heaton et al. 2025
;
Yin et al. 2025
;
Dornheim et al. 2025
;
Pratt and Young 2017
.

In the classical context, the Taylor series expansion of
F
about
t=0
is
Boon and Yip 1991
;
Choi et al. 2019
,

\displaystyle F(q,t)

\displaystyle=\omega^{(0)}(q)-\frac{t^{2}}{2!}\omega^{(2)}(q)+\frac{t^{4}}{4!}\omega^{(4)}(q)+\ldots\,,

(3)

where the
n
th coefficient is given,

\displaystyle\omega^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{n}S(q,\omega)\,.

(4)

The sum rules are these coefficients (
4
) and they determine order by order the correct intermediate scattering function,
i.e.
, the correct structure of two-body correlations. The odd moments are zero because the classical
S(q,\omega)
is symmetric about
\omega=0
.

In the quantum setting, the sum rules are given as the coefficients for the Taylor expansion of the response function
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
;
Dornheim et al. 2023

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\chi(q,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}\frac{\omega^{\prime(n+1)}}{\omega^{n+2}}

(5)

where the
(n+1)
th coefficient
\omega^{\prime(n+1)}
is given by the sum rules

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime(n+1)}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{n+1}\text{Im}\,\chi(q,\omega).

(6)

The even moments are zero due to the odd frequency parity of
\text{Im}\,\chi(q,\omega)
. Notice the quantum version (
6
) recovers the classical version (
4
), (
i.e.
,
\omega^{\prime(n+1)}\rightarrow\omega^{(n+2)}
) via the classical fluctuation dissipation theorem
S(q,\omega)=2\,\text{Im}\,\chi(q,\omega)/\omega
.

II.2
The
frequency sum rule and its relation to the continuity equation

In the classical and quantum literature
Boon and Yip 1991
;
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
the dominant contribution to the intermediate scattering function (
i.e.
, the lowest order term coefficient in the Taylor expansion) is known as
the
frequency sum (f-sum) rule.
The
f-sum rule is often associated sdawith the continuity equation and here we will demonstrate why. For clarity of presentation, we follow Boon and Yip’s classical
Boon and Yip 1991
derivation, but the continuity equation is also important to the quantum version
(
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
, eq (3.141))
.

Starting from the definition of the DSF (
2
), we multiply both sides by
\omega^{2}
to arrive at
(
Boon and Yip 1991
, (2.2.22))

\displaystyle\omega^{2}S(q,\omega)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dte^{-i\omega t}\braket{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n^{\dagger}(q,t)\right]_{t=0}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n(q,t)\right]}\,.

(7)

The two
\partial_{t}
’s are generated from
\omega^{2}
using integration by parts. Next, substituting the continuity equation
\partial_{t}n(q,t)=\mathbf{q}\cdot\textbf{j}(q,t)
produces

\displaystyle\omega^{2}S(q,\omega)=q^{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dte^{-i\omega t}\braket{j(q,0)j(q,t)}\,.

(8)

This expression along with the assumption that
j(q,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{l}\mathbf{v}_{l}(t)e^{iq\cdot r_{l}(t)}
is used to compute the f-sum rule. The result is
(
Boon and Yip 1991
, (2.4.12))

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega_{p}}\,\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{p}^{2}}\omega_{p}S(q,\omega)

\displaystyle=(q/q_{D})^{2}\,,

(9)

where we have used the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}^{2}=4\pi n_{e}e^{2}/m
, the Debye wavelength
q_{D}^{2}=4\pi n_{e}e^{2}/T
, and the thermal velocity
v_{0}^{2}=T/m=\omega_{p}^{2}/q_{D}^{2}
.

The f-sum rule has an equivalent formulation, derived in introductory material
(
Tkachenko et al. 2012
, Section 3)
and
(
Kremp et al. 2005
, section 4.4)
,

\displaystyle-\pi

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega_{p}}\,\frac{\omega}{\omega_{p}}\,\text{Im}\,\varepsilon^{-1}(q,\omega)\,.

(10)

Equation (
10
) is a general formulation that holds in both the classical and quantum setting and is the formulation we will use to evaluate the f-sum rule. Heuristically, we can arrive at this equation from (
9
) by substituting the
S(q,\omega)=-\frac{2T}{\omega n}\text{Im}\chi(q,\omega)
and recognizing the Coulomb interaction
v(q)=\frac{n}{T}(q/q_{D})^{-2}
and the dielectric function
\varepsilon^{-1}(q,\omega)=1+v(q)\chi(q,\omega)
.

In summary, if a theory satisfies the f-sum rule then its associated intermediate scattering function is correct at leading order. Additionally, in both quantum and classical systems, the f-sum rule is intimately tied to the continuity equation, as many introductory works invoke continuity to compute the f-sum rule.

II.3
Deriving Mermin’s dielectric function without the continuity equation

Often the Mermin ansatz is assumed to satisfy the f-sum rule because it satisfies the continuity equation. However, Atwal and Ashcroft
(
Atwal and Ashcroft 2002
, eq (12))
derive a generalization of Mermin’s model by linearizing the Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) kinetic equation
Bhatnagar et al. 1954
via perturbing global equilibrium with respect to local perturbations in chemical potential
\delta\mu
, velocity
\delta\mathbf{u}
, and temperature
\delta T
and then constraining these perturbations with the lowest collisional invariants (
i.e.
, number, momentum, and energy). Their approach can be greatly simplified and used to derive Mermin’s model without invoking the continuity equation,
i.e.
, by neglecting
\delta\mathbf{u}
and
\delta T
and constraining
\delta\mu
via the zeroth collisional invariant. In this subsection, we will do exactly this.
Quantum corrections arise with the inclusion of energy conservation, which is beyond our considerations. So we derive Mermin’s results starting from the classical kinetic equation to provide readers a physical intuition for each term
.

A classical kinetic equation is expressed as

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}+\textbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}})f=Q\,,

(11)

where the LHS describes the phase-space evolution of the one-body distribution function
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)
and the one-body forces are described by the acceleration
\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x},t)=-m^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}U(\mathbf{x},t)
, as such
(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}+\textbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}})f
is equivalent to a Poisson bracket. The RHS collects all the two-body terms into the collision operator
Q
.

Following Mermin, we assume the BGK relaxation time approximation (RTA) of the collision term. Thus, the RHS is expressed as the difference between the current distribution and local equilibrium
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}+\textbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}})f=\nu(\mathcal{M}-f)\,.

(12)

Here the collision frequency
\nu=1/\tau
is the inverse of the relaxation time
\tau
. Though Mermin does not justify his use of the RTA, the equation has been derived directly from the Boltzmann equation
Haack et al. 2017a
;
Garzó et al. 1989
and the applicability of the model to linear response has been discussed
Chuna and Murillo 2025
.

To produce Mermin’s dielectric function, we first focus on the LHS. We expand the one-body distribution function
f
about global equilibrium as
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)=f_{0}(v)+\delta f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)
, Fourier transform, and convert to discrete quantum states (
f_{0}(v)\rightarrow f_{k}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
,
\delta f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)\rightarrow\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\partial_{t}\rightarrow-i\omega
,
\partial_{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow i\mathbf{q}
,
\mathbf{p}=m\mathbf{v}
,
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}/2
,
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}/2
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{\partial_{t}(f_{0}+\delta f)\}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\,\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\,,

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(f_{0}+\delta f)\}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\mathbf{p}}{m}\cdot\mathbf{q}\,\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=i(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\,,

(13b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{\textbf{a}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f_{0}\}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{q}U_{\mathrm{ext}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}f_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-i(f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})U_{\mathrm{ext}}\,.

(13c)

The second equalities follow from Taylor expanding about
\mathbf{p}
. Inserting (
13
) into the LHS of (
12
) we have

\displaystyle\omega\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+(f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})U=i\nu\left(\mathcal{M}-f\right)\,.

(14)

We focus the RHS and expand the relaxation term about global equilibrium using the relations

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=f_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\approx f_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\delta\mu(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\ .

(15b)

Following Mermin, the expanded local equilibrium
\mathcal{M}
(
15b
) contains linear deviations from the global equilibrium with respect to the chemical potential
\delta\mu
. Inserting these expansions into the RHS of (
14
) produces the linearized kinetic equation,

\displaystyle\omega\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+(f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})U(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\nu\left(\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\delta\mu(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right).

(16)

Equation (
II.3
) matches Mermin
(
Mermin 1970
, eq (4))
, where Mermin distributed the negative to have
(f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-f_{\mathbf{k}})U(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
and denotes our
\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
as
\braket{\mathbf{k}|\rho^{(1)}|\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
.

Equation (
II.3
) is not closed,
i.e.
, the
\delta\mu
in (
II.3
) is yet unknown. To constrain this term, Mermin converts
\mathbf{k}
’s to
\mathbf{p}
’s using the previous
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}/2
,
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}/2
relation (

,
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rightarrow(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q})/m
) and then integrates (
II.3
) over momentum

, which removes the contribution from the potential term

and introduces
\delta n(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\int d\mathbf{p}\,\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
(units of number density),

\displaystyle\omega\delta n-\int\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{4\pi^{3}}\frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}}{m}\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-i\nu\left(\delta n+\delta\mu\int\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{4\pi^{3}}\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\right).

(17)

We recognize the second term on the RHS as the quantum ideal gas susceptibility at
\omega=0
, often denoted
\chi_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega=0)
and defined in Mermin’s paper

as,

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\int\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{4\pi^{3}}\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\omega-(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})}\,,

(18)

Mermin refers to the LHS of (
II.3
) as “
\omega\,\delta n=\mathbf{q}\cdot\delta\textbf{j}
”, so that solving for

such that the RHS is zero [

However, there is a moment closure problem in Mermin’s invocation of the continuity equation. This can be understood by considering the current’s dimensions. According to the product rule, a current perturbation
\delta\mathbf{j}
goes as,

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{u}_{0}\delta n+n_{0}\delta\mathbf{u},

(19)

where

and
\mathbf{u}_{0}
are the equilibrium mass density and velocity. Yet, local perturbations with respect to velocity
\delta\mathbf{u}
are not included in the expansion about global equilibrium (
15

Let us revisit the constraint that the RHS of (
II.3
) is zero, this can be expressed as

\displaystyle 0=\int\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{4\pi^{3}}\left(\delta f_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}\delta\mu(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right).

(20)

Since all the terms in the integrand arise from the collision integral, then (
20
) is precisely
\int d\mathbf{p}\,Q=0
at linear order. Therefore, the Mermin

is better understood as enforcing the zeroth collisional invariant, (

II.4
Dynamic response models that satisfy the continuity equation

A remedy for this moment closure problem is extending the initial expansion about global equilibrium (
15

\delta\textbf{u}
. Fortunately, this has already been investigated

;
Chuna and Murillo 2025

In both of these prior investigations,

is constrained by momentum conservation (

. Since the moment closure issue is resolved, we can invoke the continuity equation

\displaystyle\delta\textbf{u}=\frac{\omega}{q^{2}n_{0}}\mathbf{q}\,\delta n\,.

where

\displaystyle C_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\int d\mathbf{v}\frac{m_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{q}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}f(v)}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{q}-\omega},

(24a)

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv C_{0}(\mathbf{q},0).

(24b)

and

. We have suppressed the

and

and

Chuna and Murillo 2025

, also without mean field correction,
Chuna and Murillo 2025

\displaystyle\chi^{\textrm{Mermin}}=\cfrac{C_{0}}{1-\cfrac{i\nu}{\omega+i\nu}\left(1-\cfrac{C_{0}}{B_{0}}\right)}.

Notice this expression lacks the term arising from

in the denominator.

For the quantum version of (

) and (

), simply replace

and

is included and the energy conservation is enforced

II.5
Analytic form of the inverse dielectric function in the long wavelength limit

Now that we have discussed the moment closure issue in the continuity equation, we turn our attention to whether the f-sum rule is satisfied. In this section we will evaluate the long wavelength limit analytically. We consider the homogeneous one-component plasma (OCP), with its Coulomb interaction potential
v(q)=\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{q^{2}}=\frac{T}{n}\frac{q_{D}^{2}}{q^{2}}
. The long wavelength limit of the CM model for real
\tau
at order
\mathcal{O}[q^{2}]
is given by

IV
Summary, Conclusions, and Future Work

In the first half of this paper we have shown that Mermin’s derivation has a moment closure problem and that the remedy is extending Mermin’s expansion about local equilibrium to include not just deviations from the local chemical potential, but also local velocity. As such, Mermin’s
ansatz
is best understood as enforcing the zeroth collisional invariant (
i.e.
local number conservation) and not the continuity equation. In this work, we discuss two prior works
Atwal and Ashcroft 2002
;
Chuna and Murillo 2025
that constrained the local velocity perturbation using the continuity equation and the first collisional invariant (
i.e.
, conservation of momentum), leading to the so-called “completed Mermin” (CM) dielectric function.

In the second half of this paper and due to the connection between the f-sum rule and the continuity equation, we consider carefully whether Mermin’s
ansatz
satisfies the f-sum rule. We find for collision frequencies that scale as
\omega
Mermin’s first moment does not converge to the desired f-sum rule value. For constant positive collision frequencies, Mermin’s first moment converges to the desired f-sum rule value, as characterized by (
28
). The severity of this
\arctan
convergence has been made clear by Figure
1
, Figure
2
, and Figure
3
. In practice any reasonably finite
\omega
grid can incur a non-negligible error that ought to be accounted for in the error analysis; these are the consequences of Mermin’s Cauchy-like distributions. Finally, we demonstrate that a constant shift in the peak position manifests as a violation of the f-sum rule, see Figure
4
. This is expected since it is by definition a statement about the first frequency moment.

We conclude the following for the traditional approach to extracting collision frequencies from XRTS,
i.e.
, fitting a
q
- and
\omega
-dependent collision frequency to some observed signals
Sperling et al. 2015
;
Witte et al. 2017
;
Schörner et al. 2023
;
Hentschel et al. 2025
. The
q
- and
\omega
-dependent collision introduces significant flexibility, allowing the Mermin
ansatz
to reproduce a broad class of functional forms. However, unconstrained fits may violate the physical constraints on the collision frequency, which are: firstly, where the Mermin
ansatz
is expected to recover the f-sum rule, the imaginary part of the collision frequencies must go to zero in the long wavelength limit; secondly, where the collision frequency
\nu(\omega)
is interpreted as an impurity scattering frequency, then for small
\omega
the imaginary part of the collision frequency must scale linearly with
\omega
,
i.e.
,
\text{Im}\,\nu(\omega)\sim\omega
, going to zero for
\omega=0
. See
(
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
, Section 4.6.1)
as well as
Reinholz et al. 2000
for other constraints. Such constraints were not incorporated in some earlier XRTS analyses. See, e.g., Ref.
(
Sperling et al. 2015
, supplemental material)
, where the imaginary component grows larger with decreasing
\omega
. We encourage future work to explore the line of research pursued by Hentschel
et al
.
Hentschel et al. 2025
who derived a new sum rule and enforced it on their collision frequency fits.

There are additional conclusions for XRTS ray tracing codes, which use Monte Carlo methods to generate synthetic experimental spectra from a DSF model and normalize over counts to match the f-sum. Figure
1
indicates that if the Mermin
ansatz
is used, the synthetic XRTS spectra may need to sample an unphysically large spectral range to achieve the f-sum rule convergence. These convergence issues are expected to improve for frequency-dependent collision frequencies that go to zero at large
\omega
such as Born model and its various variants
Hentschel et al. 2023
, essentially leading to RPA-like behavior. Figure
1
also suggests that for a given collision frequency model different dynamic response models will lead to different conclusions on physical quantities such as the Rayleigh weight
W_{R}
or density.

Lastly, future work developing dielectric function models / dynamic structure factor models may wish to consider the following. Firstly, in practice, the CM model is not perfect. By including momentum conservation the closure issue in the continuity equation is corrected, but we have removed the dissipation that gives rise to a finite conductivity in a single component system
Atwal and Ashcroft 2002
;
Morawetz and Fuhrmann 2000a
. Enforcing a different constraint on
\delta u
may remove this trade-off; for example, constraining
\delta u
to satisfy Onsager relations.
Secondly, if both momentum conservation and a finite conductivity are desired then a multi-species model is needed
Morawetz and Fuhrmann 2000a
. However, Garzo
et al
.’s multi-species BGK equation
Garzó et al. 1989
satisfies the Onsager relations, but the entropy does not uniformly increase
Haack et al. 2017a
and Haack
et al
.’s multi-species BGK equation
Haack et al. 2017a
;
Haack et al. 2017b
satisfies the conservation laws and the entropy increases, but it does not satisfy the Onsager relations
Benilov 2024
.
Thirdly, while the present work relies on the kinetic formalism other formalisms may be used. For example, the hydrodynamic formalism has been used elsewhere. Diaw and Murillo
Diaw and Murillo 2017
compute a single species electron DSF model from quantum hydrodynamic (QHD) equations for number and momentum conservation using dynamic density functional theory and recover a Dirac delta in the long wavelenth limit. Opposite this, Svensson et al.
Svensson et al. 2025
compute a DSF from classic hydrodynamic equations for mass, momentum, energy conservation, introducing dissipation through ion-electron interaction and recover Mermin’s Lorentz-like DSF in the long wavelength limit.
Thus, the tradeoff between conservation laws and dissipation persists across both quantum and classical as well as hydrodynamic and kinetic formalisms, often manifesting in the long wavelength limit as a difference between Lorentz-like and Dirac-delta-like response. Future work ought to consider fundamental questions about the emergence of entropy from a conservative system in order to derive an appropriate dynamic response model for their multi-species system.

Appendix A
Explicitly evaluating the f-sum rule for Mermin’s dielectric function in the long wavelength limit

In this appendix, we assume that the collision frequency is real and evaluate the f-sum rule (
10
) for Mermin’s model (
27
) explicitly. Essentially, we evaluate the following expression,

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega_{p}}\,\frac{\omega}{\omega_{p}}\frac{\nu\,\omega\,\omega_{p}^{2}}{(\omega^{2}-\omega_{p}^{2})^{2}+\nu^{2}\omega^{2}}\,,

(30)

which is obtained by substituting Mermin’s long wavelength expansion (
27
) into the f-sum rule (
10
). To evaluate (
30
), we want to uncover the poles of the integrand by removing its dimensions and substituting variables.

To remove dimension, we substitute
\nu=1/\tau
and normalize all the quantities by
\omega_{p}
,
i.e.

\tilde{\omega}=\omega/\omega_{p}
and
\tilde{\nu}=\nu/\omega_{p}=(\tau\omega_{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tilde{\omega}\,\frac{\tilde{\omega}^{2}/\tilde{\tau}}{(\tilde{\omega}^{2}-1)^{2}+(\tilde{\omega}/\tilde{\tau})^{2}}\,.

Now that all quantities are dimensionless, we will drop the tilde for the remainder of this appendix and reintroduce dimensionality at the end. Next, we multiply the numerator and denominator by
\tau^{2}
and factor out
\omega^{2}
from the denominator, which cancels the
\omega^{2}
in the numerator and yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,\frac{-\tau}{\tau^{2}(\omega-\omega^{-1})^{2}+1}\,.

(32)

We use variable substitution

and

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\omega^{2}+1)}\frac{1}{u^{2}+1}\,.

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\frac{1}{u^{2}+1}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\frac{1}{(\omega^{2}+1)}\frac{1}{u^{2}+1},

(34)

\displaystyle\arctan(u)=\int du\,(u^{2}+1)^{-1}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle-\left.\arctan\left(\tau\frac{\omega^{2}-1}{\omega}\right)\right|_{-\infty}^{+\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\tau}{\tau^{2}(\omega^{2}-1)^{2}+\omega^{2}}\,,
