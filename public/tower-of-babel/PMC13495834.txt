A Lightweight, 3D‐Printable, Low‐Cost, “One‐Click” Dorsal Skin Window for High‐Quality Long‐Term Multimodal Subcutaneous Tumor Imaging
Authors: Iván Cortés Domínguez, Xabier Morales Urteaga, Maider Esparza, Lucía Grande, Ainhoa Urbiola, Cristina Ederra, Tomas Muñoz Santoro, Beatrice Pinci, Alvaro Teijeira, Carlos Ortiz de Solórzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntravital microscopy (IVM) using dorsal skinfold chambers (DSCs) enables real‐time, high‐resolution imaging of the tumor microenvironment. Conventional, metal‐based DSC systems often cause animal distress and require technically demanding surgical implantation. To address these challenges and adhere to the 3Rs principles of animal welfare, this study presents a novel, stereolithography (SLA)‐based, 3D‐printed DSC featuring a lightweight (0.69 g) biocompatible resin structure and a suture‐free “one‐click” fixation system. This affordable device simplifies surgical installation and minimizes postoperative inflammation, allowing continuous multimodal imaging—IVM, micro‐CT, ultrasound, and in vivo fluorescence—for up to four weeks. The system is validated in mice bearing subcutaneous tumors generated from PDAC93‐GFP pancreatic tumor organoids implanted in the DSC. Longitudinal, high‐resolution imaging successfully tracks tumor expansion, active stromal remodeling characterized by progressive collagen fiber compaction and alignment, and the emergence of a highly tortuous peritumoral vascular network. Furthermore, in vivo tracking in Catchup mice reveals a distinct transition of tumor‐associated neutrophils from random acute inflammatory migration to directed tumor‐driven chemotaxis. Overall, this ergonomic and robust DSC design provides a highly reliable platform for sustained, high‐quality assessment of tumor‐stroma‐immune interactions and therapeutic responses, while notably improving animal welfare.Keywords: 3D printing, animal welfare, dorsal skinfold chamber, intravital microscopy, multimodal imaging, neutrophil tracking, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Cancer is a biologically complex and heterogeneous disease, whose progression and treatment response is influenced by both cancer cells' intrinsic characteristics and their evolving interactions with the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1]. Understanding this complex interplay between mechanical and biological cues remains a major challenge in cancer research. Conventional in vitro models, based on primary or immortalized cancer cell lines, fail to accurately replicate the intricate cellular and extracellular interactions present within the TME [2]. Two‐dimensional (2D) cultures, in particular, lack essential cell‐to‐cell and cell‐to‐extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, and cannot recreate physiological gradients of oxygen or nutrients, or simulate interstitial fluid dynamics [2]. Three‐dimensional (3D) cultures more accurately recapitulate the mechanobiological features of the ECM, but lack vascularization [3] and a functional immune system [4]. As a result, these models provide only a fragmented view of tumor biology, limiting their translational value [5, 6, 7, 8].

Animal models, particularly murine models, remain indispensable in cancer research due to their genetic traceability, cost‐effectiveness, and rapid reproductive cycles [9]. Both syngeneic and humanized mouse models provide valuable platforms to study tumor progression and evaluate therapeutic strategies [10]. However, these models are often limited by their inability to provide high‐resolution image‐based insights into cellular and molecular events. Conventional imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), bioluminescence imaging, and micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) enable non‐invasive monitoring of tumor growth and treatment response, but cannot resolve microscopic scale events like immune‐tumor interactions or microvascular remodeling [11].

Intravital microscopy (IVM) overcomes these limitations by enabling real‐time, high‐resolution subcellular imaging of tumors and their microenvironment within living animals [11]. Through the surgical implantation of an optical window, IVM provides repeated access to native or implanted tumors, offering unprecedented visual insights into key biological processes such as tumor angiogenesis [12], hypoxia, drug resistance, immune cell infiltration, epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis [13], and tumor‐immune interactions [14].

Among various IVM systems, the dorsal skinfold chamber (DSC) stands out due to its versatility and technical advantages [15]. The DSC typically consists of two symmetrical frames that hold a transparent optical window, which is implanted in a dorsal fold of the mouse skin. This configuration provides real‐time optical access to subcutaneous tissues and implanted tumors [16]. When combined with confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) or two‐photon excitation microscopy (2PEM), the DSC enables longitudinal imaging of 3D tumor architecture and the surrounding stroma at high spatial resolution [17]. Compared with other intravital imaging windows—such as the abdominal imaging window (AIW), cranial imaging window (CIM), or organ‐specific imaging windows (OIW)— the DSC provides several advantages: (i) deep tissue imaging, (ii) relatively simple surgical implantation, and (iii) minimal disruption of the animal's physiology, thereby reducing host reaction and the risk of inflammation or infection [15, 18]. The DSC design also facilitates attachment to external stabilization systems, which minimizes intrinsic motion artifacts arising from respiration or organ movement. These features make the DSC particularly well‐suited for long‐term (days‐weeks), longitudinal imaging.

Despite these methodological advantages, minimizing the impact of DSCs on animal health and behavior remains a major challenge. Recent innovations in chamber materials and design address these concerns [19, 20], emphasizing biocompatibility and ergonomic design in accordance with the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) principles [21]. The use of engineered, lightweight materials has reduced postoperative inflammation, improved animal mobility, and extended experimental timelines [19]. Additionally, some design improvements enhance microscopy imaging and allow combining microscopy with micro‐CT, MRI, ultrasound, and optical fluorescence [22]. Others have led to improved anesthesia and analgesia protocols, which significantly accelerate animal recovery and reduce postoperative distress, improving both data quality and reproducibility. These improvements minimize recovery time and postoperative pain, reducing the need for surgical repositioning and enhancing the quality of the experimental outcomes. Collectively, these advancements in DSC design have not only improved imaging quality and prolonged study periods but also contributed meaningfully to animal welfare. This evolution has supported a paradigm shift, enabling seamless integration with complementary imaging modalities, thereby allowing a more comprehensive assessment of tumor progression [23]. In line with these ongoing efforts to enhance animal welfare and enable the use of IVM in long‐term studies, we present and validate a custom‐designed, 3D‐printed, suture‐free, single‐click DSC fabricated from a biocompatible resin. This innovative DSC has a lightweight structure that reduces animal distress and implements a simplified installation process. Our design is compatible with IVM, in vivo fluorescence imaging, micro‐CT, and ultrasound imaging. This affordable, robust system supports sustained investigation of tumor progression, interactions within the TME, and immune responses for up to four weeks.

Results

Design of the DSC and Peripheral Toolset

The DSC consists of two 1.25 mm thick annular frames. Both frames have the lower side transversely trimmed to ensure precise fit against the animal's back (Figure 1A). The frames have an external 17 mm diameter, reduced to 15.6 mm at the trimmed section, and display a central 9.2 mm diameter circular aperture that defines the field of view (FoV) of the DSC. The outer edges of the aperture are beveled to facilitate access to the microscope objective. The inner surface of both frames, which contact the animal's skin, feature a 200 µm deep indentation designed to house 10 mm diameter circular coverslips that function as optical windows for IVM observation, thereby enabling optical access from both sides. An annular polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) O‐ring is affixed to each coverslip, forming a post‐implantation sealed interface with the skin. This prevents leakages and reduces the risk of infection at the exposed site. At the lower side, each frame is equipped with a lateral flap, angled at 60°, which facilitates surgical implantation and stability.

A key innovation of this DSC is a one‐click fixation system that secures both frames via direct pressure. To this end, one frame contains three cylindrical stems, each 2 mm in diameter and 5.2 mm high, arranged radially at 120° intervals. The other frame contains three radially matching holes. This ensures precise alignment and secure fixation of the frames, leaving a reduced, 1 mm gap to accommodate the animal's skin. Additionally, the assembled frames exhibit a 1° angular misalignment, which results in a slightly larger gap at the bottom of the DSC (Figure 1A). This asymmetrical geometry alleviates mechanical pressure on superficial blood vessels. In addition, the distal ends of the stems have a double‐cone geometry, which enhances anchoring by engaging the skin intradermally. The continuous sloped surface of a single‐cone could inadvertently cause tissue slippage during surgical manipulation. However, this double‐cone geometry, introducing an intermediate narrowing that creates a mechanical interlocking effect with the surrounding dermal tissue, allows eliminating the need for additional materials such as tissue adhesives (Figure 1A). This self‐securing mechanism also eliminates the need for external holding devices traditionally used to stretch and stabilize the skin during suturing or implantation.

To support multimodal imaging, two customized accessories have been developed for IVM, in vivo fluorescence, and ultrasound imaging using fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing technology. For IVM, a modular support system was designed to minimize respiration‐related motion artifacts (Figure 1B). This support system securely locks the DSC in place and allows direct imaging using an inverter arm, which transforms the original microscope setup into an upright configuration. The support system consists of two primary components. The main component is a 13 mm‐thick rectangular platform (183.5 × 135.6 mm) that holds the animal in a lateral decubitus position. Built into the platform is a 132 × 57.6 mm off‐center rectangular cutout designed to house a heating pad, which is inserted laterally through a slot. This integrated heating system maintains the animal's temperature, allowing extended imaging sessions. Additionally, a securing mechanism anchors a nose cone used for anesthesia delivery. Transversely across the platform, a central column features a contoured top that matches the base of the DSC, ensuring accurate positioning and providing vertical stabilization of the DSC. Two flanking 13.25 mm high columns house M4 nuts that engage with a detachable top frame. This top frame, which includes a 16 mm central circular aperture, is screwed onto the columns to gently secure the DSC in place. The central aperture allows full optical access to the DSC's FoV. Underneath the main platform, an auxiliary base is mounted onto an x‐y‐z motorized translation stage, enabling automated scanning across the entire imaging area. This 179.5 × 152 × 5 mm auxiliary base features four corner columns designed to press‐fit the primary insert. This modular configuration allows preparing and fixing the DSC in a separate workspace before being placed on the microscope stage, thereby improving both operator accessibility and animal handling.

For in vivo fluorescence imaging, a separate modular support system was designed (Figure 1C) to maintain the animal in a stable lateral decubitus position while securely immobilizing the DSC. The support consists of a prismatic base block (63.5 × 15.8 × 18.5 mm) featuring a central column shaped to accommodate the DSC base. Two lateral pillars integrated into the block house embed M4 nuts, enabling rigid fixation of the chamber using standard screws. The support block rests directly on the heated imaging bed, ensuring maintenance of physiological body temperature throughout image acquisition. To enhance mechanical stability and prevent rotational or tilting imaging artifacts, the base incorporates three legs arranged in a tripod configuration, providing a self‐leveling and vibration‐resistant platform. A complementary top frame (135.8 × 77.5 × 1.5 mm) completes the assembly. This cover shields the animal while providing a central circular aperture (9.2 mm diameter) that allows unobstructed optical access to the DSC imaging window. The small aperture size minimizes background signal and reduces tissue autofluorescence from the surrounding skin. The top frame is secured to the lateral pillars using screws, applying uniform downward pressure that maintains the DSC in precise alignment with the central support and ensures consistent positioning of the optical plane. This platform was also employed for ultrasound imaging, as it provides effective stabilization of the DSC and maintains the coverslip parallel to the ultrasound transducer. This configuration improved acoustic coupling and enabled reproducible 3D ultrasound scanning.

DSC Surgical Procedure

The DSC implantation procedure is outlined below and illustrated in Figures 2A and S1. Thirty minutes prior to surgery, mice received subcutaneous buprenorphine for preemptive analgesia (Figure 2A, step i). The surgical area, including the entire dorsal surface and both flanks, was shaved using an electric razor. Residual hair was removed using a depilatory cream followed by gentle rinsing with warm water (step ii). After drying, the skin was disinfected twice using a 10% povidone‐iodine solution (step iii).

Surgical procedure for DSC installation. (A) Schematic representation of the key steps (i‐x) involved in the surgical implantation of the DSC, including: (i) Preoperative analgesia; (ii) Hair removal; (iii) Disinfection of the surgical site; (iv) Marking the midline, drilling points, and viewing area; (v) Drilling of the rod guides; (vi) Installation of the back frame; (vii) Excision of the skin layer and musculocutaneous sheaths; (viii) Injection of organoids; (ix) Installation of the front frame; and (x) Sealing of the DSC and administration of postoperative analgesia. (B) Schematic representation of the top‐down view of the surgical area after DSC installation. The resected area (highlighted in yellow) extends beyond the cover glass. The enlarged section on the right depicts the different skin layers: the epidermis (gray), dermis (yellow), panniculus (red), and retractor muscle (purple).

A midline was drawn along the animal's spine to guide the DSC chamber positioning. Using this midline as a reference, a circular region (∼9 mm diameter) was drawn on the left flank, along with the location of the three chamber rod entry points (step iv). Mice were then placed in a lateral decubitus position on the IVM custom‐designed platform (Figure 1B) and connected to the inhalation anesthesia system. Following confirmation of adequate anesthesia by loss of corneal and interdigital reflexes, the dorsal skin was manually stretched, keeping the midline as a folding axis. The stretched skinfold was then perforated at the marked drilling sites using a 1 mm biopsy punch (step v), carefully avoiding folds or creases that could prevent chamber sealing and lead to air bubble formation, tissue necrosis, or exudate accumulation within the imaging window.

The back‐chamber frame was then inserted through the punched holes, securing the retaining rods through the skinfold on the right flank (step vi). With the skin immobilized and stretched taut, the marked circular skin area was excised using spring scissors and fine forceps, removing the epidermis and underlying musculocutaneous layers, including the panniculus carnosus and superficial retractor muscles (step vii). The exposed tissue was rinsed with sterile saline to remove residual blood and maintain hydration. Afterward, a 10 µL bolus of PDAC93 organoid‐Matrigel suspension was injected into the panniculus carnosus using a curved 29G needle (step viii). The needle was retained in place for approximately 10 s to allow gelation, after which sterile saline was reapplied to maintain a moist interface for subsequent chamber assembly.

The front chamber frame was then carefully positioned over the exposed window and secured to the back frame using the retaining rods (step ix). The two frames locked together with a distinct mechanical click, forming a sealed structure that stabilized the tissue within the imaging window. Postoperatively (step x), meloxicam was administered subcutaneously and the animals were then placed on a 37°C warming pad until full recovery from anesthesia. The entire surgical procedure, from induction of anesthesia to full recovery, required no more than 10 min.

Please note that surgical skin excision could be performed on either the left or right flank, as the DSC enables optical access from either side of the chamber. In our case, surgery was performed on the left side, as we performed upright microscopy (Figure 2B) using an inverted arm. However, by performing surgery on the right flank, inverted microscopy could be done instead.

Dorsal Skinfold Chamber Tolerance

The DSC was implanted in 42 mice, with no reported mortality during surgery, recovery, or imaging. Importantly, no infection was observed at the site of implantation or in the DSC FoV area. Inflammatory response occurred in fewer than 5% of the animals. When it occurred, it was transient, typically lasting 2–3 days post‐implantation (dpi), and resolved without intervention. DSC‐implanted mice did not exhibit behavioral abnormalities, maintaining full mobility and weight gain comparable to non‐implanted controls throughout the 28‐day observation period (Figure 3A). Furthermore, only 15% of implanted chambers showed tilting greater than 20° over the 28‐day period (Figure 3B,C), confirming the mechanical stability and reduced skinfold strain.

Structurally, our resin‐based DSCs demonstrated mechanical robustness and functional longevity comparable to conventional titanium devices commonly used in intravital microscopy studies. Only 10% of chambers exhibited superficial bite marks at late experimental endpoints (day 28), and no cases of frame displacement, structural fracture, or device disassembly due to grooming, chewing, or inter‐animal interactions were detected. Nevertheless, in approximately 20% of animals, window integrity declined after day 21, as overgrown tumors increased intra‐chamber pressure, resulting in coverslip fracture and outward deformation of the imaging plane.

Intravital Imaging of Tumor, Vasculature, and Collagen ECM

Our custom‐engineered 3D‐printed DSC facilitated high‐resolution, longitudinal intravital imaging of PDAC93‐GFP organoid‐derived tumors from 0 to 21 dpi. Panoramic low‐magnification (×1.25) CLSM imaging revealed progressive tumor expansion within the chamber, demonstrating a consistent, time‐dependent increase in tumor volume (Figure 4A, top row). Tumor‐associated vasculature was visualized via intravenous injection of 70‐kDa Texas Red‐conjugated dextran, enabling real‐time imaging of macrovascular evolution within the TME (Figure 4A, bottom row).

Quantitative analysis of tumor growth kinetics was performed by measuring GFP‐positive tumor area between days 3 and 14 (Figure 4B). GFP fluorescence remained stable across repeated imaging sessions (Figure 4C), with no visually detectable signal decay or tissue damage throughout the entire experiment, highlighting the robustness of the DSC for extended intravital fluorescence imaging. Tumor growth exhibited a biphasic pattern: an initial lag/slow‐expansion phase (day 3 to day 7), during which the tumor volume approximately doubled, followed by an exponential growth phase (day 7 to day 14), resulting in an almost tenfold increase in tumor size.

High‐magnification CLSM and 2PEM provided detailed insights into tumor morphology, vascular dynamics—including vascular sprouting, branching complexity, tortuosity, and network density—as well as collagen microarchitecture (Figure 5A). Immediately post‐surgery, tumor organoids remained as isolated masses with minimal engagement with the surrounding ECM, and vascular networks showed no detectable perturbations (Figure S2A,B). By day 3, PDAC93‐GFP‐derived tumors predominantly displayed a cystic morphology. At this stage, there was no evidence of stromal invasion, collagen fiber compaction, or alignment (Figure 5E, F). Tumor‐associated vasculature remained largely quiescent, as indicated by baseline tortuosity and SOAM indices. Vascular reconstructions along with polar anisotropy diagrams further confirmed the presence of predominantly linear, unbranched early‐stage vessels (Figures 5B,C and S2C,D).

Between days 5 and 7, tumors transitioned into densely packed cellular aggregates, reflecting the onset of active stromal remodeling, increased vascular sprouting, and the emergence of an invasive behavior. SHG imaging revealed a progressive increase in peritumoral collagen fiber density and alignment, which became statistically significant by day 7 and exceeded remodeling observed in peri‐tumoral control regions (Figure 5D–F). In parallel, a complex peritumoral vascular plexus emerged, characterized by disordered branching, sinusoidal vessel morphology, and increased network heterogeneity, as evidenced by increased tortuosity and SOAM indices (Figures 5B,C and S2D).

By day 14, pronounced invasive features were readily apparent. Tumor cells exhibited collective and single‑cell infiltration into the surrounding stroma, with the most significant histoarchitectural disruption occurring at the tumor‐stroma interface. Although central tumor regions retained relative structural coherence, the invasive front exhibited elongated, finger‑like multicellular protrusions. Concomitant with these morphological changes, stromal microarchitecture at the periphery displayed active remodeling—including fiber compaction and alignment—indicative of TME reorganization relative to both early (day 3) post‐surgery and peritumoral control regions (Figure 5D–F). Furthermore, matrix arrangement was accompanied by a shift from straight to curly fiber bundles. In parallel with advancing tumor expansion, tumor‑associated vascularity was markedly increased compared with earlier time points. Quantitative vascular metrics revealed elevated plexus density (Figure S2E) and geometric complexity, including significant increases in both global tortuosity and angular‑based SOAM indices, accompanied by a decrease in anisotropy (⍺) (Figures 5B,C and S2D). Such a dense, aberrant vascular network correlates with the rapid volumetric expansion of the exponential growth phase (Figure 4B), indicating coordinated tumor cell proliferation and neovascular growth.

In selected instances, IVM imaging enabled visualization of tumors within the DSC for up to 21 dpi at both low and high‐magnification (Figure S2F,G). By this late stage both tumor and stromal ingrowth within the imaging field progressively reduced the effective working distance and degraded image quality. Consequently, reliable quantification of vascular metrics was not feasible at this late time point. In contrast, collagen fiber quantification remained possible, revealing a continued increase in fiber density and alignment, consistent with the progressive stromal remodeling observed at 14 dpi (Figure S2H–J).

Tumor‐Associated Neutrophil Tracking

We used the Catchup transgenic mouse model to directly analyze tumor‐associated neutrophil (TAN) dynamics during the growth of PDAC93 tumors [24]. Neutrophils were consistently observed within both intratumoral and peritumoral compartments at all examined time points. At day 3, neutrophil migration was predominantly random and nondirectional (Figure 6A, upper row), reflecting an inflammatory response to surgical implantation rather than tumor‐derived chemotactic signaling. In contrast, by day 5, neutrophil motility transitioned toward directed chemotaxis, accompanied by the emergence of localized TAN clustering (Figure 6A, middle row), indicative of active recruitment driven by tumor‐secreted chemoattractants. Neutrophil recruitment and clustering persisted through day 7 (Figure 6A, lower row).

Automated tracking of individual 3D neutrophil trajectories confirmed these observations, revealing a marked increase in directional persistence at later time points relative to day 3 (Figure 6B). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that instantaneous neutrophil velocities were highest at day 3, peaking at approximately 0.54 µm/s, and declined to ∼0.3 µm/s by days 5 and 7 (Figure 6C, D). Conversely, directional persistence increased significantly at days 5 and 7 (Figure 6E), supporting a transition from inflammation‐driven motility to tumor‐guided recruitment. Mean squared displacement (MSD) analysis revealed greater accumulated trajectory distances at day 3, with a mean slope (α) approaching 1 (0.795 ± 0.06), indicative of a predominantly diffusive, haphazard motility pattern characteristic of acute inflammatory infiltration. By contrast, trajectories at days 5 and 7 were shorter and exhibited slopes approaching 0.4, consistent with sub‐diffusive motion.

Combined Multimodal Imaging

Optical in vivo fluorescence imaging was used to track GFP‐expressing PDAC93 organoid‐derived tumors over 28 days following DSC implantation (Figure 7A). Quantitative analysis revealed a steady increase in GFP‐positive cells over time, with significant changes detected between the second and fourth weeks post‐surgery (Figure 7B). Complementary high‐resolution brightfield imaging provided insights into the temporal progression of macroscopic vasculature surrounding the tumor. Rapid revascularization was observed post‐surgery, characterized by significant vessel neoformation between days 5 and 10 (Figure 7C). By day 10, vascular density decreased, coinciding with tumor expansion and TME remodeling, reflecting the dynamic interplay between tumor growth and the surrounding stroma.

Quantitative ultrasound analysis demonstrated a significant increase in tumor volume over time (Figure 7D–F). Accurate quantification of tumor‐associated vasculature was not feasible, as only large‐caliber vessels were visible at the tumor margin (data not shown). Similarly, x‐ray imaging combined with intravenous iodine contrast enabled visualization of tumor margins and adjacent macrovasculature (Figure 7G). Quantitative analyses corroborated a progressive increase in tumor volume, consistent with ultrasound findings (Figure 7H–I).

Overall, the DSC provided reliable and consistent tumor monitoring across all imaging modalities tested. Imaging trends were particularly stable between days 14 and 28 after PDAC93 organoid implantation, highlighting the DSC's potential for longitudinal, multimodal assessment of tumor growth and TME dynamics.

Discussion

DSC systems have undergone progressive optimization aimed at improving mechanical stability, chamber design, and optical performance as required to enhance experimental reproducibility and animal welfare [25]. Early metal‐based DSCs, typically fabricated from titanium or stainless steel, offer the necessary mechanical robustness but at the cost of significant tissue compression and increased physiological burden on the host [16, 18]. Despite these well‐documented limitations, metal‐based DSCs remain widely used to this day [26, 27, 28, 29].

Recent advances in microscale manufacturing have enabled the development of a new generation of DSCs featuring reduced weight, enhanced biocompatibility, and optimized geometries. These innovations reduce mechanical stress while preserving optical access and chamber durability, thus enhancing animal welfare and facilitating surgical implantation [20, 22, 23, 30]. Building on these opportunities, we aimed to design a novel DSC guided by three main objectives: (i) minimizing the DSC footprint, (ii) reducing chamber weight, and (iii) optimizing surgical procedures.

To achieve these aims, we leveraged 3D printing, which has emerged as a transformative manufacturing strategy for next‐generation intravital imaging across multiple anatomical sites, including the brain, abdominal organs, mammary gland, subcutaneous tissues, and tumors [31, 32, 33]. Specifically, SLA‐based 3D printing enables rapid prototyping, precise geometric customization, and material optimization tailored to the mechanical and optical demands required for longitudinal IVM.

Our DSC design is a compact, low‐center‐of‐gravity system consisting of only two integrated 17 mm diameter components. This is one of the smallest existing DSC platforms (Table 1), while maintaining a FoV comparable to conventional systems. A Z‐axis printing resolution of 50 µm permitted direct integration of standard glass coverslips into the chamber body, ensuring optimal optical quality and a stable working distance compatible with high‐resolution microscopy. In the plane, a 125 µm 3D printing resolution enabled incorporation of a close‐fitting recess matched to the coverslip diameter, thereby maximizing the usable optical FoV to 9.2 mm.

The DSC weighs 0.69 g, i.e. 2.75% of the average adult mouse body weight. This represents a 41% reduction compared with previously reported 3D‐printed chambers (Table 1), and a 73% reduction compared with conventional titanium designs [34, 35]. Consistent with prior observations [19, 20], this combination of optimized geometry and lightweight materials resulted in minimal animal distress, as evidenced by postoperative gain in body weight and normal behavioral patterns, including unrestricted locomotion, climbing, and respiration. Manufacturing costs were also markedly reduced relative to metal‐based systems [19].

The versatility of 3D printing enabled integration of a novel single‐click locking mechanism directly into the DSC frame. This mechanism, aligned with previously reported minimally traumatic approaches [19, 36], eliminates the need for transdermal metallic screws and obviates sutures, which are technically demanding, prone to failure, and frequently associated with tissue strain and localized necrosis [22]. Moreover, elimination of external screws and nuts reduces the likelihood of scratching or chewing, which commonly compromises chamber stability and leads to premature detachment [37]. As a result, implantation is substantially simplified: (i) mechanical manipulation of the skinfold is minimized, and (ii) operative time is reduced to less than 10 min per mouse compared with reported durations of ∼20 min for prior 3D‐printed systems and ∼45 min for titanium‐based models [19, 36]. Collectively, these features mitigate early postoperative inflammation, an essential consideration for IVM studies of tumor initiation, immune cell recruitment, and active vascular remodeling, where surgery‐induced inflammatory artifacts can confound biological interpretation [37, 38].

SLA printing also allowed the incorporation of a 60° stabilization flap system directly into the DSC frame, consistent with prior design principles [19, 20, 39]. This minimizes lateral tilting, enhances mechanical stability, and reduces the risk of displacement or damage due to inter‐animal interactions. Consequently, only 15% of implanted chambers exhibited tilting greater than 20° at experimental endpoints, representing a substantial improvement over conventional titanium or flapless DSC designs. In such systems, excessive tilt frequently limits chamber usability beyond ∼21 days due to microvascular occlusion, ischemia, or impaired vascular remodeling [19].

The proposed DSC facilitates precise frame alignment with an embedded PDMS O‐ring seal, yielding a fully enclosed, airtight imaging chamber. This setup minimizes coverslip handling during assembly, reducing the risk of glass fracture and inadvertent damage to the panniculus carnosus muscle [39], limitations commonly associated with conventional DSCs relying on removable retaining rings [20]. Consistent with previous reports [30], this sealed setup prevents air bubble formation that promotes accumulation of turbid exudates and compromises long‐term optical clarity, while providing additional protection against infection. Consequently, only 5% of implanted DSCs exhibited small intra‐chamber air bubbles, all of which resolved spontaneously within 48 h without affecting imaging performance.

From a biocompatibility standpoint, this DSC causes minimal tissue disturbance, chronic inflammation, foreign body response, or fibrosis compared with metal‐based interfaces [37]. Furthermore, incorporation of a second coverslip in the back frame creates a 1 mm gap chamber that promotes tissue adherence to the glass surface and limits inflammatory reactions [36]. Accordingly, long‐term inflammation or signs of infection were rare (<5% of animals beyond 48 h post‐surgery) and resolved without intervention, confirming robust sealing and sustained low immunoreactivity during extended implantation periods. This is particularly relevant for oncology studies investigating stromal desmoplasia, vascular remodeling, and tumor immunomodulation [19].

The DSC frames were fabricated using Biomed Clear Resin, a USP Class VI‐certified, medical‐grade, biocompatible material suitable for prolonged skin and mucosal contact. This resin has been reported to attenuate implantation‐induced inflammatory cytokine responses [30], enabling IVM to commence on the day of implantation (Supplementary Figure S1), in contrast to prior recommendations of allowing a 1–5 dpi recovery period before imaging [39, 40, 41]. This substantially expands the available spatiotemporal imaging window (Table 1). The resin's thermal properties (heat deflection temperature of 67°C; glass transition temperature of 135°C) ensure structural stability during thermal post‐processing, steam sterilization, and heat‐assisted imaging procedures. Its elastic modulus (E = 2.1 GPa) and Izod impact strength (I = 35 J/m) provide an advantageous balance of rigidity and impact resistance—including cage‐related impacts and inter‐animal interactions, relative to other polymer‐based frames such as PEEK (E ≈ 4 GPa; I ≈ 16 J/m) [19, 42]. Consistent with these properties, implanted chambers maintained structural integrity for more than 28 dpi without fracture, disassembly, or detachment, and exhibited resistance to chewing or manipulation comparable to metal‐based designs [19, 39]. At that point, tumor volumes reached approximately 7 mm3 (Figure 7D–I), occasionally causing coverslip rupture in ∼20% of implanted mice, likely due to mechanical stress exerted by the expanding tumor against the limited 1 mm inter‐coverslip space.

Finally, the compact, modular architecture of the DSC ensures compatibility with a wide range of auxiliary tools, enabling multimodal imaging under diverse experimental conditions. The single‐click locking mechanism also serves as a robust anchoring interface for secure fixation to a microscope stage–‐mounted platform, a prerequisite for high‐quality IVM. This stabilization minimizes motion artifacts, such as image drift or blurring caused by respiration, cardiac pulsation, or spontaneous movements, thus preserving spatial registration and temporal fidelity during prolonged imaging sessions [20, 31]. Accordingly, our CLSM and 2PEM acquired images remained sharp and free of detectable motion‐induced distortions, comparable to ex vivo image quality and suitable for downstream post‐processing and quantitative analysis.

The auxiliary components fabricated from True Black PLA introduced no detectable background fluorescence compared with other materials commonly used in DSCs and reduced native tissue autofluorescence [43]. This was further enhanced by a chamber geometry that restricts optical exposure to the imaging window itself, thereby improving overall signal‐to‐noise ratios. Moreover, the PDMS‐based retaining interface, with a refractive index closely matched to glass and aqueous media, minimized refractive index mismatch and optical aberrations [31]. The stage‐mounted platform further supported animal welfare during prolonged imaging through an integrated heating pad that maintained physiological tissue temperature. External heating has been shown to preserve immune cell migration dynamics under extended imaging sessions [44], as neutrophil tracking assays (Figure 6) demonstrate, and mitigate focal drift associated with hypothermia‐induced tissue contraction [45]. Finally, the stage‐mounted platform incorporates a clamp for secure fixation of the anesthesia nose cone. During time‐lapse imaging, this configuration enables fine control of anesthesia levels to maintain a stable, nonresponsive state with slow respiration, thereby minimizing motion‐related deformations and improving survival during prolonged imaging sessions [31].

Across the presented imaging assays, the DSC consistently supported robust tumor engraftment, progression, and vascular remodeling in all implanted chambers. Notably, our results underscore the platform's reliability for long‐term tumor maintenance and intravital imaging for up to 21 dpi. The implantation of tumor organoids directly into the panniculus carnosus—a highly vascularized tissue ideal for angiogenesis and stromal remodeling studies using IVM [46]—was performed at the beginning of the surgical procedure. In contrast to postoperative inoculation [47], this strategy aligns with optimized protocols that reduce cumulative anesthesia exposure and minimize risks associated with chamber reopening, including infection, coverslip fracture, and tissue injury, while streamlining experimental workflows. By implanting Matrigel‑embedded PDAC93‑GFP organoids as a single 20 µL bolus, we limited leakage from the injection site and minimized fascia injury [36], which has been shown to enhance engraftment efficiency when combined with a hydrogel [41, 48]. No regression or dormancy of tumor implants was observed throughout the imaging period. Low‑magnification imaging revealed an initial lag phase, followed by exponential tumor growth, with stable GFP expression. No obvious imaging‐related tissue damage or signal loss was observed under our specific acquisition conditions. Unlike previous reports in which tumor regression or necrosis were associated with skin‑flap cooling, dehydration [20, 41, 49], the proposed design incorporates a one‐degree opening angle between the upper and lower frame regions. When combined with the aforementioned lateral stabilization flap, this geometry reduces mechanical compression at the host‐tissue interface and preserves local perfusion without the need for elevated ambient temperature (32°C–34°C) and humidity (>50%) sometimes employed to mitigate chamber‑induced ischemia in other systems [39, 41, 49]. Importantly, prolonged exposure to housing temperatures above 32°C can adversely affect murine metabolism and homeostasis, which could confound interpretation of longitudinal biological outcomes in IVM studies [50, 51].

In parallel with tumor phenotyping, the DSC platform enabled visualization of macrovascular architecture at lower magnification following systemic administration of fluorescent dextran. We did not pursue quantitative analysis at this scale due to elevated interstitial background signals [52, 53]. Additionally, erythrocyte extravasation and dextran leakiness, typical of aberrant tumor microvasculature [27], as well as subsequent uptake by infiltrating immune cells, further diminished signal clarity [53]. To address these limitations, other groups have traditionally employed transgenic mouse models with endothelial‑specific or inducible fluorescent reporters, which significantly enhance signal specificity [39, 54].

In contrast, high‐magnification imaging provided detailed visualization of tumor morphology, vascular remodeling, and TME microarchitecture. At early time points, PDAC93‑GFP organoids retained a cystic architecture characteristic of epithelial lineage within the Matrigel scaffold [55]. Consistent with previous DSC studies [56], no stromal invasion was observed at this stage, and tumor‐associated vasculature remained minimal, consisting predominantly of straight, hierarchically well‐organized, pre‐existing host vessels [57]. SHG imaging revealed negligible matrix remodeling during early implantation, with SHG signal intensity remaining below that of native stroma. This likely reflects the presence of Matrigel, which lacks SHG‐active fibers and had not yet been substantially remodeled or replaced by tumor‐associated stroma [58]. Collectively, these findings indicate that tumors remained in a dormant or quiescent growth phase through day 3, consistent with the lag phase identified in low‐magnification growth kinetics (Figure 4).

As tumors progressed, organoids became increasingly compact, initiating reciprocal interactions with the surrounding stroma and nascent vasculature. In accordance with other DSC tumor models, tumor‐adjacent vasculature exhibited visible sprouting angiogenesis by day 7, giving rise to a disorganized, labyrinthic network of immature and tortuous vessels with multifurcating branches and irregular diameters, hallmarks of early neovascularization observed in intravital tumor studies [59]. Concurrently, peritumoral matrix densification and collagen fiber alignment became detectable, despite suboptimal illumination associated with the upright microscope configuration used for imaging [60]. At later stages, tumors exhibited clear invasive features, with neoplastic cells infiltrating the surrounding stroma. These cells displayed elongated and aligned morphologies, widened intercellular spaces, and broad sheet‐like protrusions, resembling invasive fronts described in prior DSC intravital imaging studies [61], in which tumor cells migrate along pre‐existing vascular and collagen tracks, enabling cell dissemination. In line with other intravital studies [62], TME remodeling intensified at this stage and was characterized by pronounced collagen fiber compaction, alignment, and the emergence of an irregular fibrotic mesh, which correlated with increasing tumor size and progressive stromal desmoplasia. By day 14, tumors demonstrated complete vascularization, with an increased density of newly formed vessels penetrating deeply into the tumor core [48]. At this stage, the vascular network exhibited classical features of neoplastic vasculature, including heterogeneous spatial distribution, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, increased tortuosity, vessel dilation, and aberrant branching, as quantified by tortuosity and SOAM metrics relative to earlier time points [63]. Such vascular abnormalities are strongly associated with tumor malignancy, contributing to the formation of hypoxic and acidic niches that promote therapeutic resistance and poor prognosis [41, 59]. The resulting complex vascular architecture likely supports the heightened metabolic and oxygen demands of the rapidly expanding neoplastic tissue [59], thereby facilitating the exponential volumetric growth observed during this phase.

To interrogate how evolving tumor, vascular, and stromal features influence innate immune behavior, we used the Catchup transgenic mouse model, which is particularly well suited for high‐resolution intravital imaging and enables long‐term in vivo monitoring and quantitative analysis of time‐dependent, tumor‐driven neutrophil dynamics in early PDAC lesions [24]. Our results highlight the utility of the DSC platform for longitudinal TAN tracking, revealing a biphasic neutrophil response. An initial haphazard phase at day 3, characterized by high‐velocity, nondirectional neutrophil migration, was consistent with an acute inflammatory response to surgical implantation rather than tumor‐derived guidance cues [64]. This phase was followed by a tumor‐guided phase, marked by reduced migration speed but increased directional persistence, accompanied by focal intratumoral accumulation and the formation of dynamic TAN clusters within the tumor stroma, suggesting sustained retention and functional engagement during early tumor progression [64, 65]. This behavioral transition likely reflects the combined effects of progressive stromal densification and architectural remodeling, which impose increasing spatial confinement [66], together with localized tumor‐derived chemoattractant signaling that promotes sustained TAN recruitment and retention within the intratumoral compartment in PDAC models [67].

Despite the availability of multiple imaging modalities—including ultrasound imaging, micro‐CT, and optical bioluminescence or fluorescence imaging, IVM remains the primary strategy for visualizing cellular and subcellular events in DSC platforms [11, 63, 68]. In contrast, these alternative approaches primarily provide macroscopic information, offering limited insight into cellular behavior and tumor‐stroma interactions, and are more powerful at later time points (7–28 dpi). Notably, optical fluorescence imaging enables longitudinal monitoring of tumor‐derived GFP signals from the time of implantation up to 28 days, demonstrating a steady increase in tumor burden over time, consistent with previously reported growth kinetics in DSC‐based tumor models [27, 29, 69]. In addition, this approach enables quantitative assessment of macrovascular remodeling in parallel with tumor expansion through high‐resolution brightfield imaging, revealing a pronounced proangiogenic response between 3 and 7 dpi, consistent with the vascular sprouting patterns observed by IVM. To our knowledge, no studies have yet integrated micro‐CT with DSC platforms, and only two reports have applied ultrasound imaging [35, 70]. Both approaches enable volumetric monitoring of implanted tumors, showing growth trends comparable to those observed by intravital or fluorescence imaging. However, these techniques provide limited vascular information and typically require contrast agents for detailed visualization, constraining their use for tumor–stroma analysis [36]. Collectively, these results demonstrate the strong capabilities of the proposed DSC for longitudinal monitoring of PDAC, highlighting the platform's optical transparency and minimal imaging artifacts—similar to those designed for MRI applications [22, 30, 71, 72]—compared with traditional titanium‐derived chambers.

Conclusion

In this work, we successfully designed and developed an SLA‐printed DSC system—featuring a lightweight, miniaturized architecture, rapid, low‐trauma surgical implantation, and enhanced mechanical stability and optical performance—that provides a robust, well‐tolerated platform for long‐term, high‐resolution, multimodal imaging in preclinical cancer models. This integrated system offers a substantial advancement over conventional metal‐based chambers as well as previously reported 3D‐printed designs.It enablin comprehensive micro to macroscale visualization and quantitative interrogation of early tumorigenic events—from tumor initiation and angiogenic sprouting to stromal remodeling and coordinated immune cell dynamics—key biological events that drive tumor progression and the emergence of therapeutic resistance, within a physiologically relevant, longitudinal in vivo context. Collectively, these features establish a versatile, cost‐effective, and biologically robust tool for longitudinal multimodal studies, particularly in complex tumor microenvironment research, drug delivery investigations, and preclinical cancer therapy evaluation.

Methods

Fabrication of DSC and Auxiliary Toolset

Both the DSCs and the peripheral support tools were fabricated from AutoCAD (Autodesk, USA, RRID:SCR_022500) 3D models using SLA and FDM technologies, respectively. The DSC frames were fabricated using an SLA Form3 printer (Formlabs, USA) with Biomed Clear Resin (FLBMCG01, Formlabs, USA). To this end, the 3D STL files (File_STL 1‐2) were first loaded into the 3D printing software PreForm (Formlabs, USA, RRID:SCR_021464). The inner sides of the frames face away from the printing platform, ensuring that the area of the frames that holds the cover glass and faces the animal's skin, remains free of contact points from the support printing structure. Once the support structure is defined, the printing process is launched with a predefined layer height of 25 µm. Once the printing process is complete, the frames are washed in 99% isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 15 min in the Form Wash bath (Formlabs, USA). After drying for 15 min, a second 5‐minute bath is performed in 99% clean IPA. After extraction, the parts are left in the dark for at least 30 min to allow complete IPA evaporation. Then the printed parts are cured in the Form Cure system (Formlabs, USA) at 60°C for 15 min. Subsequently, the printing supports are removed and polished with a Dremel tool (Bosch, Germany) to clear any irregularities that could injure the animal or operator during the implantation of the DSC, or cause misalignments in the pressure connection systems.

Once the DSC frames are fabricated, the optical window components can be mounted into position. To do so, the coverslip indentation, located on the frame sides that face the animal's skin, is painted with BioMed Clear resin in a room with low lighting using a fine‐tipped brush. After allowing it to rest for two minutes, a coverslip is securely attached to the indentation by applying direct pressure with a suction cup lifter. Subsequently, the frames—with the coverslips facing upward—are exposed to 1650 mW UV light (Thorlabs, Germany) for 15 min to facilitate complete resin curing and secure adhesion of the glass to the frame structure. Then the PDMS (DC184, Sylgard 184, Dow Corning, USA) O‐rings are integrated into the frames. To this end, a mixture of PDMS and curing agent is prepared in a 10:1 ratio. The mixture is left in a desiccator for 15 min to remove air bubbles. Once the mixture is ready, it is painted onto the contour of the coverslip while each frame is positioned horizontally to facilitate the homogeneous distribution of the PDMS. The frames are then placed in an incubator at 67°C. This step ensures complete curing of the PDMS, which contributes to sealing the wounded skin of the animal upon DSC implantation, and guarantees the tightness of the junction between the coverslip and the resin frame. Once the process is complete, the frames are sterilized. To do so, the frames are immersed in IPA for 15 min, after which they are exposed to UV light for 15 min. Finally, the frames are left at room temperature for at least 2 h to evaporate IPA before use or storing the frames for future use. The DSC has a compact design and weighs 0.69 g.

The auxiliary components for IVM and fluorescence imaging were fabricated using an Ultimaker S5 FDM 3D printer (Ultimaker, Netherlands) with True Black Polylactic Acid (PLA) filament (8434000002535, SmartFil, Spain). The fabrication process begins by loading the 3D STL files (File_STL 3–7) into Cura (Ultimaker, Netherlands, RRID:SCR_018232). The DSC‐shaped socket on top of both peripheral tools is positioned in the opposite direction from the printing platform to avoid the use of supports in this area, which could compromise the assembly of the peripheral tools. Then, the support structure is defined, and the printing process is configured with a layer height of 100 µm. Once the printing process is completed, the printed pieces are removed, supports are stripped and surfaces are polished with a Dremel 3000 manual machining tool (Bosch, Germany) to smooth out any irregularities that may hinder the location of the supports.

Organoid Culture

A PDAC93 organoid‐generating cell line derived from primary pancreatic tumors of C57Bl/6Kras+/LSL‐G12D; Trp53Flox/flox; Ptf1aCre/+ mice was used. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FetalClone III and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Stable GFP‐expressing PDAC93‐GFP cells were generated through lentiviral transduction of the parental cell line. Briefly, X‐tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (6366244001, Roche, Switzerland) and a third‐generation Lentiviral Packaging Mix (SHP001, Merck, Germany) were used, following manufacturer's instructions. The PDAC93‐GFP cell population with the highest fluorescence signal was subsequently enriched using a FACSAria IIb sorting system (BD Biosciences, USA).

To generate 3D organoids, PDAC93‐GFP cells were embedded in non‐gelled Matrigel (4 mg/mL; 356231, Corning, USA) at a final concentration of 3 × 105 cells/mL. A 20 µL droplet of the cell‐hydrogel mixture was dispensed into each cylindrical loading well of a custom‐made PDMS device [55] and allowed to gel at 37°C for 15 min. Following gelation, the organoid culture media (Table 2) was added, and the organoids were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 7 days. Before being injected into the animals, Matrigel was removed using Cell Recovery Solution (354253, Corning, USA) following manufacturer's instructions. Residual Matrigel was removed by gentle centrifugation (700 rpm, 5 min) to minimize organoid fragmentation. At the time of injection during the surgical procedure, approximately 700 organoids with a median diameter of 161.86 µm were administered per DSC.

Animal Care and Handling

Male wild‐type C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old, ∼20 g body weight) were sourced from Charles River Laboratories (RRID: IMSR_CRL:027, France). Catchup mice (C57BL/6−Ly6g2621(CretdTomato)Arte), which constitutively express the Ly6G‐Tag‐tdTomato fusion protein in neutrophils [24], were bred in‐house. Mice were housed in a pathogen‐free environment at the CIMA animal facility, University of Navarra (registration number ES31‐2010000132), under controlled conditions: temperature 20°C–22°C, relative humidity 50%–60%, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle.

To minimize distress, anesthesia and analgesia were administered as appropriate. Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 5% isoflurane (469860, IsoVet, Braun, Germany) with 0.5 L/min O2 for induction, then maintained at 1%–2% isoflurane during procedures. Pre‐surgical analgesia consisted of 0.1 mg/kg buprenorphine CN 578950, (Buprecare;subcutaneously Ecuphar, Barcelona, Spain) in 0.9% saline (Braun, Germany). Post‐surgical analgesia included 5 mg/kg meloxicam (141‐219, Metacam, Boehringer, Germany) in 0.9% saline, administered immediately after surgery and 24 and 48 h after surgery. Mice also daily received 10 mg/kg enrofloxacin (2157‐ESP, Quinolcen‐100, Cenavisa Lab, Spain) in their drinking water for 72 h post‐surgery.

After DSC installation, mice were initially housed individually and provided with dietary gel to facilitate food intake, and shredded paper was added for enrichment. This housing strategy has been widely reported in the literature and was used to prevent potential conflicts that could damage the DSC frame. Nevertheless, we subsequently performed group housing (2–3 animals per cage) and observed no behavioral alterations or damage to the DSC devices. Weight and overall well‐being were monitored daily by trained personnel, with particular attention during the first 72 h post‐procedure. Dropout criteria included 20% body weight loss, inflammation or infection at the surgical site, ischemia or necrosis, 90° DSC inclination angle, or signs of pain or illness.

Body Weight and DSC Tilting

Mouse body weight was monitored longitudinally using a digital precision scale (Mettler‐Toledo, Switzerland) at consistent times between 9:00 and 9:30 a.m. on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, and 27 after DSC implantation. To account for the additional mass introduced by the chamber, the weight of each individual DSC was subtracted from the total body weight at each time point to obtain corrected measurements for each mouse.

Chamber tilting was evaluated under anesthesia at 0, 6, 15, 21, and 27 dpi using a protocol adapted from Xie et al. [19]. Following the loss of the interdigital reflex, mice were placed in a prone position with all four limbs evenly positioned and the spine aligned. The lateral tilt angle of the DSC was then measured using a manual protractor. Six animals per experimental condition were assessed to evaluate chamber alignment and stability over time.

Intravital Microscopy

Maintaining tissue stability is essential for acquiring high‐quality, reproducible images. To this end, animals were immobilized on a custom‐designed imaging platform specifically engineered to stabilize the tissue and preserve it flat, perpendicular to the objective lens (Figure 1B).

The DSC was mechanically secured between a central column and an upper plate in a sandwich‐like configuration, as described in the “5.1. Fabrication of DSC and auxiliary toolset” section. This setup minimizes image drifting and motion artifacts due to cardiac and respiratory activity, while allowing the animal to breathe freely. The microscope stage includes integrated clamp supporting connectors for gas anesthesia, surgical cannulas for fluorescent tracer injection, and electrodes for comprehensive monitoring of physiological parameters. A built‐in heating pad maintains the animal's core body temperature throughout the procedure. These features collectively ensure optimal tissue stabilization and physiological control, enabling long‐term imaging sessions with minimal disturbance once the mouse is correctly positioned.

IVM was conducted 0, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21 days after DSC implantation. However, as no relevant biological insights were obtained at day 0 (see “2.4. Intravital imaging of tumor, vasculature, and collagen ECM” section), mice were left undisturbed for at least 72 h post‐surgery to minimize any potential alterations in microvascular integrity, tumor progression, or inflammatory response. Prior to imaging, the chamber coverslip was gently cleaned with a cotton swab to prevent blurring and to eliminate external artifacts that could interfere with the fluorescence signal. When experiments required vascular visualization, an intravenous injection of Texas Red dextran (3 mg/mL, 70 kDa, P36980, ThermoFisher) was administered under inhaled anesthesia 30 min prior to imaging.

Image acquisition was performed using an inverted Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with 1.25x (EC Plan‐Neofluar 1.25x/0.03, Carl Zeiss) and 20x (W Plan‐Apochromat 20x/1.0, Carl Zeiss) objectives, mounted on an InverterScope arm (LSM Tech, USA), which transformed the setup into an upright configuration (see Figure 1B). All microscopy settings were controlled using Zen 2.3 (Carl Zeiss, Germany, RRID:SCR_013672) software. Acquisition settings were optimized according to the specific imaging application. A detailed summary of the acquisition parameters used for each experiment is provided in Table 3.

Images of Second Harmonic Generation (SHG) from collagen, PDAC93‐GFP organoid‐derived tumors, and Texas Red dextran‐stained vasculature (Figure 5) were acquired using a sequential microscopy imaging setup. For the SHG and GFP signals, the microscope was operated in two‐photon, non‐descanned mode (2PEM). A two‐photon femtosecond pulsed MaiTai DeepSeeTM laser (Spectra‐Physics, USA), tuned to a central wavelength of 880 nm, was used for this purpose. The GFP and SHG signals were collected simultaneously after spectral separation and emission re‐filtering, with SHG detected through a 420–480 nm bandpass filter and GFP detected through a 500–550 nm bandpass filter. Blood vessels were visualized in confocal, descanned (CLSM) mode using a He–Ne 543 nm excitation laser and collected in the 555–735 nm emission range. Stacks of 120–400 µm (depending on tumor thickness) were acquired, using a 5 µm z‐spacing. The scanning pixel time was set to 0.76 µs, generating 1024 × 1024 images (FoV 710 × 710 µm).

CLSM imaging was used to visualize tumor‐associated neutrophil (TAN) migration dynamics (Figure 6). For this purpose, fluorescence from PDAC93‐GFP organoids was captured within the 500–550 nm range, while tdTomato labeled neutrophils were imaged in the 580–735 nm range using 488 nm and 543 nm excitation lasers, respectively. Time‐lapse videos were acquired generating 512 × 512 images (FoV 422 × 422 µm). Stacks of 35–100 µm optical sections with 5 µm z‐spacing were acquired every 15 s to visualize TAN dynamics, generating sequences of at least 14 frames.

Image Quantification

Quantitative bioimaging metrics were used to longitudinally monitor tumor pathophysiology. Fiber density, defined as fiber deposition and compaction per tumor volume, and anisotropy (α), which describes the direction‐dependent arrangement of fibers within biological tissues, were evaluated 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21 dpi, within the DSC, using a method previously described by our group and adapted to Fiji/ImageJ (Bethesda, MD, USA; RRID:SCR_002285; SCR_003070) [77]. Briefly, tumor Z‐stack images were binarized to segment the entire tumor volume, followed by morphological closing and hole‐filling operations. The resulting mask was then denoised via median filtering. Tumor boundaries were expanded 10 µm to incorporate peri‐tumoral fibers in the analysis. Second‐harmonic generation (SHG) signal was automatically binarized using the Moments thresholding algorithm to identify tumor‐associated fibers, and objects smaller than 1 µm were excluded as noise. Once the 3D fiber mask was generated, fiber density was quantified as the fraction of area occupied by fibers relative to the tumor area. For cross‐sample normalization, all images were normalized to a control density region of interest (RoI; 100 × 100 × 100 pixels) randomly located in a non‐tumor region of the same image.

Fiber anisotropy (α) was quantified from the tumor‐associated fiber MIP images extracted above using a Fourier transform‐based approach described by Sander et al. [78]. In their framework, an isotropic network of randomly oriented fibers corresponds to α = 0, whereas a fully aligned fiber network yields α = 1. To allow cross‐sample comparisons, anisotropy measurements were normalized using a control RoI (100 × 100 pixels) from a non‐tumor region within the same image.

Vascular remodeling was assessed by quantifying vessel tortuosity (τ) using two complementary metrics: (i) the distance metric (DM) and (ii) the sum‐of‐angles metric (SOAM), evaluated 3, 5, 7, and 14 dpi. Tortuosity measurements were derived from vessel segmentation and quantification pipelines adapted from Bullitt et al. [79] and Pienn et al. [80].

The stability of the GFP fluorescence signal, derived from tumor cells, was assessed at all analyzed time points in both low‐ and high‐magnification images. To this end, images were converted to 8‐bit format (0–255 intensity range), and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified within the tumor segmentation masks generated as described above.

All image processing and analysis was conducted in Fiji/ImageJ. For each Z‐stack, vessel segmentation and centerline extraction preceded tortuosity analysis. Briefly, Texas Red vascular signal was isolated and processed using 3D morphological opening and closing operations, followed by 3D Gaussian blurring to enhance vessel lumen definition and central axis intensity. The complete vessel network was then segmented via automatic local thresholding with a fixed window size corresponding to the largest vessel branch radius. Vessel skeletons were then extracted from the preprocessed images using a ridge‐detection algorithm [81], generating a labeled mask with the spatial coordinates of each vessel centerline represented as splines.

The DM is a well‐established metric, calculated as the ratio of actual vessel path length to the straight‐line (Euclidean) distance between its endpoints. Vessel tortuosity (τ) was computed from spline coordinates and anchor points as the ratio of geodesic path length to Euclidean distance between the endpoints. In contrast, SOAM index quantified tortuosity (τ) by integrating the cumulative angular changes along the vessel centerline, normalized by total vessel length. Specifically, each vessel spline was discretized into n consecutive linear segments of equal length, and each segment was then described by a direction vector connecting adjacent points along the spline. After vector normalization, the angle between successive vectors was calculated via the dot product. Summation of these angular deviations across the vessel, obtained by summing the angles between all adjacent vector pairs, yielded the cumulative curvature, which was then normalized by the discretized total vessel length to produce a sinusoid‐sensitive, length‐independent measure of tortuosity (rad/µm). Vessels shorter than 50 µm were excluded to minimize artifact inclusion. At least 119 vessels per image were segmented and quantified for both DM and SOAM at each time point across three independent mice. The resulting measurements were averaged to generate a single mean value per image, which was used for statistical analysis and graphical representation. In contrast, vessel network anisotropy and density were quantified at the whole‐image level, with one value obtained per image for downstream statistical analysis.

Imaris v9.8 (Oxford Instruments) was used to quantify neutrophil motion. Automatic cell tracking was performed using the Autoregressive Motion algorithm, with the spot diameter set to 6.6 µm and a maximum distance of 4.55 µm. All automatically generated tracks were subsequently inspected to correct potential tracking errors. Migration parameters, including mean squared displacement (MSD), TAN straightness, and speed, were quantified using Imaris Statistics and the XTension plugin. At least 159 trajectories and 1,376 TAN instances were analyzed for neutrophil straightness and speed, respectively, at each time point across three independent mice. The resulting per‐object values were used for statistical analysis and graphical representation. For MSD analysis, values were averaged to generate a single mean value per frame, and at least 14 frames per sequence were analyzed at each time point across three independent mice. The resulting per‐frame mean values were used for downstream statistical analysis and plotting.

Optical Fluorescence Imaging

Longitudinal epifluorescence imaging of PDAC93‐GFP organoid‐derived tumors grown within the DSC was conducted using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) 0, 3, 5, 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28 dpi. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in air (IsoVet, Braun, Germany), and GFP fluorescence was captured using filter settings of λ e x = 465 nm and λ e m = 520–580 nm.

To optimize detection sensitivity, imaging was performed using automatic illumination parameters: auto‐exposure time, pixel binning set to 8, f/Stop = 2, and a 6.5 cm FoV. To minimize background signal and improve image contrast, a custom‐designed, 3D‐printed blackout chamber (Figure 1C) was used to cover the animal's body, leaving only the imaging window exposed.

Fluorescence signal analysis was done using Living Image software (v4.7.4, PerkinElmer Inc., USA, RRID:SCR_014247). Tissue autofluorescence was computationally separated from the GFP signal using the software's automatic spectral unmixing algorithm. Unmixed images were quantified by manually drawing a consistent RoI over the DSC visualization area. RoIs were kept uniform across all animals and time points to ensure analytical consistency. Background fluorescence was corrected by subtracting the signal intensity of a RoI from a sham‐operated animal imaged under identical settings. This enhanced the signal‐to‐noise ratio for each measurement. Quantitative data were normalized to average radiant efficiency [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2] to allow for reliable cross‐animal and cross‐time point comparisons. To correlate GFP intensity with tumor cell numbers, a calibration curve was generated. Serial dilutions of PDAC93‐GFP cells were seeded into a black 96‐well plate, imaged under the same acquisition settings, and analyzed identically. The resulting fluorescence intensities were used to construct a linear regression trendline, enabling estimation of GFP‐positive cell populations from in vivo data (data not shown).

High‐Resolution Ultrasound Imaging

High‐resolution ultrasound imaging of PDAC93‐derived organoids within the DSC was performed using the Vevo3100 imaging platform (Fujifilm VisualSonics, Canada) equipped with an MX550D transducer. Imaging sessions were conducted on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 post‐DSC implantation. The MX550D probe operated at a central frequency of 40 MHz, with a 7.0 mm focal length and a 557 frames‐per‐second acquisition rate (single zone, 5.08 mm width, B‐mode). The 2D FoV spanned 14.1 × 15.0 mm, offering a high spatial resolution of 90 µm (lateral) and 40 µm (axial).

Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in air (IsoVet, Braun, Germany) and placed in a lateral decubitus position on a heated stage. Electrocardiogram electrodes were affixed to the limbs and tail for continuous monitoring of heart rate and respiratory status. After achieving loss of righting reflex, the isoflurane concentration was reduced to 1%–2% to maintain physiological heart rates (300–400 bpm) throughout the imaging procedure. Body temperature was monitored via a rectal probe to ensure thermal homeostasis. Prior to scanning, the imaging site was depilated using a chemical hair removal cream (Veet, Reckitt Benckiser, Spain), and pre‐warmed ultrasound gel (99.800.20, Quick Eco‐Gel, Lessa, Spain) was applied to enhance acoustic coupling. Once the tumor mass was visualized, B‐mode images were acquired along the entire tumor axis using an integrated 3D motorized scanning module. Tumor volume (mm3) was quantified using VevoLab analysis software (v5.8.1, VisualSonics, Canada, RRID:SCR_018048). For each scan, a trained operator semi‐automatically delineated the tumor margins in each frame to generate a 3D RoI, from which volumetric reconstructions were computed.

Micro‐CT Imaging

High‐resolution 3D tomographic imaging of the DSC was performed using a Quantum GX micro‐CT system (PerkinElmer, USA) on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 following DSC implantation. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in air (IsoVet, Braun, Germany), and scanning was conducted using the high‐resolution protocol with a 90 kVp X‐ray source, 88 µA current, and a total acquisition time of 4 min. Imaging involved a full 360° gantry rotation. To mitigate motion artifacts caused by respiration, a retrospective respiratory gating protocol was employed. Each scan comprised a stack of 512 slices with isotropic voxel dimensions of 50 µm and a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels per slice. To enhance visualization of vascular structures and tumor tissue, mice received a 300 µL intravenous bolus of iodine‐based contrast agent (Omnipaque 300 mg/mL, 00407141365, GE Healthcare, Germany) 5 min prior to imaging.

For tumor volume quantification, a RoI covering the entire 10 × 10 × 10 mm chamber volume was reconstructed at a resolution of 20 µm per voxel using Quantum 3.0 software. Tumor segmentation was performed using a custom‐developed Fiji plugin designed for voxel‐based thresholding and binarization. The resulting binary masks were then imported into Amira software (v5.2, Thermo Scientific, USA) for 3D rendering and visualization.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8, GraphPad, USA). All assays included at least three biological replicates obtained from three independent DSC‐implanted mice, designated R1 to R3 (the sample size, n, is specified in the figure legends). Quantitative data are expressed as mean values, with error bars representing the standard deviation (SD) of biological replicates. The statistical pipeline followed was: i) normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov and Shapiro‐Wilk tests; ii) for parametric data, an unpaired t‐test was used for comparisons between two independent samples, and one‐ or two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison tests was used for comparisons involving more than two groups; iii) for non‐parametric data, a Mann‐Whitney test was used for comparisons between two independent samples. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted with asterisks (*, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001). (#, Statistical differences refer to comparisons between the RoI measured and the RoI control group).
