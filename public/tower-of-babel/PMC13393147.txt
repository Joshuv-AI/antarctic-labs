Chronic contained rupture of an abdominal aortic aneurysm mimicking a retroperitoneal mass with hydronephrosis
Authors: Narumitsu Kagoshima, Uchida Takayuki, Matsumoto Takashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic contained rupture (CCR) of an abdominal aortic aneurysm rarely causes hydronephrosis and may mimic retroperitoneal malignancy. We report the case of a 66-year-old woman presenting with hydronephrosis caused by a CCR-related hematoma and an accessory renal artery originating from the terminal aorta. Biopsies excluded malignancy. Because endovascular repair carried a risk of type II endoleak and renal ischemia, open aneurysm repair with direct arterial reimplantation was performed. This approach relieved ureteral compression, achieved definitive aneurysm exclusion, and preserved renal perfusion. CCR should be considered in the differential diagnosis of irregular retroperitoneal masses associated with hydronephrosis.Keywords: Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Accessory renal artery, Anatomical variation, Chronic contained rupture, Hydronephrosis, Vascular surgery

Case report

A 66-year-old woman presented with lower back pain that started 6 weeks before the initial consultation and gradually worsened over time. Her medical history included hypertension, but there was no family history of abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) or known connective tissue disease. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a mass-like lesion with irregular low-attenuation areas adjacent to the aorta, compressing the left ureter and causing ipsilateral hydronephrosis (Fig 1). The Hounsfield units of the mass were 50 to 70 Hounsfield units, which was consistent with an organized hematoma. There were no concomitant aneurysms noted elsewhere on imaging. The patient provided informed consent for the publication of this case report and accompanying images. CT angiography demonstrated an infrarenal AAA with a maximal transverse diameter of 63 mm, accompanied by a retroperitoneal hematoma measuring 63 × 35 mm. The CT angiography also showed a defect in the left posterolateral aortic wall without obvious active contrast leakage into the hematoma, and an accessory renal artery (ARA) supplying the lower pole of the right kidney, originating from the terminal aorta (Fig 2).

The initial differential diagnoses included malignant lymphoma and other retroperitoneal malignancies. Positron emission tomography-CT was considered; however, tissue diagnosis was prioritized because imaging findings strongly suggested retroperitoneal malignancy. During the 146 days from initial presentation to the definitive diagnosis, there was an increase from 63 × 16 to 63 × 35 mm in the size of the retroperitoneal hematoma. Because malignancy was strongly suspected and the patient remained hemodynamically stable, a stepwise diagnostic evaluation was undertaken. Both CT-guided and laparoscopic biopsies were performed to establish a definitive diagnosis. At the time of laparoscopy, dense fibrous tissue and an organized hematoma without obvious tumor cells were visualized. Histopathological examination revealed no evidence of malignancy; however, an organized hematoma was observed. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of chronic contained rupture (CCR) of AAA was made.

In selecting the treatment strategy, both the patient’s age and the presence of an ARA were critical considerations. Endovascular aneurysm repair poses a significant risk of type II endoleaks due to potential retrograde flow from the ARA if it was covered. Pre-emptive embolization was also considered, but it carries the risk of renal infarction. Therefore, open surgical repair was performed to ensure definitive aneurysm exclusion while preserving renal function.

Intraoperative findings and postoperative course

A midline laparotomy was performed, followed by an aorto-bi-iliac bypass using a Y-shaped graft. Severe adhesions were observed between the retroperitoneum and the vascular structures. After securing proximal and distal control, the organized hematoma was evacuated, and the ruptured aortic wall was debrided (Fig 3). The site of the rupture was confirmed at the left posterolateral aspect of the infrarenal aorta.

To preserve perfusion of the right kidney, the ARA was reimplanted into the right limb of the prosthetic graft. The ischemic period for the ARA before completion of the reconstruction was approximately 15 minutes. with cold crystalloid solution was used to minimize ischemic renal injury.

The postoperative course was uneventful, without any major complications. Renal function remained stable, and preoperative hydronephrosis resolved spontaneously after relief of ureteral compression without the need for ureteral stenting. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 15 (Fig 4), which reflects our institution's standard postoperative recovery protocol for open AAA repair in Japan, ensuring complete oral intake and pain control.

Discussion

This case highlights two major clinical challenges: the diagnostic difficulty of CCR presenting with hydronephrosis, and the management of rare renal arterial variations during AAA repair.

First, AAAs rarely cause ureteral obstruction. It is typically associated with periaortic inflammation in inflammatory AAA or compression of iliac aneurysms.1,2 Although CCR is a well-characterized phenomenon in the literature—often defined as a retroperitoneal hematoma sealed by surrounding structures—it frequently presents a diagnostic challenge. Previous reports have noted that the hematoma associated with CCR can occasionally appear as an irregular mass, making it difficult to distinguish between retroperitoneal tumors (such as lymphoma) and inflammatory AAAs using imaging alone. In our case, the CCR caused severe unilateral hydronephrosis without systemic inflammatory signs, further mimicking a retroperitoneal malignancy. Thus, although previous papers have described CCR, the present case is uniquely distinguished by this rare combination of tumor-mimicking hydronephrosis and a highly complex renal vascular anomaly.3 Although repeated biopsies may enable a correct diagnosis, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for CCR to avoid progression to free rupture.

Second, the surgical strategy was influenced by the unusual origin of the ARA. Although ARAs are present in approximately 21% of the population, they rarely originate in the terminal aorta.4 In endovascular aneurysm repair, the simple coverage of such vessels increases the risk of type II endoleaks and subsequent aneurysm sac enlargement.5 Although coil embolization is an alternative, it carries a risk of renal parenchymal loss.

Third, the relatively young age of the patient (66 years) supported the use of open surgical repair for long-term durability. Open repair allowed definitive aneurysm exclusion, direct reimplantation of the ARA to preserve renal function, and immediate decompression of the ureter. Regarding the management of hydronephrosis, perioperative ureteral stenting was considered; however, we opted against it to avoid potential stent-related urinary tract infections, anticipating that direct surgical decompression of the hematoma would suffice. As reported in cases of inflammatory AAA-related hydronephrosis, surgical decompression often leads to spontaneous resolution, as observed in the present case.2

Conclusions

We report a case of CCR of AAA with an ARA originating from the terminal aorta that required differentiation from malignancy owing to the associated hydronephrosis. Therefore, CCR should be considered in the differential diagnosis of irregular periaortic masses. In relatively young patients with a complex renal vascular anatomy, open surgical repair remains an effective strategy, enabling the simultaneous resolution of ureteral compression, prevention of endoleaks, and preservation of renal function through arterial reimplantation.
