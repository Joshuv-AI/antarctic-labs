The non-uniform electron gas
Mihály A. Csirik ∗,1 and Andre Laestadius 1,2

Abstract
. The non-uniform (or inhomogeneous) electron gas has received much attention in many-body quantum mechanics and quantum chemistry in the early days of density functional theory, mainly as a theoretical device to construct gradient approximations via linear response theory. In this article, motivated by the recent works of Lewin, Lieb and Seiringer, we propose a definition of the quantum (resp. classical) non-uniform electron gas through the use of the grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional (resp. the grand-canonical strictly correlated electrons functional), establish these systems as rigorous thermodynamic limits and analyze their basic properties. The non-uniformity of the gas comes from an arbitrary lattice-periodic background density.



1.
Introduction

Before the widespread acceptance of the name “density functional theory” early researchers of the field referred to what we call DFT today as
the theory of the “inhomogeneous electron gas”
[
16
,
26
]
.
As far as we can tell, the (weakly) inhomogeneous electron gas is regarded a “fictitious system”, or a stepping stone, and not a “real” physical system like the uniform electron gas (UEG).
Furthermore, it is always understood in a perturbative sense, i.e. through its linear response
[
11
,
9
]
. These density perturbations are measured relative to the constant density of the homogeneous gas. In
[
9
]
“both the case of a localized perturbation as well as some periodic structure” are claimed to be covered. The treatment of a localized perturbation in the thermodynamic limit remains somewhat unclear. Hence, we will consider the case of a lattice-periodic inhomogeneity in this work.
Rather than relying on linear response theory, we treat the inhomogeneous electron gas as a genuinely nonlinear (i.e. non‑perturbative) system. The weakly inhomogeneous electron gas may be considered within this nonlinear framework as a perturbation of the homogeneous gas by a slowly varying inhomogeneity.

Henceforth, we shall adopt the term
non-uniform electron gas
(NUEG) in order to better align with the existing mathematical physics literature pertaining to the UEG
[
18
]
. The main reason for this is that we will also employ density functionals to define the NUEG energy. The alternative path would be to consider the thermodynamic limit of an electronic ground-state problem like in the case of the Jellium
[
23
]
. In the Jellium model the external potential is obviously constant. However, it is unclear what external potential (or whether such potential exists) would generate a prescribed lattice-periodic inhomogeneity as a ground-state density. This issue is related to the so-called
v
-representability problem, which is poorly understood at the present—there is no analytic description of the
v
-representability set except in 1D on a finite interval with Neumann boundary conditions
[
5
,
4
]
.
The approach of
[
18
]
elegantly avoids these complications by defining the UEG energy through the use of density functionals and demanding the density to be constant.
The price to pay is that we lose the connection with the ground-state problem and have to deal with the constrained optimization problem, which may not even have the usual Schrödinger equation as its Euler–Lagrange equation. The said connection is still unresolved for the quantum UEG, but is settled for the classical case
[
6
,
19
]
.

Our definition of the NUEG in the 3D Coulomb case involves a fixed lattice-periodic
inhomogeneity

\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
having natural integrability and regularity properties.
This
\zeta
replaces the constant density
\rho_{0}
in the
definitions of
[
18
]
based on the strictly-correlated electrons (SCE) functional (classical case) and the grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional
[
22
]

(quantum case). In order to obtain a well-behaved energy per volume, we average the energy over the translations and rotations of
\zeta
. More precisely,
the (3D Coulomb) indirect energy per volume of the classical NUEG with inhomogeneity
\zeta
in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is taken to be

e_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\,\Bigg[\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{P}\,\text{GC prob.}\\
\rho_{\mathbb{P}}=\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}x_{n})}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}(x)\zeta(R(x-a))\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}(y)\zeta(R(y-a))}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\Bigg]

in the classical case and analogously in the quantum case, which we denote by
e_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
. Here, a regularization function
\eta_{\delta}
must be used, because quantum densities cannot be cut off sharply.
Noting that the
a
-integrand above is lattice-periodic and locally integrable, the mean value over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
exists and equals the mean value over
a unit cell of the lattice. This is a “floating crystal-like” construction, where
the lattice is allowed to float in the container
\Omega
.
Under the sole assumption that
\zeta^{4/3}
is locally integrable
(and that
\sqrt{\zeta}
is in
H_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}
in the quantum case) we can prove that the thermodynamic limit of
e_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)
(resp.
e_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
)
exists for rather general domain sequences. We denote the limiting functional by
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)
(resp.
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
). In the quantum case, we also offer a slightly different definition based on a transition region near the boundary where the density is allowed to “relax” in an arbitrary manner analogously to
[
18
]
.
We prove the equivalence of the two definitions.

Clearly, by putting
\zeta\equiv\rho_{0}
we recover the UEG energy exactly, even at finite volume.
We consider the slowly varying limits
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}/\hbar}(\zeta(\lambda\cdot))
as
\lambda\to 0
and establish the rate of its convergence to the UEG energy
dictated by the local density approximation (LDA)
both in the classical-, and the quantum case. These follow from an adaptation of the method of
[
20
]
.

In addition to establishing the NUEG as a physically reasonable system with expected properties, our work provides a complementary perspective on DFT—in fact, instead of integrable
densities
, the functionals
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}/\hbar}(\zeta)
introduced involve locally integrable
inhomogeneities

\zeta
that are
not
globally integrable. It would be interesting to extend the allowed set of inhomogeneities to a broader class, such as to some space of almost periodic functions.
On the technical side, we improve a very useful spatial decoupling estimate in
Theorem

5.9
that appeared first in
[
20
]
, which is a crucial tool in the quantum case.

2.2.
Lattice-periodic functions

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

A unit cell

\mathcal{L}

for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
and almost all

. Such functions are almost everywhere determined by their values in a unit cell
\Lambda
.

We will repeatedly make use of the following elementary fact, the proof of which is provided in
Appendix

for the convenience of the Reader.

.

For any
\mathcal{L}
-periodic complex-valued function

, the mean value of

over

exists and equals its mean value over a unit cell
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.

(1)

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{C_{L}+a}u=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\Lambda}u,

2.3.
Classical density functional theory

Following
[
8
]
, we say that
\mathbb{P}=(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a
grand-canonical probability
if

\mathbb{P}_{0}+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{dn})=1,

where
\mathbb{P}_{0}\in[0,1]
and
\mathbb{P}_{n}
is a finite positive symmetric Borel measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
for all
n\geqslant 1
. The symmetry of
\mathbb{P}_{n}
means that

\mathbb{P}_{n}(B_{\sigma(1)}\times\ldots\times B_{\sigma(n)})=\mathbb{P}_{n}(B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{n})

for every permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
The
density
of
\mathbb{P}
is then the marginal

\rho_{\mathbb{P}}(B)=\mathbb{P}_{1}(B)+\sum_{n\geqslant 2}n\mathbb{P}_{n}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d(n-1)}).

Suppose that
0<s<d
. The Riesz energy of a grand-canonical probability
\mathbb{P}
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{P})=\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dn}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{s}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Next, for any
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\frac{2d}{2d-s}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we introduce the (classical) indirect energy

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)=F_{\mathrm{SCE}}(\rho)-D_{s}(\rho)}

where the
(grand-canonical) strictly correlated electrons functional

F_{\mathrm{SCE}}(\rho)
is defined
as

F_{\mathrm{SCE}}(\rho)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{P}\,\text{GC prob.}\\
\rho_{\mathbb{P}}=\rho\end{subarray}}\mathcal{C}_{s}(\mathbb{P}),

and direct term is given by the positive quadratic form

D_{s}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

In the 3D Coulomb case we will drop the subscripts from
\mathcal{C}_{s}
and
D_{s}
and simply write
\mathcal{C}
and
D
.
It is immediate that
E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)
is isometry-invariant:
E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho(R(\cdot-a)))=E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)
for every
R\in SO(d)
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The Lieb–Oxford inequality and a simple trial state construction involving pure tensor products give the bounds

(2)

-c_{\mathrm{LO}}(d,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{1+s/d}\leqslant E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)\leqslant 0,

where
c_{\mathrm{LO}}(d,s)>0
is a universal constant that depends only on
d
and
s
.
Henceforth, we shall work with densities
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1+s/d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which automatically have
\rho\in L^{\frac{2d}{2d-s}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as required above.
Aside from the preceding
a priori
bound, the most important property of the indirect energy is its subadditivity

(3)

E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\leqslant E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho_{1})+E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho_{2})

for every
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1+s/d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Notice that the densities
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
may overlap.
Another useful property of the indirect energy is its simple scaling law,

(4)

E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda\rho(\lambda^{1/d}\cdot))=\lambda^{s/d}E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)

for every
\lambda>0
. In the quantum case, neither
Eq.

3
nor
Eq.

4
holds.

2.4.
Grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional

In this section, we introduce notations and recall basic facts about the quantum version of the above functionals. Throughout the paper when discussing the quantum case, we shall restrict ourselves to the 3D Coulomb case.
Also, we only consider spinless electrons for simplicity of notations.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of fermionic Fock space states on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with finite kinetic energy having the additional property that they commute with the number operator. For a state
{\bm{\Gamma}}=(\Gamma_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
in
\mathcal{D}
we denote its one-particle reduced density matrix by

\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(n-1)}}\Gamma_{n}(x,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n};y,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{n}.

Further, we denote the density of
{\bm{\Gamma}}
by
\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}(x)=\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}(x,x)
. This
has
\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
by the Hoffmann–Ostenhof inequality
\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

For any
{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}
let us introduce the notations

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}({\bm{\Gamma}})

\displaystyle=\Tr_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}(-\bm{\Delta}\gamma_{\bm{\Gamma}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Gamma}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}\Tr_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3n})}\left(\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}\Gamma_{n}\right)

for the
kinetic-
, and the
Coulomb energy of the state

{\bm{\Gamma}}
. Then the
grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional
is defined as

F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\hbar}(\rho)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\Gamma}}\in\mathcal{D}\\
\rho_{\bm{\Gamma}}=\rho\end{subarray}}\Big[\hbar^{2}\mathcal{T}({\bm{\Gamma}})+\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Gamma}})\Big]

for every
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\rho}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and
+\infty
otherwise. By dropping the Coulomb interaction, we define the
mixed-state kinetic energy functional

T^{\hbar}(\rho)
.
The infimum in
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\hbar}(\rho)
(resp.
T^{\hbar}(\rho)
) is attained
[
22
]
. Moreover, for every fixed
\rho
, the function
\hbar\mapsto F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\sqrt{\hbar}}(\rho)
is a minimum of affine functions (in
\hbar
) of positive slope, hence it is concave increasing.

Using
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\hbar}(\rho)
, we can define the
(quantum) indirect energy

\boxed{E^{\hbar}(\rho)=F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\hbar}(\rho)-D(\rho)}

Notice that contrary to the classical indirect energy
E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)
, the quantum variant may be positive. However, it can be shown that the
exchange-correlation energy

E_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\hbar}(\rho)=E^{\hbar}(\rho)-T^{\hbar}(\rho)

is nonpositive. Various
a priori
bounds on
E^{\hbar}(\rho)
are recalled in
Section

5.1
below.

There is no known simple scaling relation for
E^{\hbar}(\rho)
analogous to
Eq.

4
, because the kinetic energy scales differently from the Coulomb energy. However, if we allow
\hbar
to change, the following obvious fact holds

(5)

E^{\hbar}(\lambda\rho(\lambda^{1/3}\cdot))=\lambda^{1/3}E^{\lambda^{1/6}\hbar}(\rho),

for all
\lambda>0
.
As mentioned above,
E^{\hbar}(\rho)
is not subadditive. A substitute for the subadditivity property
pioneered by
[
20
]
is discussed in
Section

5.3
below.

3.1.
Classical non-uniform electron gas

and we recover the uniform electron gas energy

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho_{0})=c_{\mathrm{UEG}}\rho_{0}^{1+s/d}
,
where the negative constant
c_{\mathrm{UEG}}=e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(1)
is the classical UEG energy as defined in
[
18
]

gave an estimate on the Jellium ground-state energy in 3D Coulomb case, which implies

c_{\mathrm{UEG}}\geqslant-\frac{3}{5}\left(\frac{9\pi}{3}\right)^{1/3}\approx-1.4508
.

(iv)
It is clear that the real function
\rho_{0}\mapsto c_{\mathrm{UEG}}\rho_{0}^{1+s/d}
is subadditive since it is concave and vanishes at
\rho_{0}=0
. More generally, the subadditivity property of
E^{\mathrm{cl}}(\rho)
is inherited by the functional
\zeta\mapsto e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)
, i.e. we have

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime})\leqslant e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)+e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta^{\prime})

for all
\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}\in L_{\mathrm{per}}^{1+s/d}(\mathcal{L})
.

Next, we show how the NUEG-, and UEG energies are related when the inhomogeneity is slowly varying.
Here, we restrict ourselves to the 3D Coulomb case.

(Local density approximation)
.

Suppose that

and

such that

and every inhomogeneity

\left|e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)-c_{\mathrm{UEG}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta^{4/3}\right|\leqslant\varepsilon\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigl(\zeta+\zeta^{4/3}\bigr)+\frac{C_{p,\theta}}{\varepsilon^{b}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\zeta^{\theta}|^{p}

3.2.
Quantum non-uniform electron gas

Next, we introduce our definition of the quantum non-uniform gas and consider some of its basic properties. As mentioned above, we ignore spin for simplicity as (collinear) spin is straightforward to handle, but it would make proofs much more tedious.

We call a function
\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

\sqrt{\zeta}\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}(\mathcal{L})

Recall that for such functions, the mean values

and
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\zeta}|^{2}

By the Sobolev inequality, we have
\int_{\Lambda}\zeta^{p}<\infty

for all

and
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta^{4/3}

Next, we define the non-uniform electron gas energy per finite volume. We offer two definitions following

3.2.1.
Definition based smeared indicators

Recall the
N
-representability criterion from
[
24
]
, which says that a
density
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho=N\in\mathbb{N}
comes from an
N
-particle wavefunction with finite kinetic energy precisely if
\sqrt{\rho}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. So in the quantum case, we need to cut off the inhomogeneity
\zeta
smoothly near the boundary of our domain.

Let
0\leqslant\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be radial with
\supp\eta\subset B_{1}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta=1
. Define

\eta_{\delta}(x)=\delta^{-3}\eta(\delta^{-1}x)
.
Then
\supp\eta_{\delta}\subset B_{\delta}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta_{\delta}=1
. We say that
\eta_{\delta}
is a
regularization function
(or mollifier) with smearing parameter
\delta>0
. While the finite-volume quantities are clearly dependent on the particular choice of the regularization function, this dependence will disappear when passing to the thermodynamic limit.

Fix a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and an inhomogeneity
\zeta
.
Clearly,
\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
so we may consider the function
f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(9)

f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta}(a)=\frac{E^{\hbar}\bigl((\mathbb{1}_{\Omega-a}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta\bigr)}{|\Omega|}=\frac{E^{\hbar}\bigl((\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta(\cdot-a)\bigr)}{|\Omega|}.

In the second equality, the translation-invariance of the Levy–Lieb functional
F_{\mathrm{LL}}^{\hbar}(\rho)
and of the Coulomb self-energy
D(\rho)
was used.

As
\zeta
is
\mathcal{L}
-periodic, it is obvious that
f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta}
is
\mathcal{L}
-periodic.
Moreover, we have the
a priori
estimate
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta}(a)|\,\mathrm{d}a<\infty
(see
Corollary

5.7
below).
Using
Theorem

2.1
, we can deduce that the mean value of
f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta}
over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
exists and is finite.
Next, we average over all rotations
R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
in
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta},\zeta(R\cdot)}
to make our energy rotationally invariant as well. In summary, we arrive at

(10)

\boxed{e_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E^{\hbar}\bigl((\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\bigr)}{|\Omega|}}

which by construction is isometry-invariant with respect to both
\Omega
and
\zeta
, separately.
In
Corollary

5.8
below, we state an
a priori
bound on
e_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
.
Clearly, our definition collapses to the usual indirect energy per volume of the
uniform
electron gas (cf.
[
20
]
) by setting
\zeta\equiv\rho_{0}
, i.e.

e_{\Omega,\eta_{\delta}}^{\hbar}(\rho_{0})=|\Omega|^{-1}E^{\hbar}\bigl((\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\rho_{0}\bigr)
.

3.2.2.
Definition based on a transition region

Instead of using a smeared indicator function to achieve smooth cutoff, it is also reasonable to do the following. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded connected domain. For any
s>0

\Omega^{s-}=\{x\in\Omega:\dist(x,\partial\Omega)\geqslant s\}

and the outer set

\Omega^{s+}=\Omega\cup\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\dist(x,\partial\Omega)\leqslant s\},

we can give another definition of the indirect energy per volume,

(11)

\boxed{\widetilde{e}_{\Omega,s}^{\hbar}(\zeta)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sqrt{\rho}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\\
\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\mathbb{1}_{\Omega^{s-}}\leqslant\rho\leqslant\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\mathbb{1}_{\Omega^{s+}}\end{subarray}}\frac{E^{\hbar}(\rho)}{|\Omega|}}

Taking
\rho=(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta(R(\cdot-a))
for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we may use an
a priori
upper bound (see
Proposition

5.6
below), and for the lower bound using the Lieb–Oxford inequality, we obtain that the
a
-integrand is summable, hence again by
Theorem

2.1

the above mean value exists and finite.

Intuitively speaking, the above minimization allows the density to “relax” in the transition region
\Omega^{s+}\setminus\Omega^{s-}
. We will require that the scale
s
of this transition region goes to infinity in the thermodynamic limit in such a way that the ratio
s/|\Omega|^{1/3}
becomes negligible, for a regular domain sequence
\Omega
.
2
2

2

Recall that for regular domains
\diam(\Omega)\leqslant C|\Omega|^{1/3}
, see
[
10

The
a
-integrand is again
\mathcal{L}
-periodic because
\zeta
is.
Clearly,
\widetilde{e}_{\Omega,s}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
is isometry-invariant in both
\zeta
and
\Omega
.
The conceptual benefit of this definition over the previous one is that the “shape” of the
transition is allowed to be arbitrary.

For the next result, we recall the concept of localization of an

and is given by

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}|_{A})_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigl((\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A)^{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}|_{A})_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\binom{N}{n}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\cdot\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus A)^{N-n}\bigr)\quad\text{on}\;A^{n}\quad(n=1,\ldots,N-1)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}|_{A})_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\quad\text{on}\;A^{N}

and
(\mathbb{P}|_{A})_{n}\equiv 0
whenever
n\geqslant N+1
.

Next, we specify a special tetrahedral tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that will be used throughout the proofs. Recall that
C_{1}=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{3}
denotes the origin-centered open unit cube. Let
\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}=T_{j}(\mathbb{\Delta})
(
j=1,\ldots,24
) be a tiling of
C_{1}
by 24 congruent open tetrahedra, where
the isometries
T_{j}
are given by
T_{j}(x)=R_{j}x-z_{j}
for appropriate
R_{j}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
z_{j}\in C_{1}
. Here,
\mathbb{\Delta}
is a “reference” tetrahedron with barycenter 0 and
T_{j}
is the isometry that maps
\mathbb{\Delta}
to
\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}
.
Given a scale
\ell>0
, we may write

\mathbb{R}^{3}=\bigcup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\overline{(C_{\ell}+\ell z)}=\bigcup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\bigcup_{j=1}^{24}\overline{(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z)}.

The corresponding partition of unity is given by

(14)

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{24}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(\cdot-\ell z)\equiv 1\;\text{(a.e.)}.

We can now state the following very important result that allows us to spatially decouple the indirect 3D Coulomb energy on the tetrahedral tiling
[
13
,
12
,
18
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(Graf–Schenker inequality)
.

For any
N
-particle symmetric probability density
\mathbb{P}
and any
\ell>0
the bound

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})-D(\rho_{\mathbb{P}})\geqslant\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{24}\Big(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P}|_{R^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z+\tau)})-D(\mathbb{1}_{R^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z+\tau)}\rho_{\mathbb{P}})\Big)\\
-\frac{c_{\mathrm{GS}}}{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\mathbb{P}}

holds true with the constant

c_{\mathrm{GS}}=\pi\frac{1+2\sqrt{2}}{4}\approx 3.0068
. In particular, there exists an isometry
(R,\tau)\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\times C_{\ell}
such that

(15)

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})-D(\rho_{\mathbb{P}})\geqslant\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{24}\Big(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P}|_{R^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z+\tau)})-D(\mathbb{1}_{R^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z+\tau)}\rho_{\mathbb{P}})\Big)-\frac{c_{\mathrm{GS}}}{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{\mathbb{P}}.

The constant
c_{\mathrm{GS}}
is obtained by a straightforward but lengthy calculation, using the explicit formulas of
[
13
,
12
]
.
A similar estimate holds true for grand-canonical probabilities as well (with the same constant). The second term on the r.h.s. is a kind of localization error analogous to the term arising from the localization of the kinetic energy (i.e. the IMS formula).

We will use the following simple observations to replace a continuous inhomogeneity
\zeta
locally in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let

\underline{\zeta}=\min_{\overline{\Omega}}\zeta,\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\zeta}=\max_{\overline{\Omega}}\zeta

denote the minimum and maximum of
\zeta
over
\overline{\Omega}
. Then by the subadditivity and nonpositivity of
E(\rho)
, we have

(16)

E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\zeta)\leqslant E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\underline{\zeta}),

and

(17)

E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\overline{\zeta})\leqslant E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\zeta).

In order to control the fluctuations of the density locally, we need the following form
of Morrey’s inequality.

Theorem 4.3

(Morrey’s inequality on tetrahedra)
.

For every
p>3
and
u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{\Delta})
, where
\mathbb{\Delta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a tetrahedron isometric to a dilation of the tetrahedron
\mathbb{\Delta}_{1}
above, the bound

|u(x)-u(y)|\leqslant c_{\mathrm{Mo}}|x-y|^{1-3/p}\|\bm{\nabla}u\|_{L^{p}},

holds true for all
x,y\in\mathbb{\Delta}
, where the constant
c_{\mathrm{Mo}}>0
only depends on
p
. The estimate
c_{\mathrm{Mo}}\leqslant 2\cdot 24^{1/p}\frac{p-1}{p+1}
holds.

The estimate on the constant is the one obtained in the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 4.28]
.

4.2.
Thermodynamic limit for dyadic cubes

To prove that the limit
e_{C_{2^{N}}}(\zeta)
exists we first establish that the energy is nonincreasing for dyadic cubes.

Lemma 4.4
.

e_{C_{2^{N+1}}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{C_{2^{N}}}(\zeta)
for every
N\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

The big cube
C_{2^{N+1}}
is essentially composed of
2^{d}
smaller cubes of side length
2^{N}
with centers placed at the vertices of
C_{2^{N}}
. More precisely,

\overline{C_{2^{N+1}}}=\bigcup_{z\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\overline{\bigl(C_{2^{N}}+2^{N-1}z\bigr)}.

Hence, using the subadditivity and the isometry invariance of
E(\rho)
,

\displaystyle e_{C_{2^{N+1}}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E\left(\sum_{z\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{N}}+2^{N-1}z}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\right)}{|C_{2^{N+1}}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{z\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E\left(\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{N}}+2^{N-1}z}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\right)}{|C_{2^{N}}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{z\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E\left(\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{N}}}\zeta(R(\cdot+2^{N-1}z-a))\right)}{|C_{2^{N}}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{z\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E\left(\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{N}}}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\right)}{|C_{2^{N}}|}=e_{C_{2^{N}}}(\zeta).

In the next to last step we used the translational invariance of the mean value
to absorb
2^{N-1}z
in the argument of
\zeta
.
∎

Hence, the numerical sequence
\{e_{C_{2^{N+1}}}(\zeta)\}_{N\geqslant 1}
is nonincreasing and bounded from below according to
Eq.

6
.
Therefore, the limit

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}e_{C_{2^{N}}}(\zeta)

exists and is finite.

4.3.
Thermodynamic limit for general domains

First, we prove the upper bound via an inner approximation by dyadic cubes. Let

J_{0}^{n,N}=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\subset\Omega_{N}\}

and

\Omega_{N}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{z\in J_{0}^{n,N}}\bigl(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z\bigr)\subset\Omega_{N}.

By the subadditivity and nonpositivity of
E(\rho)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{N}}\zeta)}{|\Omega_{N}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{N}^{\prime}}\zeta)}{|\Omega_{N}|}+\frac{E(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega_{N}\setminus\Omega_{N}^{\prime}}\zeta)}{|\Omega_{N}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{|\Omega_{N}|}E\Biggl(\sum_{z\in J_{0}^{n,N}}\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z}\zeta\Biggr)\leqslant\frac{1}{|\Omega_{N}|}\sum_{z\in J_{0}^{n,N}}E(\mathbb{1}_{C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z}\zeta).

After replacing
\zeta
by
\zeta(R(\cdot-a))
and averaging over all translations and rotations, we find

e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta)\leqslant\frac{2^{n}|J_{0}^{n,N}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{C_{2^{n}}}(\zeta)=\frac{|\Omega_{N}^{\prime}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{C_{2^{n}}}(\zeta).

To estimate the prefactor on the r.h.s, we use the uniform
\kappa
-regularity of the sequence
\Omega_{N}
in the manner

\displaystyle|\Omega_{N}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=|\Omega_{N}|-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{N})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\cap\Omega_{N}|

\displaystyle\geqslant|\Omega_{N}|-|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\partial\Omega_{N})\leqslant 2^{n}\sqrt{d}\}|

\displaystyle\geqslant|\Omega_{N}|\left(1-\kappa(2^{n}\sqrt{d}|\Omega_{N}|^{-1/d})\right).

Since
\kappa(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
, we obtain after letting
N\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
, that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta),

where
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)
is the limit for dyadic cubes, as above.

It remains to prove the lower bound. By an appropriate translation of
\Omega_{N}
we can assume that it is contained in a dyadic cube
C_{2^{K}}
of side length
|\Omega_{N}|^{1/d}
.
We now take

J_{c}^{n,N}=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\subset C_{2^{K}}\setminus\Omega_{N}\}

and

\Omega_{N}^{\prime\prime}=\bigcup_{z\in J_{c}^{n,N}}\bigl(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z\bigr).

Therefore, we may decompose
C_{2^{K}}=\Omega_{N}\cup\Omega_{N}^{\prime\prime}\cup R_{N}
, where the remainder set is

R_{N}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in J_{c}^{n,N}\\
(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\cap\partial\Omega_{N}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\bigl(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z\bigr)\cap\bigl(C_{2^{K}}\setminus\Omega_{N}\bigr).

By
Lemma

4.4
, subadditivity and nonpositivity of
E(\rho)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|C_{2^{K}}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)\leqslant\frac{|C_{2^{K}}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{C_{2^{K}}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta)+\frac{|\Omega_{N}^{\prime\prime}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{C_{2^{n}}}(\zeta)

Rearranging, we obtain

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)+\frac{|\Omega_{N}^{\prime\prime}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}(e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)-e_{C_{2^{n}}}(\zeta))+\frac{|R_{N}|}{|\Omega_{N}|}e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta).

Here, using the uniform
\kappa
-regularity of
\{\Omega_{N}\}
, we have

\displaystyle|R_{N}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in J_{c}^{n,N}\\
(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z)\cap\partial\Omega_{N}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\bigl|\bigl(C_{2^{n}}+2^{n}z\bigr)\cap\bigl(C_{2^{K}}\setminus\Omega_{N}\bigr)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\partial\Omega_{N})\leqslant 2^{n}\sqrt{d}\}|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\Omega_{N}|\kappa\bigl(2^{n}\sqrt{d}|\Omega_{N}|^{-1/d}\bigr).

Clearly,
|\Omega_{N}^{\prime\prime}|\leqslant|C_{2^{K}}|\leqslant C|\Omega_{N}|
and so in total we get the estimate

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)+C\bigl(e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)-e_{C_{2^{n}}}(\zeta)\bigr)+\kappa\bigl(2^{n}\sqrt{d}|\Omega_{N}|^{-1/d}\bigr)e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta).

Taking the liminf as
N\to\infty
and
n\to\infty
, we arrive at

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)\leqslant\liminf_{N\to\infty}e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta).

In summary, the limits
\lim_{N\to\infty}e_{\Omega_{N}}(\zeta)
and
\lim_{N\to\infty}e_{C_{2^{N}}}(\zeta)
exist and equal to a common number

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\mathrm{cl}}(\zeta)
. This completes the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

4.4.
Convergence rate for tetrahedra

In the 3D Coulomb case, when the domain is a dilated (reference) tetrahedron, we can
obtain information about the rate of convergence
e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)\to e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}(\zeta)
as
\ell\to\infty
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that
d=3
and
s=1
. For
\ell>0
sufficiently large, the bounds

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)\leqslant e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}(\zeta)+\frac{c_{\mathrm{GS}}}{\ell}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta

hold true.

Proof.

Using the subadditivity and nonpositivity of
E(\rho)
,

\displaystyle e_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\,\frac{E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\bigr)}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\sum_{(z,j)\in J_{0}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\,E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{|J_{0}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\,E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}\zeta(R(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{|J_{0}||\ell\mathbb{\Delta}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)

where
J_{0}=\{(z,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\{1,\ldots,24\}:(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z)\subset\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}\}
.
Recall that
e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)\leqslant 0
, so using the estimate

\frac{|J_{0}||\ell\mathbb{\Delta}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\geqslant\frac{\ell^{\prime 3}-C\ell^{\prime 2}\ell}{\ell^{\prime 3}}=1-\frac{C\ell}{\ell^{\prime}},

and taking the limit
\ell^{\prime}\to\infty
gives the stated lower bound.

For the upper bound using the Graf–Schenker inequality (
Theorem

4.2
),

\displaystyle e_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\,\frac{E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))\bigr)}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{24}E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau)\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c_{\mathrm{GS}}}{\ell}\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))=:\mathrm{(I)}+\mathrm{(II)}.

Here, (II) is simply
-\frac{C}{\ell}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta
. To deal with (I), we define the index set

J:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (z,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\{1,\ldots,24\}:&\supp(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau))\cap\supp(\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}})\neq\emptyset,\\
&\text{for some}\;R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\;\text{and}\;\tau\in C_{\ell}\end{aligned}\right\},

which collects all the small tetrahedra
R^{\top}(\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}+\ell z+\tau)
that possibly intersect with the big tetrahedron
\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}
.
Furthermore, we define

J_{0}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (z,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\{1,\ldots,24\}:&\supp(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau))\subset\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta},\\
&\text{for all}\;R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\;\text{and}\;\tau\in C_{\ell}\end{aligned}\right\},

which contains the small tetrahedra that are inside
\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}
.

The
J_{0}
-part of (I) reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{(Ia)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{(z,j)\in J_{0}}E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau)\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\sum_{(z,j)\in J_{0}}E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{|J_{0}||\ell\mathbb{\Delta}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}e_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}}(\zeta).

The
J\setminus J_{0}
-part of (I) can be bounded by the Lieb–Oxford inequality as

\displaystyle\mathrm{(Ib)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\sum_{(z,j)\in J\setminus J_{0}}E\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau)\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))\bigr)

\displaystyle\geqslant-c_{\mathrm{LO}}\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}Q\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}a\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\sum_{(z,j)\in J\setminus J_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau)\zeta(Q(\cdot-a))^{4/3}

\displaystyle=-c_{\mathrm{LO}}\frac{1}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\mathrm{d}R\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{C_{\ell}}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{(z,j)\in J\setminus J_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}}\mathbb{1}_{\ell\mathbb{\Delta}_{j}}(R\cdot-\ell z-\tau)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta^{4/3}

\displaystyle\geqslant-c_{\mathrm{LO}}\frac{|\ell\mathbb{\Delta}||J\setminus J_{0}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\zeta^{4/3}.

Clearly, the volume ratios may be bounded as

\frac{|J_{0}||\ell\mathbb{\Delta}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\leqslant 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{|\ell\mathbb{\Delta}||J\setminus J_{0}|}{|\ell^{\prime}\mathbb{\Delta}|}\leqslant\frac{C\ell\ell^{\prime 2}}{\ell^{\prime 3}}=\frac{C\ell}{\ell^{\prime}}.

Hence, in the limit

Appendix B
Weak-* lower semicontinuity of
e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\hbar}

This appendix is devoted to the proof of

Theorem B.1
.

The functional
\zeta\mapsto e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)
is weak-* lower semicontinuous in the following sense: for any sequence
\{\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}\}\subset H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}(\mathcal{L})
and
\sqrt{\zeta}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{L})
such that
\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{\zeta}

H_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\hbar}(\zeta)\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}^{\hbar}(\zeta_{j}).

We set
\hbar=1
and drop it from the notation for clarity. We first recall the following weak-* lower semicontinuity property of the grand-canonical Levy–Lieb functional from
[
22
]
.

Theorem B.2

(Lewin–Lieb–Seiringer)
.

For any sequence
\{\sqrt{\rho_{j}}\}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\sqrt{\rho_{j}}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{\rho}
in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\rho)\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\rho_{j})

The crucial point here is that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho_{j}
need not converge to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho

Still, the weak convergence of the gradient of the “wavefunction”
\sqrt{\rho_{j}}
is sufficient to establish the lower semicontinuity of
F_{\mathrm{LL}}(\rho)
.

As a preparation, we need a simple lemma.

Lemma B.3
.

Suppose that
\{\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}\}\subset H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{L})
and
\sqrt{\zeta}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{L})
such that

\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{\zeta}
in
H_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Then
\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta_{j}}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta}

in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.

We have for for every
\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta_{j}}-\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\zeta}\right)\cdot\bm{\nabla}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}-\sqrt{\zeta})\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\varphi+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}*\eta_{\delta})\left(\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}-\bm{\nabla}\sqrt{\zeta}\right)\cdot\bm{\nabla}\varphi,

where in the first term,

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\Big[e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}(\zeta_{j})+\text{(error term of \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{tetrathermo} (i))}_{\ell,\delta,j}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\text{(error term of \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{tetrathermo} (iii))}_{\ell,\delta}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\delta/\ell\to 0,\,\ell\delta\to\infty]{}\liminf_{j\to\infty}e_{\mathrm{NUEG}}(\zeta_{j})

where in the last step we used the fact that the error terms in the convergence rate bound are all uniformly bounded in
