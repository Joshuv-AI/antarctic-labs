Exosome-Inspired Nanocarriers in Cancer Therapy: Bio-Derived Nanotechnology Meets Nano Drug Delivery
Authors: Bingya Zhang, Yihang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional cancer therapy has limitations due to a lack of specificity and efficiency in tumor-targeting, toxicity issues, and biological barriers, which affect the clinical efficacy of traditional agents as well as synthetically designed carriers. Exosomes have gained popularity as excellent biological carriers owing to their inherent biocompatibility, capability to overcome biological barriers, and ability to transport cargoes between cells naturally. This review discusses how engineering of exosomes, hybrid exosomes, and exosome mimics could be employed to deliver therapies, enhance tumor penetration, and enable multimodal treatment of cancers, including chemotherapy, gene therapy, immunotherapy, and theranostics. The biomimetic and hybrid platforms may overcome key limitations of native exosomes, particularly low production yield, heterogeneity, limited drug-loading efficiency, and scalability constraints, while preserving desirable biological functionality. Moreover, the review also emphasizes that the standardization in manufacturing, reproducibility in cargo, safety evaluation, and regulatory approval are the major obstacles to clinical application. Unlike many earlier reviews that were mainly centered on native exosomes, the current review discusses the engineering aspects of exosome-like nanoplatforms from a translational viewpoint.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with GLOBOCAN 2022 reporting approximately 19.3 million new cancer cases and 10.0 million cancer-related deaths worldwide, underscoring its substantial and growing disease burden. According to the World Health Organization, cancer is among the leading causes of death globally, with incidence expected to continue rising due to aging populations, environmental exposure, and lifestyle-related risk factors. The rising life expectancy, rapid urbanisation, tobacco use, physical inactivity, and dietary change all contribute to an increasing number of new cases every year.1–3 Many patients now survive longer with their disease, yet they often experience repeated cycles of treatment, relapse, and progression that affect quality of life and place a sustained burden on health systems. For a large group of solid and blood cancers, current treatments still fall short of durable control. Even when temporary remission is achieved, micrometastatic disease and resistant cell populations can persist and later drive relapse. These realities underline the continuing need for more precise and more tolerable ways to deliver anticancer therapy.4

It is anticipated that the number of cases will increase even more significantly in the coming years. While screening initiatives and supportive care measures have increased chances of survival in some places, accessibility to those interventions is not consistent across the board. Late-stage diagnoses, lack of availability of advanced treatments, and shortages of skilled personnel hamper advancements in low- and middle-income countries. However, the issue persists in wealthy nations where patients are likely to survive for long enough to undergo several rounds of treatment.5,6 Hence, there is an urgent need to develop therapeutic interventions that would enable effective regulation of the disease process without doing too much damage. There is an urgent need to come up with solutions that will be able to reach sanctuaries like the brain or bone marrow, where metastatic cells tend to take refuge. There is also an urgent need for strategies that will be able to evolve along with the tumour and account for its diversity. Clearly, the solutions sought should incorporate the use of delivery methods that can carry the active compounds to target tissues.7,8

At present, systemic treatment relies mainly on small-molecule cytotoxic drugs and molecularly targeted agents. These compounds can arrest cell division, damage DNA, or block signalling pathways that drive tumour growth. However, they circulate throughout the body and cannot easily distinguish between malignant cells and sensitive normal tissues. Bone marrow, intestinal epithelium, hair follicles, and reproductive organs are especially vulnerable. As a result, many regimens are limited by neutropenia, anaemia, mucosal injury, nausea, organ toxicity, and cumulative fatigue. Dose reductions and treatment delays are common and can weaken therapeutic impact. Even targeted agents that recognise specific receptors or kinases must reach their sites of action in adequate concentration, yet their distribution is constrained by solubility, metabolism, clearance, and physical barriers within the tumour microenvironment.9

Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy works by exploiting the higher proliferation rate of many cancer cells compared with most normal cells. Drugs such as anthracyclines, taxanes, alkylating agents, and antimetabolites damage DNA or interfere with cell division. They can shrink tumours, relieve symptoms, and in some cases cure disease. However, their action is not selective enough. Rapidly dividing normal cells are affected as well, leading to well-known side effects. Many procedures must be given in cycles separated by rest periods to allow partial recovery of normal tissues. Cumulative dose is often capped by cardiac, renal, hepatic, or neurological toxicity, even in patients whose tumours still respond.10,11 However, the targeted therapies were introduced to improve selectivity by focusing on specific molecular drivers, such as mutant kinases or overexpressed receptors. Examples include inhibitors of epidermal growth factor receptor, vascular endothelial growth factor pathways, and several tyrosine kinases, as well as monoclonal antibodies against surface antigens. These agents have transformed the care of some cancers. Nevertheless, their success is incomplete. Intrinsic and acquired resistance arise through secondary mutations, pathway redundancy, activation of compensatory networks, and influences from the tumour microenvironment. Furthermore, many targeted agents still distribute widely in the body and produce off-target effects such as skin rash, diarrhoea, hypertension, and cardiotoxicity. Their therapeutic index is better than that of many older drugs, but still finite.12 Therefore, drug delivery systems that can navigate complex microenvironments, protect their cargo, and release it in a controlled manner have attracted considerable interest.13

Nanomedicine emerged as one solution to the limitations of free drugs. Along with various efforts aimed at producing biocompatible nanocarriers, some natural or biosynthesized nanoparticles also demonstrate high cytotoxicity, antimicrobial action, and antioxidant capacities as substances able to perform selectively while being harmful to healthy cells. As an illustration, selenium nanoparticles produced through biosynthesis using the peel of Solanum tuberosum exhibited excellent cytotoxicity and antimicrobial effects, which qualified them as biocompatible nanoparticles obtained from phytochemicals of potatoes.14–16 Additionally, the prickly pear grown in Turkey was noted to have high antioxidant and antimicrobial properties; therefore, it can be used in producing beverages with functional qualities. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that carbon-based nanomaterials are able to reduce the oxidative stress of plants and improve their biological qualities. Thus, it shows the positive aspects of nanostructure combination in terms of enhancing the activity of active substances. Moreover, Cuscuta spp. extracts had an effect on cell viability.17–20

The main idea is that encapsulating active molecules in structures on the nano scale can change their behaviour in the body. Liposomes, polymer particles, and lipid-based nanoparticles are among the best-known examples. They can improve the solubility of hydrophobic drugs, shield labile compounds from rapid degradation, and alter interactions with plasma proteins and cell membranes.21 Several nano-formulated drugs have entered routine practice. Some liposomal anthracycline preparations reduce cardiotoxicity compared with the parent drug by changing distribution away from heart tissue. Other nano systems prolong circulation or concentrate drugs in tumours through enhanced permeability and retention, a phenomenon in which leaky tumour vessels and poor lymphatic drainage allow nanoparticles to accumulate. Despite these achievements, the overall impact of synthetic nanocarriers has been more modest than early expectations. Enhanced permeability and retention are not uniform across tumour types or even within a single lesion. Uptake by phagocytic cells in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow limits the fraction of the dose that reaches the target. Repeated dosing can trigger immune responses against carrier components. These lessons have prompted a search for nano-scale platforms that behave more like the body’s own communication systems.22,23

Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles released by most cell types as part of normal and pathological communication. They originate from inward budding of endosomal membranes, leading to multivesicular bodies that can either fuse with lysosomes or release their internal vesicles to the outside. Exosomes carry membrane proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and glycoconjugates that mirror the identity and state of their parent cells. They travel through blood, lymph, and other body fluids, and they can fuse with or be taken up by recipient cells in distant tissues. These features make exosomes good candidates for bio-derived nano-drug delivery.24–26 However, despite their biological advantages, natural exosomes face significant translational barriers, including limited production yield, batch-to-batch variability, complex purification procedures, and safety concerns related to endogenous cargo. These challenges have driven the development of exosome-inspired nanocarriers that mimic key biological properties while enabling better control over composition, scalability, and functionality. Their size is comparable to that of many synthetic nanoparticles, yet their membranes present native proteins and glycans that engage physiological receptors. In cancer, exosomes help remodel the tumour microenvironment by transferring oncogenic proteins, microRNA, and other factors between cancer cells, stromal cells, and immune cells. The same capacity for information transfer can be redirected to carry therapeutic agents.27 Exosomes can be seen as endogenous nanocarriers that nature has already field-tested in complex organisms. The challenge for researchers is to understand and adapt this system so that it delivers drugs, nucleic acids, and imaging agents in a controlled manner, rather than spreading malignant signals.28

However, significant enhancements in the pharmacokinetics and safety properties of various anticancer drugs thanks to nanotechnology, the efficacy of many synthesized nanoparticles as drug delivery systems is still limited due to poor tumor accumulation, fast elimination, penetration through biological barriers, heterogeneity of drug release, and low translatability from experimental models into the clinic. Moreover, recent studies demonstrated that the effect of active targeting is rather limited, while difficulties associated with fabrication, variability between batches, and regulation make it difficult for nanoparticles to enter the clinic. Therefore, there was a tendency towards the development of biological delivery systems, capable of taking advantage of natural pathways of cell communication and interaction for efficient drug delivery.29–32 Exosomes and exosome-based nanocarriers serve as alternate means of drug delivery due to their dual advantages of the inherent biological properties of extracellular vesicles and the flexibility of nanotechnology.29,33 However, native exosomes face substantial limitations, including low production yield, biological heterogeneity, limited-loading efficiency, storage instability, and difficulties in scalable manufacturing and quality control.33,34

Consequently, this review attempts to present an approach that takes into consideration not only the existing exosome-centric view, but also introduces into the nano-delivery landscape the idea of exosome-like nanoplatforms as well as hybrids thereof. Indeed, by doing so, one can easily see that biomimetic and hybrid systems provide a middle ground between natural exosomes and artificial nanoparticles for clinical application.

Biology of Exosomes as Natural Nano-Drug Delivery Systems

Exosomes are often introduced as a special class of small extracellular vesicles, but for drug delivery, it is more helpful to see them as tiny parcels that cells prepare and send in a fairly organized way. They are not random fragments of membrane. They arise through a defined route inside the cell, they have a recognisable structure, and they carry a selected set of molecules. These features explain why exosomes can behave as natural nano-scale carriers and why they have attracted attention as templates for therapeutic design.35

Biogenesis of Exosomes Through the Endosomal Pathway

Exosomes are produced inside cells through the endosomal system. The process begins at the plasma membrane, where small regions invaginate and pinch off to form early endosomes. These early endosomes receive material from the cell surface and from internal membranes. Over time, they mature into late endosomes. Within late endosomes, the limiting membrane buds inward and creates many small vesicles inside the lumen. These internal vesicles are sometimes called intraluminal vesicles, and the endosome that holds them is known as a multivesicular body.36 Cargo selection and vesicle formation are guided by several protein complexes and lipids that recognise sorting signals on proteins and nucleic acids. Some of these complexes cluster ubiquitinated membrane proteins into budding regions, while others influence the local lipid environment so that curvature is favoured. At this stage, the cell faces a choice. A multivesicular body can move toward lysosomes and fuse with them, in which case the intraluminal vesicles and their content are degraded. Alternatively, the multivesicular body can traffic toward the plasma membrane. If it fuses with the plasma membrane, the intraluminal vesicles are released to the outside. Once they enter the extracellular space, they are called exosomes.37 The balance between degradation and secretion depends on cell type, differentiation state, and signals from the surrounding environment. Growth factors, stress, inflammatory stimuli, and oncogenic pathways can all shift this balance and change both the rate of exosome production and the nature of the cargo that is packed inside them. In disease conditions such as cancer, this dynamic control contributes to the large amounts of exosomes found in the circulation and in local fluids.38

Apart from its biological importance, exosome production is crucial in dictating the efficiency of drug delivery system designs. The components of the endosomal sorting pathway define the molecular composition of the exosomes; hence, it impacts the cargo-carrying ability, trafficking, cellular uptake, and tissue specificity. Several studies have found that alteration of ESCRT-dependent or independent pathways increases the loading efficiency of cargos into exosomes, whereas genetic engineering of donor cells at multivesicular bodies facilitates endogenous cargo loading and surface modification of exosomes. Structural Organization and Molecular Cargo of Exosomes

Despite their varied source cells, exosomes exhibit certain features in common. They can be classified as membrane-enclosed extracellular vesicles of around 30–150 nm in size; however, exact dimensions depend on methodology and terminology used in various studies. The lipid bilayer membrane encompasses a cargo made up of proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and glycoconjugates, which is indicative of the cellular status of the donor cell as well as sorting mechanisms. Available information suggests that cargo selection for incorporation occurs via two sorting mechanisms: ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent pathways. Yet, the degree of contribution of each sorting mechanism remains unclear.29,30,41,42 The membrane itself is not a simple copy of the plasma membrane. It is enriched in certain lipids and proteins that give exosomes a distinct physical and biological identity. The interior cargo is also far from random. Cells make use of several sorting routes to concentrate selected proteins and nucleic acids in these vesicles while excluding others. As a result, each exosome carries a molecular message that reflects the character and the current state of the parent cell.43

Exosomal membranes contain several proteins that are widely used as markers. Such proteins are, for example, tetraspanins, specific members of the small GTP-binding protein family, annexins, and heat shock proteins. Many of them are involved in processes such as membrane organization, membrane fusion, and signaling. Repeated occurrence of such proteins in exosomes is thought to reflect their deliberate sorting from the cytoplasmic membrane during the process of exosome formation rather than random entrapment.44 The bilayer is relatively enriched in cholesterol, sphingomyelin, and other sphingolipids. All these lipids form more ordered membranes and add rigidity to the bilayer. These properties are thought to ensure stability of exosomes while moving through the extracellular space and various body fluids. Nucleic Acid Content, Including microRNA and Other Species

Among other fascinating discoveries concerning exosomes, the detection of small nucleic acids regulating gene expression has been noteworthy. Exosomes are loaded with microRNAs that have the ability to regulate gene expression in target cells following their release. Messenger RNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and DNA fragments are also transported within exosomes. In addition to the DNA fragments, which may come from the mitochondrial or genomic DNA, exosomes may also include non-DNA RNAs such as the mentioned microRNA molecules.47 It is interesting to note that the content of nucleic acids in exosomes does not exactly correspond to the content in the cytosol of the donor cell. Some nucleic acids occur more frequently in exosomes, whereas others occur less frequently. This implies that certain sequences are actively selected by the cell using binding proteins and sequence motifs to facilitate the movement of selected transcripts into intraluminal vesicles. MicroRNA profiles in tumor cells are distinct from those in healthy tissues, and affect growth, metastasis, angiogenesis, and chemoresistance.48

Surface Proteins and Glycoconjugates on Exosome Membranes

The exosome has a sophisticated and informative external surface. The membrane proteins and glycoproteins make up the surface at which exosomes come into contact with surrounding cellular structures, as well as with soluble substances in the bodily fluids. Such an outer layer performs the vital function of directing exosome traffic throughout the body, controlling their time in circulation, and defining the ultimate destination of exosomes.49 Tetraspanins represent some of the best-known exosome markers. Tetraspanins are known to be a family of transmembrane proteins that have four transmembrane domains and participate in forming protein complexes. Tetraspanins seem to assemble receptors, adhesion molecules, and signalling proteins on the exosome surface in small clusters.50 Alongside tetraspanins, exosome membranes carry a variety of integrins, immunoglobulin superfamily members, and other adhesion molecules.51

Many exosomal membrane proteins are glycosylated. They carry chains of sugar residues that extend into the surrounding fluid. Glycolipids such as gangliosides contribute further to this sugar coat. The composition and structure of these glycans influence how exosomes are seen by lectin receptors on immune cells, endothelial cells, and stromal cells.49 In tumour settings, changes in glycosylation are common. Increased branching, altered fucosylation, and higher levels of sialic acid on both proteins and lipids are often observed. The tumour-derived exosomes may engage different lectin receptors, may be captured more readily by some tissues, and may evade clearance in others. Besides facilitating cell-cell interactions via protein-glycoprotein interactions, surface proteins and glycoconjugates on exosomes have important roles in cellular uptake and thus affect the efficacy of drug delivery systems. Based on recent findings, exosomes have been shown to be capable of entering recipient cells using different pathways, such as clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-dependent endocytosis, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and membrane fusion, with the predominant uptake pathway being dependent on exosome characteristics and the type of cells being targeted. The interactions between surface proteins and glycans affect receptor-mediated uptake processes and the fate of the exosome cargo within the recipient cells.29–31,41

Physiological Roles of Exosomes in Tissue Homeostasis and Immune Communication

In healthy organisms, exosomes contribute to the quiet background of communication that supports tissue homeostasis. Immune cells use exosomes to present antigenic peptides, distribute major histocompatibility complex molecules, and share information about pathogens or damaged cells. Dendritic cell-derived exosomes can stimulate T cells even without direct cell-to-cell contact, while B cell and T cell exosomes can modulate responses within lymphoid organs.58 Outside the immune system, exosomes participate in repair and maintenance. Mesenchymal stem cells release vesicles that carry growth factors, angiogenic signals, and protective microRNA, supporting recovery after injury in muscle, heart, and nervous tissue. Epithelial cells send exosomes to neighbours to coordinate barrier function and response to stress. Neurons and glial cells exchange vesicles that influence synaptic plasticity and metabolic support. In many of these roles, exosomes act slowly but persistently, fine-tuning the balance between cell survival, differentiation, and death.59,60 These physiological functions illustrate that exosomes are integrated into the normal signalling architecture of the body. They are part of how cells share tasks and adjust to change. Any attempt to use exosomes for drug delivery must therefore respect and, where possible, align with these existing communication routes.61

While exosomes play vital roles in preserving tissue homeostasis and mediating immunocommunication, their functions are not always favorable. In accordance with the source and content of the exosomes, their role can range from immunosuppression, induction of tolerance, to an imbalance in inflammation. The presence of immunomodulating proteins or even RNA molecules and cytokines in exosomes results in reduced activation of T cells and regulation of immune cells in favor of inducing antitumor immunity. The significance of this issue must be considered before implementing exosome therapy due to possible influences that such delivery systems might have on immune responses.29–31,39

Pathological Roles of Exosomes in Cancer Progression and Metastasis

Cancer cells make use of the same vesicle systems, but often in ways that favour malignant progression. Tumour cells frequently release larger numbers of exosomes than their normal counterparts. These vesicles carry oncogenic receptors, mutated proteins, pro-angiogenic factors, and distinct sets of microRNA that together reshape the surrounding microenvironment.62 At distant sites, exosomes contribute to pre-metastatic niche formation. Vesicles that enter the circulation can lodge in organs such as the lung, liver, or brain, where they alter resident stromal and immune cells, increase vascular permeability, and create favourable conditions for the later arrival of circulating tumour cells. Specific integrin signatures on exosomes have been associated with a preference for certain organs, suggesting that exosome trafficking patterns help determine where metastases are most likely to emerge.63 Vesicles from resistant cancer cell clones may carry efflux pumps, anti-apoptotic proteins, or microRNA that suppress drug-induced death pathways. When sensitive cells take up these exosomes, they can acquire a more resistant phenotype. This horizontal sharing of resistance traits complicates therapy and contributes to relapse after an initial response.64 They shape the microenvironment, influence distant organs, and modulate therapy response.65–67

Tumor-derived exosomes have recently emerged as active mediators in the process of pre-metastatic niche development instead of being passive indicators of cancer progression. Increasing evidence has shown that the proteins, lipids, and non-coding RNA contained within exosomes are able to alter distant target tissue environments through increasing vascular permeability, ECM remodeling, inflammation, and recruiting immunosuppressive cells, as well as bone marrow-derived cells. Additionally, specific expression of integrins on exosomes appears to be involved in determining organ specificity in metastasis to particular sites, such as the liver, lungs, and brain. Moreover, the ability of exosomes to reprogram metabolism and the immune response could contribute to creating a conducive microenvironment for further metastatic colonization of tumor cells.29–31,68 Importantly, exosomes actively participate in the establishment of pre-metastatic niches by reprogramming stromal, endothelial, and immune cells at distant organs, creating a microenvironment favorable for metastatic colonization. Understanding these pathological mechanisms has directly inspired the development of exosome-inspired nanocarriers that exploit the intrinsic targeting, communication, and cargo-delivery properties of natural exosomes for precision cancer therapy.69–73

Exosomes Compared with Established Nano-Drug Delivery Systems

The idea of using small carriers to transport drugs into tumours is not new. Liposomes, polymer particles, and lipid-based nanoparticles have been part of oncology for many years. Exosomes should be viewed as complementary delivery platforms rather than complete replacements for conventional nanocarriers. To appreciate what they may add, it is useful to place them beside classical nanocarriers, to note shared features and to explore where they differ in a meaningful way.74

Overview of Classical Nanocarriers in Oncology

Liposomes are small vesicles formed from one or more phospholipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous interior. They can hold water-soluble drugs in the core and lipophilic drugs within the membrane. By adjusting size, lipid composition, and surface charge, it is possible to influence circulation time and tissue distribution. They often reduce the exposure of the heart or kidney compared with the free drug and can permit different dosing schedules.75 Variants of liposomes include stealth formulations that carry a hydrophilic coating to extend circulation, as well as vesicles that display targeting ligands such as antibodies or peptides on their surface. These features can increase tumour accumulation in some settings, but uptake still depends strongly on vascular structure and on the behaviour of phagocytic cells in liver and spleen.76

Polymeric nanoparticles employ biodegradable polymers, such as polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid, or copolymers of these, which create either solid particles or hydrogels. Drug loading can be performed via entrapment or conjugation. The release is achieved by means of diffusion and polymer decomposition. Such systems have the potential of achieving sustained drug release lasting from several days up to weeks, while being capable of delivering small molecules and large biological macromolecules.77 Lipid-based nanoparticles, which can deliver nucleic acids, comprise a mixture of ionizable lipids, helper lipids, and other compounds. This type of particle can load, stabilize, and deliver small interfering RNA and messenger RNA and alike substances, making it an attractive candidate for development of therapies in oncology.78 Together, these synthetic carriers have shown that control over size, composition, and surface properties can reshape drug behaviour. However, they remain artificial constructs that the body often treats as foreign.79–81

Liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, have significantly advanced cancer drug delivery by improving drug solubility, pharmacokinetics, and payload protection. Liposomes exhibit excellent biocompatibility and can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic therapeutics. However, they often suffer from limited targeting specificity, drug leakage, instability during storage, and rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Polymeric nanoparticles offer tunable physicochemical properties, controlled drug release, and high structural versatility, but their clinical translation is frequently hindered by potential toxicity, immunogenicity, complex manufacturing, and suboptimal biodistribution. These limitations have stimulated growing interest in exosome-inspired nanocarriers, which combine the engineering flexibility of synthetic nanoparticles with the intrinsic biocompatibility, biological communication capability, and targeting properties of natural extracellular vesicles.82–86

Strengths of Exosomes as Bio-Derived Drug Delivery Systems

The exosome derives its origin from the cellular membrane and cytosol of the host cell. The lipids and proteins within the exosome would be recognized by the immune system, especially if it is extracted from normal human cells. In numerous pre-clinical experiments, the administration of exosomes from mesenchymal stem cells or dendritic cells has not been associated with any adverse acute responses. In contrast, exosomes have been better tolerated by animal subjects and even by the first few human subjects compared to synthetic particles of similar size.87 Autologous exosomes, derived from a patient’s own cells, are expected to be seen largely as self rather than foreign. Even allogeneic vesicles from carefully selected cell lines may provoke less innate immune activation than carriers that display artificial polymers or unusual lipids. This relative biocompatibility does not remove all risk, but it gives exosomes a favourable starting position as drug delivery vehicles.88

Synthetic carriers usually require deliberate decoration with targeting ligands if they are to show any preference for specific tissues. Exosomes, in contrast, already display a rich set of surface proteins and glycans that the body uses for physiological recognition. Integrins, adhesion molecules, tetraspanins, and glycoproteins together give each exosome population a characteristic pattern of interactions.89 Studies have shown that exosomes from particular cell types tend to accumulate in matching tissues or in organs that share related receptors. For example, vesicles from certain tumour lines home preferentially to organs that later develop metastases, while immune cell-derived exosomes interact strongly with lymphoid tissues. This natural tropism can be redirected through engineering, but it remains an intrinsic feature that synthetic particles usually lack.90

Despite the widespread recognition of exosomes as being endowed with their intrinsic ability for selective targeting, the molecular mechanisms responsible for their biodistribution and cell-specific localization are yet to be fully elucidated. Indeed, the existence of a correlation between the surface molecules of exosomes, such as integrins, tetraspanins, and glycoproteins, and tissue/organ specificity or cell-type targeting observed in experimental studies does not imply the presence of an inherent targeting mechanism. The current data support the view that exosome biodistribution depends on a variety of factors, such as vesicular origin, route of delivery, physicochemical characteristics, and biological milieu of the recipient host. Thus, natural tropism must be considered a context-related property rather than a proven targeting system.29–31,41

Exosomes move through the extracellular matrix and narrow tissue spaces with relative ease because of their small size and flexible membrane. Their lipid and protein composition can reduce rapid opsonisation by serum proteins and may slow clearance in some models.91 The interior of exosomes provides a protected environment for nucleic acids and proteins. These fragile molecules often degrade quickly when given freely or in simple formulations. Encapsulation in exosomes can extend their functional half-life and allow them to reach intracellular targets in a more intact state. Pharmacokinetics can still be complex, but exosomes clearly offer a different distribution profile that can be exploited in drug design.92

Limitations and Challenges Shared with Synthetic Nanocarriers

Despite these benefits, exosomes are subject to some basic drawbacks, which are common to the classic type of nanoparticles as well. The injected dose only partially penetrates the solid tumor mass. This is because the majority of injected particles is shunted off to the body’s clearance organs, such as the liver and the spleen, where the phagocytes residing there filter out the particles. Poor perfusion or encapsulation in the dense matrix in certain areas of tumors is still a major hurdle.93 Within the tumour microenvironment, all nanocarriers face the challenge of moving through the extracellular matrix and reaching individual cancer cells in sufficient numbers. Even when exosomes reach the right tissue, they must still bind, be internalised, and productively release their cargo. These steps can be hindered by the same issues that limit other nano-systems, including high interstitial pressure, heterogeneous receptor expression, and dynamic changes in local pH or oxygenation.94 In addition, exosome formulations can trigger complement activation or mild inflammatory responses, especially when produced at high doses or from allogeneic sources. Thus, while their immune profile is often smoother than that of some synthetic carriers, it is not entirely silent.95

Though exosome-based nanoparticles have huge potential in drug delivery applications, there are some challenges associated with these nanoparticles that make it difficult for them to be applied clinically for cancer treatment. One of the main problems associated with exosome-based nanoparticles is the difficulty in loading therapeutic molecules. Exogenous loading approaches like electroporation, sonication, extrusion, and incubation have lower efficiency in loading due to potential vesicle membrane damage, aggregation, and inconsistency in encapsulation efficiency.33,96 Another big problem in the use of these nanoparticles is the issue of batch-to-batch heterogeneity. The physical and chemical characteristics of exosome-like nanocarriers depend on the type of donor cells used and other factors associated with production processes.29,82

In addition to safety issues, it should be noted that exosome-based delivery systems based on tumor cells may contain native proteins, miRNA, DNA fragments, and other biologically active components that can induce tumor growth, metastasis formation, angiogenesis, or immune response evasion. In addition, even vesicles isolated from healthy cells may contain some biologically active compounds, which may elicit unwanted biological effects.29,41 Additionally, even though these nanoparticles are derived from nature and have good biocompatibility, they normally have relatively low circulation times and tend to be easily eliminated by the body’s mononuclear phagocyte system, especially in the liver and spleen. This means that their systemic availability is very limited, making their presence at the tumor site limited.33,97 Lastly, the production process is both expensive and technologically complex. Production yield issues, tedious extraction protocols, sophisticated analysis needs, and non-existent standardized regulations contribute to making the manufacturing process expensive and difficult to scale up. Therefore, efficient and economical manufacturing approaches are needed to ensure the success of clinical development of exosome-inspired nanocarriers.29,82

Distinct Challenges Specific to Exosome-Based Formulations

Exosomes also present difficulties that are less prominent in synthetic nanomedicine. Their production depends on living cells, which introduces variability. Even a single donor cell line can change its behaviour over passages or under slightly different culture conditions, altering both the number of vesicles produced and their composition. Achieving a consistent large-scale yield is therefore challenging.98 The cargo of exosomes is complex and not fully defined. Alongside intended therapeutic molecules, vesicles may carry a wide range of endogenous proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, some of which could have unwanted biological effects. Removing or neutralising undesirable components without damaging desired cargo is not straightforward. Synthetic carriers, in contrast, can often be assembled from a small set of defined materials that are easier to control.99 Different isolation protocols can produce products with distinct biological activities, even when starting from the same cell source. This heterogeneity complicates standardisation and comparison between studies.82 Exosome-based products do not yet enjoy the same level of precedent. Developers must work with regulators to define acceptable identity markers, potency assays, and safety tests, which may lengthen the path to approval.100

Positioning Exosomes as Next-Generation Bio-Derived Drug Delivery Systems in Cancer

In light of all these characteristics, it would be inaccurate to think of exosomes as mere improvements or insignificant anomalies in the field of nanocarriers. Exosomes have a niche in the world of drug-delivery systems. Being derived from nature, exosomes have the ability to tap into channels of interaction and transport that synthetic molecules may find difficult to duplicate. They come pre-equipped with codes for tissue targeting and have spaces for nucleic acids too.101 In addition, the need for living cells, complicated contents, and sensitivity to manufacture makes them rather complex platforms. The more sophisticated characterization process as well as manufacturing procedures have to be designed for these systems. In certain cases, such as situations where the tumour microenvironment must be delicately engaged, or nucleic acids delivered, exosomes can be considered better alternatives. However, in cases where there is no need for complex engagement but simply for constant exposure to a small molecule, traditional synthetic nanocarriers are still superior.102 Considering the use of exosomes in cancer treatment, the following statement would be more appropriate, namely that they are complementary nanomedicines. With an increase in knowledge about the biology and engineering of exosomes, they are expected to have specific applications in scenarios where their unique features will suit specific therapeutic purposes.103,104

Exosome Sources and Natural Therapeutic Applications

Exosomes can come from a wide variety of sources, and their properties will vary according to the cells producing them. The selection of the source is more than just a technical consideration for applications. It affects not only the quantity produced but also the properties of the vesicles themselves and what additional biological signals will be transferred along with the drug delivered using them. The focus of this chapter is on the sources that have garnered much interest and their use in cancer research.105

Mesenchymal Stem Cell-Derived Exosomes in Regenerative and Anticancer Settings

Exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells residing in bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and other organs have been identified as one of the most extensively researched sources of exosomes. This type of stem cells is relatively simple to cultivate in vitro and has already been applied in clinical cell therapy for treating inflammatory and degenerative disorders. Such vesicles contain growth factors, angiogenic components, and microRNA regulating tissue viability.106 In oncology, this reparative profile can be both beneficial and concerning. On one side, mesenchymal exosomes may help normal tissues recover from chemotherapy or radiotherapy, lessen fibrosis, and support organ function. On the other side, the same pro-survival and pro-angiogenic signals could, in principle, assist residual tumour cells. For this reason, many cancer-focused studies do not use completely unmodified mesenchymal vesicles. Instead, the cells are engineered or exposed to specific stimuli so that their exosomes carry anticancer drugs, small interfering RNA, or microRNA that suppress oncogenic pathways.106 Several preclinical models have shown that mesenchymal exosomes loaded with doxorubicin, paclitaxel, or similar agents can reduce tumour burden while causing less systemic toxicity than free drugs. Others have used vesicles enriched in microRNA that block proliferation or invasion. In these designs, the natural homing of mesenchymal cells and their exosomes to inflamed or damaged tissue becomes a tool for guiding therapy into tumour regions that share such cues.107,108

Immune Cell-Derived Exosomes, Including Dendritic and Natural Killer Cell Vesicles

Cells of the immune system release exosomes that are deeply involved in antigen presentation and immune regulation. Dendritic cell-derived exosomes carry major histocompatibility complex molecules loaded with peptides, co-stimulatory proteins, and adhesion molecules. Early clinical work used these vesicles as cancer vaccines. Dendritic cells were pulsed with tumour antigens, allowed to process them, and then used as exosome donors. The resulting vesicles were infused into patients with melanoma or lung cancer to boost tumour-specific T cell responses.109 Natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells also shed vesicles that contain perforin, granzymes, and other molecules that mediate direct killing. In experimental systems, such exosomes can induce apoptosis in sensitive tumour cells. Macrophages and other myeloid cells release vesicles that either stimulate or suppress immune responses, depending on their polarisation state.110 For therapeutic use, immune cell-derived exosomes are good because they already know how to interact with lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells. When they are engineered to carry additional drugs or nucleic acids, they can serve as bridges between chemotherapy and immunotherapy. For example, dendritic cell exosomes that display tumour antigens on their surface can be loaded with a chemotherapeutic agent that increases antigen release from cancer cells, thereby reinforcing the vaccine effect. Natural killer cell exosomes can be combined with agents that heighten their cytotoxicity, or that target them more strongly to tumour tissue.26

Tumour Cell-Derived Exosomes and Their Dual Value as Targets and Tools

Tumour cells release large quantities of exosomes that reflect their malignant state. These vesicles carry oncogenic receptors, mutated proteins, pro-angiogenic factors, and altered sets of microRNA that together promote growth, invasion, and immune escape. They shape the tumour microenvironment and prepare distant organs for metastasis. For this reason, tumour exosomes are often considered targets for diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. Blocking their release, capture, or uptake could, in theory, slow progression and reduce metastatic spread.111 At the same time, tumour-derived exosomes have a natural preference for cells that share their origin. They home back to tumour tissue and are taken up efficiently by malignant cells. This tropism suggests that they could be used as precise carriers if their harmful content were replaced with therapeutic cargo. In experimental work, researchers have loaded tumour exosomes with cytotoxic drugs, pro-apoptotic proteins, or immune stimulatory molecules and then reinfused them into tumour-bearing animals. The idea is to exploit their homing ability while reversing the direction of the message they carry.112 This strategy remains controversial because it is difficult to remove all oncogenic and immune suppressive components from tumour exosomes. Some groups therefore use tumour vesicles mainly as antigen sources. Tumour exosomes are used to pulse dendritic cells, which then generate a vaccine-based response using their own exosomes. In this arrangement, tumour vesicles contribute information about antigens but do not directly enter the patient as drug carriers.113

Exosome-Like Vesicles from Plant and Milk Sources for Oral and Systemic Use

Not all exosome-like vesicles need to come from human cells. Edible plants and milk contain small membrane vesicles that resemble exosomes in size and general structure. These vesicles can be isolated from fruit juices, vegetable extracts, and dairy products using centrifugation and filtration. They are composed of plant or milk lipids and proteins and carry their own small RNAs and other molecules.114 There is growing evidence that plant-derived vesicles can survive passage through the gastrointestinal tract and interact with intestinal epithelial cells and microbiota. Some studies suggest that they can deliver bioactive compounds to gut tissue and even influence distant organs through secondary signalling. Milk-derived vesicles are naturally ingested in large amounts by infants and may contribute to immune and metabolic development. Their established dietary exposure has encouraged interest in their use as carriers for oral drug and nucleic acid delivery.115

There is great interest in using these plant-derived extracellular vesicles as biocompatible platforms for drug delivery systems, especially those that can be delivered orally. Nevertheless, there are still several biological features that are not well-established yet. There is enough experimental evidence suggesting the stability of certain vesicles produced by plants in the gastrointestinal tract, as well as their interaction with mammalian cells; however, it is unknown to what degree these vesicles can be absorbed systemically upon oral delivery. Engineering Strategies for Exosome-Based Nanodrug Delivery

Natural exosomes already behave as tiny transporters, but their native form is rarely sufficient for precise cancer therapy. To turn them into deliberate nanodrug delivery systems, researchers intervene at several levels. They manipulate how cargo enters vesicles, adjust surfaces to guide targeting, combine exosome material with synthetic structures, design formulations that respond to signals in the tumour microenvironment, and try to extend circulation while avoiding unwanted immune capture. These strands of work together define the engineering toolkit for exosome-based platforms.129

For exosome-based nanocarriers, engineering approaches like endogenous loading, exogenous loading, hybridization, and TME-responsive modifications have greatly boosted their effectiveness for drug delivery applications in oncology treatment. The strategy of endogenous loading, which can be accomplished either via genetic modification or transfection of donor cells, facilitates effective encapsulation of RNA or protein cargos without affecting the integrity of the membrane. Genetically modified exosomes have been shown to deliver drugs such as siRNAs, miRNAs, and CRISPR at very high intracellular efficiency and exhibit significant tumor-suppressing effects. Nevertheless, such an approach has several limitations in terms of low yields and biosafety considerations.33,96 Methods of exogenous loading, such as electroporation, sonication, extrusion, and membrane permeabilization, yield relatively higher efficiencies of drug loading when compared to incubation methods. For instance, sonication and electroporation often lead to enhanced encapsulation efficiency relative to co-incubation alone, and allow successful delivery of chemotherapy and nucleic acids. However, electroporation is known to cause aggregation of liposomes and changes in surface protein expression, while sonication is likely to damage the membranes and lead to particle fusion.73,82,130

Hybrid formulations that use both exosomes and liposomes are an effective alternative because they combine the immunoevasiveness and targeting abilities of exosomes with high loading capacities and scalability of liposomes. Hybrid formulations tend to demonstrate better pharmacokinetic profiles, cellular uptake efficiency, and higher therapeutic activity than those based on exosomes alone. Another important feature of the system is the ability to be responsive to the tumor microenvironment.29,33,131,132 Despite these advances, challenges related to manufacturing consistency, long-term safety, immunogenicity of engineered surfaces, and regulatory standardization remain significant barriers to clinical translation.

Drug Loading Strategies

The first question in any exosome-based formulation is how the therapeutic molecule is placed inside or on the vesicle. Broadly, there are two routes. Endogenous loading relies on the donor cell to package the cargo during exosome formation. Exogenous loading works on vesicles after isolation and forces or encourages entry from the outside. Newer microfluidic and light-guided approaches borrow elements from both.33

Endogenous loading makes use of the fact that cells already sort selected proteins and nucleic acids into intraluminal vesicles. Instead of pushing cargo across a completed membrane, the engineer adjusts the donor cell so that the desired molecule is favoured during biogenesis.133 A common approach is genetic modification. Donor cells are transfected or transduced with constructs that encode therapeutic proteins, enzymes, small interfering RNA, or microRNA mimics. In some designs, the therapeutic sequence is fused to a signal peptide or a protein domain known to be enriched in exosomal cargo. As the cell creates multivesicular bodies, these tagged molecules are drawn into the budding intraluminal vesicles. The exosomes that are later released carry the therapeutic element either on their surface or inside their lumen.134 It can produce vesicles with intact membranes and a stable association of cargo. Once the producer cell line is established, the process can be relatively steady. On the other hand, it introduces concerns about the safety of genetically altered donor cells, the possibility of co-packaging unwanted transcripts, and the time needed to create and qualify each engineered line. In addition, the degree of control over the exact number of therapeutic molecules per vesicle is limited.135

Exogenous loading treats exosomes as preformed shells. Vesicles are first collected and purified from conditioned medium or body fluids and then mixed with the drug or nucleic acid of interest. Physical or chemical methods are applied to help this cargo cross the membrane.136 The simplest method is passive incubation. Hydrophobic small molecules partition into the lipid bilayer when exosomes are placed in a concentrated solution of the drug. Some neutral compounds can also diffuse into the inner aqueous space. This approach is gentle and preserves vesicle structure but often yields modest loading for polar or charged agents.137 To increase uptake, investigators use electroporation, sonication, repeated freezing and thawing, or transient exposure to mild surfactants. These methods can significantly raise loading efficiency, especially for RNA molecules, but they may also cause aggregation, loss of surface proteins, or changes in size distribution if not carefully controlled.138

Microfluidic platforms aim to make exogenous loading more precise and more compatible with scale-up. In these systems, exosomes and cargo flow through narrow channels under controlled conditions. Shear forces, local electric fields, or rapid mixing can be tuned to open membranes transiently without catastrophic disruption. Some devices integrate capture, loading, and washing into a single continuous process, which may reduce batch-to-batch variability.139 Light-guided strategies represent another emerging route. Vesicle membranes can be doped with photosensitive molecules that respond to specific wavelengths by altering their conformation and making the bilayer more permeable. When exosomes and cargo are exposed to a pulse of light, the membrane briefly loosens, cargo enters, and the structure then reseals. This offers temporal control over the loading step and can be targeted to microfluidic volumes. Surface Functionalization to Enhance Targeting and Control Interactions

Loading determines what exosomes carry. Surface functionalization shapes where they go and how they behave during the journey. The outer membrane decides which serum proteins bind, which cells recognise the vesicle, and how long it remains in circulation. Functionalization can occur through donor cell engineering before vesicle release or through direct modification after isolation.141

One strategy is to equip exosomes with targeting ligands by engineering the cells that produce them. Donor cells are given genes that encode fusion proteins linking a targeting domain with an exosome-enriched scaffold protein. For example, a single-chain antibody fragment against a tumour-associated receptor can be fused to a tetraspanin or to another membrane protein that prefers exosomal sorting. As the donor cell synthesises this fusion, it appears on the plasma membrane and within endosomal compartments and becomes incorporated into intraluminal vesicles.142 The exosomes that emerge already display the targeting element in a defined orientation. Such ligands have been designed to recognise markers on tumour cells, on tumour blood vessels, or on immune cells within the tumour microenvironment.143

The alternative is to work directly on isolated vesicles. Many exosome membrane proteins and lipids present amino, thiol, or carboxyl groups that can participate in standard coupling reactions. Crosslinking reagents can attach peptides, antibody fragments, small molecules, or polymers to these groups. Through such chemistry, exosomes can be decorated with targeting ligands, stealth-forming chains, or imaging agents after they are harvested.144 Nonselective reactions may mask native proteins that are important for natural tropism or may create heterogeneous populations with variable ligand density. To gain finer control, enzymatic modification can be used. Glycosidases and glycosyl transferases can trim or extend glycan chains, shifting recognition by lectin receptors. Proteases can remove or expose protein domains. Lipid-modifying enzymes can adjust membrane composition and fluidity.145 In both chemical and enzymatic functionalization, conditions must be mild enough to preserve vesicle integrity and core cargo.146

Hybrid and Biomimetic Vesicle Systems

Some scientists have tried to integrate the good qualities of exosomes with those of conventional nanovehicles. This leads to different combinations of structures that can be referred to as either hybrids or biomimics. Vesicles with membranes derived from both exosomes and liposomes/polymer molecules fall on one side, while synthetic nanoparticles carrying only some exosome proteins constitute the other end.147,148

Exosome-liposome hybrids can be generated by fusing the two membranes via gentle heating, multiple passes through filter membranes, or controlled agitation. Hybrid vesicles possess lipids derived from both and preserve the presence of exosome proteins on their surfaces.149 They may be used for drug encapsulation either in their inner aqueous compartment or in the liposomal membrane, similarly to conventional liposomes, but have the advantage of possessing adhesion molecules and receptors derived from exosomes. Hybrid vesicles typically exhibit enhanced physical stability and better-defined sizes compared to pure exosomes but still participate in certain recognition mechanisms associated with exosomes. Design considerations include optimizing the balance between the amount of exosomal membrane and lipids in order to maintain both targeting and stability.150 Another type of hybrid consists of either solid or porous polymer particles that have been coated with exosome membranes. Degradable polymers like poly lactic acid or other similar polymers can produce particles that have higher payloads of drugs and controlled release properties. With these particles encapsulated by exosome membranes, the external coating would be very similar to that of natural vesicles with all the relevant protein and glycan components.151 Here, the core made of polymers provides strength and high payloads, whereas the shell provides biological identity 146.152

A further step away from classical exosomes is to generate vesicles directly from cell membranes without allowing full endosomal biogenesis. Cells are disrupted physically, and the membrane fraction is collected and extruded through small pores to form vesicles of roughly uniform size. These cell-derived nanovesicles carry many of the same membrane markers as exosomes but lack the sorting history of intraluminal vesicles.153 Because each cell can be converted into many vesicles in a single process, yields are higher, and production can be more easily scaled. These particles are often called exosome mimetics. They can be loaded with drugs during formation or by later methods and can be produced from cell lines that already have good manufacturing practice experience.154

At the most synthetic end of the spectrum are particles that retain an artificial core but present an exosome-inspired corona. Gold particles, polymer spheres, or other inorganic or organic cores can be coated with purified exosome proteins or with recombinant versions of selected receptors and adhesion molecules. Another route is to incubate particles in fluids rich in exosomes and allow a protein layer to adsorb.155 These constructs do not carry native exosome nucleic acids or lipids internally. Their exosome-like features are restricted to the surface, where they shape interactions with cells and serum proteins. This arrangement allows strict control over size, shape, and core composition while still harnessing certain recognition properties of exosome proteins.156

Tumour Microenvironment-Responsive Exosome Designs

The microenvironment of solid tumours is characterised by a low pH, perturbed redox environment, presence of some specific proteolytic enzymes, and abnormal blood and lymph circulation. The ability of exosomes to detect and react to such characteristics could increase the specificity of drug delivery in the area of tumours while protecting healthy organs from damage.157

Many tumours are distinguished by an acidic microenvironment than normal cells; moreover, compartments like the endosomes and lysosomes are more acidic. The use of such properties for the delivery of drugs through exosomes can be achieved by designing them in a way to facilitate faster drug release in acidic environments. For instance, coupling agents that can be cleaved only in acidic conditions can be employed to conjugate drugs to the exosomes. The cleavage will only occur in the microenvironment of tumours or intracellular compartments, thus releasing drugs inside the tumours.158 Redox conditions provide another cue. The cytosol and tumour microenvironment often contain higher concentrations of reducing agents than plasma. Linkers that are sensitive to reduction can be used to tether drugs or nucleic acids to vesicle components. These linkers stay intact in circulation but are cut when exosomes reach reductive environments inside target cells, completing payload release.159

Proteases are frequently upregulated in tumour stroma, where they help remodel the extracellular matrix and facilitate invasion. Exosome formulations can include peptides that are cleaved by specific tumour-associated proteases. These peptides can serve as removable caps that block drug binding or targeting ligands until they are cut.160 For example, a targeting peptide could be held in an inactive conformation by a protease-cleavable segment. In normal tissue, the ligand stays masked, and exosomes behave in a relatively stealthy way. In tumour regions rich in the relevant protease, the segment is removed, and the ligand becomes active, enhancing local binding and uptake. Similar designs can shield toxic drugs until they reach the protease-rich stroma.161 Multi-stimulus systems go further by requiring two or more conditions to be met before full activation occurs. A vesicle might contain a pH-sensitive coating and an enzyme-cleavable cap at the same time. Only when acidity rises, and the relevant protease is present, does the drug leave the vesicle efficiently. Strategies for Immune Evasion and Prolonged Circulation

Even though exosomes carry native membranes, they are not invisible to the immune system. Cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system can still capture them, and complement and antibody-mediated pathways can mark them for clearance. Engineering strategies, therefore, also focus on reducing unwanted immune recognition and extending the time that exosomes remain in circulation long enough to reach tumours.168

Some membrane proteins deliver inhibitory signals to phagocytes. By enriching exosomes in such molecules, either through donor cell engineering or by selective purification, it may be possible to reduce uptake by macrophages. Adjusting glycan patterns to minimise exposure of motifs that trigger rapid clearance is another route.169 From a physical perspective, covering selected regions of the exosome surface with hydrophilic polymers can reduce binding of complement factors and other opsonins. Unlike classical synthetic stealth coatings that often hide most of the particle, exosome designs may use partial coverage to preserve access of targeting ligands and to maintain contact with physiological receptors. Charge and hydrophobicity of the membrane can also be tuned to alter serum protein adsorption and to influence how vesicles interact with endothelial cells and extracellular matrix.170

Immune evasion from recognition is not dictated solely by the process of molecular engineering. The mode and timing of administration are crucial factors dictating which immune compartment will be exposed to exosomes and what kind of immune response will follow. Intravenous administration exposes exosomes to peripheral circulating macrophages located in the liver and spleen. Administration in tumours or CSF or arterial routes can alter this exposure and increase the fraction of administered drug reaching its destination.171 Multiple dosing could induce an adaptive immune response even though each individual administration may be well-tolerated. Careful planning of dosing, use of transient immunosuppression, or anti-exosome antibody testing will be helpful to overcome this problem. In some cases, alternating exosome donors or alternating carriers will minimize sensitization.172

Theranostic and Combination Exosome-Based Strategies

Theranostic design aims to bring diagnosis and treatment together in a single platform. Instead of using one procedure to locate tumours and another to treat them, a theranostic carrier carries both an imaging signal and an active therapeutic payload. Exosomes are good candidates for such work because they can carry several types of cargo at once and can navigate complex tissues in ways that partly mirror physiological communication. At the same time, the same capacity that allows exosomes to hold imaging agents and drugs can be used to support rational combination therapies in which more than one antitumour mechanism acts in the same place and at the same time.174

Concept of Theranostic Exosome Platforms in Personalised Cancer Care

Personalised cancer care seeks to match treatment to the biology of each patient and to adjust regimens as tumours evolve. For this to happen in practice, clinicians need timely information about how drugs distribute, which lesions they reach, and how the disease responds. Theranostic exosomes offer a way to obtain some of this information within the same construct that delivers treatment. A vesicle that carries both a contrast agent and a drug can reveal where it has travelled and where it has released its payload.175 This idea fits naturally with the role of exosomes as couriers of biological signals. In a theranostic setting, an engineered vesicle might be tracked by magnetic resonance imaging, nuclear medicine scans, or optical methods while at the same time delivering chemotherapy, nucleic acids, or immune modulators. Observed distribution can then guide subsequent dosing and can help explain success or failure. For example, weak accumulation in a resistant lesion may indicate that the delivery route or targeting strategy must be changed, while strong accumulation without response suggests that the choice of therapeutic agent needs revision.176

Exosomes as Carriers of Imaging Agents

To serve as theranostic platforms, exosomes must be labelled with imaging agents that can be detected in a clinical or experimental setting. Several classes of contrast material have been explored, including magnetic, nuclear, and optical labels. Labelling can be achieved by exposing donor cells to contrast agents that they internalise and route into exosomes, or by incubating purified vesicles with imaging agents using loading methods similar to those used for drugs.176

Magnetic resonance and nuclear imaging are central tools in oncology because they provide deep tissue views and whole-body maps. Exosomes can be loaded with superparamagnetic iron oxide particles or with gadolinium chelates so that they alter local magnetic fields and change image intensity. When these labelled vesicles are administered in animal models, magnetic resonance imaging can reveal where they accumulate, how long they remain in tumours, and which organs receive off-target exposure.175 For nuclear imaging, exosomes can be linked to radioisotopes that emit gamma rays or positrons. This is often done by attaching chelating groups to membrane proteins or lipids and then binding metal radionuclides. Such vesicles can be tracked by single-photon emission computed tomography or positron emission tomography after injection.177

Optical labels such as fluorescent dyes and near infrared probes offer high sensitivity in preclinical work and can provide real-time information during surgery or endoscopy. Exosomes can be tagged with such dyes through covalent attachment to membrane components or by loading lipophilic probes into the bilayer. After administration, fluorescence imaging can show how vesicles spread through superficial tumours, how they enter and leave specific regions, and how their distribution changes over time.178 Because each imaging technique has strengths and weaknesses, there is interest in multimodal exosome labels. A single vesicle population can carry both a magnetic contrast agent and a fluorescent dye, or a nuclear label and an optical probe. Exosome-Mediated Combination Therapies

Even without imaging agents, exosomes are well-suited to combination therapy because they can hold more than one class of therapeutic cargo. They can carry small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids in the same vesicle and deliver them together to target cells. This capacity can be used to design regimens in which complementary mechanisms of action converge on the same malignant cell population.181

One widely studied combination is chemotherapy plus gene modulation. Many tumours resist cytotoxic drugs by upregulating repair pathways, expressing drug efflux pumps, or reinforcing survival signals. For instance, vesicles may deliver a DNA-damaging agent together with small interfering RNA that reduces the expression of a repair enzyme or a transporter that expels the drug.182 When both components arrive in the same cell, the drug-induced damage is more likely to trigger cell death rather than repair and recovery. Endogenous loading methods can pack the nucleic acid during vesicle formation by engineering donor cells, while exogenous loading can introduce the small molecule afterward. In this way, a single exosome can bring together genetic and pharmacological pressure that would be harder to coordinate with separate delivery systems.183

Exosomes also provide a natural link between chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Vesicles derived from dendritic cells, T cells, or natural killer cells already interact with the immune system and can present antigens or carry co-stimulatory molecules. If such vesicles are loaded with chemotherapeutic drugs, they can both injure tumour cells directly and support immune recognition of the resulting damage.184 One design uses dendritic cell exosomes that display tumour antigens on their surface, and that also carry a cytotoxic agent such as doxorubicin. Another approach uses exosomes to deliver small interfering RNA that suppresses immune checkpoints on tumour cells or on regulatory immune cells, while also carrying a standard drug that increases immunogenic cell death. In both cases, the exosome functions as a coordinating unit for chemo-immunotherapy, concentrating both actions in the tumour microenvironment.185

Photothermal and photodynamic therapies rely on agents that respond to light by generating heat or reactive oxygen species. Their practical limitation is that sensitising agents often fail to reach all regions of a tumour, especially those that are poorly perfused. Exosomes can help by transporting these agents more efficiently into the tumour mass.186 In photothermal designs, exosomes can be loaded with materials such as gold-based structures or carbon-based nanomaterials that convert light into heat. After these vesicles accumulate in tumours, an external light source with the appropriate wavelength is applied. The local rise in temperature damages cancer cells while sparing distant tissues. In photodynamic strategies, exosomes carry photosensitizers that produce reactive oxygen species when illuminated. Encapsulation can reduce premature activation in skin and other normal tissues and can increase the fraction of agent that reaches the tumour. These light-based treatments can be combined with chemotherapy or gene modulation within the same exosome to provide a multi-pronged attack.187

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone of treatment for many solid tumours, but its effectiveness is limited by resistant clones and by the tolerance of surrounding normal tissues. Exosomes can be used to deliver radiosensitising agents directly to tumour cells or to specific regions such as hypoxic zones that respond poorly to radiation.188 Radiosensitising payloads include drugs that inhibit DNA repair, modulate cell cycle distribution, or alter oxygen usage. Exosomes can also carry small interfering RNA that reduces the expression of key proteins in survival pathways activated after irradiation. By concentrating these agents in tumour tissue, exosome delivery may allow lower radiation doses to achieve the same or greater tumour control.189

Limitations of Combination Therapies and Theranostic Exosome-Inspired Nanocarriers

The success of such treatment approaches on a preclinical level is not sufficient to prove the efficacy of exosome-like nanocarriers for combination therapy relative to monotherapies. Various research studies have shown that there can be synergism between gene, immuno-, chemo-, and phototherapy if used in combination with one another under a single exosome delivery vehicle. Nevertheless, the majority of these findings come from animal studies that lack quantifiable comparisons.29,33,82,131 Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying synergy are often incompletely characterized, while variations in cargo ratios, dosing schedules, and exosome sources contribute to inconsistent outcomes and limited reproducibility.

There are also challenges related to the clinical translation of theranostic exosomes that offer imaging capability and therapeutic functions simultaneously. Although they provide opportunities for concurrent tumour imaging and therapy monitoring, issues such as low sensitivity, signal stability, and high manufacturing cost represent critical bottlenecks.29,33,34,190 Moreover, PDT and PTT based on exosomes are also subject to poor tissue penetration caused by the lack of light, particularly in tumours located deep in tissues. Furthermore, PDT is affected by tumour hypoxia and, therefore, suffers from lower levels of production of reactive oxygen species, while PTT is associated with uneven heat distribution leading to incomplete destruction of tumours.191,192 Finally, prolonged exposure to external light might induce adverse effects in healthy cells adjacent to the tumour. Thus, further research is needed to prove the therapeutic and safety advantages of such approaches in vivo.

In addition, mass production is problematic since exosomes are frequently produced in relatively low yields with batch-to-batch variability and complicated purification processes. Moreover, significant variations in vesicle size, composition, cargo, and biological activity make exosomes difficult to characterize, quality test, and determine therapeutic efficacy. Modification of vesicles may enhance efficacy but can also affect biodistribution and other aspects. In particular, regulatory issues arise as the field still lacks standardization regarding definitions, production methods, release criteria, and pharmacokinetics of exosomes. While hybrid and biomimetic vesicles could help overcome some of the problems described above, a fully GMP-compliant method, standard characterization approach, and adequate safety evidence are needed for full-scale application.31,41,82,96,99,193

Manufacturing Safety and Regulatory Considerations

Turning exosome-based systems from experimental tools into clinical products demands more than elegant biology. It requires reliable production at a useful scale, careful characterisation, clear evidence of safety, and alignment with regulatory expectations. These practical aspects often determine whether a promising concept can reach patients.194

Donor Cell Selection and Banking

Selection of the appropriate donor cells serves as the basis for any exosome manufacturing process. The selection of cells not only determines the biological characteristics of the exosomes produced but also their expansion and control within the manufacturing process. Mesenchymal stem cells, dendritic cells, and certain immortalized cell lines are commonly used donors.195 When clinical applications are concerned, donor cells should be clearly defined and devoid of any infectious agent. In case primary cells are employed, stringent screening of donor individuals is required to rule out infections and other sources of risk. In the case of stable cell lines, these should be well characterised, with proper documentation of their derivation, genetic manipulation and passages. Setting up of a master cell bank and working cell bank under strict controls ensures a standard starting point for each batch production process.195

Bioreactor-Based Culture and Conditioned Medium Collection

Small-scale cultures in flasks or petri dishes can be used for initial studies but will not be sufficient to fulfill the requirements of repetitive clinical dosing. Cultures in bioreactors have higher cell concentrations, better control over environmental factors, and compatibility with downstream processing steps. Suspension cultures and microcarrier-based adherent cultures can both be adopted in bioreactors.196 For such processes, conditioned medium is obtained from the culture medium. Collection times, volumes, and frequencies have an impact on the output quantity and composition. Concentration of nutrients, presence of oxygen, and the build-up of waste products are some factors that could influence the production of vesicles. Optimization includes both biology optimization and engineering decisions with regard to reactor choice, mixing, and feed strategies.197

Exosome Isolation, Purification, and Characterization

Centrifugation and Filtration-Based Methods

The differential centrifugation technique has been a long-standing method for isolating exosomes. This involves repeated rounds of centrifugation at higher speeds to eliminate cell debris and other large vesicles before finally pelletting the smaller vesicles out from the supernatant. Although highly effective on small scales, this is a very tedious process and one that cannot be easily scaled up.198 Methods of filtration, such as tangential flow filtration, can deal with large volumes effectively. Filtration is done using membranes, which have certain pore sizes for separating exosomes from bigger particles and proteins dissolved in the solution. The parameters, such as flow rate and pressure, allow manufacturing companies to increase vesicle concentration while removing contamination from them. Nevertheless, it is important to select suitable filtration parameters not to harm vesicles.198

Chromatography and Affinity Capture Techniques

Chromatography introduces yet another level of selectivity. In Size Exclusion Chromatography, separation takes place by apparent size, thereby enabling exosomes to be fractionated from other proteins and nanoparticles. It is a mild technique that yields narrow size distributions but may suffer low throughputs unless scaled correctly.199 The affinity capture approach utilizes ligands capable of recognising exosomal surface markers, such as specific tetraspanins or carbohydrate structures, on bead or column surfaces. This strategy allows selecting certain vesicle types from the mixture. It also provides a means of eliminating unneeded vesicles when a negative selection procedure is employed. When applied for production purposes, affinity ligands should be selected based on their durability and lack of elution.200

Quality Control Assays for Identity, Purity, and Potency

Regardless of the choice of isolation technique, final products should always be characterized using suitable assays. Identification typically involves measuring size distribution, through methods such as nanoparticle tracking analysis and related assays, and the detection of markers characteristic of the exosome, including immunoblotting, flow assays, and other immunoassays. Purity determination seeks to measure any remaining protein, nucleic acid, and non-exosome particle content.201 Potency assays are used to establish a relationship between some measurable parameter of the product and its biological activity. For instance, the potency assay for exosomes which are meant to deliver small interfering RNA may involve checking the effectiveness of the suppression of the target gene’s expression in a standard cell line. Similarly, vesicles which aim to influence the activity of the immune system may be checked on the basis of their ability to enhance or suppress certain immune responses.202

Safety and Toxicology of Exosome-Based Products

The distribution and clearance are important before giving exosome products to the patients. Labelled vesicles are often used in animal studies to track where they go after administration. Common accumulation sites include the liver, spleen, lung, and, depending on route, local lymph nodes and bone marrow. Tumour targeting and retention are also assessed, especially for oncology indications.203 These studies help identify organs that may be at risk of unintended exposure to therapeutic cargo.204

The immune system may respond to exosomes when they are derived from human cells. Innate pathways involving complement and pattern recognition receptors can be activated, leading to cytokine release and infusion-related reactions. Adaptive responses may develop against donor-specific antigens or engineered surface ligands.205 Preclinical toxicology programmes therefore, include evaluation of cytokine profiles, complement activation, and antibody formation after repeated administration in relevant animal models. In early human trials, careful monitoring for fever, chills, allergic symptoms, changes in blood counts, and organ function is essential. Dose escalation must proceed stepwise, with attention to both immediate reactions and delayed immune effects.205 A particular concern for exosome-based products is the possibility of carrying unwanted oncogenic or infectious material. Vesicles from tumour cells may include mutationally altered proteins, oncogenic microRNA, and DNA fragments. Unless these components are thoroughly removed or neutralised, they could pose risks. For this reason, many clinical programmes avoid direct use of tumour derived exosomes and instead use them only as antigen sources during manufacturing of other products.206 Even exosomes from non-malignant sources can transmit infectious agents if donors or cell banks are not properly screened.207

Regulatory and Good Manufacturing Practice Frameworks

Regulatory agencies must decide how to classify exosome-based products. They share features with biologics, cell-derived therapies, gene therapy vectors, and advanced drug delivery systems. Depending on national frameworks, they may fall under categories such as biological medicinal products, advanced therapy medicinal products, or combinations of these.208 This classification influences which guidelines apply, how extensive preclinical packages must be, and what kinds of clinical trials are required. It also affects expectations for traceability, donor screening, viral safety, and post-marketing surveillance. Developers need early dialogue with regulators to agree on product definitions, critical quality attributes, and acceptable development paths.209 Once a regulatory category is clear, detailed requirements for batch release and stability studies must be met. Release criteria typically include tests for appearance, particle concentration, size distribution, key markers of identity, absence of specified contaminants, and potency in a relevant assay. Each parameter must have defined acceptance ranges supported by manufacturing experience and by links to clinical performance where available.210 Exosomes may be sensitive to repeated freezing and thawing, to temperature fluctuations, and to contact with certain container materials. Suitable buffers, storage temperatures, and shelf lives must be established through real-time and accelerated studies. These data support labelling claims and instructions for handling in clinical settings.211 Beyond formal regulations, several practical issues can slow translation. Production costs can be high when complex cell culture and purification processes are involved. Yields may be modest, particularly for autologous products, making it challenging to deliver repeated doses. Scaling up while maintaining consistent quality is technically demanding.212 Intellectual property landscapes for exosomes involve overlapping claims on donor cell lines, isolation methods, loading strategies, and therapeutic indications.213 Addressing these barriers will require collaboration between basic scientists, process engineers, clinicians, regulators, and industry partners. Only by integrating biological innovation with sound manufacturing practice and realistic clinical goals can exosome-based nano-drug delivery systems progress from experimental promise to dependable tools in cancer care.214

Clinical Translation Landscape of Exosome-Based Nano-Drug Delivery

Exosome-based approaches have moved from purely experimental work toward early testing in people. The number of registered studies is still modest when compared with trials for synthetic nanocarriers or monoclonal antibodies, but it is growing each year. Most current trials are exploratory. They focus on safety, feasibility, and basic signals of biological activity rather than on definitive survival outcomes. They also cover both therapeutic and diagnostic uses, which together give a first picture of how exosome technology behaves in real clinical settings.215

Therapeutic Exosome Trials in Oncology and Related Indications

Therapeutic trials use exosomes as active agents that are expected to change the disease course or to support recovery from treatment. In oncology, this includes vaccine-like products that aim to stimulate immunity against tumours, vesicles that deliver drugs or nucleic acids, and exosomes that help repair tissue injury caused by intensive therapy.216

Some of the earliest clinical work with exosomes in cancer used vesicles released by dendritic cells. These antigen-presenting cells were cultured from patients or donors, loaded with tumour antigens, and allowed to release exosomes rich in major histocompatibility complex molecules and co-stimulatory proteins. The exosomes were then purified and injected back into patients as cell-free vaccines.217 Another group of trials has focused on exosomes from mesenchymal stem cells. Many of these studies address inflammatory or degenerative conditions outside oncology, such as graft versus host disease or organ injury. However, they are relevant to cancer because they inform on the safety, biodistribution, and immunological impact of repeated mesenchymal exosome dosing.218 In the cancer context, mesenchymal exosomes have been explored as supportive agents to limit tissue damage from chemotherapy or radiotherapy and as carriers for anticancer molecules. Early phase studies suggest that intravenous or local administration is well tolerated. Adverse events have usually been mild and transient, such as low-grade fever or local discomfort at injection sites. Clear signs of tissue repair have been reported in some organ injury settings, although controlled data are still limited. For direct anticancer delivery, clinical experience remains very early, with small cohorts and open-label designs. Even so, these studies contribute to building a safety record for repeated dosing with exosomes from well-characterised donor cells.219

Parallel with therapeutic work, many groups have developed exosome-based diagnostics. Rather than giving vesicles as a treatment, these studies collect exosomes from blood, urine, saliva, or other fluids and analyse their content as a form of liquid biopsy. The goal is to capture information about tumours in a minimally invasive way. Lessons from Current Clinical Experience

The first and perhaps most important message from current trials is that exosome-based products can be manufactured and administered safely within controlled settings. Doses used so far have produced little severe toxicity. Infusion reactions, when they occur, have typically been mild and manageable. No consistent pattern of organ-specific injury has emerged in early studies, although longer follow-up and larger cohorts are still needed. Signals of clinical benefit are more variable. Some vaccine-style products have produced durable disease stabilization or partial responses in a subset of patients, often accompanied by measurable immune activation. Exosome-based diagnostics have, in several cases, shown better sensitivity or earlier detection of relapse than conventional markers. However, confirmatory studies in larger, independent cohorts are often lacking.225

Current Clinical Trial Landscape

The application of exosome-mimicking nanocarrier systems in oncology has witnessed rapid advancements in 2025–2026. Yet, the vast majority of such innovations still find themselves at the pre-clinical or early phases of their clinical testing. In recent reviews, it is often stressed that the majority of exosome-related research has been concentrated on assessing the safety, tolerability, biodistribution, and feasibility of these carriers rather than their efficacy as a treatment method. Although there have been impressive breakthroughs in the field of exosome design, synthesis, loading, production reproducibility, and regulation, the latter still restricts progress towards further development. As a result, only very few exosome-associated drugs managed to reach Phase III.73,226–228 In addition, recent studies have shown that exosome-assisted drug delivery in the treatment of cancers is not yet widely adopted clinically. Despite the biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, prolonged blood circulation time, and inherent tumor-targeting properties of engineered exosomes, factors such as poor drug-loading efficiency, quick elimination from circulation, heterogeneous content, and scalability still pose a barrier to translational medicine. Therefore, numerous anticancer drug delivery systems using exosomes that show promising results in preclinical studies are not equally successful when tested on human subjects.229–232

However, in the case of exosomes, their use in diagnostics and vaccination has proven more successful clinically compared to drug delivery systems. The potential use of exosomes as non-invasive biomarkers for diagnostics and early cancer detection has been studied extensively. In addition, several research studies have reported a good safety profile of dendritic cell exosomes and engineered exosome vaccines capable of generating antitumor immunity. Hence, various clinical trials are currently underway involving personalized exosome vaccines against cancer. This clearly indicates that while exosomes hold great promise for cancer therapeutics, the focus is now turning to the immunomodulatory and diagnostic role of exosomes since translational challenges in immunotherapy are relatively smaller than those of drug delivery systems. Thus, despite promising results from exosome-motivated nanocarriers for precision oncology, diagnostics, and vaccines seem to be more clinically promising than drugs.226,233–236

Comparative Advantages, Limitations, and Translational Outlook

An assessment of recent literature finds that there is a growing agreement that nanoparticle platforms based on the principles of exosomes have biological merits over traditional synthetic nanoparticles, especially in terms of their biocompatibility, protection of their payload, and interactions with biological barriers. Several laboratories have succeeded in showing better drug delivery through the use of engineered exosomes, exosome-liposome hybrids, and biomimetic vesicles; nevertheless, whether one strategy is more advantageous than another is still open to discussion. Although exosome-based nanoparticles tend to exhibit improved internalization by cells and lower immunogenicity when compared with traditional nanoparticles, experimental comparisons point to a lower level of tumor targeting in vivo in exosome-based nanoparticles compared to those attained by purely physical processes. Recent work even suggests that the origin of the extracellular vesicles does not affect their biodistribution to the extent initially presumed, calling into question the hypothesis of organotropism associated with certain cell types.29,31

Another point of agreement relates to the increasing role played by engineering techniques. The surface-functionalized vesicles, hybrid nanoplatforms, and responsive nanotechnology based on the tumor microenvironment have shown enhanced precision and effectiveness in preclinical settings. However, there is still a great deal of uncertainty regarding reproducibility, scaling up, and clinical significance. While, for example, hybrid or biomimetic systems can solve the issues of poor yield and heterogeneity typical for natural exosomes, they simultaneously increase manufacturing challenges. On the other hand, theranostic approaches that combine imaging, gene modification, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy provide great flexibility in treatment but pose problems of standardization and validation. Thus, the focus of research is slowly moving from the proof of concept to manufacturing standards and pharmacology. Future developments should be based not so much on the engineering of increasingly sophisticated nanoplatforms, but rather on solving the problems of characterization, scaling up, regulatory approval, and clinical benefits, which are the major obstacles to translation at present.29,33,39,41,73,96,131,231,237–239

Future Perspectives

Future perspectives for exosome-based nano-drug delivery in cancer lie at the intersection of deeper biology. Smarter engineering and data-informed precision medicine. As multi-omics and imaging tools reveal more about how exosomes select cargo, choose routes through the body, and interact with the tumour microenvironment, these insights can be turned into design rules for safer and more effective carriers. Artificial intelligence and systems biology are likely to play a growing role by mining large exosome datasets to identify diagnostic signatures, predict organ tropism, and suggest combinations of surface markers and payloads that match specific tumour profiles. On the clinical side, one can expect a gradual move from generic vesicle formulations toward more tailored products, ranging from autologous exosomes tuned to an individual patient’s tumour to standardised allogeneic vesicles that serve defined indications in large groups. Progress will also depend on practical advances in scalable manufacturing, gentle purification, and robust potency assays that link measurable exosome features to therapeutic effect. In the longer term, the most promising scenario is one in which exosome-based systems become part of integrated treatment pathways, supporting liquid biopsy, guiding imaging, delivering precise combinations of drugs and nucleic acids, and working alongside surgery, radiotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy to provide more durable control with less collateral damage.225,240

Combining AI with systems biology is becoming instrumental in designing nanomedicine using exosomes due to its potential to use multi-omic data for optimizing cargo delivery, biodistribution, and drug efficacy modeling. However, the application of this approach is currently hampered by EV heterogeneity and a lack of standardization in data analysis and production methods.241–243 Moreover, there are ongoing problems such as the cost involved in production, the difficulty of scaling up manufacturing processes following GMP guidelines, and the uncertainty over whether to classify EV-based drugs as biologics or combination products.97,193

Conclusion

Nanocontainers inspired by exosomes are a perfect example of the evolution of nanomedicine for cancer therapy, combining the biological function of natural extracellular vesicles with the flexibility of artificial nanocarriers. As discussed in this review, the main difference between these nanomaterials and many existing nanocarriers is not limited to drug loading but involves the capability of carrying fragile drugs, crossing biological membranes, interacting with the tumor environment, and enabling multimodal therapies and theragnostics. The latest developments in engineered exosomes, exosome-nanoparticle hybrids, and bioinspired vesicles show how this research area is developing from native vesicles into new delivery systems with higher control and clinical relevance. Nevertheless, the scientific community faces considerable challenges before reaching clinical application. Key barriers to success include low yields during production, variability among vesicles, inadequate delivery of cargo, poor knowledge of the distribution in living systems, lack of safety information in the long run, and lack of standardized protocols for production and characterization. Also, although promising preclinical studies have been reported, clear superiority to existing nanomedicine systems has yet to be shown.

One of the key findings of this review is that exosome-mimicking nanocarriers will be able to create real benefits in situations in which traditional approaches fail, especially in applications involving the transport of nucleic acid drugs, proteins, and combination treatments. In order to promote translation of such innovations into the clinic, efforts should focus on establishing standardized manufacturing protocols that conform to Good Manufacturing Practices, rigorous quality control protocols, and quantitative assessments of pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, together with comparative clinical trials confirming their therapeutic efficacy. In this way, it becomes clear that the potential of exosome-like nanocarriers lies not in adding more technology but in realizing consistent and reproducible manufacture and demonstrating clinical relevance. When all these prerequisites are met, exosome-mimicking nanocarriers have the potential to become an additional element in the toolbox of precision oncology rather than competitors to other nanocarrier platforms.
