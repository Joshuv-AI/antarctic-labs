1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. For a vertex
v\in V
, a
local complementation
at
v
is an operation where we replace the neighborhood of
v
by its complement. Let
G*v
denote the resulting graph after performing local complementation at
v
. We say that two graphs are
locally equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of local complementations. For a graph
H
with vertex set
V(H)\subseteq V
, we say that
H
is a vertex-minor of
G
if
H
can be obtained from
G
via a sequence of vertex deletions and local complementations. Equivalently,
H
is a vertex-minor of
G
if
H
is the induced subgraph on
V(H)
of a graph locally equivalent to
G
. Since their introduction in works of Bouchet
[
2
]
and Fon-Der-Flaass
[
5
]
in the 1980s, vertex-minors have been extensively studied in structural graph theory. In particular, local complementations preserve the cut-rank function of a graph. Thus, vertex-minors have close connections to the study of circle graphs, forbidden minors and related decomposition questions. See the survey on vertex-minors by Kim and Oum
[
6
]
for a more detailed overview of vertex-minors and related concepts.

Motivated by the connections between vertex-minors and quantum communication, a recent line of work considers the universality question for vertex-minors. A graph
G
is
k
-vertex-minor universal
if for every vertex subset
U\subseteq V(G)
of size
k
, every graph on
U
is a vertex-minor of
G
. Note that this is a strong condition where every graph on
k
vertices must be realized on any specified subset of
k
vertices in
G
. In this direction, Claudet, Mhalla, and Perdrix
[
4
]
first showed that there exists
k
-vertex-minor universal graphs with
O(k^{4}\log k)
vertices and any
k
-vertex-minor universal graph must contain at least
(k-2)^{2}/(2\log_{2}3)
vertices. Then, Cautrés, Claudet, Mhalla, Perdrix, Savin, and Thomassé
[
3
]
further showed the existence of
k
-vertex-minor universal graphs with
(2+o(1))k^{2}
vertices. Most recently, Ascoli, Frederickson, Frederickson, McFarland, and Post
[
1
]
showed that almost all graphs on
n
vertices are
O(\sqrt{n})
-vertex-minor universal by proving the following statement. Recall that
G(n,p)
denotes the Erdős-Renyí random graph on
n
vertices with each edge present with probability
p
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

For a positive integer
k
and
G\sim G(n,1/2)
, if
n\geqslant(1+C)\frac{1}{2\log_{2}(4/3)}k^{2}
for some
C>0
, then
G
is
k
-vertex-minor universal with probability at least
1-2^{-(1+o(1))Ck^{2}/2}
.

Furthermore, the authors conjecture that an analogous result holds for
G(n,p)
and
G(n,1-p)
where
p=\omega(1/\sqrt{n})
and
p\leqslant 1/2
. Formally, they conjecture the following statement.

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

If
p=\omega(1/\sqrt{n})
and
p\leqslant 1/2
and
G\sim G(n,p)
or
G\sim G(n,1-p)
, then
G
is
k
-vertex-minor universal with high probability for some
k=\Omega(p\sqrt{n})
.

It was remarked in
[
1
]
that the method used to prove
Theorem

1.1
cannot be naturally extended to other values of
p\neq 1/2
. This is because
Theorem

1.1
is proved by considering the random walk on the set of graphs on a fixed subset
U
of size
k
produced by a random sequence of local complementations at vertices outside of
U
. A crucial property used in the proof is the fact that each step of the random walk is independent when
p=1/2
, which no longer holds for
p\neq 1/2
. Our main result is to confirm
1.2
up to an extra logarithm factor.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a constant
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds for all
n>n_{0}
.
Suppose that
p\in[0,1]
and
q\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\min(p,1-p)\geqslant 100\log n/\sqrt{n}
. For any
k\leqslant q\sqrt{n}/100
, and
G\sim G(n,p)
, the probability that
G
is
k
-vertex-minor universal is at least
1-2^{-q^{2}n/100}
.

Our main technical contribution is
Lemma

3.1
, which formalizes the following intuition: Let
G\sim G(n,p)
and fix a set of
\Omega(k^{2})
vertices
U\subseteq V(G)
. Let
W\overset{\rm{def}}{=}V(G)\setminus U
. Then after a sequence of local complementations at
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\}=W
, the distribution of the induced subgraph
(G*w_{1}*\dots*w_{r})[U]
should be close to
G(U,1/2)
in total variation distance. Indeed, if
W
is an independent set, any edge
uv
with
u,v\in U
is toggled during local complementation at
w_{i}
if
u,v\in N(w_{i})
. Furthermore, since
W
is an independent set, local complementation at
w_{j}
for
j<i
does not toggle the edges between
w_{i}
and
U
. So, the event that
u,v\in N(w_{i})
holds with probability
p^{2}
and only depends on the edges between
w_{i}
and
U
. Moreover, the events that
u,v\in N(w_{i})
are independent for all
w_{i}
where
i\in[r]
. Therefore, by a direct calculation, the probability that
uv
is an edge in
(G*w_{1}*\dots*w_{r})[U]
is
\frac{1}{2}\pm 2^{-\Omega(q^{2}r)}
. The main difficulty is to generalize this observation from the distribution over a single edge
uv
to the distribution over all possible graphs on
U
, and to deal with the dependencies between local complementations at
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
when
W
is not an independent set in
G
.
Once we have proved
Lemma

3.1
, we can prove
Theorem

1.3
by applying
Theorem

1.1
to
(G*w_{1}*\dots*w_{r})[U]
in a black-box way.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

i\in[r]
, let
G_{i}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}G*w_{1}*\dots*w_{i}
. Let
z_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{s}
be the indicator vector of the edges in
G_{i}[U,w_{i}]
and let
X_{i}
be the
i
-th column vector of
X
. Note that
z_{i}=X_{i}+\sum_{j}z_{j}
, where the summation is over all
j<i
such that
w_{i}w_{j}
is an edge in
G_{j-1}
. This is because for each
j<i
, local complementation at
w_{j}
on
G_{j-1}
flips the edge between
w_{i}u
for
u\in U
if and only if
w_{i}w_{j}
and
w_{j}u
are both edges in
G_{j-1}
. Consider the matrix
L\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times r}
defined as

L_{ji}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }j=i,\\
1&\text{ if }j<i\text{ and }w_{i}w_{j}\text{ is an edge in }G_{j-1},\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, note that
L
is upper triangular with all diagonal entries being
1
, so
L
is invertible.
Then
XL^{-1}
is the matrix with columns exactly
(z_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
. Now, we claim that
M=L^{-1}(L^{-1})^{T}
satisfies the conditions in the claim. First, it is clear that
L
only depends on
G_{j-1}[W]
, which only depends on
G[W]
. Thus,
M
only depends on
G[W]
.

Note that from the definition of local complementation, the edge
u_{j}u_{k}
is flipped during local complementation at
w_{i}
on
G_{i-1}
if and only if both
u_{j}w_{i}
and
u_{k}w_{i}
are edges in
G_{i-1}
. Thus, the adjacency matrices of
G_{i-1}[U]
and
G_{i}[U]
differ by exactly
\textup{OffDiag}(z_{i}z_{i}^{T})
. It follows directly that the adjacency matrix of
G_{\Delta}
is exactly
\textup{OffDiag}(\sum_{i=1}^{r}z_{i}z_{i}^{T})=\textup{OffDiag}(XMX^{T})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we analyze the distribution of
G_{\Delta}
using discrete Fourier transformation. To simplify the analysis, we will always condition on
G[W]=G^{*}
from now on. We use
\mu:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{U}{2}}\rightarrow[0,1]
to denote the probability distribution of
G_{\Delta}
conditioning on
G[W]=G^{*}
. Namely, for each
H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}
, we define

\mu(H)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathbb{P}(G_{\Delta}=H\mid G[W]=G^{*}).

In addition, let
\mu_{\textup{unif}}
be the uniform distribution over
\binom{U}{2}
, i.e.
\mu_{\textup{unif}}=2^{-\binom{s}{2}}
where
s=|U|
for every
H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}
.

For a function
f:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{U}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define its Fourier transformation
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{U}{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\widehat{f}(F)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\sum_{H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}f(H)(-1)^{\left|H\cap F\right|}.

Here we abuse notation and use
H
and
F
to denote the graphs on the vertex set
U
and their corresponding edge indicator vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{U}{2}}
. We first recall the following standard fact of bounding the total variation distance between
\mu
and
\mu_{\textup{unif}}
via the Fourier coefficients of
\mu
.

Claim 3.3
.

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution over
\binom{U}{2}
and
\mu_{\textup{unif}}
be the uniform distribution over
\binom{U}{2}
. Then we have

d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\textup{unif}})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{F\neq\varnothing\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}|\widehat{\mu}(F)|.

Proof.

Note that we have

\widehat{\mu_{\textup{unif}}}(F)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }F=\varnothing,\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

and
\widehat{\mu}(\varnothing)=1
. Thus, by linearity of the Fourier transform, we have

\widehat{(\mu-\mu_{\textup{unif}})}(F)=\widehat{\mu}(F)-\widehat{\mu_{\textup{unif}}}(F)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }F=\varnothing,\\
\widehat{\mu}(F)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, recall that
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\textup{unif}})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{2}||\mu-\mu_{\textup{unif}}||_{1}
, the claim follows from the fact that
||f||_{1}\leqslant\sum_{F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\left|\widehat{f}(F)\right|
. For completeness, we include a proof of this standard fact here. By the Fourier inversion formula, we have
f(H)=2^{-\binom{s}{2}}\sum_{F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\widehat{f}(F)(-1)^{\left|H\cap F\right|}
, and hence

\left|f(H)\right|\leqslant 2^{-\binom{s}{2}}\sum_{F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\left|\widehat{f}(F)\right|.

Therefore,

||f||_{1}=\sum_{H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\left|f(H)\right|\leqslant 2^{-\binom{s}{2}}\sum_{H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\sum_{F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\left|\widehat{f}(F)\right|=\sum_{F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\left|\widehat{f}(F)\right|.\qed

Now we upper bound
\left|\widehat{\mu}(F)\right|
for each
F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}
by the rank of its adjacency matrix. From now on, we abuse notation and use
\textup{rank}(F)
to denote the rank of the adjacency matrix of
F
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Claim 3.4
.

For any
F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}
, we have

\left|\widehat{\mu}(F)\right|\leqslant(1-2q^{2})^{r\cdot\textup{rank}(F)/6-1}.

Proof.

Recall that by definition, we have

\widehat{\mu}(F)=\sum_{H\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}\mathbb{P}(G_{\Delta}=H\mid G[W]=G^{*})(-1)^{\left|H\cap F\right|}=\mathbb{E}_{H\sim\mu}[(-1)^{\left|H\cap F\right|}],

where we recall that
\mu
is the distribution of
(G_{\Delta}\mid G[W]=G^{*})
. By
3.2
, we know that there exists an invertible symmetric matrix
M
satisfying the following: Let
X\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{s\times r}
be the biadjacency matrix of
G[U,W]
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with independent
\textup{Ber}(p)
entries. Let
v_{i}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(X_{i1},\dots,X_{ir})
be the
i
-row of
X
. Then for any
\{i,j\}\in\binom{[s]}{2}
, we have
u_{i}u_{j}\in H
if and only if
v_{i}Mv_{j}^{T}=1
.

Define for convenience
F_{0}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[s]}{2}\mid u_{i}u_{j}\in F\}
so that
F_{0}
is the graph
F
relabeled on the vertex set
[s]
via the ordering
u_{i}\mapsto i
on
U
. It follows that
\left|H\cap F\right|\equiv\sum_{\{i,j\}\in F_{0}}v_{i}Mv_{j}^{T}\pmod{2}
. Therefore, we can rewrite

\widehat{\mu}(F)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{ij\in F_{0}}v_{i}Mv_{j}^{T}}\right].

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[X_{11},\dots,X_{sr}]
be the degree-
2
polynomial given by
f(X)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in F_{0}}v_{i}Mv_{j}^{T}
. Note that
f
is multilinear since there are no self-loops in
F_{0}
. Furthermore, for any
i,k\in[s]
and
j,\ell\in[r]
, the term
X_{ij}X_{k\ell}
appears in
f
if and only if
\{i,k\}\in F_{0}
and
M_{j\ell}=1
. Thus, we know that
\textup{Coef}_{2}(f)=A_{F_{0}}\otimes M
, where
A_{F_{0}}
is the adjacency matrix of
F_{0}
. Namely, the coefficient of the term
X_{ij}X_{k\ell}
in
f
is
(A_{F_{0}})_{ik}M_{j\ell}
. Therefore, we have
\textup{rank}(\textup{Coef}_{2}(f))=\textup{rank}(M)\cdot\textup{rank}(F_{0})=r\cdot\textup{rank}(F)
since
F_{0}
is just
F
on the vertex set
[s]
. By
Lemma

2.1
, we can conclude that

\left|\widehat{\mu}(F)\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(-1)^{f(X)}\right]\right|\leqslant(1-2q^{2})^{\lfloor r\cdot\textup{rank}(F)/6\rfloor}\leqslant(1-2q^{2})^{r\cdot\textup{rank}(F)/6-1}.\qed

Now for any
a\geqslant 0
, we have by
Theorem

1.3
the number of graphs
F\subseteq\binom{U}{2}
with
\textup{rank}(F)=a
is at most
2^{sa-2}
. Combined with
3.3
and
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\textup{unif}})\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{F\neq\varnothing\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}|\widehat{\mu}(F)|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{F\neq\varnothing\subseteq\binom{U}{2}}(1-2q^{2})^{r\cdot\textup{rank}(F)/6-1}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}2^{sa-2}(1-2q^{2})^{ra/6-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}2^{sa}2^{-2q^{2}ra/6},

where the last inequality follows the fact that
1-2q^{2}\geqslant 1/4
and for all
x\geqslant 0
we have
1-x\leqslant 2^{-x}
. Note that the common ratio in the geometric series is
2^{s-2q^{2}r/6}\leqslant 2^{-s}\leqslant 1/2
since
s-2q^{2}r/6\leqslant-s\leqslant-1
. Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}2^{sa-2q^{2}ra/6}\leqslant 2^{s-2q^{2}r/6}\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}r/6}.

By an averaging argument over all possible assignments
G^{*}
to
G[W]
, we can conclude that

d_{\textup{TV}}(G_{\Delta},G(U,1/2))\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}r/6}

as desired.
∎

Now, we can prove
Theorem

1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

It suffices to prove the statement for
k=\lfloor q\sqrt{n}/100\rfloor
since
k
-vertex-minor universality is monotone decreasing in
k
. Let
G\sim G(n,p)
. For any fixed
S\subseteq[n]
with size
k
. We partition the vertices
V(G)=U\sqcup W
with
S\subseteq U
,
\left|U\right|=\lfloor q^{2}n/12\rfloor
, and
W=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\}
with
r=n-\lfloor q^{2}n/12\rfloor
. When
n
is sufficiently large, we have
r\geqslant n/2
. Therefore, we have
q^{2}r/6\geqslant q^{2}n/12\geqslant\lfloor q^{2}n/12\rfloor=s
. Let
G^{\prime}=G*w_{1}*\dots*w_{r}
. By
Lemma

3.1
, we have the total variation distance between
G^{\prime}[U]
and
G(U,1/2)
is at most
2^{-q^{2}n/12}
. Therefore, there is a coupling such that

\mathbb{P}(G^{\prime}[U]\neq G(U,1/2))\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}n/12}.

Applying
Theorem

1.1
to
U
with
\left|U\right|=\lfloor q^{2}n/12\rfloor
and pick
C=2\log_{2}(4/3)\lfloor q^{2}n/12\rfloor/k^{2}-1
. When
n
is sufficiently large, we know that
C>q^{2}n/15k^{2}
, and hence we have

\mathbb{P}(G(U,1/2)\text{ is not vertex-minor universal})\leqslant 2^{-(1+o(1))Ck^{2}/2}\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}n/40}.

Since we have

\mathbb{P}(G^{\prime}[U]\text{ is not vertex-minor universal})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(G^{\prime}[U]\neq G(U,1/2))+\mathbb{P}(G(U,1/2)\text{ is not vertex-minor universal}),

we can conclude that for
n
sufficiently large, we have

\mathbb{P}(G^{\prime}[U]\text{ is not vertex-minor universal})\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}n/12}+2^{-q^{2}n/40}\leqslant 2^{1-q^{2}n/40}.

For each
S\subseteq[n]
with size
k
, we fix a partition
V(G)=U\sqcup W
as above and we have the probability that there is a graph on vertex set
S
that is not a vertex-minor of
G
is at most
2^{1-q^{2}n/40}
. Now, we take a union bound over all possible
S
. Since there are
\binom{n}{k}\leqslant n^{k}
many subsets
S
and by assumption we have
k=\lfloor q\sqrt{n}/100\rfloor
, the number of subsets
S
is at most
n^{\lfloor q\sqrt{n}/100\rfloor}\leqslant 2^{q\sqrt{n}\log n}\leqslant 2^{q^{2}n/100}
. Therefore, by the union bound,
G
is not
k
-vertex-minor universal with probability at most
2^{q^{2}n/100}\cdot 2^{1-q^{2}n/40}\leqslant 2^{-q^{2}n/100}
for
n
sufficiently large. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Concluding remarks

In
[
1
]
, the authors also considered the universality question for pivot-minors and matroid-minors and proved analogous results to
Theorem

1.1
for
G(n,1/2)
in these settings. We believe our methods should be able to generalize those results to the setting of
G(n,p)
. We briefly outline how to adapt our strategy to those settings and discuss the extra obstruction in this section.

Since minors in binary matroids can be represented by pivot-minors of their fundamental graphs in the ordered bipartite setting, let us follow the setup in
[
1
, Section 4]
and work with ordered bipartite random graph from now on. See
[
1
]
for more details on the reduction from binary matroids to pivot minors. For a simple graph
G
and an edge
uv\in E(G)
, a pivot at
uv
, denoted by
G\times uv
, is defined to be the operation
G*u*v*u
. One can check that
G*u*v*u=G*v*u*v
, so the pivot operation does not depend on the ordering of
uv
and thus is well-defined. Equivalently, the graph
G\times uv
is obtained by taking the symmetric difference between
G
with the complete tripartite graph with parts
N(v)\cap N(u)
,
N(v)\setminus(N(u)\setminus\{u\})
, and
N(u)\setminus(N(v)\setminus\{v\})
, and then swapping the labels of
u
and
v
. An ordered bipartite graph
G=(L,R,E)
has bipartition
V(G)=L\sqcup R
and edges contained in
L\times R
. Note that for
uv\in E(G)
, we have

G\times uv\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(L\triangle\{u,v\},R\triangle\{u,v\},E^{\prime})

where
E^{\prime}
is the edges of the unordered bipartite graph
(L,R,E)\times uv
. For any ordered bipartite graph
H=(V_{L},V_{R},E(H))
with
V_{L}\cup V_{R}\subseteq V(G)
, we say that
H
is a (labeled) pivot-minor of
G
if it can be obtained on
V_{L}\cup V_{R}
via a sequence of vertex deletions and pivots. We say that ordered bipartite graph
G
is
k
-pivot-minor universal
if any ordered bipartite graph
H
on any
k
vertices of
V(G)
is a pivot minor of
G
.

Let
G(a,b,p)
denote the random ordered bipartite graph on vertex bipartition
L\sqcup R
with
|L|=a
,
|R|=b
, and edges between
L\times R
present independently with probability
p
. We believe
Lemma

3.1
can be adapted in this setting with the following strategy.

We fix disjoint subsets
U_{L}\sqcup W_{L}\subseteq L
and
U_{R}\sqcup W_{R}\subseteq R
such that
|U_{L}|=|U_{R}|=s
and
|W_{L}|=|W_{R}|=r
.
Suppose that we can find disjoint pairs
w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w_{r^{\prime}}w^{\prime}_{r^{\prime}}\in W_{L}\times W_{R}
such that
w_{i+1}w^{\prime}_{i+1}
is an edge in
G_{i-1}=G\times w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1}\times\dots\times w_{i}w^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i=0,1,\dots,r^{\prime}-1
. Then we set
G^{\prime}=G_{r^{\prime}}
to be the resulting graph, and let
W^{\prime}_{L}=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r^{\prime}}\},W^{\prime}_{R}=\{w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{r^{\prime}}\}
.

Define
G_{\Delta}=G^{\prime}[U_{L},U_{R}]\triangle G[U_{L},U_{R}]
. Let
G_{i}=G\times w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1}\times\dots\times w_{i}w^{\prime}_{i}
and
U_{L}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s_{1}}\}
,
U_{R}=\{u^{\prime}_{1},\dots,u^{\prime}_{s_{2}}\}
. Then we can define
X_{L}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{|U_{L}|\times|W^{\prime}_{R}|}
with entries
(X_{L})_{ij}
being the indicator variable of the edge
u_{i}w^{\prime}_{j}
. Similarly, define
X_{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{|U_{R}|\times|W^{\prime}_{L}|}
with entries
(X_{R})_{ij}
being the indicator variable of the edge
u^{\prime}_{i}w_{j}
. Then similar to
3.2
, we claim that there is an invertible (not necessarily symmetric) matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r^{\prime}\times r^{\prime}}
only depending on
G[W_{L},W_{R}]
such that
\textup{OffDiag}(X_{L}MX_{R}^{T})
is the adjacency matrix of
G_{\Delta}
.

Indeed, we can define
z_{i}^{L}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{U_{L}}
to be the indicator vector of the edges in
G_{i}[U_{L},w^{\prime}_{i}]
and define
z_{i}^{R}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{U_{R}}
to be the indicator vector of the edges in
G_{i}[U_{R},w_{i}]
. The
i
-th pivot changes adjacency matrix of
G_{i-1}[U_{L},U_{R}]
by exactly
z_{i}^{L}(z_{i}^{R})^{T}
. We can find upper triangular matrices
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
such that
X_{L}Q_{L}
is the matrix with columns
(z_{i}^{L})_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}
and
X_{R}Q_{R}
is the matrix with columns
(z_{i}^{R})_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}
. Taking
M=Q_{L}Q_{R}^{T}
satisfies the desired conditions. The rest of the argument should follow through, and we can upper bound the total variation distance between
G^{\prime}[U_{L},U_{R}]
and
G^{\prime}(s,s,1/2)
by
2^{-\Omega(q^{2}r^{\prime})}
where
q=\min(p,1-p)
.

Thus, in order to prove an analogous result for pivot-minor universality, it is sufficient to answer the following question.

Question 4.1
.

Let
G\sim G(r,r,p)
and
\min(p,1-p)=\Omega(\log n/\sqrt{n})
. Can we find disjoint pairs of vertices
w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w_{r^{\prime}}w^{\prime}_{r^{\prime}}
such that
w_{i+1}w^{\prime}_{i+1}
is an edge in
G\times w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1}\times\dots\times w_{i}w^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i=0,1,\dots,r^{\prime}-1
and
r^{\prime}=\Theta(r)
with high probability?

We believe that the answer to this question is affirmative since
p
is much larger than the threshold of having a perfect matching.

After we put the first version of this note on arXiv, the following solution to the question is pointed out to us by Mathieu Rundström. The answer is to this question is affirmative, and it is true that
r^{\prime}\geqslant r/2
with probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega(qr)}
, where
q=\min(p,1-p)
. This can be proved with the following observation. Let
A
be the biadjacency matrix of
G
, then the maximum possible
r^{\prime}
is at least
\gamma\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\textup{rank}(A)
. Here, we recall that
\textup{rank}(A)
denotes the rank over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. To see this observation, we can restrict
A
to an invertible
\gamma\times\gamma
submatrix
B
. Let
V_{B}
be the vertex set corresponding to the rows and columns of
B
. It is sufficient to pick
w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w_{\gamma}w^{\prime}_{\gamma}
from
V_{B}
.

We can show this by inducting on
\gamma
.
We first pick an arbitrary entry with value one, say
B_{11}=1
, and set
w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1}
to be the edge correspond to this entry. Suppose
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
\beta&B^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{1\times(\gamma-1)}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(\gamma-1)\times 1}
, and
B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(\gamma-1)\times(\gamma-1)}
. It follows that the biadjacency matrix of
(G\times w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1})[V_{B}]
equals

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&B^{\prime}+\alpha\beta\end{pmatrix}
. Note that
B^{\prime}+\alpha\beta
is invertible since

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&B^{\prime}+\alpha\beta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\beta&I_{\gamma-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
\beta&B^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&I_{\gamma-1}\end{pmatrix}

is invertible. Thus, we can find
w_{2}w^{\prime}_{2},\dots,w_{\gamma}w^{\prime}_{\gamma}
by applying the inductive hypothesis to
B^{\prime}+\alpha\beta
.

Now, it is sufficient to show that with high probability
\textup{rank}(A)\geqslant r/2
. Note that
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times r}
is a random binary matrix with i.i.d.
\textup{Ber}(p)
entries. Let
A_{i}
be the
i
-th column vector of
A
. We first bound the probability
\mathbb{P}[A_{i+1}\in\textup{span}(A_{1},\dots,A_{i})\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{i}]
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{i}=\textup{span}(A_{1},\dots,A_{i})
and suppose the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
is
d_{i}
. Given
A_{1},\dots,A_{i}
, we can find a set
I_{i}
of
d_{i}
entries such that the projection
\pi_{I_{i}}(\mathcal{V}_{i})
onto
I_{i}
is surjective. Hence, for any
v\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
, the projection
\pi_{[r]\setminus I_{i}}(v)
is uniquely determined by
\pi_{I_{i}}(v)
. Indeed, we know that there is a linear map
T
, depending on
\mathcal{V}_{i}
, such that
\pi_{[r]\setminus I_{i}}(v)=T(\pi_{I_{i}}(v))
. Thus,
A_{i+1}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
only if
\pi_{[r]\setminus I_{i}}(A_{i+1})=T(\pi_{I_{i}}(A_{i+1}))
, which happens with probability at most
(1-q)^{r-d_{i}}
since we can first expose
\pi_{I_{i}}(A_{i+1})
. Hence, we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}[A_{i+1}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{i}]\leqslant(1-q)^{r-d_{i}}.

Fix
0<\gamma_{0}<r
. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{i}
be the indicator function of the event that
A_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i-1}
and
\dim\mathcal{V}_{i-1}\leqslant\gamma_{0}
for
i=1,\dots,r
, assuming that
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{0\}
. Set
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}_{i}
to be the number of indices
i
such that
A_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i-1}
happens before the rank exceeds
\gamma_{0}
. We know that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{i}]\leqslant(1-q)^{r-d_{i}}\leqslant(1-q)^{r-\gamma_{0}}
holds for all
i=1,\dots,r
. Thus, by linearity of expectation we have
\mathbb{E}[Z]\leqslant r(1-q)^{r-\gamma_{0}}
. On the other hand, we know that
\textup{rank}(A)\leqslant\gamma_{0}
only if
Z\geqslant r-\gamma_{0}
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}[\textup{rank}(A)\leqslant\gamma_{0}]\leqslant\mathbb{P}[Z\geqslant r-\gamma_{0}]\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{r-\gamma_{0}}\leqslant\frac{r(1-q)^{r-\gamma_{0}}}{r-\gamma_{0}}.

If we set
\gamma_{0}=r/2
, then the probability is at most
2(1-q)^{r/2}=2^{-\Omega(qr)}
. This proves the claim that
r^{\prime}\geqslant r/2
with probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega(qr)}
.

From all the discussion above, we can conclude in a way similar to
Section

3
that the bipartite graph
