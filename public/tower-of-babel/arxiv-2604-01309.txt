Abstract
The extraction of cosmological information from 2-point statistics relies critically on the assumed form of their likelihood. Although a Gaussian likelihood is generally adopted, the first paper of this series ( 19 ) showed that the distribution of power-spectrum estimates exhibits non-Gaussian features at both large and small scales ( k < 0.5 ​ h / Mpc k<0.5\,h/\mathrm{Mpc} ) with their amplitude depending on survey volume, masking, and shot noise. In this work we account for this skewness in parameter inference by modelling the likelihood through an Edgeworth expansion which involves the complete skewness tensor, composed of 1-point, 2-point, and 3-point correlators. To simplify the calculations of this expansion we perform a change of basis which reduces the precision matrix to the identity. In this basis, the off-diagonal elements of the skewness tensor are consistent with zero, while the amplitude of its diagonal match the level expected for a Gaussian underlying field. We perform parameter inference with this likelihood model and find that including only the diagonal part of the skewness is sufficient, while incorporating the full skewness tensor injects noise without improving accuracy. Despite the estimated excess skewness in the original basis, the cosmological constraints remain effectively unchanged when adopting a Gaussian likelihood or considering the more complete Edgeworth expansion, with variations in the figure of merit of cosmological parameters between the two cases below 5 % 5\% . This result remains unchanged against variations of the survey volume and geometry, scale-cut, and 2-point statistic (power spectrum or correlation function). Using 10 000 10\,000 cloned Euclid large mocks based on realistic galaxy catalogues with characteristics close to future Euclid data, we find no detectable excess skewness on intermediate scales, due to the level of shot noise expected for the Euclid spectroscopic sample. We conclude that the Gaussian likelihood assumption is robust for Euclid 2-point statistics analyses in both Fourier and configuration space.

Introduction

The main summary statistic, used by modern cosmological analyses to measure cosmological parameters, is the 2-point statistics of the specific observed fields. Such statistics have been and will be used to analyse the temperature and polarisation of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) observed by
Planck

(
44
)
, the South Pole Telescope
(
5
, SPT,)
, or the Atacama Cosmology Telescope
(
39
, ACT,)
, the clustering of galaxies in three dimensions (3D), for example, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument
(
17
, DESI,)
, or galaxy weak lensing that the
Vera C. Rubin
Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(
33
, LSST,)
will observe soon. The primary probes of
Euclid
, 3D clustering and the combination of weak lensing and photometric clustering (the so-called
3\times 2
-point analysis), are also based on 2-point statistics
(
22
)
.

The extraction of cosmological information from 2-point statistics is generally performed through a likelihood-based analysis, where a key assumption has to be made on the form of the likelihood function. The most common assumption is to assume the distribution of 2-point statistics to be Gaussian. However, it is known that this distribution presents deviations from Gaussianity, especially at large scales, where the number of modes or pairs is too low to approximate the distribution to be Gaussian, but rather they are
\chi^{2}
-distributed
(
30
;
56
)
. We studied this distribution in detail, in our companion paper
(
19
, hereafter B-2025)
, where we focused on the matter power spectrum. We observed, as expected, deviations from a Gaussian distribution at large scales, but also at intermediate scales
(
11
, already observed in)
. We could link the latter to the higher-order
N
-point statistics of the density field itself. In addition, we explored a variety of setups to understand what the dominant effects are on the non-Gaussianity of the power spectrum distributions. We found that deviations from a Gaussian distribution at both large and intermediate scales were greatly enhanced when a survey window function was applied. We now want to know if these deviations from Gaussianity have an impact on cosmological constraints.

Several works in the literature have studied the limits of the Gaussian likelihood assumption. The first to do so were carried out in the context of CMB analyses and they found that incorrectly assuming a Gaussian likelihood on large scales could cause significant biases in the estimation of cosmological parameters
(
14
;
52
;
46
)
. Thus, in the past two decades, CMB analyses have used different types of non-Gaussian likelihood on both large and small scales
(
45
)
. On the side of weak lensing and galaxy clustering, some works have shown that a Gaussian likelihood assumption is sufficient
(
32
;
61
;
50
;
58
;
25
;
59
;
29
)
or has a mild impact on parameter inference
(
28
)
.

In B-2025 we have seen that the amount of non-Gaussianity in the power spectrum distribution depends on many factors, like the survey volume and the density of the galaxy sample, and will thus vary from one survey to another. In this work, we want to gauge whether the Gaussian likelihood assumption can significantly bias cosmological constraints, depending on the level of non-Gaussianity. We will particularly focus on the volume and shape of the survey considering different survey window functions with varying size and geometry. We will then estimate the level of non-Gaussianity expected in the distribution of 2-point statistics for a realistic
Euclid
spectroscopic sample. This will allow us to draw a conclusion on whether the Gaussian likelihood assumption is sufficient for the analysis of the 2-point statistics of the
Euclid
spectroscopic data.

To test the impact of the Gaussian likelihood assumption, we will compare the result of a parameter inference using a Gaussian or a non-Gaussian likelihood in a realistic case where the data have a non-Gaussian distribution. To accurately model the non-Gaussian distribution of the data, we develop a likelihood model based on an Edgeworth expansion as presented in
3
. It provides a flexible way to incorporate non-Gaussian features to the likelihood without making any assumptions about the underlying field. Following B-2025, we will focus on the skewness of the distribution as we have seen that it is the dominant non-Gaussian feature. To simplify the computation of the correction terms, we will rely on the properties of the Edgeworth expansion, which allow us to perform the likelihood analysis in a basis where the data are uncorrelated. This is equivalent to a principal component analysis, as in
38
.

As in B-2025, we will rely extensively on the
COVMOS
method
(
7
;
6
)
for the fast production of a large set of mock catalogues of the nonlinear dark matter field. We will produce
10\,000
of these simulations and cut different volumes from their periodic boxes to mimick a survey window function. We will also use
COVMOS
to clone more realistic galaxy catalogues specifically tailored to simulate the
Euclid
spectroscopic sample, the
Euclid
large mocks
(
23
, ELM,)
, based on
PINOCCHIO

(
40
)
. We are confident in using
COVMOS
simulations for this type of analysis, as we demonstrated in B-2025 that
COVMOS
could accurately model the skewness of the power spectrum distribution, up to
k=1.3\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}
.

While in B-2025 we focus on Fourier space, in the present work we also extend our analysis to configuration space. Since both the power spectrum and the 2-point correlation function (2PCF) will be used for
Euclid
data analyses, we want to gauge the validity of the Gaussian likelihood approximation for both statistics. In addition, this allows us to check for consistency between the cosmological constraints obtained from the two statistics.

In Sect.
2
we present the
COVMOS
method and the different samples of simulations that we produced for this study, as well as the 2-point statistics estimators. In Sect.
3
we present the modelling of the 2-point statistics affected by a survey window function and the skewness estimated from the simulations in each geometry. In Sect.
4
we present the non-Gaussian likelihood model that we used for parameter inference, which is based on an Edgeworth expansion together with the effect of a proper change of basis on the skewness of the power spectrum and the 2PCF. The result of the parameter inference analysis is presented in Sect.
5
. In Sect.
6
we study the skewness of the power spectrum for a more realistic case, with
Euclid
-like simulations containing galaxies rather than dark matter only. We finally conclude in Sect.
7
.

2.2
Estimators

In this section, we give details on how we estimate
2
-point statistics both in Fourier and configuration space from our sample of
COVMOS
realisations. While the case of the periodic box is straightforward, in the three other cases (S500, S200, and CONE) it is necessary to take into consideration the corresponding window functions.

Note that, since we decided to perform the analysis without redshift space distortions (RSD), we only consider the monopole of the power spectrum and 2PCF.

2.2.1
Fourier space

Throughout this article, we adopt the following convention for the spatial Cartesian Fourier transform (FT)
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}
of a function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
,

f_{\boldsymbol{k}}:=\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int f(\boldsymbol{x})\,{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\ {\rm d}^{3}x\;.

(1)

Since FTs are computed using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm, the Fourier space is assumed to be discrete and regularly sampled on a 3D grid which corresponds to the fundamental frequency,
k_{\rm F}:=2\pi/L
, associated with the bounding box of size
L=1000\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}
. By defining the density contrast field as
\delta(\boldsymbol{x}):=n(\boldsymbol{x})/\bar{n}-1
, with
n(\boldsymbol{x})
the number density at spatial position
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\bar{n}
the mean number density of the Universe, the estimator of the monopole power spectrum can be written as the averaged power in spherical Fourier shells of width
k_{\rm F}
,

\hat{P}(k):=\frac{\tilde{k}_{\rm F}^{3}}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}|\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}|^{2}\;,

(2)

where
N_{k}
is the number of independent modes in each
k
-shell,
\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i}}
the FT of the density contrast, and
\tilde{k}_{\rm F}^{3}:=(2\pi)^{3}/V
is the effective fundamental mode associated with the volume of the survey. Note that we do not consider the weights introduced by
24
in this work because the mean number density does not vary across the volume.

Without loss of generality, we consider the survey window function
W(\boldsymbol{x})
which is
1
within the survey and
0
outside. As a result, the observed density field
n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})
is related to the true density field
n(\boldsymbol{x})
through

n^{\mathrm{o}}(\boldsymbol{x})=n(\boldsymbol{x})\,W(\boldsymbol{x})\;.

(3)

By defining the observed density contrast field

\delta^{\mathrm{o}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=n^{\mathrm{o}}(\boldsymbol{x})/\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}-1\;,

(4)

with the mean density
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}:=V^{-1}\int n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\;{\rm d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}
, and taking its FT, one gets

\delta^{\mathrm{o}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\tilde{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+W_{\boldsymbol{k}}\;,

(5)

where
\tilde{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is the convolution of the true density contrast in Fourier space with the FT of the window function
W_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
,

\tilde{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=\int\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}W_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\;\mathrm{d}^{3}k^{\prime}\;.

(6)

In Fourier space, there are two different effects introduced by the survey window function. (i) The observed density contrast is biased by
W_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
(see Eq.
5
) and (ii) the density contrast field is convolved with the survey window function (see Eq.
6
). From the observed contrast density field, it is possible to remove the bias if
W_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is known. However, it is not trivial to deconvolve the survey window function. It is common practice
(
42
)

to define the convolved power spectrum
\tilde{P}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=\tilde{k}_{\rm F}^{3}\,\langle|\tilde{\delta}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}|^{2}\rangle
, which we can express as

\tilde{P}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\int P_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}|W_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}^{3}k^{\prime}\;,

(7)

where
P_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is the true 3-dimensional underlying power spectrum. In that case, if one wants to compare this estimate to a model, it is needed to forward model the effect of the convolution to the theory power spectrum.

From the above equation, it is clear that we need to know the FT of the survey window functions we consider. The case of the spherical window function centred at the origin is particularly interesting because the expression of its FT is trivial

W^{\mathrm{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=4\pi R^{3}j_{1}(kR)/(kR)\;,

(8)

where
R
is the radius of the sphere and
j_{1}
the spherical Bessel function of order
1
. For the cone geometry the formal expression of
W_{\boldsymbol{k}}
takes the form of an infinite series. As a result, in the same way as B-2025, it is more efficient to evaluate it from a random uniform distribution which has 20 times the density of the
COVMOS
catalogues. We also checked that it is equivalent and more efficient to directly assign this window function to a real space 3D grid and take its FFT.

For the estimation of the power spectra we use the
NBodyKit
2
2

2

nbodykit.readthedocs.io
software
(
31
)
. The 3D grid has a sampling parameter of
N_{\mathrm{s}}=512
, we use a piecewise cubic spline (PCS) mass assignment scheme and we apply the interlacing method
(
49
)
to reduce the effect of aliasing on small scales.

2.2.2
Configuration space

The estimation of the multipoles of the 2PCF is critical for this work. The most commonly used estimator is the Landy & Szalay
(
36
)
, which is defined as

\hat{\xi}(r,\mu)=\frac{N_{\rm R}(N_{\rm R}-1)}{N_{\rm D}(N_{\rm D}-1)}\frac{{\rm DD}(r,\mu)}{{\rm RR}(r,\mu)}-2\frac{(N_{\rm R}-1)}{N_{\rm D}}\frac{{\rm DR}(r,\mu)}{{\rm RR}(r,\mu)}+1\;,

(9)

where one needs to specify a binning in
r
(i.e. the separation between two galaxies) and in
\mu=\cos{\theta}
(being
\theta
the angle between the separation vector and a reference direction). The estimator above uses a data catalogue containing
N_{\rm D}
objects for which one wants to estimate the 2PCF and a random catalogue of
N_{\rm R}
objects following the geometry of the data catalogue. The term
{\rm DD}(r,\mu)
represents the number of pairs of objects in the data catalogue inside the bin centred on
r
and
\mu
,
{\rm RR}(r,\mu)
represents the same but for a random catalogue following the geometry of the data catalogue, and
{\rm DR}(r,\mu)
is the number of cross-pairs between the two catalogues. The usual rule is to use a random catalogue which contains at least
50
times more objects than the data catalogue, to avoid being sensitive to shot noise. In our
COVMOS
catalogues we have a number density of
0.02\,(\mathrm{Mpc}/h)^{-3}
in a box of
1\,(\mathrm{Gpc}/h)^{3}
, which means that we would need
10^{9}
random points. It has been shown that one can resort to the splitting method proposed by
35
to significantly reduce the computation time of both the cross-term
{\rm DR}(r,\mu)
and the auto-term
{\rm RR}(r,\mu)
with a minimal loss in accuracy. However, for our purpose, this would still induce a prohibitive computational cost. Indeed, we need to repeat this measurement on
10\,000
realisations for each of the four geometries at the two considered redshifts (
z=0
and
z=0.5
) for a total of
80\,000
evaluations.

In order to tackle this issue we rely on the fact that we have rather simple geometries (sphere or cone) and we designed an adapted estimator. In Appendix
A
we review the key steps allowing us to show that the estimator that we use is equivalent to the Landy & Szalay estimator but implies only to compute the pairs in the data catalogue and allows us to compute the cross-pairs between data and random catalogue with a single sum over the data catalogue itself. The gain in time is tremendous because we do not need to resort to a random catalogue at all.

Based on Eq. (
7
), one can show that the 2PCF should, in principle, be affected by the presence of the window function as

\tilde{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r})=\xi(\boldsymbol{r})\,g(\boldsymbol{r})\;,

(10)

where

g(\boldsymbol{r}):=\frac{1}{V}\int|W_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}\,{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}}\,{\rm d}^{3}k\;.

(11)

The function
g(\boldsymbol{r})
is basically the 2PCF induced by the survey geometry. This is exactly what the
\rm{RR}
term is correcting for in the Landy & Szalay estimator (see Eq.
9
). More discussion on this can be found in Appendix
A
. As a result, contrary to the power spectrum estimator, in the case of the 2PCF the survey geometry does not affect directly the measurement.

3
Two-point statistics with a survey window function

3.1
Modelling

In this section, we give the details on how we model the measured
2
-point statistics. This will be relevant for the fitting procedure presented in Sect.
5
and allows us to ensure that no bias originating from the modelling will affect the parameter inference.
Since we choose
Halofit
as our input power spectrum for the
COVMOS
realisations, in the present section we do not want to validate the model itself, but we rather want to check that we are able to handle correctly the forward modelling of the effect of the window function.

3.1.1
Fourier space

For the power spectrum there are two main effects that we need to take into account. First the fact that, in non-periodic geometries, the estimated power spectrum is not the true one but it is the window-convolved power spectrum in Eq. (
7
). Second, a high-
k
filter inherent to the
COVMOS
method that can be exactly predicted.

The high-
k
filtering of the power spectrum is a step that is needed for
COVMOS
to match the variance of the density field computed directly from the
1
-point PDF and the one estimated through the integration of the power spectrum itself. This filter can be exactly predicted and applied to the model power spectrum. We will not give more details as this is not fundamental for this work and refer the interested reader to
7
;
6
for more details.

The most simple way to convolve the model
P(k)
with the window function is to make use of the convolution theorem, stating that the FT of a product of two functions is the convolution of the FTs of these two same functions. We can then take the Hankel transform of the predicted power spectrum to get the 2PCF, then multiply this predicted monopole 2PCF by the window function monopole (the monopole of Eq.
11
),
g^{(0)}(r)
, and transform it back to Fourier space. We perform the Hankel transform with the public python package
mcfit
.
3
3

3

https://github.com/eelregit/mcfit
After some optimisation tests to obtain a fast and precise Hankel transform, we choose the integral to be performed on
2^{12}
values of
k
logarithmically spaced in the range
[10^{-3.5},\,10^{3}]\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}
.

In general, the convolution of the power spectrum with the window function involves a sum over the even multipoles of the 2PCF and those of the window function
g(\boldsymbol{r})
, as described in
62
. However, as we are working in real space, only the monopole of the 2PCF (and power spectrum) is non-zero. Formally, the model-convolved power spectrum becomes

\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)=\mathcal{H}^{-1}\left\{\mathcal{H}\left[P(k)\right]\;g^{(0)}(r)\right\}\;,

(12)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a Hankel transform, and
P(k)
is the
Halofit
power spectrum with the
COVMOS
filter applied on it.

We then just need to know the window correlation function
g^{(0)}(r)
, which for the sphere can be derived analytically from Eq. (
8
) and reads (see Appendix
B
)

g^{(0)}(r)=1-\dfrac{3}{4}\dfrac{r}{R}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{r}{2R}\right)^{3}\;.

(13)

Note that, for a spherical window,
g(\boldsymbol{r})=g^{(0)}(r)
. Indeed it only depends on the radial distance
r
as it is an isotropic geometry.

For a conoidal window it is not possible to get a fully analytical form of
g(\boldsymbol{r})
and we refer to Appendix
B
where we explain the details of our procedure allowing us to obtain the monopole of the window correlation function (along with the other multipoles for completeness).

Finally, once the theoretical prediction is convolved with the survey window function we need to subtract the integral constraint (IC) contribution. This contribution comes from the fact that we estimate the mean number density from the limited surveyed volume and not from the whole Universe in which
\langle\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle=0
. For a surveyed volume this is not true since
\delta^{\mathrm{o}}
is a sub-sample of the true density field. This gives rise to an additional term in the convolved theoretical power spectrum which dominates at low
k
. The correction can be approximated as
(
62
;
16
)

\tilde{P}^{\mathrm{IC}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\tilde{P}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\tilde{P}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\dfrac{|W_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}}{|W_{\boldsymbol{0}}|^{2}}\;,

(14)

where
\tilde{P}^{\mathrm{IC}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the model that we will use hereafter. More precisely, we will use the monopole
\tilde{P}^{\rm IC}(k)
of Eq. (
14
).

In this section, we present the estimation of the skewness of both the power spectrum and 2PCF distributions from the

n
-th realisation of the data vector
\boldsymbol{X}_{n}

P(k)
or
\xi(r)
, and defining
X_{n}^{(i)}
its
i
-th element, we estimate the reduced skewness
\hat{S}_{3}^{i}
as

\hat{S}_{3}^{i}=\frac{N_{\rm s}^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\left[X_{n}^{(i)}-\bar{X}_{i}\right]^{3}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{3}}\;,

(15)

where
\bar{X}_{i}
is the estimated average of
X^{(i)}
over the
N_{\rm s}

COVMOS

\bar{X}_{i}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm s}}X_{n}^{(i)}\;,

(16)

and its estimated standard deviation is

\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\rm s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\left[X_{n}^{i}-\bar{X}_{i}\right]^{2}}\;.

(17)

For the power spectrum we want to compare this skewness estimation to its prediction in the case of a Gaussian field:
S_{3,\mathrm{G}}=2/\sqrt{N_{k}}
(see B-2025), where
N_{k}
is the number of independent modes in the Fourier shell centred on the mode
k
. This number can be counted directly from the FFT grid or can be predicted as
N_{k}=V_{k}\,\tilde{k}_{\rm F}^{-3}/2
, where
V_{k}=4\pi k^{2}\tilde{k}_{\rm F}
is the volume of the Fourier shells.
In addition, in B-2025 we found empirically that
S_{3,\mathrm{G}}=2.2/\sqrt{N_{k}}
provides a better fit for non-periodic geometries and therefore we will consider the latter as the reference preciction in non-periodic geometries.

4
The Edgeworth expansion of the likelihood

In this section, we want to include the skewness of the distribution of
2
-point statistics measurements in the likelihood. It allows us to quantify the effect of wrongly assuming a Gaussian likelihood on parameter inference.

The Edgeworth expansion is a technique used to approximate a non-Gaussian probability distribution by perturbing a Gaussian distribution based on its higher-order cumulants, such as the skewness and the kurtosis. This approach enables us to incorporate the non-Gaussian features observed in the
2
-point statistics while still retaining some of the computational advantages of the Gaussian distribution. For this work, we follow the prescription of
3
to build our non-Gaussian likelihood model.

Let us start from the Gaussian likelihood, which can be expressed as

G(\mathbf{x},\mathsf{\lambda})=\dfrac{|\mathsf{\lambda}|^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{N/2}}\exp\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\mathsf{\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}\right]\;,

(18)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{t}
is the difference between the data vector
\boldsymbol{d}
and the theory vector
\boldsymbol{t}
, and
\mathsf{\lambda}
is the inverse of the covariance matrix, also called the precision matrix. Applying the Edgeworth expansion up to first order (i.e. only including the skewness) to the Gaussian likelihood, we get the non-Gaussian likelihood

L(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda},\mathsf{k}_{3})=G(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda})\left[1+H(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda},\mathsf{k}_{3})\right]\;.

(19)

The multivariate Edgeworth expansion
H(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda},\mathsf{k}_{3})
is defined as

H(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda},\mathsf{k}_{3})=\dfrac{1}{6}\sum_{i,\,j,\,k=1}^{N}k_{3,ijk}\,h_{ijk}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda})+...\;,

(20)

where here
N
is the dimension of the vector
\boldsymbol{x}
. The
h_{ij...n}
are the Hermite tensors, defined as

h_{i_{1}...i_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda})=(-1)^{n}\,G^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda})\,\dfrac{\partial^{n}G(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda})}{\partial{x_{i_{1}}}...\,\partial{x_{i_{n}}}}\;,

(21)

and the
\mathsf{k}_{n}
are the cumulant tensors of order
n
. For
n=3
and defining
x_{i}
as the
i
-th element of
\boldsymbol{x}
this corresponds to

k_{3,ijk}=\langle x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\;,

(22)

which leads to the skewness when
i=j=k
. Indeed, in our companion paper B-2025 we only considered the normalised diagonal of this tensor,
S_{3}=\langle x^{3}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}/\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}^{3/2}
. However, as shown by Eq. (
20
), off-diagonal elements are expected to contribute to the likelihood.

In order to simplify Eq. (
20
) we can consider a change of basis such that the precision matrix becomes the identity. We call
\mathsf{P}
the matrix that operates the change of basis, such that

\boldsymbol{y}=\mathsf{P}\,\boldsymbol{x}\;,

(23)

is a random variable with unit variance. We call this new basis the uncorrelated basis, as its covariance and precision matrix are the identity. This is very similar to what was done by
38
in the context of the weak lensing likelihood in configuration space.

In the uncorrelated basis, the first order of the Edgeworth expansion reads

\begin{array}[]{rl}&H(\boldsymbol{x},\mathsf{\lambda},\mathsf{k}_{3})=\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\langle y_{i}^{3}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\,H_{3}(y_{i})\\
&\\
&\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j>i}\,\left[\langle y_{i}^{2}y_{j}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\,H_{2}(y_{i})H_{1}(y_{j})+\langle y_{i}y_{j}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\,H_{1}(y_{i})H_{2}(y_{j})\right]\\
&\\
&\displaystyle+\sum_{k>j>i}\,\langle y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\,H_{1}(y_{i})H_{1}(y_{j})H_{1}(y_{k})\;,\end{array}

(24)

where the
H_{n}
are the Hermite polynomials of order
n
. We explicitly split Eq. (
24
) in the three different sums involving the three different types of correlators: the 1-point
\langle y_{i}^{3}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
, 2-point
\langle y_{i}^{2}y_{j}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
(and
\langle y_{i}y_{j}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
), and 3-point
\langle y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
correlators. One can notice that Eq. (
24
) is not positive definite and its amplitude can potentially be higher than unity when the skweness becomes important. This, might cause the likelihood expression to be negative, we thus checked that this is not happening in the various cases that we report in the present work.

The third-order
1
-point correlator
\langle y_{i}^{3}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
is the reduced skewness
S_{3}
in the uncorrelated basis (i.e. after applying the linear transformation Eq.
23
). Therefore, it is interesting to estimate the
3
-point correlators
S_{3}^{ijk}
in both basis. We use the estimator

\hat{S}_{3}^{ijk}=\dfrac{1}{N_{\rm s}}\,\dfrac{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm s}}\,(X_{n}^{(i)}-\bar{X}_{i})\,(X_{n}^{(j)}-\bar{X}_{j})\,(X_{n}^{(k)}-\bar{X}_{k})}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}\,\hat{\sigma}_{j}\,\hat{\sigma}_{k}}\;.

(25)

In Fig.
5
, each upper left triangle displays the
2
-point correlators
S_{3}^{ijj}
(for which two of the three vector elements are the same) in the original basis both for the power spectrum (left) and 2PCF (right). It shows that on top of having non-zero diagonal elements (that are also shown in Figs.
3
and
4
) there is a large fraction of
2
-point correlators which exhibits a non-negligible amplitude. We also estimated the
3
-point correlators, and their amplitude follows the same trend.

As we designed the non-Gaussian likelihood model to be used in the uncorrelated basis we also estimate the skewness tensor in that basis. This is displayed in the lower-right triangle of each panel in Fig.
5
. One can notice that, surprisingly, when estimating the
2
-point correlators in the uncorrelated basis, the non-Gaussian contributions are reduced to a level compatible with the noise in the estimator. They fluctuate around zero with a standard deviation of
0.01
. The exact same drastic reduction happens when considering the
3
-point correlators.

However, there are data elements that remain characterised by a significant amount of

However, it appears that each row of the transformation matrix
\mathsf{P}
is highly peaked. So we use the index of the element at the position of that peak to assign the corresponding scale in the uncorrelated basis. Figure

This means that only the large-scale modes keep carrying on a significant skewness contribution. For the sake of clarity, we only represent the S200 geometry, but we checked that all other geometries exhibit the same behaviour.

6
Euclid
context

The simulations that we explored up to now and in B-2025 were very useful because they allowed us to easily explore different settings and find the ones that were maximising the skewness. However these simulations are far from the real
Euclid
data we will get, because they are composed of dark matter particles and not of galaxies but also in terms of volume, shot noise, and redshift distribution. We thus wish to look at what the 2-point statistics distribution would look like in a more realistic context.

For this we consider the ELM
(
23
)
, a set of fast simulations generated with
PINOCCHIO

(
40
)
which have characteristics close to the spectroscopic galaxy sample we will get from
Euclid

(
22
)
. These simulations have the geometry of a cone that has a half aperture angle of
30$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
and span a redshift range going from 0.9 to 1.8 divided into four redshift bins. The redshift distribution and the mean density of the simulated galaxies match the ones expected for
Euclid
’s
{\rm H}_{\alpha}
emission line galaxies. We only consider the lowest redshift bin, with edges
0.9<z<1.1
, to maximise the skewness of the sample (cf. B-2025).

We have only
1000
of these simulations which is not enough to get a precise estimation of
S_{3}
. We thus clone these 1000 ELM with
COVMOS
to get a larger set of
10\,000
realisations in both real and redshift space. All the details of the cloning procedure are described in Appendix
C
.

From these
10\,000
clones of the ELM we can now estimate the skewness of the galaxy power spectrum distribution in both real and redshift space, as shown in Fig.
12
. We can see that from these simulations we do not detect any significant amount of skewness at high
k
, so that the estimated skewness of the monopole follows the expectation in the case of a Gaussian underlying field. Here we do not show the prediction for the multipoles in redshift space, but they can be predicted and we show them in B-2025.

This absence of the high-
k
skewness is mainly due to the fact that the density in the ELM – which is close to what we expect for
Euclid
– is much smaller than the density we have in the
COVMOS
dark matter simulations, meaning that the shot noise is higher. We saw in B-2025 that the shot noise attenuates the skewness on small scales because at these scales the power spectrum distribution is affected by a Poissonian noise. In addition, the type of tracers of the matter field is expected to play a role. Since we have shown in B-2025 that the skewness depends on the ratio between the penta-spectrum (
6
-point correlation function in Fourier space) and the power spectrum it becomes clear that changing the multi-point distribution of the density field, would change the expected level of skewness. Still, it is not obvious to predict that a biased tracer of the matter would decrease the expected skewness because this is highly dependent on the mapping between matter and the considered tracer.

One can notice that, the angular mask considered in the current ELM is an over simplification of what will eventually be the
Euclid
angular footprint on the sky. However, increasing the volume reduces the skewness of the distribution of two-point statistics. Thus, despite the fact that the expected
Euclid
footprint will eventually be more complicated, we can guess that the skewness cannot exceed the level of the studied S200 geometry.

As a result, in a more realistic setting like the one presented in the current section, only the large-scale skewness can be measured. This shows that, for the considered tracers of the matter field that
Euclid
is targeting, we do not detect any excess of skewness at high
k
. The level of skewness we find here is thus similar to the level of skewness that is introduced in the Edgeworth likelihood for the parameter inference we presented in the previous section. We find this level of skewness to have no impact on cosmological parameter constraints.

7
Conclusions

In this second paper of the series, we investigated the impact of the non-Gaussianity of the likelihood on cosmological parameter inference. While in the companion paper B-2025 we studied in detail the origin of the non-Gaussian features in the distribution of 2-point statistics, we have here quantified their consequences for cosmological analyses relying on the assumption of a Gaussian likelihood.

To this end, we produced
10\,000

COVMOS
realisations based on the
DEMNUni-Cov
simulations, adopting a
Halofit
power spectrum as input, and designed four different survey geometries (BOX, S200, S500, and CONE) to mimic the impact of the survey window function. We developed a fast estimator of the 2PCF that operates in both spherical and cone-like geometries without requiring random catalogues, and measured both the power spectrum and 2PCF from all realisations at two redshifts,
z=0
and
z=0.5
(thus maximising the excess of skewness) .

We first quantified the scales over which unbiased cosmological constraints can be recovered from these 2-point statistics, properly accounting for the effects of the survey mask and the IC. We then looked at the estimated skewness from all the measurements and found, as expected, an equivalent trend of the skewness with scales and survey volume in Fourier and configuration space. As already observed in B-2025 for the power spectrum, we confirmed that reducing the effective survey volume amplifies both the overall level of skewness and the excess skewness with respect to the case of a Gaussian field observed on small scales (around
k=0.5\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}
), for both the power spectrum and the 2PCF.

To model the likelihood of 2-point statistics, we adopted an Edgeworth expansion including the skewness of the distribution, following
3
. This expansion involves the complete skewness tensor where the diagonal is what we usually call the skewness. To simplify the calculation of the terms of this expansion, we perform a change of basis that transforms the precision matrix into the identity matrix. We then estimated the full skewness tensor in both the original and the new uncorrelated basis. In the original basis we found significant non-diagonal elements in the skewness tensor, which would not be present if the underlying field was Gaussian. However, in the uncorrelated basis these non-diagonal elements are reduced to a level compatible with Gaussian noise fluctuating around 0 with a standard deviation of 0.01. In this basis only the large scale non-Gaussianity is still present in the distribution of 2pt statistics, as if the underlying field was Gaussian.

To perform parameter inference using the Edgeworth expansion we thus considered two scenarios: one in which only the 1-point correlator (the diagonal elements of the skewness tensor) is included, and another accounting for the full skewness tensor. We found that including the full tensor introduces noise in the inference process due to the limited precision of its estimation, analogously to what happens when using a noisy covariance matrix. Consequently, we restricted our final analysis to the first scenario.

Within this framework, we showed that – even in cases with high measured skewness – the cosmological constraints are remarkably insensitive to the Gaussian assumption for the likelihood: the FoM changes by less than
5
%, regardless of the amount of non-Gaussianity. In addition, we have shown that the shift of the contours are negligible. These results stay unchanged when varying the scales included in the analysis or when restricting the parameter space with a BBN prior. This robustness can be understood in light of the level of skewness that was observed in the uncorrelated basis. These results are in line with
38
who performed a similar study in the context of the likelihood of weak lensing 2-point statistics. However, in that work, they had only considered the diagonal of the skewness tensor in the Edgeworth expansion. Our results are also in good agreement with
28
who found that for the power spectrum analysis of BOSS the cosmological parameters are not affected by the Likelihood non-Gaussianity, except for
f\sigma_{8}
which exhibits a shift of
-0.44\sigma
. Being in real space to maximise the skewness, we did not consider this parameter in our work.

Finally, to know whether these conclusions would apply in a more realistic
Euclid
context, we applied a cloning procedure to
Euclid
realistic galaxy mock catalogues, the ELM. From the
1000
available mocks we could estimate the PDF, the power spectrum and the velocity dispersion of the galaxy field, which allowed us to use
COVMOS
to produce
10\,000
clones of the ELM. The estimated skewness from these more realistic mocks is compatible with the one expected for a Gaussian field. This is mainly due to the fact that the level of shot noise present in these mocks – which is close to what we will get with real
Euclid
data – is high enough to dilute the non-Gaussian features on intermediate scales, such that only large scales non-Gaussianity remains This effect of the shot noise was observed in B-2025. The skewness measured in these
Euclid
-like mocks is therefore comparable to the level included in our Edgeworth likelihood analysis, where we found no impact on the inferred cosmological parameters. Note that, since the skewness of the distribution of two-point statistics tends to be reduced when increasing the volume, the presented results are conservative.

Overall, we conclude that for
Euclid
-like analyses based on 2-point statistics – whether in Fourier or configuration space – the Gaussian likelihood assumption is well justified. The small level of residual non-Gaussianity has a negligible effect on the recovered cosmological parameters and their uncertainties. This validates the use of a Gaussian likelihood in the forthcoming
Euclid
cosmological analyses, as implemented in the
Euclid
likelihood code CLOE
(
20
, Cosmology Likelihood for Observables in
Euclid
,)
.

Appendix A
2-point correlation estimator

In this appendix we review the Landy & Szalay estimator for the
2PCF. In practice we will show that for
specific survey geometries one can predict not only the Random-Random pair
counts but also the Data-Random. This, induces a considerable gain in the time
required to estimate the 2PCF from a galaxy catalogue. This is particularly relevant when estimating the covariance matrix of the Landy & Szalay estimator from a large
sample (
\sim\!\!10\,000
) of realisations.

In the following, we will focus on estimating the multipoles
\xi^{(\ell,m)}(r)
of the
2PCF
\xi(\boldsymbol{r})
. Indeed, without lack of
generality the 2PCF can be decomposed as a spherical harmonics
expansion,

\xi(\boldsymbol{r})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\xi^{(\ell,m)}(r)\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\;,

(31)

where
\theta
,
\phi
and
r
define the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}

in spherical coordinates and the
Y_{\ell m}
are the spherical harmonic
functions normalised such that their orthogonal property satisfies

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\,Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\;{\rm d}^{2}\Omega=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{K}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}^{K}\;,

(32)

where
{\rm d}^{2}\Omega
is the differential element associated with the solid angle. With this choice of normalisation for the spherical harmonics, when
m=0
they reduce to the Legendre polynomials
{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)
of order
\ell
which is useful to map the spherical
harmonics decomposition into the more usual Legendre decomposition.
Using the orthogonality property in Eq. (
32
) one can show that the
coefficients
\xi^{(\ell,m)}
of the harmonic decomposition can be
obtained from

\xi^{(\ell,m)}=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\int\xi(\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\;{\rm d}^{2}\Omega\;.

(33)

Let us start with the most obvious estimator that one can derive from Eq. (
33
) which assumes ergodicity.
Since the 2PCF is defined as
\xi(\boldsymbol{r}):=\langle\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\rangle
one could say that for a given configuration
\boldsymbol{r}
we can estimate the 2PCF by simply taking the volume average of the product of two density contrast at separation
\boldsymbol{r}
. However, one must take care of performing this volume average within a volume containing always a pair of density contrasts. This means that this effective volume is smaller than the total volume
V
and it will depend on the configuration of the separation vector. Thus, let us define this volume as
V(\boldsymbol{r})
. This effective volume is simply the volume intersection between the geometry and its replication but translated by vector
\boldsymbol{r}
. Indeed one can define
V(\boldsymbol{r}):=\int W(\boldsymbol{x})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}
.
Based on the definition of the observed number density
n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})
(see Eq.
3
), the mean number density
\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}
and the observed density contrast (see Eq.
4
) one can define the following estimator for the 2PCF as

\hat{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r}):=\frac{1}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}\boldsymbol{x}\;.

(34)

From that simple definition one can immediately deduce an estimator for the harmonic coefficients,

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}:=\frac{2\ell+1}{v}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(35)

where the second average is made over a spherical shell within
r_{\rm min}
and
r_{\rm max}
, thus
v=4\pi/3\,(r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3})
. From Eq. (
35
) one can actually get to the Landy & Szalay estimator and this is what we show in the following. Indeed, from the definition of the density contrast one can expand the integrand of Eq. (
35
) as

\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\delta^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})=n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})-\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}\left[n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})+n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\right]+\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}\;.

(36)

It follows that one can split Eq. (
35
) into four contributions as

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}=\hat{I}_{1}-\hat{I}_{2}-\hat{I}_{3}+\hat{I}_{4}\;.

(37)

The last contribution
\hat{I}_{4}
can be trivially computed indeed,

\hat{I}_{4}:=\frac{2\ell+1}{v}\int_{v}\frac{Y^{*}_{\ell m}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\;\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(38)

for which the integral over volume
V(\boldsymbol{r})
leads to a simple integral over the shell
v
, such that

\hat{I}_{4}=\frac{2\ell+1}{v}\int_{v}\ r^{2}Y^{*}_{\ell m}\,Y_{00}\;{\rm d}^{2}\Omega\,{\rm d}r\;.

(39)

Since the volume of the shell is
v=4\pi/3\left(r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3}\right)
and using the orthogonality relation in Eq. (
32
), it turns out that
\hat{I}_{4}=\delta_{\ell 0}^{\rm K}\delta_{m0}^{\rm K}
which is
1
only if
\ell
and
m
are zero (i.e only for the monopole), so that
\hat{I}_{4}
is null for any other multipole.

In order to make an explicit calculation for the three other terms
\hat{I}_{1}
,
\hat{I}_{2}
, and
\hat{I}_{3}
we can further assume that we are dealing with a catalogue of
N
objects thus the density field can be represented by a Dirac sum such that
n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
. It can be substituted in Eq. (
37
) to explicitly compute the three missing terms. The first term is defined as

\hat{I}_{1}:=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\,\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(40)

which leads to

\hat{I}_{1}=\dfrac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}}\,\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\,\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\!\!\!\!\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\,.

(41)

In the above equation one can first integrate over
V(\boldsymbol{r})
and then integrate over the shell which leads to

\hat{I}_{1}=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta_{ij},\phi_{ij})}{V(\boldsymbol{r}_{ij})}\Theta(r_{ij})\;,

(42)

where we introduced the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
\theta_{ij}
,
\phi_{ij}
, and
r_{ij}
are the spherical coordinates of the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}_{ij}
and the function
\Theta
is null except when
r_{ij}
is inside the considered shell in which case it is
1
. Finally, by multiplying by
V
inside the sum and dividing by
V
in front, the estimator can be expressed as a weighted sum over the pairs of objects that belong to the shell,

\hat{I}_{1}=\frac{2\ell+1}{vN\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\sum_{ij}w_{ij}\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta_{ij},\phi_{ij})\,\Theta(r_{ij})\;.

(43)

The weight
w_{ij}:=V/V(\boldsymbol{r}_{ij})
can be seen as a correction due to the fact that since there is a finite geometry not all the pairs have the same occurrence probability. Indeed, given a separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
we define a region of exclusion in which one cannot find a counter part with orientation
\boldsymbol{r}
because it will be systematically outside the survey volume. Yet, another way of seeing this is from a Monte Carlo integration point of view in which the weight to be applied is the inverse probability distribution of the sampled multidimensional function. Thus, we can finally express the result as

\hat{I}_{1}=\frac{{\rm DD}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}\;,

(44)

where
{\rm DD}^{(\ell,m)}:=(2\ell+1)\,\sum_{ij}w_{ij}\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}\,(\theta_{ij},\phi_{ij})\,\Theta(r_{ij})
and
{\rm RR}:=4\pi N^{2}\,\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}\,(r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3})
are respectively the spherical harmonic decomposition of the weighted pairs inside the shell and the corresponding expected number of pairs in a random catalogue if there were no survey boundaries. Note that if one neglects the wide angle effects in the survey (distant observer approximation), which consists in assuming a single line-of-sight across the survey, then each pair will contribute twice to the multipoles (
i
and
j
can be reversed). Instead, one could use the above equation to estimate odd-order multipoles without assuming that each pairs are contributing in a symetric way, in that case the angle
\theta_{ij}
and
\phi_{ij}
should be considered as being the angular coordinates of the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}_{ij}
in a local spherical frame centred on position
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. In such configuration one should double count each pair because they won’t contribute in the same way to the multipoles.

Regarding terms
\hat{I}_{2}
and
\hat{I}_{3}
defined respectively as

\hat{I}_{2}:=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(45)

and

\hat{I}_{3}:=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\,\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(46)

one can notice that they are related one to another. Indeed, if one defines the number of objects
N_{\rm in}
inside volume
V(\boldsymbol{r})
one can compute it as

N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r}):=\int_{V(\boldsymbol{r})}n^{\rm o}(\boldsymbol{x})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\;.

(47)

Introducing the window function
W(\boldsymbol{x})
(
1
inside the survey and
0
outside) one can show that

N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r})=\int n(\boldsymbol{x})\,W(\boldsymbol{x})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\;,

(48)

where the integral is made over an infinite domain. Thus one can change integration variables to
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r}
, such that

N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r})=\int n(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\;{\rm d}^{3}x^{\prime}\;.

(49)

As a result one can express

\hat{I}_{2}=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\,N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(50)

and

\hat{I}_{3}=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\,N_{\rm in}(-\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;.

(51)

Note that in the symmetric case of the plane parallel approximation one can show that
\hat{I}_{2}=\hat{I}_{3}
because the sum is made over all possible orientation within the shell.

Be
f(\boldsymbol{r})
the average density inside the volume
V(\boldsymbol{r})
, thus
f(\boldsymbol{r}):=N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r})/V(\boldsymbol{r})
.
One can express
\hat{I}_{2}
and
\hat{I}_{3}
with respect to their spherical harmonic decompositions,

\hat{I}_{2}=\frac{3}{(r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3})\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}r^{2}f^{(\ell,m)}(r)\;{\rm d}r\;.

(52)

Again this can be further simplified when considering a catalogue of objects. Indeed, we get

\hat{I}_{2}=\frac{2\ell+1}{v\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{v}\frac{Y_{\ell m}^{*}}{V(\boldsymbol{r})}\int_{V}\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(53)

in which one can compute the integral over
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
. Then, defining

f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\dfrac{(2\ell+1)}{v}\int_{v}w(\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}\,W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(54)

one obtains

\hat{I}_{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\;,

(55)

which means that each point in the catalogue is contributing a certain amount to the various scales. And this contribution depends on the multipole considered. Once the weight function
f_{\ell m}
is known, then it is a matter of computing a simple sum over the catalogue of objects.
That term can indeed be related to the Data-Random term in the Landy & Szalay estimator. After some calculations one can show that

\hat{I}_{2}=\dfrac{N_{\rm R}^{2}}{N_{\rm R}N}\dfrac{{\rm DR}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}\;.

(56)

As a result, computing the weight
f_{\ell m}
allows one to extract in a very efficient way the cross counts between Data and Randoms. Finally, it is convenient to write the estimator of the multipoles of the 2PCF in a more standard way,

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}=\frac{{\rm DD}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}-2\frac{{\rm DR}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}+\delta_{\ell 0}^{\rm K}\delta_{m0}^{\rm K}\;,

(57)

where

{\rm DR}^{(\ell,m)}:=\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}\,(2\ell+1)\int_{v}N_{\rm in}(\boldsymbol{r})\,w(\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;.

(58)

The above expression can be directly computed from a single weighted sum over the catalogue as

{\rm DR}^{(\ell,m)}=\hat{I}_{2}\,{\rm RR}=4\pi/3\;\bar{n}_{\mathrm{V}}(r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3})\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\;.

(59)

In addition, one can further re-write the estimator as

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}=\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}_{\rm N}-2\,\hat{\xi}_{\rm X}^{(\ell,m)}\;,

(60)

where
\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}_{\rm N}
is the natural estimator for the auto-correlation and
\hat{\xi}_{\rm X}^{(\ell,m)}
is the natural estimator for the cross-correlation between Data and Randoms. Indeed,

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}_{\rm N}:=\frac{{\rm DD}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}-\delta_{\ell 0}^{\rm K}\delta_{m0}^{\rm K}\;,

(61)

and

\hat{\xi}^{(\ell,m)}_{\rm X}:=\frac{{\rm DR}^{(\ell,m)}}{{\rm RR}}-\delta_{\ell 0}^{\rm K}\delta_{m0}^{\rm K}\;.

(62)

One can notice that despite the fact that the estimator in Eq. (
57
) has a very similar form as the Landy & Szalay estimator, there is an interesting fundamental difference in the way the multipoles are estimated. Indeed, usually, in order to compute the multipoles of the Legendre expansion of the 2PCF one defines a binning in separation
r
(same as we do) and an additional binning in
\mu=\cos(\theta)
needs to be set up. The general problem in setting that binning is that it has to be thin enough to evaluate the angular integration without introducing too much errors but it cannot be too thin because the number of random pairs needs to be large enough in order to get a meaningful value which will not propagate noise in the angular integration. With the estimator in Eq. (
57
), we do not need to introduce an angular binning. Instead, we use the distribution of the pairs themselves to estimate the angular average as one would do in a Monte Carlo integration.

Note that if one wants to evaluate the odd multipoles by dropping the distant observer approximation, it is then necessary to keep both
\hat{I}_{2}
and
\hat{I}_{3}
which will not be equal any more.

Appendix B
Specific geometries

We show how to compute the relevant quantities
V(\boldsymbol{r})=Vg(\boldsymbol{r})
for the Data-Data pairs and
f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x})
for the Data-Random pairs necessary to apply our estimator of the 2PCF in the case of the sphere and the cone in the plane-parallel approximation.

B.1
Sphere

Let us start with the definition

g(\boldsymbol{r}):=\frac{1}{V}\int W(\boldsymbol{x})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}x\;.

(63)

This is the volume of the intersection between two spheres of radius
R
separated by the vector
\boldsymbol{r}
normalised with respect to the volume of the sphere. This means that it depends only on the modulus of
r=|\boldsymbol{r}|
, the separation vector. Thus, only a monopole
g^{(0)}(r)
is expected. This function can be computed either by directly integrating in Eq. (
63
) or by going to Fourier space. It leads anyway to

g^{(0)}(r)=1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{r}{R}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r}{2R}\right)^{3}

(64)

if
r<2R
and
g^{(0)}(r)=0
else. This condition is trivially explained by the fact that if the distance between the two spheres of radius
R
is greater than
2R
then their intersection is empty.

This is enough to compute the natural estimator (i.e. Eq.
61
). However, when computing the Landy & Szalay estimator, the next elements that are required are the weights that need to be applied when computing the Data-Random (see Eq.
59
). That weighting function should depend on the considered position
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
of each individual data point, on the considered multipole and on the separation. When considering the monopole it is straightforward (from consideration about the spherical symmetry of the problem) to show that the weights to be used are depending only on the distance
x
of the data point from the centre of the sphere. In general,

f_{R}^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{2\ell+1}{v}\int_{v}w(\boldsymbol{r})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(65)

where
\theta
and
\phi
are the angular coordinates of the separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
. In practice it is more convenient to split the angular summation from the radial one using the auxiliary function
h^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x},r)
defined such that

f_{R}^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{3}{r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3}}\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}r^{2}\,h^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x},r)\;{\rm d}r\;.

(66)

This way Eq. (
66
) can be evaluated with a great accuracy from a Gauss–Legendre quadrature of order
N_{\rm GL}
as soon as the spherical expansion
h^{(\ell m)}
is know for each data point at position
\boldsymbol{x}
. From the above definition one can express
h^{(\ell m)}
as

h^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x},r)=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}w(\boldsymbol{r})\,W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\;{\rm d}^{2}\Omega\;,

(67)

which might not always (i.e. for any window function) be computed analytically. However, in the case of a sphere, since the weighting function
w
depends only on the modulus
r
of the separation, it reduces to compute the spherical harmonic expansion
W^{(\ell m)}
of the window function
W
but shifted by vector
\boldsymbol{x}
. Thus,
h^{(\ell m)}=w(r)\,W^{(\ell m)}
, for which the monopole will depend only on the distance

from the Data point to the centre of the sphere and can be straightforwardly calculated,

\dfrac{1}{4xr}\,\left[R^{2}-(r-x)^{2}\right]&{\rm else}\;.\end{array}\right.

(68)

In the case of the spherical window function one can also predict the needed weight for any spherical harmonics of the Data-Random. By defining
\nu:=(R^{2}-x^{2}-r^{2})(2xr)^{-1}

\ell\neq 0

W^{(\ell m)}(\boldsymbol{x},r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\theta_{x},\phi_{x})\,\dfrac{1}{2}\,\left\{{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell+1}(\nu)-{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell-1}(\nu)\right\}&{\rm if}\,|\nu|<1\;,\\
0&{\rm else}\;,\end{array}\right.

(69)

where
x
,
\theta_{x}
, and

are the spherical coordinates of the point
\boldsymbol{x}

As in the case of the sphere it is necessary to evaluate the intersection volume between two identical cones of aperture
\alpha
and truncated between
R_{\rm min}
and
R_{\rm max}
from the vertex of the cone. The separation vector
\boldsymbol{r}
is applied between the vertices of the two cones and the axis
\boldsymbol{e}_{\rm z}
of the cones remain parallel. Thus the axial symmetry is preserved, such that
g(\boldsymbol{r})=g(r,\mu)
where
\mu=\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\rm z}/r
.

Depending on
r
and
\mu
there are several cases that can appear but within all those cases the volume of the intersection can be computed by evaluating an integral like

V(r,\mu)=\int_{z_{\rm a}}^{z_{\rm b}}{\mathcal{S}}(z)\;{\rm d}z,

(70)

where the function
{\mathcal{S}}(z)
represents the surface of the intersection between two disks of radius
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
separated by distance
\lambda
. The distance between the two disks is fixed by the distance between the two axes of the cones, thus
\lambda=r\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}
and does not depend on the integration variable
z
. Instead, both
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
will be functions of
r
,
\mu
and
z
. The disk intersection can be computed straightforwardly according to various cases

{\mathcal{S}}_{12}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\pi\;{\rm min}[r_{1}^{2},r_{2}^{2}]&{\rm if}&\lambda<|r_{1}-r_{2}|\;,\\
\theta_{1}r_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}r_{2}^{2}-\lambda r_{2}\sin(\theta_{2})&{\rm if}&|r_{1}-r_{2}|\leq\lambda\leq r_{1}+r_{2}\;,\\
0&{\rm if}&\lambda>r_{1}+r_{2}\;,\end{array}\right.

(71)

where
\cos\theta_{1}:=(\lambda^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2})(2\lambda r_{2})^{-1}
and
\cos\theta_{2}:=(\lambda^{2}+r_{1}^{2}-r_{2}^{2})(2\lambda r_{1})^{-1}
. Notice that, despite the fact that Eq. (
71
) looks asymmetric in
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, since
r_{1}\sin\theta_{1}=r_{2}\sin\theta_{2}
it turns out that the symmetry is preserved. In practice, there are some cases for which we need to evaluate the intersection between two rings of external radii
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
and internal radii
r_{1}^{\prime}
and
r_{2}^{\prime}
. In such cases the full intersection can be trivially obtained as
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{12}-{\mathcal{S}}_{1^{\prime}2}-{\mathcal{S}}_{12^{\prime}}+{\mathcal{S}}_{1^{\prime}2^{\prime}}
.

The second important step is to be able to compute the cross-counts between the data catalogue and a random one. In order to maintain the numerical efficiency, this needs to be done without explicitly computing them using a random catalogue.
The idea is to use Eqs. (
55
) and (
59
) in order to compute the
{\rm DR}^{(\ell m)}/{\rm RR}
term by running a simple sum over the data catalogue. However that would require to compute the explicit expression of the weight
f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
which needs to be applied at each object position
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
in the data catalogue.
Indeed,

f_{\ell m}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{(2\ell+1)}{v}\int_{v}w(\boldsymbol{r})\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}\,W(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{3}r\;,

(72)

which can be written in terms of the radial function
h^{(\ell)}(r,\boldsymbol{x}_{i}):=h^{(\ell\,0)}(r,\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
as

f_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\frac{3}{r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3}}\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}r^{2}\,h^{(\ell)}(r,\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\;{\rm d}r\;,

(73)

where we focus on evaluating
m=0
multipoles and even values of
\ell

h^{(\ell)}(r,\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}(-1)^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}w(\boldsymbol{r})\,{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(\mu)\,W(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r})\;{\rm d}^{2}\Omega\;,

(74)

where we exchanged
\boldsymbol{r}
into
-\boldsymbol{r}
. This means that the integrand of Eq. (
74
) can be null (due to the function
W
) if the vector
\boldsymbol{y}:=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r}
is outside the observed cone. This is also allowing us to compute the integral over the angle
\phi
which will take different values according to position
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
on the considered data point.
Let us define

G(r,\mu,x,\theta_{x}):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}W(\boldsymbol{y})\;{\rm d}\phi\;,

(75)

where
r:=|\boldsymbol{r}|
,
\mu:=\cos\theta
,
x:=|\boldsymbol{x}|
and
\theta_{x}
is the azimutal polar angle corresponding to vector
\boldsymbol{x}
. Given the simplicity of the window function
W
which is unity if
\boldsymbol{y}
is inside the cone-like geometry one can say that there are three conditions to be satisfied,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{{\rm max}}\;,

(76)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle R_{{\rm min}}\;,

(77)

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{y}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\cos\alpha\;,

(78)

where
\theta_{y}
is the azimutal polar angle of vector
\boldsymbol{y}
and
y:=|\boldsymbol{y}|
. In the following we define
\nu:=\cos\alpha
. Conditions (
76
) and (
77
) can be expressed in terms of restrictions on the angle
\phi
. Indeed,
y^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}+2xr\cos\gamma
, where
\gamma
is the angle between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
. Thus
\cos\gamma=\sin\theta_{x}\,\sin\theta\,\cos(\phi-\phi_{x})+\cos\theta_{x}\,\cos\theta
.

Introducing the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{R_{\max}^{2}-x^{2}-r^{2}}{2xr}\;,

(79)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{R_{\min}^{2}-x^{2}-r^{2}}{2xr}\;,

(80)

one can show that conditions (
76
) and (
77
) imply that

\frac{\lambda_{\min}-\mu_{x}\,\mu}{\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{x}}\leq\cos(\phi-\phi_{x})\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\mu_{x}\,\mu}{\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{x}}\;.

(81)

Introducing the functions

\displaystyle l_{\min}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\min}-\mu_{x}\,\mu}{\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{x}}\;,

(82)

\displaystyle l_{\max}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\mu_{x}\,\mu}{\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{x}}\;,

(83)

one can see that depending on the values of
l_{\min}
and
l_{\max}
one will get different values for

:

\begin{array}[]{cccl}l_{\min}<-1&{\rm and}&l_{\max}>1&G=2\pi\;,\\
l_{\min}>1&{\rm or}&l_{\max}<-1&G=0\;,\\
l_{\min}\leq-1&{\rm and}&|l_{\max}|\leq 1&G=2\left[\pi-{\rm acos}(l_{\max})\right]\;,\\
|l_{\min}|\leq 1&{\rm and}&l_{\max}\geq 1&G=2\,{\rm acos}(l_{\min})\;,\\
|l_{\min}|\leq 1&{\rm and}&|l_{\max}|\leq 1&G=2\left[{\rm acos}(l_{\min})-{\rm acos}(l_{\max})\right].\end{array}

However one must also pay attention to condition (
78
), which can be written as

y\leq\frac{x\cos\theta_{x}+r\cos\theta}{\cos\alpha}\;.

(84)

This is allowing us to introduce a new function

l_{\max}^{*}:=\frac{\lambda\left(\dfrac{x\mu_{x}+r\mu}{\nu}\right)-\mu\mu_{x}}{\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{x}}\,,

(85)

where
\lambda(u):=(u^{2}-x^{2}-r^{2})(2xr)^{-1}
. That means that in the above set of conditions one needs to substitute
l_{\max}
by
l_{\max}^{*}
if
l_{\max}^{*}<l_{\max}
. In addition, if
l_{\min}<l_{\max}^{*}
then

because contrary to the fact that

is guaranteed by the fact that

there is no guaranty that the condition

is satisfied.

From those various cases one can guess that despite the fact that
G
will be continuous it will be smooth only on pieces of intervals. This prevents us from using a Gauss–Legendre quadrature or any other usual integration methods for computing the weights for each single object positions
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. However, one can guess that if the sum over objects is made before performing the integral in
\mu
, the resulting function will appear much smoother. This piece-wise continuous function will be added randomly over the survey geometry. Thus, one can introduce the function

{\mathcal{G}}(r,\mu):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G(r,\mu,x_{i},\theta_{x_{i}})\;,

(86)

and formally express the expected normalised
\rm{DR}^{(\ell\,0)}
counts as

\hat{I}_{2}=\frac{3}{r_{\rm max}^{3}-r_{\rm min}^{3}}\int_{r_{\rm min}}^{r_{\rm max}}\!\!r^{2}\underbrace{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}(-1)^{\ell}\,{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(\mu)\,{\mathcal{G}}(r,\mu)\;{\rm d}\mu}_{:=S(r)}\,{\rm d}r\;.

(87)

Then, one can evaluate separately the angular and radial integrals with Gauss–Legendre quadrature of order
N_{\rm GL}
such that

S(r)\simeq\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}(-1)^{\ell}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm GL}}w_{j}^{\rm GL}\,w(r,\mu_{j})\,{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(\mu_{j})\,{\mathcal{G}}(r,\mu_{j})\;,

(88)

and

\hat{I}_{2}\simeq\frac{3/2}{(r_{\rm max}^{2}+r_{\rm min})\,(r_{\rm max}+r_{\rm min}^{2})}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm GL}}w_{k}^{\rm GL}\,r_{k}^{2}\,S(r_{k})\;,

(89)

where
r_{k}:=(r_{\rm max}-r_{\rm min})\,\mu_{k}/2+(r_{\rm max}+r_{\rm min})\,\mu_{k}/2
and
\mu_{j}
(or
\mu_{k}
) are the zeros of the Legendre polynomials of order
N_{\rm GL}
. Note that the order
N_{\rm GL}
does not need to be the same for the two integrations. Indeed, given that the radial integration is performed over thin bins one can choose order
1
or
3
at maximum (this had no effect in the spherical case). However given the range in
\mu
is large (between
-1
and
1
) we will use
N_{\rm GL}=15
which means that if the behaviour of the function to be integrated is captured by a polynomial of order
30
then the integral is exact. Indeed, when increasing the number of objects to infinity we expect
w(\boldsymbol{r})\,{\mathcal{G}}(r,\mu)\sim 1
which means that we are integrating functions which are at most polynomials of order
4
, thus a Gauss–Legendre quadrature of order
15
is more than enough.

As in the spherical case we made an explicit test to check our ability in predicting the cross-counts between data and randoms. To do so we computed the data-random count explicitly for two different data catalogues and each one with four different random catalogues. Each random catalogue contains ten times more object then in the data catalogue.
The result of that test is very similar to the spherical case previously described. We are able to evaluate efficiently the
{\rm DR}^{(\ell)}:={\rm DR}^{(\ell,0)}
term for the the monopole, quadrupole, and hexadecapole.

In this appendix we review how we generate a series of

However, both the power spectrum of the divergence of the velocity and the density-velocity dispersion relations are referring to the dark matter field which we do not have in the ELM realisations. Thus, those quantities will be evaluated following
6

1000

The galaxy monopole power spectrum can be estimated in the ELM mock in the first redshift bin with edges
0.9<z<1.1
in real space (without applying RSD). The
30$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$
cone consists of a cone with minimum comoving distance
R_{\rm min}=2114\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h
and a maximum comoving distance
R_{\rm max}=2447\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h
with a total aperture angle
2\alpha=\pi/3
. We keep only galaxies with
{\rm H}_{\alpha}>2\times 10^{-16}

We then Fourier transform the density grid and apply the interlacing technic to reduce the aliasing contribution and estimate the power spectrum in
256
bin of
k
. We do not include IC because we estimate the mean number density in the catalogue over the
1000
realisations. Thus, given the true power spectrum
P_{\boldsymbol{k}}
we expect the measured power spectrum
\tilde{P}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
with the cone-like geometry to be given by

\tilde{P}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\int P_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\,|W_{\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\;{\rm d}^{3}k^{\prime}\;,

(90)

where
W_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the FT of the window function. There exist at least three possibilities to perform the convolution in Eq. (
90

This can be done, either in three dimensions or integrating the angular part and performing a Hankel transform in one dimension. Finally, the last method involves the computation of a mixing kernel, allowing us to compute in Fourier space the monopole power spectrum of the convolved power spectrum. After some tests with
COVMOS
for which we know the true power spectrum and can apply a cone-like geometry we decided to use the least efficient in terms of execution speed but the most accurate method which is to apply the convolution as a product in
3
-dimensional configuration space
(
48

47
, see)
.
Thus, the necessary steps are as follows: we put the true power spectrum
P_{\boldsymbol{k}}
on a
3
-dimensional Fourier grid and inverse Fourier transform it with an FFT to obtain the corresponding
3
-dimensional 2PCF
\xi(\boldsymbol{r})
. We can compute, for each grid point, the corresponding expected 2PCF affected by the window as
\tilde{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r})=\xi(\boldsymbol{r})\,g(\boldsymbol{r})
. Then, we can apply an FFT in order to evaluate the convolved
3
-dimensional power spectrum
\tilde{P}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
on which we can finally apply the same

\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)

Thus, given
\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
we want to recover

in Eq. (
90

-point PDF in count-in-cell analysis
(
54

8
)

, then apply the convolution to it to obtain the corresponding
\tilde{P}_{0}^{(0)}(k)
, and compare it with the measured power spectrum
\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
with a basic

defined as

\chi_{0}^{2}:=\dfrac{\sum_{i}\left[\tilde{P}_{0}^{(0)}(k_{i})-\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k_{i})\right]^{2}}{\left[\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k_{i})\right]^{2}}\;.

(91)

This represents the starting point of this iterative method. Then, we can compute the ratio
R_{0}:=P_{0}(k)/\tilde{P}_{0}^{(0)}(k)
and apply it to get a new guess for the step
n+1
as
P_{n+1}(k)=R_{n}\,\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
. This process needs to be iterated enough times to obtain a good agreement between
\tilde{P}_{n}^{(0)}(k)
and
\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
quantified by a low
\chi^{2}
. The known caveat of this kind of process is that it is better to avoid too many iterations, which risks to cause non-physical behaviour in the deconvolved power spectrum. That is why it is crucial to start with a good guess. To do so, we use the linear power spectrum and compute its convolved version to deduce the initial ratio
R
such that
P_{0}(k)=R\,\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
. Indeed, since we expect the linear matter power spectrum to have the same behaviour as the galaxy power spectrum at low
k
(where the galaxy bias is linear) and given that the convolution mostly affects low
k
-modes, we expect the effect of the convolution to be well captured by the initial ratio
R
. In practice with

\tilde{P}^{(0)}(k)
monopole and the predicted one
\tilde{P}_{n}^{(0)}(k)
which indicates that

is close to being the deconvolved power spectrum. We basically stop the number of iterations when the
\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{0}
mode
P_{\boldsymbol{0}}
estimated and predicted agree within

. This corresponds to a decrease in
\chi^{2}
of
0.2

One possible way of reducing the effect of the shot noise is to use a higher-order interpolation scheme such as the PCS – that we actually use to evaluate the power spectrum. It would reduce the shot noise contribution to the one we would obtain with
10
times the mean number of objects per cell, but this is still too low. In addition, if one can directly map the true PDF into count-in-cell probability, it is not possible to link straightforwardly the true PDF to the PDF of the PCS density field. In turn, it is much easier to relate the moments of the PCS density field to the moments of the true underlying field, through standard shot noise correction.

Let us describe how we proceed. Despite being fairly intuitive, those results have, to our knowledge, never been presented in literature yet. In the present derivation we focus only on
1
-point moments of a point process which have been split on a regular grid with a given mass assignment scheme
F

\delta_{F}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\int\delta(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,F(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\;{\rm d}^{3}x^{\prime}\;,

(92)

where
F
is normalised in such a way that
\int F(\boldsymbol{x})\;{\rm d}^{3}x=1
. As a result, one can also define
f:=a^{3}F
as a dimensionless function. From Eq. (
92

1
-point cumulant moments of order
N
of the field
\delta_{F}
as

\langle\delta_{F}^{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}=\int{\rm d}^{3}x_{1}\,...{\rm d}^{3}x_{N}\;\xi_{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{x}_{N})\;F(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\,...\,F(\boldsymbol{x}_{N})\;,

(93)

which has already been presented widely in literature
(
10
)
, where
\xi_{N}
is referring to the
N
-point correlation function. Since the
N
-point correlation function is affected by shot noise we expect the cumulant moments
\langle\delta_{F}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}
to be modified too. Indeed, the shot noise contribution
\xi_{N}^{\,({\rm SN})}
to the
N
-point correlation function is expected to arise each time there is a zero separation between any
2
-,
3
-, or
N
-point configurations, thus we expect it to be of the general form

\xi_{N}^{\,(\rm SN)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{i}}\sum_{\rm configurations}\xi_{N-i}\;\prod_{j=1}^{i}\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{q})\;,

(94)

where the sum over the configurations is running over all the possible configurations leading to a zero-lag at a given order
i
. As an example, we can get that at
N=2
(i.e. for the 2PCF), there is only one correction term corresponding to two overlapping points. Thus

\xi^{(\rm SN)}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{r})}{\bar{n}}\;,

(95)

where
\boldsymbol{r}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\xi:=\xi_{2}
. Then, at three points, there are two corrective terms (
i=1
and
i=2
). The
i=1

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
or
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}
or
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}
while the

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}

\zeta^{(\rm SN)}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left[\xi(\boldsymbol{s})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{r})+2\;{\rm perm.}\right]+\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{2}}\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{s})\;,

(96)

where
\boldsymbol{s}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\zeta:=\xi_{3}
. Finally at order
4

i=1
shows all possible single collapse of two points among four, of which there are six possibilities,

corresponds to the collapse of twice two points which are only three possibilities if the two pairs of collapsed point are separated, and then there are four possibilities of collapsing twice two points at the same position (i.e. collapsing three points at the same position) and finally one single possibility for collapsing the four points at the same position. Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega^{(\rm SN)}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})&\!\!=&\!\!\!\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\left[\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{t})\,\zeta(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{s})+5\;{\rm perm.}\right]+\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{3}}\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{r})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{s})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{t})\\
&&\\
&&\!\!\!\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{2}}\left[\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{s})\,\delta^{\rm D}(\boldsymbol{t})\,\xi(r)+6\;{\rm perm.}\right]\;,\end{array}

(97)

where
\boldsymbol{t}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{4}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\omega:=\xi_{4}
. We stop at order
4
since we want to reproduce correctly the global amplitude of the trispectrum in the ELM catalogues with the
COVMOS
realisations. In general, in order to compute the shot noise contribution to the
1
