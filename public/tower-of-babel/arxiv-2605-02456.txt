Improved semidefinite programming bounds for the maximum k k -colorable subgraph problem
Mathijs Barkel, Renata Sotirov

Abstract
We study the maximum k k -colorable subgraph (M k k CS) problem, which consists in finding a largest k k -colorable induced subgraph in a given graph. We consider a Semidefinite Programming (SDP) relaxation for the M k k CS problem and regard its resulting upper bound as a graph parameter. We present several properties of this graph parameter, from which we obtain that the M k k CS problem is solvable in polynomial time for k k -perfect graphs. We further derive two novel families of valid inequalities to strengthen the SDP relaxation. The first family reduces to a family of inequalities for the Boolean quadric polytope when k = 1 k=1 , and the second family generalizes the family of rank inequalities for binary linear programming formulations of the stable set problem. We efficiently solve the strengthened SDP relaxation using a cutting-plane algorithm that is based on the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). Extensive computational experiments show that the obtained upper bounds outperform the best upper bounds from the literature. To complement our SDP-based upper bounds, we propose an integer ADMM variant that uses an exact Binary Semidefinite Programming (BSDP) formulation of the M k k CS problem to produce high-quality feasible solutions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the ADMM to compute integer solutions to a BSDP problem. Keywords k k -colorable subgraph problem; graph coloring; k k -perfect graphs; cutting-plane methods; integer ADMM; semidefinite programming. Mathematics Subject Classification 05C15, 05C17, 90C22, 90C27, 90C35.

Introduction

The maximum
k
-colorable subgraph (M
k
CS) problem is to find the largest induced subgraph in a given graph that can be colored in
k
colors such that no two adjacent vertices have the same color.
Since a partial
k
-coloring of a graph is a set of pairwise disjoint stable sets in the graph, each one corresponding to a color, the solution for the M
k
CS problem corresponds to the largest possible number of colored vertices in a partial
k
-coloring.
The M
k
CS problem is also known as the maximum
k
-partite induced subgraph problem, as well as the maximum bipartite subgraph problem for the case
k=2
, while for
k=1
it coincides with the stable set problem.
The M
k
CS problem is known to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
36
]
.

The M
k
CS problem arises in various practical applications, including channel assignment in spectrum-sharing networks

[
2
,
22
,
25
,
32
,
55
]
, very-large-scale integration design
[
15
,
41
]
, human genetic research
[
15
,
38
]
and job scheduling and register allocation
[
8
]
.
The M
k
CS problem is also of interest due to its relation to several well-known combinatorial optimization problems. For instance, it is closely related to the graph coloring problem and the clique cover problem.

Several works address the M
k
CS problem using integer programming, some of which exploit that the M
k
CS problem is invariant under color permutations and graph automorphisms.

7
introduce a Binary Linear Programming (BLP) formulation where color symmetry is handled through the selection of representatives of stable sets.
They further propose a parallelized subgradient algorithm that uses a Lagrangian relaxation to decompose the M
k
CS problem into smaller weighted stable set problems.
Lower and upper bounds are computed for instances with up to
500
vertices and
3
colors.

27
,
28
propose an alternative BLP formulation as the basis of a branch-cut-and-propagate algorithm that leverages both valid inequalities and domain propagation to handle color and graph symmetry.
They solve instances with up to
1085
vertices and
25
colors to optimality.

51
derive two quadratic unconstrained binary optimization formulations for the M
k
CS problem that are solved using D-Wave’s quantum annealing device.
The authors consider instances with up to
50
vertices and
5
colors.

Another line of research focuses on Semidefinite Programming (SDP).

43
propose an eigenvalue bound for the M
k
CS problem called the generalized
\vartheta
-number of a graph
\mathcal{G}
(denoted by
\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
), which was later reformulated by
1
as the solution of a semidefinite program.
This graph parameter coincides with the celebrated
\vartheta
-number by
39
for
k=1
, and provides also a lower bound on the minimum number of colors needed for a valid
k
-multicoloring of the complement of the considered graph.

54
study
\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
for highly symmetric graphs and provide closed-form solutions for several graph classes.

33
strengthen
\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
by incorporating nonnegativity constraints, and consider several other SDP relaxations for the M
k
CS problem.
Their strongest relaxation is derived via a vector-lifting approach, followed by a reduction in the size of the SDP relaxation that exploits the invariance of the M
k
CS problem under color permutations.
This relaxation is further strengthened using inequalities from the Boolean quadric polytope (BQP).
A weaker but more scalable relaxation is obtained through a matrix-lifting approach.
The authors compare the strength of their relaxations from a theoretical point of view, and provide computational experiments on benchmark instances with up to
500
vertices.
Several instances are solved to optimality, and the strongest upper bounds improve upon the upper bounds from
[
7
]
for all but two instances.

Motivated by this success, we continue the line of research on SDP-based approaches for the M
k
CS problem.
In particular, rather than using Interior Point Methods (IPMs), the classical approach for solving SDP problems, we consider the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM).
This is a first-order method introduced in the 1970s to solve general convex optimization problems, see e.g.,
[
4
]
.
Compared to IPMs, the time and memory requirements of the ADMM scale better with the order of the matrix variables and the number of constraints.
Indeed, the ADMM, and variants thereof, have been successfully applied to solve large-scale SDP relaxations of various combinatorial optimization problems, see e.g.,
[
26
,
37
,
46
,
53
]
.

1.1
Main results and outline

We start our study of the M
k
CS problem with a Binary Semidefinite Programming (BSDP) formulation proposed by
13
, from which we derive our basic SDP relaxation that coincides with the matrix-lifting relaxation from
[
33
]
.
We consider the optimal value of the basic SDP relaxation, denoted by
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, as a graph parameter.
We derive several properties of this graph parameter, including that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
is upper bounded by
k\vartheta(G)
for all
k\geq 1
, and that the sequence
\left(\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\right)_{k}
is monotonically increasing in
k
.
We also show that our graph parameter serves as a lower bound for the
k
-clique cover number of
\mathcal{G}
.

40
defined a
k
-perfect graph as a graph for which the number of vertices in a maximum
k
-colorable subgraph equals the
k
-clique cover number for each induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
.
Therefore, these two graph parameters can be computed for
k
-perfect graphs in polynomial time up to fixed precision by solving our SDP relaxation for the M
k
CS problem.

40
posed an open question on the computability of these parameters for perfect graphs, and we provide an answer for
k
-perfect graphs where
k>1
.
The case
k=1
is a well-known result by
20
.
Moreover, we provide an answer to Lovász question for a class of perfect graphs known as totally perfect graphs.

We also propose two novel families of valid inequalities to strengthen our SDP relaxation.
Our first family of inequalities reduces to a family of inequalities for the BQP by
48
when
k=1
.
These new inequalities are not only relevant for our SDP relaxation for the M
k
CS problem, but potentially also for matrix-lifting relaxations of other packing or partitioning problems.
Our second family of inequalities generalizes the family of rank inequalities for BLP formulations of the stable set problem by
45
.
Given that there are exponentially many generalized BQP and rank inequalities, we also consider computationally tractable special cases of these inequalities that concern triangles, cliques and odd holes.

We solve the strengthened SDP relaxation using a recently proposed extension of the ADMM, called the cutting-plane ADMM (CP-ADMM), which integrates the ADMM with Dykstra’s cyclic projection algorithm, see
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
.
We improve the performance of the CP-ADMM by limiting the number of new cuts in which each variable may appear; a feature that was not yet used in existing CP-ADMM algorithms.
Extensive computational experiments on benchmark instances show that the strengthened SDP relaxation can be solved efficiently, and that the upper bounds obtained outperform the best upper bounds from the literature.

We present a novel integer ADMM variant, denoted by INT-ADMM, that is aimed at constructing feasible solutions for the BSDP formulation, thereby yielding valid lower bounds.
Our INT-ADMM is based on the
\ell_{p}
-Box ADMM introduced by
56
. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to apply the key idea of the
\ell_{p}
-Box ADMM to solve a BSDP problem.
The INT-ADMM is not guaranteed to converge to an optimal or even a feasible solution, but our computational experiments show that our algorithm consistently leads to high-quality feasible solutions, often improving upon the best solutions in the literature.
This success is partly due to an effective mechanism for escaping local optima.

This paper is further structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we formally introduce the M
k
CS problem, its BSDP formulation and our basic SDP relaxation.
In Section
3
, we present our theoretical results on the graph parameter
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
.
In Section
4
, we derive the valid inequalities.
In Sections
5
and
6
, we present the CP-ADMM and INT-ADMM, respectively.
In Section
7
, we report the results of our computational experiments, and finally, in Section
8
, we present our conclusions.

[n]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{1,\ldots,n\}

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

, in which case the first row and column are indexed by

The rank of a matrix

{\text{rank}\left(X\right)}
.
The trace inner product of two matrices

\left\langle A,B\right\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\text{tr}(}A^{\top}B)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}B_{ij}
.

The operator

2
A BSDP formulation and SDP relaxation for the M
k
CS problem

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a simple undirected graph with vertex set
\mathcal{V}=[n]
and edge set
\mathcal{E}
, and let
k\in[n-1]
be a given number of colors.
A graph is said to be
k
-colorable if one can assign to each vertex one of the
k
colors such that no two adjacent vertices have the same color. The M
k
CS problem is to find an induced
k
-colorable subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
with maximum cardinality.
Recall that the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
induced by
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is the graph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{S}]=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{E}[\mathcal{S}])
with vertex set
\mathcal{S}
and edge set
\mathcal{E}[\mathcal{S}]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}:i,j\in\mathcal{S}\}
.

Let
P\in\{0,1\}^{n\times k}
be a binary matrix variable such that
P_{ic}=1
if and only if vertex
i\in[n]
is assigned color
c\in[k]
.
Then, the M
k
CS problem can be formulated as the following BLP problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G}):=\max\quad&\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&P_{ic}+P_{jc}\leq 1&\quad\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E},\;c\in[k]\,\\
&\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}\leq 1&\quad\forall i\in[n]\,\\
&P_{ic}\in\{0,1\}&\quad\forall i\in[n],\;c\in[k].\end{aligned}

(BLP
k
)

Next, we present a derivation from
[
13
]
of a BSDP formulation of the M
k
CS problem using a matrix-lifting approach.
The key idea of this approach is to substitute the matrix variable
P
from (
BLPk
) by another matrix variable
X
such that
X=PP^{\top}
.
This substitution can be performed as follows.
First, using that
P
is binary, we have that
X_{ii}=\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}^{2}=\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}
for each
i\in[n]
, so we can rewrite the objective as
\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle
.
Second, by again exploiting the binarity of
P
, we can replace the constraints
P_{ic}+P_{jc}\leq 1
for
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}
and
c\in[k]
, with the constraints
X_{ij}=\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}P_{jc}=0
for
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}
.
Finally, we consider the following set
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}
that captures the remaining constraints on
P
and the relation
X=PP^{\top}
:

\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:X=PP^{\top},\,\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}\leq 1\,\,\forall i\in[n],\,\,P\in\{0,1\}^{n\times k}\right\}.

(1)

Note that
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{1}
coincides with the set of extreme points of the BQP introduced in
[
48
]
.
As implied by Proposition 2 of
[
35
]
and Corollary 2 of
[
13
]
, respectively,
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}
can be described independently of the variable
P
in either of the following two ways:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:X\succeq\textbf{0},\,\,{\text{rank}\left(X\right)}\leq k,\,\,X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\right\}

(2)

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:\hat{X}=\begin{pmatrix}k&{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}^{\top}\\
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}&X\end{pmatrix}\succeq\textbf{0},\,\,X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\right\}.

(3)

Using the latter characterization, we obtain the following BSDP formulation of the M
k
CS problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\max\quad&\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&X_{ij}=0\qquad\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\\
&\hat{X}=\begin{pmatrix}k&{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}^{\top}\\
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}&X\end{pmatrix}\succeq\textbf{0}\\
&X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}.\end{aligned}

(BSDP
k
)

We denote the feasible set of (
BSDPk
) by
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
.
The binary matrix variable
X
has the interpretation that a diagonal entry
X_{ii}=1
if and only if vertex
i\in[n]
is assigned a color, and an off-diagonal entry
X_{ij}=1
if and only if vertices
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
are assigned the same color.
The appearance of the constant
k
in the PSD constraint and the binarity of the matrix variable together ensure that
{\text{rank}\left(X\right)}\leq k
, or equivalently, that at most
k
colors are used.
Note that (
BSDPk
) is free of color symmetry, whereas (
BLPk
) is not.

For
k=1
, i.e., the stable set problem, it is sufficient to impose integrality on the diagonal of
X
to ensure also integrality on the off-diagonal entries.
This follows from the nonnegativity of the determinants of the
3\times 3
principle submatrices of
\hat{X}
, see e.g., Section 3.2 of
[
24
]
.
This result does not hold in general for
k>1
.
For instance, consider the graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
\mathcal{V}=\{1,2,3\}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{\{2,3\}\}
and let
k=2
. Then
X=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&0\\
1/2&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
does not satisfy integrality on all off-diagonal entries, while
X
does satisfy all other constraints of (
BSDPk
).

By relaxing the integrality constraints on
X
in (
BSDPk
) and replacing them with the following bound constraints

X\geq\textbf{0}_{n},\quad{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}\leq e_{n},

(4)

we obtain our basic SDP relaxation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\quad&\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&X_{ij}=0\qquad\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\\
&\hat{X}=\begin{pmatrix}k&{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}^{\top}\\
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}&X\end{pmatrix}\succeq\textbf{0}\\
&X\geq\textbf{0}_{n},\,\,{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}\leq e_{n}.\end{aligned}

(SDP
k
)

We denote the feasible set of (
SDPk
) by
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
.
The SDP relaxation (
SDPk
) was first proposed in
[
33
]
, where
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
is denoted by
\theta^{3}_{k}(\mathcal{G})
.
It was proved in that paper that (
SDPk
) satisfies the Slater condition.
Observe that (
SDPk
) is a Doubly Nonnegative relaxation, since it is an SDP relaxation over the set of nonnegative matrices.
Moreover, note that the constraint
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}\leq e_{n}
is sufficient to ensure that
X\leq J_{n}
. This follows from the nonnegativity of the determinants of the
2\times 2
principle submatrices of
X
.
Furthermore, as shown in the following lemma, (
SDPk
) dominates the LP relaxation of (
BLPk
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For any graph
\mathcal{G}
and
k\in[n-1]
, we have that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq LP_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, where
LP_{k}(\mathcal{G})
denotes the optimal value of the LP relaxation of (
BLPk
).

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
. It is not difficult to show that the matrix
P\in[0,1]^{n\times k}
with
P_{ic}=X_{ii}/{k}
for
i\in[n]
and
c\in[k]
is feasible to the LP relaxation of (
BLPk
), and that its objective value
\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}
equals
\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle
.
In particular, for any
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}
and
c\in[k]
, we have
P_{ic}+P_{jc}=\frac{1}{k}(X_{ii}+X_{jj})\leq 1
, where the inequality follows from the constraint
\left\langle aa^{\top},\hat{X}\right\rangle\geq 0
for
a=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\mathbbm{1}_{\{i,j\}}\end{pmatrix}
, which holds because
\hat{X}\succeq\textbf{0}
.
∎

3
Graph parameter
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})

We consider
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
as a graph parameter and derive several of its properties.
Our main result is that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
is sandwiched between
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and the
k
-clique cover number.
Graphs for which these two values coincide for each induced subgraph are known as
k
-perfect graphs.
Since
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
can be computed in polynomial time up to fixed precision, it follows that the M
k
CS problem is also polynomially solvable for
k
-perfect graphs.

43
introduced the generalized
\vartheta
-number of
\mathcal{G}
as an eigenvalue bound for
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, which can be computed as follows
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\quad&\left\langle J_{n},Z\right\rangle\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&Z_{ij}=0\qquad\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\\
&\left\langle I_{n},Z\right\rangle=k\\
&Z\succeq\mathbf{0},\,I_{n}-Z\succeq\mathbf{0}.\end{aligned}

(
\vartheta_{k}
)

33

\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
by adding nonnegativity constraints.
The resulting graph parameter is denoted by
\vartheta_{k}^{\prime}(\mathcal{G})
, and for

it coincides with the Schrijver number
[
52
]
, which is denoted in the literature by
\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G})
.
It is known that
\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G})=\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G})

[
33
]
.
It was conjectured in the same paper that

is at least as strong as

for all

.
The authors of

]
proved that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})

Similarly to the (generalized)
\vartheta

-numbers,
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
can also be regarded as a graph parameter.
Let us first relate
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any undirected graph
\mathcal{G}
with
n
vertices, and an integer
k\in[n-1]
,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq k\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G}).

Proof.

The result follows trivially for
k=1
.
Let
k\geq 2
, and let
X^{k}
be an optimal solution to (
SDPk
).
Let
X^{1}:=\frac{1}{k}X^{k}

is feasible for (SDP

Therefore,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\left\langle I_{n},X^{k}\right\rangle=k\left\langle I_{n},\frac{1}{k}X^{k}\right\rangle=k\left\langle I_{n},X^{1}\right\rangle\leq k\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G}),

where the inequality follows from the fact that

need not be an optimal solution to (SDP

).
∎

Next, we relate
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
to the chromatic number of graph
\mathcal{G}
, denoted by
\chi(\mathcal{G})

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a given graph and

such that

, then
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})=|\mathcal{V}|
.

Proof.

Since

, we have

, and therefore

.
The fact that

.
∎

We summarize the results of the previous lemmas below.

Corollary 3.2.1
.

For a given graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
n
vertices and an integer
k\in[n-1]
, we have

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\min\left\{k\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G}),|\mathcal{V}|\right\},

where
\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G})

It was shown in
[
54
]
that the sequence

\left(\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\right)_{k}

.
We show below that
\left(\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\right)_{k}

k
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any graph
\mathcal{G}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\theta_{k+1}(\mathcal{G}).

Proof.

Let
X^{k}
be an optimal solution to (
SDPk
).
It is not difficult to verify that
X^{k}
is also a feasible solution to the SDP relaxation
(SDP_{k+1})
.
Therefore,
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\left\langle I_{n},X^{k}\right\rangle\leq\theta_{k+1}(\mathcal{G})
.
∎

The following summarizes the relationships among several graph parameters:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq k\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G})\leq k\vartheta(\mathcal{G})\leq\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}),

(5)

where
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
denotes the complement graph of
\mathcal{G}
, and

\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
the minimum number of colors needed for a valid
k
-multicoloring of
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
.
Recall that a valid
k
–multicoloring of a graph is an assignment of
k
distinct colors to each vertex in the graph such that two adjacent vertices are assigned disjoint sets of colors.
Clearly,
\chi_{1}(\mathcal{G})=\chi(\mathcal{G}).

While the first three inequalities in (
5
) follow from the previous discussion, for the proof of the last inequality, see e.g.,
[
54
]
.

Note that the parameter
\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
can be larger than the number of vertices in
\mathcal{G}
, while
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
are bounded above by
n
.
Therefore, one may ask whether there is a more appropriate graph parameter than
\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
that serves as an upper bound on
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, thereby forming a sandwich relation.
We consider here the following parameter

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}({\mathcal{G}}):=\min\left\{k\chi(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|\,:\,\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\right\},

(6)

that was studied by
19
, and
18
.
Here,
\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}
denotes the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
induced by
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.

Clearly,
\Psi_{k}({\mathcal{G}})\leq n
for any
k\geq 1
, and
\Psi_{1}({\mathcal{G}})=\chi({\mathcal{G}})
.

44
also proposed replacing
\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
with
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})

in the sandwich inequality
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\vartheta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\chi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
, but they were unable to prove that
\vartheta_{k}({\mathcal{G}})\leq\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
.

47
introduced a spectral nonconvex graph parameter, which is sandwiched between
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
and is difficult to compute.
In the sequel, we show that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
is bounded above by
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
, resulting in the desired sandwich relation.
Let us first note that

\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})=\min\left\{k\chi(\overline{\mathcal{G}}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|\,:\,\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\right\}=\min\left\{k\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}})+|\mathcal{S}|\,:\,\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\right\},

where
\overline{\chi}(\mathcal{G})
denotes the clique cover number of
\mathcal{G}
.
Here we exploit the fact that
\overline{\chi}(\mathcal{H})=\chi(\overline{\mathcal{H}})
for any graph
\mathcal{H}
.
Therefore,
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
may be seen as a generalization of the clique cover number.
We therefore refer to the minimization problem above as the
k
-clique cover problem.

First, we prove the following result.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a graph with
n
vertices, and let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
. Then,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|,

for any
k\in[n-1]
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}
, the inequality follows trivially.
Let us therefore assume that
\mathcal{S}\neq\mathcal{V}
.
Let
X
be an optimal solution to (
SDPk
), and let
X_{\mathcal{S}}
and
X_{\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{S}}
be the submatrices of
X
indexed by the vertices in the set
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, respectively.
It is not difficult to verify that
X_{\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{S}}
is feasible for the SDP relaxation (
SDPk
) induced by
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Now, we consider the following two cases.

Assume that
k<n-|\mathcal{S}|
. Then,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle=\left\langle I_{n-|\mathcal{S}|},X_{\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle+\left\langle I_{|\mathcal{S}|},X_{\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|.

Assume now that
k\geq n-|\mathcal{S}|
.
Then, we proceed similarly as above:

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|=n-|\mathcal{S}|+|\mathcal{S}|=n,

where we exploited that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})=n-|\mathcal{S}|
since
k\geq n-|\mathcal{S}|
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our main result.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a graph with
n
vertices, and let
k\in[n-1]
be an integer. Then,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}).

Proof.

We show that
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq k\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}})+|\mathcal{S}|
for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
To do that we consider three cases.
Assume first that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}
. Then,
k\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|=k\cdot 0+n
, from where it follows
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq n
.

Let us now assume that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
k<n-|\mathcal{S}|
.
Then,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|\leq k\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})+|\mathcal{S}|\leq k\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}})+|\mathcal{S}|,

where we used Proposition
3.4
for the first inequality, and Lemma
3.1
for the second inequality.
The last inequality follows from the fact that
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})=\vartheta^{\prime}(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})\leq\vartheta(\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S})
and the well-known Lovász sandwich theorem

\alpha(\mathcal{H})\leq\vartheta(\mathcal{H})\leq\chi(\overline{\mathcal{H}})
for any graph
\mathcal{H}
, see also (
5
) for
k=1
.

Assume now that
\mathcal{S}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{V}
and
k\geq n-|\mathcal{S}|
. Then,

\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq n=n-|\mathcal{S}|+|\mathcal{S}|\leq k\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}})+|\mathcal{S}|,

where we exploit Proposition
3.4
and the fact that
\overline{\chi}({\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{S}})\geq 1
.
∎

Finally, we can establish the following sandwich relation

\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})\leq\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}),

for
k\in[n-1]
.
Since
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
can be computed in polynomial time up to fixed precision, it follows that the M
k
CS problem is also solvable in polynomial time for any graph for which
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
.
In particular, this is true for
k
-perfect graphs.
Recall that a graph
\mathcal{G}
is called
k
-perfect if
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{S}])=\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{S}]})
holds for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}

[
40
]
.
A
k
-perfect graph for
k=1
is known as a perfect graph.
Perfect graphs were introduced by Claude Berge in the early sixties, and the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem by
9
fully characterizes perfect graphs.
On the other hand, only a few classes of
k
-perfect graphs for
k>1
have been identified.

18
proved that comparability graphs are
k
-perfect for every integer
k\geq 1
, and so are their complements, as shown by
19
.
A graph that is
k
-perfect for every integer
k\geq 1
is called a totally perfect graph.

40
showed that line graphs of bipartite graphs are totally perfect, and
3
proved that all balanced graphs are totally perfect.

Our result resolves the problem of finding an efficient algorithm for computing
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
for
k
-perfect graphs where
k>1
.

20
showed that there is a polynomial time algorithm for computing these parameters for perfect graphs, thus for the case
k=1
.
To the best of our knowledge, no such result was previously known for
k>1
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a
k
-perfect graph and
k\geq 1
.
Then,
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
can be computed in polynomial time up to fixed precision.

4
Valid inequalities

In this section, we derive several families of valid inequalities that strengthen (
SDPk
).
We first propose generalized BQP inequalities (Section
4.1
), and then derive generalized rank inequalities (Section
4.2
).
We further consider computationally tractable special cases of our new valid inequalities that concern triangles (Section
4.1.1
), cliques (Section
4.2.1
) and odd holes (Section
4.2.2
).

4.1
Generalized Boolean quadric polytope inequalities

We first present valid inequalities that generalize the
cut inequalities
and
clique inequalities
of
48
for the BQP.
Namely, we prove that the following inequalities are valid for the set
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}
, where
k\in[n-1]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}X_{ij}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}X_{ii}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}X_{ij}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:i<j}X_{ij}

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\text{ with }\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\emptyset

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}\leq\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}X_{ij}+k

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

(8)

Inequalities (
7
) coincide with Padberg’s cut inequalities, and inequalities (
8
) coincide with a subset of Padberg’s clique inequalities when
k=1
.
Therefore, we will further refer to inequalities (
7
) and (
8
) as the
generalized BQP inequalities
, whose validity we prove next.

Theorem 4.1
.

Inequalities (
7
) and (
8
) are valid for
\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}
,
where
k\in[n-1]
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathcal{D}^{n}_{k}
, which implies that there exists a matrix
P\in\{0,1\}^{n\times k}
such that
X=PP^{\top}
.

First, we prove the validity of inequality (
7
) for
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with
\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Let
c\in[k]
.
It can easily be shown that
xy\leq y+\binom{x}{2}+\binom{y}{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
.
Taking
x=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
y=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{ic}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{jc}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{ic}+\binom{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}}{2}+\binom{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{ic}}{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{ic}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}P_{ic}P_{jc}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:i<j}P_{ic}P_{jc},

where we also use binarity of
P
to get
\binom{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}}{2}=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S},i<j}P_{ic}P_{jc}
and
\binom{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}P_{ic}}{2}=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime},i<j}P_{ic}P_{jc}
.
Summing all
k
such inequalities for
c\in[k]
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}P_{jc}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}P_{jc}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:i<j}\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}P_{jc}.

Finally, we can substitute
X_{ii}=\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}
for each
i\in[n]
and
X_{ij}=\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}P_{jc}
for each
i,j\in[n]
(
i\neq j
) to obtain inequality (
7
) for
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Next, we prove the validity of inequality (
8
) for
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
in a similar fashion.
For each
c\in[k]
, we have that

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}\leq\binom{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ic}}{2}+1=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}P_{ic}P_{jc}+1,

where we used that
x\leq\binom{x}{2}+1
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Summing all
k
such inequalities for
c\in[k]
, and replacing the
P
-variables by the
X
-variables, we obtain inequality (
8
) for
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Note that in the proof of Theorem
4.1
we did not require that
\sum_{c\in[k]}P_{ic}\leq 1
for all
i\in[n]
.
Furthermore, it is not difficult to verify that inequalities (
8
) for sets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with
|\mathcal{S}|\leq k
are already implied by bound constraints (
4
).

4.1.1
Generalized triangle inequalities

The exponential number of generalized BQP inequalities makes it intractable to include all of them as cutting planes for strengthening (
SDPk
).
Therefore, in practice, one might restrict to the following generalized BQP inequalities that involve only three vertices:

\displaystyle X_{i\ell}+X_{j\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}+X_{ij}

\displaystyle\forall\{i,j,\ell\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}

(9)

\displaystyle X_{ii}+X_{jj}+X_{\ell\ell}\leq X_{ij}+X_{i\ell}+X_{j\ell}+k

\displaystyle\forall\{i,j,\ell\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

(10)

Inequalities (
9
) follow from inequalities (
7
) with
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j\}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{\ell\}
, and inequalities (
10
) follow from inequalities (
8
) with
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j,\ell\}
.
Inequalities (
9
), and inequalities (
10
) for
k=1
, are known as
triangle inequalities
.
They were introduced in
[
48
]
and have been used to strengthen various relaxations, including SDP relaxations for the stable set problem
[
21
]
and the strongest known SDP relaxation for the M
k
CS problem
[
33
]
.
We also considered the generalized BQP inequalities that involve only two vertices.
However, preliminary experiments showed that it is not beneficial to add those to (
SDPk
).

4.2
Generalized rank inequalities

In this section, we introduce four sets of inequalities that generalize the rank inequalities proposed by
45
, see also
[
34
]
.
We extend these inequalities from BLP to BSDP, and from
k=1
(i.e., the stable set problem) to arbitrary
k\in[n-1]
.
Specific types of rank inequalities were already extended to semidefinite programs in
[
49
,
50
]
.
These works focus on the M
k
CS for
k=1
and subgraphs inducing cliques, odd holes, and odd antiholes, while we consider arbitrary values of
k
and general induced subgraphs.

To state our
generalized rank inequalities
, we require the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
be a subset of vertices and let
\kappa\in[k]
be a number of colors.
The
\kappa
-rank of
\mathcal{S}
is defined as
r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\alpha_{\kappa}(\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{S}])
, which is the maximum number of vertices that can be colored in
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{S}]
with at most
\kappa
colors.
We also define
r_{0}(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=0
.

The
1
-rank of a set coincides with the notion of rank used in the rank inequalities for the stable set problem
[
34
,
45
]
.
Throughout this section, we further use that for any feasible solution
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
and subset of vertices
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, the sum
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}
gives the number of vertices in
\mathcal{S}
that are colored, the sum
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}X_{ij}
gives the number of pairs of vertices in
\mathcal{S}
that have the same color, and the sum
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{i\ell}
gives the number of vertices in
\mathcal{S}
that have the same color as some external vertex
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.

Our first class of generalized rank inequalities for
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
follows directly from the definition of
r_{k}(S)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}\leq r_{k}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

(11)

The next inequalities provide an upper bound on the number of pairs of vertices in a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that may be colored with the same color, when using the available
k
colors:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}X_{ij}\leq\sum_{\kappa=1}^{k}\binom{r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{S})-r_{\kappa-1}(\mathcal{S})}{2}

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}.

(12)

To prove the validity of these inequalities, we use the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3

(Proposition B.2. of
[
42
]
)
.

Let
f
be a continuous non-decreasing convex function, and let
x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
y=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be two sequences such that
y
weakly majorizes
x
.
This means that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{(i)}
for each
m\in[n]
, where
x_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq x_{(n)}
and
y_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq y_{(n)}
denote the elements of
x
and
y
in non-increasing order.
Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i})
.

We are now ready to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Inequalities (
12
) are valid for
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}x(x-1),0\right\}
, which is continuous, non-decreasing and convex in
x
.
Consider any assignment of at most
k
colors to the vertices in some set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
(possibly leaving some vertices uncolored) and let
b=(b_{c})_{c=1}^{k}
be the sequence where
b_{c}\geq 0
denotes the number of vertices in
\mathcal{S}
that are assigned color
c\in[k]
.
Furthermore, let
\delta=(\delta_{\kappa})_{\kappa=1}^{k}
be the sequence where
\delta_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{S})-r_{\kappa-1}(\mathcal{S})\geq 0
for each number of colors
\kappa\in[k]
used.
For each
m\in[k]
, we have
\sum_{c=1}^{m}b_{(c)}\leq r_{m}(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{m}\delta_{\kappa}\leq\sum_{\kappa=1}^{m}\delta_{(\kappa)}
, so
\delta
weakly majorizes
b
.
Hence, Proposition
4.3
applies.
Finally, let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
be a feasible solution corresponding to the considered color assignment. We then have that
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}:i<j}X_{ij}=\sum_{c=1}^{k}\binom{b_{c}}{2}=\sum_{c=1}^{k}f(b_{c})\leq\sum_{\kappa=1}^{k}f(\delta_{\kappa})=\sum_{\kappa=1}^{k}\binom{\delta_{\kappa}}{2}
.
∎

Next, we present inequalities that provide an upper bound on the number of vertices in a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that may have the same color as an external vertex
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
If
\ell
is colored, then by the definition of
r_{1}(\mathcal{S})
,
\ell
can have the same color as up to
r_{1}(\mathcal{S})
vertices from
\mathcal{S}
.
This leads to the following valid inequalities for
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{i\ell}\leq r_{1}(\mathcal{S})X_{\ell\ell}

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V},\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}.

(13)

Finally, the following complementary inequalities provide a lower bound on the number of vertices in a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that may have the same color as an external vertex
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, given that
k
colors are available:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}+\left(r_{k}(\mathcal{S})-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})\right)X_{\ell\ell}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{i\ell}+r_{k}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\forall\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V},\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}.

(14)

We prove the validity of these inequalities in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

Inequalities (
14
) are valid for
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, let
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, and let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
.
Observe that when coloring more than
r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})
vertices in
\mathcal{S}
with
k
colors, then each of the
k
available colors appears at least
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})>0
times in
\mathcal{S}
.
Indeed, if some color were to be assigned to fewer than
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})
vertices, then the total number of colored vertices in
\mathcal{S}
would be less than
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}
, since the remaining
k-1
colors can together cover at most
r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})
vertices.
Thus, depending on the number of colored vertices in
\mathcal{S}
, if vertex
\ell
is colored, then
\ell
has the same color as at least
\max\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S}),0\right\}
of the vertices in
\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{i\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S}),0\right\}X_{\ell\ell}\geq\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})\right)X_{\ell\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})\right)-\left(r_{k}(\mathcal{S})-r_{k-1}(\mathcal{S})\right)\left(1-X_{\ell\ell}\right),

where we used for the last inequality that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}X_{ii}\leq r_{k}(\mathcal{S})
and that
X_{\ell\ell}\in\{0,1\}
.
Rewriting the obtained inequality gives the desired result.
∎

Note that it is intractable to add all generalized rank inequalities (
11
)-(
14
) as cuts to strengthen (
SDPk
), since there are exponentially many such inequalities, and, moreover, computing the
\kappa
-rank of an arbitrary set
\mathcal{S}
is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard.
Therefore, in the following sections we restrict attention to sets
\mathcal{S}
that are cliques and odd holes, as these sets admit explicit expressions for the
\kappa
-ranks.
In our computational experiments, we further limit the size of the cliques and odd holes considered.

4.2.1
Generalized clique inequalities

This section concerns the generalized rank inequalities (
11
)-(
14
) for subsets of
\mathcal{V}
that are cliques.
Recall that a clique in
\mathcal{G}
is a set of vertices
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that induces a complete subgraph, i.e., every pair of vertices in
\mathcal{Q}
is adjacent in
\mathcal{G}
.
For any clique
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we have that
r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{Q})=\min\{\kappa,|\mathcal{Q}|\}
for each
\kappa\in[k]
.

We first show that inequalities (
11
) and (
12
) for cliques are already implied by the constraints of (
SDPk
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
and let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
be a clique.
Then
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}\leq\min\{k,|\mathcal{Q}|\}
and
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{Q}:i<j}X_{ij}=0
.

Proof.

We have that
X_{ij}=0
for each
i,j\in\mathcal{Q}
(
i\neq j
), and thus it follows that
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{Q}:i<j}X_{ij}=0
. Moreover, since
\hat{X}\succeq\textbf{0}
, we have that
\left\langle aa^{\top},\hat{X}\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
In particular, for
a=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}
we get
k-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}+2\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{Q}:i<j}X_{ij}\geq 0
.
Using that
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{Q}:i<j}X_{ij}=0
, we obtain the inequality
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}\leq k
. Finally, the inequality
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}\leq|\mathcal{Q}|
follows from the constraint
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}\leq e_{n}
.
∎

In contrast, inequalities (
13
) and (
14
) for cliques are not implied by the constraints
\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
.
For reference, these inequalities read as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{i\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}

\displaystyle\forall\text{ clique }\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V},\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{Q}

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}+X_{\ell\ell}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{i\ell}+k

\displaystyle\forall\text{ clique }\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\text{ with }|\mathcal{Q}|\geq k,\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{Q}.

(16)

We will refer to these inequalities as the
generalized clique inequalities
.
Note that (
15
) and (
16
) can also be derived from the generalized BQP inequalities (
7
) and (
8
), respectively.
To see this, take
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{\ell\}
for inequality (
7
) and
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}\cup\{\ell\}
for inequality (
8
), and use Lemma
4.6
.
Note that inequalities (
16
) for cliques
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with
|\mathcal{Q}|<k
are already implied by bound constraints (
4
).

49
also considered inequalities (
15
) and (
16
) for the stable set problem, i.e., for the M
k
CS problem for
k=1
.
They found that inequalities (
16
) can be strengthened by extending the external vertex
\ell
to a second clique
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.
Applying this idea to the M
k
CS problem for
k\in[n-1]
, we obtain the following generalized clique inequalities:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{jj}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{ij}+k

\displaystyle\forall\text{ cliques }\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\text{ with }\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\emptyset,|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|>k.

(17)

Note that inequalities (
17
) are also a special case of generalized BQP inequalities (
8
) (take
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and use Lemma
4.6
).
Moreover, observe that inequalities (
16
) are a special case of inequalities (
17
).
Preliminary experiments confirmed that inequalities (
17
) result in stronger bounds than inequalities (
16
) alone.

As implied by the following proposition, when using generalized clique inequalities (
15
) as cuts to strengthen (
SDPk
), it suffices to consider these inequalities for maximal cliques only.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
satisfy inequality (
15
) for a clique
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and an external vertex
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{Q}
.
Then, for each
h\in\mathcal{Q}
,
X
also satisfies inequality (
15
) for the clique
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{h\}
and the vertex
\ell
.

Proof.

Using that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{i\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}
and
X_{h\ell}\geq 0
, we find that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{h\}}X_{i\ell}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{i\ell}-X_{h\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}-X_{h\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}
, which is exactly inequality (
15
) for
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{h\}
and
\ell
.
∎

Furthermore, once inequalities (
15
) have been added to (
SDPk
), it is sufficient to consider inequalities (
17
) for pairs of maximal cliques, as implied by the next proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
satisfy inequality (
17
) for disjoint cliques
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
Suppose that
X
satisfies inequality (
15
) for the clique
\mathcal{Q}
and a vertex
h\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.
Then,
X
also satisfies inequality (
17
) for the cliques
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}
.

Proof.

Using that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{jj}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{ij}+k
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ih}\leq X_{hh}
, we find
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}

+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}}X_{jj}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ii}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{jj}-X_{hh}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}X_{ij}+k-X_{hh}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}}X_{ij}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ih}+k-X_{hh}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}}X_{ij}+k+\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}X_{ih}-X_{hh}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}}X_{ij}+k
,
which is exactly inequality (
17
) for
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\{h\}
.
∎

Note that this proposition also applies when interchanging the roles of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.

4.2.2
Generalized odd-hole inequalities

We now consider the generalized rank inequalities (
11
)-(
14
) for subsets of
\mathcal{V}
that are odd holes.
We first recall some definitions.
A cycle in
\mathcal{G}
is a set of vertices
\mathcal{C}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{|\mathcal{C}|}\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 3
such that
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\in\mathcal{E}
for all
i\in[|\mathcal{C}|-1]
and
\{v_{|\mathcal{C}|},v_{1}\}\in\mathcal{E}
.
Additional edges in a cycle that connect nonconsecutive vertices are called chords. A chordless cycle is called a hole. A cycle or hole is called odd (respectively even) if its cardinality is odd (respectively even).
Odd holes of cardinality
3
are also cliques, so we only consider here odd holes of cardinality at least
5
.
Observe that for any odd hole
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, the maximum number of vertices that can be colored in
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{C}]
with
\kappa\in[k]
colors equals

r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{C})=\min\left\{\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}\kappa,|\mathcal{C}|\right\}=\begin{cases}\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}&\text{if $\kappa=1$}\\
|\mathcal{C}|-1&\text{if $\kappa=2$}\\
|\mathcal{C}|&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In preliminary experiments, we considered the strength of (
SDPk
) when adding inequalities (
11
)-(
14
) for odd holes.
Among these inequalities, the ones of type (
13
) led to the largest improvements in the SDP bounds.
Motivated by this, we focus on these inequalities here. In the following, we will refer to them as
generalized odd-hole inequalities
, and they read as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}X_{\ell\ell}

\displaystyle\forall\text{ odd hole }\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V},\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{C}.

(18)

These inequalities were first proposed by
50
for SDP relaxations for the stable set problem.

Let us briefly consider even holes.
For any even hole
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
we have that
r_{1}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}
and
r_{\kappa}(\mathcal{C})=|\mathcal{C}|
for all
\kappa\geq 2
.
Therefore, for any even hole
\mathcal{C}
we can replace the factor
\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}
in inequalities (
18
) by
\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}
to obtain valid inequalities.
However, as proven in the next proposition, such inequalities are already implied by triangle inequalities (
9
).

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}}
satisfy all triangle inequalities (
9
).
Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 4
be an even hole and let
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{C}
be an external vertex.
Then
X
satisfies
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}X_{\ell\ell}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can label the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
as
1,2,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}|
so that
\{i,i+1\}\in\mathcal{E}
for each odd
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}|-1\}
.
Therefore, for each such
i
, we can combine the triangle inequality (
9
) for triplet
\{i,i+1,\ell\}
with the constraint
X_{i,i+1}=0
to obtain that
X_{i\ell}+X_{i+1,\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}
.
Summing all
\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}
such inequalities, we obtain
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{2}X_{\ell\ell}
.
∎

In a similar vein, note that inequalities (
18
) also hold for odd cycles containing chords, as such cycles
\mathcal{C}
satisfy
r_{1}(\mathcal{C})\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}
.
However, we do not need to consider such inequalities to strengthen (
SDPk
), as implied by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{BSDP}}
, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
be an odd cycle with
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 5
that contains at least one chord, and let
\ell\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{C}
be an external vertex.
Then, the valid inequality
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}X_{\ell\ell}
can be expressed as the sum of an odd-hole inequality of the form (
18
) and a number of triangle inequalities (
9
).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can label the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
as
1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{C}|
so that
\{i,i+1\}\in\mathcal{E}
for each
i\in\mathcal{C}
(where we use the convention that addition is taken modulo
|\mathcal{C}|
when dealing with cycles) and that
\{1,p\}\in\mathcal{E}
for some even
p\in\{4,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}|-1\}
.
Note that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\{p,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}|,1\}
is an odd cycle, so
\sum_{i\in\{p,\ldots,|\mathcal{C}|,1\}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-p+1}{2}X_{\ell\ell}
.
Moreover, for each even
i\in\{2,\ldots,p-2\}
we can combine the triangle inequality (
9
) for triplet
\{i,i+1,\ell\}
with the constraint
X_{i,i+1}=0
to obtain that
X_{i\ell}+X_{i+1,\ell}\leq X_{\ell\ell}
.
Summing all
\frac{p-2}{2}
such inequalities and the inequality for the odd cycle
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we obtain
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}X_{i\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{2}X_{\ell\ell}
.
If
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is chordless, the proposition follows.
Otherwise, the same decomposition can be applied recursively.
∎

5
The cutting-plane ADMM

This section describes the CP-ADMM that is used to compute valid upper bounds on
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
.
In Section
5.1
, we present the ADMM scheme for solving SDP relaxations of (
BSDPk
), and in Section
5.2
, we explain how this scheme is embedded into a cutting-plane framework.
Whereas our implementation incorporates the valid inequalities derived in the previous section, our algorithm is described independently of the specific inequalities used, emphasizing that the CP-ADMM can handle any valid inequality of the form
\left\langle A,X\right\rangle\leq b
.

5.1
The ADMM scheme

At a given iteration of the CP-ADMM, we consider a fixed index set
\mathcal{I}
of valid inequalities for (
BSDPk
), and apply the ADMM to solve

\theta_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\left\{\left\langle I_{n},X\right\rangle:X\in\mathcal{F}_{\eqref{SDP}},\left\langle A_{i},X\right\rangle\leq b_{i}\,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\right\}.

(SDP
k
-
\mathcal{I}
)

To apply the ADMM, we first reformulate (
SDPk-

I

) by separating the affine constraints from the PSD constraint.
To this end, let

\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}k&{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}^{\top}\\
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}&X\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}:X_{ij}=0\,\,\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E},\,\,\textbf{0}_{n}\leq X\leq J_{n},\,\,\left\langle A_{i},X\right\rangle\leq b_{i}\,\,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\right\},

(19)

and let
\hat{I}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0_{n}^{\top}\\
0_{n}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}
,
from where it follows that

\theta_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})=\max\left\{\left\langle\hat{I},\hat{X}\right\rangle:\hat{X}=Y,\,\,\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}},\,\,Y\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n+1}\right\}.

(20)

Note that
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
includes the redundant upper bound constraints
X_{ij}\leq 1
for off-diagonal entries
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
.
Despite being redundant for (
SDPk-

I

), these constraints do fasten the convergence of the ADMM, see e.g.,
[
10
,
11
,
37
,
46
]
.

Next, we consider the augmented Lagrangian function of (
20
) with respect to the coupling constraint
\hat{X}=Y
, which is defined as

L_{\beta}(\hat{X},Y,\Lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\langle\hat{I},\hat{X}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda,\hat{X}-Y\right\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\left\lVert\hat{X}-Y\right\rVert^{2},

(21)

where
\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
is the dual variable associated to the coupling constraint
\hat{X}=Y
, and
\beta>0
is a fixed penalty parameter (we specify our parameter values in Table
1
in Section
7
).
Starting from an initial triple
(\hat{X}^{0},Y^{0},\Lambda^{0})
, and given some stepsize parameter
\gamma
, the ADMM iteratively updates the current triple
(\hat{X}^{t},Y^{t},\Lambda^{t})
using the following update scheme:

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{t+1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}L_{\beta}(\hat{X},Y^{t},\Lambda^{t})

(22)

\displaystyle Y^{t+1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{Y\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n+1}}L_{\beta}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y,\Lambda^{t})

(23)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t+1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t}+\gamma\cdot\beta(\hat{X}^{t+1}-Y^{t+1}).

(24)

When
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2})
, the iterates
(\hat{X}^{t},Y^{t})
converge with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
to an optimal solution of (
20
), see
[
23
]
.

The efficiency of the ADMM depends on the complexity of the subproblems (
22
) and (
23
).
It can be shown that these problems reduce to the following projection problems, see
[
46
]
:

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}L_{\beta}(\hat{X},Y^{t},\Lambda^{t})=\text{arg min}_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left\lVert\hat{X}-\left(Y^{t}+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\hat{I}-\Lambda^{t}\right)\right)\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left(Y^{t}+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\hat{I}-\Lambda^{t}\right)\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}L_{\beta}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y,\Lambda^{t})=\text{arg min}_{Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}\left\lVert Y-\left(\hat{X}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\beta}\Lambda^{t}\right)\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}\left(\hat{X}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\beta}\Lambda^{t}\right).

(26)

The projection of a symmetric matrix onto
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}
is obtained through a spectral decomposition.
This decomposition is carried out in single precision instead of double precision to reduce its computational cost, see
[
53
]
.

We next discuss the projection onto
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Note that every matrix
\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is uniquely determined by the entries
\hat{X}_{ij}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq n,\{i,j\}\not\in\mathcal{E}
.
Therefore, we can reduce the projection problem onto
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
to a
weighted
projection problem (see Section
1.2
) where we only consider the
m\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=n+\binom{n}{2}-|\mathcal{E}|
free entries.
Concretely, given a matrix
\hat{U}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{00}&u_{0}^{\top}\\
u_{0}&U\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
, its projection onto
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
proceeds as follows:

1.

To account for the zeroth row and column, replace the diagonal of
U
by
\frac{1}{3}{\text{diag}\left(U\right)}+\frac{2}{3}u_{0}
, and then construct the vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that contains only the free entries of the newly obtained matrix.

2.

Find
u^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{P}^{w}_{\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}}(u)
, where weight vector
w\in\{2,3\}^{m}
assigns weight
2
to upper-triangular entries (accounting for the lower-triangular entries) and weight
3
to diagonal entries (accounting for the entries on the zeroth row and column), and

\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:0_{m}\leq x\leq e_{m},\quad a_{i}^{\top}x\leq b_{i}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\right\}.

(27)

The vectors
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are obtained from the matrices
A_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
.

3.

Map the vector
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}
to the corresponding matrix in
\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

To find
\mathcal{P}^{w}_{\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}}(u)
, we first rewrite
\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}
as the intersection of simpler sets:

\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}=[0,1]^{m}\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{H}_{i}\right),\quad\text{where }\mathcal{H}_{i}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:a_{i}^{\top}x\leq b_{i}\right\}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I}.

(28)

These sets are all easy to project onto. Indeed, the weighted projection onto the box is given by

\mathcal{P}^{w}_{[0,1]^{m}}(x)=\mathcal{P}_{[0,1]^{m}}(x)=\left(\min\{\max\{x_{j},0\},1\}\right)_{j\in[m]},

(29)

and for each valid inequality
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the weighted projection onto its corresponding halfspace is given by

\mathcal{P}^{w}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}(x)=x-\frac{\max\{a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i},0\}}{a_{i}^{\top}{\text{Diag}\left(w\right)}^{-1}a_{i}}{\text{Diag}\left(w\right)}^{-1}a_{i}.

(30)

When
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
, we have that
\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\mathcal{I}}=[0,1]^{m}
, so the projection is straightforward.
Otherwise, we apply Dykstra’s cyclic projection algorithm
[
5
]
, which is an iterative algorithm for projecting onto the intersection of convex sets.
This algorithm was successfully used within the CP-ADMM algorithms from
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
.
Importantly, these papers accelerate Dykstra’s algorithm by partitioning the valid inequalities into clusters of non-overlapping inequalities so that each iteration of Dykstra’s algorithm requires only one projection per cluster, rather than one projection per inequality.
We follow this approach, and refer to the aforementioned papers for the details.

5.2.1
).
Along the way, we find stronger valid upper bounds on
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
using a method that is described in Section
5.2.2
.

The algorithm is initialized with
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
, meaning that the first outer iteration amounts to solving (
SDPk
).
This initial iteration is significantly simpler than the subsequent ones, as the projection onto
\mathcal{X}^{vec}_{\emptyset}
is a projection onto the box and does not require Dykstra’s cyclic projection algorithm.
Therefore, initially the most time-consuming step is the projection onto
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}
, but once we start adding cuts, the bottleneck becomes Dykstra’s algorithm.
In order to improve computational efficiency, we employ a warm-start strategy where each outer iteration is initialized with the final solution from the previous outer iteration.

5.2.1
Stopping criteria

We terminate an inner while-loop when the primal and dual residual satisfy

\max\left\{\frac{\left\lVert X^{t+1}-Y^{t+1}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert X^{t+1}\right\rVert},\beta\frac{\left\lVert X^{t+1}-X^{t}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert X^{t+1}\right\rVert}\right\}\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{ADMM}},

see e.g.,
[
46
]
,
or when we reach a maximum of
maxInnerIter
iterations for the inner while-loop.
In the final outer iteration, we decrease
\varepsilon_{\texttt{ADMM}}
(see also
[
11
]
) and increase
maxInnerIter
to obtain a more accurate final valid upper bound.

We terminate the outer while-loop (and thus the entire algorithm) when
(i) the current valid upper bound rounded down to the nearest integer equals some known lower bound,
(ii) the improvement of the current valid upper bound over the previous one is less than
minImpr
,
(iii) there are fewer than
minIneq
violated inequalities,
or (iv) a time limit
timeLimitGlobal
is reached.

5.2.2
Valid upper bounds

We obtain valid upper bounds on
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
from the ADMM’s output using a procedure proposed by
46
.
The dual function of (
20
), with dual variable
\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}
, is given by

d(\Lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}},Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}\left\langle\hat{I},\hat{X}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda,\hat{X}-Y\right\rangle=\max_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left\langle\hat{I}-\Lambda,\hat{X}\right\rangle.

(31)

It follows from weak duality that
d(\Lambda)\geq\theta_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{G})

for any
\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}
.
In particular, this holds for
\Lambda=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}}(\Lambda^{t})
, where
\Lambda^{t}
is any intermediate iterate of the dual variable in the ADMM.

Note that
d(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}}(\Lambda^{t}))
can be computed in polynomial time as
\max_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathcal{I}}}\left\langle\hat{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}}(\Lambda^{t}),\hat{X}\right\rangle
is a linear program and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}}(\Lambda^{t})
can be found through a spectral decomposition of
\Lambda^{t}
. When
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
, this linear program has a closed-form solution, namely the matrix
\hat{X}=\begin{pmatrix}k&{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}^{\top}\\
{\text{diag}\left(X\right)}&X\end{pmatrix}
with
X
given by

X_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E},\\
\mathbbm{1}_{\{C_{ii}+2C_{i0}>0\}}&\text{if }i=j,\\
\mathbbm{1}_{\{C_{ij}>0\}}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\forall i,j\in[n],

(32)

where
C=\hat{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{-}}(\Lambda^{t})
.

5.2.3
Cut separation and selection

We consider the following classes of valid inequalities and corresponding separation procedures:

•

Generalized triangle inequalities (
9
) and (
10
): we enumerate all
\binom{n}{3}
triplets of vertices.

•

Generalized clique inequalities (
15
) and (
17
): to keep separation tractable, we initially restrict to maximal cliques up to size
5
and all cliques of size
6
, where our focus on maximal cliques is justified by Propositions
4.7
and
4.8
.
We enumerate these cliques using a variant of the Bron-Kerbosch algorithm
[
6
]
with a time limit
timeLimitCliques
.
For inequalities (
15
) we consider at most
maxCliques
cliques per outer iteration (where we randomly select a subset of the cliques if there are more than
maxCliques
options).
Similarly, for inequalities (
17
) we consider at most
maxCliquePairs
pairs of cliques per outer iteration.

Whenever we identify a violated inequality of the form (
15
) for a non-maximal clique
\mathcal{Q}
of size
6
and external vertex
\ell
, we greedily extend the clique to a larger clique to obtain a stronger cut.
Namely, starting from
\mathcal{Q}
, we iteratively add the vertex
i
with the highest value of
X_{i\ell}
among the vertices that are adjacent to all vertices in the current clique, continuing until the clique is maximal.

•

Generalized odd-hole inequalities (
18
): to keep separation tractable, we restrict to
5
-holes.
Recall from Propositions
4.9
and
4.10
that we do not need to consider even holes or cycles with chords.
We use a time limit
timeLimitHoles
for enumerating the
5
-holes, and consider at most
maxHoles
5-holes per outer iteration.

At every outer iteration of the CP-ADMM, we add up to
maxIneq
of the most violated inequalities, where an inequality only counts as violated if its violation is at least
minViol
.
Preliminary experiments showed that the generalized clique inequalities (
15
) are the most effective cuts among those considered.
Therefore, we implemented a two-phase approach in which we first consider only inequalities (
15
).
Once either the improvement of the current valid upper bound over the previous one is less than
minImprPhase1
or there are fewer than
minIneqPhase1
violated inequalities of type (
15
), we transition to a second phase where the remaining inequality types are also considered.

Finally, we limit the maximum number of new cuts in which each variable may appear to
maxCutsPerVar
. This promotes cut diversity and makes it easier to partition the cuts into a small number of clusters of non-overlapping cuts, speeding up the algorithm considerably. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to implement this feature in the context of the CP-ADMM.

6
The integer ADMM

In this section, we present the INT-ADMM for computing feasible solutions to (
BSDPk
). Our algorithm is based on the
\ell_{p}
-Box ADMM from
[
56
]
.
That algorithm is designed to find feasible solutions to arbitrary binary optimization programs, and was successfully used e.g., in
[
29
]
and
[
57
]
.
The
\ell_{p}
-Box ADMM relies on the observation that, for any
p>0
,

\{0,1\}^{n}=[0,1]^{n}\cap\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert x-\frac{1}{2}e\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=\frac{n}{2^{p}}\right\}.

(33)

That is, the set of binary vectors can be written as the intersection of the unit box with the
\ell_{p}
-sphere centered at
\frac{1}{2}e
with radius
\frac{n^{1/p}}{2}
.
For
p=2
, there exists a closed-form expression for projecting onto a sphere.

Extending this idea to (
BSDPk
), we first split its constraints into three components, each admitting an efficient projection: we have a variable
\hat{X}
that satisfies the affine (including box) constraints, a variable
Y
that satisfies the PSD constraint, and in addition, a third variable
Z
that satisfies a sphere constraint.
In more detail, given that we work in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
rather than
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and also that entry
(0,0)
should be equal to
k
, we consider the
\ell_{2}
-sphere in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
centered at
C\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2}J_{n+1}+\begin{pmatrix}k&0_{n}^{\top}\\
0_{n}&\textbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix}
with radius
(n+1)/2
:

\mathcal{Z}=\left\{Z\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}:\left\lVert Z-C\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{4}\right\}.

(34)

Note that
\mathcal{Z}
is a non-convex set, which impacts the convergence of the INT-ADMM, as explained in Section
6.1
.
Then, letting
\hat{\mathcal{X}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{\emptyset}
(see (
19
)), we can rewrite (
BSDPk
) as follows:

\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})=\max\left\{\left\langle\hat{I},\hat{X}\right\rangle:\hat{X}=Y,\hat{X}=Z,\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}},Y\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n+1},Z\in\mathcal{Z}\right\}.

(35)

Next, we consider the augmented Lagrangian function of (
35
) with respect to the two coupling constraints
\hat{X}=Y
and
\hat{X}=Z
, which is given by

L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X},Y,Z,\Lambda,M)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\langle\hat{I},\hat{X}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Lambda,\hat{X}-Y\right\rangle-\left\langle M,\hat{X}-Z\right\rangle-\frac{\beta_{Y}}{2}\left\lVert\hat{X}-Y\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\beta_{Z}}{2}\left\lVert\hat{X}-Z\right\rVert^{2},

(36)

where
\Lambda,M\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
are the dual variables associated to the coupling constraints
\hat{X}=Y
and
\hat{X}=Z
, respectively, and
\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}>0
are fixed penalty parameters.
We then obtain the following update rules:

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\text{arg max}_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X},Y^{t},Z^{t},\Lambda^{t},M^{t})

(37)

\displaystyle Y^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\text{arg max}_{Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y,Z^{t},\Lambda^{t},M^{t})

(38)

\displaystyle Z^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\text{arg max}_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y^{t+1},Z,\Lambda^{t},M^{t})

(39)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{t}+\beta_{Y}(\hat{X}^{t+1}-Y^{t+1})

(40)

\displaystyle M^{t+1}

\displaystyle=M^{t}+\beta_{Z}(\hat{X}^{t+1}-Z^{t+1}).

(41)

The maximization problems (
37
)-(
39
) can be rewritten as the following projection problems:

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{\hat{X}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X},Y^{t},Z^{t},\Lambda^{t},M^{t})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{\hat{X}}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{Y}+\beta_{Z}}\left(\beta_{Y}Y^{t}+\beta_{Z}Z^{t}+\hat{I}-\Lambda^{t}-M^{t}\right)\right)

(42)

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y,Z^{t},\Lambda^{t},M^{t})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}\left(\hat{X}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\beta_{Y}}\Lambda^{t}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle\text{arg max}_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}L_{\beta_{Y},\beta_{Z}}(\hat{X}^{t+1},Y^{t+1},Z,\Lambda^{t},M^{t})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Z}}\left(\hat{X}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\beta_{Z}}M^{t}\right).

(44)

The projections onto
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}
were already discussed in Section
5.1
.
The projection onto
\mathcal{Z}
admits the following closed-form expression:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Z}}(Z)=\frac{n+1}{2}\frac{Z-C}{\left\lVert Z-C\right\rVert}+C.

(45)

6.1
Achieving convergence and escaping local optima

Given that
\mathcal{Z}
is non-convex, the INT-ADMM is not guaranteed to converge, and if it converges, the obtained solution is not necessarily optimal for (
35
).
Preliminary experiments showed that the solution quality strongly depends on the choice of
\beta_{Y}
and
\beta_{Z}
.
As proposed in
[
56
]
, we set
\beta_{Y}=\beta_{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta
, and we gradually increase
\beta
over the course of the algorithm.
We initialize
\beta
to
\beta^{0}
and then multiply by a factor
\beta_{incr}>1
after every iteration.
Therefore, the emphasis initially lies on obtaining a high objective value, while over time the focus shifts to enforcing feasibility.
Using the parameter values specified in Table
2
in Section
7
, our algorithm consistently converged on all benchmark instances to a solution satisfying the following criterion:

\max\left\{\frac{\left\lVert\hat{X}^{t}-Y^{t}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert\hat{X}^{t}\right\rVert},\frac{\left\lVert\hat{X}^{t}-Z^{t}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert\hat{X}^{t}\right\rVert}\right\}\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{INT-ADMM}}.

Upon meeting this criterion, the matrix
\hat{X}^{t}
(whose entries are close to integer) is rounded to a fully integer matrix, after which its feasibility is verified.

Our algorithm also contains a novel mechanism for escaping local optima. Namely, whenever the above convergence criterion is satisfied, we save the feasible solution found, and then multiply
\beta
by a factor
\beta_{decr}\in(0,1)
(taking care that
\beta
does not drop below a minimum value
\beta_{min})
, thereby shifting the emphasis back to the objective value.
The INT-ADMM then continues for at least
10
more iterations until the convergence criterion is met again.
This process is repeated until the algorithm has not improved the best solution so far for
maxTriesWithoutImpr
consecutive tries, or the current solution value matches a known upper bound.

6.2
Reducing runtime

Finally, we discuss some additional features of the INT-ADMM that are aimed to reduce runtime.

First, we initialize the INT-ADMM using the final solution
\hat{X}^{*}
obtained from the CP-ADMM.
Specifically, we set
\hat{X}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{\hat{\mathcal{X}}}(\hat{X}^{*})
,
Y^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{S}^{n+1}_{+}}(\hat{X}^{*})
,
Z^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\hat{X}^{*})
,
\Lambda^{0}=\beta_{Y}(\hat{X}^{0}-Y^{0})
and
M^{0}=\beta_{Z}(\hat{X}^{0}-Z^{0})
.
Furthermore, we experimented with additionally including in
\mathcal{Z}
some of the equality constraints that are already in
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
.
Among the tested variants, only adding the constraint
Z_{00}=k
proved beneficial.
The projection onto
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{Z\in\mathcal{Z}:Z_{00}=k\}
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}(Z)=\frac{\sqrt{(n+1)^{2}-1}}{2}\frac{Z^{\prime}-C}{\left\lVert Z^{\prime}-C\right\rVert}+C-\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0_{n}^{\top}\\
0_{n}&\textbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }Z^{\prime}=Z+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}+k-Z_{00}&0_{n}^{\top}\\
0_{n}&\textbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(46)

Finally, observe that the
Y
- and
Z
-updates (
43
) and (
44
) are independent of each other and could therefore be carried out in parallel.
In our implementation, however, these updates are performed sequentially.

7.3
Comparing the effectiveness of valid inequalities

In this section, we compare several configurations of the CP-ADMM that include inequalities (
9
), (
10
), (
15
), (

) and/or (

).
For the results presented in this subsection only, each configuration was run with a time limit of 900 seconds.
We restrict attention to 36 out of the 68 benchmark instances for which (i) at least one configuration yields a stronger bound than
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, (ii) none of the configurations attains optimality, and (iii) the majority of the configurations terminate within the time limit.

Figures
1
and
2
summarize the results using box and whisker plots.
Each box extends from the first quartile to the third quartile of the data, with a solid line marking the median. Whiskers extend to the farthest data point lying within 1.5 times the inter-quartile range from the box.
Outliers are shown by individual markers, and means are indicated by dashed lines.

Figure
1
shows the effect of our valid inequalities on the obtained upper bounds.
The plot on the left compares upper bounds obtained by configurations in which inequalities of a single type are added to (
SDPk
).
The right plot focuses on the marginal benefit of various configurations that include generalized clique inequalities (
15
) on top of the configuration that only includes those inequalities.
Figure
2
concerns the runtimes of each of the configurations.

When adding inequalities of a single type to (
SDPk
), the generalized clique inequalities (
15
) typically yield the best bounds by a considerable margin.
On the other hand, generalized triangle inequalities (
10
) do not provide any improvement in bounds when tested on 11 instances for which
k\leq 2
.
Recall that those inequalities are redundant for
k>2
.
Conversely, triangle inequalities (
9
) do yield improved bounds.
Better bounds are typically obtained by adding generalized clique inequalities (
17
) or generalized odd-hole inequalities (
18
), with their relative effectiveness varying across the instances.
In particular, inequalities (
18
) are highly effective for the instance with graph myciel6, which does not contain any cliques of size
3
or larger (see the outlier on the top-right of the first plot).
This instance is excluded from the second plot, as it would otherwise dominate the scale of the y-axis.
In terms of computational cost, the CP-ADMM typically requires most runtime to solve the relaxations with generalized clique inequalities (
15
) and (
17
), followed by the relaxation with generalized odd-hole inequalities (
18
).

Considering the marginal benefit of various configurations that include (
15
) on top of the configuration that only includes (
15
), we observe the following.
The largest marginal benefits are obtained using generalized clique inequalities (
17
), but these bound improvements come at a significant computational cost.
The generalized odd-hole inequalities (
18
) also provide significant marginal improvements for many instances, but at a lower computational cost.
Despite being effective on their own, the generalized triangle inequalities (
9
) only lead to limited improvements on top of the other configurations that include inequalities (

).
This observation is in line with the computational results of
[

]
, where the M
k

.

7.4
Limiting the number of new cuts per variable

In this section, we report on the considerable benefit of limiting the maximum number of new cuts in which each variable may appear.
Recall that in our default configuration of the CP-ADMM,
maxCutsPerVar
is set to
5
.
On the 45 instances where the default configuration added at least one cut, we also ran a version of the algorithm without the
maxCutsPerVar
limit.
On average, the default configuration requires only 50% the runtime, 73% the number of cuts and 42% the amount of time per inner iteration compared to the configuration without the limit.
One explanation for the decrease in runtime is that the
maxCutsPerVar
limit makes it easier to partition the cuts into a small number of clusters of non-overlapping cuts, thereby reducing the runtime required for Dykstra’s cyclic projection algorithm.
On instances where the configuration without the
maxCutsPerVar
limit reaches the time limit, the default configuration often yields stronger upper bounds, as it can perform more iterations in the same amount of time.

Lower bounds on the chromatic number

33

k
CS problem to compute lower bounds for the chromatic number
\chi(\mathcal{G})
of a graph.
In particular, they used the following relation

\chi(\mathcal{G})\geq\max\left\{k:UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})<n\right\}+1,

(47)

where
UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})
is an upper bound on
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
for
k\in[n-1]
.
In this section, we show that our improved upper bounds for the M
k
CS problem yield improved lower bounds on
\chi(\mathcal{G})
.

To compute the required upper bounds, we use a slightly modified version of the CP-ADMM.
Namely, we adapt its stopping criteria by setting
minIneq
to
1
and by not requiring any longer a minimum improvement
minImpr
in the valid upper bound per outer iteration.
Moreover, the CP-ADMM is terminated early whenever a valid upper bound strictly smaller than
n
is found.
We additionally compute a valid upper bound after every 100 inner iterations during the first outer iteration.

A straightforward approach would be to sequentially call the CP-ADMM for
k=1,2,\ldots
until reaching a value of
k
for which
UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})\geq n
.
Instead, we employ a more efficient search strategy that reduces the number of calls to the CP-ADMM. Namely, we exploit the following relation:

\chi(\mathcal{G})\geq\left\lceil\frac{kn}{\left\lfloor UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})\right\rfloor}\right\rceil\geq k+1\quad\forall k\in[n-1]\text{ with }UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})<n.

(48)

Concretely, we proceed iteratively over values of
k
, starting from
k=1
.
For each considered value of
k
, we compute an upper bound
UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})
on
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
using the CP-ADMM. If
UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})<n
, we update
k
to
\left\lceil\frac{kn}{\left\lfloor UB_{k}(\mathcal{G})\right\rfloor}\right\rceil
and continue the algorithm.
Otherwise, the algorithm terminates and the final value of
k
is a valid lower bound on
\chi(\mathcal{G})
.
We also tested a binary search variant of the algorithm, but it performed worse in terms of runtime on average.

8
Conclusion

We considered the M
k
CS problem and studied an SDP relaxation that was derived from a BSDP formulation of the problem.
Among other results, we proved that the optimal solution of the SDP relaxation (
SDPk
), denoted by
\theta_{k}(\mathcal{G})
, is sandwiched between
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{G})
and the
k
-clique cover number
\Psi_{k}(\overline{\mathcal{G}})
, implying that the M
k
CS problem is solvable in polynomial time up to fixed precision for
k
-perfect graphs.
Moreover, we proposed two novel families of valid inequalities, namely generalized BQP inequalities and generalized rank inequalities.
We further considered computationally tractable special cases of these inequalities, which we used to strengthen the SDP relaxation.
We efficiently solved the strengthened relaxation using the CP-ADMM.
We also introduced the INT-ADMM, which yields feasible solutions for the BSDP formulation of the problem.
The INT-ADMM was obtained from the standard ADMM by adding a third matrix variable that satisfies a sphere constraint.
While this introduces nonconvexity, a gradual increase of the penalty parameter
\beta
, combined with a mechanism to escape local optima, led in practice to high-quality feasible solutions.

Our computational experiments showed that our valid upper and lower bounds outperform existing bounds on most benchmark instances.
Furthermore, we compared the effectiveness of our valid inequalities, showing in particular that while the generalized triangle inequalities are effective on their own, they have little added value on top of the generalized clique and odd-hole inequalities.
We also found that limiting the number of new cuts per variable considerably improves the performance of the CP-ADMM.
In addition, our valid upper bounds for the M
k
CS problem were shown to yield competitive lower bounds for the graph coloring problem.

This research can be extended in several directions.
For example, it would be interesting to incorporate the CP-ADMM and INT-ADMM in a branch-and-bound framework to obtain optimal solutions.
Moreover, the proposed generalized BQP inequalities may be useful to strengthen matrix-lifting relaxations of other packing or partitioning problems, while the generalized rank inequalities could be used for problems involving conflicts, such as the multiple knapsack problem with conflicts, the bin packing problem with conflicts, or quadratic variants thereof.
Finally, we expect that the INT-ADMM can be successfully applied for other optimization problems that can be formulated as a BSDP problem, especially when the SDP relaxation obtained by relaxing integrality can be solved efficiently by the STD-ADMM or CP-ADMM.
