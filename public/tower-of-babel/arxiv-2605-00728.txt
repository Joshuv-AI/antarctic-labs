Sion’s minimax theorem and the proximal point algorithm in Hadamard spaces
Fumiaki Kohsaka

Abstract
. We obtain Sion’s minimax theorem in Hadamard spaces and discuss its applications. Among other things, we study several fundamental properties of resolvents of saddle functions in Hadamard spaces. An application to the proximal point algorithm for minimax problems in Hadamard spaces are also included.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study minimax problems in Hadamard spaces.
We first obtain Sion’s minimax theorem in Hadamard spaces.
We next study several fundamental properties of resolvents of
saddle functions in Hadamard spaces. We finally obtain
existence and convergence theorems on the proximal point algorithm
for minimax problems in Hadamard spaces.

Minimax problems are one of fundamental problems in
nonlinear analysis, nonlinear optimization, game theory, and so on.
This is a problem of finding a point

(x_{0},y_{0})
in a product set
X\times Y
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle f(x,y_{0})\leq f(x_{0},y_{0})\leq f(x_{0},y)

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
, where

f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function.
The set of all saddle points of
f
is denoted by

\Saddle(f)
.
Defining a real function
F
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)=f(x,y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime},y)

for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
,
we know that
(x_{0},y_{0})
is an element of
\Saddle(f)

if and only if it is an equilibrium point of
F
in the
sense of Blum and Oettli
[
4
]
, i.e.,

(1.3)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr)\geq 0

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.

In 1958, Sion
[
27
]
proved the following minimax theorem
in Hausdorff topological vector spaces;
see also
[
28
, Theorem 16]
and
[
29
, Theorem 6.3.3]
:

Theorem 1.1

(Sion
[
27
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

Let
E
and
F
be real Hausdorff topological vector spaces,

X
a nonempty convex subset of
E
,

Y
a nonempty compact convex subset of
F
,
and
f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function satisfying

(i)

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
is upper semicontinuous
and quasi-concave for each
y\in Y
;

(ii)

f(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous
and quasi-convex for each
x\in X
.

Then the equality

(1.4)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)=\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)

holds.

If the set
X
is also compact in Theorem
1.1
,
the following minimax equality

(1.5)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y)=\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)

holds. This implies that
\Saddle(f)
is nonempty.

In 1988, Komiya
[
20
]
found an elementary proof of Sion’s minimax theorem
in topological vector spaces without using either Brouwer’s fixed point theorem or
KKM theorem
[
14
,
5
]
.

The proximal point algorithm first introduced by
Martinet
[
22
]
is a well known method for
approximating a solution to the equation

(1.6)

\displaystyle 0\in Au

for a maximal monotone operator
A\colon H\to 2^{H}
in a real
Hilbert space
H
. The set of all points
u\in H

satisfying (
1.6
) is denoted by
A^{-1}(0)
.
This algorithm generates a sequence
\{x_{n}\}

by
x_{1}\in H
and

(1.7)

\displaystyle 0\in Ax_{n+1}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n+1}-x_{n})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\{\lambda_{n}\}
is a sequence of positive real numbers.
In 1976, Rockafellar
[
26
]
proved
under the condition
\inf_{n}\lambda_{n}>0
that

\{x_{n}\}
is bounded if and only if
A^{-1}(0)
is nonempty;
if
A^{-1}(0)
is nonempty, then
\{x_{n}\}
is
weakly convergent to an element of
A^{-1}(0)
.

In 1968, using the concept of subdifferentials of
convex functions, Rockafellar
[
25
]
constructed
maximal monotone operators associated with
saddle functions in Banach spaces.
Further, Rockafellar
[
26
]
applied the proximal point algorithm to
the problem of finding saddle points in Hilbert spaces
as follows; see also
[
30
]
:

Theorem 1.2

(Rockafellar
[
26
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be nonempty closed convex subsets of
real Hilbert spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, respectively,

f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function satisfying

(i)

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
is upper semicontinuous
and concave for each
y\in Y
;

(ii)

f(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous
and convex for each
x\in X
,

and
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
a sequence in
X\times Y
defined by

(x_{1},y_{1})\in X\times Y
and
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})

is the unique saddle point of the function

(1.8)

\displaystyle(z,w)\mapsto f(z,w)-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}\left\lVert z-x_{n}\right\rVert_{H_{1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}\left\lVert w-y_{n}\right\rVert_{H_{2}}^{2}

on
X\times Y
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
where
\{\lambda_{n}\}
a sequence of positive real numbers such that

\inf_{n}\lambda_{n}>0
. Then

\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is bounded if and only if
\Saddle(f)

is nonempty. Further, if
\Saddle(f)
is nonempty, then

\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is weakly convergent to an element of

\Saddle(f)
.

Later, applying convergence theorems
obtained in
[
15
,
9
]
,
Kohsaka and Takahashi
[
16
]
obtained
weak and strong convergence theorems for
minimax problems in Banach spaces;
see also
[
17
,
30
]
.
Aoyama, Kimura, and Takahashi
[
1
]

studied the relation between
the zero point problem for maximal monotone operators and
the equilibrium problem for two variable functions in Banach spaces.

In recent years, the problems in convex analysis and fixed point theory
have been studied in the more general nonlinear settings
such as geodesic metric spaces including complete
\textup{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces, where

\kappa
is a real number. This space can be seen as
both a nonlinear generalization of Hilbert spaces
and a nonsmooth generalization of Riemannian manifolds
with bounded sectional curvature.
In particular, using results on resolvents of convex functions
obtained in
[
8
,
23
]
,
Bačák
[
2
]
obtained a
\Delta
-convergence
theorem for this algorithm for proper lower semicontinuous convex
functions in Hadamard spaces, i.e., complete
\textup{CAT}(0)
spaces.
More recently, Kimura and Kohsaka
[
11
]
studied two
modifications of the algorithm for such functions in Hadamard spaces.

Assuming the convex hull finite property
on Hadamard spaces, Kimura and Kishi
[
10
]

studied equilibrium problems in Hadamard spaces.
Applying KKM theorem in Hadamard spaces obtained by
Niculescu and Rovenţa
[
24
]
,
Kimura and Kishi
[
10
]
discussed fundamental properties of
resolvents of bifunctions in Hadamard spaces.
Note that a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
E
has the convex hull finite
property if each continuous mapping
of
\overline{\Co}\,F
into itself
has a fixed point whenever
F

is a nonempty finite subset of
E
.
See also Kumam and Chaipunya
[
21
]

on the study of the equilibrium problem in Hadamard spaces without
the convex hull finite property.
More recently, Kimura
[
12
]

also obtained results for solving the equilibrium problem
in complete
\textup{CAT}(1)
spaces with the convex hull finite property.

This paper is organized as follows:
In Section
2
, we give
necessary definitions and recall some results needed in this paper.
In Section
3
, we obtain an analogous result of
Theorem
1.1
in Hadamard spaces
without assuming either the convex hull finite property or
the metric completeness.
In Section
4
, we obtain saddle point theorem
for coercive saddle functions in Hadamard spaces.
In Section
5
, we give the definition of
resolvents of saddle functions and discuss their fundamental properties.
In Section
6
, we apply our results in this paper
to the proximal point algorithm for saddle functions in Hadamard spaces
and obtain a generalization of Theorem
1.2

in Hadamard spaces.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
be the sets of all positive integers and
all real numbers, respectively. We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{2}

the two dimensional Euclidean space with
norm
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
.
We denote by
(-\infty,\infty]
and
[-\infty,\infty)

the sets
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, respectively.
For a nonempty set
X
and a mapping
T\colon X\to X
,
we denote by
\Fix(T)
the set of all fixed points of
T
, i.e.,

\Fix(T)
is the set of all
u\in X
such that
Tu=u
.
We denote by
\Argmin_{X}f
or
\Argmin_{y\in X}f(y)

the set of all minimizers of a function
f\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
, i.e.,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Argmin_{X}f=\Argmin_{y\in X}f(y)=\bigl\{u\in X:f(u)=\inf f(X)\bigr\}.

We also denote by
\Argmax_{X}g
or
\Argmax_{y\in X}g(y)

the set of all maximizers of a function
g\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
, i.e.,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Argmax_{X}g=\Argmax_{y\in X}g(y)=\bigl\{u\in X:g(u)=\sup g(X)\bigr\}.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A mapping
T\colon X\to X
is said to be:

•

nonexpansive if
d(Tx,Ty)\leq d(x,y)

for all
x,y\in X
;

•

quasi-nonexpansive if

\Fix(T)
is nonempty and
d(Tx,y)\leq d(x,y)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in\Fix(T)
;

•

firmly metrically nonspreading
[
19
]
if

(2.3)

\displaystyle 2d(Tx,Ty)^{2}+d(Tx,x)^{2}+d(Ty,y)^{2}\leq d(Tx,y)^{2}+d(Ty,x)^{2}

for all
x,y\in X
;

•

metrically nonspreading
[
19
]
if

(2.4)

\displaystyle 2d(Tx,Ty)^{2}\leq d(Tx,y)^{2}+d(Ty,x)^{2}

for all
x,y\in X
.

It is obvious that if
T
is firmly metrically nonspreading, then
it is metrically nonspreading.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. The space
X
is said to be
geodesic if for each
x,y\in X
, there exists
a mapping
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to X
such that

\gamma(0)=x
,
\gamma(1)=y
, and

(2.5)

\displaystyle d\bigl(\gamma(s),\gamma(t)\bigr)=d(x,y)\left\lvert s-t\right\rvert

for all
s,t\in[0,1]
.
The mapping
\gamma
is called a normalized geodesic from
x
to
y
.
Its image is denoted by
[x,y]_{\gamma}

and is called a geodesic segment between
x
and
y
.
The convex combination between
x
and
y
is defined by

(2.6)

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)x\oplus_{\gamma}\alpha y=\gamma(\alpha)

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
The space
X
is also said to be uniquely geodesic if
for each
x,y\in X
, there exists a unique
normalized geodesic
\gamma
from
x
to
y
.
In this case, the geodesic segment and
the convex combination between
x
and
y
are simply denoted by
[x,y]

and
(1-\alpha)x\oplus\alpha y
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
respectively.

A geodesic metric space
(X,d)
is said to be a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space
if

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d\bigl((1-\alpha)x\oplus_{\gamma_{1}}\alpha y,(1-\beta)x\oplus_{\gamma_{2}}\beta z\bigr)\\
&\quad\leq\left\lVert(1-\alpha)\overline{x}+\alpha\overline{y}-\bigl((1-\beta)\overline{x}+\beta\overline{z}\bigr)\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\end{split}

whenever
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
,

x,y,z\in X
,

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are normalized geodesics
from
x
to
y
and from
x
to
z
, respectively,

\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
and

(2.8)

\displaystyle d(x,y)=\left\lVert\overline{x}-\overline{y}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2}},\quad d(y,z)=\left\lVert\overline{y}-\overline{z}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2}},\quad d(z,x)=\left\lVert\overline{z}-\overline{x}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}.

Note that if
x,y,z\in X
, then the triangle inequality on
X

implies that there exist

\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that (
2.8
) holds.

In fact, we may assume that
d(x,y)\geq d(y,z)
and

d(x,y)\geq d(z,x)
and set

(2.9)

\displaystyle a=d(y,z),\quad b=d(z,x),\quad c=d(x,y).

If
c=0
, then
a=b=c=0
and hence it suffices to set

\overline{x}=\overline{y}=\overline{z}=(0,0)
.
If
c>0
, then it suffices to
set

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{x}&=(0,0),\quad\overline{y}=\bigl(c,0\bigr),\\
\overline{z}&=\left(\frac{b^{2}+c^{2}-a^{2}}{2c},\frac{\sqrt{(a+b+c)(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b)}}{2c}\right),\end{split}

where
\overline{z}=(s,t)
is a solution to the simultaneous equation

(2.11)

\displaystyle s^{2}+t^{2}=b^{2},\quad(s-c)^{2}+t^{2}=a^{2}.

It is clear that if
(X,d)
is a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space, then
it is uniquely geodesic.

Every complete
\textup{CAT}(0)
space is called an Hadamard space.
It is known that every nonempty closed convex subset of a real Hilbert space,
every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree,
and every complete simply connected Riemannian manifold
with nonpositive sectional curvature are
Hadamard spaces.
See
[
3
,
6
,
7
]
for more details
on geodesic metric spaces and
\textup{CAT}(0)
spaces.

If
(X,d)
is a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space, then
the following fundamental inequalities

(2.12)

\displaystyle d\bigl((1-\alpha)x\oplus\alpha y,z\bigr)\leq(1-\alpha)d(x,z)+\alpha d(y,z)

and

(2.13)

\displaystyle d\bigl((1-\alpha)x\oplus\alpha y,z\bigr)^{2}\leq(1-\alpha)d(x,z)^{2}+\alpha d(y,z)^{2}-\alpha(1-\alpha)d(x,y)^{2}

hold for all
x,y,z\in X
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

We know the following results:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.3.3]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a geodesic metric space.
Then
X
is a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space if and only if the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\{d(x_{1},x_{4})^{2}+d(x_{2},x_{3})^{2}-d(x_{1},x_{3})^{2}-d(x_{2},x_{4})^{2}\right\}\leq d(x_{1},x_{2})d(x_{3},x_{4})

holds for all
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in X
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
X
be an Hadamard space and
T\colon X\to X
a metrically nonspreading mapping.
Then
\Fix(T)
is nonempty if and only if
\{T^{n}x\}
is bounded for some
x\in X
.
In this case,
\{T^{n}x\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to an element of
\Fix(T)

for each
x\in X
.

As in the proof of
[
18
, Lemma 2.5]
, we can prove the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
I
be a nonempty closed subset of
\mathbb{R}
,

\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
a net in
I
such that

\{t_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}}
is bounded
for some
\alpha_{0}\in A
, and

f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
a nondecreasing and continuous function.
Then

\displaystyle f\left(\limsup_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}\right)=\limsup_{\alpha}f(t_{\alpha}).

If
C
is a nonempty closed convex subset of an Hadamard space
and
x\in X
, then there exists a unique
\hat{x}\in C
such that

(2.15)

\displaystyle d(\hat{x},x)=\inf_{y\in C}d(y,x).

The metric projection
P_{C}
of
X
onto
C
is defined by

P_{C}(x)=\hat{x}
for all
x\in X
.
It is well known that
P_{C}
is firmly metrically nonspreading.
This implies that if
x\in X
and
y\in C
, then

(2.16)

\displaystyle d\bigl(P_{C}(x),y\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(P_{C}(x),x\bigr)^{2}\leq d(x,y)^{2}.

It also follows from the firmly metrical nonspreadingness of
P_{C}

and Theorem
2.1
that

P_{C}
is nonexpansive.
In particular,
P_{C}
is quasi-nonexpansive.

Let
(X,d)
be a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space and

\{x_{n}\}
a sequence in
X
. Then
the asymptotic center
\AC\bigl(\{x_{n}\}\bigr)
of

\{x_{n}\}
is defined by

(2.17)

\displaystyle\AC\bigl(\{x_{n}\}\bigr)=\left\{u\in X:\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(u,x_{n})=\inf_{y\in X}\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(y,x_{n})\right\}.

It is obvious that
\AC\bigl(\{x_{n}\}\bigr)=X

if
\{x_{n}\}
is unbounded.
The sequence
\{x_{n}\}
is said to be
\Delta
-convergent to
p\in X

if
\AC\bigl(\{x_{n_{i}}\}\bigr)=\{p\}

for each subsequence
\{x_{n_{i}}\}
of
\{x_{n}\}
.
In this case,
\{x_{n}\}
is bounded.
If
\{x_{n}\}
is a sequence in
a nonempty closed convex subset
X
of a real Hilbert space
and
p\in X
, then

\{x_{n}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
p

if and only if it is weakly convergent to
p
.
We denote by
\omega_{\Delta}\bigl(\{x_{n}\}\bigr)

the set of all
p\in X
such that
there exists a subsequence
\{x_{n_{i}}\}
of
\{x_{n}\}

such that
\{x_{n_{i}}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
p
.
For a net
\{x_{\alpha}\}
in
X
, we can
similarly define its asymptotic center by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\AC\bigl(\{x_{\alpha}\}\bigr)=\left\{u\in X:\limsup_{\alpha}d(u,x_{\alpha})=\inf_{y\in X}\limsup_{\alpha}d(y,x_{\alpha})\right\}.

It is said to be
\Delta
-convergent to
p\in X
if

\AC\bigl(\{x_{\alpha_{\beta}}\}\bigr)=\{p\}

for each subnet
\{x_{\alpha_{\beta}}\}
of

\{x_{\alpha}\}
.
For a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
and
p\in X
,
it seems not to be clear whether

\{x_{n}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
p

as a sequence if and only if

\{x_{n}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
p

as a net. The following lemma answers to this question
affirmatively:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a
\textup{CAT}(0)
space and
\bar{x}
an element in
X
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

If
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
is a net in
X
,
then
\{x_{\alpha}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
\bar{x}

if and only if

\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}}
is bounded
for some
\alpha_{0}\in A
and

\{P_{[\bar{x},y]}x_{\alpha}\}
is convergent to
\bar{x}

for all
y\in X
;

(ii)

if
\{x_{n}\}
is a sequence in
X
,
then
\{x_{n}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
\bar{x}

as a sequence
if and only if

\{x_{n}\}
is bounded and

\{P_{[\bar{x},y]}x_{n}\}
is convergent to
\bar{x}

for all
y\in X
;

(iii)

if
\{x_{n}\}
is a sequence in
X
,
then
\{x_{n}\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
\bar{x}

as a sequence
if and only if
it is
\Delta
-convergent to
\bar{x}

as a net.

Proof.

Note that if
u,v\in X
, then

[u,v]
is a nonempty compact convex subset of
X
.
Thus the metric projection
P_{[u,v]}
of
X
onto
[u,v]

is well defined and
satisfies the following inequality:

(2.19)

\displaystyle d\bigl(z,P_{[u,v]}x\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(P_{[u,v]}x,x\bigr)^{2}\leq d(z,x)^{2}

for all
z\in[u,v]
and
x\in X
.

We first prove the if part of (i).
Suppose that
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}}
is bounded
for some
\alpha_{0}\in A
and

\{P_{[\bar{x},y]}x_{\alpha}\}
is convergent to
\bar{x}

for all
y\in X
. Let
y\in X\setminus\{\bar{x}\}
be given
and let
\{x_{\alpha_{\beta}}\}_{\beta\in B}
be a subnet of
\{x_{\alpha}\}
.
Note that there exists
\beta_{0}\in B
such that

\beta_{0}\leq\beta
implies that
\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{\beta}
.
Thus
\{x_{\alpha_{\beta}}\}_{\beta\geq\beta_{0}}
is bounded.
This implies that all the upper limits in the proof of
this part are finite.
Set
P=P_{[\bar{x},y]}
.
Since

(2.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Bigl\lvert d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}-d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}\Bigr\lvert\\
&\quad\leq\bigl\{d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})+d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})\bigr\}d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},\bar{x})\to 0,\end{split}

we have

(2.21)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\beta}d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}=\limsup_{\beta}d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}.

Then we have

(2.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\limsup_{\beta}d(y,x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}&\geq\limsup_{\beta}\bigl\{d(y,Px_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}+d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}\bigr\}\\
&=d(y,\bar{x})^{2}+\limsup_{\beta}d(Px_{\alpha_{\beta}},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}\\
&=d(y,\bar{x})^{2}+\limsup_{\beta}d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}>\limsup_{\beta}d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}.\end{split}

Using Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

(2.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Bigl\{\limsup_{\beta}d(y,x_{\alpha_{\beta}})\Bigr\}^{2}&=\limsup_{\beta}d(y,x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}\\
&>\limsup_{\beta}d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})^{2}=\Bigl\{\limsup_{\beta}d(\bar{x},x_{\alpha_{\beta}})\Bigr\}^{2}.\end{split}

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
an Hadamard space,
(Y,d_{Y})
an bounded Hadamard space, and

f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function satisfying

(i)

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
is upper semicontinuous
and quasi-concave for each
y\in Y
;

(ii)

f(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous
and quasi-convex for each
x\in X
.

Then the equality

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)=\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)

holds.

Remark 3.2
.

For each
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, the functions

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
and
f(x,\cdot\,)
are
\Delta
-upper semicontinuous
and
\Delta
-lower semicontinuous, respectively.
Since
Y
is a bounded Hadamard space,
it follows from Lemma
2.10
that

f(x,\,\cdot\,)
has a minimizer. Thus
\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}

for all
x\in X
. This implies that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\in(-\infty,\infty].

On the other hand, the function

y\mapsto\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)

is a
\Delta
-lower semicontinuous function of
Y
into
(-\infty,\infty]
.
Then it follows from Lemma
2.10
that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)\in(-\infty,\infty].

Further, since
f(x,y)\leq\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\leq\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)

for all

and hence

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\leq\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y).

Thus we need to prove the reverse inequality.

Lemma 3.3
.

\displaystyle A=C^{\prime}_{x_{1}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad B=C^{\prime}_{x_{2}}.

It is clear that

be the sets defined by

(3.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I&=\bigl\{\lambda\in[0,1]:C_{(1-\lambda)x_{1}\oplus\lambda x_{2}}\subset A\bigr\};\\
J&=\bigl\{\lambda\in[0,1]:C_{(1-\lambda)x_{1}\oplus\lambda x_{2}}\subset B\bigr\}.\end{split}

Then we have from
A\cap B=\emptyset
that
I\cap J
is empty.
Since
C_{x_{1}}\subset C^{\prime}_{x_{1}}=A
, we have
0\in I
.
Since
C_{x_{2}}\subset C^{\prime}_{x_{2}}=B
, we have
1\in J
.
We next prove that
I
is closed.
Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be a sequence in
I
such that

\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
and set
z=(1-\lambda)x_{1}\oplus\lambda x_{2}
.
Let
y\in C_{z}
be given.
Then we know that
f(z,y)\leq\alpha<\beta
.
Setting

(3.20)

\displaystyle z_{n}=(1-\lambda_{n})x_{1}\oplus\lambda_{n}x_{2}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have from the upper semicontinuity of
f(\,\cdot\,,y)
that

(3.21)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}f(z_{n},y)\leq f(z,y)<\beta.

Thus there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sup_{k\geq n_{0}}f(z_{k},y)<\beta
.
This gives us that
f(z_{n_{0}},y)<\beta

and hence
y\in C^{\prime}_{z_{n_{0}}}
.
On the other hand, since
\lambda_{n}\in I
, we have

(3.22)

\displaystyle C_{z_{n_{0}}}\subset A.

Since
C_{z_{n_{0}}}\subset C^{\prime}_{z_{n_{0}}}
,
either
C^{\prime}_{z_{n_{0}}}\subset A
or
C^{\prime}_{z_{n_{0}}}\subset B
holds,
and
A\cap B=\emptyset
,
we know that
C^{\prime}_{z_{n_{0}}}\subset A
.
Hence we have
y\in A
.
Therefore we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle C_{(1-\lambda)y_{1}\oplus\lambda y_{2}}=C_{z}\subset A.

This implies that
\lambda\in I
. Thus
I
is closed.
Similarly, we can prove that
J
is closed.
Setting
\lambda^{*}=\sup I
, we can show that

\lambda^{*}=\max I
and
0\leq\lambda^{*}<1
.
If
\mu\in(\lambda^{*},1]
,
then
\mu\in J
. Letting
\mu\downarrow\lambda^{*}
,
we obtain
\lambda^{*}\in J
since
J
is closed. Thus we have

\lambda^{*}\in I\cap J
. This contradicts
I\cap J=\emptyset
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be given and
X
,
Y
, and
f

the same as in Theorem
3.1
.
If

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\in Y
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy

(3.24)

\displaystyle\alpha<\min_{y\in X}\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f(x_{k},y),

then there exists
x_{0}\in X
such that

\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
m=1
, then the result clearly holds.
Suppose that the result holds for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m},x_{m+1}\in X
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy

(3.25)

\displaystyle\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}\max_{1\leq k\leq m+1}f(x_{k},y).

Set

(3.26)

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}=\{y\in Y:f(x_{m+1},y)\leq\alpha\}.

Then
Y^{\prime}
is a closed and convex subset of
Y
.
If
Y^{\prime}
is empty, then

(3.27)

\displaystyle f(x_{m+1},y)>\alpha

for all
y\in Y
and hence we have

\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{m+1},y)>\alpha
.
Letting
x_{0}=x_{m+1}
, we obtain the conclusion.
Thus we may suppose that
Y^{\prime}
is nonempty. Then

Y^{\prime}
is a bounded Hadamard space. Since

f(x_{m+1},y)\leq\alpha
for all
y\in Y^{\prime}
, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}\max_{1\leq k\leq m+1}f(x_{k},y)\leq\min_{y\in Y^{\prime}}\max_{1\leq k\leq m+1}f(x_{k},y)=\min_{y\in Y^{\prime}}\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f(x_{k},y).

It follows from the assumption of our induction argument that
there exists
x^{\prime}_{0}\in X
such that

\alpha<\min_{y\in Y^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime}_{0},y)
.
If
y\in Y^{\prime}
, then
\alpha<f(x^{\prime}_{0},y)
and
f(x_{m+1},y)\leq\alpha
.
Hence we have

(3.29)

\displaystyle\max\{f(x^{\prime}_{0},y),f(x_{m+1},y)\}=f(x^{\prime}_{0},y)>\alpha.

If
y\in Y\setminus Y^{\prime}
, then

(3.30)

\displaystyle\max\{f(x^{\prime}_{0},y),f(x_{m+1},y)\}\geq f(x_{m+1},y)>\alpha.

Thus

(3.31)

\displaystyle\max\{f(x^{\prime}_{0},y),f(x_{m+1},y)\}>\alpha

for all
y\in Y
. Consequently, we have

(3.32)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\max\{f(x^{\prime}_{0},y),f(x_{m+1},y)\}>\alpha.

Lemma
3.3
ensures that
there exists
x_{0}\in X
such that

\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)>\alpha
.
This completes the proof.
∎

The proof of Theorem
3.1
.

As is noted in Remark
3.2
, we have

(3.33)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\leq\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y).

Thus we prove the reverse inequality.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy

\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)

and set

(3.34)

\displaystyle Y_{x}=\bigl\{y\in Y:f(x,y)\leq\alpha\bigr\}

for all
x\in X
. Then we can prove that

\bigcap_{x\in X}Y_{x}
is empty.
If not, then there exists
y_{0}\in Y
such that

y_{0}\in Y_{x}
for all
x\in X
and hence

(3.35)

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y_{0})\geq\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y).

This is a contradiction. Hence
\bigcap_{x\in X}Y_{x}
is empty.
Applying Lemma
2.11
, we have

m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\in X
such that

\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}Y_{x_{k}}=\emptyset
.
Since

(3.36)

\displaystyle\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}Y_{x_{k}}=\left\{y\in Y:\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f(x_{k},y)\leq\alpha\right\},

we have
\alpha<\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f(x_{k},y)
for all
y\in Y

and hence

(3.37)

\displaystyle\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f(x_{k},y).

Then Lemma
3.4
ensures that
there exists
x_{0}\in X
such that

\alpha<\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)
.
Consequently, we have

(3.38)

\displaystyle\alpha<\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y).

Letting
\alpha\uparrow\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)
yields

(3.39)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y)\leq\sup_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y).

This completes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
,
we obtain the following saddle point theorem:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be bounded Hadamard spaces and

f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function
satisfying (i) and (ii) in
Theorem
3.1
.
Then the minimax equality

(3.40)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)=\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y)

holds and
f
has a saddle point.

Proof.

Since
X
and
Y
are bounded Hadamard space,
Lemma
2.10

and Theorem
3.1
ensure that the minimax
equality holds. Then there exists
(x_{0},y_{0})\in X\times Y
such that

(3.41)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)=\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\max_{x\in X}f(x,y_{0})=\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y).

These equalities give us that

(3.42)

\displaystyle f(x,y_{0})\leq\max_{x\in X}f(x,y_{0})=\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)\leq f(x_{0},y)

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
Consequently, we obtain the desired inequalities.
∎

4.
Coercive saddle functions in Hadamard spaces

In this section, we prove the following minimax theorem
for coercive saddle functions in Hadamard spaces
without boundedness assumption:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be Hadamard spaces,

d
the
\ell^{2}
-metric on
X\times Y
,
and
f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function satisfying

(i)

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
is upper semicontinuous
and concave for each
y\in Y
;

(ii)

f(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous
and convex for each
x\in X
;

(iii)

there exists
(a,b)\in X\times Y
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty\Rightarrow\liminf_{n\to\infty}\bigl(f(x_{n},b)-f(a,y_{n})\bigr)<0

whenever
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is a sequence in
X\times Y
.

Then the minimax equality

(4.2)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y)=\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y)

holds and
f
has a saddle point.

Proof.

Note that
(X\times Y,d)
is an Hadamard space
and the unique normalized geodesic
\gamma
from
(x,y)
to
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

in
X\times Y

is given by (
2.40
).
We also define the
\ell^{\infty}
-metric
d_{\infty}
on
X\times Y
by

(4.3)

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)=\max\left\{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right\}

for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.
It is clear that

(4.4)

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\leq d\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\sqrt{2}\,d_{\infty}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)

and hence the metric topology on
(X\times Y,d)
coincides with
that on
(X\times Y,d_{\infty})
.
Let
F
be the function defined by

(4.5)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)=f(x,y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime},y)

for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.

It follows from (iii) that there exists
\delta>0
such that

(4.6)

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\bigl((x,y),(a,b)\bigr)\geq\delta\Rightarrow f(x,b)-f(a,y)\leq 0.

If not, then
we have a sequence
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
in
X\times Y
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty\quad\textrm{and}\quad f(x_{n},b)-f(a,y_{n})>0.

This gives us that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\bigl(f(x_{n},b)-f(a,y_{n})\bigr)\geq 0.

On the other hand, since

(4.9)

\displaystyle d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\geq d_{\infty}\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty,

we have from (iii) that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\bigl(f(x_{n},b)-f(a,y_{n})\bigr)<0.

This is a contradiction.

In order to apply Corollary
3.5
,
we define two sets
C
and
D
by

(4.11)

\displaystyle C=\{x\in X:d_{X}(x,a)\leq\delta\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad D=\{y\in Y:d_{Y}(y,b)\leq\delta\}.

It is obvious that

(4.12)

\displaystyle C\times D=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y:d_{\infty}\bigl((x,y),(a,b)\bigr)\leq\delta\}.

Since
C
and
D
are bounded closed convex subsets of

X
and
Y
, respectively, these spaces are bounded Hadamard spaces.
Thus Corollary
3.5
ensures that
there a saddle point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in C\times D

of the function
f
on
C\times D
.
This is equivalent to

(4.13)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr)=f(x_{0},y)-f(x,y_{0})\geq 0

for all
(x,y)\in C\times D
.

We next prove that the inequality (
4.13
) holds
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
Let
(x,y)
be a point in
X\times Y
.
Since
(x_{0},y_{0})\in C\times D
, we have

d_{\infty}\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)\leq\delta
.
We first consider the case where
d_{\infty}\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)<\delta
.
Since the function

(4.14)

\displaystyle t\mapsto(1-t)(x_{0},y_{0})\oplus t(x,y)

on
[0,1]
is convergent to
(x_{0},y_{0})
as
t\downarrow 0
and
the set

(4.15)

\displaystyle V=\bigl\{(u,v)\in X\times Y:d_{\infty}\bigl((u,v),(a,b)\bigr)<\delta\bigr\}

is an open neighborhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
,
there exists
t_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

(4.16)

\displaystyle(1-t_{0})(x_{0},y_{0})\oplus t_{0}(x,y)\in V.

Since
V\subset C\times D
and
F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),\cdot\,\bigr)
is convex
on
X\times Y
, we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(1-t_{0})(x_{0},y_{0})\oplus t_{0}(x,y)\bigr)\\
&\leq(1-t_{0})F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{0},y_{0})\bigr)+t_{0}F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr)\\
&\leq F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr).\end{split}

Thus (
4.13
) holds.
We next consider the case where

d_{\infty}\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)=\delta
.
Then we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)=f(x_{0},b)-f(a,y_{0})\leq 0.

Since
(a,b)\in C\times D
and
(x_{0},y_{0})

is a saddle point of
f
on
C\times D
,
we also have

F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)\geq 0
.
Consequently, we obtain

(4.19)

\displaystyle F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)=0.

Since the set
V
defined above is an open neighborhood of
(a,b)
,
we can choose
t_{1}\in(0,1)
such that

(4.20)

\displaystyle(1-t_{1})(a,b)\oplus t_{1}(x,y)\in V.

Then we have

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(1-t_{1})(a,b)\oplus t_{1}(x,y)\bigr)\\
&\leq(1-t_{1})F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(a,b)\bigr)+t_{1}F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr)\\
&\leq F\bigl((x_{0},y_{0}),(x,y)\bigr).\end{split}

Thus (
4.13
) holds.
Consequently,
(x_{0},y_{0})
is a saddle point of
f
on
X\times Y
.

We finally prove the minimax equality.
Since
(x_{0},y_{0})
is a saddle point of
f
on
X\times Y
, we have

(4.22)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in X}f(x,y_{0})=f(x_{0},y_{0})=\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y).

This gives us that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y)\leq\max_{x\in X}f(x,y_{0})=\min_{y\in Y}f(x_{0},y)\leq\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y).

and hence we obtain

(4.24)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y}\max_{x\in X}f(x,y)\leq\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}f(x,y).

The reverse inequality is obvious. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Resolvents of saddle functions in Hadamard spaces

In this section, we define the resolvents of
saddle functions in Hadamard spaces.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be Hadamard spaces
and
f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
a function satisfying

(i)

f(\,\cdot\,,y)
is upper semicontinuous
and concave for each
y\in Y
;

(ii)

f(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous
and convex for each
x\in X
.

If
(a,b)\in X\times Y
, then
there exists a unique saddle point
(\hat{a},\hat{b})

in
X\times Y
of the function
g\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}

defined by

(5.1)

\displaystyle g(x,y)=f(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}d_{X}(x,a)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}d_{Y}(y,b)^{2}

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.

Proof.

Let
d
be the
\ell^{2}
-metric on
X\times Y
.
It is clear that
g(\,\cdot\,,y)
is
upper semicontinuous and concave for each
y\in Y

and
g(x,\cdot\,)
is lower semicontinuous and convex
for each
x\in X
.

We then prove that

(5.2)

\displaystyle d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty\Rightarrow\frac{g(x_{n},b)-g(a,y_{n})}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}\to-\infty

whenever
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is a sequence in
X\times Y
.
Let
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
be a sequence in
X\times Y
such that

(5.3)

\displaystyle d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty.

Note that the function
\Phi\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y)=f(a,y)-f(x,b)

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y

is a real-valued lower semicontinuous convex function
on the Hadamard space
(X\times Y,d)
.
Theorem
2.7
ensures that
there exists a positive real number
C
such that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y)\geq-C\Bigl[d\bigl((x,y),(a,b)\bigr)+1\Bigr]

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. This gives us that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{g(x_{n},b)-g(a,y_{n})}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}&=\frac{f(x_{n},b)-f(a,y_{n})-2^{-1}d_{X}(x_{n},a)^{2}-2^{-1}d_{Y}(y_{n},b)^{2}}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}\\
&=\frac{-\Phi(x_{n},y_{n})-2^{-1}d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}\\
&\leq C+\frac{1}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}-\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to-\infty.\end{split}

This gives us that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\frac{g(x_{n},b)-g(a,y_{n})}{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)}\to-\infty.

Consequently, we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(a,b)\bigr)\to\infty\Rightarrow\liminf_{n\to\infty}\bigl(g(x_{n},b)-g(a,y_{n})\bigr)<0

whenever
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is a sequence in
X\times Y
.
Therefore Theorem
4.1
ensures that

g
has a saddle point
(\hat{a},\hat{b})
.

We finally prove the uniqueness of such a saddle point.
Let
(a_{i},b_{i})
be saddle points of
g
for
i=1,2
.
Then we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle g(a_{i},y)-g(x,b_{i})\geq 0

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
and
i=1,2
.
Set

(5.10)

\displaystyle(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},b_{1})\oplus\frac{1}{2}(a_{2},b_{2})

and define
F
and
G
by

(5.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)&=f(x,y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime},y);\\
G\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)&=g(x,y^{\prime})-g(x^{\prime},y)\end{split}

for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.
It is clear that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&G\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\\
&\quad=F\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((x,y),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.
We then obtain

(5.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq 2G\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(p,q)\bigr)\\
&=2F\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(p,q)\bigr)-d\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl((p,q),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq F\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(a_{1},b_{1})\bigr)+F\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(a_{2},b_{2})\bigr)-d\bigl((a_{i},b_{i}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((a_{2},b_{2}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\bigr)^{2}.\end{split}

Summing up these two inequalities yield that

(5.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq F\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\bigr)+F\bigl((a_{2},b_{2}),(a_{1},b_{1})\bigr)\\
&\quad-d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}-d\bigl((a_{2},b_{2}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl((a_{2},b_{2}),(a,b)\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\bigr)^{2}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}d\bigl((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

Hence we have
(a_{1},b_{1})=(a_{2},b_{2})
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We next give the definition of the resolvents of saddle functions
in Hadamard spaces.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
,
and
f\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
be the same as in
Theorem
5.1
.
For each point
(x,y)
in
X\times Y
,
the unique saddle point

(\hat{x},\hat{y})
of the function

(5.15)

\displaystyle(u,v)\mapsto f(u,v)-\frac{1}{2}d_{X}(u,x)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}d_{Y}(v,y)^{2}

on
X\times Y
is denoted by
R_{f}(x,y)
. The mapping

R_{f}
of
X\times Y
into itself is called the resolvent of
f
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
, and
f
be the same as in
Theorem
5.1
,

d
the
\ell^{2}
-metric on
X\times Y
,

R_{\lambda}
the resolvent of
\lambda f
for each
\lambda>0
,
and set

(5.16)

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(x,y)=\bigl(R_{1,\lambda}(x,y),R_{2,\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)

for each
(x,y)\in X\times Y
and
\lambda>0
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

If
\lambda>0
, then
R_{\lambda}
is a single-valued mapping of

X\times X
into itself and
\Fix(R_{\lambda})=\Saddle(f)
;

(ii)

if
\lambda>0
, then
the inequality

(5.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+2\lambda\bigl\{f(x^{\prime},R_{2,\lambda}(x,y))-f(R_{1,\lambda}(x,y),y^{\prime})\bigr\}\leq d\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

holds for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
;

(iii)

if
\lambda,\mu>0
, then
the inequality

(5.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\lambda+\mu)d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+\lambda d\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

holds for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
;

(iv)

if
\lambda>0
, then
R_{\lambda}

is both firmly metrically nonspreading and
nonexpansive;

(v)

if
\lambda,\mu>0
and
(x,y)\in X\times Y
, then

(5.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr).

Proof.

For each
\lambda>0
and
(x,y)\in X\times Y
,
we define
g_{\lambda,x,y}\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
by

(5.20)

\displaystyle g_{\lambda,x,y}(u,v)=f(x,y)-\frac{1}{2\lambda}d_{X}(u,x)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d_{Y}(v,y)^{2}

for all
(u,v)\in X\times Y
.
We define a real functions
F
and
G_{\lambda,x,y}

by

(5.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)&=f(x,y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime},y);\\
G_{\lambda,x,y}\bigl((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\bigr)&=g_{\lambda,x,y}(u,v^{\prime})-g_{\lambda,x,y}(u^{\prime},v)\end{split}

for all
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.
We denote by
d
the
\ell^{2}
-metric on
X\times Y
. Then we have

(5.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&G_{\lambda,x,y}\bigl((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\bigr)\\
&\quad=F\bigl((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\bigr)-\frac{1}{2\lambda}d\bigl((u,v),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d\bigl((u^{\prime},v^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

for all
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.

We first prove the part (i).
Let
\lambda>0
. It is clear from the definition of
R_{\lambda}
that

R_{\lambda}
is a single-valued mapping of
X\times Y

into itself. Let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Fix(R_{\lambda})
be given.
Then we have

(5.23)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq G_{\lambda,\bar{x},\bar{y}}\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)

for all
(u,v)\in X\times Y
. Let
(u,v)\in X\times Y
and
set

(5.24)

\displaystyle(z_{t},w_{t})=(1-t)(\bar{x},\bar{y})\oplus(1-t)(u,v)

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then we have

(5.25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq G_{\lambda,\bar{x},\bar{y}}\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(z_{t},w_{t})\bigr)\\
&\leq(1-t)F\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(\bar{x},\bar{y})\bigr)+tF\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)+\frac{t^{2}}{2\lambda}d\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq tF\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)+\frac{t^{2}}{2\lambda}d\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

and hence

(5.26)

\displaystyle 0\leq F\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)+\frac{t}{2\lambda}d\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)^{2}.

Letting
t\downarrow 0
, we have

0\leq F\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)
. Thus
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Saddle(f)
.
Conversely, if
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Saddle(f)
, then we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq F\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)\leq G_{\lambda,\bar{x},\bar{y}}\bigl((\bar{x},\bar{y}),(u,v)\bigr)

for all
(u,v)\in X\times Y
. Hence
R_{\lambda}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=(\bar{x},\bar{y})
.
Consequently, we obtain
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Fix(R_{\lambda})
.

We next prove the part (ii). Let
\lambda>0
be given
and let
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
be points in
X\times Y
.
Set

(5.28)

\displaystyle(z_{t},w_{t})=(1-t)R_{\lambda}(x,y)\oplus t(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq G_{\lambda,x,y}\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(z_{t},w_{t})\bigr)\\
&\leq(1-t)F\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)+tF\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2\lambda}d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}d\bigl((z_{t},w_{t}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq tF\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)-\frac{t}{2\lambda}d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{t}{2\lambda}d\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad-\frac{t(1-t)}{2\lambda}d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

and hence

(5.30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(1-t)d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+2\lambda F\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)\\
&\quad\leq d\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}.\end{split}

Letting
t\downarrow 0
, we obtain the conclusion.

We next prove the part (iii). Let
\lambda,\mu>0
be given.
It follows from the part (ii) that

(5.31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mu d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+2\lambda\mu F\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)\leq\mu d\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

and

(5.32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda d\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\lambda d\bigl(R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+2\lambda\mu F\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\mu}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\lambda d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}.\end{split}

Summing up these inequalities yields the conclusion.

We next prove the part (iv). Let
\lambda>0
be given.
Then we have from the part (iii) that

(5.33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&2d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\leq d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

holds for all
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in
X\times Y
.
This means that
R_{\lambda}
is firmly metrically nonspareading.
If
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are points in
X\times Y
,
then Theorem
2.1
implies that

(5.34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl\{d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}-d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\qquad-d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\bigr\}\\
&\quad\leq d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)d\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\end{split}

This implies that
R_{\lambda}
is nonexpansive.

We finally prove the part (v).
Let
\lambda,\mu>0
and
(x,y)\in X\times Y
be given.
It follows from the part (iii) that

(5.35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\lambda+\mu)d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+\lambda d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}.\end{split}

Using Theorem
2.1
, we have

(5.36)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\bigl\{d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}-d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad-d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}\bigr\}\\
&\leq\mu d\bigl(R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}(x,y),R_{\lambda}(x,y)\bigr)d\bigl(R_{\lambda}(x,y),(x,y)\bigr)\end{split}

and hence we obtain the conclusion.
∎

Using Theorems
2.2

and
5.3
, we obtain the following:

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
,
f
be the same as in
Theorem
5.1
and

R_{f}
the resolvent of

.
Then
\Saddle(f)
is nonempty if and only if

\{R_{f}^{n}(x,y)\}
is bounded for some

.
In this case,

.

6.
The proximal point algorithm for saddle functions

In this section, we study the asymptotic behavior of
sequences generated by the proximal point algorithm
for saddle functions in Hadamard spaces.

Applying the results obtained in this papers, we obtain
the following result generalizing Theorem
1.2

to the Hadamard space setting:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
,
f
be the same as in
Theorem
5.1
,

\{\lambda_{n}\}
a sequence of positive real numbers
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}=\infty
,
and
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
the sequence in
X\times Y

defined by
(x_{1},y_{1})\in X\times Y
and

(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
is the unique saddle point of
the function

(6.1)

\displaystyle(z,w)\mapsto f(z,w)-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}d_{X}(z,x_{n})^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}d_{Y}(w,y_{n})^{2}

on
X\times Y
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

\Saddle(f)
is nonempty if and only if

\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is bounded;

(ii)

if
\Saddle(f)
is nonempty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}=\infty
,

\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is
\Delta
-convergent to
an element of
\Saddle(f)
.

Proof.

Let
R_{\lambda_{n}}
be the resolvent of
\lambda_{n}f

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
d
the
\ell^{2}
-metric on
X\times Y
.
Then we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=R_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n})

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We first prove the part (i).
Suppose that
\Saddle(f)
is nonempty.
Let
(u,v)
be an element of
\Saddle(f)
.
It follows from (i) and (iv) of Theorem
5.3
that

(6.3)

\displaystyle d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\bigr)=d\bigl((u,v),R_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\leq d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and hence
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is bounded.
Conversely, suppose that
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is bounded
and let
\mu>0
be given.
Let
g\colon X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}

be a function defined by

(6.4)

\displaystyle g(x,y)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{l}d\bigl((x_{l+1},y_{l+1}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}

for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
Theorem
2.8
ensures that
g
has a unique
minimizer
(p,q)
.
Using (iii) of Theorem
5.3
, we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\lambda_{n}+\mu)&d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),R_{\mu}(p,q)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\leq\lambda_{n}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(p,q)\bigr)^{2}+\mu d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),R_{\mu}(p,q)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

and hence

(6.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{l}d\bigl((x_{l+1},y_{l+1}),R_{\mu}(p,q)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{l}d\bigl((x_{l+1},y_{l+1}),(p,q)\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\mu}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}}d\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),R_{1}(p,q)\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}=\infty
,
we have

(6.7)

\displaystyle g\bigl(R_{\mu}(p,q)\bigr)\leq g(p,q).

Consequently, we obtain
R_{\mu}(p,q)=(p,q)
.
Thus
(p,q)
is an element of
\Saddle(f)
.

We finally prove the part (ii).
Suppose that

\Saddle(f)
is nonempty
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}^{2}=\infty
.
Let
(u,v)

be an element of
\Saddle(f)
.
It follows from (ii) of Theorem
5.3
that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\geq d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad+2\lambda_{n}\bigl(f(u,y_{n+1})-f(x_{n+1},v)\bigr)\\
&\geq d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\bigr)^{2}+d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}\end{split}

and hence
\{d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\}

is convergent and

(6.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\leq d\bigl((u,v),(x_{1},y_{1})\bigr)^{2}-\lim_{n\to\infty}d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}.\end{split}

This implies that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)=0

and

(6.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\right\}^{2}<\infty

Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}=\infty
, we know that

(6.12)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)=0.

On the other hand, using (v) of Theorem
5.3
,
we also have

(6.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}}d\bigl((x_{n+2},y_{n+2}),(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\bigr)&=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}}d\bigl(R_{\lambda_{n+1}}R_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n}),R_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl(R_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n},y_{n}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\end{split}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and hence the sequence

(6.14)

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\right\}

is convergent. Thus we have from (
6.11
) that

(6.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)=0.

Let
(p,q)
be an element of
\omega_{\Delta}\bigl(\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}\bigr)
.
Then there exists a subsequence
\{(x_{n_{i}},y_{n_{i}})\}
of
\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}

which is
\Delta
-convergent to
(p,q)
.
It then follows from (
6.10
) that

\{(x_{n_{i}+1},y_{n_{i}+1})\}
is also
\Delta
-convergent to
(p,q)
.
Let
(x,y)\in X\times Y
be given.
Using (ii) of Theorem
5.3
, we have

(6.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq f(x_{n+1},y)-f(x,y_{n+1})+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}\bigl\{d\bigl((x_{n},y_{n}),(u,v)\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad-d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x,y)\bigr)^{2}-d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)^{2}\bigr\}\\
&\leq f(x_{n+1},x)-f(y,y_{n+1})+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}d\bigl((x_{n+1},y_{n+1}),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)d\bigl((x,y),(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\bigr)\end{split}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
f(\,\cdot,v)-f(u,\cdot\,)
is
\Delta
-upper semicontinuous on
X\times Y
,
we have from (
6.15
) that

(6.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\Bigl[f(x_{n_{i}+1},y)-f(x,y_{n_{i}+1})\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n_{i}}}d\bigl((x_{n_{i}+1},y_{n_{i}+1}),(x_{n_{i}},y_{n_{i}})\bigr)d\bigl((u,v),(x_{n_{i}+1},y_{n_{i}+1})\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\leq f(p,y)-f(x,q).\end{split}

This means that
(p,q)
is an element of
\Saddle(f)

and hence
\{d\bigl((p,q),(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)\}

is convergent.
Lemma
2.6
implies that

\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}
is
\Delta
-convergent
a point
(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})

in
X\times Y
.
Consequently, we have

(6.18)

\displaystyle\{(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\}=\omega_{\Delta}\bigl(\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}\bigr)\subset\Saddle(f).

Therefore the proof is completed.
∎
