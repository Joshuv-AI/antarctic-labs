Zeta functions over curves
F. Pellarin, with an appendix by G. H. Ferraro

Abstract
. In this paper we review the theory that David Goss developed, starting from 1979, to construct zeta functions around Carlitz zeta values and other remarkable formal series in local fields of positive characteristic. In the description of Goss’ theory, we will see how it is primarily motivated by analogies with the classical theory of complex valued zeta and L L -functions. We compare Goss’ theory with another way of constructing zeta and L L -functions that emerged in more recent times. The functions in the second type have as domains curves over finite fields, with the scalars extended to complete and algebraically closed fields of positive characteristic. The second type of functions interacts with Goss’ functions but remains fundamentally different. We shall review a rationality theorem of Ferraro that allows, among others, to introduce some kind of analogue of the function ξ \xi of Riemann. In the path of describing Ferraro’s proof, we present some essential tools useful to get into the theory: shtuka divisors and functions, special functions, Anderson motives, Drinfeld modules, among others. We discuss certain relative zeta functions that can be considered as counterparts of Dedekind zeta functions. In particular, we use methods introduced by Goss to prove that these functions extend to entire functions. The paper contains an appendix by Ferraro where the property of entireness of the above relative zeta functions is deduced (in a special case) from the conjunction of a formula by Anglès, Ngo Dac and Tavares Ribeiro and Ferraro’s rationality formula. Ferraro also presents a conjecture on the order of vanishing of this function at the canonical point Ξ \Xi and some numerical evidences.

1.
Introduction

The aim of the present paper is to give an overview of certain results on “zeta and
L
-values in global function fields.” More precisely, we present results in the perspective of David Goss’ attempt of developing a theory of zeta and
L
-functions interpolating the above values, which are formal series in local fields of positive characteristic. There are “zeta and
L
-values” in
local fields of positive characteristic: the first and most famous example was first considered by Carlitz in 1935 and is described in §
2
.

The next logical step is “zeta functions”. After having presented the interpolation problem in §
3
, we review Goss’ construction of zeta functions in §
4
. But there is no known analogue of functional equation for Goss zeta functions. Then we describe, in §
5
and §
6
more recent results where another technique of interpolating zeta and
L
-values is presented, “over curves” with the scalars extended to
complete algebraically closed fields (of positive characteristic).

We will notably discuss a 2022 result of Ferraro (Theorem
5.3
) providing a functional identity for these interpolating functions somehow reminiscent of the functional equation of Riemann’s zeta function. A comparison with the classical theory of zeta functions, together with their zeta functions, will be given along the way. In §
7
we explain how Goss’ theory allows to show that a certain analogue of Dedekind zeta function over a curve (relative zeta functions) extends to an entire function.
This result can be reached alternatively by using a different theory by Anglès, Ngo Dac and Tavares Ribeiro
[
3
]
. This is studied, in a special case, in the appendix by Ferraro §
9
. His use of the theory of
[
3
]
allows him to reach more information on the structure of the divisor of zeroes of these functions. In particular, with the support of computational evidences, he formulates Conjecture
11.1
on the order of vanishing of these functions
at a certain canonical point
\Xi
over the curve described in the text. We will not discuss this conjecture further in the present text leaving it for further developments we aim to investigate, but we mention that this agrees with the well known property that the order of vanishing at zero of the Dedekind zeta function associated to a number field
L
equals the rank of the unit group
of the ring of integers of
L
. Therefore the potential importance of Ferraro’s conjecture is that it opens the perspective of a
factorization of these relative zeta functions over curves in terms of Artin-like
L
-functions, and class number formulas.

2.
A glimpse of Carlitz zeta values

Carlitz zeta values are the first objects of the theory we want to describe.
In 1935 Carlitz
[
9
]
proposes a formula claiming analogy with Euler’s 1735 formula:

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

Carlitz’ formula is

(2.1)

\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]\\
\text{$a$ monic}\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{1}{a^{q-1}}=\prod_{i\geq 0}\left(1-\frac{\theta^{q^{i}}-\theta}{\theta^{q^{i+1}}-\theta}\right)^{q-1}.

Carlitz analyzes a more general class of formulas, just like Euler does, but let us stare at this one for a little while trying to interpret its meaning.

On the left-hand side of (
2.1
), the sum runs over the monic polynomials of the polynomial ring

\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
in the indeterminate
\theta
, coefficients in the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
elements and characteristic
p
.
To have such a formal series converging, we need to choose, over the fraction field
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
, a multiplicative valuation
|\cdot|
for which
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
is discrete. There is only one class of such a valuation corresponding to the infinity place of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)
. The corresponding metric is determined by the condition

|\theta|>1.

For this valuation,
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
is also co-compact, just like
\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathbb{R}
.

The completion of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)
at this infinity place
can be identified with the local field
\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{\theta}))
. The right-hand side converges for this valuation as well, but
“much faster” than the left-hand side. Experimentally, if we try to sum in the left it is convenient to collect together contributions coming from polynomials of same degree and then sum over degrees, then there are terms that formally cancel each other. So we already notice a benefit of Carlitz’s formula: instead of computing truncations of the left-hand side
(more difficult if
q
is a large power of the characteristic
p
), we can just approximate the fast converging
product on the right-hand side, where the factors are rational functions over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Additionally, we can even lift the right-hand side to a formal series of
1+\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbb{Z}[[\frac{1}{\theta}]]
and then reduce modulo
p
the coefficients. It does not seem to be possible to do this directly with the left-hand side, without transforming it.

To have a first numerical taste we give an example: if
q=9
, a computer computation yields the truncation to the power
q^{3}
of the uniformiser
\frac{1}{\theta}
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}((\frac{1}{\theta}))
, which is

1+\frac{1}{\theta^{72}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{80}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{144}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{152}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{216}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{224}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{288}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{296}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{360}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{368}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{432}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{440}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{504}}+\\
+\frac{2}{\theta^{512}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{576}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{584}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{648}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{656}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{720}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{728}},

where
0,1,2
can be identified with the corresponding elements of
\mathbb{F}_{3}\subset\mathbb{F}_{9}
.

There is no need to engrave these digits on a stone, their significance is somehow diminished by the fact that the choice of the uniformiser is anyway non-canonical (we choose here the uniformizer
\frac{1}{\theta}
just because we used
\theta
to define
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
, but there are uncountably many choices). Depending on the choice, the above truncated expansions may artificially look simpler or conversely, more complicated. Yet it is possible to prove that
the right-hand side of (
2.1
) determines an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{\theta}))
which is transcendental over

\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{\theta}))
(the first proof was obtained by Wade in 1941, see
[
38
]
).

Carlitz’s principal insight is that the series on the left-hand side of (
2.1
) is a function field analogue of a value of Riemann’s zeta function at an even positive integer, with the product on the right-hand side a rational (i. e. in
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)
) multiple of an even power of an “analogue of the complex number
2i\pi
”. This is a first instance of a “functional equation” of a zeta value at a positive integer divisible by
q-1
.

More on Carlitz’s zeta values

The left-hand side of (
2.1
) also has a eulerian factorization. For
k\geq 1
, the following product expansion holds:

(2.2)

\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]}(k):=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]\\
\text{$a$ monic}\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{1}{a^{k}}=\prod_{\begin{smallmatrix}P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]\\
\text{$P$ irreducible monic}\end{smallmatrix}}\left(1-\frac{1}{P^{k}}\right)^{-1}.

There exists, for all
k\geq 1
, a functor called
k
-th tensor power of the Carlitz module

C^{\otimes k}:\{\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]\text{-algebras}\}\rightarrow\{\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]\text{-modules}\}

such that for all
P
irreducible and monic in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
, the finite
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-module

C^{\otimes k}(\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]/P\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta])
is isomorphic to the finite cyclic
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-module
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]/(P^{k}-1)
in a way that is similar, in the case
k=1
, to the fact that
\mathbb{G}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\mathbb{Z}
as a group. For
k=1
the above functor is called
Carlitz’s functor

C=C^{\otimes 1}
, see the books
[
17
,
24
]
and
C^{\otimes k}
is a higher dimensional variant introduced by Anderson and Thakur in
[
2
]
, analogues of Tate’s twists
\mathbb{Z}(k)
. Most of the properties of Carlitz’s module we recall here are contained in
[
30
, §4.4]
.

The field
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
and the analogue of
2\pi i

We now consider a complete, algebraically closed field containing the zeta values
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]}(n)
.
Let
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
be the completion of a separable closure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}((\frac{1}{\theta}))
. So we can write:

\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:=\widehat{\mathbb{F}_{q}\Big(\!\Big(\frac{1}{\theta}\Big)\!\Big)^{\text{sep}}}

(the exponent denotes a separable closure, and the hat is the completion with respect to the unique extension of
the valuation of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\!(\frac{1}{\theta})\!)
). This field is algebraically closed and complete. Unlike
\mathbb{C}
, it is not locally compact.
It is an

\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-algebra and therefore we can consider the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-module
C(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
.

Let us inspect
C(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
more closely. The multiplication by
\theta
, determining the whole
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-module structure of
C(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
, is explicitly determined by the

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-endomorphism
\theta+\tau
, where
\tau
is the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-automorphism
c\mapsto c^{q}
over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
. There is a more general but similar structure allowing to work with
C^{\otimes k}
, see
[
2
]
.
C(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
comes equipped with the structure of a non-compact connected rigid analytic curve over
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
which is analytically isomorphic, via an analogue of the classical exponential function called the
Carlitz exponential
, to the quotient

\mathbb{C}_{\infty}/\widetilde{\pi}\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
, which is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
-module. These properties are proved in
[
30
, §4.4.2]
.

Everything can be computed explicitly! We have the formula:

(2.3)

\widetilde{\pi}=\theta(-\theta)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\prod_{i>0}\Big(1-\frac{\theta}{\theta^{q^{i}}}\Big)^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

(it is defined up to multiplication by an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, therefore uniquely determined only in the case
q=2
). This is the above mentioned Carlitz’s analogue of
2\pi i
, defined up to multiplication by an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Again in the case
q=9
with the uniformizer
\frac{1}{\theta}
the computer says that the truncation (this time to the
q^{2}
-th term) is

(-\theta)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\Big(\theta+\frac{1}{\theta^{7}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{15}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{23}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{31}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{39}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{47}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{55}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{63}}+\frac{1}{\theta^{71}}+\frac{2}{\theta^{79}}\Big).

Finally,
\widetilde{\pi}
and the left-hand side of (
2.1
) are related:

\prod_{i\geq 0}\Big(1-\frac{\theta^{q^{i}}-\theta}{\theta^{q^{i+1}}-\theta}\Big)^{q-1}=\frac{\widetilde{\pi}^{q-1}}{\theta-\theta^{q}},

this is easy to check and in fact a characteristic zero lift of this formula holds, but it can be also deduced from
[
17
, Lemma 3.2.1]
, see also
[
30
, Theorem 4.4.6 and Remark 4.4.7]
. In this game of analogies initiated by Carlitz, the polynomial
\theta-\theta^{q}
corresponds to the denominator
6
in Euler’s formula for
\zeta(2)
and we can further push our search of analogue structures. Carlitz introduced function field analogues of the factorial and Bernoulli numbers (they are elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\theta)
) in order
to obtain analogues of Euler’s famous 1735 formulas

(2.4)

\zeta(2k)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}B_{2k}}{2(2k)!}(2\pi)^{2k},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\quad\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=:\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

for
\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]}(n)
,
(q-1)\mid n
,
n\geq 1
(more details in
[
17
]
).

Carlitz further obtained an analogue of
Clausen-von Staudt theorem. The snow ball of analogies continued to roll until recent times, notably with analogues of Herbrand-Ribet theorem
[
33
,
8
]
, or the analogue for “Bernoulli-Carlitz” elements of a conjecture in
[
20
]
for the reduction modulo
p
of Bernoulli numbers
B_{p-k}
for
p
a moving prime and
k
a fixed odd integer
\geq 3
(see also
[
21
]
), proved in
[
5
]
, and other results.

3.
Goss’ Dream

Euler’s formula for
\zeta(2)
can also be deduced from the functional equation of Riemann’s zeta function, which involves Euler’s gamma function. Namely, the function of the complex variable
s

\xi(s):=\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}s(1-s)\Gamma\Big(\frac{s}{2}\Big)\zeta(s),

extends to an entire function over
\mathbb{C}
, non-vanishing over
\mathbb{R}
, and satisfies the
symmetric functional equation

(3.1)

\xi(s)=\xi(1-s).

We ask if there is an analogue of Riemann’s zeta function interpolating the Carlitz zeta values (
2.2
) that satisfies “some kind of functional equation” and if the formula
(
2.1
) can be deduced from it. Unfortunately, while there are several candidates for zeta functions (see the specimens described in the paper), at the time of writing this, the author is unaware of such a development, this question remains at the moment unanswered; we touch at a point where analogies are superseded by deeper phenomena that are not completely understood.

Nevertheless, this was one of Goss’ dreams:

Deduce Carlitz formula (
2.2
) from a “functional equation”.

This is one of the motivations for a theory of
\zeta
and
L
-functions that he initiated in 1979
[
16
]
and that we are going to review now.

Goss’ exploration is also rooted in Tate’s 1950 Thesis
[
34
]
. It is not our task to go deep in this direction but Tate’s viewpoint has some advantage in better motivating Goss’ construction of his zeta and
L
-functions.

Complex quasi-characters

Tate interprets
\zeta

“quasi-characters”.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
k
be a completion of a global field
K

A quasi-character for

k^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}.

The target space of a quasi-character is chosen in this way because it contains classical zeta and

-values; it contains the real number
\zeta(2)
for example.

Note that

(3.2)

\mathbb{C}^{\times}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}u

where

is the kernel of
|\cdot|
(unit circle) and this help in computing groups of quasi-characters. If
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
then we can decompose, uniquely,

\alpha=\tilde{\alpha}|\alpha|

with
\tilde{\alpha}\in u
and

. For example if

and

is a quasi-character of

,

with

where
\tilde{c}:u\rightarrow u
is a character. This allows, for example, to
show that the group of quasi-characters
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{C},
where the elements in
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}

are determined by the ‘‘unramified’’ (
1
1

1

This is the terminology introduced by Tate.
) quasi-characters

with

; here we see one convincing reason for which

More generally, we obtain an explicit description of quasi-characters of

for any completion

We can continue describing Tate’s approach but Carlitz zeta values do not belong to

We are rather interested in the topological group of

3.1.
Setting and notation

It is the moment to establish the perimeter of our discussion and to choose notations that we will adopt all along this paper.
Let
K
be the function field of a geometrically connected smooth projective curve

\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We choose

-rational point, an hypothesis that can be removed, but it offers the advantage of an easier introduction in the theory). We write

This is the subring of

whose elements are regular away from

of

is equipped with a multiplicative

. For

The completion
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
of a fixed separable closure

of

has a unique extension of the map

to a multiplicative valuation.
In
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
,

is discrete; note also that

is co-compact in

(see

We fix a uniformizer

To introduce analogues of Carlitz zeta values (

.

Definition 3.2
.

Let

be an algebraic extension, with residual field
\mathbb{F}_{L}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\text{alg}}
, identified with a subgroup of

.
A sign function for

\operatorname{sgn}_{L}:L^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{L}^{\times}

inducing the identity map over
\mathbb{F}_{L}^{\times}\subset L^{\times}
.

Note that we are not asking

. Also, given any sign function
\operatorname{sgn}_{L}

If

. In this case we define

Here is a partial generalization of Carlitz’s zeta values:

Z_{A}(n):=\sum_{d\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in A^{+}\\

The condition

. But let us consider the more general series

\widetilde{Z}_{A}(x,n):=\sum_{d\geq 0}x^{-dn}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in A^{+}\\

introduced in

, §2]
. It can be evaluated at

.
By

.

3.2.
The multiplicative structure of

We give an analogue of (

essentially a reformulation of

Definition 3.3
.

A

A

-vector space in
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

We can construct
\mathbb{Q}
-lines in the following way (see
[
3
, §1]
).
Set
x_{1}:=x
with
x\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
such that
|x|\neq 1
and define a

(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
by choosing, for all

x_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
with
x_{n}^{n}=x_{n-1}
(this can be done as
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

\rho\in\mathbb{Q}
we can write
\rho=\frac{m}{n!}

with

and

. We set

This is well defined and independent of the choice of

.
Denote by

the set of all these elements of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Any set

\mathbb{Q}
-line. Any
\mathbb{Q}
-line has the above structure.

Proof.

Consider two elements of

,

, with
\rho=\frac{m}{n!}
and
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{m^{\prime}}{n^{\prime}!}

. Then
\rho=\frac{m}{n!}
and
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{m^{\prime}}{n!}

y^{\prime}=x_{n}^{m^{\prime}}
. We get

Remark 3.8
.

We quote a sentence of Tate in

…we shall find no model for the group of quasi-characters, or even for the group of characters, though such a model would be of the utmost importance.

A similar problem arises for

We point out that Jeong proved

In §

4.
Goss theory of zeta functions

We choose
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
so that we have the rings
A,K,K_{\infty},\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
discussed previously. Choosing a uniformizer

\pi
of
K_{\infty}
with
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=1
we have a decomposition (
3.7
).

Goss plane
is a topological subgroup
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
of
\operatorname{Hom}_{c}(K_{\infty}^{\times},\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times})
containing a locally discrete copy of
\mathbb{Z}
as a subgroup. This space is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}

and can be easily described by using the tools developed above and (
3.8
).

Homeomorphisms are given associating

s:=(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}\mapsto c_{s}:=\Big(\alpha=\lambda\langle\alpha\rangle\pi^{n}\mapsto\lambda\langle\alpha\rangle^{y}x^{n}\Big)\in\operatorname{Hom}_{c}(K_{\infty}^{\times},\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times})

and depend on the choice of a uniformizer
\pi
of
K_{\infty}
. The group homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
is given by
n\mapsto(\pi^{-n},n)
and also depends on that choice.
Goss used this group
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
as the space of “complex exponents” in his theory of zeta and
L
-functions, see his book
[
17
]
.

Fundamental in this is his notion of exponentiation by an exponent in
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
.

4.1.
Goss’ exponentiation

We fix a uniformizer
\pi
of
K_{\infty}
and a sign function
\operatorname{sgn}:K_{\infty}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=1
.
Let
a
be an element of
A^{+}
(recall that
A^{+}=A\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{sgn})
). If
s=(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
Goss introduces the
exponentiation
of
a
by
s
:

(4.1)

a^{s}:=c_{s}(a)=\langle a\rangle^{y}x^{\deg(a)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}.

If
s=(\pi^{-n},n)
we get

a^{s}=\pi^{-n\deg(a)}\langle a\rangle^{n}=(\pi^{-\deg(a)}\langle a\rangle)^{n}=a^{n},

because by (
3.8
)

a=\pi^{-\deg(a)}\langle a\rangle.

We can also define exponentiations of fractional ideals, hence providing group homomorphisms
\mathcal{I}_{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
where
\mathcal{I}_{A}
is the group of invertible ideals of
A
.
Consider a non-zero fractional ideal
I
of
A
. Since the class group of
A
is finite, there exists an integer
h>0
such that

I^{h}=(a_{I}),

that is,
I^{h}
is principal generated by
a_{I}\in A^{+}=A\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{sgn})
(the choice is possible because
A^{\times}=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
).
Hence
h\deg(I)=\deg(a_{I})
. We can use (
3.8
) and write

a_{I}=\langle a_{I}\rangle\pi^{-\deg(a)}.

The group
v
in Proposition
3.5
is uniquely divisible and we can set, for
s=(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
,

(4.2)

I^{s}:=\langle a_{I}\rangle^{\frac{y}{h}}x^{\deg(I)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

(recall that
1+\mathfrak{m}_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}

is a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-vector space for the multiplicative structure and we can identify
\frac{y}{d}
with an element of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
s\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
the map
I\mapsto I^{s}
defines a group homomorphism

\mathcal{I}_{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
. Moreover, the
field
V
generated by
K
and the values
I^{s_{n}}
in
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
with
s_{n}=(\pi^{-n},n)
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is a finite extension of
K
.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma follows from Proposition
3.5
. The second part is proved in
[
17
, Proposition 8.2.9]
.
∎

Other properties of this ideal exponentiation map are collected in
[
17
, §8.2]
.

4.1.1.
Goss’ partial zeta functions

Choose a uniformizer
\pi
and a sign function
\operatorname{sgn}
for
K_{\infty}
with
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=1
as above.
For a non-zero fractional ideal
I
of
A
, we introduce the
partial zeta function

(4.3)

Z_{I}(s):=\sum_{d\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in I^{+}\\
\deg(a)=d\end{smallmatrix}}a^{-s}

where
I^{+}
denotes the set of elements of
I
that are in the kernel of
\operatorname{sgn}:K_{\infty}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

The series
Z_{I}(s)
converges for the valuation of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
if the “real part of
s
is
>1
”. The real part of
s
is by definition the real number
|x|
(observe that choosing any total order on
A^{+}
, we have that the sequence
a\mapsto\deg(a)

tends to infinity).
Once we choose a uniformizer
\pi
of
K_{\infty}
, we have a group map
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
and we can identify
\mathbb{Z}
with its image. So we can write

(4.4)

Z_{I}(n):=Z_{I}(s_{n})=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in I^{+}\end{smallmatrix}}a^{-s_{n}}\in K_{\infty},\quad n\geq 0

where

(4.5)

s_{n}:=(\pi^{-n},n)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty},

and the values do not depend on
\pi
. In the case
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
with
the usual point
\infty
and the usual sign function,
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
and we are back to Carlitz zeta values (
3.4
).

In fact,
Z_{I}
defines a continuous function
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
, see below.

4.2.
Goss’ zeta functions

The
Goss’ zeta function
associated to the triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
is the formal series:

(4.6)

\zeta_{A}(s):=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}I\text{ non-zero }\\
\text{ ideal of }A\end{smallmatrix}}I^{-s}.

This series too, converges for
s=(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
with
|x|>1
. Indeed the sequence
I\mapsto\deg(I)
, running over the ideals of
A
, tends to infinity.
Additionally, over the same “half-plane” of Goss’ plane, the zeta function
\zeta_{A}(s)

has a converging eulerian product expansion

\zeta_{A}(s)=\prod_{P}\Big(1-\frac{1}{P^{s}}\Big)^{-1},

where the product runs over the maximal ideals
P
of
A
, by Lemma
4.1
. Note that
if
s=(x,y)
is such that
|x|>1
,
P^{s}\neq 1
so that
\zeta_{A}(s)
does not vanish for
|x|>1
. We have familiarity with such statements, for Riemann’s zeta function,
\zeta(s)
does not vanish for
s=x+iy
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
x>1
.

For many reasons this can be considered as an analogue of Riemann’s zeta function attached to a triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
. In this text we do not tackle more general
L
-functions, but we keep focussing on these objects to seize not only the analogies, but also their important differences if compared with the classical theory of zeta functions.

To investigate the first properties of
\zeta_{A}
we decompose it in combination of partial zeta functions. We partition the group of invertible ideals
\mathcal{I}_{A}
into distinct cosets
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{n}
modulo
the subgroup of the principal ideals, choosing, as representatives, invertible ideals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
of
A
such that
I_{i}^{-1}
is an ideal of
A
for all
i
. By Lemma
4.1
we have:

(4.7)

\zeta_{A}(s):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}^{-s}Z_{I_{i}^{-1}}(s),

an identity that defines a continuous function over the “half-plane” of the elements
s=(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
such that
|x|>1
. For
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]
as
above, using (
4.7
), we find that

\zeta_{A}(s)
agrees with
Z_{A}(s)
and therefore, this function provides a continuous interpolation of Carlitz zeta values.
Properties of the partial zeta functions immediately imply corresponding properties for the zeta functions
\zeta_{A}
.

Goss also developed, more generally, a theory parallel to that of Dedekind zeta functions. Classically, Dedekind zeta functions are associated to
number field extensions
L/\mathbb{Q}
. In Goss’ theory, after the choice of a triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
one considers
E
a finite extension of
K
and
B
the integral closure of
A
in
E
.
We have the norm map
N_{E/K}:\mathcal{I}_{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{A}
which defines a group homomorphism from the
group of the invertible ideals of
B
to that of
A
. Then we define, for
s\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
:

\zeta_{E,A}(s):=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{a}\text{ ideal of }B\end{smallmatrix}}N_{E/K}(\mathfrak{a})^{-s}=\prod_{\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{p}\text{ maximal ideal of }B\end{smallmatrix}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N_{E/K}(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\Big)^{-1}

that are convergent series for
s=(x,y)
with
|x|>1
. The key tool is again the exponentiation of ideals in
\mathcal{I}_{A}
that we discussed in §
4.1
.

For the partial zeta functions
Z_{I}
one of the most important properties, discovered by Goss, is:

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
I
non-zero ideal of
A
,
Z_{I}

extends to a continuous function
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.

A detailed proof can be found in
[
17
]
, see also
[
37
]
. We omit the details also because in the present paper, inspired by Goss’ work, we will give a proof of one of
these entireness results, for a different class of functions, see Theorem
7.1
, where we use tools of essentially the same type.

Lemma
4.2
can be briefly explained by the fact that finite sums of the type

S_{I,d}(s):=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in I^{+}\\
\deg(a)=d\end{smallmatrix}}a^{-s},

if non-zero,
may have large vanishing finite sub-sums as it follows from the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 8.9.2]
, and consequently, for fixed

s
with
d\rightarrow\infty
, they have multiplicative valuation going to zero faster than what trivial estimates could detect. This is something that occurs in our characteristic
p>0
setting. Also the more general Dedekind-like
functions
\zeta_{E,A}
introduced above define continuous functions
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.

In
[
17
, Definition 8.5.1]
Goss introduces the notion of
entire function over
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
, a continuous
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-family of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
-valued entire functions of the variable
x^{-1}
the members of which are series that are uniformly convergent on bounded subsets of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.
Goss interprets the existence of entire extensions of his zeta functions as one of the “signs confirming the possible existence of functional equations”.

4.2.1.
Negative values

There are elements
s\in\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
such that
Z_{I}(s)
vanish regardless of the choice of
I
. We have a phenomenon of “trivial zeroes at negative values” that is described in the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
n\leq 0
we have
Z_{I}(n)\in A
. Moreover,
Z_{I}(0)=1
if
I
is trivial, while otherwise
Z_{I}(0)=0
, and if
q-1\mid n
with
n<0
, then

Z_{I}(n)=0
.

Proof.

Write
I^{+}(d)
for the set of elements of
I
that are monic (that is, in the kernel of

\operatorname{sgn}
, for the choice we did initially) and of degree
d
. Similarly, write
I(<d)
for the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space of the elements of
I
that
have degree
<d
. Then either
I^{+}(d)=\{0\}
(this for example happens if
I=A
and
d
is a Weierstrass gap) or

I^{+}(d)=a_{0}+I(<d)
for all
a_{0}\in I^{+}(d)
. By
[
17
, Lemma 8.8.1]
(see also
[
37
, Theorem 5.3.1]
)

S_{I,d}(-n)=\sum_{a\in I^{+}(d)}a^{n}=\sum_{a\in I(<d)}(a_{0}+a)^{n}

vanishes for all
d
such that
q-1
times the dimension of
I(<d)
is strictly larger than
n
. In particular the sequence of
partial sums (
4.3
) stabilizes when
s=s_{n}
for
n\leq 0
.
This proves that

Z_{I}(n)\in A
for all
n\leq 0
. Now, suppose additionally that
q-1\mid n
with
n<0
. Then, by the fact that
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}=A^{\times}

(we use the hypothesis that
\infty
has degree one),

S_{I,d}(n)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{a\in I(<d+1)}a^{n}.

By the previously proved property,

Z_{I}(-n)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{a\in I(<d+1)}a^{n}

for all

large enough (the sum on the right does not depend on

if

again by Lemma

and we can find

that is non-empty and such that

does not divide its cardinality is

.
∎

By (
4.7
) and Lemma

we deduce:

Corollary 4.4
.

The Goss zeta function
\zeta_{A}:\mathbb{S}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
vanishes over the image of
-(q-1)\mathbb{Z}_{>0}=\{1-q,2-2q,\ldots\}
in
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
.

Recall that
Z_{I},\zeta_{A}
also depend on the choice of a uniformizer of
\pi
, but the above vanishing property does not.
Analogously, Riemann’s zeta function, seen as a meromorphic function over
\mathbb{C}
, has zeroes at even integers

, so called

trivial zeroes
. The fact that Riemann’s
\xi(s)
is entire without real zeroes follows from the interaction of
the trivial zeroes with the poles of
\Gamma
. Goss raised the question of completing
\zeta_{A}
multiplying it by some “gamma factor”
to obtain
an analogue of the function
\xi
over
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
. There are several functions that
have something to share with Euler’s gamma function, reviewed in Goss’ book
[
17
]
. However, the raised question remains at the moment unanswered. For instance, we do not know how to answer the following

Question 4.5
.

Given
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
, characterize the set
\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}:\zeta_{A}(n)=0\}
.

Goss proves
[
16
]
that in the case

,

\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}:\zeta_{A}(n)=0\}=-(q-1)\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

A more general result is discussed in Thakur’s
[
37
, Theorem 5.3.2]
.
However, it does not answer the above question completely.

4.2.2.
Positive values

Given a triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
, with
A
the ring of functions over

that are regular away from

(Goss)
.

There exists an element
\widetilde{\pi}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}

and depending on the choice

, such that

of

the field described in Lemma
4.1
and

.

Without giving full details, we mention that one of the key points in Goss’ proof is that the choice

corresponds to a choice of a
sign-normalized Drinfeld module of rank one

As a last comment on Goss theory, we mention that the structures described by Corollary

and Theorem

5.
Zeta functions over curves

In this second part of our paper we discuss recent investigations on alternative ways of interpolating Goss’ zeta values (
4.8
). We will be mainly interested in interpolating special values of Goss’ partial zeta functions
as in (
4.4
) for example. One of the properties that we study here is that these values can be interpolated by rigid analytic functions over the curve
X
, with the scalars extended to
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
(with essential singularities). The picture that emerges escapes, at least at first sight, from Goss’ viewpoint and from Tate’s game of quasi-characters but in fact turns out to be connected with it in a subtle way. It looks like the premises of a theory parallel to that of Dedekind zeta functions appear in this direction. Some examples of such functions are discussed in §
7
The experimental data observed by Ferraro in the appendix to the paper point toward the presence of many of the principal milestones of the classical theory.

We are going to present
results of Ferraro (see Theorem
5.3
, and the more general results in
[
13
,
14
]
) which can be interpreted as a
functional identity
for a variant of
Goss partial zeta functions discussed in §
4.1.1
. In §
6
we present the tools used and we give an essentially self-contained sketch of Proof of Ferraro’s theorem
5.3
following his paper
[
13
]
.

5.1.
Functions over curves

We consider a triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
as in the previous sections. We continue to assume that
\infty
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational point of

.

If
B
is any ring with unit, there exists a unique ring homomorphism

.
But if

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra homomorphisms

For instance, there is a bijective correspondence

\{f:A\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\text{-algebra homomorphisms }\}\leftrightarrow\{\text{Closed points of }\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}A)\}.

Let us write

for the curve

with the scalars extended to
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
.
The point
\infty
extends to a point of
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
. We can identify

X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}\setminus\{\infty\}=\operatorname{Spec}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}).

Points of this affine curve are therefore
in correspondence with
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra morphisms

\chi:A\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}.

The curve
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
also has a canonical point
\Xi
, distinct from
\infty
, determined by the canonical embedding
A\subset\mathbb{C}_{\infty}

(
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
is constructed out of
A
by successively constructing
K

K_{\infty}
the completion at the place
\infty
, and then, the completion
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
of a separable closure).

There are other elementary tools that we want to use. The
q
-Frobenius

F
is the bijective map
X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\rightarrow X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
which is the identity on
\{\infty\}
and is otherwise determined by the map

\chi\mapsto\chi^{(1)}
where, with
\chi
an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra homomorphism,
\chi^{(1)}
the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra map determined by

\chi^{(1)}(a):=\chi(a)^{q},\quad a\in A.

On
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
we also have the
Anderson twist
. This is the unique
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra automorphism

A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
determined by
a\otimes c\mapsto a\otimes c^{q}
. If
h\in A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
we denote by
h^{(1)}
its Anderson twist, it uniquely extends to rational functions over
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
. Similarly, we can define
h^{(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, because the Anderson twist is bijective. Note that
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
is the ring of rational functions over
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
that are regular away from
\infty
. The evaluation of such a rational function
at a point
\chi\in X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}\setminus\{\infty\}
can be done once clarified what it means for elementary tensors. If
a\in A
and
c\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
, we have

(a\otimes c)(\chi)=c\chi(a)\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}.

Then the twist of Anderson is the unique
A\otimes 1
-linear map
h\mapsto h^{(1)}
over
\operatorname{Frac}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
such that

h^{(1)}(\chi^{(1)})=h(\chi)^{q},\quad h\text{ regular function},\quad\chi\text{ point.}

5.1.1.
Tate algebras

On the ring
A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
we have the
Gauss valuation
extending
|1\otimes(\cdot)|

over

(to distinguish it from

associated to

of points

(5.1)

\boldsymbol{D}_{X}=\{\chi:\{|(a\otimes 1)(\chi)|:a\in A\}\text{ is bounded}\}.

It can be proved that

if and only if
(a\otimes 1)(\chi)\rightarrow 0
as

runs in
A
. Moreover

-algebra isomorphism between

and the algebra of the series
\sum_{i}c_{i}a_{i}

with
(a_{i})_{i}
an arbitrarily chosen basis of the

, by noticing that any such series can be

\boldsymbol{D}_{X}

This subset

An element

.
For example, in the Carlitz case of §

\mathbb{F}_{q}[\theta]

. In this case

is a basis of

.

5.2.
Partial zeta functions over curves

Let
I
be a non-zero ideal of

If

An analogue of Lemma

holds for these functions, a proof in the particular case

[

, but the general case can be tackled with the same methods, that fundamentally draw us back to

]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
I
ideal of
A
and

, the function

extends to a rigid analytic function over

with an essential singularity at

.

We will more simply say that

extends to an

.
Variants of these functions where the variable is in

with

can be considered, see
[

]
in the Carlitz case and several papers authored by Anglès, Ngo Dac and Tavares Ribeiro among which we mention

.

5.2.1.
Link with quasi-characters

We would like to connect this construction with the discussion of §
4

We see here that our functions are not simple re-parametrizations of Goss’ functions, although they interact with them.
It is possible indeed to establish a correspondence between points
\chi\in\boldsymbol{D}_{X}\setminus X(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\text{alg}})
and
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
-valued quasi-characters via the next Lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Given
\chi\in\boldsymbol{D}_{X}\setminus X(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\text{alg}})

extending the multiplicative map
A\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
defined by the evaluation at

Proof.

First note that the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra map

defined by

For

is very ample and determines
a closed immersion

in

for some

with
\deg(a_{0})=\deg(a_{1})+1

. We can make
additional choices so that

so that every element

can be expanded as a Laurent series

, on the other side
the series

such that

\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
(to an element of its valuation ring) and this map is the quasi-character

that we wanted.
∎

Conversely, for any
evaluative quasi-character

defined by sending a chosen uniformizer

to

with

and

in

such that

and arises from the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra map

The entailed subspace of
\operatorname{Hom}_{c}(K_{\infty}^{\times},\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times})
does not equal any of the spaces
\mathbb{S}_{\infty}
of Goss.
To explain the relationship with Goss’

we use a generalization of Goss’ zeta functions introduced by Anglès

(it is only considered in the Carlitz case of §

\mathbb{S}_{n,\infty}^{\sharp}:=\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}\times\mathbb{X}_{\infty}^{n},

If

where for

The sets

5.2.2.
Review of Ferraro’s Theorem

We now review a functional identity discovered by Ferraro in
[
13
,
14
]
(see Theorem
5.3
) which serves to deduce partial analogues of the functional equation of Riemann’s
\zeta
, and also an analogue of Riemann’s
\xi
. A precursor of this result has been first obtained by Green and Papanikolas in
[
26
]
in the
case
(X,\infty)
elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The first prototype, over the projective line, is in
[
29
]
but methods of analysis are a little bit different and will not be discussed here.

To read the statement of Ferraro’s Theorem
5.3
(we give a simplified version of his results) technical preparation is requested, presented in §
6
. In particular, we need to review the theory of shtuka divisors and functions. The results in this section provide us with exactly
h_{A}
effective divisors

V
on
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
of degree
g
the genus of
X
(
h_{A}
is the class number of
A
) called shtuka divisors. They satisfy

h^{0}(V)=1
. For any shtuka divisor
V
there exists, up to a non-zero scalar factor, a unique shtuka function

f
rational over
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
such that its divisor satisfies

\operatorname{Div}(f)=V^{(1)}-V+\Xi-\infty

(see Lemma
6.1
).
Additionally, to each such shtuka divisor
V
corresponds a unique dual shtuka divisor
V^{*}
(see Lemma
6.4
), effective of degree
g
, with
h^{0}(V^{*})=1
. In fact
V^{*}
is the birational inverse of
V
in the sense of Weil. Explicitly, by Lemma
6.4
, there exists up to a scalar factor, a unique rational function
\delta
over
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
such that

\operatorname{Div}(\delta)=V+V^{*}-2g\infty.

We will not fully justify it here, but it is also possible to show that
\delta
can be chosen in
A\otimes H
and
f
in

\operatorname{Frac}(A\otimes H)
with
H
the Hilbert class field of
A
.

Ferraro’s theorem also needs the notion of
special function
(introduced in
[
4
]
). This is a class of elements of
\mathbb{T}_{A}
that extend to meromorphic functions on
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}\setminus\{\infty\}
, depending on the choice of a shtuka function
f
, and that we quickly review in §
6.4
, to play the role of the classical “gamma factor” in the corresponding functional equation of Riemann’s zeta function. Note that Anderson’s twist extends uniquely to an
A\otimes 1
-automorphism of
\mathbb{T}_{A}
.
Once
f
is chosen
\omega
is characterized, up to a factor in
A\otimes 1
, by

\omega^{(1)}=f\omega.

We finally recall the element
\widetilde{\pi}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}
, analogue of
2\pi i
, involved in Theorem
4.6
, and also depending on our choice of
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
and defined up to a multiple in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
A special case of Ferraro’s result is the following:

Theorem 5.3

(Ferraro)
.

For any triple
(X,\infty,\operatorname{sgn})
there is a unique shtuka function
f
associated to a shtuka divisor
V
together with a non-zero special function
\omega
, and a unique choice of
\delta\in A\otimes H
, such that

(5.3)

\omega^{(1)}Z_{X,A}=\widetilde{\pi}\delta^{(1)}.

More generally, Ferraro’s result provides an identity as above for any partial zeta function
Z_{X,I}
and the correspondence
I\mapsto V
is governed by Artin reciprocity law for the extension
H/K
but for simplicity we will not consider these aspects here. Note that
Z_{X,A}
and
\omega
are transcendental functions; the theorem says that they are meromorphic over
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}\setminus\{\infty\}
but both have essential singularities supported at
\infty
. Their product combines to neutralize the essential singularities and gives rise to a rational function
\delta
which is however difficult to compute in full generality, just like
f
.

The zeroes of the right-hand side of (
5.3
) correspond to the degree
2g
effective divisor

6.4.
Special functions

This is yet another crucial part in the structures we are describing. Special functions were introduced,
along with the terminology, in
[
4

. Choose a shtuka function
f
for
(X,\infty)
.

Definition 6.10
.

A special function

is an element of
\mathbb{T}_{A}
such that

(6.15)

\omega^{(1)}=f\omega.

The set of special functions is an
A
-module denoted by
\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)
. More precisely, the multiplication of the special function
\omega
by

is
(a\otimes 1)\omega
(remember that
(a\otimes 1)^{(1)}=a\otimes 1\in\mathbb{T}_{A}
).
Choose a Drinfeld
A
-module of rank one
\phi
with shtuka function
f
.
Using (
6.11
)
one proves that equivalently,

\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)=\{\omega\in\mathbb{T}_{A}:\phi_{a}(\omega)=(a\otimes 1)\omega\},

where
\phi_{a}
denotes here the
A\otimes 1
-linear extension of the operator
\phi_{a}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}[\tau]
to
\mathbb{T}_{a}
, see

[
4
, Lemma 3.6]
. Moreover, the structure of this
A
-module is satisfactorily described in
[
25
, Theorem 3.11]
. We deduce that
\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)
is isomorphic to an invertible ideal of

. In particular,
it is not the zero module. Hence:

Corollary 6.11
.

The
A
-module
\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)
, non-trivial, is contained in the field of meromorphic functions over
X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}
. Any non-zero element of this module, seen as a meromorphic function,
has a simple pole at
\Xi^{(i)}
for all
i\geq 0
.

Proof.

By
[
25
, Theorem 3.11]
,
\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)
is non-trivial, it contains non-constant functions
over
\boldsymbol{D}_{X}
the affinoid subset defined in (
5.1
). Pick one of them, call it
\omega
.
Now set, for
\rho\in|\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}|
,

\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho):=\{\chi\in X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}:\{|(a\otimes 1)(\chi)|\rho^{-\deg(a)}:a\in A\}\text{ is bounded}\}.

Of course
\boldsymbol{D}_{X}=\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(1)\subset\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho)
if
\rho\geq 1
.
We have
X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}=\cup_{\rho}\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho)
, so that we have determined a partial order
over
X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})
by means of the map
\mu:X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}\rightarrow|\mathbb{C}_{\infty}^{\times}|

defined by

\mu(\chi):=\inf\{\rho:\chi\in\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho)\}.

In a similar way we can construct totally ordered
subsets of
X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}
that are isometric with

but we will not need this here.

Since
\omega\in\mathbb{T}_{A}
we can choose a Banach basis
(a_{i}\otimes 1)_{i\geq 0}\subset A\otimes 1
of
\mathbb{T}_{A}
and expand

(6.16)

\omega=\sum_{i\geq 0}c_{i}a_{i},\quad c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{\infty}.

Then
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}c_{i}=0
.
The fact that

\omega
satisfies
\phi_{a}(\omega)=(a\otimes 1)\omega
for a non-constant

implies that the series (
6.16
) is
overconvergent
, that is,
there exists
\rho>1
such that

. Therefore there exists
\rho>1
such that

\omega
extends to a rigid analytic function over
\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho)
. At once

is rigid analytic over

\tau(\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho))=\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho^{q})\supset\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho)

\omega=\frac{\omega^{(n)}}{ff^{(1)}\cdots f^{(n-1)}}

extends to a meromorphic function over
\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho^{q^{n}})
for all

. We conclude observing that

\bigcup_{n}(\boldsymbol{D}_{X}(\rho^{q^{n}}))=X(\mathbb{C}_{\infty})\setminus\{\infty\}.

In particular, any non-zero element
\omega\in\mathfrak{Sf}(\phi)
, has simple poles at the points
\Xi,\Xi^{(1)},\ldots
.
∎

Remark 6.12

The terminology “overconvergence” has been employed, in the above proof, for the following reasons. In an only apparently separate topic Dwork,

6.5.
Partial sums of partial zeta functions

In this subsection we follow the arguments of
[
10
]
.
Define
0=j_{0}<j_{1}<\cdots
where for all
d
,
j_{d}
is minimal with the property that
\mathcal{L}(j_{d}\infty)=d+1
. By Riemann-Roch theorem
we have
j_{d}=d+g
for

large enough, and there are
g
Weierstrass gaps in this sequence. Taking
a_{0}=1
and, for all
d
, an element
a_{d}\in A
of degree

, we have a basis
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})

\mathcal{L}(j_{d}\infty)

determine an alternative basis for

.
We define the rational functions

\mathcal{S}_{j_{m}}:=\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}a\in A\\
\text{monic}\\
\deg(a)=j_{m}\end{smallmatrix}}a\otimes a^{-1}\in A\otimes K,\quad\mathcal{S}_{\leq j_{m}}:=\sum_{d=0}^{m}\mathcal{S}_{j_{d}}.

We have

converging for the Gauss norm.
The next result is proved in
[
10
, §2.2]
, compare with
[

, Propositions 6.3, 6.4]

.

The following properties hold.

(1)

For all

,
(\mathcal{S}_{j_{0}},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{j_{d}})

\mathcal{L}(j_{d}\infty)

(2)

For all

Lemma 6.14

For all

on

\Big(\mathcal{S}_{\leq j_{m}}\Big)=(V^{*}_{m})^{(1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Xi^{(i)}-j_{m}\infty.

[V^{*}_{m}-g\infty]=v^{(m)}-v

Observe that for all

,
\mathcal{S}_{j_{m}},\mathcal{S}_{\leq j_{m}}\in A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}K
. The order of the pole at infinity is exactly

by the conjunction of the two parts of Proposition

and a variant of Lemma

implies that for

,
\mathcal{S}_{j_{m}}
also vanishes at the distinct points
\Xi,\ldots,\Xi^{(m-1)}
(see
[

). By the second part of Proposition

\mathcal{S}_{\leq j_{m}}
vanishes at the points

\Xi^{(1)},\ldots,\Xi^{(m)}
for all

. Since

of degree

Compute, when

and writing

∎

The topological space underlying (

. Therefore, over a strictly increasing subsequence

and in the

For any such

Proof of Theorem

.

.

Remark 7.7
.

The proof of Theorem

we gave easily extends to prove that, for all

To conclude we observe that, up to a certain extent, we can compare Ferraro’s rationality theorem for

Addendum: Non-trivial zeros

In

Goss discusses an avatar of Riemann’s hypothesis for his function

is analytic on

, and its divisor outside
\infty
is

\mathrm{Div}^{+}(\zeta_{H_{R},A})=W+h_{R}\sum_{i\geq 1}\Xi^{(i)},

where
W
is an effective
K_{\infty}
-rational divisor of finite degree and
\Xi^{(i)}\in X(K)
corresponds to the ring homomorphism
A\to K
sending
a\in A
to
a^{q^{i}}
.

Proof.

Fix representative ideals
\{I_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in G_{R}}
of
R
for each ideal class in
\mathrm{Cl}(R)
, and define the rational functions

Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1},n}:=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in I_{\sigma}^{-1}\setminus\{0\}\\
\deg(a)\leq n\end{subarray}}a\otimes a^{-1},

so that in
\mathbb{T}_{A}
we have

\lim_{n}Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1},n}=Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}=-\sum_{a\in I_{\sigma}^{-1}\setminus\{0\}}a\otimes a^{-1}.

Denote by
d
the degree of the ideal
I_{\sigma}A\subseteq A
and by
c
the dimension of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space
A/R
; by Riemann-Roch, for
n\gg 0
, the elements of degree at most
n
in
I_{\sigma}^{-1}
form an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space of dimension
n-d-c-g+1
; in particular, for
1\leq m\leq n-d-c-g
we have

Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1},n}(\Xi^{(m)})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in I_{\sigma}^{-1}\setminus\{0\}\\
\deg(a)\leq n\end{subarray}}a^{q^{m}-1}=0.

Since the poles of
Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1},n}
outside
\infty
coincide with the effective divisor
D_{I_{\sigma}}
associated to the ideal
I_{\sigma}A\subseteq A
, we have:

\operatorname{Div}_{X}(Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1},n})=W_{\sigma,n}-D_{I_{\sigma}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-d-c-g}\Xi^{(i)}-n\infty,

where
W_{\sigma,n}
is an effective divisor of degree
c+g
. Using the same techniques as
[
13
]
, this allows us to prove that the divisor of the limit
Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}
outside
\infty
is:

\operatorname{Div}^{+}_{X}(Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}})=W_{\sigma}-D_{I_{\sigma}}+\sum_{i\geq 1}\Xi^{(i)},

where
W_{\sigma}
is an effective
K_{\infty}
-rational divisor of degree
\leq c+g
; recall that the divisor of
Z_{X,A}
outside infinity is instead:

\operatorname{Div}^{+}_{X}(Z_{X,A})=V_{*}^{(1)}+\sum_{i\geq 1}\Xi^{(i)},

where
V_{*}
is an effective divisor of degree
g
(which is a dual Drinfeld divisor). In particular, we can write

Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}=l_{\sigma}Z_{X,A}

for some rational function
l_{\sigma}
on
X_{K_{\infty}}
.

We can rewrite

\xi(\phi)=\sum_{\sigma\in G_{R}}(\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}\sigma=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in G_{R}}(\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})l_{\sigma}\sigma\right)\zeta_{A},

hence its determinant is a rational multiple of
\zeta_{A}^{h_{R}^{2}}
.
We can check that the determinant has actually no poles outside infinity by showing that the same is true for all coefficients
(\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}
:
if we denote by
Y\to X
the cover associated to the field extension
H_{R}/K
, since
\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}B_{R}=I_{\sigma}B_{R}
, the divisor of
\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1}
on
Y_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
is

\operatorname{Div}^{+}_{Y}(\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})=D_{I_{\sigma}}-\deg(I_{\sigma})\infty,

where by slight abuse of notation we denote in the same way the effective divisors
I_{\sigma}
and
\infty
on
X_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
and their pullback to
Y_{\mathbb{C}_{\infty}}
, hence

\operatorname{Div}_{Y}^{+}\left((\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}\right)=\operatorname{Div}_{Y}^{+}\left((\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})\right)+\operatorname{Div}_{Y}^{+}\left(Z_{X,I_{\sigma}^{-1}}\right)=W_{\sigma}+\sum_{i\geq 1}\Xi^{(i)}.

To conclude the proof, we simply need to show that the determinant of
\sum_{\sigma\in G_{R}}(\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}\otimes\partial\phi_{I_{\sigma}}^{-1})l_{\sigma}\sigma
has no poles in any positive twist of
\Xi
, which is obvious because the poles of any
l_{\sigma}
, namely
V_{*}^{(1)}+D_{I_{\sigma}}
, do not contain
\infty
.
∎

11.
Computations

Fix
R:=A
,
B:=B_{A}
,
H:=H_{A}
.
For a given positive degree
d
, let’s denote by
\mathcal{I}(d)
the set of ideals in
B
whose norm has degree
d
. We know that the finite sums

\zeta_{H,A}(d):=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(d)}N_{H/K}(I)\otimes N_{H/K}(I)^{-1}\in A\otimes K_{\infty}

and

\zeta_{H,A}(\leq d):=\sum_{i\leq d}\zeta_{H,A}(d)

are well defined, and that the latter converges for
d\to\infty
to the analytic function
\zeta_{H,A}\in\mathbb{T}_{A}
.
Given a nonnegative integer
r
, it can be proven using
[
17
, Lemma 8.8.1]
that, if for all
H
-rational effective divisors
D
of degree
d
with support at infinity we have
\dim H^{0}(D-\infty)>h(1+r(q-1))
, then the valuation of
\zeta_{H,A}(d)
is at least
d+q^{r}
. By Riemann–Roch, since
\deg(D-\infty)=d-h
, the first inequality is implied by
d-h-g_{H}+1>h(1+r(q-1))
, where
g_{H}
is the genus of
H
. By Riemann–Hurwitz, since the extension
H/K
is unramified, we can write
2-2g_{H}=h(2-2g)
, hence the equivalent inequalities

r<\frac{\frac{d}{h}-g-1}{q-1}\Leftrightarrow d>h(g+1+r(q-1))

imply that the valuation of
\zeta_{H,A}(d)
is at least
d+q^{r}
.

In other words, the fastest approximations of
\zeta_{H,A}
are obtained for small
q
’s and
h
’s; nevertheless, even for the optimal conditions
q=3,h=2,g=1
, we would expect the valuation of
\zeta_{H,A}(d)
to exceed e.g.
100
only for
d>20
. It turns out that, at least for the examples we used for our computation, this is not the case, and the approximations converge much faster. In particular, it looks like the valuation of
\zeta_{H,A}(d)
grows approximately like
q^{\frac{d}{h}-g}
, rather than its
q-1
-th root.

By reason of the following computations, we state the following conjecture:

Conjecture 11.1
.

The analytic function
\zeta_{H,A}
has a zero of multiplicity
h-1
at
\Xi
.

For simplicity, we have tested elliptic and hyperelliptic curves defined by equations of the form
y^{2}=D(x)
, with
q=3,5
. The product between
\zeta_{H,A}(\leq d)
and its conjugate is a polynomial
N_{H/K}(\zeta_{H,A}(\leq d))\in K_{\infty}[x]
of degree
d
, and we use its factorization to deduce properties of the zeroes of
\zeta_{H,A}
. Call
u
a uniformizer of
K_{\infty}

in

to be

.

[\infty,2,4,6,12,18,36,54,108,162]
