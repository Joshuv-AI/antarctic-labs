β-cyclodextrin-capped silver nanoparticles synthesised from Peganum harmala L. for sustainable crop protection with antimicrobial and larvicidal effects against phytopathogens and Spodoptera exigua
Authors: Chellasamy Panneerselvam, Abdulrahman Alasmari, Ra’fat F Affan, Zuhair M Mohammedsaleh, Kamalesh Balakumar Venkatesan, Kanimozhi Kaliyamoorthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study reports the green synthesis and characterisation of β-cyclodextrin-capped silver nanoparticles (β-CD@AgNPs) using Peganum harmala L. fruit extract as both a reducing and stabilising agent. Multiple spectroscopic and microscopic techniques confirmed the nanoparticles. UV–visible analysis revealed surface plasmon resonance bands at 405 and 549 nm, confirming AgNP formation and β-CD mediated stabilisation. FTIR spectra indicated the involvement of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and glycosidic functional groups in nanoparticle capping, while XRD confirmed crystalline, face-centred cubic AgNPs with additional β-CD peaks. TEM analysis showed well-dispersed spherical nanoparticles sized 4–24 nm, while DLS and zeta potential analyses confirmed colloidal stability (–26.8 mV). The antimicrobial activity of β-CD@AgNPs demonstrated strong inhibitory effects against plant pathogens, including Xanthomonas campestris, Pseudomonas syringae, Bacillus subtilis, and Aspergillus niger in a dose-dependent manner, with low MIC and MBC/MFC values indicating potent bactericidal and fungicidal activity. Insecticidal bioassays against Spodoptera exigua revealed significant concentration- and time-dependent larval mortality, reduced leaf consumption, and strong feeding deterrence. LD50 and LT50 values confirmed enhanced toxicity with prolonged exposure and higher concentrations. The findings highlight that β-CD@AgNPs exhibit dual antimicrobial and larvicidal activities, offering an eco-friendly, sustainable alternative to conventional pesticides and chemical agents. The synergistic role of β-CD enhanced nanoparticle stability, dispersibility, and bioavailability, contributing to prolonged biological efficacy. Thus, β-CD@AgNPs synthesised from P. harmala L. fruit extract hold promise as green nanobiopesticides for integrated crop protection and plant disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04737-w.Keywords: Green synthesis, β-cyclodextrin, Peganum harmala L., Antimicrobial activity, Feeding deterrence, Crop protection

Introduction

The pursuit of eco-friendly, sustainable agricultural practices has accelerated the development of innovative strategies for crop protection and disease management, particularly in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) cultivation. Peanut is one of the most economically important legumes worldwide, providing protein, oil, and nutrients to millions of people. Its cultivation supports rural economies and contributes significantly to global food security [3, 45]. Despite its importance, peanut productivity is severely constrained by a wide range of biotic stresses. Pathogens and pests collectively cause substantial yield losses and degrade produce quality, posing major challenges to sustainable peanut production [9]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for environmentally safe, efficient, and innovative strategies to mitigate these biotic threats.

Pseudomonas syringae is an opportunistic phytopathogen responsible for foliar spots and blights, leading to chlorosis, necrosis, and reduced photosynthetic activity [5]. Xanthomonas campestris is a serious threat, causing bacterial leaf spot, blight, and vascular wilt, resulting in significant yield losses in cabbage and broccoli [23]. Similarly, Bacillus subtilis, though widely recognised as a beneficial rhizobacterium and biocontrol agent, can also exhibit pathogenic interactions under stress conditions, affecting peanut seed quality and germination [8]. Other significant fungal diseases include leaf spots (early and late), rust, and stem rot, which affect various growth stages and can reduce yield under severe infection conditions [12, 50]. The infection processes typically involve initial colonisation of plant tissues, secretion of hydrolytic enzymes to degrade structural barriers, and evasion of plant immune responses [51]. For instance, Puccinia arachidis (peanut rust) penetrates stomatal openings using specialised structures called appressoria and extracts nutrients from host cells, causing characteristic chlorotic and necrotic lesions [49]. Soil-borne fungal pathogens that induce stem and collar rot compromise root and stem integrity, reducing nutrient uptake, weakening plant structure, and decreasing overall yield [30, 47].

In addition to microbial pathogens, insect pests present a significant threat to peanut production. The beet armyworm (Spodoptera exigua) is a globally distributed, polyphagous pest capable of infesting over 170 plant species, including peanut, cotton, pulses, and vegetables. Its larvae feed gregariously on foliage, skeletonising leaves, contaminating crops, and directly reducing yield. The pest’s ability to survive in off-season habitats, combined with its resistance to commonly used insecticides, amplifies the threat to sustainable peanut cultivation [32, 46]. Recent studies have highlighted the dynamic interactions between S. exigua herbivory and soil nematodes, showing that these interactions can modulate root defence pathways, hormonal signalling, and secondary susceptibility to microbial infections [17, 22]. The evolution of insecticide resistance among S. exigua populations has necessitated the development of alternative pest management strategies that move away from reliance on conventional chemicals.

Traditional pest and pathogen management strategies largely rely on synthetic pesticides and chemical bactericides. While these methods have been widely used, they are associated with several limitations, including environmental pollution, toxicity to non-target organisms, development of resistance in pests and pathogens, and potential health hazards to consumers. The adverse effects of chemical-intensive agriculture have driven the search for safer, eco-friendly, and effective alternatives that can mitigate biotic stresses while maintaining crop productivity [40, 43].

Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising solution in this regard, particularly through the use of green-synthesised nanoparticles. These nanoparticles, fabricated using plant extracts or microbial agents, possess potent antimicrobial and insecticidal properties, high surface reactivity, and enhanced biocompatibility [15]. Green nanoparticles offer significant advantages over conventional chemical agents, including lower effective concentrations, reduced environmental toxicity, and targeted activity against pathogens and pests [10, 26, 27]. Among the different nanomaterials, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are well known for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial, catalytic, and biomedical properties [1, 13]. However, AgNPs often face challenges such as aggregation and instability, which can reduce their effectiveness [14, 37].

To address these limitations, β-cyclodextrin (β-CD), a cyclic oligosaccharide, has been explored as a stabilising agent for nanoparticles. β-CD improves the solubility, dispersibility, and stability of AgNPs by forming host–guest inclusion complexes [28]. This stabilisation not only enhances the antimicrobial activity of silver nanoparticles but also enables co-delivery of bioactive compounds, thereby increasing their functional versatility. The synergy between AgNPs and β-CD has been successfully applied in fields such as drug delivery and biosensing and is now being investigated for agricultural applications [33, 48].

Peganum harmala L. (Syrian Rue) is a perennial medicinal plant with globular fruits that contain numerous brown seeds rich in β-carboline alkaloids, such as harmine and harmaline. These bioactive compounds exhibit antimicrobial, antioxidant, anticancer, and neuroprotective activities [38, 39]. The combination of P. harmala L. bioactive compounds with nanotechnology offers an innovative approach to enhancing plant protection, leveraging both the chemical defence conferred by natural metabolites and the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles.

The present study aims to investigate the synergistic potential of silver nanoparticles stabilised with β-cyclodextrin and bioactive compounds from P. harmala L. for the sustainable management of bacterial, fungal, and insect pests in peanut cultivation.

Materials and methods

Collection and extract preparation

Fresh fruits of P. harmala L. were collected from the Red Sea coast within the NEOM sector, Sharma, Saudi Arabia (28.035975, 35.227097) during the flowering and fruiting seasons. The collection of P. harmala L. plant materials was conducted in accordance with institutional, national, and international guidelines. Our collection methodology follows best practices for sustainable harvesting to ensure no long-term impact on the local ecosystem. Moreover, the collected plants are not rare or endangered; rather, they are common plants available along the Red Sea coast within the NEOM sector, Sharma, Saudi Arabia. Prof. Fahd Al-Zuaiber authenticated the plant material at the Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, University of Tabuk. A voucher specimen was prepared and deposited in the Entomology and Toxicology Laboratory at the Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, University of Tabuk, Tabuk, Saudi Arabia, under voucher number UT/PH/2024/Tabuk/KSA. The collected fruits of P. harmala L. were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove adhering dust and impurities, and air-dried under shade. The dried fruits were then finely powdered using a clean mortar and pestle. 5 g of the fruit powder was boiled in 100 mL of 70% ethanol for 15 min. The mixture was allowed to cool at room temperature and subsequently filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to obtain a clear ethanolic extract. The freshly prepared extract was stored at 4 °C and used as a reducing and stabilising agent for the green synthesis of β-CD@AgNPs.

Synthesis and characterisation

In a one-pot synthesis, a 1.0 mM silver nitrate (AgNO3) solution was prepared (0.017 g AgNO3 in 100 mL water), and β-CD was dissolved at 0.5% w/v in deionised water (0.5 g in 100 mL) with gentle heating and stirring until a clear solution was obtained. The AgNO3 and β-CD solutions were combined in a round-bottom flask, stirred vigorously, and heated to 60 °C. The P. harmala L. extract was then added dropwise (1:4 volume ratio of extract to metal solution) under continuous stirring. The pH was adjusted to mildly alkaline (pH 8) with 0.1 M NaOH to facilitate the reduction. The solution changed from colourless to pale yellow to brown, indicating the formation of AgNPs. The reaction mixture was stirred at 60 °C for an additional 2 h to ensure complete nanoparticle growth and β-CD encapsulation. The suspension was cooled to room temperature, and nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min, followed by washing 3 times with deionised water to remove unbound biomolecules. The purified nanoparticles were dried under vacuum (lyophilized—sublimation at − 40 °C, desorption at 30 °C) for storage at 4 °C.

The β-CD@AgNPs were characterised using multiple analytical techniques. UV–visible spectroscopy was performed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) to confirm nanoparticle formation via surface plasmon resonance. Functional groups involved in the reduction and stabilisation of nanoparticles were identified by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker Optics GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany). The morphology, particle size, and dispersion of the nanoparticles were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) using a JEOL microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The crystalline nature and phase composition were analysed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an X-ray diffractometer (Malvern PANalytical Ltd., Almelo, Netherlands). Surface elemental composition and oxidation states were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The hydrodynamic diameter, particle size distribution, and surface charge were evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis using a Zetasizer instrument (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK).

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the synthesised β-CD@AgNPs was evaluated against X. campestris, P. syringae, B. subtilis, and A. niger using the agar well diffusion method. Fresh bacterial cultures were grown overnight in nutrient broth at 37 °C and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (1 × 108 CFU/mL). Fungal cultures were grown in potato dextrose broth at 25 °C and standardised to 1 × 106 spores/mL using a hemocytometer. Sterile nutrient agar (for bacteria) and potato dextrose agar (for fungi) plates were uniformly swabbed with the standardised inocula. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched, and 50 µL of β-CD@AgNPs at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL were introduced into the wells. Positive controls were ciprofloxacin disc (10 µg/mL) for bacterial strains and fluconazole (25 µg/mL) for fungal strains. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for bacteria (24 h) and 25 °C for fungi (72 h). The zones of inhibition around each well were measured in millimeters (mm) to assess antimicrobial efficacy, and the results were recorded in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

MIC and MBC/MFC

The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal/Fungicidal Concentration (MBC/MFC) of the β-CD@AgNPs were determined against X. campestris, P. syringae, B. subtilis and A. niger using a microdilution method. Serial two-fold dilutions (1000 µg/mL to 1.95 µg/mL) of the β-CD@AgNPs were prepared in sterile nutrient broth for bacteria and potato dextrose broth for fungi in 96-well microtiter plates. Each well was inoculated with a standardised suspension of the respective pathogen (1 × 106 CFU/mL for bacteria and 1 × 105 spores/mL for fungi). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for bacteria (24 h) and 25 °C for fungi (72 h). MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no visible growth was observed. To determine MBC/MFC, aliquots from wells showing no visible growth were subcultured onto fresh agar plates and incubated under the same conditions. The lowest concentration that completely inhibited colony formation was recorded as the MBC/MFC.

Collection and rearing of S. exigua

Larvae of S. exigua were collected from a privately owned peanut (A. hypogaea L.) farm located in Akkur, Mayiladuthurai district, Tamil Nadu, India, with prior permission from the landowner. Ethical approval for the insect handling and experimentation was obtained in accordance with national biodiversity regulations and institutional guidelines. Healthy peanut leaves used for larvicidal bioassays were collected from the same farm. The identity of the insect species (S. exigua) was authenticated by an entomologist (Dr. Selvanarayanan, Annamalai University), while the host plant (A. hypogaea L.) was taxonomically authenticated by a botanist (Dr. Sakthivel Muthu, University of Madras-UoM/2025/4356).

Larvae of S. exigua were collected from a peanut farm in Akkur, Mayiladuthurai district, using a handpicking method during the early morning hours to ensure healthy specimens. The collected larvae were transported in ventilated plastic containers containing fresh peanut leaves to minimise stress and mortality. In the laboratory, larvae were reared under controlled conditions at 25 ± 2 °C, 70% relative humidity, and a 12:12 h light–dark photoperiod. Fresh peanut leaves were provided daily as food, and rearing continued until larvae reached the 4th instar, at which point they were selected for subsequent bioassays.

Leaf-dip bioassay

The leaf-dip method was employed to evaluate the insecticidal activity of β-CD@AgNPs. Healthy peanut leaves were immersed in nanoparticle solutions for 20 s and then air-dried under sterile conditions. 4th instar larvae were introduced into Petri dishes containing treated leaves. Untreated leaves dipped in sterile distilled water served as negative controls. Larval mortality and feeding inhibition were recorded at 24, 48 and 72 h post-treatment. The percentage mortality was calculated using Abbott's formula to correct for control mortality.

Diet incorporation bioassay

To assess systemic toxicity, β-CD@AgNPs were incorporated into an artificial diet at different concentrations. Approximately 5 g of diet was mixed thoroughly with the nanoparticle solution and allowed to solidify in Petri dishes. Larvae were introduced and observed for mortality, growth inhibition, and feeding behaviour over 24, 48, and 72 h. Control groups were maintained on a diet without nanoparticles.

Leaf-choice (feeding deterrence) bioassay

A leaf-choice test was conducted to evaluate feeding deterrence activity. Treated and untreated peanut leaf discs (2 cm diameter) were placed opposite each other in a Petri dish. Ten larvae were introduced into each dish and allowed to feed for 24 h. The leaf area consumed from treated and untreated discs was measured using a leaf area meter, and the feeding deterrence index (FDI) was calculated.

LD₅₀ and LT₅₀ determination

LD₅₀ and LT₅₀ values were calculated using probit analysis based on larval mortality data. Mortality percentages were corrected using Abbott's formula to account for control mortality. For LD₅₀ estimation, corrected mortality at 24 h was transformed into probit values and regressed against log₁₀-transformed concentrations; the concentration corresponding to probit 5.0 was recorded as the LD₅₀. LT₅₀ values were determined by regressing probit mortality against log₁₀-transformed exposure time at a fixed concentration.

All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Data were analysed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test to determine significant differences between treatments (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

β-CD@AgNPs characterization

Figure 1 represents the UV–visible spectrum of β-CD, AgNO3, P. harmala L. and green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs. The UV–Vis spectrum of P. harmala L. fruit extract exhibits peaks at 280 nm and 315 nm, indicative of polyphenolic compounds. AgNO3 shows no absorbance in the visible region, suggesting no reduction has occurred. β-CD displays a peak at 265 nm, typical of cyclodextrin. In general, the characteristic surface plasmon band of AgNPs appears near 420 nm, due to the collective oscillation of electrons [18, 29]. The broad bands were observed at 405 and 549 nm for green-synthesised β-CD@AgNPs. The peak at 405 nm is attributed to the SPR of AgNPs, arising from the movement of free surface electrons on the metallic nanoparticles. The broad band at 549 nm corresponds to nanoparticle aggregation. This aggregation arises from the intramolecular H-bonding between β-CDs, which are present over the core of AgNPs, and promotes the formation of larger NPs. The peaks observed in the UV region were attributed to the π–π* transition of aromatic constituents in β-CD and to phytochemicals in the P. harmala L. extract. The resultant absorption spectrum confirms the successful synthesis of β-CD-capped AgNPs.

UV–visible absorption spectrum of β-CD, AgNO3, P. harmala L. and green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs

FTIR analysis of nanoparticles provides detailed information on functional groups and bonding interactions present in nanoparticles. Figure 2 represents the IR spectrum of P. harmala L. mediated synthesis of β-CD-capped AgNPs. The band at 3262 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of the hydroxyl group present in the constituents of P. harmala L. fruit extract and the O–H group at the exterior of β-CD [44]. The peak at 1638 cm−1 corresponds to the C=C and C=O groups present in the phytoconstituents and in β-CD over AgNPs. The peak at 1314 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the carboxylate group. The absorption band at 1147 cm−1 represents the stretching vibration of C–O–C and C–C, arising from the glycosidic ring structure. The sharp absorption band at 1015 cm−1 corresponds to the C-O stretching vibration. The overall functional group analysis confirms the presence of phytoconstituents in P. harmala L. and β-CD as a capping agent on the surface of the Ag core.

FTIR spectrum of green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs

XRD analysis provides information on the crystalline nature and phase composition of nanoparticles. The appearance of a characteristic pattern at specific 2θ values of metals justify its crystalline structure. In Fig. 3, the emergence of peaks at 2θ of 38.3°, 43.4°, 64.8° and 77.7° corresponds to the planes (111), (200), (220) and (311), respectively. The mentioned planes are characteristic of silver nanoparticles in a face-centred cubic phase, consistent with the JCPDS data (# 04–0783) [2]. The crystalline size of β-CD@AgNPs was calculated using the Scherrer equation, yielding an average crystalline size of 21.8 nm. The additional intense peaks seen in the 2θ range of 10°–35° might arise from the monoclinic structure of β-CD [25]. The XRD results corroborate the presence of AgNPs and β-CD in the green-synthesised β-CD@AgNPs.

X-ray diffraction pattern of green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs

Figure 4a represents the TEM image of β-CD@AgNPs using the fruit extract of P. hamala as a reducing agent. The TEM image supports the formation of well-dispersed spherical nanoparticles. The inset image shows the high-resolution Ag particle with lattice fringes. Figure 4b shows the size distribution histogram of β-CD@AgNPs. The particles ranged in size from 4 to 24 nm, with an average size of 10.7 nm. Figure 4c shows the EDX spectrum of β-CD@AgNPs, indicating the presence of Ag, O, and C at dominant levels. The SAED pattern shows the semi-crystalline nature of AgNPs, as evidenced by weak diffraction rings. The presence of broadened rings that can still be indexed to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of face-centered cubic silver suggests that the particles contain small crystalline domains embedded in a partially disordered matrix, consistent with semi-crystalline Ag cores coated by an organic β-cyclodextrin shell. The combined TEM analysis results validate the effective capping of β-CD on AgNPs, which prevents NP aggregation, and the potential reduction ability of P. harmala L. fruit extract in reducing Ag(I) to Ag(0).

a TEM image, b Size distribution histogram of AgNPs and c EDAX analysis and d SAED pattern of β-CD@AgNPs

A high-resolution XPS spectrum of Ag and O present in β-CD@AgNPs is shown in Fig. 5. The Ag 3d spectrum shows two prominent peaks at 368.5 and 374.5 eV, corresponding to the 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 orbital couplings, which represent the characteristic metallic Ag peaks. The peaks confirm that Ag is predominantly present in the zero-valence state rather than Ag(I). The peaks at 531.3 eV are consistent with the oxygen in C–O and C–O–C groups of β-cyclodextrin in AgNPs. The peak at 535.8 eV is attributed to physisorbed water and to strongly bound hydrogen-bonding networks. The results confirm the existence of a layer of β-CD over the core of metallic Ag nanoparticles.

XPS spectrum of a Ag 3d and b O 1 s present in β-CD@AgNPs

Dynamic light scattering and Zeta potential analysis of β-CD@AgNPs were shown in Fig. 6a and b, respectively. The DLS analysis provided the hydrodynamic diameter of the synthesised nanoparticles. The β-CD@AgNPs exhibited an average hydrodynamic size of 189.5 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.33, indicating a relatively narrow size distribution. The size distribution curve demonstrated a predominantly monodisperse nanoparticle population with minimal aggregation, confirming good colloidal stability of the β-CD@AgNPs in aqueous suspension. The results suggest the presence of β-CD over the core of AgNPs as a stabilising agent, increasing particle dispersibility and limiting particle aggregation, thereby promoting the generation of homogeneous AgNPs. The zeta potential of β-CD@AgNPs was estimated to be − 26.8 mV, indicating a negative charge on the nanoparticle surface. The particles exhibit moderate electrostatic stability, with available repulsive forces that overcome attractive forces, thereby limiting nanoparticle agglomeration.

Antimicrobial activity of β-CD@AgNPs

The antimicrobial activity of green-synthesised β-CD@AgNPs prepared using P. harmala L. fruit extract against plant disease-associated microorganisms is presented in Tables 1 and 2. The agar well diffusion assay revealed a clear dose-dependent response across all tested organisms (Fig. 7). Green NPs interact with microbial cell membranes, increase membrane permeability, and disrupt essential metabolic pathways [24, 42]. Additionally, the presence of β-CD may enhance stability and bioavailability of the nanoparticles, allowing for sustained antimicrobial action [16, 31]. In Gram-negative bacteria, X. campestris, the causative agent of black rot in crucifers and bacterial leaf spot in beans, exhibited the highest inhibition zones, measuring 12.63 ± 0.85, 14.11 ± 0.24, 15.79 ± 0.31, and 18.05 ± 1.71 mm at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL, respectively. P. syringae, responsible for bacterial blight in tomato, bean, and pepper plants, showed moderate inhibition with zones of 13.96 ± 0.96, 16.73 ± 1.08, 18.19 ± 1.48, and 20.11 ± 0.51 mm across the same concentration range. In Gram-positive bacteria, B. subtilis, known for its dual role as both a soil inhabitant and an occasional crop pathogen affecting crops like wheat and vegetables, demonstrated considerable sensitivity, with inhibition zones of 12.32 ± 0.15, 14.45 ± 0.68, 17.14 ± 1.59, and 19.58 ± 2.54 mm. Antifungal activity against A. niger, a pathogen causing black mould rot in onion, peanut, and other crops, was also observed, though to a lesser extent, with inhibition zones of 8.06 ± 1.10, 9.52 ± 0.57, 11.98 ± 0.73, and 12.45 ± 0.35 mm at increasing concentrations. Similarly, Santos et al. [35] demonstrated that green-synthesised AgNPs exhibited antimicrobial activity against X. campestris. In another study, Jaffri et al. [11] synthesised spherical AgNPs using fruit extract of Prunus cerasifera, which displayed strong antifungal activity against plant pathogens including Aspergillus sp., Penicillium chrysogenum, Fusarium solani, and Lasiodiplodia theobromae. β-CD@AgNPs interact with bacterial cells, causing membrane damage and disruption of the electron transport system, which triggers the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The elevated ROS levels induce mitochondrial dysfunction, destabilize ribosomes, and impair key enzymes, disrupting cellular metabolism. Proteins and nucleic acids undergo oxidative damage, misfolding, and mutations, further compromising cell function (Fig. 8) [36, 41].

The MIC and MBC/MFC values further supported these findings. X. campestris and B. subtilis exhibited MIC values at 31.25 μg/mL, with corresponding MBC values at 62.5 μg/mL, reflecting strong bactericidal effects at relatively low concentrations. P. syringae required slightly higher concentrations, with an MIC of 62.5 μg/mL and MBC of 125 μg/mL. A. niger also showed MIC at 62.5 μg/mL and MFC at 125 μg/mL, suggesting that the nanoparticles were effective but required higher concentrations to suppress fungal growth compared to bacterial strains.

Effect of β-CD@AgNPs on larval mortality

The larval mortality of S. exigua in the leaf-dip bioassay after exposure to different concentrations of green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs using P. harmala L. fruit extract is presented in Fig. 9. Mortality was both concentration and time dependently elevated. At 24 h, lower concentrations (25–50 ppm) produced minimal mortality, whereas higher concentrations (200–400 ppm) induced substantial larval death. Mortality progressively increased with exposure duration, with the maximum effect observed at 72 h, particularly at 400 ppm, where nearly 91% mortality was recorded.

Mortality of S. exigua larvae in leaf-dip bioassay after treatment with β-CD@AgNPs: Heatmap representation of larval mortality (%) at different concentrations (25–400 ppm) and exposure periods (24, 48, and 72 h). Mortality increased in a dose- and time-dependent manner

The estimated median LD₅₀ values decreased significantly with time: 305 ppm at 24 h, 150 ppm at 48 h, and 64 ppm at 72 h. This progressive reduction in LD₅₀ highlights the cumulative toxic effect of β-CD@AgNPs, demonstrating that prolonged exposure enhances larvicidal activity even at comparatively lower doses. Similar time-dependent decreases in LD₅₀ have been reported for other nanoparticle-based larvicides, suggesting a sustained release and accumulation of toxic effects over time. The LT₅₀ values further supported the concentration-dependent toxicity. At 25 ppm, the LT₅₀ was 113.5 h, whereas at 400 ppm it decreased sharply to 25 h. This inverse relationship between concentration and LT₅₀ indicates that higher doses accelerate larval mortality due to rapid nanoparticle penetration and systemic toxicity. The enhanced mortality at higher concentrations may be attributed to increased surface interactions between β-CD@AgNPs and larval tissues, leading to disruption of physiological processes, including midgut epithelial damage, oxidative stress, and interference with enzymatic activity.

The larvicidal effects of AgNPs are believed to result from their ability to disrupt DNA or interfere with the insect's gastrointestinal system and enzymatic functions, ultimately causing larval death. Additionally, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may damage the chemosensory receptor cells in insects, further contributing to mortality [21]. The insecticidal efficacy of biosynthesised AgNPs is largely attributed to their distinct physicochemical and optical properties, particularly their high surface-to-volume ratio. This increased surface area enhances the delivery of toxic bioactive compounds derived from natural sources such as plants, fungi and bacteria, leading to physiological disruptions that support effective pest management [19]. These findings are consistent with earlier reports on silver nanoparticles, which attributed larvicidal activity to the generation of reactive oxygen species, disruption of cellular membranes, and interactions with vital biomolecules. Manimegalai et al. [20] demonstrated that AgNPs synthesised from Vernonia anthelmintica seeds exhibit strong larvicidal activity against Spodoptera litura and Helicoverpa armigera. Similarly, Sanjaya et al. [34] reported potent larvicidal effects of AgNPs synthesised via entomopathogenic microbes against S. exigua. The inclusion of β-cyclodextrin in the nanocomposite may further enhance stability and bioavailability, contributing to the observed potency [6].

Figure 10 shows S. exigua larvae actively feeding on untreated leaves, exhibiting normal green colouration and healthy growth. In contrast, larvae subjected to green-synthesised β-CD@AgNPs prepared from P. harmala L. fruit extract showed severe detrimental effects, becoming shrunken, brown, and ultimately dead, indicating the strong toxic and growth-inhibitory potential of the green nanoformulation. At initially stage (0 h), the leaves appeared fresh and intact. After 72 h in the presence of S. exigua, the control leaves were completely wilted and consumed, indicating severe feeding damage. In contrast, the β-CD@AgNPs-treated leaves after 72 h remained largely unconsumed, confirming the strong antifeedant and protective effect of the nanoparticles against larval herbivory.

Effect of β-CD@AgNPs on diet incorporation bioassay

Figure 11 shows the effects of green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs using P. harmala L. fruit extract on diet incorporation bioassay against larvae at different concentrations (25–400 ppm) and exposure times (24–72 h). A clear dose- and time-dependent increase in mortality was observed. At 24 h, mortality ranged from 7.56% at 25 ppm to 59.12% at 400 ppm, whereas the control showed negligible mortality. With increasing exposure period, mortality significantly increased across all concentrations. At 48 h, mortality reached 48.16% at 100 ppm, 54.41% at 200 ppm, and 71.49% at 400 ppm. By 72 h, the maximum mortality was recorded at 400 ppm (93.48%), followed by 200 ppm (69.83%) and 100 ppm (56.74%), while only 23.29% mortality was observed at the lowest concentration (25 ppm).

Values are expressed as mean ± SD of three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. *, **, and *** indicate p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively; ns = not significant

Notably, larvae exposed to higher concentrations (200–400 ppm) exhibited rapid, severe toxic effects, reflecting the strong insecticidal properties of β-CD@AgNPs. The gradual increase in mortality with time at sublethal concentrations suggests a cumulative effect of nanoparticle ingestion. These results demonstrate that diet incorporation of β-CD@AgNPs effectively impairs larval survival. The enhanced toxicity may be attributed to the synergistic action of AgNPs and β-CD, which could improve nanoparticle stability, bioavailability, and interaction with larval midgut tissues. Similar findings have been reported for nanoparticle-based formulations that exhibit potent larvicidal activity via mechanisms such as midgut disruption, oxidative stress induction, and enzyme inhibition [7].

Effect of β-CD@AgNPs on leaf-choice bioassay

Insects rely heavily on gustatory and olfactory cues to discriminate between suitable and unsuitable food sources, and nanoparticles may interfere with these signaling pathways, ultimately reducing feeding rates [4]. Silver nanoparticles, in particular, have attracted attention for their broad-spectrum insecticidal and deterrent properties. Their nanoscale size allows efficient interaction with insect cuticle and gut lining, often leading to oxidative stress, enzyme inhibition, and feeding suppression [7]. Figure 12 illustrates the feeding deterrence effect of green synthesised β-CD@AgNPs using P. harmala L. fruit extract against the test insect species in a leaf-choice bioassay. The leaf area consumed by larvae decreased progressively with increasing concentrations of β-CD@AgNPs, while the control group consistently showed higher feeding activity. At the lowest concentration (25 ppm), the feeding deterrence index (FDI) was 20.3%, indicating a mild reduction in leaf consumption compared to the untreated control. With increasing concentrations, the deterrent effect became more pronounced, reaching 35.5% at 50 ppm and 56.1% at 100 ppm. At 200 ppm, FDI increased to 79.4%, and at the highest concentration tested (400 ppm), FDI reached 89.0%, indicating strong feeding inhibition.

Feeding deterrence in leaf-choice bioassay: Heatmap showing the leaf area consumed (cm2) by S. exigua under control and different concentrations (25–400 ppm) of β-CD@AgNPs treatment. Lighter shades indicate reduced leaf consumption with increasing nanoparticle concentration, demonstrating a dose-dependent feeding deterrent effect

The results clearly demonstrate a dose-dependent feeding deterrent activity of β-CD@AgNPs. The enhanced deterrence at higher concentrations may be attributed to the nanoparticles' ability to interfere with gustatory receptors or induce antifeedant responses in larvae, thereby reducing their preference for treated leaves. Similar dose-dependent feeding inhibition has been reported for other nanoparticle-based formulations, highlighting their potential as effective biopesticides. The findings suggest that β-CD@AgNPs not only suppress feeding activity but could also contribute to long-term pest management by reducing crop damage.

The preliminary toxicity study (Supplementary data) revealed that β-CD@AgNPs exhibit a concentration-dependent effect on plant growth. At lower concentrations (up to 400 mg kg⁻1), the nanoparticles promoted seedling growth without affecting germination. However, higher concentrations (800 and 1600 mg kg⁻1) induced slight phytotoxicity, as evidenced by reduced germination and growth. These results suggest that β-CD@AgNPs can potentially serve as growth stimulants at moderate concentrations, but caution is warranted at higher doses.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study confirms that P. harmala L. fruit extract is an effective green reducing and stabilising agent for the synthesis of β-CD@AgNPs, producing stable, crystalline, and biologically active nanoparticles. The incorporation of β-CD improved nanoparticle dispersibility and bioavailability, which likely contributed to the observed enhancement in antimicrobial efficacy against plant pathogens and larvicidal activity against S. exigua. The dose- and time-dependent larval mortality, antifeedant behaviour, and reduced crop damage highlight the potential of β-CD@AgNPs as a multifunctional, eco-friendly nanobiopesticide.

These findings are based on controlled laboratory assays, and their translation to real agricultural settings remains to be validated. Future research should include large-scale field trials and evaluate long-term environmental safety, including nanoparticle persistence and soil interactions. Additionally, the study does not include AgNPs without β-CD, β-CD alone, or plant extract alone as controls, limiting validation of synergistic effects. Mechanistic studies are needed to understand the interactions of β-CD@AgNPs with microbial cells and insect physiological pathways. Optimising dosage and delivery strategies is crucial for practical, sustainable agricultural applications.
