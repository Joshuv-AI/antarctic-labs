Interpol review of forensic paint, tape, and glass evidence 2022-2025
Authors: Jose Almirall, Tatiana Trejos
Published: 2026

Introduction

This review paper covers advances in scientific methods applied to Paint, Tape, and Glass reported since the 20th Interpol Forensic Science Symposium in October 2022. The review includes peer-reviewed literature, published reports, books, and book chapters on the subjects. It also discusses the publication of standard practices, guides, and test methods (e.g., ASTM) as well as developments within the manufacturing industries, including production volumes, production locations, and current trends in the manufacture of these widely used materials.

The leading peer-reviewed journals reviewed for this paper were Forensic Science International, Forensic Chemistry, and the Journal of Forensic Sciences. In addition, more than forty-five (45) different analytical chemistry or other sources have published peer-reviewed communications on advances in forensic paint, tape, and glass examinations over the past three years.

Jeff Teitelbaum, Forensic Research Librarian at Florida International University's Global Forensic and Justice Center, contributed to the primary literature search using keywords for forensic paint, glass, and tape. In addition, literature from several search engines, including ScienceDirect, SciFinder, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, from the last three years was also considered. The literature review focuses on peer-reviewed articles and books published between January 2022 and June 2025.

This review highlights a continued interest in the trace evidence community in chemometrics and the statistical interpretation of evidence. Several studies have emphasized the use of statistics for pattern recognition, data reduction and classification, optimization and validation of analytical procedures, and assessing the evidential value of trace materials. The literature encompasses a range of methods, including regression analysis, machine learning, neural networks, and likelihood ratios, among others. The growing body of literature in this field may facilitate wider adoption in operational forensic laboratories. The authors note the recent publication of the interpretation of trace evidence materials by ASTM International, developed in collaboration with the Organization of Scientific Area Committees (OSAC), as referenced in this review [1].

Books, review articles, and consensus-based standards

Various book chapters and review papers discussing glass, paint, tape, and, more generally, trace materials evidence were published in the review period:

The van Asten's Chemical Analysis for Forensic Evidence is a comprehensive textbook in the field of forensic analytical chemistry, covering the entire process from the crime scene to the courtroom. This includes evidence sampling, laboratory analysis, quality assurance, and reporting/testifying. Chapter 2 covers these aspects for the forensic analysis of trace evidence, including paint and glass [2]. Bell's Chapter 14, “Forensic Chemistry and Trace Evidence Analysis,” in her third edition of the book Forensic Chemistry, offers an overview of the chemical analysis of trace evidence [3]. Chapter 3 in the Book Advances in Analytical Techniques for Forensic Investigation, “Vibrational Spectroscopy in Forensic Sample Analysis” by Singh et al., provides an overview of how vibrational spectroscopy, particularly Raman Spectroscopy and IR, has advanced in forensic science [4]. The chapter covers the fundamentals of each method and various applications in trace evidence, including paints.

The third edition of the Encyclopedia of Forensic Science features a series of four articles on the forensic analysis of paint, including an overview of paint chemistry [5] and the analytical scheme and interpretation of paint evidence [6]. The Paint Overview article provides a comprehensive summary of paints and coatings, highlighting their significance as physical evidence in forensic science [5]. It explains the basic composition of paint and how each component—resin (binder), pigments, additives, and solvents—contributes to its physical and chemical properties, which are essential for forensic analysis. It also describes the different components of paint, their roles in formulation, various types of paint, and application methods. The article Forensic Paint Analysis focuses on the comprehensive process of forensic paint analysis, from crime scene investigation to courtroom presentation [6]. It outlines the well-defined analytical scheme used in forensic laboratories, which includes evidence collection, pre-assessment, and laboratory examinations. The article emphasizes the importance of optical examination as the initial step, followed by advanced analytical techniques such as infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and elemental analysis. It also delves into evaluative reporting, detailing parameters such as occurrence, background, transfer, persistence, and recovery, and illustrates the evaluation of paint evidence within a logical Bayesian framework using casework scenarios.

The other two articles in this book describe two main types of paints: coatings on means of transportation (e.g., automobiles) and coatings on domestic objects (e.g., walls, tools). These articles cover a wide range of variations in pigments, extenders, solvents, binders, resins, and application methods tailored to the substrates and end-use of the products of interest. The Domestic Paint article discusses paints and coatings used in architectural, decorative, and spray paints [7]. The article highlights the versatility of domestic paints in terms of both aesthetic and performance qualities, as well as the variety of substrates to which they can be applied, including wood, masonry, drywall, concrete, and metal. It describes the primary components of paint and discusses paint production, chemical composition variability, and their implications for forensic scientists. The article also includes examples of forensic cases and provides relevant literature on the interpretation of domestic paint. Finally, the Automotive Paint article explains that, unlike architectural paint, automotive paint is applied in a controlled environment with strict specifications for film thickness and chemical composition [8]. The article clarifies the particularities of automotive chemistry and the multilayered structure, which give automotive paint its significant forensic value, especially in hit-and-run and vehicular accident investigations.

The same edition of this Encyclopedia dedicates five sections to glass: Overview, Analysis, Refractive Index Measurements, Trace Elemental Analysis, and Interpretation of Glass Evidence [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. These articles provide a general overview of the fundamentals, analytical protocols, and interpretation of glass, which represent helpful introductory material for forensic students or glass examiners in training. A review by Trejos et al. [14] focuses on the elemental analysis of glass. It discusses the current state of the use of state-of-the-art standard methods and the knowledge base on transfer, persistence, and background occurrence of glass for the interpretation of glass evidence.

Books dedicated to forensic analysis, tape, and paint were not published in this period. However, the paint section discusses books and reviews in the paint and coatings industry that can serve as a reference for current trends to forensic practitioners.

Paint evidence

Overview

This review highlights recent research on new interpretation methods for paint evidence, measurement variations within a single source and across different sources, and studies on sample preservation. FTIR remains a preferred method for analyzing paint, with recent advancements in sample preparation, microimaging, chemical mapping, and spectral data analysis. Other techniques discussed include Raman Spectroscopy, Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS), Direct Analysis in Real Time-Mass Spectrometry (DART-MS), hot-stage microscopy (HDM)-DART-MS, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), and elemental analysis methods such as SEM-EDS, Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS), and μXRF. Significant research covers a variety of paints, including automotive, architectural, tools, spray, nail polishes, and artwork. Several articles introduce new interpretation methods and demonstrate the value of paint evidence through case reports. Additionally, we include selected literature on paint chemistry and industry advancements to provide relevant context for forensic practitioners regarding current trends.

Analytical methods for paint examinations

Research on Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) in paint analysis explores the chemical composition of various coatings, including automotive, nail polish, architectural, and spray paints. Some research highlights the usefulness of chemical imaging for analyzing thin layers, complex samples, and assessing microheterogeneities. Advanced data preprocessing and chemometrics are also highlighted for FTIR alone and in combination with other methods and techniques.

Kwofie et al. introduce a novel method for the forensic analysis of original factory automotive (OEM) paint, combining transmission micro-FTIR imaging with machine learning to enhance accuracy [38]. The study focuses on analyzing Alternate Least Squares (ALS) reconstructions of infrared (IR) spectra from microtomed cross-sections of individual paint layers. Data preprocessing involves automatic baseline correction and removal of noisy spectral regions. ALS helps resolve the IR spectra of each paint layer and decomposes the data into concentration profiles and pure IR spectra of each component. Machine learning techniques, especially a prototype pattern recognition library search system, are used to identify the vehicle manufacturer and assembly plant. Search filters are developed from transformed IR spectra of the clear coat and two undercoat layers, using discrete wavelet transform (DWT) for preprocessing and genetic algorithms (GA) for feature selection. A hierarchical classification scheme is applied to determine the vehicle's make and model. The test dataset includes 16 OEM automotive paints collected by RCMP from 2000 to 2006, representing six manufacturers. The reference spectral library contains over 3000 spectra from multiple layers of 1275 OEM vehicles. In this study, a key methodological innovation is the adaptation and application of an existing algorithm to standardize IR transmission spectra from different instruments, such as high-pressure diamond cells and ambient-pressure microscopes, to correct for frequency shifts caused by residual IR beam polarization. This standardization improves data comparability and enhances analysis reliability.

Other methods to provide more objective spectral classification are discussed in the literature to overcome the limitations of traditional visual or manual interpretation, which are often time-consuming and prone to human error, especially when differentiating samples based on minor chemical components. For example, Vitale et al. propose a novel, fast, and automatic methodology for the hierarchical classification and similarity matching of FTIR mid-infrared (MIR) spectra of paint samples [39]. The methodology is based on the principles of Soft Independent Modeling of Class Analogy (SIMCA) and the Mahalanobis distance. It involves SIMCA-based classification of paint specimens according to their FTIR spectral signature and recognition of database samples most spectroscopically similar to a given specimen, performed by estimating a pairwise Mahalanobis distance. The methodology was tested on a dataset of 246 spray paint spectra collected from commercial products in Lille, France. These samples represented various manufacturers and colors. Of these, 105 samples were chemically characterized into three categories: orthophthalic alkyd-based, orthophthalic alkyd-, nitrocellulose-, and styrene-based, and polymethylmethacrylate-based paints.

In a study by Zhong et al., the authors present an improved spectral comparison method along with an ultramicrotome technique for analyzing small automotive paint chips in forensic investigations. The method enables the cross-sectioning of tiny paint chips (1 mm or less) without requiring epoxy or other embedding media, streamlining sample preparation. Traditional methods for preparing paint chips for IR spectroscopy are time-consuming and often produce mixed spectra due to the difficulty of isolating individual layers, especially from small samples [40]. The article also utilizes chemometrics for spectral comparison, employing the previously reported multivariate curve resolution ALS algorithm [38] and a modified version called MALS. The ALS technique is used on spectral data to extract a pure IR spectrum of each paint layer from the image line map. The study reports that ALS successfully identified matches for the clear coat, primer surfacer, and e-coat layers using an in-house Chrysler automotive paint spectral library. However, it faced challenges with thin peels or when the color coat was present, due to light scattering caused by metal and pearlescent flakes. The paper states that MALS was successfully used to recover spectra of all layers, even in cases where ALS failed.

The same research group reports on the use of micro ATR-FTIR on paint smears, which are challenging forensic samples due to the mixing of paint layers [41]. The researchers developed a procedure using an impact tester to simulate paint transfer during vehicle collisions, creating realistic paint smears. The in-house impactor with adjustable shock absorbers was used to produce 34 paint smears from 24 OEM automotive paint samples (General Motors, Chrysler, Toyota). The study showed that the IR spectra of layers extracted from simulated paint smears using ALS compare favorably with the in-house OEM paint infrared spectral library. In most cases, the correct vehicle make and model appeared within the top five hits. However, some discrepancies were noted, especially in the color coat layer due to scattered light from metallic and pearlescent flakes, and in some e-coat and surfacer-primer layers under more intense collision conditions, likely due to heat-induced chemical changes. The study concludes that paint smears created with this impactor can provide a realistic basis for proficiency testing in forensic laboratories.

Sharma et al. investigate the comparative examination and discrimination of white architectural paint samples from different brands for forensic purposes [42]. The study involved 102 samples from 34 distinct brands of white architectural paints, analyzed using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and multivariate analytical tools such as PCA and Partial Least Square-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). Visual analysis of FTIR spectral data alone resulted in 12 undifferentiated sample pairs, yielding a discrimination power of 97.86%. The integration of PCA and PLS-DA with visual analysis significantly improved discrimination power: PCA achieved 99.4% discrimination (3 undifferentiated sample pairs), while PLS-DA achieved 100% discrimination, completely differentiating samples of each brand. The study also assessed the impact of different substrates on paint chip examination by producing simulated samples on various materials, such as fabric, steel, iron, plastic, wood, tile, glass, wall, watch, gloves, and leather. The authors found no interference from the substrate when samples were applied in relatively thick deposits and dried before analysis.

Another similar study on white automotive paints is reported by Duarte et al. using FTIR and PLS-DA [43]. The researchers analyzed 143 white automotive paint samples collected from traffic accident scenes in Brazil, all OEM white paint from vehicles manufactured between 2011 and 2019. This study is considered pioneering as it presents infrared data and discrimination characteristics for vehicles from a region (Latin America) not previously reported in the literature using this specific methodology for automotive paint discrimination. Preprocessing of the spectral data included transformation from transmittance to pseudo-absorbance, standard normal variate (SNV) normalization, and mean centering to reduce noise and light scattering effects. The model demonstrated high accuracy. For the calibration set, sensitivity, specificity, and efficiency rates were 98.9%, 98.4%, and 98.6%, respectively. The validation group showed a 100% correct classification rate. For the external test set, the correct classification rate was 79.1%, which the authors suggest could be improved by including more samples from underrepresented classes. The study limited the age of the samples to a maximum of 10 years to prevent misclassifications caused by natural degradation and weathering.

The study of Wontor et al. evaluates the effectiveness of Fourier-transform infrared microspectroscopy (μ-FTIR) and optical photothermal infrared (O-PTIR) spectroscopy in analyzing automotive paint layers [44]. The researchers employ μ-FTIR and O-PTIR to chemically image cross-sections of paint chips without the need for manual separation. O-PTIR offers superior spatial resolution (submicron level), making it especially suitable for studying thin primer layers. As a result, O-PTIR is particularly helpful in modern vehicles that improve fuel efficiency by using thinner primer layers on plastic substrates. It also provides complementary Raman spectra, which are valuable for identifying inorganic pigments, such as titanium dioxide, that may not be clearly resolved in IR spectra alone.

Chemical imaging of paints by FTIR is also presented by the same research group from an academic perspective. The authors describe a laboratory experiment designed for undergraduate students that aims to introduce students to Fourier transform infrared (micro) spectroscopy (μ-FTIR) and its application in forensic analysis of trace evidence [45].

An application note by Thermo Fisher Scientific explores the complementary techniques of Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Raman Spectroscopy for analyzing automobile paint chips. It highlights the imaging capabilities of these methods, which are especially useful for chemical mapping of cross-sections through IR and Raman multivariate curve resolution [46].

Bolduc et al. systematically evaluate the homogeneity of automotive paint systems across entire car bodies, considering both chemical variability and degradation [47]. The study employed a comprehensive experimental design to assess paint homogeneity in OEM and aftermarket-repaired vehicles. Sixteen paint samples were collected from various locations on each of eight individual car bodies. Samples were taken from undamaged areas, including doors, fenders, hood, roof, and bumpers. Samples were embedded in paraffin, microtomed into 5 μm cross-sections, and analyzed by FTIR as well as Raman spectroscopy operated at 785 nm. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to identify differences between samples based on location, degradation, and refinishes across different paint layers. Paint degradation was quantified by calculating the Photo-Oxidation Index (POI) from FTIR-ATR spectra. The findings show that all vehicles exhibit variations across different areas, and that paint degradation can lead to noticeable differences in the NH, OH, COOH, and CH peaks. Unlike earlier studies that primarily focused on characterization, this paper thoroughly examines the chemical variability and degradation of automotive paint across an entire vehicle body, a subject not extensively covered in the existing literature. It emphasizes the importance of sampling as close as possible to the damaged area. The authors also emphasize the importance of considering spectral degradation changes when performing library searches or analyzing questioned items.

The series of papers by Suzuki et al. on perylene pigments in automotive paint details the in-situ identification and characterization of individual perylene pigments using FTIR within OEM finishes [48,49]. Most of these papers are discussed in the previous triannual report, but two new papers, XII and XIII of this series, are discussed here. The research helps forensic paint examiners by providing unique spectroscopic fingerprints and building a valuable forensic database. Each paper offers comprehensive infrared spectral data for specific perylene pigments (e.g., Perylene Red, Perylene Bordeaux), allowing their identification even in complex paint matrices or when mixed with other pigments. The research systematically identifies which perylene pigments were used, in what types of finishes (metallic vs. nonmetallic), and during what specific timeframes in North American automotive manufacturing. This information is crucial for narrowing down the potential source of an unknown paint sample. For example, Perylene Red (C.I. Pigment Red 178) was identified as being used exclusively in nonmetallic finishes and only during a limited period (1984-1992). By detailing the in-situ spectral characteristics and historical usage of these pigments, the papers significantly enhance the understanding of organic pigments in automotive paints, thereby improving forensic scientists' ability to identify and compare paint evidence.

Another method of interest for paints is Raman spectroscopy, which many researchers have evaluated due to its non-destructive nature. While conventional Raman spectroscopy can face challenges from weak signals and fluorescence interference, its enhanced version, Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS), greatly increases sensitivity. Raman spectroscopy is often used alongside other techniques like IR spectroscopy or LIBS to provide more comprehensive characterization, although studies also emphasize its high discrimination capabilities on its own.

One study by Lei and Massonnet [50] investigates how Raman spectroscopy combined with chemometrics can tell apart white automotive paint samples from the same manufacturer. The researchers examined 54 white Volkswagen automotive paint samples, measuring each layer to assess the ability of Raman spectroscopy to differentiate between the layers, both individually and collectively. PCA methods are used to classify samples and explore links between paint features and factors such as model, topcoat color code, production year, and assembly plant. Unlike earlier Raman studies, this research primarily focused on the binder and extenders rather than pigment differences due to the limited variety of white pigments. Using visual spectral comparison, the authors report up to 92.8% discrimination with all layer systems, while single-layer discrimination ranged from 49.5% (primer surfacer) to 88.0% (clearcoat). Raman can distinguish even samples sharing the same manufacturer, model, plant, and color.

Holman et al. provide a detailed and critical review of the current state and future prospects of Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) in forensic analysis, including a section on paint analysis of cultural heritage interest [51]. The review offers a critical overview of the challenges and future directions for SERS in forensic science, advocating for its standardization and integration into established forensic practices.

Merk et al. combines Raman spectroscopy and Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) to perform sequential measurements of both techniques at the same sampling points along the cross-sections of the paint layers [52]. The study investigates eight different automotive paint samples. By applying multivariate data analysis, specifically k-means clustering, to the combined data, the researchers can identify individual chemical profiles for each paint sample. This approach provides a detailed understanding of both the molecular compounds (from Raman) and the elemental composition (from LIBS) within each layer.

LIBS is presented by Lazic et al. in a study that introduces a compact and versatile LIBS sensor specifically designed for forensic analysis at crime scenes [53]. The sensor was developed to meet the requirements of law enforcement agencies involved in the project. The paper describes the sensor's architecture, functionalities, and optimization processes. It also highlights the sensor's potential applications in identifying various forensic traces, demonstrating its capability for both qualitative and quantitative analysis of substances like gunshot residue (GSR), soil, paint, and fibers. An advantage of LIBS is its ability to perform depth profiling and characterize multi-layer coatings.

Elemental analysis research is also conducted using SEM-EDS and μXRF techniques. Some advantages of SEM-EDS include its unique ability for high-resolution imaging and elemental analysis, and the method is non-destructive, allowing surface analysis of single layers and individual particles. In a study by Charles et al., the authors examine the use of SEM-EDS for analyzing a set of tool paints that are indistinguishable by conventional methods such as optical microscopy, IR, and Raman [54]. A novel aspect of this study is its investigation into using trace amounts of strontium (Sr) within barium sulfate (BaSO4), a common mineral filler in paints, as a marker for forensic analysis of tool paint via SEM-EDS elemental mapping. Statistical analysis of the strontium concentrations relative to barium (Sr/Ba ratios) demonstrates their ability to differentiate between tool paints. This ratio proves useful in distinguishing paints, even those from the same manufacturer or color group that are otherwise indistinguishable by other methods [54].

In a more traditional approach, the paper by Kaur et al. focuses on the elemental analysis of automotive paint chips, specifically those collected from Maruti Suzuki vehicles in the Kottayam region of Kerala, India [55]. The samples are examined using SEM-EDS to analyze the layers of paint from different and same-brand samples. The research highlights the presence of specific elements in various paint colors: Thulium is found in white and gray samples, while Aluminum is detected in red samples [55]. Unfortunately, the SEM-EDS spectra in the paper do not show clear signals of the Tm peaks, making it difficult for readers to verify the actual detection of the element. Additionally, the authors do not explain potential sources of this element in the paint formulations nor discuss possible analytical interferences that could lead to misinterpretation of the peaks.

A case study by Bhoir et al. demonstrates the use of X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) and UV-Vis spectroscopy for forensic analysis of paint traces, specifically to identify a suspect's vehicle in medico-legal cases like hit-and-run incidents [56]. In this case, the suspect's vehicle has blue paint, while the victim's vehicle has white paint, which shows traces of blue paint on the white surface. Both XRF and UV-Vis spectroscopy allowed for the identification of the suspect vehicle. The results show similar spectra and chemical compositions between the paint transferred onto the victim's vehicle and the known sample collected from the suspect's vehicle. The authors conclude that the evidence supports the involvement of the suspect's vehicle in the hit-and-run case.

A case study by Fang et al. highlights the important role of automotive paint flakes as forensic evidence and supports a multi-channel, scientific approach to their analysis and comparison, ensuring objective and fair results in legal cases [57]. The case compares a paint sample from the scene with two blue suspect vehicles using stereomicroscopy, IR, Raman Spectroscopy, and SEM-EDS. The study determined that one of the vehicles matched the paint trace from the scene, while the other was ruled out.

A hit-and-run case study by Chu et al. analyzes two four-layer automotive paint samples, comparing them layer by layer [58] using FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and SEM-EDS. The combined use of these three instruments proved highly effective. Even subtle differences, such as the detection of two weak peaks in one layer between the two samples by FTIR, were verified and confirmed by Raman microscopy and SEM-EDS. This indicates that using these instruments together yields more accurate results for multilayered coating analysis than using them separately, particularly when detecting inorganic additives.

A study by Wu et al. presents a new, fast, and non-destructive method for forensic identification of automotive paints using Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) combined with multivariate statistical techniques [59]. The OCT method can capture high-resolution 3D images of the internal structures of automotive paints. The researchers analyzed a dataset of 164 four-layer paint samples from seven manufacturers. The datasets were divided into training and testing sets. Multivariate statistical methods are employed to extract important features and classify the samples, including principal component analysis (PCA), multi-layer perceptron (MLP), k-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm, and Bayesian discriminant analysis (BDA), as well as combinations of these methods. The results demonstrate that combining PCA and BDA provides the best performance (89.7 to 100% depending on the dataset and preprocessing).

Jeong et al. describe the isotopic and elemental fingerprints of Cu, Zn, and Pb in antifouling paints and their potential use in environmental forensic investigations [60]. The researchers analyzed 16 different brands of commercial antifouling paints using microwave digestion and Multi-Collector Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (MC-ICP-MS). The elemental concentrations and isotopic ratios were used to characterize the fingerprints of various brands. The relationships between these parameters were then examined to assess their potential for source identification in environmental forensic investigations, especially regarding metal contamination in harbor sediments. The similarity in isotope ranges between the harbor and other anthropogenic sources suggests that antifouling paint is not the primary contributor, as they are also affected by urban runoff.

The performance of -MS is investigated in a study by Laetsch et al. and compared to micro-FTIR and SEMEDS [61]. This study analyzes 45 architectural mixed paint samples by combining five base color paints in various binary mixtures. The base colors used were white, black, blue, red, and yellow interior paint samples. DART-MS detects multiple organic compounds commonly found in architectural paints, such as tributyl citrate (TBC), polyethylene glycol (PEG), dioctyl maleate (DOM), and tert-butyldiethanolamine (TBDEA). The authors conclude that DART-MS is a feasible tool that offers complementary information to conventional methods.

The manuscript by Ashton et al. introduces HDM (hot-stage microscopy direct analysis in real-time mass spectrometry), a novel system that integrates a hot-stage, a digital microscope, and a DART-based ambient ionization source coupled to an ion trap mass spectrometer. This combined approach allows for the simultaneous detection of both physical and chemical properties of a material as it undergoes heating. HDM was applied to differentiate between two similarly colored red spray paints. The system revealed clear distinctions through color profiles and micrographs as the paint chips degraded under heating. Unique chemical profiles were also identified [62].

Special paint applications

Art and cultural heritage

Another interesting area of paint analysis is art and cultural heritage, where, although not always a forensic application, such analysis becomes necessary in cases of fraud and forgery. Therefore, some research in this field is included in this review because the methods may be applicable to specialized casework. The materials studied across these paint types cover a broad historical and functional range, including historical and modern artistic paints [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. For example, a) PVAc-based paints, specifically La Pajarita paints found in Joan Miró's studio and three of his artworks from the 1970s [66]; b) oil paintings used to study craquelure patterns, which indicate aging and can assist in authentication [63]; c) 19th-20th century paints from two vehicles at the Museum for Communications of Frankfurt [67]; d) historic paintings for authentication studies, using methods like dating and elemental analysis [64]; e) red pigments for identification in cultural heritage conservation and restoration of medieval wall paintings [68]; f) aged paintings, which often contain mixtures of pigments, making unmixing techniques necessary for identification [69]; and g) historical aircraft paints, such as paint binders used on two Messerschmitt Bf 109 planes from the 1920s to 1945 [70] and art authentication studies [71]. The methodologies used in these papers vary, encompassing various spectroscopic (FTIR, Raman, UV-Vis, XRF, SEM-EDS, and hyperspectral imaging, HSI), microscopic (optical microscopy, optical coherence tomography), and other analytical techniques (nuclear methods, Py-GCMS), focusing on both non-destructive and micro-destructive methods for sensitive cultural heritage objects.

Nail polish

Nail polishes are coatings that fall in the interface of cosmetics and paints. Forensic research in this area is gaining popularity as it recognizes the potential value of this type of evidence in cases where struggling or violent contact may lead to chipping of or breaking of nail polishes. The chemistry of nail polishes is vast, and although well published in the industry and environmental health and safety sectors, the literature in forensic studies is relatively limited.

In two papers by Kocak et al., the authors use concentrated multiple reflection ATR spectroscopy to analyze small nail polish residues [72,73]. The technique, combined with multivariate PCA statistical analysis, is employed to distinguish visually similar nail polish samples produced by different manufacturers (27 samples from 9 brands) [72]. In a follow-up study by the same group [73], the researchers analyze nail polish samples by ATR-FTIR and Raman spectroscopy and compare various machine learning methods, including Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM), Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and Logistic Regression for the classification of nail polishes. Random Forest provides the highest classification accuracy (99.95%). The study demonstrates the combination of analytical methods and the appropriate selection of machine learning as a viable approach for forensic and intelligence analysis of nail polish.

Khei et al. use ATR-FTIR spectroscopy combined with chemometric methods (PLS-DA) for forensic analysis and discrimination of 70 nail polish samples from 8 different brands [74]. The model's performance is evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and classification error rates. Nail polish traces are applied to six different substrates commonly found at crime scenes: cotton, jeans, white paper, polyester, tissue, and nylon. These samples are analyzed with the trained PLS-DA model using 13 blind samples, resulting in AUC (Area Under the Curve) values for the ROC curve of 1.0 for all samples except Sephora brand (0.9987), indicating high accuracy. Additionally, substrate background subtraction did not significantly alter the nail polish spectra.

Gupta et al. investigate the effect of washing hands with soaps, using sanitizer in the microscopic features and FTIR spectra of nail polishes [75]. The review by de Paula et al. offers a perspective on health safety and regulations of nail polishes that can serve the forensic scientist as a fundamental understanding of the evolution of nail product formulations and expected chemical shifts [76].

Interpretation of paint evidence

Like other types of trace evidence, paint can help reconstruct scenes and aid court proceedings by linking objects, people, and locations. It can answer questions about the transferred material, who might have been present, and when and how the transfer happened. Different perspectives come into play when interpreting paint evidence. Early in investigations, examiners can identify the type of paint—such as spray, automotive, architectural, marine, tool, or nail polish—which provides a crucial foundation for developing hypotheses. In hit-and-run cases where the suspect vehicle is unknown, physical and chemical analysis of paint can help estimate the make, model, and year of the vehicle, greatly aiding investigators in finding a suspect. This is often accomplished using well-maintained databases like the PDQ. When reference samples (known items) are available, paint analysis can determine if the questioned and known samples come from the same source. Additionally, since there is extensive knowledge about the occurrence, transfer, and persistence of paint, examiners can also assess the strength of the evidence at the activity level.

Defining the question of interest

The interpretation of forensic paint evidence involves a systematic and multi-faceted approach, evolving to ensure clear, objective, and standardized communication of findings. Several key approaches and methodologies are highlighted in the literature.

First, paint examinations enjoy consensus-based methods with a systematic analytical process and evaluation. The discrimination capabilities of the methods, both alone and in combination, are well-documented in scientific studies. When classification of paint is the primary purpose of the analysis, the interpretation of the data is based on the use of confirmatory methods that can identify the main physical features and chemical compositions to characterize the paint type. When databases are available, potential sources [5]. More recently, machine learning algorithms and quantitative methods are emerging to provide greater objectivity in classifications.

When the analysis is comparative, the known and questioned items are systematically compared using established analytical schemes. Forensic paint analysis begins with non-destructive methods. If no exclusionary differences are found, and sample size allows, destructive analytical techniques are employed. If differences are found at any stage, the questioned paint is eliminated as having a common source with the known paint. If the paints remain indistinguishable after comprehensive physical and chemical examination, they are deemed to share a common origin. A definitive conclusion of common origin (source identification) is only possible if a physical fit (fracture match) is made between the questioned and known samples. In the absence of a physical fit, the samples are associated with class characteristics, and the weight of such association can be expressed using various approaches.

Evaluative reporting and probabilistic frameworks

Historically, statements like “could have originated from” often understated the evidence's significance and were prone to misinterpretation by non-scientific audiences because they did not account for the rarity of characteristics in the relevant population. As a result, frameworks that can express the weight of the evidence have been proposed.

Bayesian reasoning

There's a growing consensus that Bayesian reasoning is advantageous for evaluating the probative value of evidence. This framework assesses evidence based on the similarity between the trace and control materials and the specificity (rarity) of their characteristics [6]. This review does not cover the fundamentals of Bayesian statistics, as its beyond the scope of this paper. Numerous books and scientific articles on this topic provide a solid scientific foundation for practitioners. However, to illustrate its use in paint interpretation, a case study is described below.

The investigation focuses on a potential cross-transfer of paint between the crime scene and a suspect's crowbar [77]. Evidence from the crime scene includes paint traces found at the forced entry point on the window frame. The suspect evidence consists of traces discovered on a crowbar seized from the suspect. The analysis involves two-way comparisons: green-gray paint traces from the crowbar are compared to the paint on the window frame, and black paint traces from the window frame are compared to the original paint on the crowbar. Both comparisons, using a combination of visual examination, optical microscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), Raman spectroscopy, and scanning electron microscopy coupled with X-ray microanalysis (SEM/EDS), reveal no analytical differences [77]. This suggests a possible cross-transfer.

The interpretation of the findings is based on a Bayesian framework using Likelihood Ratios (LR), comparing two competing propositions at the activity level:•Prosecution Hypothesis (Hp): The seized black crowbar was used to force the door open.•Defence Hypothesis (Hd): Another unknown tool was used to force the door open.

It also highlights the need for additional data from multiple analytical techniques to properly calculate likelihood ratios, as well as the importance of creating databases of both control samples and traces [77].

Reporting the significance of evidence using verbal scales

The Organization of Scientific Area Committees (OSAC) for Forensic Science, sponsored by NIST, developed a consensus-based guide for interpreting and reporting trace evidence. This guide utilizes a verbal scale to standardize interpretations and report content, addressing concepts like exclusionary differences, indistinguishable findings, discrimination, and rarity. The categories within this scale are based on the foundational validity of methods, discrimination capabilities of analytical protocols, and the specificity of observations. This document evolved through a consensus balloting to an ASTM standard “E3462−25 Standard Guide for the Interpretation and Reporting in Forensic Comparisons of Trace Materials” [1].

In cases of paint multiple transfers (i.e., transfer of door frame paint and wall paint to a crowbar) or cross-transfer (e.g., transfer of paint between two vehicles), forensic interpretation must consider the rarity of such events. Proper evaluation at either the source or activity level is necessary to determine whether analytical results support or oppose alternative hypotheses.

An interlaboratory study among forensic practitioners evaluates the effectiveness of this consensus-based guide for interpreting and reporting trace evidence using a verbal scale [78]. The participants in this study (n = 85) are forensic paint examiners, randomly presented with various paint comparison scenarios (15 scenarios per participant, totaling 1275 scenarios evaluated). The participants were asked to interpret the analytical data for each scenario, assign a conclusion using a verbal scale provided by the guide, and provide justifications for their conclusions. The data analysis involves several steps. First, exploratory and descriptive data analyses are used to visualize trends in the data and calculate the percentage of agreement among participants and with the SMEP's consensus responses. Agreement with the consensus is evaluated in three ways: exact match, within the SMEP consensus, and the closest category, or within the subject matter expert panel (SMEP) ranked best intended category. Second, the authors use formal statistical analysis with three generalized linear mixed-effects models. The statistical models revealed a strong positive relationship between the reported and expected conclusions, indicating alignment with the SMEP consensus. The difficulty level of the exercise and the participants' experience did not significantly impact the reported conclusions, although more experienced participants showed greater consensus for a given exercise. This study addresses the long-standing challenge of inconsistent interpretation and reporting of forensic evidence. By evaluating a standardized verbal scale, the paper contributes to efforts for improving consistency across laboratories and practitioners.

These approaches collectively aim to enhance the reliability, transparency, and clarity of forensic paint evidence interpretation for both scientific and legal audiences.

Innovations in paint chemistry and trends

The automotive and architectural coatings and paint industries, while serving different end-uses, have both seen significant innovation since 2022. These advancements focus on environmentally friendly, sustainable solutions, reducing production costs, enhancing durability, and expanding aesthetic and functional options [79]. Industry research emphasizes the enhancement of coating quality, which depends on the paint's chemistry, material composition, application methods, and film formation processes. Among various advanced coating technologies being developed are multifunctional coatings, self-cleaning and self-healing coatings, energy-generating coatings, nanotechnology in pigments and binders, and new paint atomization techniques [[79], [80], [81], [82], [83]]. In this dynamic and evolving market, forensic paint examiners must stay current and consider how these new coatings impact their forensic examination and interpretation protocols. Therefore, forensic research should closely align with relevant industry research and applications.

Good resources of information for forensic scientists include books, reviews, and scientific papers in the fields of engineering and industry. Among recent books describing innovations in the coatings industry are Nanotechnology in the Automotive Industry [80], Coatings Formulations [84], and Functional Coatings: Innovations and Challenges [82]. The book by Song et al. reviews the advancements in nanotechnology and nanomaterials that are revolutionizing the automotive sector [80]. With 36 extensive chapters, it describes the fabrication and application of various nanostructures, including nano-alloys, nanocomposites, nanodevices, nanocatalysts, and nano-coatings. The book discusses the development of thermal protection nano-coatings, self-healing nano-coatings, and self-cleaning nano-coatings for automotive applications. The book by Müller et al. explains physicochemical concepts and formulation of modern coatings, serving as a practical resource for industry formulators and offering a broad perspective for forensic practitioners [84]. The Arya et al. book provides an in-depth exploration of functional coatings that deliver tailored properties for specific industrial or application needs [82]. It includes 20 chapters covering a wide range of coatings, such as anticorrosion, antimicrobial, fire-retardant, self-healing, and nanopowder coatings. These books collectively highlight the cutting-edge innovations and ongoing challenges within the coatings industry, with a particular emphasis on the transformative role of advanced materials and enhanced functionalities.

Sustainability research continues to lead in both architectural and automotive paints, driven by tighter environmental regulations, safer worker policies, and growing consumer demand for eco-friendly products [79,83]. A strong shift toward low-VOC (volatile organic compounds) formulations is evident with increased use of waterborne and powder coatings [79,85]. Bio-based, renewable, and recycled materials are increasingly being incorporated, supporting the development of a circular economy. Sustainable polyurethane polymers from soil bean modified with silica, bio-based alkyds from castor oil and sucrose ester oil by polyesterification reactions, and alkyds and polyesters modified with L-lactide have been shown to reduce carbon footprints. Bio-based acrylates using corn-derived 1,3-propanediol, itaconic acid, and soda-derived glycerine greatly contribute to the renewable chemistry of coatings. Other renewable materials for UV-curable films of acrylate prepolymers include oils from palm, olives, peanuts, rapeseed, corn, and grape seed through one-step acrylation. For epoxy resins, sorbitol- or sugar-based epoxy resins have become popular as replacements for bisphenol A (BPA). Solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) are less harmful than aromatic hydrocarbons and alcohols while also enhancing corrosion resistance [85]. Moreover, coatings that reduce energy use—either through reflective properties or better insulation—are being developed to boost energy efficiency [83].

The integration of nanomaterials, such as nanoparticles and graphene, is enhancing properties across various applications, resulting in improved scratch and abrasion resistance, enhanced UV protection to prevent fading and degradation, and superior corrosion resistance [81]. Among key functional coatings, significant progress has been made with self-healing coatings. While more commonly used in automotive applications, self-healing capabilities are also being explored for architectural purposes to reduce maintenance and repair needs. Hydrophobic and photocatalytic coatings, such as TiO2-based ones, also called “self-cleaning” coatings, are designed to repel dirt and break down pollutants, keeping surfaces cleaner with less effort. Additionally, coatings that reflect or absorb heat are being developed to regulate temperatures, improving energy efficiency in vehicles and buildings. Nanopigments, often 1 to 100 nm in size, possess different optical properties and enable more intense and durable coloring that has transformed the color industry [83]. They offer better protection against heat, UV radiation, and chemical degradation, and lower VOC emissions [85]. Nanoparticles also make feasible the incorporation of functional groups like antiabrasive, conductive, magnetic, and self-cleaning layers. Eco-friendly and sustainable pigments are also gaining popularity in the industry, including biodegradable dyes derived from plants, minerals, and crop byproducts. Examples of natural pigments are blue indigo, red from beetroot, and yellow from turmeric. Examples of mineral pigments are titanium dioxide and iron oxide [83].

Innovation in automotive coatings (2022 - present)

The automotive industry has concentrated on expanding the limits of durability, design, and energy efficiency. Drop casting coatings, sol–gel coatings, dip coating, self-cleaning coatings, composite coatings, powder coatings, UV curing, electron beam (EB) curing, hot melt coating, atomic layer deposition (ALD), electrochemical plating, are some examples of advanced formulations [86,87]. For example, drop casting techniques enable uniform coatings, while sol–gel coatings enhance adhesion and corrosion resistance. Dip coating provides control over film thickness, and blade coating allows for precise application. Ceramic and graphene coatings are popular for their exceptional hardness, chemical resistance, hydrophobic qualities, and long-lasting protection against environmental damage and micro-scratches. Innovations include more user-friendly application techniques and improved UV blockers. Concerning energy efficiency, “cool paints” reduce heat absorption and reflect near-infrared rays, which lowers interior temperatures and enhances combustion efficiency, particularly in electric vehicles. From a cosmetic viewpoint, progress continues in creating special effects with metallic, matte, pearlescent, and even color-shifting pigments, along with advanced digital color-matching systems.

Innovation in architectural coatings (2022 - present)

Architectural coatings have seen advancements primarily focused on building performance, health, and urban sustainability [79]. Cool coatings with highly reflective acrylic and silicone-based formulas can reduce roof surface temperatures by up to 80 °F. Zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, terbium-doped, and Li3InB2O6, and Fe0.7Cr1.3O3 nanoparticles are examples of highly effective cool coatings. Other cool pigments include mineral blue pigments such as cobalt blue (CoAl2O4), ultramarine (Na7Al6Si6O24S3), Prussian blue (Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3), and azurite [Cu3(CO3)2(OH)2]. Still, due to their toxicity and instability at high temperatures, some can be replaced with rare-earth ions. Oxides of Ytrium, such as YInO3 and YMnO3, produce blue and green colors with high near-IR reflectance. Perylene bismide derivatives (PBI) are efficient black cool pigments with great functionality [88,89]. From a forensic perspective, understanding these contemporary pigments can assist in detection and interpretation through elemental analysis.

Antimicrobial and antibacterial paints have gained popularity due to increased health awareness, especially following the pandemic. Examples of antibacterial additives include nanosilver, zinc oxide pigments, and nanocomposite of silver nanoparticles (NPs) and reduced graphene oxide, Ag-RGO [[90], [91], [92], [93], [94]]. Self-cleaning facade coatings, which use acrylic latex combined with hydrophobic and photocatalytic properties, are increasingly applied to building exteriors, allowing rain to wash away dirt and pollutants or sunlight to break down organic matter—reducing maintenance costs and improving urban air quality. Examples of chemical compositions of self-cleaning coatings include micro-SiO2/nano-TiO2, epoxy-silane, nano-SiO2, methyl silicone with monofunctional siloxane units (M) and tetrafunctional siloxane units (Q) Me-MQ, Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and glycidoxypropyltriethoxysilane applied as sol-gels, as well as CeO2-TiO2, Si)2/TiO2, and CeO2 applied through the sputtering process [95,96].

The architectural industry is also seeing a rise in paints that utilize natural ingredients and bio-based binders, such as those derived from straw waste or cellulose, offering low emissions, sound absorption, and thermal insulation [85].

Interestingly, the literature highlights the use of AI and machine learning not only in forensic comparisons but also in the coating industry, where advanced application techniques and robotics improve transfer efficiency, reduce waste, and enhance the production and quality of multifunctional coatings [[97], [98], [99], [100], [101]].

Tape evidence

Overview

Over the past three years, various articles have discussed the forensic analysis of tapes. Recently, the focus has shifted toward strengthening the scientific foundations of physical fit examinations, including the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning, as well as the role that tape plays in the interaction of trace evidence, DNA, and fingerprint analysis. The literature also examines how storage conditions affect the physical and chemical properties of tape components and includes expanded studies on the discrimination and classification of tapes using chemometrics. Most research has concentrated on duct tape and electrical tape, reflecting the common occurrence of these materials at crime scenes. Consensus-based methods continue growing for tape and polymers, including new and revised ASTM standards.

Physical fit examinations of tapes

Many criminal activities, such as bombings, threats, kidnappings, sexual assaults, and homicides, often result in the fracturing of tape materials. The “match” or alignment between tape fragments found at a crime scene and those recovered from a person or object of interest is usually considered highly probative in court because fracture edges are believed to create unique, individualizing patterns. However, the forensic and legal communities have emphasized the need for studies to validate the scientific basis of this assumption and to address the subjective judgment of examiners, which lacks quantifiable uncertainty.

Consequently, several articles highlight significant progress and ongoing research in forensic physical fit examinations, especially for duct tapes, focusing on objective quantification, automation, and statistical evaluation to enhance traditional subjective methods. Key advancements and studies in this area include a) standardizing and quantifying fracture edge features, b) using automated imaging processing and machine learning to support examiners' opinions, and c) assessing the evidential value and practical considerations of physical fit examinations and interpretation.

Regarding the quantification and standardization of duct tape edge features, three research groups have contributed to this review period [102]. A research group at West Virginia University, in collaboration with Battelle and various forensic agencies, has developed methods to quantify the similarity of tape edges by systematically studying the effects of on error rates of different separation methods (e.g., hand-torn, scissor-cut) and tape grades (low, medium, and high quality). The group established a three-step methodology involving qualitative assessment, macroscopic evaluation of edge features, and bin-by-bin subunit assessment to estimate an Edge Similarity Score (ESS). This includes defining and documenting comparison features to promote consistent and transparent interpretation [102,103].

Various advantages are highlighted with the ESS method: 1) provide a systematic method to identify the relevant features of fractured tape edges and utilize qualitative descriptors and quantitative metrics to inform and substantiate the examiner opinion, 2) offer a practical mechanism to document the examiner's thought process, which adds transparency and minimize risks of bias, 3) provide a route to evaluate the scientific reliability of fit examinations and identify potential sources of error [102,103].

The studies created a large dataset of thousands of comparison pairs to support statistical power in assessing error rates, including intra-examiner variations, and interlaboratory studies [102,103]. Among the most significant findings is that not every physical fit determination holds the same probative value. There is a wide range of factors that can influence the quality of a fit and can also introduce bias; therefore, assessing the suitability of the items for physical fit examinations, conducting a thorough quantitative assessment of a fractured edge to substantiate a physical fit opinion, and understanding the factors that can influence error rate is critical. Finally, the use of a systematic method for examining and documenting fractured edge features, along with the application of a quantifiable metric, assisted practitioners in arriving at reproducible and demonstrable opinions [103].

For duct tapes, the accuracy of the multi-step approach, including ESS metrics for physical fit examinations, is generally high (over 98%) even for stretched items, except for higher-quality grade hand-torn tapes (approximately 84%). No false positives were observed in any of the sets (>3320 pairs examined), providing scientific support for the idea that false inclusions are extremely rare in duct tape physical fits [102]. Different variables affect the quality of a fit and edge similarity score in various ways. For non-fits, the impact of both the separation method and tape quality on ESS values is negligible, and the trend of ESS values remains low regardless. For true fit pairs, however, scissor-cut tapes tend to give higher scores compared to hand-torn pairs. Regarding tape quality, in true fit pairs, medium-quality tapes generally receive higher scores overall, followed by low-quality, then high-quality grades. Therefore, examiners should carefully consider the tape type and separation method during physical examinations.

A study by Lang et al. evaluates the feasibility of using computational algorithms to build physical fit databases and automate comparisons with deep neural networks [104]. The study introduces a package for preprocessing and databasing raw tape images (ForensicFit) and develops a convolutional neural network (CNN) model to support objective analysis of duct tape physical fits. The authors apply the CNN separately to the scrim and backing of scanned images, combine the results with logistic regression, and implement a pixel importance method to verify that the features used by the model align with those identified by analysts. The study shows that CNN is a practical approach to help practitioners identify the most critical features and support decision-making criteria documented by examiners.

Another paper by Prusinowski and Tavadze introduces a data analysis algorithm that utilizes mutual information and decision trees to aid forensic practitioners in interpreting physical fit evidence for duct tape and textiles [105]. The study provides a comprehensive approach by examining multiple features (e.g., backing texture, scrim weave, fiber alignment), measuring their importance with mutual information, and integrating them into a decision tree model as a decision aid. The developed algorithms achieved over 90% accuracy compared to human analysis, significantly boosting true positive rates for most duct tape subsets. While effective, the presence of false positives in some cases (e.g., high-quality scissor-cut duct tape and textiles) indicates that these algorithms should support, rather than replace, human analysis. The study also investigates the minimum sample length needed for accurate physical fit assessments and reports that edges preserve enough features for comparison if at least 35% of the original width remains [105].

Researchers at Hamline University develop an automated image processing and comparison method to measure tape end matches using cross-correlation scores (CCF, from 0 to 1) and an empirical assessment approach [106]. The algorithm uses scans of the backing side of the fractured edges as input, and random match probabilities (RMP) are calculated from correlation scores obtained through inter-comparisons to assess the strength of evidence or the relationship between edges. Similar to the Prusinowski study, the authors note that performance declines as the available width of the tape decreases, with acceptable results requiring at least 27% of the original edge. Although the Known Matching (KM) displays a bimodal distribution with most scores above 0.75, there is considerable overlap of the Known Non-Matching (KNM) across CCF scores, which impacts the overall accuracy of the method. The authors mention that image artifacts caused by loose fibers, the lack of information from the scrim layer, and color are some limitations that can be addressed in future improvements of the algorithm.

A team from the Netherlands Forensic Institute and academia (the University of Amsterdam and Utrecht University) proposes a novel method for evaluating duct tape ends, focusing on the “loop-breaking patterns” that appear when duct tape with chain-stitched warp yarns is torn [107]. The study utilizes a dataset of loop-breaking patterns derived from 136 torn duct tape pieces, resulting in 272 pairs, including 127 known matching pairs. The tape pieces were torn by three different people using various tearing directions and included three brands: Tesa, Pattex, and Supertape. In addition to the experimental dataset, the authors simulated 1000 complementary pairs of loop-breaking edges. These simulated pairs were generated randomly and independently of each other. The innovation of this method relies on the application of a Likelihood Ratio (LR) system, which evaluates the strength of these patterns using dynamic Bayesian networks. The study divides the data into training and testing sets and provides statistical tools for validation by assessing the accuracy, discrimination power, and calibration of the LRs. The log-likelihood ratio cost (Cllr), used as a measure of accuracy, consistently shows values indicating high accuracy for the LR system across various tests. Estimating LR for evidence interpretation in physical fits marks a breakthrough in this field, potentially vastly improving how this evidence is presented in court. Consistent with other research, the authors demonstrate reliable performance even with a smaller sample size, highlighting its usefulness when only partial evidence is available. The model remains effective across different levels of observation error probabilities, with lower error rates. The authors suggest that developing a larger dataset could aid future implementation in casework and propose various options to create such a dataset, aiming to make it as representative as possible of items from real scenarios, including shared information across forensic agencies.

In another study, Pinke Rodrigues et al. introduce a method for the forensic characterization of duct tape traces from a bombing scene, utilizing computer vision and machine learning techniques to identify patterns on the tapes [108]. The dataset includes images of four brands of silver duct tape available in the region where the crime occurred: Adere, Powertape, Tektape, and Tekbond. A total of 64 images are taken as references with a digital microscope at two angles (0 and 15°). Two approaches, deep learning (utilizing convolutional neural networks with YOLO algorithms) and statistical methods (Pearson's correlation, distance metrics, and Principal Component Analysis), are employed to recognize patterns on the backing and adhesive surfaces of the tape. The first approach uses object detection using deep learning, which involves dividing images into eight sub-images through data augmentation to create 512 images. The algorithm YOLOv8 is used for pattern recognition via a single-pass Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Three YOLOv8 architectures—yolov8n, yolov8s, and yolov8x—are tested, differing in their width and depth parameters, which affect both accuracy and processing time. Performance evaluation includes precision, recall, and mean average precision. Additionally, confusion matrices and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis are used to assess robustness and predictive capacity. The second method relies on pixel density statistical techniques, using image processing to extract numerical pixel intensity data. Pearson's correlation measures the degree of relationship between two variables, while Euclidean, Chebyshev, and Manhattan distances quantify dissimilarity between samples, normalized to a range of 0 to 1. Unsupervised PCA is then employed to identify grouping patterns by transforming original variables into linearly uncorrelated principal components. Thirty-eight authentic images of tape fragments from a homemade explosive device's detonation were used for testing these methods. The YOLOv8s deep learning architecture yields the best results, suggesting that 92.1% of tape fragments found at a crime scene originated from a common source, with a confidence level of 0.94 [108].

Overall, these studies have expanded foundational knowledge of tape physical examinations and demonstrate that machine learning and AI could play a role in assessing this type of evidence with more effective tools to support the forensic scientist's opinion.

Analytical methods for tape examinations

Pitts and Evans present an overview of tape examination in the third edition of the Encyclopedia of Forensic Sciences [109]. The article describes the common types of tape and the typical protocols of examination, including physical fits, microscopy, FTIR, elemental analysis, and Py-GCMS.

The elemental analysis of tape by LA-ICP-MS and μ-XRF expands the knowledge from previous studies. Two manuscripts by Bruin-Hoegée et al. present a quantitative elemental profiling of tapes using Laser Ablation-Inductively Coupled Plasma-Time-of-Flight-Mass Spectrometry (LA-ICP-TOF-MS). In the first paper, the authors develop standards for polyethylene (PE), polystyrene (PS), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) that contain 21 elements at three different concentrations [110]. The development of these new standards addresses the lack of polymer reference standards with spatial homogeneity suitable for micro-sampling elemental analysis techniques, which is critical for accurate quantification of trace elements in polymers. The standards offer acceptable homogeneity within a single standard and between batches (10% and 17% CV, respectively) and permit external calibration and internal standard with good linearity. In a follow-up study, the PVC and PE standards are used for the quantitative characterization of various polymers [111]. The dataset included over 1700 measurements from 82 tape sources, 36 tubes, 44 wires, and 13 jerrycans, which comprised various parts per source, four replicates per item, and inter-day variations. Elemental concentrations are reported as distinctive for specific colors and types of polymeric materials, aiding classification through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). For forensic comparisons, a score-based Bayesian likelihood ratio model and the t-test overlap method demonstrated superior performance in terms of misleading evidence rates compared to feature-based models and the 4-sigma criterion. The quantitative analysis of elemental attribution signatures in polymers significantly enhances the classification and forensic comparison of polymer-based evidence, opening up opportunities for creating databases.

Leatherland et al. examined the best way to integrate modern μ-XRF systems with silicon drift detectors (SDD) into the analysis of electrical tapes [112]. This directly addresses the research gap in applying these advanced tools to forensic tape analysis. The study demonstrates that the increased sensitivity and accuracy of SDD systems enhance μ-XRF's ability to analyze the elemental composition of electrical tape backings. This benefits tape analysis in two main ways. First, it enables better classification and grouping of tapes, which can aid investigators in identifying relevant leads when no known comparison sample is available. Second, it enhances discrimination power and reduces analysis time to under 2 min per sample. A comparison was made using 53 black electrical tapes of known origin across various methods, including microscopic examination, FTIR-ATR, μXRF, and Py-GCMS. The study found that μXRF outperformed the other methods, achieving similar discrimination and classification rates as those of all combined methods. Based on these results, the authors present a cost-benefit analysis and recommend moving elemental analysis earlier in the workflow to obtain faster and more effective results, thereby reducing costs and backlogs in trace evidence units.

FTIR-ATR continues to be a primary method investigated for the forensic comparison of duct tape and electrical tapes. Nimi et al. analyzed 25 brands of electrical tapes using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and chemometric methods, including PCA and PCA-LDA [113]. The study's primary novelty lies in its comprehensive application of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy supported by chemometrics for the forensic discrimination of electrical tape samples as well as the effect of substrates where these types of tapes are expected to be recovered, including cardboard, glass, plastic, and steel [113]. The study indicates that the substrates can affect the analytical results and discrimination capability of electrical tape samples, which is crucial for real-world forensic applications where tapes are often found adhered to different surfaces. The correct classification of adhesives was more influenced when presented with substrates than with the respective backing materials. This expands on existing literature by exploring a practical challenge in forensic tape analysis. Classification accuracy varied by the layer of interest, with adhesives providing superior performance (>89%) compared to backings (>80%).

The same group reported a similar study by FTIR-ATR on 15 brands of duct tapes. A key aspect in this study is the inclusion of the fabric reinforcement (both warp and fill yarns) in the ATR-FTIR analysis, alongside the adhesive and backing, to provide additional comparative criteria for more accurate source opinions on suspected tapes [114]. PCA-LDA achieved high accuracy in classifying samples with adhesives providing the best performance 96.7% to 93.3% accuracy for the training and validation sets, respectively. The backing provided accuracy ranging from 71.7 to 70.0% for the training and validation sets, while warp and fill yarns alone had lower accuracies from 64.3 to 69.6%. Combining the results from all three components (adhesive, backing, and fabric reinforcement) allowed for discrimination between all tested samples. The research also includes a preliminary investigation into the effect of different substrates such as cardboard, plastic, and skin on duct tape analysis, which can provide investigators with more informed result interpretations, particularly relevant for evidence found bound to victims. The study yielded varying results in terms of accuracy: adhesive (60%), backing (73.34%), warp yarn (60%), and fill yarn (66.67%), highlighting the need for further research to evaluate the impact of substrates in real-world cases where these tapes are encountered.

Tape evidence and workflows involving DNA and fingerprint evidence

Another area of interest in this triannual report has been the role that tape evidence plays, not only as a trace material but also as a substrate for other trace evidence, such as DNA and fingerprints.

Leatherland et al. examined the impact of fingerprint development chemicals on signatures obtained by FTIR-ATR on electrical tape backings and adhesives [112]. Only minor chemical differences were observed by FTIR for tape backings treated with Cyanocrylate (CA) and adhesives treated with Cyanocrylate followed by R.A.M. dye stain (CR), which were linked to the C=C stretching band of cyanoacrylate around 1600 cm−1. In contrast, White Wet Powder (WWP) had the greatest effect and interference on both the adhesive and the backing layer. Although only the adhesive was directly treated with WWP, the backing layer was found to carry residues likely transferred during the development process. The main interference from WWP on the FTIR spectra was attributed to titanium dioxide in the 600 cm−1 region. The study was also expanded to include micro-X-ray Fluorescence (μXRF), where interference from the elemental signatures of fingerprint chemicals was more noticeable. Elements in CA and CR include P, S, and K, with Na and Si also detected in CR. Nonetheless, these elements did not significantly influence the overall chemical profile of the tapes. WWP showed Al, Si, P, S, K, Ti, and Zr, with elevated Ti and Zr levels causing interference in the elemental analysis of the adhesives and, to a lesser extent, the backings. These interferences can result in false exclusions if analysts are unaware of the specific fingerprint development methods used prior to submission to the trace unit. Cleaning with hexane on the backing side was effective in removing fingerprint chemicals. For the adhesive layer, cleaning and microsampling with a blade were performed under a microscope; however, this approach proved ineffective against WWP contaminants. The study emphasizes the importance of effective communication and awareness between fingerprint and trace examiners to understand how their workflows can influence analytical outcomes.

Tapes are a primary source of latent prints, so research on tapes and latent developing chemicals goes hand in hand. Efforts continue to improve methods for detecting latent fingermarks on the adhesive side of tapes, a common challenge in forensic investigations [[115], [116], [117], [118], [119]]. A wet UCIO carbon-based powder suspension, first reported in 2022 and further tested over the past year, has proven to be a simple, affordable, and effective method for developing fingermarks on various adhesive tapes. It outperforms commercial products like WetWop and Adhesive-Side Powder Dark on eight different types of tapes [118]. However, Bouzin et al. addressed the limited availability of the surfactant solution used in WET UCIO outside Europe. They modified the UCIO formulation with a “frugal forensic” approach by replacing the original surfactant with an in-house sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution prepared in 5% aqueous ethanol. This new formulation, called Wet SPF, was found to be as effective as commercial powder suspensions like Wetwop™ on eight different pressure-sensitive adhesive tapes [119]. This change significantly improves the technique's accessibility in other regions and promotes sustainable development in forensic science. Schwarz et al. found that the pH level of powder suspensions greatly impacts the quality of developed fingermarks on the sticky side of adhesive tape. Optimal results were achieved at pH values between 3 and 4 [117]. As new developments are evaluated in latent print chemicals, the trace evidence community should catch up by assessing how these materials could potentially impact the chemical characterization of the tape itself.

Another concern from a forensic perspective is how different tapes can impact the efficiency of DNA and fingerprint recovery. Silvia et al. compare two different DNA extraction kits: the One Touch™ DNA Rescue Adhesives Kit and the EZ1 Investigator DNA Kit, to evaluate their efficiency in recovering DNA from latent fingerprints deposited on adhesive tapes after cyanoacrylate fuming [120]. This research is important for dual evidence processing (latent prints and DNA) from limited samples, aiming to optimize the recovery of evidence from tape substrates. The experimental design includes 100 donor latent prints (50 on duct tape, 50 on electrical tape), with half deposited on the adhesive side and half on the non-adhesive side. Additionally, 48 artificial latent prints were used as controls (24 on duct tape, 24 on electrical tape), with equal distribution on adhesive and non-adhesive sides. Each artificial print contained a known amount of 10 ng of synthetic DNA as an internal extraction control. The EZ1 method demonstrated higher sample recovery, extracting DNA from 99% of donor prints with an average STR allele recovery of 80%, while the One Touch method had a lower recovery rate (72%). Statistical significance is tested using Welch two-sample t-tests, although the influence of tape type (duct tape vs. electrical tape) is not explicitly evaluated in the manuscript. The study demonstrates the feasibility of recovering DNA after cyanoacrylate fingerprint development on both sides of the tape.

Hymus et al. evaluate the efficiency of DNA recovery based on the tackiness of the lifting tape. The study uses six commercially available adhesive tapes with varying levels of adhesiveness, focusing on their compatibility with downstream DNA extraction from buccal and shed skin cells, as well as the potential co-transfer of PCR inhibitors. [121]. The authors address the gap in characterizing different commercial and forensic-grade adhesive tapes for trace DNA recovery. Low-tack tape produced higher-quality STR profiles despite containing less DNA, as it avoided PCR inhibitors, showing that strong adhesion is not always advantageous.

A compelling, successive evidence study is presented through the sequential analysis of GSR and DNA recovered from clothing using stubs with commonly used carbon tape adhesives [122]. Unlike previous methods, this approach guarantees the entire area of interest can be sampled without needing to divide it. This technique was developed to be compatible with existing laboratory procedures for DNA stub sampling on porous substrates. The study reports that GSR analysis—including carbon-coating pre-treatment and electron beam exposure—performed before DNA testing did not negatively impact the DNA results, nor did it significantly affect the number of GSR particles recovered. A small adjustment to the GSR procedure is required: a thicker carbon layer must be deposited on the DNA stubs to prevent surface charging and damage to the tape during SEM/EDS analysis.

Data analysis and interpretation of tape evidence

Statistical analysis and chemometrics have been a focus in the literature as better alternatives to more objective spectral comparisons, such as spectral overlay. Methods such as PCA, LDA, probabilistic methods, and similarity metrics have demonstrated better discrimination performance than visual comparisons, quickly revealing the underlying structure of the data [110,111,113,114,123].

In the past, various studies have addressed spectral comparisons using similarity index approaches. A follow-up study by Prusinowskit et al. focuses on enhancing the objectivity and statistical rigor of comparing μXRF data in tapes, but has also been reported for other forensic applications, including glass [112,123,124]. The study proposes and evaluates “spectral contrast angle ratios (SCAR)" as a quantitative metric for comparing X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) spectra. This metric not only estimates the similarity between spectra but also uses a ratio of contrast angles that incorporates observed variability between different samples and variability within samples from the same source. This ratio facilitates the normalization of data across different instrumental setups, thereby improving inter-day and intra-day precision. This approach provides a more objective and transparent method for reporting findings and making probabilistic determinations based on spectral data. The study also introduces an automated method using a random forest algorithm to identify the underlying reasons for similarities or differences between compared spectra.

Glass evidence

Industry data, forensic laboratory management, government reports, and review papers

The annual sourcebook for the glass industry, published in May/June 2025 by the National Glass Association (NGA), contains a list with contact information to over 1200 companies in more than 700 product categories, including glass manufacturers, distributors, and suppliers; https://www.glassmagazine.com/sites/gm/files/2025-05/DE_GM_MayJune2025.pdf [125]. The NGA, which celebrates its 75th Anniversary in 2025, forecasts growth in the global glass market of 8% from 2024 levels (https://www.glass.org/news/2025/nga-glass-conference-carlsbad-tackles-challenges-and-opportunities-facing-glass-industry?utm_source=chatgpt.com). A downloadable global float plant database that includes dozens of different float glass manufacturers operating more than 230 plant locations with more than 450 float glass lines is available from NGA. A summary of the World of Glass 2024 Report [125] lists substantial investments in new production capacity or equipment since 2023 and rising automation of plant processes forecast to continue. The glass market size in the Asia-Pacific region is reported as surpassing the glass market in North America and Europe.

The Project FORESIGHT Annual Report for 2022-2023 (published in May 2024 by researchers at West Virginia University) [126] is a business-guided self-evaluation of forensic science laboratories around the globe, with more than 190 laboratories providing data. The report includes metrics to assist forensic science managers in evaluating work processes and laboratory productivity. The aggregate productivity for trace evidence sections, including all materials analyses (e.g., paint, tape, glass, and fiber evidence but not fire debris analysis), is reported for casework submissions from July 1, 2022, to June 30, 2023. Some important takeaways from the report include that trace evidence cases include more “items”, “samples”, and “tests” than almost every other evidence type submitted to the laboratory. The median number of trace evidence “tests” performed per 100,000 population served in a locality is reported as ∼9 “tests”, in comparison to ∼714 “tests” for DNA casework/100,000 population and ∼1400 “tests” for Controlled Substances/100,000 population. This median number of ∼9 trace evidence “tests”/100,000 population is a decrease from the 27 “tests”/100,000 population reported in the 2020-2021 FORESIGHT report. The median “cost” for the average trace evidence case is reported as ∼ $ 6000. in comparison to ∼ $ 1634/case for DNA casework and ∼ $ 400/case for Controlled Substances casework. The increased number of items of evidence (∼14 items/trace case) in comparison to other cases (∼5 items/DNA case and ∼3 items/drug case) and the complexity of trace evidence case results in a more expensive examination that is also less requested in a locality.

A recent National Institute of Justice (Department of Justice) report [127] published in October 2022 describes a research effort on the development of three (3) new matrix-matched analytical glass standards (CFGS 1-3) for elemental analysis [128], reports the expansion of an existing vehicle glass elemental analysis database now up to ∼900 samples for use in interpretation of glass evidence using likelihood ratios (LR) [129,130] and also reports the results of inter-laboratory exercises aimed at evaluating the performance of the implementation of LR calculations for the interpretation of glass evidence [131]. A recent Master's Thesis describes the sources of variability for the use of laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) and micro X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (m-XRF) for the elemental analysis of glass [132].

A new International Standardization Organization (ISO) forensic science standard (ISO 21043) was released in June 2025 and includes (as Part 4: Interpretation), standardized guidelines for interpretation processes using both expert judgment and statistical models [133]. This new ISO 21043 standard describes common types of opinions that could be applied to trace evidence examinations and comparisons, including the calculation of likelihood ratios and categorical opinions. Separately, the NIST-sponsored and USA-based Organization of Scientific Area Committees (OSAC) developed a Standard Guide for Interpretation and Reporting in Forensic Comparisons of Trace Materials [1] that provides a framework for the interpretation and reporting of trace evidence examinations and comparisons although this document focuses on the promulgation of a categorical verbal scale (with seven categories) for reporting results and “conclusions”.

A series of general articles was published within the Encyclopedia of Forensic Sciences, Third Edition, on the topics of forensic glass examinations, including an overview of the field [9] and a brief description of glass analysis [10]. Basic descriptions of refractive index measurements [11], trace elemental analysis [12], and interpretation of glass evidence [13] provide helpful introductory material for students and new glass examiners for training purposes. An excellent recent review of the literature [14] focused on the elemental analysis of glass for forensic purposes and also included a brief overview of the transfer, persistence, interpretation, and analytical schemes currently in use in the field and provided context for the use of elemental analysis within the glass examination scheme.

Case Reports, Transfer and persistence, and physical and optical measurements

A recent case report [134] describes the autopsy findings from a death investigation involving a 78-year-old man accidentally sustaining a stabbed-incised wound after falling against a glass door, leading to fatal bleeding from the subclavian artery. The report notes that the type of glass used in construction played a crucial role in the severity of injuries, emphasizing the need for safety standards and awareness in architectural design. A recent glass transfer and persistence study in Australia [135] aimed to determine the impact of clothing substrate dampness on glass retention. Three (3) test garments were used with a cotton t-shirt material found to increase in retentiveness with increasing dampness, while a wool/polyester blend jumper and a cotton hoodie were both found to increase only to a point, before decreasing again when the garment was saturated. This study also found that the size distribution of glass fragments changed with dampness. Another transfer and persistence study was conducted in the USA with an aim to determine the potential of finding glass fragments, “by chance”, in the general population [136]. The apparel of individuals assumed to be unconnected with crimes involving glass breaking was examined in four (4) different regions within the USA, including Houston, TX, Pittsburgh, PA, Huntsville, TX, and Morgantown, WV. A total of 1883 items of clothing and footwear were searched from 410 subjects using tape liftings for garment areas and scrapings from the soles of footwear. A total of eight glass fragments were recovered, five from the Houston set and one from each of the three other locations. No more than one glass fragment was recovered from any single item searched. The study findings highlight the improbable (although not impossible) nature of finding glass fragments on the garments of individuals from the general population. The recovered glass fragments were examined by refractive index (RI) and micro-X-Ray Fluorescence spectrometry (micro-XRF) for classification by end-use. The authors report the development of an R package (fitPS) to assist with the estimation of activity level likelihood ratio formulas based on this study.

A recent technical report in the USA [137] considered the potential for increased refractive index (RI) homogeneity in “modern” tempered (toughened) glass windows (manufactured in the last 2 decades). Within-source (between-fragments) and within-fragment variances were measured for 218 windows, and the RI variability was found to be similar (approximately 4.3e-5 and 4.7e-5) and somewhat lower than previously reported. The overall aim of the RI comparison study was to evaluate false exclusion and false inclusion rates based on the number of glass fragments measured for the comparisons. Another comparative analysis of glasses aimed to determine the impact of heat exposure (as in a fire) on glass microfragments on refractive index (RI) measurements [138]. Fragments from toughened (tempered) and non-toughened glass were heated in a furnace for various times (450 and 650 0C) and cooled down immediately to model different heat exposure conditions. A significant change in the RIs was found in all cases (n = 11 glasses), according to this study. When considering annealing, the measurements resulted in the same RI for glass fragments known to originate from the same source, while different source glass samples produced different RI values. The overall conclusion was that combining annealing with refractive index measurements “could be a possible way for the examination of heated fragments”.

Chemical measurements for glass

Both k0-based neutron activation analysis (k0-NAA) and Particle-induced X-ray emission (PIXE) were used to measure 29 elements (Al, Ba, Ca, Ce, Cl, Co, Cs, Cu, Eu, Fe, Hf, K, La, Mg, Mn, Na, Ni, Rb, S, Sc, Si, Sm, Sr, Tb, Th, Ti, Yb, Zn and Zr) of 48 automobile window glass for forensic comparison [139]. The elemental analysis results were evaluated using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and were able to be grouped by automobile brand when the k0-NAA dataset was used, according to this study. The study also reports grouping of “car model” using the rare earth element (REEs) concentrations in some cases and “the macro elements” in other cases. Another “comprehensive” evaluation considered Energy Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (ED-XRF) spectrometry and Direct Current Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (DC Arc AES) for the elemental characterization of automobile glasses for forensic comparisons [140]. A total of seventeen elements including five major elements (Si, Na, Ca, Mg and Fe) and twelve minor and trace elements (Al, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Ni, Cu, Zn, Rb, Mo, Zr and Sr) were “found to be present” and measured in five (5) different vehicle windshields. This study reports better performance with the use of DC Arc AES for minor and trace level determination, with a reported precision of 15–20% RSD for the measurement. However, EDXRF was reported as performing better for major elements with 5–10% RSD measurement precision. The study also reports grouping of the glass samples (n = 5) by cluster analysis. Another study by the same research group was based on instrumental neutron activation analysis (INAA) using high flux neutrons from a research reactor and was used for the chemical characterization of car windshield glass samples [141]. Energy dispersive X-ray fluorescence (ED-XRF) and direct current arc carrier distillation atomic emission spectroscopy (DC Arc AES) methods were also used for the same glass samples and compared to the analytical capabilities of INAA. Concentrations of seventeen elements (Na, Ca, Sc, Cr, Fe, Co, Zn, Rb, Zr, Ba, La, Hf, Ce, Eu, Yb, Sm, and Th) were determined in all analyzed glass samples by INAA at major, minor, and trace concentration levels and grouping of automobile glasses (n = 6) was carried out utilizing concentrations of transition elements and rare earth elements (REEs) in conjunction with statistical cluster analysis.

A recent study describes a systematic assessment of the performance rates when analyzing small and irregular glass fragments using micro-X-ray Fluorescence Spectrometry (μ-XRF) and Laser Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) for the forensic comparison of glass [142]. Glass fragments (n = 100) were collected from the inner and outer panes of a vehicle windshield to assess the false exclusion rates and glass fragments (n = 100) originating from different vehicle windshields. They were used to evaluate the discrimination capabilities. Fragment size effects were assessed, and the study found that measurement precision deteriorates for small/irregular fragments. Therefore, comparison items should have a similar size, shape, and thickness to minimize error rates. The study concludes that comparisons between full-thickness and small/irregular fragments should be avoided, regardless of the analytical methods used. While this phenomenon was no surprise for μ-XRF measurements, the effect was not previously reported for LIBS measurements and comparisons. This study additionally provides updated sampling and comparison recommendations when using “modern” silicon drift detectors (SDD) and reduced fragment sizes for comparisons. False exclusion rates of <12% for μ-XRF and <4% for LIBS were reported when using either a 3 s or 4 s (3% RSD) criterion. The study findings suggest that at least 4 known fragments are recommended for full thickness fragments and 6 to 9 known fragments for small/irregular-shaped glass comparisons. In another study [143], glass samples from 30 different portable electronic device (PEDs) screens, 15 screen protectors (SP), and 3 brands of liquid glass (LG) were analyzed using a micro-XRF instrument equipped with two silicon drift detectors (SDD). The elemental homogeneity within a single glass source was also evaluated. High sodium and potassium concentrations and the absence of calcium were expected for these glasses and were found during the study. Spectral overlay classified the PED and SP samples into major groups based on their distinctive elemental profiles (5 PED groups, 4 SP groups), and further classification of within-group samples was possible when considering reproducible differences in signal intensities (discrimination 98.4% PED, 98.1% SP). Additionally, a 3 s (3% Relative Standard Deviation, RSD) comparison criterion produced the lowest false exclusion rates among same-source fragments (3.3% PED, 0.8% SP) while maintaining a high discrimination power among different-source fragments (98.4% PED, 100% SP). This study provided a “preliminary” understanding of the elemental composition of modern PED glasses and their accessories and the discrimination capability of micro-XRF for forensic comparisons of these types of glasses. A new interlaboratory study aimed to evaluate the performance of “modern” μXRF systems equipped with silicon drift detectors (SDDs) for the forensic comparison of glass was reported [144]. While the SDD-μXRF instruments resulted in improved precision and detection limits over traditional lithium-doped silicon (SiLi) detectors and excellent discrimination (>99%) of different-source samples (n = 25), the false exclusion rates for same-source samples (n = 20) were found to be relatively high (>20%). The study reports strategies to reduce the high false exclusion rates by increasing the number of fragments collected for the known source and modifying the recommended comparison criteria. A false exclusion rate of 5% or less resulted when a minimum of five known fragments were measured for comparison, and modifying the previously recommended comparison criterion reduced the false exclusion rate from 23% to 2% for same-source samples, while maintaining low false inclusions (<1%) for different-source samples. Improved sensitivity and precision were observed in glass measurements acquired with SDD μXRF systems; however, the study found that these systems require adjustments to sampling and the comparison criteria to minimize potential error rates in the forensic comparison of glass fragments. A different study incorporating SDDs for the forensic analysis and comparison of glass samples considered both full-thickness fragments and the more typical “thin” fragments (10-50 μm) found in forensic glass casework [124]. The authors propose a modified 3s comparison criterion that results in a false exclusion rate of less than 2.5% and a false inclusion rate of less than 1.5% for the comparison of full-thickness fragments. However, the same match criteria yield false exclusion and false inclusion rates of less than 12% and 7.5%, respectively, for the comparison of the more case-typical thin fragments. The authors conclude that the findings show that while full-thickness fragments produce more precise data, μXRF-SDD is still “suitable” for fragments as thin as 10 μm.

A different study used Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) in transmission (KBr method), attenuated total reflection (ATR) FT-IR, and micro-Raman, and several other characterization methods to evaluate the commercial production of E-glass fibers [145]. The study focused on the physical structure characteristics in the fibers formed and, relevant to forensic examinations, glass homogeneity at the nanoscale. The study found that the use of FT-IR and micro-Raman techniques enables the molecular-level characterization of the fiber skin in comparison to the core and reveals a relationship between E-glass fictive temperature and the Raman band shift in order to predict the fictive temperature range of commercial fiberglass products. The authors proposed the use of a new “homogeneity index (GHI)” that was shown to be closely related to furnace operating temperature. Another study aims to evaluate the capability of μ-XRF and LIBS analyses to characterize the homogeneity (elemental variation) of glass compared to the LA-ICP-MS quantitative analysis method [146]. The variability of the elemental composition within 100 fragments from two different panes of the same windshield was characterized using chemical maps, and semi-quantitative data were used to compare fragments within and between the different panes using the three methods. When comparing pieces within the same pane, the variability for most elements was less than 10% RSD for both μ-XRF and LIBS and less than 5% RSD for LA-ICP-MS. Comparison methods simulating casework situations in which one questioned fragment is compared to more than one known fragment resulted in better performance as the number of fragments of the known sample increased (to up to 4 fragments or 12–20 measurements). The study found that error rates below 3% were obtained for μ-XRF and LIBS when selecting the appropriate number of fragments, measurements, and comparison criteria. Barium titanate glass microspheres were analyzed due to their potential as evidence using SEM-EDX and LA-ICP-MS methods, resulting in good discrimination found by a recent study [147]. Different source glass microspheres (n = 11) were reported to contain variable amounts of elements/isotopes that can be analyzed by SEM-EDX (O, Na, Mg, Al, Si, K, Ca, Ti, Mn, Fe, Zn, Sr, Zr, Ba) and LA-ICP-MS (7Li, 11B, 23Na, 24Mg, 27Al, 29Si, 39K, 42Ca, 49Ti, 51V, 52Cr, 55Mn, 57Fe, 66Zn, 75As, 85Rb, 88Sr, 90Zr, 111Cd, 118Sn, 121Sb, 137Ba, 139La, 140Ce, 146Nd, 178Hf, 208Pb). A preliminary grouping is based on Ti and Ba ratios, and high variability of other elements leads to individual chemical characterization of all except two of the 11 glass microsphere products in the study. A different study involving plastic materials (polyethylene (PE), polystyrene (PS), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC)) reported the use of laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) for the quantitative analysis of trace elements in polymers [110] and introduced a new materials standard for quantitative elemental profiling of polymers. New polyethylene (PE), polystyrene (PS), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) standards containing 23 elements at three different concentrations are reported. The LA-ICP-TOF-MS measurements showed excellent linear responses for all elements and polymer matrices and good homogeneity. The study reports that the between-run variation was substantially reduced from 42% in the absence of calibration to 30% when correcting with an elemental internal standard and to 24% when applying the novel standards. The study also reports good discrimination power between different types of polymers (4 different classes).

A recent interlaboratory study supported by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) focused on the analysis and classification of forensic glass specimens using Particle Induced X-ray Emission (PIXE) [148] by three (3) different laboratories. Vehicle window glass fragments (n = 48) collected from different car models and manufacturers provided by the Israeli police force were subjected to PIXE measurements to determine their elemental compositions. Major and trace elements were measured and used as input to machine learning (ML) algorithms in order to develop classification models to determine the origin of the glass samples. The study reports an > 80% “accuracy” on external test sets. A newly created combined “model” using the data from the three laboratories was demonstrated to perform well, paving the way towards establishing an international database that is composed of measurements from various PIXE labs. Another study involved the use of laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) data in combination with refractive index (RI) data for the analysis of sub-mm-sized (microfragment) glass samples [149]. A set of glass samples (n = 127) collected from four (4) different glass types (fused silica, flint, borosilicate, and soda-lime) was used in the study. Multivariate chemometric data evaluation methods included linear discrimination analysis, quadratic discrimination analysis, classification tree, and random forest for LIBS data, and were evaluated with and without data compression using principal component analysis. The study found that the classification tree and random forest methods produced the most consistent and accurate results, with correct classifications/identifications in 92 to 99% of the cases for soda-lime glasses.

A new study using resolved laser pulses and an inductively coupled plasma time of flight mass spectrometer (ICP-TOF-MS) was evaluated to analyze glass fragments pulse by pulse to increase chemical information from a single pulse, requiring 25× less ablated material [150]. Resolved signals of individual laser pulses were achieved by using low-dispersion ablation cells, a parallel flow ablation cell (PFAC) designed for fast washouts using small spot sizes, and a modified version (MPFAC) specifically made for using larger spot sizes. Smaller and more reproducible relative standard deviations were observed due to the improved aerosol transport using the MPFAC. The PFAC cell achieved a success rate of up to 95%, while the MPFAC achieved 98%, which are comparable to the established LA-ICP-MS methods. The authors report the potential value of single-pulse LA-ICP-TOFMS for the analysis of very small samples found in forensic applications. Another study used suspension sampling and total reflection X-ray fluorescence (TXRF) to measure the elements (Na, Mg, Al, K, Ca, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Ni, Cu, Zn, Rb, Sr, Ba, and Pb) in glass samples [151]. The authors found the limits of detection (LODs) and limits of quantitation (LOQs) to be “adequate” for the determination of these elements in glass and report relative standard deviation (RSD%) between 3.6 and 10.3% without the need for external calibration. The developed method was applied to the analysis of 31 glasses, and the authors report using principal component analysis (PCA) to discriminate all the glasses from smartphones and that the soda-lime glass could be “reasonably distinguished” from smartphone screens.

The results of an interlaboratory study by ten different laboratories using laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) and the standard test method (ASTM E2927-23) for the analysis and comparison of glass evidence were reported [128]. This work aimed to evaluate the performance of the new CFGS2 calibration standard for the quantitative analysis of simulated casework samples, evaluate the comparison criterion as recommended by the ASTM E2927 method (association or non-association), and the use of a likelihood ratio (LR) as a quantitative determination of the strength of evidence found. Each laboratory calculated an LR to report the significance of glass source comparisons for a set of glass samples of known origin. Two different types of background databases were used for the calculation of the LR to evaluate the effect of the size and composition of the databases on the calculation of the LR. As expected, glass that originated from the same windowpane was found to be indistinguishable using the ASTM E2927 match criteria and result in a high LR value (strong support for an association) and glass that originated from different vehicles are distinguished (strong support for an exclusion). Glass samples that originated from different vehicles but that were the same make, model and year (or comparisons between the inner and outer pane of the same windshield) were chemically similar and reflected in a low LR. Good agreement among the laboratories was reported with <5% relative standard deviations (RSDs) among participants, with few exceptions.

Data analysis and interpretation of glass evidence

A recent study used phosphorescent sand as a model system to study the behavior of glass particles in support of activity level interpretation of glass evidence [152]. The authors report comparable properties between the phosphorescent particles and small glass fragments. Particle imaging was achieved after ultraviolet (UV) light charging of the phosphorescent particles, and the number of particles transferred was counted on the acquired images after a number of different types of activities. As expected, “more intense” contacts led to more transfer (9-16% of the present phosphorescent sand particles transferred) while “less intense” contacts led to less transfer (up to 4% of the particles transferred) or no transfer at all. Indirect (secondary) contact by subsequent sitting on a chair led to a transfer of <1% of particles and indicated the presence of relatively large numbers of particles remaining on the bottom of the chair. A persistence experiment showed a residual of 9-55% of present particles after 1 h of regular office activity.

Another study reports the use of a “multivariate generalization” of the 4σ comparison criteria for elemental analysis by laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) in forensic glass comparisons [153]. This study introduces a multivariate extension to the 4σ comparison criteria, making use of the correlation structure between the elements. The authors claim to demonstrate that this method results in an improvement in the false positive rate, but do report a corresponding increase in the false negative rate. Another study investigated how measurements performed at different laboratories on the same set of glass fragments could be combined in the context of machine learning (ML) to facilitate the interpretation of glass evidence [154]. The authors claim to have demonstrated that different elemental analysis methods (particle-induced X-ray emission (PIXE), laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (SEM-EDS), particle-induced Gamma-ray emission (PIGE), instrumental neutron activation analysis (INAA), and prompt Gamma-ray neutron activation analysis (PGAA) could each produce lab-specific ML-based classification models. The authors also developed rules for the successful combinations of data from different laboratories and techniques and demonstrated that when followed, they give rise to improved models, and conversely, poor combinations will lead to poor-performing models. The study conclusions include that the combination of PIXE and LA-ICP-MS improves the performance of the ML models by approximately 10-15%, while combining PGAA with other techniques provides poor performance, including any use of the SEM-EDS technique, according to this study. The authors recommend replacement of SEM-EDS measurements with measurements of Fe and Ca by the PIXE technique. The authors claim that the development of the ML model can facilitate international collaborations between law enforcement agencies. Another study evaluated the use of the Particle Induced Gamma-ray Emission (PIGE) and Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis (INAA) techniques for the “complete chemical characterization” of twenty-five (25) windshield glass samples collected from six (6) different car manufacturers [155]. The elemental composition of four major elements (Si, Na, Mg, and Al) by PIGE using proton beam and nineteen (19) elements, including sixteen trace elements, by INAA was determined. Trace elemental concentrations, including rare earth elements (REEs), and a ternary plot using concentrations of major, transition elements, and REEs were utilized to group the analyzed glass samples. Different statistical analysis tools, including K-means, Cluster Analysis, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA), were used for the groupings, and the authors conclude that PCA resulted in the correct grouping of the glasses within the six vehicle manufacturers.

A different study used an open-source software (SAILR) designed to calculate forensic likelihood ratios (LR) from probability distributions of reference data from glass examinations [156]. The main purpose of this study was to validate a multivariate feature-based LR calculation method for SAILR using SEM-EDX elemental composition data for aluminum (Al), calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), sulfur (S), and silicon (Si) measured from glass fragments (n = 380). The results showed that the available data supported LR methods using three elemental features or fewer. The best performance was obtained using calcium, magnesium, and silicon. The LR output values of the system were confined to an upper boundary of LR = 158 and a lower boundary of LR = 1/512 using the empirical lower and upper boundaries (ELUB) LR method. The authors report that the limited range was a consequence of notable within-source variation, although it was not clear if this was a limitation of instrumental analysis by SEM-EDX or actual elemental within-source variation as measured by this technique.

Another study introduces the concept of an operational reference glass database, specifically designed for the interpretation of glass evidence derived from the collection and examination of broken glass fragments from ATM attacks, jewelry store robberies, and ramraids on high-end clothing stores [157]. The database was initially developed in 2014 and used to compare glass fragments collected from suspects in criminal activities. The database is comprised of elemental data from laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), and the recent study aimed to establish the methodology for collecting reference glass samples from the scenes of the crimes and events. The study reports a database containing over 3500 reference glass samples from crime scenes, which can be used to compare trace elemental profiles of glass fragments from suspected items to known reference samples. The study reports finding matches of trace materials in over 50% of case requests since 2019. The paper emphasizes the utility of chemical profiling and the use of databases in this specialized application, providing an important tool for linking glass traces to specific criminal contexts and providing forensic intelligence and investigative leads into individuals involved in ATM-related crimes.

Another recent study is reported as a continuation of work completed 25 years ago by Coulson et al. to determine the amount of non-matching glass present on the clothing of people who are suspected of breaking crimes in New Zealand as well as the presence of glass on random people using refractive index (RI) data as an input for the model. The current study aims to first derive an estimate of the uncertainty in the parameter of the previous Coulson et al. model and show how this may be used to estimate how the probabilities may vary or how to compare estimates resulting from different transfer and persistence surveys [158]. The recent work continues to use RI data as the input and extends the model by modelling the “excess” zeros (in the case of the P terms) and excess ones (in the case of the S terms). The methodology used to make these extensions relies on purely frequentist theory of estimation in keeping with the original work. The authors also report the development and application of an R package (fitPS) to facilitate the calculation. Another recent study uses quantitative analysis data from laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICPMS) collected using a standard method (ASTM E2927-23) as an input to calculate a likelihood ratio within an interlaboratory test by different forensic laboratories around the world [128]. This study introduces a new, freely available R-based Shiny graphical user interface (GUI) [129,159] to calculate calibrated likelihood ratios (LRs) using three (3) different background databases of glass composition. The authors report the creation of a new vehicle survey glass database generated at Florida International University (FIU) from LA-ICP-MS analysis, a database comprised of a combination of casework and survey samples collected from solution-digestion ICP-MS analysis from the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) laboratory, and a previously reported casework sample database collected from LA-ICP-MS analysis at the Bundeskriminalamt (BKA) laboratory. The LRs are calculated using a previously reported two-level multivariate kernel (MVK) model and calibrated using a previously described Pool Adjacent Violators (PAV) algorithm. The log LR (LLR) was calculated and compared to the match criterion recommended in the ASTM E2927-23 method, using these three background databases, using a typical glass evidence case scenario. The authors report how the LLR values increase as the size of the background database increases, as expected. Both the R Shiny app [159] and the new FIU vehicle background database are provided to researchers in the supplementary materials of the publication. Another recent work reported the use of multi-elemental features derived from Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma with Mass Spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) data to compute a likelihood ratio for the comparison of glass evidence [130]. The reported calculation reasonably assumes that the within-source variability of the elemental composition is normally distributed within a source. However, the between-source variability is generally assumed to follow a much more complex distribution, typically described with a kernel density function. In this work, instead of modeling distributions with complex densities, the authors proposed the use of simpler models and the introduction of a data pre-processing step consisting of the “Gaussianization” of the glass features. Different normalization techniques of the LA-ICP-MS glass features, namely marginal Gaussianization based on histogram matching, marginal Gaussianization based on Yeo-Johnson transformation, and a more complex joint Gaussianization using normalizing flows, are reported. The authors report an improvement in the performance of the Likelihood Ratios (LRs) computed with the previously Gaussianized feature vectors, producing a more reliable forensic glass comparison.

Another recent interlaboratory study reports the analysis of glass samples by 13 different laboratories that used laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) with a standard test method (ASTM E2927-23) for the forensic analysis and comparison of vehicle glass [131]. This study aimed to explore the performance of the application of a match criterion described in the standard test method and from likelihood ratio (LR) calculations when reporting the significance of glass evidence comparisons. Five (5) databases populated in different countries and combinations of the databases were used as background data to calculate LRs for two (2) casework scenarios involving vehicle glass comparisons as part of the interlaboratory exercises. When the ASTM E2927-23 was used to compare vehicle glass samples that originated from the same source, all laboratories (except one) correctly reported the samples to be indistinguishable, thus concluding that the possibility that the glass originated from the same source could not be eliminated. When the LR was calculated for the same comparison, most laboratories obtained large LR values (LR∼10,000) interpreted as “strong support” for the same-source proposition. The LR rate of misleading evidence for the same-source comparisons was <2% for one of the scenarios of the exercise. Comparing vehicle glass samples known to originate from different sources resulted in most laboratories reporting the glass to be “distinguishable” when using the ASTM standard method criterion or produced very small LR values (LR∼0.0001) when using the LR comparison criteria, interpreted as “strong (or very strong) support” for different-source proposition. The LR rate of misleading evidence for different-source comparisons was reduced to zero when the chemically similar glass comparisons from the same manufacturer were not treated as “different source”. Chemically similar glass samples (those that originated from different vehicles but were collected from the same make, model, and year or originated from the same vehicle but a different pane of glass) sometimes resulted in an LR∼1 that can be interpreted as no support of either proposition or that the possibility that the glass originated from the same source could not be eliminated, when using the ASTM match criterion. The authors report that all “false inclusions” were derived from the comparison of chemically similar samples, such as inner and outer panes from the same windshield; therefore, “error rates” on this dataset should not be generalized outside of the context of this study. This study also reports that a database composed of about 2000 background samples originating from different countries and analyzed in different laboratories produced consistent results. When examined for calibration, all databases and their combinations had “false exclusion” rates below 5% as well as “false inclusion” rates below 0.5% for the ASTM calculation. The rate of misleading evidence of LR for same-source comparisons and for different-source comparisons for the databases and their combinations were below 2% . An empirical cross-entropy (ECE) plot was used to evaluate the calibration of all the databases and their combinations, which resulted in the log-likelihood ratio cost (Cllr) of less than 0.02.
