Comparative analysis of survival outcomes and prognostic factors between intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma after surgical resection: a propensity score-matched study based on the SEER database
Authors: Jize Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to explore survival outcomes and prognostic factors between intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma after surgical resection.MethodsPatients diagnosed with primary intrahepatic or extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma between 2010 and 2018 who underwent cancer-directed surgery were identified from the SEER database. A 1:1 Propensity Score Matching was performed. Survival outcomes were analyzed via Kaplan-Meier curves and Log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models and Fine-Gray competing risk models were constructed.ResultsA total of 1,586 eligible patients were included. After matching, 478 patients (239 per group) were balanced. In the matched cohort, intrahepatic patients exhibited significantly superior Overall Survival (OS) compared to extrahepatic patients (median OS: 45 vs. 25 months; HR = 1.244, 95% CI: 1.001–1.545, P = 0.049). Conversely, a Fine-Gray competing risk analysis showed no significant difference in Cancer-Specific Survival (CSS) between the subtypes (SHR = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.641–1.043, P = 0.105). Independent risk factors for OS included advanced age, T3/T4 stage, N1/N2 stage, and tumor size ≥ 5 cm. Notably, the retrieval of 4 or more lymph nodes was associated with improved OS (HR = 0.630, 95% CI: 0.469–0.846, P = 0.002), though its association with CSS was not statistically significant in the competing risk model.ConclusionsResults from this PSM analysis suggest that intrahepatic location is associated with better OS compared to extrahepatic disease, but cancer-specific survival is similar between the subtypes after accounting for competing risks. This divergence implies that survival differences might be driven by competing risks, potentially distinct long-term surgical morbidities, rather than intrinsic biological lethality. Furthermore, adequate lymphadenectomy (≥ 4 nodes) correlated with improved overall survival and staging accuracy, highlighting its potential role as a surgical quality metric for both subtypes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results; Survival Outcomes; Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma; Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma; Lymphadenectomy; Propensity Score Matching; SEER database

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma, the second most common primary hepatic malignancy, has seen a steady rise in global incidence [1–3]. Despite being historically rare, its poor prognosis and resistance to conventional therapy pose significant management challenges [1, 4]. Anatomically divided into intrahepatic and extrahepatic subtypes, these tumors exhibit distinct biological behaviors [5, 6]. Intrahepatic cases frequently harbor isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 and 2 (IDH1/2) mutations and fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) fusions [7], whereas extrahepatic subtypes resemble pancreatic adenocarcinoma with KRAS, TP53, and SMAD4 mutations [6]. Such heterogeneity necessitate tailored therapeutic strategies [8–10]. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative therapy for both subtypes, offering the only potential for long-term survival [11–13]. However, the surgical procedures required differ vastly in their complexity and physiological impact [14]. Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma necessitates partial hepatectomy [12], whereas extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma often mandates pancreatoduodenectomy or major hepatectomy with biliary excision [15]. These interventions carry distinct morbidity and mortality profiles, which can significantly confound survival comparisons [14].

Comparative analyses of survival outcomes between intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma have yielded conflicting results in the existing literature [16, 17]. Some retrospective series suggest that extrahepatic disease is associated with a poorer prognosis due to a higher propensity for early lymphatic spread, perineural invasion, and longitudinal extension along the bile ducts [16, 18–20]. Others argue that extrahepatic patients may exhibit better outcomes because the onset of obstructive jaundice prompts earlier clinical presentation and diagnosis compared to the often massive, asymptomatic mass-forming tumors seen in intrahepatic cases [17, 21, 22]. A critical limitation of many previous studies is the lack of rigorous adjustment for baseline confounders [16, 23]. Patients with intrahepatic and extrahepatic disease frequently differ in age, gender, and, most importantly, tumor burden (T stage) and nodal status (N stage) at the time of diagnosis [24]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore survival outcomes and prognostic factors between intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma after surgical resection.

Methods

Data source and ethics statement

The data for this retrospective cohort study were extracted from the SEER database utilizing the SEER*Stat software (Version 9.0.41, released July 29, 2025). The specific dataset selected was the “Incidence - SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub (2000–2022),” which was released in April 2025 based on the November 2024 submission. The SEER program constitutes a premier source of cancer statistics in the United States, collecting comprehensive data on patient demographics, primary tumor site, tumor morphology, stage at diagnosis, first course of treatment, and follow-up for vital status. As the data within the SEER database are de-identified and publicly available, this study was exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and the requirement for informed consent, in accordance with local regulations regarding retrospective database research.

Study population and selection criteria

The study population was extracted from the SEER database. To ensure a homogeneous cohort of patients with localized disease suitable for curative surgery, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Patients diagnosed with primary intrahepatic (ICD-O-3 site code C22.1) or extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICD-O-3 site code C24.0) between 2010 and 2018 were identified. Conversely, patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) did not undergo cancer-directed surgery; (2) aged under 20 or over 79 years; (3) diagnosed with Stage IV disease; (4) survival time of less than one month; (5) unknown T stage; (6) unknown N stage; (7) unknown race; (8) unknown marital status; (9) unknown histologic type; (10) unknown cause of death; and (11) unknown lymphadenectomy information.

The cohort predominantly consisted of adenocarcinomas (98.9%), with a minor subset of other biliary-origin carcinomas (1.1%) included. Non-epithelial neoplasms such as neuroendocrine tumors, lymphomas, and sarcomas were excluded due to their distinct natural histories and treatment responses. The rigorous application of these criteria resulted in a final unmatched cohort of 1,586 patients.

Variable definitions

The primary exposure variable was the anatomical site of the tumor: Intrahepatic versus Extrahepatic. Several covariates were analyzed to adjust for potential confounders. Demographic variables included age, gender, race, and marital status. Age was categorized into three groups: 20–59 years, 60–69 years, and 70–79 years. Gender was classified as Male or Female. Race was categorized as White, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Asian or Pacific Islander. Marital Status was grouped into Married, Single/Unmarried/Domestic Partner, and Widowed/Divorced/Separated. Tumor characteristics included diagnosis year, tumor staging, tumor size, and histology. Diagnosis year was included to account for temporal trends. Tumor Staging was based on the AJCC 8th edition when available, supplemented by the 7th or 6th editions for missing fields (T1, T2, T3, T4 and N0, N1, N2) [25]. Tumor size was categorized into < 2 cm, 2–5 cm, ≥ 5 cm, and Unknown. Histology was dichotomized into Adenocarcinoma and Others, although “Others” represented a small subset of mixed types fitting the adenocarcinoma spectrum. Treatment variables focused on the extent of lymphadenectomy, quantified by the number of regional lymph nodes examined pathologically. This was categorized into three groups: None (0 nodes), 1 to 3 nodes, and 4 or more nodes.

The study evaluated two primary outcomes. Overall Survival (OS) was defined as the time interval from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause. Cancer-Specific Survival (CSS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death specifically attributed to cholangiocarcinoma or its direct complications. In the SEER database, this is derived from the “SEER Cause-Specific Death Classification.” Patients who died from other causes were censored at the time of death for the CSS analysis.

Statistical analysis

R software version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was utilized to perform all statistical analyses. To address the inherent selection bias associated with retrospective observational data, Propensity Score Matching was utilized [25]. This score was calculated using a non-conditional logistic regression model that included all baseline covariates: age, gender, race, marital status, diagnosis year, T stage, N stage, histology, tumor size, and lymphadenectomy count. A 1:1 nearest neighbor matching algorithm was employed with a caliper width set at 0.2 standard deviations (SD) of the logit of the propensity score. This stringent caliper ensures that matched pairs are highly similar in their baseline characteristics. Matching was performed without replacement. The quality of the match was assessed using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD). An SMD value of less than 0.1 was considered indicative of excellent balance between the two groups.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the cohort characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as means with SD, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups (Intrahepatic vs. Extrahepatic) were evaluated using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were visualized and compared using the Log-rank test [26]. To identify independent prognostic factors, Cox proportional hazards regression models and Fine-Gray model were constructed. Univariable analysis was first performed to screen for potential predictors. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 or of known clinical relevance were entered into the multivariable models. A stepwise backward elimination method was used to refine the model, while maintaining key demographic variables (age, gender) to control for confounding. The assumption of proportional hazards was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Results were reported as Hazard Ratios (HR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The initial database query identified a total of 18,556 patients diagnosed with biliary tract malignancies. Following the rigorous application of the eligibility criteria, 16,970 patients were excluded, primarily due to advanced metastatic disease (Stage IV), missing staging information, or non-surgical management. This selection process resulted in a final analytical cohort of 1,586 eligible patients, comprising 669 with intrahepatic and 917 with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. A detailed flowchart of the patient selection process is presented in Fig. 1. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Prior to matching, the two groups exhibited substantial heterogeneity, reflecting the differing clinical presentations of these malignancies. In the unmatched cohort, significant baseline disparities were evident. Intrahepatic patients were generally younger (P = 0.005) and had a more balanced gender distribution compared to the male-predominant extrahepatic group (P < 0.001). Tumor characteristics also differed markedly; intrahepatic cases presented predominantly with early T-stage (T1) disease but larger tumor sizes (≥ 5 cm). In contrast, extrahepatic patients frequently presented with advanced T-stage (T3) and N1 nodal disease (P < 0.001). Regarding surgical management, extensive lymphadenectomy (≥ 4 nodes) was performed significantly more often in extrahepatic cases than in intrahepatic cases (P < 0.001).

Following the application of Propensity Score Matching, 478 patients were matched (239 per group). The matching process successfully mitigated the baseline imbalances. In the matched cohort, no statistically significant differences remained for age (P = 0.654), gender (P = 0.356), race (P = 0.732), marital status (P = 0.881), T stage (P = 0.949), tumor size (P = 0.367), or lymphadenectomy extent (P = 0.724). The N-stage distribution showed a marginal statistical difference (P = 0.048), primarily driven by the presence of N2 disease (2.51%) in the extrahepatic cohort, a stage category not applicable to intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma in the AJCC 7th edition. However, the proportion of N0 and N1 disease remained highly comparable between groups.

Survival outcomes

In the unmatched cohort, intrahepatic patients exhibited a significantly longer median survival time (45 months, 95% CI: 40–53) compared to extrahepatic patients (26 months, 95% CI: 24–28) (P < 0.001; Fig. 2). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 85.8%, 57.1%, and 42.4% for ICC versus 77.3%, 38.5%, and 26.2% for ECC. The median follow-up was 101 months (95% CI: 96–106) and 108 months (95% CI: 103–118) for the intrahepatic and extrahepatic groups, respectively. This survival advantage persisted after matching. The Kaplan-Meier curves for the matched cohort (Fig. 3) demonstrated a clear separation, with intrahepatic patients showing superior OS compared to extrahepatic patients (P = 0.007). The median survival in the matched intrahepatic group was 45 months (95% CI: 40–53) versus 25 months (95% CI: 23–28) in the matched extrahepatic group (P = 0.013). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 85.8%, 57.1%, and 42.4% for ICC versus 78.5%, 36.5%, and 24.3% for ECC. The median follow-up for the matched cohort was 101 months (95% CI: 96–106) for intrahepatic and 87 months (95% CI: 77–93) for extrahepatic patients.

Comparison of disease-specific mortality yielded different results. In the unmatched cohort, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates were 87.3%, 62.7%, and 49.4% for ICC, compared to 82.1%, 48.2%, and 37.8% for ECC (P = 0.083; Fig. 4). Similarly, in the matched cohort, the Kaplan-Meier curves for CSS (Fig. 5) showed significant overlap (P = 0.39). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates in the matched cohort were 87.3%, 62.7%, and 49.4% for ICC, and 82.9%, 46.0%, and 34.4% for ECC. The cancer-specific mortality rate was 55.23% for intrahepatic and 52.30% for extrahepatic patients (P = 0.582).

Prognostic factors for OS

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of OS in both the unmatched cohort (Table 2) and the matched cohort (Table 3). In the multivariable model, extrahepatic location remained an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality, with a HR of 1.244 (95% CI: 1.001–1.545, P = 0.049) compared to intrahepatic location. Advanced age was a significant predictor of death. Patients aged 60–69 (HR = 1.406, P = 0.011) and 70–79 (HR = 1.384, P = 0.023) had significantly worse survival compared to those aged 20–59.

Tumor burden, represented by T-stage and N-stage, was a potent driver of mortality. Compared to T1, T3 stage increased the risk of death by 82% (HR = 1.820, P < 0.001), and T4 stage increased it by 44.5% (HR = 1.445, P = 0.028). Nodal involvement (N1) was associated with a HR of 1.801 (P < 0.001), and N2 disease carried a HR of 2.661 (P = 0.033). Tumor size ≥ 5 cm was also an independent risk factor (HR = 1.573, P = 0.019), and unknown tumor size was associated with increased mortality (HR = 1.474, P = 0.043). A critical finding was the protective effect of adequate lymphadenectomy. Patients with 4 or more lymph nodes removed had a significantly lower risk of death compared to those with no lymphadenectomy (HR = 0.630, 95% CI: 0.469–0.846, P = 0.002). Interestingly, the removal of 1–3 nodes did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.917, P = 0.555).

Prognostic factors for CSS

The multivariable analysis for CSS revealed key distinctions from the OS analysis (Tables 4 and 5). In the Fine-Gray multivariable model, the site of origin (extrahepatic vs. intrahepatic) was not a significant predictor of cancer-specific death (SHR = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.641–1.043, P = 0.105). Age was not a significant predictor of cancer-specific mortality (60–69 vs. 20–59: P = 0.165; 70–79 vs. 20–59: P = 0.126), suggesting that older patients are more likely to die from competing causes rather than the cancer itself.

Tumor stage remained highly prognostic for CSS. T3 stage (SHR = 2.158, P < 0.001) and T4 stage (SHR = 1.921, P < 0.001) were associated with doubled mortality risks. Discussion

This population-based analysis of 1,586 patients from the SEER database represents a comprehensive effort to disentangle the prognostic impact of anatomical origin in cholangiocarcinoma. By employing Propensity Score Matching to neutralize the substantial baseline disparities in age, tumor stage, and surgical management, this study isolated the independent association of tumor location on survival. The findings reveal a divergence in survival patterns: while patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma exhibited a statistically significant advantage in OS compared to those with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, this advantage does not translate to CSS. Furthermore, the data provides compelling evidence for the therapeutic value of adequate lymphadenectomy, identifying the retrieval of 4 or more nodes as a critical determinant of long-term outcomes for both subtypes.

The divergence between OS and CSS is a pivotal finding that warrants careful interpretation [27]. The borderline HR of 1.244 for OS in extrahepatic cases suggests a 24.4% higher risk of all-cause mortality, which should be interpreted with caution given the non-significant CSS result. However, the non-significant SHR of 0.818 for CSS indicates that the risk of dying from the cancer is statistically indistinguishable. This discrepancy implies that the excess mortality in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma is driven by competing risks. A plausible explanation lies in the differing morbidity profiles of the required surgical interventions [28]. Extrahepatic disease, particularly distal lesions, typically necessitates a pancreatoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) [15]. Despite improvements in perioperative mortality, the Whipple procedure is associated with long-term sequelae such as exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, pancreatogenic diabetes (Type 3c), and recurrent biliary sepsis, which can degrade overall health and increase susceptibility to non-cancer mortality [29, 30]. In contrast, hepatic resections for intrahepatic disease, while major surgeries, generally preserve the integrity of the gastrointestinal tract and pancreatic function [11]. This hypothesis is supported by the finding that advanced age (70–79 years) was a significant predictor for OS (P = 0.023) but not for CSS (P = 0.126). This discrepancy strongly suggests that the survival disadvantage observed in extrahepatic patients is largely driven by competing risks and physiological decline, possibly exacerbated by the long-term metabolic and nutritional sequelae of pancreatoduodenectomy, rather than intrinsic differences in tumor biology. Clinically, these findings suggest that improving surgical safety and postoperative management of non-cancer comorbidities (e.g., nutritional support after Whipple procedure) may be essential for improving survival in ECC patients. This aligns with population-based competing risk analyses in cholangiocarcinoma, which highlight the necessity of evaluating non-cancer mortality to accurately reflect surgical benefits [31].

Biologically, the similarity in CSS challenges the historical paradigm that views these entities as having vastly different malignant potentials [32]. While molecular studies have highlighted distinct mutational landscapes, IDH1/2 and FGFR2 fusions in intrahepatic versus KRAS and TP53 mutations in extrahepatic subtypes, the data suggests that once the tumor burden (T and N stage) is standardized, the lethality of the disease is comparable [33, 34]. This underscores the importance of TNM staging as the primary prognosticator over anatomical location. The SHRs for T3 disease (SHR 2.158 for CSS) and N1 disease (SHR 1.576 for CSS) confirm that local invasion and lymphatic spread are the dominant drivers of oncologic failure [4]. Recent molecular profiling studies further support this, indicating that while driver mutations differ, the downstream pathways of proliferation and invasion are often convergent [35]. The comparable CSS suggests that future adjuvant therapy trials could potentially enroll both subtypes if stratified by stage, rather than strictly separating them based on anatomy, a strategy already employed in trials like BILCAP [36, 37].

A major contribution of this study is the validation of lymphadenectomy thresholds. The 8th edition of the AJCC staging manual recommends a minimum of 6 lymph nodes for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [37]. However, adherence to this standard is notoriously low in Western cohorts. This analysis, utilizing a threshold of 4 nodes based on distribution data, found that retrieving ≥ 4 nodes was associated with a 37% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR = 0.630) compared to no lymphadenectomy. However, this protective effect did not reach statistical significance for cancer-specific mortality in the competing risk model (SHR = 1.131, P = 0.091). Notably, the retrieval of 1–3 nodes did not provide a statistically significant benefit [38]. This suggests a threshold effect: sampling a few nodes is insufficient to alter the clinical course [39]. The survival benefit of extensive lymph node dissection is likely twofold. First, it enables accurate staging (avoiding the “Will Rogers phenomenon”), ensuring that patients with nodal metastases are correctly identified and considered for adjuvant therapy [37]. Second, it may provide a direct therapeutic benefit by clearing locoregional micrometastases, thereby reducing the risk of regional recurrence [24, 40]. Given that nearly 45% of intrahepatic patients in the unmatched cohort had no lymph nodes examined, there is a need to standardize surgical quality [24]. Surgeons performing hepatectomy for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma should prioritize porta hepatis lymphadenectomy to meet this ≥ 4 node benchmark [12]. This aligns with recent consensus guidelines advocating for routine lymphadenectomy to improve prognostication and guide adjuvant therapy decisions.

The prognostic value of tumor size, independent of T-stage, is another finding of clinical relevance [26]. Large tumors (≥ 5 cm) were associated with a 61% increase in cancer-specific mortality risk (SHR = 1.613). In the AJCC 7th edition, size was not a staging criterion for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, but its prognostic value has been debated [40]. The data supports the re-integration of tumor size into risk stratification models [26]. Large tumors may correlate with a higher probability of occult vascular invasion or a greater burden of circulating tumor cells, reflecting a more aggressive biology that is not fully captured by the T-stage definition of vascular invasion or multiple tumors alone. This finding aligns with other recent studies advocating for size-based staging criteria. It suggests that patients with large tumors, even if technically resectable and node-negative, should be considered high-risk and potentially evaluated for neoadjuvant therapies to test biological behavior prior to major resection [8].

It was also observed that advanced age (60–79 years) was a risk factor for OS but not CSS. This has important implications for patient counseling. It suggests that chronological age alone should not be a contraindication to curative surgery. If an elderly patient is deemed fit to survive the immediate perioperative period, their risk of dying from cancer recurrence is no higher than that of a younger patient. Decision-making should therefore focus on physiological reserve and frailty rather than age per se [4]. However, the increased overall mortality in the elderly underscores the need for vigilant postoperative care and management of comorbidities to maximize the survival benefit gained from surgery.

The strengths of this study include the large sample size and the use of Propensity Score Matching to create well-balanced cohorts, which enhances the internal validity of the comparisons. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Importantly, although PSM adjusts for observed variables, it cannot account for unmeasured confounders, such as performance status or baseline liver function. Furthermore, the lack of recurrence-free survival data in SEER prevents a direct assessment of tumor biology divergence. First, as a retrospective analysis of the SEER database, unmeasured confounders such as patient comorbidities (e.g., Charlson Comorbidity Index), performance status (ECOG), or liver function (Child-Pugh score) could not be accounted for, all of which influence surgical candidacy and survival. Also, despite Propensity Score Matching, a marginal residual imbalance in N-stage distribution persisted, reflecting the inherent structural differences in nodal staging between the two subtypes. Second, the database lacks decisive surgical and pathological parameters, most notably microvascular invasion (MVI) and specific surgical margin status (R0/R1/R2). As demonstrated in recent cohorts, achieving an R0 margin significantly impacts survival outcomes, particularly in node-negative cholangiocarcinoma [18]. The omission of these gold standard prognosticators means our multivariable models may harbor residual confounding. Third, the analysis was constrained by the dataset transitional staging structure: while the current AJCC 8th edition was prioritized, missing fields necessitated supplementation with the 7th or 6th editions, introducing potential staging heterogeneity. Additionally, it was limited by the unavailability of specific diagnosis years (2016–2017) which impacts temporal trend assessment. Additionally, the significant attrition rate during patient selection and the lack of external validation from high-volume centers may limit the robustness of our findings. Fourth, information regarding adjuvant chemotherapy was not included in the matching process. While the use of adjuvant therapy (e.g., capecitabine) has become standard following the BILCAP trial, this practice varied during the study period (2010–2018) and could influence survival outcomes [36]. Furthermore, the lack of detailed records on modern systemic therapies, which are increasingly pivotal in the updated ESMO guidelines for biliary tract cancer [1], precludes us from adjusting for these critical survival determinants. Finally, the categorization of lymph nodes into 4 or more was data-driven; while valid for this cohort, future studies should continue to evaluate the AJCC recommendation of 6 nodes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this propensity score-matched analysis indicates that while patients with surgically resected intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma may exhibit superior OS compared to those with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, the risk of cancer-specific death appears similar between the two groups. This suggests that the observed survival gap could be driven by non-cancer causes, possibly related to the differing long-term morbidities of hepatic versus pancreatico-biliary resections. The findings highlight the prognostic importance of tumor stage and size and support adequate lymphadenectomy (≥ 4 nodes) as a modifiable surgical factor significantly associated with improved overall survival. Consequently, systematic lymph node dissection should be considered a priority in the surgical management of both intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma to optimize staging accuracy and therapeutic outcomes.
