Abstract
. We report on some computational experiments related to the trivial units property and unique product property for group rings of torsion-free groups. These properties are related to Kaplansky’s unit and zero-divisor conjectures. Our investigations include a classification of certain symmetric non-trivial units in the binary group ring of the Hantzsche-Wendt group; this group was used in Gardam’s refutal of Kaplansky’s unit conjecture. We also exhibit and investigate a new candidate group that fails the unique units property but may satisfy the trivial unit property. No examples of groups with these properties are known to date.



1.
Introduction

Let
L
be a ring with identity, not necessarily commutative. A
zero-divisor
in
L
is a non-zero
x\in L
such that
xy=0
or
yx=0
for some nonzero
y\in L
.
One says that
x\in L
is a
unit
if there exists
y\in L
such that
xy=1
and
yx=1
; if
L
has no zero-divisors, then one of the conditions implies the other. We will be interested in the group ring
R[G]
of a group
G
and commutative ring
R
with identity. Our paper focusses on two interrelated conditions: the trivial units property for group rings, and the unique product property for groups. We begin by introducing these conditions and giving brief background.

Definition 1.1
.

A group
G
satisfies the
trival units property (TUP) for
an integral domain
R
if the only units in the group ring
R[G]
are of the form
rg
, where
g\in G
and
r
is a unit of
R
. Such units will be called trivial.

The following long-open conjecture was refuted by Gardam
[
12
]
.

Conjecture 1.2
.

If
G
is a torsion-free group and
R
an integral domain, then
G
satisfies the TUP for
R
.

Gardam
[
12
]
found a non-trivial unit in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[P]
, where
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the field with two elements and
P
is the torsion-free group defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\langle a,b\mid b^{-1}a^{2}b=a^{-2},a^{-1}b^{2}a=b^{-2}\rangle.

We note that
P
is a crystallographic group, and
x=a^{2},y=b^{2}
and
z=abab
(in the notation of
[
12
]
) are free generators of the abelian translation subgroup of
P
, which has index 4 in
P
.

Remark 1.3
.

While the proof given in Gardam’s paper is purely mathematical, the original discovery of a non-trivial unit in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[P]
was made computationally using a SAT solver. Gardam found a non-trivial unit supported on a ball of radius
5
around the identity in the Cayley graph of
P
. To do so, he reformulated the problem of finding a unit as a Boolean satisfiability (SAT) problem, assigning Boolean variables for the coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to words in the generators of
P
and their inverses.

The following purely group-theoretic property was introduced by Rudin and Schneider
[
29
]
, who used the term
\Omega
-group.

Definition 1.4
.

A group
G
has the
unique product property
(UPP) if for all non-empty subsets
A,B\subseteq G
, there exist some
a\in A
and
b\in B
such that whenever
ab=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}\in A
,
b^{\prime}\in B
, then
a=a^{\prime}
and
b=b^{\prime}
. In other words, when viewing
AB
as a multiset, some element occurs with multiplicity
1
.

Promislow
[
25
]
showed that
P
fails the UPP. It is well known that UPP implies the TUP for every domain; see Proposition
2.1
below for a proof. Suprisingly, the converse implication is still unknown: is there a group that satisfies the TUP for some domain, but fails the UPP? This question is implicit in early work, and explicitly asked for instance in
[
12
]
.

This paper follows an experimental approach, using SAT solvers and computer algebra towards new examples, which are then evaluated for advancing the theory. We

•

describe nontrivial units in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[P]
supported on balls from radius 4 onwards, and their potential symmetry properties;

•

expose a new candidate group that fails the UPP, but may satisfy the TUP for some domain. This group, denoted
H_{4}
, is a small index extension of the usual integral Heisenberg group.

2.
Mathematical background

2.1.
Group rings

For a group
G
and a commutative ring
R
with multiplicative identity, the
group ring

R[G]
consists of all the formal sums
u=\sum_{g\in G}u_{g}g
where only finitely many coefficients
u_{g}\in R
are non-zero. The addition operation is carried out component-wise. The operation of multiplication is given by

(\sum_{g\in G}u_{g}g)(\sum_{h\in G}v_{h}h)=\sum_{r\in G}w_{r}r
,

where
w_{r}=\sum_{gh=r}u_{g}v_{h}
. By slight abuse of notation, we usually write
1=1_{G}
and
1=1_{R}
. The
support
of
\sum_{g\in G}u_{g}g
is the subset
\{g\in G\mid u_{g}\neq 0\}
.

2.2.
Some background on the unit conjecture

Higman posed Conjecture
1.2
in his unpublished 1940 PhD thesis
[
15
, p. 77]
for the case that
R={\mathbb{Z}}
. The conjecture was taken up in generality by Kaplansky
[
18
]
, and became known as the
unit conjecture
. In his paper, Kaplansky attributes it to a list of problems arising from a 1968 conference in Moldova. Note that if
R[G]
has a non-trivial unit, then after extending the integral domain
R
to its field of fractions
K
, one obtains a counterexample in the group ring
K[G]
. Kaplansky also posed the stronger
zero divisor conjecture:

K[G]
has no zero divisors, and the even stronger
idempotent conjecture
, that the only idempotents in
K[G]
are
0,1
.

There are various families of groups that have the TUP for each integral domain
R
. Higman showed this for abelian groups, and more generally for
locally indicable groups
; this means that every non-trivial finitely generated subgroup has a quotient isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.

After Gardam’s refutation
[
12
]
, his work was generalised by Murray
[
21
]
, who altered the construction to obtain non-trivial units in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[P]
for every prime
q
. Gardam
[
13
]
found a non-trivial unit in
\mathbb{C}[P]
; it has the same support as the original example over
\mathbb{F}_{2}[P]
. Thus, the conjecture is refuted for fields of each characteristics. However, the original conjecture of Higman was for integral group rings, and remains open.

2.3.
Background on the unique product property

The study of the UPP defined in Definition
1.4
has a long history, and was instrumental to the refutation of the unit conjecture
[
12
]
.
On the positive side, every bi-orderable group has UPP because the product of the maxima of two sets is unique; in fact, the weaker property of being diffuse suffices
[
2
]
; for definitions and a proof see Proposition
2.1
below.

The first example of a torsion-free group that fails the UPP was constructed by Rips and Segev
[
27
]
who made use of small cancellation theory. Passman introduced the torsion-free group
P
of (
1.1
) in connection with the Kaplansky conjectures. Promislow
[
25
]
exhibited a
14
-element subset
A\subseteq P
such that the multiset
AA
has no element with multiplicity
1
, showing that
P
does not satisfy the UPP. Carter
[
4
]
generalised
P
to an infinite family of torsion-free groups

(2.1)

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle a,b\mid a^{-1}b^{2^{k}}a=b^{-2^{k}},b^{-1}a^{2}b=a^{-2}\rangle\quad(k>0)

and showed that the
P_{k}
are pairwise non-isomorphic, do not contain
P
as a subgroup for
k>1
, and none satisfies the UPP; note that
P=P_{1}
. Craig and Linnell
[
6
]
conjectured that every uniform pro-
p
-group satisfies the UPP. They also consider some variants of
P
with more than two generators and show that each is torsion-free. None of these variants satisfies the UPP since they each contain
P
as a subgroup. Lastly, we mention that Nielsen and Soelberg proved bound for the sizes of sets in torsion-free groups that witness the non-unique product property; for example, they proved that if
AA
has a non-unique product, then
|A|\geqslant 8
and this bound is sharp, see
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
.
We refer to
[
6
]
and the references therein for more details on the unique product property.

As a quick observation, the UPP is related to zero divisors in
K[G]
as follows: Let
u,v\in K[G]
be non-zero elements with support
A
and
B
, respectively. Let
C
be the set of elements in the multiset
AB
, so that

uv=\sum_{c\in C}\big(\sum_{a\in A,b\in B\atop ab=c}u_{a}v_{b}\big)c.

If
uv=0
, then this requires that every
c\in C
occurs with multiplicity at least
2
in
AB
; in particular, if
K[G]
has zero divisors, then
G
does not satisfy the UPP.

2.4.
Implications between conditions

We describe a hierarchy of properties for
K[G]
, fromstrongest to weakest, which includes the properties on which the three Kaplansky conjectures are based. While the implications seem well-known (see also
[
2
,
23
]
), we include new short proofs and references for completeness. Recall that a group
G
is
left-orderable
if there is a total order “
\leqslant
” on
G
that is invariant under left multiplication. A group
G
is
diffuse

[
2
]
if for every non-empty finite subset
C\subseteq G
there exists an
extremal element
in
C
, that is,
c\in C
such that for all non-identity
g\in G
, either
gc\notin C
or
g^{-1}c\notin C
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
K
be a field and let
G
be a torsion-free group. Then
(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow\ldots\Rightarrow(5)
, where
(1),\ldots,(5)
are the following properties:

(1)

G
is left-orderable.

(2)

G
is diffuse.

(3)

G
satisfies the UPP.

(4)

K[G]
has only the trivial units.

(5)

K[G]
has no zero divisors.

Proof.

\eqref{lo}\Rightarrow\eqref{diff}
: Let
C\subseteq G
be finite and let
c\in C
such that
b\leqslant c
for all
b\in C
. If
g\in G
is non-trivial, then either
gc>c
(and then
gc\notin C
by the maximality of
c
), or
gc<c
(and then
c<g^{-1}c
, which implies that
g^{-1}c\notin C
). Thus,
G
is diffuse.

\eqref{diff}\Rightarrow\eqref{upp}
: Let
A,B\subseteq G
be finite subsets and let
c
be an extremal element in the
set

C=AB
. Suppose
c=ab=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
with
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
such that
g=a^{\prime}a^{-1}\neq 1
. Then
gab=a^{\prime}b\in C
and
g^{-1}ab=a(a^{\prime})^{-1}ab=ab^{\prime}\in C
, which contradicts
(
2
)
. Thus, the extremal element in
AB
viewed as a
multiset
has multiplicity
1
, that is,
G
satisfies the UPP.

\eqref{upp}\Rightarrow\eqref{uc}
: We prove the contrapositive. Suppose
A,B\subseteq G
are the support sets for a non-trivial unit
\alpha
in
K[G]
and its inverse
\beta
, respectively. By assumption, there exists
x\in A
such that
x^{-1}\in B
. By replacing
\alpha
by
x^{-1}\alpha
and
\beta
by
\beta x
, we can assume that
1\in A\cap B
.

Let
E=B^{-1}A
and
F=BA^{-1}
; we show that
E
and
F
do not have the unique product propery. Since
|A|,|B|\geqslant 2
, there exists a
a\in A-\{1\}
, which shows that the product
1=1\cdot 1=a\cdot a^{-1}
is not unique in
EF
. Now consider a non-identity product
x=(b_{0}^{-1}a_{0})(b_{1}a_{1}^{-1})
in
EF
where
a_{0},a_{1}\in A
and
b_{0},b_{1}\in B
. If
a_{0}=b_{1}=1
, then
x=b_{0}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}=b_{0}^{-1}aa^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}
is not unique. If
a_{0}b_{1}=1
and
a_{0}\neq 1
, then
x=b_{0}^{-1}a_{0}b_{1}a_{1}^{-1}=b_{0}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}
. Lastly, consider
a_{0}b_{1}\neq 1
. Since
A
and
B
are supports for a unit, there must exist
a_{2}\in A
and
b_{2}\in B
with
a_{2}\neq a_{0}
,
b_{2}\neq b_{1}
, and
a_{0}b_{1}=a_{2}b_{2}
; thus,
(b_{0}^{-1}a_{0})(b_{1}a_{1}^{-1})=(b_{0}^{-1}a_{2})(b_{2}a_{1}^{-1})
is not unique. The claim follows.

\eqref{uc}\Rightarrow\eqref{zdc}
: Since
G
is torsion-free, the only finite normal subgroup is the identity subgroup. Hence the ring
K[G]
is
prime
(see
[
24
, Theorem 4.2.10]
), that is, if
IJ=0
for ideals
I,J
in
K[G]
, then
I=0
or
J=0
. Suppose, for a contradiction, that
u,v\in K[G]
are zero divisors, and denote by
I
and
J
the principal ideals generated by
u
and by
v
, respectively. Since
I
and
J
are non-zero, the prime property shows that
IJ\neq 0
. This, in turn, implies that there exists
r\in K[G]
such that
vru\neq 0
. The element
t=vru
therefore is non-zero and satisfies
t^{2}=0
and
(1+t)(1-t)=(1-t)(1+t)=1
. Thus,
1-t
is a unit in
K[G]
. By assumption, this unit must be trivial, say
1-t=kg
for some
g\in G
and
k\in K
. Now
t=1-kg\in K[\langle g\rangle]
is a non-zero element with
t^{2}=0
. Since
\langle g\rangle
is infinite cyclic, the ring
K[\langle g\rangle]
is isomorphic to the ring of univariate Laurent polynomials over
K
; in particular, it is an integral domain. But then
t^{2}=0
forces
t=0
, a contradiction. Thus,
K[G]
has no zero divisors.
∎

Gardam’s work
[
12
]
shows that
\mathbb{F}_{2}[P]
with
P
as in (
1.1
) is an integral domain that contains non-trivial units; this shows that “(

)
\not\Rightarrow
(

)” in general. An example showing “(

)
\not\Rightarrow

)” is given by Raimbault, Kionke, and Dunfield

. It is an interesting open problem to find examples of the other non-implications. As mentioned, our experimental work aims at an example of a torsion-free group that satisfies (

) for some domain

, but not (

Cliff
[

showed that if

is a field of characteristic

and

is a torsion-free polycyclic-by-finite group, then

has no zero divisors.

4.
Fibonacci groups

We now consider a class of groups many of which are torsion-free and not left-orderable; these groups are potential counterexamples to the various conjectures. Following Johnson
[
17
, p. 74]
, for integers
2\leqslant r<n
, the
Fibonacci group

F(r,n)
is defined by

(4.1)

\displaystyle F(r,n)

\displaystyle=\langle\;x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\mid x_{i}x_{i+1}\cdots x_{i+r-1}x_{i+r}^{-1}\quad(0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1)\;\rangle,

where subscripts are understood to be modulo
n
such that all elements
x_{j}
lie in
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. These groups and their generalisations have been studied extensively in the literature; see
[
19
,
3
]
and the references therein for background. We only summarize a few facts.

(1)

Let
d=\gcd(r+1,n)
. The group
F(r,n)
is infinite whenever either
d>3
, or
d=3
and
n
is even; see
[
3
]
. Furthermore,
F(r,n)
is infinite whenever
n>5r
; see
[
17
, p. 76]
.

(2)

Every finite group is a quotient of some group
F(r,n)
.

(3)

F(2,6)\cong P
via
a=x_{1}x_{2},b=x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
.

(4)

F(2,n)
has torsion for odd
n
,
F(2,2m)
is torsion-free for
m\geqslant 3
; see
[
17
, p. 84]
and
[
14
, (P4)]
.

Fox
[
10
]
proved that
F(2,2m)
is not right-orderable (which is equivalent to being left-orderable) for
m\geqslant 2
, and asked which of these groups satisfy the UPP.

4.1.
The groups
H_{n}=F(n-1,n)
for
n\geq 4

We write
H_{n}=F(n-1,n)
for
n\geq 4
. By what is said above, such
H_{n}
is infinite. Furthermore,
H_{n}
is not left-orderable for
n\geqslant 4
by
[
8
, Lemma 4]
. The groups
H_{n}
for even
n\geq 4
are the fundamental groups of 3-manifolds, and torsion free
[
8
, Proposition 2b.ii]
. By recent work
[
9
]
they satisfy the zero divisors conjecture for any domain.

The proof of
[
8
, Lemma 4]
mentions that
x_{i}^{2}=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
for each
i
in
F(n-1,n)
: For
i=1
this follows directly from the relator
x_{2}\ldots x_{n}x_{1}^{-1}
; for
i=2
, multiply
x_{3}\ldots x_{n}x_{1}=x_{2}
from the left by
x_{2}
and use that
x_{2}\ldots x_{n}=x_{1}
, which yields
x_{1}^{2}=x_{2}^{2}
; the other relations
x_{1}^{2}=x_{i}^{2}
follow similarly. Thus, the subgroup
N
of
H_{n}
generated by
x_{1}^{2}
is central in
H_{n}
. Tietze transformations
[
16
, Section 2.4.4]
can be used to show that

(4.2)

\displaystyle H_{n}=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\mid x_{1}^{2}=\ldots=x_{n}^{2}=w_{n}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad w_{n}=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}.

Let
K_{n}
be the free product of the free cyclic groups generated by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, respectively, with amalgamation
x_{1}^{2}=\ldots=x_{n}^{2}
, that is,

(4.3)

\displaystyle K_{n}=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\mid x_{1}^{2}=\ldots=x_{n}^{2}\rangle.

Note that
H_{n}\cong K_{n}/N
, where
N
is the normal closure of
x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}
in
K_{n}
. It follows from
[
28
, Theorem 11.68]
that

(4.4)

\displaystyle g\in K_{n}\text{ has finite order}\iff g^{h}\in\langle x_{i}\rangle\text{ for some $i$ and $h\in K_{n}$}.

Since each group
\langle x_{i}\rangle
is torsion-free, this implies that
K_{n}
is torsion-free as well. We now show that elements in
K_{n}
have the following normal form.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is an algorithm that can rewrite every element in
K_{n}
into a unique normal form
x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z}
where successive
i_{u}
and
i_{u+1}
are distinct,
i_{k}\neq n
, and
z
is some integer.

Proof.

Given that
x_{n}^{2}
is central, the existence of a normal form follows directly from a generalisation of
[
20
, Theorem IV.2.6]
from an amalgamation of
2
to
n
groups. However, to expose the algorithmic content we give a short direct proof. Since each
x_{i}^{2}=x_{n}^{2}
is central in
K_{n}
, each element in
K_{n}
can be transformed into the form
x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z}
as above by replacing all even powers of
x_{j}
by the same power of
x_{n}
, and moving all these powers of
x_{n}
to the right. For the uniqueness, suppose that
x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z}=x_{j_{1}}\ldots x_{j_{m}}x_{n}^{s}
are two such forms. If
k=m=0
, then we started with
x_{n}^{z}=x_{n}^{s}
, which is an equation in the free cyclic group
\langle x_{n}\rangle
, hence
z=s
. If
k=0
and
m\neq 0
(or vice versa), then we have an equation
x_{j_{1}}\ldots x_{j_{m}}=x_{n}^{z-s}
. Since
m\neq 0
, this forces
s-z=0
and
x_{j_{1}}\ldots x_{j_{m}}=1
, but the latter is not possible by (
4.4
). Thus, we can assume that
0<m\leqslant k
, and rewrite our element equality as

(\ast)\quad 1=x_{j_{m}}^{-1}\ldots x_{j_{1}}^{-1}x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z-s}=x_{j_{m}}\ldots x_{j_{1}}x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z-s-2m}.

This element has order
1
, and therefore must lie in one of the free factors, say in
\langle x_{\ell}\rangle
. This is not possible if
j_{1}\neq i_{1}
. If
j_{1}=i_{1}
, then we can replace
x_{j_{1}}x_{i_{1}}=x_{n}^{2}
and move it to the right, and we iterate. So either we can rewrite
(\ast)
as
1=x_{n}^{z-s-2m+2m}
, in which case uniqueness follows, or we reach a word
x_{j_{m}}\ldots x_{j_{g}}x_{i_{g}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z-s-2m+2(g-1)}=1
with
x_{j_{g}}x_{i_{g}}\notin\{1,x_{n}^{2}\}
, or a word
x_{i_{m+1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}x_{n}^{z-s}=1
with
m+1\leqslant k
. Both cases are not possible by (
4.4
).
∎

4.2.
Each
H_{n}
has a solvable word problem

Recall that the
word problem
of a finitely presented group
G
is to decide membership in the set of free group words in the generators of the group that equal the identity in
G
. The word problem is
solvable
if an algorithm exists to decide this membership. If this set is merely recursively enumerable, one says that
G
is
recursively presented
.

Remark 4.2
.

If the word problem for
G
is solvable, the group is computable in the usual sense of computable algebra: there is a bijection
\theta\colon G\to\mathbb{N}
such that the images under
\theta
of group products are computable.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
H
be a recursively presented group and let
w\in Z(H)
be a non-trivial central element. If
L=H/\langle w\rangle
has a solvable word problem, then so does
H
.

Proof.

Suppose
w
has order
t\geqslant 2
, where
t=\infty
is allowed. Let
u
be a word in the generators of
H
. Since the generators of
H
map onto the generators of
L
, and
L
has solvable word problem, we can check whether
u
represents the identity in
L
. If not, we have determined that
u
is not the identity in
H
. If
u
does represent the identity in
L
, then we know that
u
represents a non-trivial element in
\langle w\rangle
, so
u=w^{r}
for some
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since
H
is recursively presented, there is an algorithm that find this
r
. Note that
r\equiv 0\bmod t
if and only if
u
is the identity in
H
. Thus, we can decide the word problem in
H
.
∎

We now show that in
H_{n}
we can solve the word problem. While not strictly necessary to compute with
H_{n}
, having a solution to the word problem does often significantly improve computational capabilities. We continue with the notation of the introduction of this section; recall that
w_{n}
is central in
H_{n}
since each
x_{i}^{2}=w_{n}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The group
H_{n}
has solvable word problem for
n\geqslant 4
.

Proof.

Define
L_{n}=H_{n}/\langle w_{n}\rangle
. In
L_{n}
, each
x_{i}^{2}=1
, and so the relator
w_{n}=1
can be written as
x_{1}\ldots x_{r}=x_{n}^{-1}\ldots x_{r+1}^{-1}=x_{n}\ldots x_{r+1}
for any
r\in\{2,\ldots,n-2\}
. Thus, we have a presentation

L_{n}=\langle\;x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mid x_{1}^{2},\;\ldots,\;x_{n}^{2},\;x_{1}\ldots x_{r}=x_{n}\ldots x_{r+1}\;\rangle
,

which shows that
L_{n}\cong A*_{\mathbb{Z}}B
is an amalgamated free product where

A=\langle\;x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\mid x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{r}^{2}\;\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad B=\langle\;x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n}\mid x_{r+1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}\;\rangle
,

and
{\mathbb{Z}}
is embedded into
A
via
1\mapsto x_{1}\ldots x_{r}
and into
B
via
1\mapsto x_{n}\ldots x_{r+1}
. Indeed, since
r
lies in
\{2,\ldots,n-2\}
, each
A
and
B
is a free product of at least two cyclic groups of order
2
, and (
4.4
) implies that
x_{1}\ldots x_{r}
and
x_{r+1}\ldots x_{n}
do not have finite order in
A
and in
B
, respectively.

By Lemma
4.3
, it suffices to show that
L_{n}
has solvable word problem. Note that
A,B
have solvable word problem by an argument similar to the one in Lemma
4.1
. We now proceed via the usual normal form of elements in an amalgam of two groups.
Let
U=A\cap B
and note

U=\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\rangle=\langle x_{r+1}\ldots x_{n}\rangle.

Below we will pick suitable right coset representatives
\mathcal{S}
of
U
in
A
, and
\mathcal{T}
of
U
in
B
, both containing
1
. We first show that each element of
L_{n}
can be uniquely written as
us_{1}t_{1}\ldots s_{\ell}t_{\ell}
where
u\in U
and each
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
and
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
, and the
s_{i},t_{j}
are non-trivial with the possible exceptions
s_{1}
and
t_{\ell}
:
note that every element in
A*_{\mathbb{Z}}B
has the form
a_{1}b_{1}\ldots a_{\ell}b_{\ell}
with each
a_{i}\in A
and
b_{i}\in B
; starting from the right, replace
b_{\ell}=ub^{\prime}_{\ell}
with
u\in U
and
b^{\prime}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{T}
. Recall that
u\in A\cap B
, so next we replace
a_{\ell}u=va_{\ell}^{\prime}
with
v\in U
and
a_{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
. An iteration of this process yields the required form.

In order to write an algorithm to create and multiply normal forms, we need to discuss the computability of
\mathcal{S}
; the discussion for
\mathcal{T}
will be similar. Recall that a normal form for elements of
A
is given by words
x_{k_{1}}\ldots x_{k_{m}}
where each
k_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
k_{i}\neq k_{i+1}
. Dropping the
x
’s, we can describe this as a sequence
\sigma=[k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}]
of numbers in
\{1,\ldots,r\}
as above. Multiplication of two sequences is induced by multiplication by elements in
A
, with the cancellation rules
x_{i}^{2}=1
for each
i
. Thus, given two such sequences
\sigma
and
\tau
, one can write
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\varrho
and
\tau=\varrho^{-1}\tau^{\prime}
where the subsequence
\varrho
is as long as possible; in this case,
\sigma\tau=\sigma^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}
. (E.g. if
\sigma=[4,2,1]
and
\tau=[1,2,1,3]
, then
\varrho=[2,1]
and
\sigma\tau=[4,1,3]
.) In the following we use the notion of computable sets, implicitly assuming an encoding of group elements by natural numbers according to Remark
4.2
. The subgroup
U=\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\rangle
of
A
is computable, because given a normal form for
A
one can check whether it represents a power of
x_{1}\ldots x_{r}
. As a right transversal
\mathcal{S}
of
U
in
A
, we now pick those elements
\sigma
that are the length-lexicographically least in their right cosets of
\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\rangle
. The latter is decidable because
\mathcal{S}
consists of all the sequences
\sigma
that do not start with
[1,2,\ldots,s]
or with
[r,r-1,\ldots,r-t]
for some
s>r/2
or
t<r/2
: to see this, observe that

\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\rangle x_{1}\ldots x_{s}=\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\rangle x_{r}x_{r-1}\ldots x_{s+1},

and
[r,r-1,\ldots,s+1]
is lexicographically smaller than
[1,2,\ldots,s]
; analogously for the representative
x_{r}x_{r-1}\ldots x_{r-t}
. Since
r
is fixed,
\mathcal{S}
is computable. A similar argument for
\mathcal{T}
(with variables
y_{i}=x_{n-i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-r
) shows that
\mathcal{T}
is computable. Thus
L_{n}
has solvable word problem, and so does
H_{n}
.
∎

5.
the structure of
H_{4}

We use the common notation
{\rm pc}\langle X\mid R\rangle
for a polycyclic group presentation
\langle X\mid R\rangle
where all trivial commutator relations
g_{i}^{g_{j}}=g_{i}
are omitted (where
g_{i},g_{j}\in X
), c.f.
[
16
, Section 8.1]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The group
H_{4}
is isomorphic to the (torsion-free) polycyclic group

{\rm pc}\langle r,a,b,z\mid r^{2}=z,\;\;a^{r}=a^{-1},\;\;b^{r}=b^{-1},\;\;b^{a}=bz^{2}\rangle.

Proof.

Let
H
be the group given by the polycyclic presentation in the proposition, with generators
r,a,b,z
. Define a map
\psi\colon\{r,a,b,z\}\to\{\tilde{r},\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{z}\}\subseteq H_{4}
, where

\tilde{r}=x_{1},\quad\tilde{a}=x_{1}x_{4}^{-1},\quad\tilde{b}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{4}^{-2},\quad\tilde{z}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}.

One can show that
\{\tilde{r},\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{z}\}
generates
H_{4}
. Recall that each
x_{i}^{2}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}
, so
\tilde{z}
is central and
\tilde{r}^{2}=\tilde{z}
. We show that the relations of
H
hold in
H_{4}
via
\psi
; then von Dyck’s Theorem
[
16
, Theorem 2.53]
proves that
\psi
extends to a unique group epimorphism
H\to H_{4}
. For this it remains to consider the images of the relations involving
a^{r}
,
b^{r}
, and
b^{a}
. Note that
\tilde{a}^{\tilde{r}}=x_{4}^{-1}x_{1}=x_{4}x_{1}^{-1}=\tilde{a}^{-1}
where the middle equation holds since
x_{4}^{-2}x_{1}^{2}=1
. Similarly, we have
\tilde{b}^{\tilde{r}}=x_{2}x_{4}^{-2}x_{1}=x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}^{-1}x_{4}^{2}=\tilde{b}^{-1}
, where the middle equation holds since
x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{4}^{-2}x_{1}=x_{1}^{2}=x_{4}^{2}
. Lastly, since
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}=x_{4}
, we have

\tilde{b}^{\tilde{a}}=x_{4}x_{2}x_{4}^{-2}x_{1}x_{4}^{-1}=x_{4}^{-1}x_{2}x_{1}x_{4}^{-1}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\tilde{b}\tilde{z}^{2}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}^{2}=x_{4}x_{3};

now
\tilde{b}^{\tilde{a}}=\tilde{b}\tilde{z}^{2}
follows from
x_{1}=x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}
and
x_{3}=x_{2}^{-1}x_{1}x_{4}^{-1}=x_{2}^{-2}x_{2}x_{1}x_{4}^{-1}=x_{4}^{-2}x_{2}x_{1}x_{4}^{-1}
. Now von Dyck implies that
\psi
induces an epimorphism
H\to H_{4}
. To prove isomorphism, consider the map
\varphi\colon\{\tilde{r},\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{z}\}\to\{r,a,b,z\}
. To apply von Dyck’s Theorem to the generators
x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}
, note that

x_{1}=\tilde{r},\quad x_{2}=\tilde{r}^{-1}\tilde{b}(\tilde{a}^{-1}\tilde{r})^{2},\quad x_{3}=(\tilde{a}^{-1}\tilde{r})^{-2}\tilde{b}^{-1}\tilde{z}\tilde{r}^{-1}\tilde{a},\quad x_{4}=\tilde{a}^{-1}\tilde{r},

so
\varphi
translates to the map

\varphi^{\prime}\colon\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}\to\{r,r^{-1}b(a^{-1}r)^{2},(a^{-1}r)^{-2}b^{-1}zr^{-1}a,a^{-1}r\}.

A straightforward calculation in the polycyclic group
H
shows that the image of
\varphi^{\prime}
satisfies the relations of
H_{4}
. Since
\varphi
and
\psi
are mutually inverse,
H\cong H_{4}
.
∎

6.
Normal forms for
\mathbb{F}_{2}[H_{4}]

Using SAT solvers, we showed that there is no non-trivial unit so that both the unit and its inverse or supported on a ball of radius 4 in the Cayley graph given by the generators
a,b,r
. For larger radius our query did not return an answer. Here we develop normal forms for the group ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}[H_{4}]
, which could also be used to search for nontrivial units.

Let
K
be a field. We consider normal forms for
K[H_{4}]
, and for this we first define a subgroup
S<H_{4}
of index
2
. Recall that
[r,a,b,z]
is a polycyclic generating set for
H_{4}
, so
[a,b,z]
is a polycyclic generating set for the subgroup
S\leqslant H_{4}
generated by
\{a,b,z\}
; in particular,

S\cong{\rm pc}\langle a,b,z\mid b^{a}=bz^{2}\rangle

has index
2
in
H_{4}
and is torsion-free. The normal forms of
S
are exactly
a^{u}b^{v}z^{w}
with
u,v,w\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using the presentation of
S
, a simple calculation shows that

(6.1)

(a^{u}b^{v}z^{w})(a^{u^{\prime}}b^{v^{\prime}}z^{w^{\prime}})=a^{u+u^{\prime}}b^{v+v^{\prime}}z^{w+w^{\prime}+2vu^{\prime}}.

Note that
r^{2}=z
is central, so conjugation with
r
is an automorphism of order
2
of
S
. Specifically, we have

(6.2)

(a^{u}b^{v}z^{w})^{r}=a^{-u}b^{-v}z^{w}.

Since
H_{4}
is the disjoint union of
S
and
rS
, we have the following.

Lemma 6.1
.

Every element
u\in K[H_{4}]
can be written uniquely as
u=\alpha+r\beta
with
\alpha,\beta\in K[S]
.

For
\beta\in K[S]
write
\beta^{r}=r^{-1}\beta r
; this can be evaluated via (
6.2
). If
\alpha+r\beta
and
\alpha^{\prime}+r\beta^{\prime}
lie in
K[H_{4}]
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle(\alpha+r\beta)(\alpha^{\prime}+r\beta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha\alpha^{\prime}+z\beta^{r}\beta^{\prime}+r(\beta\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{r}\beta^{\prime});

recall that
r^{2}=z
, so
z\beta^{r}\beta^{\prime}=r^{2}r^{-1}\beta r\beta^{\prime}=r\beta r\beta^{\prime}
.

Now consider
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the binary field. The normal forms exhibited above can be used for the search for non-trivial units: For
N\in\mathbb{N}
denote by
B_{N}
the set of all elements
a^{u}b^{v}z^{w}\in S
with
|u|+|v|+|w|\leq N
; by abuse of notation, we call this a ball of radius
N
for
S
. Now think of making a guess for
\alpha,\beta,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[S]
by picking four finite subsets of
B_{N}
; that is, assign truth values to Boolean variables, one per ball element. (It may be useful to only consider subsets of small sizes; in Gardam’s work
[
12
]
, the support sizes are
21
out of a ball of
144
elements.) By (
6.3
), the elements
u=\alpha+r\beta
and
v=\alpha^{\prime}+r\beta^{\prime}
are units if and only if

\alpha\alpha^{\prime}+z\beta^{r}\beta^{\prime}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\beta\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{r}\beta^{\prime}=0.

Since
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}
are indeterminates, we can represent these equations by a Boolean formula. Since
S
is left-orderable,
\mathbb{F}_{2}[S]
satisfies the unit conjecture. So we can impose that one of the variables in
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
is set to
1
. A SAT solver might be used to see if there are
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}
constituting non-trivial units.

7.
The unique product property

7.1.
Refuting the UPP: a computational approach

Let
G
be a group. If
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the field with
2
elements, there is a one-to-one correspondence between subsets
S\subseteq G
and elements
g_{S}=\sum_{g\in S}g
in the group ring
K[G]
. If
G
does not satisfy the UPP, there exist finite non-empty subsets
A,B\subseteq G
such that every element in the multiset
AB
occurs with multiplicity at least
2
. Thus, if
C
is the set of all elements in
AB
, then in the product

g_{A}g_{B}=\sum_{c\in C}(\sum_{a\in A,b\in B,\atop ab=c}1)c

each coefficient
\sum_{a\in A,b\in B,ab=c}1
is greater than
1
.

We now describe how to use a SAT solver to potentially find two such sets
A
and
B
within a prescribed finite super-set
S\subseteq G
. Let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and identify
A
and
B
with
a=g_{A}=\sum_{s\in S}a_{s}s
and
b=g_{B}=\sum_{s\in S}b_{s}s
, respectively, where each
a_{s}
and
b_{s}
is an indeterminate in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, considered as a Boolean variable (with
1={\tt true}
and
0={\tt false}
). We want to find assignments
a_{s},b_{s}\in\{0,1\}
for each
s\in S
such that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\Big(\bigvee_{s\in S}a_{s}\Big)\wedge\Big(\bigvee_{s\in S}b_{s}\Big)\wedge\bigwedge_{u,v\in S}\Big((a_{u}\wedge b_{v})\rightarrow\bigvee_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in S\atop{u^{\prime}\neq v\atop uv=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}}a_{u^{\prime}}\wedge b_{v^{\prime}}\Big)

is true. If we find such an assignment, then the first two parts of that formula say that the corresponding elements
a
and
b
are non-zero in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[G]
, whereas the last part says that whenever
a_{u}=b_{v}=1
, then the coefficient of
uv
in the sum describing
ab
has at least two non-zero terms
a_{u}b_{v}
and
a_{u^{\prime}}b_{v^{\prime}}
, that is,
uv=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
with
u\neq u^{\prime}
. To bring (
7.1
) into conjunctive normal form, we introduce auxiliary Boolean variables
c_{u,v}
defined by the property
c_{u,v}\leftrightarrow(a_{u}\wedge b_{v})
; this turns (
7.1
) into the following formula with indeterminates
a_{u},b_{v},c_{u,v}
for
u,v\in S
:

(7.2)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\hskip 7.74998pt\bigwedge_{u,v\in S}\Big((\neg c_{u,v}\vee a_{u})\wedge(\neg c_{u,v}\vee a_{v})\wedge(\neg a_{u}\vee\neg a_{v}\vee c_{u,v})\Big)\\[8.61108pt]
\wedge&\Big(\bigvee_{s\in S}a_{s}\Big)\wedge\Big(\bigvee_{s\in S}b_{s}\Big)\wedge\bigwedge_{u,v\in S}\Big((\neg c_{u,v})\vee\bigvee_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in S\atop{u^{\prime}\neq v\atop uv=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}}c_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}}\Big).\end{array}

For a given group
G
and given set
S
, we compute the multiplication table with rows and columns labelled by the elements of
S
, and use this table to write down the clauses of the formula (
7.2
). If a SAT solver determines that this formula can be satisfied, then the corresponding solution determines two sets
A
and
B
that demonstrate that
G
does not satisfy the UPP.

7.2.

We note that the failure of the UPP in Proposition
7.1
is not a property inherited from the group
P
in (
1.1
). Recall that the Hirsch length of a polycyclic group is the number of infinite factors in a polycyclic series for the group; this number is independent of the chosen polycyclic series.

Lemma 7.2
.

The group
P
from (
1.1
) is not isomorphic to a quotient of a subgroup of
H_{4}
.

Proof.

It is known that
P
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, see
[
4
, p. 447]
. Thus, if
P
is isomorphic to a quotient of a subgroup of
H_{4}
, then there exist
U\leqslant H_{4}
and
V\unlhd U
with
\mathbb{Z}^{3}\cong U/V
. The group
H_{4}
has Hirsch length
3
, and it follows from
[
30
, p. 16]
that the Hirsch length of
U
is at most
3
, with equality if and only if
U
has finite index in
H_{4}
. Since
U/V\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, this forces
[H_{4}:U]<\infty
and
U
has Hirsch length
3
. Since
V
is normal in
U
, it also follows from
[
30
, p. 16]
that the Hirsch length of
U
is the sum of the Hirsch lengths of
U/V
and
V
. This forces that
V
has Hirsch length
0
, so
V
is finite. Since
H_{4}
is torsion-free,
V=1
, and therefore
U\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
is a finite index subgroup of
H_{4}
. We show that this is not possible.

For a contradiction, suppose that
x=r^{w}a^{s}b^{t}z^{u}
and
x^{\prime}=r^{w^{\prime}}a^{s^{\prime}}b^{t^{\prime}}z^{u^{\prime}}
and
x^{\prime\prime}=r^{w^{\prime\prime}}a^{s^{\prime\prime}}b^{t^{\prime\prime}}z^{u^{\prime\prime}}
are generators of such a subgroup
U\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
in normal form. Recall that
w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\in\{0,1\}
. We first show that
\langle x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
forces
w=w^{\prime}=w^{\prime\prime}=0
. Suppose not all
w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}
are
0
, say
w=1
. We can now arrange that
w^{\prime}=w^{\prime\prime}=0
by replacing
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
by a product with
x^{-1}
, if necessary. Now the relations of
H_{4}
show that the exponents of
a
and
b
in
(x^{\prime})^{x}
are
{-s^{\prime}}
and
{-t^{\prime}}
, respectively, but since
x
and
x^{\prime}
commute, this forces
s^{\prime}=t^{\prime}=0
. This implies that if
w=1
, then
x^{\prime}\in\langle z\rangle
, and the same argument shows that
x^{\prime\prime}\in\langle z\rangle
. But then
\langle x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
is not possible, a contradiction. Thus, we can assume that
w=w^{\prime}=w^{\prime\prime}=0
, so
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in S=\langle a,b,z\rangle
.

Now we repeat with a similar argument. By assumption,
\mathbb{Z}^{3}\cong\langle x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\rangle\leqslant S
. Since
\langle b,z\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we can assume that
x\notin\langle b,z\rangle
, that is,
s\neq 0
. As before, we can arrange that
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}=0
by replacing
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
by suitable powers and a product with a power of
x
. That is, we assume
\langle x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in\langle b,z\rangle
and
x\in S\setminus\langle b,z\rangle
. Since
\langle b,z\rangle
is abelian, we now observe
x^{\prime}=(x^{\prime})^{x}=(x^{\prime})^{a^{s}b^{t}z^{u}}=(b^{t^{\prime}}z^{u^{\prime}})^{a^{s}}=b^{t^{\prime}}z^{2st^{\prime}+u^{\prime}}
, and
s\neq 0
forces
t^{\prime}=0
. But then
x^{\prime}\in\langle z\rangle
, and a similar argument shows that
x^{\prime\prime}\in\langle z\rangle
. But then
\langle x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
is not possible, a final contradiction.
