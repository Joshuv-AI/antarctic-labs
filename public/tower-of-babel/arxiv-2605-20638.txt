Distributed and Decentralized Optimization Algorithms via Consensus ALADIN
Xu Du, Jingzhe Wang, Karl H. Johansson, Apostolos I. Rikos

Abstract
Distributed optimization has found widespread applications in smart grids, optimal control, and machine learning. This paper studies distributed consensus optimization. We extend the Augmented Lagrangian-based Alternating Direction Inexact Newton (ALADIN) framework to propose Consensus ALADIN (C-ALADIN) with a central coordinator, which directly handles consensus constraints. Our C-ALADIN algorithm admits both a first-order variant and a second-order variant that employs a Hessian approximation, avoiding direct transmission of second-order information while preserving fast local convergence. We then develop a decentralized version of C-ALADIN that operates over directed graphs with quantized communication, using a finite-time coordination protocol. For both versions, we establish global convergence guarantees for convex problems and local convergence guarantees for non-convex problems. For the decentralized case, the iterates converge to a neighborhood of the optimum determined by the quantization level. Numerical results demonstrate that our methods retain fast convergence while substantially reducing communication and computational costs compared to existing decentralized approaches.



I
Introduction

Distributed and decentralized optimization have attracted significant attention, driven by advances in distributed control systems
[
47
,
28
,
55
]
, federated learning
[
60
,
59
]
, and distributed power systems
[
46
,
11
]
.
The growing need to solve large-scale problems with massive datasets and heterogeneous objectives motivates the use of distributed and decentralized optimization algorithms.

In distributed optimization, data are partitioned across multiple agents.
Each agent solves a local subproblem and exchanges information either with a central coordinator or directly with its neighboring agents.
When no coordinator is present and communication relies solely on peer-to-peer interactions, the setting is specifically referred to as decentralized optimization. Distributed optimization problems are typically categorized into two main classes: (i) distributed resource allocation optimization, and (ii) distributed consensus optimization
[
16
]
.
In the first class, the objective function has a finite-sum structure, and local variables are coupled through affine equality or inequality constraints, potentially with heterogeneous dimensions
[
10
]
. In the second class, problems also exhibit a sum-structured objective, but the local variables are required to reach full consensus
[
4
]
.
From an algorithmic perspective, as surveyed in
[
5
,
10
]
, distributed and decentralized optimization methods can be divided into two types: (i) primal decomposition based algorithms and (ii) dual decomposition based algorithms.
Primal decomposition based optimization includes zeroth-order
[
56
]
, first-order
[
36
,
45
,
25
,
33
,
54
,
31
,
39
,
26
]
, and second-order methods
[
49
,
58
,
9
]
. In contrast, dual decomposition based approaches build upon fundamental frameworks such as Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition
[
7
]
, the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
4
]
, and the Augmented Lagrangian Alternating Direction Inexact Newton (ALADIN) method
[
22
,
23
]
. Dual decomposition generally achieves faster convergence and higher solution accuracy than primal methods
[
32
, Sec. I]
. This work therefore focuses on dual decomposition based approaches for distributed and decentralized consensus optimization, specifically on the ALADIN method
[
22
,
23
]
.
This algorithm has demonstrated favorable convergence performance in distributed resource allocation optimization.
However, its application to consensus optimization faces structural challenges, as we discuss next.
Extending ALADIN to this setting therefore remains largely unexplored in the literature.

I-A

Literature Review.

The ALADIN algorithm
[
22
]
was originally introduced to improve the numerical convergence speed of ADMM
[
4
,
38
,
12
]
for distributed resource allocation optimization, specifically its parallelizable primal splitting variant
[
50
]
,
[
22

, and to strengthen its convergence guarantees for non-convex problems. It achieves this by integrating the coupled Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)
[
37
]
into the augmented Lagrangian framework
[
22
,
23
,
19
,
18
,
11
,
52
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
14
,
21
,
24
]
.
In convergence theory, ALADIN-type algorithms achieve local convergence guarantees for smooth non-convex problems and global convergence guarantees for convex problems
[
23
,
16
]
.
ALADIN has subsequently been applied to model predictive control (MPC)
[
24
]
, moving horizon estimation (MHE)
[
55
,
51
]
, and distributed optimal power flow (OPF)
[
19

. In each case, the problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality: for MPC and MHE, due to a long horizon, and for OPF, due to a large number of buses. By recasting these problems into distributed formulations via time-splitting for MPC/MHE or spatial decomposition for OPF, and solving them with ALADIN, the dimensionality burden is effectively alleviated.
Inspired by ALADIN, the PaDOA algorithm
[
35
]
was developed for distributed mixed-integer programming, integrating outer approximation into the distributed optimization framework, achieving global convergence for convex problems and finding applications in temperature control systems.
Despite the success of ALADIN in distributed resource allocation optimization problems, its application to distributed consensus optimization faces several challenges: (i) ALADIN requires each variable to possess local second-order information, yet the global consensus variable has no associated objective function and consequently admits no Hessian, leading to a structural mismatch;
(ii) existing ALADIN implementations typically rely on a central coordinator or assume undirected communication, which is not always feasible in many real-world applications where directed graphs are prevalent; and (iii) ALADIN relies on real-valued exchange of Hessians and gradients, which limits applicability in environments with limited communication bandwidth.

To address the first challenge, a coupling matrix scheme was proposed in
[
1
, Sec. 12]
that reformulates consensus optimization into a resource allocation form, but this requires manual intervention, leaving direct consensus solving within ALADIN underexplored.
To address the second challenge, prior work
[
18
]
employs decentralized ADMM to solve the coupled QP in ALADIN.
In an attempt to tackle decentralized consensus optimization directly, the GIANT algorithm
[
49
]
and its variants employ global gradient aggregation with exact local Hessian information. Nevertheless, they have been primarily studied only for convex problems on undirected graphs.
The ADMM-type algorithm in
[
41
]
simultaneously addresses all aforementioned challenges
through the integration of Consensus ADMM with a decentralized quantized communication scheme; however, it remains limited to convex problems and is not based on the ALADIN framework.

Based on the above literature review, three critical gaps remain for ALADIN‑type algorithms in the context of distributed consensus optimization:
(i) tailoring ALADIN specifically for consensus constraints (rather than resource allocation);
(ii) decentralization over directed graphs without a central coordinator; and
(iii) quantized communication to reduce bandwidth.
Although some prior works address subsets of these issues (e.g., quantization for ADMM, decentralized QP solvers for resource allocation), no existing ALADIN‑based method addresses all three in a unified manner, particularly for non‑convex problems.
To the best of our knowledge, apart from our recent works
[
16
,
13
]
, this gap has not been systematically addressed.

I-B

Main Contributions.

Motivated by the aforementioned open questions, we propose Consensus ALADIN (C-ALADIN) and its extensions built upon the ALADIN framework.
Our main contributions are as follows.

•
C-ALADIN framework.

To tailor ALADIN specifically for distributed consensus optimization, inspired by Consensus ADMM
[
4
, Ch. 7]
, we introduce C-ALADIN (as shown in (
8
)) as a more natural solution that replaces the generic coupled quadratic program (QP) used for information coordination in ALADIN with a consensus QP.

•
Second-order C-ALADIN.

To reduce communication and computational complexity, second-order C-ALADIN is introduced.
The coordinator employs a Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS)-based Hessian approximation for each agent, avoiding direct transmission of second-order information (Algorithm
1
).
A closed‑form solution for the large‑scale consensus QP further reduces computational complexity.
The resulting algorithm provides local convergence guarantees for non-convex consensus problems (Theorem
1
) and, under constant Hessian assumptions, ensures global convergence for convex problems (Theorem
2
and
3
).

•
First-order C-ALADIN.

For scenarios where the Hessian approximation matrices are difficult to evaluate, we introduce the first-order C-ALADIN (as shown in (
12
)) by replacing the Hessian in the BFGS-based C-ALADIN with a suitably scaled identity matrix.
This corresponds to the constant Hessian case
B_{i}=\rho I
in Second-order C-ALADIN, and therefore inherits the same global convergence guarantees for convex problems (Theorems
2
and
3
).

•
Decentralized first-order C-ALADIN with quantized communication.

For scenarios where agents communicate over directed decentralized networks,
building upon the first-order C-ALADIN, we introduce a decentralized variant for directed communication graphs (as shown in (
27
)) that incorporates a quantized finite-time consensus protocol (Algorithm
2
).
This variant enables agents to exchange quantized messages, reducing communication overhead.
We prove that under convex local cost functions, it converges globally to a quantization-dependent neighborhood of the optimal solution (see Theorem
4
).

•
Decentralized second-order C-ALADIN with quantized communication.

For scenarios involving non-convex consensus problems over directed networks with quantized communication,
we extend second-order C-ALADIN to two algorithmic variants (Algorithm
3
and Algorithm
4
).
The first adopts a bilevel structure, solving the consensus QP via the decentralized first-order C-ALADIN (as shown in (
27
)) to achieve full decentralization. The second employs Algorithm
2
(a decentralized quantized average consensus algorithm with finite-time convergence) to estimate averages of primal variables and gradients, then recovers a BFGS Hessian approximation.
Both variants are fully decentralized, support quantized communication, and achieve local convergence to a neighborhood of a local optimum for non-convex problems (Theorem
5
for Algorithm
3
and Appendix
D
for Algorithm
4
).

Problem Formulation

Consider a network of
N
agents, where each agent
i
possesses a local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\textbf{P1:}\hskip 9.24994pt\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
i=1,\dots,N\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994ptx_{i}=z.

(2)

This formulation follows the standard consensus approach
[
4
, Chapter 7.1]

\displaystyle\textbf{P2:}\hskip 9.24994pt\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
i=1,\dots,N\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

(3)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle x_{i}=x_{j}=z,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{E},

\displaystyle m_{ij}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\Delta},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{E},

where

denotes the quantized message transmitted from agent

We first make the following assumption that are important for our subsequent development.

IV
From Typical ALADIN to C-ALADIN

Distributed resource problems are generally formulated in the fashion of mathematical programming, where
N

separable objectives are linearly coupled by
m
equality constraints. In distributed resource problems,

f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes the closed proper local objective function of each agent
i
.
Formally, distributed resource problems can be described as follows
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\\
i=1,\dots,N\end{subarray}}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathop{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})\hskip 9.24994pt\mathrm{s.t.}\;\;\hskip 9.24994pt\mathop{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}x_{i}=b.

(4)

Here, the coupling matrices
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times m}
and the coupling parameter
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are given. The dimensions
n_{i}
s of local variables
x_{i}
s are potentially different.
The augmented Lagrangian of problem (
4
) is given by
\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,\Lambda)=\sum^{N}_{i=1}f_{i}(x_{i})+\Lambda^{\top}\left({\sum}_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}x_{i}-b\right)+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}x_{i}-b\right\|^{2},

where,
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter,
\Lambda
denotes the dual variables and
x=[x_{1}^{\top},\cdots,x_{N}^{\top}]^{\top}
collects the local primal variables. Focusing on the augmented Lagrangian, details of typical ALADIN to solve the resource allocation problem in (
4
) shows as (
5
),

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{i}^{[k+1]}=\mathop{\mathrm{\arg}\mathrm{\min}}_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x_{i})+\left(\Lambda^{[k]}\right)^{\top}A_{i}x_{i}+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|x_{i}-y_{i}^{[k]}\right\|^{2};\\
&g_{i}=\nabla f_{i}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right),B_{i}^{[k+1]}\approx\nabla^{2}f_{i}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)\succ 0;\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt\left(\Delta y,\Lambda^{[k+1]}\right)\\
=&\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{\arg}\mathrm{\min}}_{\Delta y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},\forall i}\;&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta y_{i}^{\top}B_{i}^{[k+1]}\Delta y_{i}+g_{i}^{\top}\Delta y_{i}\right)\\
\operatorname{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt&\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}+\Delta y_{i}\right)=b\;|\;\Lambda\end{aligned}\right\};\\
&y_{i}^{[k+1]}=x_{i}^{[k+1]}+\Delta y_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

At each iteration, the agents solve their respective augmented Lagrangian subproblems in parallel, yielding the local primal updates
x_{i}^{[k+1]}
. Subsequently, a coupled QP coordinates the agents’ decisions, simultaneously producing the dual variables
\Lambda^{[k+1]}
and the primal increments
\Delta y
.

To solve consensus optimization problems using typical ALADIN (
5
), Problem (
2
) can be reformulated as,

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
i=1,\dots,N\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})+\cdots+f_{N}(x_{N})

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\;\;\;x_{1}&-x_{2}&&&&=0\\
&\;\;\;x_{2}&-x_{3}&&&=0\\
&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\;\;\;\;\vdots\\
&&&x_{N-1}&-x_{N}&=0\\
-x_{1}&&&&\;\;\;x_{N}&\;=0.\\
\end{matrix}

The linear constraints in (
6
) can be compactly expressed as
\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}x_{i}=0
, where
A_{1}=\left(I,0,\cdots,0,-I\right)^{\top}
,
A_{2}=\left(-I,I,0,\cdots,0\right)^{\top}
,
\cdots
,
A_{N}=\left(0,\cdots,0,-I,I\right)^{\top}
,
and
I
denotes the appropriately sized identity matrix. However, this formulation has notable limitations. The choice of
{A_{i}}
is not unique, and the resulting coupled matrices are often difficult to implement in practice and may contain redundant information. Moreover, the associated communication and computational overhead further restrict the practical applicability of ALADIN, leading to inherent inefficiencies in implementing Problem (
6
). Therefore, instead of pursuing this line, we directly analyze the consensus formulation (
2
) in the sequel.

The augmented Lagrangian of problem (
2
) is given by

\small\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,z,\lambda)=\sum^{N}_{i=1}f_{i}(x_{i})+\lambda_{i}^{\top}\left(x_{i}-z\right)+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|x_{i}-z\right\|^{2},

(7)

where
\lambda=[\lambda_{1}^{\top},\cdots,\lambda_{N}^{\top}]^{\top}
denotes the dual variables. Focusing on (
7
), drawing inspiration from Consensus ADMM
[
4
]
, details of C-ALADIN to solve the consensus optimization problem in (
2
) are as follows,

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{i}^{[k+1]}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x_{i})+\left(\lambda_{i}^{[k]}\right)^{\top}x_{i}+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|x_{i}-z^{[k]}\right\|^{2};\\
&g_{i}=\nabla f_{i}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right),B_{i}^{[k+1]}\approx\nabla^{2}f_{i}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)\succ 0;\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt\left(z^{[k+1]},\Delta x,\lambda^{[k+1]}\right)\\
=&\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{\arg}\mathrm{\min}}_{\Delta x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\forall i}\;&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta x_{i}^{\top}B_{i}^{[k+1]}\Delta x_{i}+g_{i}^{\top}\Delta x_{i}\right)\\
\operatorname{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt&x_{i}^{[k+1]}+\Delta x_{i}=z\;|\;\lambda_{i}\end{aligned}\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

C-ALADIN enables each agent to independently solve its nonlinear programming (NLP) subproblem while coordinating with others through a consensus QP. In (
8
), the first phase consists of each agent updating its local minimizer to
x_{i}^{[k+1]}
via an augmented Lagrangian subproblem. Given
x_{i}^{[k+1]}
, each agent then approximates the positive definite Hessian approximation matrices
B_{i}
and evaluates the gradients
g_{i}
of
f_{i}
. In the second phase, upon receiving
B_{i}
and
g_{i}
from all agents, the master solves a large‑scale convex consensus QP. The solution to this consensus QP yields the updated primal variables
z
,
x_{i}^{[k+1]}+\Delta x_{i}
for all
i
, and the dual variables
\lambda_{i}
. Finally, the master communicates the updated primal and dual variables back to each agent, and the entire process is repeated until convergence. This use of a consensus QP allows ALADIN to directly address distributed consensus optimization without requiring the manual construction of coupling matrices as in (
6
). The following sections present concrete instantiations of C-ALADIN, where the centralized or decentralized nature of the algorithm stems from whether the consensus QP in (
8
) is solved in a centralized or decentralized manner.

V-A

Second-Order C-ALADIN

In (
8
), each agent is required to upload a substantial amount of information to the coordinator. Moreover, the coordinator must solve a large-scale consensus QP. These two factors significantly hinder the computational efficiency of (
8
), particularly when applied to high-dimensional problems. This section presents two techniques to improve the communication and computation efficiency of C-ALADIN.

V-A
1
Improvement of Uploading Communication Efficiency

In (
8
), in order to avoid uploading the gradient and Hessian approximation directly, we choose to decode the first- and second-order information on the master side, which is detailed as follows

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} &g_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})=\;\rho(z^{[k]}-x_{i}^{[k+1]})-\lambda_{i}^{[k]}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{((sub)gradient)},\\
&s_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]},x_{i}^{[k]})=\;x_{i}^{[k+1]}-x_{i}^{[k]},\\
&y_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]},x_{i}^{[k]})=\;g_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})-g_{i}(x_{i}^{[k]}),\\
&B_{i}^{[k+1]}=\;B_{i}^{[k]}-\frac{B_{i}^{[k]}s_{i}s_{i}^{\top}B_{i}^{[k]}}{s_{i}^{\top}B_{i}^{[k]}s_{i}}+\frac{y_{i}y_{i}^{\top}}{s_{i}^{\top}y_{i}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

First, assuming that the augmented NLP in (
8
) can be solved exactly, the Clarke subdifferential of
f_{i}
at
x_{i}^{[k+1]}
yields
g_{i}=\rho(z^{[k]}-x_{i}^{[k+1]})-\lambda_{i}^{[k]}\in\partial f_{i}
. Consequently, the (sub)gradient can be recovered from
x_{i}^{[k+1]}
without explicit transmission. Second, by leveraging the differences of local private variables and corresponding (sub)gradients, the BFGS Hessian approximation can also be reconstructed at the coordinator side. A detailed analysis of the reduction in agent-to-coordinator data uploads achieved by the proposed approach can be found in
[
16
, Remark 1]
.

V-A
2
Improvement of Download Communication and Computation Efficiency

The consensus QP in (

VIII
Conclusion

This paper proposes Consensus ALADIN (C-ALADIN) and its variants for efficiently solving distributed consensus optimization problems. Within this framework, we design an efficient structure that enhances both communication and computational efficiency. Building on this framework, we develop centralized and quantized decentralized variants of C-ALADIN, each of which can be further classified into first-order and second-order implementations. We establish the convergence properties of C-ALADIN for both convex and non-convex settings and observe that, under decentralized operation with quantized communication, the proposed algorithms converge to a neighborhood of the optimal solution. Finally, numerical simulations are presented to validate the performance of our algorithms and to highlight their advantages over existing methods in the literature.

by defining the finite positive scalar

to simplify our subsequent analysis. This ensures that

holds for every agent

.

We start from equation (

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(g_{i}-g_{i}^{*}\right)^{\top}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: new gradient}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\rho\left(\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}+\lambda_{i}^{*}\right)^{\top}\left(x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: lemma error}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\rho\left(\frac{\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-\hat{z}^{[k+1]}_{i}}{2}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}}{2\rho}\right)-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}+\lambda_{i}^{*}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\hskip 7.11317pt\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}}{2\rho}+\frac{\hat{z}^{[k+1]}_{i}+\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}}{2}-z^{*}\right).

We now split the right-hand side of (

) into the following components (a), (b) (c), (d), (e).
Then, we analyze each component separately.

\displaystyle\small(a)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)^{\top}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left(z_{i}^{[k]}-z^{*}\right)-\left(z_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt+\left.\left(\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-z_{i}^{[k]}\right)-\left(\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}-z_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\;\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\left(\left(\lambda_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right)-\left(\lambda_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt\left.+\left(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{[k]}\right)-\left(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)\right)

\displaystyle(b)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}}{2\rho}\right)^{\top}\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}}{2}+\lambda_{i}^{*}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: well-known equality}}{=}\frac{1}{4\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|^{2}\right).

\displaystyle(c)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}+\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}}{2}-z^{*}\right)^{\top}\left(\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)

\displaystyle(d)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}+\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-2z^{*}}{2}\right)^{\top}\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}}{2}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: error},\eqref{eq: lam}}{\leq}2N\rho\left(\sqrt{n}M_{z}\Delta+2n\Delta^{2}\right).

\displaystyle(e)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}+\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-2z^{*}}{2}\right)^{\top}\left(\lambda_{i}^{*}-\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: error},\eqref{eq: lam}}{\leq}2N\rho\left(\sqrt{n}M_{z}\Delta+2n\Delta^{2}\right).

\hat{z}=\hat{z}_{i}

, and summing (

) becomes

\small\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\left\|x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{4}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\hat{z}^{[k]},\hat{\lambda}^{[k]}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\hat{z}^{[k+1]},\hat{\lambda}^{[k+1]}\right)+\mathcal{O}(N\sqrt{n}\Delta),\end{split}

.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
5

The inner level of Algorithm
3
corresponds to (
27
), and thus its convergence properties follow directly from Theorem
4
.
We now establish the local convergence of the outer-level iterations in Algorithm
3
. Our analysis follows a similar approach to
[
16
, Theorem 3]
,
[
23
, Section 4.1]
and
[
11
]
, and for completeness, we provide the detailed derivation below.

Assume that the initial state
x_{i}
of each agent lies in a small neighborhood of
z^{*}
. From the first-order optimality conditions of Problem (
11
), we obtain:

\begin{cases}\nabla f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})+\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}+\rho(x_{i}^{[k+1]}-\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]})=0,\\
\nabla f_{i}(z^{*})+\lambda_{i}^{*}=0.\end{cases}

(59)

Subtracting the two equations yields:

\small\nabla f_{i}(z^{*})-\nabla f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})+\rho(z^{*}-x_{i}^{[k+1]})=\rho(z^{*}-\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]})+(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}).

(60)

Under Assumption
2
, there exists
\sigma>0
such that for each subproblem:

\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})+\rho I\succeq\sigma I\succeq 0,

which implies
\left\|\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})+\rho I\right\|\succeq\sigma
. Consequently, we have:

\small\left\|\nabla f_{i}(z^{*})-\nabla f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})+\rho(z^{*}-x_{i}^{[k+1]})\right\|\geq\sigma\left\|z^{*}-x_{i}^{[k+1]}\right\|.

(61)

Substituting (
60
) into (
61
) gives:

\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-z^{*}\right\|+\frac{1}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|\geq\left\|x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall i\in\mathcal{V}.

(62)

The inner level of Algorithm
3
introduces an error term
\tilde{\epsilon}=\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{[k+1]}\right\|
, where
\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}
is generated by (
27c
) and
z^{[k+1]}
is generated by the iteration in (
27
).
To simplify the subsequent analysis, we introduce the following notation. Following
[
8
, Chapter 8, Chapter 12]
, we introduce a sufficiently small parameter
\gamma>0
such that
\|B_{i}^{[k+1]}-\nabla^{2}f_{i}(x_{i}^{[k+1]})\|<\gamma
, and define
\bar{\Lambda}^{[k+1]}=\tilde{\epsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Lambda_{\max}(B_{i}^{[k+1]})
and
\bar{\Lambda}^{[k]}=\tilde{\epsilon}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Lambda_{\max}(B_{i}^{[k]})
, where
\Lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of a matrix. Following the methodologies presented in
[
18
, equation (12)]
and
[
16
, equation (31)]
, we establish the following inequalities that hold prior to convergence:

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} N\tilde{\epsilon}+N\left\|{z}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|&\leq\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|,\\
\bar{\Lambda}^{[k+1]}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|&\overset{\eqref{eq: closed form}}{\leq}\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|.\end{aligned}\right.

(63)

From (
63
), we derive the following inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rho N}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|\hskip-2.84526pt+\hskip-2.84526pt\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|{\hat{\lambda}}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|\hskip-2.84526pt+\hskip-2.84526pt\frac{\rho N\tilde{\epsilon}+\bar{\Lambda}^{[k+1]}}{\sigma}\hskip-1.42262pt\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq: step2-linear2}}{\leq}\frac{(\rho+1)\gamma}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|x_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|.

(64)

Combining (
62
) with (
C
), we derive the following inequality:

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\left(\hskip-2.84526pt\frac{\rho N}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k+1]}-z^{*}\right\|+\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k+1]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|+\frac{\rho N\tilde{\epsilon}+\bar{\Lambda}^{[k+1]}}{\sigma}\hskip-1.42262pt\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(\rho+1)\gamma}{\sigma}\left(\frac{\rho N}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-z^{*}\right\|+\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(\rho+1)\gamma}{\sigma}\hskip-2.84526pt\left(\hskip-2.84526pt\frac{\rho N}{\sigma}\left\|\hat{z}_{i}^{[k]}-z^{*}\right\|\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[k]}-\lambda_{i}^{*}\right\|\hskip-2.84526pt+\frac{\rho N\tilde{\epsilon}+\bar{\Lambda}^{[k]}}{\sigma}\hskip-2.84526pt\right).

We observe that the left-hand side of (
C
) exhibits an identical structure to the right-hand side of (
C
), differing only by the contraction factor
\frac{(\rho+1)\gamma}{\sigma}<1
for sufficiently small
\gamma
. This contraction property establishes the local linear convergence of Algorithm
3
prior to convergence.

Note that if Algorithm
3
reaches the precision threshold
\epsilon
, inequality (
63
) no longer holds. This indicates that Algorithm
1
ceases to pursue higher accuracy and instead converges to a neighborhood of the optimal solution with radius
\epsilon
. This completes the local convergence analysis of Algorithm
3
.

The proof of Algorithm

\small\left\|\left(H_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)^{-1}-\bar{B}\right\|<\frac{1}{\left\|\left(H_{i}^{[k+1]}\right)^{-1}\right\|}=M_{\epsilon},

\displaystyle\left\|H_{i}^{[k+1]}l_{i}^{[k+1]}-\bar{B}^{-1}\bar{G}\right\|
