ABSTRACT
Polymeric encapsulants are essential materials in photovoltaic modules, protecting sensitive elec-
tronics from the environment while providing mechanical integrity to the multilayered assembly.
However, these polymeric materials are susceptible to degradation processes driven by the ingress
of environmental species, ultraviolet radiation, thermal stresses, and mechanical loading. In this
study, we employ a combined atomistic simulation and accelerated aging experimental approach
to study the molecular-scale mechanisms of encapsulant degradation. Classical molecular dy-
namics simulations quantify the diffusion of environmental and degradation species through the
polymer matrix, producing composition-specific diffusion coefficients. Reactive simulations char-
acterize activation energy barriers and reaction rate constants for key chemical pathways. In par-
allel, thermal-desorption analyses coupled with mass spectrometry monitor the emergence and
concentration profiles of degradation products under controlled stressor conditions. By integrating
simulation and experiment, we establish quantitative correlations between polymer composition,
species diffusivity, and chemical reactivity. We anticipate that these relations and quantitative val-
ues could serve as high-fidelity inputs to reaction-diffusion models, enabling physics-informed
lifetime predictions and guiding the design of more durable encapsulant materials for solar energy
applications.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents the findings of an 18-month effort under the Durable Module Materials (Dura-
MAT 2.0) portfolio entitled, Developing the Science Basis for Understanding Polymer Encapsulant
Degradation Mechanisms. Conducted in fiscal years 2024 - 2025, this work advances the collabo-
rative efforts within the DuraMAT 2.0 portfolio and contributes to the larger photovoltaic reliability
community.
The primary objective of this study was to quantify the molecular-scale processes that occur during
aging and degradation of polymeric encapsulant materials used in solar photovoltaic modules.
Encapsulant degradation is governed by concentration- and diffusion-limited chemical reactions
initiated by environmental species (e.g., water and oxygen), reaction by-products (e.g. acetic acid),
thermal stresses, and ultraviolet exposure. Developing a truly predictive model requires precise,
quantitative measures of both mass-transport phenomena and chemical kinetic processes at the
molecular level.
To meet this challenge, we integrated accelerated aging experiments with atomistic materials mod-
eling, using ethylene-co-vinyl acetate as the representative encapsulant material. We accomplished
key project milestones by determining material-specific parameters for the mass transport of dis-
solved contaminants within the encapsulant. Additionally, experimental aging studies, employing
a suite of spectroscopy and mass spectrometry techniques, together with reactive molecular dy-
namics simulations, enabled us to identify chemical species formed during degradation processes
and to quantify rates for chemical kinetic reactions. The key findings of this study include:
 • Diffusion properties of contaminant species strongly depend on the chemical structure of
 EVA, with percent cross-link and molecular weight having the greatest effects.
 • When multiple dissolved species coexist, a secondary plasticization effect accelerates trans-
 port through the polymer matrix.
 • Degradation kinetics are predicated on the interactions between contaminant species and
 heterogeneous polymer structures, with deacetylation reactions being particularly sensitive
 to the presence of water, and weak structural features such as low molecular weight and low
 cross-linking.
 • Beta-scission reactions are highly sensitive to the presence of acetic acid, and are most pro-
 nounced when molecular weight is low or vinyl acetate co-monomer concentration is high.
These insights advance our understanding of polymer degradation mechanisms, while the approach
further establishes a framework for analyzing novel, untested encapsulant materials. By provid-
ing high-fidelity, physics-informed values on materials degradation processes, this work has the
potential to impact the longevity of solar photovoltaic modules.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background

The Solar Futures Study (DOE and NREL, 2021) [1] projects that solar energy could supply up
to 40% of the nation’s electricity by 2035 and 45% by 2050. Achieving this ambitious goal ne-
cessitates photovoltaic (PV) modules that can deliver decades of optimal, efficient, and stable
performance. However, encapsulant material within PV modules is susceptible to degradation due
to various environmental stressors including, temperature fluctuations, ultraviolet exposure, rela-
tive humidity, and ingress of environmental species [2, 3]. Such degradation can lead to failure
modes like delamination, corrosion, and discoloration [4, 5, 6]. As a result, understanding the
aging performance of materials within PV modules is critical to meeting the growing demand for
solar energy.
The polymeric encapsulant is a critical component of PV modules, designed to protect electrical
parts from the environment while ensuring structural stability of the panel. The current industry-
standard encapsulant material is ethylene-co-vinyl acetate (EVA). However, as the market shifts
towards increasingly cost-effective choices, novel encapsulant materials are emerging, and in most
cases a molecular-level understanding of degradation mechanisms remains incomplete.
This fact leads us to ask, can key degradation indicators can be predictively identified to (1) prevent
early, unanticipated material failure and (2) inform encapsulant material choice prior to fielding a
PV module? Encapsulant degradation is influenced by numerous factors, making it challenging
to identify a single primary driver. For instance, proprietary manufacturing additives, dissolved
species such as water, impurities or contaminants, and metallization ions can all contribute to
chemical reactions that accelerate degradation. These interactions, referred to as “combined ef-
fects,” occur when degradation is driven by one or more species, with the extent of degradation
depending on their relative concentrations.
To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneous degradation process, it is essen-
tial to study the migration of species, their concentration-dependent behavior, and the combined
effects that influence chemical reactions and degradation rates. Such insights could enable the de-
velopment of more durable encapsulant materials and improve the long-term performance of PV
modules.

1.2. Predictive Modeling of Degradation in Photovoltaics

The material failure of polymer based encapsulants is complex with many interrelated structure-
property descriptors, requiring the marrying of bottom-up and top-down approaches. The bottom-
up approach to study encapsulant degradation, which is predicated on modeling techniques like

 15

molecular modeling, has largely been concentrated to the "part-scale" (∼yr and ∼m). These mod-
els typically rely on homogenized material representations, lacking the molecular scale detail nec-
essary for a full consideration of diffusion-limited chemical reactivity. Nevertheless, these part-
scale models have had their successes. For example, predictive models based on data from fielded
PV modules have been developed using finite-element modeling approaches (FEM). Kempe et
al. [3] used experimental data to develop an FEM model for water infiltration between laminate
sheets (impermeable and breathable) and through edge seals. Mitterhofer et al. improved on single
2-D FEM simulation with a unique two-stage simulation to describe moisture ingress but failed
to capture moisture diffusion as an evolution of polymer degradation [7]. Other empirical-based
models include the works from Hasan and Arif [8], who coupled previously developed structural
and thermal models to electrical and lifetime models to compare encapsulants using experimental
data from Saudi Arabia. Hülsmann and Wallner [9] studied the permeation and diffusion of water
into PET films as a function of thickness and temperature. Gagliardi, Lenarda and Paggi [10] de-
veloped a rate equation-based tool to predict diffusion and reactions of small molecules in EVA to
help understand accelerated aging tests, specifically targeting thermo-photo oxidation.

Few efforts have examined shorter time and length scales (∼ns and ∼nm) necessary to capture
chemical kinetics. The existing studies typically leverage density functional theory (DFT) to de-
scribe localized effects of degradation. For example, Létant, et al. [11] used DFT to study degra-
dation of EVA, specifically determining the structure of the polymer and the cross-linker after
de-acetylation. They compared their predictions to aged and irradiated samples and determined
absorption spectra for use as a potential experimental signature of degradation. More recently,
Mannodi-Kanakkithodi, et al. [12] used DFT to study the degradation of EVA when exposed
to water, specifically determining energy barriers to formation of degradation products. While
these works are impactful to inform higher length scales, they are often limited in the total num-
ber of atoms they can represent. This fact makes elucidating the connections between meso- to
continuum-scale material descriptors like failure pathways originating from the heterogeneous na-
ture of polymer encapsulants, or localized defects that lead to accelerated module failure, a chal-
lenging prospect.

Within the DuraMAT portfolio, there have been several studies aimed at developing models of
encapsulants. Bosco et al. [13] used fracture models to study sub-critical de-bonding of EVA with
varying amounts of silane, an adhesion promoter. They found that changes in viscoelasticity are
dominant at low humidity, and chemical reactions are only relevant at higher humidity. Springer
and Bosco [14], and Springer, Hartley, and Bosco [15] also developed viscoelastic models to study
cracking in electrically conductive adhesives that can be used instead of metal interconnects. Fi-
nally, Bosco, Springer, and He [16] developed FEA models of temperature and humidity dependent
structural mechanics of PV encapsulants, including EVA.

To develop an understanding of chemical kinetics at the molecular scale, addressing the gaps de-
scribed above, atomistic and meso-scale modeling is a necessary contribution as it can serve as the
time and length scale connection between FEM and ab initio efforts. Additionally, atomistic mod-
eling can often complement experimental results and elucidate interpretation where experimental
characterization may fall short. As some in the DuraMAT community have noted, experimental
characterization cannot always detect small weight concentrations of additives and/or signals can
be convoluted due to noise. Here, this study aims to inform on and make these connections.

1.3. Project Objectives

Encapsulant aging and degradation are primarily driven by diffusion-limited and concentration-
dependent chemical reactions. This requires any physics-informed model of degradation to ac-
count for the dynamic evolution of materials at the atomistic or molecular scale. To address this
need, this project has developed molecular-scale models that capture the diffusion and reactivity of
dissolved species within encapsulant materials. The objective herein was to validate these models
experimentally by predicting degradation products and further analyzing the combined effects that
contribute or enhance encapsulant degradation. As illustrated in Figure 1-1, the study established
a combined computational and experimental approach. Through computational efforts, we employ
two key modeling approaches focusing on the industry-standard encapsulant EVA. We utilized
classical molecular dynamics (MD) to assess material-specific properties that include the diffu-
sional characteristics or mass transport of environmental contaminants and degradation species
across various chemical compositions of EVA. We employed reactive MD (RMD) to explore the
energetic barriers and rates associated with chemical degradation kinetics. The results from these
simulations yield quantitative predictions of transport properties and chemical kinetic behavior
under various stressors for a variety of encapsulant chemical compositions.
We conducted experimental aging studies to identify degradation products during encapsulant
degradation and compared these findings to our computational results. While providing valu-
able insight into the properties of various material chemistry, the broad goal was to demonstrate a
coupled experimental and computational approach for parameterizing a future reaction-diffusion
model.

1.4. Project Milestones

We defined two primary milestones to guide our combined computational–experimental study of
EVA encapsulant degradation. The first milestone was to employ atomistic MD simulations to
compute diffusion coefficients for dissolved water, oxygen, and acetic acid in EVA with standard

 17

conditions of 28 – 33 wt.% vinyl acetate content, at least 70 % cross-linked, at ambient temperature
and pressure, and to achieve agreement with published values to within one order of magnitude.
By meeting this target, we will demonstrate that our simulation framework reliably captures the
mobility of key species in EVA, a critical prerequisite for constructing a robust diffusion–reaction
model.
The second milestone was to establish a quantitative link between our simulated reaction kinetics
and experimental aging data. In accelerated aging tests, we tracked the formation of reaction prod-
ucts in EVA and directly compare to those predicted by our molecular-scale models. We aimed to
validate the computational model’s ability to predict chemical kinetic behavior under varied mate-
rial conditions and environmental stressors. Successful completion of this milestone can illustrate
how material heterogeneity and molecular mobility jointly impact degradation processes.
These milestones were chosen because diffusion coefficients and reaction rates are the two fun-
damental inputs to any diffusion–reaction framework for predicting long-term encapsulant perfor-
mance. Although the literature provides scattered diffusion data for EVA, there is a notable gap in
understanding how diffusion varies with cross-link density, vinyl-acetate content, temperature, and
in the presence of multiple species. By filling that gap and validating our computed values against
both literature benchmarks and new experiments, we ensure that our model parameters are both ac-
curate and representative of real-world materials. Likewise, achieving a tight correlation between
simulated kinetics and experimental aging results will confirm the fidelity and applicability of our
modeling approach.

2. EXPERIMENTAL FILM CHARACTERIZATION AND
 ACCELERATED AGING

In this work, different methods of spectroscopy and mass spectrometry were utilized to experi-
mentally determine the composition and degree of polymer degradation of two different brands of
EVA films. First, thermal desorption gas chromatography mass spectrometry (TD-GC-MS) and
laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry (LDI-MS) were used to chemically characterize the
EVA films, one which was a designated as a fast cure and the other an ultra-fast cure film by the
manufacturer. Then, the films underwent accelerated aged via a climate chamber with temperature
and relative humidity (RH) control. These films were tested with desorptive electrospray ionization
mass spectrometry (DESI-MS) as well as infrared (IR) and Raman spectroscopy.

2.1. Methods

2.1.1. EVA film properties

Two samples of each type of EVA films were purchased. Films 1a and 1b were product name
“AKC-1F/FC” from Akcome Optronic Science & Technology, and films 2a and 2b were product
name “Z1261 Extra Fast Cure” from Zhejiang Zhengxin Photovoltaic Technology. These films
will be referred to by their alphanumeric label in the rest of this report. Films 1a and 1b were
opaque with a smooth, non-adhesive surface texture, whereas films 2a and 2b were transparent and
had a bubbled, slightly sticky surface texture. The manufacture reported properties of these films
are listed in Table 2-1.

2.1.2. EVA film composition analysis techniques

Thermal Desorption Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (TD-GC-MS)

Samples were tested using a Perkin Elmer (Groton, CT) ATD 650 TurboMatrix thermal desorption
unit. Solid samples of 20 g of each film were added to a pre-conditioned thermal desorption tube
(Supleco 28174-U or Perkin Elmer M0413598, Glass Thermal Desorption Tubes, ¼” (6.35 mm)
O.D. x 3.5” (89 mm) long) and desorbed at 120 °C for 10 minutes. The outgassed species are
trapped on a M041-3628 Air Monitoring Trap cooled to -30 °C, desorbed, and then transferred
via heated line to an Agilent 6890N using a J&W DB-1701 column (60m x0.32mm x 1.0um).
Analytes were detected with an Agilent 5975 Inert XL Mass Selection device (Chemstation version
D.03.00.611). The mass spectra were analyzed for potential chemical additives in the films.

Laser Desorption Ionization Mass Spectrometry (LDI-MS)

In laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry (LDI-MS), a laser is used to desorb the sample
and ionize it before it enters the mass spectrometer. In this work, the Synapt HDMS XS (Waters
Corporation, MA, USA) quadrupole time-of-flight (QToF) mass spectrometer was used with the
MALDI ionization source. The laser in the MALDI ionization source for LDI was a frequency-
tripled Nd:YAG laser (355 nm, pulsed at ≥100 µJ/pulse) with a pulse frequency of 2 Hz during
MS data collection. Data acquisition was performed in MassLynx Version 4.2 (Waters Corporation,
MA, USA). The films were analyzed in negative ion mode and Waters resolution mode from 50
m/z to 1200 m/z with a scan rate of 0.5 s per scan. The laser was either manually targeted at a
sample, or it was set to scan over an entire area using the included imaging software.

2.1.3. EVA film aging techniques

Samples of films 1a and 2a were cut and treated one of three ways: (i) sitting out in room temper-
ature, (ii) soaking in water, or (iii) soaking in water with a portion of the film covered by a clamp.
These samples were tested for composition changes using DESI-MS. To determine the extent of
aging in EVA films over time, samples were aged under high temperature (60ºC) and relative hu-
midity (80%) in a climate chamber. Using a hollow punch tool, films 1a and 2a were cut producing
12 circular samples (1 cm diameter), which were sandwiched in-between glass slides and placed
into an ICH-110L climate chamber (Memmert USA LLC, WI, USA). The relative humidity and
temperature of the chamber were calibrated using a LogTag Temperature and Humidity Data Log-
ger (MicroDAQ LLC, NH, USA). Over the course of seven weeks, the temperature and relative
humidity were held constant at 60 °C and 80% RH, respectively, for the chamber experiment.
Samples were pulled from the chamber every four days and placed into a slide holder to keep for
IR and Raman analysis.

 21

Desorption Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (DESI-MS)

In desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (DESI-MS), an electrospray source is
targeted at a surface, where a small amount of sample is desorbed into the electrospray where it is
ionized. In this work, the Synapt HDMS XS (Waters Corporation, MA, USA) quadrupole time-of-
flight (QToF) mass spectrometer was used with the DESI XS High-Performance ionization source.
The EVA films were attached to a glass slide and loaded onto a two-dimensional stage for analysis.
The settings for the mass spectrometer were as follows: negative ionization mode, capillary voltage
0.36 V, cone voltage 47 V, source temperature 150 °C, gas pressure 0.005 MPa, heated transfer line
350 °C. The electrospray solvent was 98:2 methanol/water with 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of
10 µL min-1 . The spectra were collected in resolution mode from 50 m/z to 1200 m/z with a scan
rate of 0.5 s per scan.

Infrared and Raman spectroscopy

Samples of 1a and 2a were tested from the first and last sample set of the chamber aging exper-
iment. The upper glass slide was removed prior to analysis and the remaining slide was placed
directly under the Bruker Hyperion IR microscope and the Thermo DXR3 Raman microscope for
analysis. The circular films were sampled across the diameter of the films at approximately 500 µm
increments. The Raman spectrometer settings were as follows: DXR3 532 nm sampling method,
100 mW power, 1 s exposure time, 32 coadded scans, and 10x objective. The IR spectrometer
settings were as follows: ATR/reflection sampling method, 4 cm-1 resolution, Ge background, 32
(ATR) coadded scans.

2.2. Discussion

2.2.1. EVA film characterization

Due to the challenges associated with acquiring EVA film, initial efforts focused on chemically
characterizing the different films. TD-GCMS analysis was employed to identify the types of chem-
ical additives present in all four EVA films. Figure 2-1 presents the TD-GCMS chromatogram for
films 1a and 1b, which are nearly identical, showing only slight differences in the intensity of each
peak. Similarly, Figure 2-2 displays the TD-GCMS chromatogram for films 2a and 2b, which are
also nearly identical; however, film 2b exhibits higher intensities for each compound.

When comparing the two sets of films, significant differences emerge. Films 1a and 1b contain
considerably fewer additives than films 2a and 2b, and the intensities of the additives are much
lower. The additives in films 1a and 1b are primarily 10- or 8-carbon chain hydrocarbons, along
with some fluorinated molecules. In contrast, films 2a and 2b contain a substantial number of 6-
carbon chain hydrocarbons, as well as some siloxanes. It was concluded that films 1a and 1b are
effectively interchangeable, as are films 2a and 2b; however, testing should continue to treat the
two sets separately.

2.2.2. EVA film accelerated aging

To determine a “furthest extent” of aging, samples of each film were either (i) kept dry (plain), (ii)
submerged in water, or (iii) submerged in water with a portion of the film covered. These samples
were left overnight, and their composition mapped using DESI-MS. This measurement determined
three major peaks that were associated with the polymer backbone of the film; m/z 311, m/z 325,
and m/z 339. The m/z 311 signal was the largest of the peaks and matches the chemical formula
C18 H31 O4 - which can be broken down to [(C2 H4 )5 (C4 H6 O2 )2 – H]- , five units of ethylene and two
units of vinyl acetate. This also matches the vinyl acetate percentage of the polymer. The intensity
of this peak is shown in the heat map in Figure 2-4. In the polymer left out at room temperature,
the intensity is relatively high, whereas this intensity drops in the sample submerged in water. In
the sample that was partially covered, there is a clear separation between the portion submerged in
water and the portion protected from water. This indicates that the peak m/z 311 may be a good
indicator of polymer degradation in these films.

(iii) submerged in water with a portion of film covered (H2 O clamp), with the color scale indicating

IR and Raman spectroscopy were also performed on samples 1a and 2a which had been aged in
a climate chamber over the course of seven weeks to determine if any degradation products, such

occurring the 1238 (C-O) and 1738 (C=O) cm-1 bands but is a very slight change. It is possible but
unlikely that this is exclusively the result of deacetylation.

2.3. Interpretation and Conclusion

Measurements of the film composition using TD-GC-MS found that the two brands had signifi-
cantly different additive compositions, with 1a and 1b additives composed of primarily 8- or 10-
carbon chain molecules as well as some perfluorinated substances. The 2a and 2b additives were
primarily composed of 6-carbon chain hydrocarbons and siloxanes. LDI-MS analysis of each set
of films found that both polymer backbones had roughly 31% vinyl acetate, which was within
bounds reported by each manufacturer. These combined analyses indicated that the samples from
each individual company could be treated as identical, but that each set should be tested separately
when aging. After accelerated aging in water, DESI-MS indicated a loss of [(C2 H4 )5 (C4 H6 O2 )2 –
H]- , which may be due to β -scission degradation from the excessive presence of water. After aging
in the climate chamber for seven weeks, IR and Raman spectroscopy did not indicate significant

 28

degradation in the film which was exposed to the chamber. These results indicate that moving
forward, DESI-MS may be a useful tool to measure the degradation of EVA films, whereas IR
and Raman may not be sensitive enough to the slight chemical changes produced from this ageing
methodology.

3. COMPUTATIONAL SIMULATIONS

3.1. Materials Models

In this work, MD simulations were used to investigate the diffusion properties of small molecules
contaminants and degradation kinetics. All simulations were run using the LAMMPS molecular
dynamics code [17]. The all atom Optimized Potential for Liquid Simulation (OPLS-AA) force
field [18, 19] and the Reactive Force Field (ReaxFF) [20] were used for classical and reactive MD,
respectively.

3.1.1. Ethylene-co-vinyl acetate models

Models were created to represent individual homogeneous nano-states, with chemical variation
introduced between models to capture the true structural heterogeneity of EVA-based encapsulants
that may contribute to degradation. Specifically, the weight percent of vinyl acetate co-monomer
(%VA), the molecular weight (MW) (i.e., the degree of polymerization in monomers per chain),
and percent cross-link (%x-link) were tuned creating a sample space of 120 models. A description
of the individual models is provided in Appendix A.1.
Initial configurations of amorphous models were generated using the Enhanced Monte Carlo
(EMC) software package version 9.4.3 [21]. In EMC, polymer chains are constructed by sim-
ulating experimental polymerization through a Monte Carlo sampling method. This approach
randomly places and bonds monomers one-by-one, effectively mimicking the dynamics of poly-
merization. By utilizing energy-based acceptance criteria that explore the conformational space,
monomers are added to chains until a target molecular weight is achieved, resulting in realistic
polymer structures.
Each polymer chain was comprised of three types of monomers; ethylene monomer, vinyl acetate
monomer, and vinyl acetate monomer with cross-link chemistry. All chains were terminated with
methyl groups. The addition of cross-link chemistry was included to permit manual cross-linking
via an in-house code, avoiding the arduous process of dynamic cross-linking. Polymer chains
were constructed with MW of 100, 200, 400, or 800 monomers per chain, and with ethylene to
total vinyl acetate monomer ratios of 20:80, 25:75, 30:70, 35:65, and 40:60. The percentage of
vinyl acetate monomers with cross-link chemistry varied from 45% to 95% (increments of 10%)
of the total vinyl acetate monomers per chain.
To achieve approximately 250,000 atoms per configuration, the total number of chains per model
were held variable, ranging from 17 chains for 800 MW and 146 chains for 100 MW. All con-
figurations were generated at a density of 0.95 g/cm3 , with periodic boundary conditions and ap-

 30

proximate cell dimensions of 12 × 12 × 12 nm3 . An example EMC building script is available in
Appendix A.2.

Cross-linking EVA models

The curing or cross-linking of EVA-based encapsulants is crucial for achieving a structure-property
relationship that enables encapsulants to withstand the operating environments of solar modules.
Poorly cross-linked encapsulants may exhibit cold flow and reduced mechanical strength in the
field, which can compromise performance and longevity [22]. Experimentally, cross-linking oc-
curs when curing agents are thermally consumed forming covalent bonds between polymer chains
[23]. This produces a three-dimensional network that can be quantified by measuring gel content,
which is defined as the fraction of insoluble polymer remaining after dissolving a cross-linked poly-
mer in a specific solvent. Thus, gel content informs the degree of cross-linking but like methods
that account for unconsumed curing agents, the number of formed cross links and their distribution
is not explicitly known.
In this work, the development of a 3-D network structure is achieved through the formation of
carbon-carbon linkages or cross-links between pendant acetyl groups of vinyl acetate monomers,
in accordance with the most probable cross-linking chemistry identified in the work by McLough-
lin et al. [24]. This is accomplished using an in-house code previously developed for the cross-
linking of EPDM rubber [25], which was adapted to identify VA monomers with cross-link chem-
istry and inserts the additional vinyl acetate group into the backbone of a nearby chain, with the
stipulation that cross-linked monomers cannot be adjacent to one another. We report the fraction
of VA monomers that are cross-linked per chain as percent cross-link. Figure 3-1 illustrates a
cross-linked configuration, and Appendix A.1 provides details on the inter- and intra-molecular
cross-links formed in each model.

3.1.2. Equilibration protocol and model validation

Cross-linked EVA models were equilibrated using a 21-stage temperature-pressure equilibration
method developed by Abbott et al. [26]. This method involves systematically varying tempera-
ture and pressure to achieve fully relaxed, energy minimized configurations, as shown by Figure
3-2a. Equilibration was performed for temperatures of 100 to 600 K, with increments of 25 K,
resulting in a total of 21 temperature equilibrated state per model, simultaneously capturing the
density-temperature relationship of each system. Figure 3-2b provides an example of the density-
temperature relationship of two models with differing %VA. Using this plot, glass transition tem-
perature (Tg ) is determined as the temperature at the point of transition between low and high
density regimes, which is identified through a segmentation and regression technique [27]. Glass
transition temperatures, shown in Figure 3-2c, were used as validation to experimental EVA en-
capsulants, which are reported as having a Tg range of 233 - 293 K [28]. Notably, computational
methods often report higher Tg values when compared to experimental Tg values obtained through
techniques such as differential scanning calorimetry or dynamic mechanical analysis. This dis-
crepancy is largely due to the time- and length-scale dependencies inherent in the two approaches.

The mean squared displacement (MSD) was computed for contaminant molecules in an NVT
ensemble with center-of-mass correction performed for each atom. The resulting MSD was used
to compute diffusion coefficients (D) via the Einstein relation,

where ⟨∆r2 (t)⟩ is MSD at time t, when the system has approached steady state. Figure 3-4 illus-
trates the MSD over simulation time of water, acetic acid, and oxygen in model E0. Three distinct
regimes are shown, ballistic, sub-diffusive, and diffusive. In the ballistic regime, which occurs at
very short timescales, particles move at a constant velocity without collision leading to a quadratic
dependence of MSD on time, ⟨∆r2 (t)⟩ ∝ ∆t 2 . As time progresses, the system transitions into the
sub-diffusive regime. Here, movement is hindered resulting in MSD scaling sub-linearly with time,
⟨∆r2 (t)⟩ ∝ ∆t a , where a is between 0 and 1. At long timescales, diffusive regime is characterized
by a linear increase in MSD with time, ⟨∆r2 (t)⟩ ∝ ∆t 1 , indicating that the particles are undergo-
ing random, uncorrelated motion. The diffuse regime (slope = 1.00 ± 0.25) is observed for most
systems after 20 ns, with the exception of temperatures where contaminant species do not exist as
a true liquid or gas at atmospheric pressure, i.e., for water below 275 K and for acetic acid below
300 K. All plots of MSD as a function of time for all E-series contaminant systems are given in

Diffusion coefficients were computed using data from the last 5 ns of simulation time. Table 3-2
reports diffusion of water (mix 1) as a function of %VA (20%, 30%, and 40%), %x-link (55%,
85%, and 100%) and MW (800 and 100). When the relationship between MSD and time did not
scale linearly (i.e., exhibit diffuse behavior), the reported diffusion coefficients are grayed out.
Otherwise, diffusion coefficients are reported with order of magnitude coloring.
Consider the 275-375 K temperature range where water is a liquid in Table 3-2, there appears to
be no order of magnitude changes in diffusion coefficients as an effect of structural variables of
EVA. However, moderate trends are detected between levels of %x-link and %VA. Additionally,
the values reported in Table 3-2 are on the same order of magnitude as experimentally determined
diffusion coefficients for the same temperature range via gravimetric (3.43 − 9.03E-07 cm2 /s) [40]
and permeation (7.70E-07 − 2.00E-06 cm2 /s) [41, 42] techniques.
For acetic acid shown in Table 3-3, MW has a clear impact on diffusion coefficients, where the
decrease in MW results in order of magnitude increase in diffusion properties at all temperatures.
Trends in %x-link also affect the diffusional properties of acetic acid but the effects are most
pronounced when considering the combined effect of low %VA and %x-link. At 55% x-link and
20% VA, acetic acid has a D = 5.84E-07 cm2 /s at 300 K, compared to the value of D = 3.67E-
08 cm2 /s when EVA has 85%x-link and 30%VA. This reflects concerns expressed in the literature
[40, 43, 44] on the reliability of EVA-based encapsulants to prevent the migration of environmental
contaminants (water) into the module and production of by-products (acetic acid) when gel content
is reported to be ’deficient’. Additionally, the diffusion coefficients of acetic acid are in range of
those reported in the literature for diffusion at 300 K [31, 45, 44, 46, 47].
While oxygen was not investigated as an individual contaminant, the results of the mix 3 systems
(shown in Table 3-4) reveal that a decrease in MW and %x-link, increases diffusion coefficients.
Additionally, Wilke et al. found the diffusion of oxygen in water for a temperature range of 298-
333 K to be between 1.97E-05 to 4.82E-05 (cm2 /s) [48], which is an order of magnitude faster than
the diffusion of oxygen in ternary mixture with water and acetic acid, reported in this work.
It has been reported [3] that the increase in relative humidity results in an increase in acetic acid
production. While deacetylation reactions in the presence of dissolved water is reported in the
following section, we consider the diffusion of acetic acid in a mixture with dissolved water and
oxygen to inform diffusion-limited degradation. Table 3-5 reports that the increase in diffusion
coefficients of water in the presence of acetic acid is marginal. However, the diffusion of acetic
acid increases in the presence of water, pointing to a secondary plasticization effect, and is most
prominent in models with weak structural features, i.e., low %x-link and %VA. The propensity
for acetic acid to migrate farther as a function of dissolved water and oxygen is concerning to
the integrity and lifetime of the EVA encapsulant as well as surrounding module components,
specifically those that are vulnerable to acid corrosion.

3.2.1. Interpretation and conclusion

It is important to address the limitations within these reported results. At the lowest temperatures,
the MSD curves deviate from the diffusive trend line of ∆t 1 expected for true diffusion (see Figure
3-4), so any coefficients extracted from these sub-diffusive regimes are not representative of long-
time diffusion. Those low-temperature entries have been grayed out in the tables to indicate that
longer simulations would be required to reach true diffusive behavior and a more accurate calcu-
lation. To mitigate this, additional computational resources are required to run long time (50-100
ns) simulations.

Secondly, our simulations are of fully amorphous, entangled EVA melts, whereas real EVA co-
polymer will develop semi-crystalline regions at lower temperatures. Although transport occurs
almost entirely through the amorphous phase, crystalline domains can act as impermeable barriers,
forcing diffusing species onto more tortuous pathways. As a result, experimentally measured dif-
fusion rates in EVA depend on the degree of crystallinity and may differ from the values reported
here.

Despite these limitations, the extensive analysis presented demonstrates that diffusion in EVA can
vary by orders of magnitude depending on polymer chemistry; degree of cross-linking, and molec-
ular weight. In practice, EVA formulations are never perfectly uniform, and our results demonstrate
how even modest heterogeneity in material state can dramatically alter mass-transport behavior. As
noted by Dadaniya et al., “The wide range of [diffusion] values reported in the literature is possibly
due to the variability in the EVA cross-linking, processing parameters and additives (curing agents,
ultraviolet absorbers, photo antioxidants, and thermo antioxidants)” [40]. Our work clarifies the
structural reasons for the range of diffusion values and directs the selection of input values for
higher length-scale models, accounting for formulation heterogeneity.

3.3. Reaction

Reactive molecular dynamics simulations were performed to investigate the degradation kinetics of
EVA in the presence of the small molecules contaminants studied above, specifically deacetylation
and beta-scission visualized by Figure 3-5. A subset of the 12 E-series models; E0, E2, E3, E6,
and E9, were selected to reduce computational costs associated with RMD, while still allowing for
variation in key parameters such as %x-link, %VA, and MW, as previously detailed in Table 3-1.
Data files of the selected models with contaminant mixtures were modified for compatibility with
the reactive force field (ReaxFF). [49, 50] Simulations of 1 ns of production time were run with a
time step of 0.5 femtoseconds (fs) at temperatures ranging from 250 to 1250 K for each system,
outputting system structure and reaction species for analysis. Notably, elevated temperatures were
used to accelerate the reaction rates so that sufficient reactions would occur within the 1 ns of
simulation time.

3.3.1. Deacetylation

Deacetylation reactions, which involve the cleavage of acetyl groups (-COCH3 ) from the vinyl
acetate co-monomer in the EVA main chain, result in the production of corrosive acetic acid and
its derivatives in EVA-encapsulated modules. The rates of deacetylation reactions (kD ) and the
subsequent activation energy (ED ) were computed by tracing the cleavage of acetyl groups over
simulation time at various temperatures. Specifically, kD represents the change in the proportion
of cleaved acetate groups over time, or the slope of the line in Figure 3-6a.
In the first few picoseconds (ps) of simulation time, there is an immediate spike in the number
of deacetylation reactions for all temperatures studied (see Fig. 3-6b); however, this is an artifact
of RMD. When transitioning from classical MD to RMD, bonds are no longer fixed, allowing
for molecular reorganization or the detachment of acetyl groups. This phenomenon explains the
initial peak and subsequent plateau associated with deacetylation events. For this reason, fits were
performed after 100 ps of simulation time to exclude data attributed to the relaxation of the system
and instead capture only the temperature-dependent deacetylation. At low temperatures (250-450
K), an insignificant number of deacetylation reactions occurred during 1 ns of simulation time,
resulting in several reaction rates being zero. Consequently, the Arrhenius relationship of kD at
high temperatures was used to determine ED by taking the natural logarithm of both sides of the
Arrhenius equation,

3.3.4. Interpretation and conclusion

This study highlights the challenges and limitations that RMD simulations can present, particularly
when employing the ReaxFF. Several simulations failed to reach 1 ns of simulation time, and low-
temperature simulations exhibited few or no reaction events. In general, we observed that longer
simulation times tend to yield more reaction events; however, we were constrained by the increased
computational cost. RMD is computationally intensive due to the explicit modeling of bond break-
ing and formation required for the simulation of chemical reactions. To mitigate this, we used
higher temperatures to speed up reactions, but reached a threshold around 1500-2000 K, where the
simulations became unstable, raising concerns that excessively high temperatures altered reaction
kinetics. Additionally, the accuracy of deacetylation statistics may be compromised due to rela-
tively low number of vinyl acetate (VA) co-monomers, which ranges from 1160 to 2400 per model,
leading to a low incidence of deacetylation events. In contrast, β -scission reactions were sampled
from approximately 32,000 potential backbone bonds per model, resulting in greater number of re-
actions and confidence in the findings. Despite these limitations, the work presented demonstrates
that the chemical kinetics underlying degradation phenomena in EVA, much like diffusion, vary
based on the chemical structure and emphasizes the role of contaminants. In addition to acetic
acid, other degradation by-products were identified, specifically acetic acid derivatives like tartaric
acid, that have yet to be identified in the literature.

4. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

In this work, diffusion coefficients of environmental contaminants, water and oxygen, and degrada-
tion by-product, acetic acid, were computed in varying EVA nano-states from classical molecular
dynamics simulations. The results elucidated the chemical structures of EVA that drive or sup-
press the migration of contaminant species, and revealed a plasticizing effect, that increased acetic
acid transport in the presence of dissolved water and oxygen. Experimental aging studies were
performed to identify degradation phenomena in EVA films to support atomistic modeling seeking
to uncover diffusion and reaction kinetics occurring during EVA-based encapsulant degradation.
Experimental efforts elucidated the material properties of as received EVA films, detailing small
molecule moieties, of which carboxylic acids and siloxanes were revealed. When films were aged
in water, there was a loss of [(C2 H4 )5 (C4 H6 O2 )2 – H]- , which points to the degradation of poly-
mer chains, later confirmed via β -scission reactions by atomistic modeling. However, the aging
conditions (60ºC and %80 RH) used on as-received EVA films did not result in the production of
acetic acid when studied over a 7 week period, necessitating more sensitive characterization tech-
niques and/or increased aging time. Chemical kinetics of degradation mechanism, deacetylation
and chain scission, were investigated using reactive molecular dynamics. Reaction rates and ac-
tivation energies were reported for various EVA nano-states in the presence of dissolved species
further revealing the effect of weak structural features like low %x-link or in experimental terms
poorly cured systems. This work provides a materials-based approach necessary to parameterize
higher length scale models that consider kinetic reactions, diffusion, and evolution of material state;
a potential framework for evaluating the efficacy of current and future encapsulant materials.

APPENDIX B. Bulk Contaminant Systems

Investigating the combined effects of EVA degradation requires an understanding of diffusion prop-
erties of present small molecule contaminants. In this work, water, acetic acid, and oxygen, were
selected as contaminants for study having been identified and discussed heavily in the literature.
[31, 40, 43, 44, 45] Before modeling the diffusion of these contaminants in amorphous EVA, bulk
contaminant models were created to benchmark self-diffusion. For each bulk system, 10,000 atom
models were created by inserting molecules into a simulation cell of 35 x 35 x 35 Å3 with peri-
odic boundary conditions. Molecule insertion was performed via the create_atom command in
LAMMPS, which takes a description of molecule topology; atom, bond, angle, dihedral and im-
proper types. These atoms files were taken from either the LAMMPS documentation, as was the
case for water, or from LigParGen [19] in the case for acetic acid and oxygen. Molecules files
can be found in Appendix B.3. In addition to the bulk models, mixed systems were also studied
to investigate the diffusion of contaminant species in the presence of one another. Specifically,
iterations of the water model described above, were made with the addition of 10% (by weight of
water) of acetic acid or oxygen. A third mixed system which incorporated equal parts of all three
contaminants was also created.
Configurations of each of the six systems (bulk water, bulk acetic acid, bulk oxygen, 10:1 wa-
ter:acetic acid, 10:1 water:oxygen, 1:1:1 water:acetic acid:oxygen) were created for study across
the following temperature range; experimentally relevant temperatures 275-325 K with 5 K incre-
ments, and computationally relevant temperatures 250-500 K with 50 K increments, resulting in
16 simulations per system. Note: temperatures outside the liquid phase (or in the case of oxygen,
gas phase) of each model were not considered in validation. All simulations were performed using
LAMMPS code [17] with the all-atom Optimized Potential for Liquid Simulation (OPLS-AA) in-
teratomic force field developed by Jorgensen et al. [19] Pairwise interactions were computed using
LJ/cut/coul/long pair potentials with long range interactions approximated in k-space by Ewald
summation at a precision of 1.0E-4.
All simulations were initiated with the same starting coordinates and velocity. The SHAKE algo-
rithm was used to keep water molecules rigid; fixing O-H bond length and angle. All simulations
had a time step of 1 fs, dielectric constant was 1.0 and non-bonded interaction energies/forces had
a cutoff length of 10, 10, and 12.0 Å for water, acetic acid and oxygen systems, respectively. After
an initial push off of 10 ps with microcanonical ensemble (NVE), each model was equilibrated at
temperature for 0.5 ns with a isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT). Most systems achieved a den-
sity plateau after 1̃00-200 ps. Equilibrated densities at temperature are given in Appendix B.1.
The mean squared displacement (MSD) of contaminant molecules was computed in canonical
ensemble (NVT) for 10 ns of simulation time with center-of-mass correction for each atom. MSD
is the total squared displacement, summed and averaged over all atoms/molecules, and necessary
for determining diffusion coefficients of dissolved species. Log-log plots of MSD over simulation

 62

time (given in Appendix B.2) were used to visualize diffusion regimes, of which the slope of the
linear diffuse regime is used to compute diffusion coefficients via the Einstein equation.

APPENDIX C. Mean Squared Displacement Curves of All E-series

The mean squared displacement (MSD) was computed over 25 ns of simulation time for contam-
inant molecules in canonical ensemble (NVT) with center-of-mass correction performed for each
atom. The resulting MSD was used to compute diffusion coefficients (D) via the Einstein relation,
D = limt→∞ 6t1 ⟨∆r2 (t)⟩, where ⟨∆r2 (t)⟩ is the mean squared displacement at time t when the sys-
tem has approached steady state. Figures C-1, C-2, C-3, and C-4 show the MSD over simulation
time of acetic acid (aa), water (tip3p), and oxygen (O2 ), for all E-series models (E0-E11), where
water and acetic acid are pure contaminants in mix 1 and mix 2, respectively, and as a mixture
of contaminants in mix 3. As discussed in the Section 3.2, several figures reveal that 25 ns was
insufficient to capture a true diffuse regime for all contaminants at all temperatures.
