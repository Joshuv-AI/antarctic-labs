Corneal nerve regeneration: treatments and monitoring approaches
Authors: Karthik Reddy, Shahzad I. Mian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnostics and therapeutics for corneal nerve pathologies are rapidly evolving, with continual advancements in imaging, laser, machine learning, biologic, and surgical technologies. Recent changes in our understanding of corneal nerve structure and function underscore the need to evaluate diagnostic approaches, including advancements in esthesiometry, in vivo confocal microscopy, and anterior segment optical coherence tomography.MethodsA literature review was conducted on diagnostic and therapeutic methods used for corneal nerve regeneration. We searched Google Scholar and PubMed for relevant articles using key terms such as: ‘corneal nerve regeneration’, ‘corneal nerves’, ‘corneal nerve diagnostics’, ‘neurotrophic keratitis treatment’, ‘corneal nerve imaging’, ‘in vivo confocal microscopy’, and ‘corneal neurotization’. A total of 112 articles were included.DiscussionCorneal nerve treatments require an in-depth understanding of neurotrophic mediators and nerve function, with current key treatments, such as cenegermin, targeting one of the most well-studied pathways. Other emerging treatment areas, including insulin drops, autologous serum, and surgical approaches, are discussed as avenues for further exploration to aid the regeneration of corneal nerves and address the many related diseases resulting from corneal nerve injury or dysfunction.ConclusionThis paper provides an overview of traditional and emerging approaches to the diagnosis and management of corneal nerve pathologies and highlights clinically relevant pathophysiological mechanisms. By leveraging advanced imaging modalities such as in vivo confocal microscopy, clinicians can detect nerve injury before symptom onset, enabling critical early intervention. Furthermore, integrating emerging regenerative therapies shifts the clinical paradigm from palliative symptom management to active restoration of corneal nerve integrity.

Introduction

The cornea is among the most densely innervated anatomical structures in the human body, with long ciliary nerves carrying sensory information to the V1 distribution of the trigeminal nerve [1–3]. These nerves branch within the cornea and reside between the basal epithelium and Bowman’s layer before further branching into superficial layers [1]. This structure makes the cornea the most sensitive tissue in the human body [4].

There are multiple reasons for the cornea’s dense innervation and high sensitivity. The dense innervation serves as a protective measure to allow stimuli for adaptive protection to a wide degree of harm to one of the eye’s most important structures for visual function [5]. The cornea is innervated by a wide degree of nerve fiber types, including mechano-nociceptors, polymodal nociceptors, and cold thermoreceptors [1,5–9]. Each of these receptors is capable of responding, such as with blink or tear reflexes, to a variety of noxious stimuli that may harm ocular surface integrity. Furthermore, the cornea plays a crucial role in supporting the ocular surface, particularly the corneal epithelial and stromal cells, by promoting wound healing, maintaining epithelial integrity, and providing trophic support by generating neurotrophins and neuromediators [8,10–12]. Disruption to corneal innervation through surgical intervention, chemical or physical trauma, or disease can lead to pain, sensitivity, impaired healing, or alterations to physiological tear production or function [13,14].

Treatments and monitoring approaches for damaged or diseased corneal nerves are continually evolving. Current therapeutic advances aim to enhance innervation following ocular surface damage. These therapies include the use of autologous serum drops containing growth-promoting factors, platelet-rich plasma, instillation of neurotrophic factors, ensconcing interventions, anti-inflammatory measures, and surgical interventions such as corneal neurotization [15]. Further, innovative improvements have been made in monitoring and diagnosing injury to the corneal nerves, including various esthesiometry techniques, confocal microscopy for corneal imaging, and the application of machine learning to improve the detection of neuropathy [15–18]. This review aims to summarize and evaluate recent advances and the body of evidence in emerging therapeutic strategies for corneal nerve regeneration, as well as updates to advanced monitoring approaches.

Methods

Articles were included up to 2026. Our review examined only English-language articles, and inclusion was limited to a 15-year lookback period to the greatest extent possible.

We conducted a narrative review of key terms, reviewed by a senior cornea specialist (S.M.), as relevant for corneal nerve diagnostics and treatments. These terms included concepts such as, but not limited to, ‘corneal nerve regeneration’, ‘corneal nerves’, ‘corneal nerve diagnostics’, ‘neurotrophic keratitis treatment’, ‘corneal nerve imaging’, ‘in vivo confocal microscopy’, and ‘corneal neurotization’. Terms were searched on Google Scholar and PubMed, with inclusion subjectively assessed by citation metrics, search relevance, and domain knowledge of the authors as relevant for topic scope.

Authors manually screened abstracts and included papers based on the scope of recent advancements in corneal nerve diagnostics and treatments. The full-text articles of all papers were further examined, summarized, and synthesized for the purposes of this narrative review.

Updates to diagnostics and monitoring approaches

Diagnostics and monitoring: Neurotrophic Keratopathy Study Group (NKSG) findings

Neurotrophic keratopathy (NK), a disorder characterized by impaired epithelial healing, corneal degradation, and perforation, is directly related to underlying nerve dysfunction or damage [19,20]. As such, neurotrophic keratopathy is a hallmark condition for continued study and advancement in diagnostics and treatment. The Neurotrophic Keratopathy Study Group (NSKG) classification was created to define clearer criteria and strategies for classifying, diagnosing, and managing NK [21].

The NKSG characterized six stages of NK, beginning with alterations in corneal nerve sensation without keratopathy. Further steps include progression of NK, with punctate epithelial keratopathy in stage 2, persistent/recurrent epithelial defect in stage 3, stromal haze in stage 4, stromal ulceration in stage 5, and corneal perforation in stage 6. Before the NKSG group’s classification system, the Mackie classification for NK was the predominant methodology, which prioritized epithelial changes that occur largely after an irreparable insult to the nerves. The NSKG classification prioritizes early detection of corneal nerve injury and emphasizes stromal involvement alongside epithelial changes. Given that healthy, functioning nerves are necessary for trophic support of the ocular surface, early nerve salvage through updated disease classifications may improve long-term outcomes by the time a patient reaches Mackie stage 2 or 3, where nerve and tissue damage may be more irreversible. As treatment options rapidly progress, early detection and monitoring are crucial for early intervention and preservation of nerve function.

There are several limitations to using either the Mackie or NKSG criteria, one of which is their reliance on qualitative assessment alone. Quantitative metrics are critical for treatment monitoring. Additionally, overreliance on qualitative methods as endpoints in clinical trials may limit accurate assessment of baseline characteristics and treatment response.

Diagnostics and monitoring: characterizing corneal nerve injury

Esthesiometry

Qualitative methods for esthesiometry have traditionally included the cotton wisp test, which uses a wisp of sterile cotton to elicit a blink reflex; an absent or reduced response indicates nerve anesthesia, but this method may have limited utility in distinguishing the degree of sensation [22]. The current gold standard for esthesiometry is the Cochet-Bonnet esthesiometer, which uses a portable nylon filament with variable pressure and length adjustments to obtain a quantitative measure of corneal sensation [23]. However, several limitations exist with this device. There is a qualitative component to assessment among observers regarding the appropriate bend of the filament to ensure accurate test results. This may lead to a lack of repeatability and reproducibility in the Cochet-Bonnet esthesiometer [24]. The presence of contact stimulation of the filament against the cornea poses a risk of discomfort, potential abrasion, and infection due to the limited availability of manufacturer-specified cleaning products (e.g. alkaline-based cleaners). Lastly, the Cochet-Bonnet esthesiometer predominantly only affects Aδ mechanoreceptors, which elicit acute pain. While approximately 70% of corneal nociceptors are polymodal, there may be variation in the type and timing of receptor loss across disease processes affecting corneal nerves, making multimodal esthesiometry more suitable [5,25].

The newest non-contact esthesiometer, the BRILL esthesiometer, received FDA approval in 2023. The BRILL esthesiometer uses five levels of ambient air pulsation applied to the eye’s surface to measure corneal sensitivity. Merayo-Lloves et al. showed high agreement between the Brill and the gold-standard Cochet-Bonnet esthesiometer [26]. Additionally, the type of esthesiometer limits damage to the corneal epithelium compared with contact methods and reduces patient apprehension [27,28].

Other methods of non-contact esthesiometry include the Swiss liquid jet esthiometer and the Belmonte non-contact esthiometer [29,30]. The Swiss liquid jet uses an isotonic saline solution to trigger corneal sensation, providing the added benefit of temperature modulation, while the Belmonte uses CO2-enriched airflow. The widespread availability and adoption of the Cochet-Bonnet pose benefits for cross-physician communication and the reliability of findings. Furthermore, non-contact esthesiometers tend to be more expensive than contact esthesiometers. A 2025 study by Ruiz-Lozano et al. found a statistically significant difference (p = .001) between the mean corneal sensitivity thresholds measured by non-contact methods (0.052 ± 0.021 mN) and contact-based esthesiometry (0.046 ± 0.005 mN) [31]. This highlights another issue with widespread adoption, given the requirement for repeated testing between providers and the limitations imposed by inter-provider communication, which are influenced by device availability, cost, and ease of use [32].

In vivo confocal microscopy

In vivo confocal microscopy is among the most rapidly developing tools for assessing and quantifying morphological changes in corneal nerves and the microstructure of the ocular surface [32–35]. The two major categories of in vivo confocal microscopy relevant to clinical practice are slit-scanning and laser-scanning confocal microscopy. Slit-scanning confocal microscopy uses bright, white light through a narrow slit to reduce light intensity, thereby shortening scanning time and enabling focus on the corneal nerve plexus at a chosen depth [36]. Similarly, laser-scanning confocal microscopy uses a pinhole aperture in the detection path to block out-of-focus light. It employs a 670 nm laser to acquire thin, optically sectioned images from a defined focal plane, thereby facilitating the three-dimensional reconstruction of complex corneal structures [32,37].

Despite the similar image acquisition mechanisms in both methods, relatively few studies have compared the two approaches. Morphologic findings between these systems are broadly similar; however, specific cellular characteristics, such as endothelial cell density, may vary between devices [38]. Current limitations of in vivo confocal microscopy across methods include the inability to resolve finer nerve endings, which may underlie a disconnect between early-stage clinical symptoms, such as those captured by the updated NK guidelines, and imaging findings [21]. However, the rapid development of analytical computational techniques and the integration of automated machine learning may extend the limitations of image quality obtained from confocal microscopy in assessing corneal innervation and monitoring for pathological changes.

Current methods for quantifying corneal innervation characteristics using in vivo confocal microscopy rely on manual or semi-automated tracing, such as CCMetrics or NeuronJ. Studies using in vivo corneal confocal microscopy have reported intraclass correlation coefficients of approximately 0.50–0.80 for corneal nerve fiber density, 0.70 for nerve fiber length, and 0.44–0.67 for branch density, reflecting moderate-to-good intra- and inter-observer reproducibility of these nerve metrics [39]. One area of rapid development, however, is the use of fully automated models, such as ACCMetrics [40]. These models can receive in vivo confocal microscopy images and automatically segment and identify nervous tissue within the images. Recent studies have shown that adequately trained models have a high ability to detect biomarkers of corneal neuropathic disease (ROC: 0.97) and rapidly generalize new data from other institutions (ROC: 0.90) [41]. Additionally, these models can perform these tasks significantly faster, such as identifying up to 32 images per second in certain studies [42]. These technologies may help address the more error-prone segmentation that can occur in manual or semi-automated methods and reduce the time and effort involved. These technologies may also aid in the earlier identification of corneal diseases involving the corneal nerves, allowing treatment to be initiated at earlier stages of disease that are not yet addressed by the current NKSG guidelines or other applicable algorithms [21,43,44].

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a technology similar to ultrasound pulse-echo imaging that enables high-resolution imaging of ocular structures. OCT offers high spatial resolution down to the micrometer level [45]. Typically, OCT has been used in retinal imaging, enabling the diagnosis of pathologies such as age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular edema, central serous retinopathy, macular hole, epiretinal membrane, retinal vein occlusion, and vitreomacular traction syndrome, among others [46,47].

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) is a novel application of standard OCT technology that aids in imaging the surface structures of the anterior segment of the eye. First described in 1994 as showing promise as a diagnostic tool for non-contact ocular biometry, evaluation of the anterior chamber angle, and the detection and follow-up of intraocular tumors and masses [48]. However, the accuracy of early OCT technologies for imaging corneal nerve pathologies was not clearly established due to concerns about image fidelity and resolution, which range from 10 to 20 micrometers [49,50].

Micro-optical coherence tomography is an emerging technology that uses advanced OCT to better visualize the microstructure of the ocular surface at a resolution of 1–2 micrometers [50]. Recent work by Schenk et al. demonstrated that this technology may be an emerging, non-contact solution for better visualization of nervous system structures in the subbasal plexus of the cornea. Findings demonstrated a high degree of repeatability (88%) and greater conclusiveness than traditional confocal microscopy in the corneal tissue of non-human primates [51]. Although the use of anterior segment OCT for evaluating corneal nerve pathology is an emerging area of interest, most research and clinical applications to date have focused on other anterior segment structures and functions, such as corneal tomography, assessment of the anterior chamber angle, biometry, and intraocular lens power calculations [52]. This technology holds promise for future studies aimed at earlier diagnosis of corneal nerve damage, which often precedes clinical symptoms [53,54]. Although current AS-OCT devices cannot directly image corneal nerves, they remain highly valuable for diagnosing pathologies associated with nerve dysfunction, including dry eye disease, neurotrophic keratitis, and other related conditions [55,56].

Prior comparative and review studies have shown that image acquisition modality may influence sub-basal nerve visualization and quantitative estimates, supporting the need to carefully consider selection [57]. This is especially critical when corneal nerve quantification is a prespecified trial endpoint. White-light slit-scanning confocal microscopy may differ from laser-scanning in vivo confocal microscopy in contrast, brightness, or qualitative characteristics [38,58]. Differences in assessment choice may affect morphometric endpoints such as nerve fiber density, length, tortuosity, and branching, thereby limiting comparability. Updates to corneal nerve regeneration therapeutics

Therapeutics: review of corneal nerve physiology and trophism

It is well established that the loss of trophic support after corneal nerve injury impairs epithelial healing, cellular renewal, and corneal integrity [12,59,60]. Furthermore, impaired corneal function can lead to decreased tear production or affect blinking, leaving the ocular surface vulnerable to desiccation, mechanical stress, inflammation, and further damage [1,6,12,61].

Corneal nerve regeneration follows several broad stages, including: axon retraction, initiation of regeneration, nerve branching, and maturation/stabilization [60]. At each stage, there are a variety of signaling pathways, growth factors, and neurotrophic factors that are utilized to facilitate the regeneration of damaged corneal nerves.

Among these, the most promising pathway is nerve growth factor (NGF), which plays a critical role in the regeneration of corneal nerves after injury. It is synthesized by corneal epithelial and stromal cells and readily undergoes retrograde transport through corneal nerves to its binding site [62,63]. It binds to the TrkA receptor, which is expressed throughout the stroma and endothelium [63]. TrkA activates several pathways, including MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and PLCγ [12,64–66]. These pathways, among others, mediate the effects of NGF on corneal epithelial proliferation, nerve regeneration, and wound healing [67]. The MAPK/ERK pathway is particularly involved in promoting epithelial cell proliferation, whereas the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway supports anti-apoptotic and migratory functions [68]. The Wnt/β-Catenin pathway has been implicated in mouse studies of corneal nerve repair when insulin is used [70]. Several other neurotrophic factors have been discussed in prior studies as mechanisms for corneal nerve repair, including glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor, neurotrophin-3, neurotrophin-4, insulin-like growth factor 1, and vascular endothelial growth factor [59,71,72]. These pathways for corneal nerve regeneration, among others, have been targeted by non-invasive treatment approaches to aid the recovery of corneal nerves. Direct surgical transplantation of nerve tissue, known as neurotization, is another emerging treatment option and is described below.

Therapeutics: cenegermin

Cenegermin was the first FDA-approved treatment to restore corneal nerve function and integrity by promoting the survival of sensory nerves [73,74]. Cenegermin is a recombinant human nerve growth factor that acts via the previously described NGF-TrkA pathway [75]. Ocular doses of Cenegermin are relatively selective, distributing primarily to the anterior segment, with limited absorption through the conjunctiva and periorbital tissues, and minimal systemic absorption [76].

In the most recent Phase 4, open-label clinical trial, 37 patients at the earliest stage of NK (Mackie 1) received cenegermin 20 mcg/mL, administered six times daily, for a total duration of eight weeks. Epithelial healing was observed in 84.8% (95% CI: 68.1–94.9, p < 0.001). While 91.2% (95% CI 76.3–98.1%; p < 0.001) had improved corneal sensitivity [77]. The seminal trial on cenegermin’s use in human subjects (randomized, double-masked) demonstrated that 48 patients who received cenegermin achieved significantly higher healing rates than vehicle at 8 weeks, 69.6% vs 29.2% by conventional criteria (p = 0.006) and 65.2% vs 16.7% by conservative criteria (p < 0.001). Cenegermin also reduced lesion size and disease progression, with adverse effects that were mostly mild, local, and transient [78]. Phase I and Phase II trials, as well as other studies, have demonstrated a high degree of safety and efficacy in both healthy patients and patients with NK [79–82].

Importantly, studies have shown improvements beyond epithelial measures, with in vivo confocal microscopy and esthesiometry demonstrating that 8 weeks of cenegermin led to increased subbasal nerve regrowth and improved corneal sensitivity (p < 0.01) [83,84]. Future directions for cenegermin have been explored to further quantify its effects on nerve growth, dry eye disease, glaucoma, limbal stem cell deficiency, and pediatric NK disease [85–89].

Therapeutics: insulin drops

Insulin can have several effects on the ocular surface, including enhancing the expression of neurotrophic factors that may promote the regeneration of corneal nerves [90]. Compared to cenegermin, however, insulin instillation remains an active area of research, with no large, real-world clinical trials examining nerve growth. Nevertheless, several trials have demonstrated success in treating epithelial defects and debridement in diabetic patients [91,92]. Much of the current work on insulin use in neurotrophic corneal defects has been limited to successful, albeit case-series-based, reports of complete corneal reepithelialization [93,94].

Nerve regrowth following insulin instillation is thought to activate the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway, although the mechanism remains unclear [70]. Topical insulin has gained some popularity for the treatment of NK; however, it is not yet regulated, and human evidence remains limited.

Therapeutics: autologous serum

Autologous serum is believed to contain several neurotrophic factors, including substance P, insulin-like growth factor, and NGF, which may facilitate the regeneration of corneal nerves when applied [95]. In vivo confocal microscopy assessment of eyes treated with NK and autologous serum demonstrated significantly increased numbers, lengths, widths, and densities of subepithelial nerves, as well as significantly increased sensitivity, as measured by esthesiometry [96]. In a retrospective case series of neuropathic corneal pain, AST use over 3.8 months improved pain scores (from 9.1 to 3.1) and increased subbasal nerve length and number on in vivo confocal microscopy (from 10,935 µm/mm2 to 17,351 µm/mm2; p < 0.005) [97] Furthermore, autologous serum is well-tolerated with no apparent major systemic safety issues, given its local use and autologous origin. However, the quality of the available trials remains limited [98,99]. Additionally, concerns persist regarding cost, availability, and the risk of infection.

Therapeutics: corneal neurotization

Corneal neurotization is a surgical procedure where a healthy sensory nerve is transferred, either directly or through a nerve graft, to the cornea to restore sensory innervation in an otherwise insensate eye [100]. Over the months postoperatively, this reinnervation helps restore corneal sensation, promotes epithelial healing, and improves ocular surface stability in patients with diseases such as neurotrophic keratopathy [101]. This procedure encompasses various techniques and approaches, broadly classified into direct, indirect, and minimally invasive corneal neurotization methods. Direct corneal neurotization involves harvesting donor nerves (often the supratrochlear or supraorbital nerves) and directly transferring them to the corneal limbus without an interposing graft, typically via a more extensive surgical approach [102]. Indirect corneal neurotization uses a nerve graft (e.g. an autologous sural nerve or a processed allograft) as a bridge between the donor nerve and the cornea, thereby avoiding direct dissection across tissues and enabling more flexible routing. Minimally invasive neurotization is a refinement of the indirect approach, utilizing smaller incisions, minimal dissection, and less anterior exposure to minimize complications [101].

These approaches have been shown to be highly effective in increasing corneal sensitivity after treatment, with no significant difference in treatment response between direct and indirect approaches [103]. A recent pooled analysis of 14 studies involving 191 patients revealed that, while both approaches demonstrated significant improvements in sensitivity, indirect corneal neurotization showed a more substantial improvement in central corneal sensation (p < 0.01) [104]. Given the relatively recent advent of the minimally invasive approach, no studies exist with a direct comparison [105]. One retrospective study of minimally invasive corneal neurotization at a rural academic center (13 patients, with a mean follow-up of 18 months) by Dahshan et al. [105] found that all eyes maintained corneal epithelial stability, 8 out of 10 eyes showed improvement in corneal sensation, 9 out of 10 showed improvement in visual acuity, and the visual gains were statistically significant (p = 0.049 at 1 year), which is grossly similar to the outcomes demonstrated in other approaches [103,104]. Overall, surgical neurotization is an innovative and well-studied approach that aims to restore anatomical corneal innervation, providing long-term sensory and trophic support. Topical therapies may also be used in these cases as adjuncts by providing growth factors to promote epithelial healing and potentially further enhance nerve regeneration. However, as with some topical approaches, limitations remain regarding the availability of high-quality clinical trial evidence, as evidenced by the recent termination of a trial (NCT05809245) due to poor recruitment.

Therapeutics: associations to systemic disease

Many systemic diseases also have a significant interplay on the physiology of corneal innervation and may mediate or limit the effects of treatment intended to improve corneal nerve function and structure. For example, diabetes is associated with loss of the sub-basal nerve plexus, reduced corneal sensitivity, epithelial fragility, and impaired wound healing, making corneal nerve changes both a structural and functional manifestation of systemic metabolic disease [110]. Restoration of normoglycemia after pancreas or simultaneous pancreas and kidney transplantation has been associated with early corneal nerve regeneration, often preceding improvement in other neuropathy measures, suggesting that corneal nerves may serve as an early surrogate of therapeutic response [111]. In addition, corneal nerve alterations have been reported in a range of systemic disorders, including amyloidosis, Fabry disease, autoimmune connective tissue disease, and HIV [112]. Corneal nerve loss may be a broader marker of systemic small-fiber and neuroimmune injury. Further, systemic disease processes may play mediating or confounding roles during the diagnosis and management of corneal nerve diseases and should remain accounted for.

Conclusion

A rapidly evolving landscape for corneal nerve monitoring and treatment is emerging. The diagnostic focus should be on the earlier detection of corneal nerve damage, and machine learning may play a key role in identifying the pathology. While the existing evidence for treatment is extensive, emphasis should be placed on high-quality clinical trials to improve the clinical utility and accessibility of these approaches, as recently demonstrated with cenegermin. Currently, limited evidence exists for quantified comparisons of changes in nerve fiber density or sensory recovery rates within and between pharmacological and surgical approaches, limiting definitive conclusions about relative effectiveness. Future studies examining these comparisons should be given higher priority. Given the complex pathways and diverse neurotrophic mediators, future studies could benefit from combining topical therapies with surgical approaches to achieve a synergistic effect.
