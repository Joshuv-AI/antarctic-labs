Fair Benchmarking of Emerging One-Step Generative Models Against Multistep Diffusion and Flow Models
Advaith Ravishankar ⋆, Serena Liu ⋆, Mingyang Wang ⋆, Todd Zhou, Jeffrey Zhou, Arnav Sharma, Ziling Hu, Léopold Das, Abdulaziz Sobirov, Faizaan Siddique, Freddy Yu, Seungjoo Baek, Yan Luo †, Mengyu Wang †

Abstract
State-of-the-art text-to-image models produce high-quality images, but inference remains expensive as generation requires several sequential ODE or denoising steps. Native one-step models aim to reduce this cost by mapping noise to an image in a single step, yet fair comparisons to multi-step systems are difficult because studies use mismatched sampling steps and different classifier-free guidance (CFG) settings, where CFG can shift FID, Inception Score, and CLIP-based alignment in opposing directions. It is also unclear how well one-step models scale to multi-step inference, and there is limited standardized out-of-distribution evaluation for label-ID-conditioned generators beyond ImageNet. To address this, We benchmark eight models spanning one-step flows (MeanFlow, Improved MeanFlow, SoFlow), multi-step baselines (RAE, Scale-RAE), and established systems (SiT, Stable Diffusion 3.5, FLUX.1) under a controlled class-conditional protocol on ImageNet validation, ImageNetV2, and reLAIONet, our new proofread out-of-distribution dataset aligned to ImageNet label IDs. Using FID, Inception Score, CLIP Score, and Pick Score, we show that FID-focused model development and CFG selection can be misleading in few-step regimes, where guidance changes can improve FID while degrading text-image alignment and human preference signals and worsening perceived quality. We further show that leading one-step models benefit from step scaling and become substantially more competitive under multi-step inference, although they still exhibit characteristic local distortions. To capture these tradeoffs, we introduce MinMax Harmonic Mean (MMHM), a composite proxy over all four metrics that stabilizes hyperparameter selection across guidance and step sweeps. The code for reproducing the benchmarks is made publicly available at github.com/Harvard-AI-and-Robotics-Lab/FairBenchmarkingFlow .



1
Introduction

Recent progress in image generation has been driven by diffusion and flow-based models, producing highly realistic samples but requiring several sequential denoising or ODE steps at inference. This multi-step sampling makes state-of-the-art systems expensive to deploy at scale, since generating a single image can require dozens of forward passes through billion-parameter backbones. Motivated by this compute bottleneck, a new line of work targets native one-step generation, where a single model evaluation maps noise directly to an image. While early efforts relied on distillation, several methods now report strong one-step results without a teacher, including MeanFlow
[
6
]
, Improved MeanFlow (iMF)
[
7
]
, and SoFlow
[
15
]
. Despite this rapid progress, comparing one-step models to established multi-step systems remains nontrivial. First, models are often reported under heterogeneous inference settings: one-step flow models are typically trained and evaluated under ImageNet label-ID conditioning, while state-of-the-art systems such as Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX.1 are trained for text conditioning. Second, reported results depend on inference heuristics and hyperparameters, especially classifier-free guidance (CFG), which can substantially alter both quantitative metrics and qualitative appearance. Third, the field often centers evaluation around Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), which measures sample quality in the InceptionNetv3 feature space but does not directly capture text-image alignment or human preference. Together, these factors make it unclear how competitive one-step models are under matched guidance and step settings, and whether optimizing FID alone yields the best visual quality.

In this work, we present a controlled benchmark of emerging one-step flow models against established diffusion and flow-based systems under consistent inference protocols. We evaluate eight approaches spanning native one-step models (MeanFlow, iMF, SoFlow), established and emerging flow baselines (Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT)
[
16
]
, Representation Autoencoder (RAE)
[
25
]
, Scale-RAE
[
24
]
), and state-of-the-art text-to-image systems (Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large
[
5
]
, FLUX.1
[
1
]
). For each method, we compare matched guidance (CFG
=7
where applicable) and each model’s reported native guidance setting. We also evaluate both one-step and multi-step inference (1 vs. 25 steps) to measure how one-step models behave when iterated and how multi-step systems change under reduced step budgets. To test generalization beyond the ImageNet family while preserving label-ID conditioning, we introduce
reLAIONet
, a new proofread evaluation dataset that maps web-sourced images to ImageNet classes and is manually filtered to remove mislabeled and ambiguous samples. reLAIONet provides a larger, more diverse out-of-distribution test set than ImageNetV2 while retaining the class-conditional interface required by ImageNet-trained generators.

Finally, we study how metric choice shapes conclusions about generative quality. First, we find that minimizing FID alone can systematically trade off against Inception Score, CLIP Score, and Pick Score, while selecting configurations that balance all four metrics via MinMax Harmonic Mean (MMHM) yields more consistent performance and better qualitative samples. This pattern holds across all datasets, suggesting it is inherent to single-metric tuning rather than dataset-specific. Second, using MMHM-based ranking rather than best-FID settings, the strongest native one-step models narrow the gap to state-of-the-art baselines (SD3.5 Large and FLUX.1) under a multi-step class-conditional protocol quantitatively, indicating one-step formulations can scale under controlled inference settings. However, one-step models still lag in qualitative fidelity as they exhibit noticeable distortions with generated images. An important next step is testing whether these gains translate to text-conditioned generation at scale, since our study focuses on class conditioning. Third, MMHM is not a definitive target objective; rather, it highlights that multi-metric selection yields more reliable conclusions and outputs than any single metric. Designing a principled, optimization-ready objective that better tracks human-perceived quality remains an open direction for future work.

2
Related Work

2.1
Diffusion and Flow Models

Since 2020, diffusion-based approaches have dominated visual generation. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) initiated this trend by learning to reverse a fixed noising process through several sequential stochastic denoising steps
[
9
]
. Since DDPMs, diffusion models have been improved in two major ways. First, latent diffusion moved the diffusion process into a compressed autoencoder latent space, enabling higher-resolution generation with reduced compute and flexible cross-attention conditioning
[
19
]
. Second, denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM) replaced the stochastic reverse-process formulation with a deterministic ODE-style sampler, allowing stable trajectories and fewer effective timesteps via numerical integration
[
23
]
. This ODE view helped motivate flow-based reformulations of multi-step generation: rather than predicting noise, flow models learn a velocity field that transports samples from noise to data. Flow Matching formalized this by regressing a vector field along probability paths between noise and images
[
12
]
, and Rectified Flow improved few-step sampling by encouraging straighter trajectories and reducing discretization error
[
13
]
. Building on these ideas, SiT unified diffusion and flow objectives under a single interpolant framework and showed strong scalability with transformer backbones
[
16
]
. Today, state-of-the-art text-to-image systems increasingly adopt these flow-style designs, including Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX.1
[
5
,
1
]
. Despite these advances, sampling still requires many sequential passes through large backbones, motivating recent work on native one-step generation.

2.2
Emerging One-Step Flow Models

In order to address the computational heavy nature of iterative solving, recent work targets one-step generation. This movement began with Rectified Flow showing that rectified flow distillation can enable few step generation. InstaFlow built upon this by using Rectified Flow to improve noise-image couplings and then distilling a Stable-Diffusion style teacher into a one-step student
[
14
]
. In parallel, native one-step models trained without distillation began to emerge. In 2025, MeanFlow proposed learning an average velocity field that enables one-step sampling under ImageNet label-ID conditioning
[
6
]
. Later in 2025, Improved MeanFlow (iMF) addressed stability and guidance limitations of its predecessor by reformulating the objective as a regression on instantaneous velocity while treating guidance as explicit conditioning variables to preserve inference flexibility
[
7
]
. In addition, SoFlow combines a Flow Matching loss with a solution-consistency objective that couples the learned velocity to the ODE solution function, reporting improved one-step performance under comparable ImageNet settings with respect to Meanflow and iMF
[
15
]
. The most recent native one-step methods include Drifting Models, which view training as learning a drift (vector) field that gradually changes the model’s generated-sample distribution to the image distribution, further improving one-step ImageNet FID results
[
4
]
.

2.3
Experimental Protocol, Evaluation Metrics, and Datasets

Challenges with Benchmarking

State-of-the-art text-to-image generators like Stable Diffusion 3.5 are trained as text-conditioned models with cross-attention over large captioned corpora (LAION5B) and evaluated with multistep sampling with classifier-free guidance
[
10
,
5
]
. In contrast, the latest one-step flow models (Meanflow, iMF and SoFlow) are trained on ImageNet with label-ID conditioning and are reported under one-step or few-step settings
[
6
,
7
,
15
,
4
]
. Crucially, all these approaches use different CFG scales (selected empirically to optimize FID). This mismatch of text vs. label conditioning, multistep vs. one-step setting, different CFG scales makes comparisons nontrivial, motivating carefully controlled evaluation of conditioning setting, CFG scales and inference timesteps. Moreover, it remains unclear how these one-step models behave under multi-step inference and whether additional steps improve generation quality or introduce degradation.

Metrics

Evaluation in image generation has been anchored by GAN-era metrics, most notably Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Inception Score (IS)
[
8
,
20
]
. These metrics remain widely reported and are treated as optimization objectives, despite reflecting similarity in fixed InceptionNet feature space as a proxy for realism rather than directly measuring human-perceived realism.
As text and class conditioned models have improved, text-image alignment and human preference oriented metrics have been developed. CLIP score measures how well a prompt aligns with an image with CLIP embeddings, and human preference models such as PickScore provide an additional proxy for perceived quality
[
17
,
11
]
. However, inference heuristics such as CFG modulates the performance of each metric. As a result, reported results are often tied to empirically chosen CFG scales tuned for FID, making cross-paper comparisons between multistep diffusion systems and emerging one-step flow models difficult.

Datasets

Established text-to-image generators are trained on text-image corpora such as LAION, enabling open-vocabulary prompt-based evaluation
[
21
]
. This structure allows generalization evaluation with any image with a caption. However, native one-step models are trained and validated on ImageNet using label IDs
[
6
,
7
]
. In order to measure generalization quality, we need images matched with same IDs as ImageNet. ImageNetV2 provides an external test set for ImageNet-style evaluation, but it is relatively small (10,000 images) compared to the needs of modern generative benchmarking
[
18
]
. This lack of large adopted label-ID evaluation datasets complicates out-of-distribution validation and motivates an additional dataset to measure generalization for ImageNet-trained generators.

3
Benchmark

3.1
Benchmarking Setup

Our benchmark is designed to answer four questions: (i) how competitive ImageNet-trained one-step generators are against state-of-the-art text-to-image systems, (ii) what changes under a matched guidance scale across models, (iii) whether one-step models improve when run for more steps, and (iv) whether qualitative image generation is better under a composite metric than under FID alone.
To operationalize these 4 objectives, we benchmark recent one-step class-conditional models (MeanFlow, Improved MeanFlow (iMF), and SoFlow) against state-of-the-art text-to-image systems (Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX.1). We additionally include established and emerging flow-based baselines (SiT, RAE, and Scale-RAE) to contextualize performance across both one-step and multi-step settings.
For each method, we use the largest publicly available checkpoints and first reproduce reported results. After confirming consistency with prior work, we run a controlled evaluation that varies guidance and steps: (i) two CFG settings, CFG
=7
and a native setting (each model’s reported CFG), and (ii) two step settings, 1 step and 25 steps, where a step denotes one ODE update or denoising iteration. Across the 8 models and 4 inference settings, we evaluate on three datasets: ImageNet validation, ImageNetV2 (frequency-matched), and our proofread reLAIONet dataset, which aligns reLAION images to ImageNet classes.

3.2
Design Choices

CFG Choices

Higher CFG makes outputs better match the prompt, but can make them less diverse and more artifact-prone. On the other hand, lower CFG means weaker text guidance, ignoring prompt details. To find the correct balance, we set CFG=7 (Stable Diffusion defaults to 7.0) to match common practice in SD-based pipelines and prior SD-based editing work
[
2
]
. In addition, we also use the reported CFG setting of each model as it provides single-step and multi-step performance comparison, allowing us to understand the changes between fixed and tuned guidance scales.

Step Choices and NFE

We evaluate all models in both a one-step and a multi-step regime. Following common Flow Matching grids (NFE
\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}
), we use 25 steps as a strong multi-step anchor alongside 1 step. For consistency, we report ODE/denoising steps rather than number of function evaluations (NFE), since NFE per step varies by model: iMF and SoFlow collapse conditional and unconditional velocities, enabling 1 NFE per step, whereas SD3.5, RAE, Scale-RAE, FLUX.1, and SiT require at least 2 NFEs per step. Although MeanFlow reports 1 NFE per step, this setting ignores the conditioned velocity (i.e., removes guidance). To maintain a guided generation protocol, we instead use the training-time formulation, which requires 2 NFEs per step. Given these model-specific NFE differences, reporting step count provides the clearest cross-model comparison.

Implementation Details:

Due to SoFlow’s design, CFG scale cannot be explicitly set as they bake the value into the weights of the model. Hence, we can only vary the step size and use their reported CFG. Similarly, SiT uses a ”dropri5” ODE solver which dynamically sets the step size based on error. Even though the approach is ODE solver-agnostic (replace it with ”Euler” ODE solver which allows step control), we decide not to replace it for fair comparison. Furthermore, Meanflow, iMF, SoFlow and RAE are ImageNet class conditioned approaches whereas SD3.5, Flux.1-dev and Scale RAE take sentence based prompts. In order to standardize the conditioning as class conditioning, we set the input for SD3.5 and Flux.1 as ”a photo of a {class_name}” and Scale RAE to be ”Can you generate a photo of a {class_name}?”. Scale RAE has a different prompt structure as it uses the Qwen2 LLM which responds to conversational prompts.

3.3
Metrics

Based on prior work, we report Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Inception Score (IS), CLIP Score and PickScore. FID and IS cover image structure and diversity metrics, effectively reporting generation consistency; CLIP score covers text-image alignment and Pick Score represents a human preference metric. For each dataset, we compute FID against the full set (ImageNet Validation set, ImageNetv2 Frequency Matched set and reLAIONet) with an in ordered generation from the dataset’s classes. For CLIP score and Pick Score, we generate images based on the dataset’s classes and evaluate text-image alignment and human preference based on each generated image and the given input text with format ”a photo of a {class_name}”. As the emerging one-step models are tuned towards FID, we propose a composite metrics called MinMax Harmonic Mean (MMHM) which combines FID, IS, CLIP score and Pick Score to capture text-image consistency-alignment-preference. 3.4
Datasets

ImageNet Family

The one-step generation models are trained on ImageNet
[
3
]
(ILSVRC 2012). The dataset consists of 1.28 million training images and 50,000 validation images across 1,000 object categories organized by WordNet synsets. The images were sourced primarily from Flickr and verified by crowdsourced human annotators.
In this benchmark, we use the full 50,000-image validation split as both the reference distribution for FID computation and the source of ground-truth class labels that drive class-conditional generation: each model generates exactly one image per validation example, conditioned on that example’s label, for a total of 50,000 generated images. Moreover, ImageNetV2
[
18
]
is a frequency-matched test set constructed to probe overfitting to the original ImageNet validation split. Images were collected from Flickr using the same search queries and selection criteria as ImageNet, yielding 10,000 images (10 per class) with the identical 1,000-class label space. We use ImageNetV2 as a within-domain generalization test: drops from ImageNet val to ImageNetV2 indicate sensitivity to the specific ImageNet validation distribution, even when the label space and collection process are matched.

reLAIONet

Since the one-step models evaluated in this benchmark are trained on ImageNet, within-domain evaluation is otherwise limited to ImageNet validation and ImageNetV2
[
18
]
, both of which share the same label space and photographic distribution as the training data. To enable broader generalization assessment while preserving ImageNet label-ID class conditioning, we introduce reLAIONet, a proofread web-sourced evaluation dataset built upon the LAIONet construction methodology
[
22
]
. LAIONet maps LAION-400M image-caption pairs to ImageNet synsets by filtering captions for unique WordNet lemma matches, removing NSFW entries and multi-label ambiguities, and retaining only those pairs whose CLIP ViT-B/32 caption-synset cosine similarity exceeds 0.82. Rather than the original LAION-400M, which contains URLs no longer available, reLAIONet is constructed from reLAION-400M
[
21
]
, a re-crawled and deduplicated successor hosted on HuggingFace, which provides restored URL availability. Applying this pipeline across all 48 reLAION-400M parquet files yields images for up to 997 ImageNet classes, from which up to 70 images per class are downloaded ranked by CLIP similarity if available (classes contain between 70 and 1 image). Our addition is that the resulting images undergo manual proofreading to remove mislabeled or visually ambiguous samples, producing a final dataset of 25,252 images spanning 757 ImageNet classes. Because reLAIONet images originate from open web crawls rather than Flickr (the primary source of ImageNet) they exhibit substantially different photographic style and context, providing a challenging out-of-distribution dataset that complements ImageNet val and ImageNetV2 while retaining the same label-ID conditioning required by class-conditional generative models.

4
Results

4.1
Qualitative Results: MMHM Tracks Perceptual Quality Better Than FID

Within Model Family Qualitative Comparison:

Figure
2
compares MeanFlow and Improved MeanFlow (iMF) samples across inference settings spanning MMHM and FID. Within both families, higher MMHM consistently corresponds to better perceptual quality: for MeanFlow, samples progress from distorted outputs to sharper boundaries and more coherent structure, and for iMF the improvements are most evident in the dog and scuba diver examples (more stable shape and finer detail), while the groom example improves primarily in facial plausibility. Crucially, the lowest-FID settings are not the best-looking for either model: configurations with stronger FID can still appear less realistic than slightly worse-FID but higher-MMHM settings, showing that FID-only tuning can mis-select hyperparameters. Moreover, even single-digit FID does not guarantee high-quality generations, as blur and structural artifacts can persist, underscoring that distribution-level similarity need not imply instance-level fidelity. In addition, more steps leads to better image quality, showing that the flows learned in one step scale to better performance. Overall, MMHM tracks the qualitative improvements more reliably than FID in this class-conditional setting.

Across Model Family Qualitative Comparison:

Figure
3
compares representative generations across model families under their best performing setting by MMHM. RAE, SoFlow, iMF and Meanflow achieve relatively low FID while still producing outputs with instance-level failures, including spatial distortions, and unnatural textures, When comparing with the state-of-the-art approaches of SD3.5-large and Flux.1-dev whose FID score’s are in the 20s, it shows that low FID score’s do not mean better image generation quality and that the one step models still lag behind the state of the art in both one-step and multi-step settings. Finally, while MMHM better reflects the multi-metric tradeoffs than FID alone, it still does not perfectly track perceptual quality across families (does not allow for cross model comparison of image quality). For example, Flux.1-dev and Scale RAE scores low on MMHM with 0.69 and 0.51 but is more realistic than Meanflow (0.86) and SoFlow (0.88). Together these results highlight an open problem: designing metrics that reliably capture qualitative image fidelity and human-perceived realism across diverse generative model classes.

Excluding one-step failures, a consistent within-family trend is that configurations that minimize FID often reduce IS, CLIP Score, and Pick Score, showing that FID-only tuning can hurt conditional quality. MMHM makes this tradeoff explicit by jointly balancing FID, IS, CLIP, and Pick, and consequently the best configuration for a model is typically not the one with the best FID, but the one with the strongest overall multi-metric profile. This mismatch is largely driven by CFG dynamics: higher CFG improves text-image alignment and human preference (CLIP Score and Pick Score) but tends to worsen FID, while lower CFG improves FID at the expense of alignment and preference, biasing FID-based sweeps toward under-guided settings. Step scaling further separates model classes: native one-step flows improve substantially when iterated (25 steps), whereas multi-step systems degrade sharply when forced into one-step inference, consistent with their training objective. Overall, the strongest one-step formulations are competitive quantitatively with state-of-the-art baselines under this class-conditional protocol, but prior “tuned” CFG choices appear optimized for FID rather than overall quality. Finally, no single metric is sufficient: CLIP/Pick can remain plausible even when samples are visibly poor, while IS/FID capture diversity and distribution mismatch but not conditional correctness or preference. This motivates reporting a multi-metric view and using MMHM as a summary selection criterion rather than relying on any one score.

>1

5
Discussion

5.1
Why move from FID to a multi-metric objective?

Optimizing FID alone is a brittle selection strategy: across both within-family sweeps (Figure
2
) and across-family comparisons (Figure
3
), lower FID often coincides with worse perceptual quality (distortions, texture artifacts, and structural inconsistencies). This happens because FID is a distribution-level Inception-feature similarity score and does not directly capture conditional fidelity, alignment, or preference. In practice, tuning CFG/steps to minimize FID systematically trades off IS/CLIP/Pick, and the MeanFlow ablations show this most clearly in the few-step regime where FID is highly sensitive to CFG. These results motivate selecting models/settings using a multi-metric objective that explicitly balances realism (FID), diversity/class separability (IS), and alignment/preference signals (CLIP, Pick), rather than relying on a single score that can be skewed by inference heuristics.

5.2
What MMHM provides, and what it does not

MMHM is best viewed as a practical selection lens that prevents single-metric over-optimization and yields more stable choices across datasets and inference knobs. Empirically, MMHM favors intermediate CFG regimes and higher-step settings that jointly improve alignment and preference without collapsing diversity, and it produces consistent within-family selections that generalize from ImageNet to ImageNetV2 and reLAIONet. However, MMHM is not a perfect proxy for perceptual realism: across families, models with lower MMHM can still look more photorealistic than higher-MMHM one-step outputs, especially on localized failure modes such as facial geometry. Thus, MMHM demonstrates the value of multi-metric selection over FID-only tuning, but also highlights an open problem: designing evaluation objectives that better track human-perceived quality across diverse generative model classes.

5.3
One-step models are competitive with SOTA under controlled protocols quantitatively, but fall behind qualitatively

When compared under a controlled class-conditional protocol and selected by MMHM (rather than best-FID settings), the strongest native one-step models are competitive with established multi-step systems at multi-step inference (25 steps) quantitative as seen in the tables. However, qualitative results show that one-step models still exhibit characteristic local distortions (notably faces) even when their FID is strong, suggesting that closing the remaining gap will require both better training targets and better evaluation criteria than FID alone. In addition, one-step image quality is inferior to the state-of-the-art in a multi-step regime as seen in Figure
2
. Hence, by transitivity, one-step generation is also worse than the state of the art qualitatively. This quantitative vs. qualitative mismatch is the main point we want to highlight for further research, motivating a need for metric to capture holistic generation quality.

6
Conclusion

We presented a controlled benchmark that directly compares emerging native one-step flow models to state-of-the-art generators under matched inference conditions, including fixed guidance (CFG
=7
where applicable) and both one-step and multi-step regimes (1 vs. 25 denoising/ODE steps). To test robustness beyond the ImageNet validation distribution while preserving label-ID conditioning, we introduced reLAIONet, a new proofread out-of-distribution evaluation dataset aligned to ImageNet classes, and demonstrated that key trends persist across ImageNet, ImageNetV2, and reLAIONet. Across these benchmarks, we showed that FID-only evaluation is unreliable: minimizing FID can systematically trade off against diversity, text-image alignment, and human preference signals, and can improve even when qualitative sample fidelity degrades. To make these tradeoffs explicit, we proposed MinMax Harmonic Mean (MMHM) as a practical multi-metric selection proxy that balances FID, Inception Score, CLIP Score, and Pick Score and yields more stable hyperparameter choices across guidance and step sweeps. At the same time, MMHM is not a definitive target for cross-model comparison; its primary value is as an interpretable proxy for multi-metric optimization rather than a universal scalar notion of realism. Developing a principled, optimization-ready metric that reliably tracks human-perceived quality across diverse model families remains an important direction for future work. Finally, MMHM-based selection shows one-step models can approach state-of-the-art quantitatively under controlled multi-step inference, but they still exhibit persistent distortions (especially faces), highlighting a metric mismatch and the need for objectives that better track human-perceived realism.

Lemmas

Each of the 1,000 ILSVRC-2012 synsets

provides a set of WordNet lemma strings
\mathcal{L}_{k}

Lemmas are normalised by lowercasing and replacing underscores with

The unique lemma index
\phi:\hat{\ell}\mapsto w_{k}
retains only

multiple synsets (e.g.,

a construction machine) is excluded to prevent ambiguous assignments.

Lemma 1
(Uniqueness Exclusion)

A caption containing an ambiguous lemma
\hat{\ell}
with

|\{k:\hat{\ell}\in\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\}|>1
cannot be
unambiguously assigned to a single class and is discarded.

Lemma 2
(Multi-label Rejection)

A caption matching two or more distinct WNIDs via
\phi
is assigned
the sentinel
__multi__
and removed from the candidate pool.

Lemma 3
(Threshold Monotonicity)

Increasing the CLIP threshold
\tau
weakly decreases the candidate
set size for every class, and weakly increases the average semantic
fidelity of retained captions.

For CLIP scoring, each synset is represented by the text

\tau_{k}=\texttt{``}\langle name\rangle\texttt{ which is }\langle definition\rangle\texttt{''}
,
encoded with CLIP ViT-B/32 and
\ell_{2}
-normalised so that cosine
similarity equals the dot product.

A class or individual image is excluded if any of the following

The assigned WNID does not correspond to the WordNet synset that

2.

The class is not among the 1,000 official ILSVRC-2012 WNIDs, or the
