Assessment of construction waste aggregates as a road raw material using response surface methods
Authors: Vishal Dhiman, Swasti Sharma, Preetha Ravisree, Shivangi Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid expansion of the road network in India over the last decade has generated a significant quantity of concrete waste aggregates (CWAs), leading to serious disposal and environmental concerns. Limited efforts toward developing innovative reuse technologies have accelerated the dumping of CWAs in landfill sites and water bodies. In this study, the feasibility of utilizing bitumen emulsion pre-treated CWAs in asphalt wearing course (WC) construction was investigated to promote sustainable pavement development. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed as an optimization approach to evaluate the influence of key experimental variables, including bitumen content (VG30), percentage of pre-treated CWAs, and temperature, on the performance characteristics of asphalt mixtures. Marshall Stability, moisture resistance, stiffness, and volumetric properties were experimentally analysed and compared with MoRTH specifications. The results demonstrated that bitumen emulsion treatment significantly improved the engineering performance and durability characteristics of CWAs-based asphalt mixtures. Under optimized conditions of bitumen content = 4.79%, pre-treated CWAs = 21.02%, and temperature = 9.9 °C, all response parameters were found to satisfy the permissible limits for WC construction materials. The study highlights the novelty of integrating CWAs, bitumen emulsion treatment, and RSM optimization for sustainable pavement applications. Overall, the findings confirm that treated CWAs can be effectively reused in road construction in India, thereby reducing environmental impacts associated with waste disposal and encouraging the development of sustainable construction practices and policies.Keywords: Box Behnken approach (BBD), Waste management, sustainable development, concrete waste aggregates, central composite design (CCD), response surface methodology (RSM).Subject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Methodology

Raw materials

Aggregates as per IRC

Concrete waste aggregates (CWAs) were first collected, cleaned, crushed, and sieved according to the required gradation for asphalt wearing course mixtures. The processed CWAs were then pre-treated using bitumen emulsion to improve surface bonding characteristics and reduce moisture susceptibility before mixing with virgin aggregates. Asphalt specimens were prepared using the Marshall Mix Design method with varying proportions of bitumen content, pre-treated CWAs, and controlled temperature conditions25.

The experimental program evaluated key performance parameters, including Marshall Stability, flow value, stiffness, volumetric properties, and moisture resistance, in accordance with relevant MoRTH and ASTM standards. Testing conditions were carefully controlled to assess the behaviour of the mixtures under low-temperature conditions relevant to pavement applications. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the experimental variables and analyse their combined effects on asphalt mixture performance. The optimized mixture conditions were identified based on compliance with permissible specification limits and overall mechanical performance26.

In the current study, CWAs remained utilized as aggregates. The main collection sites of CWAs were at the Indian highway construction and repair site between Manali and Chandigarh NH21.

Laboratory testing

Preparing the specimen for optimization process

By evaluating effects of procedure variables on response parameters, the process was optimized, utilizing the CWAs as aggregate in the creation of WC. The process factors chosen for this study were the bitumen-binder percentage of the material, temperature (which was considered for calculating stiffness), and the proportion of treatment and untreated CWAs36. Three kinds of the procedure’s parameters—the three parameters as low, medium, and high—were selected, as indicated in Table below37.

The three X1–4 levels, as indicated in Table 4, were chosen based on the results of earlier scientific research and accepted norms. Since it is simpler to complete this work by hand, a coarse portion of the mixture was used in place of the CWAs. It was made sure that emulsion breaking had started before starting the process of mixing for WC. In the lab, cylindrical specimens measuring 105 millimetres in diameter and 65 millimetres in total height were created38. These specimens underwent additional testing for a number of parameters, including stiffness and volumetric properties like VMA, VFB, TSR, VA and FLOW VALUE. Stiffness test

The effect of the already treated CWAs on the specimen’s stiffness was assessed in relation to temperature41. In this case, an indirect tensile stiffness test was performed on the Marshall Strength Testing Machine specimen. For this test, five conditioning sine pulses were applied to the cylindrical Marshall Specimen, followed by sine test pulses42. The latter were assigned to the vertical diameter of the specimen. The load on the specimen was raised until 0.005% of the horizontal strain was produced. Following Eq. (2), the results of this analysis were then utilized to determine the specimen’s resilient modulus (RM) at various temperatures, namely 0, 10, and 20.

In this case, MR stands for resilient modulus, F for load repetitions, z for horizontal recoverable deformation (mm), h for specimen thickness, and v for Poisson’s ratio. The value of v was taken to be 0.2843, as the scientific literature supports. Furthermore, it should be mentioned that MoRTH 2000 contains no clauses pertaining to MR specification44. Therefore, the specimen obtained with untreated CWAs, which is referred to as the controlled specimen in this publication, also underwent similar examination in order to compare the results.

Statistical analysis

Instead of using the time-consuming and labour-intensive One-Variable-At-a-Time (OVAT) method, a multivariate statistical method was used to establish a link between process variables and response parameters. The CCD and BBD approaches were used with RSM to construct the experiments and develop models that utilized either a linear or quadratic connection between the procedure parameters and response parameters. This strategy created a link between the reaction and process parameters that can be described using Eq. (3).

The response function coefficients, Cn, X1 through X4, and Y, which represent response parameter(s), were calculated using the coefficient of regression software Stat-Ease Designs Expert (version 8.0.7.1)45. The accuracy of the models created using this application was then evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Other metrics calculated for the sufficiency assessment were goodness of fit, adjusted regression coefficients, and correlation regression coefficients. After thorough statistical analysis, 3D representations of the data were also produced using RSM. These graphs were used to evaluate the optimal circumstances46.

Volumetric properties

Figure 3 illustrates the changes in volumetric characteristics, such as VMA, VA, VFA, MS, and FLOW VALUE, when CWAs are used as WC construction materials49. With the exception of VMA, all of the volumetric attributes had a linear connection with the process parameters, according to the response surface plots. These results further demonstrate that, in accordance with the MoRTH 2009 requirements for WC, the use of CWAs had no multiple effects on any of the volumetric parameters other than VFA. Among the other volumetric characteristics, several specimens’ VMA failed to meet the standards when the proportions of BC (VG30) and CWAs were 4% and 10%, respectively. Additionally, it was found that specimens with 40% and 4%, 3.78% and 5%, of the CWAs and the BC (VG30) were unfit for WC building due to compromised VA values. The fact that MS and FLOW VALUE were found to be within the specifications required for the development of WC for the complete specimen, as designed in the lab, was encouraging, nevertheless50. A low percentage of CWAs in the specimen could have a substantial impact on the VMA, VA, and VFA, according to observations made utilizing the volumetric characteristics. Therefore, when deciding how many CWAs to utilize in the construction of the WCs, much thought should be given. The two-way ANOVA results, as presented in Table 7, also demonstrated that the percentage of CWAs is significantly (< 0.05) correlated with the volumetric features, although this is not the case for the percentage of bitumen content. Therefore, it also supports the conclusion that the percentage of CWAs should be carefully determined in order to achieve the necessary WC characteristics51.

Water resistance

When mortar is applied to CWAs, it has been shown to improve their ability to absorb water and decrease resistance. As a result, Fig. 3 displays the results of this study’s analysis of the specimen’s tensile strength ratio (TSR) at various CWA proportions.

Table 7; Fig. 3 show that bitumen pre-treatment of the CWAs has decreased their permeability because the majority of the specimens had TSR within the acceptable ranges. The only specimen with TSR values greater than 90% was the one with CWAs and BC contents of 25% and 6.41%, 3.78% and 5%, 10% and 6%, respectively. Additionally, two-way ANOVA analysis revealed no significant relationship between TSR and the percentage of CWAs in the sample. Therefore, it may be inferred that the bitumen emulsion pre-treatment improved the CWAs’ properties and caused them to behave more like natural aggregates52.

Stiffness

The resilient modulus (RM) of the epoxy determined the stiffness of the specimen created using CWAs. The RM of the CWA specimens that were treated and those that were not was compared. Both untreated and treated specimens had average RM values above 5000 MPa, which was the standard RM value for WC. Consequently, it could be concluded that the specimen created using the treated CWAs behaves similarly to natural mixtures and does not decrease its RM53.

Conversely, it was shown that substituting treated CWAs for the untreated ones reduced the specimen’s stiffness. Thus, it is also possible to deduce that the specimen’s rigidity decreases as the bitumen content rises54.

Additionally, variations in the specimen’s stiffness in relation to the ambient temperature were assessed in order to gauge the effects of weather on the specimen. Figure 4 response surface plots demonstrate that the specimen’s stiffness and temperature had a quadratic relationship. It was evident that the rigidity of the specimen—both treated and untreated—decreased as the temperature rose. Both specimen types were found to be more rigid at lower temperatures. Nevertheless, it was discovered that the untreated specimens were stiffer than the treated ones when the effects of temperature were ignored55. Likewise, a two-way ANOVA showed a stronger correlation between the specimen’s temperature and RM, followed by the proportion of the CWAs.

Conclusions

When building WCs, the results of the current investigation shown that CWAs recovered from a road maintenance site in a mountainous region of India could significantly replace natural aggregates. It has been demonstrated that bitumen emulsion pre-treating the CWAs enhances their qualities and makes them suitable for use in building. The characteristics of the laboratory specimen that were found to be similar to those of the natural aggregates included its VMA, VA, VFA, MS, FLOW VALUE, TSR, and RM. Additionally, the RSM optimization procedure revealed that all of the response parameters’ values fell within the MoRTH (2000) specified bounds under optimal conditions, which included pre-treated CWAs = 21.02%; bitumen (VG30) = 4.79%; and temperature = 9.9 °C.

Bitumen pre-treatment improves the Tensile Strength Ratio (TSR) primarily by enhancing the adhesion between the asphalt binder and concrete waste aggregates (CWAs) and by reducing moisture susceptibility. Untreated CWAs generally possess a porous surface texture and residual cement mortar, which absorb more water and weaken the bond between aggregate and bitumen. This weak interfacial bonding promotes stripping and reduces moisture resistance in asphalt mixtures.

The bitumen emulsion pre-treatment forms a thin coating around the surface of CWAs, which acts as a protective layer and minimizes water absorption. This coating improves the aggregate–binder affinity, fills surface pores and microcracks, and creates a stronger interfacial bond within the asphalt matrix. As a result, the treated aggregates exhibit better resistance against moisture-induced damage and stripping under wet conditions. Consequently, the asphalt mixtures prepared with pre-treated CWAs show higher TSR values, indicating improved durability, cohesion, and long-term pavement performance.

The decrease in stiffness with treated concrete waste aggregates (CWAs) can be attributed to the presence of the bitumen emulsion coating on the aggregate surface. The pre-treatment creates a relatively softer and more flexible interfacial layer between the aggregate and asphalt binder compared to untreated aggregates. While this coating improves adhesion and moisture resistance, it also reduces the rigidity of the aggregate skeleton within the asphalt mixture.

As a result, it was concluded that laboratory specimens made in compliance with multivariate experiments—specifically, CCD and BBD—were appropriate for use in the production of WC. As a result, it is suggested that the CWAs be kept and that their repurposing for construction be encouraged. This could help with the many efforts being carried out globally to meet the deadlines for achieving various sustainable development goals.
