Successful endoscopic evacuation of traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage: A report of two cases
Authors: Kiyotaka Wakatsuki, Taigen Sase, Gaku Hidaka, Homare Nakamura, Hidetoshi Murata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (TICH) often coexists with other traumatic intracranial lesions and presents complex pathophysiology, making surgical decision-making challenging. Although craniotomy has long been the standard treatment for space-occupying traumatic hematomas, endoscopic hematoma evacuation has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative. However, evidence supporting its application to TICH remains limited.Case Description: We report two patients with TICH who underwent endoscopic hematoma evacuation for acute traumatic management. In both cases, neurological deterioration was attributed to the localized mass effect caused by traumatic intracerebral hematomas, and endoscopic evacuation was selected to achieve focal decompression. The procedures were performed through burr holes using a transparent sheath and rigid neuroendoscope. Adequate hematoma removal was achieved without intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhagic complications. Postoperative computed tomography confirmed satisfactory evacuation, and intracranial pressure was well controlled during the acute phase. Although both patients ultimately died from nonneurological causes, no TICH-related neurological deterioration was observed during the postoperative course.Conclusion: Endoscopic hematoma evacuation may represent a technically feasible minimally invasive option for achieving focal decompression in selected patients with TICH. In the present two cases, adequate hematoma removal was achieved without procedure-related complications. However, because both patients ultimately died from severe systemic comorbidities unrelated to the neurosurgical procedure, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effect of this technique on overall survival or long-term neurological outcomes. Further studies are required to establish appropriate surgical indications and determine their clinical benefit.

INTRODUCTION

Severe head trauma involves a complex combination of conditions, including cerebral contusion, traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (TICH), and acute subdural hematoma, making treatment challenging, often with poor outcomes.[4,5] Surgical hematoma evacuation is indicated for traumatic intracranial hemorrhage associated with mass effect. Craniotomy for hematoma evacuation has long been considered the standard treatment. Depending on the intraoperative findings, this approach also allows decompressive craniectomy following hematoma removal. Meanwhile, endoscopic hematoma evacuation has recently gained attention as a minimally invasive alternative to conventional craniotomy.[11,13-15]

Endoscopic hematoma evacuation is moderately effective for hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage.[3,10,16] In traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, reports of endoscopic hematoma evacuation have increased in recent years, particularly for acute subdural hematoma.[7-9,17] However, its application to TICH is rarely reported.[11,13-15] Challenges include hematoma heterogeneity associated with traumatic lesions, intraoperative bleeding, and difficulty maintaining a clear surgical field.

We experienced two cases in which endoscopic hematoma evacuation was performed for TICH as part of acute trauma management. In both cases, postoperative intracranial conditions remained stable. However, both patients died from conditions unrelated to the traumatic brain injury. Although long-term neurological outcomes could not be evaluated, these cases demonstrate that adequate hematoma evacuation can be achieved using an endoscopic approach in selected TICH cases. Herein, we discuss the technical feasibility and surgical considerations of endoscopic hematoma evacuation in the acute management of traumatic brain injury.

CASE PRESENTATION 1

The patient was a man in his 70s with a medical history of prostate cancer, bladder cancer, kidney cancer, and diabetes mellitus. He was found collapsed on the street with impaired consciousness and was transported to our hospital by ambulance. On arrival, he exhibited moderate disturbance of consciousness with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E3V2M5. He also had a fever and tested positive for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Physical examination revealed evidence of head trauma. Head computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a skull fracture, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, and contusion in the left temporal lobe [Figure 1a]. Contrast-enhanced CT showed no obvious contrast extravasation. Because the mass effect from the hematoma was considered minimal, conservative treatment with antihypertensive therapy was initially selected, with a follow-up CT planned 3 h later. However, the patient’s level of consciousness progressively deteriorated to GCS E2V2M4. Follow-up CT performed approximately 3 h after the initial scan demonstrated enlargement and hematoma formation within the temporal lobe contusion [Figure 1b]. As neurological deterioration progressed in parallel with hematoma enlargement, the primary cause of neurological worsening was confirmed as localized compression by the hematoma, and surgical intervention was indicated. Because the hematoma was superficially located within the temporal lobe without diffuse cerebral swelling or radiological evidence suggesting the need for decompressive craniectomy, focal decompression through an endoscopic approach was considered appropriate. Conventional craniotomy remained available as an intraoperative backup if sufficient decompression could not be achieved.

(a) Head computed tomography (CT) at the initial presentation. Traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage and left temporal lobe contusion are noted. (b) Head CT obtained 3 hours later demonstrating hematoma formation and enlargement within the left temporal lobe contusion.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. The patient was placed in the supine position with the head rotated to the right and fixed using a Mayfield three-point head holder. A vertical skin incision was made 6 cm superior to the external auditory canal to allow burr-hole placement and facilitate rapid conversion to craniotomy if necessary. A burr-hole corresponding to the diameter of a No. 3 hand drill was created [Figure 2a]. After the dural incision, the hematoma cavity was punctured using a side-hole puncture needle. The tract was sequentially dilated, and a transparent sheath (NeuroSheath; 22-Fr, inner lumen 7.0 mm, outer diameter 8.1 mm, effective length 10 cm; Medikit Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was inserted into the hematoma cavity. A 2.7-mm 0° rigid neuroendoscope (Olympus Medical Systems Corp., Hachioji, Japan) was introduced, and the hematoma was evacuated under direct visualization. A satisfactory operative field within the hematoma cavity was obtained, which allowed aspiration of the hematoma in a manner similar to that used for hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage. After circumferential hematoma evacuation and confirmed absence of active bleeding, hemostasis of the hematoma cavity was achieved using FLOSEAL® Hemostatic Matrix (Baxter Healthcare Corp., Deerfield, IL, USA). The sheath was withdrawn while leaving SURGICEL® Original Absorbable Hemostat (Ethicon, Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) within the puncture tract. An intracranial pressure (ICP) sensor was inserted into the brain parenchyma through the same trajectory, and the wound was closed. Postoperative CT confirmed satisfactory hematoma evacuation [Figure 2b].

(a) Location of the trepanation hole as viewed from the skull reconstructed using postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography (CT). (b) Postoperative head CT. Adequate hematoma removal was confirmed.

Because of concurrent COVID-19, the patient was returned to the intensive care unit while intubated. No evidence of rebleeding was found on follow-up CT, and ICP remained stable during the acute phase, indicating successful ICP control. However, due to COVID-19 and associated pneumonia, impaired consciousness persisted. Extubation proved difficult, and tracheostomy was required on postoperative day 13. Subsequently, the patient’s consciousness improved to GCS E4VtM5; however, progression of underlying malignancies, congestive nephropathy, and multiple organ failure ultimately led to death on postoperative day 146.

CASE PRESENTATION 2

A man in his 60s was transported to our hospital by ambulance because of abnormal behavior after falling while intoxicated 3 days before presentation and sustaining head trauma. His medical history included poorly controlled diabetes mellitus. On admission, his GCS score was E3V3M5. Head CT demonstrated traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, acute subdural hematoma, and bilateral frontal TICH [Figure 3]. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed no obvious contrast extravasation. Because the mass effect from the hematoma was not severe, the impaired consciousness was considered possibly related to alcohol metabolism disorder, and conservative management was initially selected. No hematoma enlargement was observed during follow-up. However, because consciousness failed to improve with conservative treatment, persistent localized hematoma compression was suspected, and surgical hematoma evacuation was planned. The bilateral frontal hematomas were located superficially and were considered readily accessible through bilateral burr holes. Because diffuse cerebral swelling was absent and extensive decompression was not considered necessary, an endoscopic approach was selected. Conventional craniotomy was kept as a backup option if adequate decompression could not be achieved endoscopically. Endoscopic hematoma evacuation was therefore performed on hospital day 8.

Head computed tomography at the initial presentation. Traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage in both frontal lobes is noted.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. The patient was placed in the supine position with the head maintained in the midline and secured using a Mayfield three-point head holder. A navigation system was used. Bilateral skin incisions were made with burr-hole placement planned approximately 3 cm anterior to the coronal sutures bilaterally [Figure 4a]. Burr-hole trepanation was performed using the same technique as in case 1, and transparent sheaths were inserted. The hematoma was evacuated under direct endoscopic visualization. A clear operative field within the hematoma cavity was achieved, allowing aspiration of the hematoma in the same manner as for hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage. Circumferential evacuation of the hematoma was completed, and hemostasis was achieved after confirming the absence of active bleeding.

(a) Location of bilateral trepanation sites as seen on postoperative head computed tomography (bone-contrast mode). (b) Postoperative head computed tomography. Adequate hematoma removal was confirmed.

Postoperative CT confirmed satisfactory hematoma evacuation [Figure 4b]. No evidence of rebleeding was noted on follow-up CT, and ICP management proceeded without difficulty. Although frontal lobe dysfunction persisted and rehabilitation was planned, aspiration pneumonia worsened because of underlying diabetes mellitus, eventually resulting in lung abscess formation. Because of underlying diabetes mellitus, aspiration pneumonia and lung abscess progressively worsened despite treatment, and the patient died on hospital day 50.

DISCUSSION

In recent years, endoscopic hematoma evacuation has become increasingly performed as a treatment modality for hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage.[3,10,16] Its efficacy has been demonstrated in putaminal and subcortical hemorrhage. However, its application to traumatic intracranial hemorrhage remains to be fully established. Meanwhile, reports of endoscopic hematoma evacuation for acute subdural hematoma have increased. Endoscopic surgery via small craniotomy for acute subdural hematoma in older patients has demonstrated advantages such as reduced blood loss, shorter operative times,[8] low risk, and no postoperative rebleeding.[7] However, reports on endoscopic surgery for TICH remain limited.[11,13-15] Although reports of endoscopic hematoma evacuation for TICH remain limited, several case reports and small case series have described its application. The available literature is summarized below to provide the clinical context for the present two cases.

Nascimento et al. reported a successful case in which endoscopic hematoma evacuation through burr-hole trepanation was performed for TICH in a patient presenting with a GCS score of 13, allowing discharge home within 2 days.[11] This report suggested the efficacy of endoscopic evacuation in the acute phase for localized TICH associated with mild head trauma.

Tanaka et al. reported a case in which bilateral burr-hole endoscopic evacuation was effective for TICHs involving the left frontal and right temporal lobes in a patient with a GCS score of 8.[14] This represented the first report describing hematoma evacuation through two burr-hole sites for multiple traumatic hematomas. They suggested that this technique can contribute to survival and improve neurological status in a patient with severe head trauma. As demonstrated in case 2, even in cases of multiple intracerebral hemorrhages, extensive craniotomy or multiple craniotomies may not be necessary. In patients with bilateral frontal TICH, as in our case, the procedure can be performed using two burr holes while maintaining a single patient position.

Tamura et al. reported two older patients with cerebral contusion associated with acute subdural hematoma in whom burr-hole placement directly above the contusion enabled endoscopic decompression of the hematoma and improved consciousness.[13] Thus, this approach may represent a treatment option for high-risk patients with significant comorbidities.

Tseng et al. reported endoscopic hematoma evacuation in nine consecutive patients with severe traumatic brain injury and TICH (GCS score ≤8).[15] ICP sensors were inserted during surgery. In six patients, acute-phase intracranial management was successfully achieved without additional surgery. In the remaining three patients, decompressive craniectomy was performed after postoperative ICP elevation >20 mmHg, resulting in successful salvage. Their report suggested that ICP monitoring combined with endoscopic hematoma evacuation may be useful in the management of severe traumatic brain injury.

In the two cases presented, the primary basis for surgical indication was that neurological deterioration was mainly attributable to localized hematoma compression rather than diffuse brain injury. Endoscopic hematoma evacuation was performed during the acute or subacute phase, achieving adequate visualization within the hematoma cavity without intraoperative or postoperative hemorrhagic complications. Postoperative CT confirmed satisfactory hematoma removal. ICP remained stable, and no postoperative neurological deterioration due to TICH was observed. However, both patients ultimately died because of underlying systemic disease or deterioration of their general condition unrelated to the traumatic brain injury, which prevented assessment of long-term neurological or survival benefit. Despite these poor overall outcomes, endoscopic hematoma evacuation successfully achieved focal hematoma decompression without procedure-related complications in both cases. Therefore, these cases demonstrate the technical feasibility of achieving adequate focal decompression in selected patients with TICH but do not permit conclusions regarding the effect of this procedure on overall clinical outcomes. Larger studies are required to determine whether successful focal decompression translates into improved neurological or survival outcomes.

Based on our experience and previous reports, endoscopic hematoma evacuation for TICH may be considered appropriate in the following cases: (1) neurological deterioration is primarily attributable to localized hematoma compression, (2) diffuse cerebral edema or global intracranial hypertension requiring decompressive craniectomy is absent, (3) the hematoma is located in an area accessible through a minimally invasive transcranial approach, (4) active bleeding requiring extensive intraoperative hemostasis is not suspected, and (5) postoperative ICP monitoring is feasible, and immediate conversion to craniotomy can be performed if necessary.

Surgical intervention for traumatic brain injury is generally indicated for mass effect and neurological deterioration caused by space-occupying lesions. Moreover, surgical evacuation or decompressive craniectomy is recommended for hematoma-related compression or elevated ICP, with conventional craniotomy remaining the standard treatment option.[1,6] ICP management is a fundamental component of severe traumatic brain injury management, and ICP monitoring is primarily indicated in patients with low GCS scores.[2] Furthermore, an ICP value ≥22 mmHg was deemed associated with poor neurological outcomes, and stepwise treatment strategies based on ICP monitoring were recommended.[12]

As suggested by previous reports and the present cases, combining endoscopic hematoma evacuation with postoperative ICP monitoring may enable minimally invasive focal decompression while maintaining postoperative safety management and allowing prompt conversion to decompressive craniectomy if ICP elevation or intracranial deterioration occurs. Further studies involving larger numbers of patients are necessary to clarify surgical indications, treatment outcomes, and long-term prognosis.

CONCLUSION

Endoscopic hematoma evacuation may represent a technically feasible minimally invasive surgical option for selected patients with TICH in whom neurological deterioration is primarily attributable to localized hematoma compression. In the present two cases, adequate focal hematoma decompression was achieved without procedure-related complications. However, because both patients ultimately died from severe systemic comorbidities unrelated to the neurosurgical procedure, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effect of this technique on overall survival or long-term neurological outcomes. Further studies involving larger patient populations are required to establish appropriate surgical indications and determine their clinical benefit.
