Surgeon-Patient Sex Concordance and Postoperative Outcomes in Cataract Surgeries: A 16-Year Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Ishani P Majmudar, Sahana Aggarwal, Nikoo Hamzeh, Ying Cheung, David Aaby, Manjot K Gill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine if there is an association between surgeon-patient sex concordance and post-surgical complications for cataract surgeries.Patients and MethodsIn this 16-year retrospective cohort study, data was collected from the Northwestern Medicine Electronic Data Warehouse on patients who underwent cataract surgery at Northwestern Memorial Hospital in Chicago, IL. The self-identified sex of the surgeon and patient was recorded for each surgery. Peri-operative complications and associated procedures that occurred within 30 days of surgery as marked by diagnosis codes, billing codes, or post-operative notes were recorded. Peri-operative complications included one or more of endophthalmitis, choroidal hemorrhage, posterior capsular tear, retinal detachment, vitreous loss, retained lens fragments, or return to operating room for vitrectomy within 30 days. The primary outcome was the association between the presence of any postoperative complications from cataract surgery and surgeon-patient sex discordance.ResultsFrom 2007 to 2023, 30,804 cataract surgeries were performed on 19,178 unique patients. About 47.69% were sex concordant procedures and 51.00% were sex discordant. A post-operative or intra-operative complication occurred in 3.5% of patients. Among cataract surgeries that resulted in one or more complications, 50.3% were sex-concordant while 49.7% were sex-discordant. The most common post-operative complication experienced among individuals with complications was retinal detachment.ConclusionThe results of our study demonstrate no significant association between surgical complication rates and surgeon-patient sex discordance following cataract surgery, supporting comparable outcomes irrespective of surgeon-patient sex concordance.

Introduction

A strong physician-patient relationship is fundamental to providing high quality care with improved health outcomes and is shaped by a variety of factors, including sociodemographic characteristics. Previous literature has demonstrated that patient satisfaction is often higher when there is sex concordance between the patient and the physician, specifically among females.1

Recent studies have emphasized the value of aligning patient and physician demographics in surgical environments as well.2,3 A retrospective cohort analysis of 495,628 patients undergoing complex cancer surgeries found that sex discordance was associated with lower likelihood of optimal outcomes, higher risk of complications, and increased 90-day mortality.2 Similarly, in a Canadian-based retrospective cohort study, researchers assessed physician patient sex-concordance and postoperative complications for 21 unique surgical procedures performed on 1,320,108 patients.3 The results showed that sex discordance between the surgeon and patient was associated with a greater likelihood of adverse postoperative outcomes and death.

To date, no such study has been performed in ophthalmology. In a 2021 cross sectional study, researchers found that the number of male practicing ophthalmologists in the American Academy of Ophthalmology outnumbered female ophthalmologists by a 3:1 ratio.4 Additionally, from 2011 to 2019, there has been a 2.5% decrease in the percentage of female residents in ophthalmology programs.4 However, a more recent ophthalmology-specific study assessing trends in patient-surgeon sex concordance overtime found that female cataract surgeons at a single institution completed a higher share of surgeries over 2011–2024.5 Sex-concordant cataract surgeries at this institution increased over the same time period, likely reflecting changes in workforce composition and surgical volume rather than patient driven sex-concordance preferences.5 As gender equity trends in ophthalmology continue to evolve, it is crucial to examine the impact of gender dynamics between physicians and patients on patient care outcomes.

In our current study, we aimed to study the relationship between surgeon-patient sex concordance and postoperative outcomes for both male and female patients who underwent cataract surgery to assess whether sex concordance has impact on patient outcomes.

Materials and Methods

All cataract surgeries performed at Northwestern Memorial Hospital in Chicago, IL, from March 1, 2007, to March 1, 2023, were identified retrospectively using the Northwestern Medicine Electronic Data Warehouse (EDW). The data provided by EDW was de-identified; therefore, the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board determined that the study was exempt from further IRB approval or review. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients who underwent surgery of both eyes at separate occasions were included twice in the study cohort. In cases where multiple complications occurred during a single procedure, each complication was counted separately. Surgical records were excluded if self-reported sex was not documented in the chart for either the patient or surgeon.

The primary outcome was the presence of any perioperative complications from cataract surgery. The list of surgeries and their associated complications was generated through a comprehensive EDW search using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) billing codes and terminology found in patient charts and surgeon notes. Surgical complications were defined as one or more of the following within 30 days of surgery: endophthalmitis, choroidal hemorrhage, posterior capsular tear, retinal detachment, vitreous loss, retained lens fragments, return to operating room for vitrectomy within 30 days, or any relevant complication listed in the physician notes. A complete list of included postoperative complications is presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Complications were identified using (1) ICD-9 codes detailed in Supplemental Table 2; (2) ICD-10 codes detailed in Supplemental Table 3; (3) and CPT codes assessing vitrectomies within 30 days detailed in Supplemental Table 4.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software, version 4.4.1. We present counts and frequencies to describe sex concordance (male surgeon and male patient; female surgeon and female patient) and sex discordance (male surgeon and female patient; female surgeon and male patient) across all surgeries for each year 2007 through 2023. We also estimated the prevalence of postoperative complications and procedures by sex concordance and sex discordance, combined across all years. Linear regression was used to examine trends in sex concordance and discordance over time. Differences in trends over time by concordance and discordance were evaluated using interaction terms in the models. We excluded year 2007 and 2023 from the regression models because the number of surgeries recorded in those years does not represent a full calendar year.

We used generalized estimating equations (GEEs) to examine (1) the association between any postoperative complication and patient/surgeon sex concordance/discordance and (2) the association between postoperative complications and surgeon sex. The presence of a postoperative complication or a corresponding procedure performed was modeled as binomial with a logit link function. We used an exchangeable correlation structure to account for multiple surgeries within a patient. GEE models examining the relationship between complication rate and patient-surgeon sex concordance or discordance were stratified by patient sex. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals are reported from GEE models. A two-tailed p-value ≤0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

From 2007 to 2023, 30,804 cataract surgeries were performed on 19,178 patients. We excluded 402 surgeries due to missing sex data for either surgeon or patient. A total of 18,310 surgeries were performed on 11,343 unique female patients, and a total of 12,092 surgeries were performed on 7687 unique male patients. The procedures were performed by 124 unique surgeons.

There were 14,689 sex concordant procedures (43.82%, 8392 male surgeons with male patients and 6297 female surgeons with female patients) and 15,713 sex discordant procedures (51.68%, 12,013 male surgeons with female patients and 3700 female surgeons with male patients). Number of total surgeries performed generally increased each year. For example, in 2008, 825 surgeries were performed whereas in 2022, 2363 surgeries were performed. The most common pairing throughout the data collection period was male surgeon/female patient discordant pair whereas the least common was the female surgeon/male patient discordant pair. The most common complication experienced among the 1086 individuals with complications, regardless of sex concordance, was retinal detachment (39.0%), although we could not confirm the percentage that was present prior to or following cataract extraction, followed by return for vitrectomy (21.8%), posterior capsular tear (20.6%), retained lens fragments (14.6%), and endophthalmitis (8.6%).

The number of anterior vitrectomies is likely underreported in this data set partly due to underreporting during the procedure notes or the use of a “vitrectomy” code instead of specifying anterior vitrectomy.

No significant association was found between any complication and sex concordance among female patients (p = 1.00). Female patients that have a male surgeon have the same odds [OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.82–1.21] of experiencing a complication compared to female patients with a female surgeon. Similarly, no significant association was found between any complication and sex concordance among male patients [OR, 1.02; 95% CI 0.83–1.26; p = 0.84]. No significant association was found between complication and surgeon gender (p = 0.97). Male surgeons have the same odds 1.00 [OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.87–1.16] of having a patient with complications compared to female surgeons.

Discussion

In this retrospective review, we found no significant association between surgeon-patient sex concordance or discordance and post-surgical complications in cataract surgeries. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine surgeon-patient sex concordant outcomes in ophthalmology.

Overall, modern cataract surgery is a safe procedure with relatively low intraoperative and postoperative complication rates. The results of our study yielded an overall complication rate of 3.5% for all cataract surgeries from 2007 to 2023, similar to previously reported data (0–5%) though we acknowledge the inherent limitations of a retrospective, electronic medical record (EMR) based study that may impact our results.6,7 In our cohort, retinal detachment was found to be the most common post-operative complication (1.4%), while the most commonly reported intra-operative complication was rupture of the posterior lens capsule (0.7%) with rates varying in the literature between <1% to 4.1%.7,8 Our results did not find an association between cataract surgery complication rates and sex discordant procedures supporting the safety of cataract surgery independent of surgeon or patient sex.

Surgical outcomes across general surgical specialties have been shown to be impacted by patient-physician sex concordance.3 In a major analysis of patient–surgeon sex concordance and post-operative outcomes, Wallis et al showed that surgeon-patient sex discordance was positively associated with adverse postoperative outcomes and death.3 Worse outcomes were observed for female patients treated by male surgeons when compared to female patients treated by female surgeons.3 Additionally, the study found that male patients had overall higher rates of postoperative events compared to females. There were relatively small differences in rates of complications for male patients treated by male and female surgeons. However, when adjusted for relevant variables, female patients treated by male surgeons had consistently higher complication rates when compared to female patients treated by female surgeons.3 Wallis et al concluded that these results may be due in part to unconscious bias that warrants further sociological exploration of factors impacting the patient-physician relationship. A previous study analyzing outcomes in interventional cardiology did not describe any difference in outcomes associated with sex discordant physician-patient relationships.9

In the recently published study by Kang et al, authors demonstrate that surgeon-patient sex concordance in cataract surgery is not randomly distributed but varies overtime, with patterns reflecting changes in the relative surgical volume of male and female surgeons.5 Our study extends this line of work by evaluating whether such concordance is associated with differences in surgical outcomes. In this context, our findings indicate that although surgeon-patient sex concordance reflects underlying structural and workforce dynamics within ophthalmology, it is not associated with postoperative complication risks, suggesting that surgeon-patient sex discordance does not have a considerable clinical impact on patient safety.

Cataract surgery is often associated with less variability compared to surgical procedures in other specialties as evidenced by low overall complication rates (<5% across several studies).6,10,11 It is not surprising that based on our results, we have demonstrated no significant differences in complication rates with sex concordant and discordant procedures. Most complications related to cataract surgery are of a technical nature and less likely to be influenced by the physician-patient relationship that are more likely to be impacted by sex concordance or discordance. Further, our results revealed no difference in complication rates between male and female surgeons, suggesting comparable surgical performances across sexes within our cohort. While not directly assessed in this study, this finding is relevant in the context of broader efforts within ophthalmology to promote equity in training. We have shown that irrespective of surgeon sex, complication rates during cataract surgery are comparable, including when patient and surgeon sexes are discordant.

The effect of sex-discordant physician-patient relationships, however, are important to study as they are not limited only to surgical outcomes but the overall delivery of competent care as well. A study in the post-operative cardiology setting found patients were less likely to report the severity of their pain in front of male assessors compared to female assessors.12 Additionally, a study among patients in Florida undergoing hospitalization after myocardial infarction demonstrated higher mortality rates for female patients treated by male physicians while mortality rates were similar for male and female patients treated by female physicians.13 Whether these findings are related to patient-driven sentiments that hinder true expression of symptoms or inherent provider biases, sex discordant patient-physician relationships may garner less rapport compared to sex concordant relationships. Although our results revealed there was no association between cataract surgery complication rates and sex discordant procedures, further studies are warranted to evaluate outcomes in a non-surgical ambulatory ophthalmology clinic setting as well.

Our study data is subject to the limitations of self-reporting and a retrospective review. Patient-surgeon sex-concordance was evaluated only among patients and surgeons for whom self-reported sex of male and female was available. While this may understate the number of surgeries for which the association between sex concordance and complication rates could be assessed, we found only a small number (approximately one percent) of cases for which this data was unavailable. The billing codes, diagnosis codes, and manual audit of the post-operative notes were limited by potential human error. It is possible that CPT or ICD codes beyond the ones evaluated were not captured by a review of the postoperative note and may contribute to an underestimation of complication rates. On the contrary, it is possible that some of the events defined as a postoperative complication may have occurred independent of the procedure; for instance, if a patient had a planned vitrectomy for retinal detachment repair within 30 days of cataract surgery. Our data found that “return for vitrectomy” was a more common complication than retained lens fragments and endophthalmitis. A limitation associated with this finding is that the data could not capture specific indications for vitrectomy. It is likely that patients underwent a vitrectomy for retained lens fragments and/or endophthalmitis, but this is not reflected in the data and thus may account for discrepancies in our reported complication rates of cataract surgery compared to previous literature.

Another limitation of the study is that it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Additionally, this study did not assess patient-reported outcomes such as satisfaction or postoperative visual quality, which is a relevant dimension of care that is not captured by complication rates alone. Lack of surgeon-level data is another limitation of the study. Although 124 surgeons performed the surgeries, due to variations in surgical volume, subspecialty focus, and patient load, surgeon-level data was not included in the analysis.

Further, our data does not capture the inherent difficulty of the cataract procedure and the level of complexity of each surgery. Finally, our study only accounts for those patients with postoperative complications and corresponding procedures within 30 days of cataract surgery and not those who presented beyond this time. Despite these limitations, the strengths of this study include its strong sample size and data collection over a 16-year period.

Conclusion

The results of our study demonstrate no significant association between surgical complication rates and surgeon-patient sex discordance following cataract surgery. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the relationship between surgeon gender, patient gender, and postoperative outcomes in other ophthalmic surgeries as well as to assess outcomes in non-surgical settings. We believe the results from these studies will be valuable to advocate for evidence-based recommendations that guide optimal patient care.
