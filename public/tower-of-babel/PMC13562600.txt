Role of doping concentration on the RWGS performance of Cu doped CeO2 catalysts
Authors: Mukesh Khandla, Prerna Vinchhi, Rutvi Mistry, Manish Rawat, Shantanu Anand Shah, Jogender Singh, Mansi Negi, Rajaram Bal, Ranjan Pati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction efficiently transforms CO2 into CO at high temperatures, ensuring high CO selectivity while preventing the formation of methane as a byproduct. Nanocrystalline ceria-based materials are synthesised by using a modified co-precipitation method. This method utilizes the molecular water in the precursor materials to aid the hydroxylation process, which helps minimize the risk of agglomeration resulting from hydrogen bonding. The as prepared catalysts were calcined at high temperature to get the crystalline materials. The freshly synthesized and calcined catalysts were analysed using various characterisation techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The results suggest that enhancing the copper concentration in Cu-doped CeO2 significantly improves its catalytic activity at low and moderate temperatures. Among all the catalysts 25 mol % copper doped CeO2 gives extraordinary activity for RWGS reaction having CO2 conversion of ~ 50% at 800 ˚C.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53547-8.Keywords: Modified co-precipitation, CO2 hydrogenation, Carbon monoxide, Copper promoter, Reverse water gas shift reactionSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The atmospheric concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2), a major greenhouse gas, is rising significantly due to the combustion of fossil fuels. The increase in carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration in the atmosphere is expected to have a profound impact on the Earth’s weather and environmental equilibrium1. Higher concentrations of CO2 are responsible for the greenhouse effect, retaining heat in the atmosphere and leading to global warming. This change in temperature can disturb long-standing climate systems, leading to an increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events like hurricanes, droughts, and heavy rainfall. Additionally, altered temperature and precipitation patterns can affect ecosystems, biodiversity, and agricultural productivity, ultimately influencing the balance of natural habitats and human societies. Therefore, it is crucial not only to minimize CO2 emissions but also to convert CO2 into useful and valuable products through recycling. The total carbon content in global CO2 is estimated to be ten times higher than that in coal, oil, and natural gas combined2. The use of CO2 as a substitute for fossil fuels in the production of C1 chemicals is increasingly important. This shift is essential not only for mitigating a range of environmental challenges, such as climate change and air pollution, but also for addressing the ongoing global energy crisis. By converting CO2 into valuable resources, one can reduce the reliance on finite fossil fuel reserves, promote sustainable energy practices, and contribute to a cleaner, more sustainable future. In recent years, catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 to CO has emerged as a highly promising method for CO2 utilization. Various catalytic processes have been utilized to activate and convert CO2 into valuable products and fuels through several reactions, including CO2 hydrogenation3, CO2 reforming4,5, and oxidation using CO2 as an oxidant6. The hydrogenation of CO2 using molecular hydrogen can produce various compounds, including CH4 through the Sabatier or methanation reaction7–9, methanol (CH3OH) via selective hydrogenation10,11 and ultimately the direct formation of high-value C2+ chemicals and CO via the reverse water–gas shift reaction. Among various methods, CO2 hydrogenation through the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction (Eq. 1) has emerged as one of the most promising approaches for CO2 utilization. This process converts CO2 into the more reactive CO, which can then be utilized in the well-established Fischer-Tropsch process to produce essential industrial compounds such as alkanes, olefins, alcohols, aldehydes, dimethyl ether, gasoline, and long-chain hydrocarbons12–16.

The catalytic conversion of CO2 via the RWGS reaction is essential for optimizing the H2/CO ratio in syngas, which is later used for hydrocarbon synthesis. Additionally, the RWGS reaction proceeds in the opposite direction of the water-gas shift (WGS) reaction, a process of great industrial significance. This endothermic reaction (ΔH = + 41.2 kJ/mol) requires a high operating temperature of around 400–800 °C to achieve optimal CO2 conversion and maximize CO yield17. The RWGS reaction mechanisms are classified into two types. The first is the redox mechanism, where hydrogen functions as a reducing agent without directly contributing to intermediate formation. The second involves the formation of formate as an intermediate, generated through the adsorption and hydrogenation of CO2. In this process, formate eventually decomposes and produces CO as the final product.

In recent years, numerous catalysts have been developed for the RWGS reaction. Such as noble metal18,19, supported metal oxide20, transition metal carbide (TMC)21 catalysts. Dae Han Kim et al. synthesised an aqueous solution based method to produce unsupported iron oxide nanoparticle and the corresponding CO2 conversion was 31% at 600°C22. In another work, Yulian He et al. prepared MgO nanosheets and CO2 conversion observed was 50% at 800°C23. Ching-Shiun Chen et al. synthesised Cu/SiO2 and K2O promoted Cu/SiO2 based catalysts by using impregnating method. It was observed that the addition of potassium oxide on Cu/SiO2 gave 2.4 times higher activity than Cu/SiO2 at 600°C24. Sung Su Kim et al. studied RWGS reaction at high temperature by using Pt supported Al2O3 and TiO2 and found the CO2 conversion of 48% and 42% respectively at 875°C25. Jingyun Ye et al. prepared bimetallic Pd–In/SiO2 based catalysts and obtained a conversion of ~ 29.4% at 600°C26. Dae Han Kim et al. studied barium zinconate based perovskite doped with Y and Zn metal catalysts (BaZr0.8Y0.16Zn0.04O3) via solid state reaction and found the CO2 conversion of 37.5% at 600°C27. Wang Lu hui et al. synthesise nickel-based nanoparticle via coprecipitation method and observed CO2 conversion 42% at 750°C28.

Noble-metal catalysts supported on oxide materials have long been considered among the most effective systems for the reverse water–gas shift reaction due to their excellent activity and preference for CO formation29–32. Metals such as platinum, palladium, gold, rhodium, ruthenium, and iridium supported on CeO2 or TiO2 are particularly effective at activating hydrogen, which allows them to operate efficiently at relatively low temperatures33–38. However, the strong ability of noble metals to dissociate hydrogen and bind CO can also become a disadvantage, as it may prevent CO from desorbing easily and instead promote further hydrogenation to methane or methanol39. As a result, controlling CO adsorption strength and hydrogenation behaviour is essential for improving CO selectivity in noble-metal-based RWGS catalysts33,35,38. Moreover, the high cost, limited availability, and complex preparation methods associated with noble metals significantly restrict their practical large-scale use. These limitations have encouraged increasing research interest in developing efficient non-noble-metal catalysts as more economical and scalable alternatives.

CeO2 is a widely studied rare earth metal oxide having a face-centered cubic (FCC) fluorite structure. It possesses unique redox properties that make it valuable for enhancing catalytic performance. In a reducing environment, CeO2 can undergo surface oxygen reduction, leading to the formation of oxygen vacancies40, additionally, ceria demonstrates a strong interaction with active metals and can easily have a transition between Ce3+ and Ce4+ states41. These vacancies generated play a critical role in catalytic processes, particularly in catalytic reduction reactions. Moreover, CeO2 can undergo reduction by H2 to produce Ce2O3, resulting in the formation of numerous oxygen vacancies31. The oxygen vacancies formed can react with CO2, leading to the release of carbon monoxide CO through RWGS reaction in realistic condition42,43. Therefore, CeO2 as support to catalytic RWGS reaction has recently been investigated. Pure CeO2 typically exhibits poor catalytic performance in the RWGS reaction due to its limited ability to generate oxygen vacancies2. Incorporation of metal species into or onto the ceria lattice markedly enhances its reducibility and promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies, which are key sites for CO2 adsorption and activation44. Consequently, transition-metal-modified/supported CeO2 catalysts have gained significant interest, as appropriate metal dopants can simultaneously improve activity and CO selectivity45. Mesoporous ceria supports further benefit catalyst performance by enabling uniform dispersion of metal species across the surface, thereby providing a high density of accessible active sites.46 Under reducing atmospheres, CeO2 readily forms abundant surface oxygen vacancies, which not only facilitate CO2 activation but also stabilize metal particles through strong meta-support interactions, effectively suppressing sintering and particle aggregation.47–49 Among transition metals, copper-based oxide catalysts are particularly attractive for RWGS because of their high activity and favourable CO selectivity.50 This is because of the fact that Cu efficiently dissociates hydrogen at elevated temperatures while exhibiting relatively weak CO adsorption compared with noble metals such as Pt and Pd, enabling facile CO desorption and minimizing over-hydrogenation.51 Cu-CeO2 catalysts are widely considered as efficient non-noble metal catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation reactions, including methanol synthesis and the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction. Their good catalytic performance mainly comes from the combined effect of copper and ceria. Copper species help to activate hydrogen, while the ceria support activates CO2 through oxygen vacancies on its surface44. Many studies have shown that the most active reaction sites are located at the interface between copper and ceria. At the interface, factors such as copper particle/crystallite size, copper oxidation state (Cu⁰, Cu⁺, Cu²⁺), and the amount of cerium defects (Ce³⁺ and oxygen vacancies) play an important role in determining catalytic activity.52 Numerous studies have demonstrated that highly dispersed Cu species on CeO2 significantly enhance RWGS activity by maximizing the density of Cu-CeO2 interfacial sites and strengthening metal–support interactions. In contrast, the formation of large Cu particles or bulk Cu-Ce mixed oxide phases diminishes interfacial contact and leads to inferior catalytic performance51. The CO2 conversion achieved over Cu-CeO2-based catalysts in the RWGS reaction varies considerably across reported studies, reflecting the strong influence of catalyst preparation, operating temperature, and gas hourly space velocity. For instance, Yu et al. achieved a CO2 conversion of 24.2% at 600 °C using a co-precipitated Cu-CeO2 catalyst operated at a GHSV of 60,000 h⁻¹ with an H2/CO2 feed ratio of 3:153. At lower reaction temperatures, Ronda-Lloret et al. observed a CO₂ conversion of 16% at 400 °C for catalysts prepared by wet impregnation54, while Dai et al. reported an improved conversion of 31.3% at the same temperature using a hard-template synthesis route55. In contrast, Zhou et al. obtained a relatively low conversion of approximately 2.8% at 400 °C over an 8% Cu/CeO2−ẟ catalyst prepared by a hard-template method at a GHSV of 60,000 h⁻¹39. At elevated temperatures, Liu et al. reported CO2 conversions of around 40% at 800 °C for Cu-CeO2 catalysts synthesized by wet impregnation56. Additionally, Sala et al. achieved a conversion of approximately 16% at 600 °C using a Cu-Fe/CeO₂ catalyst prepared by impregnation57, while Zhang et al. reported about 10% CO₂ conversion at 600 °C for Cu-CeO2 nanocube catalysts tested under a high GHSV of 300,000 h⁻¹58. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that RWGS performance over Cu-CeO2 systems is governed not only by reaction temperature but also by synthesis strategy and space velocity, highlighting the need for careful catalyst design to maximize CO2 conversion.

There are several methods explored for the synthesis of CeO2 based catalysts, such as hard template method for CO methanation59, hydrothermal for oxidation of nitrogen oxide (NOx)60, reverse micro-emulation61 and flame synthesis62 method for water gas shit reaction (WGS), combustion for oxidation of hydrocarbons63, microwave assisted method for sensing application64, and co-precipitation method making electrolyte material for solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) application65. Co-precipitation technique is a simple, one step and affordable synthesis technique among all. While co-precipitation is a well-established route for Cu-CeO2 catalysts, recent literature has primarily focused on aqueous-phase methods using precipitants like NaOH or Na2CO3, often leading to challenges in controlling copper speciation and preventing the formation of large, segregated CuO crystallites during drying and calcination.66.

In the current work, nanoparticle of copper doped cerium oxide RWGS catalysts were synthesized using a modified co-precipitation method. In this method, molecular water present in the precursor materials was used to generate hydroxyl ions, which facilitate the formation of metal hydroxides. Upon heating, these hydroxides decompose into oxide nanoparticles. This study establishes a clear correlation between copper doping level, oxygen vacancy concentration, and RWGS activity, demonstrating that an optimal Cu loading of 25 mol% maximizes Cu-CeO2 interfacial synergy and minimizes the activation energy. By evaluating catalytic performance without pre-reduction, the intrinsic redox activation behavior of Cu-CeO2 under reaction conditions is revealed. As a result, the catalyst achieves high CO2 conversion (~ 50%) with complete CO selectivity and effectively suppressed methanation at elevated temperatures. Several characterization techniques such as XRD, HR-TEM, Raman, H2-TPR, XPS and BET were employed to analyse the synthesized catalysts. The role of dopant concentration over ceria on the RWGS catalytic activity has also been presented.

Experimental process

Preparation of catalysts

To synthesize the copper doped ceria, cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce (NO3)3.6H2O, 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich) and copper nitrate pentahydrate (Cu (NO3)2.5H2O, 98%, Sigma Aldrich) were used as cerium and copper sources respectively. Triethylamine (N(C2H5)3, Fisher Chemicals) was used as the precipitating agent, while anhydrous ethanol (99.9%, Fisher Chemicals) as a solvent. A series of copper-supported ceria samples were prepared at different copper-to-cerium (Cu: Ce) ratios: 5:95, 10:90, 15:85, 20:80, 25:75, and 50:50, which corresponding to molar percentages based on metal content. For instance, a 5 mol % Cu doped CeO2 corresponds to a Cu: Ce ratio of 5:95, and so on. The samples were denoted as CeO2 (pure ceria), Cu0.05Ce0.95, Cu0.10Ce0.90, Cu0.15Ce0.85, Cu0.20Ce0.80, Cu0.25Ce0.75, and Cu0.50Ce0.50, representing copper mol % of 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 50, respectively. All precipitation reactions were conducted at room temperature. A 100 mL solution of 0.4 M triethylamine in ethanol was slowly added dropwise at a rate of 2–3 mL/min using a burette into a 0.1 M ethanol-based mixed metal nitrate solution placed in an Erlenmeyer flask under continuous stirring. The stirring continued for one hour after the precipitation was completed. The formed precipitates were then collected by vacuum filtration. The completion of the precipitation was checked by adding a small amount of filter with a 1 (M) KOH solution. The precipitates were thoroughly washed with ethanol to remove the residual alcohol, unreacted triethylamine, and other byproducts. Then the precipitate was dried in air for 24 h at room temperature. Finally, the synthesized powders were calcined in air at 350 °C for 3 h with a heating rate of 10 °C min− 1.

Characterization of catalysts

The calcined powders were grinded into fine powder using a mortar pastel. The crystallographic study of the synthesized calcined material was performed using XRD with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5405 Å, PANalytical X’Pert Pro) and Raman spectroscopy (InVia Renishaw, UK) was used for further analysis in the wavelength range of 350–700 cm− 1, with a laser power of 532 nm. The crystallite size of the material was calculated using the Scherrer formula: Here, represents the shape factor of crystallite, λ represents the incident wavelength, β is the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM), and θ is the diffraction angle in degrees. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEOL 2100) was employed to analyse the particle size and surface morphology. Additionally, the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) gas adsorption method was used to determine the surface area of the synthesized calcined samples. Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was conducted on a Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920 instrument in the temperature range of 50–900 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻¹, using helium as the carrier gas. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the oxidation states of the prepared catalyst, and the spectra were recorded using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer.

RWGS performance test

The RWGS reaction was conducted to evaluate the catalytic performance of the synthesized copper doped ceria catalysts. In this investigation, the RWGS reaction was conducted without performing any pre-reduction of the catalysts. Catalytic tests were conducted at atmospheric pressure in a continuous-flow fixed-bed quartz reactor with an inner diameter of 5 mm and corresponding to a total internal reactor volume of 7.06 cm3. About 100 mg of catalyst was placed in the quartz tube reactor, which was subsequently positioned in a tubular furnace for optimal heating. The reaction temperatures were accurately measured using a thermocouple inserted directly into the catalyst bed and observed using a programmable temperature controller throughout the experiment. The reaction temperature was systematically varied between 400 and 800 °C. A mixture of CO2 and H2 gas feed (1:1 ratio) was introduced to the catalysts at a constant flow rate of 31 mL min− 1, corresponding to a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 18,600 mL g− 1 h− 1. The resulting gas products were analysed using an online gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies 7890 B, equipped with HP-PLOT Q and Al2O3 plot columns, as well as thermal conductivity (TCD) and flame ionization (FID) detectors). Result and discussion

X-ray diffraction

The crystallographic analysis was done using X-ray diffraction and Fig. 1 represents the diffraction pattern of the synthesised samples, including pure ceria and ceria doped with 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 50 mol% copper. The XRD pattern demonstrates the presence of intense (111) peak at 28.59˚ and other low intensity peaks at 33.16˚, 47.59˚, 56.43˚, 59.08˚, 69.50˚, 76.73˚,79.12˚ correspond to (200), (220), (311), (222), (400), (331), (420) lattice planes respectively. All diffraction peaks are consistent with a cubic fluorite-type structure, with d-values matching to that of CeO2 (ICDD PDF No. 34–0394). The diffraction patterns of pure and doped ceria with Cu concentration up to 25 mol % exhibit the cubic ceria diffraction peaks without the presence of any other secondary phases. It may have two possible explanations; one is that the CuO species are only on the CeO2 surface, forming a thin layer that does not alter the CeO2 structure, which are not detected in the XRD pattern. Other explanation, that Cu2+ replace Ce4+ within the ceria lattice, here No distinct peaks corresponding to Cu or CuO were observed, even at higher Cu loadings, suggesting that the Cu nanoparticles are extremely small and well-dispersed on the CeO2 support62,67. In copper doped ceria, the peak shifts were observed as the copper concentration was increased, which may be attributed to the formation of oxygen vacancies that changed the lattice parameters. The presence of copper oxide (CuO) was detected in the sample made with 50 mol% copper at 35.57˚ and 38.59˚ (JCPDS No.892531) which corresponds to (-111) and (111) lattice planes. This may result from the presence of a lesser amount of CuO as a secondary phase, along with the formation of a monolayer or sub-monolayer of CuO on the CeO2 surface. Alternatively, CuO might develop as a crystalline multilayer on top of the CeO2 surface. The crystallite size of ceria and mixed oxides was calculated using the Scherrer equation using (111) plane, which ranges from ~ 5 to 18 nm. The X-ray diffractograms of the used catalysts of Cu0.05Ce0.95, Cu0.15Ce0.85,and Cu0.25Ce0.75 (supplementary Fig S1(a)) reveals how the typical diffraction peaks of CeO2 were maintained after the catalytic tests. After the catalytic reaction, it could be seen from Fig. S1a that copper species existed mainly in the form of CuO and metallic Cu. It can also be seen from XRD that a distinct diffraction peak appears at 2θ = ~43.8°, which can be indexed to the (111) plane of metallic copper (JCPDS No. 04-0836). This indicates that part of the copper oxide (CuO) species has been reduced to Cu during RWGS. The relatively weak and broadened nature of this peak suggests that metallic Cu is present as very small, well-dispersed particles within the mesoporous CeO2. This result indicated that the surface Cu2+ species was reduced to Cu0 in the activation process52.

RAMAN spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy is powerful optical method for examining the structural properties of ceramics due to its sensitivity to lattice defects within the material. Raman analysis of both doped and un-doped ceria catalysts are presented in the Fig. 2. The Raman spectra were recorded in the wavelength range of 200 to 800 cm⁻¹ using a 514 nm visible laser at room temperature. CeO2 shows a distinct Raman band around 466 cm⁻¹ (F2g mode), which is related to the symmetric vibrational breathing of oxygen around Ce4+ ions in its fluorite-type crystal lattice. As shown in Fig. 2a, only diffraction peaks corresponding to CeO2 are observed for the all the prepared catalyst, while no peaks related to Cu, Cu2O, or CuO are detected. This indicates that copper species are highly dispersed at low Cu loading. With increasing copper content at 50%, Raman shift occurs at 285 cm[–1 that belongs to Cu2+ emerged in Cu0.50Ce0.5068 As shown in Fig. 2b, the lattice disorder band (D band) associated with lattice defects, and appears near ~ 620 cm− 1. The F2g band is clearly detected in all spectra, however, as the copper concentration increased, the F2g band show red shift. This shift can be due to the replacement of small amount of Ce4+ ions with dopant Cu2+ ions resulting in to the modification of the lattice parameters and the oxygen-metal bond lengths, subsequently influence the lattice vibrations. The band observed around 550 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the oxygen vacancies formed to maintain the charge neutrality in the material. On the other hand, the typical bands of CuO species are not detected, confirming the high dispersion of the copper species, as indicated in the XRD data (Fig. 1). The Raman spectra (Figure. S1(b)) of treated/spent catalysts show the stronger CuO Raman bands, which appear after reaction that indicate the growth of crystalline CuO domains due to copper sintering, which is consistent with XRD results. The Raman spectra of the spent catalysts display markedly intensified CuO related vibrational bands compared to the fresh one. The enhancement and sharpening of these bands indicate the formation of more crystalline and larger CuO domains as a result of copper sintering during RWGS operation. These Raman features are fully consistent with the appearance of metallic Cu0 and CuO diffraction peaks observed in XRD. This collectively confirms the structural growth and segregation of copper species after RWGS reaction69.

H2-TPR analysis

H2-TPR was used to study how easily copper and ceria can be reduced and to explain why no hydrogen pre-treatment was used. Ceria can easily change its oxidation state and form oxygen vacancies at high temperatures. Under RWGS conditions (400–800 °C), the reaction gases partially reduce Cu2+ species, creating active Cu+/Cu0 sites during the reaction. Therefore, the catalyst was activated directly under reaction conditions to preserve realistic copper-ceria interfacial structures. Figure 3a shows the H2-TPR profile of fresh and RWGS treated copper-doped ceria catalysts, which are commonly used to investigate the reduction behaviour of metal oxides. CuO is usually reduced by hydrogen at temperatures between 200 and 300 °C 42, which is within the lower end of the RWGS reaction range. This means that copper species can become active under the reaction conditions itself, and therefore an additional pre-reduction step is not necessary70 while CeO2 exhibits two distinct reduction peaks: one around 450 °C, attributed to surface oxygen, and another above 800 °C, corresponding to bulk oxygen reduction71. In the copper-doped ceria samples, two prominent reduction peaks below 300 °C, labelled α and β, were observed. Notably, as the fresh copper loading catalysts, these peaks shifted toward lower temperatures. For instance, in the spent Cu0.25Ce0.75 nanospheres, the α and β peaks appeared at 217 °C and 248 °C, respectively, whereas in the fresh Cu0.25Ce0.75 sample, they were observed at 149 °C and 211 °C. The α peak corresponds to the reduction of highly dispersed CuO species, while the β peak associated to the reduction of copper species incorporated into the CeO2 lattice. The detection of reduction peaks below 300 °C indicates that both highly dispersed copper can be reduced directly under reaction conditions. This supports that the catalysts can be reduced without any separate H2 pre-reduction step, forming a Cu-Oₓ-Ce solid solution. Additionally, the peaks for RWGS treated Cu0.25Ce0.75, γ and δ appeared at 463 and 840 and 407 and 827 for fresh Cu0.25Ce0.75 respectively, while the γ and δ peaks are attributed to the reduction of ceria’s surface and bulk lattice oxygen respectively. The fresh Cu/CeO2 catalysts show reduction at lower temperatures than the RWGS treated ones, indicating that they are more easily reducible before the RWGS reaction. This shift indicates that the fresh catalysts have more reactive oxygen species and stronger Cu-CeO2 interactions, allowing them to activate quickly during RWGS. This suggests that strong metal-support (CeO2) interactions in the fresh samples promote hydrogen activation and facilitate the reduction of surface oxygen species. After the RWGS reaction, the reduction peaks shift to higher temperatures, which could be due to the surface modifications such as sintering of Cu nanoparticle, or the loss of active oxygen sites during syngas production. These changes reduce the availability of easily reducible species and weaken the interaction between copper and the ceria support72,73. Overall, The H2-TPR results show that Cu-CeO2 catalysts can be reduced directly under RWGS conditions, leading to in situ formation of active Cu-CeO2 interfacial sites. This supports evaluating the catalysts without prior H2 pre-reduction under realistic conditions.

BET analysis

The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms, representing the amount of N2 adsorbed as a function of relative pressure at − 196 °C, are presented in Fig. 3b. Based on IUPAC classification, the isotherms correspond to type IV with a hysteresis loop, which is the characteristics of mesoporous structures. Figure 3b compares the CeO2 sample with doped CeO2 containing various Cu loadings. The BET surface areas (SBET), pore volume and pore diameter summarized in Table 1, indicate a value of about 122 m²/g for pure CeO2, while the Cu-containing samples show lower values in the range of 27–120 m²/g. The pore diameter was recorded within 4–10 nm, confirming that the materials are predominantly mesoporous in nature. The BET surface area of CuO/CeO₂ catalysts initially decreases sharply at low Cu loading (5 mol%) due to pore blocking and coverage of the ceria surface by highly dispersed CuO species. With increasing Cu content (10–25 mol%), the surface area slightly increases, likely due to a more uniform distribution of CuO and the formation of small interparticle voids, although the total surface area remains lower than that of pure CeO2 because some pores may still remain blocked. These observations are consistent with XRD, Raman, and XPS results, which confirm that CuO is well-dispersed on the ceria surface without lattice substitution74,75.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM)

The morphological analysis of pure CeO2 and 25% Cu-doped CeO2 was conducted using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), and the corresponding images are presented in Fig. 4. The high-magnification image in Fig. 4b clearly demonstrates that the particles are crystalline. The lattice fringes are attributed to the most dominant (111) lattice plane, with 0.3119 nm between the fringes. This value aligns well with the d-value calculated for the (111) plane from the X-ray diffraction data of the crystalline cubic fluorite phase of CeO2. The pure CeO2 nanoparticles exhibit a particle size of approximately 7–8 nm and were found to be polycrystalline, as indicated by the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern shown in Fig. 4c. The HRTEM analysis of Cu0.25Ce0.75 sample revealed a spherical morphology with a particle diameter of about ~ 4.8 nm. CuO/CeO2 particles exhibited a uniform and reduced particle size compared to pure CeO2, indicating improved dispersion, which is likely due to the co-precipitation method used to prepare the materials, which is advantageous for catalytic reactions. The lattice fringe spacing of CuO/CeO2 sample were measured at 2.77 Å and 3.12 Å (Fig. 4e), corresponding to the (200) and (111) crystal planes, respectively. The SAED pattern is indexed to the crystalline cubic fluorite structure (Fig. 4f). In contrast, HRTEM images of the spent Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst show the presence of large agglomerated particles (Figure S2, Supporting Information) indicating that particle sintering occurred during the high-temperature RWGS reaction (particle diameter is ~ 11–12 nm). This is mainly due to the presence of relatively large Cu particles that interact weakly with the CeO2 support. Because of this weak interaction, Cu species can easily move on the ceria surface at high temperatures, leading to particle merging and growth into larger Cu-rich clusters. As a result, the contact between Cu and CeO2 decreases, which reduces the number of active Cu+ interfacial sites and lowers the structural stability of the catalyst under reaction conditions.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

In order to investigate the surface composition and oxidation states of the synthesized Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst, x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed. Peak analysis and deconvolution were performed using a Gaussian fitting model. The survey spectrum for the as prepared and RWGS treated Cu0.25Ce0.75 are shown in Figure S1, which clearly displays the characteristic peaks of Ce 3d, Cu 2p, and O 1s. The Ce 3d spectra of both fresh and RWGS treated catalysts are split into two spin–orbit components, 3d5/2 and 3d3/2, labeled as v and u, respectively (Fig. 5a and b). The deconvoluted spectra revealed the coexistence of Ce3+ and Ce4+ species in the catalyst. Peaks denoted as ν’(883.6 eV) and µ’ (902.44 eV)correspond to Ce3+, whereas ν(882.3), ν″(888.8 eV), ν’’’ (898.6 eV), µ(900.6 eV), µ’’’ (916.8), and µ″(907.43) represent Ce4+. The prominent peaks at approximately 898.60 eV (ν’’’) and 916.800 eV (µ’’’) are typical of Ce4+ and correspond to transitions from the 3d104f0 configuration to the final state76,77. The formation of Ce3+ is closely associated with oxygen vacancies, which are promoted by the addition of copper. This effect is more pronounced in nanoparticles due to their high surface-to-volume ratio, which increases the number of surface atoms and reduces bulk coordination. The ability of ceria to switch oxidation states enhances its reducibility and facilitates electron transfer78,79. XPS analysis (Fig. 5a and b) of the as prepared and RWGS catalyst showed a decrease in Ce4+ and an increase in Ce3+ after the reaction, highlighting the role of oxygen vacancies in enabling the reversible Ce4+/Ce3+ redox cycle necessary for the RWGS reaction. Additionally, Cu–CeO2 nanoparticles exhibit a higher Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio, supporting their superior redox properties and catalytic performance. Figure 5c and d present the high-resolution Cu 2p spectra of the as prepared and RWGS treated Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalysts. The fresh sample displays Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks at 933.28 eV and 953.28 eV, respectively, along with distinct shake-up satellite peaks at ~ 942.68 eV and 962.06 eV, indicative of Cu2+80. These satellite peak features arise from charge transfer between the Cu 3d orbitals and surrounding O 2p orbitals, confirming the presence of Cu in the + 2-oxidation state. In addition to that Cu LMM Auger spectroscopy was used to determine the surface oxidation states of copper because Cu 2p XPS alone cannot clearly distinguish between Cu+ and metallic Cu. The fresh calcined Cu-CeO2 catalyst shows only one Cu LMM peak at about 571 eV, which corresponds to Cu²⁺ species in the form of CuO (shown in Figure S4 (b) in the supporting information)81. This agrees with the strong shake-up satellite features observed in the Cu 2p XPS spectra. In the treated catalyst, peaks at 932.24 eV and 933.5 eV, together with satellite features, indicate the coexistence of metallic Cu0 and Cu2+/+1 species (shown in Figure S4 (b) of the supporting information)82, an additional Cu LMM peak appears at lower binding energy (around ~ 568.1,~569.9, ~ 571.3 eV)83–85. This new peak indicates that part of the Cu2+ species is reduced during the reaction, leading to the formation of Cu0/+ species under the H2/CO2 atmosphere. The appearance of this peak confirms the creation of Cu+ sites at the Cu-CeO2 interface, which are known to be the main active sites for CO2 activation and selective CO formation in RWGS. The presence of both Cu2+ and Cu+ species in the spent catalyst shows that the catalyst surface undergoes significant changes under high-temperature RWGS conditions. The O 1s spectra (Fig. 5e and f) were deconvoluted into two peaks representing different types of surface oxygen. The peak at lower binding energy (~ 529.47 eV) corresponds to lattice oxygen (Oα) tightly bound to the metal oxide, while the high binding energy peak at ~ 531.52 eV is attributed to defect-related oxygen species (Oβ), such as oxygen vacancies as well as surface species such as hydroxyl group and adsorbed carbonate, which are commonly observed on ceria based catalysts86,87. In the RWGS treated catalyst, the peak positions remain similar; however, an increase in oxygen vacancies accompanied by a decrease in lattice oxygen was observed after the reaction. This suggests that the catalytic process occurs at oxygen vacancy sites, where missing lattice oxygen is replenished by oxygen from these vacancies.

Catalytic activity tests

The catalytic activity of pure CeO2 and different loading of CuO on CeO2 catalysts for the RWGS reaction was evaluated in the temperature range of 400–800 °C (Fig. 6). All catalysts exhibit the improvement of CO2 conversion with rising temperature, which is consistent with the thermodynamically beneficial feature of RWGS. For all the samples, the rate of CO2 conversion increases as the temperature rises, showing a positive activation energy requirement and that the high temperatures favour CO2 conversion. The as-prepared CeO2 catalyst exhibited the lowest catalytic activity among all the calcined samples. It was observed that CO2 conversion increased with rising temperature, indicating that higher temperatures enhanced the oxygen-ion conductivity of ceria, thereby increasing lattice oxygen mobility and the number of oxygen vacancies76. Interestingly, at elevated temperatures (around 800 °C), the CO₂ conversion achieved by ceria nanoparticles approached that of Cu/Ceria NPs. This suggests that the enhanced oxygen vacancy concentration and CO2 dissociation occurring at these vacancies, rather than dissociation on Cu sites, played a dominant role in improving the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction58,88. The results also reveal that the pure CeO2 catalysts have a lower CO2 conversion than the CuO/CeO2 catalysts. Indicating that the oxygen vacancies and active copper species on the Cu/CeO2 catalyst’s surface have synergistic effect in the CO2 RWGS reaction. Notably, the catalyst with 25 mol % Cu concentration (Cu0.25Ce0.75) demonstrated superior CO2 conversion compared to those with other Cu loadings. This enhanced performance was particularly evident at high temperatures, with the highest CO2 conversion of 49.5% observed at 800 °C on the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst. It was also confirmed that only carbon monoxide was observed as a carbon-containing product with the absence of methane resulting 100% selectivity towards CO, indicating a selective behaviour for RWGS reaction over the methanation reaction. To ensure data reliability, all catalytic measurements were performed in triplicate under identical experimental conditions. The values reported in Fig. 6 represent the average of three independent runs, and the corresponding error bars denote the standard deviation. The small error bars indicate excellent reproducibility and confirm the statistical reliability of the observed catalytic trends.

The activation energy (Ea) of the synthesized pure and doped CeO2 with different amount of Cu loading has been calculated. The Arrhenius plot is used to measure the Ea for CO2 hydrogenation over different Cu doped CeO2 samples, which is shown in Fig. 7a. It is observed from the graph (Fig. 7a) that among all the catalysts, the 25 mol% loaded CeO2 sample provides the lowest activation energy of 6.1 kJ/mole over the specified condition described in Table 2. It is evident from the study that with increase in the Cu content from 10 to 20 mol% in the catalysts, the activation energy does not change significantly. However, the activation energy is drastically reduced in case of 25 mol% doped CeO2 sample. Lower activation energy means the reaction can occur easily and efficiently. Thus, higher Cu content in the catalyst enhances its performance by reducing the energy barrier needed for the reaction to proceed. Moreover, as the Cu content goes beyond 25 mol%, the activation energy shoots up, which is observed in case of 50 mol% Cu loaded CeO2 sample. This could be because of the small Cu/CuO nanocrystals dispersed uniformly over ceria, which leads to a strong metal/metal oxide support (CeO2) interaction. This was confirmed from XRD (Fig. 1) where no lattice plane of metallic Cu or CuO was identified for 25 mol% Cu doped CeO2 sample, which indicates that very small Cu/CuO crystallites are highly dispersed over CeO2 support.

The stability of the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst for CO₂-to-carbon monoxide conversion is presented in Fig. 8. Catalyst deactivation is commonly caused by changes in the chemical state of active metals, sintering of the metal particles, or sintering of the support, which disrupts metal-support interfaces89. As shown in Fig. 8a, the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst exhibited a continuous decline in performance. This deactivation is likely associated with Cu species sintering during reaction. Subsequently, formation of larger CuO crystallites due to surface saturation, which decreases the Cu-CeO2 interfacial area, blocks oxygen vacancy sites, and accelerates sintering, thereby causing a decline in catalytic activity and durability90. In addition, diffraction peaks, attributed to copper species, were detected in the XRD profiles of the materials, which is shown in supplementary Figure S5 a. Post-reaction characterization was carried out to evaluate the structural stability of the catalysts after prolonged RWGS operation. XRD analysis of the spent Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst after 80 h on stream reveals the appearance of diffraction peaks corresponding to both CuO and metallic Cu0 phases (at 2θ = ~35.8°, ~ 38.9° (CuO) (JCPDS card No.892531) and Cu metallic 2θ at ~ 43.3°, referenced in JCPDS card no. 04–0836). This indicates that copper species undergo partial reduction under RWGS conditions90. Compared to the fresh catalysts, the spent samples show sharper and more intense Cu-related diffraction peaks, suggesting significant growth and agglomeration of copper particles during long-term operation91.This observation confirms a partial loss of the strong metal–support interaction originally present in the fresh Cu-CeO2 system, leading to copper sintering. In other evidence, Raman spectra of the spent Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst after 80 h on stream((shown in supplementary figure S5 b) further support the structural changes observed by XRD Compared with the fresh catalyst, the main F₂g vibrational mode of fluorite CeO2 shifts from ~ 460 cm⁻¹ to ~ 452 cm⁻¹, which may be lattice distortion induced by prolonged RWGS operation. In addition, new Raman bands assigned to CuO species appear `283 cm⁻¹ in the spent catalyst, confirming the formation of segregated copper oxide domains after long-term reaction68. The emergence of these CuO-related bands demonstrates that a fraction of the initially well-dispersed Cu-Ce interfacial species has migrated and agglomerated into bulk-like CuO phases. The recyclability of the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst for CO2 hydrogenation to carbon monoxide (RWGS reaction) was thoroughly examined, and the results demonstrated its excellent stability and consistent catalytic performance over multiple cycles, as illustrated in Fig. 8b. In the first cycle, the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst exhibited a remarkable conversion of 49.5%, indicating its high activity and efficiency in promoting the RWGS reaction. After the first cycle, the catalyst was subjected to subsequent cycles to evaluate its recyclability. Surprisingly, even after multiple cycles, the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst retained its catalytic activity with consistently high conversions of 49.3%, 48.8%, and 48.4% in the second, third, and fourth cycles, respectively, with an associated 100% CO selectivity. The negligible loss of catalytic activity over multiple cycles highlights the remarkable recyclability of the Cu0.25Ce0.75 catalyst throughout the reaction.

Conclusion

In this work, copper-doped CeO2 samples were effectively synthesized using a simple and versatile co-precipitation method, with copper concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 mol%. The synthesized particles exhibited crystalline cubic fluorite structure. XRD and Raman analyses show that the Cu/CuO are well dispersed on the surface of crystalline CeO2. It was found that the diameter of the particles is around 5 nm, which was confirmed by HRTEM. BET analysis concluded that copper doped ceria catalysts possess mesoporous structures with high specific surface areas. The surface areas of the synthesized particles are in the range of ~ 27–122 m²/g. XPS confirmed the presence of Ce4+, Ce3+, Cu2+ and Cu0 in the sample. It is confirmed from the RWGS study that the 25% Cu-loaded CeO2 sample is the best catalyst for catalytic activity among others. It is important to note that the CO2 conversion efficiency of 25% Cu-loaded CeO2 sample is 49.6% having an activation energy of 6.1 KJ/mol. The high conversion efficiency may be because of the combined effect of oxygen vacancy, the well dispersed Cu/CuO on the surface of CeO2 and metal (Cu) support (CeO2) interaction in the catalysts. Additionally, the catalysts did not produce any methane as by product under the conditions they are tested. Moreover, the catalysts were stable even after running for more than 80 h.
