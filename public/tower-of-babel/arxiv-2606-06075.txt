Toward Mobile and Converged Backhaul: The Promise of Wireless Access and Backhaul
Chiara Rubaltelli, Marcello Morini, Eugenio Moro, Ilario Filippini, Antonio Capone

Abstract
Wireless Access and Backhaul (WAB) is emerging as a key enabler for flexible and cost-efficient 5G deployments, offering a modular architecture that decouples access and backhaul while supporting multi-technology and mobile backhaul links. This article introduces the WAB framework standardized in 3GPP Release 19, outlining its architecture and operational principles. A practical implementation built with commercial hardware and open-source software demonstrates the feasibility and efficiency of WAB systems. We further explore four representative application scenarios – ranging from on-demand coverage to mobile Software-Defined Wide Area Network (SD-WAN) connectivity – and discuss the technical challenges that must be addressed for large-scale adoption. These insights highlight WAB as a promising foundation for 5G-Advanced and a stepping stone toward future 6G networks.



I
Introduction

Providing ubiquitous mobile coverage in a cost efficient and scalable manner remains a fundamental challenge for cellular networks. To address this,
3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)
has long explored relay-based solutions as a promising approach
[
6
]
. Early implementations emerged as home-based femtocells and later evolved into small cells within
Heterogeneous Network (HetNet)
architectures. Femtocells
[
5
]
, introduced in the early 2000s, offered a simple and cost effective way to improve indoor coverage and offload traffic from macrocells. However, their reliance on conventional backhaul solutions, typically wired or satellite based, inherently limited their flexibility and hindered large scale adoption.

This limitation motivated the evolution toward wireless backhaul, culminating in the
Integrated Access and Backhaul (IAB)
framework introduced in Release 15
[
14
]
.
IAB
extends coverage through multi-hop tree topologies in which relay nodes can serve both downstream nodes and
User Equipments (UEs)
. While this represents a significant step forward, it preserves a tight coupling between access and backhaul. This coupling increases the complexity of resource management, interference coordination, and control plane operation, ultimately constraining flexibility and deployment scalability, and preventing widespread adoption.

The introduction of
Wireless Access and Backhaul (WAB)
in Release 19
[
2
,
3
]
marks a decisive shift in this design space. Unlike femtocells and
IAB
,
WAB
decouples access and backhaul and adopts a modular architecture in which connectivity is provided through wireless backhaul links that can leverage both
3GPP
and non-
3GPP
technologies
[
10
]
. This enables seamless integration of heterogeneous networks, including mmWave and
Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN)
systems, whose direct support in commercial
UEs
remains limited, by relocating their role to the backhaul segment. Moreover,
WAB
is inherently designed to support mobility at the backhaul level, enabling
Mobile Radio Networks (MRNs)
where entire network segments can move while maintaining connectivity, thereby defining a new class of moving networks
[
13
]
.

In this perspective,
WAB
is not merely an incremental evolution of relay-based solutions, but a fundamental rethinking of how access and backhaul are composed and operated. This paradigm shift unlocks unprecedented flexibility, while simultaneously introducing new technical challenges, including radio access reconfiguration under unreliable backhaul conditions, joint access and backhaul mobility management, and intelligent network and interference management. These challenges open unexplored research directions and call for new design principles.

Given that
WAB
is approaching standardization and that, to the best of our knowledge, it has not yet been systematically analyzed in the literature, this article provides a timely and comprehensive first overview of the technology. In particular, beyond presenting the architecture and a practical implementation, we place a strong emphasis on the analysis of the key technical challenges introduced by
WAB
and on the identification of the research directions they enable. We complement this discussion with experimental results obtained from a real world testbed, including mmWave-based backhaul, demonstrating the feasibility and performance of
WAB
deployments based on
Commercial Off-the-Shelf (COTS)
hardware and open-source software. Specifically, the article aims to:

•

outline the key technical features and benefits of
WAB
;

•

present and validate a practical implementation of
WAB
;

•

discuss the fundamental technical challenges and emerging research directions;

•

describe representative application scenarios enabled by
WAB
.

II
Wireless Access and Backhaul in
3GPP

Fig. 1
:
WAB
architecture and protocol stack on the user plane. In light-blue the elements forming the access segment, while in red the elements composing the backhaul segment.

In late 2023, the
3GPP
launched two
Study Items (SIs)
related to
WAB
, which were later adopted into Release 19 specifications as a topological enhancement for 5G-Advanced (5G-A). Within the Technical Specification Group (TSG)
Radio Access Network (RAN)
, the
WAB
technology was introduced and technically assessed
[
2
]
, while the Service and System Aspects (SA) group addressed the associated management framework, termed
Mobile Wireless Access and Backhaul (MWAB)

[
3
]
.
3GPP standardization efforts mainly target the definition of the architecture and configuration procedures, partially addressing performance optimization in the case of 3GPP backhaul.

A primary motivation for this work is the increasing momentum of
NTN
in the 6G standardization process.
WAB
nodes are expected to play a central role in enabling non-terrestrial backhaul by providing 5G access in areas lacking terrestrial network (TN) coverage
[
4
,
1
]
. This includes support for mobile backhaul links and seamless
NTN
–TN handovers.
In addition,
WAB
has been envisioned as a facilitator for roaming
UEs
, enabling them to connect to foreign networks by attaching to hybrid
WAB
nodes that provide home-network radio access. This approach removes the need for
RAN
sharing, as well as formal roaming agreements between operators.

Architecture

The
WAB
architecture comprises two communication systems – the access segment and the backhaul segment – capable of operating in either in-band or out-of-band configurations. End-to-end connectivity is achieved by tunneling the fundamental 3GPP interfaces that interconnect the functional elements of the access segment across the links of the backhaul segment. A schematic representation of the basic architecture, along with the corresponding user-plane protocol stacks, is shown in Figure
1
.

At the core of the architecture lies the
WAB
Node, which integrates two main components: a WAB
Next Generation Node Base (gNB)
(
WAB-gNB
) and a WAB Mobile Termination (
WAB-MT
). The
WAB-gNB
is a 5G-compliant
gNB
that provides radio access to end
UEs
. The
WAB-MT
, in the basic configuration, establishes a wireless link with a
BH-gNB
, and this link is then used by the
WAB-gNB
as a backhaul connection to forward
UE
traffic – tunneled by the
WAB-gNB
– toward the access network’s core, referred to as the
5GC-serving-UE
. It is worth noting that, according to current
3GPP
specifications, WAB Nodes are not permitted to obtain backhaul connectivity from other WAB Nodes, thereby preventing multi-hop topologies among them.
This simplifies backhaul handovers and reduces procedural complexity, while avoiding issues related to insufficient resources to support the required backhaul
Packet Data Unit (PDU)
sessions.

Within the backhaul segment, the
BH-gNB
connects to its core,
BH-5GC
, via conventional transport technologies. The
WAB-MT
,
BH-gNB
, and
BH-5GC
jointly establish a
PDU
session responsible for maintaining connectivity between access-network interfaces, providing a transparent, tunneled backhaul transport service. Finally, the
BH-5GC
connects to the
5GC-serving-UE
, completing the end-to-end architecture.

On the control plane, the wireless backhaul is established through a backhaul-segment
PDU
session that carries the N2 interface – including the Next Generation Application Protocol (NGAP),
Stream Control Transmission Protocol (SCTP)
, and
Internet Protocol (IP)
protocol layers – connecting the
WAB-gNB
to the
5GC-serving-UE

Access and Mobility Management Function (AMF)
. On the user plane, a separate backhaul
PDU
session supports the N3 interface – including
GPRS Tunnelling Protocol User Plane (GTP-U)
,
User Datagram Protocol (UDP)
, and
IP
layers – linking the
WAB-gNB
with the
5GC-serving-UE

User Plane Function (UPF)
.

While, in the basic configuration, the access and backhaul segments operate as two standard-compliant, logically independent 5G networks, the
WAB
architecture also allows the backhaul segment to be implemented using non-
3GPP
systems, unconventional networks, or even wired technologies. In all cases, the end
UE
remains agnostic to the specific implementation of the backhaul.

Basic Functioning and Procedures

The integration of a
WAB
node into an existing network begins with the setup of the
WAB-MT
. This component connects to the
BH-gNB
as a standard 5G
UE
, following conventional access procedures. Once authorized by the
BH-5GC
, the
WAB-MT
can establish one or more backhaul
PDU
sessions with the
BH-5GC
’s
UPF
. Multiple backhaul
PDU
sessions may be created to transport different logical interfaces or to enable differentiated traffic handling according to specific quality-of-service (QoS) requirements.

Following this initial setup, the
WAB-gNB
is initialized and registers with the
5GC-serving-UE
, enabling the establishment of
PDU
sessions for connected
UEs
. Each session is supported by a Data Radio Bearer (DRB) between the
UE
and the
WAB-gNB
, and by an N3
GPRS Tunneling Protocol (GTP)
tunnel between the
WAB-gNB
and the
5GC-serving-UE
, which is, in turn, encapsulated within the backhaul-segment
PDU
session. During setup or update procedures, the
WAB-gNB
can include an identifier of the associated
WAB-MT
in its messages to explicitly indicate its belonging to a WAB node. This information can be used to prevent unintended connections between two
WAB-gNBs
in cascade.

When a WAB node is in motion, the
WAB-MT
relies on standard
UE
mobility procedures to enable seamless handovers in the backhaul segment.
As it moves within the network, the
WAB-MT
may also request the establishment or modification of backhaul-segment
PDU
sessions on demand based on the
WAB-gNB
traffic requirements. In addition, the
WAB-gNB
may establish
3GPP Xn
interface connections with neighboring gNBs to improve the handover experience.

III
The
WAB
revolution

Relay-based architectures are designed to position smaller
Base Station (BS)
-equivalent nodes closer to
UEs
in order to enhance coverage.

Despite their conceptual appeal, such solutions have historically struggled to reach large-scale deployment. Notably,
IAB
and femtocell systems – two of the most representative attempts – have proven difficult to integrate flexibly into existing networks and have faced practical limitations in terms of deployment complexity, management, and adaptability.

In the following, we compare
WAB
with these two established technologies, highlighting the key differences that allow
WAB
to overcome the constraints that have limited their adoption.

WAB
vs
IAB

Conceptually,
IAB
shares similarities with
WAB
, as it provides wireless backhaul to relay nodes that, in turn, offer access to end
UEs
. However,
IAB
nodes – the functional counterparts of
WAB
nodes – are based on the
Central Unit (CU)
/
Distributed Unit (DU)
split and include only a
DU
entity for the access segment. This architecture requires full network control and tight coordination between the access and backhaul segments. In contrast,
WAB
nodes incorporate a full-fledged
gNB
for the access segment, enabling a radically
modular design
in which the access and backhaul segment can belong to independent third-party operators. These operators may interact with varying levels of coordination, depending on the deployment scenario and specific service agreements.

A major limitation of
IAB
lies in the complexity of its operational procedures, particularly those related to
IAB
node handovers.
Backhaul handovers in
IAB
are rigid and intricate, as they must account for both rerooting within the parent node’s subtree and the mobility of the parent node itself, further complicating system management
[
14
]
.
To mitigate these issues, the
3GPP
introduced the
Mobile Base Station Relay (MBSR)
technology in Release 18
[
15
]
, extending the basic
IAB
architecture with an additional
CU
component to enable more efficient node handovers.

By contrast,
WAB
was conceived with intrinsic
simplicity in mobility management
: the backhaul segment handles handovers by reusing its own legacy mobility procedures, without requiring additional architectural components or introducing complexity into the access segment. Moreover, during
WAB
standardization, discussions within
3GPP
emphasized the avoidance of multi-hop topologies, thereby eliminating related complexities and reinforcing procedural simplicity.

In addition, while accommodating
DU
modules within
IAB
nodes requires extending the 5G protocol stack and associated functionalities,
WAB
nodes can be readily
implemented by interconnecting
COTS
devices
, such as existing
gNB
and
UE
implementations, through standard IP-based techniques. This approach greatly simplifies deployment and fosters cost-efficient, flexible integration of heterogeneous components.

WAB
vs Femtocells

Both femtocells and
WAB
are included among the topological enhancements introduced in Release 19 of the
3GPP
specifications
[
2
]
. Although these technologies share the goal of enhancing coverage, a crucial distinction sets them apart.

Femtocells have a long history within
3GPP
, with deployments primarily targeting static indoor environments where macrocell signals struggle to penetrate
[
5
]
. Their backhaul is typically provided through conventional local broadband or user-supplied connectivity, such as satellite or fiber links. In contrast,
WAB
represents a new paradigm that enables seamless,
multi-technology mobile backhaul
. Through this approach, 5G access can be guaranteed in low-coverage areas, continuously and on demand, by means of
WAB
nodes that obtain backhaul connectivity through multiple technologies while maintaining service continuity via smooth vertical and horizontal backhaul handovers.

WAB
’s shift

The previous comparison exposes a fundamental limitation of traditional backhaul solutions: they are inherently constrained either by rigid architectures and complex coordination mechanisms, or by static designs that fail to support mobility and adaptability.
WAB
breaks with these paradigms by introducing a decoupled, modular architecture in which access and backhaul are independently managed, potentially by different operators. This shift unlocks
unprecedented flexibility
, allowing
MRNs
to embed entire networks within others, recursively and across multiple hierarchical levels. As a result,
WAB
enables dynamic, multi-technology backhaul selection, seamless interoperability among heterogeneous components, and streamlined deployment based on
COTS
solutions. Most importantly, it establishes a new operational model in which
MRNs
inherently support
two coordinated mobility layers
: a higher-level backhaul-node mobility and a lower-level UE mobility, both enabling seamless handovers. In this sense,
WAB
represents not merely an evolution of relay-based architectures, but a fundamental rethinking of how backhaul and access are composed in future mobile networks.

IV
An experimental implementation

Fig. 2
:
WAB testbed implementation: devices composing the access network are framed in light-blue, while those of the backhaul segment are framed in orange.

To demonstrate the feasibility of
WAB
systems, we present here the realization of the first practical deployment of this technology.
The testbed follows the standard
WAB
architecture, with its main components illustrated in Figure
2
.
It is implemented using a combination of commercial hardware and open-source software, ensuring full compatibility with
COTS

UEs
. The setup adopts an out-of-band configuration, in which the access segment operates in FR1, while the backhaul operates in FR2.

The backhaul segment leverages the FR2 5G network of the High-Frequency Campus Lab at Politecnico di Milano
[
11
]
. The
WAB-MT
is implemented using a
COTS

Customer Premises Equipment (CPE)
, connected at a carrier frequency of 27.2 GHz to the
BH-gNB
, a
BS
installed at a height of 22 meters on a building rooftop. The
BH-gNB
is in turn connected via fiber to the
BH-5GC
, a commercial
5G Core (5GC)
instance. This FR2 network, used exclusively for research purposes, was dedicated to the testbed during all experiments and carried no external traffic. Additional system parameters are reported in
[
11
]
.

The access segment provides connectivity to a
COTS
smartphone operating in the FR1 band. The smartphone connects to the
WAB-gNB
, a
Software-defined Radio (SDR)
-based
OpenAirInterface (OAI)
gNB
[
9
]
, which is linked via Ethernet to the
WAB-MT
providing backhaul connectivity. The
5GC-serving UE
is implemented using an Open5GS core deployed on a general-purpose server connected to the
BH-5GC
.
Under ideal conditions, when the
OAI
gNB is directly connected via Ethernet to the Open5GS core, speed tests between the FR1
UE
and the core reach approximately 100 Mbps in
Downlink (DL)
and 10 Mbps in
Uplink (UL)
.

End-to-end connectivity in the system is ensured by establishing
Virtual Private Network (VPN)
tunnels that transport the N2 and N3 interfaces between the
WAB-gNB
and the
5GC-serving UE
.
Specifically, we employ a WireGuard tunnel, with a modified
Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU)
dimension.
The use of the
VPN
tunnel
is necessary because the
WAB-MT
, implemented as a conventional
CPE
, introduces a
Network Address Translation (NAT)
mechanism that prevents direct communication between the
WAB-gNB
and the
5GC-serving-UE
. As a result, connectivity relies on a three-layer tunneling structure: the backhaul
PDU
tunnels encapsulate the
VPN
tunnel, which transports the N2 and N3 access interfaces, and these in turn encapsulate the
PDU
sessions of the end
UE
.

This implementation demonstrates that
WAB
enables rapid and cost-effective deployments following standard-compliant architectures built from commercial hardware and open-source software, while maintaining full compatibility with
COTS

UEs
. Its flexibility and hardware independence position
WAB
as a practical and interoperable solution for a wide range of deployment scenarios.

We then conducted an experimental campaign to validate the system in mobile and
Outdoor-to-indoor (O2I)
scenarios

]
.
The experiments consisted of speed tests performed using
iperf3

Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)
connections.

In the mobile case, the
WAB
node was mounted on a vehicle, with the
WAB-MT
installed on the rooftop and the
WAB-gNB
placed inside the cabin to serve the
UE
. The vehicle circulated around a sports center, traversing areas where trees and buildings intermittently obstructed the signal.
DL
results, shown in Fig.
3a
, confirm that the end-to-end throughput closely follows the performance of the backhaul link. Under
Line of Sight (LoS)
conditions – characterized by the highest FR2
Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP)
– the

throughput reached approximately 50 Mbps (with a 40 MHz FR1 bandwidth), and degraded progressively as the vehicle moved into
Non-Line of Sight (NLoS)
regions. These results validate the correct operation of the
WAB
implementation, ensuring reliable service both in
LoS
and in moderately shadowed
NLoS
conditions.

Additional indoor tests were conducted by placing the
WAB
node behind a ground-level glass façade in
LoS
with the
BH-gNB
.
Speed tests were performed with the
UE
at multiple indoor locations (Fig.
3b
), and repeated by placing the FR2
CPE
at the same points to assess FR2-only performance.

Results show that FR2-only achieves higher DL spectral efficiency than the end-to-end
WAB
system, while in UL, especially in deep indoor conditions,
WAB
outperforms FR2-only. This advantage becomes more pronounced when the
WAB
node is moved further indoors, closer to the
UE
, partially compensating for the low transmit power of the
SDR
-based WAB-gNB. The histogram in Fig.
3b
, focusing on position 4, explicitly shows the increase in UL spectral efficiency compared to the FR2-only CPE case.
These results highlight
WAB
as a practical solution to mitigate key FR2 limitations, particularly for uplink and indoor coverage. A more extensive discussion and additional results are provided in
[
7
]
.

V
Application scenarios and Challenges

Fig. 4
:
Application scenarios enabled by
WAB
technology: 1) on-demand coverage, 2) multi-technology backhaul, 3) mobile SD-WAN, 4) public 5G access islands.

In the evolution of mobile radio networks, several visionary application scenarios have been revisited numerous times, yet their practical realization has often proved more challenging than initially anticipated.
We argue that
WAB
can serve as a key enabler for four such recurrent themes, supporting intelligent and cost-efficient deployment strategies that may finally render these long-sought applications feasible in practice.

Following the presentation of these scenarios in Figure
4
, we explore the technical challenges they introduce for
WAB
technologies. This discussion aims to guide future research directions and highlight how
WAB
could mature into a central building block of next-generation network architectures.

Application Scenarios

V-
1
Rapid and On-Demand Coverage

Crowded events and emergency situations often require temporary, rapidly deployable mobile coverage in locations where fixed
BSs
are impractical or unavailable. During large gatherings, such as demonstrations, marathons, or festivals, traffic surges can easily overload existing infrastructure. Likewise, natural disasters or rescue operations frequently take place in areas with limited coverage and without access to traditional backhaul.

WAB
enables fast and flexible deployment of mobile access nodes. While trailer-mounted
BSs
exist, they typically require dedicated backhaul, and involve complex setup procedures. In contrast, mobile
WAB
nodes can be mounted on vehicles, aircraft, or vessels, dynamically obtaining backhaul from multiple technologies, including terrestrial
BSs
, non-
3GPP
APs, or satellites, and form reconfigurable topologies as needed. This capability allows operators and public-safety agencies to rapidly restore, or augment, coverage for nomadic users and people in distress, without relying on fixed infrastructure.

V-
2
Wireless Mobile Backhaul through Multiple Technologies

Wireless backhaul has long been available, with various technologies proposed to meet evolving connectivity needs. However, solutions based on mmWave links and next-generation satellite systems often rely on custom-designed and costly solutions, which hinders large-scale deployment
in areas where terrestrial coverage is unavailable, such as onboard aircraft, or cruise ships.

In this context,
WAB
can serve as a nomadic gateway for commercial
UEs
, providing 5G access while multiple backhaul technologies operate transparently in the background. This includes horizontal backhaul handovers, across different nodes while in motion, and vertical handovers, across distinct backhaul technologies. Through this,
WAB
nodes can form architectures that ensure continuous and reliable access for
UEs
via multi-technology mobile backhaul. This approach also benefits backhaul operators, who can collaborate with access operators to deliver services, and share revenue opportunities.

V-
3
Mobile
SD-WAN
with First-Class 5G Access

Traditional
Software-Defined Wide Area Networks (SD-WANs)
interconnect static enterprise branches through wired, or fixed wireless links, and therefore intrinsically lack mobility support. With
WAB
, organizations in logistics, public transportation, or fleet management can extend
SD-WANs
to nomadic branches, seamlessly connecting them to corporate networks while providing private 5G services to remote workers in the branch.

Beyond mobility,
WAB
enables modular composition of the
SD-WAN
underlay, integrating multiple wireless backhaul technologies while enabling 5G in the access segment. Thanks to the flexibility of the 5G radio interface, a single
WAB
node can offer first-class radio access for diverse needs in the remote mobile branch – ranging from IoT services, to industrial low-latency applications, and broadband connectivity – using a unified access platform. By leveraging satellite communications, and backhaul mobility, it becomes possible to deploy 5G networks onboard trains, buses, airplanes, or cargo ships, integrating them into the enterprise
SD-WAN
and enabling access to centralized services and applications.

V-
4
Public 5G Access Islands over Non-Owned Backhaul Networks

Economic viability has long been the main hurdle faced by mobile network operators in providing ubiquitous connectivity in remote areas with low population density. Interestingly, similar challenges also arise in dense urban environments, where increasing traffic demand requires additional sites, and acquiring new locations, or installing new fiber links, may be equally impractical.

From a perspective complementary to the previous scenario, the
WAB
architecture enables operators to deploy public 5G access islands by leveraging non-owned backhaul networks.
WAB
nodes can exploit existing wireless coverage from other mobile networks, or private infrastructures, such as those operated by municipalities, utilities, or public service providers.

This flexibility unlocks new deployment opportunities. Private networks used for fleet monitoring or public transportation could backhaul onboard
WAB
nodes, providing 5G access to passengers or enhancing surrounding coverage. Similarly, mobile
WAB
nodes mounted on vehicles or vessels traveling abroad could create a home-network 5G “access island” for transported customers, independent of local operator agreements.

Technical Challenges

The application scenarios outlined above highlight the potential of
WAB
technology. However, realizing this potential requires addressing several technical challenges. In the following, we identify the most critical ones, along with the associated research questions and possible solution directions.

V-
1
Radio Access Reconfiguration in Unreliable Backhaul

The mobile, flexible, and multi-technology backhaul that characterizes the
WAB
paradigm provides clear advantages, but its capacity may fluctuate due to congestion or propagation impairments. This can lead to outages at the
WAB-gNB
when the available backhaul capacity is insufficient to sustain the bitrate required by the current radio access configuration (e.g., bandwidth, number of antenna ports, or numerology)
[
12
]
.

A key research question is how to dynamically adapt the radio access configuration to match time-varying backhaul conditions. A promising direction is the introduction of adaptive
gear-shifting
mechanisms, where access capacity is continuously tuned to the available backhaul bitrate. However, such reconfiguration incurs non-negligible delays, during which
UEs
may experience service degradation or temporary disconnection. This raises further questions on when to trigger reconfiguration and which parameters to adjust to minimize service disruption.

In addition, backhaul interfaces in current 5G systems are typically designed under the assumption of near error-free transport, an assumption that does not hold in
WAB
deployments. This opens a research direction on how to redesign backhaul protocols to tolerate transmission errors and intermittent connectivity. Potential solutions include predictive link monitoring, multi-backhaul connectivity, and fast backhaul reselection mechanisms, which can jointly enhance robustness under highly dynamic conditions.

V-
2
Interference Management and Spectrum Sharing

WAB
nodes are expected to be rapidly deployed and dynamically positioned within existing wireless environments, introducing coexistence challenges that are not typically encountered in traditional 5G
MRNs
. When the
WAB-gNB
and surrounding
gNBs
belong to the same operator, interference may arise due to imperfect time and frequency synchronization. A non-ideal backhaul can impair synchronization mechanisms such as
Precision Time Protocol (PTP)
or
Network Time Protocol (NTP)
, leading to misalignment between neighboring cells.

This issue is particularly critical in
Time Division Duplexing (TDD)
systems, where even small UL/DL configuration deviations may cause severe interference. The effect is further exacerbated by Doppler shifts under high-speed
WAB
mobility. Emerging applications such as
Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC)
are even more sensitive to desynchronization
[
8
]
, potentially experiencing greater degradation than conventional interference.

A central research question is how to enable
WAB
nodes to autonomously detect and align with existing synchronization patterns, while ensuring seamless coexistence with legacy infrastructure and other
WAB
nodes.

Interference challenges also emerge from spectrum usage. In in-band deployments, access and backhaul share the same resources, leading to self-interference. In out-of-band or multi-RAT scenarios, backhaul links may still suffer from external interference generated by heterogeneous systems. This motivates research on flexible and adaptive spectrum-sharing mechanisms tailored to
WAB
.

Promising solution directions include both centralized and distributed coordination approaches, possibly leveraging frameworks such as
Citizens Broadband Radio Service (CBRS)
or
Remote Interference Management (RIM)
, as well as the design of lightweight, ad-hoc interference mitigation techniques suitable for highly dynamic and mobile
WAB
deployments.

V-
3
Handover Optimization

A distinctive feature of
WAB
systems is that mobile
WAB
nodes may move together with the group of
UEs
they serve. In such cases, handovers are avoided, as
UEs
remain attached to the same NR Cell Identity (NCI). Conversely, when static
UEs
are served by mobile
WAB
nodes, such as UAVs, public transport vehicles, or fleet assets, the serving node may change frequently, leading to large-scale and highly dynamic handover events.

This duality introduces a fundamental research question: how to design handover mechanisms that remain efficient under both synchronized mobility (node and UE moving together) and asynchronous mobility (mobile nodes serving static users).

Potential solutions include mobility-aware handover strategies that adapt to the movement patterns of
WAB
nodes, reduce signaling overhead, and minimize service interruption. Tight integration between handover management and interference coordination is also a key design guideline to ensure stable coexistence with legacy infrastructure.

V-
4
Smart Network Management and Planning

The dynamic and heterogeneous nature of
WAB
deployments opens new research directions in autonomous and intelligent network management. In particular, embedding
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
capabilities within
WAB
nodes can enable real-time self-organization, adaptive control, and improved robustness in complex environments. However, these solutions must operate under strict hardware and energy constraints, as
WAB
nodes are expected to be low-cost and resource-limited.

A key research question is how to design lightweight, distributed
AI
models that can operate efficiently at the network edge while maintaining high performance.
A promising solution direction is the integration of
WAB
with advanced
RAN
architectures such as Open-
RAN

Finally, the inherent mobility of
WAB

This motivates research on dynamic and adaptive planning frameworks capable of continuously optimizing network configuration in response to node mobility, evolving topologies, and time-varying traffic demands.

VI
Conclusion

This article introduced the
WAB
architecture as a key enabler for next-generation relay-based networks, highlighting its role not simply as an evolution of existing solutions, but as a fundamental shift toward a modular and decoupled access–backhaul paradigm. This redesign enables unprecedented flexibility, including multi-technology backhaul and seamless mobility at both backhaul and access levels.

A practical implementation based on commercial hardware and open-source software demonstrated the feasibility of
WAB
deployments, while four representative application scenarios illustrated its potential to unlock use cases that have long remained impractical with conventional approaches.

At the same time,
WAB
introduces new system-level challenges that define a clear research agenda, including adaptive radio access under unreliable backhaul, mobility-aware handover strategies, scalable interference management, and lightweight AI-based mechanisms for network management and control.

As Release 19 standardization progresses,
WAB
stands as a pivotal enabler for 5G-Advanced and a promising foundation for future 6G network architectures.
