Carotenoids modulate fermentation of inulin ex vivo in IBS and overweight adult gut microbiota
Authors: Robert E Steinert, Pieter Van den Abbeele, Christian Schaefer, Jonas Poppe, Lam Dai Vu, Danica Bajic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarotenoids have direct antioxidant and anti-inflammatory health benefits. Owing to their low bioavailability, they also reach the colon, yet their microbiome interactions remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe investigated how β-carotene (BC), lutein (LU), lycopene (LY) and zeaxanthin (ZE), at biological relevant doses (between 1 and 60 mg/d), impact composition and metabolite production of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and overweight (OW) adult gut microbiota using the ex vivo SIFR® fermentation system (systemic intestinal fermentation research). Fresh fecal samples were collected from 12 donors (n = 6 per cohort) and incubated for 24 h under anaerobic conditions, with carotenoids administered alone or in combination with prebiotic inulin (IN, tested at 2.5 g/day).ResultsWe observed phenotype-specific microbiota characteristics for each cohort at baseline. Carotenoids alone had only minor effects but in combination with prebiotic inulin significantly altered community composition (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, P < 0.05) and enhanced short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. Most carotenoids increased butyrate production vs. inulin alone with ZE (at 2 mg/d) in IBS (+ 7.6%) and LU (at 60 mg/d) in OW (+ 8.0%) showing the strongest effects (padjusted<0.10). In contrast, LY (at 45 mg/d) uniquely increased acetate (+ 3.5%) and propionate (+ 8.0%) at the expense of butyrate (-7.5%, padjusted<0.10) when compared vs. inulin only. Changes in SCFA correlated with carotenoid- and cohort-specific shifts in the abundance of Bifidobacterium, Alistipes, Akkermansia, Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus. ZE also enhanced the effect of inulin on the health-related metabolites 3-phenyllactic acid (PLA) and 2-hydroxyisocaproic acid (HICA), particularly IBS subjects (padjusted<0.20).ConclusionsAntioxidant carotenoids may support anaerobic gut microbes including dominant butyrate producers and counteract phenotype-specific dysbiosis, potentially offering novel dietary approaches for IBS and obesity-associated dysbiosis. As these effects were observed when carotenoids were combined with inulin, carotenoids may have greater impacts when consumed as part of a fiber-rich diet, reflecting their natural occurrence in plant-based foods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05271-6.Keywords: SCFA, Microbiota, Ex vivo, Carotenoid, Prebiotic, SIFR®

Background

The gut microbiome comprises trillions of microorganisms essential for host health notably by fermenting dietary and host-derived glycans into short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate with broad health benefits [1–3]. An imbalanced microbiome (‘dysbiosis’) has been increasingly linked to conditions such as Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) [4] and obesity [5].

IBS is a highly prevalent functional bowel disorder in which recurrent abdominal pain is associated with irregular defecation or other changes in bowel habits [6]. Despite some inter-study variability, a consistent dysbiotic pattern is observed in IBS, characterized by reduced fecal Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium and increased Escherichia coli abundance [7]. Other changes include an increase of Ruminococcus gnavus at the expense of Coprococcus spp. [8], the latter which is also observed in patients with depression [9]. Moreover, increased Alistipes spp. have been reported in IBS patients responding to fecal microbiota transplantation. As SCFA-producing taxa, Coprococcus and Alistipes may therefore exert protective effects in IBS [10, 11].

Also obesity is highly prevalent [5] and linked to an altered gut microbiome [12, 13]. Obesity-associated microbiota typically exhibit reduced richness and diversity, with increased Ruminococcus gnavus and Roseburia, and decreased Eubacterium eligens and Akkermansia muciniphila, compared with lean individuals [12, 14]. In obesity, fecal SCFA levels are elevated in humans, while genetically obese mice show enhanced dietary SCFA extraction, contributing up to ~ 10% of total energy intake compared with lean controls [15–17]. The causal role of SCFA in obesity remains debated, as SCFAs also stimulate leptin, GLP-1, and PYY secretion, promoting appetite suppression [18–21]. A better understanding of the relationship between the gut microbiota and obesity could inform more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Carotenoids are isoprenoid metabolites naturally occurring in plants, algae and photosynthetic bacteria, in which they contribute as light harvesting pigments and to protect cells from free oxygen radicals [22]. Since the 1980s, there has been a significant rise in recognition of carotenoids as potential health-promoting compounds primarily based on their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [23]. Adequate carotenoid intake has been associated with reduced likelihood of heart disease, bone, skin and eye conditions as well as chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, obesity and cancer [23, 24]. This includes lycopene (LY) to be advantageous for prostate cancer and other oxidative stress related health conditions [25], lutein (LU) and zeaxanthin (ZE) to be useful for bone and eye health [26] as well as β-carotene (BC), as a precursor to vitamin A, being vital for immune functioning [27].

Given that carotenoids are fat-soluble, they are poorly bioavailable and reach the colon at about 60–90% of the ingested dose [28]. Nevertheless, the potential interaction between carotenoids and the gut microbiome is poorly understood [28, 29] and some of the reported health benefits may in fact result from a direct modulatory effect of carotenoids on the colonic microbiota. Carotenoids may also modulate the effects of dietary fibre and prebiotics on the gut microbiota, as these components are typically co-consumed in phytochemical-rich plant foods. These mechanisms are particularly relevant given reports of significantly lower intakes of dietary fibre, prebiotics, and carotenoids, as well as reduced serum carotenoid levels, in individuals with IBS, overweight and obesity [30–33].

In this study, we investigated the effect of four carotenoid extracts (BC/LU/LY/ZE), tested at three biorelevant doses, alone and in combination with prebiotic inulin, on gut microbiome composition and metabolic activity using fecal inoculum of IBS and healthy overweight (OW) subjects (Fig. 1). The study employed the ex vivo SIFR® system (systemic intestinal fermentation research), a recently validated high-throughput, bioreactor-based fermentation technology [34, 35].

Methods

Donor selection

To recreate biorelevant human gut microbiota ex vivo using the SIFR® technology, fresh microbiome samples were collected from human adults. Based on Lavelle et al. demonstrating minor longitudinal differences along the colon of human adults [36], fecal samples were used as a proxy for the colonic microbiota. The first cohort comprised of 6 healthy overweight adults with 25 < BMI < 30 (OW). The second cohort comprised of 6 subjects with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), either mixed type (IBS-M) or with diarrhoea (IBS-D). The additional selection criteria for all donors were: age 25–65, no antibiotic/probiotic intake in 3 months prior to study, no other gastrointestinal disorders (cancer, ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease), no chronical disease or immune disease, non-smoking, alcohol consumption < 3 units/d. Further, stool consistency measured as Bristol Stool Score (BSS) was used to rule out abnormal stool pointing at a dysbiotic gut microbiota [37]. Overall, this resulted in the enrolment of 12 specific subjects of which the gender, age and BSS is reported in Table S1. Fresh fecal samples were collected according to a procedure approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Ghent (reference number BC-09977). All participants signed an informed consent to use their anonymised fecal donation for the current study.

Test product preparation and dosing

Beta-carotene (BC), lutein (LU), lycopene (LY) and zeaxanthin (ZE) were provided by DSM-Firmenich (Kaiseraugst, Switzerland): BC and LY were pure crystalline materials whereas LU and ZE were purified and concentrated plant extracts from Tagetes erecta L. Given their hydrophobicity, all carotenoid extracts are poorly absorbed in the small intestine, ensuring delivery to the colon under physiological in vivo conditions. BC was tested at 3.5/7/21 mg/d, LU at 10/20/60 mg/d, LY at 7.5/15/45 mg/d and ZE at 1/2/6 mg/d. The administered amounts were chosen to span a biorelevant range based on average daily upper limits across EU member states. The low, medium, and high doses represented 0.5×, 1×, and 3× of this average upper limit, reflecting lower-than-average, typical, and transient peak colonic exposures that may occur in vivo—for example after bolus intake of a capsule or tablet, where limited dilution can lead to temporarily higher local concentrations. Carotenoids were tested alone or in combination with 2.5 g/d of prebiotic inulin from chicory (I2255, Merck, Overijse, Belgium) (Fig. 1A). This dose is in line with the estimated daily intake of inulin as part of an average diet [38], while it is also expected to significantly impact the gut microbiome in a clinically relevant manner based on previous ex vivo studies, in which inulin was tested from 1.5 g/d [39] to 5 g/d [35].

Ex vivo bioreactor design and incubation

The effect of the four carotenoids (BC, LU, LY, ZE), each at three different doses, referred to as L/M/H (Low/Medium/High), both, alone without inulin (indicated by ´No IN´) or with inulin (indicated by ‘+IN’), was investigated using fecal inoculum of OW subjects and subjects with IBS (n = 6 per group) (Fig. 1A). Also included was a no substrate control (NSC), in which the microbial inoculum was grown in the absence of additional test product.

Individual bioreactors were processed in parallel in a bioreactor management device (Cryptobiotix, Ghent, Belgium). Each reactor contained 5mL of nutritional medium-fecal inoculum blend supplemented with test products (carotenoids and/or IN), then sealed, before being rendered anaerobic. Blend M0017 (Cryptobiotix, Ghent, Belgium) was used for preparation of nutritional medium. Subsequently, bioreactors were incubated under continuous agitation (140 rpm) at 37 °C for 24 h (MaxQ 6000, Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Merelbeke, Belgium). Upon gas pressure measurement, liquid samples were collected and analyzed for microbial composition, key fermentative parameter and in-depth metabolite analysis via metabolomics (Fig. 1B).

The unsupplemented control (NSC) was run in technical triplicate and monitored for pH, gas and SCFA production, with coefficients of variation on average being as low as 0.92%, confirming the high technical reproducibility, as established for the SIFR® technology [35]. Compositional analysis of NSC samples additionally confirmed the high biorelevance of the gut microbiome incubations, i.e. (i) strong growth of gut microbes during incubation, (ii) preservation of microbial diversity, and (iii) highly similar microbial composition between in vivo-derived samples and SIFR®-grown communities (Figure S1).

Microbial community composition: quantitative shallow shotgun sequencing

Quantitative data was obtained by correcting abundances (%; shallow shotgun sequencing (3 M reads) with total cell counts for each sample (cells/mL; flow cytometry), resulting in estimated cell counts/mL. DNA was extracted via the SPINeasy DNA Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Eschwege, Germany), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, DNA libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) and IDT Unique Dual Indexes with total DNA input of 1ng. Genomic DNA was fragmented using a proportional amount of Illumina Nextera XT fragmentation enzyme. Unique dual indexes were added to each sample followed by 12 cycles of PCR to construct libraries. DNA libraries were purified using AMpure magnetic Beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, United States), eluted in QIAGEN EB buffer, quantified using a Qubit 4 fluorometer and a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq 2000 platform 2 × 150 bp. Unassembled sequencing reads were converted to relative abundances (%) using the CosmosID-HUB Microbiome Platform (CosmosID Inc., Germantown, MD, United States; accessed on 8 August 2023) [40, 41]. This platform was also used to calculate the observed number of species along with the Chao1 and Simson diversity index. To determine total counts, samples were diluted in anaerobic phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by cell staining with SYTO 16 at a final concentration of 1µM, and counted via a BD FACS Verse flow cytometer (BD, Erembodegem, Belgium). Data were analysed using FlowJo, version 10.8.1.

Chemical and metabolite analysis

Key fermentative parameters

SCFA (acetate, propionate, butyrate and valerate) were determined via gas chromatography with flame ionization detection, upon diethyl ether extraction, as previously described [39]. Briefly, 0.5 mL samples were diluted in distilled water (1:3), acidified with 0.5 mL of 48% sulfuric acid, after which an excess of sodium chloride was added along with 0.2 mL of internal standard (2-methylhexanoic acid) and 2 mL of diethyl ether. Upon homogenization and separation of the water and diethyl ether layer, diethyl ether extracts were collected and analysed using a Trace 1300 chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Merelbeke, Belgium) equipped with a Stabil wax-DA capillary GC column, a flame ionization detector, and a split injector using nitrogen gas as the carrier and makeup gas. The injection volume was 1 µL and the temperature profile was set from 110 °C to 240 °C. The carrier gas was nitrogen, and the temperatures of the injector and detector were 240 and 250 °C, respectively. pH was measured using an electrode (Hannah Instruments Edge HI2002, Temse, Belgium).

Untargeted metabolite profiling (metabolomics)

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was carried out on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish LC coupled to Thermo Q Exactive HF MS (Thermo Scientific), using an electrospray ionization source. The analysis was performed both in negative and positive ionization mode. The UPLC was performed applying a slightly modified version of the protocol described by Doneanu et al. [42]. Peak areas were extracted using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Scientific), along with a manual extraction based on an in-house library using Skyline 21.1 (MacCoss Lab Software) [43]. Identification of compounds was performed at different levels, i.e., level 1 (retention times (compared against in-house authentic standards), accurate mass (with an accepted deviation of 3ppm), and MS/MS spectra) and level 2a (retention times and accurate mass). Technical variability was assessed by running a QC sample (pooled sample of all samples) every six samples. This confirmed that coefficients of variation were on average as low as 6.2% and 8.3% for level 1 and 2a metabolites, respectively, thus confirming the high technical reproducibility of the LC-MS/MS method.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; www.r-project.org; accessed on 17 June 2024), which was used to make a series of dot plots (Fig. 4), bar charts and heat maps. While bar charts present the actual values, heat maps present log2-transformed fold changes for treatments compared to the respective control (NSC for carotenoids alone, inulin tested as such for combinations of carotenoids with inulin). This way, when a microbial group or metabolite increased by a treatment, a positive value is displayed, while negative values reveal a decrease. Likewise, dot plots visualize the proportional increase for a given treatment compared to the respective control, with vertical lines representing the 95% confidence interval on the estimate for each study arm.

Significance of treatment effects were assessed via repeated measures ANOVA analyses. P-values were corrected according to Benjamini-Hochberg [44]. Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this study, combined with the high dimensionality of the datasets (microbial community and untargeted metabolomics analysis) and the limited number of biological replicates per cohort (n = 6), an adjusted p-value (FDR) threshold of < 0.20 was applied. This approach is used in early-phase microbiome and omics studies to reduce the risk of overlooking potentially biologically relevant signals that may not reach more stringent thresholds due to limited statistical power. Results obtained using this threshold should therefore be interpreted cautiously and primarily as candidates for confirmation in future studies with larger sample sizes and increased statistical robustness.

For principal component analysis (PCA), the FactoMineR package was used [45]. For analysis of microbial composition, a regularized Canonical Correlation Analysis (rCCA) between SCFAs and relative abundance of the 25 most abundant species was performed, using the mixOmics package with the shrinkage method for estimation of penalization parameters (version 6.20.3) [46]. Subsequently, the analysis examined whether the abundances of specific species that correlated with SCFA levels were significantly affected by any of the carotenoids in the presence of IN+. To this end, linear mixed effects models were applied for each species-cohort combination (OW or IBS), with log2-transformed species abundance as the dependent variable, carotenoid as the fixed effect and donor identity as the random effect. Finally, differences in baseline microbiota composition between OW and IBS subjects were assessed via a Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) at species level. This enabled identifying the taxa most likely to explain differences between both cohorts.

Results

Fecal microbiome composition and metabolite production differs between IBS and healthy OW subjects

Independent of subject cohort (OW vs. IBS), we observed marked interpersonal differences in microbiome composition at family level at baseline (%), in line with the concept of enterotypes [47–49] (Fig. 2A, B), i.e. the microbiomes of subjects 1/2/3/12, 6/7/8 and 4/9/10 were enriched with Prevotellaceae (~ Prevotella enterotype), Bacteroidaceae (~ Bacteroides enterotype) and Ruminococcaceae (~ Firmicutes enterotype), respectively. Moreover, Bifidobacteriaceae abundances also greatly differed ranging from low (1/2/6/12) to intermediate (3/4/7/10/11) and high (5/8/9). When analysing the microbiota at greater phylogenetic resolution (species level, %), the OW and IBS cohorts stratified into two phenotype-specific groups (Fig. 2C), e.g. IBS subjects had higher levels of Ruminococcus gnavus and Clostridioides difficile, while Roseburia and Coprococcus spp. were enriched for healthy OW subjects. Following 24 h of incubation under control conditions (NSC), the microbiota of OW subjects resulted in significantly higher acetate, butyrate and valerate production when compared with microbiota of IBS subjects (Fig. 2D).

Carotenoids exert a stronger impact on microbiome composition when administered in combination with inulin

When analyzing microbiome composition at species level (cells/mL) across all test conditions, we found that carotenoids alone exerted only minor effects on microbial composition when compared with NSC. In contrast, when carotenoids were combined with inulin, we observed stronger effects apparent by the larger spread of samples in the ‘+IN’ vs. ‘No IN’ cluster, both for OW subjects and IBS subjects (Fig. 3A, B). This visual observation was confirmed by a formal statistical analysis of dissimilarity in microbial composition (= β-diversity; Bray-Curtis index) showing that carotenoids induced significantly stronger differences in microbial composition when combined with inulin than when dosed alone, in both, subjects with OW (p = 0.0001) and IBS (p = 0.0126, Fig. 3C).

Carotenoids potentiate the effect of inulin on SCFA production

When carotenoids were co-administered with inulin, we observed a potentiating effect on SCFA production depending on carotenoid type and dose (Fig. 4). The majority of carotenoids increased butyrate production compared to inulin only. In OW subjects, this increase was significant for all carotenoids (except LU_M+, LY_L+, LY_H + and ZE_L+) and most pronounced for LU_H+ with an 8.0% increase. In subjects with IBS, BC_M + and ZE_M+ significantly increased butyrate production most notably for ZE_M+ which showed a + 7.6% increase. Interestingly, the combination of LY_H and inulin (LY_H+) revealed marked effects on acetate (+ 3.5% vs. inulin only) and propionate (+ 8.0% vs. inulin only), at the expense of butyrate (-7.5% vs. inulin only) in both cohorts which was in contrast to all other carotenoids.

The combined administration of carotenoids and inulin is associated with SCFA production by specific gut microbial taxa

To determine which bacterial species contributed to the changes in SCFA production following the combined administration of carotenoids and inulin, a rCCA was performed in both subject cohorts (Fig. 5A). To specifically focus on carotenoid-driven correlations, the analysis was based on averages across all donors within each subject cohort (including all carotenoids, each tested at 3 doses), thus, removing variation caused by interpersonal differences. The rCCA analysis revealed the following strong correlations: (i) acetate with Bifidobacterium adolescentis, particularly in OW subjects, (ii) acetate/propionate with Alistipes_u_s/Phocaeicola dorei/Akkermansia muciniphila in both, OW and IBS subjects, and (iii) butyrate with Faecalibacterium prausnitzii/Faecalibacterium_u_s/ Coprococcus comes in IBS subjects (Fig. 5A). These correlations were confirmed by statistical analysis of carotenoid-specific treatment effects on microbial composition showing that several carotenoids, when combined with inulin, indeed increased key butyrate producers such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Faecalibacterium_u_s and Coprococcus comes beyond the effect of inulin only. The responses were specific to the IBS cohort and strongest for ZE-containing combinations (Fig. 5B and Figure S2). In contrast, only minor effects were noted when carotenoids were tested alone without inulin (Figure S3). Carotenoid-specific analysis of treatment effects also revealed that LY-containing combinations exerted a unique effect on the relative abundance of acetate/propionate producing species such as Parabacteroides distasonis or Phocaeicola massiliensis in both OW subjects and those with IBS, beyond the effect of inulin alone (Figure S2). In line with the observed correlations, this also included a specific increase of Alistipes species which are acid-resistant [50] and prominent propionate producers of the gut microbiota (Fig. 5C).

In-depth metabolomics analysis confirmed that carotenoids exert specific effects on microbial metabolite production when combined with inulin which are above the effects seen with inulin or carotenoids only (Figure S4 and S5, respectively). Most notably was the effect of ZE_M + to potentiate the effect of inulin on the health-related metabolites 2-hydroxyisocaproic acid (HICA) and 3-phenyllactic acid (PLA), particularly in subjects with IBS (Fig. 6). Discussion

In this ex vivo fermentation study, we examined the effects of biologically relevant doses of carotenoids on gut microbiota composition and metabolite production in adults with IBS and overweight (OW) using the SIFR® system. Regardless of subject cohort, we found that baseline microbiota showed pronounced inter-individual variability at family level, consistent with the enterotype concept [47–49]. At species level, IBS and OW subjects separated into distinct profiles, with IBS microbiota enriched in Ruminococcus gnavus and Clostridioides difficile, while Roseburia and Coprococcus spp. predominated in OW subjects. Moreover, after 24 h under control conditions, OW microbiota produced significantly higher acetate, butyrate, and valerate than IBS microbiota. These compositional and functional differences partly align with previous reports linking increased R. gnavus with IBS, at the expense of taxa such as Coprococcus that are more abundant in healthy adults [8, 9]. In addition, higher Roseburia abundance [12, 13, 51] and short-chain fatty acid production [16, 17] have been associated with elevated BMI, suggesting enhanced energy-harvesting capacity in OW microbiota [16, 17].

Species-level analysis across all conditions indicated that carotenoids alone had only limited effects on microbiome composition, whereas co-administration with inulin elicited more pronounced changes in both cohorts. This supports the notion that carotenoids may contribute to health benefits partly via gut microbiome modulation, particularly when consumed in conjunction with dietary fibre, consistent with their natural occurrence in fibre-rich, plant-based foods.

The modest, but consistent, increase in butyrate production in both OW and IBS subjects following combined carotenoids and prebiotic inulin supplementation suggests a shared mode of action. This effect may be linked to the antioxidant properties of carotenoids and their capacity to protect strictly anaerobic, butyrate-producing microbes [3]. However, the magnitude of this response is likely to vary across individuals, reflecting inter-individual differences in oxidative stress status, which has been implicated in both IBS and obesity [52, 53]. Interestingly, certain obligate anaerobic bacteria have been found to biosynthesize carotenoids suggesting that microbially-derived carotenoids may confer a survival advantage under transiently aerobic conditions [54]. Collectively, these findings further support the pantryome hypothesis, which proposes that gut microbes contribute to intestinal homeostasis by modulating redox balance, thereby sustaining a diverse and functionally resilient microbial ecosystem [55, 56].

Nevertheless, alternative mechanisms cannot be excluded, including direct interactions between carotenoids and microbial membranes, enhanced substrate availability, or shifts in fermentation pathways driven by changes in pH or intermediate metabolites. Consequently, further mechanistic studies are warranted e.g. by measuring redox potential, reactive oxygen species, or oxidative stress markers. Moreover, given the inherent limitations of ex vivo models and the lack of direct mechanistic validation, our findings should be interpreted with caution and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Previous in vitro studies also reported increased SCFA production following β-carotene supplementation [57, 58], however, the use of supraphysiological doses (100–1000 mg/L) or formulated blends (also including vitamins E, B2 and C as well as excipients or other food components such as modified food starch) limits translational relevance and underscores the importance of biorelevant dosing and well-defined test materials.

To identify taxa associated with the increased SCFA production following combined carotenoid–inulin treatments, we performed a rCCA and found strong associations between acetate and Bifidobacterium adolescentis (particularly in OW subjects), acetate/propionate and Alistipes spp., Phocaeicola dorei, and Akkermansia muciniphila in both cohorts, and butyrate with Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Coprococcus comes in IBS subjects. These correlations are consistent with the known SCFA-producing capacities of these taxa [11, 59] supporting their potential contribution to carotenoid-mediated SCFA modulation. An additional statistical analysis of carotenoid-specific effects supported the observed correlations, showing that several carotenoids combined with inulin increased key butyrate producers (Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Faecalibacterium_u_s, Coprococcus comes) beyond the effect of inulin alone. These effects were IBS-specific and most pronounced for ZE-containing combinations, which is notable given the central role of butyrate in colonic health [3]. The enrichment of Coprococcus and F. prausnitzii may be particularly relevant for IBS, as both taxa can be depleted in patients and/or are associated with anti-inflammatory functions [8, 9, 60]. In addition, LY-containing combinations selectively increased acetate- and propionate-producing taxa (e.g. Parabacteroides distasonis, Phocaeicola massiliensis) in both cohorts, including acid-resistant Alistipes species. This may be relevant for OW subject, given the reported roles of propionate in weight regulation [61]. Nevertheless, extrapolation to in vivo outcomes should be made cautiously due to inherent limitations of ex vivo models including host–microbe interactions, absence of systemic physiology or missing adaptation and host feedback mechanisms as well as considerable donor-to-donor variability (only 6 donors).

Metabolomics analysis confirmed that carotenoids augment inulin-driven microbial metabolite production, most notably was ZE_M+ showing the strongest enhancement of HICA and PLA, particularly in IBS samples. HICA, a leucine-derived metabolite, has documented antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties and has been linked to enhanced muscle protein synthesis via mTOR signalling [62–65]. PLA, produced by Bifidobacterium spp. from phenylalanine, selectively activates the human-specific HCA3 receptor, modulating immune responses, while related metabolites improve epithelial barrier integrity through AhR activation [66–68]. PLA has also been implicated in improved mitochondrial function and stress resilience, suggesting potential anti-aging effects [69]. Together, these findings highlight compound-specific effects of carotenoids on health-relevant microbial metabolites beyond SCFAs.

Conclusions

In summary, ex vivo SIFR® fermentation showed that carotenoids (BC, LU, LY, and ZE) alone had only small effects on microbial composition across two independent subject cohorts (OW and IBS) exhibiting phenotype-specific compositions. In contrast, co-administered with prebiotic inulin induced pronounced, cohort specific shifts in microbial composition, reflected in a larger spread of samples and significantly higher dissimilarity (Bray-Curtis index). Carotenoids also potentiated inulin-driven SCFA production particularly butyrate, with ZE and LU showing the strongest effects, while LY uniquely influenced acetate and propionate production at the expense of butyrate. These metabolic changes were associated with carotenoid- and cohort-specific shifts in the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium, Alistipes, Faecalibacterium, and Coprococcus. ZE further enhanced inulin-induced production of the health-related metabolites PLA and HICA, especially in IBS samples, suggesting relevance for IBS-related inflammation and barrier dysfunction [70, 71]. Together, these effects may involve antioxidant-mediated protection of obligate anaerobes, although additional mechanisms cannot be excluded.

Overall, our findings indicate that carotenoids may confer particular benefits in cohorts characterized by elevated oxidative stress, such as overweight individuals [72] or those with IBS [52], by improving anti-inflammatory metabolic output or restoring phenotype-specific dysbiosis. While our findings align with prior animal data [73], they should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only and require confirmation through dedicated mechanistic experiments and rigorously controlled clinical trials. Ideally, such trials should adopt longitudinal, placebo-controlled designs with physiologically relevant dosing, include normal-weight and healthy control groups, and incorporate stratification by donor or metabotype to account for inter-individual variability.

Notably, the observed effects occurred only in the presence of inulin, reflecting the natural occurrence of carotenoids in plant-based foods rich in phytochemicals.
