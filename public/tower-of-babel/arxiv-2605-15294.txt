An L # L^{\#} Based Algorithm for Active Learning of Minimal Separating Automata
Jasper Laumen, Leonne Snel, Frits Vaandrager (✉)

Abstract
A DFA separates two disjoint languages L 1 L_{1} and L 2 L_{2} if it accepts every word in L 1 L_{1} and rejects every word in L 2 L_{2} . Algorithms for active learning of small separating DFAs have many applications, e.g. for learning network invariants, learning contextual assumptions in compositional verification, learning state machines from large amounts of log data, and learning bug pattern descriptions. We propose a simple active learning algorithm, inspired by L # L^{\#} , that learns a minimal separating DFA for disjoint languages L 1 L_{1} and L 2 L_{2} if one exists. Experiments show that our algorithm significantly outperforms existing active learning algorithms on both randomly generated and industrial benchmarks.

Introduction

Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be disjoint languages over some alphabet
\Sigma
. We say that a deterministic finite automaton (DFA)
\mathcal{A}

separates

L_{1}
and
L_{2}
if it accepts every word in
L_{1}
and rejects every word in
L_{2}
, that is,
L_{1}\subseteq L(\mathcal{A})
and
L_{2}\cap L(\mathcal{A})=\emptyset
, with
L(\mathcal{A})
the language accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.
A minimal separating DFA is a separating DFA that has the least number
of states among all separating DFAs.
Algorithms for finding a minimal (or small) separating DFA have many applications, e.g. for computing network invariants
[
14
]
, contextual assumptions in compositional verification
[
6
]
, minimal DFAs that are consistent with a set of positively and negatively classified samples
[
8
]
, and bug pattern descriptions
[
36
]
.
Finding a minimal separating DFA is nontrivial: for regular languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, the decision problem “Does a separating DFA with
k
states exist?” is NP-hard
[
27
,
13
]
. If languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are context-free, then the decision problem whether there exists a separating DFA is already undecidable
[
20
]
.

SAT and SMT solvers may be used to compute minimal separating DFAs if
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are regular
[
23
]
.
However, in applications the minimal DFAs for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are typically big, whereas a minimal separating DFA
\mathcal{A}
is small.
Since solvers are unable to handle such large instances of an NP-hard problem, researchers have resorted to the use of active learning algorithms for the construction of a separating DFA. Different types of queries have been used, also depending on the characteristics of the applications.
A natural setting for learning separating DFAs is obtained by considering a slight variation of the Minimally Adequate Teacher (MAT) framework of
[
1
]
in which a
learner
tries to identify a minimal separating DFA by posing two types of queries to a
teacher

[
20
,
21
]
:

•

A
membership query

w\in\Sigma^{*}
.
In response, the teacher answers “
+
” if
w\in L_{1}
, “
-
” if
w\in L_{2}
, and “don’t care” (or “don’t now”, “maybe”, etc), denoted by
\square
, in any other case.

•

A
validity query
for a hypothesis DFA
\mathcal{H}
.
In response, the teacher answers “yes” if
\mathcal{H}
separates
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, and otherwise returns a counterexample, that is, a sequence
w
such that
w\in L_{1}\setminus L(\mathcal{H})
or
w\in L_{2}\cap L(\mathcal{H})
.

A first active learning algorithm for learning a minimal separating DFA for regular languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
using membership and validity queries was proposed by Grinchtein
et al.

[
14
]
.
Their algorithm, which is inspired by earlier work of Pena & Oliveira
[
26
]
, adapts Angluin’s
L^{*}
algorithm
[
1
]
by extending the observation table with don’t care values (
\square
) besides accept (
+
) and reject values (
-
), and uses the algorithm of Biermann & Feldman
[
4
]
to construct a DFA that is consistent with the observation table.
Chen
et al.

[
6
]
presented an
L^{*}
-based algorithm using 3DFAs that significantly outperformed the algorithm of Grinchtein
et al.

[
14
]
.
Their algorithm also uses membership queries, but instead of validity queries it poses the (strictly more powerful)
containment queries
:

•

A
containment query
for a hypothesis DFA
\mathcal{H}
has one of the following four types:
(i)
L_{1}\subseteq L(\mathcal{H})
, (ii)
L(\mathcal{H})\subseteq L_{1}
, (iii)
\overline{L_{2}}\subseteq L(\mathcal{H})
, and (iv)
L(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\overline{L_{2}}
. The teacher returns “yes” if the containment holds, and provides a counterexample otherwise.

Leucker & Neider
[
20
]
presented a survey of learning algorithms for separating DFAs, suggested some improvements of the algorithms of
[

,
6

, but did not explore any implementation or benchmarking.
Recently, however, Yaacov

However, in order to make their approach sufficiently scalable, they used the state merging algorithm RPNI

(rather than SAT/SMT solving as suggested in

) to obtain small (rather than minimal) separating DFAs.
Moeller

[

adapted Angluin’s

algorithm for learning minimal separating DFAs using membership and validity queries, and evaluated their

and some smaller benchmarks of Lee

. Their implementation can only handle about half of the benchmarks of

, due to scalability issues.
An interesting new type of queries was recently proposed by Walinga

[

]
. They consider the problem of learning a state machine from an amount of log data that is so big that conventional state merging algorithms cannot handle it. In order to solve this problem, they use database technology to efficiently query a large dataset stored on disk, and

•

The main result of this article is
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, a new and simple algorithm inspired by
L^{\#}

[
31
,
32
]
, for learning a minimal separating DFA using membership and validity queries.
Our algorithm is based on the following key ideas:

1.

By storing query results in an observation tree (PTA) and using apartness of basis states as the main driver for membership queries, our algorithm has more options to make progress with learning than
L^{*}
-based algorithms, which are limited by the prefixes and suffices in an observation table; this helps when the teacher gives many “don’t care” answers.

2.

We don’t need the assumption of
L^{\#}
that the basis is prefix closed.

3.

We don’t need a worklist of candidate trees/tables as used in
L^{*}_{\square}

[
21
]
.

4.

Both the
L^{\#}_{\Box}
algorithm and its correctness proof can be elegantly phrased in terms of morphisms between 3DFAs.

5.

By adding redundant clauses, which assert that states can only be merged when they are not apart, we significantly improve the SMT encoding of
[
29
]
of the problem whether a morphism exists from a PTA to a DFA with
n
states.

We also present two further optimisations of
L^{\#}_{\Box}
: a first one in which basis states are required to be pairwise incompatible rather than pairwise apart, and a second one in which we replace basis states by states that are closer to the root of the observation tree.

Experiments show that
L^{\#}_{\Box}
outperforms

L^{*}_{\square}

[
21
]
on the benchmarks of
[
24
]
, and the algorithm of
[
36
]
on the benchmarks of
[
18
]
.
In particular, unlike
L^{*}_{\square}
, our
L^{\#}_{\Box}
algorithm can solve almost all the benchmarks (within 200 seconds) of
[
24
]
, and the running times of
L^{\#}_{\Box}
for the RERS benchmarks
[

are up to two orders of magnitude faster than for the algorithm of
[
36
]
, even though our separating DFAs are minimal, unlike those of
[
36
]
.
So our results really move the boundary of applications for which minimal separating DFAs can be computed.

3
The
L^{\#}_{\square}
Algorithm

In this section we introduce
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, an algorithm that learns a minimal separating DFA for two disjoint languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
(if it exists) using membership and validity queries as defined in the introduction. Our algorithm is inspired by
L^{\#}

[
31
,
32
]
, a recent algorithm for active learning of DFAs.
The main challenge compared to the traditional MAT framework of Angluin
[
1
]
for learning DFAs is that a teacher can respond with “don’t care” to membership queries. Existing active learning algorithms,
L^{\#}
included, cannot handle this type of uncertainty.

3.1
Algorithm

L^{\#}_{\Box}
maintains the following data structures:

1.

A PTA
\mathcal{T}=(Q,q^{0},F,\rightarrow)
, called the
observation tree
, to store the outcomes of all membership queries:
after a membership query for a word
w

\mathcal{T}
to represent
w
and all its prefixes (unless they are already present).
Initial state
q^{0}
represents the empty sequence
\epsilon
.
Whenever state
q^{\prime}
represents sequence
wa
and state
q
represents
w
, then we add a transition
q\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{T}
.
In this way each state
q
represents its own access sequence.
If the response to a query

is
+
and
q
represents
w
, then we set
F(q)=+
.
Similarly, if the response to a query
w
is
-
and
q
represents
w
, then we set
F(q)=-
.
The final state function

is undefined for all other states of
\mathcal{T}
,
that is, states that either represent queries for which a
\square
-response was received, or do not represent queries but rather proper prefixes of queries.

Remark.

Unlike
L^{\#}
, the
L^{\#}_{\Box}
algorithm does not require that the basis is connected (prefixed closed).
This assumption is useful in
L^{\#}
because construction of a hypothesis becomes trivial: just merge frontier states with nonconflicting basis states. In
L^{\#}
, without this assumption, some basis states may become unreachable in the hypothesis when we merge a preceding frontier state with another basis state. Thus
L^{\#}
’s hypothesis construction method no longer works when the basis is not prefix closed. In
L^{\#}_{\Box}
the SMT solver takes care of hypothesis construction and dropping prefix closure allows us to make progress with learning even when we fail to identify frontier states due to don’t care answers.

Heuristics.

L^{\#}_{\Box}
is a nondeterministic algorithm and its rules can be applied in an arbitrary order.
An implementation of
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, however, will typically use heuristics to select the next rule that will be applied, and this influences performance.
Thus, for instance, our implementation applies the four rules with the following priority order: promotion
>
extension
>
identification
>
validity.
In particular, the validity rule (with its expensive call to an SMT solver) is used only when none of the other rules can be applied.
In applications where
n-|S|
becomes big, the number of applications of the extension rule may grow exponentially, and a different heuristics may be required.
Our implementation just picks some arbitrary access sequence
w
and suffix
v
when it applies the extension rule.
Clever heuristics might help to select those sequences
w
and
v
for which the likelihood that membership query
wv
produces an informative (
+
or
-
) answer is maximal.
The main goal of processing a counterexample after a validity query
\mathcal{H}
is ensuring that
\mathcal{H}
is no longer a valid hypothesis. To this end, querying just
w
would suffice, but we act under the assumption that prefixes of
w
are more likely to not give a “don’t care” answer.
Experimental evaluation shows that our approach works well for the benchmarks that we have tested. Clearly, the optimal learning strategy depends on the nature of the system under learning.

3.2
SMT Encoding

In order to decide whether there exists a complete DFA
\mathcal{H}
with at most
n
states with a morphism
f:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{H}
, we use the SMT solver Z3
[
9
]
, which has support for the theory of uninterpreted functions.
We directly encode the definition of a morphism as given in
Definition

2
. This encoding is largely based on the encoding given in
[
29
]
, but has been adapted to our setting and extended with redundant clauses. We introduce the following functions and encodings:

•

The states of
\mathcal{H}
are numbered
1
to
n=\left|Q_{\mathcal{H}}\right|
.

•

The states of
\mathcal{T}
are numbered
1
to
m=\left|Q_{\mathcal{T}}\right|
; we use
I_{q}
to denote the number associated with state
q\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}
.

•

We ensure that
I_{q}\leq\left|S\right|(\leq n)
for each
q\in S

•

The transition relation is encoded as a function
\delta_{\mathcal{H}}:[1..n]\times\Sigma\to[1..n]
.

•

The final state function is encoded by a function
F_{\mathcal{H}}:[1..n]\to\{1,0\}
, with
1
representing
+
, and
0
representing
-
.

•

The morphism is encoded by a function
f:[1..m]\to[1..n]
.

We introduce the following constraints:

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{q\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}}\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(q,\sigma)\mathord{\downarrow}\implies f(I_{\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(q,\sigma)})=\delta_{\mathcal{H}}(f(I_{q}),\sigma)

(1)

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{q\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}}F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)\mathord{\downarrow}\implies F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)=F_{\mathcal{H}}(f(I_{q}))

(2)

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{q\in S}f(I_{q})=I_{q}

(3)

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{q\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}\setminus S}\left(\left(\bigvee_{\left|S\right|<k\leq n}f(I_{q})=k\right)\lor\left(\bigvee_{p\in C(q)}f(I_{q})=I_{p}\right)\right)

(4)

Constraints (1) and (2) encode the definition of a morphism. Constraints (3) and (4) are redundant, but significantly speed up the solving time by specifying that two states that are apart in
\mathcal{T}
can never be mapped to the same state in
\mathcal{H}
. Notice that the premises of the conditions in constraints (1) and (2) are known before calling the solver. Thus, to further speed up the solving time, we can simply include the conclusion itself as a constraint, only if the premise holds.

When the solver returns
SAT
, it is easy to extract a hypothesis from
\delta_{\mathcal{H}}
and
F_{\mathcal{H}}
. In the case that the solver returns
UNSAT
, we know that no hypothesis exists.

Proposition 1

When an SMT solver with constraints (1) to (4) returns
SAT
, then there exists a complete DFA
\mathcal{H}
with at most
n
states with a morphism
f:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{H}
. Likewise, when the solver returns
UNSAT
, then there does not exist such a DFA.

3.3
Correctness and Query Complexity of
L^{\#}_{\square}

In this subsection, we will prove that the
L^{\#}_{\square}
algorithm is correct in the sense that it always terminates, and when it terminates, it returns a correct and minimal DFA. We will start by proving the minimality of the returned DFA. To this end, we will show that
n
is only incremented when there is no complete DFA with
n
states that the teacher would accept.

Lemma 3

Consider a learner that uses
L^{\#}_{\Box}
to interact with a teacher that answers membership and validity queries for disjoint languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.
Suppose that, at some point, the learner has constructed PTA
\mathcal{T}
, and suppose further that
\mathcal{B}
is a complete separating DFA for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.
Then
\mathcal{T}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}
.

Proof

Since
\mathcal{B}
is a complete DFA, we may easily construct a unique mapping
f
from
Q_{\mathcal{T}}
to
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
that satisfies the first and third condition of a morphism:

f(q^{0}_{\mathcal{T}})=q^{0}_{\mathcal{B}}
and

q\xrightarrow{a}_{\mathcal{T}}q^{\prime}

\implies

f(q)\xrightarrow{a}_{\mathcal{B}}f(q^{\prime})
.
It remains to show that
f
satisfies the second condition of a morphism.
Suppose that, for some state
q\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}
,
F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)\mathord{\downarrow}
.
Let
w=\textsf{access}(q)
. Then the learner has performed a membership query to which the teacher has replied, leading to value
F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)
being defined. There are two cases:
(1) The teacher replied
+
. This means that
w\in L_{1}
.
Since
\mathcal{B}
is a separating DFA for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
,
L_{1}\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
.
Hence
w\in L(\mathcal{B})
. This implies
F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)=F_{\mathcal{B}}(f(q))=1
, as required.
(2) The teacher replied
-
. This means that
w\in L_{2}
.
Since
\mathcal{B}
is a separating DFA for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
,
L_{2}\cap L(\mathcal{B})=\emptyset
.
Hence
w\not\in L(\mathcal{B})
. This implies
F_{\mathcal{T}}(q)=F_{\mathcal{B}}(f(q))=0
, as required.

Lemma 4

When the SMT solver returns
UNSAT
, then there is no complete DFA
\mathcal{H}
with
n
states that would be accepted by the teacher.

Proof

The proof follows from the second part of

Lemma 5

There does not exist a complete DFA

with fewer than

states such that

is a PTA for
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof

We know that all states in

are pairwise apart. Thus, for any morphism

from
\mathcal{T}
to

, we know from

that every state in

. Thus,
\mathcal{H}

Proposition 2

When the
L^{\#}_{\square}
algorithm returns a DFA
\mathcal{H}
, then
\mathcal{H}
is a minimal separating DFA for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. In other words, there does not exist a (complete) DFA
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with fewer states than
\mathcal{H}
that would be accepted by the teacher.

Proof

From
Lemma

4
and
Lemma

5
, we know that whenever we increment

, then there does not exist a valid hypothesis with
n
or fewer states. Thus, when the teacher accepts our hypothesis with
n

n

Next, we will show that
L^{\#}_{\square}
always terminates if
L_{1}
is regular. We use the following bound on the number of regular languages that are accepted by a DFA with up to
N

Lemma 6
(Ishigami & Tani
[
17
]
)

Let
k
be the number of input symbols in alphabet
\Sigma
,
N
a natural number, and

\displaystyle\mathit{DFA}(N)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{L(\mathcal{A})\mid\mathcal{A}\mbox{ is a DFA with at most }N\mbox{ states over alphabet }\Sigma\}.

Then
|\mathit{DFA}(N)|\leq 2^{(k-1+o(1))N\log N}
.

Theorem 3.1

If
L_{1}
is regular then the
L^{\#}_{\square}
algorithm terminates.
The total number of membership queries is in
\mathcal{O}(kN2^{kN\log N})
, and the total number of validity queries is at most
2^{(k-1+o(1))N\log N}
.

Proof

If
L_{1}
is regular then there is DFA
\mathcal{A}
with
L(\mathcal{A})=L_{1}
. This DFA separates
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. This implies that a minimal, complete separating DFA exists.
Let
N
be the number of states of a minimal, complete separating DFA for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.
We verify that each of the four rules can only be applied finitely often:

•

Promotion rule.

By
Lemma

3
and
Lemma

5
, the size of the basis is at most
N
, and thus the promotion rule can be applied at most
N-1
times.

•

Extension rule.

The extension rule may explore, for each basis state, all outgoing paths of length at most
n-|S|+1
. Since
n
is at most
N
and
|S|
is at least 1, the rule may explore all outgoing paths of length at most
N
for each basis state.
Hence the extension rule may be applied at most

N(1+k+k^{2}+\cdots+k^{N})\in\mathcal{O}(Nk^{N})

many times.

•

Validity rule.

Since we increment
n
whenever the SMT solver answers “no”,
such a “no” answer may occur at most
N-1
times during a run
of the algorithm.
In case of a “yes” answer, the learner poses a validity query.
When it sends a hypothesis
\mathcal{H}
to the teacher and receives a counterexample
w
, then any
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with
L(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})=L(\mathcal{H})
is no longer a valid hypothesis after adding
w
to
\mathcal{T}
. From the first part of
Proposition

1
we know that the SMT solver will only give a valid hypothesis. Thus,
the total number of validity queries (and applications of the validity rule) is
in
\mathcal{O}(2^{kN\log N})
, by
Lemma

6
.

•

Identification rule.

During each application of the identification rule, the learner performs a single membership query.
If this membership query does not return
\square
, then the candidate set of some successor of a basis state decreases in size. Since this can happen at most
\mathcal{O}(kN^{2})
times, from some moment on all membership queries performed during application of the identification rule will return
\square
.
This means that no new witnesses will be generated and therefore at some point we will have have explored all witnesses for all successors of all basis states, and no more applications of the identification rule are possible.
We may bound the number of applications of the identification rule as follows:
each identification query runs a witness starting from a frontier state.
The number of frontier states is bounded by
kN
.
The number of witnesses is bounded by the total number of queries resulting in a
+
or
-
answer, which in turn is bounded by the sum of
(a) the number of extension queries, which is in
\mathcal{O}(Nk^{N})
,
(b) the number of validity queries, which is in
\mathcal{O}(2^{kN\log N})
, and
(c) the number of identification queries resulting in a
+
or
-
answer, which is in
\mathcal{O}(kN^{2})
. Thus the number of identification queries is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(kN2^{kN\log N})
.

The overall bound of
\mathcal{O}(kN2^{kN\log N})
on the number of membership queries is directly implied by the derived bounds for the number of extension and identification queries.

4.2
Replacing Basis States

During a run of the algorithm, it may occur that we add a state to the basis that is disconnected from the other basis states. Such a disconnected basis state can potentially be quite deep in the observation tree. However, it is often the case that nodes that are not as deep in the observation tree are more likely to be informative compared to nodes that are deeper in the tree. Furthermore, in practice, performing queries for words with fewer symbols can be faster than when using more symbols. Thus, it is desirable to choose a basis such that its states are as close as possible to the root of the observation tree.
To achieve this, we allow replacing deep basis states with closer basis states: whenever
q\in Q\setminus S
with
C(q)=\left\{p\right\}
and
\left|\textsf{access}(q)\right|<\left|\textsf{access}(p)\right|
, we may replace
p
with
q
in
S
.

5.3
Evaluation of Optimisations

To evaluate the performance of the optimisations of
Section

4
and the redundant clauses of the SMT solver, we performed an ablation-style study
for the benchmarks of Oliveira and Silva
[
24
]
.

The use of redundant clauses in the SMT encoding leads to a significant improvement in the running time, as shown in
Fig.

11
. This effect increases for larger benchmarks. For benchmarks of sizes 12 and above, the algorithm already becomes impractical without the redundant clauses.

Replacing basis states with states that have shorter access sequences leads to a small improvement, and reduces the number of input symbols in membership queries with
7\%
, as shown in
Fig.

12
.

Finally, requiring compatibility of all pairs of basis states, rather than apartness, does not lead to a significant improvement. The running time for the version with compatibility is only
2\%
faster on average, as shown in

6
Discussion

We introduced
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, a simple algorithm for learning a minimal separating DFA using membership and validity queries.
We see several possibilities for further improving
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, e.g., the SMT encoding can be further optimized using ideas from
[
15
]
and/or incremental SMT solving, and we may develop heuristics to predict for which membership queries we can expect a useful (
+
or
-
) answer by learning an approximation of
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
.

When Angluin
[
1
]
introduced the notion of
Minimally Adequate Teacher (MAT)
, she argued that a teacher is only “adequate” when it is possible for a learner to learn a concept efficiently using only a polynomial number of queries.
Even when
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are regular, we have no polynomial bound on the number of queries required by
L^{\#}_{\Box}
, and indeed it is an open problem whether teachers that answer membership+validity queries are adequate, in the sense that they allow a learner to learn a separating DFA with a polynomial number of queries.
So one might argue that the name
iMAT
for the learning framework of
[
21
]
was chosen prematurely.
Adequacy is obtained trivially when the teacher, besides membership queries, also answers equivalence queries for
L_{1}
and
\overline{L_{2}}
. A learner may then first learn DFAs for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
using a state-of-the-art DFA learner, and then compute a separating automaton using an SMT solver.
The query complexity of such an approach is
\mathcal{O}(|\Sigma|(n_{1}+n_{2})^{2}+(n_{1}+n_{2})\log m)
, where
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are the number of states of the minimal DFAs for
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, respectively, and
m
is the length of the longest counterexample.
As equivalence queries are incomparable with validity queries, one might argue that membership+equivalence queries provide a MAT framework for learning separating automata.
However, a naive algorithm that uses equivalence queries is impractical as it requires one to solve a big instance of an NP-hard problem.
Clearly, the trade off between query and time complexity of learning algorithms deserves further study.

Even in a restricted setting where no “don’t care” answer is ever returned, the
L^{\#}_{\Box}
and
L^{\#}
algorithms behave differently. Whereas
L^{\#}
poses extra queries (using binary search) to analyze counterexamples,
L^{\#}_{\Box}
does not have or need counterexample processing. Also, unlike
L^{\#}
,
L^{\#}_{\Box}
does not require the basis to be prefix closed. Whereas
L^{\#}
extends the basis with a single input at a time,
L^{\#}_{\Box}
may use longer sequences. It will be interesting to compare both algorithms in a setting without “don’t care” answers, but we expect
L^{\#}
to be far more efficient in terms of the required number of queries.

An obvious direction for future research is to extend our new algorithm to richer model classes such as Mealy machines, symbolic automata
[
11
]
, register automata
[
10
]
, weighted automata
[
2
]
, and automata with timers
[
5
]
.
The SMT encoding used by
L^{\#}_{\Box}
for hypothesis construction only involves propositional logic and uninterpreted functions, but the
ability of SMT solvers to also handle real numbers, integers, and various data structures may effectively support learning of richer automata classes, even in settings without “don’t care” answers.

Appendix 0.B
State Incompatibility and Apartness

For every 3NFA
\mathcal{A}
, an equivalent 3DFA can be constructed using the classical subset construction
[
16
]
, but this may lead to an exponential blowup of the number of (reachable) states.
In this appendix, we consider an alternative, stronger notion of determinizability: the existence of a 3DFA
\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{A}\sqsubseteq_{f}\mathcal{B}
, for some
f
.
W.l.o.g. we may assume that all states of
\mathcal{B}
are in the image of
f
, which implies that the number of states of
\mathcal{B}
is less than or equal to the number of states of
\mathcal{A}
.
Not every 3NFA is strongly determinizable and we prove how, using the RPNI state merging algorithm
[
25
]
, we may efficiently decide strong determinizability.

As a corollary, we also obtain an algorithm for deciding incompatibility.
We then show how, based on a run of the RPNI algorithm that establishes incompatibility of states
p
and
q
, we may compute a collection of membership queries that, when answered with a “yes” or “no”’answer by the teacher, establish apartness of
p
and
q
.

Definition 6

An 3NFA

\mathcal{B}
such that

with
\mathcal{A}\sqsubseteq\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{A}
to a 3DFA

Note that for any state

of
\mathcal{B}
with unknown status, all states of
\mathcal{A}
that are mapped to

by

is also a morphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

In the
L^{\#}_{\Box}
algorithm, rather than requiring that states in the basis are pairwise
apart, we may just require them to be pairwise incompatible.
However, a key advantage of apartness over incompatibility is that witnesses for the apartness of basis states may subsequently be used to identify frontier states.
Therefore, whenever we add a state to the basis that is not apart from some of the basis states, it may pay off to perform some additional queries and try to establish apartness of incompatible basis states
(queries that may or may not be answered by the incomplete teacher).

1

Basically, there are three cases to consider.

In the second case,
