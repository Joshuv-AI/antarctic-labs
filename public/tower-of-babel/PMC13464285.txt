Staged Cesarean and Cardiac Surgery for Recurrent Left Atrial Myxofibrosarcoma in Pregnancy
Authors: Yoshiyuki Watanabe, Shun-ichiro Sakamoto, Norio Motoda, Motohiro Maeda, Tomohiro Murata, Yosuke Ishii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac surgery in late pregnancy requires balancing maternal rescue against fetal risk.Case SummaryA 31-week pregnant woman with prior resection of a left atrial “myxoma” presented with acute heart failure caused by recurrent masses prolapsing through the mitral valve. Because the fetus was viable and maternal collapse was imminent, a 7-F, 35-mL intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted, cesarean delivery was performed, and redo open-heart surgery followed the next day. A 1,638-g male infant with Apgar scores of 7 and 8 was delivered. Two posterior left atrial tumors were resected; one involved the mitral leaflet and required mechanical mitral valve replacement. Re-review of the recurrent and original specimens showed morphology and immunophenotype consistent with myxofibrosarcoma.DiscussionStaged delivery before cardiopulmonary bypass can protect a viable fetus in hemodynamically critical maternal disease.Take-Home MessagePathological re-evaluation is essential when “recurrent myxoma” behaves atypically.Key Words: hemodynamics, mitral valve, pregnancy, valve replacement

History of Presentation

A pregnant woman at 31 weeks’ gestation presented with acute dyspnea and clinical features of acute heart failure. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography revealed 2 large, mobile left atrial masses prolapsing through the mitral valve into the left ventricle, producing intermittent functional mitral inflow obstruction and moderate mitral regurgitation (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Because threatened circulatory collapse was evident, urgent intervention was required.

Past Medical History

Three years earlier, she had undergone resection of a left atrial tumor with patch reconstruction at another hospital. The lesion had been diagnosed as cardiac myxoma. No adjuvant therapy had been given. The current pregnancy had been followed by our obstetric team until the onset of symptoms.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included recurrent atrial myxoma, left atrial thrombus, other primary cardiac sarcoma, and, less likely, vegetation. The marked mobility, large size, prior tumor history, and atrial-wall attachment favored recurrent tumor.

Investigations

Chest radiography showed severe pulmonary congestion. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed 2 recurrent masses measuring approximately 51 mm and 48 mm that prolapsed across the mitral valve (Videos 1 and 2). Computed tomography also demonstrated recurrent left atrial tumor. After obstetric and neonatal assessment, the fetus was considered viable at 31 weeks. Given the hemodynamic consequences of intermittent mitral obstruction, the risks of sudden death and worsening heart failure were judged to be high.

Management

After multidisciplinary discussion among cardiac surgery, cardiology, obstetrics, anesthesiology, and neonatology, staged management was chosen because fetal viability was judged sufficient and cardiopulmonary bypass during ongoing pregnancy carries substantial fetal risk.1, 2, 3 To stabilize maternal hemodynamics, a 7-F, 35-mL intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted preoperatively. Cesarean delivery was performed first. A 1,638-g male infant with Apgar scores of 7 and 8 was delivered and admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit. After confirming the absence of major postpartum bleeding, redo open-heart surgery was undertaken the next day with the balloon pump in place.

The right common femoral artery and vein were exposed before redo median sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated using ascending aortic inflow and bicaval drainage through the superior vena cava and right femoral vein. During adhesiolysis, the ascending aorta was injured. Arterial inflow was therefore converted emergently to the right femoral artery, the aorta was cross-clamped, and antegrade crystalloid cardioplegia was administered at 30-minute intervals. The aortic injury was repaired with bovine pericardium. Because the left atrial roof had been patched previously, the left atrium was entered with a Dubost's incision.

Two yellow-white tumors, 51 mm and 48 mm in diameter, were attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium. One tumor involved the mitral leaflet. Grossly, the lesions resembled recurrent myxoma. Both tumors were resected as completely as feasible. Cryoablation of each attachment site was then performed at −40 °C for 120 seconds for local control (Figure 2). Because the mitral valve could not be preserved owing to severe anterior leaflet shortening and degeneration, mitral valve replacement with a 23-mm mechanical prosthesis was performed. The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass under balloon pump support. Operative time was 395 minutes, cardiopulmonary bypass time was 216 minutes, and aortic cross-clamp time was 143 minutes.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated on the day of surgery and the balloon pump was removed on postoperative day 1. No major bleeding occurred. She developed recurrent syncope due to severe bradycardia and underwent permanent pacemaker implantation on postoperative day 4. The infant's neonatal intensive care unit course was uneventful, and subsequent growth has been normal. The mother was discharged on postoperative day 17, and both mother and infant remained well. At 6 months, imaging has shown no recurrence.

Histopathology showed a densely cellular spindle-cell neoplasm in a myxofibrous stroma with curvilinear vessels and focal coagulative necrosis (Figure 3). The tumor was negative for CD31, CD34, calretinin, α-SMA, desmin, S-100, c-kit, and epithelial membrane antigen, but positive for p53. The Ki-67 labeling index measured with the MIB-1 antibody (MIB-1 index) reached 40% in hotspot areas (mean approximately 10%) (Figure 4). Review of the original specimen demonstrated similar morphology, a hotspot MIB-1 index of approximately 30%, and 3 to 5 mitoses per high-power field, supporting recurrence of the same tumor. The final diagnosis was cardiac myxofibrosarcoma (Figure 5). The procedural equipment used throughout diagnosis and treatment is summarized in Figure 6.

Histology of the Recurrent Tumor

Histopathology (top two images: hematoxylin and eosin stain ×20; bottom left image: hematoxylin and eosin stain ×40; bottom right image: Alcian-blue and Periodic acid-Schiff stain ×10) of the recurrent tumor demonstrates dense spindle-cell proliferation in a myxofibrous stroma with characteristic curvilinear vascularity (Δ). Immunohistochemistry of the Recurrent Tumor

The recurrent tumor was negative for CD31, CD34, and calretinin, markers that are often positive in cardiac myxoma. Other lineage markers, including α-SMA, desmin, S-100, c-kit, and EMA, were also negative. In contrast, p53 was positive in many cells, and the MIB-1 index reached 40% in hotspot areas, supporting malignant mesenchymal neoplasia. EMA = epithelial membrane antigen.

Discussion

This case is notable for 2 reasons: staged management of a hemodynamically critical cardiac tumor in late pregnancy and pathological reclassification of a presumed recurrent myxoma as myxofibrosarcoma. Cardiac surgery during pregnancy remains uncommon, and fetal risk exceeds maternal risk. In the patient-level meta-analysis by van Steenbergen et al,1 maternal mortality was 7.3% and fetal mortality was 26.5%.1 The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend considering delivery before maternal cardiac surgery when the fetus is viable,2 and the Mayo Clinic 40-year experience likewise showed lower fetal loss after delivery-first management than after surgery during ongoing pregnancy.3 In our patient at 31 weeks’ gestation, staged cesarean delivery before cardiopulmonary bypass was therefore consistent with contemporary evidence. The 1-day interval allowed obstetric hemostasis without dangerous delay in resection.

Preoperative intra-aortic balloon support likely helped bridge a hemodynamically unstable patient across cesarean delivery and definitive surgery. Although experience is limited, this strategy has been used in selected high-risk obstetric cardiac patients.4

The pathological diagnosis was equally important. Conventional cardiac myxoma is typically calretinin positive, whereas our tumor was calretinin negative and showed increased cellularity, curvilinear vessels, p53 positivity, and a high proliferative index.5, 6, 7 Primary cardiac myxofibrosarcoma often arises in the left atrium, may mimic myxoma grossly, and carries high rates of local recurrence and metastasis with poor survival.5,8,9 Re-review of the original specimen showed similar histology and proliferative activity, making initial under-recognition of sarcoma more likely than true malignant transformation. Das et al10 described a similar diagnostic dilemma between myxoma and myxofibrosarcoma.

From a surgical perspective, complete resection was limited by leaflet involvement, so mechanical mitral valve replacement and cryoablation of the attachment sites were used for local control. Even after aggressive resection, primary cardiac sarcomas recur frequently, so the current 6-month disease-free interval should be interpreted cautiously. Close imaging surveillance and multidisciplinary oncologic follow-up are warranted.8,9

Conclusions

In late pregnancy with a viable fetus and a hemodynamically critical cardiac tumor, staged cesarean delivery followed by prompt open-heart surgery is feasible. Recurrent left atrial “myxoma” with atypical pathology should prompt expert re-evaluation for cardiac sarcoma.
