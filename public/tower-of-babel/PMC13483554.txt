Changing the narrative: stories reduce biases against anomalous faces
Authors: Nadir Bilici, Mariola Paruzel-Czachura, Clifford I. Workman, Stacey Humphries, Roy H. Hamilton, Anjan Chatterjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Facial anomalies, such as scars, are often linked to an “anomalous-is-bad” stereotype, in which people with visible differences are seen as less warm or less moral. We developed the “Normalizing Anomalies with Mobile Exposure” (NAME) intervention based on the idea that repeated exposure and narratives can shift automatic evaluations of others. In a between-subjects design, one group of participants was repeatedly exposed to faces with visible anomalies paired with narratives describing morally praiseworthy actions, while a second group viewed phenotypically typical faces of people of color paired with the same type of narratives. Each group completed two daily sessions for five consecutive days via a mobile application. Across both versions of the intervention, implicit evaluations of the targeted faces became more positive over time, with the anomalous faces version showing the clearest target-specific improvement. Explicit attitudes did not change. These findings suggest that exposure-based storytelling can alter automatic evaluations of individuals with facial anomalies and may offer a promising, scalable approach to reducing stigma associated with visible differences, although further research is needed to test durability and generalizability to other groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04964-x.

Background

Most people rarely encounter individuals with facial differences, which is unsurprising given that only about one in every 100 people has a facial anomaly [1, 2]. In this article, we use facial anomalies to refer to visible departures from typical facial appearance—such as scars, burns, or facial palsy—that are salient to observers but do not themselves imply anything about a person’s moral character. Prior research suggests that observers sometimes avoid social interactions with individuals whose appearance deviates from local norms [3–6]. Over time, such reactions can foster an “anomalous-is-bad” stereotype that links visible facial differences to moral failings, for example by interpreting scars as signs of past wrongdoing or personal weakness [7, 8]. Empirically, this prejudice has been operationalized mainly with explicit rating scales and choice tasks: participants are asked to evaluate photographs of people with and without visible differences on traits such as warmth, trustworthiness, morality, competence, attractiveness, and dehumanization, or to indicate whether they would like to interact with or hire them. Across several studies, faces with scars or facial palsy tend to receive lower ratings on warmth- and competence-related traits and are chosen less often as preferred interaction partners or job candidates [2, 9–11], although these patterns are not uniform across all contexts. These negative inferences have been explained both in terms of an overactive behavioral immune system that treats some anomalies as potential disease cues [12, 13] and in terms of culturally learned associations—for instance, recurrent portrayals of scarred villains in popular media [14].

Because most people have limited opportunities to interact with individuals with facial anomalies, they have few chances to develop positive or neutral impressions. Popular culture compounds the problem: villains in films like Scar in The Lion King often have visible facial anomalies, while heroes seldom do [15, 16]. This depiction reinforces that a “flawed” face means a flawed character. Evidence-based interventions to counteract these biases could reduce the stigma associated with facial anomalies [17], which can cause severe psychological harm, including suicidal ideation [18].

From a social-cognitive perspective, this combination of rarity and skewed representation is likely to foster illusory correlations: people may overestimate how often visible anomalies co-occur with negative behaviors simply because such pairings are distinctive and memorable [19]. Related work on disability and other visible differences suggests that limited opportunities for positive contact, together with stereotype-consistent portrayals, can sustain biased expectations even when personal experience would not warrant them [20]. These considerations motivate interventions that both increase exposure to counterstereotypical exemplars and alter the narratives that accompany them.

Strategies to decrease “anomalous-is-bad” association

We identified two complementary strategies to reduce the “anomalous-is-bad” stereotype: (1) increasing exposure to faces with anomalies and (2) pairing anomalous faces with positive narratives, and we combined both in our intervention. Regarding exposure, classic intergroup-contact research shows that structured, repeated contact with out-group members can reduce prejudice and intergroup anxiety [21]. At a more perceptual level, expertise-based accounts of face recognition suggest that frequent exposure to particular categories of faces increases processing fluency, which in turn is associated with more favorable evaluations—for example, in the context of the cross-race effect [22, 23]. The mere-exposure effect likewise indicates that repeated contact with a stimulus, including faces, can increase liking [24–26]. In racial-prejudice paradigms, even very rapid and brief exposures of White participants to out-group faces—without any additional moral or biographical information—can enhance liking for those faces [27]. By analogy, more frequent, low-threat contact with individuals with facial anomalies could reduce negative automatic reactions by increasing familiarity and processing fluency.

Narratives can leverage the power of moral information. Morality is an important trait people consider when forming impressions of others [28–30] and self [31–33], and anomalous-is-bad relates to moral traits [10]. Consistent with this idea, Stone and Fisher (2020) found that a brief online intervention, in which individuals with facial disfigurement shared personal narratives (delivered via audio or video), led to more positive evaluations and greater willingness for future interaction compared with viewing a still facial photograph. Their findings suggest that even short, narrative-based indirect contact can shift explicit attitudes toward people with facial differences. To the extent that visible facial differences sometimes trigger pathogen-related disgust responses [11, 12], repeatedly pairing anomalous faces with healthy, prosocial exemplars may also provide disconfirming evidence that weakens overgeneralized disease-avoidance inferences. Unfortunately, popular culture often delivers countering narratives: with frequent negative portrayals of people with facial anomalies—for instance, villains who have pronounced facial scars—enhance reflexive negative emotional responses such as disgust and fear [34]. Hollywood and Bollywood films depict villains with more pronounced facial anomalies than heroes. By contrast, telling stories about moral heroes and their prosocial actions can foster empathy and encourage prosocial behavior [35–37]. In these narratives, the perceived similarity between the audience and the exemplar, along with the sense that the exemplar’s moral actions are attainable, determines how effective the stories are in changing attitudes and behaviors [38, 39].

The current research

We created the “Normalizing Anomalies with Mobile Exposure” (NAME) intervention to mitigate biases against people with facial anomalies. This approach combines repeated exposure to photographs of individuals with facial anomalies and moral exemplar stories about them. In this work, we distinguish between explicit and implicit bias. Explicit bias refers to consciously endorsed beliefs and evaluations that people can report on questionnaires—for example, beliefs about the character, capabilities, and everyday lives of people with visible facial differences. Implicit bias refers to more automatic associations that can influence judgments even when individuals endorse egalitarian values; here, we assess it with the Implicit Association Test (IAT), which indexes the relative speed of pairing anomalous versus typical faces with positive and negative words. Although researchers have documented both implicit and explicit biases toward people with facial differences [1, 7], the NAME intervention was designed primarily to target negative implicit attitudes, with explicit evaluations examined as a secondary outcome.

Our preregistered analyses focused on three main questions. First, we tested whether NAME would reduce the specific implicit bias it was designed to target, pooling across the anomalous faces and control versions of the intervention. Second, we asked whether the two variants of NAME would differ in the magnitude of change in their respective target biases. This question was motivated by work on intergroup contact and cross-category generalization, which suggests that different forms of contact can sometimes show different levels of specificity, but did not lead us to a strong directional prediction. Third, we examined whether any changes would generalize beyond the targeted bias—that is, whether each intervention would affect both anomalous faces and people of color biases—or would instead remain relatively specific to the targeted category. We expected any effects to be strongest for the bias directly targeted by a given intervention, while remaining agnostic about how much they might generalize across categories.

Implicit bias training programs attempt to promote equity [40]. They can enhance individuals’ awareness of their own implicit biases [41], reduce negative attitudes [42], and ease discomfort related to disability [43]. However, meta-analytic reviews suggest that many one-shot trainings produce only small and sometimes short-lived changes in implicit measures, underscoring the importance of multi-session, contact-based approaches that incorporate positive exemplars and perspective taking [44–46]. The NAME intervention was designed with these lessons in mind, delivering repeated, narrative-based indirect contact via a mobile platform. In our study, participants first completed a baseline assessment of implicit biases. We then randomly assigned them to one of two remote, mobile-based interventions: exposure to people with facial anomalies or exposure to control faces. Over five consecutive days, participants completed two sessions per day. Each session began with exposure to 10 face photographs (anomalous or control), followed by a moral exemplar story paired with another person’s face from the same group. Before ending each session, participants answered questions about the story and their emotional responses.

We pre-registered the prediction that exposure to people with facial anomalies relative to a set of control faces would reduce implicit bias scores acquired before and after completing an exposure intervention. We treated all other analyses as exploratory.

We provided monetary compensation to participants after they completed their second set of study visits. To be eligible, participants had to own a smartphone (iPhone or Android) with sufficient disk space to install our custom phone application, have consistent internet access to allow secure backups of de-identified data, speak English fluently, and have no significant visual impairments that would interfere with the study procedures. Individual IAT administrations were excluded according to prespecified IAT scoring criteria when error rates exceeded 30% or latencies below 300 ms exceeded 10% of trials. As a result, the primary paired analysis included N = 98 participants with complete valid pre- and post-intervention congruent IAT scores.

We conducted the study according to the American Psychological Association’s Ethical Principles in the Conduct of Research with Human Participants, and the Institutional Review Board at the University of Pennsylvania approved all procedures. Before starting, participants provided written informed consent. They were prompted to confirm their consent when they opened the phone application.

Participants were enrolled through an online participant pool from the University of Pennsylvania. They scheduled their baseline and follow-up visits within 5–8 days of one another (with two exceptions: one participant returned after 9 days and another after 10). Between these visits, participants completed the smartphone-based NAME intervention. Each session in the intervention took place twice a day over five consecutive days and consisted of two parts. In the first part, we adapted methods from Zebrowitz and colleagues [27] to capitalize on the mere-exposure effect, showing participants a series of faces from a stigmatized social group. In the second part, inspired by Majdandžić and colleagues [47], we presented a brief story that described a moral exemplar and encouraged participants to represent that person’s mental state.

Power analysis

We used G*Power [48] to conduct an a priori power analysis for the preregistered primary analysis: a paired-samples comparison of pre- versus post-intervention IAT D-scores for the intervention-congruent bias. During planning, we could not identify a directly analogous effect-size estimate for the present combination of target population, repeated mobile exposure, moral-exemplar narratives, and IAT-based pre-post outcome. We therefore relied on the closest effect-size estimates we had identified from adjacent literatures. Zebrowitz, White, and Wieneke [27] provided the closest face-exposure analogue, although their study used an independent-groups design and therefore served as a heuristic planning proxy rather than a direct estimate of the present within-subjects effect. Majdandžić et al. [47] provided a smaller, conceptually relevant humanization/perspective-taking analogue. Using the Zebrowitz-based planning estimate, the planned paired-samples test required approximately 42 participants per condition for 80% power at alpha = 0.05. We preregistered a larger sample of N = 100 total to allow for attrition and quality-control exclusions. The preregistration also specified an optional second recruitment wave if the initial sample produced a marginal primary result; this wave was not triggered because the primary paired-samples test was not marginal, t(97) = 3.96, p = .00014. For the final sample used in the primary analysis (N = 98 complete pairs), a two-tailed paired-samples test at alpha = 0.05 had 80% power to detect d_z = 0.286. The study was less sensitive for secondary between-condition and higher-order interaction effects: with approximately 49 participants per intervention arm, between-condition comparisons of change had 80% power only for effects of approximately d_s = 0.57 or larger. Accordingly, null interaction effects should be interpreted cautiously.

Self-reported materials

Psychological dispositions

We asked participants to complete a brief online Qualtrics survey before their baseline study visit. This survey included demographic questions and self-report measures (detailed in the Supplementary Materials) that assessed beliefs about justice and egalitarianism, as well as sensitivity to disgust and various facets of empathy. Prior research highlights the effectiveness of perspective taking in enhancing morally relevant attitudes about others [47], so we focused on the dispositional perspective-taking subscale from the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) [49]. We collected self-report measures for exploratory purposes.

Implicit biases

Our primary measure was the change in implicit bias following the NAME intervention. Recent research shows that people harbor strong implicit biases against individuals with visible facial anomalies [10]. We defined implicit bias as the pre-reflective attribution of characteristics to others based on group memberships [50]. We assessed implicit bias with the Implicit Association Test [51] (IAT), a widely used tool for inferring the strength of automatic intergroup attitudes [52, 53]. Although it has been scrutinized for its predictive validity for real-world behavior [45, 54], the IAT remains the measure with the most favorable psychometric properties among available alternatives [55]. At the same time, substantial debate remains regarding what exactly IAT scores capture and the extent to which they predict discriminatory behavior outside laboratory settings. Recent reviews and theoretical discussions suggest that implicit measures may partly reflect a mixture of associative knowledge, cultural exposure, situational context, and task-specific processes rather than stable “hidden attitudes” alone (e.g., [56–58]). We therefore interpret IAT scores in the present study as indices of automatic evaluative associations rather than direct proxies for overt discriminatory behavior.

At the baseline study visit, participants completed two versions of the IAT, both of which used “good” and “bad” words (e.g., adore, friendly, spectacular vs. disgust, rotten, ugly) paired with facial images. In the first version, the faces did or did not feature visible anomalies. In the second version, the faces were either White or a person of color (none of these faces had anomalies). Participants completed the same two IATs again at the follow-up visit.

We computed IAT D-scores using the improved scoring algorithm described in the Supplementary Materials, based on Greenwald et al.’s approach [59]. In our coding, higher (more positive) D-scores indicate stronger negative implicit bias toward the focal group (anomalous faces or faces of people of color), scores around zero indicate little or no bias, and negative D-scores indicate relatively more positive associations with that group (i.e., faster associations between the focal faces and good words than bad words).

Explicit biases

We also measured explicit biases before and after the intervention using the 33-item Explicit Bias Questionnaire (EBQ) [7]. The EBQ evaluates personal history with, and general beliefs about, people with visible facial anomalies; higher scores reflect stronger explicit biases.

NAME intervention design and delivery

After completing the baseline IATs and EBQ, we pseudo-randomly assigned participants to one of two conditions. In the “anomalous faces” (n = 50), participants viewed photographs of people with visible facial anomalies. In the control condition (n = 50), participants viewed photographs of people of color. We asked participants to complete two intervention sessions per day—one in the morning and one in the afternoon—for five consecutive days, resulting in a total of 10 sessions (approximately 30 min of intervention time). Participants received two daily push notifications (at 8:00 a.m. and 12:30 p.m. local time) reminding them to complete a session. These times were chosen for practical reasons: to avoid very early or very late notifications, to coincide with periods when most participants would likely be awake, and to distribute reminders across the morning and early afternoon. Participants were free to complete sessions at any time during the day, regardless of when they received the notification. Participants completed 91.4% of all scheduled sessions, missing less than one session on average. No participants were excluded on the basis of intervention-session completion. Follow-up visits were scheduled as a short-term post-intervention assessment, and all participants returned within 1.75 ± 1.03 days of concluding the intervention.

Smartphone application

We created the NAME application in React Native (https://reactnative.dev/) using the Expo framework (https://expo.io/) for iOS and Android. We wrote the source code in JavaScript and made it publicly available at https://osf.io/vm7d4/?view_only=None. Participants installed the application on their personal devices during the baseline visit and completed a practice session. At the end of each intervention session, the app transmitted newly recorded responses to a secure REDCap server (https://www.project-redcap.org/). Face photographs

During the first part of every app session, participants viewed 10 non-repeating faces drawn from the group that defined their experimental condition. Across 10 sessions, each participant therefore saw 100 unique faces.

Anomalous faces condition (n = 50)

Stimuli for the anomalous faces condition were 100 photographs drawn from the ChatLab Facial Anomaly Database (CFAD). CFAD is a curated set of face photographs featuring visible facial differences of various types (e.g., scars, cleft lip, pigmentation differences, swelling, facial paralysis), etiologies (e.g., surgical, traumatic, congenital), sizes, and locations, and depicts individuals from a range of age groups and racial/ethnic backgrounds [60]. All images are preprocessed to a common format—faces are converted to grayscale, normalized to interpupillary distance, cropped to a fixed size, presented on a uniform black background, and luminance-normalized—providing standardized stimuli for research on perception and stigma. In the current study, we selected images in which the facial anomaly was clearly visible at conversational distance; as noted in the Limitations, most of these anomalies involved scarring or other acquired injuries rather than congenital differences.

Control condition (n = 50)

Stimuli consisted of 100 photographs of phenotypically typical people of color from the Chicago Face Database (CFD; 47 female, 28 Asian, 38 Black, 34 Latinx) [61]. None showed facial anomalies. We selected these faces to examine whether NAME-style narratives might also influence race-related implicit biases, but this choice means that anomaly status and race were not fully orthogonal across intervention arms (see Limitations).

For each image, participants made a binary sex classification, after which a 500-ms black screen appeared, followed by the next trial. Critically, at both laboratory visits, all participants completed the same two Implicit Association Tests (IATs): one contrasting anomalous versus typical faces and one contrasting people of color versus White faces. Thus, the outcome measures were identical and comparable across groups. Additional details on the face photographs are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Moral exemplar characteristics

In the second part of each session, participants were provided with four pieces of information intended to highlight a moral exemplar’s praiseworthy actions. The first piece of information was the exemplar’s photograph, randomly selected to represent a single individual. In the anomalous faces condition, we selected one of 50 images (out of 100 total) from the Chatlab Facial Anomaly Database, ensuring each had nearly complete demographic information (see Table S2). All 100 images were used in the control condition in both parts of each session.

After viewing the exemplar’s face, participants saw three additional details: the exemplar’s name, age, and occupation. We drew on a pool of 200 names (100 stereotypically male, 100 stereotypically female; Table S1), each randomly paired with a matching-gender face. These names reflect some of the most common baby names in the United States from 1919 to 2018, to foster familiarity and relatability. We presented the exemplar’s age, which was provided by the CFAD or Chicago Face Database, alongside one of five highly “warm” and “competent” occupations identified by Jenkins and colleagues [62]: athlete, farmer, nurse, parent, or surgeon. To avoid repetition, we generated six variations of each occupation—for instance, “athlete” also included “pro-gamer,” “sports coach,” and “gymnast” (see Table S3).

Moral exemplar stories

We then paired the exemplar’s photograph and characteristics with a short vignette describing the individual’s commendable actions, drawn from 41 real-world, first-person moral scenarios [63]. Table 2 provides five examples of these moral exemplar stories. After each story, participants answered three questions: “Which emotion do you feel most strongly toward [exemplar name]?” (admiration, disgust, guilt, shame, sympathy, anger, or pity), “How strongly do you feel that emotion?” (1 = Not strongly, 5 = Very strongly), and “I find it easy to put myself in this person’s shoes” (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree). As shown by Majdandžić and colleagues [47], simply responding to these questions can reduce implicit bias by prompting participants to adopt the perspectives of moral exemplars from stigmatized groups.

Each intervention session ended with a reminder about the next session and the upcoming in-person follow-up visit. At this point, the NAME application automatically transmitted participants’ data to a secure REDCap database. Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in JASP (version 0.17). At each time point, every participant contributed two Implicit Association Test D-scores—one indexing bias toward anomalous faces and one indexing bias toward faces of people of color (POC). Consequently, the data had a factorial structure defined by Intervention Group (anomalous faces versus control), Bias Type (anomalous faces versus POC faces), and Time (pre- versus post-intervention).

The analytic strategy consisted of three sequential stages, corresponding to the preregistered research questions. To address the basic question of whether the NAME intervention reduced the bias it was intended to target, we first selected, for each participant, the IAT D-score that matched the faces shown during the mobile-app sessions. Thus, participants in the anomalous faces group contributed their anomalous faces bias, whereas participants in the control group contributed their POC face bias. These intervention-congruent scores were compared before and after the intervention using a paired-samples t-test.

Next, we evaluated whether one version of the intervention was more effective than the other. The same intervention-congruent scores were entered into a mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Intervention Group as the between-subjects factor and Time as the within-subjects factor. An Intervention Group × Time interaction would indicate that the magnitude of change in congruent implicit bias differed across the two interventions.

Finally, we examined the specificity of the intervention effects: did each intervention reduce only its target bias, or did the effects generalize to other biases? Both bias scores from every participant—anomalous faces and POC faces—were entered into a three-factor mixed ANOVA with Intervention Group as the between-subjects factor and Bias Type and Time as within-subjects factors. A larger pre-to-post reduction for the intervention-congruent bias than for the incongruent bias was interpreted as evidence of a target-specific effect. Whenever omnibus tests required clarification, follow-up t-tests were conducted with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment. Effect sizes are labeled according to the analytic design: paired-samples t-test effects are reported as dz, between-subjects effects are reported as ds where applicable, and ANOVA effects are reported as generalized eta-squared (ηG2) in the main text, with additional effect-size information provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Beyond these confirmatory tests, exploratory correlations were used to examine psychological mechanisms. For each participant, the change in intervention-congruent bias (post minus pre; more negative values indicate greater reductions in negative implicit bias) was correlated with three session-level variables: the average intensity of prosocial emotions such as sympathy and admiration, the average intensity of antisocial emotions such as disgust and anger, and the average ease of perspective taking, a state measure of cognitive empathy. Correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed before being compared to determine whether any psychological factor emerged as a stronger predictor of intervention efficacy.

IAT scores were computed using the standard Greenwald, Nosek, and Banaji [59] D-score algorithm, as detailed in the Supplementary Materials. In this coding scheme, higher (more positive) D-scores indicate stronger negative implicit bias toward the focal group (anomalous faces or faces of people of color), values around zero indicate little or no bias, and negative D-scores indicate relatively more positive associations with that group. Confirmatory tests were two-tailed with alpha = 0.05. Directional one-tailed tests were retained only for directional exploratory hypotheses specified a priori and these tests are labeled explicitly. Where model assumptions required clarification, assumption checks and nonparametric robustness checks are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Because all focal within-subject ANOVA factors had only two levels, sphericity was not an issue for those effects.

Results

Pre-registered analysis 1: Did overall intervention reduce targeted implicit biases?

To examine the overall effect of the NAME intervention, we pooled data from both intervention variants and focused on the bias congruent with the faces seen during the app sessions. IAT D-scores for the targeted bias were significantly lower at Time 2 (M = -0.22, SD = 0.57) than at Time 1 (M = 0.04, SD = 0.48), t(97) = 3.96, p = .00014, 95% CI [0.13, 0.38], dz = 0.40 (see Fig. 2). One-sample tests against the “no-bias” value of zero showed that the mean targeted D-score at Time 1 did not differ significantly from zero, t(98) = 0.71, p = .480, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.13], whereas the mean at Time 2 was significantly below zero, t(98) = -3.81, p = .00024, 95% CI [-0.33, -0.10]. Thus, at the group level, participants’ automatic evaluations shifted from near-neutral to modestly more positive associations with the targeted group. Additional one-sample tests conducted separately for anomalous faces and POC IAT D-scores within each intervention arm are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Pre-registered analysis 2 (Secondary): Did either intervention produce more robust effects than the other?

As a secondary preregistered question, we examined whether the two intervention types differed in their efficacy at reducing congruent implicit biases. A 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA on the intervention-congruent IAT D-scores, with Intervention Group (anomalous faces vs. POC faces) as the between-subjects factor and Time (pre vs. post) as the within-subjects factor, revealed a main effect of Time, F(1,96) = 15.61, p = .00015, ηG2= 0.06. Congruent implicit biases were lower at Time 2 than at Time 1 (Time 1: anomalous faces M = 0.02, SD = 0.54; POC faces M = 0.05, SD = 0.42; Time 2: anomalous faces M = -0.27, SD = 0.64; POC faces M = -0.17, SD = 0.48), reflecting a reduction in negative implicit bias across both interventions. There was no main effect of Intervention Group, F(1,96) = 0.65, p = .422, ηG2= 0.01, and no Intervention Group x Time interaction, F(1,96) = 0.43, p = .514, ηG2 = 0.00 (see Fig. 3). Because the final sample was less sensitive for secondary between-condition comparisons of change than for the primary paired test, this null interaction should be interpreted cautiously.

Pre-registered analysis 3 (Secondary): Did either intervention have nonspecific effects on untargeted implicit biases?

Anomalous faces condition

A further secondary preregistered analysis examined the specificity of each intervention’s effects. In the anomalous faces condition, a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA with Bias Type (anomalous faces vs. POC faces) and Time (pre vs. post) showed a main effect of Time, F(1,48) = 6.49, p = .014, ηG2 = 0.03, and a Bias Type x Time interaction, F(1,48) = 4.71, p = .035, ηG2 = 0.02; the main effect of Bias Type was not significant, F(1,48) = 0.17, p = .684, ηG2 = 0.00. Follow-up paired t-tests with Holm correction indicated that bias against anomalous faces declined from pre-test (M = 0.02, SD = 0.54) to post-test (M = -0.27, SD = 0.64), t(48) = 3.07, pHolm = 0.007, dz = 0.44, reflecting a reduction in negative implicit bias toward anomalous faces. In contrast, bias against POC faces was essentially unchanged, moving from M = -0.08, SD = 0.45 at pre-test to M = -0.13, SD = 0.40 at post-test, t(48) = 0.53, pHolm = 0.599, dz = 0.08. Thus, the reduction in implicit bias was specific to the anomalous face bias. Explicit attitudes, measured with the EBQ, did not change (pre: M = 3.91, SD = 0.57; post: M = 3.91, SD = 0.56), t(49) = -0.11, p = .909, 95% CI [-0.11, 0.09], dz = -0.02.

Control condition

For the control group, a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA with Bias Type (anomalous faces vs. POC faces) and Time (pre vs. post) revealed a main effect of Time, F(1,48) = 5.42, p = .024, ηG2 = 0.03, indicating that implicit bias scores decreased overall from the first to the second test. There was also a main effect of Bias Type, F(1,48) = 4.62, p = .037, ηG2 = 0.02, such that participants were more negative toward POC faces than toward anomalous faces at both time points. The Bias Type x Time interaction was not significant, F(1,48) = 0.17, p = .684, ηG2 = 0.00, indicating that the reduction over time was similar for both types of bias. Descriptively, bias against POC faces declined from M = 0.05, SD = 0.42 at pre-test to M = -0.17, SD = 0.49 at post-test, whereas bias against anomalous faces declined from M = -0.12, SD = 0.64 to M = -0.27, SD = 0.50. Because this decline occurred similarly for both bias types and did not interact with the experimental manipulation, it is best interpreted cautiously as a nonspecific reduction rather than as clear evidence of target-specific change in the control condition (see Fig. 4).

Pre-registered analysis 4: did prosocial affect or perspective taking relate to the efficacy of the intervention?

Finally, we examined whether participants’ emotional responses and their self-reported ability to take the exemplar’s perspective were related to the efficacy of the intervention. Efficacy was indexed by a change score reflecting reductions in intervention-congruent implicit bias. These directional exploratory correlations are reported with one-tailed p-values. Although participants could select both prosocial emotions (e.g., admiration) and antisocial emotions (e.g., disgust), they overwhelmingly reported prosocial emotions. Discussion

The NAME intervention reduced implicit bias toward people with facial anomalies

We examined whether repeated exposure combined with moral-exemplar stories could lessen implicit biases toward people with visible facial anomalies. Using the mobile “Normalizing Anomalies with Mobile Exposure” (NAME) program, one group of participants viewed anomalous faces paired with prosocial narratives, whereas a control group viewed phenotypically typical faces of people of color paired with the same type of narratives. The anomalous faces version of NAME produced a clear, target-specific effect: participants’ IAT D-scores for anomalous faces shifted from values close to zero at baseline to modestly negative values after the intervention (indicating relatively more positive automatic associations with anomalous faces), whereas their D-scores for control faces in that condition did not reliably change. The control condition showed a small, nonspecific decline in both measured biases - a pattern that may reflect practice or retest effects rather than a target-specific intervention benefit. However, future studies are needed to replicate these findings. Across conditions, the greatest bias reductions were observed in participants who reported that taking the exemplar’s perspective felt easy, suggesting that momentary perspective-taking contributed to the intervention’s impact. At the same time, we emphasize that the present findings come from a single study with a specific intervention design, stimulus set, and cultural context. Accordingly, they should be interpreted as an initial demonstration rather than as definitive evidence regarding the mechanisms, scope, or generalizability of bias reduction toward facial anomalies.

Understanding the reduction of anomalous-is-bad bias

These findings shed light on stereotypes rooted in facial appearance. The “anomalous-is-bad” label refers to the tendency to draw negative moral and social inferences (e.g., seeing people as less warm or less moral) from facial differences. One prominent explanation links these moral impressions to an adaptive behavioral immune system—geared toward detecting and avoiding pathogens—that overgeneralizes and produces disgust and avoidance responses to atypical facial features [1, 64–68]. Pathogen-avoidance mechanisms may provide an initial negative affective reaction that can later be interpreted in moral terms. At the same time, the empirical literature does not report a uniform “anomalous-is-bad” pattern. Although many studies document more negative evaluations of people with facial anomalies—on traits such as morality, competence, and attractiveness—other work has found equal or even higher ratings on warmth-related dimensions (e.g., friendliness, trustworthiness) for faces with visible differences under some conditions. More broadly, social-perception findings for physical disability are mixed across traits and contexts [2, 9, 20, 69–71]. Thus, “anomalous-is-bad” is better understood as a predominant, but not universal, pattern whose strength and direction depend on the specific traits and situations being evaluated. From this perspective, pathogen-avoidance mechanisms might make negative first reactions to anomalous faces relatively sticky. Even so, our findings are consistent with emerging research suggesting that some stereotypes are driven more by learning than by innate pathogen-avoidance mechanisms [14]. In our sample, the implicit “anomalous-is-bad” effect at baseline was modest, with average D-scores close to zero; the NAME intervention reliably shifted these automatic evaluations in a more positive direction rather than eliminating a large pre-existing negative bias.

The role of moral narratives, media representations, and perspective taking

Our results indicate that pairing people with visible facial differences with positive, individuating narratives about their morally praiseworthy actions and socially valued roles, together with repeated exposure to their faces, can reduce negative implicit biases against them. This pattern is consistent with, but does not directly test the hypothesis that moral vignettes motivate prosocial behavior in readers [39]. It also aligns with Stone and Fisher’s (2020) finding that a brief personal narrative from a person with facial disfigurement enhances explicit evaluations and expectations of future interaction compared to a static facial photograph. Because we did not measure participants’ behavior, we cannot determine whether the observed changes in implicit bias translated into more prosocial actions toward people with facial anomalies. Individuals with facial anomalies often face dehumanizing treatment that has been linked to implicit biases [1, 3–5], so we view the possibility that reductions in negative implicit bias might contribute to fewer dehumanizing behaviors as an important hypothesis for future research rather than a conclusion drawn from the present data. We did not observe changes in explicit biases toward people with visible differences after the NAME intervention, even though explicit evaluations on our scale were relatively positive at baseline. This pattern is consistent with the idea that self-reported attitudes, which are more strongly shaped by social norms and deliberative self-presentation, may be less malleable to brief interventions than automatic responses indexed by the IAT. It also underscores that changes in implicit bias do not necessarily imply parallel changes in explicit self-reports; our conclusions therefore pertain primarily to automatic evaluative responses rather than to consciously endorsed attitudes. However, the relationship between implicit bias and behavior remains uncertain [45, 53], and future studies will need to pair interventions like NAME with direct behavioral outcomes.

Media portrayals, by contrast, often pair facial differences with antisocial content, potentially increasing the salience of negative biases [16, 72]. A striking example is Disney’s The Lion King, where the villain’s name—“Scar”—connotes moral corruption. Using visible facial differences as a storytelling device can reinforce harmful stereotypes. Recent evidence shows that pairing typical faces with antisocial vignettes lowers judgments of attractiveness, confidence, and friendliness, whereas prosocial vignettes yield the opposite effect [73]. Although not tested here, antisocial vignettes might exacerbate negative implicit biases toward people with facial anomalies, underscoring the importance of interventions that disrupt these learned associations.

We tracked participants’ emotional and cognitive responses immediately after each face-and-story pairing. They reported feelings like sympathy, admiration, guilt, pity, disgust, anger, shame, and these emotions did not correlate with the change in negative implicit biases. Instead, ease of perspective-taking—a measure of state cognitive empathy in the current moment—predicted reductions in negative implicit biases, consistent with past results showing that participants with higher perspective taking assessed persons with facial anomalies as more competent, and dehumanized them less [11]. Notably, this stands in contrast to a study by Workman and colleagues (2021), who found that trait affective empathy (and not cognitive) was tied to the dehumanization of people with anomalous faces. The discrepancy may reflect methodological differences: that cross-sectional study measured rapid brain responses to anomalous faces, whereas we used a prospective design involving explicit reflection on moral stories. One possibility is that affective empathy may be more relevant for quick, automatic reactions, while cognitive empathy may be more influential in interventions that prompt thoughtful reflection. However, we emphasize that these effects emerged only for state perspective taking measured during the intervention, and not for trait perspective taking measured with the IRI. Future studies are needed to better understand these distinctions.

Specificity and generalization of intervention effects

We also observed that the anomalous faces variant of the NAME intervention produced a clear reduction in implicit bias against people with anomalous faces, whereas bias toward people of color in that condition remained essentially unchanged. In contrast, participants in the control condition exhibited a smaller, similar-sized reduction in both bias types—a pattern consistent with general retest or practice effects and/or simple normalization through repeated exposure to faces paired with everyday prosocial narratives. Because the Bias Type × Time interaction was not significant in the control condition and our sample was not optimized to detect higher-order interactions, we treat these differences in specificity as suggestive rather than definitive. Taken together, our data are consistent with the possibility that repeated, targeted exposure to individuals with facial differences, embedded in positive narratives and opportunities for perspective taking, can help reduce negative implicit biases associated with limited contact, while also leaving room for more general repetition-based mechanisms to contribute.

Another, not mutually exclusive, possibility is that the control intervention generalized more strongly across groups than the anomalous faces intervention. If faces with visible anomalies are categorized more rigidly as a distinct out-group than faces of people of color, prosocial information about anomalous faces may primarily update beliefs about that specific group, whereas prosocial information about people of color may more readily transfer to other stigmatized groups, including people with facial anomalies. This interpretation is consistent with suggestions in the literature that anomalous faces are perceived as highly distinctive and strongly categorized, and with the observation that most bias-reduction efforts have focused on racism rather than on bias against people with facial anomalies. Future research with designs that directly test asymmetric generalization across multiple out-groups will be needed to adjudicate between these mechanisms.

Limits and future directions

We measured implicit bias using the Implicit Association Test (IAT), a well-known yet sometimes controversial tool, as it does not always accurately predict real-world behavior [54]. We chose the IAT to remain consistent with prior studies on implicit biases toward people with visible facial differences [1, 7, 74] and because considerable research documents how different types of implicit biases can be strengthened or weakened via specific interventions [44, 75–81]. Future work could complement these findings with other measures, such as the Affect Misattribution Procedure (AMP), which focuses on automatic affective responses and has been shown to predict real-world behavior [82–84]. To date, little research has examined how interventions might alter AMP-based measures of implicit bias, leaving a promising avenue for future exploration open. More broadly, contemporary debates suggest caution in interpreting IAT scores as direct indicators of stable, unconscious prejudice or as strong predictors of real-world discriminatory behavior [56–58]. Some scholars argue that implicit measures may partially reflect culturally learned associations, task structure, or context-dependent responses rather than enduring individual attitudes. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted specifically as changes in automatic evaluative associations measured within the IAT paradigm, not as evidence that the intervention necessarily altered long-term personal beliefs or downstream discriminatory behaviors. Future work should therefore incorporate behavioral outcomes, longitudinal follow-ups, and convergent implicit measures (e.g., AMP) to better clarify the practical significance and durability of these effects.

A further limitation concerns the structure of the narratives used in the NAME intervention. Each vignette combined moral content (e.g., prosocial behavior) with additional positive, individuating information about the protagonist’s occupation and everyday life, and these roles were generally high in perceived warmth and competence. As a result, manipulation confounds moral valence with other favorable attributes and with the simple provision of more detailed information about the target group. We therefore cannot determine whether the observed reductions in implicit bias are driven primarily by moral-exemplar content, by associating people with facial anomalies with socially valued occupations, or by increased individuating information and familiarity. In addition, we did not include a condition in which anomalous (or control) faces were paired with neutral or negative vignettes, so the present design cannot establish whether positive valence is necessary for change, as opposed to any increase in background information about the group. Future work should orthogonally manipulate story valence and role desirability, and include neutral-information control conditions, to isolate which components of the intervention are most effective. For this reason, the present study does not allow strong causal claims about which component of the intervention was necessary or sufficient for change. The current findings should therefore be understood as evidence for the effectiveness of the intervention tested here, not for any one mechanism in isolation.

We delivered the NAME intervention through a custom mobile application, illustrating how mobile platforms can promote positive shifts in attitudes. However, this limits the generalizability of our findings to such types of interventions. To optimize these effects, future iterations could vary how participants are exposed (for example, altering session frequency or intervention duration). Expanding NAME into a broader implicit-bias training curriculum might enhance its impact, though adding more sessions or components could reduce completion rates. We note that our participants completed roughly 90% of sessions, and the NAME intervention reduced negative implicit biases against people with facial anomalies. A key question for further research is whether these short-term gains persist over longer periods. Our follow-up assessment took place within a few days of the final session, so we cannot say how durable these changes are over weeks or months. Although some similar interventions have documented longer-term changes in implicit measures [52], future studies will need to incorporate extended follow-up intervals and repeated assessments to determine how well NAME-type interventions hold up over time in the face of recurring stereotype-consistent media portrayals. We also did not systematically manipulate or evaluate the timing of sessions; future work could examine whether the time of day at which participants engage with NAME (e.g., morning vs. evening use) moderates its effectiveness.

A further limitation concerns our assessment of explicit attitudes. The EBQ is a direct self-report measure and is therefore more susceptible to social desirability and demand characteristics than implicit measures. Administering the EBQ at baseline may also have made the topic of bias toward people with facial anomalies more salient before the intervention. Although we did not observe changes in explicit attitudes over time and baseline scores were already relatively positive, future work could reduce potential priming by varying the timing or format of explicit measures (e.g., post-only assessments, indirect questioning, or embedding items among broader attitudinal scales).

Another limitation of our study is that the intervention was tested on a single sample drawn from one cultural and national context—the United States—and the control set of facial photographs was also region-specific. Moreover, the anomalous faces were drawn from a database in which most individuals were White, whereas the control stimuli comprised phenotypically typical faces of people of color. In addition, most of the anomalous faces in our stimulus set depicted scarring or other acquired injuries rather than congenital differences. Scars may carry distinct social and moral connotations (e.g., associations with violence, danger, or victimization) that differ from those attached to other kinds of visible differences, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to all people with facial anomalies. At the same time, initial evidence suggests that reactions to faces with scars versus facial palsy do not differ in perceived warmth or competence, indicating that not all social impressions necessarily vary across these anomaly types [10]. Together, these features of our sample and stimuli raise questions about the generalizability of our findings to other cultural settings, where both facial norms and moral interpretations of appearance may differ. Future research should replicate this intervention in more diverse cultural contexts, ideally using matched control stimuli that reflect each region’s demographic and aesthetic norms. This design limitation also means that the present study cannot fully determine whether the observed changes were driven specifically by anomaly status, by anomaly status in combination with other visual or social features, or by broader properties of the intervention stimuli. More tightly controlled stimulus matching will be necessary in future work. Additionally, it should test whether NAME-type interventions are similarly effective for a broader range of facial differences, including congenital conditions and other types of visible anomalies. Additionally, future work could explore whether the intervention’s effectiveness varies across specific demographic groups, such as individuals differing in age, educational background, or cultural exposure. Tailoring interventions to such subgroups may enhance their efficacy and provide insight into how different populations process and respond to facial anomalies. Taken together, these limitations mean that the present article should be read as a first proof-of-concept study whose conclusions are intentionally bounded. Replication with more controlled designs, broader anomaly types, and more orthogonal stimulus comparisons will be essential before stronger generalizations can be made.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that automatic evaluations of people with visible differences may be more malleable than often assumed and that carefully designed, story-based interventions may represent a promising direction for future research. At the same time, the present results are preliminary and should be extended through more controlled studies before broader claims are made about generalizability or real-world impact. The “Normalizing Anomalies with Mobile Exposure” (NAME) intervention, delivered via a mobile platform, showed that pairing visible facial differences with repeated exposure to positive, individuating narratives and opportunities for perspective-taking shifted implicit evaluations of those individuals in a more positive direction. In our sample, the anomalous-faces version of NAME produced a clear, target-specific improvement for faces with visible anomalies, whereas the control intervention showed smaller, more general shifts across both face types. These findings underscore that automatic evaluations of people with visible differences are malleable and that carefully designed, story-based interventions may help reshape automatic evaluations of those who look different, with future work needed to determine whether such changes translate into real-world social behavior. By “changing the story” around facial anomalies, we may take a step toward dismantling harmful stereotypes and, in future work, explore whether similar approaches can be adapted and tested for other socially penalized groups.
