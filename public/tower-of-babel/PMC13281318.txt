Examining the strategic utilization of Community-Academic Partnerships as a research engagement method in clinical and public health research within urban settings: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Carson J Peters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-Academic Partnerships (CAPs) are a promising method for enhancing research engagement among communities of interest, including key populations in clinical and public health research. Expanding the evidence base on utilizing CAPs in clinical and public health research can strengthen the clinical research enterprise, contributing to optimal recruitment outcomes. This study explored CAPs as a research method to engage both partners and patients who were involved in a clinical trial focused on maternal and child health, public health research.MethodsUsing a reflexive thematic analysis, in-depth, semi-structured interviews (N = 15) with community collaborators from urban settings, were analyzed through NVivo software. Interviews focused on key components of CAPs, including their structure, characteristics, and factors contributing to clinical and public health research engagement. A five-step process for the thematic analysis supported emergent high-level themes and interpretation of patterns.FindingsThe reflexive thematic analysis generated five themes about CAPs as a research engagement method including: (1) Leveraging existing community systems, (2) Cultivating community-oriented research spaces, (3) Recognizing the community as a shared stakeholder, (4) Ensuring a community-aligned research team, and (5) Practicing authentic engagement.ConclusionCAPs prioritized community values and interests that reflected community knowledge and systems, thereby creating research pipelines for partners and patients. Findings suggest that CAPs can be a tool for research engagement in clinical and public health research, which can inform best practices in research design and investigators’ approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00911-7.Keywords: Patient and public involvement, Community Academic Partnership, Community engagement, Clinical research, Public health

Background

Community-Academic Partnerships (CAPs) are a promising method to engage communities in clinical and public health research. CAPs support research engagement by promoting research interest, participation, recruitment, and retention in clinical trials [1]. This approach emphasizes rapport building, authentic attention, and sensitivity to communities’ concerns and needs. It also involves flexible scheduling, follow-up to address concerns related to and outside of the research scope, consistent and regular contact with communities, commitment to non-research related interests, and general support [1].

CAPs also support effective and sustainable research engagement [2–5]. Using these partnerships as a mechanism to engage communities, including key patient populations, in public health research has gained recent noticeability, evidenced by the emerging literature base [6, 7]. Recently and since the pandemic, scholarship has identified CAPs to address emerging public health concerns, including health outcomes [8–11]. These CAPs play a critical role in promoting health access, community resilience, and fostering trust, including among groups less represented in clinical practice and public health research [12, 13].

Theoretical underpinnings from the Participatory Research Model and Community-Based Participatory Research emphasize how CAPs strive to promote research that positively impacts the community and enhances relevance to patient interests [14, 15]. Participatory Research includes levels of inform, consult, involve, collaborate, and empower. This model centers collaboration through decision-making, research foci, development, data analysis and interpretation, dissemination, and implementation, supported by co-construction of knowledge, co-learning, and co-designing. In addition, Community-Based Participatory Research is an encompassing framework highlighting partnerships between researchers and the community across all aspects of the research process. This model involves transformational paradigm shifts and the necessity of having partnerships. Both models translate knowledge to the community, enhance high-quality research findings, and improve population health [14–22].

Major funders and national initiatives have influenced and transformed the community-engaged research space, with aims to promote more inclusive research. Programs coordinated by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Community Engagement Alliance (CEAL), Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL), and All of Us prioritize patient and community engagement across phases of clinical research, including planning, implementation, and dissemination [23–25]. Likewise, NIH funding mechanisms, such as the Clinical and Translational Science Awards (CTSA), support similar efforts to enhance participation by leveraging community engagement practices and infrastructure [26]. Guidance from the NIH and Centers for Disease Control (CDC) underscores trust and institutional accountability, and recent reviews emphasize strategies to enhance active participation, promote authentic involvement, and center community prioritization [27–29]. Other funding streams also emphasize community engagement; for example, the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) mandates meaningful engagement in all funded research [30]. Organizations such as the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) and the National Academies of Medicine (NAM) offer guidance, principles, and programs that strengthen collaborative partnerships [13, 31, 32]. As funding expectations continue to expand and become more aligned with community-engaged and inclusive work, these recent developments have critically shaped the field.

Community partners are pivotal in translating clinical trial outcomes into real-world practice, yet their perspectives on research engagement and study implementation remain underexplored. This qualitative study draws directly from the established community partner database of Rosie the Chatbot, a national clinical trial focused on an AI-powered mobile chatbot application in maternal and child health [33–35]. This analysis of community and external partner perspectives supports an in-depth exploration of leveraging partnerships for recruitment. The study illuminates themes of strategic integration of community partnerships for recruitment efforts, addressing a critical gap in clinical and public health research.

There is a need for an expanded evidence base on CAPs as a strategic approach to overcome barriers and optimize research engagement, particularly from the perspective of community partners. This study aimed to explore CAPs as a research engagement method to involve both partners and patients. Partners included community academic partners, and patients were research participants from a key population in clinical and public health research. Research engagement was defined as involvement in the research process, including in design, planning, recruitment, implementation, and dissemination. It was expected that salient themes of CAP structure, characteristics, and factors contributing to clinical and public health research engagement would be identified from the reflexive thematic analysis.

Methods

Study sample

The study sample (N = 15) included community partners who were involved with recruitment in a national, clinical trial based at the University of Maryland. Below, a demographic characteristic summary of the larger clinical trial is described.

Clinical trial

Participants (N = 400) enrolled in the maternal and child health clinical trial represented those across racial and ethnic groups, household income strata, and resided in 48 states in the United States (U.S). A majority (66%) obtained a bachelor’s degree or higher.

Community partner involvement included, but was not limited to, sharing the recruitment package, supporting recruitment efforts, conducting research or health presentations, assisting with research engagement, and/or engaging with other collaborative efforts. Community partners conducted community work across the U.S. Community partners were invited to participate in this study. Of the community partners who agreed, they resided in urban settings, with a substantial subset (n = 12) concentrated in the greater DC area and the minority residing in the Eastern and Western U.S. Urban settings are defined as cities and surrounding areas such as suburbs, towns, and smaller populous areas. The greater DC area includes DC, Maryland, and Virginia. The Eastern United States was defined as states east of the Mississippi River, excluding DC, Maryland, and Virginia. The Western United States was defined as the states west of the Mississippi River. Inclusion criteria included those aged 18 years or older. The appropriate sample size was reached once data saturation and the reliability benchmark were met, which was identified through coding and theme generation [36–39]. Community and External Partner Type included Community Group (included locally organized groups or committees), Faith-Based Community (included church leader), Government (included local and state public health departments), Medical Facilities (included hospital-affiliated programs focused on community outreach), Network (included community service-oriented and solidarity-based organizations advancing efforts of interest), and Nonprofit (included mission-driven 501c aimed at improving health outcomes for groups with unmet needs). Demographic variables, including gender, were collected, which were of most interest in the interpretation of the data. Demographic information is presented in Table 1.

Data collection

Primary data collection of in-depth, semi-structured interviews was administered through purposive sampling. Interviews were conducted via Zoom videoconferencing software. Community partners gave informed consent prior to participation. Community partners also selected a pseudonym before the interview, which served as their unique identifier. Community partners were compensated for their participation. A virtual recruitment strategy was employed, where community partners were identified using the clinical trial’s community partner database. Community partners were invited to participate via electronic communications (i.e., email notices, e-newsletters). Recruitment materials were approved by the Institutional Review Board and reviewed by the study’s Advisory Board.

Study instrument

A semi-structured interview guide focused on partnerships, including design, recruitment patterns, approaches, and dissemination. Interview questions reflected communication, motivations, community, collaboration, facilitators, and barriers, where a few hypothetical recruitment scenarios were explored. The interview guide was developed by the researcher/community partner and reviewed by the Advisory Board. The interview guide was also aligned with theoretical underpinnings drawn from the Participatory Research Model and Community-Based Participatory Research [14, 15]. This interview guide supported the interpretation of patterns and high-level meanings emerging from the reflexive thematic analysis.

Design

A qualitative study design using reflexive thematic analysis was conducted [40]. The study was conducted in compliance with the GRIPP2 Checklist (Supplemental 1) [41].

Analysis

Prior to analysis, interviews were transcribed using a two-step process: initial automatic transcription generated from Zoom videoconferencing software, followed by manual verification reviewed in comparison with interview recordings for comparison; this process ensured accuracy and maintained consistency of interview transcription. Data were analyzed utilizing reflexive thematic analysis, where themes were identified, analyzed, and summarized [40]. The steps included: (1) Data Familiarization (2), Data Coding (3), Generating Initial Themes (4), Developing and Reviewing Themes (5), Defining and Naming Themes, and (6) Analysis Writing [40]. A codebook was developed for the initial and deductive coding scheme focused on direct meaning and content. Inductive codes were added later to describe high-level interpretation and emergent themes generated by partners [42]. Using this iterative process, both deductive and inductive codes existed in the codebook, which were summarized into salient themes [42]. Theoretical coding of concepts was assessed, and salient themes of structure, characteristics, and factors contributing to CAPs as a recruitment strategy were identified. Data coding was conducted by the first author, and analysis occurred through NVivo software. Memos and reflexive statements were analyzed in tandem through triangulation, which supported study rigor [43].

Advisory board

The Advisory Board was composed of four diverse experts, including specialists focused on active participation and recruitment of groups not often represented in research, practitioners in community engagement practices and approaches, as well as individuals with experiences aligned directly to health areas, and in this case, maternal and child health. The Advisory Board represented individuals from a non-profit, community group, a public health network, and a parent from the local community. The Advisory Board reviewed the proposed study aims and interview guide to reflect relevant domains of interest. The Advisory Board also provided feedback on the preliminary and final results presented throughout the research process during debrief sessions. Based on feedback received, the interview guide and preliminary results were refined and adjusted to support the study’s strength and caliber. This community-engaged approach drew from conceptual underpinnings of the following frameworks: Foundational Expectations for Partnerships in Research developed by PCORI and Community-Based Participatory Research developed by Israel et al. [15, 44].

Rigor

A series of approaches were conducted to enhance study rigor, including the debrief sessions and member checking. The debrief sessions and member checking were conducted to ensure the strength and credibility of findings. Debrief sessions were conducted with the Advisory Board, focused on study design, implementation, and dissemination. Member checking was held with community partners who participated in the study, during which preliminary findings and final results were shared through individualized written summaries and oral presentations. These approaches, informed by community-engaged frameworks, helped ensure the quality, credibility, and rigor of the findings [45]. Furthermore, positionality was emphasized through reflexive practice and reflexive thematic analysis [40]. Reflexivity ensured that findings were critically analyzed and that meanings were interpreted within a larger, culturally informed context [46]. This process was maintained through a reflexive statement, research memos, and debrief sessions. Credibility was strengthened through multiple analytic strategies, such as triangulating data across sources, conducting member checks with participants, and engaging in peer debriefing to validate initial and developing interpretations [36, 47, 48]. Confirmability and neutrality were reinforced by maintaining an audit trail and using reflexive journaling to reduce potential researcher bias and ensure that findings remained grounded in participants’ perspectives [36, 40]. Transferability was supported by offering detailed descriptions of the study setting, research methods, participant interpretations, contextual factors, and limitations, and situating work within broader environments and research procedures, enabling the assessment of relevant findings in other contexts [36, 40].

Reflexivity

The lead author of this study is a community partner, and public health researcher. Prior to the author receiving training in public health research, the author committed more than four years as a community member supporting mobilization efforts in coordination with university settings. The author drew from their experiences as a community member to inform project direction, development of research design, and study goals. The author’s role was continually considered in discussions, member checking, and listening sessions, and actively leveraged during the process of the study to enhance design analysis. Through reflexive journaling and peer debriefing, the author reflected on how this influenced the interpretation of the data, thereby enriching the overall research process and outcomes.

Findings

From the reflexive thematic analysis, five themes were generated: (1) Leveraging existing community systems (2), Cultivating community-oriented research spaces (3), Recognizing the community as a shared stakeholder (4), Ensuring a community-aligned research team, and (5) Practicing authentic engagement. * indicates self-selected pseudonym.

Theme One

Leveraging existing community ecosystems.

Majority of partners described how CAPs can support and increase research engagement when the community ecosystem is leveraged. The community ecosystems include the community’s knowledge, values, language, and social networks. The community ecosystem underscores a formal, informal, or organically created system built on shared beliefs and operationalized into practice. This equitable approach to foregrounding community knowledge is described by Susan.*

There’s no need to recreate the wheel and it’s actually quite disrespectful to attempt to do so. It completely overwrites and ignores years of work that have gone in by local organizations and relationships that have been built with the community’s trust. (Susan, Nonprofit)*

Many partners discussed how community ecosystems, for example, networks and social capital, are an effective strategy for research engagement, as shared below:

Community, partnership, and linkages with other Black women is so strong. Using a strategy of reaching out to Black women within their own community, rather than relying on flyers to an organization is more effective. (Diana, Medical Facility)*

Partners also underscored the importance of researchers understanding the community’s ecosystem as a way to better engage. This partner uses the example of the community’s sociological background:

I think that researchers need to know or be familiar with the community that they’re studying, not just data, not just numbers, but they need to have a simpatico, a feeling, some kind of a real understanding of what the community is, the norms, the practices, the customs. Even understanding some of the jargon that may be used in the community. Understanding the language so that they can communicate effectively. (Louise, Network)*

Theme Two

Cultivating community-oriented research spaces.

Community-oriented research spaces enhance both access and proximity to research, which increases the likelihood of research engagement and recruitment. Partners suggested that such spaces could include, but are not limited to, community summits, open research nights, parties, coffee or social hours, or luncheons.

Focus on education, give them some of the materials, and have a coffee and conversations once a month. (Jordan, Community Group)*

A key step for researchers to achieve community-oriented research includes an understanding of key drivers, facilitators, and barriers affecting the community, as reflected by in the quote below:

Without embedding yourself in that community, you don’t truly know what could be the drivers of what the problem is. Come into the community, that could be participating in anything around there. (Monica, Network)*

Another step for researchers to cultivate community-oriented research spaces is directly engaging with the community and designing research opportunities aligned with the community’s identified priorities and goals. This sentiment was described by a few partners, with an example quote below:

Recognizing the community as people, not just research subjects…So really highlighting the community, using the community as a connector to the population of interest, and interfacing with the community more directly. (Jane, Nonprofit)*

Theme Three

Recognizing the community as a shared stakeholder.

The community and researchers should strive for shared power, as expressed by several partners. Partners discussed this theme, underscoring shared power between the community and researchers.

They’re also contextual scholars and I would say the community are also scholars in the sense that they are well researched and well learned in the context of their situation. (Jane, Nonprofit)*.

For them [the community] to be on the same level with you, to understand the benefits they need to genuinely be part of this program… the university always has to look at and see what can they do to help in improving the community that they are going to. (Martha, Nonprofit)*

Several partners demonstrated that partnerships that uphold an egalitarian structure and equitable approaches are a way to demonstrate well-intentioned partnerships, as emphasized by Alexis* below.

There’s a difference in doing research on the community and asking them questions here and there versus making them a true partner. It just starts with that, bringing people in from the beginning, learning from their expertise as the community and also teaching them your expertise as a community. (Alexis, Network)*

Theme Four

Ensuring a community-aligned research team.

Many partners discussed the importance of having the research team be aligned socially, culturally, or geographically, as a method to facilitate research engagement. This sentiment reflects more broadly a research team that is representative of the community of focus.

When you’re seeing your fellow sister reaching out and is also part of this study it makes you want [to] be a part of this research because there’s a sense of shared connection and understanding. (Jane, Nonprofit)*

Partners also emphasized that having someone from the community supports the research process due to the shared understanding and community-oriented work.

There should be people who identify with the community that are on the research team itself. And also people who have that knack for community-oriented dissemination and communication. (Diana, Medical Facility)*

Theme Five

Practicing authentic engagement.

Authentic engagement requires researchers to build trust through consistent presence and meaningful involvement that extends beyond project-specific obligations. Practicing authentic engagement includes collaborative approaches throughout all phases of the research process, from design and implementation to dissemination. Demonstrating trustworthiness is essential to building partnerships and encouraging collaboration. This sentiment is reflected in the quote below:

Trust is a superpower in that in that space. That’s probably first being trustworthy and then being relatable. So that any new people can say ‘okay, we’re willing to try this as well.’ (Stanley, Faith-based Community)*

Showing an investment in the community of focus resonated with many partners, as described by Nia* below. Partners described that investment can be shown by researchers through commitment to long-term change, community progress, advancement, and a positive impact.

That’s part of our responsibility and making the commitment to say, ‘Hey, we’re going to stick to this. We’re going to stay working together. Budgets may change. People may change. But we are still having this commitment to the community to help bring change.’ (Nia, Community Group)*

Building relationships with partners requires time and intentional investment of resources. Allocating sufficient time to initiate and sustain relationships is described by Alexis* below.

You need to build a relationship with that community group. Nothing is easy, nothing is quick. I think a lot of times academic researchers don’t necessarily think about the time it takes to build relationships. (Alexis, Network)*

Discussion

Summary

Community-Academic Partnerships reflect the underlying characteristics of re-centering power structures, which shift traditional research practices into community-oriented work. Through CAPs, the research process is optimized at all levels, including research engagement, where the community’s systems, spaces, resources, and leadership are emphasized. Aligned with our conceptual findings, CAP components facilitating research engagement include commitment, dedication, inclusion, and respect, underscored by the shared leadership between the community and researchers [20, 49, 50]. In our study, the working definition of partners and patients remains the same, while recognizing that partners and patients are interlocutors involved in the research process to varying degrees within the broader research ecosystem. From the study findings, research practices that overwrite community knowledge or experiences have long-term consequences and contribute to narratives fueling distrust and research neglect. Factors identified underscore the importance of community accountability for researchers.

Insights from study findings exemplify that CAPs serve as an intermediary between the research team and patients through a community partner, that is, a trusted collaborative pathway, rooted in the co-creation of interlocking systems. CAPs offer significant potential to enhance research engagement across the translational research continuum, bridging the gap between scientific discovery and practical application, as evidenced by this study’s qualitative findings and further supported by the Black Infant Health Community Collaborative (BIHCC) case study. BIHCC addressed approaches to advance infant health and reduce infant mortality by using CAPs to identify community-driven action plans to expedite the research process and recruitment efforts across state-level counties and nationally [50]. The working definition used in this study for partners and patients remains the same, while recognizing that partners and patients are interlocutors involved in the research process to differing degrees in the larger research ecosystem.

CAPs subvert historical power systems within research and instead promote a more egalitarian structure because community knowledge is a focal point, and community voice is centered [4, 20, 49, 51]. This shift allows community experience and expertise to be legitimized within the rigid power structure of research. By changing the power dynamics, community values and interests are prioritized; pipelines into research are created; access is enhanced, which expedites the research process; and research is more community aligned, which is supported by this study’s qualitative findings and evidenced by Clark et al. (2019) and Nanda et al. (2023) [1, 52]. These findings complement broader efforts to develop practical resources for integrating such strategies into clinical research practice, as reflected in a toolkit for research investigators [53]. Study findings contribute to an emerging evidence base for defining investment into the community from community partners’ perspectives, thereby facilitating research engagement. Our study findings also shed insight into authentic community engagement, supporting the sustainability of CAPs. Conceptual findings from this study contribute to future theoretical development of CAP’s ability to promote research engagement from the perspectives of community partners. Future work should examine tools for evaluating community investment and to advance definitions of CAP success and metrics from the perspective of community partners.

Contextualization

In translating these study findings into practice, particularly in urban settings and in maternal and child public health contexts, researchers can take steps to improve active and community-engaged research practices, which are outlined. From the first theme of leveraging existing community ecosystems, findings suggest that elevating community knowledge embeds community perspectives within the project design and cultivates cultural humility. This approach allows researchers to develop a deeper, more nuanced understanding of the community. Recognizing the legitimacy and validity of community systems is essential to centering community voices in the design, structure, and implementation. Specifically, scholars highlight the importance of researchers leaning into community governance and systems of practice [54, 55]. Drawing from the second theme of cultivating community-oriented research spaces, creating opportunities for the community to engage with research, either in traditional or non-traditional research spaces (also referred to as community-oriented research spaces), generates a stronger pipeline into research. Having community-oriented research spaces enhances access to research while also ensuring that the research team demonstrates a commitment to showing up intentionally and translating research into and within the community, as shared by previous scholars [20, 28, 56]. In recognizing the community as a shared stakeholder, this third theme can build capacity and elevate the community. When the community shares power and acts as brokers and decision-makers, not only is community endorsement strengthened, but it also leads to more efficient, streamlined research engagement processes. Previous studies underscore the influence of egalitarian structures and strengthened community capacity on advancing engagement among communities who are participating in or are affected by study outcomes, ultimately enhancing participatory outcomes [20, 51]. Recognizing the community as an expert and part of a community consortium of scholars re-centers power back into the community. The fourth theme reflects ensuring the research team is community-aligned, highlighting that having someone from the community is a key driver of representation. Fryer et al. argue that concordance drives research engagement [7]. Lastly, the fifth theme of practicing authentic community engagement reinforces the long-term sustainability of partnerships and strengthens mutual accountability between researchers and community members. A consistent track record of authenticity and purpose-driven engagement strengthens these relationships. Conversely, as CAPs require extended time periods to build and sustain, a challenge for some researchers is the lack of accounting for partnership timelines. Other challenges include omitting openness and an aptitude to engage and learn from the beginning of the research project, or even prior to it. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that authentic or meaningful community engagement improves recruitment among key groups [29, 32, 49, 51]. Taken together, translated practical findings provide insights into specific processes to advance enhanced participation in research. Identifying strategic areas of focus can potentially promote the critical utilization of CAPs to drive research engagement.

Strengths and limitations

A strength is that this study uniquely contributes to the field, as it is among one of several to explore CAPs as a research engagement method within the clinical research enterprise, specifically focusing on perspectives from community academic partners. Another strength is the focus on community partners who collaborated with a research project over a five-year period, demonstrating an emphasis on sustainability. A limitation is the small sample size of community partners and their concentrated geographic coverage. Specifically, the dataset was skewed to urban settings, which bounds the study findings. The clinical trial participants were mostly women, and those with higher education, which limits the representativeness of the sample. The sample size was due in part to the community partners available at our organizing research site. These sample constraints may have limited the opportunity to deeply explore differences across urbanity and rurality subgroups, which limits generalizability. Other limitations included the full trial only including participants and community partners who spoke English, which limited linguistic diversity reflected in the DC area. Demographics of race and age, political affiliation, and income were not collected systematically. Furthermore, a limitation is the lack of information gleaned from potential partners who chose not to be involved with the larger clinical trial study, given that the community partner database was a secondary outreach.

Future directions

Future work should expand on the study’s findings by adapting and testing them across diverse geographic subgroups to identify shared or differing values and priorities. Additional exploration of institutional barriers limiting community-engaged practice among these subgroups, as well as related recommendations to overcome, are opportunity areas moving forward. Also, future work can better assess collaborative models rooted in authentic engagement.

Conclusion

CAPs prioritize community values and interests, which can strategically support the research process and create better pipelines into research. Findings suggest that CAPs are a strategy to influence community-oriented research designs and investigator approaches, thereby potentially improving the clinical and public health research continuum. Future work should adapt and test findings in varied geographic contexts and better address institutional barriers to engagement.
