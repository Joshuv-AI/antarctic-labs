Sub-20 nm Hard X‑ray Spectromicroscopy Reveals Nanoscale Redox Heterogeneity in Ultrafine Particulates
Authors: Ajith Pattammattel, Robert Root, Zirui Gao, Dmitri Gavrilov, Randy Smith, Yong S. Chu, Xiaojing Huang, Jon Chorover, Hanfei Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spatially resolving chemical state variations within complex hierarchical materials is essential for understanding their reactivity. Yet, achieving sub-50 nm resolution for hard X-ray spectromicroscopy remains a long-standing challenge. Here, we demonstrate high-sensitivity, sub-20 nm X-ray absorption near-edge structure spectromicroscopy or nano-XANES enabled by high resolution imaging using multilayer Laue lenses. This approach achieved sub-20 nm chemical speciation mapping and a chemical state detection threshold of <18,000 atoms, a regime where current techniques are limited by insufficient sensitivity. We showcase the power of this methodology through the chemical speciation of ultrafine arsenopyrite particles, which are widespread environmental contaminants. Our results reveal previously inaccessible insights into the oxidation pathways of submicrometer particles, including the discovery of a labile As­(III) corona that creates an inversion of the expected oxidation gradient. These findings establish nano-XANES as a high-fidelity probe for mapping localized redox gradients, surface phase transformations, and heterogeneous charge transfer at (buried) interfaces and nanoscale boundaries.

Introduction

Probing spatial distribution of chemical states in nano- and microscale materials remains a fundamental challenge in spectromicroscopy. Electron-based techniques provide excellent spatial resolution but are limited by shallow penetration depths, typically less than 150 nm. These methods also require extensive sample preparation, which restricts their ability to examine materials in their native or embedded states. X-ray spectromicroscopy offers a powerful alternative by combining deep penetration, minimal sample preparation, and multimodal detection capabilities to study complex, heterogeneous systems in realistic environments.

Soft X-ray techniques have historically led the development of high-resolution spectromicroscopy. Scanning transmission X-ray microscopy (STXM) has been widely used because of its strong absorption contrast and the availability of efficient Fresnel zone plates at low photon energies. Despite lower fluorescence yields in this energy range, STXM-XANES (X-ray absorption near edge structure) routinely achieves spatial resolutions below 20 nm and has been applied in environmental, biological, and energy-related research. Recently, ptychographic XANES, which combines coherent diffraction imaging with energy-resolved spectroscopy, has further advanced chemical imaging by reaching sub-10 nm resolution and providing access to nanoscale electronic structure.

In comparison, hard X-ray spectromicroscopy has developed more slowly due to the difficulty of fabricating nanofocusing optics that perform efficiently at higher photon energies. However, hard X-rays offer substantial advantages, most notably deep penetration into complex or embedded samples. This property initially favored the development of full-field Transmission X-ray Microscopy (TXM) for hard X-ray XANES, enabling rapid, high-throughput chemical state mapping in two and three dimensions. TXM-XANES achieves spatial resolutions of 30–50 nm and is particularly effective for tomographic imaging of mesostructured materials.

Yet, for many scientific applications, especially those involving trace-level analytes or complex elemental mixtures where fluorescence detection is preferred. Above 2 keV, the fluorescence yield increases significantly, making XRF (X-ray fluorescence) a powerful detection mode for trace-level mapping. For example, detection of impurities down to ∼3,000 Ga atoms in a silicon matrix was demonstrated using scanning probe nano-XRF. Thus, chemical state mapping using XRF detection offers superior detection sensitivity and simultaneous multielement analysiscapabilities that are not possible with TXM or STXM.

Early efforts in hard X-ray XANES imaging using XRF detection employed Kirkpatrick–Baez (K–B) mirror systems, achieving spatial resolutions of approximately 50–100 nm. , The adoption of hard X-ray Fresnel zone plates later improved this to approximately 50 nm, as demonstrated in our previous work and by others. Subsequently, high spatial resolution, penetration depth, and sensitivity of the hard X-ray nanoprobe were utilized to study nanoscale transformations in microscale battery particles, nanoscale domains and defects formed in additively manufactured metallic alloys, trace metal speciation in environmental pollutants, transport behavior in electronic devices, and so on. However, spatial resolution beyond 50 nm remained limited by the fabrication limits of high aspect ratio zone plates. To overcome this barrier, advanced nanofocusing optics such as multilayer Laue lenses (MLLs) and multilayer K–B mirrors have been developed, enabling fixed-energy imaging at sub-20 nm resolution in the hard X-ray regime. − Despite these advances, high-resolution spectromicroscopy beyond 20 nm, essential for chemical state mapping, had not yet been demonstrated. Achieving high-resolution XANES at the tens-of-nanometers scale is critical for studying localized chemical processes such as surface oxidation, adsorption, and catalytic interface reactions. These phenomena often involve redox-active metals, where subtle changes in trace speciation directly influence material reactivity and performance.

Here, we present the first demonstration of hard X-ray XANES imaging at sub-20 nm spatial resolution, enabled by MLLs as nanofocusing optics at the National Synchrotron Light Sources II (NSLS-II) Hard X-ray Nanoprobe (HXN) beamline (Figure A). Realizing energy scanning with MLLs requires maintaining precise alignment across eight degrees of freedom for the orthogonal lens pair, a technical challenge we successfully addressed. We show that the narrow energy range typical for XANES (∼150 eV) allows the MLLs focus to remain stable by compensating for energy-dependent focal length shifts, similar to correction strategies used with zone plates. This advancement marks a significant step forward in nanoscale spectromicroscopy (Figure B), combining deep penetration, trace-level sensitivity, and spatial resolution at the sub-20 nm scale.

We applied this capability to a pressing environmental challenge: the speciation of arsenic in ultrafine particles derived from arsenopyrite-bearing mine tailings. These materials pose significant public health risks, particularly in arid and semiarid mining regions where wind erosion generates fine, inhalable particulates. Arsenopyrite (FeAsS), the most common arsenic sulfide mineral in chalcophile ore deposits, is a primary source of arsenic in many legacy wastes. Upon weathering, arsenopyrite transforms into poorly crystalline secondary minerals whose speciation and environmental reactivity remain poorly understood. The risks posed by these ultrafine particles are amplified by their small size, often less than 1 μm, which enhances their transport through the air, their ability to reach deep into the lungs, and their bioaccessibility. − These risks are especially severe when As is present in the more mobile and toxic trivalent As­(III) form.

Despite growing concern, the chemical state and spatial distribution of As in submicron particles remain poorly characterized due to the lack of techniques that combine high spatial resolution with sufficient chemical sensitivity. In this study, we addressed this limitation by applying hard X-ray nano-XANES with a ∼15 nm probe (Figure S1). Using MLL optics, we directly visualize the oxidation state and spatial distribution of As within individual submicron particles. This capability reveals previously inaccessible insights into As transformation pathways, bioavailability, and environmental risk. Our findings establish a powerful new approach for investigating the behavior of ultrafine toxicants in natural systems.

Results

Nano-XANES Imaging

To validate the spatial resolution, chemical sensitivity, and reliability of high-resolution nano-XANES using MLLs, we first analyzed arsenopyrite (As–Pyr; Fe2+(AsS)2–) as a model system with well-characterized XANES features. Arsenopyrite contains As in a reduced As­(-I) state coordinated with sulfur and is known to exhibit a sharp white-line peak near 11.867 keV at the As K-edge. Note that the As–S bond in As–Pyr is strongly covalent and that the reported oxidation states are formal electron-counting assignments. Although this compound can oxidize in air to produce As­(III) and As­(V), the kinetics are relatively slow. Figure A shows the nano-XRF map of an As–Pyr particle, highlighting the colocalization of arsenic, iron, and sulfur within a large As–Pyr grain surrounded by smaller fragments. The corresponding nano-XANES map (Figure B), collected across a 100 eV range spanning the As K-edge, reveals the spatial distribution of As­(-I), As­(III), and As­(V) within the particle.

These oxidized states are attributed to air-induced surface weathering. B. Composite nano-XRF map displaying the spatial distribution of As, Fe, and S. The spectral features match the expected white-line positions and line shapes of As­(-I), As­(III), and As­(V), confirming the accuracy of speciation assignments through spectral fitting.

Nano-XANES spectra from the central region of the micron-scale particle exhibited a dominant As­(-I) signature, expected for pristine As–Pyr. In contrast, spectra from the surrounding submicron domains revealed a mixture of As­(-I), As­(III), and As­(V) oxidation states (Figure B). These findings suggest partial oxidation of surface layers or smaller fragments due to ambient air exposure during the sample preparation and transfer to the microscope, which is in an inert He environment. Further insight was obtained by comparing elemental ratios. Deviations from the expected 1:1 As:S stoichiometry were observed in the submicron regions, which exhibited both elevated sulfur concentrations and As­(V) enrichment (Figure S2). This correlation suggests that oxidative weathering may involve partial substitution of sulfur by oxygen, potentially forming sulfate or other S–O species. These results highlight the value of combining chemical-state imaging with quantitative XRF mapping to probe oxidation pathways in chemically heterogeneous systems.

This measurement also served to optimize beam alignment and scanning stability and assess sample radiation tolerance. Arsenic compounds are known to be sensitive to X-ray radiation, and we monitored both XANES line shape and morphological changes throughout the scan to determine an acceptable dose threshold (Table S1). These precautions ensured that the observed chemical states reflected intrinsic sample properties rather than beam-induced artifacts.

After validating the performance of MLL-based nano-XANES using As–Pyr, we applied the technique to a more heterogeneous system designed to challenge its spatial resolution and trace-level sensitivity. For this purpose, we examined a physical mixture of As–Pyr and arseniosiderite (As–Sid; Ca2Fe3(As5+O4)3O2·3H2O), which contains chemically distinct and spatially separable arsenic oxidation states.

A submicron particle composed of both phases, referred to here as As–Pyr/Sid, was imaged using a 10 × 10 nm2 step size to acquire single-pixel XANES spectra (Figure ). Nano-XANES chemical mapping revealed dominant As­(-I) speciation within the crystalline core, corresponding to As–Pyr, while surrounding sub-100 nm aggregates showed a mixture of As­(III) and As­(V) states (Figure A). This observation raised the possibility that the mixed speciation originated either from the oxidation of As–Pyr or from the presence of nearby As–Sid.

Composite XRF map depicting the spatial distribution of As, Fe, and S. The absence of sulfur in the aggregates surrounding the particles in the upper region indicates the presence of a mixture of As–siderite and As–pyrite. Tiny sulfur-rich local regions in the bottom half of the image suggest the oxidation of As–Pyr to As­(III). C. Single-pixel XANES spectra corresponding to pixels a, b, and c shown in A. The spectra show high-quality signals, allowing reliable multicomponent fitting.

To clarify the origin of the As­(V) signal, we examined the nano-XRF maps. Since As–Sid lacks sulfur, sulfur distribution can serve as an indirect marker to distinguish the two phases. The composite XRF maps of As, Fe, and S (Figure B) showed that sulfur was absent in the regions corresponding to the As­(V)-rich aggregates, consistent with their assignment as As–Sid. These regions also matched the morphology of pristine As–Sid particles characterized separately (Figure S3). Interestingly, As­(III) was also detected within these As–Sid-like regions (spectrum c, Figure C). We hypothesize that labile As­(III), generated during partial oxidation of As–Pyr, may have adsorbed onto As–Sid surfaces, indicating possible interparticle redox interactions. Importantly, nano-XANES measurements from pristine As–Sid alone showed only As­(V), supporting this interpretation (Figure S3).

A key technical milestone in this dataset is the successful acquisition of single-pixel XANES spectra from regions spanning a wide range of arsenic concentrations. These ranged from approximately 1 μg/cm2 in low-density aggregates to over 45 μg/cm2 in the As–Pyr core (Figure C). This dynamic range demonstrates the sensitivity and versatility of nano-XANES for resolving spatially localized chemical states, even in trace-level regimes. While further improvements in sensitivity are possible by increasing dwell time per pixel, this comes at the expense of longer total acquisition times. These results highlight the power of high-resolution nano-XANES for investigating chemically complex systems.

Chemical Speciation of Arsenopyrite Reacted in In Vitro Bioassay

To examine the nanoscale chemical reactivity of ultrafine environmentally relevant particles, we studied As–Pyr samples subjected to an in vitro bioassay (IVBA) in synthetic gastric fluid (SGF). Previous studies on weathered As–Pyr from a Superfund site showed high bioaccessibility, with As­(V) as the dominant aqueous species released during IVBA, as determined by HPLC–ICP–MS. In addition, bulk XANES analyses, however, provided no evidence of intermediate As­(III) species or thiolated products in solid residues. Moreover, while it is known that fine particles (<40 μm) are particularly reactive and bioavailable. But, characterization of the ultrafine fraction (<1 μm) has remained elusive due to limitations in spatially resolved chemical-state imaging. Unraveling the chemical speciation of ultrafine, inhalable As particles is the key to assessing the health risks, bioaccessibility, and potential toxicity of As-rich particles in bioenvironmental interfaces.

Nano-XANES, with its high spatial resolution and trace-level sensitivity, provides a unique opportunity to resolve the speciation of As in individual reacted particles. Thus, we analyzed As–Pyr particles reacted with SGF for 1 h, referred to as Rctd–As–Pyr. Note that, to minimize radiation-induced artifacts, the X-ray dose was maintained below the threshold used for the artificial mixtures (Table S1). We analyzed four distinct regions of the sample to assess chemical state variation across multiple length scales and resolutions, ensuring data consistency across different radiation doses and representative sampling of the material (Figures S4 and S5). The highest-resolution dataset (10 nm pixels), presented in Figure , highlights the resolving power and sensitivity enabled by MLL-based nano-XANES. Nano-XRF mapping revealed two morphologically distinct As-containing regions: (i) submicron crystalline-like aggregates (200–500 nm) and (ii) nanoparticle-sized clusters (<200 nm) (Figure S4). This morphological distinction reflects differential oxidation and dissolution behavior of As–Pyr in the acidic bio-simulant fluid.

High-resolution nano-XANES mapping showed that the submicron particles were dominated by As­(V), while the smaller nanoparticles were enriched in As­(III) (Figure B). Notably, As­(III), the more toxic and labile form, accounted for 80–98% of the total As signal in the nanoparticle regions. A striking feature in the speciation map was a halo of As­(III) forming a corona around the As­(V)-rich submicron particles, an inversion of the expected oxidation gradient. This observation contrasts with simple surface oxidation models and points toward complex reaction mechanisms at the solid–fluid interface.

To assess the influence of residual registration error on the observed As­(III) corona, the nano-XANES chemical-state analysis was repeated using the raw unregistered stack, which contained experimental translational drift of up to approximately ±7 pixels, and an intentionally degraded stack with additional random shifts of up to ±15 pixels between successive energy images. The unregistered dataset yielded an As­(III) distribution and apparent corona width that were essentially indistinguishable from those obtained after registration, indicating that the residual alignment uncertainty remaining after image correction does not measurably affect the chemical-state assignment at the particle boundary. In contrast, the artificially misregistered stack produced clear distortions of the As­(III) corona and boundary shape, confirming that only substantially larger misalignment errors compromise the spatial fidelity of the reconstructed chemical map (Figure S8). Representative aligned and unaligned nano-XANES energy-stack animations illustrating the effect of image registration are provided as Supporting Videos S1–S4.

To investigate this As­(III) corona further, we performed k-means clustering of the nano-XANES dataset. Five spectral classes were identified based on progressive shifts in the As­(III)/As­(V) peak intensity ratio (Figure E), revealing a gradient from As­(III)-rich surface layers to As­(V)-dominated cores. Furthermore, elemental correlation analysis showed an inverse relationship between As­(III) and Fe, suggesting that surface As­(III) may exist as a mononuclear or oxyanion species.

These findings suggest two possible pathways for As­(III) formation: (i) readsorption of mononuclear As­(III) species onto oxidized As­(V) surfaces or (ii) reduction of As­(V) to As­(III) coupled to interfacial electron transfer associated with continued arsenopyrite dissolution. We suggest that the reduction of As­(V) to As­(III) coupled with the accumulation of Fe­(II) and arsenopyrite dissolution is the more plausible pathway for the emergence of As­(III) in the corona. This pathway aligns with the known geochemical coupling between the Fe­(II)/Fe­(III) and As­(III)/As­(V) redox pairs. Under low-oxygen conditions of gastric IVBA, As arsenopyrite dissolves and generates Fe­(II) and reducing equivalents at the mineral-fluid interface. These reducing conditions may stabilize As­(III) or promote localized reduction for sorbed As­(V), driving the generation of the As­(III)-rich corona, while Fe­(II) itself is oxidized to Fe­(III). This redox interplay is known to influence iron dissolution kinetics during arsenopyrite oxidation, as Fe­(II) oxidation both accelerates and regulates Fe release, which, in turn, modulates As chemistry and lability.

While the current experimental setup does not allow Fe K-edge analysis due to the MLLs configuration limits, the observed nanoscale speciation features offer critical insights into the behavior of As during early-stage bioaccessibility reactions. Note that the sulfur XRF maps presented in Figure are intended for qualitative interpretation only. Owing to the relatively low energy of the S Kα fluorescence, the measured signal is susceptible to self-attenuation within the sample matrix and was therefore not used for quantitative analysis without correction. By comparison, the attenuation of the As K-edge X-rays and Fe K fluorescence is small for the particle thicknesses investigated (calculated transmission ≈ 93–94% and 86–90%, respectively, for a 2 μm-thick FeAsS particle) and is therefore not expected to significantly affect the XANES-based chemical speciation.

The detection of labile As­(III) at the sub-100 nm scale has important toxicological implications. These species are highly mobile and are more readily absorbed in biological systems. Our study demonstrates how nano-XANES enables a mechanistic understanding of mineral reactivity and trace speciation at a scale relevant to inhalable particulate matter. This approach opens new pathways for assessing environmental health risks associated with ultrafine As-rich particles from legacy mine sites.

Conclusions

We demonstrate the unprecedented capability of high-resolution, high-sensitivity chemical state mapping using MLLs as nanofocusing optics. Our approach achieves a probe size of 16 × 15 nm2 and operates at photon energies above 11.5 keV, extending the energy reach of XANES spectromicroscopy beyond the ∼12 keV limit of Fresnel zone plate-based system's nanofocusing efficiency. This enables access to several environmentally and technologically relevant K-edges of heavy metals, noble catalysts, and critical elements. While K–B mirrors can access high energies, sub-50 nm spatial resolution chemical state mapping is not currently achievable with K–B optics at synchrotron beamlines.

A defining parameter of chemical state sensitivity is the ability to distinguish distinct phases within complex, heterogeneous samples. Our analysis establishes a detection threshold at the As K-edge of approximately 1 μg/cm2, equivalent to ∼18,000 As atoms in a 16 × 15 nm2 area with a 0.05 s dwell time. Below this limit, spectral fitting quality drops (R2 < 0.9; see Figure S6). Notably, at such low concentrations, the sample shows transmission of 99.7% at 11.9 keV and 97.4% at 12 keV, highlighting the limitations of transmission-based XANES techniques such as TXM-XANES and STXM in this regime. Further sensitivity gains are possible by increasing exposure time and flux, albeit with trade-offs in throughput and spatial resolution. The practical spatial resolution of the reconstructed chemical-state maps was independently estimated using two-dimensional power spectral density analysis, yielding a resolution of approximately 18.8 nm (Figure S7, Supporting Information), in good agreement with the demonstrated sub-20 nm chemical imaging capability

A key advantage of nano-XANES lies in its integration with nano-XRF mapping, which provides an enhanced elemental sensitivity. In the As–Pyr system, XRF imaging revealed sulfur depletion in As­(V)-rich regions, suggesting oxidation of arsenopyritea detail that is difficult to confirm with XANES alone. Similarly, the absence of iron in As­(III)-rich regions of reacted particles indicates the presence of labile, mobilized As, supporting mechanisms inferred from in vitro bioassays. In such complex systems, where overlapping absorption edges obscure interpretation, the synergy of XANES and XRF proves to be essential for robust chemical-state analysis.

Nano-XANES has emerged as a powerful method for detecting trace reaction products and characterizing nanoscale surface transformations under environmentally and biologically relevant conditions. The ability to resolve speciation variations across tens of nanometers was essential for capturing the formation of an As­(III) corona around As­(V)-containing cores in vitro. This finding, along with the detection of toxic As­(III) in air-oxidized and bioaccessible particles, underscores the importance of spatially resolved chemical imaging in evaluating the environmental health risks of inhalable ultrafine materials. Our ongoing efforts focus on time-resolved measurements to reveal the redox transformation kinetics.

Beyond environmental science applications, the integration of nano-XRF with nano-XANES offers significant potential for addressing challenges across diverse research fields. In energy storage materials, nanoscale chemical speciation can elucidate degradation mechanisms and charge distribution in battery electrodes and solid electrolytes. , For catalysis research, the technique enables nanoscale mapping of the spatial distribution of active sites and catalyst oxidation states, providing mechanistic insight into structure–activity relationships and catalyst deactivation processes. In the analysis of advanced materials, nano-XANES can identify localized chemical-state changes associated with mechanical degradation, corrosion, or electrical failure. The high spatial resolution and chemical sensitivity demonstrated here establish nano-XANES as a powerful tool for multimodal characterization of chemically heterogeneous materials across a broad range of scientific disciplines.

While highly promising, nano-XANES presents opportunities for continued technical development. Long acquisition times remain a challenge due to point-by-point imaging, particularly for large areas. Beam-induced sample damage is another concern, especially when scanning across the absorption edge. In our repeated scans of the same As–Pyr particle, we observed no changes in morphology or spectral features, suggesting minimal damage under the given conditions. However, the extent of radiation damage depends strongly on the nature of the sample, and preliminary measurements with reference compounds are necessary to determine dose thresholds for safe data collection. While cryogenic sample environments have proven effective for mitigating radiation damage in macromolecular crystallography and other X-ray techniques, their implementation in nano-XANES is constrained by the extremely short working distance (∼500 μm) required for nanofocusing optics and having the XRF detector 90° to the sample, presenting significant engineering challenges that currently limit their implementation. Future advances in sparse data acquisition and AI-assisted reconstruction methods offer more immediately promising solutions for accelerating measurements while minimizing beam exposure. Advanced computing and machine learning approaches enable high-quality spectroscopic reconstruction from sparsely sampled data, significantly reducing beam exposure requirements for radiation-sensitive samples.

Finally, the current MLLs configuration is practically limited to an operating energy range of approximately 11.5–18 keV. The lower energy limit is imposed by the short focal length and associated mechanical constraints on the MLLs working distance, while the upper limit is primarily dictated by the available coherent photon flux from the beamline's photon delivery system. Although the MLLs optics remain capable of focusing higher-energy X-rays, the substantial reduction in incident flux above approximately 18 keV results in longer acquisition times and reduced signal-to-noise ratios for nano-XANES measurements. Within this energy window, the instrument provides access to K-edge nano-XANES for elements from As (11.867 keV) to Zr (17.998 keV), as well as L-edge spectroscopy for many heavy elements, including Ta through U. One consequence of the present lower-energy limit is the inability to simultaneously access adjacent absorption edges in some redox-coupled systems, such as the As and Fe K-edges. Ongoing developments aimed at extending the operational energy range will broaden the applicability of high-resolution nano-XANES to additional elements and multimodal spectroscopic investigations.

Methods

As-Minerals

Pure arsenopyrite (Shelter Rock Minerals, Source: Yaogangxian Mine, Hunan Province, China), confirmed by XRD (X-ray diffraction) and S, Fe, and As XAS (X-ray absorption spectroscopy), was ground and sieved to <50 μm. It was cleaned according to ref by boiling 2 g in 10 mL deoxygenated 6 M HCl for 15 min and rinsed with 100 mL of deoxygenated Milli-Q DI water (18.3 MΩ·cm) in an anaerobic chamber (COY, MI). Solutions were deoxygenated by sparging at 100 °C with N2 for 1 h and stored under anoxic conditions for 24 h. The particles were suspended in 50 mL of acetone and cleaned by ultrasonic bath at 385 kHz for 12 15-min cycles. After each cycle, the acetone was decanted and replaced. The cleaned particles were lyophilized and stored in the anaerobic chamber. Arseniosiderite was synthesized following ref , confirmed by XRD and Fe and As XAS. The precipitate was centrifuged and washed 5×, then freeze-dried prior to solid-phase analysis.

In Vitro Bioassay (IVBA)

To evaluate the human health risk of fine As-rich particles, the bioaccessibility was evaluated by the IVBA using synthetic gastric fluid (SGF) according to US EPA SW-846 Method 1340: In Vitro Bioaccessibility Assay for Lead and Arsenic in Soil. Briefly, the SGF was added to the As-rich particles at a 1:100 solid-to-solution mass ratio in a rotary reactor (7 rpm) at 37 °C for 1 h to simulate the physiological conditions of the human gastrointestinal system. The SGF was a 0.4 M glycine solution at pH 1.5 using reagent-grade concentrated HCl. All reagents and solutions were deoxygenated and preheated to 37 °C. Bioreactors were centrifuged at 5,000 g (Flexifuge Microcentrifuge, Argos) for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred for ICP-MS and HPLC-ESI-MS/MS analyses to determine the concentration and speciation of As released and compared to the total As in the solid determined by HCl+HNO3 acid digestion and ICP-MS analysis. To eschew experimental redox changes, IVBA was performed under controlled anoxic conditions in a vinyl glove bag (Coy, MI). The solids were washed with deoxygenated Milli-Q DI water, recentrifuged 2×, lyophilized, and stored under anoxic conditions in the glove bag prior to solid-phase characterization.

To isolate the fine fraction, particles were suspended in ethanol and sonicated for 10 min. Gravimetric Stokes’ separation of quiescent suspensions for 15 min removed larger particles (>2000 nm), and the supernatant containing the colloidal fraction was collected from 2 mm below the meniscus. Fines were isolated by serial dilutions of the IVBA-reacted and unreacted solids. An aliquot of 5 μL of the suspension was micropipetted to a custom-made silicon substrate (Norcada). All the X-ray imaging was performed at room temperature under a He environment at 250 Torr. Note that the depth of focus of the MLLs at this energy is approximately 4 μm (Figure S1), substantially greater than the thickness of the particles studied, ensuring that the nanofocused probe remained in focus throughout the entire sample volume.

Nano-XANES Data Collection

Nano-XANES experiments were conducted at the Hard X-ray Nanoprobe beamline at NSLS-II, utilizing MLLs) as nanofocusing optics. Detailed descriptions of the instrumentation and beam stability can be found elsewhere. A Si(111) double-crystal monochromator was used to produce a monochromatic X-ray beam. The monochromatic beam was then focused onto a secondary source aperture using vertical and horizontal focusing mirror systems. The photon energy was calibrated using the Au L3 absorption edge at 11.919 keV, using an Au-foil standard. Within the microscope chamber, a nanofocused X-ray beam was produced under a helium environment (250 Torr) using horizontally and vertically aligned MLLs. A central beamstop and order sorting apertures (OSAs) were utilized to block higher-order diffraction from the lenses, with the sample-OSA distance set to approximately 500 μm. The reconstructed probe propagation through the focal plane is shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and Supporting Video S5. The sample stage was raster scanned to collect XRF and transmission data at each position, using step sizes of 10–30 nm and exposure times of 30–50 ms. For XRF spectrum acquisition, a silicon drift detector (Vortex, Hitachi) was positioned at a 90-degree angle to the sample, while a transmission area detector (Eiger 1M) was placed 2.05 m from the sample to capture far-field probe intensity to reconstruct the phase image using ptychography. Detailed experimental parameters and dose delivered to the samples are provided in Table S1 in Supporting Information. Note that the in vitro sample dose was kept lower than the model samples.

For nano-XANES experiments, XRF maps were acquired across the As K-edge (11.867 keV) for 40–60 energy points with 1 eV energy resolution at around the white lines. To ensure optimal focusing at each energy point, focal length adjustments were made based on precalculated positions of the lens and sample stage, similar to our FZP-based nano-XANES methodology. Pixel-wise, the XRF spectrum fitted was done using PyXRF, and phase reconstruction was carried out using an in-house ptychography reconstruction program. Analysis of all nano-XANES data, such as XANES fitting, correlation analysis, and multicolor image stacks, was conducted using XMIDAS software. Detailed experimental details are available in the Supporting Information, containing several technical details of the nano-XANES workflow.
