Macrocytosis combined with monoclonal gammopathy identifies patients carrying VEXAS‐linked UBA1 mutations
Authors: Verena Petzer, Wolfram Mayr, Eva Spoegler, Emina Jukic, Christoph Winkler, Verena Vogi, Normann Steiner, Christina Duftner, Andrea Griesmacher, Günter Weiss, Dominik Wolf, Florian Kocher, David Haschka
Published: 2026

Since the first description of VEXAS syndrome in 2020, 1 an inflammatory disease caused by somatic mutations of the UBA1 gene has gained increasing attention. Although the prevalence of UBA1 mutations has been estimated at approximately 1:4000 males >50 years, 2 diagnosis remains challenging due to the highly heterogeneous clinical presentation and difficulties in preselection of patients for targeted molecular testing. This particularly holds true in patients with mild or atypical symptoms. Consistently, UBA1 mutations—initially considered pathognomonic for VEXAS—may show incomplete penetrance, particularly at low variant allele frequencies (VAFs), and can occur without overt inflammation. 3 , 4

From a hematologic perspective, myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS) are observed in up to 50% of patients with VEXAS, 5 and plasma cell dyscrasias have emerged as another common comorbidity, reported in approximately 20% of cases. 6 In addition, macrocytosis is a near‐universal finding associated with VEXAS syndrome and was already highlighted in the initial report. 1 Maeda et al. further emphasized the significance of macrocytosis by creating a clinical scoring system in which macrocytic anemia uniquely received a two‐point weighting, while all other parameters were assigned single points. 7

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the frequency of UBA1 mutations in the presence or absence of macrocytosis in male patients with concomitant monoclonal gammopathy (MG).

Therefore, we retrospectively identified 1245 patients with MG. Next, we excluded female patients (n = 624), male patients with missing MCV data or if they were still alive (n = 395), and a residual bone marrow (BM) aspirate sample was missing (n = 36). Moreover, n = 31 males were excluded as they either received an allo‐HSCT or MG was most likely therapy‐induced (monoclonal antibody, i.e., anti‐CD38 therapy). After applying these exclusion criteria, n = 160 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1A). The study was approved by the local ethics committee (No. 1194/2023). All analyses were performed in R statistical software (version 4.5.0), with a two‐sided significance threshold of P < 0.05. Further details are provided in the Supplemental Data file.

The patients were then stratified according to their mean corpuscular volume (MCV) as normocytic (MCV < 96 fL, MGNORM, n = 65) or macrocytic (MCV ≥ 96 fL, MGMAC, n = 95) at the time of MG diagnosis (Figure 1A,B). MGMAC were significantly older (median 70 years, interquartile range [IQR] 61–78) when compared to the MGNORM cohort (median 65 years, IQR 57–74, P = 0.015). The median time interval between the initial diagnosis of the MG and the BM aspiration was comparable between the two groups. Vitamin B12 and folate levels highlighted that MGMAC was not related to vitamin deficiencies (Figure 1B).

For genotyping of UBA1 mutations, genomic DNA was extracted from archived methanol–acetic acid fixed BM culture suspensions using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA samples were analyzed by next‐generation sequencing (NGS) using a custom panel covering the coding sequence of the gene UBA1 (NM_003334.4) and a hybridization‐capture enrichment‐based sequencing approach (IDT, Coralville, USA) with a 2% limit of detection (for further details, see the Supplemental Data file). Strikingly, the prevalence of UBA1 mutations was significantly higher in MGMAC individuals when compared to the MGNORM group (7.7%, 95% CI [2.5%–17%] vs. 0%, P = 0.01) (Figure 1A,B). This finding underpins that the pre‐test likelihood of UBA1 mutation detection, although generally very low, can be increased by applying these two simple clinical selection criteria. Thus, while screening for UBA1 mutations is well established in MDS, our data uncover that patients with MG and concomitant macrocytosis have a clearly higher prevalence of UBA1 mutations and should be considered for genetic UBA1 testing.

MCV distribution demonstrated a moderately increased mean MCV (101.7 fL) in the MGMAC group, with a substantial proportion of patients in the MGMAC exhibited only mildly elevated values ranging from 96 to 100 fL (Figure 2A). Hemoglobin levels were not significantly different between MGMAC and MGNORM (Figure 1B). The isotype distribution of the M‐protein did not differ substantially between the two groups. IgG was by far the most prevalent isotype in both MCV groups, followed by IgM, IgA, light‐chain, and a combination of isotypes with no significant differences in distribution between the groups (Figure 2B). Regarding the underlying hematological disorders, patients in the MGMAC group more frequently developed myeloid neoplasms over time, whereas MGNORM patients showed a higher prevalence of lymphoid diseases, including plasma cell disorders (Figure 2C). This is in line with the literature, as macrocytosis is a hallmark of myeloid diseases, especially MDS and other myeloid clonal diseases, and confirms that lymphoid malignancies per se are not associated with intrinsic macrocytosis. 12 All other disease entities were equally distributed between the groups without any notable differences (Figure 2B). Although lymphoid cells do not carry UBA1 mutations, UBA1‐mutated myeloid clones may promote MG through a cell‐extrinsic mechanism. By generating a chronically pro‐inflammatory milieu enriched in IL‐6, TNF‐α, and other cytokines, it has been shown that the mutant myeloid compartment skews B‐cell differentiation towards an expansion of plasmablasts and impaired early B‐cell tolerance, reflected by the loss of transitional B cells. 13 Thus, VEXAS‐associated inflammation may provide a permissive niche for aberrant clonal plasmablast differentiation and expansion finally leading to MG production. This may at least in part contribute to enrichment of UBA1 mutations in MGMAC individuals.

Among the five UBA1 mutations identified in the MGMAC group, three canonical exon 3 variants (p.(Met41Leu), p.(Met41Thr), and p.(Met41Val)) were detected, all of which have previously been described in the initial report. 1 Furthermore, we identified additional variants: one non‐canonical mutation in the active adenylation domain (p.(Asp506Gly)), a known pathogenic variant of UBA1, 14 and one located in the second catalytic cysteine half‐domain (p.(Lys746Asn), which is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance. However, a mutation at the same position has been classified as likely pathogenic, and patients carrying one of these variants in combination with (p.(Asp506Gly) have been reported and classified as pathogenic 15 (Figure 2D). Multiple in silico prediction tools consistently indicate a likely pathogenic effect of the variant, including a CADD Phred score of 24.8 (>20), a REVEL score of 0.514 (>0.5), an AlphaMissense score of 0.994 (likely pathogenic), and a SIFT score of 0.02 (deleterious).

Retrospective review of the clinical symptoms in the medical reports of the five patients carrying a UBA1 mutation suggested at least in two a so far unrecognized and in two a possible VEXAS syndrome (Figure 2E). Patient #1 harbored a (p.(Met41Val) mutation with a high VAF (80%) and presented with fever, pancytopenia, orbital phlegmon, and Sweet syndrome and was treated with corticosteroids and dapsone. Patient #4 was found to carry a (p.Met41Thr) mutation (VAF 72%) and exhibited exanthema, thrombosis, bicytopenia, bilateral diffuse bronchiolitis, and chondritis of the left ear and was also empirically treated with corticosteroids. For the other two patients (#2 and #5), VEXAS may have contributed to the observed symptoms; however, alternative explanations, including infection, were also supported by the available records. Only one patient with a non‐canonical mutation p.(Asp506Gly) (#3) had an underlying MDS without documented inflammatory symptoms. This patient received 37 cycles of 5‐azacitidine for MDS. Interestingly, the VAF of the UBA1 clone in this patient was lower than in the other patients (VAF 18%) and did not increase over time during 5‐Aza therapy. This observation raises the possibility that 5‐Aza potentially limited the emergence of inflammatory VEXAS‐associated clinical symptoms (Figure 2E).

The central finding of this study is the identification of a markedly increased prevalence of UBA1 mutations in male patients characterized by an MCV > 96 fL and concomitant M‐protein. This detection rate is comparable to that reported in male patients with relapsing polychondritis 9 or male patients over 50 years with suspected autoinflammatory disease. 8 In contrast, large prevalence studies conducted in unselected cohorts have reported substantially lower mutation frequencies ranging from 0.0006% to 0.3%, 2 , 3 , 8 underscoring the importance of clinical preselection strategies. In patients with BM failure, particularly those with MDS undergoing stem cell transplantation, a prevalence as low as 0.11% has been described. 10 Similarly, in the Leeds Haematological Malignancy Diagnostic Service cohort, a prevalence of approximately 1% was reported among MDS patients aged over 50 years. 8 These findings were confirmed by Sirenko et al., but the observed prevalence increased to up to 7% when the analysis was restricted to a selected subgroup of patients lacking other myeloid mutations or established disease classification 11 (Figure 1B).

Our study has several limitations: The retrospective design represents an important limitation, as it may be associated with selection bias and restricts causal interpretation of the findings. We also acknowledge that the relatively small sample size of our patient cohort is a limitation and that our proposed stratification requires validation in a larger prospective MG cohort. Moreover, the time point of BM aspiration partly deviates from the time point of MG diagnosis, with some patients having received therapies for their hematological disease in between.

To summarize, our findings suggest that combined macrocytosis and MG may serve as a simple and clinically accessible screening criterion to identify patients at increased risk for underlying UBA1 mutation and, consequently, unrecognized VEXAS syndrome. Given the often highly heterogeneous clinical symptoms of VEXAS syndrome, such an approach may facilitate early diagnosis and improve patient stratification in both hematologic and rheumatologic settings. Prospective studies and independent validation cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and to optimize screening and management strategies for identifying “at‐risk” populations.
