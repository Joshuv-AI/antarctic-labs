Understanding Australian healthcare provider attitudes towards smart inhalers for paediatric asthma management: a qualitative study
Authors: Natasha E Noble, Lindsey Cooke, Jamie Bryant, Dane Falcioni, Bernadette Goddard, Janine Curtis, Ellen Newman, Donna Vaughan, Nicholas Zwar, Simon Deeming, Christopher Oldmeadow, Joerg Mattes, Megan Freund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the perceptions of Australian healthcare providers (HCPs) of the usefulness of smart inhalers for children with asthma and considerations for their effective integration into routine care.DesignQualitative descriptive study using semi-structured interviews. A hybrid thematic analysis approach was used to analyse the data.SettingHealthcare services in Australia including general practice, hospitals and Local Health Districts.ParticipantsEight general practitioners, six paediatric specialists and nine respiratory nurses were recruited using a unique purposive sampling strategy for each HCP group.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPerceptions of the facilitators and challenges to the adoption of smart inhalers for childhood asthma management.ResultsMost HCPs had not previously used smart inhalers. Perceived benefits of smart inhalers included access to objective medication use data to inform asthma treatment decisions and enable opportunities to discuss asthma management with parents and carers. Key facilitators for implementation included evidence of clinical effectiveness, financial incentives (both in terms of subsidised smart inhaler costs for users and reimbursement for HCPs) and integration of smart inhaler data into existing medical record systems. The primary barrier to implementation identified was related to workflow issues, including available resourcing, support and time.ConclusionsHCPs expressed generally positive views about the potential of smart inhalers to improve asthma management among children. However, they also identified several practical, logistical and clinical barriers that must be addressed prior to widespread implementation of smart inhalers. Targeted strategies that address these barriers will be essential for successful implementation into routine care.

Background

Childhood asthma imposes substantial healthcare costs and significant limitations on the daily physical and social lives of children with asthma and their families.1 Suboptimal use of inhaled corticosteroids (‘preventer’ medications) and over reliance on short-acting beta agonist therapy (‘reliever’ medications) are recognised risk factors for severe asthma exacerbations in children and sudden asthma death.2 Preventer medication adherence rates of between 75% and 85% are required to achieve good asthma control,3 yet the average rate of adherence with preventer medication among children aged <18 years is approximately 50% or below.46 Non-adherence with prescribed preventer medication is associated with more frequent emergency department visits and hospitalisations among children.5 7 The overuse of reliever medications is also associated with adverse consequences including increased risk of severe exacerbations and asthma-related mortality.8 9

Digital health interventions such as technology-based monitoring and feedback are a potentially effective strategy to improve medication adherence and paediatric asthma control.10 11 Electronic monitoring devices can provide real-time data on medication use to both patients and their healthcare providers (HCPs).12 ‘Smart inhalers’ use electronic sensors, either attached as an add-on or integrated with regular asthma inhalers to detect and record the date and time of inhaler use.10 13 They can also connect with smart device applications to upload real-time data to the patient or caregiver’s device (eg, smartphone), providing additional features such as reminders or prompts for medication administration, assessment of inhaler technique, monitoring of symptoms and provision of real-time adherence feedback.14 15 The data generated can also support clinical decision-making as well as prompt discussions between clinicians, patients and their carers about self-management of asthma.1619

Evidence from systematic reviews suggests that use of smart inhalers can improve children’s adherence with their prescribed inhaler schedule by 50%,10 with more limited evidence also suggesting their use can improve children’s asthma control,20 reduce the need for oral steroids and reduce healthcare utilisation including hospital admissions.3 However, smart inhalers are not yet widely integrated into routine clinical practice.15 In Australia, their use remains largely limited to research studies and pilot programmes, rather than being broadly accessible for prescription and routine use. Parents generally report positive experiences with using such smart technology to manage their children’s asthma in trial settings.21 22 Understanding HCPs’ perceptions may therefore help inform future efforts to support their availability, uptake and integration into care. Within clinical trial settings, a range of smart inhalers with differing functionalities have been tested for children and their parents/caregivers.10 Most of the smart inhalers trialled provided a reminder function for planned preventer medication use; however, the majority of devices did not allow parent/caregivers, children or HCPs to track or review medication adherence in real time.32325 As an emerging technology, there are likely to be many challenges to the successful implementation and integration of smart inhalers into clinical practice and daily asthma management.26 27 Given the potential benefits of smart inhalers for paediatric asthma, there is a need to understand how smart inhalers might be successfully implemented in real-world settings and potential barriers to implementation.

Limited research has explored HCP perceptions of the facilitators and challenges to the adoption of smart inhalers for asthma management,15 17 and few studies have focused specifically on their use for childhood asthma.28 29 Two previous studies examined HCP experiences of implementing smart inhalers for children’s asthma (aged <18 years), highlighting technical problems with the inhalers28 29 and the need for additional resources—in particular staffing—as potential implementation barriers.28 While these studies provide some insight into HCP experiences with smart inhalers for childhood asthma,28 29 they are limited in terms of scope, setting and sampling. Both primarily examined experiences of HCPs within the specific trial setting and encompassed aspects of study recruitment and randomisation. The study by Lawrie et al29 was not designed as an in-depth qualitative study29 and no details about the interview topic guide were provided. Importantly, neither study captured the views of respiratory specialists, respiratory nurses (RNs) or general practitioners (GPs).

Given these gaps, particularly the lack of research with GPs and within the Australian healthcare context, the current study aimed to explore the perceptions of Australian paediatric respiratory specialists, paediatric RNs and GPs who provide paediatric asthma care, of the usefulness of smart inhalers for children with asthma, and issues to be considered for their effective integration into routine care. In the Australian healthcare setting, childhood asthma management is delivered primarily through GP-led care, with specialist and respiratory nursing support generally provided for more severe or complex asthma management.30 This sample thus reflects the views of clinicians with direct influence on the potential adoption and use of smart inhalers for Australian childhood asthma care.

Methods

Study design

Qualitative one-on-one semistructured interviews were undertaken between November 2023 and May 2024 with HCPs including hospital-based paediatric respiratory specialists, GPs and RNs.

Patient and public involvement

Alongside the research team, a Consumer Advisory Group was involved in project planning and implementation including the development of the interview guide, project protocols, data interpretation and report writing. The Consumer Advisory Group included three community members who are parents of children with asthma and with important insight into HCP roles and responsibilities related to paediatric asthma management. The Consumer Advisory Group reviewed all study materials for face and content validity and will be engaged in developing strategies for dissemination of the study findings.

Participant eligibility and recruitment

A unique purposive sampling strategy was used for each HCP group.

General practitioners

GPs or GP registrars were eligible to participate if their practice was located within the Hunter, Central Coast and Manning regions of New South Wales (NSW) and they provided care to children with asthma. GP registrars and their GP supervisors in eligible regions were identified via the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) GP Training Research Unit. Invitations to participate including a copy of the study information sheet were emailed to eligible GPs by a senior academic advisor at the GP Training Research Unit.

Hospital-based paediatric respiratory clinicians

Hospital-based paediatric specialists (PSs) were eligible to participate if they were based in NSW Australia, provided care to children with asthma and had a focused interest in paediatric asthma. One of the study authors (JM) developed a list of specialists across NSW. An email including a copy of the study information sheet was sent from JM inviting participants to take part in an interview.

Respiratory nurses

In the Australian setting, RNs are specialist registered nurses who provide care for people with acute and chronic respiratory conditions, often in hospital or specialist outpatient services. RNs were eligible to participate if their place of work was located within any Australian Local Health District and they provided care to children with asthma. This population was recruited through study author and respiratory clinical nurse consultant (BG), who sent an invitation email and copy of the study information sheet to members of the Australian Paediatric Asthma Nurses’ Special Interest Group.

Providers were asked to respond to the email invitation if they were interested in participating in the study. On expression of interest, a research assistant followed up to confirm their interest and consent to participate. On consent, the research assistant scheduled an interview with the provider at a mutually convenient time. HCPs received a AU$100 gift voucher as reimbursement for their time following completion of the interview.

Interview development and procedure

A semistructured interview guide was developed using a best practice framework.31 To inform the domains included in the interview guide, a review of the published literature on barriers to smart inhaler adoption was conducted.6 26 27 The final domains included in the interview guide were adapted from van de Hei et al15 and encompassed: knowledge of and motivations to using smart inhalers; functionality of smart inhalers and integration into treatment; factors influencing implementation of smart inhalers into clinical practice and the healthcare system; alternative approaches and the relative effectiveness of smart inhalers; and any additional insights on the potential of smart inhalers in managing paediatric asthma.

Interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (DF (BPsychSc(Hons); MClinPsych), male and NN (PhD; BPsych(Hons)), female). Both interviewers had prior experience interviewing health professionals. Neither interviewer had a pre-existing relationship with participants prior to study commencement and no personal experiences or opinions regarding asthma management or smart inhalers were disclosed during interviews. Participants were informed that the interviewer was a senior researcher with a professional interest in smart inhalers for paediatric asthma management. Interviews were conducted using Zoom video conferencing software and were audio-recorded for transcription with participant consent. Interviewers did not make field notes. Each interview began with a standardised introduction to the research, which included an overview of the interview scope and a description of smart inhaler functionality. Interviewers then asked open-ended questions aligned with each domain of the interview guide (eg, ‘Would you consider using a smart inhaler in the treatment of childhood asthma?’), followed by prompts to encourage discussion and elicit general and specific responses (eg, ‘What are some of the benefits you can see?’ ‘What are some of the disadvantages?’). A copy of the interview guide is included in online supplemental file S1. No repeat interviews were conducted and transcripts were not returned to participants for review. Data saturation was not formally assessed; however, interviews were conducted until no new themes or concepts emerged, indicating that the collected data were sufficient to address the research questions.

Analysis

The methods, analysis and results are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist32 to ensure transparency and rigour. Interview audio recording was transcribed verbatim by an Australian-based transcription company. All transcripts were deidentified, with each participant allotted a unique identifier (eg, GP 01). Transcripts were read and reread by three members of the research team (DF, NEN, LC). Data were analysed using a hybrid thematic analysis, incorporating both deductive and inductive coding.33 An initial coding framework was informed by the study aims and relevant literature, aligned with the key domains in the interview guide, while additional codes and themes were generated inductively from the data to capture additional or unanticipated perspectives. Transcripts were independently coded by three research team members in Microsoft Word (NEN, LC, DF). After coding two transcripts from each participant group, the coders met to discuss and refine the predefined (deductive) codes and develop new (inductive) codes as needed. Remaining transcripts were then interpreted and coded by one coder, with the final coding frame developed collaboratively with other members of the research team.

Following coding, data for each participant group were charted into tables based on the codes and interpreted to identify themes relevant to each research question. Both within-participant and between-participant contradictions and inconsistencies were considered. Coders drew on their differing lived experiences to ensure multiple perspectives were considered reducing the risk of confirmation bias. Theme labelling and interpretation were continually discussed and reviewed by coders in regular research team meetings. Once consensus was reached on the final themes, the transcripts were re-examined by two members of the research team (NEN and LC) to ensure the themes reflected the entire body of data and no relevant aspects were missed. Anonymised direct quotations from participants were selected to provide illustrative evidence of key analytic themes. Quotations from HCPs are indicated with the following abbreviations: GP, PS and RN.

Results

Study sample

13 GPs/GP registrars, 8 PSs and 12 RNs expressed interest and were invited to participate. A total of 23 HCPs, comprising 8 GPs/registrars, 6 PSs and 9 RNs provided consent and completed an interview. Most participants completed the interview from their workplace. Interviews lasted an average of 26 mins (range 14–36 mins). No demographic data were collected from study participants. Just over a quarter of study participants reported some prior experience using smart inhalers in practice, generally via small pilot or in-service level programmes. This included two PSs and four RNs. No GPs reported prior use of smart inhalers.

HCP knowledge and use of smart inhalers

Most HCPs, particularly GPs, had overall low knowledge of and familiarity with smart inhalers. Some respiratory specialists and nurses reported previously using them in clinical trials. Smart inhalers had typically been trialled to monitor preventer medication adherence in high-risk or complex patients with asthma.

So we have used them though, my experience in using them has been in high risk asthma patients where we felt that it perhaps wasn't treatment resistant asthma, but actually just poor adherence that was causing the severe asthma exacerbations. So to tease that out I've seen them be used in that setting. [PS05]

HCPs also generally perceived that their patients had limited knowledge or awareness of smart inhalers.

But no, I haven't had a patient or parent come and talk to me about it [smart devices]. When we were doing smart inhaler devices, there wasn't anyone who was sort of, seemed to have prior knowledge of them. So I don't think it’s something that’s made it into the consumer focus much. [RS02]

While attitudes towards smart inhalers among HCPs were generally positive, several respondents indicated that smart inhalers would need to show benefits for patients before they would recommend their use.

Perceived benefits of smart inhalers

HCPs identified several key benefits of smart inhalers, most notably the value of having objective data on medication use. This included being able to monitor adherence with preventer medications and track the frequency of use of reliever medications, data which could inform treatment and medication decisions. HCPs noted that it is often difficult for parents to remember exactly how many doses of medications have been taken and when, and that smart inhalers could provide accurate data to address this.

Yeah. well, I mean it would be amazing to know exactly how much the patient uses. That would be really helpful to help determine is their asthma under control? Do you need to change the medication or dose or is it just that they're not taking it? And so then you could also then use that for as educating the patient as well. So it wouldn't just be sort of providing clinical information and it could potentially help compliance as well. [GP01]

They'll say, oh yes, I'm using my preventer every day, morning and night, without fail. And then you think, oh gosh, well they're needing such high doses of steroid. And then using a smart inhaler device can actually just answer the question for you. Well, are they actually telling the truth or not? [PS05]

HCPs also perceived benefits of having remote access to the data provided by the smart inhalers for informing care. For example, GPs mentioned that they could contact patients if needed or request an appointment if the data from the inhaler was concerning or indicated an issue with asthma management. Smart inhalers were also seen as a useful tool for patient education and engagement during consultations, providing opportunities to discuss adherence and asthma management based on data in a non-judgemental way.

I think for rural healthcare, it would be very beneficial, especially since they don't necessarily go to the doctor or ED right away it may postpone things until it gets really bad. They could call and we could look at the data together. And then decide if it’s necessary for them to come in or if it can be managed over the phone. [GP04]

So it’s a good opportunity to talk to the patients based on that about how it all works. You just have to be really careful to be non-judgmental and, "Oh, that’s interesting that you've done that here [missed preventer doses]. And you'll be surprised to know how many of our other young people have done something similar. [PS02]

Another perceived benefit was time efficiency during consultations. GPs mentioned that access to inhaler data could reduce the need for lengthy history-taking about inhaler use patterns and history, streamlining appointments and clinical decision-making.

I personally for the way that I practice, would find that it could potentially save time because if you're going to take a proper history to try and find out what someone’s asthma is like having a look at that information [device data] actually gives you a lot of information. [GP01]

A key perceived benefit of smart inhalers for parents and families was the reminder or prompt function for use of preventer medication. HCPs perceived that smart inhalers were helpful for parents to monitor or oversee their children’s use of their inhaler medications, potentially allowing children to have greater independence in their asthma management and reducing the need for adult supervision.

If there is a device that tells them that it hasn't been given, I think that would be really helpful. Particularly if it does also remind the child, because I think a lot of children over six if a device is reminding them, then they can go get their parent and say, it’s time now. Yeah I think it would be really helpful. [RN07]

It’s also good for parents who maybe are trying to give their children a little bit more independence about taking their inhaler and say, that either that they're hoping the child is taking it themselves or they tell them to take it and the child goes off and they don't know whether they actually took it or not. [PS01]

However, HCPs acknowledged some potential limitations of reminders, noting that regular reminders had the potential to become annoying or less effective over time. They also suggested that use of the App might decline over time as it becomes less novel.

And I guess we've also had, like some, some people say that’s all good for the first couple of weeks, and then a busy parent with multiple children how many times are you going to look at that and go, that’s just another, another being a ping, you know? [GP01]

Variable applicability of smart inhalers

HCPs identified several potential issues with the appropriateness or applicability of the smart inhaler technology, with HCPs indicating that smart inhalers may be more useful to some families than others. For example, some HCPs commented that many patients are already managing their asthma well without the smart inhalers, or that the smart inhalers would have limited additional value for organised families. Conversely, they noted that families who could benefit most from the smart inhaler technology may also be the least likely to use them. Some HCPs also expressed concern that smart inhalers and the associated App could add pressure and overwhelm some families.

I think it’s hard because the ones that probably need a little bit more [help with asthma control] are the ones that would probably be a little bit more difficult to keep on track with the app as well. [RN02]

I think some would definitely not want to be involved in it because they'll just see it as another thing that’s going to add something to their busy schedules that they, you know and there'll be some that'll be really interested. Yeah. I think it’s a mixed, it'll be a mixed thing. [RN04]

HCPs also expressed concern that smart inhalers may make patients and families feel guilty or like they are being watched, undermining their trust in their healthcare team.

Another thing as well that I could be a barrier is like maybe they [parents] were thinking oh, are you not trusting me enough that I'm saying we are good with adherence and you need the extra proof of it. [GP06]

Facilitators to the integration of smart inhalers into treatment and practice

(a) Evidence of benefit

HCPs emphasised the need for robust evidence of the benefits of smart inhalers before promoting their use among patients. This included evidence that smart inhalers are effective for improving asthma control or outcomes, improve adherence to medications, and/or evidence that smart inhalers reduce hospital admissions for asthma.

Yeah, I think like, I suppose the biggest factor would be the ability to have better asthma control. Yeah, that would be the number one. I would say that if you could prove that your asthma would be much better controlled by using this device or app that would be really positive and interesting for families. Yeah, I think that would be the number one. [RN04]

(b) Patient advocacy

HCPs suggested that patient advocacy could help to overcome some of the reluctance among professionals to drive implementation of the smart inhalers, with the idea that a ‘groundswell’ of patient interest would be needed to get HCPs onboard. GPs, in particular, noted that the patient-led nature of the smart inhalers and app would encourage them to learn about and adopt the technology.

So once you got, once every GP had four or five patients using the app and they came back and said, oh this really works. It’s great. The GPs almost then retrospectively forced into to learning how it works once they had patients on board and they're like, oh this works, the patients love it, the parents love it. And it’s like, oh I guess I better use that, learn how to use this cause now it’s now for, it’s now become a thing that I have to do. [GP03]

(c) Financial subsidies

The cost of the smart inhalers was identified as a major issue for implementation, with HCPs noting the need for financial incentives to support their implementation into practice. HCPs felt that the cost of smart inhalers for patients would need to be subsidised, through either private or public health insurance rebates (eg, Medicare in Australia). One provider suggested the possibility of pharmacies being involved in the subsidised provision of smart inhalers.

The other thing will be cost. Because I remember those smart inhaler devices were about $200 each. And the families that we wanted to use it well and the ones that lost them would not be able to afford them in the first place…And if you were trying to do it across the board in the clinics, you wouldn't as a clinic be able to pay for them. It would have to be on an individual or Medicare basis. And so the cost would definitely be preventive. [PS02]

Look, I think that would have to be, that wouldn't necessarily come from clinicians that might be more liaising with the, the pharmacies and the, you know inhaler companies. If you had a deal you got, you know, you buy [an inhaler] and you get a smart inhaler device as part of a deal, then yeah, potentially you could do it at a wider scale. So it becomes normal that when you get your inhaler, you get put it in your smart inhaler device. I think it could be done. [PS01]

(d) Integration into existing medical systems

HCPs highlighted the value of smart inhaler data being available to the multiple clinicians involved in asthma management and the need for smooth integration of the data into existing medical record systems. RNs, in particular, emphasised the need for automated uploading of inhaler data to ensure that it does not get missed by providers. They also noted potential problems associated with the use of different medical software systems across primary, secondary and tertiary healthcare institutions.

I think that the software, if it’s possible that it’s compatible with, which I know would be a very big ask with, with the hospital system [to promote implementation]. We're going through another change in, in, in software, because I think whatever we do with the kids, if it’s not documented, it doesn't, it, it doesn't exist. If you can't upload that information in a way that can be streamlined, then this is, I think when things get missed then, then you've got to come up with other ways of making sure the information is there to make sure that the documentation is correct… [RN09]

Oh gosh, we've just got this whole new system. So we've just gone from paper to electronics. And just making any little changes is so much work. So I'm not sure how it would work from that perspective or how valuable [the smart inhaler device data] would be… Obviously everything is going more digital, so possibly down the track that would automatically talk to the new [medical system], the system we have and automatically record that. [RN05]

(e) Potential appeal of smart inhaler technology

PSs noted that smart inhalers and the associated App may be especially appealing to older children who are generally comfortable using technology. One specialist also mentioned the possibility of gamification of the App to increase its appeal to children and adolescents, and the potential role of artificial intelligence (AI) to tailor the App to individual users.

And so it would be really fascinating if you could get your gamification, your AI all working together… AI being triggered to contact after four days of no medication, whatever it is, and saying, "Oh look, I've noticed that you haven't been using your medication and we've noticed there’s a high pollen count in the next few days, probably going to set off your asthma. What do you think you should do? [PS02]

Clinical barriers to the integration of smart inhalers into treatment and practice

(a) Practical issues

Practical issues with the smart inhalers mentioned by HCPs included their longevity, cost and affordability, especially for lower income families, user-friendliness and potential technical or connection problems. HCPs had concerns about the accuracy of the data captured by the inhalers, the inability of the inhalers to assess inhaler technique and the potential inappropriate use of smart inhalers including ‘puffing in the air’ rather than taking doses correctly. HCPs also mentioned practical problems such as inhalers or add-ons getting lost or broken, the availability of the inhalers or add-ons for different types of inhalers, concerns around environmental impacts and some concerns around privacy and data sharing. One GP also noted that data may be missed where children have multiple puffers at home, school or at separated parents’ houses. Concern about the transition to a new technology for some patients and families was also raised, including how confident some families would be with the new technology and some families having older phones which may not support the smart inhalers or App.

I think less technical glitches. So if the companies could provide a product that was very easy to Bluetooth to a device, was very user friendly, was not going to have any issues with allowing full actuation of the preventer itself, because that definitely happened with the smart inhaler device. All of those things would mean uptake would be more likely. [RS02]

(b) Resourcing, time and reimbursement

A key concern regarding implementation was around the time, resourcing and support required by HCPs to implement smart inhalers. HCPs were concerned about reimbursement to cover the additional time required for set up, patient education and ongoing monitoring of inhaler data. HCPs questioned who would be responsible for education, implementation, ongoing monitoring and troubleshooting. GPs, RNs and respiratory specialists alike noted the already limited time they have with patients, and expressed concern about time being required to set up and explain a new technology to their patients, the need to be confident with the technology, as well as a lack of time and reimbursement for learning a new technology and for monitoring the data outside of appointments.

Just in general, who does all that education? Who do the parents contact if that’s not working or something goes wrong? And then we could if there is a nurse or anywhere, but who pays them to do that? So in tertiary children’s hospital where there are set up respiratory departments, they have CNCs for education around that. Whereas in a public hospital, similar to mine, it’s a general paediatric department and I'm a specialty physician, but the nursing staff there, we don't have a dedicated nurse for asthma education. [PS03]

GPs in particular highlighted time constraints, including that they are often required to spend their own unpaid time learning about new approaches, and may not feel confident trying to explain a new system to their patients.

So an element is the GP not being lazy, like in the sense that you do just have to sit there for an hour and probably unpaid and just sit there and figure it out yourself so you're not learning on the go. Like, and that is just a reality of doing that. So you have to make that decision to do that. I think the program has to be as simple as possible. [GP03]

No, I wouldn't be logging into software that would not be useful…Yeah, I would not be spending my time, like even if when they registered for example like if then I get, I don't know, like a notification or whatever, I would never do that. It would just be when they're here in front of me, how’s the asthma going? Open up the app, let me have a look. Okay. That’s it. [GP01]

(c) Collaboration and commitment

The need for a collaborative approach among HCPs and supported implementation of smart inhalers was noted, with commitment from both HCPs and parents needed for successful adoption. Suggestions included holding clinic-wide educational sessions and having a clinic-wide roll out of smart inhalers, ensuring that practice managers and practice nurses are supportive and involved, and the need for collaboration between specialists and GPs. There was also some consensus among GPs and respiratory specialists that practice nurses or nurse practitioners would have more time and would be best placed to lead implementation of the smart inhalers, but also that financial incentives would be required to support implementation. A potential role for pharmacists in patient education and implementation of the smart inhalers was also mentioned. There was an overall sense among HCPs that while they supported smart inhalers, their implementation should be led and managed by others.

I think it would be useful if the GP was collaborating with us in the care of their child’s asthma in a very proactive way. You would absolutely have to have a collaborative approach…but if they had a, a regular GP who is on board and wanted to use this style of monitoring and or, or introduce it and advocate for it if we were involved as well, very actively rather than just on the periphery, I think it would be helpful to have a collaborative approach and to work out how that might look… [A] lot of GPs don't have time for that. We, we do find it quite hard sometimes to engage with in a collaborative approach with GPs in the care of children’s asthma management…It’s not because they don't want to, it’s because it is time. So yeah, it needs to be a collaborative approach. [RN10]

So it would be helpful to have a nurse practitioner who has more time in a consultation to not only introduce the device but to follow up how they're using the device at home. That’s something that as practitioners, we don't have a lot of time to do. [PS06]

Alternatives to smart inhalers

HCPs were asked about possible alternative approaches to asthma management that might achieve the same outcomes as smart inhalers. Most HCPs agreed that there is no direct available equivalent to the smart inhalers, particularly to enable providers to know if patients are adhering to their preventer and reliever medications. Some providers suggested that more frequent appointments could assist with this, with some providers preferring good patient education and regular monitoring to smart inhaler use. HCPs also noted that some of the same asthma outcomes can be achieved using existing resources/tools. For example, HCPs suggested that they can ask patients about adherence or use available self-report measures to check adherence; check inhaler dose counters; and check dispensed scripts through the Australian Government Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme or with the pharmacy. They also noted that families can access online resources and alternative Apps for asthma; use phone alarms as reminders to take asthma medications; and families can use written diaries or charts to document medication use. However, HCPs noted that the smart inhalers are likely to be a more convenient and possibly more cost-effective approach (eg, compared with more frequent appointments).

Discussion

This is the first Australian study to qualitatively explore the perceptions of HCPs regarding the usefulness of smart inhalers for children with asthma and the factors influencing their effective integration into routine care. Overall, Australian HCPs had positive perceptions of the potential usefulness and effectiveness of smart inhalers for improving asthma management among children. However, they also identified several practical, logistical and clinical barriers that need to be addressed prior to widespread implementation.

Our findings largely align with previous research examining HCP perspectives regarding smart inhalers for both adult and paediatric asthma management. Consistent with previous findings,15 17 28 29 HCPs in the current study identified the objective medication use data captured by smart inhalers as their key benefit, enabling more informed treatment decisions and opportunities to discuss asthma management with patients and carers. Other identified benefits mirror previous research findings, including improved medication adherence due to automated reminders, time savings during consultations due to the easy availability of inhaler use data, and promoting patient motivation and independence.15 17 Uniquely, HCPs in this study emphasised the potential of smart inhalers to support children to have greater independence and autonomy in their asthma management and reduce the need for adult supervision. They also noted the potential benefits of smart inhalers for rural healthcare—highly relevant to the Australian context—where remote GP review of inhaler data could help determine whether a patient needs to travel for urgent or additional respiratory specialist medical care.

HCPs in this and previous studies expressed concerns that use of smart inhalers may make families feel guilty or judged, potentially undermining trust between patient/caregivers and HCPs.29 This risk of mistrust is well documented in both the broad digital health literature34 and for smart inhalers specifically.17 Similar to HCPs in the Netherlands and the UK, HCPs in our study felt that smart inhalers may be most suitable for more motivated or engaged patients15 17 and those with uncontrolled asthma.15 However, they also noted that families who could most benefit may also be least likely to be able to afford smart inhalers. These financial barriers and access inequities for smart technologies are well documented34 and continue to represent a significant challenge to equitable adoption and widespread use that will need to be addressed. Most countries outside of the USA do not currently have reimbursement schemes to cover the costs of smart inhalers or associated clinical care.35

Consistent with previous research, the most significant barrier to smart inhaler use in clinical practice related to workflow logistics, especially who would be responsible for managing, interpreting and discussing captured data with the patient.15 17 28 29 HCPs in the current study suggested the need for patient advocacy and support from practice managers and nurse practitioners to promote their adoption. Other barriers reported here and in previous research include potential technical issues with the inhalers15 17 28 29 and the need for smooth integration of smart inhaler data into existing electronic medical record systems.15 17 35 36 The most significant facilitator for the integration of smart inhalers into treatment and practice identified was the clinical effectiveness of smart inhalers for improving asthma outcomes. While some studies have evaluated clinical outcomes,15 17 26 few have specifically evaluated the cost-effectiveness of smart inhalers for paediatric or adult asthma management, with further research required to quantify their potential economic benefits.26 35 37 HCPs also noted the potential appeal of smart inhalers for older children, echoing previous findings29 and suggested gamification and AI for enhancing functionality and adoption of smart inhalers. Gamification and AI are increasingly recognised as promising approaches for supporting self-management of chronic diseases including asthma, where there is a strong link between daily adherence to preventative medication and acute symptoms.38 39 Interestingly, unlike in other studies,15 35 data security and privacy were not a key concern raised by HCPs in this study. HCPs in the current study also did not express medico-legal concerns related to clinical responsibility for responding to the large volume of data generated by smart inhalers.40

To enable successful integration of smart inhalers into paediatric asthma care, this study highlights that both clinical and policy-level strategies are essential. In the clinical context, clear protocols are needed to define roles and responsibilities for data review, communication and follow-up, alongside training to build confidence and familiarity among HCPs and families. Given the significant time, workload and service pressures articulated by HCPs, additional training is unlikely to be a standalone or easily achievable solution without broader system-level support. At the policy level, reimbursement models, equitable access frameworks and interoperability standards will be critical to ensure that smart inhalers can be sustainably and fairly incorporated into routine care. Collaboration between clinicians, health services, technology developers and policymakers will be vital to address implementation barriers and to ensure that smart inhaler technologies complement, rather than complicate, existing care pathways. Future research should focus on real-world evaluation of cost-effectiveness, equity of access and long-term impacts on clinical outcomes and patient engagement to untangle the potential of smart inhalers to improve asthma management and health outcomes for children across Australia.

Strengths and limitations

Almost three-quarters of the HCPs in the current study had not previously used smart inhalers, so the reported perceived benefits and limitations are based largely on hypothetical expectations about the use and functionality of the inhalers, rather than real-world experience. Despite this, many of the reported potential facilitators and barriers to smart inhaler use align with those reported by HCPs previously involved in trial implementation of smart inhalers for paediatric asthma.28 29 Future studies which include a more direct comparison between clinicians both experienced and non-experienced in the use of smart inhaler may yield additional insights into the practicalities of smart inhaler adoption. The current study was conducted with Australian HCPs, with GPs/GP registrars recruited from three Local Health Districts, and PSs from one state (NSW) of Australia, potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to other Australian health districts and states and territories, as well as beyond the Australian healthcare context. In addition, HCPs with a particular interest in the topic of smart inhalers may have been more inclined to participate in the study, so the findings may not represent the views of all HCPs working with paediatric patients with asthma.

Conclusions

HCPs are generally positive about the potential use of smart inhalers for improving medication adherence and outcomes in paediatric asthma. Perceptions about implementation raised a range of workflow/responsibility and reimbursement issues which will need to be addressed in future research prior to the widespread adoption of this technology.
