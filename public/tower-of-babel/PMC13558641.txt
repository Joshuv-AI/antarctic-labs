Multi-criteria-based determination of optimal probability distribution with four parameter estimation methods using wind speed data from ten tropical sites in the South Pacific
Authors: Ruhama Ilyas, M. Rafiuddin Ahmed, M. Golam Mostafa Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of wind energy potential is crucial for the transition to wind power. For numerous years, extensive research has been conducted to assess wind energy potential. However, determining the optimal and most reliable distribution for wind speed modeling remains challenging. Most wind resource assessments rely on the 2p-Weibull distribution; yet, it is inappropriate to use the same distribution for every location because of the varying nature of wind speed geographically and temporally. In this study, ten statistical distributions: Normal, Log-normal, 3p-Weibull, 2p-Weibull, Gamma, Rayleigh, Burr, Log-logistic, Inverse-Gaussian, and Inverse-Gamma are employed to fit the wind speed data recorded from ten sites across Pacific Island Countries (PICs). Model performance was evaluated using ten model evaluation criteria: R2, COE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, KS, CVM, AD, AIC, and BIC. Additionally, four estimation methods were employed to assess statistical distribution and model evaluation criteria: MLE, MME, QME, and MGE. Furthermore, the best estimation method is identified using the TOPSIS method. The performance of distributions was ranked on a scale of 1(best) to 3(third best); the findings reveal that the 3p-Weibull provides the best result for 42.5%, and MGE performed best for most of the studied sites, whereas the traditional 2p-Weibull, Burr, or Rayleigh were better candidates for some sites. Overall, 3p-Weibull emerges as the best-fitted distribution across the studied sites, though its performance is not universal. These findings emphasize the importance of considering multiple statistical distributions and estimation methods for accurate wind speed modeling. Additionally, wind power densities are estimated for each site using the best-fitted distribution, which showed discrepancies between the estimated and actual wind power densities, highlighting the importance of an appropriate distribution and estimation method.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-48714-w.

Introduction

In the last few decades, the world’s population has seen a significant increase, which has caused a huge rise in global energy consumption1. Scientists and researchers have been promoting cleaner and renewable energy resources, which will help meet the energy demands, promote industrial development, limit the use of fossil fuels, and arrest the pollution crisis2. British Petroleum Report (2021) stated that fossil fuels, renewable energy sources, and nuclear energy contributed 83%, 12.6%, and 6.3%, respectively, of the total energy consumption in 20203. The extensive use of fossil fuels results in the emission of harmful gases, which cause climate change2,4. Climate change is a global issue, especially in warmer regions such as the Pacific Island Countries (PICs), because they are close to the equator, surrounded by the sea, and many of them have low-lying atolls. Some islands in PICs are already experiencing severe climate change impacts on communities, infrastructure, water supply, and fisheries, etc.5. Lots of research work is being carried out to deal with the climate issues and to assess renewable energy resources6.

To fulfill their energy needs, PICs heavily rely on imported fossil fuels due to the lack of domestic sources of fossil fuels. It causes an economic burden, and still, PICs are among the countries with the lowest access to electricity5,7. In the last few years, numerous studies have been carried out that support the transition to renewable energy, particularly for the estimation of wind energy potential to generate electricity in PICs, including Tuvalu, Kiribati, Vanuatu, and Fiji2,4,8,9. Wind energy generation will help reduce fossil fuel imports of PICs in the future. The majority of PICs intend to generate most of their electricity from renewable sources by 2020–20302. Energy Fiji Limited (EFL) targets to generate 90% renewable energy by 2025 and 99% by 203010. The Vanuatu Government aims to achieve 100% renewable energy generation by 203011.

Wind and solar energy are highly promising renewable energy resources, particularly for nations that do not produce oil12. Additionally, accurate modeling of wind speed is pivotal in determining the selection of suitable sites for wind turbine installations by estimating the amount of wind energy that can be extracted per year9,12.

Historical measurements of wind speed and direction are very helpful in determining the patterns and variability in wind conditions. Wind speed distribution is described by the use of probability density functions such as Weibull, Rayleigh, Gamma, Log-normal, Exponential, and Gaussian distributions, out of which Weibull is the most commonly used2. Although wind energy is a highly used renewable energy resource, its major disadvantage is the variation in its energy density. The present research focuses on fitting an appropriate distribution to estimate the accurate wind energy potential at several sites in the tropical region, which will play a key role in the selection of sites and will be helpful for engineers to design appropriate turbine blades as well as wind turbines. Further, this research will also be advantageous for evaluating the correct wind power density through the comparison of wind speed distributions.

Background

The use of fossil fuels leads to environmental pollution as well as economic issues in society. It releases harmful gases into the atmosphere, resulting in the depletion of the ozone layer and global warming. Due to global warming, polar ice caps are shrinking, which is causing sea-level rise2,4. These gases can cause extreme weather events like cyclones, and also threaten human life13, the marine ecosystem, and natural habitats that support tourism and adversely affect the earnings14. The utilization of renewable energy resources will be sustainable, less harmful to the environment, and will help in minimizing climate change and its consequences15.

In recent years, many countries are transitioning to renewable energy resources such as hydro, wind, solar, and tidal energy. Wind energy is one of the world’s fastest-growing sources of energy8. Therefore, there has been a considerable rise in wind power plants. Wind turbine design has improved a lot with the use of advanced technologies. Now, engineers are producing stronger, lighter, and more efficient blades for the turbines, which are helping to increase energy output and decrease noise16.

The use of wind energy has led to many socio-economic and environmental benefits, such as a reduction in harmful GHG (Greenhouse Gas) emissions and increased employment opportunities. It also reduces the risk to human health and the environmental crisis17,18. One of the central objectives of energy planning policies of several countries is to increase the proportion of renewable energy in the electricity generation mix18. Wind power technology has become commercialized and is economically competitive with traditional energy sources. Wind turbines are easy to install and require a shorter time compared to other power plants. Renewable energy sources around the world are increasingly playing a prominent role in general, and wind in particular, in meeting electricity demands19. Energy technology has developed a lot in recent years, but the installation cost and turbine design still need improvement20. Wind energy is one of the highly promising resources of electricity generation, particularly for nations that are dependent on imported fossil fuels21. Wind energy is a major source of energy that is produced through the conversion of kinetic energy into mechanical energy, which is then converted to electrical energy22. The optimal design of renewable power plants involves many factors, including the assessment of annual power availability. Accurate modeling of wind speed is vital for determining the amount of annual wind energy production12.

PICs are still strongly dependent on imported fossil fuels, which poses an economic burden7. Petroleum is used for transportation and electricity needs in the majority of PICs23. PICs have plenty of renewable energy resources, which can help a quick transition with proper planning and implementation.

Wind resource assessments and wind turbine installation have been carried out in the South Pacific region in the past by researchers. In Fiji, a 10 MW wind farm was commissioned in Butoni in 2007 by the Fiji Electricity Authority (FEA)24. Further, research is also conducted in Kadavu Island and the Suva Peninsula to assess the wind resources25. PICs possess abundant renewable energy resources, especially the larger mountainous islands of Melanesia, as well as Fiji and Mt. Le pu’e, which are rich in opportunities for hydro, geothermal, and biomass energy26–28. The PICs near the equator, such as Fiji, Solomon Islands, Kiribati, Vanuatu, Tonga, Mt. Le pu’e, and Niue, experience a summer-like season round the year with small temperature fluctuations and atmospheric pressure changes29. A major barrier to the development of renewable energy in PICs is the absence of trustworthy energy data8.

Wind speed distribution is described by fitting of probability density functions, and the most widely used distribution found in the literature is 2p-Weibull30,31, whereas there is limited practical application of other statistical distributions such as Normal, Gamma, Chi-square, 3p-Weibull, Exponential, Pareto, Burr, Log-logistic, Inverse-Gaussian, Inverse-Gamma, and the Rayleigh distributions32–34.

Researchers in Rwanda studied five distribution functions: Weibull, Rayleigh, Gamma, Log-normal, Exponential, and Gaussian distributions by a graphical comparison with the empirical distributions and evaluated the performance of selected distributions using root mean square error (RMSE) and mean biased error (MBE). The Weibull distribution was identified to be the best distribution and most extensively suggested tool to assess the potential of wind energy in recent years, representing the wind data35. Chiodo and Lauria36 discussed the characteristics of the popular Weibull distribution widely adopted in the field of wind-speed statistics and compared it with the less popular model, the Log-logistic one. This latter model appears to be another suitable candidate for wind speed data modeling. Similarly, Drobinski et al.37 stated that the 2p-Weibull distribution is a powerful approach for wind speed modeling, but it has limitations due to its empirical basis. A study comparing the 2p-Weibull distribution with two other distributions, elliptical and non-Gaussian two-parameter probability density functions for wind speed, was conducted at 89 locations throughout France. The results showed that in complex regions, close to the mountains in southern and eastern France, the elliptical or the non-Gaussian distribution can be better suited than the Weibull to represent the wind statistics. Weibull distribution is more valid in areas with certain wind characteristics, such as fluctuations and anisotropies. Additionally, Zaharim et al.38 also suggested that the 2p-Weibull distribution might be the probability distribution that can fit the data well.

However, some studies39,40 highlighted the concerns about relying on the 2p-Weibull distribution for wind power estimation, focusing on the need to develop techniques to investigate various probability distributions to determine the best fit. This lowers the possibility of inaccuracy and guarantees a more accurate depiction of the wind data, which can better capture the variability and complexity of wind, leading to accurate assessments of energy potential41. Pobocikova et al.42 considered four different probability distributions: 2p-Weibull, 3p-Weibull, 2p-Gamma, and 2p-Lognormal. For parameter estimation, the MLE was used, and model evaluation was done based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the coefficient of determination R2, and the root mean square error (RMSE). Results showed that the 3p-Weibull performs the best, and the 2p-Weibull distribution performs the second best. Wais40 also suggested that 3p-Weibull might be an optimal option for wind speed modeling for high percentages of low wind speeds and may give better results. Further, some investigators conducted research to compare the 2p-Weibull and 3p-Weibull distributions and found that 3p-Weibull performed best for lower wind speeds with greater frequency5,43. Present researchers prefer to assess multiple distributions to find the best-fitted distribution44–46.

In the present work, ten statistical distributions are considered to identify the most suitable distribution for modeling wind speed data from ten sites across PICs ranging from 1° N to 18° S of the Equator. Ten statistical distributions: Normal, Log-normal, 3p-Weibull, 2p-Weibull, Gamma, Rayleigh, Burr, Log-logistic, Inverse-Gaussian, and Inverse-Gamma were assessed alongside four estimation methods, including Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), Matching Moment Estimation (MME), Quantile Matching Estimation (QME), and Maximum Goodness of Fit Estimation (MGE). These distributions and estimation methods are assessed based on ten model evaluation criteria: R2, AIC, BIC, RMSE, KS, the coefficient of efficiency (COE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), Cramer-Von Mises (CVM), and Anderson–Darling (AD). The best estimation method is identified using the TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) method. The wind power densities are then estimated for each site using the best-fitted distribution, and the results are compared with the actual wind power densities.

Details of data collection sites and wind speed statistics.

Abaiang is a coral atoll of Kiribati in the Northern Gilbert Islands and consists mostly of flat terrain. Tarawa is the capital atoll of Kiribati with a population of over 50,000 people. The topography of both atolls is mostly flat terrain. Predominant wind direction is east-northeast, with maximum wind speeds of 19 m/s and 18.8 m/s, respectively. The heaviest rainfall generally occurs between March and May, while August and October are the driest, with minimum and maximum 10-min average annual temperatures of 28 and 28.3 °C respectively6. The measurement period of wind speed data for both sites was from 2012 to 2013.

Raviravi and Sanasana are located in the Western part of Viti Levu, which has a dense population. Both sites have wet and dry seasons, with an average temperature in Raviravi generally ranging from 22 to 31 °C, and this region receives less rainfall than Eastern Viti Levu. The direction of the wind at both sites is affected by trade winds, which come from the southeast. Suva is the capital city of Fiji, located on the southeastern coast of Viti Levu. The region is surrounded by mountains and has a tropical rainforest climate because it rains throughout the year, with more rain from November to April47. In Suva, winds blow from the southeast or east, and temperatures range between 21 and 30 °C.

Waisa is located on the island of Vanua Levu, which is the second-largest island of Fiji. It is surrounded by hilly terrain and experiences warm temperatures the whole year, ranging between 22 and 30 °C. Like other South Pacific regions, Waisa also has northeast predominant wind, with higher wind speeds of around 6 m/s. These wind patterns provide relatively consistent conditions. Mt. Le pu’e is an island nation in the South Pacific, which is surrounded by volcanic mountains and coastal plains. It has an average temperature between 24 and 31 °C. Wind direction is mostly from the east to east-northeast and is affected by seasonal trade winds.

Data collection method

For wind data collection, measurements were performed using towers of two different heights, 34 m and 50 m; for sites S5 and S7, data were collected using towers of 50 m height above ground level (a.g.l.), and for the remaining sites, using 34 m tall towers. Data is collected using NRG system (NRG) meteorological towers installed as IRERASs (Integrated Renewable Energy Resource Assessment Systems); these towers are used globally for wind measurements. NRG SymphoniePlus3, a data logger, was used in the present work, which was connected to seven different sensors installed on the tower. These sensors measure the wind speed, temperature, pressure, rainfall, solar insolation, humidity, and wind direction. The wind speed measurements were recorded at 10-min consecutive intervals on the SD card through these data loggers, which are good for detailed analysis and help track changes in wind behavior over time. The comprehensive data sets were sent using a GSM-based network to a data bank located at the Japan-Pacific ICT Center of the University of the South Pacific, Laucala Campus, Suva, Fiji. The anemometers (serial numbers 179500189054-57, 179500189089-90) have an accuracy of 0.1 m/s and a range of 0.4–96 m/s5. Statistical distribution

The wind is fluctuating in nature, and this has a direct impact on the potential for energy generation46. Therefore, to evaluate the wind energy potential, assessing the wind speed probability distribution is pivotal. Probability distributions are essential for capturing the statistical properties of wind speed data, such as variability, central tendency, and skewness. In this section, ten distributions are used for the ten sites, and their results are evaluated to determine the most appropriate probability distribution.

Estimation methods

As discussed, appropriate parameter estimation method selection is crucial for assessing the suitability of a site for turbine installation58. In this section, four estimation methods have been discussed to determine the most suitable distribution fitted for wind speed data at a site, which include MLE, QME, MME, and MGE.

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

By maximizing a likelihood function, MLE estimation determines if the observed data is most likely to occur under the assumed statistical model. The maximum likelihood estimate is the point in the parameter space that maximizes the likelihood function21. MLE involves numerical iteration to estimate parameters59.

If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{1} ,U_{2} ,...,U_{n}$$\end{document} are the values of wind speeds and f(U;θ ) be any general pdf with θ represent parameters of the distribution, the likelihood function to be maximized is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\theta |U_{1} ,...,U_{n} ) = \prod\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {f(U_{i} ;\theta )}$$\end{document}. By taking the natural logarithm, the log-likelihood becomes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ln L(\theta ) = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {\ln f(U_{i} ;\theta )}$$\end{document}. In order to maximize the function, partial derivatives are set to zero:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial \ln L(\theta )/\partial \theta_{j} = 0,\;{\mathrm{for}}\;{\mathrm{each}}\;\theta_{j} \in \theta$$\end{document}

The MLE of the parameters θ can be obtained by simultaneously solving Eq. (15). If the equation for estimating parameters θ does not have a closed-form of solution, then it can be solved using the Newton–Raphson method or a numerical procedure59.

MME estimates parameters by matching the theoretical moments (such as mean, variance, skewness, etc.) with sample moments calculated from the observed data. Maximum goodness-of-fit estimation (MGE)

MGE is used to determine the parameters by minimizing the distance between the empirical data distribution and the theoretical model distribution, which allows the model to fit the observed data as described below63,64.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{n} \left( U \right)$$\end{document} denote the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) based on a sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{1} ,U_{2} ,...,U_{n}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F\left( {U;\theta } \right)$$\end{document} denote the theoretical CDF parametrized by θ. Then, MGE seeks the parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\theta }$$\end{document} that minimizes a distance measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left( {F_{n} ,F_{\theta } } \right)$$\end{document}, such that:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\theta } = \arg \mathop {\min }\limits_{\theta } D\left( {F_{n} \left( U \right),F\left( {U;\theta } \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

When fitting continuous distributions, three common goodness-of-fit statistics for the distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left( {F_{n} ,F_{\theta } } \right)$$\end{document} considered are: KS, CVM, and AD.

Model evaluation criteria

The performance of different statistical distributions and estimation methods is evaluated by utilizing several model evaluation criteria. These criteria help to decide which model is more effective for wind speed data representation2. The choice of the model evaluation criteria can influence the outcomes, which is pivotal in a comprehensive evaluation. In this work, ten different model evaluation criteria have been employed to evaluate the performance of the estimation methods.

Coefficient of determination (R2)

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document} is computed by Eq. RMSE ensures the model’s accuracy through item-by-item comparison between the predicted and experimental values. A lower value of RMSE ensures a good fit; it is calculated as follows2,65,66:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{RMSE}} = \sqrt {\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{n} {\left( {U_{i} - \widehat{U}_{i} } \right)}^{2} }$$\end{document}

AD test compares the empirical and theoretical cumulative probability distributions. Results and discussion

Comparison of the statistical distributions of wind speed

The fitting of an appropriate distribution and the parameter estimation method for wind speed may vary according to different locations46,78. In this study, ten statistical distributions were fitted to wind speed data from ten sites to determine the best distribution for each site using four estimation methods: MLE, MME, QME, and MGE. A study was also carried out to determine the best estimation method by using the multicriteria optimization technique, namely TOPSIS. It is a decision-making process that enables us to rank and prioritize multiple alternatives based on various criteria. All computations were performed using the ‘R’ statistical software.

As multiple distributions are evaluated based on several estimation methods and model evaluation criteria, this may produce different rankings across individual measures. To maintain consistency, a two-step approach was applied. The preliminary 1–10 (used in descending order) score-based evaluation was used to discriminate among shortlisted distributions across several model evaluation criteria while the subsequent 1–3 ranking was applied to identify the best-fitted distributions.

The best distribution of wind data fitted for each site was evaluated using ten model evaluation criteria: R2, COE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, KS, AD, AIC, BIC, and CVM statistics. First, to shortlist the best-fitted distribution, a score-based evaluation was applied by assigning a score of 1 to 10 based on its performance across various model evaluation criteria. Descending order was adopted to assign the weights; the best performing distribution was assigned the highest score (10) for the given criterion, and the lowest score (1), indicating the worst performing. The lower error values of RMSE, MAE, MAPE, KS, CVM, AD, BIC, and AIC indicate better performance. For R2, a higher value shows better results, while closer to one is considered better for COE6,79. Finally, a total score was obtained for each distribution by summing the assigned weights, which was then averaged to obtain a total performance score for each distribution. The distributions were then ranked on the basis of final score. The highest average value indicates a best-fitted distribution, while a lower value indicates a poor-performing distribution.

Second, to identify the best-fitted distribution across each site, a preliminary weighted ranking (1–3) approach was applied, where Rank 1 represents the best fit, Rank 2 the second best, and Rank 3 the third best. The purpose of this approach is to determine the top-performing distributions across each site, as shown in Table 4. To illustrate this two-step process, an example of ranking the fitted distributions for Site S1 with the MGE results is presented in Tables A1–A4 in Appendix A . Further, to improve clarity, a frequency table (Table 5) is included, presenting the best-performing distribution under each estimation method across all sites.

For all the sites, the parameters for each distribution were obtained using the four estimation methods: MLE, MME, QME, and MGE. For illustration purposes, this section presents the results of the distributions fitted for Site S1. Figures 2, 3, 4 and 5 present histograms, which provide a comprehensive visual representation of the wind speed data for the site alongside the density and curves of all the fitted distributions using the MLE, MME, QME, and MGE methods. Figure 2 displays the results of the MLE method, indicating that 3p-Weibull achieved the best-fitted distribution, followed by 2p-Weibull and Burr distributions. The 3p-Weibull distribution excelled in capturing better peak and tail behavior, which is crucial for accurate wind speed modeling. Figure 3 depicts the results of the MME method. Here, the Burr distribution emerged as the best-fitted distribution, demonstrating superior alignment with all regions of the distribution curve of wind speed data. The 2p-Weibull and 3p-Weibull ranked second and third, respectively. Figure 4 highlights the result of QME; in this case, the 3p-Weibull distribution was also identified as the best fit, closely modeling the wind speed data’s peak and middle sections. Across all the sites, the 3p-Weibull distribution consistently outperforms others, particularly under MLE and MGE estimation methods, which makes it robust for wind speed modeling for the studied sites, as highlighted in bold in Table 5. This dominant performance suggested the additional flexibility of 3p-Weuibull for wind speed modeling across the studied sites. In contrast, 2p-Weibull performed better under MME and QME.

This variation emphasized that there is no universally optimal distribution across all estimation techniques, and the choice of method can substantially influence the outcome of distribution analysis. Practitioners may therefore consider multiple estimation methods and assess model evaluation criteria comprehensively to ensure robust distributional modeling.

Comparison of the best estimation method

As discussed in the previous Section “Comparison of the statistical distributions of wind speed”, sometimes different estimation methods identify different distributions as the best for the same site. Therefore, it is challenging to determine the best distribution that fits the wind data of a site, as well as to determine the best estimation method to ensure an accurate selection of an appropriate distribution for the wind speed modeling.

In this section, we use the TOPSIS method discussed in Section “Technique for order of preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS)”. to determine the most effective estimation method for modeling. To apply the TOPSIS method, we assigned different weights to the model evaluation criteria to reflect their relative importance, as shown in Table 6. To ensure the transparent and consistent selection of the best-fitted distribution and estimation method for each site, a well-structured three step framework was adopted.

Step 1 Ranking of distribution under each estimation method: For each site, all candidate distributions were evaluated under each estimation method employed. As explained in Section “Comparison of the statistical distributions of wind speed”, distributions were ranked by assigning score based on the performance of model evaluation criteria under each estimation method.

Step 2 Ranking of each estimation method: The estimation methods are then ranked using model evaluation criteria of the best-fitted distribution under each estimation method found in Step 1, by employing a multi-criteria decision-making approach (TOPSIS), as discussed in Section “Comparison of the best estimation method”.

Step 3 Final selection of the best distribution: Finally, for each site, the best distribution was selected by considering the estimation method identified as optimal in step 2. Then, the candidate distributions that received the 1st rank in Step 1 were chosen as the final best distribution for each site.

A comparison was made for model selection using several model evaluation criteria, such as R2, RMSE, COE, and other statistical measures. Conclusion

Realistic wind speed modeling is dependent on proper statistical distribution and estimation techniques. A comprehensive evaluation of ten distributions and four estimation methods to describe their suitability and capability of assessing wind energy potential across ten different locations in PICs: Abaiang, Anabar, Funafuti, Pentecost, Raviravi, Mt. Le pu’e, Sanasana, Suva, Tarawa, and Waisa, was carried out. For this purpose, wind speed data at 34 m a.g.l. or 50 m a.g.l. were considered using Normal, Log-normal, 3p-Weibull, 2p-Weibull, Gamma, Rayleigh, Burr, Log-logistic, Inverse-Gaussian, and Inverse-Gamma distributions. The selected estimation methods were MLE, MME, QME, and MGE. Initially, a detailed evaluation was done to assess the best-fitted distribution at each site based on ten model evaluation criteria: R2, COE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, KS, CVM, AD, AIC, and BIC. Further, using a comprehensive TOPSIS framework, the best estimation method was identified for each site. These findings indicate that no single distribution or estimation method characterizes wind speed data perfectly for all sites; instead, different methods performed best for different sites. In this way, for each site, the most suitable distribution and estimated method were identified; the 3p-Weibull distribution with 42.5% emerged as the most suitable distribution at seven sites, with less skewness with wind speed data. The normal distribution is identified as the best-fit for two sites, while the Burr distribution turned out to be the best for only one site. Among the estimation techniques, MGE proved to be the most reliable estimation method for seven sites, while for the remaining sites, MLE emerged as the most reliable. Additionally, 2p-Weibull, Rayleigh, and Gamma also showed better results as compared to other distributions. Finally, the results suggested the importance of considering multiple distribution and estimation methods for estimating wind energy potential at a given site. Further, this research also provided another key aspect related to wind energy estimation, i.e., wind power density. A brief wind power density analysis based on the best-fitted distribution and estimation method demonstrated that there exists a minor discrepancy between the estimated and actual density. This study provides an extensive initial assessment of wind speed modeling.

Additionally, future research can focus on evaluating annual energy production by selecting the optimal turbines based on site-specific wind factors through the calculation of capacity factors, evaluating economic viability, and analyzing turbine performance in various wind conditions. To optimize energy production and grid stability, additional research can look into hybrid renewable energy systems and the effects of climate variability on the wind resource availability.

One limitation of this study is that the wind speed data were collected for a duration of one year at some of the sites. It would be good to acquire data for a longer duration of time, typically three years or more, to obtain more reliable results. These results can also be extended to sites that are closer to the ten sites investigated in this work.
