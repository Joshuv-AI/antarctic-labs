FIFA World Cup 2026 ready, infection steady: A call for coordinated prevention
Authors: Om Prakash Choudhary, Priyanka Choudhary
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The 2026 FIFA World Cup will draw nearly 6 million visitors from around the world to 16 vibrant cities across the United States, Canada, and Mexico, making it the most expansive tournament yet with 48 teams. This unprecedented convergence of global fans transforms the host cities into a crossroads of cultures—and a potential hotspot for infectious diseases. The rallying call, “World Cup 2026 Ready, Infection Steady,” captures the urgent need for health systems to stay sharp and act swiftly to prevent outbreaks. Without a united front, public health could be jeopardized, the games disrupted, and fans, athletes, staff, and communities put at risk.

Mass gatherings is defined as any organized or spontaneous occasion that attracts sufficient number of people to strain the planning and response sources of the city or nation hosting the event. From a public health perspective, several major concerns arise during such events, including the risk of participants spreading the infectious diseases worldwide. The concentration of people can significantly impact local health systems, potentially overwhelming healthcare capacity and straining resources normally available for routine care. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that hosts of international and large mass gatherings improve public health preparedness to prevent and mitigate the impact of potential public health emergencies with international concerns, such as Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) and similar infectious diseases [1][2].

The risks of infectious disease swirling around the 2026 World Cup are both intricate and far-reaching. Stadiums pulsing with energy, jam-packed transit lines, bustling hotels, and lively nightlife all become breeding grounds for respiratory illnesses like influenza, measles, and emerging viruses. As fans stream across borders, rare diseases can travel with them, raising the stakes for host countries. Sporting events have a history of sparking outbreaks—respiratory, gastrointestinal, vector-borne, and vaccine-preventable diseases alike. With this World Cup spanning three nations and diverse health systems, only seamless international collaboration can safeguard everyone involved.

Getting ready for the World Cup demands bold, cross-disciplinary teamwork. Activating the Incident Command System a month before kickoff unites emergency responders, hospitals, public health teams, security, and venue organizers in a coordinated effort. Medical advisory groups made up of clinical leaders and public health experts drive nimble, informed planning for any crisis. At the heart of preparedness is the Emergency Mass Critical Care framework, enabling health systems to swiftly scale up ICU-level care—deploying ventilators, medications, and skilled staff—even in unexpected spaces like postanesthesia units or telemetry wards for up to 10 days.

Vigilant public health surveillance stands as the first line of defense against outbreaks at mass gatherings. By weaving together surveillance at airports, stadiums, and transit hubs, authorities can detect infectious threats early and move quickly to contain them. These systems must cast a wide net, tracking everything from measles to gastrointestinal and vector-borne diseases. Expanding syndromic surveillance and keeping participants in the loop about health events keeps everyone alert. Gathering real-time data on Emergency Medical Services (EMS) activity, Intensive Care Unit (ICU) occupancy, and infection rates is vital for rapid response and for learning valuable lessons after the event.

On game days, medical teams must be primed for anything—from scraped knees to critical emergencies. With structured triage systems like Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment, teams can swiftly sort patients, deliver lifesaving care, and coordinate safe hospital transfers. Doctors, Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs), and nurses working shoulder to shoulder within the ICS framework guarantee a rapid response when every second counts. Venue clinics need to be ready for infectious disease cases, equipped with isolation rooms, respiratory protection, plenty of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), and fit-tested respirators. Training health workers to spot, report, and contain infections quickly is essential.

Infection prevention and control acts as a shield, guarding patients and health workers from preventable harm. Simple yet powerful habits—source control, timely masking, and thorough hand hygiene—are crucial in crowded arenas. Public health leaders should seize this global stage to teach fans about hand and respiratory hygiene through FIFA and federation websites. The WHO's "SAVE LIVES: Clean Your Hands" campaign highlights the life-saving power of handwashing, especially after COVID-19. When infection control falters, the results can be deadly, making strong measures non-negotiable for safe care.[3]

Antimicrobial resistance is a looming threat for World Cup planners. When infection control falters, resistant germs can spread quickly. Hospitals must champion antibiotic stewardship and use antimicrobials wisely throughout the tournament. With the World Cup stretching acrossthethree countries, unified infection control standards are a must. Strong Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) programs are especially crucial where healthcare and hygiene could be challenged by secondary infections.

Before the World Cup whistle blows, multiagency training is essential. Scenario-based drills that mimic FIFA event conditions let health systems stress-test their readiness and sharpen their plans. Everyone—from healthcare workers and EMS to venue staff and public health officials—needs hands-on practice in infection control, triage, and emergency response. Behind the scenes, regional partners are already staging outbreak simulations and ramping up preparedness for special pathogens. This training must be ongoing, strengthened by regular drills. Effective planning, clear health communication, interagency coordination, and accessible biosafety resources are essential to prevent the escalation of epidemics in densely populated areas. Travelers should seek medical evaluation and inform healthcare providers of their travel destinations and dates. The Latin American Society of Travel Medicine (SLAMVI) has released a set of essential recommendations for international travelers attending the 2026 FIFA World Cup. These guidelines, accompanied by an infographic to assist with travel preparations, are available on the SLAMVI website (https://www.slamvi.org) (Fig. 1).[1,4,5]

Finally, collecting actionable data is the key for post-event debriefs and building a legacy of stronger preparedness. Structured reviews led by critical care teams help refine Emergency Medicine and Critical Care (EMCC) triggers, triage protocols, infrastructure improvements, and training programs. Rolling out recovery plans for critical systems and staff, tracking costs and lessons learned, creating After-Action Reports, and launching staff well-being initiatives ensure that every investment in preparedness pays off long-term. As the 2026 World Cup approaches, North American health systems should embrace evidence-based, internationally proven protocols: set up ICS-based medical advisory groups, embed intensivist-led EMCC plans, invest in surveillance infrastructure, and prioritize multiagency training.
