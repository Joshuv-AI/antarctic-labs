Abstract
High temperature reactor materials will experience a combination of mechanical degradation
caused by creep and fatigue and environmental degradation caused by neutron irradiation and, in
some cases, exposure to corrosive coolants. A materials surveillance program is one option for
ensuring the safe, reliable operation of key reactor components under these conditions. Such a
program would monitor the degradation in key material properties over time, using this data to
predict how changes in the material properties affect components performance. This could then
be used to make plant operational decisions and support eventual plant license extensions. Past
work describes such a material surveillance program based on passively actuated mechanical test
articles that impose creep-fatigue type loading on test materials driven only by changes in temper-
ature, for example those experienced by components under standard operating cycles. This report
focuses on two aspects of the proposed material surveillance program: how to design the test arti-
cles to mimic the mechanical response of the corresponding component and how to use ex-situ test
data to determine the amount of damaged experienced by the test article (and hence the corre-
sponding component) in service. Specifically, this report derives and validated simplified methods
for both tasks, replacing earlier, more complicated approaches based on simulating the test article
response and finding the best specimen design or current damage via complex numerical optimi-
zation. The simplified methods for each task developed here can be implemented in spreadsheet
software and are simple enough for practical use in future operating plants. This report derives
the methods (one for sizing and two options for damage inference), verifies the simplified ap-
proaches versus more sophisticated methods, and compares the results of applying the simplified
methods to previous results using the complex numerical optimization approach. The report also
provides a worked sampled problem applying the simplified techniques to a realistic high temper-
ature reactor component.

1 Introduction
Materials in future advanced nuclear reactors will experience a combination of high temperature
mechanical loads along with exposure to the reactor environment. The environment, including
neutron irradiation and potentially corrosive coolants, will affect the mechanical strength of the
reactor materials and hence the safety and reliability of the reactor. While some data exists, par-
ticularly on the effect of neutron irradiation on the mechanical properties of likely reactor materi-
als, detailed studies combining the effects of temperature, stress, radiation, and the reactor coolant
are extremely uncommon [1] [2]. This lack of data, combined with the comparative lack of oper-
ational experience for advanced reactors in the United States, introduces considerable uncertainty
in the long-term reliability of future high temperature reactors.
There is, at least was historically, an analogous challenge for Light Water Reactors (LWRs). From
nearly the beginning of nuclear energy engineers knew that neutron irradiation could reduce the
toughness of the steels used in the construction of reactor pressure vessels and other components.
However, like today with advanced reactors, there were no long-term, combined-effects experi-
ments to provide data to bound the degradation the reactor materials would see in service, partic-
ularly for reactors with long service lives. The solution was a materials surveillance program. The
ASTM E185 [3] standard summarizes the history of the approach along with providing the stand-
ard for the modern implementation. Essentially the idea is to expose mechanical test articles to
the key features of the reactor environment, specifically the neutron flux through the reactor vessel
beltline, periodically remove samples, perform mechanical tests to determine how the properties
of the material have changed, and assess the reactor vessel design against the current properties of
the steel, accounting for embrittlement. Originally the surveillance program was broad, including
multiple types of mechanical tests articles and a variety of tests assessing the degradation of a
range of mechanical properties. As operators gained experience with the design the modern pro-
gram focuses on the toughness of the reactor vessel steel as measured with miniaturized Charpy
impact specimens. A surveillance program of this type for LWRs is codified into NRC require-
ments in Appendix H to 10 CFR Part 50 [4].

A material surveillance program could be a solution for high temperature advanced reactors. How-
ever, high temperature reactors face an additional challenge not experienced by LWRs. In the
LWR fleet the assumption is that design requires a constant value of the key material property and
the reactor environment degrades the actual material property over time (see Figure 1.1). For
example, in the PWR fleet the design requires a certain toughness for the beltline RPV steel and
radiation embrittlement diminishes the actual toughness over time. The acceptance requirement
is then to measure the current toughness of the steel, using the surveillance articles, and compare
that measured value against the fixed requirement.
By contrast, in advanced reactors, even without exposure to the reactor environment the strength
of the component material is constantly diminishing due to creep and creep-fatigue degradation in
the sample due to only stress and temperature effects (again, see Figure 1.1). This has two conse-
quences for a surveillance program:
 1. The acceptance criteria, i.e. the target material strength, changes over time.
 2. The surveillance articles must induce representative stress (or cyclic strain) and tempera-
 ture, going beyond static exposure to the reactor environment.
Past work by Argonne National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory addresses both these
challenges. This work developed a complete material surveillance program for high temperature
reactors (Figure 1.2) based on passively actuated mechanical test articles (Figure 1.3).
The passively actuated test article drives periodic mechanical strain and stress in the test section
from differential thermal expansion between the test article and pillar materials and the driver.
With this specimen design the driver material must have a lower coefficient of thermal expansion
compared to the test and pillar materials and should ideally also have higher high temperature
material strength. INL has developed both welded and interlocking (not shown) variants of the
test article, both working on the same principle. The key challenge in successfully fabricating this
specimen is the strength of the bimaterial welds connecting the test article to the driver and the
driver to the pillars. For additional details on the test article design and the testing being done to
ensure the long-term reliability of the articles see recent INL technical reports [5] [6].
The complete surveillance procedure includes four basic steps:
 1. Sizing the test article so that it mimics the mechanical loads at the critical point in the
 component when exposed to the temperature cycle the test article will experience in the
 operating reactor.
 2. Inserting a number of test articles into the reactor so that they experience bounding tem-
 perature and environmental effects. For example, they might be located in contact with the
 reactor coolant at a location that experiences the highest temperatures and neutron fluxes
 experienced by the component under surveillance.
 3. Periodically removing one or more samples and performing out-of-reactor mechanical
 property testing and characterization.
 4. Using the results of that testing and characterization to determine if the reactor materials
 will continue to fulfill their design function over the next surveillance period, i.e. until the
 next set of specimens are removed from the reactor.

An ANL technical report provides a complete description of the surveillance program [7], includ-
ing a full description of three options for how to execute the crucial step of determining if the
reactor materials remain adequate over the next inspection intervals. A recently published tech-
nical article provides a higher-level summary of the different approaches [8].
This report focuses on two specific aspects of this general four-part strategy: the techniques for
sizing the test articles to mimic the structural response of the corresponding component and the
methods for inferring the amount of degradation the sample experienced in the reactor based on
the out-of-reactor testing. The past work provides methods for both components of the surveil-
lance program. However, these existing methods suffer similar problems, notably:
 1. They require sophisticated material constitutive models describing the deformation and, in
 some cases, damage accumulation in the test article materials. These sophisticated models
 may not exist for many materials, and the complex model description of the material be-
 havior makes implementing new models for other materials difficult.
 2. The existing sizing and damage inference methods apply a complicated numerical optimi-
 zation technique to size the specimen and infer the amount of material degradation. This
 approach can be highly accurate but is difficult to implement.
The goal of the work described in this report is to provide simplified methods for specimen sizing
and damage/degradation inference. The goal in both cases to provides techniques that could be
apply by operating engineers at future plants and do not require complex descriptions of the spec-
imen material behavior. Our goal is to provide methods that could be implemented in spreadsheet
software and used in day-to-day operation of future reactors.
Chapter 2 of this report covers specimen sizing and Chapter 3 covers damage inference. Both
chapters are laid out similarly, with the first section briefly summarizing the current method with

references to past work and then subsequent sections describing the new, simplified approach.
Chapter 3 describes two potential methods to infer creep damage in the test articles based on out

describes independent verification of the simplified methods versus more sophisticated numerical
simulations and provides an example of applying the techniques to a realistic component design.
Chapter 5 summarizes the advantages and disadvantages of the new methods and discusses future
work on high temperature reactor surveillance.

2 Simplified specimen sizing tool
2.1 Previous approach
An ANL technical report describes the previous approach to specimen sizing [8]. The method
simulates the response of the test article to the imposed temperature cycle, using complex but
accurate constitutive models to determine the cyclic response of the test section. The approach
uses this “forward model” to numerical optimize the specimen geometry, expressed as the length
and cross-sectional area of the three bars in the three bar model (see Figure 1.3) so that the simu-
lated stress-strain hysteresis loop of the test article best matches the target stress-strain response of
the component. That report contains methods for determining the target stress-strain response of
the component from an ASME Boiler & Pressure Vessel Code Section III, Division 5 design anal-
ysis – these methods can be applied to the new, simplified approach described here without mod-
ification.
The problems with this method are that it requires sophisticated constitutive models to describe
the material response of the test and driver materials and that implementing the numerical optimi-
zation to size the specimen requires a complex computer program. The advantages are that the
method can be highly accurate and that it attempts to match the complete stress-strain hysteresis
experienced by the component. This chapter develops a simplified approach, still based on the
three-bar model, which does not require complicated constitutive models and can be implemented
in a computer spreadsheet.

3 Simplified damage inference
3.1 Previous approach
Two technical reports [8] [13] describe the previous method developed for damage inference. The
approach is again based on the three-bar model and numerical optimization. The damage inference
method assumes that creep-fatigue and environmental effects have degraded the response of the
test section via a continuum damage mechanics model. The input to the approach is results from
out of reactor thermal cycling tests on the sample, removed from the reactor, measuring the strain
versus time response of the test section. The optimization problem tries to match this measured
strain versus time history under a known thermal cycle by finding the amount of damage in the
test section (expressed as the continuum damage parameter in a model) that causes the simulated
and measured strain histories to best match. There are two variants of this method: one that as-
sumes a specific continuum damage mechanics model for how the test section will degrade over
time and another that makes no assumptions on the form of the damage model. The first method
can better determine material damage early in the damage process, where the change in mechanical
properties is relatively limited, but requires additional information on the reactor material behav-
ior. The latter method is less sensitive to small amounts of material degradation but does not
require a material specific damage model.
Both methods require sophisticated constitutive models for how the test and driver materials de-
form at elevated temperatures. The first variant also requires a model for how creep damage ac-
cumulates in the test section under thermomechanical loading. And, like the specimen sizing
method, the technique requires a complex computer program to implement the numerical optimi-
zation.
The current method also requires measuring the strain in the test section during a known thermal
cycle in the out of reactor testing. Measuring the strain in the sample is not trivial as it cannot be
done with a standard extensometer. INL has used Digital Image Correlation (DIC) to provide this
strain data, however it is not clear how this technique might be applied to irradiated samples re-
moved from an operating reactor.
The main goal for the new methods described here is to simplify the process of calculating the
amount of damage, down to a technique that could be implanted in spreadsheet software. A sec-
ondary goal is to eliminate the need for instrumented strain measurements. Ideally the input to the
damage inference method should be a standard mechanical test, ideally one with existing experi-
ence with irradiated samples.
This report considers two options fulfilling these criteria:
 1. A method based on measuring changes in the elastic properties of the test section by measuring the
 change in length of the test article under a known thermal load.

 2. The MPC Omega method, which infers the amount of damage in the test section based on a meas-
 urement of the current creep rate of the material.

3.3 The MPC Omega method

3.3.1 Introduction to the MPC Omega model
MPC Omega method is a creep damage assessment method developed by the Materials Properties
Council (MPC) led by Dr. Martin Prager and American Petroleum Institute (API), and it has been
included in API 579 FFS-1 Fitness-for-service since 2000 [16]. This method neglects material
primary creep and uses an idealized model of tertiary creep to relate the current creep rate in a
sample to the accumulated damage. See Part 10 of API 579 [12] for more details on this method.

3.3.2 Calculation methodology
In the 3-bar model, it is assumed that creep only occurs during high temperature holding period,
i.e., during stress relaxation, so, the total creep damage is the summation of the creep damage
during stress relaxation for each thermal load cycle. Figure 3.7 is the calculation process flowchart
for creep damage during one thermal cycle.

3.3.3.1 Comparison between spreadsheet results and FEA results
For Case 1 and Case 2, the spreadsheet results show that the 1st cycle introduces the highest creep
damage, and other cycles cause no creep damage, as they are in a state of elastic “shakedown”,
with negligible creep relaxation. The FEA results also demonstrate that the 1st cycle shows the
highest damage, and other cycles show a decreasing creep damage, because of decreasing σb, from
cycle to cycle. The spreadsheet gives both cases a bar_1 remaining life of “infinite”, as the rest
thermal cycles of both cases cause no creep damage. The 1st thermal cycle causes more creep
damage in Case 2 than that in Case 1, because on that cycle, in Case 1, creep relaxation starts from
σt , a stress level less than SS316H yield strength at T2, Sy,T2 , while in Case 2, creep relaxation
starts from Sy,T2 , so causes more creep damage. FEA results demonstrate remaining lives in the
orders of 100 million hours, that is more than 10,000 years.
For Case 3, the spreadsheet provides higher damage values for the 1st cycle than the FEA results
do. However, for other four cycles, FEA results provide higher values, because spreadsheet re-
gards σb as constant value and FEA does not.

3.3.3.2 Comparison among FEA results
Case 1 shows lower damage than Case 2 in the first cycle, the reason is described in Section 3.3.3.1.
For the other four cycles in these two cases, the difference among damage values are in the order

because in Case 3 in those cycles, bar_1 relaxes from a stress level of 𝜎/ − 𝑆%,'" , which is lower
than 𝑆%,'" , the stress level from which bar_1 in Case 4 starts to relax stress during creep. The

4 Verification and example problems
This chapter provides a worked example demonstrating how to apply the simplified spreadsheet
method, discussed in Chapter 2, to a realistic high-temperature component design. The process
includes performing ASME design calculations to determine the target strain range for the
component and then using this target strain range, along with an elastic follow-up factor, to
determine the size of the surveillance test article.
The geometry of the surveillance article is first verified using a three-bar analytical model by
comparing the results of the analytical model with the target strain range. This verification covers
two loading scenarios for the component and four different elastic follow-up factors for each
loading scenario.
Subsequently, finite element thermal-structural analyses are performed on realistic 3D geometries
of the surveillance article for two cases. Actual 3D geometries are created based on the dimensions
provided by the spreadsheet method. For each case, several geometric configurations are
considered to evaluate the results of the finite element analysis and to provide preliminary, general
recommendations on translating the simplified dimensions from the spreadsheet method into the
design of the actual surveillance article geometry.

4.1 Sample problem description
The sample problem considers a 316H pressure vessel with two nozzles, similar to the pressure
vessel and primary sodium inlet nozzle in the intermediate heat exchanger of the Clinch River
Breeder Reactor Plant (CRBRP) [17]. However, for this sample problem, the coolant is assumed
to be FLiBe molten salt, and the design includes two nozzles instead of one, as in the original
CRBRP configuration.
Figure 4.1 shows the geometry of the vessel. Primary salt from the reactor enters the vessel through
the nozzle and transfers heat to the intermediate, non-radioactive heat transfer fluid, which flows
through tubes (not shown in Figure 4.1) within the intermediate heat exchanger. The shell-side
FLiBe molten salt flows downward in both axial and crossflow directions relative to the tubes and
exits the intermediate heat exchanger at the bottom of the vessel through the primary outlet nozzle
(not shown in Figure 4.1). As indicated in Figure 4.1, only the upper part (red block) of the vessel’s
inner surface is in contact with FLiBe.
The convective heat transfer coefficient between FLiBe and the upper part of the vessel’s inner
surface is taken as 4 W/m²-K, while the coefficient between FLiBe and the nozzle’s inner surface
is 1667 W/m²-K. These values are based on FLiBe properties at 650°C and a thermal power output
of 100 MW. The inlet temperature of FLiBe cycles between 500°C and 650°C, and the operating
pressure is 0.2 MPa. Figure 4.2 shows the finite element model used to analyze the vessel and the
structural boundary conditions employed in the finite element analysis.
Two different loading conditions are considered: one with a faster heat-up and cool-down rate and
shorter hold time, and another with a slower heat-up and cool-down rate and longer hold time.
These loading conditions for the FLiBe inlet temperature result in two distinct strain ranges,
differing by approximately an order of magnitude. Table 4.1 lists the loading conditions, including
the heat-up, hold, and cool-down times for the FLiBe inlet temperature. Figure 4.3 shows example
fringe plots of temperature distribution in the vessel at different times during the loading cycle.

loading scenarios.

III, Division 5 design-by-elastic analysis rules. Table 4.1 lists both the elastically calculated strain

4.4.1 Loading Scenario-1 with q=3
Three different surveillance article geometries were analyzed under Loading Scenario-1 with q=3,
as shown in Figure 4.7. These configurations were designed using the dimensions listed in Table

all configurations, the thickness of the surveillance article is taken to be equal to the width of the

The primary distinction among the three configurations is how the length of the test article, L1, is
measured:
 • Configuration-1: L1 is defined as the distance between the bottom of the gripping regions
 at both ends of the test article.
 • Configuration-2: L1 is measured as the distance between the top of the gripping regions.
 • Configuration-3: L1 is taken as the distance between the midsections of the gripping re-
 gions.
Since the total length of the pillar, L1+L2, is fixed, the definition of L1 affects the relative propor-
tions of material assigned to the test article and the driver, and thus their relative stiffnesses. The
stiffness ratio between the test article and the driver determines the load transferred to the test
article and its resulting strain response.
This effect is reflected in the simulated strain ranges presented in Table 4.6. Configuration-1,
which has the lowest ratio of driver material to test article material, yields the minimum simulated
strain range in the test article. Configuration-2, with the highest ratio of driver material to test
article material, produces the maximum strain range. Configuration-3 falls between Configurations
1 and 2 in terms of both material ratio and simulated strain range.
When comparing the simulated strain ranges to the target value, Configuration-1 provides the clos-
est match – the equivalent strain range in the test article is 0.20%, and the axial strain is 0.18%,
both very close to the target strain range of 0.21%. Configuration-3 also provides reasonably close
results, while Configuration-2 deviates the most from the target.
Overall, for all three configurations, the simulated strain range in the test article is reasonably close
to the target strain range. In each case, the maximum strain range occurs in the midsection of the
test article as indicated by Figure 4.8, further validating the surveillance article sizing approach
using the spreadsheet sizing tool.

5 Conclusions
This report describes simplified methods for sizing and inferring damage from passively actuated
mechanical test articles, intended for material surveillance in future high temperature reactors.
These approaches supplement or replace earlier, more sophisticated, methods with techniques sim-
ple enough to implement in a computer spreadsheet and apply at future operating plants. Both
methods are based on the three-bar model of the test article but, unlike past approaches, make
simplifying assumptions on the sample and material behavior to yield analytic equations for the
specimen response. These analytic equations can then be solved to either size the test article, given
a target strain range and follow up factor, or determine the amount of damage accumulated in the
test section, given either strain measurements resulting from a known thermal cycle or the current
creep rate of the test section.
The method described here for sizing the test article first determines the general regime of behavior
an elastic-perfectly-plastic-power-law-creep three bar model of the test section will fall into for a
given thermal cycle. The work identifies four basic regimes of behavior depending on the tem-
perature range and the material properties of the test, driver, and casing. The report then derives
a solution for the test section strain range and follow up factor for each regime. These analytic
solutions can be used to optimize the test article geometry to best match the target component
response.
This method greatly simplifies the constitutive response of the test section and of the casing and
driver, which are assumed to remain linear elastic. For many specimen designs where the casing
and driver cross sections are much greater than the test article, this assumption is reasonable. How-
ever, the approach will not be as accurate as the numerical optimization technique using more
accurate constitutive models (and accounting for inelasticity in the driver and casing). However,
this disadvantage is outweighed by the simplicity of the approach and its implementation. Future
work will compare the accuracy of the simplified model to more detailed numerical calculations
and against experimental measurements. Additional work will also explore the geometry of test
articles sized to mimic actual reactor components to ensure these designs are feasible to manufac-
ture with our current methods.
 The report then describes two simplified approaches for inferring creep damage in the test section.
The first uses strain measurements, now of the casing section rather than the test article, as with
past approaches, and the simplified, analytic three bar model to determine the reduction in elastic
stiffness of the test section. This reduction in elastic stiffness directly translates to the accumula-
tion of creep damage in the test section. The other approach applies the MPC Omega method to
determine the accumulated damage and remaining life of the test section. This technique only
requires measuring the creep rate of the test section in isolation. However, it does require Omega
coefficients for the test material. These are available for a wide range of structural materials in
ASME FFS-1 or could be easily calculated from Larson-Miller correlations for new materials.
Of the two methods, the MPC Omega approach seems more promising. The technique has a
long-established history in non-nuclear applications. Many plant engineers will already be famil-
iar with the approach. The test measurement, a creep test of the test section, will be easier to
manage for irradiated materials compared to thermal cycling the entire sample while measuring
the strain in the pillar section. A direct creep test could even be replaced with Small Punch Testing
(SPT) or indentation creep testing to further simplify the out-of-reactor test procedure. Finally,

 55

while elegant, the method based on inferring changes in elastic properties will be less sensitive
compared to the Omega method. The material elastic stiffness will only change after the accumu-
lation of a large amount of creep damage in the material, meaning this method may not provide
adequate early warning when applied in material surveillance programs.
Finally, the report provides verification examples for the new techniques via a comparison to more
sophisticated finite element analysis calculations. The report also provides a single worked exam-
ple of how to apply the simplified sizing calculations to a realistic high temperature component,
including determining the input target strain range and follow up factor from ASME design calcu-
lations. Additional sample problems of this kind would provide more worked examples for plant
engineers to follow and could further verify the feasibility of the methods when applied to actual
components.
