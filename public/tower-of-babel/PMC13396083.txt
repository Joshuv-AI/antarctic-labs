Real-World, Long-Term Outcomes with Cladribine Tablets Versus Other Oral Treatments Among Patients with Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Ajay S Gupta, Joanna P MacEwan, Jeffrey Anderson, Xin Zhao, Sonia Kim, Andy Surinach, Yu Hong, Amy L Phillips, Xiaoxue Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, often associated with significant disability. As treatment options increase, comparative effectiveness data for disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) are essential. This study evaluated treatment switching, healthcare resource utilization, and cost outcomes among US patients with MS (PwMS) treated with cladribine tablets (CladT) vs. fingolimod (FTY), dimethyl fumarate (DMF), and teriflunomide (TER) during a 4-year follow-up period.MethodsThis retrospective study used claims data from the Komodo Healthcare Map (4/1/2018–3/31/2024). Adult PwMS with ≥ 1 claim for CladT, FTY, DMF, and TER were included. Index date was the first claim date for the respective DMT. Continuous enrollment for 12 months pre-index and 48 months post-index was required. Cohorts were balanced using propensity score weighting and 4-year outcomes were assessed using generalized linear and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOverall, 3038 PwMS were included: CladT (n = 140), FTY (n = 454), DMF (n = 1465), and TER (n = 979). In the weighted cohorts, mean age was 48 years, and 74–77% were female. Treatment switching during a 4-year follow-up period was lower for CladT (11%), vs. FTY (42%), DMF (57%), and TER (42%) cohorts. CladT showed lower all-cause medical costs per patient per year (PPPY): $13,377 vs. FTY (adjusted mean difference [AMD] $10,073; 95% confidence interval (CI) $5006–15,148), DMF (AMD $12,013; 95% CI $7490–16,706), and TER (AMD $9917; 95% CI $6558–13,997) cohorts. All-cause total costs PPPY were lower with CladT ($53,007) vs. FTY (AMD $14,140; 95% CI $7181–22,021) and TER (AMD $6652; 95% CI $567–12,395) cohorts, but similar to DMF cohort (AMD $4472; 95% CI − $2011, $11,111).ConclusionPwMS treated with CladT had significantly fewer treatment switches and lower all-cause medical costs vs. FTY, DMF, and TER. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune condition of the central nervous system that is associated with progressive disability and affects approximately one million adults in the USA.Despite a wide array of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) available for MS treatment, clinicians frequently lack direct real-world comparative data to inform evidence-based treatment decisions.This study aims to address this evidence gap by conducting a comparative analysis of cladribine tablets (CladT) versus other oral DMTs using a large US claims database. What was learned from this study? Compared with other oral DMTs, CladT were associated with significantly fewer treatment switches and lower all-cause medical costs over a 4-year observation period.These study findings provide real-world evidence that may assist healthcare professionals and payers in evaluating treatment options with regards to treatment switching, healthcare utilization, and associated costs.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune condition characterized by inflammatory demyelination and progressive neurodegeneration within the central nervous system [1, 2]. The prevalence of MS in the USA continues to rise, with approximately one million adults living with the condition, predominantly affecting individuals aged 20–40 years, with women being disproportionately represented [3–6]. MS imposes significant clinical and economic burden on patients, caregivers, and health systems [7–9]. A recent report estimated that the direct medical cost of MS in the US reaches $63.3 billion annually, with prescription medications representing the largest cost component (54%) [10].

Currently, more than 20 disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) are approved for MS treatment in the USA [11]. However, there are no clear and uniform treatment algorithms owing to the heterogenous and unpredictable nature of MS and varying DMT risk–benefit profiles and treatment response [12, 13]. Hence, personalized treatment approaches that consider patient preferences, disease characteristics, and comorbidities are emphasized by the American Academy of Neurology and European Committee for Treatment and Research in MS [12, 14]. Given the absence of a cure for MS, primary treatment objectives focus on reducing relapse and preventing disability progression [14]. This individualized approach necessitates that clinicians have access to a diverse array of DMTs to effectively navigate the complex therapeutic landscape and optimize care for the heterogeneous patient population with MS [12, 14].

In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration approved oral cladribine tablets (CladT) 10 mg for relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS) and active secondary progressive MS (SPMS). CladT is a short-course oral treatment administered in two annual treatment courses, each comprising two cycles—one at the start of the first month and one at the start of the second month in years 1 and 2. Each cycle comprises one or two tablets once daily for four or five consecutive days, for a recommended cumulative dose of 3.5 mg/kg over 2 years. No further treatment with cladribine tablets is required in years 3 and 4 [15]. The efficacy and safety of CladT versus placebo in reducing relapse rates, slowing disability progression, and decreasing disease activity have been demonstrated in clinical studies including CLARITY (NCT00213135), CLARITY Extension (NCT00641537), and CLASSIC-MS (NCT03961204) [16–18].

Given the increasing number of approved therapies for MS, comparative assessment of recently approved DMTs with different mechanisms of action, side effects profiles, and dosing strategies is important. Emerging real-world comparative studies have begun to explore the clinical outcomes of CladT in relation to other DMTs [19, 20]. The Generating Learnings in MultiPle SclErosis (GLIMPSE) study, using the MSBase registry, demonstrated that CladT was associated with longer time-to-treatment discontinuation, delayed time-to-first relapse, and lower annualized relapse rate than other oral DMT cohorts (fingolimod [FTY], dimethyl fumarate [DMF], and teriflunomide [TER]) [20]. Additional comparative analyses using electronic medical records and claims data in the USA have shown the real-world effectiveness of CladT in terms of improved walking speeds, better NeuroQoL fatigue scores, and reduced switch rates over 2–4 years relative to other DMTs [21, 22].

Given that DMTs represent a substantial proportion of total healthcare cost, comprehensive understanding of treatment profiles, cost implications, and downstream healthcare impacts is valuable beyond clinical effectiveness. These factors may inform treatment utilization and decision-making for patients, healthcare professionals, and health systems.

To date, real-world data on treatment switching, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), and cost related to CladT and other DMTs remain limited, particularly over extended follow-up periods. This real-world study aimed to compare treatment switching, HCRU, and cost outcomes among US patients with MS (PwMS) treated with CladT, FTY, DMF, and TER over 4 years.

Methods

Data Source

This retrospective study was an extension of a previously conducted 2-year study using data from the Komodo Healthcare Map™ database from April 1, 2018 to November 30, 2024 [23]. The Komodo Healthcare Map™ is a de-identified, longitudinal claims dataset containing open and payer-complete medical and pharmacy claims for a nationally representative sample of US patients across the USA (Healthcare Map, Komodohealth.com) [24].

This non-interventional, retrospective study involved analysis of secondary, deidentified data. Therefore, independent ethics committee/institutional review board approval was not required.

Study Design and Patient Selection

PwMS were identified as having ≥ 2 diagnosis claims for MS (ICD-10-CM: G35) separated by ≥ 30 days [25–28]. Eligible patients initiated treatment with CladT, FTY, DMF, or TER between April 1, 2019 and November 30, 2022, known as the identification period. The index date was defined as the date of the first claim for a DMT of interest during the identification period. Patients were assigned to a treatment cohort based on the index DMT and contributed to no more than a single cohort for analysis. Patients were required to be ≥ 18 years of age at the index date and have continuous health plan enrollment for 12 months before the index date and at least 48 months after the index date without a gap in enrollment of > 30 days. The study was limited to patients with no prior claims for the index DMT during the 12 months before the index date (new user design).

This study excluded patients with any code that could indicate pregnancy in the 12 months before or 24 months after the index date. Patients with a place of service code indicating hospice, inpatient mental health, or inpatient rehabilitation in the 12 months before the index date as well as with any claims for CladT infusions were excluded. Patients with multiple treatments on the index date or excessive claims for CladT (threshold > 6) that may indicate data quality issues according to clinical input were also excluded. Furthermore, patients residing outside the 50 states and Washington D.C. or with unknown insurance status were excluded from the study. Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material depicts the study design.

Covariates

Patient demographics included age, sex, index year, geographic region, and insurance type at index. Clinical covariates captured within 12 months prior to the index date (baseline period) included the Charlson Comorbidity Index, MS severity score, MS-specific individual comorbidities, MS relapse, MS-related hospitalizations, and MS-related treatment history, including prior use of DMT and dalfampridine (indicating walking disability) [29, 30].

To measure MS severity, we utilized a claims-based algorithm developed by Toliver et al., which estimates MS disease severity based on HCRU and symptoms [31]. This severity scale categorizes severity as low (0–4 points), moderate (5–10 points), or high (≥ 11 points) [31]. MS relapse was identified using a claims-based algorithm based on inpatient claims with a primary MS diagnosis or claims for corticosteroids or corticotrophin on or within 7 days after a medical claim for an ambulatory visit or emergency room (ER) visit with an MS diagnosis code in any position [28, 32]. Infused/injected corticosteroid claims with the same date of service as an infused DMT were not considered as an MS relapse, as corticosteroids may be administered prophylactically with infused DMTs.

Study Outcomes

Study outcomes included treatment switching, time to switch, all-cause and MS-related HCRU, and costs over a 4-year post-index period. The proportion of patients who had a treatment switch was determined by any subsequent prescription claim for a non-index DMT. Time to switch was defined as the number of days from the index date to the date of the subsequent DMT claim. All-cause and MS-related HCRU were measured by setting of services (e.g., hospitalization, ER visits, outpatient visits). MS-related HCRU was defined as a primary diagnosis of MS for inpatient hospitalization and a MS diagnosis in any position for outpatient setting. Additionally, all DMT prescription claims were assigned as MS-related. For cost measures in the Komodo Healthcare Map™ database, medical costs were imputed using Medicare-based or actual-based rates or a blended rate, while pharmacy costs were imputed using the median value for each national drug code conditional on payer type when actual amounts were unavailable [33].

Statistical Analysis

Study variables, including baseline and follow-up outcome measures, were analyzed using descriptive statistics for each treatment group. Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD), while discrete variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. The baseline characteristics of the treatment groups were compared using standardized mean differences (SMDs) to assess the balance in demographic and clinical covariates between treatment groups.

Propensity score (PS) weighting was employed by balancing study cohorts on observed measures that may affect treatment assignment in the real world [34]. The analysis involved three pairwise comparisons: CladT vs. FTY, CladT vs. DMF, and CladT vs. TER. Each pairwise comparison was PS weighted to adjust for demographic and clinical covariates. The PS was generated using a logistic regression model estimating the probability of receiving CladT relative to each comparator DMT, conditioned on covariates such as age, index year, sex, region, insurance type, CCI score, MS-related comorbidities (presented in Table S1 in Supplementary Material), MS relapse, MS severity score, baseline DMT use, and dalfampridine use. PS weights were calculated using the weighting by odds approach to estimate the average treatment effect among the treated (ATT). Comparative analyses of outcomes between treatment groups were conducted using an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, assigning outcomes to the index DMT over the 4-year follow-up period.

Balance between treatment groups after weighting was assessed using SMDs. A SMD of > 0.1 was considered indicative of an imbalance. Any remaining imbalanced covariates were included as additional covariates in the generalized linear models (GLM) and Cox proportional hazards model. This approach helps account for residual differences in these covariates when balance is not completely achieved after weighting.

Odds ratios (ORs) for switching were estimated using logistic regression, comparing each DMT comparator group with CladT, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Hazard ratios (HRs) for time to switch were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, comparing each DMT comparison group to CladT along with 95% CIs. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves were plotted to illustrate the time to switch for each treatment group. All-cause and MS-related HCRU and healthcare costs were expressed as number of visits and costs (USD [$]), respectively, per patient per year (PPPY). Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% CI for HCRU comparing each DMT comparator group to the CladT group were estimated using GLMs with Poisson distribution; negative binomial models were used if overdispersion was present, or two-part models were employed when outcomes were zero inflated. Adjusted mean differences (AMDs) in healthcare costs comparing each comparison group to CladT were estimated along with bootstrapped 95% CI using GLMs with gamma distribution or two-part models for excessive zeros. The 95% CIs for IRRs and AMDs were calculated using the bootstrapping method with 1000 replicates.

R software was used for statistical computing, and graphics supported by the R Foundation were used for statistical computing.

Results

In total, 3038 PwMS met the inclusion criteria between April 1, 2018 and November 30, 2024. Among these, 140 received CladT, 1465 received DMF, 454 received FTY, and 979 received TER (Fig. 1).

At the index date, the mean age ranged from 42 years (SD 11) in the FTY cohort to 50 years (SD 11) in the TER cohort. More than 70% of the patients were female across all treatment groups. Commercial insurance coverage ranged from 62% in the CladT group to 76% in the FTY group. Index medications were initiated by 66–75% of the comparator cohorts in 2019 compared to 49% in the CladT cohort.

The CladT cohort had a higher CCI score (mean 0.67) versus the FTY (0.46), DMF (0.42), and TER (0.55) cohorts. The CladT cohort had higher mean MS severity score (5.4) as measured by the claims-based algorithm, compared to other DMT comparators (range 3.6–3.9). The rate of MS relapse within 12 months prior to index date was also higher in the CladT cohort (39%) compared with other DMT comparator groups (31–34%). Prior DMT use within 12 months before index date was higher in the CladT cohort (56%) versus 28–44% in the DMT comparator groups. The baseline use of dalfampridine use was higher in the CladT cohort (7.1%) compared with the FTY (1.5%) and DMF (2.3%) cohorts, but similar to the TER cohort (4.9%). HCRU Within 4 Years

Following weighting, the mean number of all-cause outpatient visits PPPY was higher in the DMF cohort (29.4) than in the CladT cohort (23.7), equating to a 32% increase in utilization rate (IRR 1.32; 95% CI 1.06, 1.63). A similar trend was observed for all-cause outpatient visits in the TER cohort (26.9) versus CladT cohort (23.7), corresponding to a 19% increase (IRR 1.19; 95% CI 1.01, 1.43). The FTY cohort showed a 20% increase in all-cause outpatient visits (IRR 1.20; 95% CI 0.96, 1.53); although this finding did not reach statistical significance.

No significant differences were observed in the mean number of ER visits and inpatient admissions between CladT and comparator DMT cohorts. Table 3 summarizes the all-cause HCRU within 4 years.

Healthcare Costs Within 4 Years

The mean (SD) all-cause medical costs PPPY were higher in the DMF ($23,133 [$31,736]) than in the CladT cohort ($13,377 [$17,305]), with an AMD of $12,013 (95% CI $7490, $16,706). The FTY and TER cohorts displayed higher mean (SD) all-cause medical costs PPPY ($21,724 [$27,380] and $21,542 [$31,023], respectively) than the CladT cohort ($13,377 [$17,305]), with an AMD of $10,073 (95% CI $5006, $15,148) and $9917 (95% CI $6558, $13,997), respectively (Table 4).

No significant differences were observed in all-cause inpatient hospitalization or ER costs between the CladT and DMT comparator cohorts.

The mean (SD) all-cause pharmacy costs PPPY were lower in DMF cohort ($31,392 [$29,669], AMD − $6751, 95% CI − $11,410, − $2161) than in CladT cohort ($39,630 [$21,454]), while no significant differences were observed with the FTY and TER cohorts.

The mean all-cause total costs were higher in the FTY ($64,646 [$33,640]; AMD $14,140, 95% CI $7181, $22,021) and TER cohorts ($57,618 [$38,153]; AMD $6652, 95% CI $567, $12,395) compared with the CladT cohort ($53,007 [$29,681]). No significant difference was observed between the CladT and DMF cohorts.

Similar trends were observed for MS-related costs (see Table S5 in the Supplementary Material).

Discussion

This real-world study in the USA provides valuable insights into treatment switching, HCRU, and costs over a long-term follow-up period of 4 years, comparing CladT with other oral DMTs, specifically, FTY, DMF, and TER. The results showed that patients with MS treated with CladT had significantly fewer treatment switches and lower medical costs than those treated with FTY, DMF, and TER.

The study found that treatment switching was significantly lower for CladT (11%) compared to FTY (42%), DMF (57%), and TER (42%) over 4 years. The low treatment switching of CladT aligns with findings from previous research demonstrating the limited need for switching therapies. Multicenter retrospective cohort studies reported similar treatment switching of approximately 14% over 4 years in both Polish and German populations [35, 36]. Additionally, the CLASSIC-MS study suggested 58% of patients who received ≥ 1 CladT dose in the CLARITY/CLARITY Extension study required no further DMTs over a median follow-up of 10.9 years [18]. However, some variation exists in the literature, with a Finnish retrospective study reporting treatment switching in 33% of PwMS over 4 years [37]. Other large registries and cohort studies (e.g., the Italian Multiple Sclerosis and Related Disorders Register [RISM], MS registry in Finland, CLARIFY-MS, MAGNIFY-MS) did not report on switching, HCRU, or cost outcomes; however, they complement claims-based findings by demonstrating that patients treated with CladT experienced sustained effectiveness, such as reduced relapse rate and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) activity, over 4 years after the initial dose [37–42].

Comparative findings on treatment switching for oral DMTs are largely consistent with the literature. The GLIMPSE study, which compared treatment persistence and switching in patients with RRMS treated with CladT versus FTY, DMF, and TER using KM survival analysis, estimated switching rates of 5–10% for CladT, 30–35% for FTY, 40–45% for DMF, and TER within 3 years. Despite differences in follow-up duration and geographic location, our study demonstrated a similar magnitude of increased switching hazard ratios for the comparator DMTs versus the CladT cohort [20]. Notably, Racsa et al. reported much lower treatment switching for FTY (15%), TER (10%), and DMF (12%); however, discontinuation of these index DMT ranged from 75% to 85% over approximately 4 years of follow-up. This significant discrepancy between switching and discontinuation suggests potential underreporting of true treatment switching, likely due to patient censoring at the time of relapse [43].

To date, limited data exist comparing HCRU and costs of CladT versus other oral DMTs [44]. Our study evaluated all-cause and MS-related HCRU and cost outcomes with CladT compared with DMF, FTY, and TER. We found all-cause outpatient visits were significantly higher in the DMF and TER cohort (32%, and 19%, respectively) than in the CladT cohort. The outpatient visits in the FTY cohort were numerically higher than in the CladT cohort; however, it was not statistically significant. All-cause ER and inpatient hospitalizations did not differ significantly between CladT and the comparator DMTs. A retrospective study of the Campania region (south Italy) comparing ocrelizumab with various oral, infused and injectable DMTs reported that CladT had low and similar annualized rate of MS-related hospitalizations compared to FTY, TER, DMF [45]. A recent US claims-based study found no significant differences in HCRU (both all-cause and MS-related) and costs between fumarates and CladT [44]. However, this study was likely limited by a smaller sample size (178 patients treated with fumarate and 89 patients treated with CladT after matching), shorter study period (2017–2022), and a shorter follow-up period due to patient censoring at treatment discontinuation, switch, or disenrollment. Another US claims database study reported that FTY demonstrated lower inpatient admissions (30% vs. 41% vs. 34%) and ER visits (46% vs. 61% vs. 54%) compared to TER and DMF, respectively [43]. As our study population was weighted to reflect patients treated with CladT, who typically present with more severe MS and greater prior DMT exposure, these baseline differences may contribute to the nuanced treatment response observed across varying patient profiles and highlight the importance of considering disease severity and treatment history when interpreting HCRU outcomes. The reduced outpatient visits associated with CladT can be attributed to several factors, including shorter treatment courses that lessen the need for frequent monitoring compared to continuous oral DMTs. This is supported by Rog et al., who demonstrated that CladT required less healthcare professionals’ time for administration and monitoring versus other oral and infused DMTs [46]. The intermittent nature of CladT therapy may therefore contribute to reduced healthcare system burden while maintaining sustained health benefits.

Regarding cost outcomes, the CladT cohort exhibited lower all-cause medical costs PPPY than DMF, FTY, and TER, primarily driven by reduced all-cause outpatient costs. Specifically, average medical costs PPPY were lower by $9917–12,013 and average outpatient visit costs PPPY were lower by $8910–10,989 compared with the DMT comparator cohorts, corresponding with the reduced outpatient visits frequency associated with CladT treatment. While the average pharmacy costs PPPY were higher for CladT than for DMF, they were comparable to FTY and TER. Notably, over the 4-year follow-up period, annual pharmacy costs for the comparator DMTs remained stable, whereas CladT demonstrated a substantial decline from $57,078 in year 2 to $6415 in year 3, reflecting its front-loaded treatment schedule where therapy is administered only in the first 2 years. Over the 4-year follow-up period, the average total healthcare costs (medical plus pharmacy) PPPY were significantly lower for CladT than for FTY and TER by $14,140 and $6652, respectively, and were comparable to DMF. These findings contrast with a recent claims-based study comparing fumarates versus other oral DMTs between 2017 and 2022, which reported similar healthcare cost between CladT and fumarates [44]. However, this comparison may have been influenced by the front-loaded treatment cost of CladT during the initial two treatment years within the shorter study timeframe. Previous literature indicates the FTY cohort may incur higher all-cause costs compared with DMF, TER, or injectable interferon beta-1a [43, 47]. In our study, these elevated costs appear to be driven primarily by higher pharmacy cost relative to TER and DMF, and higher medical cost than CladT. Our findings suggest that medical cost savings associated with CladT can offset the higher initial drug acquisition costs. The economic advantage observed in the CladT cohort over 4 years may have been contributed by several factors, including the unique short-course dosing paradigm, lower rates of switching to other DMTs, and reduced utilization of outpatient services. Our 4-year cost evaluation, supported by data from the CLASSIC-MS, MAGNIFY-MS Extension study, and other studies, suggests that CladT can deliver durable clinical and economic benefits in the management of MS [18, 48]. The comparative findings shown in our study further highlight role of CladT as a valuable treatment option. Further studies are needed to explore the nuances of these benefits in specific patient subgroups and to validate these findings across diverse healthcare settings.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, administrative claims only provided information about dispensed medications and did not indicate whether patients took the medication. Second, claims data may lack sufficient clinical information, requiring proxy measures for MS relapse and severity to be derived from claims data, which may be subject to misclassification bias. Third, eligible patients were required to have at least 4 years of continuous enrollment following the initiation of the index DMT. Those who died or were lost to follow-up were excluded, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability to all initiators of DMTs in the target population. Fourth, this study adopted a modified ITT approach, which may yield conservative estimates for the differences between the CladT and comparator DMT cohorts relative to as-treated/per-protocol estimates due to nondifferential misclassification of the treatment received. Fifth, cost imputation was used because costs were not always available in the Komodo Healthcare Map™ which may not accurately represent true costs and can underestimate variance. Sixth, imbalances in baseline characteristics, such as prior DMT drug categories, remained after PS weighting and were adjusted for in the outcome model. Adjustment for MS hospitalization in the CladT vs. FTY comparison was not feasible due to the very low frequency of such events especially in the CladT group. Hence, the small number of cases limited the ability to provide a reliable model and estimate. However, MS hospitalization was balanced before weighting for the other two comparisons. Residual imbalance and unmeasured confounding may bias the results toward over- or underestimation of comparative outcomes. Finally, as CladT was launched in the USA in 2019 and the study period spanned 2018–2024, the number of patients treated with CladT and the follow-up time were limited.

Conclusions

This real-world study, using a large US claims database, demonstrated that patients with MS treated with CladT had significantly fewer treatment switches and lower all-cause medical costs vs. FTY, DMF, and TER. Total healthcare costs were lower vs. TER and FTY, but similar to DMF during a 4-year follow-up period. These findings suggest that CladT may represent a valuable treatment option in MS management, offering both clinical and economic advantages. Further research should evaluate outcomes across the overall patient population as well as different patient subgroups to further characterize the clinical utility of various DMTs in diverse MS populations.
