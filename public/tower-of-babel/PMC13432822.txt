Feasibility and safety of recombinant zoster vaccination in patients undergoing active cancer treatments: A real-world single-center experience
Authors: Giuliana Ciappina, Giordana Di Mauro, Enrica Toscano, Angela Alibrandi, Tindara Franchina, Mariacarmela Santarpia, Antonio Picone, Marco Donato, Claudia Garipoli, Benedetta Raneri, Patrizia Carroccio, Salvatore Ferrotto, Enrica Maiorana, Paola Muscolino, Giovanni Francesco Pellicanò, Maria Ilenia Passalacqua, Dalila Incognito, Massimiliano Berretta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with solid tumors receiving systemic anticancer therapy face an increased risk of herpes zoster (HZ), a complication that can delay or disrupt ongoing oncologic treatments. Although the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is recommended for immunocompromised adults, real-world data on its implementation and tolerability during active cancer treatment settings remain limited. We conducted a retrospective, single-center observational study at the Oncology Department of the “G. Martino” University Hospital, Messina, Italy. Between July 2022 and January 2025, the standard two-dose RZV schedule was systematically offered to adult patients with solid tumors who were receiving or scheduled to start systemic therapies. Endpoints included the operational feasibility of vaccination during oncologic care, safety, and HZ occurrence during follow-up. Overall, 42 patients received at least one RZV dose, and 37 (88.1%) completed the two-dose schedule. Patients were receiving different systemic anticancer treatments, mainly chemotherapy (59.5%), followed by chemo-immunotherapy and immunotherapy. RZV showed a favorable safety profile. Injection-site pain was the most frequently reported local adverse event, while systemic reactions, including fever and headache, were mild and transient. No severe vaccine-related adverse events or delays in oncologic treatment were observed. After a median follow-up of 11.5 months, no clinically documented HZ episodes occurred, although this finding should be interpreted descriptively given the limited sample size. Our experience suggests that integrating RZV into routine outpatient oncology workflows is feasible and well tolerated in patients undergoing active systemic treatments.KEYWORDS: Herpes zoster vaccination, cancer patients, oncologic treatments

Introduction

Cancer is a major driver of secondary immunodeficiency. Both the underlying malignancy and antineoplastic treatments, including cytotoxic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and frequent corticosteroid use, compromise cellular and humoral immunity, thereby increasing susceptibility to infections and viral reactivations.1 Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) reactivation causing herpes zoster (HZ) is particularly relevant in oncology due to its high incidence, meaningful clinical complications, and measurable impact on healthcare utilization.2–4 While the lifetime risk of HZ in the general population is substantial, immunocompromised individuals are at increased risk of HZ, complications, and recurrence.2 In oncology populations, epidemiological data indicate that several malignancies, including central nervous system (CNS), lung, oral cavity, and esophageal cancers, are strongly associated with increased HZ incidence.3 Beyond incidence, the clinical relevance of HZ in oncology lies in its potential complications and downstream healthcare burden. HZ may be complicated by postherpetic neuralgia, ophthalmic involvement, disseminated disease, and, in selected cases, visceral or neurological involvement.2,4 Of particular concern is CNS involvement (such as meningitis, encephalitis, and vasculitis).4 A nationwide case-control analysis of VZV-associated CNS infections identified malignancy-related immunosuppression as an important risk factor: hematological cancers and immunosuppressive therapies emerged as among the strongest risk factors, while solid tumors were also associated with increased risk.5 These severe manifestations are especially consequential in oncology because they can destabilize patients already rendered frail by their disease and treatments, leading to unplanned healthcare encounters and complex clinical management. In addition, HZ episodes may interfere with anticancer treatment pathways, contributing to delays, dose reductions, or temporary discontinuation of therapy and thereby adding an indirect burden alongside direct infectious morbidity.6

This dual impact (patient-level harm and system-level resource use) positions HZ prevention as an important supportive-care priority within cancer services. Consequently, HZ prevention through the adjuvanted recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; Shingrix®), a non-live subunit vaccine containing VZV glycoprotein E and the AS01B adjuvant, has become a relevant strategy for immunocompromised adults, including patients with solid tumors when clinically appropriate. Oncology-oriented recommendations support its use in patients with solid tumors, and regulatory and public health guidance recommend RZV for immunocompromised adults.6–8 Clinical trial data in patients with solid tumors receiving chemotherapy show that RZV can induce both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses, supporting its biological plausibility in this setting.9 Additional data in cancer patients receiving immunotherapy further support the immunogenicity and documented safety profile of RZV in selected oncology populations.10 At the population level, modeling studies in cancer cohorts suggest that RZV implementation may reduce the burden of HZ and related complications.11 A US-based model estimated that RZV could avert thousands of HZ cases and hundreds of postherpetic neuralgia cases in selected cancer populations, with a low number needed to vaccinate to prevent one HZ case.11 In parallel, real-world data show that HZ in patients with solid tumors receiving chemotherapy is associated with healthcare utilization and costs, reinforcing the potential value of preventive strategies in this population.12 However, modeling and healthcare-utilization studies do not replace implementation data from routine oncology workflows, where treatment scheduling, competing clinical priorities, and patient frailty may influence vaccine delivery and completion.

In this context, real-world evidence from oncology settings is needed to document the feasibility and tolerability of RZV delivery during routine cancer care, and to inform implementation strategies that maximize completion of the two-dose schedule. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective, single-center study to evaluate the operational feasibility and safety of RZV in patients with solid tumors undergoing active systemic treatment, and to describe clinical outcomes during routine practice follow-up.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective, single-center observational study at the Oncology Department of the “G. Martino” University Hospital (Messina, Italy) between July 2022 and January 2025 to evaluate the feasibility and safety of HZ vaccination in patients with solid tumors. Eligible patients were adults (≥18 y) with a confirmed diagnosis of a solid malignancy who were either planning to initiate or actively receiving systemic anticancer therapy. Further inclusion criteria required a documented history of varicella infection and no prior vaccination against HZ at baseline. Patients were also required to provide written informed consent and demonstrate the ability to comply with the two-dose vaccination schedule. Individuals with any clinical contraindications to vaccine administration, as determined by the treating physician, were excluded. The RZV was offered consecutively to eligible patients during routine outpatient oncology visits as part of standard supportive care. Depending on clinical practice and individual treatment schedules, vaccine doses were administered either prior to the initiation of anticancer treatment or concurrently with active systemic therapy. The study design was strictly descriptive and did not include a non-vaccinated comparator group.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) principles. The study protocol was retrospectively approved by the Ethics Committee of Azienda Ospedaliera Universitaria “G. Martino” of Messina (protocol code 97–25, date of approval: 16 december 2025). Written informed consent for data collection was obtained from all participants involved in the study.

Vaccination protocol

Participants received the recombinant adjuvanted, non-live zoster vaccine (RZV; Shingrix®) according to guidance for immunocompromised populations. The first dose was administered either 2–3 weeks before the start of systemic anticancer therapy or, for patients already on treatment, shortly before a planned cycle to avoid expected nadirs and to ensure adequate leukocyte counts. The second dose was scheduled 1–2 months after the first dose (preferred schedule for immunocompromised adults), with appointment planning integrated into routine oncology visits whenever feasible to maximize completion of the series.

Outcomes and data collection

The primary study outcomes were vaccine safety and operational feasibility. Feasibility was defined descriptively as the ability to integrate RZV administration into routine outpatient oncology care among patients who initiated vaccination, including completion of the two-dose schedule and absence of documented vaccination-related delays or disruptions of systemic anticancer treatment. Safety assessments relied on adverse events documented in routine clinical records and spontaneous patient reports during standard follow-up visits. Local reactions (such as pain, erythema, and swelling) and systemic symptoms (including fatigue, fever, headache, and gastrointestinal issues) were recorded as clinically reported. Serious adverse events and any reactions requiring subsequent medical evaluation were captured when documented in the patient files.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, safety data were analyzed descriptively without the application of formal toxicity grading scales. Finally, patient-reported outcome measures were excluded from the analysis due to the absence of systematically retrievable data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were descriptive in nature and performed using R software, version 4.2.0. Continuous variables were summarized as median and range when available. Categorical variables are presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Given the observational design of the study and the lack of a non-vaccinated comparator group, no formal hypothesis testing or multivariable modeling was conducted.

Results

Feasibility and patient characteristics

Between July 2022 and January 2025, 42 patients with solid tumors were considered for RZV administration within routine oncologic care. Of these, 37/42 patients (88.1%) received both planned doses, while 5/42 patients did not complete the schedule due to loss to follow-up. Patients were either about to start or were already receiving systemic anticancer treatment. The cohort had a mean age of 68.5 y and was evenly distributed by sex, with males accounting for 50% of patients. Pancreatic cancer was the most frequent tumor type (28.6%), followed by breast cancer (16.7%), colorectal cancer (11.9%), and lung cancer (9.5%). Systemic treatment consisted mainly of chemotherapy (59.5%), chemo-immunotherapy (14.3%), or immunotherapy alone (11.9%). Among patients with metastatic disease, the most common sites of involvement were lymph nodes (30.6%) and lung (16.7%). Regarding vaccination timing, 23/42 patients received the first RZV dose before initiation of systemic anticancer therapy, whereas 19/42 patients were vaccinated while already receiving active treatment. Among patients vaccinated during treatment, administration was scheduled pragmatically according to the planned treatment cycle and clinical status, with particular attention to hematologic recovery in patients receiving cytotoxic chemotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy. No vaccine dose was delayed because of cytopenia, intercurrent infection, disease progression, or treatment-related toxicity. Vaccination timing was broadly similar across treatment groups, although greater scheduling individualization was required for patients receiving chemotherapy-based regimens, while patients treated with immunotherapy or endocrine therapy generally did not require specific timing adjustments.

Safety profile

Documented vaccine-related adverse events were described and classified as either local or systemic. Safety data were extracted retrospectively from routine clinical documentation and spontaneous patient reports recorded during follow-up; adverse events were not actively solicited through standardized questionnaires, prospective symptom diaries, or formal toxicity grading systems. After the first dose, injection-site pain was documented in 4/42 patients (9.5%), while after the second dose it was reported in 4/37 patients (10.8%). Fever occurred in 4/42 patients (9.5%) following the first dose and in 5/37 patients (13.5%) following the second dose. Headache was reported in 1/42 patient (2.4%) after the first dose and in 1/37 patient (2.7%) after the second dose. All documented adverse events were transient and managed conservatively. No vaccine-related serious adverse events were documented in routine clinical records during the observation period.

Clinical follow-up

Clinical outcomes regarding VZV reactivation were evaluated based on available medical records. After a median follow-up of 11.5 months, no cases of HZ were documented among the vaccinated patients.

Discussion

The present study documents the integration of RZV into routine oncology care for patients receiving systemic anticancer therapy. Unlike controlled trials designed to assess immunogenicity or efficacy, our analysis was descriptive and focused on feasibility and safety within everyday clinical practice. This distinction is relevant because patients treated outside trial settings often present with advanced disease, multiple comorbidities, and ongoing treatment-related immune suppression, all of which are factors that may influence both vaccine timing and adherence.

Patients with solid tumors experience higher rates of HZ compared with the general population, with increased risk across several malignancy types.3 Severe complications, including central nervous system involvement, are more frequently reported in immunocompromised hosts, particularly in the context of malignancy and immunosuppressive therapies.5 In this scenario, vaccination represents a clinically justified preventive intervention. However, evidence supporting its use during active anticancer treatment has largely derived from controlled settings evaluating immunogenicity and short-term safety, rather than from implementation studies embedded in routine oncology workflows.13 Immunogenicity data indicate that RZV is capable of inducing both humoral and cell-mediated responses in patients receiving systemic therapy.10 Nevertheless, biological responsiveness does not necessarily translate into operational feasibility. The practical challenges of administering a two-dose vaccine in patients undergoing chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or combination regimens include treatment scheduling, hematologic variability, and competing clinical priorities. Our findings therefore contribute implementation-level data, demonstrating that vaccine delivery and completion can be achieved within standard outpatient oncology practice without clinically relevant safety concerns. Although no HZ episodes were documented during follow-up, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be considered evidence of vaccine effectiveness. In patients with solid tumors receiving immunosuppressive anticancer therapy, the expected incidence of HZ is higher than in the general population; however, the absolute number of events within a cohort of this size and over a median follow-up of approximately one year may still be limited, particularly in the absence of a non-vaccinated comparator.14 HZ events in cancer populations are heterogeneous across tumor types and treatment exposures, and a substantial proportion of the clinical burden derives from complicated presentations and downstream healthcare utilization rather than from incidence alone.12 In addition, treatment modality may influence background risk in different directions, and available observational data on HZ during immune checkpoint inhibition remain inconsistent across studies, making event rates difficult to anticipate in small single-center cohorts.15,16 For these reasons, the absence of recorded HZ episodes in our cohort should be regarded as a descriptive finding, while the principal contribution of the study remains the documentation of feasibility and safety of delivering a two-dose RZV schedule during active oncologic treatment within routine care. Completion of the two-dose schedule represents a clinically meaningful endpoint in immunocompromised populations, in whom incomplete vaccination may compromise the durability of protection. Real-world studies have shown that adherence to multidose vaccination schedules in patients with cancer is frequently suboptimal, particularly when vaccination is not structurally integrated into oncology care pathways.17 In this context, the high proportion of patients completing the full RZV schedule in our cohort suggests that incorporation of vaccination into routine outpatient visits may mitigate logistical barriers commonly encountered in fragmented care models.

Implementation challenges in oncology are substantial. Timing relative to treatment cycles, transient cytopenias, disease progression, and competing clinical priorities may all interfere with adherence to recommended schedules.6 In addition, observational data indicate that vaccine uptake in immunocompromised adults often remains below recommended targets, even when formal indications are established.18,19 Against this background, feasibility data derived from routine oncology settings assume practical relevance, as they address the operational dimension that is not captured by immunogenicity trials. At the same time, it should be acknowledged that feasibility observed in a single institutional context may not be directly generalizable. Integration of vaccination into oncology workflows requires coordination between clinicians, nursing staff, and administrative scheduling, and institutional factors may influence completion rates (Table 2). The relatively small cohort size reflects the enrollment scale of a single-center vaccination pathway. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted within the limits of a monocentric experience, and larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm the feasibility of RZV delivery across diverse oncology settings, evaluate completion rates in broader patient populations, and better define long-term clinical outcomes.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several methodological constraints inherent to its design. The retrospective and monocentric nature of the analysis limits external validity and precludes causal inference. Importantly, this study was not designed to assess vaccine efficacy, vaccine effectiveness, or comparative effectiveness. In the absence of a non-vaccinated comparator group or an alternative-vaccine comparator group, it does not allow estimation of vaccine efficacy, relative risk reduction, or comparative protection. Because the total number of potentially eligible patients during the study period was not systematically captured, this study cannot estimate overall vaccine uptake; feasibility should therefore be interpreted as feasibility of RZV delivery and schedule completion among patients who initiated vaccination within the institutional pathway. Given the small sample size and the limited number of patients in several treatment categories, treatment-specific differences in completion and tolerability could not be reliably assessed. The modest sample size and the follow-up duration of approximately one year further restrict the ability to assess long-term protection and to capture infrequent adverse events. Safety information was derived from routine clinical documentation and spontaneous reporting rather than from prospective adverse-event collection using standardized questionnaires, symptom diaries, or formal toxicity grading systems. Therefore, minor reactions may have been underreported, and adverse events could not be graded systematically according to standardized severity criteria. In addition, immunogenicity parameters, such as anti-gE antibody titers or cell-mediated immune responses, were not systematically collected. As a result, correlations between clinical outcomes and biological markers of vaccine response could not be explored.

Within these limits, the study provides descriptive real-world data on the integration of RZV into active oncologic care and contributes implementation-level data that complement controlled immunogenicity studies. Further prospective investigations with standardized data collection, systematic immunogenicity assessment, formal toxicity grading, multicenter participation, and longer-term surveillance are warranted to better define sustained vaccine protection and long-term clinical outcomes in patients with solid tumors.

Conclusions

Effective implementation of VZV vaccination in oncology requires structured, oncology-driven delivery models rather than opportunistic referral. Core components include the systematic early identification of eligible patients, proactive scheduling of vaccination (ideally before the initiation of cytotoxic or otherwise immunosuppressive regimens), and the integration of RZV into standard diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, such as during treatment planning visits or pre-treatment assessments. Multidisciplinary coordination between oncologists and vaccination services can facilitate appropriate timing and continuity across care settings. Operationally, digital tools (including electronic checklists, shared registries, and automated reminders) can help document vaccination status, prompt clinicians at key decision points, and improve completion rates of the two-dose schedule. In parallel, targeted patient counseling and staff training are essential to address common barriers, such as competing priorities during treatment initiation, concerns regarding safety during therapy, and missed opportunities due to fragmented care.

From a clinical perspective, the oncology treatment context strongly influences adherence and schedule completion. This underscores the value of offering vaccination early in the care trajectory, when patients are clinically stable and before periods of anticipated cytopenia. In our real-world experience, RZV administration was feasible during routine outpatient oncology care, with no serious vaccine-related adverse events documented in routine clinical records. No clinically documented HZ episodes were observed during follow-up; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered evidence of vaccine effectiveness, given the limited sample size, retrospective design, and lack of a non-vaccinated comparator group. These findings support early, individualized vaccination strategies as a practical supportive care approach aimed at reducing missed opportunities for HZ prevention and preserving treatment continuity.
