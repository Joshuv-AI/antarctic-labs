Tractability versus curse of dimensionality for geometric L p L_{p} -discrepancies
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
This paper studies tractability versus the curse of dimensionality for several geometric L p L_{p} -discrepancies through a unified discrepancy–integration duality framework, where worst case integration errors in suitable function spaces equal the corresponding discrepancies. A general lower bound method for non-negative linear rules in spaces under broad tensor-product assumptions establishes exponential information complexity in the dimension d d , yielding the curse of dimensionality for the respective discrepancy. We complement this overview by new results on discrepancy–integration duality and the curse of dimensionality for the periodic L p L_{p} -discrepancy. The current state of research on this general problem is summarized in a clearly laid out table, which also highlights the remaining open questions.



Keywords:
Discrepancy, numerical integration, curse of dimensionality, tractability, quasi-Monte Carlo

1
Geometric Discrepancy

For a set
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
consisting of
N
points in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
the local discrepancy with respect to a measurable test set
C\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(C):=\frac{|\{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\in C\}|}{N}-{\rm volume}(C).

Choosing a suitable class
\mathcal{C}
of test sets, that are suitably parametrized, the local discrepancy can be considered as a function of this parameter which is usually emphasized by the nomenclature “local discrepancy function”. Taking a norm of the local discrepancy functions leads to the notion of a (geometric) discrepancy with respect to the given class
\mathcal{C}
of test sets. This yields a quantitative measure for the uniformity of the point set
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\mathcal{C}
. The term “geometric” appears in
[
15
, Sections 1.2 and 1.3]
and refers to underlying geometric classes
\mathcal{C}
of test sets.

Many notions of discrepancy are based on a suitable
L_{p}
-norm. There are a lot of examples in literature. In the present paper we consider the prominent notions of anchored/quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy, a concept that contains the star- and centered discrepancies as special cases, and unanchored
L_{p}
-discrepancy, such as the extreme- and periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy.

In applications (numerical analysis) one usually studies a slightly more general concept of discrepancy with nodes weighted by reals
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
. In this sense the local discrepancy is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(C):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}{\bf 1}_{C}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})-{\rm volume}(C).

If
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, we speak about “QMC weights” and denote the corresponding set by
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm qmc}}
, then we are in the classical case, i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{{\rm qmc}}}(C)=\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(C)
.

Let
D_{N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\|
be any (geometric) discrepancy, where the norm is not yet specified (often it is an
L_{p}
norm). Then for
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
the
N
-th minimal discrepancy in dimension
d
is defined as

{\rm disc}(N,d):=\inf_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}D_{N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}),

where the infimum is extended over all
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
and any real weights
\mathcal{A}
. We write
{\rm disc}^{+}
, if the infimum is extended over all
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
but exclusively over non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
, and
{\rm disc}^{{\rm qmc}}
if we allow only QMC weights and extend the infimum only over
N
-point sets
\mathcal{P}
.

Define further
{\rm disc}(0,d):=\|{\rm Vol}\|
as the norm of the volume functional
{\rm Vol}:C\mapsto{\rm volume}(C)
. We interpret this quantity as the discrepancy of the empty set and call it therefore the “initial discrepancy”. The inverse of discrepancy is defined as the minimal number of points that is needed in order to reduce the initial discrepancy by a factor of
\varepsilon
. In more detail, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we define

N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}:{\rm disc}^{\bullet}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,{\rm disc}(0,d)\},

where
\bullet\in\{\text{blank},+,{\rm qmc}\}
.
A central question is the growth rate of
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}

and/or
d\rightarrow\infty
(i.e., when
d+\varepsilon^{-1}\rightarrow\infty
). Such questions are typically studied in the field “Information Based Complexity” (see
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
).

If
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
grows at least exponentially fast in
d
for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
more precisely, if there exists a real
C>1
such that
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}

for infinitely many
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then the discrepancy (for arbitrary weights or non-negative weights or QMC-weights, respectively, depending on
\bullet\in\{\text{blank},+,{\rm qmc}\}
) is said to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. Of course, this is a very unpleasant behavior. Naturally, one would prefer a subexponential growth rate of
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
in
d
and
\varepsilon^{-1}
for
d+\varepsilon^{-1}\rightarrow\infty
. In this case a discrepancy is said to be tractable and there are various notions of tractability in order to classify the growth rate of
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)
more accurately. For example, polynomially tractability means that there exist numbers
C,\sigma>0
and
\tau\geq 0
such that
N_{{\rm disc}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d)\leq Cd^{\tau}\varepsilon^{-\sigma}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. If this holds even for
\tau=0
, then one speaks about strong polynomial tractability.

To prove for a discrepancy the curse of dimensionality, one needs general lower bounds for arbitrary sets of points and weights. Conversely, to prove tractability, a good upper bound for specific sets of points and weights suffices. Recently, we have developed a useful method to prove the curse of dimensionality for various geometric
L_{p}
-discrepancies (see
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
). This method, which is based on the duality between discrepancy and integration, will be presented in a general context in Section
3
. But first (Section
2
) we explain the framework of a “discrepancy–integration duality”. In Section
4
we present several concrete examples. Most of the results are scattered across various publications. Here we provide a systematic overview with precise references or proofs, if no reference is available. In the final Section
5
, we summarize everything again in a clearly laid out table, which also clearly shows the remaining open questions.

2
Discrepancy–integration duality

Often a discrepancy is related to the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) integration problem in the sense that there exists a suitable normed space of functions over
[0,1]^{d}
such that the worst-case error of a QMC rule for this space is exactly a suitable (geometric) discrepancy. Historically, the famous Koksma-Hlawka inequality was the first result in this direction (see
[
12
,
13
]
). We call such a framework a “discrepancy–integration duality” and we call the corresponding integration problem a dual integration problem.

We consider linear rules based on a point set
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
and corresponding real (integration) weights
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
of the form

Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\quad\mbox{for functions $f:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$.}

(1)

Sometimes we restrict ourselves to non-negative weights
a_{k}>0
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, which is indicated by the notation
\mathcal{A}^{+}
. A QMC rule is the special case of
a_{k}=1/N
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. We indicate this by the notation
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm qmc}}

Assume there exists a Banach space
(\mathcal{F}_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
of measurable functions over
[0,1]^{d}
for which we consider integration

I(f):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\qquad\mbox{for }f\in\mathcal{F}_{d}

by means of linear rules or QMC rules
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}
, such that the worst-case error

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};\mathcal{F}_{d}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{d}\atop\|f\|_{d}\leq 1}|I(f)-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f)|

satisfies

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};\mathcal{F}_{d})=D_{N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Then we call this a “discrepancy–integration duality” and we call integration in
\mathcal{F}_{d}
the dual integration problem to the discrepancy
D_{N}
.

A discrepancy–integration duality assigns an important interpretation to the discrepancy beyond its purely geometric definition, thereby enabling important applications in numerical analysis. A detailed discussion for the
L_{2}
case can be found in
[
23
, Section 9.6]
. On the other hand, a discrepancy–integration duality is also a very useful tool for making theoretical statements about the discrepancy through the study of the integration problem itself. This approach has been pursued in several papers, one particular example is
[
9
, Proof of Theorem 5]
. Here we present our most recent approach to proving lower bounds for worst-case integration errors/discrepancies, which has led to new insights into the curse of dimensionality for various geometric
L_{p}
-discrepancies.

3
Lower bound method

For an integration problem over
(\mathcal{F}_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
the
N
-th minimal error is defined as

e(N,d)=e(N,d;\mathcal{F}_{d}):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};\mathcal{F}_{d}),

where the minimum is extended over all
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
with corresponding weights
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, if we restrict to non-negative weights we write

e^{+}(N,d)=e^{+}(N,d;\mathcal{F}_{d}):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d}),

and in the QMC-case we write

e^{{\rm qmc}}(N,d)=e^{{\rm qmc}}(N,d;\mathcal{F}_{d}):=\min_{\mathcal{P}}e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{{\rm qmc}}};\mathcal{F}_{d}).

The initial error is

e(0,d)=e(0,d;\mathcal{F}_{d}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{d}\atop\|f\|_{d}\leq 1}|I(f)|.

Furthermore, we define the information complexity as

N_{{\rm int}}^{\bullet}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}:e^{\bullet}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)\}\quad\mbox{for }\varepsilon\in(0,1),\ d\in\mathbb{N},

where
\bullet\in\{{\rm blank},+,{\rm qmc}\}
. Obviously,
N_{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{{\rm int}}^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N_{{\rm int}}^{{\rm qmc}}(\varepsilon,d)
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the following holds:

1.

For functions
f\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
of the form
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{d}(x_{d})
we have
\|f\|_{d}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{1}\cdots\|f_{d}\|_{1}
, and

2.

e(0,d)=e(0,1)^{d}
.

3.

There exists a non-negative function
h\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
, such that
I(h/\|h\|_{1})=e(0,1)
.

4.

There exists for every
y\in[0,1]
a function
s_{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
such that
s_{y}\geq 0
,
s_{y}(y)=h(y)
, and

A:=\inf_{y\in[0,1]}\frac{I(h)}{I(s_{y})}>1\quad\mbox{ and }\quad B:=\inf_{y\in[0,1]}\frac{\|h\|_{1}}{\|s_{y}\|_{1}}>1.

Then, for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
we have

N_{{\rm int}}^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}(1-2\varepsilon),

where
C:=\min(A,B)>1
. In particular, the integration problem (for non-negative weigths and especially for QMC weights) suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Remark 1
.

Assumptions 1 and 2 are readily satisfied for tensor-product problems. The function
h
from Assumption 3 is usually referred to as
worst-case function
since its integral normalized by the norm of
h
equals the initial error in dimension
d=1
. Together with Assumption 2 this implies that for
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=h(x_{1})\cdots h(x_{d})
we have
I(h_{d}/\|h\|_{1}^{d})=e(0,d)
. The functions
s_{y}

from Assumption 4 can be considered as “splines”: They have small integrals and norms, but fulfill
s_{y}(y)=h(y)
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Consider a linear algorithm
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}
of the form (
1
) based in nodes
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1]^{d}
and with non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
let
x_{k,j}
be the
j
-th coordinate of the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define functions

P_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=s_{x_{k,1}}(x_{1})s_{x_{k,2}}(x_{2})\cdots s_{x_{k,d}}(x_{d}),\quad\mbox{for ${\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}$.}

Consider the two functions
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=h(x_{1})\cdots h(x_{d})
and
f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
Since
Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}
uses only non-negative weights we have

\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(f^{*})=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}P_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}s_{x_{k,1}}(x_{k,1})\cdots s_{x_{k,d}}(x_{k,d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}h(x_{k,1})\cdots h(x_{k,d})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(h_{d}).

Now for real
y
we use the notation
(y)_{+}:=\max(y,0)
. Then we have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d})\geq\frac{(I(h_{d})-I(f^{*}))_{+}}{2\max(\|h\|_{1}^{d},\|f^{*}\|_{d})},

(2)

which is trivially true if
I(h_{d})\leq I(f^{*})
and which is easily shown if
I(h_{d})>I(f^{*})
, because then

\displaystyle(I(h_{d})-I(f^{*}))_{+}\leq

\displaystyle I(h_{d})-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(h_{d})+Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}(f^{*})-I(f^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{d}\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d})+\|f^{*}\|_{d}\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\max(\|h\|_{1}^{d},\|f^{*}\|_{d})\,e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d}),

where we used that
\|h_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h\|_{1}^{d}
. This implies (
2
).

From the triangle inequality we obtain

\|f^{*}\|_{d}\leq\frac{N\,\|h\|_{1}^{d}}{B^{d}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad I(f^{*})\leq N\left(\frac{I(h)}{A}\right)^{d}.

Inserting into (
2
) yields

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}};\mathcal{F}_{d})\geq\frac{I(h)^{d}(1-NA^{-d})_{+}}{2\|h\|_{1}^{d}\max(1,NB^{-d})}=\frac{e(0,d)(1-NA^{-d})_{+}}{2\max(1,NB^{-d})},

where we used that
(I(h)/\|h\|_{1})^{d}=I(h/\|h\|_{1})^{d}=e(0,1)^{d}=e(0,d)
.
This yields

e^{+}(N,d)\geq\frac{e(0,d)(1-NA^{-d})_{+}}{2\max(1,NB^{-d})}.

Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and assume that
e^{+}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)
. This implies that

2\varepsilon\max(1,NB^{-d})\geq(1-NA^{-d})_{+}.

Assumption 4 yields
C:=\min(A,B)>1
. If
N\leq C^{d}
, then we obtain

\displaystyle 1-NA^{-d}=

\displaystyle(1-NA^{-d})_{+}\leq 2\varepsilon\max(1,NB^{-d})=2\varepsilon.

Hence
N\geq A^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)\geq C^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
. If
N\geq C^{d}
, then we trivially have
N\geq C^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
. This yields
N_{{\rm int}}^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
, as claimed. ∎∎

Examples

We present several important examples of
L_{p}
-discrepancies where the method from Sec.
3
was successfully applied. In the case of periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy, we can also add a new result. Throughout, let
p,q\in[1,\infty]
be Hölder conjugates, i.e.,
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1

4.1
Anchored discrepancies

A general representation of the anchored discrepancies can best be introduced using the concept of quadrant discrepancy,
which uses the following class of test sets: Fix
a\in[0,1]
. For
t\in[0,1]
define the intervals

Q(t)=Q_{a}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[0,t)&\mbox{if }t\leq a,\\
{[}t,1)&\mbox{if }t>a.\end{array}\right.

Since
a
is fixed we will suppress the index
a
in the notation. In the multidimensional case, for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
we define
Q({\boldsymbol{t}}):=Q(t_{1})\times\ldots\times Q(t_{d})
. Obviously, the volume is
\lambda(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(t_{j}\,{\bf 1}_{[0,a]}(t_{j})+(1-t_{j})\,{\bf 1}_{(a,1]}(t_{j}))
. Using as test sets the class
\mathcal{C}=\{Q({\boldsymbol{t}}):{\boldsymbol{t}}\in[0,1]^{d}\}
, the
a
-quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy is defined as

L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{d})}\quad\mbox{ for }p\in[1,\infty].

The initial
a
-quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy equals

{\rm disc}_{p}^{\boxplus_{a}}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{d/p}&\mbox{for }p\in[1,\infty),\\[5.0pt]
\max(a,1-a)^{d}&\mbox{for }p=\infty.\end{array}\right.

Note that the initial
a
-quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy is exponentially small in the dimension
d
for almost all instances. The only exception is the case
p=\infty
,
a\in\{0,1\}
, where the initial discrepancy equals 1.

The general definition of quadrant discrepancy includes two particularly important special cases:

•

Star discrepancy:
For
a=1
we obtain the classical star
L_{p}
-discrepancy, which is the most prominent notion in discrepancy theory. We denote it by
L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
for
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
with corresponding weights
\mathcal{A}
.
Obviously, in the limiting case
a=1
one would hardly speak of a quadrant, but purely formally the star
L_{p}
-discrepancy falls under the term quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy.

•

Centered discrepancy:
For
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
the notion of centered
L_{p}
-discrepancy is covered. For
p=2
centered
L_{2}
-discrepancy was studied by Hickernell
[
7
]
. See also
[
21
]
and
[
23
, Sec. 9.8.2 and 11.4.3]
.

The general definition of quadrant discrepancy is closely related to another discrepancy concept, which is also categorized under the umbrella term of anchored discrepancy (see
[
11
, Example 1]
and
[
23
, Section 9.5.3]
) and which uses so-called anchored intervals as test sets. Again, fix
a\in[0,1]
, the anchor. A one-dimensional, in
a
anchored interval is defined as the set
J(t):=[\min(t,a),\max(t,a))
for
t\in[0,1]
. In the multidimensional case, for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
define
J({\boldsymbol{t}}):=J(t_{1})\times\ldots\times J(t_{d})
. Obviously,
the volume is
\lambda(J({\boldsymbol{t}}))=\prod_{j=1}^{d}|t_{j}-a|
. Using as test sets the class
\mathcal{C}=\{J({\boldsymbol{t}}):{\boldsymbol{t}}\in[0,1]^{d}\}
, the
a
-anchored
L_{p}
-discrepancy of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

L_{p,N}^{\pitchfork_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(J(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{d})}\quad\mbox{ for }p\in[1,\infty].

The initial
a
-anchored
L_{p}
-discrepancy equals

{\rm disc}_{p}^{\pitchfork_{a}}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{d/p}&\mbox{for }p\in[1,\infty),\\[5.0pt]
\max(a,1-a)^{d}&\mbox{for }p=\infty,\end{array}\right.

which coincides with the initial
a
-quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy.

Obviously, the end point
a=0
recovers the star
L_{p}
-discrepancy and
a=1
the
0
-quadrant
L_{p}
-discrepancy. In general, the close relationship with quadrant discrepancy is described by the following proposition.
The presented result in its form and generality is new. The special case
p=2
is implicitly contained in
[
23
, Sections 9.5.3 and 9.5.4]
and derived there by means of a discrepancy–integration duality with regard to integration in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

Proposition 2
.

For
a\in[0,1]
and a point set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
define
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{a}:=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\pmod{1}:{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a,\ldots,a)
(vector of length
d
). Then we have

L_{p,N}^{\pitchfork_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{a},\mathcal{A}).

In particular

{\rm disc}_{p}^{\pitchfork_{a}}(N,d)={\rm disc}_{p}^{\boxplus_{a}}(N,d).

Proof.

To avoid confusing notation problems, we will restrict ourselves to the one-dimensional case. For
x\in[0,1]
we write
\overline{x}:=a-x\pmod{1}
. Then for any point
x_{k}\in\mathcal{P}
we have
x_{k}\in J(t)
if and only if
\overline{x}_{k}\in Q(\overline{t})
. Furthermore,
\lambda(J(t))=\lambda(Q(\overline{t}))
. This yields
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(J(t))=\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\overline{t}))
, from which we obtain the result. ∎∎

Proposition
2
tells us that all statements about the

N
-th minimal quadrant discrepancy (such as curse/tractability) apply directly to the
N
-th minimal anchored discrepancy and vice versa. Furthermore, the discrepancy–integration duality from the forthcoming Theorem
3
can be easily re-written in terms of

-anchored

Discrepancy–integration duality:
For
q\in[1,\infty]
and
a\in[0,1]
let

W_{a,q}^{1}([0,1]):=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\ f\,\mbox{ abs. cont., $f(a)=0$ and}\,f^{\prime}\in L_{q}([0,1])\},

equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{1,q}:=\|f^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}}
. The
d
-fold tensor product space is

W_{a,q}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}}:=W_{a,q}^{1}([0,1])\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{a,q}^{1}([0,1])

equipped with the crossnorm
\|f\|_{d,q}:=\|f^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}\|_{L_{q}}
. Then a discrepancy–integration duality is given in the following way:

Theorem 3
.

We have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};W_{a,q}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})=L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In particular

e(N,d;W_{a,q}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})={\rm disc}_{p}^{\boxplus_{a}}(N,d).

(3)

A proof for general
a
and
p=2
can be found in
[
23
, Sec. 9.5.4]
. For general
p
and
a=1
see
[

, Sec. 9.8.1]
and for general
p
and
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
see
[

]
or
[

Proof of Theorem
3
.

For a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
let
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}=:[d]
be the set of indices
j
for which
x_{j}\leq a
. Hence, for
j\in\mathfrak{u}^{c}
, where
\mathfrak{u}^{c}:=[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}
, we have
x_{j}>a
. We write
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathfrak{u}}
for the projection of the vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
to the coordinates whose indices belong to
\mathfrak{u}
. Furthermore, let
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a,\ldots,a)
(length
d
). Then

f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}f^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}.

Let
\mu:=\lambda-\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}
where
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}
is the Dirac measure concentrated in
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
and
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
and let
L(f):=I(f)-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f)
. Then we have

\displaystyle L(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}f^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}f^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\int_{[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{t}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{\mathfrak{u}},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}}]}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}},{\boldsymbol{1}}_{\mathfrak{u}^{c}}]}f^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\int_{Q({\boldsymbol{t}})}\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\mu(Q(t))\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}.

Hence

|L(f)|\leq\|f^{(1,\ldots,1)}\|_{L_{q}}\|\mu(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}=\|f\|_{d,q}L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}),

because

\mu(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))=\lambda(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))-\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}{\bf 1}_{Q({\boldsymbol{t}})}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=-\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))

and hence

\|\mu(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}=L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

This yields

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};W_{a,q}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})\leq L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In order to prove equality let first
p,q\in(1,\infty)
. Set

f_{\ast}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=-\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p-1}}\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{x}}]}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))|^{p-2}\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}.

Then we have
f_{\ast}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
whenever at least one component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
equals
a
, and
f_{\ast}^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{x}})=-\frac{|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{x}}))|^{p-2}\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{x}}))}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p-1}}
. Hence

\displaystyle L(f_{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f_{\ast}^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{t}})\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p-1}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p}}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p-1}}=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}=L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Furthermore, using
(p-1)q=p
for Hölder conjugates
p,q\in(1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\|f_{\ast}\|_{d,q}^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f_{\ast}^{(1,\ldots,1)}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))|^{(p-1)q}}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{(p-1)q}}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p}}\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q(\cdot))\|_{L_{p}}^{p}=1.

This yields
e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};W_{a,q}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})\geq L_{p,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
. Together with the upper bound we obtain the desired result.

For
p=1
,
q=\infty
choose
f_{\ast}(x):=-\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{x}}]}{\rm sgn}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}})))\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}
. Then, again
L(f_{\ast})=L_{1,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
and
\|f_{\ast}\|_{1,\infty}=1
such that the result follows in the same way as above.

For
p=\infty
,
q=1
and
\varepsilon>0
set

E_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{t}}\in[0,1]^{d}:|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))|\geq L_{\infty,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon\right\}

and

f_{\ast}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=-\int_{[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{x}}]}\frac{{\rm sgn}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))){\bf 1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{t}})}{\lambda(E_{\varepsilon})}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}.

Then,
f_{\ast}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
whenever at least one component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
equals
a
, and
f_{\ast}^{(1,\ldots,1)}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\frac{{\rm sgn}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{x}}))){\bf 1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\lambda(E_{\varepsilon})}
, which yields
\|f_{\ast}\|_{d,1}=\|f_{\ast}^{(1,\ldots,1)}\|_{L_{1}}=1
and

L(f_{\ast})=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{{\bf 1}_{E_{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{t}})}{\lambda(E_{\varepsilon})}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))|\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\geq L_{\infty,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
yields
e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};W_{a,1}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})\geq L_{\infty,N}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
and this finishes the proof. ∎∎

Tractablility vs. curse of dimensionality:
We distinguish the cases
a\in\{0,1\}
and
a\in(0,1)
.

a=1
:

This is the case of classical star
L_{p}
-discrepancy. For
p\in(1,\infty)
the star
L_{p}
-discrepancy for non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality. This was shown in
[
19
]
with the method from Theorem
1
based on the worst-case function
h(x)=1-(1-x)^{p}
. For
p=2
this was already shown much earlier by Woźniakowski
[
27
]
(nowadays this paper, together with
[
6
]
could be seen as the initiation of the whole story considered in the present paper). The case
a=0
is almost identical.

The case
p=1
is still open.

For
p=\infty
the star
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is polynomially tractable as shown first in a
paper by Heinrich, Novak, Wasilkowski and Woźniakowski
[
6
]
(the result even holds for QMC weights
\mathcal{A}^{{\rm qmc}}
). More precisely there exists a
C>0
such that

N_{\infty}^{\ast,{\rm qmc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq Cd\varepsilon^{-2}\quad\mbox{ for all $d\in\mathbb{N}$ and $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$.}

(4)

The linear dependence in
d
cannot be improved, see
[
6
, Theorem 8]
or
[
8
, Theorem 1]
for details.
One may choose
C=6.067\ldots
, see
[
26
]
.

a\in(0,1)
:

For
p\in[1,\infty)
the
a
-anchored
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality (here the case
p=1
is included). This was shown in
[
18
, Corollary 2]
, where the proof is based on the worst-case function
h(x)={\bf 1}_{[0,a]}(x)(a^{p}-x^{p})+{\bf 1}_{[a,1]}(x)((1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p})
.

For
p=\infty
and
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
the
\frac{1}{2}
-quadrant (=centered)
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy suffers also from the curse of dimensionality. This follows immediately from the fact that for
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
with
N<2^{d}
at least one of the
2^{d}
quadrants of the unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
remains empty and hence
{\rm disc}_{\infty}^{\boxplus_{1/2}}(N,d)=2^{-d}={\rm disc}_{\infty}^{\boxplus_{1/2}}(0,d)
(this is already pointed out in
[
21
, p. 406]
and
[
23
, p. 177]
).

The case
p=\infty
but
a\not\in\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}
was still open, see the following remark.

Remark 2
.

Here we consider the “intermediate case”
p=\infty
and
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, where the problem is not polynomially tractable
but there is also no curse since the complexity is polynomial for each fixed

\varepsilon>0
; of course the case
a\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
is identical.

We start with the lower bound and use the discrepancy-integration duality from Theorem
3
, i.e., we consider integration in
W_{a,1}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}}
.
For
d=1
we can define two fooling functions in
W_{a,1}^{1}
with disjoint supports
and integrals arbitrarily close to
a
and
1-a
, i.e., the normalized integrals are close to 1 and
\frac{1-a}{a}
,
respectively, since the initial error is exactly
a
in the present case.
For
d>1
we take tensor products and, for
\varepsilon_{k}=\left(\frac{1-a}{a}\right)^{k}
and
d\geq k
, we obtain

{d\choose k}
functions with disjoint supports and integrals close to
\varepsilon_{k}
,
hence
N_{\infty}^{\boxplus_{a}}(\varepsilon_{k},d)=N_{{\rm int}}^{W_{a,1}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}}}(\varepsilon_{k},d)\geq{d\choose k}
,
since for any
N
-element set in
[0,1)^{d}
with less then
{d\choose k}
points, the support of at least one of the functions contains no point of the point set.
It follows that the integration problem, and therefore the
L_{\infty}^{\boxplus_{a}}
-discrepancy is not polynomially tractable.

Now we consider the upper bound for a given
d
and normalized error
\varepsilon_{k}=\left(\frac{1-a}{a}\right)^{k}
.
By the break point
a
we have, in dimension
d
, altogether
2^{d}
subproblems, defined on subcubes
\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}[a,1)\times\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}[0,a)
of
[0,1)^{d}
for
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
of volume
(1-a)^{|\mathfrak{u}|}a^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}
whose normalization is
\left(\frac{1-a}{a}\right)^{|\mathfrak{u}|}
. We have to consider only those problems with initial error at least
\varepsilon_{k}
, i.e., those
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
for which
|\mathfrak{u}|\leq k
. The number of these problems is
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}{d\choose\ell}
, which is a polynomial in
d
of degree
k
. No sampling point is needed in all the other subcubes. Due to the known upper bound
N\leq Cd\varepsilon^{-2}
for the classical star
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy from (
4
), we need at most
N\leq\widetilde{C}d^{k+1}\varepsilon_{k}^{-2}
function values in the large subcubes to solve the problem, with another constant
\widetilde{C}>0
. Hence the number of points increases only polynomially in
d
, as long as
\varepsilon
is fixed.

4.2
Unanchored discrepancies

Unanchored discrepancies are characterized by the fact that all rectangles in the hypercube or on the torus are used as test sets.

4.2.1
Extreme discrepancy

The most prominent representative in the class of unanchored discrepancies is the extreme discrepancy, which uses arbitrary axis-parallel boxes as test sets. Let
\mathbb{D}_{d}:=\{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in[0,1]^{d},{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq{\boldsymbol{y}}\}
, where
\leq
is understood component-wise, and, for
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\mathbb{D}_{d}
let
[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=[x_{1},y_{1})\times[x_{2},y_{2})\times\ldots\times[x_{d},y_{d})
with volume
\lambda([{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))=(y_{1}-x_{1})\cdots(y_{d}-x_{d})
. Using as test sets the class
\mathcal{C}=\{[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\mathbb{D}_{d}\}
, the extreme
L_{p}
-discrepancy of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}([\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}\quad\mbox{ for }p\in[1,\infty].

The initial extreme
L_{p}
-discrepancy is

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext}}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}((p+1)(p+2))^{-d/p}&\mbox{for }p\in[1,\infty),\\
1&\mbox{for }p=\infty.\end{array}\right.

Discrepancy–integration duality:
Define a linear operator
T_{d}
on
L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})
via

(T_{d}c)({\boldsymbol{t}}):=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{d}}c({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,{\bf 1}_{[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}]}({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\quad\text{for }c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}).

(5)

Define

F_{d,q}:=\{f\ :\ c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}),\ f=T_{d}c\}

and equip this space with the norm

\|f\|_{F_{d,q},{\rm box}}:=\inf\{\|c\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d})}:\;c\in L_{q}(\mathbb{D}_{d}),\ f=T_{d}c\}.

(6)

Then a discrepancy–integration duality is known from
[
20
, Theorem 2]
:

Theorem 4
.

We have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q})\;=\;L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In particular

e(N,d;F_{d,q})={\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm ext}}(N,d).

(7)

Tractablility vs. curse of dimensionality:
For
p\in(1,\infty)
the extreme
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality. This was shown in
[
20
]
with the method from Theorem
1
based on the worst-case function
h(x)=1-x^{p+1}-(1-x)^{p+1}
. The case
p=1
is still open. For
p=\infty
the extreme
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is polynomially tractable as shown in
[
6
]
(see also
[
5
]
).

4.2.2
Periodic discrepancy

Another important representative in the class of unanchored discrepancies is the periodic discrepancy, which is also known as wrap-around discrepancy or torus-discrepancy. Here periodic rectangles are used as test sets. For
x,y\in[0,1]
set

I(x,y):=\begin{cases}[x,y)&\text{if $x\leq y$},\\
[0,y)\cup[x,1)&\text{if $x>y$,}\end{cases}

and for
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in[0,1]^{2d}
we set
B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=I(x_{1},y_{1})\times\ldots\times I(x_{d},y_{d})
with volume
\lambda(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(y_{j}-x_{j}\pmod{1})
. Using as test sets the class
\mathcal{C}=\{B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in[0,1]^{2d}\}
, the periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}):=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{2d})}\quad\mbox{ for }p\in[1,\infty].

The initial periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy is

{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm per}}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(p+1)^{-d/p}&\mbox{for }p\in[1,\infty),\\
1&\mbox{for }p=\infty,\end{array}\right.

which coincides with the initial star
L_{p}
-discrepancy.

It is easily seen (or see
[
14
]
) that
L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})\geq L_{p,N}^{{\rm ext}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
for every
p\geq 1
.

Discrepancy–integration duality:
First consider the special case
p=q=2
,
for which a discrepancy–integration duality is well known. Let

H^{1}_{{\rm per},2}([0,1]):=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\ f\,\mbox{abs. cont., $f(0)=f(1)$ and}\,f^{\prime}\in L_{2}([0,1])\}

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{1,2}^{{\rm per}}:=\left(3\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|f^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The
d
-fold tensor product space is

H_{{\rm per},2}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}}:=H_{{\rm per},2}^{1}([0,1])\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{{\rm per},2}^{1}([0,1])

equipped with the corresponding crossnorm
\|f\|_{d,2}^{{\rm per}}
. Then we know from
[
10
]
:

Theorem 5
.

We have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};H_{{\rm per},2}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})=L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In particular,

e(N,d;H_{{\rm per},2}^{{\boldsymbol{1}}})={\rm disc}_{2}^{{\rm per}}(N,d).

Now consider the case of general Hölder conjugates
p,q\in[1,\infty]
. Similarly to (
5
) define a linear operator
T_{d}^{{\rm per}}
on
L_{q}([0,1]^{2d})
via

(T_{d}^{{\rm per}}c)({\boldsymbol{t}}):=\int_{[0,1]^{2d}}c({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,{\bf 1}_{B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})}({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\quad\text{for }c\in L_{q}([0,1]^{2d}).

Define

F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}}:=\{f\ :\ c\in L_{q}([0,1]^{2d}),\,f=T_{d}^{{\rm per}}c\}

and equip this space with the norm

\|f\|_{F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}}}:=\inf\{\|c\|_{L_{q}([0,1]^{2d})}:\;c\in L_{q}([0,1]^{2d}),\ f=T_{d}^{{\rm per}}c\}.

Remark 3
.

For every
c\in L_{q}([0,1]^{2d})
the function
f=T_{d}^{{\rm per}}c
is well defined a.e., belongs to
L_{q}([0,1]^{d})
, is absolutely continuous in each variable, and is periodic with period 1 in every coordinate. For example, in the univariate case,

(T_{1}^{{\rm per}}c)(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{y}^{1}c(x,y)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{x}c(x,y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x=(T_{1}^{{\rm per}}c)(1).

Now a discrepancy–integration duality is given in the following way:

Theorem 6
.

We have

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}})=L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In particular,

e(N,d;F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}})={\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm per}}(N,d).

Proof.

The result and its proof are new but they are similar to the one of Theorem
3
and the one of Theorem
4
in
[
20
]
. Therefore we provide only the cornerstones. Let
L(f):=I(f)-Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(f)
and
\mu=\lambda-\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}}

as in the proof of Theorem
3
.
Then, for
f=T_{d}^{\rm per}c\in F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}}
, we have
L(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}\mu({\boldsymbol{t}})
. From here, using Fubini, we derive

L(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{2d}}c({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

(8)

and then Hölder’s inequality yields
|L(f)|\leq\|c\|_{L_{q}([0,1]^{2d})}\,L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
. Taking the infimum over all representations of
f
yields
|L(f)|\leq\|f\|_{F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}}}\,L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
and hence

e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}})\leq L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

In order to prove equality we first assume
p,q\in(1,\infty)
. If
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\equiv 0
then
L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=0
and also
L(f)\equiv 0
,
so equality holds. Assume now
\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\not\equiv 0
and define

c^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\frac{|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))|^{p-2}\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{2d})}^{p-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\ast}:=T_{d}^{{\rm per}}c^{\ast}

(9)

so that
\|c^{\ast}\|_{L_{q}([0,1]^{2d})}=1
and then
\|f^{\ast}\|_{F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}}}\leq 1
by definition of the norm. By (
8
),

\displaystyle|L(f^{\ast})|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{2d})}^{p-1}}\int_{[0,1]^{2d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{p}([0,1]^{2d})}=L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}).

Thus,
e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,q}^{{\rm per}})\geq L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
. Combining with the upper bound yields the identity.

For the case
p=1
we take
c^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})={\rm sgn}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})))
and obtain
again
|L(f^{\ast})|=\|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B(\cdot,\cdot))\|_{L_{1}([0,1]^{2d})}=L_{1,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
.

For
p=\infty
,
q=1
and
\varepsilon>0
, define

E_{\varepsilon}:=\{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in[0,1]^{2d}:|\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))|\geq L_{\infty,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon\}.

Assume
\lambda(E_{\varepsilon})>0
and define
c_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):={\rm sgn}(\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})))\,{\mathbf{1}}_{E_{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})/\lambda(E_{\varepsilon})
. Then
\|c_{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{1}([0,1]^{2d})}=1
and

\int_{[0,1]^{2d}}c_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,\Delta_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}(B({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}))\,{\rm d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\geq L_{\infty,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})-\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
gives
e(Q_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}};F_{d,1})=L_{\infty,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})
, as claimed. ∎∎

Tractablility vs. curse of dimensionality:
It is known from
[
2
]
that the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality. We can extend this result to all
p\in(1,\infty)
, at least for non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

Let
{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm per},+}(N,d):=\inf_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+}}L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+})
, where the infimum is extended over all
N
-element sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
and all corresponding non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
, and let

N_{p}^{{\rm per},+}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\left\{N\in\mathbb{N}:{\rm disc}_{p}^{{\rm per},+}(N,d)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{(p+1)^{d/p}}\right\}.

Theorem 7
.

For every
p
in
(1,\infty)
there exists a real
C_{p}
that is strictly larger than 1, such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
we have

N_{p}^{{\rm per},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon).

In particular, for all
p
in
(1,\infty)
the periodic
L_{p}
-discrepancy for non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
be a point set in
[0,1)^{d}
and let
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in[0,1)^{d}
. Then we define
\mathcal{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}:=\{\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\},\ldots,\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\}\}
, where
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\}
denotes component-wise addition modulo one. It is known that

L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}(L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}},\mathcal{A}))^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)^{1/p}.

This is the
L_{p}
-version of
[
2
, Proposition 1]
for
p=2
whose proof immediately applies to the general case. In particular, for every
N
-element set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
there exists a
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\ast}\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})\geq L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\ast}},\mathcal{A})
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and assume that
\mathcal{P}
is an
N
-element set in
[0,1)^{d}
such that
L_{p,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}^{+})\leq\varepsilon\,(p+1)^{-d/p}
. Then there exists a
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\ast}\in[0,1]^{d}
such that

L_{p,N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\ast}},\mathcal{A}^{+})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{(p+1)^{d/p}}.

Then
[
19
, Theorem 3]
implies that
N\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
, where

C_{p}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p+1}{p}\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}\right)^{-1}>1.

Thus,
N_{p}^{{\rm per},+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)
, as claimed. ∎∎

For
p=\infty
the periodic
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is polynomially tractable, more precisely there exists a
C>0
such that

N_{\infty}^{{\rm per},{\rm qmc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq Cd\log(d+1)\varepsilon^{-2}\qquad\mbox{for all }d\in\mathbb{N}\mbox{ and }\varepsilon\in(0,1).

This follows from
[
6
, Theorem 4]
together with
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
, which states that the VC dimension of the class of periodic intervals is asymptotically
d\log_{2}d
.

The case
p=1
is still open.

5
Summary, open questions and further remarks

We summarize the situation (all instances for non-negative weights
\mathcal{A}^{+}
) in the following table, which also highlights the remaining open questions. For quadrant/anchored discrepancy we assume
a\in(0,1)
, since
a\in\{0,1\}
is covered by
the star discrepancy.

\begin{array}[]{l||c|c|c|c}\text{discrepancy}&\text{curse}&\text{PT}&\text{open}&\text{source}\\
\hline\cr L_{p}^{\ast}&p\in(1,\infty)&p=\infty&p=1&\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{NP25}{}{}]},\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hnww}{}{}]}\\
L_{p}^{\boxplus_{a}},L_{p}^{\pitchfork_{a}}&(p,a)\in[1,\infty)\times(0,1)&-&-&\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{NP25b}{}{}]},\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{NW01}{}{}]}\\
&\text{or }(p,a)=(\infty,\tfrac{1}{2})&&&\\
L_{p}^{{\rm ext}}&p\in(1,\infty)&p=\infty&p=1&\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{NP26a}{}{}]},\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hnww,Gne05}{}{}]}\\
L_{p}^{{\rm per}}&p\in(1,\infty)&p=\infty&p=1&\text{Thm.~\ref{thm4}},\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hnww,GLM}{}{}]}\end{array}

Also other norms of discrepancy functions are considered in the literature.
In
[
25
]
it is shown that the star discrepancy in the BMO seminorm suffers
from the curse of dimensionality. Curse vs. tractability of star discrepancy in Orlicz norms is studied in
[
3
]
. The spherical cap
L_{2}
-discrepancy is studied in
[
1
]
. Also the case of weighted discrepancy is studied intensively, see
[
17
]
and the references therein.

It would be interesting to identify other discrepancies/problems where the method from Section
3
can be successfully applied.

Furthermore, we mention that for
L_{p}
-discrepancies, if the Hölder conjugate
q
is an even number,
the method of decomposable kernels (see
[
23
, Chapter 11]
) can be generalized,
and in this way lower bounds and statements about the curse of dimensionality can be obtained–even
for arbitrary quadrature rules. For more details see
[
16
,
18
]
.

We stress that it is a great annoyance that we know so little about the
L_{1}
-discrepancies. This is the big open question that we pose in conclusion.

Open Question
.

Prove or disprove the curse of dimensionality for the star-, extreme-, or periodic
L_{1}
-discrepancy.
