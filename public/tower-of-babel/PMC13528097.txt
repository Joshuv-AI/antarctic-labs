Timing of anesthetic emergence and early postoperative recovery in older adults undergoing thoracic surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wei Wang, Chengya Huang, Zuojing Zhang, Yuwei Qiu, Jingxiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults are at heightened risk of adverse early postoperative outcomes, yet whether the time of day at which patients emerge from anesthesia influences these outcomes remains unclear. Introduction

Recovery from general anesthesia represents a physiologically vulnerable period, particularly in older adults who are at increased risk of delirium, prolonged post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay, and airway-related complications [1–4]. Age-related reductions in physiological reserve, altered drug pharmacokinetics, and delayed neurocognitive recovery may further increase susceptibility during this period [5]. In thoracic surgery, these risks may be amplified by the physiological stress of lung resection and one-lung ventilation.

With increasing surgical demand and operating room pressures, surgical schedules increasingly extend into evening hours to accommodate clinical workload and reduce waiting times [6]. Consequently, many patients now initiate anesthetic emergence and early postoperative recovery outside routine daytime hours [7, 8]. Prior studies examining after-hours surgery have reported increased risks of postoperative morbidity, mortality, and perioperative complications [9, 10]. However, most prior studies have focused on surgical start time or the intraoperative period as the primary exposure, rather than the timing of anesthetic emergence itself, a physiologically distinct phase in which patients must actively recover consciousness, ventilation, airway protective reflexes, and neurocognitive function. In addition, arousal, respiratory control, cognitive performance, and autonomic regulation exhibit circadian variation [11, 12]. Despite these observations, whether the timing of anesthetic emergence is independently associated with early postoperative outcomes in older surgical patients remains poorly characterized.

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate whether late-day anesthetic emergence, compared with daytime emergence, was associated with emergence delirium and other early postoperative outcomes in older adults undergoing thoracic surgery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Shanghai Chest Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, a high-volume tertiary referral center for thoracic surgery. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval number: IS25155). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500108924). The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [13].

Patient population

Consecutive patients undergoing elective non-cardiac thoracic surgery between January 1 and December 31, 2024, were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: age 65–90 years; elective thoracic surgery under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation; and planned admission to the PACU for postoperative recovery. Exclusion criteria included: PACU stay < 50 min (institutional practice mandates routine postoperative observation of approximately 1 h); gastrointestinal or airway endoscopic procedures; prolonged PACU stay attributable to identifiable non-emergence reasons, including administrative delay, resuscitation of critical illness, active bleeding management, or awaiting intraoperative pathology results; severe psychiatric illness; severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; or significant hearing or visual impairment that would preclude reliable delirium assessment.

Patients were classified into daytime (08:00–16:00) and late-day (16:00–24:00) groups according to the start time recorded in the standardized anesthetic emergence record. These intervals corresponded to routine daytime and late-day anesthetic emergence and early recovery periods in clinical practice at our institution.

Postoperative recovery protocols

Postoperative recovery followed institutional protocols in a dedicated PACU staffed by attending anesthesiologists and trained recovery nurses. PACU staffing, monitoring protocols, and recovery procedures were consistent across daytime and late-day periods. Standard monitoring included electrocardiography, invasive arterial pressure, pulse oximetry, end-tidal carbon dioxide, and core temperature monitoring.

After completion of surgery, patients were transferred directly to the PACU under continuous monitoring for postoperative recovery care. Neuromuscular blockade reversal was routinely performed upon PACU admission using sugammadex according to institutional clinical practice. Tracheal extubation was performed after predefined criteria had been met, including recovery of consciousness, ability to follow verbal commands, adequate spontaneous ventilation, and recovery of muscle strength.

Each patient was assigned a dedicated PACU nurse responsible for continuous physiological monitoring and documentation of recovery-related events under the supervision of attending anesthesiologists. Delirium assessment and post-extubation arterial blood gas analysis were routinely performed during early postoperative recovery, generally approximately 10 min after extubation. At least one structured delirium assessment was completed before PACU discharge, with additional assessments repeated when clinically indicated during the PACU stay. Patients identified with emergence delirium received supportive management, analgesia, and pharmacological intervention when indicated. Patients with persistent or severe symptoms were transferred to the ICU for further management, and delirium-related clinical events were documented in the PACU recovery record.

Patients were discharged from the PACU only after meeting institutional discharge criteria, including adequate recovery of consciousness, stable hemodynamic and respiratory status, adequate oxygenation without significant airway obstruction, satisfactory pain control with a numeric rating scale pain score ≤ 4, absence of active bleeding or major PACU adverse events, a modified Aldrete score ≥ 9, and completion of at least 1 h of postoperative observation. Final discharge from the PACU was confirmed by an attending anesthesiologist.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was emergence delirium during the PACU stay. Delirium screening was routinely performed by trained PACU nurses using the Brief Confusion Assessment Method (bCAM) [14]. Patients with a positive or indeterminate bCAM screening result underwent confirmatory assessment using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) [14, 15], and delirium was defined as a positive CAM-ICU result [16, 17]. Initial assessments were generally performed after extubation and repeated when clinically indicated during the PACU stay.

Secondary outcomes included prolonged PACU stay, defined as PACU stay ≥ 90 min based on institutional discharge criteria; serious PACU adverse events, defined as unplanned ICU admission, reintubation, or extubation failure; and post-extubation arterial blood gas values. Arterial blood gas parameters included indices of ventilation (PaCO₂), oxygenation (PaO₂ and SaO₂), acid–base status (pH and base excess), metabolic variables (glucose, lactate, and potassium), and hemoglobin.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, and compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (%) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test.

Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including prespecified covariates: age, sex, body weight, ASA physical status, comorbidities, surgical type, anesthetic technique, anesthesia duration, surgery duration, and intraoperative fluid administration. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature [18, 19]. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline characteristics. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized differences (ASDs), with values < 0.10 indicating adequate balance.

IPTW-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for binary outcomes. The late-day group was analyzed as the exposure group, with the daytime group as the reference. Post-extubation arterial blood gas parameters were compared between groups and interpreted descriptively because these continuous secondary outcomes were exploratory.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted for the primary outcome to assess the consistency of the observed association across clinically relevant patient and surgical characteristics, including age (< 75 vs. ≥75 years), sex, ASA physical status (I–II vs. III–IV), comorbidity burden, surgical duration (< 3 vs. ≥3 h), and surgical type (esophageal vs. non-esophageal). Interaction terms between group assignment and subgroup variables were included to assess potential effect modification. Raw P values for interaction were reported, with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustment applied to nominally significant interaction findings. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) conventional multivariable logistic regression in the unweighted cohort; (2) complete-case IPTW analysis after excluding patients with missing data and trimming extreme weights, defined as weights exceeding the 99th percentile; and (3) 1:1 propensity score matching using a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

Two supplementary analyses were performed for emergence delirium to assess whether the primary findings depended on the dichotomous exposure definition, using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for the same prespecified covariates as in the propensity score model. First, restricted cubic spline modelling was used to examine emergence timing as a continuous variable, with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the observed emergence-time distribution, corresponding to approximately 10:00, 14:00, and 20:00, respectively. Second, a four-stratum analysis was performed by categorizing emergence timing as 08:00–12:00, 12:00–16:00, 16:00–20:00, and 20:00–24:00, with 08:00–12:00 as the reference group.

Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis without imputation, with patients excluded only from analyses involving variables with missing values. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied across secondary outcomes; these findings were interpreted as exploratory.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Participants and covariate balance

Between January 1 and December 31, 2024, 28,118 PACU admissions were screened for eligibility. Of 8,357 patients meeting the inclusion criteria, 211 were excluded according to prespecified exclusion criteria. An additional 28 patients whose anesthetic emergence was initiated between 00:00 and 08:00 were excluded because this interval was outside the prespecified emergence periods of interest, yielding a final analytic cohort of 8,118 patients. Of these, 4,816 patients (59.3%) were classified into the daytime group and 3,302 patients (40.7%) into the late-day group (Fig. 1). In this cohort, tracheal extubation was performed during standardized postoperative recovery management in 8,101 patients (99.8%), whereas only 17 patients (0.2%) were extubated in the operating room. Transfer from the operating room to the PACU was brief, with a median transport time of 3.3 min [IQR 0.4–6.5]. Before weighting, baseline characteristics differed between groups across several demographic and perioperative variables. After IPTW, adequate covariate balance was achieved for all prespecified variables (ASD < 0.10) (Table 1).

Primary and secondary outcomes

Emergence delirium occurred in 648/4,816 (13.5%) patients in the daytime group and 519/3,302 (15.7%) in the late-day group (unadjusted OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.06–1.36; P = 0.004). After IPTW adjustment, the late-day group remained at higher odds of delirium (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.08–1.29; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

The late-day group also had higher odds of prolonged PACU stay (15.8% vs. 14.2%; aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.22; P = 0.012). Serious PACU adverse events were infrequent but more common in the late-day group (0.5% vs. 0.2%; aOR 2.67, 95% CI 1.52–4.97; P = 0.001), with more reintubation, extubation failure, and unplanned ICU admission events. After IPTW adjustment, the late-day group showed small but statistically significant differences in post-extubation arterial blood gas parameters, including higher PaCO₂ (46.2 ± 5.8 vs. 45.7 ± 5.8 mmHg), lower PaO₂ (173.1 ± 71.6 vs. 174.5 ± 66.9 mmHg), and lower SaO₂ (98.9 ± 1.8 vs. 99.0 ± 1.4%) (all P < 0.05). Hemoglobin (12.0 ± 1.5 vs. 11.8 ± 1.5 g/dL) and glucose (6.9 ± 2.5 vs. 6.6 ± 1.6 mmol/L) concentrations were also higher in the late-day group (both P < 0.001). Other arterial blood gas differences were small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful (Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

The associations between the late-day group and emergence delirium were generally consistent across prespecified subgroups (Fig. 2). Before adjustment for multiple comparisons, nominal interactions were observed for ASA physical status and pre-existing cardiac disease, with stronger associations among patients with ASA I–II status (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.17–1.69; raw P for interaction = 0.007) and among those without cardiac disease (aOR 1.24, 95% CI 1.08–1.42; raw P for interaction = 0.021). After adjustment using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, no interaction remained statistically significant; therefore, subgroup analyses were interpreted as exploratory (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses produced findings similar to the primary analysis. In the unweighted cohort, conventional multivariable logistic regression yielded an adjusted odds ratio of 1.24 (95% CI 1.08–1.41; P = 0.002) for emergence delirium. Complete-case IPTW analysis after excluding patients with missing data and trimming extreme weights produced comparable estimates (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.08–1.30; P < 0.001). Propensity score matching resulted in 3,222 matched pairs, with a matched odds ratio of 1.27 (95% CI 1.10–1.46; P = 0.001). Secondary outcomes demonstrated directionally consistent associations across analytic approaches (Supplementary Tables S1–S3).

Temporal pattern of emergence delirium

Restricted cubic spline modelling revealed a non-linear temporal pattern in the association between emergence timing and emergence delirium, with the adjusted probability of delirium peaking around 18:00–19:00 and declining thereafter (Fig. 3A). In the four-stratum exposure analysis (Fig. 3B), compared with the 08:00–12:00 reference interval, the 16:00–20:00 interval was associated with higher odds of emergence delirium (aOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.08–1.55; P = 0.005), whereas the 12:00–16:00 interval (aOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.78–1.11) and the 20:00–24:00 interval (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.69–1.16) did not differ significantly from the reference. Direct ICU transfer became increasingly frequent across later time intervals, occurring in 0.10%, 1.29%, 5.21%, and 24.60% of patients during 08:00–12:00, 12:00–16:00, 16:00–20:00, and 20:00–24:00, respectively. This pattern suggests potential differential selection of patients undergoing PACU-based emergence delirium assessment during later hours (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of older adults undergoing elective thoracic surgery, late-day anesthetic emergence was independently associated with modestly increased odds of emergence delirium, prolonged PACU stay, and serious PACU adverse events. These associations were consistent across multiple analytic approaches, including IPTW, multivariable regression, and propensity score matching. This consistency suggests that anesthetic emergence timing may be relevant to early postoperative recovery assessment and PACU management.

These findings are consistent with prior evidence suggesting that the timing of perioperative care may be associated with postoperative outcomes [10, 20–23]. Previous studies have mainly focused on night-time surgery, after-hours surgery, or surgical start time, reporting associations with postoperative morbidity, mortality, and resource utilization, often attributed to system-level factors such as staffing patterns, resource availability, and provider workload [9, 24]. In contrast, the present study focused specifically on anesthetic emergence and early PACU recovery, a phase that has received less attention than the intraoperative period or longer-term postoperative outcomes. This distinction is important because early recovery in the PACU is influenced not only by surgical and anesthetic exposure, but also by the patient’s ability to regain stable consciousness, ventilation, airway protection, and responsiveness to clinical assessment.

Several mechanisms may plausibly contribute to the observed associations. From a system-level perspective, late-day emergence may occur under conditions of accumulated workload, personnel transitions, and reduced operational flexibility, even when formal PACU staffing and monitoring protocols remain standardized. These factors could influence the efficiency of clinical assessment, symptom recognition, and escalation of care. From a physiological perspective, circadian variation in arousal, respiratory control, autonomic regulation, and metabolic homeostasis may influence the recovery processes required during anesthetic emergence [11, 12, 25, 26]. In older adults, reduced physiological reserve and weakened circadian robustness may further increase vulnerability to such time-of-day effects [27, 28]. However, these mechanisms remain speculative, and residual case-mix differences or unmeasured peri-emergence variables may also explain part of the observed associations.

The secondary outcomes were generally consistent with the primary finding. Late-day emergence was associated with modestly higher odds of prolonged PACU stay and serious PACU adverse events, suggesting a broader pattern of less favorable early recovery. The late-day group also showed slightly higher PaCO₂ and lower PaO₂ after extubation; although these differences were small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful in isolation, they may reflect subtle time-of-day variation in ventilatory recovery. The higher hemoglobin concentrations observed in the late-day group may reflect mild hemoconcentration related to longer preoperative fasting or waiting times. Overall, these secondary outcomes should be interpreted as supportive evidence of modest differences in early PACU recovery rather than as indicators of major respiratory impairment.

Prespecified subgroup analyses suggested potential heterogeneity according to ASA physical status and pre-existing cardiac disease; however, these interaction findings did not remain significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. This apparent heterogeneity may reflect, at least in part, differential patient selection, as higher-acuity patients were more likely to be transferred directly to the ICU rather than managed in the PACU, particularly during later hours. Supplementary exposure analyses further suggested a nonlinear temporal pattern, with the highest risk of emergence delirium observed during the 16:00–20:00 interval. The lack of a similarly elevated risk during the 20:00–24:00 interval should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of lower biological vulnerability. Instead, it may reflect selective exclusion of higher-risk patients from PACU-based assessment, because direct ICU transfer was more frequent during later hours and these patients therefore did not undergo standardized PACU-based emergence delirium assessment.

From an operational perspective, these findings may have implications for postoperative recovery management. Although causal inference cannot be made from this retrospective observational study, awareness of potential time-of-day variation in early recovery quality may help guide PACU monitoring, delirium surveillance, and resource planning during later recovery periods, while informing future prospective investigations.

This study has several strengths, including a large real-world cohort, consistent findings across complementary analytic strategies, and standardized PACU monitoring with structured two-step delirium assessment. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective single-center study, causal inference cannot be established, and residual confounding remains possible despite propensity score–based adjustment. Second, key peri-emergence variables, including body temperature, pain intensity, residual anesthetic or opioid effects, neuromuscular recovery, sedation depth, and transient hemodynamic fluctuations, were unavailable for comprehensive adjustment. Third, emergence timing was defined by the start time recorded in standardized anesthetic emergence documentation, which enhanced recording consistency but may not fully capture the true biological onset of emergence; moreover, dichotomization may have simplified an inherently continuous temporal exposure. Fourth, patients with emergence times between 00:00 and 08:00 were not included because this interval was outside the prespecified study windows, which limits inference regarding the nocturnal circadian period. Fifth, higher-risk patients, particularly during later hours, may have been transferred directly to the ICU without undergoing PACU recovery, potentially shifting the late-day group toward lower-risk patients and underestimating the incidence of adverse outcomes. Finally, hypoactive delirium may have been under-recognized during routine PACU screening, and the retrospective design limited the granularity of peri-emergence assessment.

Conclusion

In older adults undergoing elective thoracic surgery, late-day anesthetic emergence was associated with modestly higher odds of emergence delirium, prolonged PACU stay, and serious PACU adverse events. Although absolute differences were modest, the consistency of these findings across analytic approaches suggests that emergence timing may be relevant to perioperative recovery planning and PACU management. Prospective studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to evaluate whether targeted late-day recovery protocols, resource planning, or scheduling strategies can improve early postoperative outcomes.
