Metabolic and Psychiatric Comorbidities in Dissecting Cellulitis of the Scalp: A Retrospective Cohort Study and Scoping Review
Authors: Lana Salloum, Katherine Sanchez, Eliza Dewey, Samantha Gregoire, Ursula Biba, Basil Alex McIntosh, Abizairie Sanchez-Feliciano, Nora Bensellam, John S. Barbieri, Arash Mostaghimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDissecting cellulitis of the scalp (DCS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder traditionally described as affecting young Black men; however, its broader epidemiology and comorbidity profile remain incompletely characterized. We aimed to evaluate demographic characteristics and systemic comorbidities associated with DCS.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with DCS across the Mass General Brigham network from 1 January 2015 to 1 February 2025. Demographic characteristics and comorbidities, including metabolic, psychiatric, autoimmune, and smoking history, were extracted. In parallel, a structured scoping review of PubMed was performed to identify cohort studies reporting demographic and clinical data in DCS.ResultsTwenty-nine patients met inclusion criteria (89.7% male; mean age 35.9 ± 10.2 years), with diverse racial representation. Nearly half had at least one additional disorder on the follicular occlusion spectrum, and 82.8% had at least one metabolic dysfunction-associated comorbidity. Psychiatric diagnoses were present in 41.4% of patients. Seventeen published cohorts comprising 710 patients were identified. Across studies, DCS consistently demonstrated strong male predominance and heterogeneous racial distribution. Overweight or obese body mass index and metabolic comorbidities were frequently reported, while psychiatric comorbidity was underreported.ConclusionsThese findings challenge the conventional characterization of DCS as a disorder primarily of Black men and instead emphasize global heterogeneity. We instead support an association of DCS with male sex, metabolic dysfunction associated comorbidities, and psychiatric comorbidities. Limitations include the retrospective design, small institutional sample size, absence of control group, use of a single database, and heterogeneity in reporting across published cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01861-4.

Introduction

Dissecting cellulitis of the scalp (DCS) is a rare, chronic neutrophilic inflammatory disorder characterized by painful nodules, pustules, and abscesses that typically presents in the second to third decade of life [1–6]. Clinically, DCS often presents in the vertex and occipital scalp and frequently causes sinus tract formation, ultimately leading to patchy, scarring alopecia [2, 7–9]. The disease often follows a relapsing course and can result in significant cosmetic disfigurement, chronic pain, and psychosocial burden [10, 11]. DCS is positioned within the follicular occlusion spectrum (FOS) alongside hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), acne conglobata (AC), and pilonidal disease [1–3]. Comorbidities, such as obesity and seborrheic dermatitis, have also been frequently reported [1, 2].

Historically, DCS has been described as a rare condition affecting young Black men [2, 7, 9, 12, 13]. While the exact etiology of DCS is unknown, one hypothesis for this predilection is related to unique structural characteristics of the hair follicle and shaft in individuals of African descent, which may predispose to follicular occlusion [13]. However, this characterization is largely derived from small case series and retrospective cohorts and may not fully capture the broader epidemiology or clinical associations of the disease [1, 2, 8, 14, 15].

While disorders within the follicular occlusion spectrum such as hidradenitis suppurativa have increasingly been recognized as systemic inflammatory diseases with metabolic and psychiatric associations, similar patterns in DCS have received relatively little investigation [16–23]. In HS, obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus occur at disproportionately high rates and metabolic syndrome has been reported in up to half of affected patients [18, 24]. Emerging evidence suggests that DCS may demonstrate similar associations. One population-based study identified increased odds of dyslipidemia, diabetes, and hypertension among patients with DCS compared with controls, while smaller cohort studies have reported frequent overweight or obese body mass index (BMI) and other metabolic abnormalities [1, 2, 25].

The psychiatric burden associated with follicular occlusion disorders is also increasingly recognized. Meta-analyses in HS demonstrate elevated rates of depression, substance use disorders, and suicidality [20, 26]. Despite the potentially disfiguring nature of DCS, psychiatric comorbidity has received relatively limited investigation and remains inconsistently reported across cohorts [27]. Improved characterization of these metabolic and psychiatric comorbidities may provide insight into shared pathophysiologic mechanisms and inform more comprehensive patient management. We therefore aimed to evaluate demographic characteristics and systemic comorbidities associated with DCS through a synthesis of data from an institutional cohort alongside a scoping review of published cases.

Methods

Institutional Cohort

A retrospective review of electronic medical records across the Mass General Brigham (MGB) network was conducted to identify patients evaluated for DCS from 1 January 2015 to 1 February 2025 using the Research Patient Data Repository (RPDR). Potential cases were ascertained using ICD codes—L66.3 (perifolliculitis capitis abscedens) and ED70.51 (dissecting cellulitis). Inclusion criteria included confirmed DCS diagnosis along with documentation of DCS as a primary diagnosis in ≥ 2 clinical encounters. Diagnosis was confirmed through manual chart review by three independent chart reviewers. Any disputes were taken to the group and further discussed to achieve resolution. Exclusion criteria included care provided outside of MGB, diagnoses of folliculitis decalvans, and encounters in which DCS was not confirmed.

Patient demographics and preexisting comorbidities, including metabolic dysfunction-associated disorders, such as elevated BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, along with psychiatric diagnoses, autoimmune disease, and tobacco-use history were extracted through retrospective chart review. Psychiatric comorbidities were identified on the basis of documented diagnoses in the electronic medical record rather than standardized psychiatric assessment tools. This study was reviewed and approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (Protocol no.: 2022P002893). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Scoping Review

In parallel, we performed a structured search of PubMed from database inception to 9 December 2025 using the terms (“Dissecting cellulitis of the scalp”[MeSH Terms] OR “dissecting cellulitis”[tiab] OR “perifolliculitis capitis abscedens”[tiab] OR “dissecting folliculitis”[tiab] OR “Hoffman disease”[tiab]), adapted from prior reviews [12]. We used PubMed as it comprehensively indexes biomedical literature. The study selection process was reported using a PRISMA flow diagram, adapted for scoping reviews [28, 29]. Studies were included if they presented original cohort data, enrolled > five patients, and reported demographic information. Case reports and case series without epidemiologic data were excluded. Data on sample size, age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and reported medical and psychiatric comorbidities were extracted. Psychiatric diagnoses were extracted from the patients’ problem list. We did note substance use disorder (SUD) under psychiatric diseases, as it is a Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) acknowledged diagnosis. The study protocol was not registered.

Results

We identified 213 patients, of which 29 were included in final analyses after excluding those without a confirmed DCS diagnosis (n = 174), with alternative diagnoses (n = 8), or who received care outside our institution (n = 2) (Supplemental Fig. S1). Metabolic dysfunction-associated comorbidities were also prevalent: 70.0% of cases had a documented BMI > 25 (overweight or obese categories) and 82.8% had ≥ 1 metabolic dysfunction-associated condition, including hypertension (51.7%), hypercholesterolemia (41.4%), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (34.5%). Five patients (17.2%) did not have a documented metabolic syndrome-associated condition. Autoimmune diseases were seen in 27.6% of patients and included psoriasis (10.3%) and systemic lupus erythematosus (6.9%), among others. Current or former tobacco use was seen in 41.3% of patients. Psychiatric comorbidities were common, with 41.4% of patients carrying at least one confirmed psychiatric diagnosis documented within their problem list. Anxiety disorders (24.1%) and major depressive disorder (20.7%) were most frequently documented.

Scoping Review of Published Cohorts

The database search identified 1352 records from PubMed. After removal of 721 duplicate records, 631 titles and abstracts were screened, of which 601 were excluded as irrelevant. A total of 30 full-text articles were sought for retrieval, of which 1 could not be obtained. Twenty-nine full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 12 were excluded for reasons including insufficient epidemiologic information (n = 5), inappropriate study design (n = 5), nonEnglish language (n = 1) and small sample size < 5 (n = 1). Seventeen studies, including a total of 710 patients, were included in the final review (Fig. 1). Most studies employed a retrospective design with sample sizes ranging from 6 to 169 patients (Table 2; Supplemental Table S1). Few studies employed standardized severity metrics and definitions of metabolic comorbidities varied across studies. DCS consistently demonstrated association with male sex (n = 665; 93.7%), and age at presentation commonly fell within the second and third decades of life (Table 2). Race and ethnicity reporting varied substantially. While some US-based cohorts reported higher proportions of Black patients, large cohorts from China, Brazil, and Europe reflected region-specific populations, including entirely Asian cohorts and predominantly white European cohorts.

BMI was inconsistently reported but, when included, frequently fell into overweight and obese categories. Of the nine studies that reported a mean or median BMI, eight (88.9%) had BMI in the overweight to obese categories (BMI > 25). Several studies documented hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, fatty liver disease, and other comorbid metabolic abnormalities. Psychiatric comorbidity was rarely systematically reported, though recent studies highlighted concomitant major depressive disorder and related quality-of-life impairment. Smoking prevalence ranged widely, from < 5 to > 50%, reflecting both geographic variability and inconsistent reporting.

Discussion

This comprehensive analysis of DCS supports reframing DCS as a male-predominant, and psychiatrically burdensome inflammatory disease associated with metabolic dysfunction instead of its existing, limited concept as a scalp disorder primarily affecting Black men.

While male predominance is consistent across global cohorts, racial distribution appears heterogeneous and likely reflects underlying population demographics over an intrinsic racial predilection [1–3]. Although several US-based studies report higher proportions of Black patients, large cohorts from China, Brazil, and Europe demonstrate that DCS occurs across diverse populations. In our cohort, Black and white patients were equally represented (37.9% each), despite the overall Mass General Brigham institutional population being predominantly white (73.7%) with a smaller proportion of Black patients (6.0%) [30]. While this disproportionate representation aligns with prior US studies reporting higher proportions of Black patients, the disease is not exclusively seen in that population [15, 23]. The most stable demographic feature for DCS is sex, not race [1, 2, 4–7, 9, 15, 23, 31–38]. Continued characterization of DCS as a disease of Black men risks oversimplification and may obscure diagnosis in nonBlack patients.

A high rate of metabolic disease was observed across cohorts. In our institutional series, over two-thirds of patients were overweight or obese and > 80% had at least one metabolic comorbidity. Comparable signals appear in several international DCS cohorts [1, 2, 15, 23, 31–37, 39, 40]. These findings mirror the emerging relationship between hidradenitis suppurativa and metabolic dysfunction-associated comorbidities and raise the possibility that DCS shares overlapping inflammatory-metabolic pathways [18, 24, 41]. The chronic low-grade systemic inflammation driven by cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17 contributes to endothelial dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation seen in HS and DCS [17, 18, 42, 43]. The recurring presence of obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia across DCS datasets further suggests that metabolic dysfunction may not be incidental and its relation to disease initiation, severity, or chronicity merits deeper investigation. These observations also raise the possibility that systemic therapeutic strategies targeting inflammatory pathways or metabolic dysfunction may play a role in the management of DCS.

Psychiatric comorbidity was present in over 40% of patients in our institutional cohort. Although a small number of studies have systematically assessed psychiatric comorbidity, most commonly documenting depression and anxiety, our cohort demonstrated a broader and more frequent spectrum of psychiatric diagnoses [15, 39]. While our data in no way supports a causal interpretation of psychiatric diagnoses with DCS, we believe it is a notable association. Conditions similar to DCS within the follicular occlusion spectrum such as acne and hidradenitis suppurativa have been associated with elevated rates of depression, anxiety, impaired quality of life, and suicidality [20–22, 26, 44, 45]. These findings may be underestimated given inconsistent screening and reporting practices across studies, underscoring the need for standardized psychiatric assessment in future DCS research to better characterize its psychosocial impact.

Our analysis also highlights a fundamental limitation of current DCS literature: inconsistent and incomplete reporting. Across seventeen cohorts, key variables including race, BMI, smoking, and comorbidities were inconsistently defined or omitted, limiting cross-study comparison. Lack of standardized diagnostic criteria limits our ability to rigorously validate cases. This heterogeneity limits cross-study comparison and further restricts establishment of comprehensive disease understanding and management. Limitations of our multi-institutional cohort study include its retrospective design, which limits conclusions to associations rather than causation, the absence of a control group, reliance on a single database, a relatively small sample size, lack of psychiatrist-confirmed psychiatric diagnoses or validated screening measures, and an inability to determine the temporal relationship between DCS onset and psychiatric comorbidities. Additionally, since not all psychiatric comorbidities will be reported and documented in the medical records, our findings may underestimate the mental health burden of DCS.

Conclusion

In summary, DCS appears consistently male-predominant but globally racially heterogeneous, associated with metabolic dysfunction, and with significant psychological comorbidity. Future large, prospective cohorts with standardized methodologies are needed to clarify pathobiological drivers, determine whether metabolic dysfunction is mechanistically central to disease pathogenesis, further characterize the psychiatric burden and psychiatric comorbidities in DCS, and guide the development of targeted therapeutic interventions.
