ABSTRACT

 This report presents a preliminary plan to guide the qualification testing of advanced heat
exchanger (HX) components for nuclear-to-industrial heat transfer applications. The objective is
to establish a defensible, physics-based methodology that integrates computational modeling,
targeted experimentation, and in-service inspection considerations to demonstrate component
performance and reliability under representative reactor conditions.
 The analysis identifies Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor (SFR) and High-Temperature Gas-cooled
Reactor (HTGR) systems as reference configurations in terms of temperature, pressure, and
chemical environment. Within these operating envelopes, dominant degradation mechanisms—
including creep–fatigue interaction, flow-induced vibration, corrosion, and diffusion-bond
deterioration—were evaluated to define test requirements. A comprehensive computational-
experimental framework is proposed to support life prediction and qualification activities. The
framework couples high-fidelity structural-mechanics, thermal-hydraulic, and fluid-structure
interaction models with accelerated degradation testing to produce a traceable linkage between
microstructural evolution, mechanical performance, and remaining useful life (RUL). The
approach adheres to established Verification, Validation, and Uncertainty Quantification (VVUQ)
standards (ASME V&V 10/20; NUREG-2152) and incorporates a digital-twin architecture for
continuous model refinement through data assimilation.
 The plan further outlines testing methodologies, including pre-test analyses, test-loop design
parameters, and sensor placement strategies that maximize information yield while maintaining
mechanistic fidelity. Complementary sections describe in-service inspection (ISI), on-line
monitoring (OLM), and structural-health-monitoring (SHM) techniques applicable to compact HX
geometries typical of advanced reactors. Collectively, these activities establish the technical
foundation for demonstrating 40-60-year equivalent service life of advanced heat exchangers in
support of the U.S. Department of Energy’s Advanced Reactor and Integrated Energy Systems
programs. The forthcoming phase will execute the defined pre-test analyses, initiate hardware
fabrication, and implement the integrated testing campaign to validate the proposed qualification
methodology.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background and Scope
 Decarbonization of the industrial sector depends on the ability to deliver reliable, long-duration
heat transfer from advanced nuclear reactors to high-temperature industrial processes. Achieving
this objective requires the deployment of qualified heat-exchanger (HX) technologies capable of
sustaining continuous operation under demanding thermal, mechanical, and chemical conditions.
These systems serve as the critical interface enabling nuclear-assisted production of hydrogen,
synthetic fuels, and other energy-intensive commodities. Among advanced reactor (AR) concepts,
Sodium-cooled Fast Reactors (SFRs) and High-Temperature Gas-cooled Reactors (HTGRs)
define the bounding extremes of the operating envelope—ranging from approximately 500 °C at
near-atmospheric pressure in SFRs to nearly 950 °C and several megapascals in HTGRs. The wide
disparity in coolant properties, pressure regimes, and environmental chemistry across these
systems drives distinct design philosophies, material-selection criteria, and degradation
mechanisms that must be explicitly considered in the qualification process for long-life heat
exchangers.
 Despite decades of experience with conventional light-water-reactor Steam Generators (SGs),
the extended service conditions, alternative coolants, and compact geometries envisioned for
advanced reactors introduce new challenges in materials performance, fabrication, inspection, and
qualification. The current regulatory framework lacks a unified, mechanistic basis for
demonstrating long-term HX reliability under these non-traditional operating regimes. This
document provides the preliminary framework needed to bridge that gap by integrating modeling,
accelerated testing, and inspection strategies within a consistent qualification approach.

1.2. Objectives
 The objective of this report is to define a preliminary test plan that informs the qualification of
advanced heat exchangers for 40-60 years of equivalent service life in nuclear-to-industrial heat
applications. Specifically, the plan:

 Identifies representative reactor environments (SFR and HTGR) that bound relevant
 temperature, pressure, and chemical conditions.

 Characterizes the dominant degradation mechanisms governing HX performance and
 longevity.

 Establishes an integrated computational–experimental framework, consistent with ASME
 V&V 10/20 and NRC NUREG-2152 guidance, to link modeling and testing activities.

 Outlines the methodology for accelerated testing, model validation, and digital-twin
 implementation to predict remaining useful life with quantified uncertainty.

 8

 Describes the path toward experimental demonstration and qualification of prototype HX
 components in support of DOE’s Advanced Reactor and Integrated Energy Systems (IES)
 programs.

2. CONFIGURATION AND OPERATING CONDITIONS

2.1. Reactor-specific Conditions
 Heat exchangers serving as nuclear-to-industrial interfaces must accommodate the distinct
thermal-hydraulic and chemical environments characteristic of each advanced reactor concept.
These operating envelopes vary significantly in temperature, pressure, and coolant chemistry,
necessitating tailored design solutions and material selections. The thermal-to-electric conversion
architectures associated with each Advanced Reactor (AR) type impose different functional and
structural demands on the heat exchangers that couple reactor, power-conversion, and process-
heat loops. In liquid-metal and molten-salt systems, the design focus is on achieving high heat-
transfer efficiency while maintaining chemical compatibility and minimizing corrosion or
embrittlement of structural alloys. In contrast, gas-cooled systems emphasize precise management
of helium chemistry and thermal-stress gradients to ensure long-term mechanical stability. Across
all reactor classes, working fluids such as helium, sodium, molten salts, and water/steam introduce
unique challenges involving corrosion resistance, creep–fatigue interaction, and flow-induced
vibration. Achieving multidecade reliability therefore requires integrated approaches that combine
appropriate material systems, controlled operating conditions, and validated models of degradation
behavior.

2.1.1. Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor (SFR) Systems
 SFR concepts operate at comparatively moderate temperatures (typically 500-550 °C at the
hot-leg outlet and 350-400 °C at the cold-leg inlet) under near-atmospheric pressure (~0.1 MPa).
Within this operating envelope, the Intermediate Heat Exchanger (IHX) transfers heat from
primary sodium to a secondary sodium loop, which subsequently feeds a Helical-Coil Steam
Generator (HCSG) producing high-pressure steam (16.7–17.8 MPa) at roughly 528 °C. The low
operating pressure and high heat capacity of liquid sodium enable compact designs but introduce
unique materials and safety challenges. Sodium is chemically active with air and water, demanding
double-walled containment and leak-tight boundary integrity. Flow-induced vibration, fretting at
tube supports, and sodium-side corrosion are the dominant degradation concerns, with design
emphasis on pressure-balanced configurations and robust weld joints to minimize thermal and
mechanical stress concentrations. The relatively narrow temperature range simplifies thermal-
stress management compared with gas-cooled systems, but the presence of oxygen- and hydrogen-
sensitive sodium chemistry requires stringent control of impurity levels to preserve long-term
component integrity. In Figure 2-1, the four-loop PRISM reactor concept adapted to include an
industrial heat takeoff loop is shown. The four heat transfer loops appear in the figure and are
identified below.

  Primary Loop: Transfers fission heat from the reactor core to the intermediate sodium.
  Intermediate Loop: Conveys heat from the primary sodium circuit to the HCSG.

 Rankine cycle via a HCSG to produce electricity.

2.1.2. High-Temperature Gas-cooled Reactor (HTGR) Systems
 HTGR designs define the high-temperature extreme for nuclear-industrial integration, with
helium coolant outlet temperatures ranging from 700 °C in steam-cycle configurations (e.g., HTR-
10) up to 950 °C for process-heat applications envisioned under the NGNP program [1]. Operating
pressures vary between 3.0 MPa (indirect steam generation) and 7.0 MPa for direct or indirect
Brayton-cycle systems such as the GT-MHR. The primary heat exchangers—IHXs and Indirect
Power-Cycle Heat Exchangers (IPHXs)—operate in this elevated regime, while downstream
recuperators, pre-coolers, and intercoolers span lower-temperature ranges from approximately 125
°C to ambient. High-temperature creep and creep–fatigue interaction dominate the life-limiting
mechanisms, necessitating alloys such as Alloy 617, Alloy 800H, and Hastelloy XR with proven
microstructural stability and oxidation resistance. The high-pressure operation amplifies
mechanical loading on diffusion-bonded or compact heat-exchanger geometries (e.g., PCHEs).

2.2. Form Factors and Design Considerations
 The design of a nuclear-grade heat exchanger is dictated by a balance between thermal
performance, mechanical integrity, inspectability, and manufacturability under severe operating
environments. The chosen form factor also dictates instrumentation strategies for qualification
testing—ranging from accessible surface thermocouples in shell-and-tube designs to embedded
sensors or fiber-optic arrays in PCHEs—underscoring the need for design–test integration from
the earliest stages of component development. For long-lived service in SFR and HTGR systems,
these constraints converge toward a limited set of geometries and fabrication methods.

 Shell-and-Tube Configurations.
 Conventional shell-and-tube heat exchangers remain a baseline design due to their proven
 reliability and ease of mechanical analysis. Straight-tube layouts simplify cleaning and
 inspection but can incur significant differential thermal expansion between tube and shell,
 requiring carefully engineered expansion joints or floating-head assemblies. U-tube variants
 alleviate thermal expansion mismatch but complicate flow distribution and tube-support
 design. For sodium or water/steam environments, these configurations offer the advantage of
 replaceable tube bundles and mature code coverage within ASME Section III and VIII, making
 them a natural starting point for qualification studies.

 Helical-Coil and Compact Geometries
 Helical-coil exchangers, extensively adopted in SFR intermediate and steam-generation
 service, achieve high surface-area-to-volume ratios and enhance secondary-side turbulence
 while maintaining low shell-side pressure drop. Their curved geometry, however, introduces
 bending stresses that must be evaluated for fatigue and creep-fatigue interaction. Support-plate
 spacing and contact conditions strongly influence fretting-wear rates and flow-induced
 vibration susceptibility, both of which drive long-term reliability considerations. Helical

 13

 configurations are thus often coupled with computational fluid-structure interaction analyses
 to ensure vibration margins and uniform flow distribution.

 Printed-Circuit and Diffusion-Bonded Heat Exchangers (PCHEs)
 Diffusion-bonded micro-channel designs such as PCHEs offer exceptional thermal
 effectiveness and compact form factors suited for high-pressure, high-temperature helium
 environments typical of HTGR systems. Channels etched into stacked metal plates are joined
 by solid-state bonding to form a monolithic core capable of withstanding differential pressures
 exceeding 10 MPa. This geometry minimizes inventory and enhances thermal response, but
 places stringent requirements on material purity, surface finish, and bond-line integrity. Long-
 term qualification must therefore address diffusion-bond degradation, thermal-cycling
 performance, and environmental attack at the microstructural scale. The absence of
 conventional welds and the difficulty of in-service inspection motivate parallel development
 of nondestructive evaluation techniques specifically adapted to diffusion-bonded materials.

2.3. Materials and Their Role in Remaining Useful Life
 The long-term performance and qualification of nuclear heat exchangers are determined by
how materials respond to the combined influence of thermal, mechanical, and environmental
stresses.

2.3.1. Material Property Requirements
 Heat exchanger materials must simultaneously satisfy structural integrity and environmental
compatibility requirements across a wide range of temperatures—from approximately 500 °C in
SFR systems to nearly 950 °C in HTGR applications. At these temperatures, creep resistance and
creep-fatigue interaction become dominant design considerations. Materials must retain tensile
strength and ductility over long dwell periods while accommodating cyclic thermal strains during
start-up and shutdown operations.
 Equally important is environmental compatibility with the working fluid. In helium systems,
carburization, decarburization, and oxidation are driven by trace impurities whose equilibrium
ratios (H₂/H₂O, CO/CO₂) can vary with operating chemistry. In sodium-cooled environments,
materials must exhibit low solubility for key alloying elements, high resistance to sodium
corrosion, and tolerance to thermal transients that induce differential expansion between adjacent
components. In addition, weldability and fabricability are critical for practical deployment. Many
compact geometries, such as diffusion-bonded PCHEs or helical-coil assemblies, require
specialized joining techniques that preserve microstructural integrity across multiple heat-affected
zones. Materials must maintain mechanical uniformity in welds, joints, and diffusion-bonded
interfaces to prevent premature localized degradation.

 14

2.3.2. Candidate Materials
 The spectrum of candidate alloys for nuclear heat exchanger service can be grouped into three
broad categories: high-temperature alloys, stainless steels, and corrosion-resistant nickel-based
alloys.

 High-Temperature Alloys:
 For HTGR applications, Alloy 617 remains the reference material due to its excellent creep
 strength, oxidation resistance, and established ASME Code Case coverage for high-
 temperature service. Alloy 800H provides a lower-cost alternative with favorable fabrication
 characteristics and good creep-fatigue resistance up to 800 °C. Hastelloy XR, specifically
 developed for impure helium environments, offers superior oxidation and carburization
 resistance and is a leading candidate for long-duration exposure in NGNP-type systems.

 Stainless Steels:
 Austenitic stainless steels remain the workhorse materials for moderate-temperature service,
 particularly within SFR systems. Type 304/304H alloys are suitable for temperatures up to
 approximately 550 °C, while Type 316/316H provides enhanced corrosion resistance in water
 and sodium environments through molybdenum additions. Type 347, stabilized with niobium
 or tantalum, offers improved weldability and resistance to sensitization, making it preferred
 for components subjected to repeated thermal cycling or post-weld heat treatments.

 Corrosion-Resistant and Ferritic-Martensitic Alloys:
 Inconel 690 and Inconel 625 are widely applied in steam-generator tubing and high-strength
 applications requiring superior oxidation and chloride-stress-corrosion resistance. 9Cr-1Mo-V
 ferritic-martensitic steels are attractive for intermediate-temperature components such as
 secondary sodium piping or steam generators, where they combine high thermal conductivity
 with good resistance to thermal fatigue and liquid-metal embrittlement.

Ultimately, material qualification extends beyond static property evaluation. It requires coupling
microstructural evolution modeling with accelerated aging experiments to quantify property
degradation under representative duty cycles. Grain growth, carbide coarsening, and phase
instability must be characterized to ensure that creep-rupture and fatigue life predictions remain
valid throughout the component’s operating life. Integration of these material models into digital-
twin and life-prediction frameworks provides the quantitative link between observed degradation
and predicted remaining useful life.

3. DEGRADATION MECHANISMS AND FAILURE MODES

 In nuclear-to-industrial heat-transfer applications, degradation results from the combined
effects of elevated temperature, cyclic loading, and coolant chemistry. Three primary classes of
mechanisms—thermomechanical, flow-induced, and environmental degradation—dominate
across AR concepts. Each mechanism contributes uniquely to damage accumulation, yet in
practice they often interact, producing coupled degradation modes that accelerate failure beyond
what isolated models predict.

3.1. Thermomechanical Degradation
 At the high temperatures characteristic of HTGR and SFR systems, thermomechanical
degradation governs the majority of life-limiting phenomena. The most critical modes include
creep–fatigue interaction, thermal fatigue, and thermal shock.

 Creep–fatigue interaction arises under cyclic loading with sustained dwell periods at elevated
 temperature. Plastic strain accumulates during cyclic operation, while creep deformation
 continues during holds, leading to intergranular void nucleation and coalescence. This
 mechanism is dominant in components operating between 850 °C and 950 °C, particularly in
 compact geometries (e.g., PCHEs) or welded regions where local stress concentrations amplify
 strain. Accurate life prediction requires constitutive models capable of partitioning total strain
 into elastic, plastic, and creep components, and of representing hold-time effects that
 substantially reduce fatigue life.

 Thermal fatigue originates from repeated thermal cycling during start-up, shutdown, or load-
 following operation. Nonuniform temperature fields induce alternating tensile and
 compressive stresses across wall thicknesses, promoting surface crack initiation at geometrical
 discontinuities such as joints or baffle edges. The phenomenon is exacerbated by thermal
 striping, a condition arising in mixing regions where hot and cold streams impinge, creating
 high-frequency temperature fluctuations and steep through-wall gradients. Long-term thermal
 fatigue is among the leading causes of inspection and maintenance interventions in both
 sodium and water/steam heat exchangers.

 Thermal shock represents the transient extreme of thermomechanical degradation. Rapid
 temperature excursions—resulting from emergency cooling, sudden loss of flow, or liquid-
 level fluctuations—induce steep thermal gradients and high transient stresses. Such events can
 cause immediate fracture in brittle materials or local debonding in diffusion-bonded structures
 if not properly accommodated by design margins.

 16

3.2. Flow-Induced Degradation
 Flow-induced degradation encompasses mechanisms driven by fluid–structure interactions and
particulate impingement under dynamic flow conditions.

 Fretting wear at tube supports is a primary degradation concern for helical- and straight-tube
 exchangers exposed to turbulent flow (it is a critical issue for SFR steam generators). Relative
 motion between tubes and support plates, amplified by flow-induced vibration (FIV), leads to
 surface wear, microcrack initiation, and eventual wall thinning. The geometry and spacing of
 supports critically affect the contact load distribution and wear rate.

 Flow-induced vibration itself arises from several sources—vortex shedding, turbulent
 buffeting, and fluidelastic instability. Vortex shedding generates oscillatory lift forces with
 frequencies governed by the Strouhal number, while fluidelastic instability can trigger large-
 amplitude oscillations once cross-flow velocities exceed a critical threshold. Excessive
 vibration results in fatigue failure or accelerated fretting, making FIV analysis an essential
 component of design qualification and pre-test modeling.

 Erosion–corrosion represents another hybrid mechanism, especially in two-phase flow or
 high-velocity sodium and steam environments. Turbulent eddies impinge entrained particles
 or bubbles against tube surfaces, removing protective oxide layers and accelerating material
 loss. The combined mechanical and electrochemical attack reduces wall thickness and
 degrades heat-transfer performance, particularly near flow entrances or sharp turns.

3.3. Environmental Degradation
 Environmental degradation mechanisms reflect the chemical reactivity between structural
materials and the coolant or process fluids. They differ substantially between helium, sodium, and
water/steam environments.

 In high-temperature helium, impurity species such as H₂, H₂O, CO, and CO₂ establish redox
 equilibria that control oxidation and carburization kinetics. Carburization hardens near-surface
 regions and embrittles grain boundaries, while decarburization depletes strengthening
 carbides, reducing creep resistance. Even at impurity levels below 10 ppm, these reactions can
 alter alloy microstructure over time, necessitating continuous gas chemistry monitoring and
 controlled impurity ratios to maintain stable protective films.

 In aqueous environments, general corrosion, pitting, and stress corrosion cracking (SCC) are
 major concerns. Localized attack often initiates beneath deposits or within crevices, leading to
 under-deposit corrosion and fouling that degrades thermal performance. Microbiologically
 influenced corrosion (MIC) may occur in stagnant regions or during shutdown conditions.

 17

 Material selection and water chemistry control—especially pH and dissolved oxygen
 content—are critical mitigations.

 For sodium-cooled systems, corrosion and mass transfer are governed by the solubility of
 alloying elements in liquid sodium and by the presence of traces of oxygen impurities.
 Dissolution of nickel and chromium from structural alloys can lead to compositional changes
 and embrittlement. Oxygen ingress promotes oxide deposition in cooler regions, potentially
 clogging narrow passages and degrading heat-transfer efficiency. Mitigation requires strict
 sodium purification, oxygen monitoring, and material compatibility testing under prototypical
 thermal conditions.

3.4. Component-Specific Failure Modes
 The combined effects of thermomechanical, flow-induced, and environmental degradation
manifest through distinct component-specific failure modes that dictate qualification priorities.

 In PCHEs, diffusion-bond integrity governs structural reliability. Bond-line degradation due
 to cyclic thermal stresses or localized oxidation can compromise monolithic strength and lead
 to delamination. Accelerated thermal-cycling tests are therefore required to quantify bond
 durability under representative helium or sodium atmospheres.

 For tube-type exchangers, tube-to-tubesheet joint failures remain a recurrent issue.
 Differential thermal expansion, crevice corrosion, and weld residual stresses concentrate
 damage in this region, often becoming the first site of leakage. Enhanced joint designs
 employing stress-relief grooves or compliant sleeves can mitigate this risk but require
 verification through targeted testing.

 In helical-coil designs, geometry-induced stresses play a dominant role. Tube ovalization
 during bending and nonuniform support loading lead to localized plasticity and early crack
 initiation. Detailed finite-element models and vibration analyses are essential to identify these
 critical locations and guide test instrumentation placement.

A comprehensive understanding of these degradation pathways is indispensable for developing
predictive life-assessment tools, optimizing test matrices, and informing the integrated
computational–experimental framework described in Section 5.

4. DUTY CYCLE ANALYSIS AND TEST REQUIREMENTS

 Accurate qualification of nuclear heat exchangers requires that the imposed test conditions
faithfully reproduce the thermal, mechanical, and environmental duty cycles expected during plant
operation. Because long-term service exposures extend over multiple decades, test campaigns must
capture not only the steady-state operating envelope but also the full spectrum of transient and off-
normal conditions that drive degradation. This section defines representative duty cycles,
environmental exposure conditions, and accelerated testing methodologies that collectively
provide a defensible basis for extrapolating laboratory observations to 40–60 years of service.

4.1. Representative Duty Cycles
 The operating history of a nuclear heat exchanger can be characterized as a sequence of normal
transients, upset events, and rare emergency conditions, each contributing distinct stress–strain and
temperature histories. Quantifying their frequency, amplitude, and dwell times enables realistic
prediction of cumulative damage over decades of operation.

 Normal operating transients include reactor start-up and shutdown, load-following
 adjustments, and seasonal power-level variations associated with coupled industrial demand.
 During these transients, wall-temperature excursions of 100–200 °C and flow-rate fluctuations
 of 10–20 % are common, generating the cyclic thermal and mechanical loads that dominate
 fatigue damage accumulation. Start-up and shutdown cycles are particularly influential
 because differential thermal expansion between shell and tube materials imposes high transient
 stresses, even when overall operating pressures remain within design limits.

 Upset conditions—such as reactor trips, turbine trips, or sudden loss of secondary flow—
 introduce more severe thermal shocks and pressure reversals. These events impose large but
 infrequent stress cycles that must be included in lifetime predictions through equivalent-cycle
 methods or cumulative-damage models. System-level transient analyses performed with codes
 such as SAM or RELAP5-3D define the temporal evolution of boundary conditions
 (temperature, pressure, flow) for subsequent component-scale finite-element analysis.

 Emergency transients are rarer but essential for demonstrating design robustness. Examples
 include rapid depressurization in gas-cooled systems or emergency sodium-drain events in
 SFRs. Although these events typically occupy less than 0.1 % of total operating time, they can
 dominate failure risk if not captured in qualification testing.

4.2. Environmental Exposure Conditions
 Heat-exchanger materials experience simultaneous exposure to thermal cycling, chemical
species, and pressure gradients that together define the operative environment. Representative

 19

exposure conditions must therefore capture not only nominal parameters but also their dynamic
variations.

 Temperature cycling parameters—including ramp rates, dwell durations, and maximum
 gradients—govern creep–fatigue interaction. Ramp rates in the range of 1–5 °C s⁻¹ during start-
 up and shutdown are typical for SFR systems, while HTGR components may experience
 slower but larger-amplitude cycles due to higher bulk temperatures. The combination of
 temperature and mechanical loading histories forms the input to constitutive models for
 predicting strain range partitioning and life consumption.

 Chemical environment specifications differ by reactor type. For HTGR applications,
 controlled impurity levels in helium determine the balance between oxidation and
 carburization. For SFR systems, sodium purity requirements limit oxygen concentrations to
 the low-ppm range to prevent corrosion and oxide plugging. In water/steam systems, dissolved
 oxygen and pH must be tightly controlled to mitigate stress-corrosion cracking and fouling.
 These compositional bounds define the environmental envelopes for both steady-state and
 accelerated testing.

 Pressure and flow conditions complete the operating specification. High-pressure helium
 systems (up to 7 MPa) impose substantial structural loading, while liquid-metal and steam
 systems operate at lower pressures but higher densities and Reynolds numbers, amplifying
 flow-induced vibration risk. Reproducing the correct combination of Reynolds and Mach
 numbers in scaled test facilities ensures that flow distribution, turbulence spectra, and heat-
 transfer coefficients remain representative of prototypical operation.

4.3. Accelerated Testing Strategies
 The objective of current qualification methodologies is to accelerate testing so that years of
service exposure can be replicated within months, while faithfully preserving the governing
degradation mechanisms. Acceleration parameters must be selected carefully to prevent the onset
of non-representative damage modes that could compromise the validity of life-extrapolation
models.

 Time-compression methods increase the rate of damage accumulation by elevating test
 severity in a physically interpretable way. For creep-dominated phenomena, raising the
 temperature by 20–30 °C above service conditions yields roughly a ten-fold acceleration factor
 according to Larson–Miller or equivalent time–temperature relationships. For fatigue
 processes, cycling frequencies may be increased by one to two orders of magnitude, provided
 that thermal equilibration within specimens is maintained and environmental interactions
 remain representative.

 20

 Severity-factor determination requires quantifying the ratio of accelerated to nominal
 damage rates. Physics-based correlations derived from constitutive models and deformation-
 mechanism maps verify that testing remains within the same mechanistic regime—e.g.,
 dislocation-controlled creep rather than diffusion creep, or transgranular rather than
 intergranular cracking. Environmental acceleration can be introduced through controlled
 increases in reactive-species concentration (e.g., impurity-enriched helium, elevated sodium
 oxygen content), again constrained to avoid forming non-prototypical reaction products.

 Extrapolation to design life then proceeds through validated damage-accumulation models
 and uncertainty quantification. Statistical confidence bounds (typically 95 %) are derived from
 probabilistic propagation of material-property scatter and model-form uncertainty. Combining
 these results with inspection-based validation provides a quantitative measure of safety margin,
 establishing a defensible link between accelerated testing and long-term service performance.

5. INTEGRATED COMPUTATIONAL-EXPERIMENTAL FRAMEWORK FOR
 COMPONENT LIFE PREDICTION

 The qualification of nuclear heat exchangers requires a rigorous integration of computational
modeling and targeted experimental testing. Because no practical test program can span the full
service duration, computational models must provide mechanistic extrapolation of short-term data,
while experiments must supply the validation data that anchor model credibility. The proposed
framework establishes a closed-loop cycle where modeling guides experiment design, testing
validates model assumptions, and both streams converge toward a predictive assessment of
remaining useful life (RUL). It is structured around five elements that together establish a
traceable, physics-based pathway from material characterization to full-scale component
qualification.

5.1. Computational Modeling Framework for Qualification
 The computational framework provides the analytical foundation for understanding
component behavior under the thermal, mechanical, and chemical environments characteristic of
advanced reactors. It integrates structural mechanics, thermal-hydraulics, and fluid–structure
interaction into a unified workflow that directly supports qualification activities.

 Structural Mechanics: Finite-Element Analysis (FEA) resolve stress, strain, and inelastic
 deformation in full-scale components using temperature-dependent elastic-plastic and
 viscoplastic constitutive laws. The models incorporate creep, fatigue, and creep–fatigue
 interaction to identify critical locations such as tube-to-tubesheet joints, welds, or diffusion-
 bonded regions.

 Thermal-Hydraulics: Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations using tools such as
 STAR-CCM+ or ANSYS Fluent capture local heat-transfer coefficients, flow distribution, and
 temperature gradients that define boundary conditions for structural analysis.

 Fluid–Structure Interaction (FSI): Coupled CFD–FEA calculations evaluate dynamic
 response to flow-induced excitation, including vortex shedding, fluidelastic instability, and
 turbulent buffeting, thereby predicting vibration amplitudes and fatigue damage accumulation.

5.1.1. Engineering Framework for Degradation Modeling
 The engineering framework must explicitly model the dominant degradation mechanisms that
govern heat exchanger service life through high-fidelity simulations incorporating experimentally
calibrated constitutive relationships and mechanistic damage-evolution laws. The specific models
are selected according to the governing degradation modes in each reactor environment. For
HTGR applications, the framework employs advanced viscoplastic constitutive models that
capture coupled creep-fatigue behavior, oxidation-induced embrittlement, and environmental
degradation at 850-950 °C. In SFR systems, analyses emphasize flow-induced vibration (FIV),
fretting wear, and sodium corrosion within the 500–550 °C temperature regime. System-level
transient analyses using codes such as RELAP5-3D or SAM provide time-dependent boundary
conditions that link component-scale stress and temperature fields to plant-level duty cycles and
operating histories.
 Within this structure, creep-fatigue interaction—the principal life-limiting mechanism for
high-temperature components—is modeled through constitutive formulations that track the
evolution of internal damage variables at critical regions such as tube-to-tubesheet joints and
geometric discontinuities [4]. FIV susceptibility is evaluated through modal and transient dynamic
analyses to identify tube configurations and flow velocities that may trigger fluidelastic instability
or vortex shedding, while thermal-striping zones—regions of turbulent mixing that generate high-
frequency temperature fluctuations—are mapped to assess thermal-fatigue damage using models
that incorporate mean-stress effects, environmental interactions, and microstructural degradation
kinetics. Most importantly, the framework integrates all degradation modes (mechanical, thermal,
and environmental) into a unified, coupled assessment that yields comprehensive and

 23

mechanistically defensible predictions of remaining useful life (RUL). Specifically, these
predictions include uncertainty bounds derived from propagation of material variability and model
uncertainty throughout the analysis chain, expressed as probability distributions for time to failure
at specified confidence levels. Spatial maps of critical locations identify regions of highest damage
accumulation, guiding inspection planning and optimizing defect-detection probability.
 These predictive capabilities—grounded in validated, physics-based models and supported by
rigorous uncertainty quantification—form the technical foundation for component qualification,
risk-informed maintenance planning, and regulatory acceptance of next-generation nuclear HXs.

5.1.2. Case Study: Validated Feedwater Heater Tube Fatigue Analysis
 A demonstration of this framework is provided by the validated fatigue analysis of feedwater-
heater tubes, which exemplifies the integration of multi-physics modeling with decades of
operational experience in commercial nuclear power plants [4]. The methodology employs three-
dimensional simulations that couple condensation phenomena, conjugate heat transfer, and one-
way FSI within a shell-and-tube heat exchanger model developed in STAR-CCM+. The analysis
integrates von Mises stress calculations, Rainflow cycle counting, and Miner’s cumulative damage
rule, enhanced through Gaussian Process regression to interpolate stress responses across a wide
range of operating conditions.
 The study revealed that stress concentrations are strongly localized at tube-support-plate (TSP)
regions, where stresses reach approximately 331 MPa, compared to only 4 MPa in U-bend
sections. Each start-up cycle was found to reduce the effective tube life by 2.8–4.5%,
demonstrating the dominant influence of cyclic thermal loading on long-term fatigue degradation.
The computational predictions exhibited strong agreement with eddy-current inspection data and
maintenance records from operating units, accurately reproducing the observed maintenance
intervals—initial repairs at 23–25 years and replacement at 27–29 years.
 This study illustrates how physics-based qualification methodologies can enhance nuclear heat
exchanger reliability prediction, support data-driven maintenance strategies, and provide the
rigorous validation against operating experience essential for regulatory acceptance. The modeling
framework establishes the computational foundation for predicting heat exchanger behavior under
realistic operating conditions. It also highlights a central tenet of the proposed framework: while
advanced computational tools can explore broad parametric and mechanistic spaces, their
predictive credibility ultimately depends on systematic verification and validation (V&V) against
well-designed experiments. This principle forms the foundation for the comprehensive V&V
framework described in the following section.

5.2. Model Verification and Validation Framework
 A rigorous V&V framework is essential for regulatory acceptance of computational
predictions. Model credibility is established through a sequential Verification, Validation, and
Uncertainty Quantification (VVUQ) process in accordance with ASME V&V 10 and 20 [5][6][7]
and the NRC’s NUREG-2152 [8].

 24

5.2.1. Code Verification
 Code verification establishes the numerical accuracy of the computational framework and
confirms that the governing equations are solved correctly. This process precedes any physical
validation, and follows a structured protocol designed to quantify discretization and solver errors
across FEA, CFD, and FSI domains:

 Spatial and Temporal Convergence:
 Mesh-refinement and time-step-sensitivity studies are conducted until key response
 quantities—stress, strain, temperature, or pressure—change by less than ~5 % between
 successive refinements. Adaptive meshing and time-stepping are used where multiple time
 scales coexist, such as in creep-fatigue simulations.

 Benchmarking Against Analytical Solutions:
 Numerical results are compared to canonical cases (1-D heat conduction, laminar flow, linear-
 elastic bending) to confirm solver fidelity and identify residual discretization error.

 Coupling and Solver Consistency:
 For multi-physics analyses, data exchange between thermal, structural, and fluid solvers is
 verified for numerical stability and convergence in both one-way and two-way coupling
 modes.

Verification is complete when numerical uncertainties are shown to fall within acceptable limits.
These verified models then provide the foundation for subsequent validation, ensuring that later
discrepancies with experiment can be attributed to physical modeling rather than numerical error.

5.2.2. Validation Approach
 Model validation determines whether the right equations are being solved for the physics of
interest. It follows a building-block hierarchy that progresses from simple, highly controlled tests
to full-scale, coupled behavior under prototypical transients. This methodology, established as
industry best practice through AIAA and ASME standards, ensures that each level of model
complexity is validated before proceeding to the next, building confidence through incremental
steps. The building block approach not only confirms the accuracy of individual physics models
but also validates their coupling and interaction under increasingly realistic conditions.
 The mathematical basis of this validation process is provided by ASME V&V 20 [6], which
defines the framework for systematic comparison between simulation results and experimental
data. The comparison error is expressed as 𝐸 = 𝑆 − 𝐷, where 𝑆 represents simulation results
and 𝐷 represents experimental data, followed by decomposing this error to: 𝐸 = 𝛿 +
 (𝛿 + 𝛿 − 𝛿 ), where 𝛿 is the uncertainty inherent in the mathematical model’s
physical assumptions, 𝛿 represents the numerical discretization errors, 𝛿 captures

 25

uncertainty in input and boundary conditions, and 𝛿 represents experimental measurement
uncertainties.
The Nuclear Regulatory Commission has effectively elevated this framework from best practice
to quasi-regulatory expectation through NUREG-2152, which recommends ASME V&V 20
compliance for computational analyses supporting safety-related applications [8]. The NRC
implements this validation philosophy using a graded approach, in which the level of rigor scales
with (i) the safety significance of the modeled system and (ii) the novelty or maturity of the
computational methods involved. This principle is codified in NUREG/CR-7278, which defines
acceptance processes for probabilistic failure-modeling and reliability codes [9]. Building on these
standards, the proposed validation hierarchy progresses through four levels that progress from
fundamental characterization to integrated component performance under operating conditions.

 Level 1: Material Coupon Validation
 Purpose: calibrate and validate the constitutive relationships describing elastic–plastic,
 viscoplastic, and creep–fatigue behavior under controlled loading and environmental
 conditions. Representative testing includes uniaxial tensile, creep, and low-cycle fatigue
 experiments performed per ASTM standards in the relevant environments (e.g., controlled-
 impurity helium for HTGRs, liquid sodium for SFRs) [10].
 Acceptance criteria: reproduction of elastic moduli and yield strength within ± 5 %, accurate
 capture of cyclic hardening/softening trends, and correct prediction of time-dependent creep
 strain. Environmental modifiers are introduced through empirically validated degradation
 factors.

 Level 2: Feature and Sub-Component Validation
 Purpose: validate local stress concentration, multiaxial response, and damage initiation in
 geometries representative of service conditions—such as notched specimens, welded joints, or
 tube-to-tubesheet mockups. Thermal-mechanical testing validates the coupled thermal-
 structural response under cyclic conditions. This level confirms that the models can accurately
 predict the transition from uniform deformation to localized damage accumulation that governs
 component life.
 Acceptance criteria: FEA-predicted strain-field distributions within ±15 % of measured values,
 correct identification of initiation sites and damage morphology, and consistent prediction of
 life-to-crack-initiation within a factor of two.

 Level 3: Sub-Assembly and Multi-Physics Validation
 Purpose: assess coupled thermal-hydraulic and structural performance in instrumented sub-
 assemblies (typically 1/4 to 1/2 scale) containing representative tube bundles, headers, and
 support structures. Experiments provide temperature, pressure, strain, and vibration data for
 direct comparison with CFD–FEA or FSI simulations.
 Acceptance criteria: prediction of FIV onset velocity within ± 20 %, dominant vibration

 26

 frequencies within ± 10 %, and accurate reproduction of flow and temperature distributions
 across measurement planes. Consistency of dynamic damping ratios and amplitude trends
 further supports coupled-physics validation.

 Level 4: Full-Scale Component Validation
 Purpose: demonstrate predictive capability of the fully integrated model under realistic duty
 cycles and environmental conditions, thereby establishing confidence for long-term life
 extrapolation. Testing involves accelerated but mechanistically equivalent transients applied
 to fully instrumented heat-exchanger prototypes.
 Acceptance criteria: agreement between predicted and measured stress/temperature fields
 within defined tolerances, replication of observed degradation locations and morphologies, and
 prediction of degradation rates and RUL within a factor of two.

The economic value of the building-block validation approach arises from its capacity to identify
and correct modeling deficiencies early in the development cycle—when design modifications are
least costly. Industrial experience demonstrates that systematic validation frameworks can deliver
average cost savings of $12–50 million per project by reducing reliance on large-scale physical
testing and shortening regulatory review timelines [11]. The resulting return on investment (ROI)
for integrated computational-experimental programs typically exceeds eightfold, driven by the
ability to optimize designs computationally before committing to costly prototype fabrication [11].

5.2.3. Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)
 Reliable assessment of model credibility requires explicit quantification of the uncertainties
affecting simulation-based predictions of component performance and remaining useful life
(RUL). Two principal uncertainty classes are considered:

 Aleatory uncertainty, representing inherent physical variability such as material property
 scatter, manufacturing tolerances, or stochastic operating conditions. This uncertainty is
 irreducible and must be characterized statistically to capture the true range of physical
 outcomes.
 Epistemic uncertainty, arising from incomplete knowledge—model-form limitations,
 parameter uncertainty due to limited data, or simplifications in boundary conditions. Unlike
 aleatory variability, epistemic uncertainty can be progressively reduced through targeted
 experiments, model refinement, and data assimilation.

Distinguishing between these categories is essential: aleatory uncertainty defines variability
bounds, while epistemic uncertainty defines confidence in the model itself. Treating them
separately prevents conflating random variability with correctable knowledge gaps. To manage
the computational expense associated with high-fidelity simulations, surrogate models (e.g.,
Gaussian-process emulators or reduced-order neural networks) are employed to enable efficient

 27

large-scale sampling and sensitivity analysis. The uncertainty quantification (UQ) framework
combines forward propagation techniques—such as Monte Carlo sampling and polynomial chaos
expansion—with Bayesian calibration, which rigorously updates model parameters and associated
uncertainty as new experimental data become available [12]. To ensure predictions are reliable for
decision-making, quantitative acceptance criteria are established based on NRC Regulatory Guide
1.245 [13].

5.3. Pre-Test Analysis and Smart Test Design
 Experimental resources for nuclear-component qualification are inherently limited; therefore,
computational analyses must be leveraged to maximize the information yield of each test. Pre-test
modeling plays a central role in identifying the most influential degradation mechanisms, selecting
representative test conditions, and defining instrumentation strategies that enable high-value data
collection.

5.3.1. Physics-Based Test Acceleration
 The process begins with a Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) exercise,
which systematically evaluates all physical processes influencing component performance and
degradation [14]. Each phenomenon is ranked according to its importance (impact on safety,
lifetime, or performance) and knowledge level (degree of existing experimental or modeling
understanding). For heat exchangers, high-importance, low-knowledge phenomena—such as
creep–fatigue interaction, flow-induced vibration (FIV), sodium corrosion kinetics, or high-
temperature helium oxidation—are prioritized for experimental investigation and detailed
modeling. The PIRT thus serves as the formal interface between computational analysts and test
engineers, ensuring alignment between simulation objectives, test-matrix design, and available
diagnostics. The result is a defensible, traceable rationale for allocating experimental effort where
it yields maximum reduction of uncertainty.
 To replicate long-term degradation within feasible timeframes while preserving mechanistic
fidelity, accelerated test protocols are developed. They are derived from established kinetic and
deformation-mechanism relationships, ensuring that the governing physics remain unchanged.

 Temperature Acceleration provides the primary means of time compression for creep-
 dominated degradation, guided by time-temperature parameters such as Larson-Miller
 relationships [15]. Deformation mechanism maps verify that accelerated conditions remain
 within the same mechanistic regime as service conditions. Maximum temperature
 accelerations of 20-30°C above service temperature provide approximately 10-fold time
 compression while maintaining mechanistic relevance.

 Environmental Acceleration focuses on increasing reactive species concentrations while
 preserving fundamental corrosion and mass transfer mechanisms. For HTGR applications, this
 involves controlled increases in helium coolant impurity levels that govern oxidation-

 28

 carburization equilibria. The accepted upper limit of approximately 10-fold enrichment
 represents a practical balance between achieving measurable acceleration and preventing the
 formation of non-prototypical phases not observed under nominal service conditions. CFD
 models can verify that accelerated mass transfer rates do not fundamentally alter flow patterns
 or create artificial concentration gradients.

 Fatigue Acceleration through increased cycling frequency offers straightforward time
 compression but requires careful consideration of temperature effects and deformation
 mechanisms. The upper cycling limit for high-temperature fatigue tests allows sufficient time
 for environmental interactions, and respects equipment limitations. This provides substantial
 acceleration compared to service loading rates (10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ Hz), enabling about a hundred-
 fold reductions in test duration.

Acceleration factors are verified through deformation-mechanism maps, thermochemical analysis,
and microstructural examination to confirm that testing remains within the intended mechanistic
regime. These practices typically shorten test campaigns from years to months without
compromising physical relevance.

5.3.2. Pre-Test Structural and Thermal-Hydraulic Analysis Protocol
 Prior to hardware fabrication, computational analysis are essential to identify critical regions
and operating parameters for targeted instrumentation. For instance, feedwater-heater simulations
reveal distinct stress-concentration patterns: tube support plate (TSP) regions experience peak
stresses of approximately 331 MPa, compared with only 4 MPa in U-bend sections. Moreover,
cold-leg TSPs exhibit higher degradation rates than their hot-leg counterparts [16]. The innermost
tubes sustain the highest stresses due to their proximity to fixed boundary conditions, while tube-
to-tubesheet joints consistently emerge as primary failure locations. Cumulative damage
projections indicate that each start-up cycle reduces component useful life by 2.8–4.5 %, with TSP
regions degrading at roughly twice the rate of non-TSP areas.
 Damage-mechanism evaluation employs multiple evaluation criteria. Creep damage is
assessed using time-fraction models that account for stress and temperature history. Fatigue life is
estimated through strain-range partitioning, which decomposes the total strain into elastic, plastic,
and creep components for accurate cycle-based damage accumulation [17]. Environmental
degradation is modeled using diffusion–reaction formulations that couple corrosion or oxidation
kinetics with local stress and temperature fields. FSI analyses identify susceptibility to FIV by
coupling CFD and FEA simulations to extract modal frequencies, amplitudes, and damping
ratios—where values below 1% indicate potential for destructive resonance [2]. Collectively, these
pre-test simulations establish quantitative predictions of component behavior that define the
baseline for model–test integration, serving as the critical reference point where computational
prediction meets experimental measurement.

5.4. Model-Test Integration and Digital Twin Implementation
 The ultimate goal of the computational-experimental framework is to establish a closed
validation loop in which model predictions and experimental measurements inform one another in
real time. Within this paradigm, the Digital Twin acts as the unifying platform that fuses
simulation, test data, and operational monitoring into a dynamic, continuously updated
representation of component health.

5.4.1. Integration of Computational and Experimental Results
 Model-test integration begins with the systematic comparison of simulation outputs against
measured quantities obtained during testing. Each dataset is accompanied by quantified
uncertainty bounds so that agreement or discrepancy can be assessed statistically.

 Mechanistic Analysis compares predicted failure modes with observed damage patterns to
 validate assumed degradation mechanisms. Any divergence—such as unanticipated failure
 locations or previously unmodeled damage patterns—triggers immediate reassessment of the
 underlying physics, ensuring that agreement is achieved through mechanistic fidelity rather
 than through parameter adjustment.

 Quantitative Validation follows the methodology established in ASME V&V 20 and
 summarized in Section 5.2.2. Numerical uncertainty is quantified using Grid Convergence
 Index (GCI) evaluations, while input-parameter uncertainty is addressed through Latin Hyper-
 cube Sampling or equivalent probabilistic sampling methods. These procedures ensure that
 simulation-to-experiment comparisons account for both discretization errors and input
 variability.

 Statistical Correlation evaluates overall model performance by comparing predicted versus
 measured component lifetimes and other key response metrics. Validation metrics include
 coverage factors indicating whether the predicted uncertainty bounds encompass the
 experimental scatter within the desired confidence level. Systematic trends in prediction error
 are used to compute bias factors, identifying persistent over- or under-prediction tendencies
 that guide subsequent model refinement and calibration.

5.4.2. Model Calibration and Refinement
 When validation results reveal discrepancies that exceed the combined uncertainty bounds,
model calibration and adaptive refinement are performed. Calibration uses methodologies like
Bayesian updating to refine material constants and environmental coefficients based on new
experimental data [12]. Uncertain model parameters (e.g., creep constants, fatigue coefficients,
oxidation kinetics, or friction factors) are treated as random variables with prior probability
distributions reflecting their initial uncertainty. As new experimental or operational data become
available, these priors are updated to posterior distributions using Bayesian inference, yielding

 30

parameter estimates that incorporate both model predictions and measurement evidence. This
approach provides a statistically rigorous means of reducing epistemic uncertainty while
quantifying residual variability in the calibrated parameters.
 When discrepancies indicate missing or incomplete physics, the model is augmented to capture
additional mechanisms (e.g., coupled corrosion-fatigue interaction, environmental creep damage,
thermal ratcheting, or stress-relaxation effects). Each refinement is introduced in a controlled,
traceable manner to prevent over-fitting or loss of interpretability [18][19]. Revised models
undergo re-verification to confirm numerical stability and consistency with lower-level validation
benchmarks before being re-integrated into the qualification framework.

5.4.3. Digital Twin Validation Loop
 The Digital Twin represents the convergence of computational modeling, experimental
validation, and operational monitoring into a continuously updated, physics-informed
representation of component condition [16][19]. It serves as a living analytical tool that evolves
with accumulating data, providing real-time insight into system health and predictive estimates of
remaining useful life (RUL).

 Simulation Predictions: The digital twin generates physics-based predictions of critical
 performance parameters (including temperature and stress distributions, damage accumulation,
 and failure probabilities) under a variety of duty cycles and environmental conditions. This
 includes predictions for creep-fatigue life, fretting wear, and corrosion rates using validated
 physics-based models.

 Experimental Data Correlation: Laboratory-scale test data are used to calibrate and validate
 the material constitutive relationships and environmental degradation models embedded within
 the digital twin. This correlation ensures that the simulated behavior faithfully represents real
 material response. For example, creep-fatigue testing in impure helium directly validates the
 oxidation-assisted degradation mechanisms relevant to HTGR heat exchangers, anchoring the
 predictive models to experimentally verified physics.

 Operational and Inspection Data Integration: As heat exchangers accrue service hours,
 operational data (e.g., temperature, pressure, and flow-rate histories) and inspection data (e.g.,
 eddy-current measurements, visual inspections) are continuously assimilated into the digital
 twin [4].

 Model Refinement Process: Discrepancies between predicted and measured responses
 initiate a model refinement process, in which parameters are adjusted, constitutive laws are
 improved, or additional physical mechanisms are incorporated. Through this feedback
 mechanism, the digital twin evolves in parallel with the physical asset, maintaining alignment
 between predicted and observed degradation states.

 31

Digital-twin implementation offers substantial economic benefits by supporting condition-based
maintenance (CBM) and reducing unplanned outages. Operation and maintenance (O&M) costs
in nuclear power plants account for up to 32% of the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE)—
significantly higher than the 10-25% typical of conventional power generation.
A single feedwater-heater tube failure can result in annual losses exceeding $1 million, and with
more than 1,000 such heaters installed across 94 U.S. reactors, the aggregate financial risk is
considerable [4].

5.5. Life Prediction and Extrapolation
 Predicting long-term component performance (often spanning several decades of service)
requires extrapolating short-term test data beyond experimentally accessible timescales. Because
direct, full-life testing is infeasible, life extrapolation must be grounded in mechanistic
understanding, validated accelerated testing, and rigorous uncertainty quantification.

5.5.1. Degradation Mechanism Modeling
 Reliable long-term extrapolation depends on maintaining mechanistic fidelity between
accelerated test conditions and actual service environments. Acceleration strategies that alter
dominant degradation mechanisms lead to non-conservative or misleading predictions. For
example, non-Arrhenius behavior—manifested as curvature in Arrhenius plots—signals a change
in rate-controlling mechanisms, where competing reactions with different activation energies
dominate across temperature regimes [20]. Similarly, diffusion-limited oxidation can shift
degradation from a surface-controlled to a bulk-controlled process, altering both kinetics and
failure morphology. Other long-term uncertainties arise from cliff-edge effects (sudden
acceleration in degradation), trace-impurity interactions, and synergistic degradation modes (e.g.,
creep-oxidation coupling) that evolve slowly over tens of thousands of hours. To mitigate these
risks, in-service monitoring provides empirical feedback that validates long-term degradation
predictions, while conservative design margins accommodate residual uncertainty in extrapolated
behavior. Given the practical constraints of time and cost, extrapolation is unavoidable; the critical
question becomes not “Can we avoid extrapolation?” but rather “How can we extrapolate with
defensible, quantified uncertainty bounds?”
 A relevant example is provided by Haynes 230 testing [21]. A unified viscoplasticity model,
meticulously calibrated against low-cycle fatigue and strain-dwell experiments, accurately
reproduced short-term behavior but systematically over-predicted strain accumulation in long-
term, stress-controlled creep tests. The discrepancy revealed that material parameters governing
stress relaxation during 120-second hold cycles do not necessarily predict creep deformation over
10,000 + hours. This finding underscores a fundamental limitation: the assumption that a single
constitutive state formulation can span both short-term and long-term time scales may be flawed.
Consequently, extrapolating models calibrated on sub-10,000-hour data to 60-year service

 32

intervals introduces irreducible epistemic uncertainty that cannot be eliminated through parameter
refinement alone.

5.5.2. Extrapolation Methodology
 Given these inherent challenges, a robust extrapolation strategy combining mechanistic
modeling with statistical uncertainty quantification is required to produce credible long-term life
predictions.

 Physics-Based Extrapolation with Uncertainty Quantification: The foundation of reliable
 extrapolation rests on mechanistic models that explicitly separate different degradation modes,
 ensuring that each damage mechanism is captured through appropriate physical laws rather
 than empirical correlations. Temperature-time parameters, particularly Larson-Miller
 relationships [22], must be validated across multiple material heats to establish confidence in
 their applicability beyond the original calibration data. Damage accumulation laws require
 mechanism-specific validation to ensure that the mathematical formulations accurately
 represent the underlying physics of creep, fatigue, and environmental attack.

 Statistical Bounding Approaches: Complementing physics-based models, statistical
 methods provide essential uncertainty quantification for extrapolation scenarios. Bayesian
 methods offer a rigorous framework for incorporating all available prior knowledge,
 systematically combining data from different sources and experimental conditions to
 strengthen predictions [23]. Probabilistic resampling (e.g., bootstrap or Monte Carlo
 techniques) generates confidence intervals, typically at the 95 % level, that capture
 experimental scatter and limited sample size effects. Model averaging across multiple
 formulations captures epistemic uncertainty by combining predictions from different
 mechanistic models, recognizing that no single model may capture all relevant physics and that
 the true behavior likely lies within the envelope of competing formulations.

 33

6. PROPOSED TEST PLAN AND METHODOLOGY

 This section defines the integrated strategy for experimental qualification of the heat-
exchanger test article. The plan combines (i) pre-test computational analyses that identify critical
degradation mechanisms, (ii) targeted experiments designed to reproduce representative operating
and off-normal conditions, and (iii) an iterative validation loop coupling test data with model
refinement inside the digital-twin environment.

6.1. Pre-Test Analysis Procedures
 Prior to hardware fabrication, high-fidelity simulations will be performed to define the
mechanical and thermal boundary conditions expected under prototypical service. The objectives
of this phase are to:

 Identify critical locations and load paths.
 Structural analyses will resolve local stress, strain, and temperature gradients throughout the
 HX, focusing on tube-to-tubesheet joints, helical-coil bends, diffusion-bond interfaces, and
 support-plate contacts. Regions exhibiting high equivalent stress (> 300 MPa) or elevated
 creep-strain accumulation will be targeted for instrumentation.

 Quantify residual-stress and contact-mechanics effects.
 Manufacturing-induced residual stresses and contact loads at support points will be included
 to replicate realistic assembly conditions that strongly influence fatigue and fretting initiation.

 Define test-loop transients.
 System-level models (SAM or RELAP5-3D) will prescribe pressure, temperature, and flow-
 rate histories for both steady-state and transient conditions. These will establish the operating
 matrix for the subsequent experimental phase.

 Select instrumentation and sensor locations.
 Thermal, strain, and flow diagnostics will be positioned at predicted hot spots and high-
 gradient regions. Embedded thermocouples and fiber-optic strain gauges will be prioritized
 where direct optical access is limited.

6.2. Degradation Mechanism Modeling
 The test plan explicitly targets the dominant degradation mechanisms identified in Sections 3
and 4 and represented in the computational framework of Section 5. These include:

 Creep–fatigue interaction: Tests will impose combined thermal and mechanical cycling
 consistent with 40–60-year duty-cycle equivalence. Damage accumulation will be evaluated

 34

 through time-fraction and strain-fraction rules and benchmarked against predictive creep–
 fatigue diagrams.

 Corrosion and environmental attack: Controlled-chemistry loops (helium or sodium) will
 reproduce representative impurity ratios and oxygen levels. Corrosion kinetics will be assessed
 via parabolic-rate law fitting and surface characterization to calibrate environment-specific
 degradation coefficients.

 Fretting and wear: Specialized vibration fixtures will reproduce flow-induced contact
 conditions between tube supports. Wear coefficients will be derived from measured mass-loss
 rates and surface profilometry.

Each mechanism will be exercised both individually (through focused sub-component tests) and
in combination within the integrated HX assembly to capture synergistic effects. Constitutive
parameters extracted from these tests will feed back into the digital twin for Bayesian calibration
and uncertainty reduction.

6.3. Integration of Analysis and Test Data
  Model-test integration will follow a formal Validation Loop Methodology consistent with
ASME V&V 10/20:

1. Prediction:
 The verified models predict spatial distributions of temperature, stress, and damage for each
 planned transient.

2. Measurement:
 Experimental campaigns acquire synchronized data streams (temperature, strain, vibration,
 chemistry) with quantified uncertainty.

3. Comparison and Updating:
 Statistical consistency between predicted and measured responses is evaluated using coverage
 factors and bias metrics. Discrepancies beyond combined uncertainty bounds trigger model-
 parameter updating via Bayesian inference.

4. Iteration:
 Refined models re-predict subsequent test sequences, closing the validation loop and
 progressively improving predictive capability.

Uncertainty propagation will be performed using Latin-hypercube Monte Carlo sampling to
distinguish aleatory from epistemic components. The resulting confidence intervals on predicted
failure time or damage accumulation define quantitative credibility metrics for qualification

 35

decisions. Finally, all validated models and experimental datasets will populate the Digital Twin
Validation Loop, enabling real-time health monitoring and remaining-useful-life (RUL) estimation
during extended test operation. This continuously updated framework provides the traceable
foundation for extrapolating accelerated-test observations to full-life performance and for
demonstrating compliance with DOE reliability and safety objectives.

7. QUALIFICATION CONSIDERATIONS

7.1. In-service Inspection and Monitoring Methods
 Given the wide diversity of reactor coolants under consideration, the designs of Heat
Exchangers (HXs) and Steam Generators (SGs) proposed for AR systems exhibit substantial
variation in configuration and operating environment. Table 7-1 summarizes representative HX
types reported in the literature for AR concepts. According to U.S. NRC pre-application reviews
[24], to date, formal design certification has been granted only for the NuScale SMR—a
Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR) employing a once-through helical-coil steam generator
(HCSG). All other AR concepts remain in various stages of pre-application evaluation. Informed
decisions regarding degradation assessment, inspection methodologies, and inspection frequency
depend on the availability of relevant historical performance data. Even for AR systems that retain
conventional SG architectures, established nondestructive examination (NDE) techniques may
require modification to accommodate alternative support structures, materials, and degradation
modes not typically encountered in Light Water reactors (LWRs). Consequently, the design of
HXs and SGs for AR applications should prioritize in-service inspectability as a fundamental
criterion from the earliest stages of development.
 A number of factors must be considered to effectively address the challenges associated with
inspection and monitoring of HXs in AR systems. These components employ a wide range of
structural materials (e.g., high-strength alloys, corrosion-resistant steels, and, in some cases,
ceramics) each requiring tailored expertise and inspection techniques to accurately detect damage
and degradation. Elevated operating temperatures, chemically aggressive coolants, and, in certain
concepts such as HTGRs, particulate environments (e.g., graphite dust) further complicate sensor
durability and the interpretation of inspection data. Moreover, HX designs may exhibit degradation
and failure mechanisms not typically encountered in LWRs, reinforcing the need for specialized
and targeted inspection approaches. Because some advanced HX geometries are inherently
difficult to access for inspection or repair, remote and automated inspection technologies—
including robotic, fiber-optic, or ultrasonic systems—will be essential. The selection of specific
inspection and monitoring methods will depend on the reactor type, structural material, and
dominant degradation modes. Given the limited operational experience with these novel designs,
performance demonstrations of in-service inspection (ISI) techniques should be carried out using
scaled mock-up experiments. Such campaigns enable adaptation of conventional NDE methods to
new design configurations and support the development of specialized tools not yet available for
deployment.

 In summary, reliable inspection of safety-critical HXs is essential to safe operation of advanced
reactors, as the tubes or flow channels are the primary barrier between the primary and secondary
fluids. Degradation or leakage can lead to costly downtime and pose safety risks. Selecting the
appropriate NDE techniques based on the HX design and material and expected defect types is
crucial for implementation of effective ISIs. Further research and development are ongoing to
improve the reliability and applicability of NDE methods for the unique challenges presented by
advanced reactor HXs.
 Past and ongoing research activities associated with NDE of nuclear power plant components
at Argonne have primarily revolved around life-extension and management strategies. A wide
range of NDE techniques including electromagnetic and acoustic methods have been evaluated in
application to ISI of nuclear power plant components including HXs and SGs [26]. Multinational
collaborations are being conducted to help assess the reliability of conventional and emerging NDE
technologies for ISI of SGs. The NDE test facilities at Argonne have been used in support of such
research activities for LWRs and for ARs. Analytical and numerical modeling tools are utilized to
accurately simulate the NDE probe interaction with complex media. These tools can help optimize
the probe design and to further minimize costly destructive examinations for validation of NDE
results. Advanced signal processing algorithms and automated data analysis software have also
been implemented for more effective and efficient processing of inspection data. Degradation-
specific management requires detailed knowledge about the specific nature and severity of flaws.
Improved NDE, OLM, and SHM techniques are needed for more reliable ISIs. The reliability and
accuracy of the techniques need to be quantified so that repair criteria and the consequences of
degraded parts remaining in service after inspections can be evaluated. Extensive experience with
NDE of HXs and access to DOE national user facilities such as the Mechanisms Engineering Test
Loop (METL) uniquely positions Argonne to contribute to the development and evaluation of
viable inspection and monitoring methods for the future fleet of advanced reactors.

7.2. Inspection Requirements and Performance Metrics
 A Technical Specification (TS) document is a regulatory-controlled licensing document that
defines the minimum operating limits, conditions, and surveillance requirements (SRs) necessary
to ensure safe operation of a nuclear power plant (NPP). It specifies the minimum plant
configuration and performance criteria for systems, structures, and components (SSCs) important
to safety. Technical specifications are legally binding, so plant operators must always comply with
their requirements. The TS document covers broad systems and safety functions that for LWRs
include SG tube integrity limits, leakage limits, and tube plugging criteria. In practice, TS
documents for LWRs are highly standardized across NPPs. In the U.S., the standard technical
specifications (STS) developed by the US NRC and industry groups (e.g., the Westinghouse
Owners Group, BWR Owners Group, etc.) serve as templates, tailored to each plant. The SR
section of the TS document specifies the tests, inspections, calibrations, and monitoring needed to
verify compliance with limiting conditions for operation. Regarding SG tube integrity limits, the
primary purpose of the TS is to ensure SG tubes can maintain the reactor coolant pressure boundary

 42

(i.e., structural integrity), primary-to-secondary leakage remains below regulatory limits (i.e.,
leakage control), and SGs provide adequate heat transfer and support reactor shutdown (i.e.,
functional performance). Inspection requirements for SGs that are defined in the TS document
mandate the use of NDE methods capable of detecting tube degradation mechanisms (e.g., wall
thinning or stress corrosion cracking (SCC)) with sufficient sensitivity to identify flaws of 20% or
greater of the tube wall (TW) thickness. Any indications of tube degradation approaching TS limits
trigger expanded inspection or corrective action. Table 7-4 provides common TS limits for SGs,
in line with STS established by NRC. Technical specification limits summarized in this table are

NUREG-1571, and EPRI Steam Generator Integrity Guidelines. The exact numbers could vary
slightly by vendor (Westinghouse, CE, B&W, etc.).

 A degradation assessment (DA) document on the other hand is a structured engineering
evaluation prepared by NPP’s materials, systems, or aging management team to demonstrate that
safety-related SSCs remain capable of performing their intended functions throughout plant life.

 43

While the TS document specifies binding requirements, the DA document for a plant provides
evidence and engineering basis that demonstrates compliance with TS limits, especially for aging
management and continued operation. The DA process serves to identify credible degradation
mechanisms, document inspection and monitoring results, evaluate the current component
condition and degradation trends, and define appropriate mitigation or repair strategies. It provides
the technical basis for ISI programs, compliance with the 10 CFR 50.65 maintenance rule, and
implementation of license renewal aging management programs (AMPs). Table 7-5 summarizes
the safety limits associated with SG tube degradation mechanisms. These limits are derived from
the criteria established in NRC Generic Letter 95-05, the EPRI Steam Generator Integrity
Guidelines, and the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section XI.
 As a requirement, DA is first performed prior to the pre-service inspection of SGs and
subsequently prior to each ISI. The inspection and monitoring results include a summary of NDE
results, documentation of trends and uncertainties, and identification of areas with limited
inspection coverage. Other components of DA include condition monitoring (CM) and operational
assessment (OA). For CM, the actual findings (e.g., measured flaw sizes in SG tubes) are compared
to the acceptance limits (e.g., TS document, ASME Section XI, EPRI guidelines). Flaw evaluation
methods such as fracture mechanics are leak-before-break are used for this purpose. OA involves
projecting the current condition of components (e.g., SG tubes) to the next ISI. The predicted
results are then used to determine acceptability relative to the integrity performance criteria.
Degradation growth rates are predicted using models that employ SCC crack growth laws. This
allows identification of components at risk of reaching limits before the end of operating period.
Strict limits are placed on SG tube integrity as tube failure is of high consequence, even at low
frequency.
 Degradation assessment documents are also applicable and necessary for advanced reactors,
though their scope, methods, and regulatory context may differ from those used for current LWRs.
For ARs, the DA document contributes to a) design certification and safety analysis reports
(SARs), b) aging management and reliability assurance programs, (c) risk-informed inspection
planning, and (d) life-cycle management. New reactor designs introduce novel materials, coolants,
and operating conditions (e.g., high temperature, liquid metals, molten salts). For advanced
reactors (e.g., SMRs, HTGRs, MSRs, SFRs, or LFRs), DA is essential because materials could be
subject to higher levels of thermal, mechanical, and radiation stresses than LWRs. This assessment
is thus necessary to address regulatory requirements regarding assurance of structural integrity and
long-term performance for new HX designs. As discussed below, DOE-NE and NRC have
guidance on degradation assessments as part of materials and component performance programs
for non-LWRs. ASME BPVC Section III, Division 5 (high-temperature reactors), includes
material degradation evaluation requirements.

7.3. Code Compliance and Standards
In general, the primary objectives of ISI programs for advanced reactor HXs are to:

 Detect and characterize active and potential damage and degradation, including cracks,
 corrosion, erosion, and fouling.
 Verify pressure boundary integrity (i.e., no through-wall leaks).
 Monitor thermal degradation or material embrittlement.
 Support license renewal and risk-informed maintenance.

ISI intervals depend on risk-informed assessment and commonly consist of post-installation initial
baseline inspections, periodic inspections that depend on design class, and condition-based
monitoring for HXs equipped with SHM systems. Typical regulatory frameworks that may be used

 45

to establish an ISI program include ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) Section XI
[27], ASME BPVC Section III [28], and NRC 10 CFR 50.55a [29] requirements.
 ASME Section XI prescribes the ISI program rules used by licensed nuclear plants, including
examination categories, intervals, procedure and personnel qualification, and acceptance criteria.
ASME also issues Code Cases which provide alternative or supplemental rules for novel materials
and methods. The NRC typically endorses selected Code Cases via Federal Register rulemaking
so licensees can apply them without prior NRC approval. Section XI is the primary baseline for
ISI requirements where HXs are within the plant’s code jurisdiction—it is part of safety-related
pressure boundaries. For non-traditional exchangers such as diffusion-bonded PCHEs and brazed
plate-fin blocks licensed plants may rely on applicable Code Cases or develop and justify
equivalent rules because standard Section XI rules assume conventional welds, tubing, and
accessible geometries. The NRC’s recent actions on which Code Cases are accepted need to be
taken into perspective as they define which alternative inspection and repair approaches may be
allowed.
 ASME BPVC Section III, Division 5 contains rules for the design and construction of
components intended for high-temperature reactors, addressing creep-fatigue, material properties,
weld and joint acceptance, material testing [17]. The NRC has been actively reviewing Division 5
editions via regulatory guides. Division 5 provides design and material rules that directly affect
inspectability—allowable stresses, expected failure modes (creep, time-dependent strain), weld
qualification and the expectation that components be designed to allow inspection where feasible.
For IHXs and PCHEs intended for elevated temperature service, Division 5 compliance, or
justified deviation, is central to convincing a regulator that the HX is inspectable and maintainable.
The NRC has a recent (2023–2025) activity stream to endorse the 2023 Edition of Division 5 [30],
indicating regulator attention to high-temperature component rules.
 The NRC regulation 10 CFR 50.55a requires licensees to comply with the rules and accepted
Code Cases of the ASME BPVC for the design, fabrication, and ISI of designated plant
components. The regulation further specifies the process for implementing Code Cases, mandates
the use of the most recent applicable edition at the time of first application, and defines the
conditions under which Code Cases may be adopted without prior NRC approval [31]. For any
HX that is safety-related, or part of systems regulated by the NRC, compliance with 10 CFR 50.55a
dictates whether a given ASME Code Case or Section XI rule is directly applicable. If a new HX
design uses methods not yet codified, licensees must either: (a) use an approved Code Case; (b)
request NRC approval for an equivalent alternative method; or (c) propose design or inspection
features that meet the regulation’s safety intent. The Federal Register actions and RG updates
determine which Code Cases are pre-approved for straightforward use.
 NRC regulatory guides (RGs) show methods acceptable to the NRC staff for meeting
regulations (e.g., endorsing editions of ASME codes like Division 5). The NRC has also been
publishing Advanced Reactor Application Guidance to support technology-inclusive, risk-
informed licensing for non-LWRs. Draft and revision activity (e.g., DG-1436, RG 1.87 Revision
3) reflect updates to accept newer ASME editions and provide guidance on how to apply them to

 46

HTGRs and other advanced designs. Regulatory Guides describe which editions of ASME code
the NRC accepts, with exceptions, and outline expectations for justification of proposed
deviations. The Advanced Reactor Application Guidance gives pointers for a technology-
inclusive, risk-informed case, including how to demonstrate inspection adequacy when standard
code coverage is weak. Recent draft RG activity (Dec 2024 public draft) indicates the NRC is
actively adapting guidance to Division 5 in its application to ARs.
 The NRC has pursued a risk-informed, technology-inclusive regulatory approach—
rulemaking, Commission papers, Federal Register notices—to make licensing of advanced
reactors more practicable [32][33]. Separately, ASME Code Cases and the ASME code-case
process exist for formally introducing alternative rules for new materials, fabrication methods, and
NDE techniques. Risk-informed regulation supports alternatives to prescriptive ISI schedules and
allows licensees to demonstrate safety through PRA and condition-based monitoring. Code Cases
give a structured pathway to accept novel fabrication and inspection approaches (e.g., diffusion
bonded microchannels, embedded SHM). Federal-register inclusion of Code Cases means
licensees can apply those cases without unique NRC approval, which is intended to shorten review
times.

8. CONCLUDING REMARKS
 This preliminary plan defines the foundational methodology for qualification of advanced HXs
intended for nuclear-to-industrial heat transfer applications. The report establishes SFR and HTGR
configurations as bounding reference systems that capture the range of thermal, mechanical, and
chemical environments relevant to long-duration service. Within these envelopes, the principal
degradation mechanisms—creep–fatigue interaction, flow-induced vibration, corrosion, and
diffusion-bond deterioration—were identified and analyzed as dominant contributors to life-
limiting behavior. A rigorous, computational-experimental framework has been formulated to
support qualification testing and life prediction. Pre-test analyses derived from this framework will
define test-loop operating conditions, instrumentation requirements, and acceleration factors that
preserve mechanistic fidelity while enabling time-compressed evaluation of long-term
phenomena. The associated VVUQ hierarchy follows ASME V&V 10/20 and NRC NUREG-2152
guidance, establishing a stepwise approach from material coupon validation to full-scale
component testing.
 The integration of modeling, experimentation, and monitoring within a digital-twin
architecture provides a continuously updating representation of HX condition and remaining useful
life. The digital twin serves as the convergence platform for simulation, test data, and in-service
monitoring, enabling real-time assessment of degradation and adaptive model refinement through
Bayesian calibration. This capability supports risk-informed maintenance planning and underpins
regulatory confidence in predictive life-management methodologies. The proposed test plan will
emphasize (i) accelerated creep-fatigue and corrosion testing under prototypical sodium and
helium conditions, (ii) vibration and fretting-wear characterization in representative geometries,
and (iii) validation of environmental degradation models through controlled impurity and
temperature variation. Data generated from these campaigns will be used to calibrate and validate
the computational framework and to refine material-specific constitutive parameters for long-term
extrapolation. In parallel, the report identifies inspection and monitoring requirements essential
for safe, reliable operation of advanced HXs. Adaptation of established NDE methods, combined
with emerging OLM and SHM technologies, will enhance detectability of degradation in compact
geometries. Integration of these data streams into the digital-twin environment will provide
continuous validation and inform condition-based maintenance strategies.

 The next phase of work will implement the pre-test computational analyses, finalize the
experimental matrix, and initiate fabrication of the HX test article and supporting loop hardware.
Subsequent milestones will include (1) completion of the integrated test-loop design review, (2)
execution of initial accelerated degradation tests, and (3) assimilation of results into the digital-
twin framework for model calibration. Collectively, these activities will deliver a validated,
mechanistically grounded approach for demonstrating 40–60-year equivalent service life with
quantified uncertainty bounds, thereby providing the technical basis for qualification and eventual
deployment of advanced nuclear heat-transfer components.
