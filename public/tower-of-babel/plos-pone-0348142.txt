Household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and larval indices surveillance systems in southern Thailand: A mixed methods study

Charuai Suwanbamrung, Kusubeeyah Babathee, Sikarin Aiedchuthong, Juthaphon Detaran, Farusdeen Madsen, Lafi Munira, Dereje Haile, Aseb Arba Kinfe

ABSTRACT
Background: Dengue fever remains a major public health concern in tropical regions, particularly in Southeast Asia. Effective prevention depends on the community’s understanding of both dengue control measures and vector surveillance systems. This study assessed and explored household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions (care and preventive measures) and the Aedes larval index surveillance system in southern Thailand. Methods: A community-based convergent mixed-methods study was conducted among 824 household representatives selected through stratified random sampling. Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire and analyzed using logistic regression to identify factors associated with understanding. Qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews with 13 purposively selected participants and analyzed using thematic analysis. Findings were integrated using a joint display approach. Results: Only 28.9% of participants demonstrated a high understanding of the dengue solution, and 1.2% had a high understanding of the larval indices surveillance system. Training attendance (AOR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.45–2.78), fewer water containers (AOR 2.06, 95% CI: 1.20–3.54), and higher larvae-positive containers (AOR 0.64, 95% CI: 0.43–0.97) were significantly associated with lower understanding. Qualitative findings revealed the following key themes: recognition of dengue fever, awareness of preventive practices, risky season, and the importance of training. Training was identified as a key factor in improving understanding and practices. Conclusion: Household heads’ understanding of dengue prevention is limited, particularly regarding larval surveillance systems. Household representatives were familiar with dengue symptoms; they lacked technical knowledge of surveillance systems. Strengthening community-based training and improving the communication of technical concepts are essential to enhance dengue prevention and control efforts.

1 Introduction Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by antigenically related dengue virus serotypes (DENV 1–4) and remains one of the most important vector-borne diseases worldwide. Dengue fever is endemic in more than 100 countries, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions, with Southeast Asia representing the global epicenter of dengue fever [ 1 – 3 ]. Over the past two decades, the global burden of dengue has increased substantially, with reported cases rising from half a million in 2000 to 5.2 million in 2019 [ 4 , 5 ]. Following a temporary decline during the COVID-19 pandemic, dengue resurged dramatically, with more than 6 million cases and over 6,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2023, representing the largest epidemic on history [ 4 , 6 ]. In Asia, there were more than 1.6 million cases and 3,637 deaths in 2023 [ 7 ]. The majority of the Asian population, particularly in Southeast Asia, is estimated to be living in areas where dengue transmission susceptibility is high [ 5 , 8 ]. Thailand is one of the 30 countries with the highest dengue fever endemicity, where transmission is influenced by serotype shifts, climatic conditions, and vector ecology. Although the dengue incidence declined during the COVID-19 period, the number of reported cases increased again in 2022, with Southern Thailand continuing to experience a considerable burden of disease [ 3 , 9 , 10 ]. According to the WHO South-East Asia Regional Office Epidemiological Bulletin, Thailand reported approximately 46,000–47,000 dengue cases in 2022. In 2023, dengue cases increased markedly to 150,808, with 165 deaths reported by December. This was the highest dengue burden recorded in Thailand since 2018, highlighting the resurgence of dengue transmission [ 11 ]. Southern Thailand remains a dengue-endemic region characterized by recurrent seasonal outbreaks and persistent Aedes aegypti breeding sites within the community [ 8 , 12 ]. Previous surveillance and operational research conducted in the region, including the development of the “Laskan Model” and community-based larval index surveillance systems, have demonstrated that dengue transmission is closely associated with household water storage practices and environmental management [ 12 ]. Despite control efforts, the persistence of household breeding sites and recurrent outbreaks highlights the importance of community participation in dengue prevention and larval surveillance. Understanding household representatives’ knowledge of dengue prevention measures and larval surveillance systems is therefore essential for strengthening community-based dengue control programs in endemic settings such as southern Thailand [ 12 , 13 ]. Dengue fever can be prevented through vector control, source reduction, personal protection, and community engagement in surveillance and monitoring. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of prevention depends on the community’s and other stakeholders’ understanding and action of the recommended dengue fever solutions and the larval indices surveillance system [ 10 , 13 – 17 ]. Previous studies conducted in Thailand have reported moderate to good understanding of dengue prevention among community members, although preventive practices often remain suboptimal, while poor understanding has been reported among high school students, particularly regarding larval surveillance concepts [ 18 – 20 ]. According to Bloom’s revised taxonomy, understanding is a cognitive process that enables individuals to interpret, apply, and translate knowledge into practice [ 21 ]. In the context of dengue control, understanding of disease transmission, symptoms and management, vector ecology, and surveillance strategies is essential for effective household-level prevention. Previous studies have shown that understanding is influenced by educational attainment, access to health information, socioeconomic status, prior dengue experience, participation in community programs, and dengue-related training [ 13 , 15 , 16 , 22 ] Sichon District represents an environment where favorable climatic conditions for Aedes mosquito breeding, recurrent dengue outbreaks, and an active community network intersect. Household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes larval index surveillance system is crucial, as it enables them to take responsibility for early treatment, manage their environment effectively, and educate family members and neighbors on controlling the breeding sites of Aedes mosquitoes. However, limited research has investigated household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions or care and preventive measures, and larval surveillance systems among household representatives, who are the primary role players in environmental management indoors and outdoors. Therefore, this study aimed to fill the gap through achieving the following objectives: 1) To describe the household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions or care and preventive measures and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system; 2) To identify factors associated with household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system; 3) To explore the household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system. 2 Methods and materials 2.1 Research design This study employed a community-based, convergent parallel mixed-methods design to assess the understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes Mosquito Larvae Surveillance System among household representatives in Sichon District, Nakhon Si Thammarat Province. Data were collected concurrently for a better explanation of household representatives’ understanding of both dengue fever solutions and the Aedes Mosquito Larvae Index surveillance system, the associated factors, their experience with dengue solutions, and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system from November 15, 2023, to January 30, 2024. 2.2 Setting Sichon District is one of the northernmost districts of Nakhon Si Thammarat and is located along the eastern coast of southern Thailand, bordering the Gulf of Thailand. The district covers both coastal lowlands and hilly inland areas, with a tropical monsoon climate that provides favorable conditions for Aedes mosquito breeding throughout the year. Sichon District has a predominantly rural population engaged in agriculture, fishing, and small-scale commerce, with scattered urban centers serving as local hubs for education and healthcare. The district’s public health infrastructure includes subdistrict health-promoting hospitals and village health volunteers, who play a central role in dengue surveillance and prevention activities. Owing to its climatic and ecological conditions, the Sichon district has experienced recurrent dengue outbreaks, making it a suitable and representative setting for studying community understanding and participation in the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system. The study area comprises 9 subdistricts with 27,767 households. 2.3 Population and eligibility The population was household representatives aged 20 years and older residing in Sichon District, Nakhon Si Thammarat Province. 2.3.1 Inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: Household representatives aged 20 years or older, residing at a registered address in Sichon District, Nakhon Si Thammarat Province, and who consented to participate and provide information participated in the quantitative study, whereas household representatives who had experience with dengue fever and the larval index surveillance system and were willing to participate in the in-depth interview were involved in the qualitative study. 2.4 Sample size determination The quantitative sample size was determined as part of the planning of the broader dengue prevention implementation project that followed the baseline mixed methods assessment. It was calculated using the GPower 3.1 program, with an effect size of 0.15 (small effect), a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95. The degrees of freedom were set at 2, and the effect size was determined based on the research findings, which reported that 33.9% of participants had a good understanding before training [ 23 ], This was used to estimate that exposure to information would improve understanding by 7%. Using these parameters, the GPower analysis produced a required sample size of 687 participants. To accommodate an expected 20% nonresponse rate and reduce potential errors, the final sample size was increased to 824 participants. A total of 824 household representatives were therefore included in the quantitative baseline assessment. The sample size of the qualitative study was 13 household representatives, and the interviews were stopped because of information saturation. 2.5 Sampling method The stratified random sampling technique was employed to ensure that the participants selected were representative of all districts within the study area. This method allowed for the inclusion of diverse household characteristics across the district while minimizing sampling bias. The total households in the Sichon district were stratified into households under subdistrict health-promoting hospitals. The total number of participants was proportionally allocated to each subdistrict health-promoting hospital on the basis of the number of households residing in that subdistrict health-promoting hospital, as shown in the Supplementary file. Within each stratum, households in randomly selected villages were involved in the study to maintain random participant inclusion. A purposive sampling technique was used to select participants for in-depth interviews to obtain information directly relevant to the research objectives. The key informants were household representatives who had experience with dengue fever and the larval index surveillance system. Participation was voluntary, and the number of informants was determined based on data saturation, meaning that data collection continued until participant 13, when no new information or themes emerged. 2.6 Data collection tools The researchers developed a questionnaire based on a review of the relevant literature and reviewed by experts, ensuring that each question aligned with the research objectives and variables of interest. The quantitative data collection questionnaire comprises four sections as follows: Personal Information : includes gender, age, religion, marital status, education level, occupation, history of dengue fever, dengue history among family members, and participation in dengue-related training within the past 12 months. Environmental conditions: comprises housing characteristics, water usage systems, waste management practices, and larval index data. Understanding of Dengue Solutions (UDS): The questionnaire consisted of 10 multiple-choice items focusing on dengue, causes, symptoms and signs, treatment, and prevention with five answer options for each question, which were later converted to correct (1) and incorrect responses (0). The understanding levels were scored as low when the respondent’s score was less than 80% or high if the respondent’s score was equal to or greater than 80% [ 13 , 20 ]. Understanding of the Dengue Larval Index Surveillance System (UDLSS): This section consisted of 10 multiple-choice items comprising the Container Index (CI), the meaning of larval indices, mosquito breeding site elimination, individuals responsible for larval surveys, and methods for larval inspection, with five answer options for each question, which were later converted to correct (1) and incorrect responses (0). This tool was developed from the local larval surveillance system operational procedure and validated by earlier studies. An understanding level of the Aedes mosquito larval index surveillance system was classified as low if the respondent scored less than 80% and high if the household representative answered 80% or more of the questions correctly [ 13 , 20 ]. The researchers developed an in-depth interview guide based on a comprehensive review of the relevant literature, ensuring alignment with the research objectives. The in-depth interview questionnaire included sections on basic personal details and experiences related to dengue fever, insights into environmental assessments, and practices related to dengue fever and the larval index surveillance system. 2.7 Definition of terms Understanding of Dengue Solution : refers to the respondents’ ability to identify key concepts related to dengue fever, including its definition, causes, signs and symptoms, treatment, and prevention. This understanding was assessed using a ten-item questionnaire. Understanding of the Larval Index Surveillance System : refers to the respondents’ ability to identify essential aspects of the larval index surveillance system, such as the Container Index (CI), the meaning of larval indices, mosquito breeding site elimination, individuals responsible for larval surveys, and methods of larval inspection. This understanding was measured using a ten-item questionnaire. Pathogen-related Factors : refer to the causative agent of dengue fever; the dengue virus is transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, which is measured through the larval index, which indicates mosquito density in a given area using the Container Index (CI). The acceptable standard value should not exceed 1%. Household Representative: Refers to an individual recognized by household members as responsible for the financial management, welfare, and health care of household members. The household representative may not necessarily be the house owner or the parents, but could be any family member who assumes these responsibilities. Surveillance System: refers to a systematic and continuous process of monitoring, recording, analyzing, and interpreting data related to disease occurrence and distribution, public health events, or problems. The surveillance process includes data collection, organization, analysis, and dissemination of findings to support effective planning, prevention, and control measures for public health management. 2.8 Quality verification of instruments The research instrument was designed to achieve the research objectives and was checked for its consistency and comprehensive coverage of all relevant research variables in consultation with subject matter experts related to the study. The interview guide was developed based on a review of various studies to ensure its alignment with the research objective. The content validity of the questionnaire and interview guide was examined by three experts, including a nursing lecturer from Walailak University, a public health specialist from the Office of Disease Prevention and Control in Nakhon Si Thammarat Province, and a public health officer from a subdistrict health-promoting hospital. The pilot study of the research instrument was tested with 30 household representatives in Sichon District, Nakhon Si Thammarat Province. Because UDS and UDLSS were scored dichotomously, internal consistency was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson Formula 20. KR-20 was calculated separately for both UDS and UDLSS. The UDS showed good reliability (0.855), whereas UDLSS items had moderate internal consistency (0.65). However, the researchers identified items affecting the internal consistency in UDLSS and revised them before conducting the main study. Both tools had previously undergone language adaptation and pretesting in a population comparable to the study population using the same language and administration format. The final version of the research instrument was used for data collection in this study. The triangulation of the responses of household representatives who participated in the qualitative study was done through observation of water containers indoors and outdoors, and the environmental conditions surrounding their households. The research team members were invited to check for consistency of findings and to ensure the rigor of the study. 2.9 Data collection procedure Quantitative data were collected using a Thai version of a structured questionnaire in collaboration with village health volunteer leaders (VHLs) from each subdistrict health-promoting hospital to aid in data collection from household representatives. Training sessions were conducted for voluntary health leaders during their monthly meetings at each subdistrict health-promoting hospital. VH leaders then guided the data collectors by showing the direction of randomly selected household representatives in their respective villages according to the inclusion criteria. Strict supervision was performed to manage incomplete data reports on time. The researchers conducted an in-depth interview in Thai by scheduling sessions depending on the participants’ availability, with each session lasting approximately 45–60 minutes to minimize disruption. Field notes with an observation checklist were used to record the response of the participants. The interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved, which occurred with the 13 th participant, indicating that no new insights emerged and marking the end of the interview process. 2.10 Ethical approval and consent This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethical Review Board of Walailak University (Project No. WU-EC-PU-1-259-66), and Approval No. WUEC-23-277-01. Informed consent was obtained from each household representative before conducting the interview. 2.11 Data analysis The researcher reviewed all the completed questionnaires for completeness and organized the data systematically. The data in the spreadsheet were exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software for analysis. Continuous variables were then categorized, and latent variables were computed from the collected data. Descriptive statistics were performed for both personal and environmental variables, which are presented as frequencies and percentages. The dependent variables were derived from the specific questions designed to measure each respective construct. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions (UDS) and the larval index surveillance system (UDLSS). Initially, bivariate analyses were conducted to assess each independent variable and the outcome variables. Variables with p-values less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were considered candidates for multivariate analysis to avoid excluding potentially important predictors. Multivariate logistic regression models were then constructed using the Enter approach. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to estimate the strength and direction of associations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Before model fitting, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The assumptions of logistic regression were checked. Because only ten (1.2%) participants met the predefined criterion for high UDLSS, there were insufficient outcome events to support reliable logistic regression modelling for this outcome. Therefore, UDLSS was analyzed descriptively, and no regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with high UDLSS. Qualitative data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach following an inductive coding process. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and reviewed multiple times to ensure familiarity with the data. Initial codes were generated from meaningful segments of the text, and similar codes were grouped into categories based on conceptual similarities. The categories were then synthesized into broader themes that reflected participants’ experience and understanding of dengue prevention and larval surveillance. 2.12 Integration of the quantitative and qualitative findings Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was conducted using a convergent parallel mixed-methods approach. After separate analysis, findings were compared and merged through a joint display technique to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, and divergence. This approach enabled the development of meta-inferences by linking statistical associations with participants’ experiences and perceptions. The qualitative findings were used to explain and contextualize the quantitative results, particularly in understanding the underlying reasons for low levels of knowledge. 3 Results 3.1 Sociodemographic and personal information of the respondents This study involved all 824 targeted household representatives, achieving a complete response rate. In the analysis, three themes emerged: recognition of dengue fever, preventive practices and risky seasons, and the importance of training. 3.10.1 Theme 1. Recognition of dengue fever. Subtheme 1.1: Identification of symptoms of dengue fever Participants of the in-depth interview explained the signs, symptoms, and where to seek treatment when they or a family member or someone else develops the manifestation. The participants listed the signs as high fever, for three to five days, which is not lowered by sponging. The patient develops headaches, cannot eat or drink, experiences body aches, cannot move easily, and wants to rest all the time. When the fever begins to subside, the symptoms alleviate, and the patient resumes eating. The participant stated: “There was only a high fever of 40 degrees Celsius; the patient could not eat at all when having a fever, but when the body temperature returned to normal, the patient started to eat again and regained appetite. However, the recognition of seasonal risk does not appear to be sufficient to prevent high larval indices, suggesting a gap between risk awareness and sustained effective reduction practices of the sources. 4 Discussion This mixed-methods study aimed to: 1) To describe the household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system; 2) To identify factors associated with household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system; 3) To explore the household representatives’ understanding of dengue solutions and the Aedes mosquito larvae index surveillance system. In the current study, only 238 (28.9%) of the household representatives demonstrated a high understanding of dengue solutions, while an extremely small proportion, 10 (1.2%), showed adequate understanding of the larval surveillance system. Attending training related to dengue fever in the last 12 months, the number of water containers and Aedes larvae-positive containers were variables found to be associated with the household head’s understanding of dengue solutions in the multivariate analysis. None of the variables were found to be associated with the understanding of the Aedes larvae indices surveillance system. A high level of understanding of dengue fever solutions was found only among 28.9% of household representatives in the study area. The current finding is lower than the findings of the study conducted on the dengue hotspot in Malaysia, where the good understanding of dengue fever was 50.7% [ 24 ] and the findings of the study conducted on the knowledge and practice of dengue fever among university students in Dhaka City, Bangladesh [ 25 ]. This finding is higher than a study conducted as the baseline of household heads’ understanding of dengue solutions, conducted before the implementation of the training program in high- and low-risk dengue villages in South Thailand, which was 6.3% [ 23 ] and on the primary school students’ knowledge (12.3%) [ 26 ]. But the finding is lower than the high school students’ understanding of dengue solutions (49.5%) in southern Thailand [ 20 ]. This variation may be attributed to differences in the population characteristics, the measurement tools, participants’ prior exposure to information sources, or dengue risk levels across areas. The participants in the in-depth interview explained their understanding of dengue fever, which supports the above findings. Participants explained the common manifestations of dengue fever with recommended actions, and noted that weather conditions contribute to diseases like dengue fever, which is transmitted by the bite of infected female Aedes mosquitoes. They emphasized that these mosquitoes breed in stagnant water and their population surges during the rainy season when such breeding sites become abundant. As noted by one informant: “Dengue fever originates from Aedes mosquitoes, especially females. The disease spreads when mosquito breeding sites increase. Weather influences this; during the rainy season, mosquitoes are abundant, but during the dry season, there are fewer because rainfall creates more stagnant water, and …vomited all day about five to six times. Suddenly red rashes appeared all over the body, completely covered”, FM13. These complementary insights highlight that formal knowledge remains limited, and contextual understanding and disease experience contribute significantly to the findings among study participants. The proportion of household representatives with a high understanding of the Aedes mosquito larval surveillance system was only 1.2%. This finding is lower than the baseline household representatives’ understanding of the larval indices surveillance system, conducted for the implementation of the training program in South Thailand, which was 4.4% [ 23 ], and a quasi-experimental study conducted in Pathum Thani province [ 15 ]. However, the current finding is higher than the high school students’ understanding of the larvae indices surveillance system, which was 0.2% [ 20 ]. The qualitative findings contrast with the quantitative findings, where participants of the qualitative findings described their experience as they actively engaged in campaigns, eliminated stagnant water, and used preventive measures. Participants stressed the engagement as, “we take care of our own houses, observe where there is stagnant water, and destroy it, and observe where there is stagnant water and remove it. The best way is to sleep under a mosquito net, FM7.” This might be because most households believe that Aedes mosquito larvae surveys are the responsibility of public health volunteers and the health care system, as stated by a participant: “VHVs spread information so that everyone knows, and VHVs regularly take care of larvae elimination, FM 5, FM6.” However, in reality, the household head is the most important person in surveying each household every 7 days for potential water sources that favor mosquito breeding. This indicates the potential gap in communication among the health system, village health volunteers, and the community. These findings also indicate poor translation of knowledge into dengue fever prevention practice among household representatives. Household representatives who participated in dengue-related training within the past 12 months demonstrated a significantly better level of understanding of dengue fever solutions than those who did not attend training, with trained participants being twice as likely to have a high understanding. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted among household heads, village health volunteers, and high school students in South Thailand, which reported a significant improvement in dengue-related understanding following educational intervention [ 13 , 23 ]. The qualitative findings further support this association, as participants emphasized the important role of training provided by health centers and village health volunteers in enhancing their knowledge of dengue prevention, water container management, and vector control. Participants underscored that, “I learned how to protect myself and my family correctly and understood what type of mosquito causes dengue fever, FM7”, while other noted, “There are frequent dengue training sessions, which are good because we get to see and learn how others manage their homes and whether there are mosquito breeding sites, FM 13.” These findings underscore the positive impact of training and emphasize the importance of such programs in enhancing understanding and skills, which in turn lead to timely measures for dengue fever and proactive preventive actions. The number of water containers indoors and outdoors was found to be a significant determinant of household representatives’ understanding of dengue fever solutions. Participants who had fewer indoor and outdoor water containers in their household were twice as likely to have a high understanding of dengue fever. The studies conducted in Southeast Asia and Colombia also link household water management and container profiles to entomological risk and to the uptake of control measures, suggesting that households with many or large-purpose containers are less able to implement routine cleaning or covering, even when they know the prevention messages [ 24 , 27 ]. However, another earlier study found no direct association between overall dengue knowledge and the number of water containers [ 28 ], highlighting methodological and contextual differences, including season and knowledge domain, the current study focused on understanding what is beyond merely knowledge. This finding is supported by the observation results of qualitative research participants’ living environment, and it can be further explained by the fact that those household representatives with a high understanding can manage or remove indoor and outdoor water containers to avoid acquiring dengue fever. In addition, the qualitative study finding supports this; participants explained their experience of removing water containers to prevent mosquito breeding as, “we check around the house to see where mosquitoes are there and eliminate breeding sources by pouring out standing water and turning containers upside down, people in the village can protect themselves (FM7).” This highlights that understanding the dengue solution enables the community in the prevention and control of dengue fever through control of Aedes mosquito breeding sites. Regarding the association between the participants’ understanding of dengue solutions and the number of water containers positive for Aedes larvae, those participants whose household surroundings had two or more water containers that tested positive for Aedes larvae were 35% less likely to have a high understanding of dengue fever solutions. This finding goes in line with the KAP study conducted in Vietnam, and an entomological study in Jamaica showed larvae-positive container clusters in households with poor container management and weak source management practices associated with lower knowledge or constrained ability to act on knowledge [ 28 , 29 ]. The findings suggest that understanding dengue solutions is an important basis to act by eliminating potential mosquito breeding sites, even though understanding is not the sole predictor of the presence of higher Aedes larvae-positive containers. The present study found that 40.5% of households had a container index (CI) exceeding the standard threshold, indicating that a considerable proportion of households had Aedes breeding conditions above the acceptable limits. Similar findings have been reported in various parts of Thailand and Southeast Asia, where household-level container index values often rise above the operational threshold during the rainy seasons or in areas with inadequate water management practices [ 23 , 30 ]. The container index of the current study is higher than the findings of a study conducted in Dire Dawa, Ethiopia, where the container index was 21% [ 31 ]. The observed pattern in Sichon aligns with previous evidence that container management practices and climatic variability are primary determinants of larval proliferation in endemic regions. Similar studies recommended focusing on integrated source reduction through improved water storage practices timed to precede peak transmission periods because CI greater than one is a potential early warning threshold for dengue transmission [ 30 , 32 , 33 ]. An important meta-inference from the mixed methods findings is that the community members may possess considerable experiential knowledge of dengue prevention without having corresponding technical literacy regarding the larval index surveillance system. The quantitative assessment showed that only 1.2% of household representatives met the predefined threshold for high understanding of larval surveillance, whereas qualitative participants described detailed experience of identifying stagnant water, eliminating breeding sites, and participating in dengue-prevention campaigns. These findings are not necessarily contradictory because the two components captured different dimensions of knowledge. Moreover, the qualitative sample was purposively selected to provide rich information regarding their experience with dengue prevention activities. Thus, the integrated findings suggest that community experience with households’ vector-control practices may be substantially greater than understanding of formal surveillance concepts. This distinction is important because effective community-based dengue control requires both practical capacities to eliminate breeding sites and sufficient understanding of surveillance information to support meaningful participation in monitoring activities. 4.1 Strengths and limitations of the study This study involved a mixed-methods approach, which is believed to generate rich information on the understanding of dengue fever solutions and the understanding of the Aedes larval index surveillance system. A mixed-methods approach also helped compare the quantitative and qualitative findings. In addition, the study employed environmental observations for the potential risk of mosquito breeding. This study also computed risk indicators of dengue fever transmission. The cross-sectional nature of the study affects causal effect determination, and asking for experience of 12months might introduce minimal recall bias. In addition, the very low prevalence of high understanding of the Aedes larval index surveillance system was observed, with only 10 of 824 participants (1.2%). This resulted in too few outcome events to support reliable logistic regression analysis. Consequently, factors associated with high UDLSS couldn’t be meaningfully examined. The UDLSS findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive rather than as evidence regarding determinants of larval surveillance understanding. The findings should also be interpreted in light of the higher proportion of women who participated in both the qualitative and quantitative components of the study. 5 Conclusions and recommendations The level of understanding of household representatives of dengue solutions was low, and the Aedes larvae surveillance system was very low. The Container Index (CI), the Breteau Index (BI), and the House Index (HI) were above the threshold in all subdistricts in Sichon District, so they are at risk of dengue fever outbreaks. The number of water containers in and around the home, the number of Aedes larvae-positive containers, and training on dengue solutions and prevention were independent determinants of understanding of dengue solutions in the current study. Despite the participants in the qualitative study explaining their good understanding and practice, there was a critical gap in the understanding of dengue fever solutions and the Aedes larval surveillance system among household representatives. Therefore, enhancing the network of village health volunteers and household representatives is essential to enable household representatives to actively participate in dengue fever prevention and control. Household representatives should play a key role in managing dengue fever and the environment with village health volunteers and district health offices. In addition to being aware of what to do for people suspected of having dengue fever, trained village health volunteers should supervise larval breeding sites while assisting the household representatives in water storage management, both indoors and outdoors, which could serve as Aedes mosquitoes breeding sites. Strengthening health education and awareness programs is essential to improve household representatives’ knowledge, motivation, and commitment to implementing the dengue prevention and control measures in their homes and communities. Public health authorities should also consider developing pictorial educational materials that enable community members to distinguish Aedes mosquitoes from other mosquito species and identify common breeding sites. The socioeconomic interpretation of water storage among residents should be considered when designing water container management strategies. More importantly, developing the workforce for vector-borne disease prevention and control would be an important strategy to address the community, in addition to village health volunteers.
