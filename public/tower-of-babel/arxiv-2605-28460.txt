Locally recoverable codes from elliptic surfaces with availability and hierarchical locality
Elena Berardini, Andrea Fornetto

Abstract
In this paper, we propose several constructions of Locally Recoverable Codes from elliptic surfaces. In particular, we are able to obtain codes with availability t > 2 t>2 , codes with hierarchical locality and, finally, codes which combine availability and hierarchical locality. Our constructions rely on the properties of the torsion groups of elliptic curves and on the fibered structure of elliptic surfaces. In particular, the geometry of the surface is used to introduce a multi-dimensional setting, allowing for more recovery sets, eventually nested one within another.

Introduction

In the last decade, the development of distributed cloud storage systems has motivated the search for methods to improve the resilience of the storage infrastructures against data loss. To achieve this goal, Locally Recoverable Codes (LRCs) were introduced
[
24
]
: a linear code
\mathcal{C}
is said to have
locality

r
if, for every symbol in a codeword, there exists a recovery set of
r
coordinates such that the symbol can be recovered from the symbols at those coordinates whenever it is erased.
In the context of cloud storage, this property allows the information on an unavailable server to be retrieved from a small set of other servers.

It is possible to equip LR codes with two main additional properties:
availability
and
hierarchical locality
. An LRC
\mathcal{C}
has availability
t\geq 2
if every symbol has
t
disjoint recovery sets. On the other hand,
\mathcal{C}
is a hierarchical LRC (HLRC) if every symbol has at least two nested recovery sets and the hierarchical structure consists of the full code, the middle codes and the lower codes. All these features can be achieved, for instance, with well-chosen Algebraic Geometry (AG) codes, exploiting the arithmetic properties and rich geometry of algebraic varieties.
In the literature, this solution has been primarily addressed by working on algebraic curves (see,
e.g.,
[
5
,
12
]
). Subsequently, there have been some developments in the construction of LRCs from algebraic surfaces, with a particular focus on fibered surfaces
[
4
,
18
,
6
,
19
,
7
]
.

The main goal of this paper is to propose a construction of LRCs codes from surfaces which combines availability and hierarchical locality. Elliptic curves and elliptic surfaces play the main role in our setting: the group of points on the former and the fibered structure on the latter furnish us with two relevant tools for obtaining the features we want.

Contributions.

The main contributions of this paper on LR codes from elliptic surfaces are summarized below.

•

High availability on elliptic surfaces via torsion subgroups.
The
m
-torsion subgroups of the elliptic fibers allow us to achieve availability
t=m>2
(see
3.1
), by interpreting the torsion group as a direct product of cyclic subgroups of order
m
, generalizing the work made in
[
18
, Section VI]
. Indeed, we obtain a Cartesian grid on the evaluation set of each elliptic fiber which, applying Abel’s Theorem, guarantees the existence of several disjoint recovery sets for each point, avoiding any restriction on the dimension of the code.

•

Hierarchical locality on elliptic surfaces through formal lifting via the generic fiber.
We obtain hierarchical locality on elliptic surfaces by exploiting the same property from codes on elliptic curves (see
4.1
). The transition of the constructions from elliptic curves to elliptic surfaces is performed using the formal definition of the generic fiber and the specialization maps.

•

Combined hierarchical locality and availability on elliptic surfaces.
The simultaneous realization of availability and hierarchical locality on elliptic surfaces (see
5.1
) is made possible by combining the two approaches introduced above. While the combination of these two properties has been achieved in the literature before by evaluating functions over fibered products of curves (
[
3
]
), their coexistence on higher dimensional varieties, such as surfaces, was unexplored until now, to our knowledge.

In all our code constructions, we carefully design the spaces of rational functions through towers of quotients and Riemann–Roch spaces, obtaining good control on the function evaluations at the points of the evaluation set. We avoid restrictive constraints on the choice of the evaluation set, while increasing the minimum distance of the resulting codes.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
, we recall background notions used throughout the work. We start with fundamental definitions from coding theory, focusing on LRCs, availability, and hierarchical locality, together with the known estimates for the parameters. The second part of the section contains essential results from algebraic geometry, concerning quotients of curves, elliptic curves and elliptic surfaces, leading to a brief overview of AG codes. In Section
3
, we present our construction of LRCs with availability from elliptic surfaces, generalizing the work done in
[
18
]
. In Section
4
, we start building a hierarchical structure on elliptic curves using sequences of quotient isogenies. Then, we discuss the lifting of these codes to elliptic surfaces via the generic fiber and the specialization maps. In Section
5
, we present our final construction, obtaining LR codes with hierarchical locality and availability
t=2
at each level of the hierarchy, mainly revising the construction obtained in the previous section, adding more recovery sets. For each of the aforementioned constructions, we prove bounds for the resulting parameters. Finally, Section
6
contains some concluding remarks, together with the presentation of potential further developments of our work.

2.1
Coding theory

A linear
code
is a linear subspace
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
for some

. Every string contained in
\mathcal{C}
is called

, whose components are named

are usually respectively called

\mathcal{C}
.
Given two vectors

d(v,w)=\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ |\ v_{i}\neq w_{i}\}.

The

is its distance from the zero vector.
Given a linear code
\mathcal{C}
, we say that

is its

Not all the combinations among the three parameters
[n,k,d]
can be achieved, as claimed by the famous Singleton’s bound, stating that we have the inequality
d+k\leq n+1.

Now, we can introduce our main objects of study: Locally Recoverable Codes (LRCs). In what follows, given a subset
A\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{A}
the image of the code
\mathcal{C}
with respect to the linear projection
\pi_{A}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mid A\mid}
. In coding theory,
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is called the
punctured
of
C
at the complementary set of
A
,
\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus A

Definition 2.1
.

A code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
and dimension
k
is said to be
[r,\rho]
-locally recoverable (LR), with

, if every coordinate

is contained in a subset

of size at most

\mathcal{C}_{J_{i}}
is a code of minimum distance

.

In the context of the previous definition, the values of any
\rho-1

are determined by the remaining

symbols, allowing local recovery.

LRCs can be equipped with some additional properties, which are extremely useful for the recovery procedure: availability and hierarchical locality.

Definition 2.2
.

A code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
and dimension

is said to be an LRC with

t\geq 2
(

) if, for every coordinate

holds true for at least
t
multiple subsets
\{J_{i,m}\}_{m=1,\ldots,t}
such that
J_{i,m}\cap J_{i,m^{\prime}}=\{i\}
for every pair
(m,m^{\prime})
.

Definition 2.3
.

A linear code
\mathcal{C}
of length

is said to have

with parameters
([n_{1},k_{1},d_{1}]
,
[n_{2},k_{2},d_{2}])
if for every

there exists a subset
i\in J_{i}\subset[n]
such that the punctured code
\mathcal{C}_{i,1}=\mathcal{C}_{J_{i}}
has parameters
[n_{1},k_{1},d_{1}]
and it is
[k_{2},d_{2}]
-locally recoverable, with local recovery set
\mathcal{C}_{i,2}
of length

. The codes
\mathcal{C}_{i,1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i,2}
are usually called respectively middle codes and lower codes for the

-th position.

We conclude this brief subsection by presenting some known results that generalize the Singleton’s bound for LRCs.

Theorem 2.1

(
Singleton’s bound for LRCs)
.

The minimum distance of an
[r,\rho]
-locally recoverable code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
and dimension
k
satisfies the inequality:

d\leq n-k+1-\bigg(\left\lceil\frac{k}{r}-1\right\rceil\bigg)(\rho-1).

If the LRC has availability
t\geq 2
, two more general versions of the inequality hold true:

d\leq n-k+2-\left\lceil\frac{t(k-1)+1}{t(r-1)+1}\right\rceil;

d\leq n-\sum_{i=0}^{t}\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{r^{i}}\right\rfloor.

If the
[n,k,d]
-code
\mathcal{C}
is an HLRC with parameters
([n_{1},k_{1},d_{1}],[n_{2},k_{2},d_{2}])
, then

d\leq n-k+1-\bigg(\left\lceil\frac{k}{k_{1}}\right\rceil-1\bigg)(d_{1}-d_{2})-\bigg(\left\lceil\frac{k}{k_{2}}\right\rceil-1\bigg)(d_{2}-1).

We refer the reader to
[
14
, Appendix A]
,
[
17
, Appendix A]
,
[
23
, §IV.A]
and
[
20
, §II.B]
for the proofs of the bounds above.

2.2.1
Curves

By a

In algebraic geometry, there exists a fundamental correspondence between geometric objects and algebraic objects. This relation allows us to study curves by working with their function fields. In fact, when dealing with quotients of curves, one can approach this topic by shifting from geometry to field extensions.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a curve
X

\mathbb{F}

\mathrm{Div}(X)
generated by the set of closed points

where the set
\{p\in X^{(1)}\ |\ n_{p}\neq 0\}

For a fixed field extension

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}

Usually, the set

. Moreover, one can associate a divisor to every rational function defined over a curve, as follows.

Definition 2.5
.

Given an
\mathbb{F}
-curve

and a rational function

\mathrm{div}(f)=\sum_{p\in X^{(1)}}v_{p}(f)p,

One can find a complete dissertation on discrete valuations over
\mathbb{F}(X)

[

Notation 2.1
.

Given an
\mathbb{F}

•

The support of

•

If

If there exists

•

A principal divisor can be expressed as

If

Maintaining the previous setup, the degree of a divisor

\deg(D)=\sum_{p\in\mathrm{Supp}(D)}n_{p}\cdot[\mathbb{F}(p):\mathbb{F}],

Two divisors

Given a divisor

\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)(U)=\{f\in\mathbb{F}(U)=\mathbb{F}(X)\ |\ v_{p}(f)+n_{p}\geq 0\ \forall p\in U^{(1)}\}.

(

Given a projective curve

Proposition 2.1
.

Given an
\mathbb{F}
-curve
X
and a finite group
G<\mathrm{Aut}(X)
, the field of rational functions
\mathbb{F}(X/G)
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}=\{f\in\mathbb{F}(X)\ |\ g\cdot f=f\ \forall\ g\in G\}.

Here, the action of an element
g\in G
on a rational function
f\in\mathbb{F}(X)
is defined as follows: for every point
p\in X
,
(g\cdot f)(p)=(f\circ g)(p)
.

Moreover, if
m=|G|
is coprime to
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
, then the field extension
\mathbb{F}(X)/\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
is separable and has two additional useful properties:

•

[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}]=m
,

•

there exists
f\in\mathbb{F}(X)
such that

\mathbb{F}(X)=\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ f\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)),

where
D
is a divisor corresponding to a
G
-orbit in

.

The isomorphism between
\mathbb{F}(X/G)
and
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
can be proved as follows. Given any affine open subset
U=\mathrm{Spec}(A)\subseteq X
invariant under the action of
G
, its quotient (
[
10
, §12.7]
) is defined as the open affine set
\mathrm{Spec}(A^{G})\subseteq X/G
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{F}(X)=\mathrm{Frac}(A)
and
\mathbb{F}(X/G)=\mathrm{Frac}(A^{G})
. The isomorphism is proved if we show that
\mathrm{Frac}(A^{G})=\mathrm{Frac}(A)^{G}
. In fact, the inclusion
\mathrm{Frac}(A^{G})\subseteq\mathrm{Frac}(A)^{G}
is trivial. Conversely, given a
G
-invariant element
f\in\mathrm{Frac}(A)
, it can be written as
f=\frac{a}{b}
, with
a,b\neq 0
in
A
. It can also be expressed as

f=\frac{a\prod_{g\in G\setminus\{1\}}g(b)}{b\prod_{g\in G\setminus\{1\}}g(b)}=\frac{a\prod_{g\in G\setminus\{1\}}g(b)}{\prod_{g\in G}g(b)}.

Thus,
f
and the denominator are both
G
-invariant: this implies that the numerator must be in
A^{G}
and
f
is contained in
\mathrm{Frac}(A^{G})
.

The field extension
\mathbb{F}(X)/\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
is a finite Galois extension, and we have that
[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}]=|G|=m
: we refer the reader to
[
15
, Ch. VI, Thm. 1.8]
for these claims. For the second part of the statement, the existence of
f
such that
\mathbb{F}(X)=\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)
is an immediate consequence of the primitive element theorem. Moreover, it is possible to give a characterization for the rational function
f
. Take
f\in\mathbb{F}(X)
such that for every non-trivial
g\in G
,
g\cdot f\neq f
,
i.e.,
f
has trivial stabilizer in
G
. We know that the field extension
\mathbb{F}(X)/\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
is Galois with Galois group
G
, since it is normal and separable. If we show that

P_{f}(T)=\prod_{g\in G}(T-g\cdot f)\in\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}[T]

is the minimal polynomial of
f
over
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
, the proof is concluded: since the degree of
P_{f}(T)
is exactly
m
, we would have that
[\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}]=m
, thus

m=[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}]=[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)]\cdot[\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}]=[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)]\cdot m,

which implies that
[\mathbb{F}(X):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)]=1
, that is equivalent to saying that
\mathbb{F}(X)=\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f)
. Every coefficient of the polynomial
P_{f}(T)
is contained in
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
because, for every
h\in G
, we have

h\cdot P_{f}(T)=\prod_{g\in G}\big(T-h\cdot(g\cdot f)\big)=\prod_{g\in G}\big(T-(hg)\cdot f\big)=P_{f}(T).

As a consequence, we have that the minimal polynomial of
f
divides
P_{f}
. We just need to prove that
P_{f}
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
among the polynomials that have
f
as a root. By hypothesis
\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(f)
is trivial and, from classical results in Galois theory, we have that

\frac{|G|}{|\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(f)|}=[\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}(f):\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}].

Thus, the minimal polynomial of
f
over
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
must have degree
m
, which is exactly the degree of
P_{f}
: this concludes the proof.

In addition, one can prescribe the poles of this primitive element, such that they are contained in the support of a divisor
D
on
X
obtained from a
G
-orbit. Indeed, if
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
is a
G
-orbit, the Riemann–Roch space associated to the divisor
D=\sum p_{i}
has dimension

h^{0}(X,D)\geq m-g(X)+1\geq 1.

For every non-trivial
g\in G
, the vector subspace

V_{g}=\{f\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))\ |\ g\cdot f=f\}

is proper. If the field
\mathbb{F}
is infinite, there exists
\overline{f}\in H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))\setminus\cup_{g\neq Id}V_{g}
since a finite union of proper subspaces cannot cover the entire vector space. In fact, this is a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}(X)
over
\mathbb{F}(X)^{G}
, since
\overline{f}
has trivial stabilizer in

and its poles are contained in the support of the divisor

. If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the finite union
\cup_{g\neq Id}V_{g}
may cover

. In this case, one can notice that:

\#H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))=q^{h^{0}(X,D)}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \#V_{g}\leq q^{h^{0}(X,D)-1}.

Thus,
\#\cup_{g\neq Id}V_{g}\leq(m-1)\cdot q^{h^{0}(X,D)-1}
and, if

, then

H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(D))\setminus\cup_{g\neq Id}V_{g}\neq\emptyset

and we can conclude as above.

Elliptic curves form the most studied classes of curves in algebraic geometry, number theory and complex analysis, with many real-world applications. In particular, these curves and their properties will be crucial in the next sections of this paper.

Definition 2.6

Construction 2.1
.

Given an elliptic curve
(E,\infty)
defined over a field
\mathbb{F}
, define the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\Phi:}&{E(\mathbb{F})}&\longrightarrow&{{\mathrm{Pic}}^{0}(E)}\\
&{p}&\longmapsto&{\mathcal{O}_{E}(p-\infty)}\end{array},

where
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(E)
denotes the subgroup of
\mathrm{Pic}(E)
which consists of the classes of invertible sheaves corresponding to divisors of degree
0
. This map gives a bijective correspondence between the set of points
E(\mathbb{F})
and the elements of the group
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(E)
, which furnishes us with a group structure on
E(\mathbb{F})
, with
\infty
as identity and operation denoted by
\oplus
.

Notation 2.2
.

Given a divisor
D=\sum n_{p}\cdot p
on an elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathrm{Supp}(D)\subseteq E(\mathbb{F})
, we use the following notation:

\sum^{\oplus}D=\sum^{\oplus}p^{\oplus n_{p}}\in E(\mathbb{F}).

In fact,
\sum^{\oplus}D
is the point on
E
obtained as the sum, with multiplicity, of the points in the support of the divisor
D
, with respect to the operation of the group law on
E(\mathbb{F})
. Moreover, if
G<E(\mathbb{F})
is a subgroup of points, we denote by
D_{G}\in\mathrm{Div}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)
the divisor defined by the formal sum of the points contained in the subgroup
G
.

We now recall the definition of isogenies and torsion points.

Definition 2.7
.

Given two elliptic curves
(E_{1},\infty_{1})
and
(E_{2},\infty_{2})
over
\mathbb{F}
, an isogeny is a non-constant morphism of
\mathbb{F}
-schemes
f:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
such that
f(\infty_{1})=\infty_{2}
.

We have two relevant examples of isogenies.

Example 2.1
.

Given an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}=\overline{\mathbb{F}}
and an integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, the multiplication-by-
m
homomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{[m]:}&{E}&\longrightarrow&{E}\\
&{p}&\longmapsto&{p^{\oplus m}}\end{array}

is an isogeny.

Example 2.2
.

Another source of isogenies between elliptic curves is given by the quotients with respect to a finite subgroup
G<E(\mathbb{F})
of order which is not divisible by
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
. Indeed,
G
can be seen as a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(E)
, since every point
p\in G
defines the translation

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\tau_{p}:}&{E}&\longrightarrow&{E}\\
&{q}&\longmapsto&{q\oplus p}\end{array}

The quotient
(E/G,\pi)
is still an elliptic curve and every point
q\in E/G(\mathbb{F})
has exactly
|G|
points in the pre-image. One can conclude that
\pi:E\longrightarrow E/G
is an isogeny between elliptic curves because
\pi(\infty_{E})=\infty_{E/G}
and
\pi
is clearly non-constant.

The first example leads us to the formal definition of torsion points. The second example, associated with quotients of elliptic curves, will be crucial for the construction in
Section

4
and
Section

5
.

Definition 2.8
.

Given an elliptic curve
(E,\infty)
defined over a field
\mathbb{F}
and an integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,

\mathrm{ker}[m]=\{p\in E(\mathbb{F})\ |\ [m]p=\infty\}

is called
m
-torsion subgroup of
E(\mathbb{F})
and usually denoted by
E(\mathbb{F})[m]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(E,\infty)
be an elliptic curve defined over a field
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=p\geq 0
and consider an integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Given a fixed algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, we have

\mathbf{(a)}

if
\mathrm{gcd}(p,m)=1
,
E(\overline{\mathbb{F}})[m]\cong\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/m
;

\mathbf{(b)}

if
p>0
, we have two possibilities:

–

E(\overline{\mathbb{F}})[p^{r}]\cong\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}
for every
r\geq 1
;

–

E(\overline{\mathbb{F}})[p^{r}]=\{\infty\}
for every
r\geq 1
;

\mathbf{(c)}

if
p>0
,
q=p^{n}
, the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points define a group of the form

E(\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/a\times\mathbb{Z}/b,

with
a|b
and
\mathrm{gcd}(a,p)=1
.

The last result of this subsection is Abel’s Theorem, which will be crucial in our first construction.

Theorem 2.3

(
Abel)
.

Let
E/\mathbb{F}
be an elliptic curve, whose group of points is defined as
(E(\mathbb{F}),\infty)
. Let
D=\sum n_{p}\cdot p
be a divisor in
\mathrm{Div}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)
. Then

D\sim\bigg(\sum^{\oplus}D\bigg)+(\deg(D)-1)\cdot\infty.

In particular,
D
is a principal divisor if and only if

\sum^{\oplus}D=\infty\in E(\mathbb{F})\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \deg(D)=0.

We refer the reader to
[
21
, Ch. III, Prop. 3.4 and Cor. 3.5]
for a proof of the second part of the statement. The first claim on a divisor
D\in\mathrm{Div}_{\mathbb{F}}(E)
can be proved by considering the degree zero divisor
D^{\prime}=D-\deg(D)\cdot\infty
.
∎

2.2.2
Surfaces

By a
surface
we mean a smooth and geometrically irreducible
2
-dimensional variety over
\mathbb{F}
.
In this paper, we will work on a specific class of surfaces, that is, elliptic surfaces.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
B
be a projective curve. An elliptic surface
\mathcal{E}
over
B
consists of the following data:

•

a projective
\mathbb{F}
-surface
\mathcal{E}
;

•

a morphism
\pi:\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow B
such that for all but finitely many
b\in B(\overline{\mathbb{F}})
, the fiber
E_{b}=\pi^{-1}(b)=\mathcal{E}\times_{B}\mathrm{Spec}\ \mathbb{F}(b)
is a smooth projective curve of genus

;

•

a section
\sigma_{0}:B\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}
, i.e., a morphism
\sigma_{0}
such that
\pi\circ\sigma_{0}=Id_{B}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The section
\sigma_{0}
of the definition above fixes an
\mathbb{F}
-rational point on every fiber of the morphism
\pi
. Thus, given
b\in B
such that
E_{b}
is a smooth projective curve of genus
1
, it is in fact an elliptic curve according to Definition
2.6
.

There exists a correspondence between elliptic
\mathbb{F}
-surfaces
\mathcal{E}
over a curve
B
and elliptic curves

over the function field

, as stated in the following proposition
[

, Cha. III, Proposition 3.8]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Given a curve
B
over
\mathbb{F}
, let

be an elliptic curve defined over the field
\mathbb{F}(B)
, with long Weierstrass equation

. We have a corresponding elliptic surface

Y^{2}Z+a_{1}XYZ+a_{3}YZ^{2}=X^{3}+a_{2}X^{2}Z+a_{4}XZ^{2}+a_{6}Z^{3},

where the fibration is given by the projection to the second factor of

Given an elliptic surface
\pi:\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow B

\mathbb{F}
, there exist

such that

is
\mathbb{F}
-birationally equivalent to

Given an elliptic surface
\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow B
associated to an elliptic curve

-algebras:

\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{E})\cong\mathbb{F}(B)(E).

Remark 2.2
.

Given an elliptic
\mathbb{F}
-surface

defined over the field
\mathbb{F}(B)
is the fiber over the generic point

\mathcal{E}
over

and the set of

-rational points of

In

4
and
Section

5
, we will use the generic fiber to pass from a construction on an elliptic curve to one on an elliptic surface. For this purpose, a crucial algebro-geometric instrument is the specialization map, defined from the generic fiber to another elliptic fiber.

is a global section

, while
\gamma

and
\pi_{2}

.

In the setting described above, the specialization map at

is the function

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathrm{sp}_{\gamma}:}&{E(\mathbb{F}(B))}&\longrightarrow&{E_{\gamma}(\mathbb{F})}\\

.

Given the above setup, the set-theoretic specialization map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathrm{sp}_{\gamma}:}&{E(\mathbb{F}(B))}&\longrightarrow&{E_{\gamma}(\mathbb{F})}\\

This theorem can be proved relying on the standard theory of reduction for elliptic curves over local fields: indeed, a non–singular fiber

in our setting corresponds to an elliptic curve

with good reduction at a place

corresponds to a reduction map. Thus, the theorem is a consequence of Propositions

Remark 2.3
.

Given the above setup, let
K
be the field
\mathbb{F}(B)
and consider a function
f\in K(E)=\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{E})
, where
E/K
is the generic fiber of
\mathcal{E}
and take
p\in E(K)
and
\gamma\in B(\mathbb{F})
such that
f\in\mathcal{O}_{E,p}\subset K(E)
and
f(p)\in\mathcal{O}_{B,\gamma}\subset\mathbb{F}(B)
. We want to prove that:

f(p)(\gamma)=f_{\gamma}(p_{\gamma}),

where
p_{\gamma}=\text{sp}_{\gamma}(p)
and
f_{\gamma}=f_{|E_{\gamma}}=f\circ j_{\gamma}
, with
j_{\gamma}:E_{\gamma}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}
inclusion. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{p}
the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{E,p}
and by
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{B,\gamma}
. By definition,

f(p)(\gamma)=\big[\overline{f_{p}}\big]\in\mathcal{O}_{B,\gamma}/\mathcal{M}_{\gamma},

where
\overline{f_{p}}
is the class of

in
\mathcal{O}_{B,\gamma}\subset\mathcal{O}_{E,p}/\mathcal{M}_{p}=K
. In fact, we are taking the class of

modulo
\mathcal{M}_{p}
and modulo
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
.

On the other hand, taking the restricted function
f_{\gamma}
is equivalent to considering the quotient of
f
with respect to the ideal of the closed fiber
E_{\gamma}
, which is clearly generated by the local parameter
t_{\gamma}
at
\gamma
. After that, evaluating

at

corresponds to taking its class modulo
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
.

Thus, geometrically both sequences of quotients evaluate

at the intersection point between the section
\sigma_{p}
and the closed fiber

. Algebraically, both sequences correspond to the quotient with respect to the maximal ideal of the aforementioned intersection point.

2.3
Algebraic Geometry codes

Algebraic Geometry codes (AG codes) were introduced by Goppa in
[
9
]
over smooth absolutely irreducible curves
X
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. His idea was to evaluate the rational functions contained in a certain vector subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
over a given subset of the rational points
X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Goppa’s construction was successively extended to projective varieties of arbitrary dimension, as presented below.

Definition 2.11
.

Consider an absolutely irreducible smooth projective variety
X
defined over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a set
T=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}\subseteq X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
be a vector space of finite dimension, consisting of rational functions such that the supports of their divisors of poles avoid the collection of points
T
.
The AG code
\mathcal{C}(X,V,T)
is defined as the image of the following
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear evaluation map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathrm{ev}_{T}:}&{V}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\\
&{f}&\longmapsto&{(f(p_{1}),\ldots,f(p_{n}))}\end{array}.

One can choose the vector space
V
as a specific Riemann-Roch space
H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(G))
associated to a divisor
G\in\mathrm{Div}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(X)
such that its support does not contain any of the points of
T
. In this case, if there is no ambiguity regarding the divisor
G
, the corresponding AG code is denoted by
\mathcal{C}(X,G,T)
. In fact, the hypothesis on the divisor
G
avoiding the set of rational points
T
is not necessary: indeed, we have that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(G)
is an invertible sheaf, so for every point
p\in X
the stalk of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(G)
at
p
is an
\mathcal{O}_{X,p}
-module generated by
s_{p}
. Then any global section
f
can be locally written as
f_{p}s_{p}
for some
f_{p}\in\mathcal{O}_{X,p}
, which one can evaluate at the point
p
. Thus, we can define any AG code over a variety of arbitrary dimensions on the whole set of rational points.

To end this section, we give a survey of the known results about the parameters of AG codes from curves, which we shall use in the paper.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
X
be a curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
G\in\mathrm{Div}(X)
,
T=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}\subseteq X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
D=\sum_{p\in T}p
the associated divisor. Then, the parameters
[n,k,d]
of the code
\mathcal{C}(X,G,T)
satisfy:

•

n=\#T
,

•

k=h^{0}(G)-h^{0}(G-D)
. If
n=\deg(D)>\deg(G)
, then
k=h^{0}(G)
,

•

d\geq n-\deg(G)
.

One can consult
[
25
, §4.1.1]
for a proof of the theorem above. There exist various analog estimates for the parameters of AG codes on surfaces—see, for example,
[
2
,
8
]
. These often make use of intersection theory on surfaces and go beyond our interests, since the constructions contained in the following sections strongly rely on curves.

3
Availability from elliptic surfaces

In this section, we are going to define our locally recoverable code on the elliptic surface
\pi:\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow B
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We take inspiration from the construction in
[
18
]
, but slightly modify the key lemma in it to obtain several disjoint recovery sets for every point in the evaluation set, thus LRC with availability.

The crucial point of this construction lies in the choice of the evaluation set for the code. Consider a subset
S\subset B(\mathbb{F}_{q})
such that for every element
\gamma\in S
, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\gamma)
is an elliptic curve. Take now a prime number
m>3
, different from the characteristic
p
of the field. The subgroup of
m
-torsion of an elliptic curve
E_{\gamma}
defined over the algebraically closed field
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/m
.

In this section, we will work over the finite extension
\mathbb{F}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
over which the
m
-torsion subgroup of every elliptic curve
E_{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in S
, is defined. We will denote by
G_{\gamma}
the
m
-torsion subgroup of
E_{\gamma}
, and by
T_{\gamma}
the subset
G_{\gamma}\setminus\{\infty_{\gamma}\}
, consisting of exactly
m^{2}-1
distinct points.

Then, the evaluation set for our construction is defined as

T=\bigcup_{\gamma\in S}T_{\gamma}=\bigcup_{\gamma\in S}\big(G_{\gamma}\setminus\{\infty_{\gamma}\}\big).

Given
\gamma\in S
, the group
G_{\gamma}
can be seen as the linear space
\mathbb{F}_{m}^{2}
over
\mathbb{Z}/m=\mathbb{F}_{m}
of dimension
2
. Thus, if we take a point
p_{0}\in G_{\gamma}
, exactly
m+1
lines are passing through
p_{0}
, corresponding to the possible slopes, which, in fact, are the elements of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{m}}(\mathbb{F}_{m})
.
One can also prove that any two of these lines intersect only at the point
p_{0}
. Let
L_{\gamma,p_{0},i}
(or simply
L_{\gamma,i}
if no confusion arises on the point
p_{0}
) be one of the lines in
(\mathbb{F}_{m})^{2}
passing through
p_{0}
, where
i=1,\ldots,m+1
. We index this collection of lines such that
L_{\gamma,p_{0},m+1}
is the line passing through
p_{0}
and
\infty_{\gamma}
in
(\mathbb{F}_{m})^{2}
. Finally, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we let
T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}
denote the set of
m
points contained in the support of the line
L_{\gamma,p_{0},i}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given
\gamma\in S
,
m
an odd prime different from
p=\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
and a point
p_{0}\in T_{\gamma}
, the sets
T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}\subset T_{\gamma}
satisfy the following condition, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
:

\sum_{p\in T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}}^{\oplus}p=\infty_{\gamma}.

Moreover, none of the sets
T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}
, with
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, contains the point
\infty_{\gamma}
.

The second claim follows immediately from the fact that there exists only one line passing through
p_{0}
and
\infty_{\gamma}
, and we fixed this line to be
L_{\gamma,p_{0},m+1}
. For the main part of the proof, we are going to work with the group
\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/m
isomorphic to
G_{\gamma}
. We will prove that the sum of the points contained in the support of a line in
\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/m=(\mathbb{F}_{m})^{2}
gives the point
(0,0)
, which corresponds to the point
\infty_{\gamma}\in G_{\gamma}
.

Every line
L
in
(\mathbb{F}_{m})^{2}
can be parametrized considering its direction
v=(a,b)
and a point
(x_{0},y_{0})
contained in its support. In particular, any point
p\in\mathrm{Supp}(L)
is then of the form

p=p_{t}=(x_{0},y_{0})+(ta,tb),\ \ \ \text{for some }\ t\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}.

Thus, the sum of the points in
\mathrm{Supp}(L)
corresponds to the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{t}^{\oplus}p_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}(x_{0}+ta),\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}(y_{0}+tb)\bigg)=\bigg(mx_{0}+a\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}t,my_{0}+b\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}t\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(mx_{0}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}a,my_{0}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}b\bigg).

By hypothesis,
m
is an odd prime number, so
\frac{m-1}{2}
is an integer and both coordinates of the point defined above are divisible by
m
, thus, it is the point
(0,0)
, which corresponds to the point
\infty_{\gamma}\in G_{\gamma}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

This lemma furnishes us with the necessary assumptions for the complete construction of the LRC with availability
t=m
that will be explained in the following proposition. Recall that given a prime number
m>3
different from the characteristic of the field
\mathbb{F}
,
\mathcal{E}
is an elliptic
\mathbb{F}
-surface, with a fibration
\pi:\mathcal{E}\longrightarrow B
such that
E_{\gamma}[m]\subseteq E_{\gamma}(\mathbb{F})
for every
\gamma\in S\subset B(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, defined as in the first paragraph of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider a vector space
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{E})
such that for every
\gamma\in S
the image of the restriction

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathrm{res}_{E_{\gamma}}:}&{V}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{F}(E_{\gamma})}\\
&{f}&\longmapsto&{f_{|E_{\gamma}}}\end{array}

is exactly
H^{0}(E_{\gamma},\mathcal{O}_{E_{\gamma}}((m-1)\infty_{\gamma}))
. The evaluation code
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E},V,T)
obtained evaluating the rational functions of
V
on the points of

T=\bigcup_{\gamma\in S}T_{\gamma}=\bigcup_{\gamma\in S}\bigg(G_{\gamma}\setminus\{\infty_{\gamma}\}\bigg)

is locally recoverable, with locality
r=m-1
and availability
t=m
. In particular, every point
p_{0}\in T_{\gamma}
has
m
disjoint recovery sets
T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}
, with
i=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. This means that the evaluation of a rational function at a point
p_{0}\in T_{\gamma}
can be recovered using the points in the support of one of the lines passing through
p_{0}
, except for the line through
(0,0)
in
(\mathbb{F}_{m})^{2}
.

The proof of this proposition is largely inspired by that of
[
18
, Lem. VI.1]
. By definition, for every point
p_{0}\in T
there exists a unique
\gamma\in S
such that
p_{0}\in T_{\gamma}
. Given a rational function
f\in V
and a point
p_{0}\in T_{\gamma}
such that
f(p_{0})
is unknown, we want to prove that this value can be recovered using the evaluations of
f
at the
r=m-1
points contained in
T_{\gamma,p_{0},i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}
and this for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. This is equivalent to proving that the following
\mathbb{F}
-linear evaluation map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathrm{ev}_{T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}}:}&{H^{0}(E_{\gamma},\mathcal{O}_{E_{\gamma}}(r\infty_{\gamma}))}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{F}^{r}}\\
&{f}&\longmapsto&{(f(p))_{p\in T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}}}\end{array}

is an isomorphism for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Applying the Riemann–Roch theorem, we know that
h^{0}(E_{\gamma},\mathcal{O}_{E_{\gamma}}(r\infty_{\gamma}))=\mathrm{deg}(r\infty_{\gamma})=r
, so we just need to prove that the map above is injective.

Suppose that two distinct rational functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
assume the same values at the points of
T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}
. Then, the function
g=f_{1}-f_{2}
has an associated divisor given by

\mathrm{div}(g)=\sum_{p\in T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}}p-r\infty_{\gamma}.

Applying Abel’s theorem, we obtain

\sum_{p\in T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}}^{\oplus}p=\infty_{\gamma}.

This implies that

\sum_{p\in T_{\gamma,i}}^{\oplus}p=p_{0}\oplus\sum_{p\in T_{\gamma,i}\setminus\{p_{0}\}}^{\oplus}p=p_{0}\oplus\infty_{\gamma}=p_{0},

which contradicts
3.1
. Thus, the evaluation map above is an isomorphism and the code is locally recoverable, with locality
r=m-1
and availability
t=m
.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

Above, we have proved that for every

, the restriction of the evaluation map to the points contained in

is an isomorphism, but we have not mentioned the recovery procedure, which we explain here. If

has long Weierstrass equation

is generated by

\{x^{i}\cdot y^{j}\ |\ 2i+3j\leq r\ \text{and}\ j\in\{0,1\}\}.

Then, we can see it as an interpolation problem and use the classic bivariate interpolation to recover the missing value.

Let

\mathbf{(b)}

The length

, as claimed.

The idea to prove the statement on the minimum distance is the following: we want to count the maximum number of elliptic curves

over which a non-trivial function

can be identically zero; then we count the maximum number of zeros that can occur on a single elliptic curve. If we take a non-trivial function

j\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}b_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j},

with

for every

such that

. The support of this divisor of zeros has cardinality

(it is not necessarily an equality since we are not counting multiplicities in this set). Therefore, a non-trivial

can be identically zero on at most
\delta

For the last part of the proof, we are going to count the maximum number of zeros of a non-trivial function on an elliptic curve using the fact that every rational function

The second equality follows from the definition of the vector space

∎

4
Hierarchical locality from elliptic curves and surfaces

This section aims to construct hierarchical LRCs (HLRCs) from elliptic surfaces. We will use some well-chosen quotients of elliptic curves and exploit results exposed in
[
3
, §IV]
. In the latter paper, the authors worked on arbitrary curves and separable morphisms between them. By restricting our focus to the specific case of elliptic curves, we refine their results into a systematic approach for the selection of the evaluation set and the rational functions to be evaluated at it. One can notice that a hierarchical structure can be achieved on individual elliptic curves, while the transition to elliptic surfaces adds complexity to the hierarchical structure: the fibers over a base curve are naturally elliptic curves, thus our construction acquires an additional layer. The structure of the code will be made explicit in the subsequent paragraphs.

Proposition 4.1

Consider an integer

and

Remark 4.4
.

The existence of a non-empty subset

is a crucial requirement for our construction. Since we have defined it as the complement of

in

, we want to estimate the maximum number of points that we must exclude. The set

consists of three types of points:

1.

is singular. If the elliptic surface

is defined by a Weierstrass equation with coefficients

, these points correspond to the roots of the discriminant

, whose number is bounded by

points

that are poles of the coefficients in

defining

, or

. Since these functions belong to specific Riemann–Roch spaces, the number of their poles is bounded by the degrees of their associated divisors;

or

. These are non-identically zero rational functions in

. Therefore, to guarantee that

-rational points.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented some new constructions of LRCs from elliptic surfaces, which allow us to get availability, hierarchical locality and the combination of the two. Our first construction is a generalization and systematization of
[
18
, §VI]
, leading to the achievement of locality with availability strictly greater than
2
from elliptic surfaces, defined over an arbitrary base curve. The fibered structure is used to build the evaluation set and the vector space, but it does not give the code hierarchical locality, since the restriction of the code to every elliptic curve of the form
E_{\gamma}
, with
\gamma
contained in the base curve, is not effectively an LRC.

The constructions developed in Sections
4
and
5
are intrinsically distinct from the one detailed in Section
3
. Rather than working directly with the geometry of the surface, these constructions originate from the study of elliptic curves and their sequences of quotients, and are lifted to elliptic surfaces via the generic fiber.

Another difference lies in how the recovery capabilities of the codes are established and proven to work. In Section
3
, the evaluation set and the spaces of rational functions are chosen to satisfy the hypotheses of Abel’s Theorem, which guarantees the existence of several disjoint recovery sets. In Sections
4
and
5
, the repair procedure, which relies on polynomial interpolation, is made possible by the algebraic conditions imposed on the evaluations of the rational functions: mainly the requirements outlined in Conditions
(f.

3

)
and
(g.

3

)
. We ensure that the interpolation remains well-posed, while avoiding restrictive limitations on the evaluation set itself.

Although these constructions are made on elliptic surfaces, they are strongly based on the rich spectrum of arithmetic properties of elliptic curves. The geometry of the surface is used to introduce a multi-dimensional setting, with two or more levels for the recovery patterns.

Finally, the estimates of the parameters have been carried out using techniques already explored in the literature on curves and fibered surfaces. Better estimates can be performed, for example, by taking into consideration specific equations for the definition of the algebraic varieties.

6.1
Further developments

We conclude by offering some ideas for further developments, starting from the results and the constructions presented in this paper.

Investigating specific algebraic conditions to impose on the evaluation functions could be a first step forward: by working with the underlying Riemann–Roch spaces, one could force their zeros to fall outside the evaluation set. This approach may lead to increasing the minimum distance of the resulting codes.

The focus of this paper is on elliptic surfaces. Local properties of codes from ruled surfaces and other fibered surfaces have been recently studied in
[
7
,
19
]
. It would be interesting to investigate other fibered surfaces, of general type, for instance, to construct LRCs with additional properties and/or improved parameters.

Finally, the other tool we have extensively used in our work is the group structure of elliptic curves. It is natural to ask whether our work can be generalized to abelian varieties of higher dimension. For instance, codes from abelian surfaces have already been studied in
[
1
]
, but their local properties have not been investigated yet. Abelian varieties could serve as a good source of LRCs with additional properties, considering their rich group structure on the set of points.
