Safety evaluation of dofetilide administration per enteral tube

Introduction

Dofetilide, a class III antiarrhythmic drug, is clinically indicated for conversion to and maintenance of sinus rhythm in patients diagnosed with atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter.1 Its effect on ventricular repolarization prolongs the QT interval, increasing the risk of torsades de pointes, and necessitating inpatient initiation with continuous telemetry and QT monitoring for the first 5 doses. Dofetilide is commercially available as a capsule in 3 strengths: 125, 250, and 500 μg. Occasionally there are circumstances in which a patient indicated for dofetilide therapy cannot take capsules by mouth, such as those who require mechanical ventilation or those with chronic dysphagia, in whom medications are administered via an enteral tube.

Dofetilide does not appear on the Institute for Safe Medication Practices’ “Do Not Crush” list, suggesting no established concerns regarding altered absorption when capsules are opened or otherwise manipulated.2, 3, 4 There are no published studies that have evaluated clinical outcomes of opening dofetilide capsules. There are limited data to guide the choice of vehicle that opened capsule contents are delivered in (eg, water or applesauce), which can alter bioavailability and plasma concentrations of a given drug.4 Our study retrospectively evaluates the safety and efficacy of dofetilide administration via an enteral tube.

Methods

Patients who received at least 1 dose of dofetilide via an enteral tube across 14 hospitals in a large integrated health care system were retrospectively reviewed. The primary safety outcome was the incidence of corrected QT (QTc) prolongation (>500 ms, or >550 ms with ventricular conduction abnormalities or ventricular pacing), ventricular arrhythmia, or arrhythmogenic death. The primary efficacy outcome was recurrent atrial fibrillation.

Patients were evaluated from the first enteral tube dose through 48 hours after resumption of enteral administration. Available electrocardiograms (ECGs) and telemetry strips were reviewed, with manual QT measurement and QTc calculation using Bazett’s formula. Data were abstracted retrospectively without patient identifiers, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board.

Results

In total, 250 doses of dofetilide were administered via an enteral tube (Figure 2). Only 1 incident of QTc prolongation >500 ms was observed in the setting of hypokalemia. 3 patients experienced monomorphic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (ranging from 3 to 18 beats), which did not require treatment. 2 of 3 documented episodes occurred in the setting of hypokalemia, and none of the patients experiencing nonsustained ventricular tachycardia had a prolonged QTc interval (Figures 3 and 4). Discussion

In this study, 250 dofetilide doses were administered via an enteral tube to 22 patients. No recurrent atrial fibrillation or QTc prolongation leading to significant ventricular arrhythmias occurred, suggesting that administration via an enteral tube is safe and effective.

Opening capsules for enteral tube delivery is common in patients unable to take oral medications. Our findings address a gap in the literature and have clinical relevance, as administration via an enteral tube may prevent interruptions in dofetilide therapy and avoid the need for 72-hour reinitiation monitoring, potentially reducing hospital length of stay.

Study limitations include the small sample size and retrospective design, which may limit generalizability. Most patients were continuing outpatient dofetilide rather than initiating therapy, and frequent ECGs were not consistently obtained, so prolonged QTc intervals may have been underdetected. 17 patients (77.2%) had ECGs available; in the remaining 5 (22.8%), QTc assessment relied solely on telemetry.

Because no commercial assay exists to measure serum dofetilide concentrations, pharmacokinetic changes could not be assessed. However, these findings provide insight into the pharmacodynamic effects of administration via an enteral tube.

Conclusion

Dofetilide administration via an enteral tube for the maintenance of sinus rhythm appears to be safe and effective, with no instance of torsades de pointes or recurrence of atrial fibrillation detected. Of the patients who experienced an adverse event (ventricular arrhythmia or prolonged QTc), 3 had a potassium level of <4.0 mmol/L and 1 had a potassium level of 5.5 mmol/L.
