Summation-by-parts operators for general function spaces: Optimal nodes
Nicholas Hale, Charis Harley, Prince Nchupang, Jan Nordström #

Abstract
Gauss–Lobatto quadrature nodes and weights are optimal for closed summation-by-parts (SBP) formulations based on polynomial approximation spaces in the sense that for a prescribed function space they yield an SBP operator of minimal dimension. We show that the same principle extends to general (possibly non-polynomial) function spaces: an associated generalised Gauss–Lobatto quadrature provides the optimal nodes and weights for the SBP formulation. We present an algorithm for computing these quadrature rules, demonstrate their accuracy and efficiency across a range of function spaces, and illustrate their use in solving initial boundary value problems.

Preliminaries

2.1
FSBP operators

Following Glaubitz
et al.

19
, we define an
\mathcal{F}
-based summation-by-parts (FSBP) operator as follows.

Definition 1
(FSBP operators).

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset C^{1}([a,b])
be a finite dimensional function space and
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\subset[a,b]
be a set of
n
distinct nodes.
1
1

1

For the remainder of the paper, we assume that all nodes are ordered, distinct, and contained within
[a,b]
, i.e.,
a\leq x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{n}\leq b
.
An operator
D=P^{-1}Q
is an
\mathcal{F}
-based summation-by-parts (FSBP) operator if

i.

Df(\textbf{x})=f^{\prime}(\textbf{x})
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

ii.

P
is a symmetric positive definite matrix, and

iii.

Q+Q^{T}=B=\text{diag}(-1,0,\ldots,1).

An FSBP operator has a diagonal norm if the matrix
P
is diagonal and it is closed if
a,b\in\bm{x}
.

Definition 2
(Optimal FSBP operator).

For a given function space,
\mathcal{F}\subset C^{1}([a,b])
, an
n\times n
FSBP operator is optimal if there is no FSBP operator of dimension less than
n
. Optimal nodes for the function space are nodes
\bm{x}
corresponding to an optimal FSBP operator.

Remark 3
.

Note that, for a given function space, the optimal nodes, and hence the optimal FSBP operator, need not be unique—see Section
3.5
.

Remark 4
.

Note that if
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{P}_{d}
, the space of polynomials of degree
\leq d
, then Definition 2.1 recovers the classical SBP framework. In this sense, FSBP operators extend SBP methods from polynomial approximation to general finite-dimensional function spaces. Note also that the optimal nodes for closed polynomial-based SBP operators are known to be Gauss–Lobatto quadrature nodes; see the discussion in Section
2.2
below.

Consider two functions
\phi,\psi\in C^{1}[a,b]

and denote by
\bm{\phi}=\phi(\bm{x})
and
\bm{\psi}=\psi(\bm{x})
vectors of these functions evaluated at the nodes
\bm{x}
. From Definition
1
we have that

\bm{\phi}P(D\bm{\psi})=\bm{\phi}^{\top}Q\bm{\psi}=\bm{\phi}^{\top}\left(B-Q^{T}\right)\bm{\psi}=\bm{\phi}\bm{\psi}\big|_{1}^{n}-(D\bm{\phi})^{\top}P\bm{\psi}.

(1)

Thus, we see that an FSBP operator discretely mimics the IBP identity, namely,

\int_{a}^{b}\phi\psi_{x}\,dx=(\phi\psi)\big|^{b}_{a}-\int_{a}^{b}\phi_{x}\psi\,dx.

(2)

As mentioned in Section
1
, this
discrete IBP
or
summation-by-parts
(SBP) property is crucial for deriving energy stable semi-discretized IBVPs
48
.

Comparing (
1
) and (
2
), we can see that the matrix
P
is the discrete analogue of the definite integral operator on
[a,b]
. For a diagonal norm FSBP, the diagonal entries can therefore be interpreted as weights in a positive
n
-point quadrature rule with
P=\text{diag}(\bm{w})
.

Definition 5
(Quadrature rule).

An
n
-point quadrature rule takes the form

I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}[g]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}g(x_{k}),

where
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}
are nodes and
\bm{w}=[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}]^{\top}
are weights. A quadrature rule
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
is said to be
{\mathcal{G}}
-exact with respect to the weight function
\omega(x)
if it integrates each element of
\mathcal{G}
exactly, i.e.,

\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\omega(x)\,dx=I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}},\quad\forall g\in{\mathcal{G}}.

A quadrature rule
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
is positive if
w_{k}>0
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
and closed if
x_{1}=a
and
x_{n}=b
.

The following result
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an FSBP operator.

Theorem 6
.

(
19
, Corollary 4.6)

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset C^{1}[a,b]
be a finite-dimensional function space that forms linearly independent vectors when evaluated at the node points. Then there exists an
\mathcal{F}
-based SBP operator
D=P^{-1}Q
with a
positive definite diagonal-norm matrix
P=\text{diag}(\bm{w})
if and only if there exists a positive
(\mathcal{FF)^{\prime}}
-exact
quadrature rule
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
.

This result reduces the problem of constructing FSBP operators to the problem of finding positive quadrature rules exact for
(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F})^{\prime}
. Thus, our focus will be (i) identifying function spaces
\mathcal{F}
for which such quadrature rules exist, and (ii) developing practical methods to compute them.

2.2
Gauss quadrature and node selection

The following definitions generalise those of standard Gauss quadrature; see
10
;
15
;
26
and Section
3
.

Definition 7
(Gauss and Gauss–Lobatto).

An
m
-point quadrature rule
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
is called Gaussian if it is exact for
2n
functions
{g_{1},\dots,g_{2n}}
with respect to a positive weight function
\omega(x)
, where
m=n
, i.e.,

\int_{a}^{b}g_{j}(x)\omega(x)\,dx=I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}[g_{j}],\qquad j=1,\ldots,2n.

If a rule
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
is exact for the same
2n
functions
{g_{1},\dots,g_{2n}}
, again with respect to
\omega(x)
,
but the quadrature rule is closed (i.e., includes
a
and
b
) and
m=n+1
, then it is of Gauss–Lobatto-type.

In classical Gauss quadrature, one takes
\mathcal{G}={\mathbb{P}}_{2n-1}
, i.e., the space of polynomials of degree
2n-1
or less. When
\omega(x)=1
and
[a,b]=[-1,1]
, this gives rise to Gauss–Legendre quadrature, where the nodes are given by roots of the degree
n
Legendre polynomial and the weights are positive
15
. Therefore, in the case of an SBP operator for a polynomial basis of degree
n
,
\mathcal{F}={\mathbb{P}}_{n}
, for which
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F})^{\prime}={\mathbb{P}}_{2n-1}
, an
n
-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature rule satisfies the conditions of Theorem
6
. However, in SBP-based methods, closed quadrature formulas are typically preferred, leading to the use of
n+1
point Gauss–Legendre–Lobatto quadrature formulas
14
;
30
;
31
. An
n+1
point Gauss–Legendre–Lobatto quadrature is positive and exact for polynomials of degree
2n-1

(
15
, Section 2.1)
, therefore satisfying the conditions of Theorem
6
.
By contrast, Newton–Cotes rules on equally spaced nodes can suffer from negative weights (e.g., for
n=9
or
n\geq 11

3
), violating condition
ii.
of Definition
1
. Even if the weights are positive, an equally spaced rule will typically require
2n
points to exactly integrate a polynomial of degree
2n-1
(as fixing the
2n
nodes leaves only the
2n
degrees of freedom in the weights). For large
n
, this discrepancy—
2n
points for uniform nodes versus
n+1
points for optimal nodes—can be considerable, making Gauss–Legendre–Lobatto-based SBP formulations significantly more efficient and, in fact, optimal.

In the next Section we discuss how these ideas can be extended to non-polynomial function spaces and how the associated
generalised Gauss quadrature rules
(GGQs) can be constructed.

3
Generalised Gauss quadrature

In the polynomial case, Gauss–Legendre and Gauss–Legendre–Lobatto quadrature rules underpin the construction of SBP operators. For more general function spaces, a natural analogue arises: generalised Gauss quadrature (GGQ) rules. Since an
n
-point quadrature has
2n
free parameters (nodes and weights), it is reasonable to expect exactness for
2n
functions. This observation, which goes back to Markov
41
and later developed by Karlin & Studden
29
and Krein
33
, motivates the modern theory of GGQs
40
. Whereas the theory of classical Gauss quadratures is closely connected to that of orthogonal polynomials, the theory of generalised Gauss quadratures is tied to Tchebyshev systems.

Definition 8
(Tchebyshev system).

The sequence of functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in C^{1}[a,b]
is referred to as a Tchebyshev system on a finite interval
[a,b]
if and only if each of them is real-valued and continuous on
[a,b]
and the determinants

\left|\begin{matrix}g_{1}(x_{1})&g_{2}(x_{1})&\ldots&g_{n}(x_{1})\\
g_{1}(x_{2})&g_{2}(x_{2})&\ldots&g_{n}(x_{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{1}(x_{n})&g_{2}(x_{n})&\ldots&g_{n}(x_{n})\end{matrix}\right|

are non-zero for any set of distinct points
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset[a,b]
.

Theorem 9
.

(
40
, Theorem 2.1)
Suppose that the functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}
constitute a Tchebyshev system on the interval
[a,b]
. Then there exists a unique
n
-point quadrature
rule that is Gaussian with respect to the functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}
. Furthermore, all the weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
, of the quadrature are positive.

Corollary 10
.

If
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{FF})^{\prime}
forms a Tchebyshev system on
[a,b]
, then an
\mathcal{F}
-based SBP operator exists. Furthermore, the optimal
\mathcal{F}
-based SBP operator has nodes and weights given by the GGQ rule for the function space
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof of Corollary
10
.

The proof follows from Theorems
6
and
9
. In particular, if
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{FF})^{\prime}
forms a Tchebyshev system on
[a,b]
then from Theorem
9
there exists a positive quadrature rule that is
{\mathcal{G}}
-exact. From Theorem
6
it follows that a diagonal-norm FSBP operator exists. The optimality follows from the fact that the rule in Theorem
9
is Gaussian on
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem
9
above is, more precisely, a corollary of various results from Karlin & Studden
29
given by
40
(although the latter credits the former with the result, and similar results can be found in
33
and
41

upper and lower principal representations of moment vectors from the moment space
induced by the Tchebyshev set
g_{1},\dots,g_{2n}

(
29
, Theorem 3.1)
and (ii) that the
lower
principal representation corresponds to an
n
-point quadrature rule which is exact for
g_{1},\dots,g_{2n}

(
29

. Importantly, their results also show that the
upper
principal representation results in a
closed

n+1

g_{1},\dots,g_{2n}
, i.e., a generalized Gauss–Lobatto quadrature (GGLQ) rule. (See
(
26

for a summary.) This can be summarised in the following result.

Theorem 11
(Karlin & Studden

).

Suppose that the functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}
constitute a Tchebyshev system on the interval
[a,b]
. Then there exists a unique
(n+1)
-point Gauss–Lobatto quadrature with respect to the
2n
functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}
. Furthermore, all the weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n+1}
, of the quadrature are positive.

Proof.

Follows from results in §3 and §8 of
(
29

and the discussion above.

Remark 12
.

Karlin & Studden discuss what amounts to a GGLQ formula in

Corollary 13
.

If
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{FF})^{\prime}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}\}
forms a Tchebyshev system on
[a,b]
, then a closed
\mathcal{F}
-based SBP operator exists. Furthermore, the optimal closed
\mathcal{F}
-based SBP operator has nodes and weights given by the
(n+1)
-point GGLQ rule for the function space
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Follows from Theorems
6
and

Thus, both GGQ and GGLQ rules provide the optimal building blocks for FSBP operators whenever

forms a Tchebyshev system. The practical question, however, is how to compute such rules in concrete function spaces. While the computation of GGQ rules have received some attention, e.g.,

3.1
Computation of GGQs

Recall from Definition
7
that, for a given function space
\mathcal{G}
of dimension
2n
on
[a,b]
, spanned by
\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{2n}\}
, the defining property of a GGQ is that

\int_{a}^{b}g_{j}(x)\omega(x)\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}g_{j}(x_{k}),\quad j=1,\ldots,2n.

(3)

Remark 14
.

In principle, for the construction of FSBP operators, we need consider only the weight function
\omega(x)=1
. However, as discussed below, it will be beneficial to use modified weight functions during the continuation process used in the construction procedure described below.

Equation (
3
) results in a
2n\times 2n
nonlinear system of equations to solve for the unknown nodes
x_{k}
and weights
w_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
. One can attempt to solve this nonlinear system directly via, say, the Newton–Raphson method, but in general (i) obtaining initial guesses is difficult and (ii) the computation is expensive since the Jacobians are dense. Ma
et al
.
40
were the first to resolve the issues above by (i) using a continuation approach to obtain suitable initial conditions and (ii) reformulating (
3
) to an equivalent system which, when
\mathcal{G}
forms an
extended Hermite system
can be solved via a quadratically convergent
n

Definition 15
(Hermite–Vandermonde matrix).

For a finite sequence of functions
g_{1}(x)
, …,
g_{2n}(x)
and set of
n

\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}

V=\left[\begin{matrix}g_{1}(x_{1})&g_{2}(x_{1})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{1})\\
g_{1}(x_{2})&g_{2}(x_{2})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{1}(x_{n})&g_{2}(x_{n})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{n})\\
g^{\prime}_{1}(x_{1})&g^{\prime}_{2}(x_{1})&\ldots&g^{\prime}_{2n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g^{\prime}_{1}(x_{n})&g^{\prime}_{2}(x_{n})&\ldots&g^{\prime}_{2n}(x_{n})\end{matrix}\right].

Definition 16
(Hermite system).

A finite sequence of functions
g_{1}
, …,
g_{2n}
is said to form a Hermite system on
[a,b]

g_{k}\in C^{1}[a,b]
and
\det(V)\not=0

\{x_{k}\}\subset[a,b]

Definition 17
(Extended Hermite system).

A finite sequence of functions

, …,

The algorithm of Ma

\sigma_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}g_{j}(x),\qquad\eta_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}g_{j}(x),

\sigma_{i}(x_{k})=0,\quad\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})=\delta_{ik},\quad\eta_{i}(x_{k})=\delta_{ik},\quad\eta^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})=0,\qquad i,k=1,\ldots,n.

(4)

Since we assume that

.
2
2

2

. To choose

, they use the interlacing properties of GGQ nodes—see
(
29
, Theorem 3.2)
—and compute GGQs of increasing degree. We employ this approach in our computations, taking
\omega(x)=1
.

The approach of Huybrechs
26
also employs continuation. There, GGQs of increasing degree are computed by continuously transforming between upper and lower principal representations, resulting in GGQ and generalised Gauss–Radau quadrature (GGRQ) rules, respectively. When applied to Tchebyshev sets, this requires only the solution of a sequence of piecewise-smooth, monotonic minimisation problems. Since the deformations are continuous, this results in (essentially) arbitrarily good initial guesses, making convergence of the Newton iteration more robust. However, modifying the approach to compute GGLQs is more involved and requires a different continuation mechanism. We leave this for future investigations.

3.2
Computation of GGLQs

Modifying the algorithm of Section
3.1
to enable computation of GGLQ nodes and weights is relatively straightforward. As before, consider a given function space
\mathcal{G}=\text{span}\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{2n}\}
which forms a Tchebyshev system on
[a,b]
, and a positive weight function
\omega(x)
. Now, consider a closed
n+1
-point grid of distinct points,
\bm{x}=\{x_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}\subset[a,b]
with
x_{0}=a
and
x_{n}=b
, and construct a
quasi-Hermite–Lagrange basis
,
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1},\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{n}\}
, so that

\sigma_{i}(x_{k})=0,\quad\eta_{i}(x_{k})=\delta_{ik},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n

and

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})=\delta_{ik}\quad\eta^{\prime}_{i}(x_{k})=0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,n-1,

(7)

where in both cases
i
runs over
1,\ldots,n-1
for the
\sigma_{i}
and
0,\ldots,n
for the
\eta_{i}
.
The key differences from the algorithm in Section
3.1
are that: our grid now consists of
n+1
points, rather than
n
; the derivative conditions in (
7
) are not enforced at the first and last nodes as they are in (
4
); there are
n-1
terms in
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}\}
and
n+1
terms in
\{\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{n}\}
compared to
n
in both previously. However, this still results in a square system of
2n
linear equations and, if the matrix

\widehat{V}=\left[\begin{matrix}g_{1}(x_{0})&g_{2}(x_{0})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{0})\\
g_{1}(x_{1})&g_{2}(x_{1})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{1}(x_{n})&g_{2}(x_{n})&\ldots&g_{2n}(x_{n})\\
g^{\prime}_{1}(x_{1})&g^{\prime}_{2}(x_{1})&\ldots&g^{\prime}_{2n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
g^{\prime}_{1}(x_{n-1})&g^{\prime}_{2}(x_{n-1})&\ldots&g^{\prime}_{2n}(x_{n-1})\end{matrix}\right].

can be inverted (taking the place of (
15
)), then we may expand the
\sigma_{i}
and
\eta_{i}
as linear combinations of the
g_{j}
(and vice-versa), as in the original algorithm.

One can readily adapt the theorems and proofs of
(
40
, Section 4)
to show that if
x_{0}=a
and
x_{n}=b
, then
I_{\bm{x},\bm{w}}
is a generalised Gauss–Lobatto rule on
{\mathcal{G}}
with respect to
\omega(x)
if and only if the points

are such that

\int_{a}^{b}\sigma_{i}(x)\omega(x)\,dx=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n-1,

in which case the weights are

w_{i}=\int_{a}^{b}\eta_{i}(x)\omega(x)\,dx,\quad i=0,\ldots,n.

Furthermore, as before, the interior points can be found by the locally quadratically convergent quasi-Newton iteration

x^{[\ell+1]}_{k}=x_{k}^{[\ell]}+\frac{\int_{a}^{b}\sigma_{k}^{[\ell]}(x)\omega(x)\,dx}{\int_{a}^{b}\eta_{k}^{[\ell]}(x)\omega(x)\,dx},\quad k=1,\ldots,n-1,\ \ell=0,1,\ldots.

The continuation scheme (
6
) of
54
can be employed without modification. Examples are given in Section

Once optimized nodes

and the associated positive weights

D=P^{-1}Q
that satisfies Definition
1
. Here,

where

is its corresponding derivative. Next, using

)

SF=PF_{x}-\frac{1}{2}BF.

The only unknown here is

and it can be obtained by solving the constrained least squares problem

3.4
Numerical considerations

We employ the Chebfun package in our implementation of the algorithm above, which provides a powerful and flexible framework for numerical computations involving general function spaces
13
. Its design—representing functions as piecewise polynomial interpolants—makes it especially well suited to problems such as ours, which requires high-accuracy numerical orthogonalisation, differentiation, and integration.

One key advantage of using Chebfun is its ability to adaptively determine piecewise discretisations of given functions. As noted in
40
, this is crucial when working with general function spaces, especially those with localized features, discontinuities in derivatives, or rapidly changing behaviour. Rather than fixing a discretisation
a priori
, Chebfun refines the representation until a desired accuracy is achieved, thereby enabling robust numerical integration and differentiation.

Another major benefit is that, given a basis for
\mathcal{F}
, Chebfun allows us to automatically compute a basis for the space
(\mathcal{FF})^{\prime}
. Specifically, Chebfun’s singular value decomposition
svd
implementation enables the extraction of an orthogonal basis of
(\mathcal{FF})^{\prime}
. Working with an orthogonal basis helps mitigate ill-conditioning when constructing and inverting the Hermite–Vandermonde matrices, and is recommended in
40
. A similar procedure is employed in
54
.

Finally, Chebfun can adaptively compute accurate numerical approximations of the integrals and derivatives over
\mathcal{G}
, as needed in the quasi-Newton iterations for node updates. Taken together, these features make Chebfun an ideal tool for implementing and extending the GGQ and GGLQ algorithms in the context of general function spaces and FSBP operator construction. A MATLAB code to generate these nodes, weights, and the corresponding SBP differentiation matrix is available at
23
.

5
Conclusion

We have studied the construction of diagonal-norm FSBP operators for general (non-polynomial) approximation spaces by exploiting the equivalence between such operators and positive quadrature rules exact for
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F})^{\prime}
.
When
\mathcal{G}
forms a Tchebyshev system, the theory of generalised Gauss quadrature implies existence, uniqueness, and positivity of the corresponding GGQ and GGLQ rules, and therefore yields
optimal
node sets in the sense of minimal operator dimension.

To build a closed diagonal-norm FSBP operator for a prescribed space
\mathcal{F}
, one should compute a positive GGLQ rule for
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F})^{\prime}
and then construct
D=P^{-1}Q
from these nodes and weights.
This directly transfers the efficiency of Gauss–Lobatto nodes in polynomial SBP to the setting of general function spaces.

Our numerical examples demonstrate that the resulting optimised nodes reduce the number of required grid points substantially.
For instance, for the Bessel function space in Example II we obtain a positive 25-point GGLQ-based construction, whereas an equally spaced grid required 100 points in the optimisation-based approach of
16
.

The main practical limitations are (i) reliability in characterising (and numerically working with) the structure of
\mathcal{G}

Promising directions include: robust detection of Tchebyshev-system structure for

In summary, generalised Gauss and Gauss–Lobatto quadrature provide a direct route to node selection for FSBP operators, yielding minimal-size stable discretisations whenever the underlying function-space assumptions hold. We also demonstrated the efficiency and accuracy of our improvement when solving initial boundary value problems.
