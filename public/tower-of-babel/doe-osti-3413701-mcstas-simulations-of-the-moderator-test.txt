 1. INTRODUCTION
We are building a Moderator Test Station (MTS) at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) with which
we will verify such performance gains and test new moderator concepts. These concepts include both
high-brightness and large-volume parahydrogen moderators, as well as moderators with tailored ortho:para
hydrogen levels, heterogeneous moderator concepts such as the convoluted moderator (Iverson et al. 2014),
pelletized moderators (Ananiev et al. 2014), moving moderators (Harrisson et al. 2020), and moderators
made of materials like ammonia (Iverson et al. 2018), ethane (Cantargi, Granada, and Damián 2017), and
oxygen clathrates (Zimmer 2016), which have not been widely tested let alone deployed at neutron source
facilities.
We are adding to the SNS Beam Test Facility (BTF) a proton beam chopper similar to that already used
in the SNS front end, various proton beam transport components, a neutron-producing target, a cryogenic
moderator test stand, a reflector-shielding assembly, and a performance assessment neutron beamline. The
MTS will provide the ability to:
 1. validate Second Target Station (STS) moderator designs,
 2. develop First Target Station (FTS) moderator options,
 3. benchmark scattering kernels for neutron moderating materials, and
 4. explore new strategic directions for neutron beam production,
all in a prototypical “wing” moderator configuration more appropriate to deployment at production
facilities than the current generation of test facilities, such as the Low Energy Neutron Source (LENS) (Baxter,
Leung, et al. 2012) or the Hokkaido University Neutron Source (HUNS) (Kiyanagi et al. 2003). While
many of the moderator designs and options would be tested in a prototypical fashion, where the test
moderator closely resembles that proposed for the production facility, we will also use the MTS to test,
validate, and benchmark scattering kernels and simulation methods. Having reliable scattering kernels and
simulation methods for new moderator materials and concepts will permit more rapid, reproducible, and
complete exploration of design phase space than a purely experimental approach.
Quantification of moderator performance at the MTS will include the measurement of neutron energy-dependent
emission time distributions (sometimes called pulse shapes) via time-focused crystal analyzer arrays
(Graham and Carpenter 1970), which select a variety of specific neutron energies and diffract them
into a detector which can measure their arrival distribution in time and relate that to their emission
time distribution from the moderator surface. The MTS will include two such analyzer arrays (Iverson
et al. 2024), both in time-focused geometry. This document documents the McStas (Willendrup and
Lefmann 2020) model developed to:
 • confirm that the analytically-derived analyzer configurations are indeed time-focused, and will
 satisfactorially measure the desired neutron emission time distributions (during the design process),
 • support uncertainty quantification to the analyzer design perimeters, informing the setting of
 permissible tolerances for manufacture and installation (during construction), and
 • provide instrumental resolution function estimation for deconvolution from measured neutron pulse
 shapes (during operation).

2.1 TIME-FOCUSING
Time-focused emission-time analyzers can be used to efficiently sample the wavelength-specific emission
time distributions of neutrons from a pulsed neutron source (Graham and Carpenter 1972). The analyzer
arrays are nominally conventional crystal analyzers, which select out a narrow range of neutron wavelengths
satisfying Bragg’s law and diffract them into a detector. Unfortunately, for the very narrow wavelength
range diffracted by a typical crystal analyzer, the instrumental resolution associated with even geometrically
small sources, small analyzer crystals, and small detectors is typically larger than the time distribution
being study, unless careful attention is given to the analyzer configuration. Typically, this is implemented

For the MTS, we chose to have one symmetric array in order to be able to use a mica analyzer, enabling
measurements of long wavelength neutrons with its large d-spacing. Mica, with its strong cleavage planes
along the (ab) crystal planes, can only practically be used with those planes parallel to the morphological
surface of the crystal—the crystal cannot be provided in an off-cut geometry. This immediately implies (as
can be found from the above equations) that θM = θDA = θCA , that θBA = π − 2 · θM , and that LA1 = LA2 .
That is, the primary and secondary flight path and component orientations are symmetric.
For the asymmetric array, we chose a simple cubic analyzer (copper), which we anticipated that we could
obtain with fairly high mosaic (Courtois et al. 2006). The simple cubic geometry, unlike the face-centered
cubic used previously (Baxter, Leung, et al. 2012; Micklich, Iverson, and Carpenter 2003; Iverson and
Carpenter 1998b; Bywater 1989; Graham and Carpenter 1970), is not missing any reflections from the
⟨n n n⟩ series, and thus will provide more reflections.
We chose to use an offcut angle θxB such that the normal to the crystal face would be a “typical” orientation
in which crystals are provided, in order to facilitate manufacturing. As we wished to use the ⟨n n n⟩ series,
we chose the ⟨2 2 1⟩ for the crystal normal, providing the overall geometry shown in Figure 2.

 4. SIMULATION RESULTS
The McStas model described above was run to assess the instrument resolution anticipated from the MTS
analyzer arms measuring neutron emission time distributions from a moderator under test. It should be
noted that the current goal of the simulations only concerns the instrument resolution, and not absolute
intensities or countrates, installation tolerances, etc., although those would be seen as long range goals
to be pursued at a later time. The simulation was performed for neutrons from 0.4–3.7 Å. While there
will be additional wavelengths measured in the symmetric mica analyzer, the wavelengths studied here
will accurately document the expected instrument resolution. As the goal of the simulations included
the instrument response, not the behavior of a moderator being tested, the source was modeled as an
instantaneous source—that is, without any dispersion in time, so that the time-of-arrival distribution in the
simulated detector includes only instrument resolution effects. The instrument resolution for both analyzer
arms is shown in Figure 3. The time-of-flight data from each detector is combined in this plot, but there

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
McStas modeling of the neutron beamline for the Moderator Test Station demonstrates that the conceptual
design meets the resolution goals of the instrument, supporting the measurement of emission time
distributions over a broad wavelength range. Additionally, the desired pulse shapes will be sufficiently
intense relative to background in order to make an effective measurement.
There are number of enhancements and extensions of, and studies using, the McStas model that will enable
additional useful information, and are planned for future implementation:
 1. We will explore a Bragg mirror option for moderator imaging as a backing for the symmetric (mica)
 analyzer. A small mosaic crystal could image the moderator surface (at the appropriate wavelengths)
 to the detector with sub-mm resolution.
 2. We will develop temperature appropriate structure factors, as implemented in a .lau file, in order to
 more accurately predict the resolution and the relative peak intensities.
 3. We will implement an alternative description of the analyzer crystals using Ncrystal (Cai and
 Kittelmann 2020) in order to have better control over the details of the analyzer physics.
 4. We will use the model to quantify the variation in the predicted instrument resolution to tune
 installation tolerances to meet our resolution requirements efficiently.
