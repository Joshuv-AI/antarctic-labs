Treating Severe Obesity: A Medical Perspective
Authors: Sujaytha Paknikar, Kalpana Muthusamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global prevalence of obesity has increased substantially over the past several decades, with the most rapid growth occurring among individuals at the highest levels of body mass index (BMI). Greater degrees of adiposity are associated with a disproportionately high burden of cardiometabolic disease, functional impairment, and healthcare utilization. Individuals with severe obesity exhibit distinct clinical characteristics that may influence disease risk, therapeutic response, and long-term outcomes. Clinician knowledge of unique attributes of this population and effective therapy is imperative for successful weight management and prevention of progression of metabolic burden. Optimizing outcomes frequently requires a multimodal treatment approach, and a clear understanding of how to effectively integrate these therapies is essential for successful management. Severe obesity as defined as BMI ≥ 50 kg/m2 is explored in this review.

Introduction

There is growing interest in the field of obesity medicine in developing effective tools for individualizing obesity management. This review will focus on the clinical management of severe obesity defined by a body mass index (BMI) greater than 50 kg/m2. As recently addressed by the Lancet commission, there is interest in redefining clinical obesity to consider body composition and comorbid factors [1]. The new definition of clinical obesity sets the stage to steer away from BMI as sole diagnostic criteria and improve metabolic risk stratification, with broader endorsement. The newer approach could potentially lead to undertreatment of preclinical obesity, with additional concerns related to increased prevalence estimates and challenges with body composition estimation measures [2, 3]. While this debate prevails, historical data often relies on BMI as a defining threshold so it will be used for this review. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention defines class III obesity of BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 as severe obesity [4]. As increasing categories of obesities are defined, BMI ≥ 50 kg/m2 has been noted to represent a distinct risk category with disproportionately worse outcomes compared to class III obesity [5, 6]. For the purpose of this review, the category of severe obesity with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m2 will be reviewed.

The aim of this review is to describe the clinical attributes of patients living with severe obesity, review medical therapy options, and describe the role of medical therapy as a bridge or adjunctive therapy to bariatric surgery in patients with severe obesity.

The prevalence of severe obesity has increased rapidly in the past 20 years [7]. Data from The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System for the period 1986–2005 demonstrated increase in prevalence of obesity in all BMI categories. Within the subgroup of individuals with a BMI > 50 kg/m2, prevalence rose markedly by 75% in 2005 compared to 2000. This category demonstrated the fastest increase in prevalence compared with all other BMI groups. Other studies have also demonstrated that individuals with a BMI exceeding 60 kg/m2 represent the fasting growing obesity cohort [6, 8]. Understanding the specific complexities involved in caring for this population is an essential competency and a critical component of effective clinical practice.

Research Design and Methods

This narrative review summarizes current evidence regarding the clinical characteristics and management of severe obesity, defined for the purposes of this review as BMI ≥ 50 kg/m2. Relevant literature, including clinical trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, and clinical practice guidelines, was reviewed and selected based on relevance to the topic and clinical applicability. Particular emphasis was placed on emerging evidence related to incretin-based therapies and their integration with bariatric surgical approaches. Findings were synthesized qualitatively to provide a clinically focused overview of current management considerations and evidence gaps in severe obesity care. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Unique Clinical and Behavioral Attributes

Patients living with severe obesity have a significantly increased risk of developing medical comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and coronary artery disease [6]. All-cause mortality is also associated with increased BMI [9]. Patients with severe obesity are more likely to have younger age of onset of obesity [10]. However, severe obesity is not associated with increased risk of monogenic or syndromic forms of obesity [11]. Incidence of monogenic and syndromic obesity is overall not significantly elevated, at around 3–6% in cohorts of severe obesity and BMI is a poor predictor of genetic mutation related to obesity [11, 12].

From a socioeconomic standpoint, patients in this cohort experience a higher rate of unemployment, food insecurity, marital breakdown, and lower average income [10]. From a psychosocial standpoint, patients are more likely to report a history of childhood trauma [13]. Health behaviors including increased late-night eating, fast food intake, and reduced physical activity are more commonly reported in this cohort [10]. Additionally, treating clinicians may face therapeutic dilemma due to limited evidence for higher BMI strata. Care of individuals with severe obesity may also impose a disproportionate physical burden on nursing staff and caregivers, often requiring additional resources and assistance for safe patient handling and mobility [14]. These factors underscore the complexities involved in caring for this cohort and support the need for further research to provide multidisciplinary support most effectively and understand linkage to therapeutic implications.

Impact of Medical Therapy in Management of Severe Obesity

Anti-obesity medications (AOM) have an increasingly prominent role in the management of obesity given improved efficacy and tolerability of newer agents. Landmark trials for anti-obesity have not specifically studied efficacy of these agents in patients with severe obesity and there is concern for under-recruitment of patients with higher BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 across AOM clinical trials [15]. A systematic review of 242 trials over the past 3 decades found that recruitment of patients with class III obesity in AOM trials has not increased proportionately to the increased prevalence in the population over that time period [15]. The average BMI of individuals in most AOM clinical trials range from 34 to 38 kg/m2, with most trials not separately reported on inclusion of different BMI categories. In the newer incretin-based therapy trials, the percentages of the cohort with baseline BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 have been reported, with inclusion ranging from 22% to 33% of the entire cohort. However, weight loss outcome reporting is not stratified by baseline BMI [16–18]. One exclusion was the EQUIP trial evaluating phentermine/topiramate in individuals with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2. This study examined weight loss stratified by BMI thresholds and found similar weight loss among groups, with 10.5% patient of patients having a BMI exceeding 50 kg/m2 [19]. An exploratory post hoc analyses of SURMOUNT-1 and SURMOUNT-4 tirzepatide clinical trials demonstrated similar weight decrease across all BMI categories for the first 24 weeks. Interestingly, the weight loss was greater from 24 to 72 weeks in the higher BMI categories, with 4.6% weight loss in the overweight category compared to 7% in class I, 8.8% in class II, and 9.7% in class III obesity categories [20]. A post hoc analyses of semaglutide clinical trial data demonstrated similar weight loss outcomes in individuals with BMI < 35 and ≥ 35 kg/m2 [21]. The intentional inclusion of individuals with higher BMI, along with a priori reporting of outcomes stratified by BMI category, is essential to most effectively inform and guide care for this cohort.

Integration of Medical Therapy in a Multimodal Approach for Severe Obesity

In patients with severe obesity, the foundation of treatment has historically emphasized bariatric or metabolic surgery owing to efficacy. Currently available AOMs have weight loss outcomes ranging from 5% to 20% [22]. Medical therapy in isolation might not be sufficient for the management of severe obesity, based on individual weight management goals. In relevant situations, the role of bariatric surgery and the strategic implementation of a multimodal approach should be considered, to most effectively achieve meaningful weight loss and improved health outcomes. Anticipated weight loss outcomes should be carefully evaluated to establish realistic patient expectations and to facilitate discussion of the need for a multimodal approach to achieving weight management goals. Early engagement with the bariatric surgical team can aid in devising risk mitigation strategies, while also helping to address patient concerns and increase acceptance of surgery as a treatment option.

In patients with BMI > 50 kg/m2, however, there is concern for potentially increased perioperative risk and thus there is growing interest in use of AOM as a bridge or neoadjuvant therapy before bariatric surgery. Much of the literature exploring use of AOM in conjunction with bariatric surgery focuses on GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP1RA)-based therapies. A systematic review of 11 studies of patients who received GLP1RA therapy preoperatively and control groups who did not receive GLP1RA demonstrated similar weight loss [23]. With respect to complications, findings showed an inconsistent association with longer operative time or increased adhesions. An increased risk of postoperative nausea and emesis was noted in individuals on GLP1RA therapy [23]. A key factor influencing postoperative gastrointestinal symptoms may be the duration for which GLP1RA therapy was withheld prior to surgery. Historically, guidelines have recommended holding 1–2 doses prior to surgery and use of liquid diet prior to surgery which is often in place prior to bariatric surgery. New guidelines from the American Society of Anesthesiology emphasize the need to balance risk of retained gastric contents, and aspiration, with hyperglycemia related to disruption in medication in individuals with diabetes mellitus [24]. Guidance must be individualized to consider dose of medication, current GI symptom burden, motility concerns, and glycemic control in individuals with diabetes mellitus [24].

Medical therapy for weight management used as a bridge to bariatric surgery is generally not continued postoperatively, given the surgery’s favorable effects on appetite regulation. Existing data demonstrate weight regain with GLP1RA discontinuation despite attention to lifestyle measures [25]. In this context, weight loss outcome after bariatric surgery in patients with preoperative use of GLP1RA is of interest. Several recent observational studies have examined this issue and have reported comparable weight loss outcomes between individuals who used GLP1RA prior to bariatric surgery and those who did not receive any AOM preoperatively [26–28]. There is also evidence suggesting a slightly lower post bariatric surgery weight loss in individuals exposed to GLP1RA therapy prior to surgery, of the magnitude of 2–5% less total body weight loss, that was reported to be statistically significant [29, 30]. It is without doubt that a multimodal approach would offer incremental weight loss, leading to a lower body weight for the individual after bariatric surgery [31]. This cumulative benefit has also been demonstrated in other layered weight loss approaches as in SURMOUNT-3 with intensive lifestyle intervention followed by tirzepatide increasing the total body weight loss to 25%, as opposed to the average 20% weight loss noted on this medication [32]. In summary, use of AOM as bridge therapy to bariatric surgery is safe, effective, and without significant adverse impact on postoperative weight loss outcome [33].

The role of endoscopic bariatric options such as endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty and intragastric balloon as bridge therapy is outside the scope of this review, but these surgical options have been explored and can provide safe and effective alternatives to medical therapy [34, 35]. There is currently lack of data on direct comparisons between these two modalities preceding bariatric surgery. Liquid low calorie diets can achieve a BMI reduction of 9.8 kg/m2 and can be an effective bridging strategy [36]. Use of very low calorie diets preceding bariatric surgery can improve weight loss outcomes at 12 months [37]. Employing these strategies can be driven by patient preference and desire to optimize overall weight loss outcomes, but a mandatory weight loss might lead to inadvertent delay in a more durable and effective bariatric surgery option.

In patients with BMI > 50 kg/m2, bariatric surgery has been a well-established tool due to efficacy in terms of total weight loss, improvement of metabolic comorbidities, and mortality benefits [38, 39]. However, long-term data surrounding bariatric surgery does suggest that weight recurrence is experienced by a third to half of patients, with higher recurrence rates in patients who undergo sleeve gastrectomy as compared to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) [40, 41]. As a result, AOMs have been utilized as adjunctive therapy to bariatric surgery when there is suboptimal weight loss, or weight regain after achieving nadir weight loss [42]. A systematic review of 19 studies, including 1290 individuals, showed an average weight loss of 10–15% on incretin-based therapies 12 months after bariatric surgery [42], comparable to clinical trial outcomes. GLP1RA therapies were well tolerated following bariatric surgery, with no increase in gastrointestinal symptoms compared with rates reported in clinical trials [42]. There was also favorable impact, with GLP1RA use, on weight-related comorbidities that emerged with weight regain after bariatric surgery [43]. Currently there is no standardized approach to timing of initiation of AOM after bariatric surgery. Variable approaches including initiation of medical therapy with suboptimal initial weight loss, defined as < 20% total weight loss, < 50% excess weight loss at 18–24 months and BMI > 35 kg/m2 and weight regain, defined as > 10–15% regain from nadir weight, > 15% regain of weight lost, any weight regain after nadir at 12 months, and continuous weight regain after an initial weight loss, have all been utilized in the studies in this space. A consistent definition across the literature is essential for accurate interpretation and for meaningful translation into clinical practice. In most practices, AOM utilization is not considered until 12 months after index bariatric surgery.

For management of weight regain post bariatric surgery, surgical or endoscopic revision procedures have also been employed. In one analysis done on patients who experienced weight regain after sleeve gastrectomy, there was greater weight loss of the magnitude of 11–12% after conversion Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass (cRYGB) as compared to after use of semaglutide or tirzepatide [44]. The cRYGB group demonstrated a higher rate of subsequent surgical or endoscopic interventions, suggesting greater procedural complexity and the need for experienced surgical expertise in this population [45]. For individuals who experience weight regain after RYGB, transoral outlet reduction (TORe) has been used as an endoscopic revision strategy. Pooled data across 12 studies has suggested an average total weight loss of 8.9% at 12 months post-TORe [46]. To further augment the impact of the TORe procedure one study added liraglutide post TORe. The study demonstrated that early compared to later use of liraglutide enhanced total weight loss by 15% vs 11% [47]. An endoscopic revision option is also available after sleeve gastrectomy and noted to be superior to GLP1RA therapy, but a subgroup analysis showed similar weight loss specifically with tirzepatide in comparison to revisional endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty [48]. For patients with severe obesity there is a need to further examine optimal timing of medical and surgical interventions to optimize short-term and long-term outcomes.

Barriers to Implementation and Pragmatic Aspects of Medical Therapy Management

The real-world data from incretin-based therapies for weight loss have revealed that there are significant barriers to adoption and ongoing use of these therapies. There is evidence to suggest that patients of racial and ethnic minority backgrounds, specifically patients who identify as Black or Hispanic, are less likely to be prescribed AOM therapy even correcting for education, employment, and insurance status [49, 50]. When patients are prescribed AOM, real-world data has shown a reduced degree of weight loss of 8% with semaglutide and tirzepatide, significantly lower in comparison to clinical trials, likely driven by reduced maintenance dose and frequent drug discontinuation [51]. In fact, GLP1RA therapy discontinuation has been found to be as high as 40–70% in the first year of use [50]. Patients are at higher risk of discontinuation if they are age 65 or older, report lower income, experience moderate to severe gastrointestinal side effects, have unmet weight loss expectations, or experience significant weight stigma [52, 53].

AOM use should be personalized based upon patient clinical and psychosocial factors and with clear understanding of patient health goals and expectations [54]. AOM use is expected to be longitudinal and medication titration requires ongoing feedback from patients and use of a multidisciplinary team to ensure that maximal benefit and tolerance can be achieved [54].

Limitations

Narrative description of literature was utilized for this scoping review. Given the clinical significance of the topic, future research would benefit from a quantitative synthesis. Also, utilization of a specific search strategy would minimize the risk of exclusion of relevant studies. Current literature demonstrates gaps in inclusion of individuals with higher BMI categories in clinical trials, limiting the applicability of current evidence to the cohort with severe obesity. Additional studies are needed to best guide layering of multimodal therapy to improve total weight loss and overall metabolic and functional outcome.

Conclusion

Understanding the nuances of managing patients with severe obesity is increasingly important given the rising prevalence of this population. Existing data favors using a multimodal approach for longitudinal care of individuals with severe obesity. Incretin-based therapies in severe obesity remain understudied, highlighting the need for further research, especially as novel and more potent agents are developed and increasingly integrated into patient care. Challenges exist with concerns related to health inequity in access to AOM and also in overcoming barriers to long-term use. Given the chronic nature of obesity, individuals are more likely to require use of multiple modalities of treatment over their lifetime. AOM can serve as a safe and effective bridge therapy to bariatric surgery. For patients who have experienced weight recurrence after bariatric surgery, AOMs are a safe and effective adjuvant tool alongside endoscopic and laparoscopic surgical revision. As there is growing research looking at phenotyping obesity and individualizing therapy there is a need to advance the management of individuals living with severe obesity.
