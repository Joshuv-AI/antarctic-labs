Mechanical Thrombectomy for Pediatric Acute Ischemic Stroke With Large Vessel Occlusion: Technical Guide and Case Series
Authors: Zachary A. Sorrentino, Wuyang Yang, Brandon P. Lucke-Wold, Danyas Sarathy, Amanda Stanton, Zahra Hasanpour Segherlou, Hunter Hutchinson, Alexandra Stern, Shawna Amini, Alfred P. See, Matthew J. Koch, Brian L. Hoh, Nohra Chalouhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Pediatric acute ischemic stroke is a rare entity, and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) in this population can be technically challenging. We aim to discuss key technical aspects of pediatric MT and report our experience in this population.METHODS:Pediatric patients were identified from our prospectively maintained database of MT patients. Inclusion criteria were pediatric patients’ age >28 days and <18 years undergoing MT for large vessel occlusion at a single center between 2012 and 2023.RESULTS:Seven patients were included in this case series. Patient age ranged from ≈1 to 17 (10.0±6.2) years. Causes of large vessel occlusion included hypercoagulability disorders, cardiac disorders, and iatrogenic during neurointerventions. Most patients (n=5, 71.4%) had middle cerebral artery or internal carotid artery occlusions. Six patients (85.7%) were successfully treated with MT, achieving modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 2C or above. Five patients were treated with stent retriever MT and the other 2 patients with aspiration MT alone, with an average of 2.3±1.5 passes total. Five patients (71.4%) made an excellent clinical recovery with a pediatric modified Rankin Scale score ≤2 at last outpatient follow-up (pediatric modified Rankin Scale score, 1.8±1.2). Average follow-up duration was 2.7±2.4 years.CONCLUSIONS:Recent advancements in catheter technology have facilitated MT in pediatric patients. Pediatric MT appears to be safe and effective for treating cerebral large vessel occlusions; however, technical limitations can arise secondary to small vessel diameters. A technical guide based on patient age and femoral artery diameter is provided.

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) in pediatric patients, typically secondary to middle cerebral artery (MCA) or internal carotid artery (ICA) large vessel occlusion (LVO), is a neurological emergency associated with significant morbidity and mortality.1,2 AIS in pediatric patients occurs at a lower incidence than in adults, estimated between 1.3 and 13 per 100 000 annually, and the cause differs compared with adults.1–3 In pediatric AIS, LVO is frequently associated with cardioembolic causes, including thrombotic emboli from cardiopulmonary support systems such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, typically in the context of congenital cardiac anomalies; many of these patients have extensive comorbidities, which can complicate LVO diagnosis and treatment.1,2 Despite its rarity, the long-term impact on pediatric patients who suffer from an AIS is striking, with ≈70% experiencing permanent deficits.4–6

Arterial recanalization therapies, such as intravenous tPA (tissue-type plasminogen activator) and mechanical thrombectomy (MT), have demonstrated excellent clinical benefit in adults with AIS secondary to LVO,7,8 and MT in particular in adults is superior to medical management alone.8–10 Indeed, MT is now the standard of care in adult patients with AIS and LVO; however, MT is rarely utilized in pediatric patients.2,4 The safety profile of MT for pediatric AIS with large vessel occlusion is unclear due to the smaller vessel diameter and concerns regarding the use of catheters, which are primarily designed for adult use. However, the potential benefit of MT in pediatric AIS due to LVO could be substantial.4 Multiple case series, case-control studies, and at least 1 large prospective study have demonstrated improvement in functional outcomes for pediatric patients undergoing MT for LVO.2,3,11–14 The current literature suggests that for pediatric patients aged 1 to 18 years, MT can be considered based on level C evidence and class IIb recommendations.15,16 Special pediatric considerations for MT include the challenges of navigating large catheters in smaller caliber arteries, the need for weight-based adjustments for contrast, concerns about radiation exposure in pediatrics, and the unique arteriopathies that contribute to pediatric AIS.2,15,16 The recent development of smaller aspiration catheters designed for more distal occlusions in adults may be utilized in pediatric patients and could improve the safety and efficacy of MT in this population.17 No technical algorithm is currently available in the literature to guide neurointerventionalists performing MT in these rare but critical pediatric emergencies.

Herein, we describe our institutional experience with MT for cerebral LVO in pediatric patients and compare our indications and outcomes to cases reported in the literature. We also provide a description of our approach and provide a technical guide for pediatric MT based on patient age and femoral vessel diameter.

Methods

Study Cohort and Variable Definition

Institutional review board approval for the prospective stroke database and retrospective review of cases was obtained. Patient consents were exempted as both studies were observational in nature without identifiable risks or benefits. A database of patients undergoing MT for LVO at the University of Florida was queried, and pediatric patients aged <18 years undergoing MT between 2012 and 2023 were included, with the exclusion of neonates ≤28 days old, which is classified as perinatal stroke.1 There were no other inclusion or exclusion criteria. Patients with incomplete procedural or outcome data were excluded from this analysis. The checklist required by the journal is available in the Supplemental Material.

Data Collection

Patient information was extracted from the electronic medical record, which included demographics, pertinent medication usage including anticoagulation or antiplatelet use status, history of prior AIS or LVO, clinical AIS symptoms and pediatric National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (pedNIHSS) if recorded, suspected cause of LVO, technical details of MT including number of passes and aspiration versus stent retriever (SR), site of LVO, adverse procedural related events including access-site vascular injury, intracranial hematoma, or periprocedural LVO or vessel dissection, and clinical outcomes following MT. Follow-up was defined as any documented outpatient neurology or stroke clinic visit where the pediatric modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was assessed, with time from discharge to the latest follow-up measured in years.

Outcomes

The principal clinical outcomes evaluated included long-term functional status defined by pediatric mRS score, with a score of 0 to 2 at final follow-up being favorable, and the rate of successful angiographic recanalization, measured as a modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) grade 2C-3.18 Any adverse procedural or periprocedural events listed above were reported.

Decision-Making and Technical Approaches

Given the rarity of pediatric LVO and subsequent MT treatment, the decision-making and technical approach were evaluated and reported on a case-by-case basis. There are few existing standard workflows or published recommended technical approaches for these procedures. For each case, the underlying cause leading to LVO is described, and the technical approach regarding access and equipment used is listed. To align with recommendations for technical approaches, the patients were stratified into 3 age groups based on FDA pediatric group classification: group I: infants (28 days to <2 years), group II: children (2–5 years), and group III: adolescents (5–18 years). A proposed recommendation of approach and system is suggested in this study based on patient age and the femoral artery vessel diameter.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Seven pediatric patients with acute ischemic stroke due to LVO who underwent MT were included. These patients have not been reported in any prior case series, published data sets, or outcomes. A summary of patient demographic and clinical characteristics is listed in Table 1. One patient belongs to group I, 2 patients belong to group II, and 4 patients belong to group III. The approximate age range (not specified for anonymity), mean weight, and mean height of each group are group I (n=1, ≈0–2 years old, 7 kg, and 65 cm), group II (n=2, ≈3–5 years, 20.5 kg, and 113.5 cm), and group III (n=4, ≈6–17 years, 56.3 kg, and 169 cm). The overall average age was 10.0±6.2 years. No patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study time period were excluded. Two cases from each group (except for group I with only 1 case) are discussed in detail.

Among these patients, underlying preexisting conditions varied widely, including congenital heart disease, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, protein-losing enteropathy, and hypercoagulability disorders. One patient presented while on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, 2 had a history of significant congenital cardiac anomalies, and 2 had prior embolic risk factors (eg, patent foramen ovale and thrombophilia). Three patients (42.8%) had iatrogenic LVO from treatment of other diseases, while 4 others (57.2%) were of cardiac origin.

The average pedNIHSS score at presentation (n=5, 2 patients excluded due to intraoperative finding) was 11.4±5.4 (range, 6–21). Two (28.6%) patients were actively taking low-dose aspirin (81 mg) before admission for preexisting cardiac disease. One of these patients was also taking apixaban 2.5 mg for prophylactic anticoagulation. Only 1 patient received tPA before MT (Table 1). The majority (71.4%) of patients were imaged with computed tomography angiography (CTA) and computed tomography perfusion, and only 1 patient (14.3%) underwent magnetic resonance imaging preoperatively.

Five patients (71.4%) had anterior circulation LVO, while 2 had basilar occlusion. Five patients underwent MT with an SR, while the other 2 patients had aspiration only, with an average of 2.3±1.5 passes total (range, 1–5 passes). Five patients had mTICI 3, 1 had mTICI 2C, and 1 had mTICI 2B reperfusion. Only patient 2 (group II) received Iodixanol as contrast due to a preexisting renal condition, while others received Iohexol. For clinical outcomes, 5 patients (71.4%) made an excellent clinical recovery with a pediatric mRS score ≤2 (average 1.8±1.2) at last outpatient follow-up, with an average follow-up duration of 2.7±2.4 years (Table 1). Patient 3 initially presented with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage and poor neurological exam; post-aneurysm embolization, a left M2 LVO was noted for which MT was performed; however, given persistent poor neurological status, the decision was made to withdraw care, and subsequently, a pediatric mRS score of 6 was assigned.

Average contrast amount for patient groups was 55.0 mL for group 1, 55.0 mL for group II, and 66.1 mL for group III; overall, this was 61.3±23.5 mL. Average radiation per group was 1550 mGy for group 1, 258 mGy for group 2, and 352 mGy for group 3; overall, this was 496±472 mGy. Of the 6 patients who had femoral access, closure devices were utilized for 3 patients, as detailed in Table 2, while the other 3 achieved hemostasis with manual pressure. There were no noted access-site hematomas or signs of vascular injury that required treatment. There were no noted postoperative intracerebral hemorrhages, pseudoaneurysms, or vascular dissections along traversed arteries to suggest catheter-related complications after MT in all treated patients.

Case Presentations

Patient 1 (Group III: Iatrogenic Coil Migration and Left M2 Occlusion)

A patient aged ≈10 to 15 years (45 kg/170 cm) who had undergone coil embolization of a juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma 48 hours prior developed intermittent right-sided weakness, facial droop, and aphasia (pedNIHSS score, 9). CTA demonstrated coil migration and LVO in the left MCA M2 segment. Emergent MT was performed via right transfemoral approach with a 6F sheath. A 90-cm Envoy (CERENOVUS, Irvine, CA) with a 038 wire was navigated to the left proximal ICA. A Vecta 46 intermediate catheter (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) was advanced to the petrous ICA, and multiple runs confirmed coil migration into the left M2 segment with superimposed thrombus. A Trak-21 microcatheter (Stryker) was advanced over an Aristotle 014 wire (Scientia Vascular, West Valley City, UT) to the thrombus, at which point the Vecta 46 was advanced to the M3 segment to deploy a 3 mm×32 mm Trevo SR (Stryker). Aspiration from the Vecta 46 successfully mobilized the coil to the M1 segment, and patency of the M2 and M3 segments was visualized on angiogram. An additional 4×41 mm Trevo SR was deployed to remove the coil from the M1 segment, and mTICI 3 revascularization was achieved. Postoperatively and at 12-month follow-up, they had no residual deficits and a pediatric mRS score of 0.

Patient 2 (Group II: Basilar Occlusion)

A patient aged ≈2 to 5 years (21 kg/110 cm) with Uhl anomaly and prior bidirectional Glenn shunt, on aspirin prophylaxis, presented with sudden-onset aphasia, encephalopathy, and left-sided weakness (pedNIHSS score, 21). Magnetic resonance imaging and MRA revealed a developing posterior circulation infarct due to a basilar artery occlusion with extension into bilateral posterior cerebral artery P1 segments. Emergent MT via the left vertebral artery was performed through right femoral access with a 5F slender sheath. The left vertebral artery was navigated with a 035 wire through a 5F Envoy. Angiogram showed a basilar occlusion (Figure [A]). A 6 mm×37 mm Trevo SR was placed in the right posterior cerebral artery through the microcatheter. Angiogram after the first pass showed additional thrombus at the basilar apex (Figure [B]). A second SR (4 mm×41 mm) was placed in the right posterior cerebral artery, and repeat MT resulted in mTICI 2B reperfusion (Figure [C]). Postoperatively, the patient showed initial improvement but developed a new infarct on day 11 in a distal posterior cerebral artery territory without new LVO amenable to MT, necessitating initiation of rivaroxaban in addition to bivalirudin. At the last follow-up, they were able to ambulate with assistance and a pediatric mRS score of 4.

Patient 4 (Group I: Right ICA Terminus Occlusion)

A patient aged ≈0 to 2 years (7 kg/65 cm) presented with seizures due to a grade I pilocytic astrocytoma. Two weeks after biopsy and chemotherapy, they developed left hemiparesis and refractory seizures. Imaging revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage (Figure [D]), infarcts, and 2 anterior cerebral artery pseudoaneurysms (Figure [E]). Endovascular coiling achieved aneurysm obliteration, but an iatrogenic right ICA terminus occlusion with extension into the right M1 occurred intraoperatively (Figure [F]). After the occlusion, the patient was loaded with heparin at 20 IU/kg. The 4F Berenstein catheter (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT) for postcoiling angiogram was exchanged for a 3.9F distal access catheter. First pass MT was achieved with a Solitaire SR (4 mm×20 mm; Medtronic) through a Prowler 10 microcatheter (CERENOVUS) with aspiration on the distal access catheter. Although complete ICA/MCA revascularization was achieved, the right MCA reoccluded on repeat angiogram during the same procedure. Immediately after the MCA reocclusion was noted, another round of MT with Solitaire SR (Medtronic) restored MCA flow except for the right anterior temporal artery (Figure [G]). Repeat angiogram at the end of the procedure showed persistent right MCA revascularization. Iatrogenic ICA/MCA occlusion and reocclusion were thought to be secondary to prolonged catheterization time with potential stasis in the right MCA during treatment of the 2 anterior cerebral artery pseudoaneurysms. Post-op imaging revealed bilateral multifocal MCA distribution infarcts. They were managed with anticoagulation and improved clinically, with pediatric mRS score improving from 3 at 6 months to 2 at 12 months.

Patient 5 (Group II: Left M1 Occlusion)

A patient aged ≈2 to 5 years (20 kg/117 cm) on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after atrial septostomy developed right-sided weakness and facial droop (pedNIHSS score, 8). CTA perfusion demonstrated proximal left M1 occlusion without a large core infarct. The patient was taken for MT; right femoral access was achieved with a 6F sheath and exchanged for an 80-cm Shuttle catheter (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN), navigated to the left proximal ICA. An AXS Catalyst-5 catheter (Stryker) was navigated over a Trak-21 microcatheter and a Synchro2 soft 014 wire (Stryker) past the M1 LVO. Suction aspiration through the Catalyst-5 revascularized M1 with continued thrombus in the superior M2 branch. After 2 further aspiration attempts, a 3×37 Trevo SR was deployed along with an additional suction aspiration through the catalyst-5. Following this, mTICI 3 reperfusion was achieved. Post-MT, pedNIHSS score improved to 2. The patient was hospitalized until heart transplant 7 months later, and at 1-year post-MT follow-up, the patient had a pediatric mRS score of 1.

Patient 6 (Group III: Right M1/M2 Occlusion)

A ≈10 to 16-year-old patient (59 kg/173 cm) with a hypercoagulability disorder presented with left-sided weakness and slurred speech (pedNIHSS score, 6). CTA showed a right M1 thrombus. The patient’s symptoms worsened shortly after arrival to the emergency department, and a decision was made to proceed with MT. Right radial access was achieved with a 7F slender sheath, and a 088 Ballast long sheath catheter (90 cm; Balt, Montmorency, France) was advanced to the petrous ICA. Angiogram showed right M1/2 occlusion (Figure [H]). The right MCA was navigated with a Synchro 2 standard wire (Stryker) and Phenom 21 microcatheter (Medtronic), and then, a 6F Sofia (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced and interfaced to the occlusion site. One pass with aspiration achieved mTICI 3 reperfusion (Figure [I]), and his post-MT National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score returned to 0. At 1-year follow-up, they progressed to a pediatric mRS score of 0.

Proposed Technical Approach for Each Age Group

We propose an approach for MT in each age group derived from our results. These proposals are detailed in Table 3 and are based on age and femoral artery diameter just above the bifurcation measured with ultrasound guidance at the time of vascular access. All pediatric patients are generally recommended to undergo femoral access with a pulse oximeter. For age group I (28 days to 2 years with femoral artery diameter <2.6 mm), we recommend a 4F femoral sheath, a 4F catheter (eg, a 3.9F distal access catheter or 4F Midway; Penumbra, Alameda, CA), and a 3-mm-wide and short SR (eg, Solitaire 3 mm×20 mm; Medtronic). Alternatively, a 5F slender sheath (outer diameter is similar to that of a 4F sheath) can be used, and a 4F selection catheter (eg, Berenstein; Merit Medical) can be exchanged for a Zoom 35 (Imperative Care, Campbell, CA) or similar small-diameter aspiration catheter with or without an SR. For age group II (2–5 years, femoral arterial diameter, 2.6–3.5 mm), a 6F sheath is recommended for femoral access, with a 6F base catheter (eg, a 6F Envoy; Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ), and intermediate diameter suction catheters, such as Vecta 46 (Stryker), Red 43 (Penumbra), or Zoom 45 (Imperative Care, Campbell, CA). If an SR is needed, the length of the stent is more forgiving than age group I, but, similarly, we recommend a 3-mm small and short SR (eg, Solitaire 3 mm×20 mm; Medtronic).17 Group III patients (>5 years, femoral arterial diameter >3.5 mm) can be considered similar to an adult approach if the femoral arterial diameter is in the adult range of >3.5 mm, and radial artery access can also be considered in this group. We recommend a 6F femoral sheath with exchange to an 8F long sheath for use as the base catheter, followed by use of an aspiration catheter with an inner diameter of either 55, 62, 68, or 71 thousandths of an inch with a larger diameter for patients on the older age of the spectrum of group III. Closure is per operator preference and directed by vessel diameter; younger patients in groups I and II would more likely require manual pressure as the femoral artery diameter may be too small for a closure device. Although we do not have a specific institutional protocol, a closure device is used for any femoral access with a sheath of 5F or larger; closure devices for smaller sheaths are not routinely available, and thus, manual pressure is utilized if a smaller sheath is used. In general, closure devices for sheaths smaller than 5F are not readily available, and thus, we use manual pressure for these patients (4F or 5F slender). Discussion

Summary of Key Results

AIS in childhood and adolescence can significantly impact patients due to long-term morbidity. The rarity of pediatric AIS limits the amount of data available regarding the safety and efficacy of MT in pediatric patients. Herein, we describe our institution’s experience with 7 pediatric patients who underwent MT for AIS with a focus on technical aspects. These patients were classified into 3 distinct groups based on age, and a representative illustrated case from each group was presented. The causes of pediatric AIS in our series are consistent with causes identified in the literature although we additionally highlight that iatrogenic LVO during neurointerventional procedures can be safely treated with MT, as occurred for 3 patients in this series.2,5,11,19,20 A comparison with other pediatric MT series in Table 4 demonstrates a comparable age range, initial pedNIHSS score, and positive functional outcomes after MT both in this series and others.2,3,11–14 The average pedNIHSS score at presentation was 11.4±5.4 in our series compared with 14.4±5.6 in the study by Bhatia et al2 and 11.4±9.6 in the study by Dicpinigaitis et al,13 and the average pediatric mRS score at last follow-up was 1.8±2.2 in our series compared with 2.3±1.3 in the study by Bhatia et al2 and 1.6±1.5 in the study by Sporns et al.11 More granular data such as school performance was not available for long-term follow-up in this series.

Cumulatively, these studies, including at least 1 large prospective study, all demonstrate that patients with severe neurological deficits in the setting of an LVO can be treated with MT and achieve favorable neurological outcomes.2,3,11–14

Decision-Making Regarding MT in Pediatric Stroke

While recent randomized trials in adult AIS have established a key role for MT in patients with LVO, the role of MT in pediatric AIS remains unclear. A 2019 meta-analysis of 113 pediatric MTs revealed that 90% of patients had good long-term neurological outcomes with few complications.16 A cross-sectional survey of the National Inpatient Sample database yielded 190 pediatric patients who underwent MT for AIS, and these patients had decreased rates of hemicraniectomies and similar functional outcomes compared with medically managed patients13; however, the infrequent occurrence of pediatric AIS poses a research challenge by limiting the ability to perform prospective studies.21–23 It has been shown that cranial growth and the development of intracranial arteries are nearly complete by the age of 5 years, reaching about 85% of their adult caliber by that time.24,25 From a technical perspective, the procedure for group III (older than the age of 5 years) should theoretically be similar to adult MT.3 Concerning whether to offer MT versus medical management alone, the prospective Save ChildS Pro study had a higher proportion of patients with severe PedNIHSS scores and cardiac anomalies in their MT cohort compared with medical treatment alone cohort, which may indicate that patients with an LVO of cardioembolic origin may be more frequently offered MT compared with other causes or AIS without LVO.11 Our case series adds to the evidence that MT is safe and effective in appropriately selected pediatric patients with LVO, including patients with iatrogenic LVO.

Diagnosis and Management of Pediatric Stroke

Diagnosis of AIS is challenging due to the low prevalence of pediatric AIS and the high prevalence of mimicking diseases in patients presenting with focal neurological deficits.26–28 Recent work identified facial weakness, arm weakness, and ambulatory impairment in the preceding week as risk factors for stroke compared with mimicking conditions.27 In terms of management of pediatric AIS, there is evidence supporting the use of tPA and MT for appropriately selected patients though randomized trials are still needed in this population.15,16,29 The American Heart Association 2019 guidelines for pediatric stroke also recommend management of hyper/hypoglycemia, hypertension above the 95th percentile, and hyperthermia >38 °C.15 Focal cerebral arteriopathy also represents a key differential diagnosis to exclude, given the risk of manipulating a vessel with inflamed endothelium, which may not represent an embolic occlusion but rather a stenotic occlusion.

Technical Approach and Other Considerations in Pediatric MT

The pediatric stroke group is extremely heterogeneous with delicate vessel structures and, thereby, requires meticulous individualization. We grossly categorized our patients based on the Food and Drug Administration age group classification but also incorporated our considerations of femoral arterial diameter. Although our approach for each patient was individualized, a systematic approach can be summarized by our proposed approach outlined in Table 3. This approach includes the use of recently developed small aspiration catheters (035 or 046 inner diameter) designed for distal vasculature in adult patients, which could be used for proximal occlusions in the younger age group patients. The system used in pediatric MT is primarily limited by the size of the access sheath, thereby determining whether a base catheter or suction catheter should be used. If the patient is group II or above, a 6F or even 8F sheaths can be used, then a triaxial system with base support catheter, and a suction catheter and microcatheter can be utilized, as described by Bhogal et al3 in 2018 with their cohort of 5 patients mainly in age of teens, of which 4 patients were able to insert an 8F catheter, similar to adult patients. In contrast, for smaller patients (age <2 years), the distal access to the thrombus is more restricted, and more than often one must use a stent-retrieve system only without a suction catheter. The recent introduction of smaller aspiration catheters (035 inner diameter) can allow aspiration to be used in this age group.

Additional technical considerations that impact angiography in pediatrics include radiation exposure and contrast dosing. Minimizing radiation exposure in pediatric patients is critical, given that adverse clinical effects are possible at lower dosages along with risk for long-term neoplastic sequalae,30 and exposure should be kept as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) per pediatric interventional radiology guidelines.30 In adults, radiation dosage in terms of cumulative air kerma during an MT is ≈854 mGy based on large retrospective data sets.31 In our case series, an average of 496 mGy across all patients was noted. Weight-based contrast dosing limit in the pediatric population is another technical consideration in performing neurointerventional procedures, and in this case series, only the lightest patient (7 kg) received more total contrast than the manufacturer recommendations of 5.0 mL/kg. If the contrast dose is exceeded, ensuring adequate hydration orally or parenterally along with monitoring renal function postoperatively is recommended to ameliorate any contrast-induced kidney injury.32

In addition to these technical considerations, intraoperative medical management of pediatric patients with AIS can differ markedly from that of adult patients. Concerning anticoagulation, bivalirudin is often utilized in pediatric patients with cardiac conditions, and the utilization of heparin during neurointerventional procedures should consider any preoperative administration or bivalirudin.33 Bivalirudin activity can be measured using the activated clotting time, and thus, it can be transitioned to heparin using the same intraoperative activated clotting time goals in our experience.34 Given the short half-life of 25 minutes, bivalirudin can be quickly transitioned to heparin for neurointerventional procedures.34 Anesthesiawise, this patient population is often complex as AIS cause is frequently in the context of severe cardiac comorbidities, which may require specialized cardiac anesthesia and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation equipment to be brought into the intervention suite. While these accommodations may pose delays in revascularization time compared with typical adult MT procedures, it is critical that cardiopulmonary stability can be ensured throughout the case before proceeding.

Limitations

This case series is limited inherently by the small sample size, retrospective nature of the study, and collection at a single institution. In addition, generalizability to the pediatric AIS population may be limited as 3 of the included patients had an AIS cause due to an iatrogenic cause during an interventional procedure, which is not the most common cause of pediatric AIS. There was no direct comparison group, such as patients with AIS and LVO who did not undergo MT.

Future Directions

While MT appears to be beneficial in pediatric patients with LVO, it is not standard of care, given the lack of large clinical trials compared with adults with LVO. Utilization of national registries and clinical trial enrollment as feasible, to better understand whether MT is truly beneficial compared with medical management, will help establish standard guidelines for when MT should be offered in this population. As catheters continue to improve with the availability of smaller sizes usable in pediatric patients or even pediatric-specific devices, the feasibility of offering MT to pediatric AIS with patients with LVO will continue to expand, and interventionalists should be prepared to offer this treatment when indicated.

Conclusions

We report on pediatric patients with acute ischemic stroke, including iatrogenic LVO, treated with MT, contributing to the growing body of literature supporting MT in pediatric patients. A technical guide for performing modern MT in pediatric patients is provided to guide interventionalists in offering this treatment.
