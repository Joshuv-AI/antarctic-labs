Inulin enhances systemic health and egg quality by regulating gut microbiota and metabolomic profiles in laying hens
Authors: Yipeng Li, Jiamei Cui, Yuemeng Shang, Vasileios Paraskevas, Yunkyoung Lee, Guiguo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of dietary inulin supplementation on systemic health and egg quality in laying hens through changes in the gut microbiota and metabolome. A total of 288 healthy 15-week-old laying hens were randomly assigned to a basal diet (CON) or the same diet supplemented with 1.5 g/kg inulin (INU). At the peak laying age of 35 weeks, egg yolk fatty acid composition was determined, cecal microbial composition was analyzed by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, and cecal metabolites were assessed using untargeted metabolomics. Compared with the CON group, the INU group showed significantly improved laying performance, systemic health-related indicators, egg quality, serum and yolk antioxidant capacity, and yolk fatty acid composition (P < 0.05), along with a significantly lower yolk malondialdehyde concentration (P < 0.05). In addition, compared with the CON group, the INU group had a higher relative abundance of potentially beneficial microbiota, particularly Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium, and higher concentrations of acetic acid, butyric acid, and isobutyric acid (P < 0.01). Cecal metabolomic analysis further showed that, compared with the CON group, the INU group had significantly altered metabolic pathways mainly related to histidine metabolism, biotin metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. These results indicate that dietary inulin supplementation improved laying performance, systemic health, yolk antioxidant capacity, and egg quality in laying hens, and these beneficial effects were associated with cecal microbiota shifts, increased microbial fermentation products, altered metabolic pathways, and improved antioxidant status.Keywords: Inulin, Antioxidant capacity, Egg quality, Intestinal microbiome and metabolome, Laying hen

Introduction

The growing consumer demand for eggs has made egg quality a key determinant influencing both market value and consumer acceptance (Erensoy et al., 2022). During the peak laying period, sustained nutrient partitioning and yolk-precursor synthesis place substantial metabolic demands on hens, making systemic health essential for maintaining egg production and egg quality. During this stage, multiple physiological systems are coordinately regulated to sustain key biological functions and egg formation (Xi et al., 2025). Consequently, hens are particularly susceptible to severe metabolic disorders (Gu et al., 2021). These disorders typically manifest as diminished antioxidant capacity (De Baets et al., 2024), impaired reproductive function (Huang et al., 2025), and heightened inflammatory responses (Wei et al., 2024). Consequently, these systemic disorders not only compromise hen health but also markedly reduce productivity and egg quality. Studies have demonstrated that pre-laying interventions in laying hens can significantly enhance egg production rates (Han et al., 2025), with the regulation of sex hormones playing a critical role in the egg-laying process (Yao et al., 2024). Therefore, nutritional interventions aimed at supporting systemic health during the peak laying period may provide a feasible strategy to sustain production efficiency while improving egg quality.

Among other functional feed additives, dietary fiber and prebiotics are widely known for their ability to promote growth performance and enhance the quality of animal-derived products (Ravanal et al., 2025). Meanwhile, they can improve antioxidant capacity (Wassie et al., 2022), satiety regulation (Bastings et al., 2023), and intestinal health of poultry through modulation of the gut microbiota composition and metabolites (Huang et al., 2024). Numerous studies have demonstrated the impact of dietary fiber on the intestinal microbiome and egg quality in hens (Hu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021).

Inulin, a plant-derived water-soluble dietary fiber composed of fructose polymers, has garnered significant attention for its prebiotic properties and health-promoting functions (Shoaib et al., 2016). As a prebiotic, inulin promotes the population of beneficial gut microbiota and enhances digestive system health (Qin et al., 2023b). The intestinal microbiota ferments inulin to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which can directly supply energy to intestinal epithelial cells and play a role in regulating immune cell differentiation (Sheng et al., 2023). SCFAs contribute to lowering intestinal pH, inhibiting the growth of pathogenic bacteria, maintaining the balance of the gut microbiota, activating antioxidant pathways, and reducing tissue damage (Ikeda et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2023b).

Eggs are an excellent source of essential fatty acids, which are vital for human nutrition. The antioxidant capacity of egg yolk is a crucial indicator for evaluating egg quality and freshness. Antioxidant-rich foods help alleviate oxidative stress in humans by scavenging reactive oxygen species, enhancing endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity, and lowering biomarkers of oxidative damage (Aiello et al., 2024; Salari-Moghaddam et al., 2022). In particular, the consumption of eggs rich in antioxidant substances by humans may also help alleviate oxidative stress (Nolasco et al., 2024). Similarly, the content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) in egg yolk is a key marker of nutritional quality. exhibit a range of physiological functions, such as modulating inflammatory immune responses, supporting cardiovascular health, and supporting neurocognitive development (Wu et al., 2020). Increased PUFA intake may help improve lipid metabolism disorders (Gui et al., 2024). Therefore, eggs rich in PUFA are considered high-value products, appealing to health-conscious consumers and providing economic benefits to producers. Because egg yolk PUFA are highly susceptible to lipid peroxidation during storage and processing, oxidative deterioration can result in off-flavors, discoloration, and losses in nutritional value (Luo et al., 2023). Given this susceptibility, yolk antioxidant capacity is a key indicator of quality stability. Accordingly, the antioxidant capacity of egg yolk is closely associated with essential fatty acids and serves as a key indicator of overall egg quality stability.

Traditional strategies to improve egg quality are often constrained by prolonged genetic improvement cycles and potential environmental impacts (Payandeh et al., 2025). In recent years, the intestinal microbiome and metabolites, acting as a crucial interface between host physiology and nutrient metabolism, have provided new insights and potential intervention targets for enhancing egg quality (Miao et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024). The cecum of poultry hosts a diverse and complex microbial community. Beyond its role in fiber degradation (Jebessa et al., 2022), the cecum is a key metabolic organ that contributes to host nutrient metabolism (Liu et al., 2021), immune regulation and barrier function (Tang et al., 2022), and endocrine signaling pathways (Roy et al., 2025). Intestinal microbiota generate numerous bioactive compounds through microbial metabolism, such as SCFAs, amino acid metabolites, precursors of neurotransmitters, and various bioactive peptides (Ikeda et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). These microbial metabolites may play a role in regulating nutrient absorption and metabolism, follicle development, and the synthesis of egg yolk components (Fusco et al., 2026; Ghasemi et al., 2022; Hood et al., 2023).

Therefore, this study hypothesized that dietary inulin supplementation modulates the gut microbiome and metabolome through its prebiotic effects on specific microbial communities. The resulting metabolites may regulate lipid, amino acid, and vitamin metabolism in laying hens, thereby contributing to improved egg quality, yolk fatty acid composition, and yolk antioxidant properties.

Materials and methods

Animal trials, design, and diet

A total of 288 Lohmann Brown hens at 15 weeks of age were selected for the experiment. None of the pullets had started laying at the beginning of the trial. In this study, the hens were randomly assigned to two experimental groups, with six replicates per group. Each replicate consisted of 24 hens housed in six cages, with four hens per cage (60 × 60 × 50 cm). To minimize potential tier-related environmental effects, the six cages within each replicate were evenly distributed across the three-tier ladder-type housing system, with two adjacent cages placed on each tier. The two treatments were as follows: the control group (CON) fed the basal diet (Table 1) and the inulin group (INU) fed the basal diet supplemented with 1.5 g/kg of inulin. The inulin obtained in this experiment was sourced from Shandong Shengyao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Catalog No: #20241021, Jining, China). According to our previous extraction method, inulin contained 95% fructan linked by 2,1 glycosidic bonds (degree of polymerization [DP] = 10-60) (Cui et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019). During the study, all hens had unrestricted access to clean water and were fed at two-hour intervals from 5:00 AM to 5:00 PM, ensuring that 5% of the feed remained in the feed trough. The light-cycling program was implemented using a 9:15 h pattern at 15 and 16 weeks of age. From 17 weeks onward, an 11:13 h pattern was introduced, with the duration of light increasing by 1 hour per week until 20 weeks of age. Starting at 21 weeks of age, the lighting regimen was maintained at a 16:8 h pattern until the end of this study. The temperature of the room was consistently maintained at 24–26 °C, with an average relative humidity of 50%.

Data record and sample collection

For growth and laying performance measurements, each replicate was considered the experimental unit. Daily laying number and egg weight were documented for each group. At the end of the feeding trial (at 35 weeks of age), all hens were fasted for 12 h. Twelve hens were randomly selected from each group and euthanized for sample collection. Blood samples were collected from the wing veins of each hen immediately before euthanasia by cervical dislocation. Serum was collected by centrifuging blood at 1,500 × g for 15 min and stored at −80 °C. Fresh cecal digesta samples were collected within 5 min after euthanasia, transferred into sterile 5 mL centrifuge tubes, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the samples were stored at −80 °C until further processing for DNA extraction, microbial community profiling, and metabolome analysis. Five eggs from each replicate were collected at the end of the experiment to determine egg quality, and one egg from each replicate was collected to determine the egg yolk fatty acid composition.

Measurement of laying performance and egg quality

The body weight (BW) of the laying hens was determined biweekly. Egg number and weight were determined daily to calculate laying rate, egg weight, and egg mass. The average of all cages of one replicate unit was used to calculate average daily gain (ADG) and average daily feed intake (ADFI). Feed efficiency was calculated by feed to egg ratio. Eggshell color was determined using the colorimeter (HAD-N, Beijing Heng Odd Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to determine the brightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) of eggshell. Eggshell thickness (the average of air cell, equator, and sharp end) was determined using the eggshell thickness measuring instrument (ETG1061, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). Eggshell strength was determined using an egg force analyzer (EFG0503, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). The longitudinal and transverse lengths of the eggs were measured using a vernier caliper, and the ratio of the longitudinal and transverse lengths of the eggs was calculated as the egg shape index. Haugh unit and yolk color score were determined using egg quality analyzer (EMT-5200, Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan).

Biochemical measures and ELISA assay

Serum concentrations of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-Cho), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-Cho), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), calcium (Ca), and phosphorus (P) were measured using an automatic biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Additionally, the serum concentrations of luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2), immunoglobulin A (IgA), and immunoglobulin G (IgG) were measured using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Jingmei Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). The serum and egg yolk levels of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and catalase (CAT) were measured using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). The egg yolk levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were measured using assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The egg yolk levels of lecithin (LEC) and TC were measured using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). The total phenolic content (TPC) of each egg yolk was measured by the Folin-Ciocalteu assay (Shang et al., 2020a).

Histological analysis

The jejunum and ileum were rinsed with 0.9% saline solution and fixed in 10% formalin for 48 h. The tissues were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned with a microtome (4.5 μm), stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined for intestinal morphology using a light microscope (Nikon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Intestinal morphology was evaluated using ImageJ software (version 6.0, NIH, Bethesda, USA) by measuring villus height, crypt depth, and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (V/C).

SCFA concentrations analysis

Cecal SCFA concentrations were measured by high-performance gas chromatography (FULI, Zhejiang, China). Briefly, cecal contents (1 g) were homogenized in 5 mL of 250 mg/g metaphosphoric acid using a vortex mixer for 15 min and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters and subsequently injected into an HP UF-FFAP-M3025 capillary column. A flame ionization detector was used to quantify SCFA contents. The oven temperature was set at 140 °C. The injector and detector temperatures were set at 250 and 260 °C, respectively. The nitrogen flow was 50 mL/min, and this setting was maintained for 12 min. The acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, and isovaleric acid peaks in a standard solution (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were analyzed using the same parameters. The peaks of individual SCFAs in each cecal sample were analyzed under consistent parameters. The molar concentration of short-chain fatty acids was determined by calculating the ratio of the peak area of the individual SCFAs to the peak area of the standard solution, then multiplying this ratio by the known concentration of the standard solution.

Determination of fatty acid profile in egg yolk

Total lipids were extracted from egg yolk samples using a mixture of chloroform and methanol in a 2:1 (v/v) ratio. The egg yolk fatty acid analysis was performed using an Agilent gas chromatography system (Agilent 7820, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a CP-Sil 88 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm, Agilent, USA). A 1 µL sample was injected with a split ratio of 10:1, and high-purity helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature program was initiated at 100 °C and held for 5.0 minutes, followed by a ramp of 4 °C/min to 240 °C, where it was held for an additional 15 minutes. Based on the gas chromatography peaks, the total amounts of saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and PUFA in egg yolk were determined. The total unsaturated fatty acids (USFA) were calculated as the sum of MUFA and PUFA.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

Total DNA was extracted from the cecal contents using the extraction kit (Omega, Georgia, USA). The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was subjected to PCR amplification using primers 341F (CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG) and 806R (GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT). PCR products were purified using magnetic bead purification. Quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms based on effective library concentration and the required data output.

Paired-end reads were allocated to corresponding samples according to their unique barcodes, followed by truncation to eliminate the barcode and primer regions. Subsequently, paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (V1.2.11, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/) (Magoč and Salzberg, 2011). Quality filtering on the raw tags was performed using the fastp (Version 0.23.1) software to obtain high-quality clean tags (Bokulich et al., 2013). The previously obtained effective tags were subjected to denoising through the use of DADA2 or the deblur module within QIIME2 software, resulting in the generation of initial Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Subsequently, QIIME2 was employed to calculate alpha diversity indices, including Shannon and Chao1. The microbial community similarity across various samples was evaluated through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), utilizing the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity as the basis for comparison. The Heatmap Illustrator software (Version 1.0.3.7) was employed to generate bar charts and heatmaps. Significant differences in microbiota composition across treatment groups at each taxonomic level were determined through independent-samples t-tests, followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) assessment.

Metabolomics Analyses

The cecal content metabolome was analyzed by Novogene Corporation Ltd., Beijing, China, using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Cecal contents (100 mg) were separately homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and the resulting homogenate was resuspended in prechilled 80% methanol by vigorous vortexing. Following a 5-minute incubation on ice, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g and 4 °C for 20 minutes. Part of the supernatant was then diluted with LC-MS grade water to obtain a final methanol concentration of 53%. Afterward, the samples were transferred into new Eppendorf tubes and subjected to another round of centrifugation under the same conditions. The final supernatant was then collected and introduced into the LC-MS/MS system for subsequent analysis (Want et al., 2013). The UHPLC-MS/MS experiments were carried out using a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher, Bremen, Germany) connected to either an Orbitrap Q Exactive™ HF or an Orbitrap Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The samples were introduced into a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) and separated using a 12-minute linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. For both positive and negative ionization modes, the mobile phases consisted of eluent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and eluent B (methanol). The gradient program was as follows: 2% B for 1.5 minutes, increased to 85% B over 3 minutes, further increased to 100% B by 10 minutes, decreased back to 2% B at 10.1 minutes, and maintained at 2% B until the end of the run at 12 minutes. The Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer was used in both positive and negative ion modes, with the following parameters: spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi, auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 L/min, S-lens RF level of 60, and auxiliary gas heater temperature of 350 °C. The detected metabolites were identified and categorized utilizing the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. To investigate the global metabolic profiles among the two experimental groups, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied. Additionally, metabolic enrichment and pathway analyses were carried out using the MetaboAnalyst web platform, Version 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/ModuleView.xhtml).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., USA) by independent-samples t-tests to compare the means between the two groups. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and variance homogeneity using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics were used to report the standard errors of the mean (SEM). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the correlations between key differential microbes, differential metabolites, sex hormones, SCFAs, yolk antioxidants, and yolk fatty acids. For the correlation analyses, P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method, and FDR adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Meanwhile, partial-least-squares path modeling (PLS-PM) was performed to quantify the underlying mechanism of dietary inulin supplementation on gut microbes, antioxidant capacity, and egg quality using R v4.2.2 (“PLSPM” package).

Results

Effect of dietary inulin on the growth and laying performance

As shown in Fig. 1A, during this study, the body weight of the laying hens showed no significant difference between the CON and INU groups (P > 0.05). Additionally, dietary inulin supplementation significantly decreased ADFI in hens (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B). Compared with the CON group, the egg production rate in the INU group significantly increased from 21 weeks of age (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C), and the peak egg-laying period occurred at 25 weeks of age. With the dietary inulin supplementation, the feed to egg ratio significantly decreased in the INU group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1D).

Effects of dietary inulin on serum sex hormones, immune factors, and antioxidant capacity

As shown in Fig. 2A, dietary inulin significantly decreased serum TG and TC levels while concurrently elevating HDL-Cho level compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, dietary supplementation with inulin significantly increased the serum IgA and IgG levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 2B.1 and B.2). In addition, as shown in Fig. 2C.1-C.3, the concentrations of serum sex hormones (LH, FSH, and E2) significantly increased in the INU group with dietary inulin supplementation (P < 0.001). As shown in Fig. 2D.1-D.3, the supplementation of inulin increased serum levels of ALP and Ca (P < 0.05), with a tendency toward elevated P levels (P = 0.158). Additionally, dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased serum levels of T-AOC and CAT (P < 0.05, Fig. 2E.1 and E.2).

Effects of inulin on egg yolk fatty acid composition

As shown in Fig. 5A, the dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased the concentrations of USFA (unsaturated fatty acids), MUFA, and PUFA in egg yolk compared with the CON group (P < 0.01), whereas there was no significant difference in SFA between the CON and INU groups. Furthermore, the compositions of fatty acids in egg yolk showed a significant difference between the CON and INU groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5B). In detail, as shown in Fig. 5C, there were no significant differences in the SFA compositions between the CON and INU groups. As shown in Fig. 5D, for the MUFA, the dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased the level of oleic acid (C18:1n9c) in the INU group compared to the CON group (P < 0.05), while heptadecenoic acid (C17:1) and eicosenoic acid (C20:1) showed increasing trends (P = 0.073 and P = 0.056). As shown in Fig. 5E, for the PUFA, inulin supplementation significantly increased the levels of linoleic acid (C18:2n6c), gamma-linolenic acid (C18:3n6), alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3n3), and dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (C20:3n6) in the INU group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). These findings illustrate the beneficial impacts of inulin on egg quality, notably through the augmentation of MUFA and PUFA levels in egg yolks.

Dietary inulin supplementation showed a tendency to increase intestinal length (P = 0.061, Fig. 6A). Cecal pH was significantly lower in the INU group (6.79 ± 0.066) than in the CON group (7.09 ± 0.056) (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B). Furthermore, dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased the concentration of acetic acid, butyric acid, and isobutyric acid (P < 0.01, Fig. 6F). The villus height and crypt depth of the jejunum, as well as the V/C ratio, were significantly higher in the INU group (P < 0.001, Fig. 6D.1-D.2). Profile and characteristics of the intestinal microbial community of hens

A total of 2161 (CON) and 2369 (INU) ASVs were detected in the cecal content samples. As the beta diversity index, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances demonstrated significant differences (P < 0.05) in cecum microbiota composition between the CON and INU groups (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, dietary inulin supplementation significantly decreased the alpha diversity of Chao1 and Shannon indices (P < 0.05). The composition of cecal microbiota at different levels was further analyzed. Dietary inulin supplementation significantly decreased the Firmicutes to Bacteroidota ratio (P < 0.05). Cecal microbiota with a relative abundance greater than 0.5% at the phylum, family, and genus levels were compared and analyzed (Supplement Table S1). At the phylum level (Fig. 7E.1), Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were the predominant bacterial phyla, collectively comprising nearly 90% of the total microbial population, in two distinct groups. Differential cecal microbiota of hens

As shown in Fig. 8A.1–A.3, random forest analysis was conducted to assess the variation in microbiota composition between the CON and INU groups based on the importance of features for model accuracy. A threshold of mean decrease in accuracy > 3 was applied. At phylum level, the Fusobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Euryarchaeota were identified (Fig. 8A.1). At family level, the Fusobacteriaceae, Methanobacteriaceae, Rikenellaceae, Lactobacillaceae, F082, and Bacteroidaceae were identified (Fig. 8A.2). (Fig. 8B-D). At phylum level, dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased relative abundances of Bacteroidota and Campylobacterota (P < 0.01), while decreasing those of Euryarchaeota, Synergistota, and Fusobacteriota (P < 0.05). At family level, dietary inulin supplementation significantly increased relative abundances of Bacteroidaceae and F082 (P < 0.01). In contrast, relative abundances of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Methanobrevibacter, Fournierella, and Fusobacterium were significantly decreased by dietary inulin supplementation (P < 0.05). Profile of cecum metabolites and enrichment of metabolic pathways

A total of 2696 cecum metabolites (including both positive and negative ions) were detected in the CON and INU groups. The metabolite profiles were distinctly separated using OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 9A). Differential cecal metabolites were identified by comparing INU vs. CON, based on fold change thresholds (FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.667), variable importance in projection (VIP > 1), and P-value < 0.05. A total of 198 differential metabolites were detected between INU and CON, comprising 103 up-regulated and 95 down-regulated metabolites (Fig. 9B, Table S2). To further characterize pathways associated with these differential metabolites, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on the differential metabolites to identify enriched metabolic pathways (Fig. 9C). Four pathways were significantly enriched (P < 0.05), including histidine metabolism, beta-alanine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and phosphonate and phosphinate metabolism. Subsequently, the differential metabolites were analyzed using the KEGG database for G. gallus (chicken). Pathway impact values were calculated, and pathways with impact > 0.05 were selected (Fig. 9D). In the comparison between the INU and CON groups, the key impacted metabolic pathways included histidine metabolism, biotin metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, one-carbon pool by folate, and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (pathway impact > 0.05). As shown in Fig. 9E, dietary inulin supplementation increased cecal levels of 4,6-Dihydroxyquinoline, vitamin B5, beta-l-Fucose 1-phosphate, (2-Aminoethyl)phosphonic acid, indoleacetic acid, and PE(16:1(9Z)/P-18:0) while simultaneously decreasing the levels of anserine, l-carnitine, palmitoylcarnitine, dimethylglycine, l-histidine, and biotin in the cecum contents.

Potential mechanisms by which dietary inulin regulates yolk antioxidant capacity and fatty acid composition

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations among differential cecal microbes, differential metabolites, sex hormones, SCFAs, yolk antioxidant indices, and yolk fatty acid composition (Fig. 10A and B). After FDR correction, microbial genera enriched in the INU group showed positive associations with several cecal metabolites, sex hormones, SCFAs, yolk antioxidant indices, and USFA-related fatty acid indices, whereas genera enriched in the CON group showed largely opposite correlation patterns. In addition, yolk fatty acid indices were positively associated with SCFAs and antioxidant indices, while MDA showed negative associations with most increased fatty acid indices. Furthermore, PLS-PM was employed to investigate the potential mechanisms by which dietary inulin supplementation regulates cecal microbes and metabolites, SCFAs, serum and yolk antioxidant capacity, sex hormones, and yolk fatty acids (Fig. 10C). This model indicated a potential association linking microbial genera increased by inulin supplementation with cecal metabolites, microbial fermentation products, antioxidant indices, endocrine responses, and yolk fatty acid composition.

Discussion

The present study provides an integrated microbiome–metabolome perspective on how dietary inulin may improve physiological status and egg quality in laying hens. Inulin may improve physiological status and egg quality by coordinating cecal microbial composition, fermentation related metabolites, antioxidant homeostasis, and yolk lipid profile. Using an integrated microbiome-metabolome approach, this study investigated the effects of dietary inulin supplementation on the gut microbiome and metabolome of laying hens, as well as their association with improved egg quality, thereby providing a scientific basis for the application of inulin in laying hen production.

Effects on laying performance and sex hormone regulation

Laying performance is a key determinant of production efficiency, as it directly influences economic returns through egg production rate, feed conversion efficiency, and egg quality. The improvement in feed efficiency may be partly related to the fermentability of inulin and its potential effects on satiety signaling and nutrient utilization (Xu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). Conversely, egg production rate and serum sex hormone levels (FSH, LH, and E2) increased with the addition of inulin. Sex hormones play a critical role in regulating ovarian function and influencing the rate of egg production (Prastiya et al., 2022). Inulin may be associated with abundance of Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium, which may in turn influence glycine and serine metabolism and folate metabolism, thereby improving the metabolic environment for sex hormone synthesis (Li et al., 2024b). Faecalibacterium has been associated with improved ovarian structure, optimized sex hormone levels, and altered metabolic processes, thereby alleviating ovarian dysfunction (Bao et al., 2022). Prevotellaceae can participate in the steroid hormone biosynthetic pathway, thereby exerting an indirect regulatory influence on sex hormone levels (Li et al., 2022a). Collectively, these results suggest that the effects of inulin on laying performance may involve coordinated changes in satiety regulation, microbial metabolism, and sex hormone regulation.

Effects on eggshell pigmentation and mineralization

Improving eggshell quality is crucial for reducing egg breakage rates and enhancing economic returns. The color and luster of eggshells influence consumers’ purchasing intentions, particularly concerning a marked preference for brown eggs (Sass et al., 2021). Protoporphyrin IX is the primary pigment responsible for the brown coloration of eggshells, histidine and glycine are involved in the regulation of protoporphyrin IX synthesis (Key et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022). Dietary inulin supplementation was found to regulate histidine and glycine metabolism, potentially enhancing the supply of protoporphyrin IX precursors. This metabolic shift may contribute to the observed increase in eggshell redness and decrease in lightness. In addition, changes in intestinal fermentation may provide a physiological basis for eggshell mineralization. Butyrate may enhance eggshell quality by indirectly influencing calcium metabolism through the regulation of calcium-binding proteins, intestinal environment, and gut microbiota (Bakirhan and Karabudak, 2023; Shang et al., 2020b). Overall, inulin may influence eggshell pigmentation and mineralization through gut microbiota associated histidine and glycine metabolism.

Antioxidant mechanisms and yolk fatty acid composition

The moderate improvement in systemic antioxidant status and immune function may contribute to key physiological mechanisms that maintain intestinal homeostasis, thereby enhancing disease resistance and supporting high egg production in laying hens (Brouklogiannis et al., 2023). Similarly, agricultural products with high antioxidant capacity generally have higher market value and quality potential due to their functional ingredients and health benefits (Zúñiga-López et al., 2021). Serum antioxidant substances can be directly transferred into the yolk (Yang et al., 2020). The improvement in antioxidant ability may be linked to microbial fermentation and tryptophan derived metabolites. One plausible mechanism is that inulin shifts tryptophan metabolism toward indole metabolite production, which strengthens intestinal barrier function, and activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, thereby attenuating inflammation and enhancing antioxidant capacity (Kou et al., 2024; Shang et al., 2020a). Inulin derived fermentation products may also support epithelial energy metabolism and immune function (Sheng et al., 2023). Furthermore, the enhanced antioxidant capacity reduces the oxidative loss of PUFA, thereby protecting the stability of highly unsaturated fatty acids (such as alpha-linolenic acid) (Bai et al., 2023; Shahid et al., 2022). Consequently, the systemic antioxidant status influences the fatty acid composition within the yolk. Additionally, SCFAs not only serve as an essential energy source for intestinal epithelial cells but also participate in the synthesis and metabolism of unsaturated fatty acids (Hu et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023a). Intestinal microbiota and their metabolites (such as SCFAs, bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives) are suggested to indirectly modulate egg quality (Dai et al., 2022). Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that microbial fermentation and tryptophan derived metabolites may connect intestinal microbiota with redox regulation and yolk lipid metabolism.

Regulation of lipid metabolism and yolk nutrient deposition

Furthermore, SCFAs can enhance hepatic lipid synthesis and transport through the regulation of host metabolic pathways (Yang et al., 2023b). The lipid related changes observed in serum and yolk. These changes may alleviate lipid accumulation, and the elevation of HDL-Cho may facilitate reverse cholesterol transport, thereby decreasing cholesterol deposition in the yolk (Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021). Meanwhile, alterations in microbial-mediated metabolic pathways may play a role in modulating host lipid metabolism (Yang et al., 2023c). As a prebiotic, inulin may enhance phosphatidylcholine synthesis by modulating the gut microbiota and promoting the glycerophosphatidylcholine metabolic pathway, thereby increasing the amount of lecithin transported to the yolk (Li et al., 2024a). Simultaneously, the enrichment of the tryptophan metabolic pathway following inulin intervention may synergistically enhance phospholipid synthesis in coordination with the glycerolipid metabolic pathway (Yang et al., 2023c). In summary, inulin modulated host lipid metabolism through intestinal SCFAs. It reduced serum TC level by promoting reverse cholesterol transport, thereby inhibiting cholesterol accumulation in egg yolk. Therefore, host lipid metabolism is closely linked to yolk nutrient composition. Moreover, glycerophospholipid and tryptophan related pathways may be associated with yolk lecithin deposition and lipid distribution.

Effects on intestinal morphology and microbial fermentation

As a fermentable dietary fiber, inulin can reshape the cecal environment (Tawfick et al., 2022). The intestinal morphological changes observed in this study are consistent with enhanced microbial fermentation (Aoki et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023c). SCFAs are a primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells, promoting their proliferation and differentiation, which contributes to increased villus height and crypt depth (Corrêa et al., 2023). Furthermore, inulin-associated shifts in tryptophan metabolism may increase indoleacetic acid; together with SCFAs, these metabolites can support barrier function and villus development (Yang et al., 2023c). These metabolites act directly on the gastrointestinal tract and subsequently regulate host metabolic physiology (Muller et al., 2021). The intestine is the primary site for nutrient digestion, and its structural integrity and functional state play a crucial role in determining the efficiency of nutrient absorption (Zhou et al., 2025a). Improvements in villus morphology may enhance the expression of nutrient transporters, thereby promoting the absorption of amino acids (Wang et al., 2022). In summary, the improvement in intestinal health may provide a physiological basis for improved nutrient utilization and egg quality.

Altered cecal metabolic pathways associated with inulin supplementation

Histidine metabolism may be associated with eggshell pigmentation and redox regulation through its involvement in amino acid metabolism. In terms of lipid metabolism, histidine metabolism primarily suppresses fatty acid synthesis and mitigates lipid accumulation by down-regulating key genes within the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling pathway (Liang et al., 2022). Meanwhile, histidine may ameliorate nonalcoholic fatty liver disease through modulation of the intestinal microbiota, reduction of lipogenesis, and regulation of lipid metabolism pathways, including sphingolipid metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Quesada-Vázquez et al., 2023). Additionally, histidine has been reported to affect cellular stress responses, including NF-κB signaling and antioxidant defenses (SOD and GSH expression), accompanied by reduced MDA level (Bai et al., 2018). Furthermore, histidine supplementation is associated with reduced oxidative stress markers and enhanced overall antioxidant status in obesity models (Yang et al., 2023a).

Dietary fiber deficiency can reduce gut microbiota–derived B vitamins, whereas inulin supplementation has been reported to restore microbial B-vitamin pools (Grant et al., 2024). Because biotin is a member of the B-vitamin family, inulin may modulate biotin metabolism by reshaping microbial community functions. Furthermore, biotin suppresses the activity of acetyl-CoA carboxylase through the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), thereby reducing fatty acid synthesis (Moreno-Méndez et al., 2019). Biotin-activated AMPK may not only suppress lipid synthesis but also enhance the expression of antioxidant genes by activating downstream signaling pathways, thereby exerting a synergistic protective effect (Sghaier et al., 2019).

Tryptophan derived metabolites, particularly indole related compounds, may contribute to lipid metabolism and redox regulation through microbiota associated signaling pathways. Indole derivatives produced from tryptophan metabolism can modulate microbial community structure and function, thereby influencing host intestinal lipid metabolic pathways, including glycerophospholipid and fatty acid metabolism (Zhao et al., 2025). Modulating tryptophan metabolism may alleviate oxidative stress by improving lipid metabolic disorders, such as reducing hepatic lipid accumulation (Xue et al., 2023). Specifically, dietary supplementation with tryptophan-related compounds in laying hens enhanced yolk antioxidant capacity and increased yolk concentrations of tryptophan and tyrosine, suggesting that tryptophan-derived metabolites may contribute to antioxidant deposition in the yolk (Zhou et al., 2025b).

Integrated associations and study limitations

To integrate these findings, Spearman correlation analysis and PLS-PM were used to assess potential links among cecal microbial remodeling, cecal metabolic profiles, host physiological responses, and yolk fatty acid composition. The gut microbiota is a key mediator regulating both egg production performance and quality (Zhang et al., 2023), and the early shaping of gut microbiota plays a crucial role in subsequent production performance (Li et al., 2022b). Adequate dietary fiber source that supports gastrointestinal health is closely associated with maintaining a balanced microbiome (Han et al., 2023). In conclusion, dietary inulin supplementation may modulate the gut microbiota and influenced the composition of metabolites in intestinal metabolic pathways and SCFAs, thereby enhancing systemic and yolk antioxidant capacity and ultimately modulating yolk fatty acid composition. However, several limitations warrant further investigation: (1) the molecular mechanisms linking inulin-driven microbiota changes to systemic lipid metabolism and yolk fatty acid/cholesterol deposition; (2) whether these microbial associations directly regulate host hormone and antioxidant responses; and (3) the key host targets along the gut–liver–ovary axis through which microbial metabolites influence endocrine function and oxidative homeostasis during peak lay.

Conclusions

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with 1.5 g/kg inulin improved laying performance, systemic health, and egg quality in laying hens. More importantly, the integrated microbiome–metabolome analysis revealed that these effects were associated with coordinated changes in cecal microbial composition, microbial fermentation products, and cecal metabolic profiles, suggesting a potential gut-derived regulatory pathway linking inulin supplementation to antioxidant status and yolk lipid composition. These findings highlight microbiome–metabolome interactions as a novel component of the nutritional response to inulin and support its practical application as a microbiome-targeted feed additive for improving egg production and egg quality in poultry production.
