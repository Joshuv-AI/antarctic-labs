Determination of background concentrations and risk-screening values for elements in oasis soils in the arid zone of the Tibetan Plateau using multifractal models
Authors: Ying Ma, Yao Niu, Yan Bai, Siqi Han, Jie Luo, Ling Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oases on the Tibetan Plateau serve as vital buffers in the arid alpine zone, sustaining ecological security under high-altitude conditions; however, their soil quality remains underexplored. This study addresses the limitations of traditional soil assessment methods—which lack defined thresholds and are poorly adapted to spatial heterogeneity—by applying multifractal analyses to determine risk-screening values and background concentrations for 20 soil elements in the Delingha-Ulan Oasis, northeastern Tibetan Plateau, using 1517 samples. The model was validated by comparing the screening values of six regulated elements (e.g., Cd, Hg, As, Pb, Cu, Zn) with the corresponding national standards, enabling robust evaluation of unregulated elements (e.g., B, Ge, Se). Results reveal multifractal patterns, with nine elements (e.g., Cd, I) showing heavy-tailed distribution indicative of localized contamination risks, and Hg, N, and P reflecting subtle anthropogenic impacts from wolfberry farming and pastoral activities. Notably, iodine exhibits extreme enrichment, posing significant ecological threats. This framework fills a critical gap in alpine oasis soil benchmarks, enhancing pollution identification and ecological management in fragile plateau ecosystems. These findings offer a scalable tool for sustainable soil protection in arid, high-altitude regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57692-y.

Introduction

Oases, as small-scale, non-zonal landscapes with high primary productivity, arise through the interplay of natural runoff and human activities within arid desert contexts1,2. The Tibetan Plateau, dubbed the “Water Tower of Asia”, has oases that serve as critical water sources and ecological buffers in the arid alpine zone, supporting human livelihoods, socio-economic stability, and regional water cycles3. These systems, shaped by extreme high-altitude and cold-arid conditions, regulate hydrology through wetlands and grasslands, mitigate wind and sand erosion through vegetation, and provide adaptive buffers for biodiversity against environmental shifts4,5. As a vital component of these fragile ecosystems, soil quality underpins their stability and sustainability, directly influencing ecological security in this unique highland oasis ecosystem.

Research on oasis soils of the Tibetan Plateau remains limited, with existing studies primarily focusing on heavy metal contamination in agricultural or urban-adjacent zones, often overlooking the distinct pastoral and arid-alpine settings6,7. The effects of some potentially toxic elements such as Mo and Ge have also been neglected, especially Mo, which exhibits an HQ index between 1.1 and 5.9 and poses a high risk to human health8. Inorganic Ge, which is highly toxic and readily enriched in humans and animals9, may lead to kidney damage. Soil serves as a key medium for material cycling and energy conversion, yet its vulnerability to contamination, exacerbated by the plateau’s harsh climate and slow recovery capacity, poses significant risks to ecosystem health10,11. Traditional assessment methods, such as the geoaccumulation index and single-factor pollution index, rely on comparing elemental concentrations to national standards like GB15618-2018 or regional background values12,13. However, these approaches face dual limitations in plateau oases: first, GB15618-2018, tailored for agricultural land, covers only eight heavy metals (e.g., Cd, Hg, Pb, As, Cr, Cu, Zn, Ni), lacking thresholds for other critical elements (e.g., B, Ge, N, P) relevant to oasis ecology and human health; second, the linear assumptions of these methods fail to capture the multi-scale spatial heterogeneity driven by diverse soil matrices, topography, and sparse human activities like grazing14,15. In addition, the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau has a cold, arid climate, and its soil parent materials consist primarily of glacial till, slope deposits, and lacustrine sediments; in contrast, the eastern regions—where national standards are primarily calibrated—have a warm, humid climate, with parent materials consisting mainly of river alluvium and loess. The soil in these areas is deep, uniform in texture, and has a long history of agricultural cultivation. This inadequacy may introduce systematic bias into pollution identification and misguides ecological restoration, threatening the fragile alpine environment.

Multifractal analysis offers a robust alternative by quantifying the nonlinear, multi-scale variability of soil element distributions through parameters like the generalized dimension spectrum D(q) and singularity index f(α)16,17. This approach excels in distinguishing natural baselines from anomalies in heterogeneous systems, overcoming the shortcomings of fixed-threshold methods. Targeting the Delingha-Ulan Oasis on the northeastern Tibetan Plateau, this study employs multifractal analyses to determine background and risk assessment values for 20 elements—including six regulated heavy metals (e.g., Cd, Hg, As, Pb, Cu, Zn) and 14 unregulated elements (e.g., N, P, B, Ge, Al, F, Fe, I, K, Mn, Mo, Se, Sr, Si)—based on 1,517 soil samples. By validating regulated element thresholds against national standards and extending assessments to uncharted elements, this framework fills a critical gap in soil environmental benchmarks for alpine oases. It provides a scientific basis for early pollution detection and precise ecological management, safeguarding the sustainability of these vital arid-zone ecosystems.

Study area and methodology

Overview of the study area

This study area (Fig. S1) lies between 36° 55′–38° 22′ N and 96° 15′–98° 15′ E. and is under the jurisdiction of Haixi Mongol and Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture. The region primarily lies within the Qin-Qikun stratigraphic zone, with stratigraphic distributions ranging from the Early Proterozoic to the Quaternary periods. Potential evaporation varies between 2074.1 and 2439.4 mm. Sampling and analysis of soil data

Sampling points were laid out using officially published 1:50,000 topographic maps and Second Land Survey land use map sheets as base maps. A 0.5 km × 0.5 km square grid was used as the sampling framework, with points collected at the center of each grid to avoid periods of fertilizer application, visible point-source pollution, and areas with recent soil accumulation. A total of 1517 samples were collected, all located at least 100 m from major highways and railroads. The sampling density of surface soil samples was 4.25 points/km2, and the sampling depth ranged from 0 to 20 cm, ensuring both representativeness and uniformity. Visible impurities were removed during sampling, and the original weight of the sample exceeded 1000 g. Soil samples were collected from various land types within the oasis areas of the Tibetan Plateau. The majority of sampling sites were located on public or unowned lands where no specific permissions were required. For samples collected from privately owned agricultural land, verbal consent was obtained from the landowners prior to sampling, following a full explanation of the study objectives. All sampling activities were non-invasive, ensured minimal site disturbance, and complied with local institutional and environmental regulations for scientific research.

Twenty indicators were analyzed and measured, including Al, As, B, Cd, Cu, F, Ge, Hg, I, K, Mn, Mo, N, P, Pb, Se, Si, Sr, Fe, and Zn, using national standard methods and relevant industry-standard analytical procedures. The specific methods and detection limits for each element are detailed in the supplementary materials. The accuracy and precision of the analysis were ensured using a multi-objective analytical method support program. Each batch of 50 coded soil samples included four certified national standard reference materials (GSS-4, GSS-8, GSS-9, and GSS-14) and four certified effective-state soil standards (GBW07412a, GBW07412a, GBW07413a, GBW07414a, and GBW07460) for quality control. All results met the requirements of Specification DZ/T0258-2014, with a 100% pass rate across all elements.

Research methods

Multifractal analysis methods

Multifractal analysis effectively captures the spatial heterogeneity of soil element distributions, making it particularly suited to the Delingha-Ulan Oasis’s complex farm-pastoral-mineral systems. It has been applied in geochemistry to analyze complex structures and anomalous patterns in geologic data that indicate spatial heterogeneity of elements. For instance, the multifractal moving average interpolation method enhances the treatment of localized high concentrations and smooths geochemical data more effectively18. The multifractal model is also used to effectively for extract mineralogenic geochemical anomalies, supporting subsequent mineral exploration19. It has been shown that geochemical backgrounds and anomalies follow distinct power-law distributions, resulting in multifractal patterns20, and that the spatial distributions of the elements exhibit multifractal characteristics21. Multifractal distributions typically align with lognormal models. Therefore, for distinguishing elemental background values, the multifractal technique identifies cut-off points between background and anomalous values using log–log plots, enabling more precise separation20,22.

In this study, the surface soils of the Delingha-Ulan Oasis were sampled using a grid sampling, and the elemental contents of the soil samples were interpolated using the multifractal technique to form geochemical gridded data covering the district. The fractal dimension D

Fractal dimension is an important parameter used to describe the trend of the spatial distribution density of a variable, and many studies have been conducted to relate fractal dimension to elemental content; for instance, in the alluvial fan area downstream of the Yangshuo Pb–Zn mine, it was found that fractal dimension can be used to indicate heavy metal content when heavy metals migrate due to grain size migration23; and in the western Tianshan region of Xinjiang, it was used to interpret spatial element distribution patterns24. Therefore, in this study, to quantitatively describe the distribution of elemental concentration over various spatial scales, the fractal dimension is employed; the smaller the value, the higher the degree of spatial clustering of elemental content, the more uneven the distribution, and the greater the tendency of enrichment and mineralization25. Conversely, the larger the value, the more dispersed the high-concentration zones. The estimation of subdimensions D was performed by linear regression using the least squares method. Establishment of background and risk screening values

In this study, soil samples were collected from several agricultural sites in the Delingha–Ulan region and analyzed for the contents of 20 elements (Al, As, B, Cd, Cu, F, Ge, Hg, I, K, Mn, Mo, N, P, Pb, Se, Si, Sr, Fe, and Zn). A double logarithmic scatter plot was plotted using Origin 2022 software, with elemental content on the horizontal axis and cumulative frequency on the vertical axis. The double logarithmic scatter plots of the 20 elements in the region are shown in Fig. 2. If the projected points of elemental content and counts show a continuous curved distribution, it indicates that it conforms to a multifractal distribution rather than a linear distribution. Depending on the trend, two or three approximate straight-line segments can be fitted. The first straight line segment is relatively flat, approximating a horizontal line, reflecting the low-value area or background zone, whereas the second straight line segment is steeper and represents the high-value area or anomalous area26. In some cases, a third straight line segment may appear, corresponding to extremely high-value anomalies associated with local contamination or deep-source superimposed mineralization27. Specifically, when two straight line segments are fitted, the element content at the intersection of the first and second straight line segments is defined as the background value26, and the content at the midpoint of the second straight line segment is taken as the risk-screening value. If multiple consecutive and nearly horizontal points (i.e., “star trails”) appear in the scatter plot, indicating the presence of extremely high-value points, this can be considered evidence of local contamination. In such cases, the risk-screening value can be determined by looking at the element content at the beginning of the star trail. When three fitted straight-line segments are drawn, the backdrop value can be thought of as the element content that corresponds to the inflection point between the first and second straight line segments, and the element content corresponding to the intersection of the second and third straight line segments can be regarded as the risk-screening value28. Based on this method, the background values and risk-screening values for each element in the study area were determined, as shown in Table 2.

Analysis of background and risk assessment values for soil elements

The double logarithmic scatter plot of the 20 elements in the region is shown in Fig. 2, and the risk screening and background values for every element in the research area, calculated from the double logarithmic scatter plot, are shown in Table 2. From Fig. 2, we can observe the phenomenon of heavy-tailed distribution for some elements, such as B, Cd, Cu, Ge, I, Mo, N, Pb, Se, Sr, and nine other elements. Among these, Cd and Mo have more heavy-tailed distribution, suggesting that compared with other elements, these two elements have higher concentration in the soil, and some areas may have a higher risk of pollution. When comparing the risk screening values of six elements, including As, Cd, Cu, Hg, Pb, and Zn, with the standard values in Table 2, it is found that the risk screening values of As, Cu, Hg, Pb, and Zn are smaller than the standard values. This may be because the national standard (GB15618-2018) is based on a nationwide universal standard that ignores local spatial variability and does not consider the differences in soil components and physicochemical properties in different regions31. It may also be because the Tibetan Plateau region is vast and sparsely populated, and the weathering input of the geological parent rocks is the primary source of the heavy metal enrichment, with fewer anthropogenic pollution sources. The background value for molybdenum is higher than the average, likely because, in the multifractal rules we used, the background value is defined as the boundary between the background segment and the positive anomaly segment (i.e., the upper limit of the background). This value is naturally higher than the concentration in most samples within the background segment. At the same time, the arithmetic mean of all samples is pulled down by the inclusion of low values from negative anomalies, resulting in a background value that exceeds the average. Observation of Table 2 shows that the mean values of the 20 elements exceeded the background values. The background values of the 20 elements are, in descending order, Cd, Se, Hg, Ge, Mo, I, K, Fe, As, Al, Pb, Cu, B, Si, Zn, Sr, F, P, Mn, and N. Among these, the high background values of the five elements Sr, F, P, Mn, and N are worthy of attention. At present, there are several areas where Paleozoic strata are exposed in the study area, and siliciclastic rocks in the Cambrian-Ordovician stratigraphic clastic rock group contain Fe–Mn strips and form Mn layers, while most of the study area is located in the Pb–Zn–Mn–Cr–Au-white mica metallogenic belt at the northern edge of Chai Damu; therefore, the high background of Mn (616.60 mg/kg) may be influenced by weathering of Cambrian-Ordovician Mn-rich strata and mining activities. The high background of N (741.31 mg/kg) and P (562.34 mg/kg) may be related to agricultural fertilization due to the strong development of crop production in the study area, for instance, the cultivation area of crops such as wolfberry, fruits, and vegetables has been increasing year by year. Furthermore, the land use types in the study area are predominantly natural grasslands and irrigated farmland (Fig. S2). This dual natural-anthropogenic signature, set against the oasis’s fragile high-altitude ecosystem, underscores the need for tailored thresholds to protect this critical arid-zone buffer.

The background values for Cu (22.39 mg/kg), As (10.96 mg/kg), and Zn (61.66 mg/kg) obtained in this study were lower than the background values for soils in the irrigated agricultural areas of the Gansu desert oases (Cu: 24.1 mg/kg; As:12.6 mg/kg; Zn: 68.5 mg/kg)33, However, the background level of mercury (Hg) in this study (0.01 mg/kg) is comparable to that in the Korla Oasis (Hg: 0.017 mg/kg)34. At the same time, the background concentration of zinc (61.66 mg/kg) in this study area is significantly lower than that in other parts of the Tibetan Plateau35. The above comparison confirms that the soil heavy metal background values obtained using the methodology employed in this study are consistent with regional geochemical patterns. Specifically, the background values for Cu, As, and Zn are slightly lower than those in the neighboring Gansu desert oasis region, while the background value for Hg is on the same order of magnitude as that in the Korla oasis region. The “similar or slightly lower” pattern aligns with the natural gradient of regional soil element distribution, showing no systematic deviation in the results from this method. The slightly lower background values of elements like Cu, As, and Zn compared to other regions may result from differences in soil-forming parent materials. For instance, weathered materials from basic and carbonate rocks are typically rich in zinc, while those from acidic rocks and coarse-grained sandstones are relatively zinc-poor.

Soil heavy metal multifractal fractal dimension number analysis

In multifractals, the number of dimensions is a key parameter to characterize the heterogeneity and complexity of the spatial distribution of soil elements. D1 is the information dimension, which measures the information content or complexity of the dataset, reflecting the average amount of information required to describe the dataset, and D2 is the association dimension, which measures the degree of association between pairs of points in the dataset36. In this study, the negative numbers of the gradients of two roughly straight lines fitted using the minimum-squares approach are denoted by the parameters D1 and D2, respectively; D1 reflects the homogeneity of the elemental distribution, while D2 characterizes the degree of aggregation in areas of high concentration. The smaller D1 is, the more homogeneous the distribution of elements; the smaller D2 is, the more likely the enrichment phenomenon occurs in the high-value area of elements and the higher the risk of pollution. Conversely, the larger D2 is, the more the elements are mainly distributed in the low-value area, and the pollution risk is lower37. Based on Table 3, the D1 values of all elements are relatively small, indicating a generally uniform distribution of elements in the study area. In contrast, the D2 values show significant variation: most elements have large D2 values, suggesting a low trend of contamination development and a low contamination risk. Specifically, the D1 and D2 values of N and P are small, indicating that the distribution of N and P elements is more uniform, but there is local enrichment in the high-value area. The mean value of N in Table 2 is notably higher than the background level, with N and P exceeding rates of 3.96% and 44.43%, respectively. This is likely due to long-term fertilization, especially over-fertilization, which raises nitrogen and phosphorus levels in the topsoil (0–20 cm) to near or full saturation. Furthermore, when nitrogen fertilizers are applied in excess or in an unbalanced manner, nitrate nitrogen that crops cannot fully absorb leaches into deeper soil layers and accumulates there. At the same time, studies have shown that, compared to dryland conditions, phosphorus applied under irrigated conditions can leach into deeper soil layers below 100 cm. These factors can lead to the accumulation of nitrogen and phosphorus in the soil.

The D1 of Hg is small, suggesting that the distribution of Hg is more uniform in the study area. This may be due to the wet and dry deposition of mercury following its long-range transport in the atmosphere, resulting in a uniform regional background. Furthermore, the D2 value for Hg is also relatively low, indicating localized enrichment in the high-value areas, which may be influenced by mining activities38, such as thermal anomalies near mining sites or localized hotspots formed by leaching of tailings. The highest value of D2 for Al indicates that the distribution of Al is concentrated in the low content area with low risk of contamination, and its exceedance rate is 4.55%, which may be caused by the weathering of rocks; the higher value of D1 may be due to the uneven distribution due to the topographic relief. For example, Johnson et al.39 suggest that the ridges may have relatively low Al content due to erosion, while the valleys’ part may have a higher Al accumulation. The D2 value of K is second only to Al, indicating that K is mainly distributed in the low-value area, and the exceedance rate of K is 3.82%, which is related to the potassium-rich strata (e.g., dolomite mineralization zone) in the study area; the lower D1 indicates that the distribution of potassium is relatively homogeneous, which is probably due to the migration of potassium from the cultivation of oilseed rape in the study area40.

D1 value of Pb is the highest, indicating that the distribution of Pb is highly heterogeneous, and the current study area spans four Class III mineralized zones, all of which contain Pb. Therefore, its uneven distribution may be affected by both natural parent rock and anthropogenic activities. The D2 value is moderate, indicating that extreme aggregation has not been formed in the high-concentration area, but we need to be wary of the cumulative effect. The low D1 value for Cd reflects the uniform distribution of Cd, possibly due to the adsorption–desorption equilibrium of Cd in agricultural soils41, whereas the low D2 value is consistent with the high number of Cd star tails in Table 3 and the possible presence of point-source contamination due to wastewater irrigation or rainy day leaching from the mine42.

The D2 value of I is the lowest among the 20 elements, indicating a high concentration of iodine in the zone of high concentration. It has been shown that soils developed on limestone usually contain more iodine than soils developed on other rocks43, and several limestone species have been found in the study area, suggesting that the enrichment of elemental iodine may be related to the weathering of rocks. Although iodine is generally more soluble and mobile in alkaline environments, carbonate minerals in limestone soils (such as calcite) may exhibit specific adsorption or coprecipitation effects on iodine. Some studies suggest that iodine may bind to calcium compounds, leading to increased iodine levels. The exceedance rate of iodine in the study area was 41.86%, so it cannot be ruled out that there was a factory for industrial iodine production in the study area, which may have influenced it. The source needs to be further verified with field investigation and isotope tracing. The small D2 value of F indicates that it is enriched in the high-value area; meanwhile, the exceedance rate of F was 29.86% in this study area, may be caused by improper deposition of industrial wastes such as phosphogypsum and the use of highly fluoridated groundwater or hot spring water to irrigate farmland44,45.

Based on the Kriging interpolation maps of the five elements and the Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot maps (Fig. S3a, b), Al is distributed uniformly across the area, while Cd, F, I, and N exhibit significant localized hotspots. In the multifractal analysis, Al has a relatively high D2 value, whereas the D2 values for the other four elements are relatively low. The conclusions drawn from both methods regarding spatial clustering and heterogeneity are consistent and mutually corroborative.

Conclusions

This study applied multifractal analyses to determine background and risk assessment values for 20 soil elements in the Delingha-Ulan Oasis, northeastern Tibetan Plateau, offering critical insights into soil contamination dynamics. The multifractal approach proved scientifically robust and essential by capturing multi-scale spatial heterogeneity, enabling the derivation of screening values for 14 unregulated elements (e.g., B, Ge, I, N, P) not included in GB15618-2018. Validation against six regulated elements (e.g., Cd, Hg) confirmed its reliability, with lower screening values (e.g., Hg: 0.07 vs. 2.4 mg/kg) reflecting local conditions over national generalizations. This methodology addresses a key limitation of traditional assessments, providing a comprehensive framework for risk evaluation where standards are lacking.

Mean element concentrations exceeded background values but remained below screening thresholds, indicating manageable contamination levels. However, elements such as I, P, N, Hg, and Cd have low D2 values and exhibit high-risk accumulation, driven by both geological weathering and human activities such as fertilization and mining. In the arid alpine oasis of the Tibetan Plateau, where intense agricultural and mineral exploitation contrast with the region’s low-disturbance baseline, these findings underscore the urgent need for tailored soil benchmarks to safeguard its fragile ecological balance. Conducting such research in plateau oases is uniquely significant, as their role as water-sustaining buffers amplifies the impact of contamination on regional sustainability.

For high-altitude arid zones, our approach closes a significant gap in soil environmental criteria, offering a scalable tool for pollution monitoring and ecological management. Future studies should integrate isotope tracing and dynamic modeling to refine source attribution and ensure the sustainable protection of these vital ecosystems.
