Genomic characterization of Bacillus clausii isolated from a case of bacteremia
Authors: Santiago Campos-Miño, Gabriela Sevillano, Andrés Herrera-Yela, José Rubén Ramírez-Iglesias, Camilo Zurita-Salinas, Jeannete Zurita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•B. clausii can cause bacteremia or sepsis in pediatric or immunocompromised patients.•In immunocompromised hosts, epithelial damage and adhesion favor bloodstream entry.•WGS revealed a close genomic relationship between both B. clausii isolates.•Multiple genomic methods consistently supported isolate relatedness.

Dear Editor,

Bacillus clausii (Enterogermina®) is a probiotic used to support gastrointestinal health and prevent antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Nevertheless, opportunistic bloodstream infections have occasionally been reported in susceptible individuals, particularly pediatric patients with severe underlying diseases. [1]. Although several cases of B. clausii bacteremia have been described, evidence linking commercial probiotic preparations to bloodstream isolates has relied exclusively on phenotypic identification methods, without high-resolution genomic confirmation.

Here, we report the case of an 8-year-old girl with severe malnutrition, cerebral palsy, and epilepsy, and recent SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia who was admitted to the pediatric intensive care in March 2024 with sepsis and respiratory failure due to nosocomial pneumonia. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae was isolated from bronchial secretions and successfully treated with meropenem plus amikacin, along with oral Bacillus clausii (Enterogermina™). One week later, she developed diarrhea. Five days after, she developed fever, and blood cultures yielded B. clausii, confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing. She was treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin for 14 days, with full recovery (Fig. 1A). To determine whether the bloodstream isolate was genomically related to the administered probiotic, both the commercial strain (B. clausii 4771) and the bloodstream isolate (B. clausii 2082) underwent whole-genome sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore MinION Mk1B platform with R10.4.1 flow cells. Comparative genomic analysis integrated complementary approaches, including assembly-based single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis using Clair3, average nucleotide identity (ANI), core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST), and phylogenomic reconstruction. Assembly-based analysis identified 60 SNPs; however, read-based variant calling with Clair3 detected no SNPs or indels, indicating assembly artifacts. ANI reached 99.5299%, indicating very high nucleotide identity while remaining below the threshold generally accepted for strict strain identity. Likewise, cgMLST identified 220 shared loci with complete allelic concordance, and phylogenomic reconstruction and phylogenomic reconstruction clustered both isolates with the probiotic reference strains B. clausii B106 and B. clausii ENTPro, supporting a common evolutionary origin (Fig. 1B). The concordance among these independent genomic approaches provides robust evidence of close genomic relationship between the administered commercial probiotic strain and the corresponding bloodstream isolate. Although probiotic consumption generally poses minimal risk, safety concerns remain particularly for strains designed or selected with strong mucosal adherence, which may promote translocation and increase the risk of sepsis, especially in immunocompromised individuals [2].

The occurrence of bacteremia in these patients suggests bacterial translocation from the gastrointestinal tract into the bloodstream, a phenomenon that may be facilitated by the strong mucosal adhesion properties of probiotic B. clausii strains [3].

In our case, the patient also presented with impaired immunity (severe chronic malnutrition), which increases the risk of probiotic-associated sepsis due to reduced clearance of exogenous bacteria. In pediatric patients, malnutrition causes intestinal epithelial damage, facilitating bacterial translocation into the bloodstream and increasing the risk of sepsis. Furthermore, B. clausii spores are thermostable and can survive under conditions of nutrient deprivation, dehydration, and low pH, favoring persistence within the gastrointestinal tract [3]. Furthermore, gastrointestinal dysfunction linked to COVID-19 may facilitate bacterial translocation, increasing the risk of opportunistic infections [4]. B. clausii survives transit in the intestine and maintains a considerable intestinal titer for up to 12 days after a single oral administration [5]. The temporal association between probiotic administration and bacteremia, together with severe malnutrition, supports intestinal translocation as the most likely source of infection.

Unlike previously reported pediatric cases relying on MALDI-TOF MS or conventional microbiological identification, our study integrates whole-genome approaches, including read-based SNP validation, ANI, cgMLST, and phylogenomic reconstruction, to evaluate the genomic relationship between the administered probiotic strain and the bloodstream isolate. Although the genomic evidence does not establish strict strain identity, these complementary analyses strongly support an exceptionally close genomic relationship between the two isolates.

Our findings support that probiotic strains such as B. clausii could cause bacteremia and potentially adapt within the host, especially in immunocompromised individuals. This highlights the need for cautious, individualized probiotic use in vulnerable pediatric populations, where microbial translocation may enhance virulence. The link between B. clausii and bloodstream infection underscores the importance of close clinical monitoring and continued research to better define probiotic safety. Therapeutic decisions should consider immune status and infection severity.
