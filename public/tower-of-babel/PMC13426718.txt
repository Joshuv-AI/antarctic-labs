Data matter: the creation of an acute care surgery registry
Authors: Laura Allen, Shane Smith, Muriel Brackstone, Daryl Gray, Brad Moffat, Rich Hilsden, Ken Leslie, Theo Wigle, Shakeel Rashid, Fatima Nadeem, Neil Parry, Kelly Vogt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Patients presenting to hospital with emergency general surgery (EGS) conditions such as appendicitis, biliary disease, and hernias represent 11% of hospital admissions in Canada. This study evaluated the establishment of a local registry of EGS patients by auditing the accuracy of data abstracted from administrative records compared with manual chart review.Methods:Patients admitted to and consulted by the Acute Care Surgery (ACS) service at London Health Sciences Centre during a 1-year period were entered into a prospective registry. Data on demographics, comorbidities, imaging, diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes were collected from both manual chart review of patient electronic health records and routinely collected administrative data. Accuracy of data from administrative sources compared with those from manual chart review was audited for a 6-month period. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for comorbidities, imaging, and complications to determine which variables can be accurately collected using administrative data.Results:A total of 2159 patients were included in the analysis. Overall accuracy was good for most comorbidities, ranging from 83.8% to 100%; however, sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV ranged drastically, suggesting that data on some comorbidities may not be accurately collected through health records. Imaging procedures were consistent between the 2 data collection methods, with good overall accuracy of 97.6%. For assessed complications, accuracy was excellent overall, with an average accuracy of 99.6% (range 98.3% to 100%).Conclusion:This study shows that a substantial portion of data necessary for an ACS registry can be reliably extracted from routinely collected administrative data.

Patients presenting to hospital with emergency general surgery (EGS) conditions such as appendicitis, biliary disease, hernias, and bowel obstructions represent 11% of hospital admissions in Canada.1 The advent of the Acute Care Surgery (ACS) service model in Canada recognizes that EGS patients require coordinated systems of care to optimize outcomes and has been associated with greater access to timely operative care and improved outcomes for many of these conditions.2–5 The ACS service model typically involves a dedicated “surgeon of the week” who suspends their elective practice during their assigned week of service. During his or her week on the ACS service, this surgeon manages all patients presenting to hospital with EGS conditions. In this relatively new field, research supporting the ideal structure and function of ACS services, modern clinical guidelines for management of common pathologies, and a structured system of data collection and analysis have lagged.

Timely access to data is paramount to gaining an understanding of both the current state and opportunities for improvement in ACS service models. Previous efforts locally and nationally have focused on individual project data collection; while ultimately successful, such efforts have proven to be time consuming and have had delayed results. Real-time data collection and reporting are essential to ensuring and improving the quality of ACS programs. Attempts at integrating EGS data collection into existing data collection platforms have been made, specifically through the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP).6 The initial pilot of this EGS-QIP was successful; however, participating NSQIP sites are not required to contribute data to the EGS module. While patients undergoing EGS are still captured as part of the NSQIP program, nonoperative patients are not and, as such, represent an incomplete picture of EGS data.

Our research group previously completed preliminary work to understand the feasibility of using routinely collected health administrative data in the creation of a local ACS registry.7 We were able to show that, of the 41 variables considered by content experts to be essential to a core ACS data set, 32 (78%) were accurately captured in routinely collected data, and a further 6 (15%) could also be determined to be accurate after further validation. The present study aimed to build on that preliminary work through the establishment of a local prospective registry of EGS patients. Data points collected from both administrative data and manual chart abstraction were compared for agreement to determine which variables could be reliably collected through health records in an effort to alleviate the manual data collection workload of such a registry.

Methods

This prospective cohort study reflects the creation of a registry of all patients admitted to and consulted by the ACS service at the London Health Sciences Centre (LHSC) in London, Ontario, Canada. The LHSC comprises 2 hospital sites: Victoria Hospital and University Hospital. Patients with general surgical conditions requiring emergent or urgent care are seen by the ACS service. They may be admitted to hospital under the care of this service or seen on a consultation basis. The ACS service does not provide critical care services but manages EGS patients admitted to critical care. Similarly, this service provides trauma surgical care but does not admit patients with multisystem trauma.

All patients admitted to or consulted by the ACS service were identified daily from the service-specific lists on the electronic medical record (EMR); these were generated as reports through information technology services and the patient EMR. All adult patients seen by the ACS service were included in the registry. This included admissions and consultations, as well as both operative and nonoperative cases.

Patient lists were shared with decision support services monthly to obtain health record data that are routinely collected for administrative purposes. These data were formatted by research personnel using IBM SPSS Satistics for Windows 2011 software and uploaded into our Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database,8 which is a secure online platform where the ACS registry is housed. Previously validated variables found to be accurate through health records7 were uploaded directly into the registry (e.g., age, sex, length of stay, operative details). Diagnosis codes (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision, Canada [ICD-10-CA]) provided in the administrative data were used to identify comorbidities and complications. Comorbidities of interest included common conditions known to affect patient outcomes as well as diagnoses used to calculate a Charlson Comorbidity Index score.9 Complications were coded based on a validation study conducted by our group to identify major complications.10 Imaging performed was also identified and coded using these administrative data from text descriptions. Variables not available using administrative data were collected through manual chart review by trained data extractors. For the purposes of this validation study, a number of variables were collected using both administrative data and manual chart review in order to later be compared for agreement. A full list of variables included in the registry and their data collection source can be found in Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.022325/tab-related-content.

Cases were enrolled in the registry starting Jan. 1, 2023. To account for improved proficiency in data coding and manual chart extraction over time, the validation assessment was performed for the 6-month period starting July 1, 2023, and ending Dec. 31, 2023. Variables that were collected using both sources (administrative data and manual chart abstraction) were assessed for agreement and accuracy. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated for each variable of interest, including 95% confidence intervals. The use of these 4 metrics in combination was done to assess the likelihood that these variables can be accurately collected through administrative data moving forward, alleviating much of the data collection workload. Diagnosis, although collected using both administrative data and chart review, was not assessed for accuracy as this variable can be nuanced, making agreement difficult to assess, particularly with multiple diagnoses.

Though no formal sample size calculation was completed, based on preliminary local data,8 we anticipated inclusion of 4000 to 4500 EGS patients for the 2023 calendar year. As such, 2000 to 2250 patients were expected to be included in the validation portion of this study. This study received approval from the Health Sciences Research Ethics Board at Western University (WREM ID 122144).

Results

Between Jan. 1 and Dec. 31, 2023, there were 4211 records captured in the registry, representing 3770 unique patients. For the purposes of this validation study, patients admitted to or consulted by the ACS service between July 1 and Dec. 31, 2023, were included in the analysis (n = 2159). The mean age of this cohort was 57.1 (standard deviation 19.8) years, and 51.3% were male.

The most common EGS diagnosis was cholecystitis (12.7%), followed by cholelithiasis (12.2%) and appendicitis (11.5%). A breakdown of the most common diagnoses can be found in Table 1. Most patients (88.4%) were admitted to hospital, and 11.6% were seen in the emergency department without hospital admission, most commonly for postoperative concerns (25.6%). Only 38% of patients underwent initial operative management.

Comorbidities

Overall, 50% of patients were found to have a comorbidity on chart review compared with 41% identified by health records. The most common comorbidity found using both data collection methods was hypertension (32%). The least common from either source were hemiplegia, liver disease, connective tissue disease, pregnancy, and HIV, all with fewer than 15 occurrences (< 2%). Imaging

The most commonly performed imaging was abdominal computed tomography (CT), with 63% of patients indicated as having undergone abdominal CT on chart review, and 60% identified through administrative data. Abdominal ultrasonography and abdominal radiography were also commonly performed (25% and 19%, respectively, as identified by chart review). Rates of imaging performed are summarized in Figure 2.

Imaging data were identified consistently between the 2 data collection methods, with sensitivity ranging from 61.9% to 95.5% and specificity from 95.1% to 100%. Similarly, PPV and NPV were good, ranging from 27.8% to 97.1% and from 97.0% to 99.9%, respectively. Overall accuracy was good, with an average of 97.6%. Discussion

Although many academic centres have had ACS services for more than 10 years,3 there are currently few centres with an ACS registry. Parallels can be drawn to the advent of trauma systems in the 1970s, when a recognition of the need to coordinate care as well as to measure and report on epidemiology and outcomes led to a significant decrease in morbidity and mortality after injury throughout the world.11 Emergency general surgery is just now reaching a phase of system organization — aided substantially by the cohorting of patients on ACS services — where we are able to entertain a similar level of commitment to data. There remains tremendous national interest in the creation of a surveillance and outcome data source for ACS in Canada, and it is clear that a method to capture data on EGS patients will be required to continue progress in this specialty.

This study demonstrates that a substantial portion of data necessary for an ACS registry can be reliably extracted from routinely collected administrative data, reducing the workload associated with manual chart abstraction. Our findings support the feasibility of leveraging health records to maintain a robust and sustainable ACS data registry, though caution is warranted for specific data elements, particularly certain comorbidities.

Comorbidities were the least consistently captured variables among administrative data. Although conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, pregnancy, HIV/AIDS, chronic kidney disease, COPD, hemiplegia, and malignancy were captured with high sensitivity and specificity via health records administrative data, others such as congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, stroke/transient ischemic attack, dementia, and connective tissue disease had poor sensitivity and low PPV. These discrepancies are consistent with the results of prior validation studies comparing administrative data with clinician-documented diagnoses or self-reported patient history, where coding often underestimates the presence of comorbid conditions.7,12,13 Misclassification may arise from coding errors, incomplete documentation, or ambiguous temporal relationships between the diagnosis and the acute care encounter (e.g., whether myocardial infarction or stroke occurred before or during admission).13 Additionally, conditions such as COPD or dementia may be overreported in charts owing to patient-reported symptoms or past medical history without confirmatory testing, and under-captured in administrative databases owing to coding specificity requirements.14,15

Imaging studies were captured consistently across both administrative data and manual chart abstraction. High sensitivity and specificity for common modalities such as abdominal CT, ultrasonography, and radiography suggest that imaging data can be reliably sourced from administrative records. This aligns with prior registry studies, which found procedural and diagnostic imaging codes to be among the most reliably captured administrative variables.16,17 Imaging modalities with low utilization, such as magnetic resonance imaging, were more variable, though this likely reflects sample size limitations rather than systematic misclassification.

Complication data also showed strong agreement between sources, with excellent specificity and NPV across nearly all outcomes. Notably, most complications identified in chart review were also captured through administrative coding, but the reverse was not always true. This is somewhat in contrast with previous findings where complications were more frequently detected via chart review than from administrative data.10 The higher detection of complications in administrative data in our study may reflect more inclusive coding strategies or a heightened coder sensitivity during the study period. However, for rare complications, even a small number of missed or misclassified events can greatly influence sensitivity and PPV calculations. This limitation is especially relevant for low-frequency outcomes such as cardiac arrest or bleeding requiring transfusion, as shown in our data and echoed in other studies.10,18 The overall low rate of complications reported in this study is likely a result of the inclusion of a wide range of EGS conditions, including a high incidence of less severe conditions such as cholecystitis and appendicitis, as well as a somewhat low rate of operative management (38%) and the inclusion of patients assessed only in consultation and subsequently discharged from the emergency department.

Overall, the observed high specificity and accuracy across complications and imaging data confirm that administrative data can serve as a reliable foundation for an ACS registry. However, comorbidity data — particularly for less common or ambiguously defined conditions — require cautious interpretation or supplementation through chart review. Similar challenges have been noted in trauma and critical care registries, where structured chart abstraction remains necessary to complement administrative records.11,19,20 As such, future iterations of this registry will continue to manually supplement comorbidity data obtained through health records. Although not formally assessed in this study, diagnosis will also continue to be collected from both the patient EMR and health records to reflect the nuanced and often complex nature of diagnosis, particularly in patients with multiple conditions.

Limitations

A major strength of this study is its inclusion of more than 2000 patients across 2 hospital sites, allowing for robust estimates of agreement across multiple variable types. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre study, and findings may not generalize to other institutions with differing documentation or coding practices. Second, data were abstracted by multiple individuals, which could have introduced variability despite standardized training. Third, the lack of a definitive gold standard for certain variables — especially comorbidities with complex or nonstandardized definitions — limits the certainty of some validation metrics. Finally, low-frequency complications may have yielded unstable performance measures and should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study supports the use of administrative data for the majority of data collection in an ACS registry, particularly for demographic, imaging, and complication variables. However, selected comorbidities should either be interpreted cautiously or verified through manual chart abstraction. As ACS systems continue to evolve nationally, ongoing validation of data quality and consistency will be essential to ensure accurate surveillance and quality improvement efforts.
