Executive Summary
Building sciences and technology directly impact human health, productivity, and resource
consumption (e.g., energy and critical minerals). Addressing emerging challenges in this field
requires skilled building science professionals. JUMP Into STEM seeks to inspire the next
generation of building scientists through a real-world competition that introduces participants to
the field and connects them with national laboratory and industry professionals.

This report highlights projects, or challenges, from past competitions that are tailored for
academic and industry professionals who aim to develop or recruit multidisciplinary talent. It
also includes additional guidance to support the effective replication of these challenges.

1 Introduction
The field of building science focuses on advancing building technologies, services, policies, and
practices. It explores how these innovations connect with the energy grid, the digital economy,
artificial intelligence, and Internet of Things (IoT). With workforce gaps in building
specializations on the rise, there is a growing need for skilled professionals from building science
programs in undergraduate, graduate, or vocational settings to keep up with the demand (Law
2025).

JUMP (Join the Discussion, Unveil Innovation, Make Connections, Promote Technology-to-
Market) Into STEM is a U.S. collegiate building science competition that aims to inspire the next
generation of building scientists by attracting student talent from disciplines like engineering,
science, computer science, architecture, and others that might not be traditional pathways to a
career in technology. It encourages innovative thinking and multidisciplinary collaboration to
advance building technologies. The team-based competition directs participants to select a
challenge and develop a comprehensive solution that addresses the problem statement,
stakeholder needs, and technology-to-market barriers (Hubbard et al. 2021; Rippy et al. 2022).
Submissions are then reviewed by a panel of judges representing the private sector, national
laboratories, and academia. Lastly, winners from each challenge are invited to a final
competition, where they refine their ideas and compete for a 10-week paid summer internship.
To date, there have been seven annual cycles of JUMP Into STEM (2018–2025). The
competition has engaged more than 1,200 student participants across 20 states. The program
continues to inspire the upcoming generation of building scientists by presenting real-world
challenge topics that are connected to contemporary problems and by promoting its aims and
achievements through conference presentations and journal publications.

This report presents a selection of key challenges from past competitions that are tailored for
academic and industry professionals who aim to train or attract multidisciplinary talent to
address current building science challenges. Additional considerations are included to facilitate
effective replication of the challenge prompts.

2 Challenges
The problem statements or challenges developed for each competition period are designed to
reflect issues critical to the building industry at that time, so the topics vary from year to year.
Administered challenges over the last seven years of the competition can be grouped into four
core themes: building envelopes, energy management, indoor environmental quality, and
technology adoption.

The prompts are technical in nature but require holistic solutions that address specific
stakeholder needs, incorporate cost/benefit and market analysis, and capture technology-to-
market opportunities and barriers.

2.1 Envelope
A building envelope is the physical barrier (e.g., walls, roof, windows) separating a building’s
interior from the exterior. Advances in envelope materials and construction methods are crucial
for energy efficiency and cost improvements.

2.1.1 Pushing the Envelope With Wall Retrofits

2.1.1.1 Objective
This challenge focuses on developing innovative solutions for building envelope wall retrofits.

2.1.1.2 Background
Building envelopes separate conditioned indoor spaces from outdoor environments and shelter
building occupants from weather, sunlight, and noise. The building envelope includes the
building’s foundation, walls, roof, ceiling, windows, and items inside these building elements,
such as insulation. The envelope serves as a means to support the structure and control the
transfer of heat, light, and air between the inside and outside, while being aesthetically pleasing.
However, many building envelopes are not tightly sealed, allowing uncontrolled and unwanted
air exchange between indoor and outdoor environments.

Poor envelopes allow conditioned air to “escape” to the outside (exfiltration) and unconditioned
air to enter (infiltration). These airflows increase the total amount of air that needs to be heated
or cooled in the building. Improving the building envelope reduces the need for heating and
cooling, in turn saving energy and money for the owner or occupants of the building. Insulation
in walls and ceilings also helps reduce the heating and cooling needs of buildings by preventing
energy loss (from heat transfer) through the building envelope.

Many homes do not have insulation because they were built before insulation requirements were
in place. Residences can have energy bills that equal 20%–30% of total annual household
income, much higher than the 6% considered affordable by economists (Boyce and Wirfs-Brock
2016; Chandler 2016; Shahyd 2016). High energy costs can limit the amount of money
American families have for basic necessities.

Building envelopes can also become health hazards to occupants if they contain asbestos, lead
paint, mold, or mildew. Public health entities have enacted laws and guidelines for the abatement
of asbestos and lead paints. Mold and mildew are caused in part by the infiltration of moisture or

 2

humid air into the airspaces between layers of materials in the building envelope. Mold spores
can detach from these surfaces and reenter the building. Among other consequences, mold has
been shown to contribute to the development of asthma in children (Fields 2012).

Solutions are needed that allow the existing building envelope to be retrofitted (i.e., updated) to
make walls more energy-efficient, air-tight, and moisture-resistant—and therefore healthier.
These walls can also improve occupant comfort, which is impacted by temperature, humidity,
and airflow. An envelope retrofit is also an opportunity to improve durability, so that the
envelope can better handle moisture without suffering short-term damage or long-term decay.

2.1.1.3 Challenge
Design a product or system that can be used for a residential building wall retrofit intended to
replace or supplement current leaky and unhealthy walls. The submission should include a
solution that prevents moisture buildup in the wall to reduce the potential for mold growth. The
submission should also be structurally sound and address air infiltration and insulation needs.
The wall retrofit solution must be appropriate for the stakeholder’s climate region and should
meet or exceed the 2015 International Energy Conservation Code for the climate in question
(Table R402.1.2 (International Code Council 2015)). Assume that the original infrastructure the
product will update or replace is from a base model single-family home of 2,400 ft2, built in
1975 (U.S. Energy Information Agency 2015).

The response should include:

 • How the wall will be retrofitted (replaced or supplemented, materials added, efficiency
 levels, airtightness, permeability, constructability, installation)
 • How the proposed solution will prevent moisture buildup in the wall
 • How the proposed solution will address the issue of airtightness
 • How the proposed solution will address some or all of the problems described in the
 background section, including improving indoor air quality, reducing energy costs
 associated with heating and cooling, and providing a cost-effective solution that will help
 end users
 • How the stakeholder will benefit from the innovation.

2.1.2 Advanced Building Construction Methods

2.1.2.1 Objective
The objective of this challenge is to develop an innovative solution that incorporates substantial
changes in building materials or construction methods, leading to benefits such as increased
productivity and worker safety through reduced construction time, reduced cost and waste,
improvements to occupant comfort and health, and reduced energy use.

2.1.2.2 Background
Building construction methods have changed little over the past century, but they have the
potential to transform thanks to recent advancements in manufacturing, fabrication, materials,
and logistics (Barbosa et al. 2017). Presently, the industry uses traditional construction methods,
including transporting building components individually, for on-site construction. Advances in

 3

materials and methods have improved energy efficiency, indoor air quality, and occupant
comfort, and novel construction practices could maximize the benefits from these advancements.
These Advanced Building Construction (ABC) efforts focus on integrating energy-efficient
solutions into highly productive U.S. construction practices for new buildings and retrofits
(EERE 2020).

There have been significant advances in manufacturing processes and materials science in recent
years, and, while industries such as vehicle and airline manufacturing have taken advantage of
these improvements to build vehicles that are lighter, more durable, and more energy-efficient,
the construction industry has been slower to adapt. Significant opportunities exist for radical
improvements in the building construction industry, and we should look to the technologies
developed in other manufacturing industries for inspiration. Dramatic changes to how buildings
are built and what materials are used could lead to improvements in energy efficiency, worker
safety, and occupant health and comfort; new business opportunities and reduced costs; and
specific improvements in flexible usage or rapid on-site construction.

The building construction industry has recently seen several companies modularizing the
construction process and building portions of buildings (walls or apartment units) in an
assembly-line-style factory (Modular Building Institute, n.d.; ModX, n.d.). This allows
construction to occur indoors, unaffected by weather, and reduces completion time by creating
tasks that can either be automated or improved by repetition. Modularization offers many new
business opportunities and jobs while simultaneously improving worker safety. In addition, the
cost of construction is lower—potentially up to 20% less—and the process is streamlined
because of these process improvements. Even so, companies are still generally using traditional
construction materials and methods (Bertram et al. 2019). Cost savings under current practices
come from buying material in bulk and reducing on-site delays from weather, among other
tactics.

Novel solutions for materials or manufacturing processes could lead to dramatic improvements
in energy efficiency, durability, and other benefits. 3D printing and increasing the use of
automation are examples of innovative methods that have been applied to buildings, but there are
likely other new processes and materials that could also be used in building construction (Bendix
2019; Novotny 2019; ORNL 2015). Energy savings, improved occupant comfort, and better
indoor environments can also be achieved by increasing the precision of the materials (less air
leakage, for example) and by reducing the time that materials are exposed to the elements.

Other benefits could be realized by bringing more creativity to building construction. For
example, the COVID-19 pandemic forced people to rethink how offices should be configured,
what meets the definition of a hospital, and even how homes are used (McGregor 2020).
Buildings are currently designed and built for a single primary use, and changing that use
requires significant construction for remodeling. Novel materials and designs could make
buildings more configurable. Another potential driver could be localized disaster response. The
Federal Emergency Management Agency often delivers trailers for temporary housing following
disasters such as hurricanes or forest fires (Keeton 2017). These could be designed to be more
conducive to adaptation. For example, durable, energy-efficient, and comfortable housing could
be prebuilt and flat-packed, ready to be shipped to places in need with minimal notice, and later
deconstructed, relocated, and repurposed as needed.

2.1.2.3 Challenge
If you were not limited by traditional building materials or methods, what could you do? If you
did not need to build a house (or other building) at a site, but could instead build pieces in a
factory, what could you do? What materials could you incorporate that would not work well
when building vertical walls? (Maybe a material that starts off as a liquid?) How would you
transport the pieces from the factory to the building site? Could you create a totally different
paradigm for buildings, such as easily reconfigurable rooms? Are there aspects to how buildings
are built today that are practical but result in missed opportunities for improving health, comfort,
or environmental impact?

The challenge is to develop an innovative solution that incorporates substantial changes in
building materials or construction methods, leading to benefits such as increased productivity
through reduced construction time, new business models or jobs, improvements to worker safety,
reduced cost and waste, improvements to occupant comfort and health, and reduced energy
use. Other benefits, such as creating flexible interior spaces, can also be a key driver. The
solution could include one or more of the following strategies:

 • The application of a new construction method or material. This method or material does
 not have to be completely novel; it could be an existing process or material used in other
 manufacturing sectors. The solution will include details about how this change can be
 applied to buildings and why it is an improvement over traditional options.
 • A solution for buildings that will allow rooms to be more easily reconfigured. This can be
 beneficial during extreme circumstances but will also be beneficial under normal
 circumstances, such as a fluctuation in the number of on-site employees for commercial
 buildings or special events. Be sure to consider how heating, ventilating, and air
 conditioning (HVAC); electrical; data; and fire protection will be easily reconfigured
 with the rooms.
 • A solution that takes advantage of new construction processes, new materials, or both to
 substantially improve the functionality of disaster-relief housing in both design and
 logistics.
 • A solution focused on building materials, construction methods, or design that promotes
 recovery, reuse, or recycling of material to eliminate waste and encourage continual use
 of resources (e.g., design for disassembly and reuse).

2.2 Indoor Environmental Quality
Indoor environmental quality is the combined impact of indoor air quality (IAQ), thermal
comfort, lighting, and acoustics (Gudeman 2020). Thermal comfort and IAQ in particular are
essential for human health, well-being, and productivity in buildings.

2.2.1 Designing a Healthier and More Energy-Efficient Air Distribution System

2.2.1.1 Objective
Design a novel air distribution system for a small (<50,000-ft2) commercial building within the
climate zone of the stakeholder.

 5

2.2.1.2 Background
On average, Americans spend 87% of their time inside buildings, and IAQ in buildings has a
direct effect on human health (Klepeis et al. 2001; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2025).
Buildings can even develop “sick building syndrome,” a situation in which IAQ is so poor that
building occupants become ill. In the workplace, this can cause absenteeism and decreased
productivity (Joshi 2008). Sick building syndrome is caused by the presence of chemical
compounds or pathogens that can affect human health and comfort. These include volatile
organic compounds, bacteria, viruses, mold, and dust, as well as internal nuisance odors and the
heat, moisture, and carbon dioxide generated by occupants.

Even when building owners, architects, and interior designers select and install building
materials and interior furnishings with minimal hazardous chemical compounds, IAQ can be an
issue. Currently, the most common mitigation strategy is ventilating the building interior with
fresh outdoor air. Building codes mandate minimum ventilation thresholds based on occupancy
classification and space usage.

Buildings need to be healthy, but they also need to be comfortable. Modern HVAC systems keep
temperature and humidity levels in the human comfort range and condition the fresh outdoor air
to the level required for occupant health. Mechanical engineers and HVAC system designers
have the challenge of balancing proper ventilation for occupant health with the need to minimize
the energy required for space conditioning.

Many air distribution systems use metal ductwork to move conditioned air between the outdoors,
the HVAC system and conditioning equipment, and indoor spaces. These systems have many
issues that impact energy use, occupant comfort, and IAQ. For example:

 • Ductwork can leak conditioned air, increasing the energy required to condition the
 occupied spaces.
 • Conditioned air can gain or lose heat as it travels through the air distribution system,
 which wastes energy.
 • Improper placement of the supply and return air grilles can negatively impact air
 temperature and quality. For example, if the supply air diffuser is placed near the return
 air grille, the conditioned air may short-circuit to the return grille and never reach the
 occupied zone in the room.
There is a need for more HVAC system designs that are energy-efficient as well as healthy and
comfortable for occupants.

2.2.1.3 Challenge
Design a novel air distribution system for a small (<50,000-ft2) commercial building within the
climate zone of the stakeholder that will:

 • Provide proper ventilation to all building occupants
 • Maintain occupant comfort (temperature and humidity)
 • Eliminate IAQ hazards
 • Reduce energy use compared to current systems
 • Meet the functional needs and code requirements of the commercial building.

2.2.2 Taking Comfort to the Extreme

2.2.2.1 Objective
The objective of this challenge is to improve occupant indoor thermal comfort in U.S. buildings
in extreme climates or locations prone to extreme weather events by focusing on occupant
comfort.

2.2.2.2 Background
Have you ever had to wear a sweater indoors in the middle of summer because of a cold
building? In winter, do you feel too warm inside because of the interior temperature? These
reactions point to your indoor thermal comfort, or the personal perception of being too hot or
cold in a building, which is also “inextricably linked to health” (Ormandy and Ezratty 2012).
Thermal comfort can be assessed qualitatively by polling occupants (e.g., using the American
Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers [ASHRAE] seven-point
thermal sensation scale) or quantitatively via measurements (e.g., infrared coupled with
computer vision), and is often determined by six elements (ANSI/ASHRAE 2022; Ghahramani
et al. 2022). These include environmental factors such as air temperature, radiation, relative
humidity, and air movement, along with personal factors like clothing level and metabolic rate
(Chow 2024).

Thermal comfort goes beyond having a properly sized heating/cooling system that meets a
temperature set point in a room. Thermal gradients in a “well-conditioned” space (e.g., due to
poor air mixing, the exterior façade, or outdoor environmental conditions), as well as occupant-
specific characteristics, can result in discomfort. The degree of discomfort varies depending on
climatic and weather conditions, among other factors. The United States is composed of eight
climate zones, ranging from very hot-humid regions (e.g., Hawaii, southern Texas) to subarctic
regions (e.g., Alaska). In cold climates where the use of hydronic heating systems is common,
temperature imbalance can result in significant occupant discomfort (Ruch et al. 2014). Extreme
weather events, or the occurrence of unusually severe weather conditions compared to historical
distributions, can also cause conditions found in extreme climates to occur in traditionally mild
environments (Easterling and et al. 2000; Squire 2024). The Great Texas Freeze of 2021 and the
2021 Northwest Heat Dome, which ravaged regions in the United States, are two such examples
(NOAA 2023; Thompson and et al. 2022). With HVAC systems already taxed, failure of the
electric grid and the lack of resilient building design (e.g., use of walls as thermal storage, lack of
radiant slab heating/cooling, or lack of dedicated conditioned rooms) make it very difficult for
communities to withstand and recover from these stressors (Winfield et al. 2021).

Poor indoor thermal comfort often results in negative health outcomes. Excessive exposure to
heat can exacerbate underlying illnesses, including cardiovascular disease and asthma, and
resulted in ~500,000 deaths globally each year between 2000 and 2019 (World Health
Organization, n.d.). Similar risks are faced by occupants experiencing extreme cold, resulting in
an estimated 38,000 deaths yearly (“Low Indoor Temperatures and Insulation” 2018). Though
causal research is ongoing, many studies indicate that children and elderly populations over 65
years of age are especially vulnerable to poor indoor thermal comfort conditions (Hughes et al.

 7

2019). It is vital to identify resilient solutions that address occupant needs for indoor thermal
comfort.

Currently, personal comfort devices such as space heaters or tabletop fans are commonly used. In
some cases, advanced equipment controls (e.g., enhanced humidity control, IoT-based comfort
control) are implemented, or mechanical heating and cooling systems are oversized, to address
the need (Mallay 2024; O’Neill et al. 2024). These existing solutions can be difficult to
implement due to cost, installation logistics, effectiveness, or practicality. A tiered approach is
recommended and should first prioritize basic building design (e.g., site location, form, shape,
orientation, and window sizes), followed by passive strategies appropriate for the local climate
(e.g., use of thermal mass, dedicated solar rooms, and natural ventilation), and lastly mechanical
heating and cooling systems. Care must be taken not to increase the operational cost of buildings
as a result of the proposed technology, as major challenges to adoption typically include upfront
costs and long payback periods. It is essential that solutions are affordable and address occupant
needs.

2.2.2.3 Challenge
This challenge asks student teams to improve occupant indoor thermal comfort in U.S. buildings
(residential, commercial, new, or existing) in extreme climates or locations prone to extreme
weather events by focusing on the environmental factors that determine individual satisfaction
within indoor atmospheres. Teams should first build out a focused problem statement for a
specific stakeholder group (i.e., by climate zone) and then develop a technical solution that meets
the needs of building occupants. For example, students may consider solutions that can measure
real-time thermal comfort in subarctic climates and adjust environmental controls accordingly.
Solutions should also prioritize electrical grid and building reliability. Teams must develop
technical and holistic solutions to address the problem and should include at least one
nontechnical component (e.g., an economic, policy, or commercialization component). Solutions
that only consider stand-alone nontechnical components will not be considered.

Suggestions for the student teams include (but are not limited to) the following:

 • Real-time sensing and response to occupant indoor thermal comfort needs
 • Advanced humidity control and measurement of occupant comfort in high-humidity
 climates
 • Passive approaches, such as solar spaces and radiant slabs, that extend the period of
 thermal comfort following an extreme weather event
 • Fuel switching to optimize low-cost operation, low energy use, or air quality concerns
 • Load shifting methods to increase system efficiencies or shift electrical loads to off-peak
 times.

2.3 Energy Management
Energy management is the process of strategically monitoring, controlling, and optimizing the
use of energy resources to reduce cost and consumption. It involves analyzing energy usage data,
implementing energy-saving strategies and technologies, and evaluating the results to
continuously improve energy performance. The ultimate goals are to achieve greater energy
efficiency, save money, and promote reliability.

2.3.1 Energi-zing!

2.3.1.1 Objective
Develop a solution to improve energy usage in U.S. buildings (residential, commercial, new, or
existing). Student team solutions should lead to reductions in energy use, and students should
emphasize reducing barriers to obtaining technologies.

2.3.1.2 Background
Demand for electricity is growing across the residential and commercial buildings space because
of increases in the built infrastructure, plug-in vehicles, appliances, plug loads (televisions, video
games, IoT devices), and electrically powered space conditioning. As demand grows, so will the
requirement for market transformation across multiple sectors, including power generation and
storage options, increased energy efficiency adoption, and timely use of energy across homes
and places of work. New infrastructure will be needed to support these technologies, and
managing electricity usage in buildings will play an integral role in achieving these objectives.

Electricity use in buildings has increased threefold to fivefold between 1970 and 2020, with
space conditioning equipment and plug loads accounting for the largest shares (U.S. EIA 2023).
These realities provide opportunities to improve energy management strategies, adopt energy-
efficient technologies, and implement other efforts that can address energy challenges. However,
there are key barriers that impact improvements. Consider that almost half of the U.S. housing
stock was built before 1970 (Sarkar 2011). Unless a home has had a substantial infrastructure
upgrade, most of these homes have electrical breaker panels with 100-amp service or less. The
average cost to upgrade a home to 200-amp service ranges from $1,300–$2,500, not to mention
additional constraints and implications from charging electric vehicles (The Home Guide 2022;
Thiele 2022). Meeting the electricity demand of contemporary buildings while balancing
technical and capital constraints will require innovative solutions. Such a substantial
transformation across the residential and commercial building sectors will also depend on
widespread adoption of new and existing technologies.

2.3.1.3 Challenge
Develop an innovative solution that will address energy use in buildings. Solutions can be
considered at the individual building and multibuilding scale. Teams should first develop a
focused problem statement for a specific stakeholder group and then develop a technical solution
or process to solve the chosen problem.

Suggestions for student teams include (but are not limited to) the following:

 • Develop solutions (equipment, technologies, or processes) to address the limited
 electrical infrastructure in individual buildings, communities, or regions.
 • Improve existing equipment or technologies to significantly increase capabilities.
 • Develop technologies or processes to identify optimal combinations of high-performance
 HVAC equipment and building envelope system upgrades.
 • Develop solutions that include advanced controls with the specific intent to optimize
 existing electrical infrastructure to accommodate new electrical loads.

2.3.2 No Peaking! Managing Peak Power Demand in Buildings

2.3.2.1 Objective
The objective is to reduce peak power demand in U.S. buildings by focusing on their thermal
loads and HVAC systems.

2.3.2.2 Background
Building end uses account for nearly 75% of the electricity produced in the United States, so
improving our buildings and how they interact with the electric grid will have a significant
impact on overall energy use (U.S. Department of Energy, n.d.). One way to achieve this is by
adopting energy-efficient appliances and equipment, such as electric or dual-fuel high-
performance HVAC systems.

However, a primary challenge in this transformation is reducing peak power demand. Electrical
grids always need to maintain a balance between demand and supply. This means that all the
electricity that is consumed (demand) needs to be generated instantaneously or come from
energy storage (supply). In 2022, grid-level energy storage accounted for less than 3% of
installed generation (Center for Sustainable Systems 2023). Power demand varies constantly
throughout the day and year. To meet these variations, the power sector relies on baseload
generation plants, intermediate plants, and peaking power plants that can ramp up capacity very
quickly (McNamara 2020; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.). Baseload plants run
near full capacity with minimum interruption throughout the year and are usually less expensive
to run. Intermediate plants adjust their output to meet common fluctuations in demand. Peaking
power plants, or “peaker plants,” are only brought online to meet peak power demand when
baseload or intermediate units cannot meet unanticipated surges.

Unlike baseload power plants, which are slow to ramp up, peaker plants can come online within
minutes and stay online for short periods of time, typically four hours or less. While in operation,
peaker plants cost more to run and have lower efficiencies. Because the electric grid needs to be
sized to meet the expected highest load of the year, these peaker plants sit idle most of the time.
In fact, most peaker plants are in operation 10% or less of the year. Managing peaks on the
demand side would help lower our dependance on these inefficient power plants.

Peak demand in buildings is mainly driven by HVAC systems. Summer peaking is typical of
most regions in the United States today, though some regions of the United States could become
winter peaking regions if HVAC systems are replaced with those that rely more on electricity.
There are four primary ways to reduce peaking events in HVAC systems:

 • Increase system efficiency. This approach will reduce the load whenever the system is
 running. For specific regions with high humidity loads, a system that provides separate
 sensible cooling and dehumidification can significantly increase efficiency (Blue
 Frontier, n.d.; Copeland, n.d.; Mojave Energy Systems, n.d.).
 • Improve the building envelope, thereby reducing the heat gains and losses as well as
 reducing the infiltration of outside air. This reduces the overall thermal load the HVAC
 system needs to condition, thereby reducing its power consumption (Harris 2021;
 Wijesuriya et al. 2023).

 10

 • Add energy storage systems. This approach will store energy during off-peak periods and
 release it during peak periods, with the potential to significantly reduce power demand
 when it matters the most. Energy storage in buildings can be electrical or thermal, with
 thermal energy storage (TES) promising a more cost-effective solution than traditional
 electric batteries (Odukomaiya et al. 2021). Traditionally, TES systems have been used
 only for cooling or heating, but more recently, systems that can use TES with a high-
 performing HVAC system both for cooling and heating peaks have started being
 developed (U.S. Department of Energy 2024).
 • Develop advanced controls. This approach can be used with existing systems to
 precool/preheat the building to prevent spikes in demand during peak times (Xiong and
 Kim 2024). It can also be used in combination with new solutions to maximize their
 performance and capabilities.

2.3.2.3 Challenge
Develop an innovative solution that will significantly reduce peak power demand in buildings.
Students can focus on reducing thermal loads and improving HVAC systems. Teams should first
develop a focused problem statement for a specific building type and location (weather region
and power generation sources) and then develop a technical solution or process.

Suggestions for student teams include (but are not limited to) the following:

 • Develop new cost-effective, high-efficiency systems that can replace or complement
 HVAC systems.
 • Develop technologies or processes that cost-effectively add energy storage to buildings.
 • Improve high-performance HVAC systems to maintain high efficiency during extreme
 cold days and reduce oversizing.
 • Develop a cost-effective combination of envelope improvements, energy storage, and
 advanced controls to minimize power consumption during peak periods.
Of special interest are packaged solutions that combine hardware with advanced controls.

2.4 Technology Adoption
Developing energy-efficient and cost-effective building technologies is only one side of the
challenge. It is equally important to promote technology adoption so that solutions are accepted,
integrated, and effectively used to improve energy management, building envelopes, and indoor
environmental quality.

2.4.1 Solving Market Adoption for Emerging Efficiency Technologies

2.4.1.1 Objective
Develop an innovative, holistic solution that will increase the purchase, installation, and use of
energy-efficient technologies in buildings (residential, commercial, new, or existing). This will
lead to reductions in energy use, cost savings, and fewer challenges in obtaining new
technologies for identified stakeholder groups.

 11

2.4.1.2 Background
Energy efficiency in the building industry is not new. The first Energy Policy and Conservation
Act was signed into law by President Gerald Ford in 1975. So why don’t we see the latest and
greatest building technologies everywhere?

For example, light-emitting diode (LED) lightbulbs have been on the market for nearly two
decades at lower and lower costs, yet only 54% of high-income households and 39% of low-
income households report having LED bulbs installed (U.S. Energy Information Administration
2022). Similarly, wireless IoT sensors and devices are improving how we interact with and
control devices in our homes and our workplaces. Many of these systems require broadband
internet, but only 57% of low-income households in the United States have broadband internet
(Pew Research Center 2021). In addition, there is an almost 10% difference in access to
broadband internet for rural communities compared to urban and suburban communities. An
estimated 39% of people living in rural areas lack access to the basic fixed broadband service
needed for many of these technologies to function (Levin and Mattey 2017).

Heat pump water heaters (HPWHs) are another example of a newer technology that can save a
substantial portion of energy for domestic hot water building loads—particularly for residential
and multifamily dwellings. An apartment building with multiple generations of families who are
often home will have an elevated hot water usage profile compared to an apartment building with
single-family tenants who are mostly away during the day. HPWHs subjected to higher usage
profiles will require the use of a backup electric coil more often, leading to less efficient
operation and potentially higher utility costs. Adding to the complexity of the situation, tenants
renting an apartment are often responsible for utility bills but do not typically own their water
heaters or have a say in the type of equipment purchased by the landlord or owners. This
seemingly energy-efficient technology investment might actually increase the utility bill for
certain tenant behaviors. The decision to incorporate HPWHs should consider performance,
usage, and costs.

The initial cost of a new technology is an important factor for deployment, and solutions to
reduce this cost are needed to help increase market adoption and impact. However, this approach
may overlook subtleties specific to subsets of the population where reducing cost may not be
enough to achieve market adoption potential. For example, there could be perception or lack of
awareness barriers, or barriers specific to certain stakeholder groups that cannot be seen through
a cost-only lens (Cort 2013). Market transformation does not occur overnight, and sometimes
strategic intervention is necessary to accelerate technology adoption.

2.4.1.3 Challenge
This challenge requires students to develop a holistic deployment solution that will increase
market adoption of an emerging technology for building energy conservation and cost reduction.
Teams will first select an emerging technology and a specific stakeholder group with limited
adoption of the technology. Teams must then perform market research, identify adoption barriers
through stakeholder engagement, and develop a holistic solution (technical, policy, and/or
economic) to increase deployment. The solution must lead to higher market adoption rates and
address barriers for the chosen stakeholder group. A holistic deployment solution—including
technical and nontechnical aspects, such as policy and economic solutions—is required.

3 Key Considerations
Successfully attracting and training multidisciplinary talent to address current building
challenges hinges on the process by which challenges are developed and implemented. Key
considerations are presented from the perspective of challenge developers, implementers, and
competition participants. By following the practices and processes discussed below, JUMP Into
STEM has had a significant effect on the career choices and development of over 80% of
participants (Rippy et al. 2022).

3.1 Challenge Development
Topics for each challenge were identified through teaming sessions with academic and industry
professionals. Working group members indicated key areas requiring new or increased research,
and then program leadership selected final topics out of that list. This ensured that the challenges
were relevant to immediate and emerging issues within building sciences. Finally, challenge
descriptions were developed, which:

 • Emphasized direct connection to real stakeholder groups and consequences (e.g., high
 energy use or human health)
 • Warranted both a technical solution and one or more of the following components, as
 appropriate: economic, policy, commercialization, or other
 • Provided references and additional resources to support participants.

3.2 Challenge Implementation
Team submissions were required to have a team background section and a challenge solution
section. Participants discussed how their teaming structures incorporated multidisciplinary skill
sets and majors, a key aim of the program. The latter section went into detail on the technical
solution and market considerations.

Participants also received evaluation criteria and a scoring rubric that mirrored the competition
rules (see Section A.1). The criteria and rubric emphasized the weight of each element of the
project while providing guidance for successful submission and scoring.

In many cases, the challenges were implemented by professors in classroom settings, which
broadened the reach and impact of the competition. No two implementation styles were the
same, but notable practices that led to successful outcomes included:

 • Making participation in the competition voluntary so that students could develop skills in
 building sciences based on a personal disposition toward project- or competition-based
 learning
 • Providing resources and support to facilitate participation at all academic levels
 • Creating avenues to pursue challenge solutions beyond the competition period (e.g.,
 summer research opportunities or internships) (Bruxvoort et al. 2025).

4 Further Ideas
4.1 Industry Engagement
The program sought opportunities to connect the challenges and participants to industry voices
by establishing an industry team that supported the competition in various ways, including
sponsorship, early-career panels, and in-person field trips. These activities provided an
opportunity for JUMP Into STEM participants to share their work with an industry partner and
build connections. Collaboration with industry partners throughout the program helped make the
JUMP Into STEM challenge more real and engaging for all participants.

4.2 Technology-to-Market Guidance
Building science is a practical field where innovation is motivated by market needs. The
technology-to-market requirement outlined in the project rules and rubric asked student
participants to characterize the market in which their innovation might be deployed, develop a
technology-to-market plan for potential deployment, and identify a key market adoption barrier.
Because these market analysis ideas are often not part of building-science-related curricula,
technology-to-market webinars and support documents were developed to provide some
guidance. Strategies for a strong technology-to-market discussion include:

 • Clear identification of where the technology-to-market section is in the submission
 • Alignment of the discussion with the problem and solution
 • Indication of the scope of the market
 • Expression of the interconnectivity of stakeholders, cost-benefit analysis, and adoption
 barriers.
Future implementations may benefit from additional discussion and examples, which can provide
students with guidance while taking care to avoid causing bias that may lead to formulaic
technology-to-market responses.
