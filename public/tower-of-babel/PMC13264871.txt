Exploring global access to healthcare and utilisation for neurocutaneous syndromes: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Bezie Kebede, Abebaw Molla, Bezuayehu Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBoth dermatological and neurological manifestations characterise neurocutaneous syndromes (NCSs). Although individually rare, collectively they impose a substantial clinical, humanitarian and economic burden, often contributing to barriers in healthcare access. This scoping review aims to map global evidence on healthcare access and service utilisation in NCSs and identify barriers, facilitators and gaps in care.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. Bibliographic databases and hand searches will be used to identify relevant studies. Published and grey literature addressing healthcare access will be included while non-English studies will be excluded. Two independent reviewers will perform study selection and data extraction. Quality assessment will be conducted for full-text studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute tools. Findings will be mapped to evidence on healthcare access, service utilisation, treatment patterns, barriers and facilitators and will be presented using tables and geographic mapping.Ethics and disseminationThis scoping review will use publicly available data and therefore does not require ethical approval. The findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Introduction

Neurocutaneous syndromes (NCSs) are characterised by combined skin and neurological manifestations, and recognising external skin changes is crucial because they can offer early clues to the underlying disease.1 These disorders that simultaneously affect the nervous system and the skin, including neurofibromatosis and tuberous sclerosis complex, are the two most common conditions alongside other less common disorders.2

Although these diseases seem rare individually, the syndrome collectively affects millions of people worldwide and exposes patients, family members and societies to substantial clinical, humanistic35 and economic burden, including direct medical and direct non-medical (accommodation, food and transportation).6 The chronic nature of the syndromes and highly variable manifestations, ranging from skin lesions and cutaneous neurofibromas to plexiform neurofibromas,7 poor oral hygiene and enamel defects,8 skeletal deformities and psychiatric disorders such as cognitive impairments or mental disability.9 In addition, other neurological manifestations include attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, headaches, cognitive impairment, motor deficits and epilepsy.10 These chronic progressive disorders, high variability in clinical manifestations, multisystem complications and frequent requirement for multidisciplinary care make patients uniquely vulnerable to inequities in healthcare access and utilisation.

Although there is no definitive treatment, strategies aim to manage symptoms, prevent complications and improve quality of life through individualised and multidisciplinary care.11 Recent progress in managing NCS has led to the introduction of new targeted and pathway-specific treatments designed to alter the course of the disease.12 13 For instance, innovative molecular and pharmacological methods including targeted therapies have demonstrated potential in decreasing tumour size and enhancing clinical outcomes for certain patients.14 These advancements signify a move towards strategies that modify the disease itself.15 One study has shown that only 12.2% of patients had received complete resections of their tumours, 39.0% reported ≥1 debulking surgery, 15.6% and 53.1% had debulking-related complications and surgery-related hospitalisations, respectively.16 These findings emphasise how treatment access barriers can manifest in both underutilisation and suboptimal outcomes of delayed surgical treatment.

Globally, healthcare access for this population is uneven.17 Currently, overall more than 2 billion people globally have no access to essential medicines, particularly in lower- and middle-income countries.18 This situation is pronounced in patients with rare diseases.19 For 23 rare diseases, indirect costs averaged 45% of the total burden without treatment, dropping to 29% with treatment,20 reflecting that accessing treatment reduces financial strain on patients and families.

Healthcare access is a complex concept that can be evaluated in various ways.21 The most widely used framework is the Levesque Framework, which illustrates the interaction between population-related and health system-related factors including approachability, acceptability, availability, affordability and appropriateness/the quality of care. It also considers the individual’s ability to understand needs, seek care, reach services, pay for care and engage with healthcare.22 Applying this framework highlights several critical vulnerabilities including healthcare access, delayed diagnosis, delayed treatment and inappropriate and inconsistent delivery. Within this framework, the diagnostic procedure is a critical component of healthcare access, especially for chronic diseases that need early detection.

Advances in diagnostic approaches have improved the early recognition and classification of NCS.23 The integration of genetic testing, molecular diagnostics and advanced imaging has enhanced diagnostic accuracy and reduced delays.24 In addition, multidisciplinary diagnostic pathways are increasingly used to support earlier and more standardised evaluations.25 However, despite these improvements, significant barriers to timely and appropriate healthcare access remain in different settings.

Patients with NCSs often face poor healthcare access because the immediate healthcare professionals may have limited knowledge and skills to identify and recognise the disease’s signs and symptoms, leading to delays in care. The limited availability of counselling options and ineffective communication reduce patient acceptability, engagement and decision-making. In resource-limited countries, specialist professionals are scarce and their knowledge and practice are poor, resulting in healthcare access being inaccessible.26 Left untreated or delayed treatment leads to multisystem complications and further increases the economic burden through diagnostic imaging, genetic testing, targeted treatment or treatment for complications, creating a vicious cycle of healthcare inaccessibility. Furthermore, evidence from multiple studies showed that the need for appropriate healthcare services, characterised by coordinated, multidisciplinary care involvement and an integrated referral system, is frequently fragmented and gaps in clinical care.2729

Despite its substantial clinical, humanistic and financial burden and limited healthcare providers for this population, no comprehensively synthesised evidence that shows the current situation of healthcare access is not well understood, limiting the ability of healthcare systems and policymakers to develop effective, equitable strategies. In addition, currently no evidence that can identify personal and system-related barriers and facilitators.

Therefore, understanding potential barriers to patient healthcare access and utilisation requires a rigorous synthesis of the available data, which is essential to address these gaps and guide targeted interventions for individuals affected by NCSs. By conducting a scoping review of key services and technologies, this review aims to provide comprehensive, relevant evidence to inform equitable and sustainable NCS care worldwide. It can also serve as a foundation for future research and systematic reviews through identifying evidence gaps, potential research areas and improving healthcare access and service utilisation to reduce disease-related burden.

A scoping review is the most suitable method to explore the review questions for the following reasons. First, since the available data on access to healthcare is too limited globally, a scoping review can identify gaps, trends and regions with limited evidence, which is valuable for further research. Second, there is no systematically synthesised evidence to date on this specific topic; this review allows us to map existing evidence and a backbone for further studies. Finally, this type of review allows for the inclusion of diverse types of online records, especially in the case of insufficient data. The objective of this scoping review is to explore access to healthcare and service utilisation for patients with NCSs.

Research questions

What evidence exists on healthcare access and geographic distribution? What barriers and facilitators were identified for healthcare access to NCSs in different healthcare settings (financial, knowledge-related, regulatory or policy, geographical)? What policies, programmes, guidelines, strategies and multidisciplinary care models have been implemented internationally to improve access to healthcare for NCSs?

Methods

The protocol of this scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework and is publicly available at https://osf.io/6h3vm. The review is expected to commence in June 2026 and be completed within the following 6 months. Such a review allows researchers to identify key concepts and definitions on a topic, determine the main dimensions of a concept, uncover the types of available evidence, reveal gaps in the literature, serve as a preliminary step toward a systematic review and enhance understanding of how research has been conducted on the topic.30 This review will synthesise worldwide evidence on NCS patients’ healthcare access and follow the methodological steps developed by Khalil et al, which are based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) framework.31 These steps include defining research questions and objectives, systematically searching for and identifying relevant studies, selecting studies, organising data through quantitative and qualitative analysis and synthesising findings with health policy implications. In addition, this review will be based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews extension checklist32 to guide and strengthen systematic reporting (online supplemental table 2). Methodological procedures for this scoping review will define the review questions, identify relevant records, select studies, chart data and summarise results. Eligibility criteria were in accordance with the Population, Concept, Context framework.33

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Population (P)

The inclusion of this scoping review is in accordance with the updated methodological guidance for scoping reviews.34 All records involving individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of at least one of the NCSs (Schwannomatosis, neurofibromatosis type 2, tuberous sclerosis complex, sturge-weber syndrome and, ataxia-telangiectasia) and their attributable complications, regardless of age, sex or ethnicity, will be eligible for inclusion. By including all demographic domains, the review will ensure a comprehensive understanding of healthcare access and services utilisation of NCSs across diverse groups of patient populations. Regarding records, given the limited body of literature, all records addressing access to healthcare, including barriers and facilitators affecting patients with NCSs or service utilisation globally, will be included.

Concept (C)

Eligible evidence will cover healthcare access dimensions (accessibility, availability/accommodation, acceptability, affordability, appropriateness/quality of services) or service utilisation (outpatient and emergency visits, hospital admissions, medication use and other services). Particular attention is given to identifying barriers and facilitators that influence the ability of people living with NCSs to obtain appropriate medical attention and specialised services. Barriers refer to any circumstances or factors that restrict, delay or prevent patients with NCSs from accessing healthcare services. On the other hand, facilitators refer to any factors that promote, enable or strengthen access to healthcare, helping to overcome the challenges posed by their fragile condition.

Context (C)

This review will include any records available regardless of geographic setting (low- or high-income countries), time of publication, study setting (general clinics, specialised clinics, registries, communities) and age groups. Peer-reviewed records (primary studies and reviews), grey literature (government and non-governmental organisation reports, guidelines and theses or dissertations) and policy documents will be included. Only the most recent version will be retained when duplicate records are identified. However, records will be excluded if they lack information on healthcare access or utilisation or if they focus primarily on treatment safety and efficacy. Records not published in English or presenting only personal opinions without a systematic report will be excluded. In addition, records that primarily focus on personal opinions or subjective reflections will be excluded. In this review, “records” refers broadly to any source of information relevant to the study objectives, including but not limited to research articles, reviews, guidelines, policy papers and organisational reports.

Patient and public involvement

This study is based on data from published studies; therefore, patients or members of the public were not involved in the development of this study protocol. In addition, the findings are intended primarily for use by policymakers, researchers and healthcare practitioners rather than individual study participants. As a result, no plan for patient or public involvement during the conduct of the review or in the dissemination of its findings.

Databases and search strategies

According to the JBI recommendation,35 three stages of searches will be conducted to identify eligible records. First, we will search from two bibliographic databases (PubMed and CINAHL) using prespecified search terms and database-specific syntax, including (“neurocutaneous syndromes” OR “neurocutaneous disorder*” OR Neurofibromatosis OR “Neurofibromatosis type II” OR NF2 OR “Tuberous sclerosis complex” OR TSC) AND (“healthcare access” OR “access to care” OR availability OR affordability OR “diagnostic delay” OR “early diagnosis”) OR “healthcare utilisation” OR “health service utilisation” OR “service use” OR “follow-up care”). The initial search will help identify the keywords used in titles and abstracts, as well as the index terms assigned to the relevant articles.

Second, a search will be conducted using revised search terms, if any, from all bibliographic databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, CINAHL, Scopus and ERIC), academic (ProQuest), organisational databases and publishers’ specific sources. Bibliographic databases are selected based on previous similar reviews of diseases of interest and any scoping reviews on healthcare access.3643 A search will be rerun for final analysis to capture newly published and indexed articles. Thirdly, both backwards and forward citation searches of included articles will be conducted.

To minimise publication bias and ensure comprehensive evidence capture, grey literature will be retrieved from Google and Google Scholar. The first 10 pages of both Google and Google Scholar will be searched using the following keywords: NCSs, healthcare access, accessibility, facilitators or barriers. Data screening and extraction

After removing duplicate records with EndNote, the records will be passed through three screening phases. Two authors (BK and AM) will independently review the data based on predefined criteria, which will be established before screening begins. The third author will handle any disagreement between the two authors. The first phase involves screening the titles to assess their eligibility. The second phase involves abstract screening to determine whether studies are relevant to our question. The third phase will involve reviewing the full texts of the records. Any disagreements about study inclusion will be resolved through ongoing discussion between the two reviewers and, when necessary, involving senior authors. Both reviewers will be blinded to each other’s decisions to minimise potential bias. This approach ensures objectivity and consistency in selecting studies for inclusion.

Data extraction will also be conducted by two independent reviewers (BK and AM) using a pre-prepared data collection tool. Types of evidence (primary studies, reviews, guidelines and government reports), evidence status (published or grey), characteristics of evidence (region, country and publication year), geographical scope of the study (local, national and international), population characteristics (age, disease conditions), treatment types (supportive treatment, surgical, medical and rehabilitation), healthcare access information (accessibility, affordability, acceptability and quality of services), facilitators or barriers and service utilisation will be extracted.

Quality assessment

Although a scoping review is primarily intended to map existing evidence and highlight research gaps, we will also assess the quality of the included studies to ensure that our synthesis is as reliable and meaningful as possible. Various studies have different degrees of study quality; this can make comparisons challenging across studies. To address these issues, different experts developed various types of quality assessment tools. For this scoping review, we will assess the quality of full-text articles. The assessment will be conducted using the JBI quality assessment tools, ensuring a systematic evaluation of methodological rigour and the reliability of the included studies.

These tools are a set of standardised instruments developed to assess the methodological quality, rigour, and risk of bias in health research articles. They are designed for use across a wide range of study designs, including randomised controlled trials, case studies, observational studies, qualitative research, systematic reviews, textual evidence (narrative) and textual evidence (policy). Each tool consists of a checklist with study-design-specific questions that evaluate aspects such as inclusion criteria, study setting, measurement validity, dealing with confounding factors, statistical analysis and reporting transparency.44 Applying such a checklist enables a structured appraisal of the strengths and limitations of each included study. In this scoping review, quality assessment was used to provide an overview of the methodological rigour of the included studies and to aid interpretation of the evidence.

Two reviewers independently conducted the quality assessment of included studies. Both authors assessed the quality of papers in detail, regardless of the authors’ profiles or journals published in or other parameters that might bias reviewers. The third reviewer was responsible for handling disputes between the reviewers and we discussed to arrive at a common consensus. Finally, all relevant studies will be included in the review irrespective of quality, with critical appraisal and implications addressed in the discussion section.

Evidence mapping

Data from the included records will be synthesised using a narrative synthesis approach, following established guidance for conducting narrative synthesis in systematic reviews.45 Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise the types of evidence, healthcare access, identified barriers, enablers and service utilisation. Data will be visualised using a map to emphasise the geographical and country-specific distribution of healthcare access. This approach will allow identification of regions with limited or no records on healthcare access to NCSs and facilitate comparison across different healthcare settings. Frequencies and percentages for categorical variables will be used.

The findings will first be organised using inductive thematic analysis, informed by the conceptual framework of access to care by Levesque et al.22 This framework includes both supply-side dimensions and demand-side abilities. The five supply-side dimensions include approachability, acceptability, availability/accommodation, affordability and appropriateness, while the corresponding demand-side abilities are the ability to perceive, seek, reach, pay and engage. This framework is suitable to systematically map barriers and facilitators affecting healthcare access for patients with NF1 across global settings, capturing both health system characteristics, healthcare service utilisation and patient-related determinants of treatment access. Countries will be grouped according to the World Bank income classifications to examine how the financial context influences variation in treatment access for NCSs. Discussion

This scoping review will be the first to comprehensively map and characterise the available literature related to healthcare access and service utilisation for patients with NCSs. In addition, this review will show existing research and gaps that could be addressed in the future. Despite the multidimensional impact of NCSs and their lifelong healthcare needs, limited evidence exists on how patients access diagnostic, therapeutic and supportive services across different health systems. Understanding the presence, type and distribution of studies on this topic is essential for identifying gaps in research and informing equitable service delivery strategies.

The review will specifically explore access to healthcare through the five key dimensions of healthcare access (approachability, acceptability, availability/accommodation, affordability and appropriateness) and health services utilisation. These dimensions provide a comprehensive framework for assessing how individuals recognise their healthcare needs, reach and obtain available services, afford care and receive appropriate and culturally acceptable treatment. By structuring the synthesis around these dimensions, the review will offer a nuanced understanding of the multifaceted barriers and facilitators influencing access to NCSs care across different regions and healthcare contexts.

Implications of scoping review

The findings from this scoping review will have several potential contributions. First, this review will assist researchers by identifying specific research questions that warrant further investigation through systematic reviews. Given the scarcity of evidence on healthcare access among individuals with NCSs, the findings of this review will serve as a foundational reference for future research efforts in this field. Secondly, the review will provide valuable insights for healthcare professionals by revealing barriers that impede both awareness and effective management of NCSs. In addition, by consolidating evidence on patients’ experiences and perceptions of healthcare utilisation, it will guide clinicians in addressing these challenges within clinical practice. Thirdly, it will identify key barriers and facilitators reported in the literature, which can inform policy, advocacy and healthcare planning aimed at improving healthcare access for individuals affected by NCSs. Finally, as this review is expected to reveal variations in healthcare access across different regions of the world, it will create an opportunity for cross-country learning and adaptation of effective healthcare practices. This knowledge exchange can support the development of contextually appropriate strategies to improve global healthcare delivery.

Strengths and limitations

This review will be the first global scoping review on rare diseases to thoroughly map and synthesise the available studies on healthcare access and utilisation. It will have the potential to examine the scope and features of existing evidence and identify gaps in knowledge across various settings. Through this process, the review will help to refine and specify research questions that can be addressed in a subsequent systematic review, serving as a valuable precursor for more targeted evidence synthesis. Additionally, a comprehensive search will be carried out across multiple bibliographic databases and supplementary sources to ensure broad coverage and improve the chances of capturing all relevant records.

However, anticipated limitations will be acknowledged. Due to resource constraints, only studies published in English will be included, which may introduce language and publication bias. In addition, since most of the available records are likely to originate from developed countries, the findings may not fully represent global diversity, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. These limitations may affect the generalisability and global representativeness of the review’s conclusions.

Dissemination plan

In addition to submitting the manuscript for publication in a reputable open-access journal, we plan to present the findings at relevant scientific conferences. When appropriate, the results will also be shared with local policymakers to inform decision-making and align their activities with the objectives of this review. In doing so, the scientific community will benefit from the evidence generated, which may serve as a valuable reference for clinical practice, policy formulation and future research.

Protocol amendment

At this stage, we do not anticipate making any modifications to the protocol during the conduct of this scoping review. However, if any modifications become necessary in response to unforeseen circumstances or methodological considerations, we will transparently report and justify all changes. Specifically, the reasons for the amendments and the sections of the review affected by these modifications will be clearly documented in the final report.
