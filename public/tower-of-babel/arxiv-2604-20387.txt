Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Sharp
l_{p}
-bound

For a parameter
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the
l_{p}
-
Hausdorff distance from convex hull
of a nonempty compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the distance

(1)

d^{(l_{p})}(A):=\sup_{x\in\textup{conv}(A)}\inf_{a\in A}\|x-a\|_{p},

where
\textup{conv}(A)
is the convex hull of the set
A
, and
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
l_{p}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Intuitively, the value
d^{(l_{p})}(A)
is the farthest possible
l_{p}
-distance to the set
A
from a point in the convex hull of
A
. In fact, the name
Hausdorff distance
originates from the fact the quantity
d^{(l_{2})}(A)
is exactly the distance between the set
A
and and its convex hull, measured by the Hausdorff metric.

The
Minkowski sum
of nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the set

A+B:=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}.

The following theorem, which is the main result of this paper, establishes that the function
(d^{(l_{p})})^{p}
is subadditive (with respect to Minkowski summation) on nonempty compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
up to multiplication by a constant that depends on the parameter
p
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Then the bound

(2)

d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)\leq\max\{1,2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\}\sqrt[p]{(d^{(l_{p})}(A))^{p}+(d^{(l_{p})}(B))^{p}}

holds for any nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover, the constant
\max\{1,2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\}
is sharp in the following sense:

(1)

If
1\leq p\leq 2
, then for any nonnegative real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=\alpha
,
d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\beta
, and (
2
) is equality.

(2)

If
p>2
, then for any positive real number
\alpha
there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\alpha
and (
2
) is equality.

The significance of Theorem
1.1
originates from the Shapley–Folkman–Starr theorem, whose original proof is in the appendix of the 1969 paper
[
13
]
of Starr. A 1969 paper of Emerson and Greenleaf observes the same phenomenon in the setting of open relatively compact sets
[
4
, Theorem 1.3]
. The Shapely–Folkman–Starr theorem states that if
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are nonempty compact sets, then we have the bound

(3)

(d^{(l_{2})}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{m}))^{2}\leq\sum_{\min(m,n)}\textup{rad}^{2}(A_{i}),

where we take the summation on the right side over the largest
\min(m,n)
terms
\textup{rad}(A_{i})
, and the term

\textup{rad}(A):=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sup_{a\in A}\|x-a\|_{2}

is the
outer radius
of a compact set
A
. If each set
A_{i}
in (
3
) is the same set
A
, then for
m\geq n
we obtain the bound

(4)

d^{(l_{2})}\left(A(m)\right)\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{m}\cdot\textup{rad}(A),

where
A(m):=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}A
denotes the
m
th set average
of the set
A
. We deduce from (
4
) that the sequence of
m
th set averages of a set
A
converges to
\textup{conv}(A)
in the Hausdorff metric as
m
tends to infinity. Some authors refer to this phenomenon as the convexification effect of Minkowski summation. The reader can reference the survey
[
5
]
of Fradelizi, Madiman, Marsiglietti, and Zvavitch for more information on this topic.

On a related note, Dyn and Farkhi
[
3
, Page 5]
conjectured in 2005 that for any nonempty compact
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the bound

(5)

(d^{(l_{2})}(A+B))^{2}\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}

holds. To see the significance of this conjecture in the context of the present discussion, we observe that if the bound (
5
) were true, then we could replace the term
\textup{rad}(A)
on the right side of (
4
) with the term
d^{(l_{2})}(A)
. This in turn would make an improvement on the right side of (
4
) due to the fact that
d^{(l_{2})}(A)\leq\textup{rad}(A)
.

In 2018, Fradelizi, Madiman, Marsiglietti, and Zvavitch disproved the Dyn–Farkhi conjecture
[
5
, Theorem 7.3]
in the case
n\geq 3
. However, their counterexample relied on the construction of two two-dimensional sets whose sum is a three-dimensional set, and as a result the conjecture remained open for the case
n=2
. Recent work
[
11
]
of the author confirmed the Dyn–Farkhi conjecture when
n=2
, establishing an improvement of (
4
). In the present paper, we make further improvements on the techniques used in
[
11
]
to obtain Theorem
1.1
, which is the
l_{p}
analogue of the bound (
5
).

We emphasize that the counterexample of Fradelizi, Madiman, Marsiglietti, and Zvavitch relies fundamentally on the fact that
A\neq B
, so the Dyn–Farkhi conjecture (
5
) remains open for the special case
A=B
when
n\geq 3
. One may also ask whether (
5
) holds when
n=1
, since we have only mentioned results for
n\geq 2
. Not only is (
5
) true in the one-dimensional case, but it is possible to derive stronger bounds. For example, the reader may reference
[
10
, Lemma 2.4]
for a sharp bound involving sums of two or more compact sets in
\mathbb{R}
, and
[
5
, Theorem 7.5]
for strong fractional subadditive bounds, which in the case of
n=1
hold for sums of two or more compact sets.

In the following subsections, we highlight other interesting developments related to the Hausdorff distance from convex hull.

1.2.
The convex hull of a sumset in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

A
convex body

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set that is compact, convex, and has nonempty interior. A convex body
K
is
symmetric
if
K=-K
. The
Minkowski functional
of
K
is the function
\|\cdot\|_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
that satisfies

(6)

\|x\|_{K}:=\inf\{\lambda>0:x\in\lambda K\}.

If
K
is a symmetric convex body, then the function
\|\cdot\|_{K}
is a symmetric norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so it makes sense to replace the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
in (
1
) with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
to arrive at the generalized Hausdorff distance from convex hull

(7)

d^{(K)}(A):=\sup_{x\in\textup{conv}(A)}d^{(K)}(x,A),

where we define

d^{(K)}(x,A):=\inf_{a\in A}\|x-a\|_{K}

to be the
K
-distance from the point
x
to the set
A
. The idea of the generalized Hausdorff distance from convex hull originates from the survey
[
5
, Section 7]
.

We begin with a generalization of a key lemma
[
11
, Lemma 3.8]
that gives a characterization of the convex hull of the Minkowski sum of compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the statement below, we refer to the
K
-length
of an interval
[a,b]
, which is the difference
\|a-b\|_{K}
of the endpoints in the
K
-norm.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body, and let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be nonempty compact sets. If

(8)

x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)\cap(A+\textup{conv}(B))^{c}\cap(\textup{conv}(A)+B)^{c},

then
x
is contained in a parallelogram whose vertex set is contained in the sumset
A+B
, and whose sides have
K
-length at most
2d^{(K)}(A)
and
2d^{(K)}(B)
respectively (See Figure
1
).

We use Theorem
1.2
to reduce the problem of proving Theorem
1.1
to the simpler problem of maximizing the distance
d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(P))
over all parallelograms
P
(with vertex set
\textup{vert}(P)
) whose sides have
l_{p}
-length bounded by
2d^{(l_{p})}(A)
and
2d^{(l_{p})}(B)
. In addition, as discussed further in Section
1.4
, we are able to use Theorem
1.2
and convenient properties of the
l_{2}
-norm to characterize the equality conditions for Theorem
1.1
when
p=2
.

1.3.
Generalized Hausdorff distance

The author observed in
[
11
]
that the bound (
5
) holds in a more general setting: if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an ellipse centered at the origin, then the bound

(d^{(E)}(A+B))^{2}\leq(d^{(E)}(A))^{2}+(d^{(E)}(B))^{2}

holds for any nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The next result shows that the converse statement holds as well.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body. Then the bound

(9)

(d^{(K)}(A+B))^{2}\leq(d^{(K)}(A))^{2}+(d^{(K)}(B))^{2}

holds for any nonempty compact
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if
K
is an ellipse.

For a concrete example of Theorem
1.3
, consider the function
d^{(l_{p})}
with
p>2
. The bound
\sqrt[p]{x^{p}+y^{p}}\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, which holds for any
x,y\geq 0
and
p>2
, hints that maybe
(d^{(l_{p})})^{2}
is subadditive. However, we see in Proposition
3.1
that the
p
-dependent constant in Theorem
1.1
permits a counterexample. In the general case, we find that the equivalence in Theorem
1.3
is true precisely due to John’s ellipsoid theorem, which establishes the existence of a unique ellipsoid in
K
of maximal volume, and gives necessary and sufficient conditions that the maximal ellipsoid is the unit
l_{2}
-ball.

We will also conjecture an improvement of Theorem
1.1
. Consider a symmetric convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary that is
strictly convex
(i.e., the boundary
\partial K
does not contain any intervals) and
C^{1}
. If
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a triangle, we can use a result of Kramer and Németh
[
6
, Theorem 2]
to find a unique homothetic copy
h(K)
of
K
that circumscribes
T
in the sense that
\textup{vert}(T)\subset\partial h(K)
. The triangle
T
is a
K
-
right
triangle if the boundary of
T
contains the center
c
of the homothetic copy
h(K)
. For positive real numbers
a
and
b
, define
\mathcal{R}_{K}(a,b)
to be the set of all
K
-right triangles for which two of the sides have
K
-length
a
and
b
respectively. In the case that either
a=0
or
b=0
, define
\mathcal{R}_{K}(a,0)
to be the set of all intervals with
K
-length
a
, and define
\mathcal{R}_{K}(0,b)
be the set of all intervals with
K
-length
b
. When both
a=b=0
, define
\mathcal{R}_{K}(0,0):=\{0\}
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body with strictly convex
C^{1}
boundary. If
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are nonempty compact sets, then

(10)

d^{(K)}(A+B)\leq 2\max\left\{d^{(K)}(\textup{vert}(T)):T\in\mathcal{R}_{K}(d^{(K)}(A),d^{(K)}(B))\right\}.

Moreover, for any nonnegative real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(K)}(A)=\alpha
,
d^{(K)}(B)=\beta
, and (
10
) is equality.

We know that Conjecture
1.1
is equivalent to Theorem
1.1
in the case that
K
is the ball
B_{p}^{2}
with
1<p\leq 2
. But in the case that
p>2
, Conjecture
1.1
claims an improvement of Theorem
1.1
. We explain this in more detail in the Appendix, Section
6.1
.

1.4.
Equality conditions

As a further application of Theorem
1.2
, we derive equality conditions for the bound (
5
). The statement of the next result uses the notion of the
dimension
of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is the vector space dimension of the affine hull of
A
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be compact sets. Then

(11)

(d^{(l_{2})}(A+B))^{2}=(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}

holds if and only if at least one of the following properties is true:

(1)

Either
A
or
B
is a point.

(2)

Both of
A
and
B
are convex.

(3)

The sets
\textup{aff}(A)
and
\textup{aff}(B)
are orthogonal lines.

(4)

One of the sets is convex with dimension
1
or
2
, the other set is nonconvex with dimension
2
, and there exists
x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)
such that the interior of the
l_{2}
-ball with center
x
and radius
\max\{d^{(l_{2})}(A),d^{(l_{2})}(B)\}
does not intersect the sumset
A+B
.

Theorem
1.1
implies the estimate

(12)

d^{(l_{2})}(A(2))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}d^{(l_{2})}(A)

for a nonempty compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Using Theorem
1.4
, we deduce that (
12
) is strict when
A
is nonconvex. It is natural to ask if we can improve this bound.

Question 1.1
.

Does there exist a sequence
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of compact sets
A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(l_{2})}(A_{k})=1
for each
k
, and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}d^{(l_{2})}(A_{k}(2))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
?

We give two reasons why Question
1.1
is worth consideration. To begin with, a negative answer would establish an improvement of the bound (
12
) when
n=2
, showing that in a certain sense some efficiency is gained if we take sums
A+A
of a set with itself rather than the sums
A+B
of arbitrary sets. On the other hand, it remains an open problem to prove or disprove the bound (
12
) for compact sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
, and it may be possible to generalize the solution to Question
1.1
(positive or negative) to resolve this problem.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We prove Theorem
1.2
by cases on the dimension of the sets
A
and
B
. The paper
[
11
]
essentially contains the proof except for the cases where both
A
and
B
have dimension
1
, and the case where exactly one of
A
or
B
has dimension
1
. For the sake of completion, we provide the full proof below.

We will make frequent use of the relation

\textup{conv}(A+B)=\textup{conv}(A)+\textup{conv}(B),

which holds for arbitrary nonempty sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is an exercise to verify this fact, but the reader may also reference
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
for its proof.

The next lemma resolves the case where both sets have dimension
1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body, and let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty compact sets of dimension
1
. If

x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)\cap(A+\textup{conv}(B))^{c}\cap(\textup{conv}(A)+B)^{c},

then
x
belongs to a parallelogram with vertex set in the sumset
A+B
and sides of
K
-length at most
2d^{(K)}(A)
and
2d^{(K)}(B)
respectively.

Proof.

Let
x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)
, and write
x=a+b
, where
a\in\textup{conv}(A)
and
b\in\textup{conv}(B)
. Since
A
and
B
have dimension
1
, there exist
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
a\in[a_{1},a_{2}]
,
b\in[b_{1},b_{2}]
,
\|a_{1}-a_{2}\|_{K}\leq 2d^{(K)}(A)
, and
\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|_{K}\leq 2d^{(K)}(B)
. Then
x\in[a_{1},a_{2}]+[b_{1},b_{2}]
. To complete the proof, we need to show that
A
and
B
are not parallel. In other words, we need to show that their affine hulls
\textup{aff}(A)
and
\textup{aff}(B)
are not parallel lines.

Suppose for contradiction that
A
and
B
are parallel. Then there exists a direction
u\in S^{n-1}
that is parallel to both
\textup{aff}(A)
and
\textup{aff}(B)
. Since
a\in[a_{1},a_{2}]
, there exist
\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}\geq 0
such that
[a_{1},a_{2}]=[a-\alpha_{a}u,a+\beta_{a}u]
. Similarly, there exist
\alpha_{b},\beta_{b}\geq 0
such that
[b_{1},b_{2}]=[b-\alpha_{b}u,b+\beta_{b}u]
. Suppose that
\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|_{2}\geq\|a_{1}-a_{2}\|_{2}
, so that
\alpha_{b}+\beta_{b}\geq\alpha_{a}+\beta_{a}
. We observe that

\begin{split}\{-\alpha_{a}u,\beta_{a}u\}+[-\alpha_{b}u,\beta_{b}u]&\supset[-\alpha_{a}u,-\alpha_{a}u+\beta_{b}u]\cup[\beta_{a}u-\alpha_{b}u,\beta_{a}u]\\
&=[-\alpha_{a}u,(\beta_{b}-\alpha_{a})u]\cup[(\beta_{a}-\alpha_{b})u,\beta_{a}u]\\
&=[-\alpha_{a}u,\beta_{a}u],\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\beta_{b}-\alpha_{a}\geq\beta_{a}-\alpha_{b}
. Then

0\in[-\alpha_{a}u,\beta_{a}u]\subset(\{a_{1},a_{2}\}-a)+([b_{1},b_{2}]-b)\subset(A-a)+(\textup{conv}(B)-b).

We arrive at the contradiction
a+b\in A+\textup{conv}(B)
. We handle the case
\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|_{2}\leq\|a_{1}-a_{2}\|_{2}
in a similar way to arrive at another contradiction. Therefore, the assumption that
A
and
B
are parallel is false. It follows that
A
and
B
are not parallel, which implies that the sumset
[a_{1},a_{2}]+[b_{1},b_{2}]
is a parallelogram whose vertex set is contained in the sumset
A+B
, and whose sides have length at most
2d^{(K)}(A)
and
2d^{(K)}(B)
respectively.
∎

The next lemma helps to resolve Theorem
1.2
when either
A
or
B
has dimension
2
. To prepare for the proof, we recall that the
extreme points
of a convex body
K
are the points of
K
that are not properly contained in an interval whose endpoints are in
K
. We use notation
\textup{extreme}(K)
to denote the extreme points of
K
. We will use the Krein–Milman theorem, which states that a convex body is the convex hull of its extreme points, and we will use the useful corollary that the containment
\textup{extreme}(\textup{conv}(B))\subset B
holds for any compact
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a symmetric convex body, let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty compact set, and let
H
be a hyperplane that intersects
\textup{conv}(B)
. If
H^{+}
and
H^{-}
are the closed half-spaces bounded by
H
, then there exist
b_{1}^{*}\in B\cap H^{+}
and
b_{2}^{*}\in B\cap H^{-}
such that

(13)

\|b_{1}^{*}-b_{2}^{*}\|_{K}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1}\in B\cap H^{+}\\
b_{2}\in B\cap H^{-}\end{subarray}}\|b_{1}-b_{2}\|_{K}.

Moreover,
\|b_{1}^{*}-b_{2}^{*}\|_{K}\leq 2d^{(K)}(B)
.

Proof.

First, we show that
B\cap H^{+}
and
B\cap H^{-}
are nonempty. If
H\cap\textup{relint}(\textup{conv}(B))
is empty, then
H
is a supporting hyperplane for
\textup{conv}(B)
, and therefore contains points of
\textup{extreme}(\textup{conv}(B))
. Since
\textup{extreme}(\textup{conv}(B))
is a subset of
B
, the set
H\cap B
must be nonempty. But this implies that the sets
B\cap H^{+}
and
B\cap H^{-}
are nonempty, because
H^{+}
and
H^{-}
both contain
H
.

If
H\cap\textup{relint}(\textup{conv}(B))
is nonempty, then either
\textup{conv}(B)\subset H
, or
\textup{int}(H^{+})\cap\textup{conv}(B)
and
\textup{int}(H^{-})\cap\textup{conv}(B)
are nonempty. In the first case, it is immediate that
B\cap H^{+}
and
B\cap H^{-}
are nonempty, so we will assume that the second case holds. We will show that
\textup{int}(H^{+})
and
\textup{int}(H^{-})
must contain extreme points of
\textup{conv}(B)
, and thus points in
B
. Assume for contradiction that
\textup{extreme}(\textup{conv}(B))\subset H^{-}
. By the Krein–Milman theorem,
\textup{conv}(B)\subset H^{-}
. It follows that
\textup{int}(H^{+})\cap\textup{conv}(B)
is empty, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\textup{int}(H^{+})
must contain extreme points of
\textup{conv}(B)
. We use the same reasoning to see that
\textup{int}(H^{-})
contains extreme points of
\textup{conv}(B)
, proving that the sets
B\cap H^{+}
and
B\cap H^{-}
are nonempty.

Next, we prove the existence of
b_{1}^{*}\in B\cap H^{+}
and
b_{2}^{*}\in B\cap H^{-}
that solve the minimization problem (
13
). Denote the right side of (
13
) by
M
. Let
b_{1}^{k}\in B\cap H^{+}
and
b_{2}^{k}\in B\cap H^{-}
be sequences such that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|b_{1}^{k}-b_{2}^{k}\|_{K}=M
. Since the sequences
b_{1}^{k}
and
b_{2}^{k}
are bounded, there exist
b_{1}^{*}\in B\cap H^{+}
and
b_{2}^{*}\in B\cap H^{-}
such that (up to subsequences)
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|b_{1}^{k}-b_{1}^{*}\|_{K}=0
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|b_{2}^{k}-b_{2}^{*}\|_{K}=0
. By the triangle inequality,

\|b_{1}^{*}-b_{2}^{*}\|_{K}\leq\|b_{1}^{*}-b_{1}^{k}\|_{K}+\|b_{2}^{k}-b_{2}^{*}\|_{K}+\|b_{1}^{k}-b_{2}^{k}\|_{K}.

Taking the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
, we see that

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body, and let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex polygon with vertex set
\textup{vert}(P)=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
. Then
\textup{Haus}_{K}(P)
intersects at least one of the bisectors
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
for some
1\leq i<j\leq m
. Moreover, if
K
is strictly convex, then
\textup{Haus}_{K}(P)
is contained in the union
\cup_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
.

Proof.

Let
y\in\textup{Haus}_{K}(P)
, and assume that
y
does not lie on any bisector
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
. We will make two conclusions. First, we will show that when

is strictly convex there does not exist such a point

. After that, we will argue that if
K
is not strictly convex, it is possible to locate a different point in
\textup{Haus}_{K}(P)
that does belong to a
K

.

Arrange the vertices of

so that
\|y-v_{1}\|_{K}=d^{(K)}(\textup{vert}(P))
. For the rest of the proof we will denote

. Because

does not belong to any bisectors, we have

0<d<\min\{\|y-v_{i}\|_{K}:2\leq i\leq m\},

so that

is achieved at exactly one point
v_{1}
. If

is strictly convex, then Lemma

is strictly convex, every point of

must belong to a

. In the case that

is not strictly convex, according to Lemma
3.2
, we arrive at a contradiction unless
y\in(v_{i},v_{j})
for vertices
v_{i}
and

different from
v_{1}
, the line

that passes through the vertex
v_{1}
and is parallel to the interval
(v_{i},v_{j})
is a supporting line for the ball

, and the corresponding support set is an interval. To handle this case, let

be a unit vector that is parallel to the interval
(v_{i},v_{j})
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define the convex body

(v_{i},v_{j})
when

. It follows that there exist

For contradiction, assume that the set

has center in the interval

and does not contain any of the vertices

. By definition of
\alpha_{2}
and by compactness of

, there exists
\varepsilon>0
so small that

(v_{i},v_{j})

Next, define

which from the previous paragraph is well-defined and positive. Choose a sequence

3.3.
Continuous parametrization of
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})

We devote this subsection to the proof that the
K
-bisectors
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
are continuous curves if the convex body
K
is strictly convex. To prove this, we will use the fact that the cross-sectional lengths of a strictly convex body, in a certain sense, form a strictly concave function. Using strict concavity we can conclude that the nonempty intersection of the boundaries of a strictly convex planar body and any of its translates consist of exactly two points.

At this point, it is worth mentioning the survey on Minkowski spaces
[
9
]
of Martini, Swanepoel, and Weiß. Of special interest is Section 3.2, where the authors summarize known characterizations of strictly convex spaces. In fact, they make the almost the same observation that we make in Lemma
3.4
below, except they do not show how to locate the points of intersection relative to the direction of translation. For our purposes, we find it convenient to construct a proof that uses properties of concave functions.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body with strictly convex boundary, and let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be nonzero such that the set
K\cap(K+v)
has nonempty interior. Then there exist exactly two points in the intersection
\partial K\cap\partial(K+v)
, and they lie on opposite sides of the line through
0
and
v
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to use Brunn’s theorem. We will start the proof according to
[
12
, Corollary 2.1]
. For a direction
\xi\in S^{1}
, define the function
A_{K,\xi}(t):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

A_{K,\xi}(t):=|K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t\xi\}|_{1},

where
\xi^{\bot}
is the subspace orthogonal to
\xi
. We claim that the function
A_{K,\xi}(t)
is strictly concave on its support. To show this, let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
be distinct such that
A_{K,\xi}(t_{i})>0
, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Define the convex polygon

P:=\textup{conv}(K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{1}\xi\},K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{2}\xi\}).

Then
P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{i}\xi\}=K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{i}\xi\}
for each
i
. We also have the proper containment

(18)

P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+(\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{2})\xi\}\subsetneq K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+(\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{2})\xi\},

because otherwise there would exist three boundary points of
K
along a side of
P
, which contradicts strict convexity of
K
. By the convexity of
P
, we have the containment

\lambda(P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{1}\xi\})+(1-\lambda)(P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{2}\xi\})\subset P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+(\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{2})\xi\}.

Replacing each set
P\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{i}\xi\}
with
K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{i}\xi\}
and using the strict containment (
18
), we end up with

\lambda(K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{1}\xi\})+(1-\lambda)(K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{2}\xi\})\subsetneq K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+(\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{2})\xi\},

and it follows immediately that
A_{K,\xi}(t)
is strictly concave on its support.

The next step is to locate points that belong to boundaries of both
K
and
K+v
. Begin by choosing a direction
\xi
that is orthogonal to
v
, so that
v\in\xi^{\bot}
. Denote the support of
A_{K,\xi}
by
(t_{-},t_{+})
, with
t_{-}<0<t_{+}
. Since
K
is symmetric, we have
A_{K,\xi}(0)\geq A_{K,\xi}(t)
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Combining this with the fact that the interior of
K\cap(K+v)
is nonempty, we deduce that
A_{K,\xi}(0)>\|v\|_{2}
. Because
A_{K,\xi}(t_{-})=A_{K,\xi}(t_{+})=0
, and because
A_{K,\xi}
is continuous on its support, there must exist
t_{-}^{*}\in(t_{-},0)
and
t_{+}^{*}\in(0,t_{+})
such that
A_{K,\xi}(t_{-}^{*})=A_{K,\xi}(t_{+}^{*})=\|v\|_{2}
. Then each of the sets
K\cap\{\xi^{\bot}+t_{\pm}^{*}\xi\}
are of the form
[x_{\pm}-v,x_{\pm}]
with
x_{\pm}\in\partial K\cap\partial(K+v)
, and the points
x_{\pm}
lie on opposite sides of the line
\xi^{\bot}
that passes through
0
and
v
.

Finally, assume for contradiction that there exist
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}
such that
A_{K,\xi}(t_{i})=\|v\|_{2}
for each
i
. Then there exists
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
t_{2}=\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{3}
. Using the strict convexity of
A_{K,\xi}
, we compute

\|v\|_{2}=A_{K,\xi}(t_{2})>\lambda A_{K,\xi}(t_{1})+(1-\lambda)A_{K,\xi}(t_{3})=\|v\|_{2},

which is a contradiction. As a result, there do not exist more than two values of
t\in\mathbb{R}
for which
A_{K,\xi}(t)=\|v\|_{2}
, and the points
x_{\pm}
are the only two points that lie on the intersection
\partial K\cap\partial(K+v)
.
∎

We just proved that the boundaries of a strictly convex planar body and any of its translates intersect at exactly two points, or they do not intersect at all. We will see below that this is a sufficient condition that the
K
-bisectors are continuous curves.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body with strictly convex boundary. Then for any distinct points
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exists a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{1},v_{2})=\{f(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Moreover, for each
1\leq i\leq 2
the distance
\|f(t)-v_{i}\|_{K}
increases strictly as
|t|
increases.

Proof.

Let
L(v_{1},v_{2})
be the line through
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, and let
L^{+}(v_{1},v_{2})
and
L^{-}(v_{1},v_{2})
be the closed half-spaces bounded by
L(v_{1},v_{2})
. It follows from Lemma
3.4
that for each
t\geq 0
there exists a unique point
x_{+}(t)\in L^{+}(v_{1},v_{2})
such that

(19)

\|x_{+}(t)-v_{1}\|_{K}=\|x_{+}(t)-v_{2}\|_{K}=t+r_{K},

where
r_{K}:=\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{K}/2
. Analogously, for each
t\geq 0
there is a unique point
x_{-}(t)\in L^{-}(v_{1},v_{2})
that satisfies (
19
) with
x_{-}(t)
in place of
x_{+}(t)
.

We claim that the functions
x_{+}
and
x_{-}
are continuous on the interval
[0,\infty)
. To see this, let
t_{0}\geq 0
, let
t_{k}\geq 0
be a sequence that converges to
t_{0}
, and let
x_{+}(u_{k})
be a subsequence of the sequence
x_{+}(t_{k})
. Since the sequence
u_{k}
is bounded, the sequence
x_{+}(u_{k})
is bounded. Then there exists a subsequence
x_{+}(u_{j_{k}})
of the sequence
x_{+}(u_{k})
and there is a point
x\in L^{+}(v_{1},v_{2})
that satisfy
\|x_{+}(u_{j_{k}})-x\|_{K}\rightarrow 0
as
j_{k}\rightarrow\infty
. By the triangle inequality,

\|x-v_{1}\|_{K}\leq\|x-x_{+}(u_{j_{k}})\|_{K}+\|x_{+}(u_{j_{k}})-v_{1}\|_{K}\leq 2\|x-x_{+}(u_{j_{k}})\|_{K}+\|x-v_{1}\|_{K}.

Taking the limit as
k
tends to infinity, we get

\|x-v_{1}\|_{K}\leq t_{0}+r_{K}\leq\|x-v_{1}\|_{K},

and we obtain
\|x-v_{1}\|_{K}=t_{0}+r_{K}
. By the same reasoning,
\|x-v_{2}\|_{K}=t_{0}+r_{K}
, and we find that
x
lies on the boundaries of the balls
\alpha(t_{0})K+v_{1}
and
\alpha(t_{0})K+v_{2}
, where
\alpha(t_{0}):=t_{0}+r_{K}
. By Lemma
3.4
, there is only one such element
x
in the given closed half-space, so by uniqueness
x=x_{+}(t_{0})
. We have shown that every subsequence of the sequence
x_{+}(t_{k})
has a subsequence that converges to
x_{+}(t_{0})
. It follows that the sequence itself must converge to
x_{+}(t_{0})
, proving that
x_{+}
is continuous. We use the exact same method to show that
x_{-}
is continuous.

We define the parametrization function
f
as follows. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and an arbitrary set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
I_{A}(t)
denote the identity function of the set
A
that takes on the value
1
for
t\in A
and the value
0
for
t\notin A
. Define

f(t):=I_{(-\infty,0)}(t)\cdot x_{-}(|t|)+I_{[0,\infty)}(t)\cdot x_{+}(t).

We will check that
f
parametrizes the bisector
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{1},v_{2})
. First, if
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then either
t\geq 0
, or
t<0
. In the first case,
f(t)=x_{+}(t)
, and in the second case,
f(t)=x_{-}(|t|)
. By definition, each of the points
x_{+}(t)
and
x_{-}(|t|)
belongs to the bisector
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{1},v_{2})
, so we have
f(t)\in\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{1},v_{2})
. On the other hand, if a point
x
belongs to the bisector
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{1},v_{2})
, then
x
belongs to the intersection of the boundaries
\partial(\alpha(t)K+v_{1})\cap\partial(\alpha(t)K+v_{2})
for some
t\geq 0
. If
t=0
, then
x
is the only point of intersection and we have
x=x_{+}(0)=f(0)
. If
t>0
, then by Lemma
3.4
there are exactly two points that belong to the intersection
\partial(\alpha(t)K+v_{1})\cap\partial(\alpha(t)K+v_{2})
, one that lies in the half-space
L^{+}(v_{1},v_{2})
, and another that lies in the half-space
L^{-}(v_{1},v_{2})
. These are exactly the points
x_{+}(t)
and
x_{-}(t)
, so we have that either
x=x_{+}(t)=f(t)
, or
x=x_{-}(t)=f(-|t|)
. In either case we have proved what we need.
∎

3.4.
Characterizing
\textup{Haus}_{K}(T)
for triangles

The objective of this subsection is to study the set
\textup{Haus}_{K}(T)
in the case where
T
is a triangle, and
K
has strictly convex
C^{1}
boundary. We will begin with the following lemma due to Kramer and Németh
[
6
, Theorem 2]
that gives conditions so that a fixed convex body circumscribes a unique homothetic copy of a given triangle, or equivalently, a unique homothetic copy of a given convex body circumscribes a fixed triangle.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a triangle, and let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a strictly convex closed arc of class
C^{1}
. Then there exists a unique triangle
T_{1}
that is homothetic to
T
and is inscribed in the set
S
in the sense that
\textup{vert}(T_{1})\subset S
.

Remark 3.1
.

Lemma
3.6
applies in the special case where
K=B_{p}^{2}
is the unit
l_{p}
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
1<p<\infty
. The fact that
B_{p}^{2}
has
C^{1}
boundary follows from the fact that the function
|x|^{p}
of the variable
x\in\mathbb{R}
is
C^{1}
when
p>1
. The fact that
\partial B_{p}^{2}
is strictly convex follows from Minkowski’s inequality.

We will use Lemma
3.6
in the following way to classify a triangle into one of three types. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body with strictly convex
C^{1}
boundary, and let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a triangle. By Lemma
3.6
there exists a unique homothety
h
such that
h(K)
circumscribes
T
in the sense that
\textup{vert}(T)\subset\partial h(K)
. Let
c
denote the center of
h(K)
that satisfies
h(K)-c=c-h(K)
. We classify
T
as follows:

(1)

If
c\in\textup{int}(T)
, then
T
is a
K
-acute
triangle.

(2)

If
c\in\partial T
, then
T
is a
K
-right
triangle.

(3)

If
c\notin T
, then
T
is a
K
-obtuse
triangle.

The following lemmas show how to locate the points of
\textup{Haus}_{K}(T)
depending on the classification of
T
. We start with the case where
T
is
K
-acute (right).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a symmetric convex body that has strictly convex
C^{1}
boundary, let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a triangle with vertex set
\textup{vert}(T):=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
, and let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the unique point guaranteed by Lemma
3.6
that lies in the intersection
\cap_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
. If
x_{0}\in T
, then
\|x_{0}-v_{1}\|_{K}=d^{(K)}(\textup{vert}(T))
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there exists
y\in\textup{Haus}_{K}(T)
such that
y\neq x_{0}
. By Lemma
3.3
and uniqueness of
x_{0}
, the point
y
belongs to exactly one bisector
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
. Denote the remaining vertex by
v_{k}
. It follows from Lemma
3.2
that

d^{(K)}(\textup{vert}(T))=d^{(K)}(y,\textup{vert}(T))=\|y-v_{i}\|_{K}.

Using Lemma
3.5
we find a continuous function
f
that parametrizes
\textup{bis}_{K}(v_{i},v_{j})
, so that
y=f(t_{0})
for some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

First, assume that
y\in\textup{int}(T)
. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\textup{sgn}(t)

Putting the above cases together, we have the contradiction

3.5.

If
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a triangle and

is a symmetric convex body that has strictly convex

3.7
and

to locate the points of
\textup{Haus}_{K}(T)
. According to Remark

, we can apply these results in the case where

is the unit

-ball

with

. With the additional help of Clarkson’s inequality

, we will obtain a sharp

Lemma 3.10

(Clarkson’s inequality)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Then the inequality

\left\|\frac{u+v}{2}\right\|_{p}^{p}+\left\|\frac{u-v}{2}\right\|_{p}^{p}\leq\max\{2^{1-p},2^{-1}\}(\|u\|_{p}^{p}+\|v\|_{p}^{p})

holds for any
u,v\in l_{p}
.

It is worth noting that for our purposes we can also use Hanner’s inequality
[
8
, Page 49]
in place of Clarkson’s inequality in the case
1\leq p\leq 2
.

Our first estimate concerns the case where the triangle
T
is
l_{p}
-acute (right).

Lemma 3.11
.

For
1<p<\infty
, let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an
l_{p}
-acute (right) triangle such that two of the sides have
l_{p}
-lengths
a
and
b
respectively. Then the bound

d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))\leq\max\{1,2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\}\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}

holds for any such
p
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.6
, there exists a unique homothety
h
for which the ball
h(B_{p}^{2})
circumscribes
T
. Since
T
is
l_{p}
-acute (right), it follows from Lemma
3.7
that

d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))=\|v_{j}-c\|_{p}

for each

, where
c
denotes the center of
h(B_{p}^{2})
, and
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
are the vertices of
T
. We may arrange the vertices so that
a=\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{p}
and
b=\|v_{1}-v_{3}\|_{p}
. Since the required bound is symmetric in

and

, no generality is lost if we assume that the vertices
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
occur in counterclockwise order on
\partial h(B_{p}^{2})

Let

be the line that passes through the center

and is parallel to the side
[v_{2},v_{3}]
. Because

, the line

at points

. We label these points so that the points

occur in counterclockwise order on

, and the points

occur in clockwise order on
\partial h(B_{p}^{2})
. Define

and

We will use Clarkson’s inequality to obtain the desired upper bound. To do this, define

and

, so that
\|u\|_{p}=a^{\prime}
and
\|v\|_{p}=b^{\prime}
. We use the relation

to get

\left\|\frac{u+v}{2}\right\|_{p}=\left\|\frac{u-v}{2}\right\|_{p}=d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T)).

Apply Lemma
3.10

2\{d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))\}^{p}\leq\max\{2^{1-p},2^{-1}\}\cdot\{(a^{\prime})^{p}+(b^{\prime})^{p}\}.

Use the fact that

and

\max\{2^{1-p},2^{-1}\}\cdot\{(a^{\prime})^{p}+(b^{\prime})^{p}\}\leq\max\{2^{p-1},2\}\cdot\left\{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}\right\}.

The required bound follows immediately.

(2)

Choose

\alpha K+y\subset K

Lemma 3.13

-length at most

into a union of triangles

and

3.7.
Proof of sharpness

We end this section with an example that shows the bound (
2
) is sharp, and furthermore that the function
(d^{(l_{p})})^{2}
is not subadditive on compact sets when
p>2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The following statements are true:

(1)

If
1\leq p\leq 2
, then for any nonnegative real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=\alpha
,
d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\beta
, and (
2
) is equality.

(2)

If
p>2
, then for any positive real number
\alpha
there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\alpha
and (
2
) is equality.

(3)

If
p>2
, then there exist nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(d^{(l_{p})}(A+B))^{2}>(d^{(l_{p})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{p})}(B))^{2}.

That is, the function
(d^{(l_{p})})^{2}
is not subadditive on compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
1\leq p\leq 2
, so that
\max\{1,2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\}=1
. For positive
\alpha
and
\beta
, define

\displaystyle A=\{(0,0),(2\alpha,0)\}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad B=\{(0,0),(0,2\beta)\}.

Then
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=\alpha
and
d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\beta
. The sum of these sets is

A+B=\{(0,0),(2\alpha,0),(0,2\beta),(2\alpha,2\beta)\},

which is the vertex set of a rectangle whose sides have
l_{p}
-lengths
2d^{(l_{p})}(A)
and
2d^{(l_{p})}(B)
. We can easily compute

\left(d^{(l_{p})}((\alpha,\beta),A+B)\right)^{p}=\alpha^{p}+\beta^{p}.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\textup{conv}(A+B)
with
(x_{1},x_{2})\neq(\alpha,\beta)
. Then

\displaystyle\min\{x_{1},2\alpha-x_{1}\}<\alpha\qquad\textup{or}\qquad\min\{x_{2},2\beta-x_{2}\}<\beta.

It follows that

\left(d^{(l_{p})}((x_{1},x_{2}),A+B)\right)^{p}=\min\{x_{1}^{p},(2\alpha-x_{1})^{p}\}+\min\{x_{2}^{p},(2\beta-x_{2})^{p}\}<\alpha^{p}+\beta^{p}.

Using the above bounds with the fact that
\alpha=d^{(l_{p})}(A)
and
\beta=d^{(l_{p})}(B)
, we have

\left(d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)\right)^{p}=\left(d^{(l_{p})}((\alpha,\beta),A+B)\right)^{p}=\left(d^{(l_{p})}(A)\right)^{p}+\left(d^{(l_{p})}(B)\right)^{p}.

If
\beta=0
and
\alpha>0
, we take
A=\{0,2\alpha\}
and
B=\{0\}
. We compute
d^{(l_{p})}(A)=\alpha
and
d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\beta
. The sum of the sets is
A+B=A
, which gives
d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)=d^{(l_{p})}(A)
. The same idea works in the case that
\alpha=0
and
\beta>0
. Finally, if
\alpha=\beta=0
, we take
A=B=\{0\}
. In any case, we have shown that the bound (
2
) is sharp when
1\leq p\leq 2
.

Next, suppose that
p\geq 2
, so that
\max\{1,2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\}=2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}
. For positive
\alpha
, define

\displaystyle A=\{(0,0),(2\alpha,2\alpha)\}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad B=\{(0,0),(-2\alpha,2\alpha)\}.

Then we compute

d^{(l_{p})}(A)=d^{(l_{p})}(B)=\alpha\sqrt[p]{2}.

The sum of
A
and
B
is

\begin{split}A+B=\{(0,0),(2\alpha,2\alpha),(-2\alpha,2\alpha),(0,4\alpha)\},\end{split}

which is the vertex set of a parallelogram. Define
m:=(0,2\alpha)
to be the midpoint of the interval connecting the points
(2\alpha,2\alpha)
and
(-2\alpha,2\alpha)
. Then

\|m\|_{p}=\|m-(0,4\alpha)\|_{p}=2\alpha.

We also have

\|m-(2\alpha,2\alpha)\|_{p}=\|m-(-2\alpha,2\alpha)\|_{p}=2\alpha.

It follows from Lemma
3.7
that
d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)=2\alpha
. On the other hand,

2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\sqrt[p]{(d^{(l_{p})}(A))^{p}+(d^{(l_{p})}(B))^{p}}=2\alpha.

Then

d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)=2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\sqrt[p]{(d^{(l_{p})}(A))^{p}+(d^{(l_{p})}(B))^{p}},

which shows that the constant
2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}
is optimal.

We can use the above computation to verify the third claim of the proposition. We just observe that

d^{(l_{p})}(A+B)=2\alpha>\sqrt{2\left(\alpha\sqrt[p]{2}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\left(d^{(l_{p})}(A)\right)^{2}+\left(d^{(l_{p})}(B)\right)^{2}}

when
p>2
, so that
(d^{(l_{p})})^{2}
is not subadditive on compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for such
p
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

As in the previous sections, we consider a centrally symmetric convex body

Lemma 4.1

We begin by proving the lemma in the case where

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}u_{i}=0\qquad\textup{and}\qquad I_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}u_{i}\otimes u_{i},

where

is the

must be different from the vectors
\pm u_{j}
. Therefore, the collection
\{\pm u_{i},\pm u_{j}\}

\partial B_{2}^{2}\cap\partial K
.

If
\mathcal{E}(K)
is different from the ball

, choose a nonsingular linear transform

Before we state the next lemma, we need to give a formal definition of an arc of the boundary of a convex body. If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a convex body and

, then we can define a function

\kappa(u):=\frac{\hskip-16.00008ptu}{\|u\|_{K-c}}+c.

In fact, the function

in the sense that

\partial K=\{\kappa(\cos(t),\sin(t)):t\in\mathbb{R}\},

and the point
\kappa(\cos(t),\sin(t))
moves along

, then there is

such that

, and there is a minimal

to be the open arc of
\partial K
that contains all points

are distinct and occur on
\partial K

In the next technical lemma, we study the situation where

s_{2}>0
. Since

x-(a_{1}+b_{1})\in\textup{conv}(\{0,a_{2}-a_{1}\}+\{0,b_{2}-b_{1}\})=[0,r_{2}u]+[0,s_{2}v],

there exist
0<r<r_{2}
and
0<s<s_{2}
such that
x-(a_{1}+b_{1})=ru+sv
. Next, set
r_{1}=s_{1}=0
, choose
j\in\{1,2\}
so that
|r-r_{j}|
is minimal, and choose
k\in\{1,2\}
so that
|s-s_{k}|
is minimal. Then

\begin{split}(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}&\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(x,\{a_{1},a_{2}\}+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}))^{2}\\
&=(d^{(l_{2})}(x-(a_{1}+b_{1}),\{0,a_{2}-a_{1}\}+\{0,b_{2}-b_{1}\}))^{2}\\
&=(d^{(l_{2})}(ru+sv,\{r_{1}u,r_{2}u\}+\{s_{1}v,s_{2}v\}))^{2}\\
&\leq\|(r-r_{j})u+(s-s_{k})v\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=|r-r_{j}|^{2}+|s-s_{k}|^{2}\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2},\end{split}

where the last inequality is strict if either
|r-r_{j}|<d^{(l_{2})}(A)
or
|s-s_{k}|<d^{(l_{2})}(B)
. In the case that
|r-r_{j}|=d^{(l_{2})}(A)
and
|s-s_{k}|=d^{(l_{2})}(B)
, we reason that
r=d^{(l_{2})}(A)
,
r_{2}=2d^{(l_{2})}(A)
, and that
s=d^{(l_{2})}(B)
,
s_{2}=2d^{(l_{2})}(B)
.

We will assume that
B
has dimension
2
. The same technique will work in the case where
A
has dimension
2
. Since
d^{(l_{2})}(B)v
is contained in an interval which has endpoints in
B-b_{1}
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\delta\in\{-1,1\}
such that
d^{(l_{2})}(B)v+[0,\delta\varepsilon_{0}u]\subset\textup{conv}(B-b_{1})
. Choose a sequence
0<\varepsilon_{k}<\varepsilon_{0}
that decreases to
0
, and set
c_{k}:=d^{(l_{2})}(B)v+\delta\varepsilon_{k}u
. Note that
c_{k}\in\textup{conv}(B-b_{1})
. By definition of
d^{(l_{2})}
, the ball
c_{k}+d^{(l_{2})}(B)B_{2}^{2}
intersects
B-b_{1}
at a point
p_{k}
. Write

p_{k}=(\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k})u+(d^{(l_{2})}(B)+\beta_{k})v,

where
\alpha_{k}u+\beta_{k}v\in d^{(l_{2})}(B)B_{2}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(x,\{a_{1},a_{2}\}+(p_{k}+b_{1})))^{2}

\displaystyle=(d^{(l_{2})}(ru+sv,\{0,2d^{(l_{2})}(A)u\}+p_{k}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\min\{\|(ru+sv)-(p_{k}+0)\|_{2}^{2},\|(ru+sv)-(p_{k}+2d^{(l_{2})}(A)u)\|_{2}^{2}\}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(A)-|\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k}|)^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}.

Define

\mathcal{M}(\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}):=(d^{(l_{2})}(A)-|\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k}|)^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}.

Since the sequences
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are bounded, there exist
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
such that (up to subsequences)
\alpha_{k}\rightarrow\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{k}\rightarrow\beta_{0}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. If
\alpha_{0}\neq 0
, then

\mathcal{M}(0,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})=(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(\alpha_{0}^{2}+\beta_{0}^{2})-2|\alpha_{0}|d^{(l_{2})}(A).

It follows from (
43
) and the fact that
\mathcal{M}
is continuous in its arguments that

(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}\leq\mathcal{M}(0,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})<(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}.

Finally, suppose that
\alpha_{0}=0
, so that
|\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k}|
converges to
0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Choose
k
so large that
|\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k}|<d^{(l_{2})}(A)
. If
\delta\varepsilon_{k}=-\alpha_{k}
, then
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
and
\beta_{k}<d^{(l_{2})}(B)
. That is, (
43
) becomes

(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}<(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}.

On the other hand, if
|\delta\varepsilon_{k}+\alpha_{k}|>0
, then (
43
) becomes

(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}<(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}\leq(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}.

In any case, we have shown that the strict bound

(44)

(d^{(l_{2})}(x,A+B))^{2}<(d^{(l_{2})}(A))^{2}+(d^{(l_{2})}(B))^{2}

holds for any
x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)
. To finish the proof, choose
x_{0}\in\textup{conv}(A+B)
such that
d^{(l_{2})}(x_{0},A+B)=d^{(l_{2})}(A+B)
. Then substitute
x_{0}
for
x
in (
44
) to obtain (
41
).
∎

.

6.1.
Sharp global bound

The goal of this section is to show that Conjecture
1.1
is true when
K=B_{p}^{2}
for
1<p\leq 2
. We also show that the conjectured bound is an improvement of the bound (
2
) when
p>2
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any
1<p\leq 2
and for any nonnegative real numbers
a
and
b
, we have

(45)

\sup\left\{d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T)):T\in\mathcal{R}_{p}(a,b)\right\}=\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

If
p>2
, then there exist nonnegative real numbers
a
and
b
such that

(46)

\sup\left\{d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T)):T\in\mathcal{R}_{p}(a,b)\right\}<2^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

Proof.

If
a=b=0
, then both sides of (
45
) are equal to zero and we are done. Suppose that
a>0
and that
b=0
. Then any
T\in\mathcal{R}_{K}(a,0)
is an interval of
l_{p}
-length
a
, which gives
d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))=a/2
. It follows immediately that (
45
) holds. We use the same reasoning in the case that
a=0
and
b>0
. Finally, suppose that
a
and
b
are positive. It follows from Lemmas
3.11
and
3.14
that if
T\in\mathcal{R}_{K}(a,b)
, then

d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))\leq\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

As a result, we have

\sup\left\{d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T)):T\in\mathcal{R}_{p}(a,b)\right\}\leq\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

To see that this is equality, define the triangle
T_{0}=\textup{conv}((0,0),(a,0),(0,b)\}
. We see that the point
p_{0}:=(a/2,b/2)\in T_{0}
satisfies

d^{(l_{p})}(p_{0},\textup{vert}(T))=\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

If
p
is any point of
T_{0}
that is different from
p_{0}
, then we write
p=(p_{x},p_{y})
, where
\min\{|p_{x}|,|p_{x}-a|\}<a/2
and
\min\{|p_{y}|,|p_{y}-b|\}<b/2
. This implies that

d^{(l_{p})}(p,\textup{vert}(T_{0}))=\sqrt[p]{\min\{|p_{x}|^{p},|p_{x}-a|^{p}\}+\min\{|p_{y}|^{p},|p_{y}-b|^{p}\}}<\sqrt[p]{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{p}}.

As a result, we have
d^{(l_{p})}(p_{0},\textup{vert}(T_{0}))=d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T_{0}))
, which verifies (
45
).

Finally, if
p>2
we can consider the case
a>0
and
b=0
. As done above, if we take
T\in\mathcal{R}_{p}(a,0)
, then
T
is an interval with
l_{p}
-length
a
, so that
d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T))=a/2
. Therefore, we have

\sup\left\{d^{(l_{p})}(\textup{vert}(T)):T\in\mathcal{R}_{p}(a,0)\right\}=\frac{a}{2},

from which the strict bound (
46
) follows.
∎

6.2.
Inner radius

We will show that it is possible to generalize Theorem
1.2
to dimension
n\geq 3
if we consider the
inner radius
of a compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is the quantity

r(A):=\sup_{x\in\textup{conv}(A)}\inf\{\textup{rad}(S):S=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset A,\textup{ and }x\in\textup{conv}(S)\},

where we recall that the distance

\textup{rad}(A):=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sup_{a\in A}\|x-a\|_{2}

is the
outer radius
of the set
A
. Due to Carathéodory’s theorem, we can assume that the sets
S
in the definition of
r(A)
are the vertex sets of simplices.

Recall the Dyn–Farkhi conjecture (
5
), that the function
(d^{(l_{2})})^{2}
is subadditive on nonempty compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This conjecture holds if we consider the inner radius instead of the Hausdorff distance from convex hull. It follows directly from a result of Wegmann
[
5
, Theorem 2.15]
and a result of Cassels
[
5
, Theorem 6.1]
that

(47)

r^{2}(A+B)\leq r^{2}(A)+r^{2}(B)

for nonempty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. See
[
14
]
and
[
1
]
for the original papers by Wegmann and Cassels. The square subadditive bound (
5
) is true when
n=2
because we can use Theorem
1.2
to reduce to the problem of finding an upper bound for the distance
d^{(l_{2})}(\textup{vert}(P))
, where
P
is a parallelogram with constraints on its side lengths. For this reason, and the fact that the bound (
47
) is true for any
n\geq 3
, it is reasonable to expect that we can find an
n
-dimensional generalization of Theorem
1.2
in terms of the inner radius. We will show that this is possible below.

We need to define the following terminology in order to state the result. A
simplex
is a set
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is the convex hull of exactly
k
affinely independent points of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
1\leq k\leq n+1
. A
face
of the simplex
Q
is a simplex
F
that satisfies
\textup{vert}(F)\subset\textup{vert}(Q)
. Note that by this definition the simplex
Q
is a face of itself. The
affine hull
of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the smallest (vector space dimension) translate of a subspace that contains
S
. Finally, if
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are nonempty sets, then their affine hulls
\textup{aff}(A)
and
\textup{aff}(B)
are translates of subspaces
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
respectively. The sets
A
and
B
are
parallel
if either
V_{A}\subset V_{B}
or
V_{B}\subset V_{A}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

For an integer
n\geq 2
, let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty compact sets, and let

(48)

x\in\textup{conv}(A+B)\cap(\textup{conv}(A)+B)^{c}\cap(A+\textup{conv}(B))^{c}.

Then
x\in S_{A}(x)+S_{B}(x)
, where
S_{A}(x)
and
S_{B}(x)
are nonparallel simplices with vertex sets contained in
A
and
B
respectively such that
\textup{rad}(S_{A}(x))\leq r(A)
, and
\textup{rad}(S_{B}(x))\leq r(B)
. Moreover, the simplices
S_{A}(x)
and
S_{B}(x)
are minimal in the sense that if
S_{A}^{\prime}(x)
and
S_{B}^{\prime}(x)
are faces of
S_{A}(x)
and
S_{B}(x)
respectively, at least one of them proper, then
x\notin S_{A}^{\prime}(x)+S_{B}^{\prime}(x)
.

Proof.

Let
x
satisfy (
48
), and write
x=a+b
, where
a\in\textup{conv}(A)
and
b\in\textup{conv}(B)
. By definition of
r(A)
, there exists a simplex
S_{A}
containing
a
with vertex set in
A
such that
\textup{rad}(S_{A})\leq r(A)
. Similarly, there exists a simplex
S_{B}
containing
b
with vertex set in
B
such that
\textup{rad}(S_{B})\leq r(B)
. We have
x\in S_{A}+S_{B}
. Let
N_{x}(S_{A},S_{B})
be the minimum of all numbers
\textup{dim}(F_{A})+\textup{dim}(F_{B})
, where
F_{A}
and
F_{B}
are faces of
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
respectively such that
x\in F_{A}+F_{B}
. Then choose faces
S_{A}(x)
of
S_{A}
and
S_{B}(x)
of
S_{B}
such that
x\in S_{A}(x)+S_{B}(x)
and
\textup{dim}(S_{A}(x))+\textup{dim}(S_{B}(x))=N_{x}(S_{A},S_{B})
.

Assume for contradiction that
S_{A}(x)
and
S_{B}(x)
are parallel. Choose
v_{A}\in\textup{vert}(S_{A}(x))
so that
0\in\textup{vert}(S_{A}(x)-v_{A})
, and choose
v_{B}\in\textup{vert}(S_{B}(x))
so that
0\in\textup{vert}(S_{B}(x)-v_{B})
. By assumption, we must have

\displaystyle\textup{aff}(S_{A}(x)-v_{A})\subset\textup{aff}(S_{B}(x)-v_{B})\qquad\textup{or}\qquad\textup{aff}(S_{B}(x)-v_{B})\subset\textup{aff}(S_{A}(x)-v_{A}).

If the first containment holds, apply Lemma
2.3
with
L:=S_{A}(x)-v_{A}
and
V:=S_{B}(x)-v_{B}
to get

x-(v_{A}+v_{B})\in(S_{B}(x)-v_{B})\cup(\partial(S_{B}(x)-v_{B})+(S_{A}(x)-v_{A})).

The point
x-(v_{A}+v_{B})
does not belong to the first set in the above union, because otherwise
x\in v_{A}+S_{B}(x)
, which contradicts (
48
). As a result,
x-(v_{A}+v_{B})
must belong to the second set in the above union. That is,
x\in\partial S_{B}(x)+S_{A}(x)
.
It follows that
x\in S_{A}(x)+S_{B}^{\prime}(x)
, where
S_{B}^{\prime}(x)
is a proper face of
S_{B}(x)
. This contradicts the minimality of
N_{x}(S_{A},S_{B})
. We arrive at the same contradiction if the second of the above two containments holds, so consequently
S_{A}(x)
and
S_{B}(x)
must be nonparallel.

Since
S_{A}(x)
is a face of
S_{A}
, we have
\textup{rad}(S_{A}(x))\leq\textup{rad}(S_{A})
, and since
S_{B}(x)
is a face of
S_{B}
, we have
\textup{rad}(S_{B}(x))\leq\textup{rad}(S_{B})
. This completes the proof because
\textup{rad}(S_{A})\leq r(A)
and
\textup{rad}(S_{B})\leq r(B)
.
∎
