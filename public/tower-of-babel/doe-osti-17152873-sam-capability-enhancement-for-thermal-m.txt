 ABSTRACT

 Accurate and computationally efﬁcient representation of key thermal-ﬂuid phenomena is essential for
improving the transient simulation capabilities of liquid-metal fast reactors (LMFRs). This report addresses
an important modeling gap associated with thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation in large plena through the
development and integration of advanced data-driven turbulence models in the System Analysis Module
(SAM). The proposed approach retains the conventional numerical solution of the mass, momentum, and
energy equations while using neural networks to provide the turbulent-viscosity closure required by the
multidimensional ﬂuid solver. Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) thereby offer a means
to improve the ﬁdelity of reactor system simulations while retaining the computational efﬁciency required
for engineering-scale transient analysis.

 Two neural network architectures, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and transformers, are inves-
tigated and compared using CFD-generated training data. The results demonstrate that the data-driven model
can reproduce the turbulent-viscosity ﬁeld with substantially improved accuracy compared with the simpli-
ﬁed Prandtl mixing-length model currently used in SAM. The transformer architecture provides further im-
provement over CNNs, achieving a 32% lower RMSE than the CNN for the same benchmark problem. To
address the computational and memory requirements associated with the quadratic scaling of conventional
self-attention, a compressed-attention transformer is subsequently developed. The approach compresses the
full mesh representation into a ﬁxed number of latent tokens, reducing the attention complexity from O(N 2 )
to O(NL + L2 ), where L  N. The resulting model enables application to mesh sizes that are compatible
with the typical requirements of coarse-mesh reactor simulations.

 Finally, the AI-driven turbulence model is integrated into a system-level simulation of an advanced
sodium fast reactor. For comparison, a protected loss-of-ﬂow transient is simulated using three different
modeling approaches for the sodium hot pool: a 0D perfectly mixed volume, a 1D mixing and stratiﬁcation
model, and a 2D axisymmetric multi-dimensional model with AI-driven turbulence closure. The results
demonstrate that the spatial distribution of temperature within the hot pool can produce non-negligible
differences in natural circulation and reactor temperatures, highlighting the need for detailed simulation
capabilities for thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation, such as those developed in this work. Overall, the work
demonstrates the feasibility of integrating AI-based physical models into SAM and combining system-level
and multi-dimensional simulations to enable AI-enhanced multiscale modeling of nuclear reactor systems.

1 Introduction

 SAM [1] is an advanced system analysis tool for the transient safety analysis of advanced non-light-
water reactors (non-LWRs). It is developed at Argonne National Laboratory within the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) Ofﬁce of Nuclear Energy’s Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
program. SAM aims to advance physical modeling, numerical methods, and software engineering to enhance
its capabilities and user experience for reactor transient analyses. To facilitate code development, SAM
utilizes the object-oriented MOOSE application framework [2], together with its underlying meshing and
ﬁnite-element library, libMesh, and linear and nonlinear solver library, PETSc. These modern software
environments and numerical methods provide the foundation for continued advancement of SAM. The code
is continuously developed to incorporate new physics and phenomena relevant to advanced reactor concepts.
In the early years of SAM development, efforts primarily focused on modeling capabilities required for
liquid-metal-cooled fast reactor (LMFR) transient analysis. Over time, these capabilities have signiﬁcantly
expanded in scope and now address the modeling needs of nearly all types of non-LWR concepts. The goal
of the present work is to improve the transient simulation capabilities of LMFRs by addressing modeling
gaps associated with key thermal-ﬂuid phenomena. This report focuses speciﬁcally on the development of
advanced data-driven models for thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation in large plena, where multidimensional
ﬂow and heat-transfer effects can strongly inﬂuence system response during transients. Enhancements to
SAM for modeling inter-assembly heat transfer in SFRs constitute a complementary effort and will be
documented in a separate report.

 Thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation may occur when ﬂuid enters a pool or tank at a temperature signiﬁcantly
different from that of the surrounding bulk ﬂuid. In pool-type nuclear reactors, this phenomenon can occur
during a variety of transient scenarios. For example, in sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs), a sudden change
in core outlet temperature can induce stratiﬁcation in the upper hot pool, producing layers of sodium with
different temperatures and affecting the temperature distribution and heat transfer within the pool. This
behavior can inﬂuence important phenomena such as the transition to and establishment of natural circulation,
which is critical to passive cooling in pool-type SFRs and other advanced nuclear reactors. Consequently,
accurate representation of thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation is important for assessing the inherent safety
characteristics of these systems and requires appropriate mathematical models and numerical tools.

 Advanced and computationally efﬁcient thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation capabilities embedded within a
system analysis code are highly desirable for improving the accuracy of advanced reactor safety analyses while
reducing modeling uncertainties. Depending on the required ﬁdelity and available computational resources,
several approaches can be used to model stratiﬁcation. At the lowest ﬁdelity level, lumped-parameter
approaches use zero-dimensional (0D) volumes to represent the thermal inertia of the pool. The simplest
approach represents the entire pool as a single volume and assumes perfect mixing between the inﬂowing
coolant and the bulk ﬂuid. Although this approach is simple, easy to implement, and computationally
inexpensive, it signiﬁcantly oversimpliﬁes the underlying mixing and stratiﬁcation phenomena and cannot
capture effects associated with the ﬁnite spatial extent of the pool, such as transport delays that are absent
from a 0D representation. More sophisticated lumped-parameter models divide the tank into multiple discrete
sub-volumes, as implemented, for example, in the SAS4A/SASSYS-1 [3] code developed at Argonne National
Laboratory. However, such models are generally limited to relatively simple conﬁgurations, as demonstrated
in [4], and can be difﬁcult to extend to the complex geometries encountered in SFR hot pools.

 1

 At the opposite end of the ﬁdelity spectrum, computational ﬂuid dynamics (CFD) approaches can be used
to model large pools in three dimensions, providing a high-resolution representation of the multidimensional
mixing phenomena occurring within the pool. Multiscale modeling approaches couple a one-dimensional
(1D) system analysis code with a three-dimensional CFD code, allowing large and complex systems to be
modeled by representing the pool with a CFD model and the remainder of the reactor system with a system
code. This approach provides detailed information in regions where high ﬁdelity is required while retaining
system-level modeling capabilities elsewhere. One key challenge is the need to solve the governing equations
of a turbulence model. Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations can provide relatively accurate
predictions but are not well suited to SAM’s design goals because of their high computational cost. Instead,
SAM currently employs a zero-dimensional (0D) turbulence model based on Prandtl’s mixing-length theory.
While computationally efﬁcient, this approach has limited accuracy, primarily because of the difﬁculty in
reliably characterizing the mixing length, resulting in substantial modeling uncertainties.

 At an intermediate ﬁdelity level, one-dimensional approaches offer an alternative to both 0D and
three-dimensional models by providing a balance among accuracy, computational efﬁciency, and ease of
implementation within a system thermal-hydraulics code [4]. In previous work, we developed an advanced
1D model in SAM that combines 1D channels representing jets within a pool with 0D volumes representing
the surrounding bulk ﬂuid. Heat transfer between the 1D jets and 0D volumes is modeled using closure
relations speciﬁcally developed to represent different ﬂow regimes, including hot jets entering a colder pool
and cold jets entering a hotter pool. For a complete description of the 1D model and its associated V&V
campaign, the reader is referred to [5], [6]. Although the model demonstrated signiﬁcant improvements over
state-of-the-art approaches, the calibration of its closure relations requires substantial trial-and-error effort. In
addition, the closure relation was developed for hot pools with a vertical inlet jet entering from the bottom.
However, challenges remain in generalizing the model to all the other geometry conﬁgurations encountered
in reactor applications – e.g., the horizontal jets in the lower plenum of a SFR.

 With the rapid development of computing capabilities, artiﬁcial intelligence and machine learning
approaches are becoming increasingly promising tools for overcoming the limitations described above.
Their potential lies in both their low computational cost during inference and their ability to learn complex
physical relationships directly from high-ﬁdelity data. First, neural network models offer negligible inference
costs compared with the computational cost of PDE-based methods such as RANS approaches. Second,
training models on high-ﬁdelity data, such as CFD simulations, can achieve accuracy comparable to that of
high-resolution methods. This allows overcoming the modeling simpliﬁcations inherent to 0D methods, semi-
empirical 1D methods, as well as simpliﬁed turbulence models such as the Prandtl mixing length approach.
Several studies have investigated the use of neural networks and other machine-learning approaches to model
turbulent ﬂow phenomena. Although a complete review of the ﬁeld is beyond the scope of this report, notable
examples encompass several model architectures, including physics-informed neural networks [7], DeepONet
[8], Graph Neural Networks [9], and generative approaches such as diffusion models [10]. Applications to
advanced reactor analysis and integration into multiscale, system-level modeling, however, remain limited.
The goal of this work is to help close that gap and improve LMFR transient simulation capabilities by
identifying data-driven approaches suitable for representing thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation in large plena
and implementing them in SAM. Accordingly, this report describes the work performed over the past year to
develop AI-driven turbulence-modeling capabilities for that purpose.

 The report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the overall modeling approach and the archi-
tectures employed, including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in Section 2.1 and Transformers in

Section 2.2. Section 3 presents the incremental development undertaken to test, optimize, and demonstrate
SAM’s new capability. The organization of the document is not only chronological, but also reﬂects the
progressive improvements achieved throughout the work. More speciﬁcally, the CNN is initially demonstrated
to provide improved turbulent-viscosity predictions compared with SAM’s existing Prandtl mixing-length
model (Section 2.1). Then, the theoretical and benchmark advantages of transformers over CNNs are
examined, highlighting their greater potential to capture physical couplings over large domains (Sections
2.2 and 3.1). Section 3.2 then describes the architectural developments introduced to overcome one of the
major limitations of transformers, namely their signiﬁcant memory requirements. Thereafter, Section 3.3

section, the comparison with alternative models for thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation – namely 0D and 1D
approaches – is further discussed. Finally, conclusions are drawn in Section 4.

2 Model Description

 Many state-of-the-art neural network models for turbulent ﬂows rely on physics-informed approaches,
in which the residuals of the Navier–Stokes equations are directly incorporated into the model loss function
[7], [8], [9], [10]. Minimizing this loss function during model training is therefore closely related to solving
the governing equations. These approaches can directly predict the pressure and velocity ﬁelds of a ﬂuid,
potentially eliminating the need for a conventional ﬂuid-dynamics solver. In addition, high-resolution
approaches such as [10] can reproduce the effects of turbulence through the direct simulation of turbulent
vortices. While powerful and general, physics-informed methods still present signiﬁcant challenges compared
with conventional PDE solvers. In particular, training convergence becomes difﬁcult when multiple spatial and
temporal scales or complex three-dimensional geometries are involved. Consequently, their application has
so far been largely limited to simpliﬁed problems involving two-dimensional or highly idealized geometries.
This makes these models insufﬁciently mature for engineering-scale reactor analysis and, while they offer an
interesting path for future research, they were deemed unsuitable for near-term development. In this vein,
the approach adopted in SAM differs by relying on conventional ﬂuid solvers to solve the Navier–Stokes
equations, while using AI to provide closure relations for the turbulent viscosity only.

 As described in Section 1, SAM can perform coupled one-dimensional and multidimensional simulations
of nuclear systems, allowing different levels of ﬁdelity to be used where appropriate. In particular, 1D
simulations are used to model the overall power plant, while coarse-mesh 2D/3D simulations are employed
in regions where higher spatial resolution is required. A notable example is the hot pool of liquid-metal
fast reactors, where multidimensional simulation is required to capture thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation
processes. SAM’s multidimensional ﬂuid-dynamics solver relies on MOOSE kernels (in the Navier-Stokes
module) to implement the mass, momentum, and energy balance equations, with both ﬁnite-element and
ﬁnite-volume formulations available. As described above, a key challenge for this multi-scale approach is
the accurate description of turbulence. RANS models are relatively accurate, but they introduce additional
equations that must be solved, making them incompatible with the computational requirements of fast
system analysis simulations. Conversely, the simpliﬁed algebraic models currently used in SAM, such as
Prandtl’s mixing-length model, are limited by signiﬁcant modeling inaccuracies. Within this framework,
data-driven methods can be used to predict the turbulent viscosity while retaining the conventional PDE-based
solution of the mass, momentum, and energy balance equations. When trained on high-ﬁdelity simulation
data, AI models can potentially achieve accuracy comparable to CFD while requiring only a fraction of the
computational cost during inference.

 In this frame, AI-driven closure relations for turbulent viscosity were implemented as additional kernels,
following the standard implementation of MOOSE and SAM. These new kernels take as input the ﬂow ﬁelds
calculated by the multidimensional model, such as the velocity ﬁeld, and produce the corresponding eddy
viscosity to be used in the Navier–Stokes equations by the coarse-mesh ﬂuid solver (Fig. 2.1). However,
unlike traditional MOOSE kernels, which contain the residuals of the governing equations, the AI kernels
evaluate the eddy viscosity using a neural network. The neural network is pretrained on CFD data using
PyTorch, and the trained weights are imported into the SAM kernels using LibTorch. Note that this approach
does not alter the nonlinear solution procedure of MOOSE. The only difference is in how the eddy viscosity
is calculated. This general implementation includes two different kernels that enable the use of two different
neural network architectures: Convolutional Neural Networks, described in Section 2.1 and Transformers,
described in Section 2.2.

2.1 The Convolutional Neural Network Model

 The ﬁrst model implemented in SAM was based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [11], [12].
CNNs perform a mapping from d input ﬁelds, such as the velocity, pressure, and temperature ﬁelds, to one
or more output ﬁelds, such as the eddy-viscosity ﬁeld. In two dimensions, the convolution operation is
performed by sliding a square ﬁlter, or kernel K, over the input ﬁelds Q (Fig. 2.2):

                               (Q ∗ K)(x, y) = ∑ ∑ Q(x − m, y − n)K(m, n)                              (2-1)
 m n

where (x, y) denote the spatial coordinates of the ﬁeld, while (m, n) denote the indices of the kernel. The
convolution operation can be extended to three dimensions by replacing the square kernel with a cubic kernel.

 CNNs are particularly suitable for learning from 3D CFD data because their weight sharing and
sparse connectivity allow spatial features to be learned more efﬁciently than with conventional feedforward
neural networks. This simpliﬁes the learning problem compared with traditional feedforward approaches.
Speciﬁcally, this work employs a densely connected convolutional neural network (DCNN) (Fig. 2.3). Unlike
a conventional CNN, the DCNN is composed of multiple dense blocks in which each convolutional layer
is directly connected to all subsequent layers within the block. These connections improve the propagation
of both information and gradients through the network, facilitating training. In 3D, the network uses ﬁve

ﬂow variables as inputs: the three velocity components Vx , Vy , and Vz , pressure p, and temperature T . The
output is the turbulent viscosity ﬁeld μt . The DCNN uses an encoder–decoder architecture containing an
odd number of dense blocks. The encoding and decoding layers consist of convolutional layers separated
by ReLU activation. In the encoder, each convolution reduces the spatial resolution by a factor of two. The
decoder follows a similar structure but uses a transpose convolution to increase the spatial resolution by a
factor of two, ensuring that the ﬁnal turbulent-viscosity ﬁeld has the same spatial dimensions as the input
ﬂow ﬁelds.

 In [12], this DCNN model was applied to the time-dependent simulation of a sodium hot pool (Fig.
2.4). The considered transient is representative of a protected loss-of-ﬂow (PLOF) accident, in which forced
circulation is lost and the reactor is scrammed. Suitable boundary conditions are prescribed at the tank inlet
to simulate the transient (Fig. 2.5). In particular, the inlet velocity decreases exponentially to a small value, at
which point the ﬂow is sustained primarily by natural circulation. The inlet temperature, on the other hand,

6

initially decreases sharply following the reactor scram and subsequently increases due to decay heat as the
ﬂow rate decreases. Three approaches were compared: (1) a 3D CFD simulation using the Realizable k-ε
turbulence model, (2) a 2D axisymmetric SAM simulation using the DCNN turbulence model, and (3) a 2D
axisymmetric SAM simulation using the conventional mixing-length turbulence model (Fig. 2.6). Compared
to SAM’s mixing-length turbulence model, the AI model yields more accurate eddy-viscosity predictions.
In turn, this allows the multidimensional solver to calculate more accurate velocity and temperature ﬁelds,
in better agreement with the CFD solution. This demonstrates that neural network approaches can achieve
superior performance than simpliﬁed zero-equation of turbulence models.

2.2 The Transformer Model

 CNNs have two main limitations. First, the convolution operation aggregates information primarily
from local and neighboring points, implicitly assuming that nearby data are more relevant than data located
farther away in the domain. Long-range dependencies can be captured by sliding the convolutional kernel

 7

Figure 2.6: Comparison of the results. Left: CFD simulation. Center: SAM 2D simulation with DCNN
turbulence model. Right: SAM 2D simulation with mixing length turbulence model.

across the domain and stacking multiple convolutional layers. However, the preference for local interactions
remains an inherent modeling bias of the architecture. This can be a limit for the modeling of turbulence
which, albeit originating at microscopic scales, depends on macroscopic processes such as large eddies and
recirculation patterns. These, in turn, are inﬂuenced by the global geometrical features of the system rather
than solely on the nearest neighbor ﬂow conditions. Second, square or cubic convolutional kernels can be
applied directly to regular meshes but require additional treatment for unstructured meshes and complex
geometries involving corners and curved boundaries. It is therefore desirable to develop an architecture that
(1) can autonomously weight both long- and short-range dependencies from all the domain, learning their
relative importance directly from data, and (2) provides greater ﬂexibility in handling unstructured data.

 To address these issues, a new model was developed based on the transformer architecture. One
key advantage of transformers is that they transform the input data into a ﬁxed-dimensional feature space,
enabling the use of a consistent neural network architecture regardless of the number or arrangement of

8

spatial points. This feature is enabled by the core mechanism of the transformer architecture, namely self-
attention. Self-attention is a neural network algorithm that allows each point in a dataset to dynamically
weigh and incorporate information from all other points. Transformers build on self-attention as their
fundamental component. This can represent an advantage over convolutional or graph-based methods that
rely preferentially on local and neighboring points.

 Self-attention consists in a weighted sum of values, where the weights, called attention scores, are
derived from learned relationships between queries and keys, all computed from the input data itself:

The input to the self-attention mechanism is a feature matrix X of size N × d, where N is the number of mesh
points and d is the number of physical quantities used to describe each point, namely position x, velocity
u, acceleration a, and Reynolds number Re. This matrix is linearly projected into three separate matrices Q
(queries), K (keys), and V (values), using learnable weight matrices. That is,

                                   Q = XWQ ,        K = XWK ,        V = XWV ,                                (2-3)

where WQ , WK , and WV are trainable parameters shared across all input points [13]. The attention scores are
computed by taking the dot product between each query and all keys and dividing by the square root of dk ,
the dimensionality of the key vectors. These scores measure how much attention a given point should pay to
every other point. The scores are normalized using the softmax function and used to compute a weighted
sum of the value vectors. The result is a ﬂexible representation that can adapt to varying input sizes and
geometries, making self-attention especially well suited for modeling complex, non-local phenomena such as
turbulence.

 In a transformer, several self-attention layers are stacked both in series and parallel [13] (Fig. 2.7).
Each self-attention block is followed by a position-wise feedforward (fully connected) network, which
enhances the model’s capacity to capture non-linear relationships in the data. To facilitate training and
improve convergence, layer normalization and residual (shortcut) connections are employed throughout the
architecture [13]. The resulting architecture is preceded by an embedding layer, implemented as a fully
connected neural network that projects the input from a d-dimensional space into a higher-dimensional
latent space of size demb . This transformation serves to translate the physical input quantities into a learned
internal representation that can be effectively processed by the subsequent self-attention layers. A ﬁnal fully
connected layer transforms the demb -dimensional latent representation into a vector of predicted turbulent
viscosity values.

 A ﬁrst demonstration case of this new architecture was performed in [14]. As a simpliﬁed benchmark,
the transformer model is trained on a 2D tank geometry that captures the essential ﬂow features of a hot pool.

2.3 SAM’s 1D Model for Thermal Mixing and Stratiﬁcation

 In this section, SAM’s 1D thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation model is described [5] [6]. Although the
model is not data-driven, it is used in Section 3.3 and is therefore presented here for completeness. The 1D
model aims to capture the macroscopic features of thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation in a vertical pool. In the
hot pool of an SFR system, two primary scenarios can be identiﬁed:

 • Hot coolant entering a colder pool – This occurs when the core outlet temperature rises, either due
 to a power excursion or a decrease in the coolant ﬂow rate. Here, both the coolant jet velocity and
 buoyancy forces are directed upward (Figure 2.11a). Below the tank outlet height, a stable jet ﬂow
 forms, resembling a free jet with a visible conical shape and distinct temperature compared to the
 surrounding pool. However, above the outlet, the ﬂow changes direction due to the proximity of the
 ceiling or free surface, enhancing mixing between the jet and the pool.

 • Cold coolant entering a hot pool – This may happen when the core outlet temperature is signiﬁcantly
 lower than the hot pool temperature, such as during a reactor scram. In this case, the jet velocity and
 buoyancy forces point in opposite directions, resulting in an unstable jet ﬂow (Figure 2.11b). As a
 result, the jet only brieﬂy behaves like a free jet, before mixing with the pool in both the lower and
 upper parts of the tank.

 The geometric setup used to describe these ﬂow conﬁgurations is depicted in Figure 2.11c. The jet
ﬂow in the lower part of the tank is modeled as a vertical 1D channel, followed by a horizontal channel
representing ﬂow in the upper part of the pool. Conversely, the lower and upper portions of the tank are
modeled as 0D volumes exchanging heat with the 1D jet. While the velocity distribution and ﬂow shape
differ between the two scenarios (Figure 2.11a vs. b), capturing such ﬂow variations accurately and in a
time-dependent manner in 1D would require implementing corresponding analytical solutions (generally
unavailable) or performing their numerical simulation (cancelling any advantage in terms of simplicity and
computational requirements compared to CFD). This motivates the chosen setup shown in Figure 2.11c
as it simpliﬁes capturing the main features of both ﬂow conﬁgurations. To compensate for this geometry
simpliﬁcation, closure relations are adopted to distinguish between the different ﬂow patterns arising with hot
and cold jets.

3 Model Testing, Optimization and Application

 This section presents the incremental development undertaken to test, optimize, and demonstrate SAM’s
new capability. The discussion focuses on three aspects. First, CNNs and transformers are directly compared
using a common benchmark, highlighting the greater potential of transformers to capture the dependencies
governing turbulent viscosity. Second, the transformer architecture is further optimized to overcome one of
its major limitations, namely the signiﬁcant memory requirements associated with the self-attention matrix.
Third, the data-driven turbulence model is demonstrated in the system-level simulation of an advanced reactor
power plant, showcasing the feasibility of the proposed approach for multiscale simulation of nuclear systems.

3.1 Comparison in between the CNN and transformer models

 This section presents a comparison of the optimized CNN and transformer architectures for turbulent-
viscosity prediction. The adopted geometry is similar to the benchmark presented in the previous section
(Section 2.2, Figure 2.8), but only tanks with H/L = 1 are considered in this case. A total of 200 steady-state
simulations were performed, with the inlet vertical velocity varied between 0.01 and 10 m/s to cover a broad
range of ﬂow and mixing conditions relevant to reactor thermal-hydraulics. The model hyperparameters
were optimized using Optuna. For each architecture, the hyperparameter combination that produced the
minimum validation loss during the Optuna optimization was selected for the ﬁnal evaluation. Because
both models were trained using the same CFD dataset, input features, target variable, data preprocessing,
and train/test partition, the comparison directly evaluates the effect of the neural network architecture on
predictive performance.

within a ±20% relative error, denoted as P20. RMSE measures the overall deviation between the predicted
and reference turbulent viscosity, while P20 provides an engineering-oriented measure of prediction reliability
by quantifying the fraction of predictions that fall within a prescribed relative-error range. The metrics are

 The improved predictive accuracy of the transformer comes at an increased computational cost during

available CFD data, while the transformer provides a measurable improvement in predictive accuracy. The
higher P20 and lower RMSE indicate that the transformer is better able to reproduce the spatially varying
turbulent viscosity ﬁeld over the range of ﬂow conditions represented in the test dataset. This result is
consistent with the ability of the self-attention mechanism to account for interactions between spatially
separated points, rather than relying primarily on local spatial correlations as in the CNN – see Section 2.2.

3.2 Development of a memory efﬁcient algorithm using attention matrix compression

 In the previous section, it was demonstrated that transformers can achieve superior predictive perfor-
mance compared with CNNs. However, the scalability of conventional transformers is limited by their
substantial memory and computational requirements, which increase quadratically with the number of mesh
cells, O(N 2 ). This cost can quickly become prohibitive even for mesh sizes typical of coarse-mesh simula-
tions, which may contain thousands of cells. To address this limitation, a compressed-attention approach is

16

 Figure 3.1: Parity plots of the optimized CNN and transformer models.

developed in this work, in which the information from the high-resolution mesh is compressed into a ﬁxed
number of L learnable latent tokens, with L  N. A conventional transformer applies full self-attention to all
N mesh points – requiring an N × N attention matrix and O(N 2 ) computational and memory scaling. Con-
versely, the proposed architecture performs a cross-attention between mesh data and latent tokens (O(NL))
followed by a self-attention step restricted to the latent sequence O(L2 ). Consequently, the attention cost
becomes O(NL + L2 ), which is linear in N for ﬁxed L, rather than quadratic. The complete information ﬂow
and tensor dimensions are illustrated in Fig. 3.3. After pretraining, the developed architecture can be utilized
by SAM’s transformer kernel without requiring any additional code readaptation.

 To evaluate the scaling of the new architecture on a larger mesh representative of multidimensional
reactor calculations, the model was applied to a pool geometry containing 2,850 cells, as shown in Fig. 3.2.
The computational domain consists of a two-dimensional axisymmetric r–z slice of the tank. The cylinder
centerline corresponds to the r = 0 axis of symmetry, the inlet is located in the lower central region, and the
outlet is located on the side of the pool. For visualization purposes, the axisymmetric slice is also shown as
a 5◦ extruded sector. Compared with the benchmark cases presented in Sections 2.2 and 3.1, this reﬁned
geometry increases the number of mesh cells of approximately a factor 2.8. Because the computational cost
of the standard transformer scales as O(N 2 ), this increase in mesh size results in an approximately 8-fold
increase in the attention cost. In the present work, the number of latent tokens is ﬁxed at L = 64, reducing the
corresponding attention cost by a factor of N/L = 2, 850/64 ≈ 44 compared with full self-attention on the
entire mesh.

 The predictive performance of the latent compression transformer is presented in the parity plot in
Fig. 3.4. The results show close agreement between the predicted and CFD-derived turbulent-viscosity
values for both interpolation datasets (inlet velocity within training range) and extrapolation datasets (inlet
velocity outside of the training range), with most predictions falling in the ±20% relative-error band. For
the interpolation dataset, the model achieves an RMSE of 6.53 × 10−4 , with 95.37% of predictions falling
within the ±20% criterion. For the extrapolation dataset, the RMSE increases to 1.57 × 10−3 , while 97.01%
of predictions remain within the same relative-error band. These results demonstrate that the latent-space
formulation can retain the ability of the transformer to represent nonlocal turbulence-related ﬂow behavior
while substantially reducing the computational and memory costs associated with transformer training and
inference.

 This section demonstrates the application of the new data-driven turbulence model to the system-level
simulation of a complete nuclear power plant. To this purpose, the Advanced Burner Test Reactor (ABTR)

is selected as the case study. The reactor system is represented using a simpliﬁed ﬁve-channel model, with
each channel representing a different region of the reactor. Channel 1 represents the peak-power inner-core
assembly, Channels 2–4 represent the average inner-core, fuel-test, and outer-core assemblies, respectively,
while Channel 5 represents the reﬂector and shield region (Fig. 3.5 (a)).

 For comparison, the hot pool is modeled using three different approaches with increasing levels of
spatial resolution:

 1. Zero-dimensional model: at the lowest resolution, the hot pool is modeled as a 0D volume, assuming
 perfect and instantaneous mixing between the incoming jet ﬂow and the surrounding bulk ﬂuid.

 2. One-dimensional model: at an intermediate level of ﬁdelity, the hot pool is modeled using the 1D
 mixing and stratiﬁcation model available in SAM. This approach, described in Section 2.3, represents
 the incoming jet using two consecutive 1D channels (Fig. 3.5 (b)): the ﬁrst extends vertically from
 the core inlet to the outlet elevation, while the second is located horizontally at the outlet elevation.
 The two 1D channels are thermally coupled to two 0D volumes, with one volume representing the
 lower half of the pool below the outlet elevation and the other representing the upper half above the
 outlet. Heat transfer between the 1D jet channels and the 0D pool volumes is governed by the closure
 relations described above.

 3. Multi-dimensional model with AI turbulence model: at the highest spatial resolution considered, the
 SAM coarse-mesh multi-dimensional solver is used. A simpliﬁed 2D axisymmetric model of the hot
 pool is adopted, allowing the detailed jet ﬂow to be resolved while neglecting geometric features such
 as tank deviations from the cylindrical shape and outlet asymmetries (Fig. 3.5 (c)). The turbulent
 viscosity used in the Navier–Stokes equations is calculated using the data-driven approach described
 in this report, with the transformer architecture selected for this application. Notably, the mesh is the
 same utilized in Section 3.2. Thus, the compressed attention algorithm described in Section 3.2 is
 utilized to decrease the memory and computational requirements of the AI model.

A protected loss-of-ﬂow (PLOF) accident is simulated, characterized by simultaneous trips of the primary

and intermediate pumps according to Fig. 3.5 (top), while the reactor is scrammed and the reactor channels

 Fig. 3.7 shows the outlet temperature of each channel during the transient, together with the maximum
fuel temperature and the primary ﬂow rate. The results from all three models are nearly coincident until
shortly before the temperature maximum, i.e., until approximately t = 500 s. After this point, the 0D approach
predicts the highest temperatures for both the channel outlet and the fuel. This behavior can be explained by
the temperature distribution in the hot pool, shown in Fig. 3.8. More speciﬁcally, colder sodium accumulates
in the lower part of the pool, while hotter sodium rises above the outlet elevation due to buoyancy effects.
The sodium immediately adjacent to the outlet, which is the ﬁrst to recirculate through the loop, is therefore
at a lower temperature than the pool average, explaining the lower overall temperatures predicted by the
multidimensional model. This effect cannot be captured by the 0D approach, in which the hot pool is
represented by a single average temperature.

 This temperature stratiﬁcation also enhances natural circulation. The hot sodium entering the pool
encounters the colder sodium accumulated in the lower region, resulting in buoyancy forces. The accumulation
of cold sodium in the lower part of the pool magniﬁes this effect compared to the 0D case, due to the higher
temperature difference in between the entering ﬂow and the surrounding ﬂuid. This behavior is reﬂected in
the ﬂow-rate predictions, with the multidimensional model producing slightly higher ﬂow rates than the 0D
model. Similar trends can be observed in the 1D model results, which predict lower temperatures than the 0D
approach and closely align with the multidimensional results.

 Overall, although all three approaches predict similar qualitative transient behavior, the different
representations of thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation result in temperature differences of approximately 20 K.
As discussed throughout this section, these discrepancies arise from the detailed temperature distribution
within the hot pool, which can only be captured by a multidimensional model. This demonstrates the
importance of resolving thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation with sufﬁcient spatial ﬁdelity when predicting
system-level reactor behavior.

4 Conclusions

 This report documents work performed over the past year to improve the transient simulation capabil-
ities of LMFRs by addressing modeling gaps in key thermal-ﬂuid phenomena. The effort presented here
focuses on advanced data-driven models for thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation in large plena, including the
development and training of neural-network architectures and the integration of pretrained models into SAM.
The report compares the new AI capability with established approaches for representing thermal mixing and
stratiﬁcation across different levels of ﬁdelity and spatial resolution. On this basis, the following conclusions
are established:

 1. As demonstrated in Section 2.1, AI-driven closure relations, speciﬁcally CNN architectures, can
 achieve higher accuracy than simpliﬁed zero-equation turbulence models such as SAM’s existing
 Prandtl mixing-length model. This can be attributed to the difﬁculty of characterizing an appropriate
 mixing length across different geometries and ﬂow conditions, whereas AI models, owing to their
 universal approximation capabilities, can learn complex relationships from the high-ﬁdelity models
 used to generate their training data.

 2. As demonstrated in Sections 2.2 and 3.1, transformers can provide signiﬁcant advantages over CNNs,
 primarily owing to their ability to capture physical relationships across large and complex domains.
 This resulted in a 32% lower RMSE compared with a CNN model applied to the same benchmark case.

 3. While standard transformers are limited in scalability by their computational and memory requirements,
 Section 3.2 demonstrates that these costs can be substantially reduced through efﬁcient attention-
 compression algorithms. This enables the application of transformer architectures to relatively large
 mesh sizes representative of reactor coarse-mesh simulations.

 4. As demonstrated in Section 3.3, the multidimensional approach can have non-negligible effects on
 system-level simulations involving concurrent thermal mixing, stratiﬁcation, and natural circulation.
 Because these effects arise from the detailed temperature distribution within the hot pool, a multidimen-
 sional model provides the spatial resolution required to capture them. Accurate turbulence modeling is
 crucial in this context, and AI-based approaches can therefore provide a valuable means of accurately
 predicting the turbulent viscosity required by the multidimensional solver.

 Beyond this progressive comparison of modeling approaches, the system-level simulation presented in
Section 3.3 provides a ﬁrst application of the new AI capability to a full-plant simulation. The results demon-
strate the ability of SAM to perform multiscale, AI-enhanced simulations of nuclear systems, combining
the computational efﬁciency of system-level modeling with the increased spatial ﬁdelity provided by the
data-driven multidimensional turbulence model.

 Future development will extend the assessment of the data-driven thermal mixing and stratiﬁcation
capability, including validation against experimental data and and application to reactor geometries more
detailed than the two-dimensional axisymmetric conﬁgurations considered in this work. More broadly, this
capability supports the continuing improvement of LMFR transient simulations by combining ML-based
physical models with efﬁcient system-level analysis.
