Barriers and opportunities in pediatric ophthalmology: a cross-sectional survey among Brazilian ophthalmologists
Authors: Érika M. Pereira, Júlia D. Rossetto, Luisa M. Hopker, Barbara Venturin, Carolina P. B. Gracitelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric ophthalmology is an understudied field in many regions, including Brazil, where barriers to care persist. This study aimed to identify the barriers faced by general ophthalmologists and assess their interest in digital training tools. A cross-sectional online survey comprising 10 questions was conducted among 496 ophthalmologists affiliated with the Brazilian Council of Ophthalmology between September and November 2022. Data on demographics, practice characteristics, barriers to pediatric care, and interest in digital tools were collected and analyzed descriptively. A total of 315 ophthalmologists (63.5%), 73.4% (229/312) of whom had practiced for more than 10 years, completed the survey. Among the respondents, 32.1% (101/315; 95% CI 27.1–37.1%) did not treat children under five years of age, primarily because of insufficient pediatric training or the extended time required for pediatric consultations (78/101; 77.2%; 95% CI 68.4–84.6%). Conversely, 67.9% (214/315; 95% CI 62.8–73.1%) of the respondents reported providing care for children under five years of age. Across all respondents, 87.7% (263/300; 95% CI 83.6–91.0%) expressed interest in an online pediatric ophthalmology training tool, including 92% of those who were already treating children. Brazilian ophthalmologists are strongly interested in the adoption of digital tools to enhance pediatric care. Introduction

Globally, there is a significant challenge in ensuring that children receive proper eye examinations1–5. Several causes of this challenge have been suggested, including the lack of interest in pediatric ophthalmology training among ophthalmology residents or the relatively low monetary remuneration of the subspecialty1–4,6–10. This may be due in part to the complexity of comprehensive eye exams for children, which often decreases clinical productivity by reducing the number of patients seen, as well as the overall reimbursement for pediatric eye care1,8.

Inadequate management of the most common conditions in children, such as refractive errors and amblyopia, remains a major cause of preventable visual impairment. The authors of a Brazilian study examined 2441 low-middle income school children and reported that refractive error was the main cause of visual impairment in one or both eyes (76.8%), followed by amblyopia (11.4%)11. Childhood blindness not only poses a significant economic burden on the health care system but also has a profound impact on quality of life, particularly when considering the number of years of potential blindness12,13. Visual impairment in childhood is a major public health issue, with lifelong social and educational consequences. Untreated refractive errors and amblyopia can adversely affect school performance, psychosocial development, and future opportunities14.

Brazil’s Public Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS) faces persistent challenges related to the uneven distribution and shortage of pediatric specialists. Despite universal coverage goals, most pediatric subspecialists are concentrated in large urban centers, leaving rural and underserved regions with limited access to specialized care. Moreover, insufficient incentives and weak career development pathways contribute to the difficulty of attracting and maintaining pediatric specialists within SUS, particularly in the North and Northeast regions of the country. These disparities highlight the urgent need for targeted policies to strengthen pediatric training programs15–17.

At the same time, recent reports from the United States and United Kingdom have highlighted a growing workforce shortage in pediatric ophthalmology, with an insufficient number of specialists available to meet clinical demand18,19. The challenges associated with examining and managing young children have been identified as important barriers to recruitment into the subspecialty. Together, these factors underscore the need for scalable educational strategies capable of strengthening pediatric ophthalmology knowledge and clinical confidence among general ophthalmologists, particularly in regions with limited access to pediatric eye care specialists.

To address these challenges, a survey targeting ophthalmologists across Brazil was conducted. This approach directly assesses the perceived barriers and the potential for online training tools to mitigate these issues. This study advances the field by providing a national quantitative analysis of Brazilian ophthalmologists’ experiences and their perspectives. This investigation offers novel insights into systemic barriers, addressing an urgent gap in understanding how to strengthen the pediatric ophthalmology workforce nationwide.

Subjects and methods

This was an analytical, cross-sectional survey conducted among ophthalmologists affiliated with the Brazilian Council of Ophthalmology (Conselho Brasileiro de Oftalmologia, CBO). The study aimed to assess barriers to pediatric ophthalmology practice and to evaluate the interest of ophthalmologists in digital training tools.

Data collection was conducted between September and November 2022 using an online self-administered questionnaire distributed via WhatsApp to a national group of 496 ophthalmologists registered with the CBO. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal de São Paulo (UNIFESP/EPM) (protocol 69724523.6.0000.5505) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki20,21, Eligible participants included ophthalmologists and ophthalmology trainees affiliated with the Brazilian Council of Ophthalmology (CBO) who received the survey invitation through the organization’s leadership network. No geographic restrictions were applied. Ophthalmologists with fellowship training in pediatric ophthalmology and strabismus were intentionally included, as the study aimed to capture the full spectrum of perspectives regarding pediatric eye care and educational needs. Individuals who did not complete the survey or did not provide consent were excluded from the analysis.

Questionnaire design and validation

A concise 10-item close-ended questionnaire (Fig. 1) was developed specifically for this study to ensure conciseness and maximize response rates while minimizing respondent fatigue22-25. The questionnaire was purpose-built for this study because no validated instrument addressing barriers to pediatric ophthalmology practice and interest in digital educational tools was identified in the literature. Questions were generated from the study objectives and themes repeatedly identified in the pediatric ophthalmology workforce literature. The questionnaire was initially drafted by the lead author and subsequently reviewed by three pediatric ophthalmologists with more than 10 years of clinical experience To verify comprehension and identify potential sources of bias, a pilot test was conducted with five practicing ophthalmologists who were not included in the final sample. These procedures were undertaken to improve content relevance, clarity, and face validity of the questionnaire prior to implementation. The under-5 age group was addressed in the questionnaire because examinations of children in this group are considered the most challenging for ophthalmologists with specialties other than pediatric ophthalmology.

Data collection

The final version of the questionnaire was distributed electronically via a secure SurveyMonkey link. Each participant could submit only one response per device to avoid duplication. Responses were automatically recorded and anonymized to maintain confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported to Microsoft Excel® and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and responses, presented as absolute frequencies and percentages, with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated where applicable.

For group comparisons, Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate associations between categorical variables (e.g., whether participants treated children under 5 years of age). Odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% CI were calculated to measure the strength of associations between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

This comprehensive statistical approach ensured the robustness of findings and allowed for reliable interpretation of patterns and associations within the survey data.

Results

Respondents represented all five geographic regions. Subspecialties included strabismus/paediatric ophthalmology (7.8%), retina (18.4%), cornea and anterior segment (41.4%), glaucoma (23%) and others (23.3%).

Discussion

This nationwide survey provides the first comprehensive portrait of pediatric ophthalmology practice patterns among Brazilian ophthalmologists. The results confirm that most practitioners manage pediatric cases despite feeling inadequately trained, highlighting systemic and educational barriers that could be addressed through structured training initiatives and digital support.

In this cross-sectional study, the majority of the ophthalmologists provided care for children under 5 years of age. This suggests their potential to address pediatric ophthalmic needs, which may reflect a readiness to incorporate pediatric care into routine practice. In particular, professionals who provide care for children are more likely to understand the benefits of using a digital tool to assist in pediatric ophthalmological care than those who do not care for children. However, in this study, we did not assess the depth of the respondents’ previous experience in this area.

An analysis of ophthalmology fellowships and the job market between 2010 and 2013 revealed that on average, 75% of the positions in pediatric ophthalmology were filled, whereas 91% of the positions in other ophthalmology subspecialties were filled9. Additionally, recent data collected from the Brazilian Society of Pediatric Ophthalmology highlight the limited availability of pediatric ophthalmologists, with 279 specialists in Brazil (2024) compared with 1056 in the United States in 20228. This number corresponds to a prevalence of 1.37 pediatric ophthalmologists per million people in Brazil (data from two online public databases: the Brazilian Society of Pediatric Ophthalmology (https://sbop.com.br/paciente/encontre-seu-medico/) and the Brazilian Census updated in December 2023 (https://www.ibge.gov.br/)) versus 3.2 pediatric ophthalmologists per million people in the United States8. Despite this relatively high prevalence, nearly 90% of U.S. counties lack pediatric ophthalmologists to meet their population’s needs8.

Similar to our findings, a lack of necessary skills and lengthy consultations were also found to be barriers in a European study that included nonpediatric ophthalmologists in community-based clinics who treated pediatric patients5. Although the impact of reimbursement was not a primary concern in our investigation, other studies have highlighted it as a contributing factor1,3,4,7–9.

Approximately 90% of the ophthalmologists surveyed in our study expressed interest in digital training tools for pediatric ophthalmology. Nearly half of the ophthalmologists who did not typically treat children indicated that they would be more likely to provide care for this age group if such digital tools were available.

The proposed digital tool refers to an e-learning platform integrating complementary educational components, including video-based instruction, interactive clinical cases, practical examination simulations, and decision-support resources. To our knowledge, no standardized educational model specifically designed to address the needs of general ophthalmologists in pediatric ophthalmology has been established in the literature. Therefore, the platform structure was developed by experienced pediatric ophthalmologists based on the core competencies required for pediatric eye examinations and the most frequently reported challenges in clinical practice, particularly retinoscopy, spectacle prescription, amblyopia management, and clinical decision-making. The combination of theoretical and practical learning resources was intentionally designed to reinforce both knowledge acquisition and clinical confidence in a format that could be accessed asynchronously and applied to real-world patient care.

A recent web-based survey of members of the American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus revealed optimism among pediatric ophthalmologists regarding the potential of artificial intelligence to improve ophthalmic care26.

Additionally, a review of digital education in ophthalmology showed that web-based programs are useful for acquiring knowledge in ophthalmology27. Finally, as Burton et al.28 noted, “Technology and treatment developments offer new tools to improve eye health; thoughtful application is needed to maximize the potential to improve coverage, accessibility, quality, efficiency, and affordability”. In summary, a digital solution may be useful for addressing shortages in pediatric eye-care support.

Our team recently developed a digital solution called the CHildren Assistant Guide Eye (CHANGE) program, which was designed to offer comprehensive training in pediatric ophthalmology. This virtual learning platform aims to provide thorough instruction on conducting eye examinations for children. The program is currently being validated and refined as part of the lead author’s doctoral thesis, and the initial results have been promising.

Limitations of the present study include the restriction of one survey per electronic device, potentially allowing multiple submissions from participants using different devices, although this seems unlikely. In addition, the survey targeted ophthalmologists affiliated with the CBO, possibly skewing the target population toward a more engaged population. Nevertheless, the geographic diversity of the respondents, spanning 24 of Brazil’s 27 states, suggests broad representation.

The questionnaire was specifically developed for this study because no validated instrument addressing barriers to pediatric ophthalmology practice and interest in digital educational support among Brazilian ophthalmologists was identified in the literature. To improve content relevance and face validity, the survey items were designed according to the study objectives, reviewed by experienced pediatric ophthalmologists, and pilot-tested prior to implementation. The questionnaire was intentionally concise, comprising 10 predominantly close-ended items, to maximize participation, reduce respondent burden, and facilitate completion among busy clinicians. This design allowed the efficient collection of standardized quantitative data aligned with the study objectives; however, it also limited the depth of information obtained regarding participants’ motivations, experiences, and unmet educational needs. Although respondents selecting the option “other” could provide free-text comments, these responses were insufficient to support a formal qualitative analysis. Furthermore, formal psychometric validation, including assessments of construct validity and test–retest reliability, was not performed. Future studies incorporating validated instruments, additional open-ended questions, and qualitative interviews may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the barriers and educational needs related to pediatric ophthalmology practice. Finally, the self-reported nature of the questionnaire and the nonmandatory format of some questions may have introduced variability and response bias.

In conclusion, Brazilian ophthalmologists are strongly interested in adopting digital tools for pediatric training. Addressing these training gaps could improve access to pediatric eye care, particularly in regions with limited specialist availability. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating digital training tools and assessing their impact on service accessibility and patient outcomes.
