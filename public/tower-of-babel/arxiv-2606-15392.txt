Ring-induced localizations of nilpotent groups Thanks: The third author was supported by the Basic Research Program of the National Research University Higher School of Economics.
Sergei O. Ivanov, Georgii Kadantsev, Aleksandr Krasilnikov

Abstract
. For a commutative ring R R , we study the R R -localization functor on the category of groups, defined as localization with respect to the homomorphism ℤ → R \mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R . Our main result is that if R R admits a λ \lambda -ring structure (for instance, if R R is binomial) then the R R -localization of a nilpotent group is again nilpotent. In particular, taking R = ℤ p R=\mathbb{Z}_{p} , the ring of p p -adic integers, yields a new example of a localization functor that preserves nilpotency. Along the way, we characterize R R -local groups in terms of the R R -groups of Myasnikov–Remeslennikov and show that nilpotent R R -local groups satisfy a version of the Hall–Petresco identity.



Introduction

Recall that a localization on a category
C
is a functor
L:C\rightarrow C
equipped
with a natural transformation
\eta:\mathrm{Id}_{C}\rightarrow L
such that the two natural
transformations
\eta L,L\eta:L\rightarrow L^{2}
coincide and are isomorphisms.
Localizations arise throughout mathematics, including homological localizations
of spaces in algebraic topology, sheafification of presheaves in algebraic
geometry, and abelianization of groups in algebra.

A key example is localization with respect to a morphism
f:a\rightarrow b
. An object

y
is called
f
-local if the map
f^{*}:\Hom(b,y)\rightarrow\Hom(a,y)
is a bijection.
If the category
C
is locally presentable, then for every morphism
f
in
C

there exists a localization
L_{f}:C\rightarrow C
such that
\eta_{x}:x\rightarrow L_{f}(x)
is the
universal map from
x
to an
f
-local object.

This paper is part of a broader effort to understand localizations on the
category of groups and the properties they preserve. Several works have
addressed this subject; we refer the reader
to
[
Cas00
,
Lib00
,
BC03
,
Cha08
,
Iva23
]

for a detailed overview. We focus on a long-standing question posed by Emmanuel
Farjoun in the 1990s: whether every localization of a nilpotent group is again
nilpotent.

This problem was recently advanced in
[
AIP21
]
. We call a functor

weakly right exact
if it preserves reflexive coequalizers (the authors
of
[
AIP21
]
call such functors simply right exact, but we reserve the
term
right exact
for functors that preserve all finite colimits). They
showed that every weakly right exact localization maps nilpotent groups to
nilpotent groups, and that many classical localizations on the category of
groups are weakly right exact. They also exhibited localizations that are not.

For a commutative ring
R
, the
R
-localization

(0.1)

L_{R}:\mathrm{Grp}\rightarrow\mathrm{Grp}

is the localization with respect to the ring homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R
,
viewed as a homomorphism of additive groups. One of the simplest known
localizations on the category of groups that is not weakly right exact appears
to be the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-localization, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
denotes the ring of

p
-adic integers. This raises a natural question: is the

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-localization of a nilpotent group nilpotent? Our main result
answers this affirmatively in greater generality.

Theorem

(Theorem
6.6
)
.

For a commutative ring
R
admitting a
\lambda
-ring structure,
the
R
-localization of a nilpotent group is nilpotent.

In fact, we prove a more general statement: the same conclusion holds under the
weaker assumption that
R
admits a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure.

At the same time, Farjoun posed another, similar question: is it true that for
every localization
L
and every finite
p
-group
G
, the coaugmentation
G\rightarrow L(G)
is an epimorphism? For
R
-localizations the answer is affirmative: in her
(unpublished) thesis, Gemma Bastardas proved that
L_{f}
has this property
whenever
f:A\rightarrow B
is a homomorphism of abelian
groups
[
BC03
, Th. B, p. 27]
.

If
P
is a set of primes and
R=\mathbb{Z}[P^{-1}]\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
, then
the
R
-localization coincides with Baumslag’s
P
-localization, which is known
to preserve reflexive coequalizers. Thus, our main result yields an alternative
proof that Baumslag’s
P
-localization preserves the class of nilpotent groups.
It also gives an affirmative answer to a question posed
in
[
War06
]
, namely whether the
P
-localization in the
category of nilpotent groups coincides with the
P
-localization in the category
of all groups.

By definition, the
R
-localization of a group
G
is the universal map

G\rightarrow L_{R}(G)
to an
R
-local group, where an
R
-local group is a group that is
local with respect to the homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R
. The notion of an

R
-local group is closely related to that of an
R
-group, introduced by
Lyndon
[
Lyn60
]
and later refined by
Myasnikov–Remeslennikov
[
MR94
]
. An
R
-group is a group

equipped with an exponentiation map

(0.2)

G\times R\rightarrow G,\qquad(g,r)\mapstochar\rightarrow g^{r},

satisfying a list of axioms (Definition
1.7
). We show that a group

is
R
-local if and only if it admits an
R
-group structure and

\Hom(R^{+}/\langle 1_{R}\rangle,G)=0
(Theorem
2.11
).

A potential disadvantage of
R
-groups is that they may fail to be closed under
taking quotients by the center: we do not know whether an
R
-group structure on

G
always induces one on the quotient
G/Z(G)
, and we expect that in general it
does not. For a
\lambda
-ring

, following ideas of Hall
[

]
,
we circumvent this difficulty by introducing the notion of a

-group
(Definition

). Recall that
the Hall–Petresco identity expresses a product of powers of elements in a
nilpotent group in terms of words whose exponents are binomial coefficients
(Definition

). We call an
R
-group

a

-group
if a version of this identity holds for the

R
-powers of elements in every nilpotent

-subgroup of

, with the binomial
coefficients replaced by the
\lambda
-operations of

. In contrast to
arbitrary

-groups, these are well behaved under central quotients: we prove
that if

is a Hall–Petresco

inherits a

-group structure. The crucial step in the proof of our main
result is that every
R
-local group is a Hall–Petresco

-group
(Proposition

).

The

-groups form a quasi-variety over a suitable
signature
[
AMN23
]
.
When

is a

-ring, the Hall–Petresco

-groups form a

R
-local group and is closed under
quotients by the center. For an arbitrary ring

, we know of no such
sub-quasi-variety, and it is precisely this obstruction that confines our main
result to the
\lambda
-ring setting. This raises the following question.

Problem 1
.

For a commutative ring
R
, is there a sub-quasi-variety of the quasi-variety of

R
-groups that contains every
R
-local group and is closed under quotients by
the center?

The categories of

-groups and Hall-Petresco

-groups

admits a left adjoint

, the

-completion

; likewise, the forgetful functor from the category of Hall-Petresco

-completion functor
. As an intermediate step toward the main theorem, we

)
.

For a
\lambda
-ring

This proposition also bears on the work of Jaikin-Zapirain. The

[

]
contained an error at this point: it assumed that the

-completion of a nilpotent group is again nilpotent (see the proof of

; we expect that the argument can alternatively

To prove the Hall–Petresco identities for

-local groups, we develop a general
method for establishing polynomial identities in nilpotent groups. For a group

, let

denote the group of polynomial

(Theorem

)
.

Let

be a group homomorphism, and let

be a nilpotent group such
that the map

(0.4)

(0.5)

The question of whether
R
-localization maps nilpotent groups to nilpotent
groups can be reduced from arbitrary commutative rings to the case of an

E
-ring. Recall that a commutative ring

is called an

-ring if the

(0.6)

in other words,

is
R
-local. For
example,
\mathbb{Z}[P^{-1}]
and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
are

on the category of groups, the group

carries a

-ring structure, and there is a unique morphism of localizations

. We show that for every commutative ring

, the

-ring

(0.7)

is a quotient of

, and that the natural map

is an

). Consequently, if the

-localization.

If

admits a
\lambda
-ring structure, then

is a binomial ring
(Corollary

). However, not every

-ring is binomial. We
show that the subring

of

(0.8)

This leaves open the following problem.

Problem 2
.

Does

map nilpotent groups to nilpotent groups?

As an application of our results, we compute the
R
-localizations of free groups
and finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent groups. We show that, for a

-ring

and a set

(0.9)

where

is the free

,

]
and by

]

(Proposition

1.
R
-groups

In this section we introduce the notions of a weak
R
-group and an
R
-group,
the category
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}
of
R
-groups, and the
R
-completion functor

C_{R}:\mathrm{Grp}\rightarrow R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}

Notation 1.1
.

For elements
x,y
of a group, we define conjugation and the commutator by

(1.1)

x^{y}=y^{-1}xy,\qquad[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy.

We will freely use the following formulas:

(1.2)

[xy,z]=[x,z]^{y}\cdot[y,z],\qquad[x,yz]=[x,z]\cdot[x,y]^{z}.

Definition 1.2

(Weak
R
-group)
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. A
weak
R
-group
(or an
R
-group in the
sense of Lyndon) is a group
G
equipped with a function

(1.3)

G\times R\rightarrow G,\quad(x,r)\mapstochar\rightarrow x^{r}

satisfying the following axioms:

(R1)

x^{r+s}=x^{r}x^{s}
;

(R2)

x^{1}=x
;

(R3)

(x^{r})^{s}=x^{rs}
;

(R4)

(x^{y})^{r}=(x^{r})^{y}
,

for all
x,y\in G
and
r,s\in R
. These axioms imply the identities

(1.4)

x^{0}=1,\qquad(x^{r})^{-1}=x^{-r},\qquad 1^{r}=1.

Moreover, it is known that

(1.5)

xy=yx\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ x^{r}y^{s}=y^{s}x^{r}

for all
r,s\in R

[
MR94
, Property 2]
. A subgroup of a weak

R
-group is called a
weak
R
-subgroup
if it is closed under the

R
-power operations
x\mapstochar\rightarrow x^{r}
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
G
be a weak
R
-group and let
X\subseteq G
be a subset. Then the
centralizer
\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X)
is a weak
R
-subgroup. In particular, the
center
Z(G)
is a weak
R
-subgroup.

Proof.

This follows from (
1.5
): if
g
commutes with every
element of
X
, then so does
g^{r}
for every
r\in R
.
∎

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
G
be a weak
R
-group and let
X\subseteq G
be a set of pairwise commuting
elements. Then the weak
R
-subgroup
\langle X\rangle_{R}
generated by

is
abelian.

Proof.

Since the elements of
X
commute pairwise,
X\subseteq\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X)
, and
hence
\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X))\subseteq\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X)
. In
particular
\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X))
contains
X
and is abelian, as
any two of its elements both lie in
\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X)
and so commute. By
Lemma
1.3
,
\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(\mathsf{Cent}_{G}(X))
is a weak

R
-subgroup; therefore it contains
\langle X\rangle_{R}
, which is consequently
abelian.
∎

Definition 1.5

(

-homomorphism)

An

-homomorphism

. The category of weak

Let

The kernel of every

Conversely, if

is a weak

-ideal of

inherits a natural weak

is an

-homomorphism

Definition 1.7

(
R
-group)
.

A weak

-group

is called an

-group
(or an

-group in the sense

A weak

. The

(

-ideal)
.

Let

Proposition 1.9
.

If

-group, then the center

is a weak

By Lemma

Remark 1.10

(Universal algebra)
.

The category of
R
-groups can be described in terms of universal algebra (see
[
AMN23
]
).
Consider the algebraic signature
\sigma_{R}
consisting of the binary
multiplication operation, the constant symbol
1
, and a unary operation

(-)^{r}
for each

. Then the

-groups form a quasi-variety of

\sigma_{R}
-algebras, defined by the monoid identities, the identities
(R1)–(R4), and the quasi-identity (R5). That every object of this
quasi-variety is a group follows because the unary operation corresponding
to
-1\in R
assigns to each element its inverse.

Remark 1.11

(
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}
is locally presentable)
.

The category

of

-groups is locally finitely presentable. Indeed, a
cocomplete category with a strong generator consisting of finitely presentable
objects is locally finitely presentable
[
AR94
, Th. 1.11]
; the
category
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}
is a quasi-variety, hence cocomplete, and the free

-group on
one generator is a finitely presentable strong

[
AR94
, §3B]
).

(

(

If

Example 1.15
.

Regard

as an

-group with

for all

of a group

Definition 2.1

(Localization with respect to a morphism)
.

Let
C
be a category and
f:a\rightarrow b
be its morphism. An object

is called
f
-local

if

induces a bijection

(2.1)

f^{*}:\Hom(b,x)\overset{\cong}{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\Hom(a,x).

If

is locally presentable, then the full subcategory of

-local objects is reflective
[
AR94
, Th. 1.39]
, and the associated
localization functor is denoted by

(2.2)

L_{f}:C\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow C.

Recall that a
coaugmentation
of an endofunctor
\mathcal{F}
is a natural
transformation
\mathrm{Id}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
from the identity functor, and a

coaugmented functor
is an endofunctor equipped with a coaugmentation. The
functor

admits a natural coaugmentation
\eta:\mathrm{Id}\rightarrow L_{f}
for which

\eta_{x}:x\rightarrow L_{f}(x)
is the universal map from

-local object.
See
[

, §1C]
,
[

]
for further details.

Definition 2.2

(
R
-localization)
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. We regard the unique ring homomorphism

\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R
as a morphism in the category of groups; by abuse of notation, we
also write

for its additive group. Groups local with respect to this
homomorphism are called

-local
, and the associated functor is denoted
by

(2.3)

L_{R}:\mathrm{Grp}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathrm{Grp}.

Since
G\cong\Hom(\mathbb{Z},G)
for every group

, the group

is

-local if
and only if the map

(2.4)

\Hom(R,G)\rightarrow G,\qquad\varphi\mapstochar\rightarrow\varphi(1)

is a bijection; equivalently,

is

-local if and only if, for every

\varphi_{x}:R\rightarrow G

.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
G
be an
R
-local group. Then:

(1)

G
admits a unique
R
-group structure;

(2)

for every
R
-group
H
, every homomorphism
H\rightarrow G
is an

R
-homomorphism.

Proof.

Consider the function
G\times R\rightarrow G
given by
(x,r)\mapstochar\rightarrow x^{r}:=\varphi_{x}(r)
. We check that it is an
R
-group structure.

(R1) follows from the fact that
\varphi_{x}
is a homomorphism.

(R2) follows from
\varphi_{x}(1)=x
.

(R3) Fix
r\in R
and
x\in G
, and consider the homomorphism
R\rightarrow G
,

s\mapstochar\rightarrow x^{rs}
. Since it sends
1
to
x^{r}
, it coincides with

\varphi_{x^{r}}
, and hence
x^{rs}=(x^{r})^{s}
.

(R4) Consider the homomorphism
R\rightarrow G
,
r\mapstochar\rightarrow y^{-1}x^{r}y
. Since it
sends
1
to
y^{-1}xy
, it coincides with
\varphi_{y^{-1}xy}
, and hence

y^{-1}x^{r}y=(y^{-1}xy)^{r}
.

(R5) Suppose

commute. By (R4),
y^{-1}x^{r}y=x^{r}
, so
x^{r}

commutes with

for every

; similarly,
x^{r}
commutes with
y^{s}

for all
r,s\in R
. Consider the map
R\rightarrow G

r\mapstochar\rightarrow x^{r}y^{r}
. Since

x^{r+s}y^{r+s}=x^{r}x^{s}y^{r}y^{s}=(x^{r}y^{r})(x^{s}y^{s})
, it is a homomorphism

(xy)^{r}=x^{r}y^{r}

To prove uniqueness, let
(x,r)\mapstochar\rightarrow\psi_{x}(r)
be any

-group structure
on

. By (R1) and (R2), each
\psi_{x}:R\rightarrow G
is a homomorphism sending

to

, so
\psi_{x}=\varphi_{x}
.

Now let

be a homomorphism from an

-group

. The equality

holds because the maps

and

are homomorphisms

.
∎

Remark 2.4

Definition 2.5

(

-null groups)

For a commutative ring

for the quotient of the additive group

Proposition 2.6
.

A group is

-local if and only if it admits an

-group structure and is

-null.

Proof.

If

-local, then it admits an

). Moreover,

Conversely, assume that

Since the additive group of

and

3.
Polynomial functions

The goal of this section is to provide a general method for proving polynomial
identities in nilpotent groups (Theorem
3.5
). We will
use it later to show that the extended Hall–Petresco identities hold in
nilpotent
R
-local groups. We begin by introducing the notion of a polynomial
function, following
[
Lei02
]
closely.

Definition 3.1

(Polynomial functions)
.

Let
A
and
G
be groups and let
\varphi:A\rightarrow G
be a set function. For

a\in A
, we define the
left
and
right forward differences

D^{\ell}_{a}\varphi,D^{r}_{a}\varphi:A\rightarrow G
by

(3.1)

(D^{\ell}_{a}\varphi)(x)=\varphi(ax)\,\varphi(x)^{-1},\qquad(D^{r}_{a}\varphi)(x)=\varphi(x)^{-1}\varphi(xa).

The function
\varphi
is called
left polynomial of left degree at most

d
if

(3.2)

D^{\ell}_{a_{1}}D^{\ell}_{a_{2}}\cdots D^{\ell}_{a_{d+1}}\varphi\equiv 1

for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d+1}\in A
. By convention, the constant function

\varphi\equiv 1
is left polynomial of left degree
-\infty
.
Right
polynomial functions
and their right degree are defined similarly.

Remark 3.2

(Polynomial functions into nilpotent groups)
.

If
G
is nilpotent, then a function
\varphi:A\rightarrow G
is left polynomial if and
only if it is right polynomial
[
Lei02
, Prop. 3.16]
.
In this case we call
\varphi
simply a
polynomial function
, and we denote
the set of polynomial functions by

(3.3)

\Poly(A,G).

Moreover,
\Poly(A,G)
is a group under pointwise
multiplication
[
Lei02
, Th. 3.2]
. The left and
right degrees of a polynomial function into a nilpotent group may nevertheless
differ.

Remark 3.3

(Polynomial functions into abelian groups)
.

If
G
is abelian and
A
is any group, then a function
\varphi:A\rightarrow G
has left
degree at most
d
if and only if it has right degree at most
d
(although

D^{\ell}_{a}\varphi
and
D^{r}_{a}\varphi
need not
coincide)
[
Lei02
, Cor. 2.13]
. In this case we
call the common value simply the
degree
. Moreover, the functions of degree
at most
d
form a subgroup
of
\Poly(A,G)

[
Lei02
, Lemma 2.1]
, which we
denote by

(3.4)

\Poly_{d}(A,G)\leq\Poly(A,G).

Remark 3.4

(Composition of a polynomial function with homomorphisms)
.

Let
A
and
B
be groups, let
G
and
H
be nilpotent groups, and let

\varphi:B\rightarrow G
be a polynomial function of left degree at most
d
. Then, for
any homomorphisms
f:A\rightarrow B
and
f^{\prime}:G\rightarrow H
, the composite
f^{\prime}\circ\varphi\circ f
is a polynomial function of left degree at most

d

[
Lei02
, Prop. 1.10, 1.11]
. In particular,
f
and
f^{\prime}

induce a homomorphism

(3.5)

(f^{*},f^{\prime}_{*}):\Poly(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly(A,H).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f:A\rightarrow B
be a group homomorphism, and let
G
be a nilpotent group
such that the map

(3.6)

f^{*}:\Hom(B,G)\rightarrow\Hom(A,G)

is injective. Then the homomorphism

(3.7)

f^{*}:\Poly(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly(A,G)

is a monomorphism.

Lemma 3.6
.

Theorem
3.5
holds for every abelian group
G
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that the homomorphism
\Poly_{d}(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly_{d}(A,G)
is a
monomorphism for every
d\geq 0
. We argue by induction on
d
. The case
d=0
is
clear.

Assume that
\Poly_{d}(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly_{d}(A,G)
is a monomorphism; we show that

\Poly_{d+1}(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly_{d+1}(A,G)
is a monomorphism, that is, that its kernel
is trivial. Let
\varphi\in\Poly_{d+1}(B,G)
satisfy
\varphi\circ f\equiv 1
,
and consider the function
\xi:B\times B\rightarrow G
defined by

(3.8)

\xi(x,y)=\varphi(y)^{-1}\varphi(x)^{-1}\varphi(xy).

We claim that
\xi
has degree at most
d
in each variable. For fixed
x_{0}\in B
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\xi(x_{0},y)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x_{0})^{-1}\varphi(y)^{-1}\varphi(x_{0}y)

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x_{0})^{-1}\,(D^{\ell}_{x_{0}}\varphi)(y),

so
\xi(x_{0},-)
has degree at most
d
, being the product of the constant function

\varphi(x_{0})^{-1}
and the function
D^{\ell}_{x_{0}}\varphi
, both of degree at most

d
. For fixed
y_{0}\in B
,

(3.11)

\xi(x,y_{0})=\varphi(y_{0})^{-1}\,(D^{r}_{y_{0}}\varphi)(x),

and the same argument shows that
\xi(-,y_{0})
has degree at most
d
.

Next we show that
\xi\equiv 1
. If
x_{0},y\in\Im(f)
, then
\varphi\circ f\equiv 1
gives
\xi(x_{0},y)=1
. Fixing
x_{0}\in\Im(f)
and using the inductive hypothesis
together with
\xi(x_{0},-)\in\Poly_{d}(B,G)
, we conclude that
\xi(x_{0},y)=1
for all

y\in B
. Similarly, fixing
y_{0}\in B
shows that
\xi(-,y_{0})\equiv 1
. Therefore

\xi\equiv 1
. Since
\varphi(1)=\varphi(f(1))=1
and
\xi\equiv 1
, the function

\varphi
is a homomorphism, and the claim follows from the injectivity of

f^{*}:\Hom(B,G)\rightarrow\Hom(A,G)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We argue by induction on the nilpotency class of
G
. If
G
is abelian, the
statement is Lemma
3.6
. Assume that
\Poly(B,H)\rightarrow\Poly(A,H)
is a monomorphism for every nilpotent group
H
of class
c
; we show
that
\Poly(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly(A,G)
is a monomorphism for every nilpotent group
G
of
class
c+1
.

We must show that the kernel of
\Poly(B,G)\rightarrow\Poly(A,G)
is trivial. Let

\varphi:B\rightarrow G
be a polynomial function with
\varphi\circ f\equiv 1
. For fixed

g\in G
, consider the polynomial function
\psi:B\rightarrow G
given by

(3.12)

\psi(x)=[\varphi(x),g].

Since
\psi(B)\subseteq[G,G]
, the function
\psi
corestricts to a polynomial
function
\psi^{\prime}:B\rightarrow[G,G]
with
\psi^{\prime}\circ f\equiv 1
. As
[G,G]
is nilpotent
of class at most
c
, the inductive hypothesis gives
\psi^{\prime}\equiv 1
, and hence

\psi\equiv 1
for every
g\in G
. It follows that
\varphi(B)\subseteq Z(G)
,
where
Z(G)
denotes the center of
G
. Thus
\varphi
corestricts to a polynomial
function
\varphi^{\prime}:B\rightarrow Z(G)
with
\varphi^{\prime}\circ f\equiv 1
. By
Lemma
3.6
,
\varphi^{\prime}\equiv 1
, and therefore
\varphi\equiv 1
.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
G
is a nilpotent

-local group, then the map

\Poly(R,G)\rightarrow\Poly(\mathbb{Z},G)

is injective.

4.
\lambda
-rings and binomial rings

In this section we recall the definitions of a
\lambda
-ring and a normed
pre-
\lambda
-ring, and their relationship to binomial rings.

Definition 4.1

(Pre-
\lambda
-ring)
.

A
pre-
\lambda
-ring
is a commutative ring
R
equipped with functions

\lambda^{n}:R\rightarrow R
,
n\geq 0
, called
\lambda
-operations
, such that
for all
r,s\in R
the following axioms hold:

(
\lambda 1
)

\lambda^{0}(r)=1
,

(
\lambda 2
)

\lambda^{1}(r)=r
,

(
\lambda 3
)

\lambda^{n}(r+s)=\sum_{i+j=n}\lambda^{i}(r)\,\lambda^{j}(s)
.

Notation 4.2
.

For a commutative ring
R
, we write

(4.1)

W(R)=(1+tR[[t]])^{\times}

for the group, under multiplication, of power series whose constant term is
1
.
If
R
is a pre-
\lambda
-ring, then each
x\in R
determines a power series

\lambda_{t}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\lambda^{n}(x)\,t^{n}
, and (
\lambda 3
) is equivalent to
the statement that

(4.2)

\lambda_{t}:R^{+}\rightarrow W(R)

is a group homomorphism.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
R
be a pre-
\lambda
-ring. Then, for every
n\geq 1
, the map

\lambda^{n}:R\rightarrow R
is a polynomial function of degree at most
n
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
. The base cases
n=0
and
n=1
follow from
(
\lambda 1
) and (
\lambda 2
):
\lambda^{0}
is constant and
\lambda^{1}=\mathrm{id}

is a homomorphism, hence of degree at most
1
. For the inductive step, axioms
(
\lambda 1
) and (
\lambda 3
) give, for fixed
s
,

(4.3)

(D_{s}\lambda^{n})(r)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda^{n-i}(s)\,\lambda^{i}(r).

By the inductive hypothesis
\lambda^{i}
has degree at most
i
, and since

\lambda^{n-i}(s)
is a constant, the map
r\mapstochar\rightarrow\lambda^{n-i}(s)\,\lambda^{i}(r)

also has degree at most
i
. Hence
D_{s}\lambda^{n}
has degree at most
n-1
, and
therefore
\lambda^{n}
has degree at most
n
.
∎

Definition 4.4

(Normed pre-
\lambda
-ring)
.

A pre-
\lambda
-ring is called
normed
if it satisfies the following axiom:

(
\lambda 4
)

\lambda^{n}(1)=0
for
n\geq 2
.

Definition 4.5

(
\lambda
-ring)
.

A normed pre-
\lambda
-ring is called a
\lambda
-ring
if it satisfies the
following axioms:

(
\lambda 5
)

\lambda^{n}(rs)=P_{n}(\lambda^{1}(r),\dotsc,\lambda^{n}(r);\,\lambda^{1}(s),\dotsc,\lambda^{n}(s))
,

(
\lambda 6
)

\lambda^{n}(\lambda^{m}(r))=P_{n,m}(\lambda^{1}(r),\dotsc,\lambda^{nm}(r))
,

where
P_{n}
and
P_{n,m}
are universal polynomials with integer coefficients
(see
[
Yau10
, Examples 1.7 and 1.9]
).

Definition 4.6

(Binomial ring)
.

A
binomial ring
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-torsion-free ring
R
such that the binomial
coefficient

(4.4)

\binom{r}{n}=\frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-(n-1))}{n!}\in R\otimes\mathbb{Q}

lies in
R
for all
r\in R
and all
n\geq 1
.

Definition 4.7

(Adams operations)
.

Let
R
be a
\lambda
-ring. For every
r\in R
, the power series

\lambda_{t}(r)\in R[[t]]
is invertible, since its constant term is

\lambda^{0}(r)=1
. Consider the power series
\psi_{t}(r)\in R[[t]]
defined by

(4.5)

\psi_{-t}(r)=(-t)\,\lambda_{t}(r)^{\prime}\,\lambda_{t}(r)^{-1},

where
\lambda_{t}(r)^{\prime}
denotes the derivative with respect to
t
. The coefficients
of
\psi_{t}(r)
are denoted by
\psi^{n}(r)\in R
, and the maps

(4.6)

\psi^{n}:R\rightarrow R,\quad n\geq 0,

are called the
Adams operations
. The Adams operations are ring
homomorphisms
[
Yau10
, Th. 3.6]
.

Theorem 4.8

(
[
Yau10
, Th. 5.3]
)
.

A binomial ring carries a unique
\lambda
-ring structure in which all Adams
operations are the identity. Conversely, a
\lambda
-ring in which all Adams
operations are the identity is a binomial ring. In either case the

\lambda
-operations are given by
\lambda^{n}(r)=\binom{r}{n}
.

5.
Hall–Petresco
R
-groups

In this section we recall the Hall–Petresco identity
(see
[
CMZ17
,
War06
]
). Then, for a normed
pre-
\lambda
-ring
R
, we define the notion of a Hall–Petresco
R
-group and
show that a Hall–Petresco
R
-group structure on a group
G
induces one on the
quotient
G/Z(G)
by its center. Throughout this section,
R
denotes a normed
pre-
\lambda
-ring.

Definition 5.1

(Hall–Petresco words)
.

Let
F=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
be the free group on
m
generators, with lower central
series
\gamma_{k}(F)
,
k\geq 1
. The
k
-th Hall–Petresco word
\tau_{k}\in F
is
defined recursively by
\tau_{1}=x_{1}\cdots x_{m}
and the relation

(5.1)

x_{1}^{k}\cdots x_{m}^{k}=\tau_{1}^{k}\,\tau_{2}^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdots\tau_{k-1}^{\binom{k}{k-1}}\,\tau_{k}.

The key property of the Hall–Petresco words is that
\tau_{k}\in\gamma_{k}(F)

[
CMZ17
, Cor. 4.1]
. The
Hall–Petresco
identity
states that for every nilpotent group
G
of class
n
, all elements

g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
, and every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(5.2)

g_{1}^{k}\cdots g_{m}^{k}=\tau_{1}(\bar{g})^{k}\,\tau_{2}(\bar{g})^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdots\tau_{n-1}(\bar{g})^{\binom{k}{n-1}}\,\tau_{n}(\bar{g})^{\binom{k}{n}},

where
\bar{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
and
\tau_{k}(\bar{g})\in\gamma_{k}(G)
is obtained
from
\tau_{k}
by the substitution
x_{i}\mapstochar\rightarrow g_{i}
.

Definition 5.2

(Hall–Petresco
R
-group)
.

Let
R
be a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring. An
R
-group
G
is called a

Hall–Petresco
R
-group
if, for all elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
for
which the
R
-subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
is nilpotent of class at
most
n
, the identity

(5.3)

g_{1}^{r}\cdots g_{m}^{r}=\tau_{1}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{1}(r)}\,\tau_{2}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{2}(r)}\cdots\tau_{n-1}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{n-1}(r)}\,\tau_{n}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{n}(r)}

holds for all
r\in R
. We call (
5.3
) the
Hall–Petresco
identity for the
R
-powers of
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
. The full subcategory of
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}

spanned by the Hall–Petresco
R
-groups is denoted by

(5.4)

R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}^{\mathrm{HP}}\subseteq R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}.

Remark 5.3

(Equivalent definition of a Hall–Petresco
R
-group)
.

Later, as a corollary of one of our main results
(Corollary
6.5
), we will show the following: for a
Hall–Petresco
R
-group
G
, elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
, and
n\geq 0
, the

R
-subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
is nilpotent of class at most
n

if and only if the ordinary subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle
is nilpotent
of class at most
n
. Consequently, in the definition of a Hall–Petresco

R
-group, one may replace
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
by

\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle
.

Remark 5.4

(
R
-powered nilpotent groups in the sense of Hall)
.

If
R
is a binomial ring, then a nilpotent Hall–Petresco
R
-group is precisely
an
R
-powered nilpotent group in the sense of Hall
[
Hal57
]
.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
G
is a Hall–Petresco
R
-group, then its center
Z(G)
is an
R
-ideal, and
the quotient
G/Z(G)
is again a Hall–Petresco
R
-group.

Proof.

Proposition
1.9
implies that the center
Z(G)
is a weak
R
-ideal. Then
G/Z(G)
is a weak
R
-group. Let us prove that
Z(G)
is an
R
-ideal. Take

g,h\in G
with
[g,h]\in Z(G)
, and let
r\in R
. Denote by
\bar{g},\bar{h}
their images in
G/Z(G)
. Since
\bar{g}
commutes with
\bar{h}
, Proposition
1.4
implies that the weak
R
-subgroup
\langle\bar{g},\bar{h}\rangle_{R}
of
G/Z(G)
is commutative. Therefore the
R
-subgroup
\langle g,h\rangle_{R}
of
G
is nilpotent of class at most
2
. The Hall–Petresco identity for
the
R
-powers of
g,h
gives

(5.5)

g^{r}h^{r}=(gh)^{r}\,[h,g]^{\lambda^{2}(r)},

and therefore

(5.6)

h^{-r}g^{-r}(gh)^{r}=[h,g]^{-\lambda^{2}(r)}\in Z(G).

Thus
Z(G)
is an
R
-ideal.

We now show that
G/Z(G)
is a Hall–Petresco
R
-group. Let
g^{\prime}_{1},\dots,g^{\prime}_{m}\in G/Z(G)
lie in a nilpotent
R
-subgroup
H^{\prime}\leq G/Z(G)
of class at most
n
.
Let
H\subseteq G
be the preimage of
H^{\prime}
, and choose preimages
g_{i}\in H
of the

g^{\prime}_{i}
; then
H
is an
R
-subgroup of
G
. Since
H\cap Z(G)
is central in
H

and
H/(H\cap Z(G))\cong H^{\prime}
, the group
H
is nilpotent of class at most
n+1
,
and
\gamma_{n+1}(H)\subseteq H\cap Z(G)
. In particular
\tau_{n+1}(\bar{g})\in H\cap Z(G)
, and since
H\cap Z(G)
is an
R
-subgroup,

\tau_{n+1}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{n+1}(r)}\in Z(G)
. Projecting the Hall–Petresco
identity for the
R
-powers of
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
along
G\rightarrow G/Z(G)
, the final
factor
\tau_{n+1}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{n+1}(r)}
maps to the identity, and we obtain
the Hall–Petresco identity for the
R
-powers of
g^{\prime}_{1},\dots,g^{\prime}_{m}
.
∎

Remark 5.6

(Hall–Petresco
R
-groups form a quasi-variety)
.

In the language of universal algebra, the Hall–Petresco
R
-groups form a
quasi-subvariety of the quasi-variety of
R
-groups. Indeed, the condition
that
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
are contained in a nilpotent
R
-subgroup of class
at most
c
can be expressed as the vanishing of the
(c+1)
-fold commutators
of the elements obtained from
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
by the
R
-group operations.

Remark 5.7

(Hall–Petresco
R
-groups form a reflective subcategory)
.

For an
R
-group
G
, there is a universal
R
-homomorphism
G\rightarrow\mathrm{HP}(G)

to a Hall–Petresco
R
-group: the group
\mathrm{HP}(G)
is a quotient of
G

obtained by a transfinite construction in which each step passes to the quotient
of the previous one by all Hall–Petresco identities. Hence
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}^{\mathrm{HP}}

is a reflective subcategory of
R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}
.

Definition 5.8

(Hall–Petresco
R
-completion)
.

For a group
G
, we write
C_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}}(G)
for the Hall–Petresco
R
-group

\mathrm{HP}(C_{R}(G))
obtained from the
R
-completion
C_{R}(G)
of

. This yields an adjunction

(5.7)

C_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}}:\mathrm{Grp}\leftrightarrows R\hskip 0.50003pt\mathrm{Grp}^{\mathrm{HP}}:U_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}},

where
U_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}}
is the forgetful functor.

6.
Nilpotency preservation

In this section we prove, for a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring
R
, that if
G
is a
nilpotent group then the associated Hall–Petresco
R
-group

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
is again nilpotent. As a corollary we obtain our main
result: the
R
-localization of a nilpotent group is nilpotent. Throughout this
section,
R
denotes a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring.

Proposition 6.1
.

Every
R
-local group is a Hall–Petresco
R
-group.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
R
-local group, and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in H
, where
H\leq G
is a
nilpotent
R
-subgroup. Then
H
is also
R
-local, so
Corollary
3.7
shows that the map

(6.1)

\Poly(R,H)\rightarrow\Poly(\mathbb{Z},H)

is injective. By Proposition
4.3
, each
\lambda^{n}:R\rightarrow R
is
polynomial, and the composite of a polynomial function with a homomorphism is
again polynomial (Remark
3.4
); hence, for every
n
, the
map
R\rightarrow H
,
r\mapstochar\rightarrow\tau_{n}(\bar{g})^{\lambda^{n}(r)}
, is polynomial. Since a
product of polynomial functions into a nilpotent group is polynomial, both sides
of (
5.3
) are polynomial maps
R\rightarrow H
. They agree on

\mathbb{Z}\subseteq R
by the classical Hall–Petresco identities, so by injectivity
of (
6.1
) they agree on all of
R
. This is precisely the
Hall–Petresco identity for the
R
-powers of
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

For a group
G
, the map
G\rightarrow L_{R}(G)
induces a surjective
R
-homomorphism

(6.2)

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)\rightarrow L_{R}(G),

with kernel
r_{R/1}(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G))
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
2.11
and
Proposition
6.1
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For a group
G
, the canonical map
\eta:G\rightarrow C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
sends the
center into the center:

(6.3)

\eta(Z(G))\subseteq Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)).

Proof.

Let
z\in Z(G)
. Axiom (R4) implies that the centralizer
\mathsf{Cent}(\eta(z))
in

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
is an
R
-subgroup. Since it contains
\Im(\eta)
, which
generates
C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
as an
R
-group, we have

\mathsf{Cent}(\eta(z))=C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
. Therefore
\eta(z)\in Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G))
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Hall-Petresco
R
-completion of a nilpotent group of class at most
n
is a nilpotent group of class at most
n
.

Proof.

Recall that
\eta:G\rightarrow C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
is the universal
homomorphism to a Hall–Petresco
R
-group: every homomorphism
G\rightarrow A
to a
Hall–Petresco
R
-group lifts uniquely to an
R
-homomorphism

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)\rightarrow A
.

We argue by induction on
n
. If
n=0
then
G=1
, and the statement is clear.
Suppose the claim holds for nilpotent groups of class at most
n
, and let
G
be
nilpotent of class at most
n+1
. Then
G/Z(G)
is nilpotent of class at most
n
, so
by the inductive hypothesis
C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G/Z(G))
is nilpotent of class at most

n
. By Lemma
6.3
, the map
\eta
induces a homomorphism

G/Z(G)\rightarrow C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)/Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G))
. By
Proposition
5.5
, the quotient

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)/Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G))
is a Hall–Petresco

-group, so
this homomorphism lifts to an
R
-homomorphism

(6.4)

C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G/Z(G))\rightarrow C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)/Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)).

Both
R
-groups are generated by the image of

, so (
6.4
) is
surjective. Hence
C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)/Z(C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G))
is nilpotent of
class at most
n
, and therefore
C^{\mathrm{HP}}_{R}(G)
is nilpotent of class at
most
n+1
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
G
be a Hall–Petresco
R
-group, let
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
, and let

. Then the
R
-subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
is nilpotent of class
at most
n
if and only if the ordinary subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle

is nilpotent of class at most
n
.

Proof.

If
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
is nilpotent of class at most
n
, then so is
its subgroup
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle
. Conversely, suppose
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle
is nilpotent of class at most
n
. Since
G
is a
Hall–Petresco
R
-group, the inclusion
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle\hookrightarrow G
induces an
R
-homomorphism
C_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}}(\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle)\rightarrow G
whose image is
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
. By
Proposition
6.4
,

C_{R}^{\mathrm{HP}}(\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle)
is nilpotent of class at most

n
, and hence so is its quotient
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\rangle_{R}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

For a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring

, the

-localization of a nilpotent

Proof.

This follows from Proposition

and

.
∎

7.
E
-rings

In this section, for a commutative ring
R
, we define a quotient ring
R^{e}
that
is an
E
-ring. We show that the
R
-localization is the
R/1
-nullification of
the
R^{e}
-localization; consequently, many questions about
R
-localizations
reduce to the case of
E
-rings. We also recall the notion of a solid ring,
introduced by Bousfield–Kan, and show that if
R
is an
E
-ring, then
L_{R}
is
weakly right exact only if
R
is solid.

Definition 7.1

(
E
-ring)
.

A commutative ring
R
is called an
E
-ring
if the additive group

R^{+}
is
R
-local.

Remark 7.2

(Equivalent definition of an
E
-ring)
.

A commutative ring
R
is an
E
-ring if and only if the evaluation map

(7.1)

\mathrm{ev}_{1}:\End_{\mathbb{Z}}(R^{+})\rightarrow R,\qquad\varphi\mapstochar\rightarrow\varphi(1),

is a ring isomorphism. (This explains the “
E
” in the name: it stands for

endomorphism
.) Equivalently,
R
is an
E
-ring if and only if every group
endomorphism of
R^{+}
is multiplication by an element of
R
. Note that the ring
structure on an
E
-ring
R
is determined by the abelian group
R^{+}
together
with the choice of unit
1\in R
.

Remark 7.3

(Uniqueness of the ring structure on an
E
-ring)
.

If an abelian group
R^{+}
admits an
E
-ring structure with unit
1\in R^{+}
, then
a ring structure on
R^{+}
with this unit is unique. Indeed, a ring structure is
determined by a homomorphism
R\rightarrow\End_{\mathbb{Z}}(R^{+})
, which must be the inverse
of
\mathrm{ev}_{1}:\End_{\mathbb{Z}}(R^{+})\rightarrow R
.

Definition 7.4

(The
E
-ring of a ring)
.

By
[
Cas00
, Th. 3.7]
, for every localization
L
on the
category of groups,
L(\mathbb{Z})
carries a unique
E
-ring structure for which the
coaugmentation
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow L(\mathbb{Z})
is a ring homomorphism. For a commutative
ring
R
, we denote the
E
-ring associated with the
R
-localization by

(7.2)

R^{e}=L_{R}(\mathbb{Z}).

Since
R^{e}
is
R
-local, the map
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R^{e}
lifts uniquely to a map

(7.3)

q_{R}:R\rightarrow R^{e}.

Proposition 7.5
.

The map
q_{R}:R\rightarrow R^{e}
is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel

r_{R/1}(R^{+})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.11
and the identification
C_{R}(\mathbb{Z})\cong R
,
we have
R^{e}\cong N_{R/1}(R^{+})
, and hence
R^{e}\cong R^{+}/r_{R/1}(R^{+})

(see (
2.9
)), with
q_{R}
the canonical projection. A straightforward
transfinite induction shows that
r_{R/1}^{\alpha}(R^{+})
is an ideal of
R
for
every ordinal
\alpha
; therefore
r_{R/1}(R^{+})
is an ideal of
R
, and
R^{e}
is
a quotient ring of
R
. By Remark
7.3
, this quotient-ring
structure on

coincides with its
E
-ring structure.
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

The abelian group
(R^{e})^{+}
is the
R/1
-nullification of
R^{+}
.

Remark 7.7
.

If
R
is an
E
-ring, then
q_{R}
is an isomorphism
R\cong R^{e}
. Indeed, in this
case
R^{+}
is
R
-local, hence
R/1
-null, so
r_{R/1}(R^{+})=0
. It follows that
for every commutative ring

,

(7.4)

L_{R^{e}}(\mathbb{Z})\cong R^{e}.

Proposition 7.8
.

A group is
R
-local if and only if it is both
R^{e}
-local and
R/1
-null.
Moreover, for every group
G
, the map
G\rightarrow L_{R}(G)
induces an epimorphism

(7.5)

L_{R^{e}}(G)\rightarrow L_{R}(G)

with kernel
r_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G))
. In other words, there is an isomorphism of

-groups

(7.6)

L_{R}(G)\cong N_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G)).

Proof.

Let
G
be an
R
-local group. Since
\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow R^{e}
is the universal map to an

R
-local group, the map
\Hom(R^{e},G)\rightarrow\Hom(\mathbb{Z},G)
is a bijection; thus
G

is
R^{e}
-local, and it is
R/1
-null by Theorem
2.11
.

Conversely, assume that
G
is
R^{e}
-local and
R/1
-null. Then
G
admits an

R^{e}
-group structure, and the homomorphism
q_{R}:R\rightarrow R^{e}
pulls it back to an

R
-group structure on

. By Theorem
2.11
,
G
is

R
-local.

For the functorial statement, let
X
be an
R
-local group,
\tilde{X}
be the group

treated as an
R
-group and

be any group. Note that

is an

-group, and hence, it is

-local. Then using Proposition

(2), we obtain

(7.7)

\displaystyle\Hom_{\mathrm{Grp}}(U_{R}(N_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G))),X)

\displaystyle=\Hom_{R\hskip 0.40974pt\mathrm{Grp}}(N_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G)),\tilde{X})

(7.8)

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{R\hskip 0.40974pt\mathrm{Grp}}(L_{R^{e}}(G),\tilde{X})

(7.9)

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{R^{e}\mathrm{Grp}}(L_{R^{e}}(G),\tilde{X})

(7.10)

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{\mathrm{Grp}}(L_{R^{e}}(G),X)

Therefore,
G\rightarrow U_{R}(N_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G)))
is the

-localization:
L_{R}(G)\cong U_{R}(N_{R/1}(L_{R^{e}}(G)))
.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

Since every
R
-local group

is also

-local, the map
\Hom(R^{e},G)\rightarrow\Hom(R,G)
is a bijection. Therefore

induces an isomorphism

Lemma 7.10
.

For an
E
-ring
R
and a free abelian group

, there is a natural isomorphism

(7.13)

L_{R}(A)\cong A\otimes R.

Proof.

Let
X
be a set; we show that
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X}\rightarrow R^{\oplus X}
is the

R
-localization. Being reflective, the subcategory of
R
-local groups is closed
under limits, and in particular under products. Since
R
is an
E
-ring, the
additive group
R^{+}
is
R
-local, so the product
R^{X}
is
R
-local as well.
Finally,
R^{\oplus X}
is an
R
-subgroup of the

-local group

, and hence
is itself
R
-local (Corollary

).

Because

takes abelian groups to abelian groups, it suffices to check that

\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X}\rightarrow R^{\oplus X}
is a universal map to an abelian

-local
group. Let

be an
R
-local abelian group. Then

(7.14)

\Hom(R^{\oplus X},G)\cong\Hom(R,G)^{X}\cong\Hom(\mathbb{Z},G)^{X}\cong\Hom(\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X},G),

where the middle isomorphism uses that

is
R
-local. Therefore

\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus X}\rightarrow R^{\oplus X}
is the

-localization.
∎

Definition 7.11

(Solid ring)
.

A commutative ring
R
is called

if the multiplication map

R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R\rightarrow R
is an isomorphism. For example,
\mathbb{Z}[P^{-1}]
is
solid, whereas
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is not.

Lemma 7.12
.

Every solid ring is an
E
-ring.

Proof.

Let
f:R^{+}\rightarrow R^{+}
be a group homomorphism. Then
R\otimes f:R\otimes R^{+}\rightarrow R\otimes R^{+}
is an
R
-module homomorphism, and under the isomorphism
R\otimes R^{+}\cong R^{+}
(which holds since
R
is solid) it is identified with
f
. Hence

f
is an
R
-module homomorphism, so
f(r)=f(1)\,r
for all
r\in R
; that is,

f
is multiplication by
f(1)
.
∎

Definition 7.13

(Weakly right exact functor)
.

Let
C
and
D
be categories admitting reflexive coequalizers. A functor

is called

if it preserves reflexive
coequalizers. Many equivalent characterizations of the weakly right exact
endofunctors of the category of groups can be found
in
[

.

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
R
be an
E
-ring. If

is weakly right exact, then

is solid.

Proof.

Choose an exact sequence
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow R\rightarrow 0
with
A
and
B
free abelian. Since

L_{R}
is weakly right exact, the sequence
L_{R}(A)\rightarrow L_{R}(B)\rightarrow L_{R}(R)\rightarrow 0
is
again exact
[
AIP21
, Lemma 3.2]
. By
Lemma
7.10
,
L_{R}(A)\cong A\otimes R
and

L_{R}(B)\cong B\otimes R
, and by Remark
7.9
,
L_{R}(R)\cong R
.
Therefore the sequence
A\otimes R\rightarrow B\otimes R\rightarrow R\rightarrow 0
is exact. Since

-\otimes R
is right exact, applying it to

shows that the

; comparing with the

∎

Corollary 7.15

8.
\lambda
-ring structures on
E
-rings

In this section we show that an
E
-ring admits a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring
structure if and only if it is binomial. We also show that if a commutative ring

R
admits a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure, then
R^{e}
is binomial, and we
give an example of an
E
-ring that is not binomial.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
R
be an
E
-ring. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

R
admits a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure;

(2)

R
is binomial;

(3)

R
admits a unique
\lambda
-ring structure.

Proof.

First we show that any normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure on
R
is automatically
a
\lambda
-ring structure; that is, it satisfies the polynomial identities
(
\lambda 5
) and (
\lambda 6
). Since
R
is an
E
-ring and

L_{R}(\mathbb{Z}^{m})\cong R^{m}
for every
m\geq 1

(Lemma
7.10
),
Theorem
3.5
shows that the map

\Poly(R^{m},R)\rightarrow\Poly(\mathbb{Z}^{m},R)
is injective; it therefore suffices to
verify these identities for integer arguments. Axioms (
\lambda 1
)–(
\lambda 4
)
give
\lambda^{n}(r+1)=\lambda^{n}(r)+\lambda^{n-1}(r)
for all
r\in R
, from
which it follows by induction that
\lambda^{n}(k\cdot 1_{R})=\binom{k}{n}\,1_{R}

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, on integer arguments, both sides of (
\lambda 5
)
and (
\lambda 6
) reduce to the corresponding identities for binomial
coefficients, which hold. Hence every normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure on
R

is a
\lambda
-ring structure. In particular, (1) implies (3); the converse is
trivial, since every
\lambda
-ring is by definition a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring.

Now assume that
R
is a
\lambda
-ring. Since the Adams operations
\psi^{n}:R\rightarrow R
are additive and
R
is an
E
-ring, we have
\psi^{n}(r)=\psi^{n}(1)\,r=r

for all
r\in R
. By Theorem
4.8
,
R
is therefore binomial,
and any
\lambda
-ring structure on
R
is given by the binomial coefficients

\lambda^{n}(r)=\binom{r}{n}
. This proves that (3) implies (2). Finally, a
binomial ring carries a
\lambda
-ring structure
(Theorem
4.8
), which is in particular a normed
pre-
\lambda
-ring structure, so (2) implies (1).
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
R
be a
E
-ring. Then
W(R)
is
R/1
-null.

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
, set
U_{n}=1+t^{n}R[[t]]
. There is a short exact sequence

(8.1)

0\rightarrow R^{+}\rightarrow W(R)/U_{n+1}\rightarrow W(R)/U_{n}\rightarrow 0

and an isomorphism
W(R)\cong\varprojlim_{n}W(R)/U_{n}
. The statement therefore
follows from the fact that the class of
R/1
-null groups is closed under extensions and limits.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Any normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure on
R
induces a
\lambda
-ring structure
on
R^{e}
for which
q_{R}:R\rightarrow R^{e}
is a morphism of pre-
\lambda
-rings.

Proof.

The group
(R^{e})^{+}
is
R/1
-null by Theorem
2.11
, so

W(R^{e})
is
R/1
-null by Lemma
8.2
. Therefore the composite

(8.2)

R^{+}\xrightarrow{\lambda_{t}}W(R)\xrightarrow{W(q_{R})}W(R^{e})

factors uniquely through the
R/1
-nullification of
R^{+}
, which is
(R^{e})^{+}

(Corollary
7.6
). This yields a homomorphism
\lambda_{t}^{e}:(R^{e})^{+}\rightarrow W(R^{e})
with
\lambda_{t}^{e}\circ q_{R}=W(q_{R})\circ\lambda_{t}
. The
homomorphism
\lambda_{t}^{e}
defines a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure on
R^{e}
,
and the identity
\lambda_{t}^{e}\circ q_{R}=W(q_{R})\circ\lambda_{t}
says exactly
that
q_{R}
is a morphism of pre-
\lambda
-rings. Since
R^{e}
is an
E
-ring,
Proposition
8.1
shows that this structure is a

\lambda
-ring structure.
∎

Corollary 8.4
.

If
R
admits a normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure, then
R^{e}
is binomial.

Proof.

This follows from Propositions
8.3

and
8.1
.
∎

Proposition 8.5
.

Fix
\sqrt{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}
, and let
\mathcal{E}
be the
7
-saturation of

\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
in
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
:

(8.3)

\mathcal{E}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}\mid\exists\,n\geq 0:7^{n}x\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]\}.

Then
\mathcal{E}
is a countable,
\mathbb{Z}
-torsion-free
E
-ring that admits no
normed pre-
\lambda
-ring structure. Moreover,
\mathcal{E}
is not solid.

Proof.

An element
\sqrt{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}
exists by Hensel’s lemma, since

3^{2}\equiv 2\pmod{7}
. As
\mathcal{E}
is contained in the image of

\mathbb{Z}[1/7,\sqrt{2}]
in
\mathbb{Q}_{7}
, it is countable, and it is

\mathbb{Z}
-torsion-free because it is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
.

We first show that
\mathcal{E}
is an
E
-ring. The subgroup

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{7}
is
7
-saturated, i.e.
\mathcal{E}\cap 7^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{7}=7^{m}\mathcal{E}
: indeed, if
x\in\mathcal{E}\cap 7^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{7}

then
7^{-m}x\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}
, and if
7^{n}x\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
then

7^{n}(7^{-m}x)\cdot 7^{m}=7^{n}x\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
, so
7^{-m}x\in\mathcal{E}
.
Consequently the subspace topology on
\mathcal{E}
from
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
coincides
with its
7
-adic topology, so every endomorphism
f:\mathcal{E}^{+}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{+}
is continuous for this topology. Since
\mathcal{E}
is dense
in
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
, the map
f
extends to a continuous endomorphism

\hat{f}:\mathbb{Z}_{7}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{7}
. As
\mathbb{Z}_{7}
is an
E
-ring,

\hat{f}(x)=f(1)\,x
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{7}
; in particular
f(x)=f(1)\,x

for all
x\in\mathcal{E}
. Thus
\mathcal{E}
is an
E
-ring.

The element
\sqrt{2}
lies in
\mathcal{E}
, but

\binom{\sqrt{2}}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}-1)}{2}=\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{2}
does
not. Indeed, otherwise
7^{n}(2-\sqrt{2})\in 2\,\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
for some

n\geq 0
; reducing modulo
2
, this forces the class of
\sqrt{2}
to vanish
in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]/2\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}[u]/(u^{2})
, which is false. Hence

\mathcal{E}
is not binomial, and so by
Proposition
8.1
it admits no normed
pre-
\lambda
-ring structure.

Finally, we show that
\mathcal{E}
is not solid. Suppose it were. Then the
map
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{E}
,
x\mapstochar\rightarrow x\otimes 1
, would be an isomorphism. Since
\mathcal{E}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]
, tensoring with
\mathbb{Q}
would make

\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]
,

x\mapstochar\rightarrow x\otimes 1
, an isomorphism. This is impossible, since

\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of dimension
2
, so the
codomain has dimension
4
. Therefore
\mathcal{E}
is not solid.
∎

Corollary 8.6
.

The
\mathcal{E}
-localization is not weakly right exact.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{E}
is an
E
-ring that is not solid
(Proposition
8.5
), this follows from
Proposition
7.14
.
∎

In this section we compute the

Proposition 9.1

10.
R
-localization of torsion-free nilpotent groups

In this section we describe the
R
-localization of a finitely generated
torsion-free nilpotent group in terms of Mal’cev bases.

Definition 10.1

(Poly-infinite cyclic central series)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent group. A
poly-infinite
cyclic central series
in
G
is a central series

(10.1)

G=G_{1}\supset G_{2}\supset\dots\supset G_{k+1}=1

with
G_{i}/G_{i+1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
for all
i
. Every finitely generated torsion-free
nilpotent group has such a series
(see
[
CMZ17
, Th. 4.5]
).

Definition 10.2

(Mal’cev basis)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent group. A
Mal’cev
basis
of
G
is a generating set
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in G
for which the subgroups

G_{i}=\langle u_{i},\dots,u_{k}\rangle
form a poly-infinite cyclic central series.
Every poly-infinite cyclic central series arises from a Mal’cev basis in this
way. Each element
g\in G
then has a unique expression
g=u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{a_{k}}
with
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the isomorphism

G_{i}/G_{i+1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
is given by
u_{i}^{a_{i}}\cdots u_{k}^{a_{k}}\mapstochar\rightarrow a_{i}
.
Moreover, there are integer-valued polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k}]
describing the product,

(10.2)

(u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{a_{k}})\cdot(u_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{b_{k}})=u_{1}^{p_{1}(\bar{a},\bar{b})}\cdots u_{k}^{p_{k}(\bar{a},\bar{b})},

and integer-valued polynomials
q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k},z]

describing the powers,

(10.3)

(u_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{a_{k}})^{m}=u_{1}^{q_{1}(\bar{a},m)}\cdots u_{k}^{q_{k}(\bar{a},m)}\qquad(m\in\mathbb{Z})

(see
[
CMZ17
, Th. 4.9]
).

Definition 10.3

(Hall
R
-completion)
.

Let
R
be a binomial ring and let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free
nilpotent group with Mal’cev basis
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
. Since the polynomials
p_{i}

and
q_{i}
are integer-valued, each can be written as an integral linear
combination of products of binomial polynomials, and hence defines polynomial
maps
\tilde{p}_{i}:R^{2k}\rightarrow R
and
\tilde{q}_{i}:R^{k+1}\rightarrow R
. The
Hall

R
-completion
of
G
is the group
H_{R}(G)
whose elements are the formal products

u_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{r_{k}}
with
r_{i}\in R
, with multiplication

(10.4)

(u_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{r_{k}})\cdot(u_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{s_{k}})=u_{1}^{\tilde{p}_{1}(\bar{r},\bar{s})}\cdots u_{k}^{\tilde{p}_{k}(\bar{r},\bar{s})}

(see
[
CMZ17
, Th. 4.11]
). Then
H_{R}(G)
is an
R
-group, with

R
-power operation

(10.5)

(u_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots u_{k}^{r_{k}})^{s}=u_{1}^{\tilde{q}_{1}(\bar{r},s)}\cdots u_{k}^{\tilde{q}_{k}(\bar{r},s)}.

Moreover,
H_{R}(G)
has a central series given by the subgroups

(10.6)

\tilde{G}_{i}=\{u_{i}^{r_{i}}\cdots u_{k}^{r_{k}}\mid r_{i},\dots,r_{k}\in R\},\qquad 1\leq i\leq k+1,

for which
\tilde{G}_{i}/\tilde{G}_{i+1}\rightarrow R^{+}
,
u_{i}^{r_{i}}\cdots u_{k}^{r_{k}}\mapstochar\rightarrow r_{i}
, is an isomorphism of
R
-groups.

Theorem 10.4

(
[
CMZ17
, Th. 4.23]
)
.

Let
R
be a binomial ring and let
G
be a finitely generated torsion-free
nilpotent group. Then
H_{R}(G)
is
R
-powered nilpotent in the sense of Hall, and
the homomorphism

(10.7)

G\rightarrow H_{R}(G),\qquad u_{i}\mapstochar\rightarrow u_{i},

is the universal homomorphism from

to an
R
-powered nilpotent group in the
sense of Hall.

Example 10.5
.

If

is the ring of

-adic integers, then
H_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(G)

coincides with the
p
-adic completion of

G

[
CMZ17
, p. 28]
.

,
