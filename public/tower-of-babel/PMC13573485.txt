Estimation of leaf nitrogen content in fruit trees within Arid Oasis Orchards using UAV-based spectral–texture fusion
Authors: Jiaxi Liang, Jiawen Yu, Yue Wu, Muqiao Li, Jiansuo Liu, Kai Nian, Aihua Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeaf Nitrogen Content (LNC) is an important indicator of the nutritional status of fruit trees. To facilitate precise nutrient management in orchards within Xinjiang’s arid region, this study focused on walnut (Juglans regia L.), jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill.), and apricot (Prunus armeniaca L.) trees in the Weigan–Kuqa River Delta Oasis (Weiku Oasis) of southern Xinjiang (50 samples per species), and used Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) multispectral imagery to develop an optimal inversion method for LNC. Spectral indices and Texture Features (TF) related to LNC were extracted, including eight Two-Band Optimized Spectral Indices (2B-OSI), eight Three-Band Optimized Spectral Indices (3B-OSI), and eight TF. These were integrated with Random Forest Regression (RFR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Quadratic Polynomial Regression (QPR) models to assess their performance.ResultsThe RFR model integrating 2B-OSI, 3B-OSI, and TF achieved the best performance, with validation set Coefficient of Determination (R2) ranging from 0.89 to 0.93, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) from 0.84 to 1.13 g/kg, and Ratio of Performance to Deviation (RPD) from 2.81 to 3.12. After TF were incorporated, R2 increased by 1.95%, RMSE reduced by 6.73%, and RPD increased by 5.89%, indicating that TF further improved model robustness. Robustness and uncertainty analyses indicated that, under small-sample conditions, the spectral–textural fusion framework still exhibited relatively stable predictive performance across different validation conditions. Spatial analysis revealed clear species-specific distribution patterns of LNC: jujube showed clustered high values, apricot exhibited a relatively balanced distribution, and walnut had overall lower nitrogen levels.ConclusionsThe integration of spectral indices and TF based on UAV multispectral imagery can effectively achieve inversion of LNC in fruit trees in arid regions, with the RFR model showing the best performance. However, the relatively limited sample size and validation uncertainty should be considered when interpreting model performance. These findings can provide theoretical support and technical guidance for precise fertilization and nutrient management in orchards in the arid region of Xinjiang.Keywords: Leaf Nitrogen Content (LNC), Feature fusion, UAV multispectral imagery, Precision nutrient management, Fruit trees, Arid Oasis Orchards

Introduction

Fruit tree production constitutes an important component of the global agricultural economy and plays a vital role in ensuring food security and maintaining agroecosystem sustainability [1]. Nitrogen is an essential macronutrient involved in chlorophyll synthesis, protein metabolism, and photosynthetic regulation, thereby substantially influencing tree vigor, fruit quality, and overall productivity [2]. Accordingly, Leaf Nitrogen Content (LNC) is widely recognized as a key physiological indicator of fruit tree nutritional status and photosynthetic potential, providing an important basis for precision nitrogen management [3, 4]. However, nitrogen fertilizer use efficiency remains suboptimal worldwide, and excessive nitrogen application may lead to soil acidification, nitrate contamination of groundwater, and greenhouse gas emissions [5, 6]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in arid agricultural systems, where limited precipitation, intense evaporation, and nutrient-poor soils further complicate nutrient management [7, 8]. The Weigan–Kuqa River Delta Oasis (Weiku Oasis) in Xinjiang, China, represents a typical arid fruit-growing region where orchard production heavily depends on irrigation and nitrogen fertilization [9, 10]. In this region, excessive nitrogen input may aggravate soil salinization and nutrient imbalance, whereas insufficient nitrogen supply can constrain vegetative growth and reduce fruit quality [11]. Therefore, rapid, accurate, and high-resolution monitoring of fruit tree LNC is essential for improving nitrogen use efficiency, supporting precision fertilization, and promoting the sustainable development of oasis agriculture.

Traditional LNC measurement methods, such as Kjeldahl and dry combustion, are accurate but labor-intensive and time-consuming, unsuitable for large-scale, real-time monitoring [12]. Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) remote sensing, with its high resolution, flexibility, and cost-effectiveness, has emerged as a powerful tool for LNC monitoring [13]. Compared to satellite remote sensing, UAV multispectral imagery captures finer canopy spectral and spatial features, providing high-quality data for inversion models [14, 15].

During remote sensing inversion, spectral indices are widely used to estimate physiological and biochemical parameters of fruit trees. Two-band spectral indices have been extensively applied in vegetation monitoring because of their computational simplicity and broad applicability. In contrast, three-band spectral indices can capture nonlinear relationships among complex spectral features and dynamic vegetation responses, and have therefore become an important direction in vegetation parameter retrieval [16]. For example, Yuan et al. developed a Three-Band Enhanced Vegetation Index (EDVI) based on Sentinel-2 MSI imagery and demonstrated that it outperformed two-band indices in distinguishing Non-Photosynthetic Vegetation (NPV). The index also effectively separated Photosynthetic Vegetation (PV) and Bare Soil (BS), highlighting the potential of three-band indices to capture complex spectral characteristics. Furthermore, the incorporation of Texture Features (TF) enhances the information dimension of inversion models [17]. By analyzing TF such as mean, variance, and contrast from UAV multispectral imagery, the spatial distribution of vegetation parameters can be more precisely characterized, providing valuable support for improving inversion accuracy [18]. For instance, Zhu et al. extracted TF from UAV multispectral imagery and combined them with vegetation indices to estimate rice chlorophyll content. Their results showed that this fusion strategy significantly improved model accuracy, emphasizing the role of TF in characterizing spatial heterogeneity [19].

The current research gap lies in the fact that, although previous studies have demonstrated the potential of two-band spectral indices, three-band spectral indices, and TF in vegetation parameter retrieval, and progress has also been made in fruit tree LNC estimation, most existing studies remain limited to the use of single feature types or simple feature combinations. Systematic analyses of multi-feature fusion strategies, model performance, and differences in applicability are still lacking. In particular, given the high-resolution information provided by UAV multispectral imagery, how to effectively integrate two-band spectral indices, three-band spectral indices, and TF to improve the accuracy and robustness of LNC inversion across different fruit tree species remains an issue that requires further investigation.

This study aims to fill this gap by focusing on typical orchards in Xinjiang’s Weiku Oasis, utilizing UAV multispectral imagery to develop an efficient LNC inversion model. By optimizing two- and three-band spectral indices and TF, and combining them with machine learning methods, the study seeks to: (1) evaluate the potential of UAV multispectral imagery for LNC monitoring, (2) analyze the relationships between spectral indices, TF, and LNC, and (3) construct an effective LNC prediction model by integrating optimized features through various combinations, ultimately providing data support for precise orchard nutrient management.

Materials and data

Study area

The study area is located in the Weiku Oasis of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, situated in the northern Tarim Basin and southern Tianshan Mountains, forming a typical piedmont alluvial fan plain. The region has a typical temperate continental arid climate, with scarce precipitation and intense evaporation, making fruit trees highly sensitive to changes in water and fertilizer. The forestry and fruit industry plays a significant role in the regional economy, with the main crops being walnuts, jujubes, apricots, and pears. Data acquisition

UAV remote sensing data acquisition

Multispectral imagery was acquired using a DJI Phantom 4 Multispectral (P4 Multispectral) UAV. Field flights were conducted from 31 August to 9 September 2023 during the fruit maturation stage. All flights were performed between 12:00 and 15:00 local time under clear and low-wind conditions to minimize illumination and motion disturbances. Prior to each flight, a single image was captured with the camera positioned approximately 1 m above a calibrated white reference panel for reflectance calibration. Flights followed an S-shaped pattern at an altitude of 100 m, with 80% forward and 75% side overlap, and the multispectral camera maintained a nadir orientation. Fruit tree leaf data collection

Foliar samples were collected from Xiaoerkuile Airike Village, Akewusitang Township, Kuqa City, between 31 August and 9 September 2023. Sampling sites were arranged using a uniform grid based on orchard distribution and planting density, and geographic coordinates were recorded with a high-precision GPS device. Leaf samples were collected from the upper, middle, and lower canopy layers using the five-point sampling method, immediately sealed in polyethylene bags, and stored in shaded containers to minimize moisture loss. The dataset comprised 50 samples each of walnut (Juglans regia L., cultivar 'Xinwen185', 23 years old), jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill., cultivar 'Huizao', 19 years old), and apricot (Prunus armeniaca L., cultivar 'Kucha small white apricots', 11 years old). The samples were collected from the fruit tree demonstration orchard in the Township, and the species and cultivar information were confirmed based on on-site labels and orchard records. As leaf samples were ground for nitrogen analysis, no standard voucher specimens were retained. These samples were randomly divided into modeling and validation subsets at an 8:2 ratio.

Data processing

UAV imagery processing

All UAV images were processed using Pix4Dmapper to generate orthorectified multispectral mosaics, including image import, feature-based alignment, point cloud generation, and orthorectification. To improve spatial accuracy, thirteen Ground Control Points (GCPs) were manually deployed and georeferenced. Radiometric calibration was performed using a white reference panel and solar irradiance data from the onboard sensor, after which Digital Number (DN) values were converted to reflectance and the five spectral bands were composited in the Environment for Visualizing Images (ENVI). Finally, the mean reflectance of canopy pixels within each tree crown was extracted for subsequent inversion and model development.

LNC measurement

The determination of LNC in fruit trees was conducted in accordance with the standard "NY/T 2017–2011: Determination of Nitrogen, Phosphorus and Potassium in Plants", and the laboratory analysis was entrusted to the Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences. All leaf samples were immediately placed into kraft paper sample bags after field collection and temporarily stored in insulated coolers containing ice packs. Upon transport to the laboratory, the samples were first subjected to oven-drying at 105 °C for 30 min to deactivate enzymes, followed by continuous drying at 65 °C until constant weight was achieved. The dried samples were then ground using a grinder, passed through a 0.25 mm sieve, and subsequently stored in sealed sample vials placed in desiccators for further use. The measurements were performed between 17 and 22 May 2024. The experimental determination included sample acid digestion, dilution, and final quantification using the Kjeldahl method with blank correction. Plant samples that have undergone drying, grinding, and sealed storage contain nitrogen in a stable form, which can remain stable over extended periods under ambient dry conditions, thereby ensuring that the measured results accurately reflect the nitrogen nutritional status at the time of sampling. Methods

Research workflow

This study developed a workflow for LNC estimation using UAV multispectral imagery (Fig. 3). First, data preprocessing was conducted, including acquisition of UAV multispectral images and ground-measured data, followed by radiometric correction, geometric correction, and sample alignment. Next, feature construction and correlation analysis were performed, generating Two-Band Optimized Spectral Indices (2B-OSI), Three-Band Optimized Spectral Indices (3B-OSI), and TF from the five spectral bands (B, G, R, RE, NIR) and selecting the optimal feature combinations. Finally, inversion modeling and spatial mapping were carried out using six feature-combination plans (A–F) and three regression methods: Random Forest Regression (RFR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Quadratic Polynomial Regression (QPR). Model performance was evaluated using the R2, RMSE, and Ratio of Performance to Deviation (RPD), and the optimal model was applied to produce orchard-scale LNC distribution maps.

In this study, eight 2B-OSI were constructed using all possible combinations of five UAV spectral bands. Their correlations with LNC were analyzed to determine optimal band combinations for model input. The 2B-OSI includes: Difference Index (DI), Ratio Index (RI), Sum Index (SI), Product Index (PI), Normalized Difference Index (NDI), Normalized Perpendicular Drought Index (NPDI), Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index (SAVI), and Nonlinear Vegetation Index (NLI).

To further capture the complex nonlinear interactions among multiple spectral bands, 3B-OSI (TBI1–TBI8) were constructed by combining any three of the five available bands. By exploring higher-order band combinations, the 3B-OSI are expected to capture more subtle spectral variations associated with chlorophyll and protein absorption features, as well as internal scattering effects within leaf tissues linked to nitrogen status [27, 28].

Texture features

Using the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), TF were extracted from the five multispectral bands with a 4 × 4 sliding window. The GLCM was constructed with a pixel offset distance of 1 and four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°) to characterize canopy grayscale spatial relationships from multiple orientations. For each band, eight TF were calculated for each direction and then averaged across the four directions to reduce directional dependence, as shown in Table 3. TF describes variations in crop surface grayscale characteristics. By integrating TF with spectral features, crop LNC can be effectively estimated [29]. Furthermore, exploring the correlation between these TF and LNC helps to reveal the influence of canopy structural characteristics on nitrogen distribution [30].

Inversion model construction and validation

It should be noted that, considering the limited sample size for each individual species (N = 50), the full dataset was used in the preliminary correlation analysis and feature optimization stage to reduce random fluctuations caused by a single random split. However, we also recognize that this procedure may introduce a risk of information leakage in a strictly predictive modeling workflow. Therefore, to evaluate the influence of potential information leakage on model generalization ability, a robustness test with a strictly isolated validation set was further conducted, in which feature re-selection and model training were performed only within the modeling set.

Three machine learning models were developed to establish quantitative relationships between spectral features and measured LNC: RFR, SVR, and QPR. RFR is an ensemble-based nonparametric method that integrates multiple decision trees to capture complex nonlinear dependencies between spectral predictors and target variables, while effectively mitigating overfitting [31]. To ensure the stability and generalization ability of the model, the key hyperparameters for RFR were set as follows: the number of trees was 250, and the maximum depth was set to 10. SVR applies kernel-based optimization to model nonlinear regression surfaces in high-dimensional space, offering strong generalization capability even with limited samples [32]. The hyperparameters for the SVR model were set as follows: the kernel type was Radial Basis Function (RBF), the penalty coefficient (C) was 10.0, the kernel coefficient (gamma) was set to “scale” based on the number of features and their variance, and the tolerance boundary (epsilon) was 0.1. These model parameters were mainly determined based on previous studies, model characteristics, and preliminary experiments to balance model accuracy, stability, and overfitting risk. QPR provides a baseline polynomial model that captures second-order interactions among variables and serves as a benchmark for comparison [33]. To prevent dimensionality issues, the QPR model structure was constrained to only include the most important variables selected through stepwise regression, retaining linear terms and pure quadratic terms. Optimizing spectral indices and texture features for fruit tree LNC correlation

This study conducted a correlation analysis between LNC and 2B-OSI, 3B-OSI, and TF, and for each tree species, eight optimal two-band, three-band, and texture-feature combinations were selected for detailed evaluation. The results showed that the 2B-OSI exhibited a consistent correlation pattern across the four fruit tree species (Fig. 5, only the 2B-OSI results for apricot are presented here, with the remaining results provided in Appendix A). The RE and NIR bands contributed most strongly to LNC characterization, while the G and R bands served as auxiliary enhancers. The B band generally exhibited weaker correlations. Furthermore, the 3B-OSI (Fig. 6, only the 3B-OSI results for apricot are presented here; the remaining results are provided in Appendix B.) maintained the NIR and RE bands as the primary sensitive bands but strengthened band complementarity through the inclusion of visible bands, resulting in higher and more uniform correlation levels. The correlation between TF and LNC (Fig. 7) was most pronounced in the Mean and Variance features associated with the RE and NIR; however, the overall correlation remained lower.

Correlation between texture features and LNC

For all fruit tree species, the correlations between the 2B-OSI and 3B-OSI (constructed based on the optimal band combinations) and LNC passed the significance test at the 0.01 level (p < 0.01), indicating highly significant relationships. Meanwhile, the correlations between TF and LNC also passed the significance test at the 0.05 level (p < 0.05), demonstrating the effectiveness and reliability of the selected features in LNC characterization.

In terms of the correlation coefficients, for apricot trees (Table 5), both the 2B-OSI and 3B-OSI exhibited high correlations with LNC. Among the 2B-OSI, NLI (RE, R) showed the highest correlation (|r|= 0.79), while other RE–based indices such as RI, NDI, NPDI, and SAVI also reached 0.78, indicating strong stability. In the 3B-OSI, TBI2 (RE, B, G), TBI4 (RE, B, G), and TBI6(B, RE, G) achieved the highest correlation (|r|= 0.80), demonstrating that incorporating B and G bands further enhances LNC characterization, with overall performance exceeding that of the 2B-OSI and TF. Among the TF, NIR_Mea showed the highest correlation (|r|= 0.73), followed by RE_Mea (|r|= 0.62), while the correlations of the other TF did not exceed 0.6. Overall, however, the correlations between TF and LNC were lower than those of the spectral indices, which may be because TF mainly characterizes LNC indirectly through spatial structural features and therefore has relatively limited descriptive capability. Overall, the results confirm the effectiveness of the optimized spectral indices and TF in characterizing fruit tree LNC. The 3B-OSI substantially enhanced LNC characterization through multi-band integration, while the 2B-OSI offered practical advantages due to its simplicity and strong band sensitivity. TF provided supplementary information on spatial structure, with NIR_Mea showing relatively superior performance. In addition, the frequent occurrence of RE and NIR bands in the optimal combinations across all species highlights their crucial role in LNC characterization. These findings provide a reliable data foundation and methodological support for the precise monitoring and nutrient management of LNC in orchard systems.

Inversion results and analysis of fruit tree LNC based on the RFR model

Table 7 presents the modeling and validation results for apricot LNC inversion. The scatter plots of measured versus predicted values for the apricot tree species are shown in Fig. 8. Overall, the RFR model exhibited a relatively strong ability to explain LNC variability, with both the modeling and validation sets achieving R2 values above 0.8. Under Plan F, which fuses 2B-OSI, 3B-OSI, and TF, the modeling set R2, RMSE, and RPD reached 0.92, 0.94 g/kg, and 3.11, respectively, while the validation set values reached 0.93, 0.84 g/kg, and 3.09, indicating a clear advantage in inversion accuracy. Compared with Plan C, which used only spectral indices, Plan F increased R2 by 0.02, reduced RMSE by 0.01 g/kg, and improved RPD by 0.04 in the modeling set; in the validation set, it increased R2 by 0.01, reduced RMSE by 0.04 g/kg, and improved RPD by 0.11, suggesting that the incorporation of TF provided additional support for model stability and accuracy. In addition, compared with Plans D and E, Plan F improved modeling set R2 by 0.04 and 0.02, respectively, and validation set R2 by 0.04 and 0.03, respectively. Compared with Plans A and B, Plan F improved modeling set R2 by 0.07 and 0.06, respectively, and validation set R2 by 0.07 and 0.06, respectively. These results indicate that the fusion of all three feature types outperformed both two-feature fusion and single-feature input. Robustness analysis and uncertainty quantification

Although the spectral–textural fusion framework (Plan F) exhibited stronger predictive performance, model evaluation based on a single random split may overestimate performance under limited sample size conditions (N = 50 per species). To more rigorously quantify model uncertainty, a multi-tiered validation analysis was conducted using the apricot dataset as a representative case.

First, to avoid potential information leakage during feature selection, feature selection was re-performed using only the 80% modeling subset, while the remaining 20% validation set was kept completely isolated. Prior to model training, the features extracted from the modeling subset were analyzed (Table 10). The results showed that the optimal feature combination was highly consistent with that obtained from the full dataset (Table 5): the core indices driven by the RE and NIR bands, together with the NIR_Mea TF, consistently emerged as the most sensitive predictors. This consistency indicates that the identified relationship between LNC and spectral–textural features was relatively stable and was not an incidental result caused by data leakage.

Spatial distribution analysis of fruit tree LNC

To reduce the interference of bare soil with the spatial statistics of LNC, this study combined the natural breaks classification method with visual interpretation of UAV imagery to remove low-value pixels that were potentially affected by soil background reflectance, including pixels with LNC values below 17.00 g/kg for walnut, 18.24 g/kg for jujube, and 17.29 g/kg for apricot. This procedure was applied to ensure that the statistical results accurately reflected the actual nitrogen status of the vegetation.

After removing these disturbed pixels, the three orchard types exhibited distinct dominant LNC ranges and spatial patterns (Fig. 12). In the walnut orchard, LNC was mainly distributed within 17.00–21.73 g/kg, with the 17.00–18.62 g/kg and 18.62–20.43 g/kg intervals accounting for 15.67% and 17.43%, respectively, whereas the 20.43–21.73 g/kg and > 21.73 g/kg intervals accounted for only 6.29% and 10.26%, respectively. This pattern indicates that the LNC of the walnut orchard was dominated by low-to-moderate values with a relatively uniform and dispersed spatial distribution, which may be attributed to its low planting density and loose canopy structure. In the jujube orchard, LNC ranged from 18.24 to 25.79 g/kg, with the 18.24–21.32 g/kg, 21.32–23.50 g/kg, and 23.50–25.79 g/kg intervals accounting for 20.57%, 22.48%, and 25.67%, respectively, forming continuous high-value belts. This pattern is likely associated with the dense canopy and vigorous vegetative growth of jujube, which favors local nitrogen accumulation. In the apricot orchard, LNC was mainly concentrated within 17.29–23.01 g/kg, and the proportions of the different intervals were relatively balanced, with 17.29–19.25 g/kg, 19.25–20.97 g/kg, 20.97–23.01 g/kg, and > 23.01 g/kg accounting for 20.10%, 19.90%, 19.90%, and 19.89%, respectively. Its spatial distribution was relatively uniform and exhibited low variability, which is consistent with its regular canopy architecture and high, stable coverage.

Overall, the jujube orchard exhibited a clustered high-value pattern, the apricot orchard showed a balanced and stable distribution, whereas the walnut orchard was characterized by uniformly distributed low-to-moderate values and the lowest overall nitrogen level. These differences suggest that the spatial distribution of LNC is influenced not only by nitrogen status itself but also by species-specific canopy structure, planting density, and vegetation coverage.

Discussion

Mechanism of spectral–textural fusion and cross-species generalization

The core mechanism of this approach lies in the deep coupling and complementarity between two heterogeneous feature types, namely spectral features and TF, within the dimension of information representation. Spectral indices, serving as remote sensing indicator variables of vegetation biochemical properties, are highly sensitive to variations in chlorophyll concentration and cellular structure induced by nitrogen stress [35]. However, feature extraction from the spectral domain alone is prone to nonlinear interferences caused by canopy structural complexity and the signal saturation effect commonly occurring in areas with dense vegetation, thereby constraining further improvement in inversion accuracy [36]. The incorporation of TF effectively alleviates this limitation. By characterizing the spatial correlation of gray-level distributions within pixel neighborhoods, TF provide supplementary information on canopy structural attributes, such as leaf aggregation and canopy homogeneity. As a result, the model can capture both physiological and structural characteristics of vegetation simultaneously [37]. The results of this study provide direct evidence for this mechanism: taking the apricot RFR model as an example, compared with the plans using only spectral indices (Plan A, B, C), the plans incorporating TF (Plan D, E, F) achieved average increases of 0.03 and 0.02 in R2 for the modeling and validation sets, average reduced of 0.05 g/kg and 0.04 g/kg in RMSE, and average increases of 0.13 and 0.11 in RPD, respectively. Consistent trends were observed for the SVR and QPR models, as well as for walnut, jujube, and mixed species in Appendices D and E. Overall, the spectral variables describe the biochemical status of vegetation, while texture variables characterize its structural attributes. Their synergistic integration constructs a high-dimensional, nonlinear feature space. This feature space can be efficiently analyzed by machine learning algorithms such as RFR, enabling the establishment of a precise relationship with LNC and thereby contributing to improved inversion accuracy and model stability [38].

However, when the model was transferred from a single-species scenario to a mixed-species planting environment, its generalization performance showed a noticeable decline [39]. Appendices D and E show that, across all models and feature selection plans, the model accuracy for the mixed-species dataset was consistently lower than that for single-species datasets. This performance degradation primarily arises from substantial inter-species variability in leaf morphology, canopy architecture, and nitrogen distribution characteristics [40]. Such variability leads to heterogeneous spectral–textural responses to nitrogen dynamics, making it difficult for the model to maintain stable predictive performance under diverse canopy and background conditions [41]. This suggests that although the current model achieves high accuracy in single-species scenarios, its direct transferability across species remains limited, which is a key issue that must be addressed in mixed-species modeling.

Nevertheless, the spectral-textural fusion method proposed in this study still provides a foundational framework with considerable potential for cross-species modeling. Compared with approaches relying solely on spectral information, this method incorporates both biochemical and canopy structural information when characterizing vegetation physiological status, thereby reducing, to some extent, the excessive dependence of a single feature type on species-specific traits [42]. Therefore, its advantage does not lie in having already achieved ideal cross-species generalization, but rather in offering a richer feature basis and greater room for optimization for future improvement in model transferability. This potential is particularly relevant in arid and semi-arid regions, where complex surface backgrounds and pronounced differences in vegetation cover make single-spectrum models more vulnerable to interference from soil background and canopy heterogeneity, whereas the fusion-based method shows stronger adaptability under such complex conditions [43].

Based on this foundational framework, future studies may further improve cross-species model transferability in several ways. First, a multi-species dataset covering different fruit tree species, phenological stages, canopy structural types, and management conditions should be established to enhance the representativeness of training samples and improve the model’s ability to learn interspecific variation [44]. Second, relatively species-insensitive variables, such as canopy structural parameters and soil background correction factors, could be incorporated to reduce the influence of species-specific differences on model stability [45]. Furthermore, domain adaptation and transfer learning methods could be introduced to learn shared feature representations across different species, while a small number of samples from the target species could be used to fine-tune the model, thereby improving its rapid transferability to new species or orchard scenarios [46]. Through these improvements, the spectral-textural fusion method is expected to evolve from a high-accuracy inversion model for single-species conditions into a more general modeling framework applicable to mixed-species and broader orchard environments.

It should be noted that the model results should still be interpreted with caution in relation to sample size, overfitting risk, and spatial dependence. The limited sample size for each individual species may lead to instability in random validation results. In addition, the relatively high-dimensional feature combination may increase the risk of overfitting under small-sample conditions, particularly for dimension-sensitive models. Moreover, the spatial similarity among adjacent canopies may weaken the independence of random validation and lead to an overly optimistic evaluation of model performance. Although this study further quantified these uncertainties through information leakage correction, standard fivefold cross-validation, spatial block fivefold cross-validation, and RFE analysis, future studies should still incorporate larger sample sizes, multi-year and multi-orchard datasets, and stricter spatially independent validation strategies to further evaluate model generalization ability [47].

Overall, this study indicates that the spectral–textural fusion method can improve fruit tree LNC retrieval, while also demonstrating that model generalization under mixed-species conditions still requires further improvement. In addition, the interpretation of the results should still consider the limited sample size, overfitting risk, and spatial dependence. Future studies should combine larger datasets, species-insensitive features, and transfer learning methods to further improve model generalization.

The problem of scale generalization for the inversion model

The UAV imagery employed in this study provides high spatial resolution, enabling precise retrieval of physiological and biochemical parameters of fruit trees at small spatial scales [48]. However, its limited spatial coverage constrains its capacity to support large-scale or regional monitoring and management [49]. Future research should focus on developing upscaling inversion strategies [50] to extend UAV-scale high-precision models to medium- and high-resolution satellite remote sensing data, thereby achieving continuous estimation of fruit tree nutritional parameters across broader spatial domains [51]. Potential approaches include geostatistics-based scale conversion analysis [52], feature mapping methods using machine learning algorithms [53], and the establishment of empirical or mechanistic relationship models between UAV and satellite scales through data fusion techniques such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) [54]. These approaches are expected to enable cross-scale transferability and generalization [55] of models, facilitating the expansion of fruit tree remote sensing inversion applications from plot to regional and even larger scales.

Currently, the multi-temporal dataset used in this study mainly covers a single growing season, which limits its ability to capture the physiological dynamics of fruit trees under different phenological and environmental conditions [56]. Future research should integrate multi-season field measurements and long-term time-series imagery to establish a continuous monitoring system, thereby improving the temporal and environmental robustness of the model [57].

Building on these advancements, the proposed modeling framework demonstrates strong scalability and policy relevance [58]. The results from the fusion model can be integrated into agricultural nutrient monitoring and intelligent management systems [59]. By integrating remotely estimated LNC with soil characteristics and climatic variables, it becomes possible to dynamically optimize fertilizer application strategies, enhance nutrient use efficiency, and reduce environmental impacts [60]. Furthermore, the findings from this study provide significant implications for agricultural structural optimization and sustainable development in arid and semi-arid regions [61]. Establishing a regional nutrient monitoring system based on remote sensing inversion can provide policymakers with scientific evidence for differentiated fruit tree management, agricultural layout optimization, and green production transformation [62]. From a global perspective, the proposed multi-scale modeling and application framework aligns closely with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [63]. It supports SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), and SDG 13 (Climate Action) by enhancing agricultural productivity and food security, promoting the efficient use of agricultural inputs, and strengthening the climate resilience of agricultural systems.

Overall, the study bridges the gap between modeling and policy, and underscores the scientific and strategic significance of fruit tree LNC remote sensing inversion in addressing future agricultural and climate challenges.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated an integrated UAV-based framework for LNC across walnut, jujube, apricot, and mixed species. By constructing a high-dimensional feature space through the fusion of 2B-OSI, 3B-OSI, and spatial TF, and by coupling this with advanced machine learning models, the study successfully achieved prediction and spatial mapping of LNC.

Optimized spectral indices showed clear advantages in correlating with LNC, with 3B-OSI generally outperforming the 2B-OSI. Further analysis revealed that the RE and NIR bands were consistently critical components in the optimal band combinations, underscoring their physiological relevance to nitrogen status. While TF exhibited a lower direct correlation with LNC, they were essential for supplementing spatial-structural information.

High-resolution LNC maps can support precision fertilization and resource optimization, promoting efficient nutrient use and sustainable orchard management. In the future, incorporating larger sample sizes, multi-year observations, and multi-orchard datasets could further evaluate and enhance the general applicability and robustness of this framework.

The framework may provide practical value for advancing precision agriculture and contribute to broader agricultural sustainability and food-security objectives, aligning closely with SDG 2, SDG 12, and SDG 13.

Appendix E

For walnut trees, under Plan F, the SVR model achieved R² values of 0.76 and 0.78 for the modeling and validation sets, respectively, with RMSE values of 1.46 g/kg and 1.55 g/kg and RPD values of 2.07 and 2.19. The QPR model performed even weaker, with R² values of 0.68 and 0.70, RMSE values of 1.68 g/kg and 1.77 g/kg, and RPD values of 1.80 and 1.92. In contrast, the RFR model showed markedly superior performance. Compared with SVR, the RFR model achieved relative increases in R² of 17.11% and 17.95%, relative reductions in RMSE of 39.05% and 42.20%, and improvements in RPD of 38.65% and 42.47% for the modeling and validation sets. Relative to the QPR model, the RFR model improved R² by 30.88% and 31.43%, reduced RMSE by 60.00% and 62.39%, and increased RPD by 59.44% and 62.50%, demonstrating its much stronger ability to capture features.

For jujube trees, the SVR model achieved R² values of 0.84 and 0.81 for the modeling and validation sets under Plan F, with RMSE values of 1.66 g/kg and 1.60 g/kg and RPD values of 2.51 and 1.93. The QPR model yielded R² values of 0.74 and 0.77, RMSE values of 2.09 g/kg and 1.44 g/kg, and RPD values of 1.99 and 2.15. Although both SVR and QPR performed better under Plan F than under low-dimensional feature schemes, their ability to capture characteristics of LNC remained inferior to that of the RFR model.

For mixed tree species, model performance was consistently lower than for single species. Under Plan F, the SVR model achieved R² values of 0.75 and 0.77, RMSE values of 1.88 g/kg and 1.68 g/kg, and RPD values of 1.96 and 2.04. These values were substantially lower than those of the RFR model (validation R² = 0.89, RMSE = 1.13 g/kg, RPD = 3.03). The QPR model performed even more poorly for mixed species, yielding a validation R² of 0.69, an RMSE of 1.90 g/kg, and an RPD of 1.81 under Plan F, indicating its limited ability to accommodate the high heterogeneity present in mixed-species data.
