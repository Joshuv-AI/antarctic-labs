Relativistic Elastic Response to Gravitational Waves: Explicit Solutions for a Rectangular Plate Thanks: <jnatar@math.ist.utl.pt> , <filipe.alexandre.nazare.pereira.dos.santos@tecnico.ulisboa.pt>
José Natário, Filipe Nazaré

Abstract
. We study the interaction between gravitational waves and elastic bodies within the framework of relativistic elasticity. Starting from the Lagrangian formulation of relativistic elasticity, we derive the linearized equations governing the response of a homogeneous and isotropic solid to a weak gravitational wave by expanding the action to quadratic order in the elastic field derivatives and to linear order in the metric perturbation. In this way, Dyson’s interaction term from the effective potential approach emerges naturally from the relativistic theory. We then apply the formalism to a thin rectangular elastic plate aligned with the direction of propagation and polarization of a plus-polarized gravitational wave. For a material with vanishing Poisson ratio, the equations decouple and admit simple explicit solutions. We obtain closed-form expressions for the induced displacements and for the energy deposited on the plate by both short gravitational wave bursts and continuous harmonic waves. Finally, we compute the gravitational wave emission generated by the oscillating plate itself under continuous harmonic excitation. These results provide a fully relativistic derivation of the elastic response to gravitational waves together with explicit solvable examples relevant to resonant gravitational wave detectors.



Introduction

The direct detection of gravitational waves by the LIGO and Virgo collaborations
[
1
]
marked a turning point in modern physics: not only did it confirm a fundamental prediction of general relativity, first derived by Einstein
[

Long before these interferometric detections, gravitational waves were sought using resonant detectors, most notably the bar detectors pioneered by Joseph Weber
[
28

under the influence of a passing gravitational wave. Although Weber’s reported detections in the late 1960s
[
29
]
were not confirmed and are now generally regarded as spurious, the conceptual framework he introduced played a foundational role in the development of gravitational-wave detection technology. In particular, these early efforts established the basic principles underlying resonant detectors and motivated continued study of the interaction between gravitational waves and elastic bodies, including the experiments described in
[

4
,
13

. More recently, there has been renewed interest in the elastic response of matter to gravitational waves
[

,

The theoretical description of resonant detectors naturally leads to the theory of relativistic elasticity, originally formulated by Carter and Quintana

The equations governing the interaction of a homogeneous and isotropic solid with a gravitational wave were first obtained by Dyson

In this current work, we give yet another an initio derivation of the equations by directly expanding the Lagrangian for relativistic elasticity to quadratic order in the field derivatives and to linear order in the metric perturbation, obtaining, as a byproduct, Dyson’s interaction term. We then apply the formalism to the case of a thin rectangular plate aligned with the direction of propagation and polarization of the gravitational wave. By choosing a material with zero Poisson ratio, we are able to obtain explicit solutions, including expressions for the energy deposited on the plate by the gravitational wave. 1.
Relativistic elasticity

We start by briefly recalling the basic setup for the Lagrangian formulation of relativistic elasticity (see
[
25
]
for more details). Given a spacetime
(M,g)
, we model a continuous medium as a Riemannian 3-manifold
(S,k)
(which we call the
relaxed configuration
) and a projection map
\pi:M\to S
whose level sets are timelike curves (the worldlines of the medium particles). If we choose local coordinates
(\bar{x}^{1},\bar{x}^{2},\bar{x}^{3})
in
S
then we can think of
\pi
as a set of three scalar fields
\bar{x}^{1},\bar{x}^{2},\bar{x}^{3}
defined on
M
. The deformations of the medium with respect to the natural metric
k_{ij}
of
S
are then given by the (inverse) metric

(1)

h^{ij}=g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\bar{x}^{i}\partial_{\nu}\bar{x}^{j}\,.

An
elastic
Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}
is one that depends on the derivatives of the fields
\bar{x}^{1},\bar{x}^{2},\bar{x}^{3}
only through
h^{ij}
; the choice of
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\bar{x}^{i},h^{ij})
, which turns out to be the rest energy density measured by a comoving observer, is called the
elastic law
of the continuous medium.

We define
homogeneous and isotropic materials
to be those for which
\mathcal{L}
depends only on the eigenvalues of
h_{ij}
with respect to
k_{ij}
(that is, the eigenvalues of the matrix
(h_{ij})
in a frame where
k_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
). If
k_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
, that is, if
(S,k)
is the Euclidean space, then we can define the more convenient variables

(2)

\lambda_{0}=\det(h^{ij})\,,\qquad\lambda_{1}=\operatorname{tr}(h^{ij})\,,\qquad\lambda_{2}=\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{cof}(h^{ij})

for the Lagrangian of a homogeneous and isotropic material (where
\operatorname{cof}(h^{ij})
is the matrix of cofactors of
(h^{ij})
). Note that in the relaxed configuration, that is, when
h^{ij}=\delta^{ij}
, these variables take the values
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=3
.

2.
Linear elasticity and gravitational waves

In this section we re-derive the linearized equations of motion for a homogeneous and isotropic solid excited by a gravitational wave. Our approach differs slightly from those in
[
12
,
17
,
6
]
in that we directly expand the Lagrangian for relativistic elasticity to quadratic order in the field derivatives and to linear order in the metric perturbation. Dyson’s interaction term emerges naturally from this expansion, again confirming the validity of the effective potential approach.

We will derive the linearized equations of motion for the metric of a
+
polarized linear gravitational wave written in the
TT

(3)

g=-dt^{2}+\left[1+\varphi(t-z)\right]dx^{2}+\left[1-\varphi(t-z)\right]dy^{2}+dz^{2}\,.

The equations of motion for a
\times
polarized wave can be easily obtained by rotating the
xy

45^{\circ}
, and the equations of motion in the general case follow by superposition.

We look for solutions of the equations of motion of the form
2
2

2

Note that
\xi^{i}=\bar{x}^{i}-x^{i}
can be thought of as minus the displacement of the particles with respect to their relaxed positions in this approximately Minkowskian spacetime.

(4)

\bar{x}^{i}=x^{i}+\xi^{i}\,,

which can be written out in full as

(5)

\begin{cases}\bar{x}(t,x,y,z)=x+\xi(t,x,y,z)\\
\bar{y}(t,x,y,z)=y+\eta(t,x,y,z)\\
\bar{z}(t,x,y,z)=z+\zeta(t,x,y,z)\end{cases}\,.

We then have

(6)

d\bar{x}^{i}=dx^{i}+d\xi^{i}\,,

that is,

(7)

\begin{cases}d\bar{x}=dx+d\xi\\
d\bar{y}=dy+d\eta\\
d\bar{z}=dz+d\zeta\end{cases}.

Let us assume that
|d\xi^{i}|\ll 1
(in addition to the standard linearized gravity condition
|\varphi|\ll 1
).
Since the linearized inverse metric is

(8)

g^{-1}=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\left[1-\varphi(t-z)\right]\partial_{x}^{2}+\left[1+\varphi(t-z)\right]\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}\,,

we obtain, to second order on the main diagonal and to first order elsewhere,

(9)

\displaystyle\left(\gamma^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle:=g^{-1}(d\bar{x}^{i},d\bar{x}^{j})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}1+2\xi_{x}+\left\langle d\xi,d\xi\right\rangle-\varphi(1+2\xi_{x})&\eta_{x}+\xi_{y}&\zeta_{x}+\xi_{z}\\
\eta_{x}+\xi_{y}&1+2\eta_{y}+\left\langle d\eta,d\eta\right\rangle+\varphi(1+2\eta_{y})&\zeta_{y}+\eta_{z}\\
\zeta_{x}+\xi_{z}&\zeta_{y}+\eta_{z}&1+2\zeta_{z}+\left\langle d\zeta,d\zeta\right\rangle\end{matrix}\right)\,,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the Minkowski inner product of covectors. Therefore, to second order in
\xi^{i}
and
\varphi
(but ignoring quadratic terms in
\varphi
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=

\displaystyle\,\,\det\left(\gamma^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,1+2\xi_{x}+2\eta_{y}+2\zeta_{z}+4\xi_{x}\eta_{y}+4\xi_{x}\zeta_{z}+4\eta_{y}\zeta_{z}

\displaystyle+\left\langle d\xi,d\xi\right\rangle+\left\langle d\eta,d\eta\right\rangle+\left\langle d\zeta,d\zeta\right\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle-(\eta_{x}+\xi_{y})^{2}-(\zeta_{x}+\xi_{z})^{2}-(\zeta_{y}+\eta_{z})^{2}\,,

as well as

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=

\displaystyle\,\,\operatorname{tr}\left(\gamma^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,3+2\xi_{x}+2\eta_{y}+2\zeta_{z}

(11)

\displaystyle+\left\langle d\xi,d\xi\right\rangle+\left\langle d\eta,d\eta\right\rangle+\left\langle d\zeta,d\zeta\right\rangle+2\varphi(\eta_{y}-\xi_{x})

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=

\displaystyle\,\,\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{cof}\left(\gamma^{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,3+4\xi_{x}+4\eta_{y}+4\zeta_{z}+4\xi_{x}\eta_{y}+4\xi_{x}\zeta_{z}+4\eta_{y}\zeta_{z}

\displaystyle+2\left\langle d\xi,d\xi\right\rangle+2\left\langle d\eta,d\eta\right\rangle+2\left\langle d\zeta,d\zeta\right\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle-(\eta_{x}+\xi_{y})^{2}-(\zeta_{x}+\xi_{z})^{2}-(\zeta_{y}+\eta_{z})^{2}+2\varphi(\eta_{y}-\xi_{x})\,.

Note that, to this order,

(13)

\lambda_{2}-3=(\lambda_{0}-1)+(\lambda_{1}-3)

and

\displaystyle(\lambda_{0}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{1}-3)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(\lambda_{2}-3)^{2}=(\lambda_{0}-1)(\lambda_{1}-3)

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)(\lambda_{2}-3)=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}-3)(\lambda_{2}-3)\,.

To second order, the elastic Lagrangian is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\,\,a_{0}+a_{1}(\lambda_{0}-1)+b_{1}(\lambda_{1}-3)+c_{1}(\lambda_{2}-3)

\displaystyle+a_{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)^{2}+b_{2}(\lambda_{1}-3)^{2}+c_{2}(\lambda_{2}-3)^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle+d_{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)(\lambda_{1}-3)+e_{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)(\lambda_{2}-3)+f_{2}(\lambda_{1}-3)(\lambda_{2}-3)\,,

or, using (
13
) and (
14
),

(16)

\mathcal{L}=a_{0}+\tilde{a}_{1}(\lambda_{0}-1)+\tilde{b}_{1}(\lambda_{1}-3)+\tilde{a}_{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)^{2}\,,

where

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{1}=a_{1}+c_{1}\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}=b_{1}+c_{1}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{2}=a_{2}+b_{2}+4c_{2}+d_{2}+2e_{2}+2f_{2}\,.

As shown in
[
25
]
, these coefficients can be related to the rest energy density
\rho_{0}
, the longitudinal speed of sound
c_{L}
and the transverse speed of sound
c_{T}
by the formulas

(20)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=a_{0}=2\tilde{a}_{1}+2\tilde{b}_{1}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle c_{L}^{2}=\frac{\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{b}_{1}+4\tilde{a}_{2}}{\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{b}_{1}}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle c_{T}^{2}=\frac{\tilde{b}_{1}}{\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{b}_{1}}\,,

so that the Lagrangian
16
can be rewritten as

(23)

\boxed{\mathcal{L}=\rho_{0}\left[1+\frac{1-c_{T}^{2}}{2}(\lambda_{0}-1)+\frac{c_{T}^{2}}{2}(\lambda_{1}-3)+\frac{c_{L}^{2}-1}{8}(\lambda_{0}-1)^{2}\right]}\,\,.

Note that the Lagrangian for an elastic solid in Minkowski spacetime can be obtained by setting
\varphi=0
. Since
\lambda_{0}
does not depend on
\varphi
, we see that (
23
) differs from the Lagrangian in Minkowski spacetime only by the interaction term

(24)

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}\varphi(\eta_{y}-\xi_{x})\,.

Perfect fluids, in particular, are not affected by gravitational waves, since they do not carry transverse sound waves (
c_{T}^{2}=0
). Moreover, because
\varphi=\varphi(t-z)
, we have

(25)

\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\partial\xi_{x}}\right)=\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\partial\eta_{y}}\right)=0\,,

and so the equations of motion are exactly the same as in Minkowski spacetime. In particular,
\xi\equiv\eta\equiv\zeta\equiv 0
is a solution of the equations of motion. Unlike in Minkowski spacetime, however, this solution is not stress-free when a gravitational wave is present. In fact, from

(26)

T^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\,\nu}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\bar{x}^{i})}\partial_{\nu}\bar{x}^{i}-\mathcal{L}\delta^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\,\nu}

(here we can use the canonical energy-momentum tensor as its covariant divergence coincides with its divergence to first order), we obtain, for the solution
\xi\equiv\eta\equiv\zeta\equiv 0
(for which
d\bar{x}=dx
,
d\bar{y}=dy
and
\mathcal{L^{\prime}}=0
), the nonvanishing first order components

(27)

T^{x}_{\,\,\,\,x}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L^{\prime}}}{\partial\xi_{x}}\partial_{x}\bar{x}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\delta^{x}_{\,\,\,\,x}=-\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}\varphi

and

(28)

T^{y}_{\,\,\,\,y}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L^{\prime}}}{\partial\eta_{y}}\partial_{y}\bar{y}-\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\delta^{y}_{\,\,\,\,y}=\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}\varphi\,.

Notice that

(29)

\partial_{x}T^{x}_{\,\,\,\,x}=\partial_{y}T^{y}_{\,\,\,\,y}=0

as
\varphi=\varphi(t-z)
. This again shows that the Euler-Lagrange equations are the same as in Minkowski, namely the Navier-Cauchy equations for linear elasticity of homogeneous and isotropic materials:

(30)

\boxed{\partial^{2}_{t}\xi^{i}=c_{T}^{2}\Delta\xi^{i}+(c_{L}^{2}-c_{T}^{2})\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}\xi^{j})}\,\,.

The gravitational wave will affect the motion of the elastic body only through the boundary conditions for these equations.

As shown in
[
25
]
(after using (
20
), (
21
) and (
22
)), the spatial components of the energy-momentum tensor when
\varphi\equiv 0
are given by

(31)

T^{ij}=\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}(\partial_{i}\xi^{j}+\partial_{j}\xi^{i})+\rho_{0}(c_{L}^{2}-2c_{T}^{2})(\partial_{k}\xi^{k})\delta_{ij}\,,

and so in the general case we have

(32)

T^{ij}=\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}(\partial_{i}\xi^{j}+\partial_{j}\xi^{i})+\rho_{0}(c_{L}^{2}-2c_{T}^{2})(\partial_{k}\xi^{k})\delta_{ij}-\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}\varphi\delta_{i1}\delta_{j1}+\rho_{0}c_{T}^{2}\varphi\delta_{i2}\delta_{j2}\,.

If
n^{i}
is the unit normal to the boundary of the elastic body then the natural boundary condition
T^{ij}n^{j}=0
reads

(33)

\boxed{c_{T}^{2}(\partial_{i}\xi^{j}+\partial_{j}\xi^{i})n^{j}+(c_{L}^{2}-2c_{T}^{2})(\partial_{k}\xi^{k})n^{i}-c_{T}^{2}\varphi\delta_{i1}n^{1}+c_{T}^{2}\varphi\delta_{i2}n^{2}=0}\,.

Therefore, the gravitational wave modifies the boundary conditions whenever the body is not a perfect fluid, that is, whenever
c_{T}^{2}\neq 0
.

Finally, it is interesting to note that if we write

(34)

h=g-\eta=\varphi(t-z)dx^{2}-\varphi(t-z)dy^{2}\,,

where
\eta
denotes the Minkowski metric, and if
T^{\mu\nu}
is the energy-momentum tensor (
31
) for the elastic material in Minkowski spacetime, then the interaction term can be written as

(35)

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}T^{xx}\varphi+\frac{1}{2}T^{yy}\varphi=-\frac{1}{2}T^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}\,,

which is precisely Dyson’s interaction Lagrangian in the effective potential approach
[
15
]
.

3.
Elastic response of a rectangular plate to a gravitational wave

We now apply the linearized equations of motion to the problem of a thin rectangular plate under the influence of a passing gravitational wave. In order to obtain explicit expressions, we assume that the plate is orthogonal to the direction of propagation of the wave and aligned with its polarization.

Iterating this identity, and assuming that

that is, that the plate is at rest in the remote past, leads to

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=f(u-4A)+\frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{u-2A}{c_{L}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{u-3A}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{u}{c_{L}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{u-A}{c_{L}}\right)=\dots

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n}\varphi\left(\frac{u-nA}{c_{L}}\right)\,,

and consequently to

(61)

\boxed{\xi^{\prime}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n}\varphi\left(\frac{c_{L}t-x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\varphi\left(\frac{c_{L}t+x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)}\,.

Setting

\Phi(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{u}\varphi(s)ds\,,

we have

(63)

\boxed{\xi(t,x)=\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\Phi\left(\frac{c_{L}t-x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\Phi\left(\frac{c_{L}t+x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)}\,,

whence

(64)

\boxed{\dot{\xi}(t,x)=\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\varphi\left(\frac{c_{L}t-x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\varphi\left(\frac{c_{L}t+x-nA}{c_{L}}\right)}\,.

4.
Example: short gravitational wave burst

Let us now compute the plate’s reaction to different types of gravitational wave signals. As a first example, consider a gravitational wave burst whose duration is shorter than half the minimum sound-crossing time of the plate. Accordingly, we assume that the support of
\varphi
satisfies

\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subset\left[-\frac{L}{2c_{L}},0\right]\,,

where
L=\min\{A,B\}
. Then, for
t=0

) reduces to a single term, yielding, for

and

(67)

\dot{\xi}\left(0,x\right)=-\frac{c_{L}}{2}\varphi\left(-\frac{x}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{c_{L}}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{x-A}{c_{L}}\right)\,,

with similar expressions for
\eta^{\prime}\left(0,y\right)
and
\dot{\eta}\left(0,y\right)

\displaystyle E(0)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}AB+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}B\int_{0}^{A}\left(2\times\frac{c_{L}^{2}}{4}\varphi^{2}\left(-\frac{x}{c_{L}}\right)+2\times\frac{c_{L}^{2}}{4}\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{x-A}{c_{L}}\right)\right)dx

(68)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}A\int_{0}^{B}\left(2\times\frac{c_{L}^{2}}{4}\varphi^{2}\left(-\frac{y}{c_{L}}\right)+2\times\frac{c_{L}^{2}}{4}\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{y-B}{c_{L}}\right)\right)dy

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}AB+\sigma_{0}c_{L}^{3}\frac{A+B}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(t)dt\,.

In other words, the energy absorbed by the plate is

(69)

\boxed{\Delta E=\sigma_{0}c_{L}^{3}\frac{A+B}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(t)dt}\,.

Let us compare this with the total gravitational wave energy incident on the plate. The energy density of the gravitational wave is
[
24
, pp. 955]

(70)

T_{00}^{\rm(GW)}=\frac{1}{32\pi}\left\langle\partial_{t}h_{ij}^{\rm TT}\partial_{t}h_{ij}^{\rm TT}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{16\pi}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(t-z)\right)^{2}\,,

and so the total energy incident on the rectangular plate is

(71)

E^{\rm(GW)}=\frac{AB}{16\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}dz\,.

We can compare
\Delta E
with
E^{\rm(GW)}
by using Poincaré’s inequality for compactly supported smooth functions, which in this case can be deduced from (
65
): since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}z^{\prime}\varphi^{2}(z)dz=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\varphi(z)\varphi^{\prime}(z)dz\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{L}{2c_{L}}\left|\varphi(z)\right|\left|\varphi^{\prime}(z)\right|dz

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{c_{L}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(z)dz\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}dz\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

(73)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(z)dz\leq\frac{L^{2}}{c_{L}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}dz\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{0}L^{2}c_{L}\frac{A+B}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{2}dz

(74)

\displaystyle=16\pi\sigma_{0}L^{2}c_{L}\frac{A+B}{2AB}E^{\rm(GW)}\leq\frac{16\pi Mc_{L}}{L}E^{\rm(GW)}\,,

where
M=\sigma_{0}AB
is the total rest mass of the plate, and we used the fact that
\frac{2AB}{A+B}\geq L
. In our test body approximation, we must of course have

(75)

\frac{M}{L}\ll 1\,,

and, since
c_{L}\leq 1
, we see that

(76)

\boxed{\Delta E\ll E^{\rm(GW)}}\,,

that is, the energy deposited on the plate is much smaller than the total incident energy of the gravitational wave.

5.
Example: continuous harmonic gravitational wave

As a second example, let us take

(77)

\varphi(t)=\begin{cases}h\sin\left(\omega t\right)\text{ if }t\leq 0\\
0\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}\,,

corresponding to an infinitely long harmonic gravitational wave signal with amplitude
h
and angular frequency
\omega
, which terminates at time
t=0
. Since the duration of a physically realistic signal is expected to be much longer than the vibration periods of the plate, but much shorter than the relaxation time due to internal dissipation (which is not included in our model), we will use Abel regularization (which “forgets” about very early times) to sum the series and integrals that might diverge due to the infinite duration of the signal. We have

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega(-x-nA)}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega(x-nA)}{c_{L}}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}+n\left(\pi+\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}+n\left(\pi-\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)\right)

and

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega(-x-nA)}{c_{L}}\right)+\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega(x-nA)}{c_{L}}\right)

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}+n\left(\pi+\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)\right)-\frac{c_{L}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}h\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}+n\left(\pi-\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)\right)\,.

From the partial sum expression

(80)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin\left(n\alpha+\beta\right)=\operatorname{Im}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{i(n\alpha+\beta)}=\operatorname{Im}\left(e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\frac{1-e^{iN\alpha}}{1-e^{i\alpha}}\right)

it is clear that the series

(81)

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\sin\left(n\alpha+\beta\right)

diverges.
5
5

5

This reflects the fact that the wave interacts with the plate over the infinite interval
t\in(-\infty,0)
in the absence of dissipation.
However, we can still obtain a solution by noting that the series is Abel-summable since, for
\alpha\not\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\sin\left(n\alpha+\beta\right)e^{-\varepsilon n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{Im}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{(i\alpha-\varepsilon)n+i\beta}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{Im}\frac{e^{i(\alpha+\beta)-\varepsilon}}{1-e^{i\alpha-\varepsilon}}

(82)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\frac{e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}}{1-e^{i\alpha}}=\frac{\sin(\alpha+\beta)-\sin\beta}{2(1-\cos\alpha)}\,.

If we then take

(83)

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\sin\left(n\alpha+\beta\right)=\frac{\sin(\alpha+\beta)-\sin\beta}{2(1-\cos\alpha)}\Rightarrow\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sin\left(n\alpha+\beta\right)=\frac{\sin(\alpha-\beta)+\sin\beta}{2(1-\cos\alpha)}

we have, for
\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\not\in\pi+2\pi\mathbb{Z}

(84)

\xi^{\prime}(0,x)=-\frac{h}{4}\,\frac{\sin\left(\pi+\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}-\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}\right)}{1-\cos\left(\pi+\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)}-\frac{h}{4}\,\frac{\sin\left(\pi-\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}+\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}\right)-\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}\right)}{1-\cos\left(\pi-\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)}=0

and analogously

(85)

\dot{\xi}(0,x)=\frac{hc_{L}}{2}\,\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}-\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}\right)}{1+\cos\left(\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\right)}=\frac{hc_{L}\sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{c_{L}}-\frac{\omega A}{2c_{L}}\right)}{2\cos\left(\frac{\omega A}{2c_{L}}\right)}\,,

with similar expressions for
\eta^{\prime}(0,y)
and
\dot{\eta}(0,y)
, where we should make the substitutions
A\mapsto B
,
x\mapsto y
and change the overall sign. Therefore, we have from (
54
)

\displaystyle E(0)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}AB+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}\int_{0}^{B}\int_{0}^{A}\left(\dot{\xi}^{2}(0,x)+\dot{\eta}^{2}(0,y)\right)dx\,dy

(86)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}AB+\sigma_{0}\frac{ABc_{L}^{2}h^{2}}{16}\left[\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\frac{\omega A}{2c_{L}})}+\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\frac{\omega B}{2c_{L}})}\right]\,,

that is, the total energy absorbed by the plate is

(87)

\boxed{\Delta E=\frac{Mc_{L}^{2}h^{2}}{16}\left[2+\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\omega A}{2c_{L}}\right)+\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\omega B}{2c_{L}}\right)\right]}\,.

Note that this expression becomes singular for

(88)

\frac{\omega A}{c_{L}}\in\pi+2\pi\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{ or }\quad\frac{\omega B}{c_{L}}\in\pi+2\pi\mathbb{Z}\,,

that is, when the frequency of the gravitational wave coincides with one of the longitudinal normal mode frequencies of the plate. This resonant divergence is to be expected, since our model does not include any damping mechanism.

These results are, of course, perturbative, and there is no reason why they should hold at higher orders in perturbation theory; it is nonetheless interesting to note that, for the values of

Physically, these non-emission configurations arise from a cancellation between two competing effects in the quadrupole moment: the decrease in density caused by the stretching of the plate and the increase associated with its changing physical size. At the points where these contributions balance exactly, the quadrupole moment remains constant and no gravitational radiation is emitted at linear order.

Finally, we compute the ratio between the power of the gravitational wave emitted by the plate and that of the incident wave. Using the expression for the energy flux of a gravitational wave, also given by (

while the energy carried by the incident wave into the plate is simply

(104)

(105)

Although this ratio is typically very small, due to the factor
\frac{M^{2}}{AB}
, it can become appreciable near the resonant frequencies, as should be expected, since our model does not take damping into account.

7.
Conclusions

In this paper, we re-derived the model describing the interaction between a gravitational wave and an elastic body. A distinctive feature of the formulation is that the motion of the elastic medium is governed by the usual Navier–Cauchy equations (
30
); the gravitational wave affects the motion only through the boundary conditions (
33
), rather than through an external forcing term in the bulk equations. We then specialized to the case of a thin rectangular plate with vanishing Poisson ratio interacting with a plane gravitational wave whose propagation direction and polarization are aligned with the plate. In this configuration, we showed that the equations of motion reduce to the two decoupled one-dimensional wave equations (
38
), together with the boundary conditions (
42
)–(
45
). This reduction makes it possible to obtain explicit analytical expressions for the displacement fields and their derivatives, namely equations (
61
), (
63
), and (
64
), directly in terms of the gravitational wave signal.

We further derived explicit formulas for the energy transferred from the gravitational wave to the elastic body, both for a short burst, equation (
69
), and for a long harmonic signal, equation (
87
). In the harmonic case, Abel regularization was employed to describe the physically relevant regime in which the duration of the signal is much longer than the oscillation period of the plate while remaining shorter than the dissipation timescale. Finally, we computed the gravitational radiation emitted by the oscillating plate when driven by a continuous harmonic wave, equations (
100
)–(
101
). Although the plate oscillates at the same frequency as the incident wave, we found that, for certain parameter values, the emitted gravitational radiation vanishes identically. This unexpected result appears to originate from a cancellation between the contributions arising from the oscillating density and those associated with the changing physical dimensions of the plate.

Several natural directions for future work emerge from the present analysis. One particularly important extension would be the inclusion of dissipative effects, such as viscosity or internal damping, which would regularize the resonant divergences in this idealized model and provide a more realistic description of elastic materials. It would also be worthwhile to investigate more general constitutive relations, anisotropic or inhomogeneous media, and fully three-dimensional elastic bodies with nontrivial geometries.

Another natural direction would be to study more general gravitational wave backgrounds, including arbitrary incidence angles, different polarizations, wave packets, and nonlinear gravitational waves. It would also be interesting to determine whether similar cancellation effects arise in more realistic detector models or in astrophysical elastic media such as neutron star crusts. More broadly, the framework developed here may provide a useful starting point for constructing analytically tractable relativistic models of gravitational wave detectors and for exploring further connections between elasticity theory and gravitational wave physics.
