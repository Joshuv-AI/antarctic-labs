Betti numbers for cochordal zero-divisor graphs of commutative rings
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
This paper studies the zero-divisor graphs attached to several finite chain-ring families and computes the homological invariants of their edge ideals by using cochordal constructible systems. We begin with a general layered graph C ⁡ ( q , L ) \displaystyle C(q,L) , whose vertices are arranged according to valuation layers and whose adjacency is governed by the single rule k + ℓ ≥ L \displaystyle k+\ell\geq L , form some integers k \displaystyle k and ℓ \displaystyle\ell . This graph models the zero-divisor structure of a finite chain ring with residue field of order q \displaystyle q and nilpotency index L \displaystyle L . We prove that C ⁡ ( q , L ) \displaystyle C(q,L) is cochordal, determine its type sequence, then correct and refine the Betti formula of its edge ideal [Dung and Vu, Cochordal zero divisor graphs and Betti numbers of their edge ideals, Comm. Algebra 54(2) (2026) 736–744]. The results are then specialized to the Gaussian quotient rings ℤ 2 m ​ [ i ] \displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i] and to the truncated polynomial rings ℤ p ​ [ x ] / ( x c ) \displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}) . We compute projective dimension, regularity, independence number, height, Hilbert series, and Cohen–Macaulay behavior. The computations show that these quotient rings have 2 \displaystyle 2 -linear resolutions, while Cohen–Macaulayness occurs only in the expected degenerate or complete-graph cases.

Introduction

The zero-divisor graph of a commutative ring records multiplication-to-zero by a simple graph. Since Beck’s original study of colorings of commutative rings
[
7
]
and the subsequent structural formulation of Anderson and Livingston
[
3
]
, this graph has served as a useful interface between ring theory and finite graph theory. For the residue class ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, the graph is especially concrete, as its vertices are the nonzero residues whose greatest common divisor with
\displaystyle n
is not one, and two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y
are adjacent precisely when
\displaystyle n
divides
\displaystyle xy
. The study of zero-divisor graphs began with Beck’s work on coloring problems for commutative rings
[
7
]
, and was later developed systematically by Anderson and Livingston, who introduced the standard zero-divisor graph associated with a commutative ring
[
3
]
. Subsequent work investigated structural properties of these graphs, for example, Akbari, Maimani and Yassemi characterized important cases in which zero-divisor graphs are planar
[
1
]
, while Anderson and Badawi further refined the theory by studying zero-divisor graphs from the perspective of ring-theoretic annihilation and adjacency relations
[
2
]
. In
[
4
]
, the authors showed that zero divisor graphs are universal graphs.

The graph-theoretic simplicity of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
hides a rather rich homological problem. If
\displaystyle G
is a finite simple graph on vertices
\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}
, its edge ideal is the squarefree quadratic monomial ideal
\displaystyle I(G)=(x_{i}x_{j}:\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G))
in a standard graded polynomial ring. The minimal free resolution of
\displaystyle S/I(G)
measures the arrangement of edges, induced subgraphs, and vertex covers of
\displaystyle G
in a way that is sensitive enough to detect induced matchings and chordality, see the standard references
[
24
,
46
]
.

A decisive point is Fröberg’s theorem: the edge ideal
\displaystyle I(G)
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution if and only if the complement graph
\displaystyle\overline{G}
is chordal
[
19
]
. Thus, a cochordal graph is not only a graph with a forbidden-cycle condition in its complement, but also a graph whose edge ideal has the cleanest possible homological shape. For such a graph all nonzero graded Betti numbers of
\displaystyle S/I(G)
lie on the linear strand
\displaystyle\beta_{i,i+1}
, and hence the total Betti number
\displaystyle\beta_{i}
is already the relevant graded invariant.

The residue class graphs
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
therefore provide a natural test family for the interaction between arithmetic and homological algebra. The arithmetic supplies valuation classes, and the edge relation becomes an inequality on the exponents of prime factors. The homological task is to convert this valuation data into a free resolution. Earlier work by Rather, Imran and Diene
[
41
]
and by Rather, Imran and Pirzada
[
42
]
computed parts of the linear strand in several small arithmetic families, including cases such as
\displaystyle p^{4}
,
\displaystyle p^{2}q
, and
\displaystyle pqr
. Pirzada and Rather
[
34
]
studied the linear strand of edge ideals of some zero divisor graphs.

A recent manuscript of Dung and Vu
[
15
]
proposed a broader method based on cochordal constructible systems and type sequences. The manuscript proves, in essence, that
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal exactly when
\displaystyle n
has one of the forms
\displaystyle p^{a}
,
\displaystyle p^{a}q
, or
\displaystyle pqr
, where the primes appearing are distinct. It also proposes closed Betti-number formulae for the corresponding edge ideals. The present paper validates the useful part of that approach and corrects the parts that are not compatible with the actual edge relation. The first correction is global and affects every family: the type-sequence Betti formula contains a subtractive term
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1}
, where
\displaystyle k
is the number of center steps in the construction. When the arithmetic type sequence is evaluated, this term does not disappear. Omitting it already gives the wrong answer for mentioned values in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
, whose edge ideal is principal but for which the uncorrected expression gives two first syzygy generators.
The second correction is structural. In the family
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
, a class represented by
\displaystyle p^{i}q
is not a clique unless
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
. Similarly, in the square-free family
\displaystyle n=pqr
, the classes represented by
\displaystyle pq
,
\displaystyle pr
, and
\displaystyle qr
are independent sets, not cliques, because the square of such a representative misses one prime factor. Any constructible system that incorporates previously introduced vertices from the same independent class in the cover generates edges absent from the graph. This changes the type sequences and, consequently, the Betti formulae.

The corrected formulae require a mild refinement of the type calculus. A clique block contributes repeated identical binomial terms after the type entries are shifted by their positions. An independent repeated block contributes a hockey-stick sum (binomial-coefficient summation formula like
\displaystyle\sum_{j=r}^{m}\binom{j}{r}=\binom{m+1}{r+1}
) of binomial coefficients, because the type entries are constant while the positional shifts increase. This distinction is the technical source of the new formulae in Sections
6
, and
7
.

The problem studied here can now be stated precisely. Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2
, let
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, let
\displaystyle G_{n}=\Gamma(R)
, and let
\displaystyle I_{n}=I(G_{n})\subseteq S_{n}
, where
\displaystyle S_{n}
is the polynomial ring whose variables correspond to the nonzero zero-divisors of
\displaystyle R
. We ask: for which
\displaystyle n
is
\displaystyle G_{n}
cochordal, and, in those cases, what are the exact graded Betti numbers of
\displaystyle S_{n}/I_{n}
?

Our answer is complete for the cochordal cases. The classification theorem remains
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
,
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
, or
\displaystyle n=pqr
. For
\displaystyle p^{a}
the type blocks from the valuation classes with exponent at least
\displaystyle\lceil a/2\rceil
are clique blocks, so the corrected Betti formula is the earlier block sum minus the missing global binomial term. For
\displaystyle p^{a}q
the high valuation blocks are clique blocks but the low valuation blocks are independent repeated blocks, giving a mixed formula. For
\displaystyle pqr
all three center classes are independent repeated blocks, and the formula is a sum of three hockey-stick differences minus the same global correction.

The corrected results also clarify projective dimension. In the prime-power case, the projective dimension is still
\displaystyle p^{a-1}-2
for
\displaystyle a\geq 2
, with the convention that the graph is edgeless when this number is negative. In the two-prime case, it is the maximum of the high-clique and low-independent block endpoints, and therefore it is not generally
\displaystyle qp^{a-1}-2
. In the square-free three-prime case, for
\displaystyle p<q<r
, the projective dimension remains
\displaystyle qr+p-3
, but the full Betti table differs from the uncorrected one.

The purpose of this study is to carry out an analogous program for other zero divisor graphs: (1)
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{m})
of the Gaussian integer ring modulo powers of
\displaystyle 2
, that is,
\displaystyle R_{m}=\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]/(2^{m}),
where
\displaystyle m
is a positive integer, and (2) for zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{p,c})
of the truncated polynomial rings
\displaystyle R_{p,c}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/\langle x^{c}\rangle
, where
\displaystyle p
is prime and
\displaystyle c\geq 2
is a positive integer. Our goals are the following:

(1) Determine the type sequence of
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{m})
explicitly and, compute
all Betti numbers of the edge ideal
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(R_{m}))
, which gives a closed
formulas for the projective dimension and regularity.

(2) We study the Cohen–Macaulay property and the Hilbert series of
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{m}))
. We show that
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{m}))

is Cohen–Macaulay only for
\displaystyle m=1
, while for
\displaystyle m\geq 2
the depth is
\displaystyle 1
and the
dimension is at least
\displaystyle 2
. We further give a combinatorial description of
the Hilbert series of
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{m}))
in terms of independent sets in

\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{m})
, viewed as the Stanley–Reisner ring of the independence
complex, in the spirit of Stanley
[
45
]
. A similar type of study is carried for the zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{p,c})
.

. Banerjee

The paper is organized as: Section
2
fixes notation, recalls zero-divisor graphs, chordal and cochordal graphs, edge ideals, Betti splittings, and the type-sequence formula used later. Section
3
develops a corrected type-sequence calculus. It proves the block-evaluation rules used in all later formulae and gives an algorithm that turns a valid constructible system into Betti numbers. Section
4
proves the arithmetic classification of cochordal zero-divisor graphs. The necessity is obtained from induced matching obstructions, while sufficiency follows from explicit corrected constructible systems in the three surviving arithmetic families.
Section
5
treats
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
. We prove the corrected type sequence, the corrected Betti formula, projective dimension, and several small numerical checks. Section
6
treats
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
. The main point is the mixed nature of the type sequence: high
\displaystyle p
-valuation
\displaystyle q
-classes are clique blocks, whereas low ones are independent repeated blocks.
Section
7
treats
\displaystyle n=pqr
. We prove that all three center blocks are independent repeated blocks and derive the corrected closed formula for the whole linear strand.
Section
8
derives Hilbert series from the corrected linear resolutions. It gives a uniform numerator formula, family-specific vertex counts, numerical Hilbert-series examples.
Section
9
studies Cohen–Macaulayness. It proves that, among the cochordal residue-class cases, the quotient
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))
is Cohen–Macaulay only in the trivial prime case and in the complete-graph case
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
.
Section
10
develops a uniform template for finite chain rings. Sections
11
–
14
specialize the template to
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]
. Sections
15
–
17
treat the truncated polynomial rings
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})
. The final section summarizes the results and points to possible extensions.

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper all graphs are finite and simple. If
\displaystyle G
is a graph, then
\displaystyle V(G)
and
\displaystyle E(G)
denote its vertex set and edge set. For a vertex
\displaystyle v\in V(G)
, the
degree
of
\displaystyle v
in
\displaystyle G
, denoted by

\displaystyle\deg_{G}(v)
, is the number of vertices adjacent to
\displaystyle v
.
The minimum and maximum degrees of
\displaystyle G
are denoted by

\displaystyle\delta(G)=\min\{\deg_{G}(v):v\in V(G)\}

and

\displaystyle\Delta(G)=\max\{\deg_{G}(v):v\in V(G)\}.

For a vertex
\displaystyle v\in V(G)
, its open neighborhood is

\displaystyle N_{G}(v)=\{u\in V(G):\{u,v\}\in E(G)\}.
For a set
\displaystyle A\subseteq V(G)
, we use

\displaystyle N_{G}(A)=\left(\bigcup_{v\in A}N_{G}(v)\right)\setminus A

for the open neighborhood of
\displaystyle A
, and

\displaystyle N_{G}[A]=A\cup N_{G}(A)

for its closed neighborhood. Let
\displaystyle G
be a simple graph with vertex set
\displaystyle V(G)
and edge set
\displaystyle E(G)
.
The
complement
of
\displaystyle G
, denoted by
\displaystyle\overline{G}
, is the graph with the same
vertex set
\displaystyle V(\overline{G})=V(G)
, where two distinct vertices
\displaystyle u,v\in V(G)
are
adjacent in
\displaystyle\overline{G}
if and only if they are not adjacent in
\displaystyle G
. Equivalently,

E(\overline{G})=\bigl\{\{u,v\}:u,v\in V(G),\ u\neq v,\ \{u,v\}\notin E(G)\bigr\}.

A subset
\displaystyle A\subseteq V(G)
is called an
independent set
of
\displaystyle G
if no two
distinct vertices of
\displaystyle A
are adjacent in
\displaystyle G
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle\{u,v\}\notin E(G)
for all distinct
\displaystyle u,v\in A.
A subset
\displaystyle C\subseteq V(G)
is called a
clique
of
\displaystyle G
if every two distinct
vertices of
\displaystyle C
are adjacent in
\displaystyle G
.
A simple graph
\displaystyle G
is called
chordal
if every cycle of length at least
\displaystyle 4

has a chord. Equivalently,
\displaystyle G
is chordal if there is no induced cycle of length
greater than
\displaystyle 3
. Here, a
chord
of a cycle is an edge joining two non-consecutive vertices
of the cycle. A simple graph
\displaystyle G
is called
cochordal
if its complement
\displaystyle\overline{G}
is
chordal. That is,
\displaystyle G
is cochordal if every induced cycle of length at least
\displaystyle 4

is absent from
\displaystyle\overline{G}
.
A subset
\displaystyle U\subseteq V(G)
is called a
vertex cover
of
\displaystyle G
if every edge
of
\displaystyle G
has at least one endpoint in
\displaystyle U
. Equivalently, for every edge

\displaystyle\{u,v\}\in E(G)
, one has
\displaystyle u\in U
or
\displaystyle v\in U
. The minimum cardinality of a
vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
is called the
vertex cover number
of
\displaystyle G
and is
denoted by
\displaystyle\tau(G)
. The
independence number
of
\displaystyle G
, denoted by
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
, is the maximum
cardinality of an independent set of
\displaystyle G
. Thus

\alpha(G)=\max\{|A|:A\subseteq V(G)\text{ is independent}\}.

The
clique number
of
\displaystyle G
, denoted by
\displaystyle\omega(G)
, is the maximum
cardinality of a clique of
\displaystyle G
. Thus

\omega(G)=\max\{|C|:C\subseteq V(G)\text{ is a clique}\}.

A
matching
in
\displaystyle G
is a set of pairwise disjoint edges, that is, no two
edges in a matching share a common endpoint. The maximum cardinality of a
matching in
\displaystyle G
is called the
matching number
of
\displaystyle G
and is denoted by

\displaystyle\nu(G)
.
An
induced matching
in
\displaystyle G
is a matching
\displaystyle M
such that the subgraph of

\displaystyle G
induced by the vertices appearing in the edges of
\displaystyle M
has edge set exactly

\displaystyle M
. Equivalently, if
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
and
\displaystyle\{x,y\}
are two distinct edges in
\displaystyle M
,
then there is no edge of
\displaystyle G
joining a vertex of
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
to a vertex of

\displaystyle\{x,y\}
. The maximum cardinality of an induced matching in
\displaystyle G
is called the

induced matching number
of
\displaystyle G
and is denoted by
\displaystyle\operatorname{im}(G)
.
A graph
\displaystyle G
is called a
threshold graph
if there exist real numbers

\displaystyle w(v)
for
\displaystyle v\in V(G)
and a real number
\displaystyle T
such that, for any two distinct
vertices
\displaystyle u,v\in V(G)
,

\{u,v\}\in E(G)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w(u)+w(v)\geq T.

The numbers
\displaystyle w(v)
are called threshold weights, and
\displaystyle T
is called a threshold.

Let
\displaystyle R
be a commutative ring with unity, and let
\displaystyle Z(R)
denote the set of zero
divisors of
\displaystyle R
. The
zero-divisor graph
of
\displaystyle R
, denoted by
\displaystyle\Gamma(R)
,
is the simple graph whose vertex set is the set of nonzero zero divisors of
\displaystyle R
,
that is,

\displaystyle V(\Gamma(R))=Z(R)\setminus\{0\}.

Two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y\in V(\Gamma(R))
are adjacent if and only if their
product is zero in
\displaystyle R
. Equivalently,

\{x,y\}\in E(\Gamma(R))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad xy=0.

Thus,
\displaystyle\Gamma(R)
records the multiplicative annihilation relation among the
nonzero zero divisors of
\displaystyle R
. Since loops are not allowed, a vertex
\displaystyle x
is not
joined to itself, even when
\displaystyle x^{2}=0
.

The
independence polynomial
of
\displaystyle G
is the generating function

F_{G}(y)=\sum_{A\in\operatorname{Ind}(G)}y^{|A|},

where
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}(G)
denotes the set of all independent sets of
\displaystyle G
.
Equivalently, if
\displaystyle f_{r-1}
denotes the number of independent sets of cardinality

\displaystyle r
, then

F_{G}(y)=\sum_{r\geq 0}f_{r-1}y^{r}.

The
independence complex
of
\displaystyle G
, denoted by
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}(G)
, is
the simplicial complex whose faces are precisely the independent sets of
\displaystyle G
.
Thus

\operatorname{Ind}(G)=\{A\subseteq V(G):A\text{ is an independent set of }G\}.

A
facet
of
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}(G)
is a maximal independent set of
\displaystyle G
,
where maximal means maximal with respect to inclusion. A maximum independent set
is an independent set of cardinality
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
. Every maximum independent set
is maximal, but a maximal independent set need not have maximum cardinality.

Let
\displaystyle S=\mathbb{K}[x_{v}:v\in V(G)]
be a standard graded polynomial ring over a field
\displaystyle\mathbb{K}
. The edge ideal of
\displaystyle G
is

I(G)=(x_{u}x_{v}:\{u,v\}\in E(G))\subseteq S.

For a finitely generated graded
\displaystyle S
-module
\displaystyle M
, the graded Betti numbers are

\beta_{i,j}^{S}(M)=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(\mathbb{K},M)_{j}.

We write
\displaystyle\beta_{i}(M)=\sum_{j}\beta_{i,j}(M)
,
\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}(M)=\sup\{i:\beta_{i}(M)\neq 0\}
, and
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(M)=\sup\{j-i:\beta_{i,j}(M)\neq 0\}
.

For an edge ideal
\displaystyle I(G)
, the height of
\displaystyle I(G)
is equal to the vertex cover
number of
\displaystyle G
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{height}I(G)=\tau(G).

and
\displaystyle\dim S/I(G)=\alpha(G).

Indeed, the minimal primes of
\displaystyle I(G)
correspond to the minimal vertex covers of

\displaystyle G
, while the faces of the Stanley–Reisner complex of
\displaystyle S/I(G)
are the
independent sets of
\displaystyle G
.

A minimal graded free resolution of
\displaystyle S/I
records the syzygies among the generators of
\displaystyle I
and encodes the homological invariants of the quotient through its graded Betti numbers.

A minimal graded free resolution of
\displaystyle S/I
has the form

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{p,j}(S/I)}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{1,j}(S/I)}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow S/I\longrightarrow 0,

where
\displaystyle p=\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I)
and
\displaystyle\beta_{r,j}(S/I)
are the graded Betti numbers.

If
\displaystyle I
is generated in degree
\displaystyle 2
and has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution, then the resolution of
\displaystyle S/I
is

0\longrightarrow S(-(p+1))^{\beta_{p}(S/I)}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow S(-3)^{\beta_{2}(S/I)}\longrightarrow S(-2)^{\beta_{1}(S/I)}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow S/I\longrightarrow 0.

Equivalently, the minimal graded free resolution of
\displaystyle I
itself is

0\longrightarrow S(-(p+2))^{\beta_{p+1}(S/I)}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow S(-4)^{\beta_{3}(S/I)}\longrightarrow S(-3)^{\beta_{2}(S/I)}\longrightarrow S(-2)^{\beta_{1}(S/I)}\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow 0.

The ideal
\displaystyle I
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution if all its nonzero graded Betti numbers occur in degree
\displaystyle r+2
, equivalently
\displaystyle\beta_{r,j}(I)=0
for
\displaystyle j\neq r+2
. For the quotient
\displaystyle S/I
, this corresponds to nonzero Betti numbers only in degree
\displaystyle r+1
.

For a positive integer
\displaystyle n
, the zero-divisor graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
has as vertices the nonzero zero-divisors of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y
are adjacent when
\displaystyle xy\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Equivalently, if representatives are chosen in
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, then
\displaystyle\{x,y\}
is an edge exactly when
\displaystyle n\mid xy
.

If
\displaystyle R
is a commutative ring and
\displaystyle x\in R
, then
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\{r\in R:rx=0\}
. The compressed zero-divisor graph
\displaystyle\Gamma_{E}(R)
has vertices as the equivalence classes of nonzero zero-divisors with respect to equality of annihilators, and two classes are adjacent if the product of their representatives is zero. This graph is useful because, for
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, the classes are described by the divisor type of
\displaystyle\gcd(x,n)
.

For
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
, the nonzero zero-divisors are partitioned into classes

V_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}:[x]=[p^{i}]\},\qquad 1\leq i\leq a-1,

and
\displaystyle|V_{i}|=\varphi(p^{a-i})=p^{a-i-1}(p-1)
. If
\displaystyle x\in V_{i}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{j}
, then
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent exactly when
\displaystyle i+j\geq a
.

For
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
, where
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q
are distinct primes, we use the classes

A_{t}=\{x:[x]=[p^{t}]\},\quad 1\leq t\leq a,\qquad B_{i}=\{x:[x]=[p^{i}q]\},\quad 0\leq i\leq a-1.

The sizes are
\displaystyle|A_{a}|=q-1
,
\displaystyle|A_{t}|=p^{a-t-1}(p-1)(q-1)
for
\displaystyle 1\leq t\leq a-1
, and
\displaystyle|B_{i}|=p^{a-i-1}(p-1)
. The edges satisfy
\displaystyle A_{t}A_{u}\notin E
, while
\displaystyle A_{t}B_{i}\in E
exactly when
\displaystyle t+i\geq a
, and
\displaystyle B_{i}B_{j}\in E
exactly when
\displaystyle i+j\geq a
for distinct vertices.

For
\displaystyle n=pqr
with distinct primes, we use the classes represented by
\displaystyle p,q,r,pq,pr,qr
. Edges occur precisely when the product of the two labels contains all three primes. In particular, the classes represented by
\displaystyle pq
,
\displaystyle pr
, and
\displaystyle qr
are independent classes, even though different such classes are mutually adjacent.

A
valuation class
is the set of residue classes in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
having the same divisibility pattern with respect to the prime powers dividing
\displaystyle n
. If
\displaystyle n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{t}^{a_{t}},
then for a nonzero residue
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, define the
\displaystyle p_{i}
-adic valuation truncated at
\displaystyle a_{i}
by

v_{p_{i}}(x)=\max\{e:p_{i}^{e}\mid x\},\qquad 0\leq e\leq a_{i},

where
\displaystyle v_{p_{i}}(0)
is treated as
\displaystyle a_{i}
modulo
\displaystyle p_{i}^{a_{i}}
. Then the valuation vector of
\displaystyle x
is

\nu(x)=\bigl(v_{p_{1}}(x),\ldots,v_{p_{t}}(x)\bigr).

The valuation class corresponding to a vector
\displaystyle\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{t})
is

C_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}:\nu(x)=\alpha\}.

So two elements
\displaystyle x,y
lie in the same valuation class exactly when they are divisible by the same powers of each prime divisor of
\displaystyle n
. For example, if
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
, the relevant classes include

\displaystyle C_{(i,0)}=\{x:v_{p}(x)=i,\ v_{q}(x)=0\},

and

\displaystyle C_{(i,1)}=\{x:v_{p}(x)=i,\ v_{q}(x)=1\}.

in our case, these correspond roughly to classes represented by divisors such as
\displaystyle[p^{i}]
and
\displaystyle[p^{i}q]
.

The reason valuation classes are useful is that adjacency in the zero-divisor graph can be read directly from valuations. In
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
, two nonzero zero divisors
\displaystyle x,y
are adjacent precisely when

\displaystyle xy\equiv 0\pmod{n},
which is equivalent to

v_{p_{i}}(x)+v_{p_{i}}(y)\geq a_{i}\quad\text{for every }i.

Thus, all vertices in the same valuation class have the same external adjacency behavior. This makes valuation classes a natural way to organize the zero-divisor graph.

The following definition records the construction of a graph by repeatedly adding a star whose leaf set is a vertex cover of the graph already built.

Definition 2.7

(Cochordal constructible system,
[

]
)

Let
\displaystyle P=(u_{k},U_{k},u_{k-1},U_{k-1},\ldots,u_{1},U_{1})

\displaystyle u_{j}\notin U_{\ell}
whenever
\displaystyle\ell\leq j
. Set
\displaystyle G_{1}=K_{\{u_{1}\},U_{1}}
and, recursively,

V(G_{j})=V(G_{j-1})\cup\{u_{j}\}\cup U_{j},\qquad E(G_{j})=E(G_{j-1})\cup E(K_{\{u_{j}\},U_{j}}).

The list
\displaystyle P
is a cochordal constructible system for
\displaystyle G
if
\displaystyle G=G_{k}
and
\displaystyle U_{j}
is a vertex cover of
\displaystyle G_{j-1}
for
\displaystyle j\geq 2
. Its type is the sequence
\displaystyle(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})
, where
\displaystyle a_{j}=|U_{j}|
.

(Constructible characterization, see

A finite simple graph is cochordal if and only if it admits a cochordal constructible system.

The following formula is the central Betti-number identity used in the paper

Theorem 2.9

(Type-sequence Betti formula, see
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a cochordal graph with a constructible system of type
\displaystyle(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})
. Then
\displaystyle S/I(G)
has a linear resolution and, for every
\displaystyle i\geq 1
,

\beta_{i,i+1}(S/I(G))=\beta_{i}(S/I(G))=\binom{a_{k}}{i}+\binom{a_{k-1}+1}{i}+\cdots+\binom{a_{1}+k-1}{i}-\binom{k}{i+1}.

Consequently,

\operatorname{pd}(S/I(G))=\max\{a_{k},a_{k-1}+1,\ldots,a_{1}+k-1\}.

The literature supplies the construction principle and the general type formula, but it does not automatically validate a proposed arithmetic type sequence. The point that must be checked is whether every vertex placed in a cover is truly adjacent to the new center. In
\displaystyle n\in{p^{a}q,pqr}
case, some valuation classes in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
are independent rather than cliques, and including earlier vertices from the same class creates false edges. Moreover, the term
\displaystyle-\binom{k}{i+1}
in Theorem

is essential and cannot be dropped when evaluating arithmetic blocks. Furthermore, we extend theory for new classes of zero divisor graphs.

3
Corrected type calculus and diagnostic principles

The results in this section do not change the abstract constructible-system (Theorem
2.8
,
[
15
]
). Instead, we give the corrected arithmetic evaluation rules needed for zero-divisor graphs. The distinction between decreasing clique blocks and constant independent blocks is absent from the formulae in
[
15
]
.

Before applying the type formula form Definition
2.7
, we shall often group the type sequence

\displaystyle(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})
into consecutive blocks. If
\displaystyle(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})=(T_{s},T_{s-1},\ldots,T_{1}),
then each
\displaystyle T_{i}
is called a
type block
. Thus
\displaystyle T_{i}
is only a convenient notation for the
subsequence of type entries arising from one valuation layer or one arithmetic
class. If the vertices in a layer
\displaystyle V_{i}
are ordered as

\displaystyle V_{i}=\{v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,n_{i}}\}
with corresponding cover sets

\displaystyle U_{i,1},\ldots,U_{i,n_{i}}
, then the associated type block is

T_{i}=(|U_{i,n_{i}}|,|U_{i,n_{i}-1}|,\ldots,|U_{i,1}|),

because the type sequence is written in reverse order from the construction
order.

The following result gives the corrected evaluation of a constructible type sequence in block form.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let a valid constructible system have type obtained by concatenating blocks
\displaystyle T_{s},\ldots,T_{1}
. Suppose block
\displaystyle T_{h}
has length
\displaystyle\ell_{h}
, and let
\displaystyle K_{>h}=\ell_{s}+\cdots+\ell_{h+1}
. If the entries of
\displaystyle T_{h}
are
\displaystyle c_{h},c_{h}-1,\ldots,c_{h}-\ell_{h}+1
, then its contribution to
\displaystyle\beta_{i}(S/I(G))
before the global correction is

\displaystyle\ell_{h}\binom{c_{h}+K_{>h}}{i}.

If the entries of
\displaystyle T_{h}
are all equal to
\displaystyle d_{h}
, then its contribution before the global correction is

\sum_{t=0}^{\ell_{h}-1}\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+t}{i}=\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+\ell_{h}}{i+1}-\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}}{i+1}.

The total Betti number is the sum of all block contributions minus
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1}
, where
\displaystyle k=\ell_{s}+\cdots+\ell_{1}
.

Proof.

Let the type sequence of the constructible system be

\displaystyle(a_{k},a_{k-1},\ldots,a_{1}),

where
\displaystyle k=\ell_{s}+\ell_{s-1}+\cdots+\ell_{1}
. By Theorem

2.9
, the uncorrected part of the
\displaystyle i
-th Betti number is

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{a_{j}+k-j}{i}.

Equivalently, if we read the type sequence from left to right, the entry in
the first position contributes with shift
\displaystyle 0
, the entry in the second
position contributes with shift
\displaystyle 1
, and so on. Thus, if the entry in the

\displaystyle t
-th position from the left is denoted by
\displaystyle b_{t}
, where

\displaystyle 0\leq t\leq k-1
, then its contribution before the global correction is

\displaystyle\binom{b_{t}+t}{i}.

This is the same formula as

\displaystyle\binom{a_{j}+k-j}{i},
since the entry
\displaystyle a_{j}
occupies the position
\displaystyle t=k-j
from the left.

Now fix a block
\displaystyle T_{h}
. Since the full type sequence is obtained by
concatenating the blocks

\displaystyle T_{s},T_{s-1},\ldots,T_{1},

the number of entries lying strictly to the left of
\displaystyle T_{h}
is

\displaystyle K_{>h}=\ell_{s}+\ell_{s-1}+\cdots+\ell_{h+1}.

Therefore the entries of
\displaystyle T_{h}
occupy the left-to-right positions

\displaystyle K_{>h},\ K_{>h}+1,\ \ldots,\ K_{>h}+\ell_{h}-1.

Suppose that

\displaystyle T_{h}=(c_{h},c_{h}-1,\ldots,c_{h}-\ell_{h}+1).

The
\displaystyle r
-th entry of this block, counted from
\displaystyle r=0
to
\displaystyle r=\ell_{h}-1
, is

\displaystyle c_{h}-r,

and its position from the left is

\displaystyle K_{>h}+r.

Hence its contribution before the global correction is

\binom{(c_{h}-r)+(K_{>h}+r)}{i}=\binom{c_{h}+K_{>h}}{i}.

The dependence on
\displaystyle r
cancels, as the entry decreases by
\displaystyle 1
at each step,
while the positional shift increases by
\displaystyle 1
at each step. Since there are

\displaystyle\ell_{h}
entries in the block, the whole block contributes

\sum_{r=0}^{\ell_{h}-1}\binom{c_{h}+K_{>h}}{i}=\ell_{h}\binom{c_{h}+K_{>h}}{i}.

Next suppose that

\displaystyle T_{h}=(d_{h},d_{h},\ldots,d_{h})
is a constant block of length
\displaystyle\ell_{h}
. The
\displaystyle r
-th entry of this block is

\displaystyle d_{h}
, and again its position from the left is
\displaystyle K_{>h}+r
. Therefore its
contribution is
\displaystyle\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+r}{i}.

Now, summing over all entries of the block, we have

\sum_{r=0}^{\ell_{h}-1}\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+r}{i}.

Let
\displaystyle N=d_{h}+K_{>h}.

Then this sum becomes

\sum_{r=0}^{\ell_{h}-1}\binom{N+r}{i}=\sum_{m=N}^{N+\ell_{h}-1}\binom{m}{i}.

By the hockey-stick identity, we have

\sum_{m=N}^{N+\ell_{h}-1}\binom{m}{i}=\binom{N+\ell_{h}}{i+1}-\binom{N}{i+1}.

Substituting back
\displaystyle N=d_{h}+K_{>h}
, we derive

\sum_{t=0}^{\ell_{h}-1}\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+t}{i}=\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}+\ell_{h}}{i+1}-\binom{d_{h}+K_{>h}}{i+1}.

Finally, Theorem
2.9
states that the complete Betti
number is obtained by subtracting the global correction term

\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1},

where

\displaystyle k=\ell_{s}+\ell_{s-1}+\cdots+\ell_{1}.

Hence the total value of
\displaystyle\beta_{i}(S/I(G))
is the sum of the block
contributions described above, minus
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1}
. This proves the
claim.
∎

The above proposition is new in its use here, as it separates the two arithmetic phenomena that were previously conflated. Ferrers graphs studied by Corso and Nagel
[
12
,
11
]
naturally lead to decreasing blocks. Zero-divisor graphs also produce constant blocks when a valuation class is independent.

Let
\displaystyle n=\prod_{t=1}^{s}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
and let
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. For a residue
\displaystyle x
, define

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)=\min\{v_{p_{t}}(x),\alpha_{t}\}.

Thus
\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)=\alpha_{t}
means that
\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
divides the chosen representative of
\displaystyle x
.

The following lemma identifies the ordinary zero-divisor vertices with annihilators and valuation classes.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
be nonzero zero divisors. Then
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\operatorname{ann}(y)
if and only if
\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)=\nu_{t}(y)
for every
\displaystyle t
. Moreover, the number of residues in the valuation class
\displaystyle\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s})
is
\displaystyle|C(\mathbf{e})|=\prod_{t=1}^{s}c_{t}(e_{t}),
where

c_{t}(e_{t})=\begin{cases}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}-e_{t}-1}(p_{t}-1),&0\leq e_{t}<\alpha_{t},\\
1,&e_{t}=\alpha_{t}.\end{cases}

The all-maximal vector
\displaystyle(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
corresponds to the zero residue and is omitted from the vertex set.

Proof.

Choose integer representatives of
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
, with

\displaystyle n=\prod_{t=1}^{s}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}.

For a residue
\displaystyle x
, let

\displaystyle d_{x}=\gcd(x,n).

Then

\displaystyle d_{x}=\prod_{t=1}^{s}p_{t}^{\nu_{t}(x)}.

Clearly, the exponent of
\displaystyle p_{t}
in
\displaystyle\gcd(x,n)
is exactly the truncated

\displaystyle p_{t}
-adic valuation of
\displaystyle x
modulo
\displaystyle n
. We first determine the annihilator of
\displaystyle x
. A residue
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
belongs
to
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)
if and only if

\displaystyle xz\equiv 0\pmod{n},

or equivalently,

\displaystyle n\mid xz,
which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{n}{d_{x}}\mid z.

Hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\left(\frac{n}{d_{x}}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.

Therefore
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)
is determined by
\displaystyle d_{x}
, and
\displaystyle d_{x}
is determined by the
valuation vector

\displaystyle\nu(x)=(\nu_{1}(x),\ldots,\nu_{s}(x)).

Consequently, if
\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)=\nu_{t}(y)
for every
\displaystyle t
, then
\displaystyle d_{x}=d_{y}
, and hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\operatorname{ann}(y)
.

Conversely, suppose that
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\operatorname{ann}(y)
. Since

\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(x)=\left(\frac{n}{d_{x}}\right)
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{ann}(y)=\left(\frac{n}{d_{y}}\right),
so
the two principal ideals generated by
\displaystyle n/d_{x}
and
\displaystyle n/d_{y}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
are
equal. In
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, ideals are determined by divisors of
\displaystyle n
, so we have

\displaystyle\frac{n}{d_{x}}=\frac{n}{d_{y}},

and hence
\displaystyle d_{x}=d_{y}
. Comparing the prime-power decompositions of
\displaystyle d_{x}
and

\displaystyle d_{y}
gives

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(x)=\nu_{t}(y)
for every
\displaystyle t.

This proves the equivalence between equality of annihilators and equality of
valuation vectors.

For counting the size of a valuation class, we use Chinese remainder theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\prod_{t=1}^{s}\mathbb{Z}/p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}\mathbb{Z}.

Thus the choices in different prime-power components are independent. In the
component
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}\mathbb{Z}
, the number of residues with exact
valuation
\displaystyle e_{t}<\alpha_{t}
is the number of multiples of
\displaystyle p_{t}^{e_{t}}
which are
not multiples of
\displaystyle p_{t}^{e_{t}+1}
, that is, such a number is

p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}-e_{t}}-p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}-e_{t}-1}=p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}-e_{t}-1}(p_{t}-1).

If
\displaystyle e_{t}=\alpha_{t}
, then there is only one residue with that valuation, namely
the zero residue modulo
\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
. Therefore, for

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s}),

the number of residues with valuation vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{e}
is

\displaystyle|C(\mathbf{e})|=\prod_{t=1}^{s}c_{t}(e_{t}),

where

c_{t}(e_{t})=\begin{cases}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}-e_{t}-1}(p_{t}-1),&0\leq e_{t}<\alpha_{t},\\
1,&e_{t}=\alpha_{t}.\end{cases}

Finally, the vector

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s})

means that every prime-power component is zero, and hence it represents the
zero residue modulo
\displaystyle n
. Since the zero-divisor graph uses only nonzero zero
divisors as vertices, this class is omitted from the vertex set.
∎

The above lemma explains why the same notation can be used for compressed classes and for ordinary vertex blocks. The homological calculation, however, must use the size and internal adjacency of the lifted block.

The following result gives a quick test for whether same-class vertices may be inserted into a cover.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle n=\prod_{t=1}^{s}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
, and let
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
be the class of residues whose representative has valuation vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s})
with respect to the primes
\displaystyle p_{t}
. Two distinct vertices in
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
are adjacent in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
only if
\displaystyle 2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}
for every
\displaystyle t
.

Proof.

Choose two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y\in C(\mathbf{e})
. By the definition of the
valuation class, both
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
have the same valuation vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s}),

that is,

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(x)=e_{t}
and
\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(y)=e_{t}

for every
\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,s
, where the valuations are understood in the usual
truncated sense modulo
\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
.
As

\displaystyle n=\prod_{t=1}^{s}p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}},

the congruence of adjacency in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is equivalent to the divisibility condition

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}\mid xy
for every
\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,s.

In terms of
\displaystyle p_{t}
-adic valuations, this means

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(xy)\geq\alpha_{t}
for every
\displaystyle t.
Now fix an index
\displaystyle t
. If
\displaystyle e_{t}<\alpha_{t}
, then the representatives of
\displaystyle x
and

\displaystyle y
may be written in the form

\displaystyle x=p_{t}^{e_{t}}u,
and
\displaystyle y=p_{t}^{e_{t}}v,

where neither
\displaystyle u
nor
\displaystyle v
is divisible by
\displaystyle p_{t}
. Hence,

\displaystyle xy=p_{t}^{2e_{t}}uv,

with
\displaystyle p_{t}\nmid uv
, and

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(xy)=2e_{t}.

Thus, in order that
\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
divide
\displaystyle xy
, it is necessary that

\displaystyle 2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}.
If
\displaystyle e_{t}=\alpha_{t}
, then the same inequality is automatic. Consequently, for
every prime factor
\displaystyle p_{t}
of
\displaystyle n
, adjacency of two distinct vertices in the
same valuation class forces

\displaystyle 2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}.

∎

Compared with the compressed-graph viewpoint of Mulay
[
32
]
and Spiroff–Wickham
[
44
]
, Lemma
3.3
is deliberately elementary. Its role is to prevent a compressed adjacency from being mistakenly interpreted as a clique inside a lifted equivalence class.

The following result identifies precisely when a repeated block is decreasing or constant in the families studied later.

Corollary 3.4
.

In
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z})
, the class
\displaystyle[p^{i}]
is a clique if and only if
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
. In
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}q\mathbb{Z})
, the class
\displaystyle[p^{i}q]
is a clique if and only if
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
. In
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
, the classes
\displaystyle[pq]
,
\displaystyle[pr]
, and
\displaystyle[qr]
are independent sets.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, for
\displaystyle p^{a}
the condition is
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
. For
\displaystyle p^{a}q
, the
\displaystyle q
-exponent in
\displaystyle[p^{i}q]
already satisfies
\displaystyle 2\geq 1
, so the only condition is again
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
. For
\displaystyle pqr
, the class
\displaystyle[pq]
has valuation vector
\displaystyle(1,1,0)
, whose square still has
\displaystyle r
-valuation zero, and the other two cases are identical by symmetry.
∎

The above corollary is the first point at which the present paper departs from the invalid type sequences in the
[
15
]
. It implies, for example, that two distinct vertices in the class
\displaystyle[q]
of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z})
are not adjacent, so a cover for one such vertex cannot contain another such vertex.

The following theorem gives a corrected validation rule for proposed constructible systems in zero-divisor graphs.

Theorem 3.5
.

Consider a proposed constructible step in
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
with center in valuation class
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
and cover equal to a union of previously available valuation classes and possibly previous vertices from
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
. The step is valid only if every included class
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{f})
satisfies
\displaystyle e_{t}+f_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}
for all
\displaystyle t
, and previous vertices from
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
may be included only if
\displaystyle 2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}
for all
\displaystyle t
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{m}^{\alpha_{m}},

and let the chosen center be a vertex
\displaystyle x\in C(\mathbf{e})
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
. By definition of the valuation class, this means
that
\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(x)=e_{t}
or every
\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,m.

In a constructible step with center
\displaystyle x
, one attaches the complete bipartite
piece between
\displaystyle x
and the prescribed cover. Thus, for each vertex
\displaystyle y
placed
in the cover, the construction asserts that
\displaystyle\{x,y\}
is an edge of

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
. Since the zero-divisor graph contains an edge precisely
when the product is zero modulo
\displaystyle n
, this forces

\displaystyle xy\equiv 0\pmod{n},
or equivalently,

\displaystyle n\mid xy.

Now suppose that
\displaystyle y\in C(\mathbf{f})
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
. Then

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(y)=f_{t}
for every
\displaystyle t.
The divisibility condition
\displaystyle n\mid xy
is equivalent to saying that each prime
power
\displaystyle p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
divides
\displaystyle xy
. In valuation form this is

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(xy)\geq\alpha_{t}
for every
\displaystyle t.

Since valuations add under multiplication, we have

v_{p_{t}}(xy)=v_{p_{t}}(x)+v_{p_{t}}(y)=e_{t}+f_{t}.

Therefore, every class
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{f})
included in the cover must satisfy

\displaystyle e_{t}+f_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}
for all
\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,m.

It remains only to discuss the case where the cover contains earlier vertices
from the same valuation class as the center. If
\displaystyle y
is another vertex of

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
, then

\displaystyle v_{p_{t}}(y)=e_{t}
for every
\displaystyle t.

Thus the edge condition between
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
becomes

v_{p_{t}}(xy)=e_{t}+e_{t}=2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}\qquad\text{for every }t.

Hence previous vertices from
\displaystyle C(\mathbf{e})
can be included in the cover only
when

\displaystyle 2e_{t}\geq\alpha_{t}
for all
\displaystyle t.
This is exactly the same-class adjacency criterion stated in Lemma

3.3
. Consequently, a proposed constructible step cannot be valid unless all
valuation classes placed in its cover are genuine neighbor classes of the
center, and unless any same-class vertices included in the cover are adjacent
to the center. These are precisely the two stated conditions.
∎

The above theorem is a diagnostic tool. It does not replace chordal-elimination theory. It checks whether the arithmetic data used in such an elimination is faithful to the graph before any Betti computation is attempted.

The following corollary records the corrected global subtraction in a form useful for examples.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let a valid constructible system have
\displaystyle k
center steps. Any formula obtained by summing only the shifted cover-size terms overcounts the
\displaystyle i
th total Betti number by
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from Theorem
2.9
. The term
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i+1}
depends only on the number of center steps and is independent of the cover sizes. It is therefore still present after all arithmetic simplifications.
∎

The correction is already visible for a single edge. The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z})
is
\displaystyle K_{2}
, so
\displaystyle I(G)
is principal and
\displaystyle\beta_{1}(S/I(G))=1
. The raw shifted block sum gives
\displaystyle 2
, and the missing term
\displaystyle\binom{2}{2}=1
restores the correct value.

4
Classification of cochordal zero-divisor graphs

The classification below agrees with the recent classification in
[
15
]
, but the proof is rewritten to avoid an invalid induced-matching choice in the two-prime obstruction and to make the dependence on induced matching number explicit.

The following theorem gives the complete arithmetic classification.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2
. Then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal if and only if
\displaystyle n
has one of the following forms:

(i)

\displaystyle n=p^{a}
;

(ii)

\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
;

(iii)

\displaystyle n=pqr
;

where
\displaystyle p,q,r
are distinct primes and
\displaystyle a\geq 1
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. By Theorem
2.1
, the edge ideal has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution, and by Theorem
2.6
the graph has no induced matching of size two.

If
\displaystyle n
has at least four distinct prime divisors, write
\displaystyle n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}p_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}p_{4}^{\alpha_{4}}m
, where
\displaystyle m
is divisible by no
\displaystyle p_{i}
necessarily after absorbing the remaining prime powers. Let
\displaystyle x=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}m
,
\displaystyle y=p_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}p_{4}^{\alpha_{4}}m
,
\displaystyle u=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}m
, and
\displaystyle v=p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}p_{4}^{\alpha_{4}}m
. Then
\displaystyle xy
and
\displaystyle uv
are divisible by
\displaystyle n
, while the four cross products miss one of the prime powers
\displaystyle p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
. Hence
\displaystyle\{x,y\}
and
\displaystyle\{u,v\}
form an induced matching, a contradiction.

If
\displaystyle n
has exactly three distinct prime divisors and some exponent exceeds one, say
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q^{b}r^{c}
with
\displaystyle a>1
, choose
\displaystyle x=p^{a}
,
\displaystyle y=q^{b}r^{c}
,
\displaystyle u=p^{a-1}q^{b}
, and
\displaystyle v=pr^{c}
. Then
\displaystyle xy
and
\displaystyle uv
are zero modulo
\displaystyle n
, while every cross product misses one required prime power. This again gives an induced matching of size two. Therefore a cochordal case with three prime divisors must be square-free, namely
\displaystyle pqr
.

If
\displaystyle n
has exactly two distinct prime divisors and both exponents exceed one, write
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q^{b}
with
\displaystyle a,b\geq 2
. The edge
\displaystyle\{p^{a},q^{b}\}
is present. If
\displaystyle(a,b)\neq(2,2)
, then
\displaystyle u=p^{a-1}q^{b-1}
and
\displaystyle v=pq
are distinct vertices and
\displaystyle uv
is zero modulo
\displaystyle n
. So all cross products with
\displaystyle p^{a}
and
\displaystyle q^{b}
miss one full prime power. If
\displaystyle(a,b)=(2,2)
, choose two distinct unit multiples
\displaystyle u=pq
and
\displaystyle v=cpq
, where
\displaystyle c
is coprime to
\displaystyle pq
and
\displaystyle c\not\equiv 1\pmod{pq}
, such a
\displaystyle c
exists because
\displaystyle\varphi(pq)\geq 2
. The same cross-product check applies. Thus both exponents cannot exceed one. Hence a cochordal two-prime case has the form
\displaystyle p^{a}q
.

It remains to prove sufficiency. For
\displaystyle p^{a}
,
\displaystyle p^{a}q
, and
\displaystyle pqr
, explicit constructible systems are given in Theorems
5.1
,
6.1
and
7.1
. By Theorem
2.8
, each corresponding zero-divisor graph is cochordal.
∎

The proof validates the classification but corrects the two-prime obstruction by using vertices that are always residues of the ring and always distinct after the stated small exception is handled. This avoids the unsupported use of an auxiliary prime divisor of
\displaystyle pq-1
.

The following lemma isolates the four-prime obstruction used in the classification.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\displaystyle n
has at least four distinct prime divisors, then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
contains an induced matching of size two.

Proof.

The first paragraph of the proof of Theorem
4.1
gives explicit vertices. The two displayed products
\displaystyle xy
and
\displaystyle uv
contain every prime power dividing
\displaystyle n
, whereas each cross product omits one of
\displaystyle p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}
,
\displaystyle p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
,
\displaystyle p_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
, or
\displaystyle p_{4}^{\alpha_{4}}
. Thus exactly the two matching edges occur among those four vertices.
∎

This lemma is the arithmetic reason that a cochordal zero-divisor graph cannot encode too many independent prime directions. It is stronger than merely saying that the complement has an induced cycle, as it exhibits the homological obstruction detected by regularity.

The following lemma treats the three-prime nonsquare-free obstruction.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q^{b}r^{c}
and at least one of
\displaystyle a,b,c
is larger than one, then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is not cochordal.

Proof.

By symmetry assume
\displaystyle a>1
, so the vertices
\displaystyle p^{a}
,
\displaystyle q^{b}r^{c}
,
\displaystyle p^{a-1}q^{b}
, and
\displaystyle pr^{c}
induce exactly two disjoint edges, as verified in the second paragraph of Theorem
4.1
. Hence the graph has an induced matching of size two and cannot be cochordal.
∎

The above lemma explains why
\displaystyle pqr
is the only surviving three-prime case. Any repeated exponent supplies enough room to split the prime powers into two disjoint zero products.

The following lemma treats the two-prime obstruction.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q^{b}
with distinct primes and
\displaystyle a,b\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is not cochordal.

Proof.

The third paragraph of Theorem
4.1
constructs an induced matching of size two. The exceptional case
\displaystyle a=b=2
is handled by taking two distinct unit multiples of
\displaystyle pq
in the class
\displaystyle[pq]
.
∎

The following proposition summarizes the sufficiency mechanism.

Proposition 4.5
.

Each graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z})
,
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}q\mathbb{Z})
, and
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
admits a valid cochordal constructible system.

Proof.

For prime powers, the system is built from classes
\displaystyle[p^{i}]
with
\displaystyle i\geq\lceil a/2\rceil
, and same-class vertices may be used because those classes are cliques. For
\displaystyle p^{a}q
, the system uses the classes
\displaystyle[p^{i}q]
in increasing construction order, and same-class previous vertices are included only when
\displaystyle i\geq\lceil a/2\rceil
. For
\displaystyle pqr
, the system uses the classes
\displaystyle[pq]
, then
\displaystyle[pr]
, then
\displaystyle[qr]
in construction order, and no same-class previous vertices are included. The detailed verification appears in Sections
5
,
6
and
7
.
∎

Proposition
4.5
highlights that the corrected construction is not a cosmetic modification. The validity of sufficiency depends exactly on the same-class rule of Theorem
3.4
.

5
The prime-power case

The constructible system for
\displaystyle p^{a}
is valid as in
[
15
]
, but the Betti formula must retain the global subtraction. The corrected formula below is the first closed expression in this paper and already changes numerical values for
\displaystyle p>2
and
\displaystyle a=2
.

Let
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
with
\displaystyle a\geq 2
. For
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq a-1
, let

\displaystyle V_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z}:[x]=[p^{i}]\},
with
\displaystyle\phi_{i}=|V_{i}|=p^{a-i-1}(p-1)
and let
\displaystyle m=\lceil a/2\rceil
.

The following theorem gives the valid type sequence for prime powers.

Theorem 5.1
.

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. A valid constructible system has one decreasing block for each
\displaystyle i=m,m+1,\ldots,a-1
, namely

T_{i}=\bigl(p^{i}-p^{a-i-1}-1,\ p^{i}-p^{a-i-1}-2,\ \ldots,\ p^{i}-p^{a-i}\bigr),

of length
\displaystyle\phi_{i}
. The type sequence is
\displaystyle T_{a-1},T_{a-2},\ldots,T_{m}
.

Proof.

Order the vertices of each
\displaystyle V_{i}
as
\displaystyle v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,\phi_{i}}
. For
\displaystyle i\geq m
and
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq\phi_{i}
, let

U_{i,j}=V_{a-i}\cup V_{a-i+1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{i-1}\cup\{v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,j-1}\}.

A vertex in
\displaystyle V_{i}
is adjacent to every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{t}
with
\displaystyle t\geq a-i
, and, because
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
, it is also adjacent to earlier vertices of
\displaystyle V_{i}
. Thus each listed cover contains only true neighbors of the new center. The same valuation inequality shows that
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
covers the graph constructed before that step. More explicitly, if an earlier edge has endpoints in
\displaystyle V_{s}
and
\displaystyle V_{t}
with
\displaystyle s\leq t
, then
\displaystyle s+t\geq a
forces
\displaystyle t\geq a-i
whenever both endpoints have already appeared before the
\displaystyle V_{i}
-step, so at least one endpoint lies in the displayed union or among the earlier same-class vertices. Hence, these data form a cochordal constructible system.
The cover size is

|U_{i,j}|=\sum_{t=a-i}^{i-1}p^{a-t-1}(p-1)+j-1=p^{i}-p^{a-i}+j-1.

When the vertices in
\displaystyle V_{i}
are listed in reverse order in the type sequence, the block is exactly
\displaystyle T_{i}
.
∎

The above theorem agrees with the valid part of the earlier construction. Its importance here is that it supplies decreasing blocks, so Theorem
3.1
applies in its first case.

The following theorem gives the corrected Betti numbers for the prime-power case.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle a\geq 2
,
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
,
\displaystyle G=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
,
\displaystyle I=I(G)
,
\displaystyle m=\lceil a/2\rceil
, and let

K_{p,a}=\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)=p^{a-m}-1.

Then
\displaystyle S/I
has a linear resolution and, for every
\displaystyle i\geq 1
,

\beta_{i,i+1}(S/I)=\beta_{i}(S/I)=\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{j}-2}{i}-\binom{K_{p,a}}{i+1}.

Proof.

For the block
\displaystyle T_{j}
in Theorem
5.1
, the number of earlier blocks to its left in the type sequence is

K_{>j}=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{a-1}p^{a-\ell-1}(p-1)=p^{a-j-1}-1.

The first entry of
\displaystyle T_{j}
is
\displaystyle p^{j}-p^{a-j-1}-1
, so the shifted value is
\displaystyle p^{j}-2
. Since
\displaystyle T_{j}
is decreasing, Theorem
3.1
gives the contribution
\displaystyle\phi_{j}\binom{p^{j}-2}{i}
. Now, summing over
\displaystyle j=m,\ldots,a-1
and subtracting the global term
\displaystyle\binom{K_{p,a}}{i+1}
proves the formula. The linearity of the resolution follows from cochordality and Theorem
2.1
.
∎

The novelty compared with
[
15
]
is the final term
\displaystyle-\binom{K_{p,a}}{i+1}
. Without it the formula overcounts, beginning with the graph
\displaystyle K_{p-1}
obtained from
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
.

The following corollary gives projective dimension and regularity.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
\displaystyle a\geq 2
and
\displaystyle G=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}(S/I(G))=p^{a-1}-2,
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(S/I(G))=1,

whenever
\displaystyle G
has at least one edge. If
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle a=2

\displaystyle G
is edgeless and
\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}(S/I(G))=0
.

Proof.

The projective dimension is the maximum shifted value in Theorem
2.9
. By the proof of Theorem
5.2
, these shifted values are
\displaystyle p^{j}-2
for
\displaystyle j=m,\ldots,a-1
, whose maximum is
\displaystyle p^{a-1}-2
. The regularity statement follows, since the resolution is linear for
\displaystyle S/I(G)
, so the nonzero Betti numbers lie in degrees
\displaystyle i+1
.
∎

The above corollary confirms the projective dimension stated in the earlier manuscript for prime powers, but the agreement of projective dimension does not imply agreement of the full Betti table.

. Let

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=|B_{i}|=p^{a-i-1}(p-1),
with
\displaystyle m=\lceil a/2\rceil.

The total number of center vertices in the construction below is

K_{p,a}^{(q)}=\sum_{i=0}^{a-1}\phi_{i}=p^{a}-1.

The following theorem gives the corrected type sequence for
\displaystyle p^{a}q
.

Theorem 6.1
.

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}q\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. A valid constructible system has, for each
\displaystyle i\geq m
, a decreasing block

T_{i}=\bigl(qp^{i}-p^{a-i-1}-1,\ qp^{i}-p^{a-i-1}-2,\ \ldots,\ qp^{i}-p^{a-i}\bigr)

of length
\displaystyle\phi_{i}
, and, for each
\displaystyle 0\leq i<m
, a constant block

T_{i}=\bigl((q-1)p^{i},\ (q-1)p^{i},\ \ldots,\ (q-1)p^{i}\bigr)

of length
\displaystyle\phi_{i}
. The type sequence is
\displaystyle T_{a-1},T_{a-2},\ldots,T_{0}
.

Proof.

We order
\displaystyle B_{i}
as
\displaystyle b_{i,1},\ldots,b_{i,\phi_{i}}
. In construction order, the blocks are added from
\displaystyle B_{0}
up to
\displaystyle B_{a-1}
. For a center
\displaystyle b_{i,j}
, the true neighbors among the
\displaystyle A
-classes are exactly
\displaystyle A_{a-i}\cup A_{a-i+1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{a}
, whose total size is
\displaystyle(q-1)p^{i}
.

Among previously constructed
\displaystyle B
-classes, the center
\displaystyle b_{i,j}
is adjacent exactly to the classes
\displaystyle B_{t}
with
\displaystyle a-i\leq t\leq i-1
. This range is empty when
\displaystyle i<m
. When
\displaystyle i\geq m
, it has total size
\displaystyle p^{i}-p^{a-i}
. Finally, previous vertices of the same class
\displaystyle B_{i}
may be included exactly when
\displaystyle 2i\geq a
, again equivalent to
\displaystyle i\geq m
.

Thus for
\displaystyle i<m
the cover size is the constant
\displaystyle(q-1)p^{i}
for all
\displaystyle j
. For
\displaystyle i\geq m
the cover size is

(q-1)p^{i}+(p^{i}-p^{a-i})+j-1=qp^{i}-p^{a-i}+j-1.

Listing the vertices inside each block in reverse order gives the displayed type sequence. The same valuation inequalities show that each cover is a vertex cover of the previously constructed graph. Indeed, every earlier edge either joins an earlier
\displaystyle B
-center to an eligible
\displaystyle A
-class already listed in the cover, or joins two earlier
\displaystyle B
-classes whose valuation sum is at least
\displaystyle a
. In the latter case the endpoint with larger index lies among the
\displaystyle B_{t}
satisfying
\displaystyle t\geq a-i
and is therefore included. Hence the system is valid.
∎

The above theorem corrects the low block in the earlier proposed type sequence in
[
15
]
. The expression that decreases through a low block would add same-class edges that do not exist when
\displaystyle 2i<a
.

The following theorem gives the corrected two-prime Betti formula.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}q\mathbb{Z})
with
\displaystyle I=I(G)
, and for every
\displaystyle h\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\beta_{h,h+1}(S/I)=\beta_{h}(S/I)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)\binom{qp^{j}-2}{h}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Bigg[\binom{(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j}-1}{h+1}

\displaystyle-\binom{(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j-1}-1}{h+1}\Bigg]-\binom{p^{a}-1}{h+1}.

Here the first sum is empty if
\displaystyle a=1
.

Proof.

For a high block
\displaystyle j\geq m
, the first entry is
\displaystyle qp^{j}-p^{a-j-1}-1
, and the number of blocks to its left is

K_{>j}=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{a-1}p^{a-\ell-1}(p-1)=p^{a-j-1}-1.

Hence the shifted value is
\displaystyle qp^{j}-2
, and Theorem
3.1
gives the first sum.

For a low block
\displaystyle 0\leq j<m
, the repeated value is
\displaystyle d_{j}=(q-1)p^{j}
. The number of entries to its left is again
\displaystyle K_{>j}=p^{a-j-1}-1
, and the length is
\displaystyle\phi_{j}=p^{a-j-1}(p-1)
. Thus its contribution is

\binom{d_{j}+K_{>j}+\phi_{j}}{h+1}-\binom{d_{j}+K_{>j}}{h+1}=\binom{(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j}-1}{h+1}-\binom{(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j-1}-1}{h+1}.

The total number of center steps is
\displaystyle p^{a}-1
, so Theorem
3.1
gives the final subtraction.
∎

The formula is new relative to
[
15
]
in two ways, as the low part is a hockey-stick difference, and the global correction remains. In the special case
\displaystyle a=1
it reduces to the known complete bipartite formula.

The following corollary records the projective dimension.

Corollary 6.3
.

With the notation of Theorem
6.2
,

\operatorname{pd}(S/I)=\max\left(\{qp^{j}-2:m\leq j\leq a-1\}\cup\{(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j}-2:0\leq j<m\}\right),

where the first set is omitted when
\displaystyle a=1
. Also
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(S/I)=1
whenever the graph has an edge.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.9
, the projective dimension is the maximum shifted type value. High blocks give shifted values
\displaystyle qp^{j}-2
. Low constant blocks have shifted values from
\displaystyle(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j-1}-1
up to
\displaystyle(q-1)p^{j}+p^{a-j}-2
, so their maximum is the second displayed family. The linearity is a consequence of cochordality.
∎

This statement corrects the claim that the projective dimension is always
\displaystyle qp^{a-1}-2
for
\displaystyle a\geq 2
. That value can fail when a low independent block is larger, for instance,
\displaystyle n=18

\displaystyle 8

\displaystyle 4

The following corollary recovers the complete bipartite case (case
\displaystyle a=1

Corollary 6.4
.

If
\displaystyle n=pq
, then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z})=K_{q-1,p-1}
and

\beta_{h}(S/I)=\binom{p+q-2}{h+1}-\binom{q-1}{h+1}-\binom{p-1}{h+1}.

Proof.

When
\displaystyle a=1
, the first sum in Theorem
6.2
is empty, and the second sum has only
\displaystyle j=0
. Now, substitution gives

\binom{p+q-2}{h+1}-\binom{q-1}{h+1}-\binom{p-1}{h+1}.

The graph description is immediate from the edge relation, as every nonzero multiple of
\displaystyle p
is adjacent to every nonzero multiple of
\displaystyle q
, and there are no edges inside either part.
∎

The above corollary is a useful consistency check because the Betti numbers of complete bipartite edge ideals are classical and follow from the product ideal
\displaystyle(X)(Y)

7
The square-free three-prime case

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal, and its projective dimension stated in
[
15
]
remains correct for
\displaystyle p<q<r
. However, the type blocks for
\displaystyle[pq]
,
\displaystyle[pr]
, and
\displaystyle[qr]
are constant blocks, not decreasing blocks, and the full Betti formula (Theorem 1.10
[
15
]
) must be corrected accordingly.

Let
\displaystyle p<q<r
be primes and let
\displaystyle n=pqr
. Denote the classes represented by
\displaystyle p,q,r
by
\displaystyle A_{p},A_{q},A_{r}
, and the classes represented by
\displaystyle pq,pr,qr
by
\displaystyle B_{pq},B_{pr},B_{qr}
. Their sizes are

\displaystyle|A_{p}|=(q-1)(r-1),|A_{q}|=(p-1)(r-1),|A_{r}|=(p-1)(q-1),|B_{pq}|=r-1,|B_{pr}|=q-1,
and
\displaystyle|B_{qr}|=p-1.

The following theorem gives the corrected type sequence for
\displaystyle pqr
.

Theorem 7.1
.

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. A valid constructible system has three constant blocks,

\displaystyle T_{pq}=\bigl((p-1)(q-1),\ldots,(p-1)(q-1)\bigr)

of length
\displaystyle r-1
,
\displaystyle T_{pr}=\bigl(pr-p,\ldots,pr-p\bigr)

of length
\displaystyle q-1
, and
\displaystyle T_{qr}=\bigl(qr-1,\ldots,qr-1\bigr)

of length
\displaystyle p-1
. The type sequence is
\displaystyle T_{qr},T_{pr},T_{pq}
.

Proof.

We construct the graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
by taking centers first from
\displaystyle B_{pq}
, then from
\displaystyle B_{pr}
, then from
\displaystyle B_{qr}
, or equivalently, the displayed type sequence is written in reverse construction order. A vertex of
\displaystyle B_{pq}
is adjacent to all vertices of
\displaystyle A_{r}
and to no previous same-class vertex, because
\displaystyle(pq)^{2}
is not divisible by
\displaystyle pqr
. Hence every cover in the first construction block has size
\displaystyle|A_{r}|=(p-1)(q-1)
.

A vertex of
\displaystyle B_{pr}
is adjacent to all vertices of
\displaystyle A_{q}
and all vertices of the previously constructed class
\displaystyle B_{pq}
, but not to previous vertices of
\displaystyle B_{pr}
. The cover size is

|A_{q}|+|B_{pq}|=(p-1)(r-1)+(r-1)=pr-p.

A vertex of
\displaystyle B_{qr}
is adjacent to all vertices of
\displaystyle A_{p}
,
\displaystyle B_{pq}
, and
\displaystyle B_{pr}
, but not to previous vertices of
\displaystyle B_{qr}
. The cover size is

|A_{p}|+|B_{pq}|+|B_{pr}|=(q-1)(r-1)+(r-1)+(q-1)=qr-1.

These covers contain only true neighbors. They also cover the graph built before each step. In the first block there are no earlier center classes. In the second block the only earlier edges involve
\displaystyle A_{r}
and
\displaystyle B_{pq}
, and
\displaystyle B_{pq}
is included. In the third block all earlier edges have an endpoint in
\displaystyle B_{pq}
,
\displaystyle B_{pr}
, or
\displaystyle A_{p}
, all of which are included. Thus the system is valid.
∎

The correction is visible in each block, as none of
\displaystyle B_{pq}
,
\displaystyle B_{pr}
, or
\displaystyle B_{qr}
is a clique. Therefore none of the three blocks should decrease by one along same-class vertices.

The following theorem gives the corrected Betti formula for the square-free three-prime case.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})
with
\displaystyle p<q<r
, and let
\displaystyle I=I(G)
. For every
\displaystyle h\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\beta_{h,h+1}(S/I)=\beta_{h}(S/I)={}

\displaystyle\binom{qr+p-2}{h+1}-\binom{qr-1}{h+1}+\binom{pr+q-2}{h+1}-\binom{pr-1}{h+1}

\displaystyle+\binom{pq+r-2}{h+1}-\binom{pq-1}{h+1}-\binom{p+q+r-3}{h+1}.

Proof.

We us the constant-block case of Theorem
3.1
to the three blocks in Theorem
7.1
. For
\displaystyle T_{qr}
there are no blocks to its left, its repeated value is
\displaystyle qr-1
, and its length is
\displaystyle p-1
, thereby giving

\binom{qr+p-2}{h+1}-\binom{qr-1}{h+1}.

For
\displaystyle T_{pr}
the left offset is
\displaystyle p-1
, its repeated value is
\displaystyle pr-p
, and its length is
\displaystyle q-1
, thereby giving

\binom{pr+q-2}{h+1}-\binom{pr-1}{h+1}.

For
\displaystyle T_{pq}
the left offset is
\displaystyle(p-1)+(q-1)
, its repeated value is
\displaystyle(p-1)(q-1)
, and its length is
\displaystyle r-1
, thereby giving

\binom{pq+r-2}{h+1}-\binom{pq-1}{h+1}.

The total number of center steps is
\displaystyle p+q+r-3
, so the global subtraction is
\displaystyle\binom{p+q+r-3}{h+1}
, and the result follows.
∎

The above formula replaces the shorter repeated-binomial expression. The two expressions have the same largest endpoint when
\displaystyle p<q<r
, but they do not have the same Betti numbers.

The following corollary gives projective dimension.

Corollary 7.3
.

If
\displaystyle p<q<r
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})))=qr+p-3,
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pqr\mathbb{Z})))=1.

Proof.

The shifted endpoints of the three constant blocks are
\displaystyle qr+p-3
,
\displaystyle pr+q-3
, and
\displaystyle pq+r-3
. Since
\displaystyle p<q<r
, we have

\displaystyle qr+p-3>pr+q-3

and
\displaystyle qr+p-3>pq+r-3.

Now, the projective dimension follows from Theorem
2.9
, and regularity follows from linearity.
∎

Thus the projective dimension alone is not sensitive enough to detect the error in the earlier Betti formula (Theorem 1.10
[

\displaystyle B_{h}(n)=\beta_{h}(S/I)
denote the corrected Betti number supplied by Theorems
5.2
,
6.2
and
7.2
. If
\displaystyle D=\operatorname{pd}(S/I)
and
\displaystyle N=|V(G)|
, then

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{h=1}^{D}(-1)^{h}B_{h}(n)t^{h+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

If
\displaystyle G
has no edge, the same formula is interpreted as
\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle I=I(G)
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution, all nonzero graded Betti numbers
of
\displaystyle S/I
occur in bidegrees
\displaystyle(h,h+1)
for
\displaystyle h\geq 1
. Thus the minimal graded
free resolution has the form

0\longrightarrow S(-D-1)^{B_{D}(n)}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow S(-4)^{B_{3}(n)}\longrightarrow S(-3)^{B_{2}(n)}\longrightarrow S(-2)^{B_{1}(n)}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow S/I\longrightarrow 0.

Here
\displaystyle D=\operatorname{pd}(S/I)
, and
\displaystyle B_{h}(n)=\beta_{h}(S/I)
denotes the total Betti number in
homological degree
\displaystyle h
. Hilbert series is additive on short exact sequences and, equivalently,
alternating on a finite free resolution. Since

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}

and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S(-j)}(t)=\frac{t^{j}}{(1-t)^{N}},

the above resolution gives

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}+\sum_{h=1}^{D}(-1)^{h}B_{h}(n)\frac{t^{h+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

Now, combining the terms over the common denominator
\displaystyle(1-t)^{N}
, we have

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{h=1}^{D}(-1)^{h}B_{h}(n)t^{h+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

If
\displaystyle G
has no edge, then
\displaystyle I(G)=0
. The minimal free resolution is simply

0\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow S/I\longrightarrow 0,

so
\displaystyle S/I=S
and therefore
\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I}(t)=\operatorname{Hilb}_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}.

∎

The above theorem is often the fastest route from the corrected Betti formulae to enumerative data. The numerator is sensitive to both corrections made in this paper, as changing a low block or omitting the global subtraction changes the coefficient of
\displaystyle t^{2}
and therefore the number of quadratic generators.

The following proposition gives the number of variables in the three arithmetic families.

Proposition 8.2
.

The denominator exponent
\displaystyle N=|V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))|
is as follows:

N(p^{a})=p^{a-1}-1\quad(a\geq 2),

N(p^{a}q)=p^{a-1}(p+q-1)-1,

and

N(pqr)=pq+pr+qr-p-q-r.

For
\displaystyle n=p
prime,
\displaystyle N=0
.

Proof.

The vertices are the nonzero zero-divisors. For
\displaystyle p^{a}
, these are the nonzero multiples of
\displaystyle p
, so there are
\displaystyle p^{a-1}-1
. For
\displaystyle p^{a}q
, subtract the units and zero from all residues:

p^{a}q-\varphi(p^{a}q)-1=p^{a}q-p^{a-1}(p-1)(q-1)-1=p^{a-1}(p+q-1)-1.

For
\displaystyle pqr
, similarly,

pqr-(p-1)(q-1)(r-1)-1=pq+pr+qr-p-q-r.

If
\displaystyle n=p
is prime, there are no nonzero zero-divisors.
∎

The proposition supplies the denominator in unreduced form. Reduction to a denominator of the form
\displaystyle(1-t)^{\dim S/I}
is possible, but the unreduced expression is the direct output of the minimal free resolution and is the most convenient for computation.

The following corollary writes the three family-specific Hilbert series in closed form.

Corollary 8.3
.

In the prime-power, two-prime, and square-free three-prime cases, the Hilbert series are obtained by substituting the corresponding corrected Betti functions into Theorem
8.1
. Explicitly,

\operatorname{Hilb}_{p^{a}}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{h=1}^{p^{a-1}-2}(-1)^{h}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{j}-2}{h}-\binom{p^{a-m}-1}{h+1}\right)t^{h+1}}{(1-t)^{p^{a-1}-1}}

for
\displaystyle a\geq 2
, with the evident zero-edge convention. The corresponding formulae for
\displaystyle p^{a}q
and
\displaystyle pqr
are obtained by replacing the parenthesized coefficient with the right sides of Theorems
6.2
and
7.2
and using the denominator exponents from Proposition
8.2
.

Proof.

The assertion is obtained by combining the uniform Hilbert-series formula
with the corrected Betti formulae already proved for the three arithmetic
families. For the prime-power case, let

\displaystyle m=\left\lceil\frac{a}{2}\right\rceil.
Then by Theorem
5.2
, the corrected Betti number is

B_{h}(p^{a})=\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{j}-2}{h}-\binom{p^{a-m}-1}{h+1}.

Also, by Proposition
8.2
,

\displaystyle N=p^{a-1}-1.

Substituting these two expressions into Theorem
8.1
gives

\operatorname{Hilb}_{p^{a}}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{h=1}^{p^{a-1}-2}(-1)^{h}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{a-1}p^{a-j-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{j}-2}{h}-\binom{p^{a-m}-1}{h+1}\right)t^{h+1}}{(1-t)^{p^{a-1}-1}}.

The same argument applies to the two-prime and three-prime cases. Namely,
one replaces
\displaystyle B_{h}(p^{a})
by the corrected coefficient supplied by Theorem

6.2
or by Theorem
7.2
, and we use
the denominator exponent from Proposition
8.2
. The upper summation
limit is the corresponding projective dimension, as recorded in Corollaries

6.3
and
7.3
. The zero-edge cases are covered by the convention in
Theorem
8.1
.
∎

The above corollary is intentionally written with the same corrected coefficients as the Betti theorems. This prevents a common error, using corrected projective dimension but an uncorrected numerator.

The following proposition extracts the Hilbert function from the same numerator.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be one of the cochordal zero-divisor graphs considered in this paper, let
\displaystyle N=|V(G)|
, and let
\displaystyle\beta_{i}=\beta_{i}(S/I(G))
. For every
\displaystyle d\geq 0
,

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(S/I(G))_{d}=\binom{N+d-1}{d}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\geq 1\\
d\geq i+1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{i}\beta_{i}\binom{N+d-i-2}{d-i-1}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(S/I(G))_{0}=1,\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(S/I(G))_{1}=N,

and
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(S/I(G))_{2}=\binom{N+1}{2}-|E(G)|.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.1
, the Hilbert series is

\frac{1+\sum_{i\geq 1}(-1)^{i}\beta_{i}t^{i+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

Since
\displaystyle(1-t)^{-N}=\sum_{d\geq 0}\binom{N+d-1}{d}t^{d}
, multiplying the numerator by this power series and reading the coefficient of
\displaystyle t^{d}
gives the required formula.
∎

The above proposition gives a direct way to check the Hilbert series without recomputing a resolution. The degree-two coefficient is especially useful because it counts all quadratic monomials except the edge monomials.

The next proposition records the dimension interpretation used when comparing Hilbert series and Cohen–Macaulayness.

Proposition 8.5
.

For any finite simple graph
\displaystyle G
,

\dim S/I(G)=\alpha(G),

where
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
is the independence number of
\displaystyle G
. Equivalently, the height of
\displaystyle I(G)
is the minimum size of a vertex cover of
\displaystyle G
.

Proof.

The minimal primes of
\displaystyle I(G)
are precisely the ideals generated by complements of maximal independent sets, or equivalently by minimal vertex covers. Therefore the largest dimension of a quotient by a minimal prime is the largest size of an independent set. This gives
\displaystyle\dim S/I(G)=\alpha(G)
, and the equivalent height statement follows from
\displaystyle\operatorname{ht}I(G)=|V(G)|-\alpha(G)
.
∎

The proposition is standard, but it clarifies why the denominator
\displaystyle(1-t)^{N}
in Theorem
8.1
is not the final Krull dimension. Cancellation in the rational function may reduce the denominator to
\displaystyle(1-t)^{\alpha(G)}
after simplification.

The following corollary gives a simplified denominator after cancellation.

Corollary 8.6
.

After cancelling the largest possible power of
\displaystyle 1-t
from the Hilbert numerator in Theorem
8.1
, the Hilbert series of
\displaystyle S/I(G)
has denominator
\displaystyle(1-t)^{\alpha(G)}
.

Proof.

For every standard graded algebra, the order of the pole of its Hilbert series at
\displaystyle t=1
is the Krull dimension. By Proposition
8.5
, this dimension is
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
for an edge ideal. Hence the reduced denominator has the stated exponent.
∎

The above form is sometimes preferable for asymptotic questions. The unreduced form is better for computation from Betti numbers, while the reduced form displays the dimension.

9
Cohen–Macaulay properties

The quotient rings considered in this paper have linear resolutions, but they are almost never Cohen–Macaulay. The result below separates linearity from Cohen–Macaulayness and gives a complete classification inside the cochordal residue-class families.

We use two standard facts from Stanley–Reisner theory. First,
\displaystyle S/I(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay only if the independence complex of
\displaystyle G
is pure, or equivalently,
\displaystyle G
must be well-covered. Second, by the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula,
\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(S/I(G))=N-\operatorname{pd}(S/I(G))
for
\displaystyle N=|V(G)|
, see
[
8
,
46
]
.

The following theorem gives the complete Cohen–Macaulay classification for the cochordal families.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2
and suppose that
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. Then
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\displaystyle n
is prime or
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
for some prime
\displaystyle p
.

Proof.

In order to prove that
\displaystyle S/I(G)
is not Cohen–Macaulay, it
is enough to exhibit two maximal independent sets of different sizes.
First suppose that
\displaystyle n=p
is prime. Then
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a field, so it has no
nonzero zero-divisors. Hence
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
has no vertices and

\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}))=0
. The quotient is just the ground field
\displaystyle\mathbb{K}
, and is
therefore Cohen–Macaulay. Now suppose that
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
. The nonzero zero-divisors modulo
\displaystyle p^{2}
are precisely
the nonzero multiples of
\displaystyle p
, and therefore

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z})=K_{p-1}.
For
\displaystyle N=p-1
, the minimal vertex covers of
\displaystyle K_{N}
are exactly the sets obtained by deleting
one vertex from
\displaystyle V(K_{N})
. Hence every minimal vertex cover has size
\displaystyle N-1=p-2
,
so
\displaystyle\operatorname{ht}I(K_{N})=p-2.

By Corollary
5.3
, we also have

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}(S/I(K_{N}))=p-2.

The Auslander–Buchsbaum formula gives

\operatorname{depth}(S/I(K_{N}))=N-\operatorname{pd}(S/I(K_{N}))=(p-1)-(p-2)=1.

On the other hand,

\dim(S/I(K_{N}))=N-\operatorname{ht}I(K_{N})=(p-1)-(p-2)=1.

Thus
\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(S/I(K_{N}))=\dim(S/I(K_{N})),

and
\displaystyle S/I(K_{N})
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Now assume
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
with
\displaystyle a\geq 3
. Any vertex in
\displaystyle V_{a-1}
is adjacent to every other vertex of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{a}\mathbb{Z})
, so a singleton from
\displaystyle V_{a-1}
is a maximal independent set. On the other hand,
\displaystyle V_{1}
contains at least two vertices and is independent, since
\displaystyle 2<a
. Extending
\displaystyle V_{1}
to a maximal independent set gives a maximal independent set of size at least two. Thus the graph is not well-covered, and
\displaystyle S/I
is not Cohen–Macaulay.

Next let
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
with distinct primes. If
\displaystyle a=1
, then
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z})=K_{q-1,p-1}
, whose two bipartition classes are maximal independent sets of different sizes because
\displaystyle p\neq q
. Hence the graph is not well-covered. If
\displaystyle a\geq 2
, the union
\displaystyle A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{a}
is a maximal independent set of size
\displaystyle(q-1)p^{a-1}
. Also,
\displaystyle B_{0}\cup\{b\}
, where
\displaystyle b
is one fixed vertex of
\displaystyle B_{a-1}
, is a maximal independent set of size
\displaystyle p^{a-1}(p-1)+1
. These two sizes are different, since equality would imply
\displaystyle(q-p)p^{a-1}=1
, impossible for
\displaystyle a\geq 2
and distinct primes. Therefore
\displaystyle S/I
is not Cohen–Macaulay.

Finally let
\displaystyle n=pqr
with
\displaystyle p<q<r
. The set
\displaystyle A_{p}\cup A_{q}\cup A_{r}
is a maximal independent set. The set
\displaystyle B_{qr}\cup A_{q}\cup A_{r}
is also maximal independent, as every excluded vertex in
\displaystyle A_{p}
,
\displaystyle B_{pq}
, or
\displaystyle B_{pr}
is adjacent to an included vertex. Their sizes differ by

|A_{p}|-|B_{qr}|=(q-1)(r-1)-(p-1)>0.

Thus the independence complex is not pure, so
\displaystyle S/I
is not Cohen–Macaulay. The cochordal zero-divisor graphs are exactly the graphs arising from
\displaystyle n=p
,
\displaystyle n=p^{a}
,
\displaystyle n=p^{a}q
, and
\displaystyle n=pqr
in the classified cases earlier. Among
them, the only Cohen–Macaulay quotients occur for
\displaystyle n=p
and
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
.
∎

The above theorem shows that the linearity obtained from cochordality is much weaker than Cohen–Macaulayness. Except for the empty graph and complete graphs arising from
\displaystyle p^{2}
, the independence complexes have facets of different dimensions.

The following corollary records the depth in the Cohen–Macaulay cases and the general depth formula used for comparison.

Corollary 9.2
.

For every cochordal case with
\displaystyle N
vertices,

\operatorname{depth}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})))=N-\operatorname{pd}(S/I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))).

In particular, for
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
this depth equals
\displaystyle 1
, while for the non-Cohen–Macaulay cases the Cohen–Macaulay defect is

\displaystyle\dim(S/I)-\operatorname{depth}(S/I)>0.

Proof.

The first equality is the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula applied to the standard graded polynomial ring
\displaystyle S
. In the case
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
,
\displaystyle N=p-1
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}=p-2
, so the depth is
\displaystyle 1
, equal to the dimension. In all remaining cases, Theorem
9.1
says that the quotient is not Cohen–Macaulay, and therefore the dimension is strictly larger than the depth.
∎

Corollary
9.2
is useful computationally because the projective dimension has already been computed. It also quantifies the failure of Cohen–Macaulayness without requiring the full independence complex.

Example 9.3
.

For
\displaystyle n=9
, the graph is a single edge
\displaystyle K_{2}
, so
\displaystyle S/I
is the hypersurface
\displaystyle\mathbb{K}[x_{3},x_{6}]/(x_{3}x_{6})
and is Cohen–Macaulay. For
\displaystyle n=8
, the graph is a two-edge star, the singleton consisting of the center is maximal independent, while the two leaves form another maximal independent set, so the quotient is not Cohen–Macaulay. For
\displaystyle n=15
, the graph is
\displaystyle K_{4,2}
, whose two parts have different sizes, so the quotient is not Cohen–Macaulay.

The next result records the corresponding unmixedness statement. It is weaker than Cohen–Macaulayness in general, but in the present families the same exceptions occur.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2
and suppose that
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is cochordal. Then
\displaystyle I(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))
is unmixed if and only if
\displaystyle n
is prime or
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
for some prime
\displaystyle p
.

Proof.

For an edge ideal, unmixedness is equivalent to all minimal vertex covers having the same cardinality, or equivalently to all maximal independent sets having the same cardinality. The cases
\displaystyle n=p
and
\displaystyle n=p^{2}
are respectively the empty graph and a complete graph, so they are unmixed. In every other cochordal case, Theorem
9.1
gives two maximal independent sets of different sizes. Hence the corresponding minimal vertex covers have different sizes, and the edge ideal is not unmixed.
∎

Proposition
9.4
strengthens the negative part of Theorem
9.1
. The failure is not a subtle depth phenomenon, as it is already visible at the level of minimal primes.

Example 9.5
.

For
\displaystyle n=8
, the graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is the star with center
\displaystyle 4
and leaves
\displaystyle 2,6

\displaystyle\{4\}
and
\displaystyle\{2,6\}
, so the minimal primes are
\displaystyle(x_{2},x_{6})
and

, of heights
\displaystyle 2
and
\displaystyle 1
.

For
\displaystyle n=15
, the graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is
\displaystyle K_{4,2}
. The two bipartition classes give minimal primes of heights
\displaystyle 2
and
\displaystyle 4
. Thus the quotient has a linear resolution but is not equidimensional.

The above examples display the obstruction in the simplest possible algebraic language. Different minimal-prime heights rule out Cohen–Macaulayness before any depth computation is needed.

A chain-ring structure for the graphs

The purpose of this section is to separate the homological computation from the particular presentation of a finite chain ring. The same layered graph occurs for
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/p^{L}\mathbb{Z}
, for
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(x^{L})

It is useful to isolate the graph-theoretic structure shared by the two families studied later. Throughout this section
\displaystyle q\geq 2
is an integer and
\displaystyle L\geq 2
. In applications,
\displaystyle q
will be the cardinality of a residue field and
\displaystyle L
will be the nilpotency index of the maximal ideal.

.

\displaystyle V(C(q,L))=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{L-1},

where
\displaystyle|V_{k}|=(q-1)q^{L-k-1}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq L-1.

Two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
are adjacent in
\displaystyle C(q,L)
if and only if
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq L
. Figure

shows
\displaystyle C(q,L)
with
\displaystyle q=3
and
\displaystyle L=4
, where the blue layer has
\displaystyle 18
vertices, the green layer has
\displaystyle 6

vertices. The edge rule is
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq 4
.

This definition is modeled on a finite chain ring with residue field of size
\displaystyle q
and nilpotency index
\displaystyle L
. In that setting
\displaystyle V_{k}
consists of the nonzero elements of valuation
\displaystyle k
, and the product of elements of valuations
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k+\ell\geq L

|V(C(q,L))|=\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}(q-1)q^{L-k-1}=q^{L-1}-1.

The next lemma gives the explicit cochordal constructible system for
\displaystyle C(q,L)
. The statement is written in the same language as as in Definition
2.7
, but the proof spells out the cover condition because this is the step at which false same-layer edges can otherwise enter the calculation.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=C(q,L)
with
\displaystyle h=\lceil L/2\rceil
, and for
\displaystyle h\leq i\leq L-1
, let

\displaystyle n_{i}=|V_{i}|=(q-1)q^{L-i-1}

and

\displaystyle M_{i}=\sum_{s=L-i}^{i-1}|V_{s}|=q^{i}-q^{L-i},

where the sum is interpreted as
\displaystyle 0
when
\displaystyle L-i>i-1
. With vertex ordering
\displaystyle V_{i}=\{v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,n_{i}}\},
and for
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq n_{i}
, let

U_{i,j}=\left(\bigcup_{s=L-i}^{i-1}V_{s}\right)\cup\{v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,j-1}\}.

Then the ordered construction with centers

(i,j)=(h,1),(h,2),\ldots,(h,n_{h}),(h+1,1),\ldots,(L-1,n_{L-1})

and covers
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
is a cochordal constructible system for
\displaystyle G
, and
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is cochordal.

Proof.

For fixed
\displaystyle i\geq h
and let the current center be
\displaystyle v_{i,j}
. A vertex in
\displaystyle V_{i}
is adjacent exactly to the vertices in layers
\displaystyle V_{s}
satisfying
\displaystyle s\geq L-i
. If
\displaystyle 2i\geq L
, it is also adjacent to the other vertices in
\displaystyle V_{i}
. Since
\displaystyle i\geq h
, the inequality
\displaystyle 2i\geq L
holds, so the same-layer vertices that have already appeared are true neighbors of
\displaystyle v_{i,j}
. Hence every vertex listed in
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
is adjacent to
\displaystyle v_{i,j}
, and the star with center
\displaystyle v_{i,j}
and leaf set
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
introduces only genuine edges of
\displaystyle G
.

It remains to verify the vertex-cover condition. Suppose the construction has reached the pair
\displaystyle(i,j)
, and let
\displaystyle e=\{x,y\}
be an edge already constructed. If one endpoint of
\displaystyle e
lies in the current layer
\displaystyle V_{i}
, then it must be one of
\displaystyle v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,j-1}
, and hence it belongs to
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
. Otherwise both endpoints lie in layers that occurred before
\displaystyle V_{i}
, or in lower layers connected to an earlier center. If
\displaystyle x\in V_{a}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{b}
with
\displaystyle a\leq b
. Since
\displaystyle e
is an edge,
\displaystyle a+b\geq L
. Because the larger layer already used as a center is at most
\displaystyle i
, we have
\displaystyle b\leq i
. The inequality
\displaystyle a+b\geq L
then gives
\displaystyle a\geq L-b\geq L-i
. Thus at least one endpoint, indeed the smaller endpoint
\displaystyle x
unless
\displaystyle b=i
is the current same layer, lies in
\displaystyle\bigcup_{s=L-i}^{i-1}V_{s}
or among the earlier vertices of
\displaystyle V_{i}
. Therefore every previously constructed edge meets
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
.

Finally, every edge of
\displaystyle G
appears exactly once, as if an edge joins
\displaystyle V_{a}
and
\displaystyle V_{b}
with
\displaystyle a<b
, it is introduced when the center in
\displaystyle V_{b}
is processed. If it joins two vertices in the same layer
\displaystyle V_{i}
, it is introduced when the later of the two vertices in the fixed order is processed. Hence the construction produces precisely
\displaystyle G
. The cochordality follows from the constructible characterization in Theorem
2.8
.
∎

The next result records the type sequence determined by the construction.

Corollary 10.3
.

With notation as in Theorem
10.2
, the corresponding type list is the concatenation

\displaystyle(s_{L-1},s_{L-2},\ldots,s_{h}),
where

s_{i}=\bigl(q^{i}-q^{L-i-1}-1,q^{i}-q^{L-i-1}-2,\ldots,q^{i}-q^{L-i}\bigr).

The length of
\displaystyle s_{i}
is
\displaystyle n_{i}=(q-1)q^{L-i-1}
.

Proof.

For fixed
\displaystyle i
, the size of
\displaystyle U_{i,j}
is

|U_{i,j}|=M_{i}+j-1=q^{i}-q^{L-i}+j-1.

The type sequence is written in reverse order from the construction order. Hence the block contributed by
\displaystyle V_{i}
is the decreasing sequence

M_{i}+n_{i}-1,\ M_{i}+n_{i}-2,\ \ldots,\ M_{i}.

Since
\displaystyle n_{i}=(q-1)q^{L-i-1}
, its first term is

q^{i}-q^{L-i}+(q-1)q^{L-i-1}-1=q^{i}-q^{L-i-1}-1,

and its last term is
\displaystyle q^{i}-q^{L-i}
.
∎

For the graph
\displaystyle C(q,L),
there is, however, a second structural point which is worth recording. The same graph is a threshold graph. Thus the valuation layers used in the construction are not only algebraic layers, as they are also threshold-weight classes.

We recall the standard definition. A finite simple graph
\displaystyle H
is called a threshold graph if there are real numbers
\displaystyle w(v)
for
\displaystyle v\in V(H)
and a real number
\displaystyle T
such that, for any two distinct vertices
\displaystyle u,v
, we have

\{u,v\}\in E(H)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w(u)+w(v)\geq T.

Equivalently, threshold graphs are precisely the graphs that can be built from one vertex by repeatedly adding either an isolated vertex or a dominating vertex. They are also characterized as the graphs with no induced
\displaystyle P_{4}
,
\displaystyle C_{4}
, or
\displaystyle 2K_{2}
, see
[
10
,
29
]
.

The following result gives the promised threshold representation of
\displaystyle C(q,L)
.

Theorem 10.4
.

For
\displaystyle q\geq 2
and
\displaystyle L\geq 2
, the graph
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is a threshold graph. More precisely, if
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
, with
\displaystyle w(x)=k
, and take the threshold
\displaystyle T=L
. Then, for distinct vertices
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
,

\{x,y\}\in E(C(q,L))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w(x)+w(y)\geq T.

Proof.

By definition, the vertex set of
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is the disjoint union

\displaystyle V(C(q,L))=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{L-1},

where
\displaystyle|V_{k}|=(q-1)q^{L-k-1}
. The adjacency rule is also part of the definition of threshold, as if
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
are distinct, then

\{x,y\}\in E(C(q,L))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k+\ell\geq L.

Now assign to every vertex in the
\displaystyle k
-th layer the weight
\displaystyle k
. Thus
\displaystyle w(x)=k
for
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle w(y)=\ell
for
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
. Thus,

\displaystyle w(x)+w(y)=k+\ell,
and therefore

\{x,y\}\in E(C(q,L))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k+\ell\geq L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w(x)+w(y)\geq T,

where
\displaystyle T=L
. This is exactly the weighted definition of a threshold graph. Hence
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is threshold.
∎

The proof shows that no artificial weights are being introduced. The threshold weight of a vertex is precisely the index of the valuation layer in which the vertex lies. In the chain-ring examples, this is the same integer that measures the order of divisibility by the chosen generator of the maximal ideal.

It is also useful to record the corresponding statement for the complement.

Lemma 10.5
.

The complement
\displaystyle\overline{C(q,L)}
is a threshold graph. More explicitly, if
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
, then the weights

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)=-k

and the threshold
\displaystyle T^{\prime}=1-L
give a threshold representation of
\displaystyle\overline{C(q,L)}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
be distinct vertices. In the complement,
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent exactly when they are not adjacent in
\displaystyle C(q,L)
. By the defining rule of
\displaystyle C(q,L)
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle k+\ell<L.

Since
\displaystyle k+\ell
and
\displaystyle L
are integers, the preceding strict inequality is the same as

\displaystyle k+\ell\leq L-1
or

\displaystyle-k-\ell\geq 1-L.

With
\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)=-k
,
\displaystyle w^{\prime}(y)=-\ell
, and
\displaystyle T^{\prime}=1-L
, the last inequality becomes

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)+w^{\prime}(y)\geq T^{\prime}.

Thus two distinct vertices are adjacent in
\displaystyle\overline{C(q,L)}
exactly when the sum of their new weights is at least
\displaystyle T^{\prime}
. This proves that the complement is threshold.
∎

The next corollary explains why the threshold viewpoint is compatible with the cochordal theory used earlier.

Corollary 10.6
.

For all
\displaystyle q\geq 2
and
\displaystyle L\geq 2
, the graph
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is chordal and cochordal. Consequently, whenever
\displaystyle I(C(q,L))\neq 0
, the edge ideal
\displaystyle I(C(q,L))
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution.

Proof.

A threshold graph has no induced
\displaystyle P_{4}
,
\displaystyle C_{4}
, or
\displaystyle 2K_{2}
. In particular, it has no induced
\displaystyle C_{4}
. It also has no induced cycle of length at least
\displaystyle 5
, because any induced cycle with at least five vertices contains four consecutive vertices inducing a
\displaystyle P_{4}
. Hence a threshold graph has no induced cycle of length at least
\displaystyle 4
, and therefore it is chordal.

By Theorem
10.4
, the graph
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is threshold, so it is chordal. By Lemma
10.5
, the complement
\displaystyle\overline{C(q,L)}
is also threshold, so it is chordal as well. Thus
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is cochordal.

Finally, from Theorem
2.1
, the edge ideal of a graph has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution if and only if the complement of the graph is chordal. Since
\displaystyle\overline{C(q,L)}
is chordal, the edge ideal
\displaystyle I(C(q,L))
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution when it is nonzero.
∎

We next spell out the nested-neighborhood structure. This is often the most convenient internal test for recognizing the threshold property in a layered graph.

Proposition 10.7
.

Let
\displaystyle h=\lceil L/2\rceil
and let

\displaystyle A=V_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{h-1},
and
\displaystyle B=V_{h}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{L-1}.

Then
\displaystyle A
is an independent set and
\displaystyle B
is a clique. Moreover, the neighborhoods are nested by layer, if
\displaystyle 1\leq i<i^{\prime}<h
, then

\displaystyle N(V_{i})\cap B\subseteq N(V_{i^{\prime}})\cap B,

and if
\displaystyle h\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq L-1
, then the adjacency range of
\displaystyle V_{j^{\prime}}
contains the adjacency range of
\displaystyle V_{j}
.

Proof.

First suppose
\displaystyle x\in V_{i}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{j}
with
\displaystyle i,j<h
. If
\displaystyle L=2a
, then
\displaystyle h=a
, so
\displaystyle i+j\leq 2a-2<L
. If
\displaystyle L=2a+1
, then
\displaystyle h=a+1
, so
\displaystyle i+j\leq 2a<L
. In both cases
\displaystyle i+j<L
, and hence
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are not adjacent. Therefore
\displaystyle A
is independent.
Now suppose
\displaystyle x\in V_{i}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{j}
are distinct vertices with
\displaystyle i,j\geq h
. Then
\displaystyle i+j\geq 2h\geq L
, so
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are adjacent. Therefore
\displaystyle B
is a clique.
For the first nesting assertion, take
\displaystyle 1\leq i<i^{\prime}<h
and let
\displaystyle z\in B
be adjacent to a vertex of
\displaystyle V_{i}
. If
\displaystyle z\in V_{s}
, then
\displaystyle i+s\geq L
. Since
\displaystyle i^{\prime}>i
, we have
\displaystyle i^{\prime}+s\geq i+s+1>L
, and in particular
\displaystyle i^{\prime}+s\geq L
. Thus
\displaystyle z
is adjacent to every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{i^{\prime}}
, and hence
\displaystyle N(V_{i})\cap B\subseteq N(V_{i^{\prime}})\cap B
.

For the second assertion, assume
\displaystyle h\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq L-1
. If a vertex
\displaystyle z\in V_{s}
is adjacent to a vertex in
\displaystyle V_{j}
, then
\displaystyle s+j\geq L
. Since
\displaystyle j^{\prime}>j
, it follows that
\displaystyle s+j^{\prime}\geq L
. Therefore every layer that is adjacent to
\displaystyle V_{j}
is also adjacent to
\displaystyle V_{j^{\prime}}
, apart from the ordinary convention that a vertex is not adjacent to itself. Thus the neighborhood ranges increase with the layer index.
∎

Remark 10.9
.

When
\displaystyle L
is even and
\displaystyle i=L/2
, the last entry of
\displaystyle s_{i}
is
\displaystyle 0
. This entry corresponds to an empty star and does not change the graph. If one insists that type sequences contain only positive entries, this terminal zero may be deleted. The Betti numbers are unchanged because adding a final zero adds
\displaystyle\binom{k-1}{r}
to the shifted type sum and changes the global correction from
\displaystyle\binom{k-1}{r+1}
to
\displaystyle\binom{k}{r+1}
, since Pascal’s identity shows that the two changes cancel.

The next theorem is the closed Betti formula for the universal chain-layer graph. It is the formula that will be specialized in the two ring families below.

Theorem 10.10
.

Let
\displaystyle G=C(q,L)
be a graph with order
\displaystyle N=q^{L-1}-1
, and let
\displaystyle I=I(G)\subseteq S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
. Then, for every
\displaystyle r\geq 1
,

\beta_{r}(S/I)=\beta_{r,r+1}(S/I)=\sum_{j=\lceil L/2\rceil}^{L-1}(q-1)q^{L-j-1}\binom{q^{j}-2}{r}-\binom{q^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}-1}{r+1}.

All other graded Betti numbers of
\displaystyle S/I
, except
\displaystyle\beta_{0,0}(S/I)=1
, are zero. In particular,

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I)=q^{L-1}-2.

If
\displaystyle I\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle I
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution and
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(S/I)=1
, equivalently
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(I)=2
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle h=\lceil L/2\rceil
, and let
\displaystyle K
be the total number of entries in the type list of Corollary
10.3
. Then

K=\sum_{j=h}^{L-1}(q-1)q^{L-j-1}=q^{L-h}-1=q^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}-1.

For a fixed block indexed by
\displaystyle j
, the number of type entries lying to its left is

K_{j}=\sum_{s=j+1}^{L-1}(q-1)q^{L-s-1}=q^{L-j-1}-1.

The entries in the block are

M_{j}+n_{j}-1,\ M_{j}+n_{j}-2,\ldots,\ M_{j},

where
\displaystyle M_{j}=q^{j}-q^{L-j}
and
\displaystyle n_{j}=(q-1)q^{L-j-1}
.

In the formula of Theorem
2.9
, the offset increases by one as one moves from left to right through the type list. Therefore the
\displaystyle u
th entry in the
\displaystyle j
th block, counted from the left inside that block, is

\displaystyle M_{j}+n_{j}-u
and its offset is
\displaystyle K_{j}+u-1
. The upper argument of the corresponding binomial coefficient is

(M_{j}+n_{j}-u)+(K_{j}+u-1)=M_{j}+n_{j}+K_{j}-1=q^{j}-q^{L-j}+(q-1)q^{L-j-1}-1+q^{L-j-1}-1=q^{j}-2.

Thus every entry in the same block contributes the same binomial coefficient, and the whole block contributes

n_{j}\binom{q^{j}-2}{r}=(q-1)q^{L-j-1}\binom{q^{j}-2}{r}.

Summing over
\displaystyle j=h,\ldots,L-1
and subtracting the global correction
\displaystyle\binom{K}{r+1}
gives the required formula.

The largest possible homological degree comes from the block
\displaystyle j=L-1
, where the upper binomial argument is
\displaystyle q^{L-1}-2=N-1
. The correction term has upper argument
\displaystyle q^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}-1
, which is strictly smaller when
\displaystyle L\geq 3
, and the case
\displaystyle L=2
is immediate. Hence the last nonzero total Betti number occurs in homological degree
\displaystyle q^{L-1}-2
. The graded positions and the linearity statement follow from cochordality and Theorem
2.9
.
∎

The next proposition computes the independence number and the height of the edge ideal. It will later give the Krull dimension of the quotient rings.

Proposition 10.11
.

Let
\displaystyle G=C(q,L)
. Then

\alpha(G)=\begin{cases}q^{2a-1}-q^{a}+1,&L=2a,\\[2.0pt]
q^{2a}-q^{a},&L=2a+1.\end{cases}

Consequently,

\operatorname{height}I(G)=|V(G)|-\alpha(G)=\begin{cases}q^{a}-2,&L=2a,\\[2.0pt]
q^{a}-1,&L=2a+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle F
be an independent set and let
\displaystyle s
be the largest layer index represented in
\displaystyle F
. If
\displaystyle F
contains a vertex from
\displaystyle V_{s}
, then every other layer represented in
\displaystyle F
must be among
\displaystyle V_{1},\ldots,V_{L-s-1}
, because a vertex in
\displaystyle V_{k}
with
\displaystyle k+s\geq L
would be adjacent to the chosen vertex in
\displaystyle V_{s}
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle 2s\geq L
, then
\displaystyle F
contains at most one vertex from
\displaystyle V_{s}
.

Assume first that
\displaystyle L=2a
. The set

\displaystyle V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup\cdots\cup V_{a-1}
is independent, and one may add one vertex from
\displaystyle V_{a}
. Hence

\alpha(G)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{a-1}(q-1)q^{2a-k-1}+1=q^{2a-1}-q^{a}+1.

If an independent set uses no layer
\displaystyle V_{s}
with
\displaystyle s\geq a
, it is contained in
\displaystyle V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{a-1}
and is smaller by one. If it uses some
\displaystyle V_{s}
with
\displaystyle s\geq a
, then it contains at most one vertex from
\displaystyle V_{s}
and may use only the lower layers
\displaystyle V_{1},\ldots,V_{2a-s-1}
; this number is maximized at
\displaystyle s=a
. Therefore the displayed lower bound is sharp.

Now assume that
\displaystyle L=2a+1
. The union
\displaystyle V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{a}
is independent because
\displaystyle a+a<2a+1
, and its size is

\sum_{k=1}^{a}(q-1)q^{2a+1-k-1}=q^{2a}-q^{a}.

If an independent set uses a layer
\displaystyle V_{s}
with
\displaystyle s\geq a+1
, then it loses all layers
\displaystyle V_{2a+1-s},\ldots,V_{a}
and gains at most one vertex from
\displaystyle V_{s}
. This cannot increase the size, since the omitted layer
\displaystyle V_{a}
alone has
\displaystyle q-1\geq 1
vertices and the lower layers have nonnegative size. Thus the asserted value of
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
follows. The height formula is the standard equality between the height of an edge ideal and the vertex-cover number.
∎

\displaystyle n_{k}=(q-1)q^{L-k-1}

Consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I(G)}(t)=F_{G}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right),

\displaystyle V_{s}

\displaystyle 2s<L

\displaystyle V_{s}

\displaystyle V_{s}

\displaystyle V_{k}

\displaystyle k<s

\displaystyle k+s<L

The ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]
and its zero-divisor graph

This section records that the Gaussian residue ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]
has the same zero-divisor graph as the integer residue ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/2^{2m}\mathbb{Z}
. The observation is elementary, but it is useful because it lets the corrected type calculus be applied without recomputing a new family of Betti splittings.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[i]
be the ring of Gaussian integers. It is a Euclidean domain and hence a principal ideal domain. The rational prime
\displaystyle 2
factors as
\displaystyle 2=-i(1+i)^{2}.

If
\displaystyle\pi=1+i
, then
\displaystyle(2^{m})=(\pi^{2m})
up to a unit, and therefore

R_{m}=\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]/(2^{m})\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi^{2m}).

Thus
\displaystyle R_{m}
is a finite local principal ideal ring, indeed a finite chain ring, with maximal ideal

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}=(\overline{\pi}),
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{2m}=0\neq\mathfrak{m}^{2m-1}.
Thus, every nonzero element of
\displaystyle R_{m}
has the form
\displaystyle u\pi^{k}
, where
\displaystyle u\in R_{m}^{\times}
and
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq 2m-1
. Now, we define

v(x)=\begin{cases}k,&x=u\pi^{k}\neq 0\text{ with }u\in R_{m}^{\times},\\[2.0pt]
+\infty,&x=0.\end{cases}

Then
\displaystyle x
is a unit if and only if
\displaystyle v(x)=0
, and
\displaystyle x\in Z^{*}(R_{m})
if and only if
\displaystyle 1\leq v(x)\leq 2m-1
.

Thus, an element
\displaystyle x\in R_{m}
is a unit if and only if
\displaystyle v(x)=0
, and a nonzero
zero divisor if and only if
\displaystyle 1\leq v(x)\leq 2m-1
. For
\displaystyle k=0,1,\dots,2m
, we define the ideals
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{k}=(\pi^{k})\subseteq R_{m}.
The standard facts about finite chain rings (or a direct counting argument)
gives
\displaystyle|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|=2^{2m-k}
for
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq 2m.
We have the following lemma.

Lemma 11.1
.

For
\displaystyle k=0,1,\dots,2m
consider
\displaystyle V_{k}=\{x\in R_{m}\mid v(x)=k\}=\mathfrak{m}^{k}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}.

Then
\displaystyle|V_{0}|=2^{2m-1}
and, for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq 2m-1
,
\displaystyle|V_{k}|=2^{2m-k-1}.
In particular, the set of nonzero zero divisors of
\displaystyle R_{m}
is
\displaystyle Z^{*}(R_{m})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{2m-1}V_{k}
and its cardinality

|Z^{*}(R_{m})|=\sum_{k=1}^{2m-1}2^{2m-k-1}=2^{2m-1}-1.

Proof.

As
\displaystyle|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|=2^{2m-k}
for all
\displaystyle k
. So, for
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq 2m-1
, we have

|V_{k}|=|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|-|\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}|=2^{2m-k}-2^{2m-k-1}=2^{2m-k-1}.

Now, summing from
\displaystyle k=1
to
\displaystyle 2m-1
, we obtain

|Z^{*}(R_{m})|=\sum_{k=1}^{2m-1}2^{2m-k-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{2m-2}2^{j}=2^{2m-1}-1,

where we used the substitution
\displaystyle j=2m-k-1
.
∎

The next lemma gives the zero-product rule between layers.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
\displaystyle x\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle y\in V_{\ell}
, where
\displaystyle 1\leq k,\ell\leq 2m-1
and
\displaystyle x\neq y
. Then

xy=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k+\ell\geq 2m.

Thus

E(\Gamma(R_{m}))=\bigl\{\{x,y\}\mid x\in V_{k},\ y\in V_{\ell},\ x\neq y,\ k+\ell\geq 2m\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x=u\pi^{k}
and
\displaystyle y=w\pi^{\ell}
with
\displaystyle u,w\in R_{m}^{\times}
. Then

\displaystyle xy=uw\pi^{k+\ell}.

The product
\displaystyle uw
is a unit. Hence
\displaystyle xy
is zero in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi^{2m})
if and only if
\displaystyle\pi^{2m}
divides
\displaystyle\pi^{k+\ell}
, and this is equivalent to
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq 2m
.
∎

In particular, for
\displaystyle k\geq m
, the induced subgraph on
\displaystyle V_{k}
is complete, because two distinct vertices in
\displaystyle V_{k}
have valuation sum
\displaystyle 2k\geq 2m
.

The preceding section identifies

13
Cohen–Macaulayness and dimension for
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]

The projective dimension obtained from the corrected Betti formula almost reaches the number of variables. This forces depth one in every nontrivial Gaussian case, and therefore separates the homological linearity of the edge ideal from the Cohen–Macaulay property of the quotient.

In this section we examine the Cohen–Macaulayness of
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
, where
\displaystyle I_{m}=I(G_{m})
and
\displaystyle G_{m}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i])
. We use the standard graded convention: a quotient is Cohen–Macaulay precisely when its depth equals its Krull dimension, see, for example,
[
8
,
24
]
.

The polynomial ring
\displaystyle S
has

\displaystyle N=|V(G_{m})|=2^{2m-1}-1

variables. From Theorem
12.2
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{m})=2^{2m-1}-2

for every
\displaystyle m\geq 1
, with the value
\displaystyle 0
in the degenerate case
\displaystyle m=1
. Auslander–Buchsbaum
[
5
]

\operatorname{depth}_{S}(S/I_{m})=n-\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{m})=(2^{2m-1}-1)-(2^{2m-1}-2)=1.

(1)

Thus depth alone does not detect the exceptional case; the dimension must also be computed.

Next, we compute the Krull dimension of
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
. As
\displaystyle I_{m}
is a squarefree monomial ideal generated by quadratic monomials corresponding to the edges of
\displaystyle G_{m}
. So, its height equals the minimal size of a vertex cover of
\displaystyle G_{m}
, and hence
\displaystyle\dim(S/I_{m})=n-\operatorname{ht}(I_{m})\geq 2
as soon as
\displaystyle G_{m}
admits an independent set of size
\displaystyle 2
. For
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, it follows immediately. Take any two distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y\in V_{1}
. Then
\displaystyle v(x)=v(y)=1
, so
\displaystyle v(x)+v(y)=1+1=2<2m,
and by the adjacency rule
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
are not adjacent. Thus
\displaystyle\{x,y\}
is an
independent set, and so there exists a vertex cover of size at most
\displaystyle n-2
, and whence for all
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, we obtain

\dim(S/I_{m})=n-\operatorname{ht}(I_{m})\;\geq\;n-(n-2)=2.

For
\displaystyle m=1
, the situation is degenerate,
\displaystyle G_{1}
has a single vertex and no edges, so
\displaystyle I_{1}=(0)
and
\displaystyle S/I_{1}\cong K[x]
is a polynomial ring in one variable, of dimension
\displaystyle 1
. Therefore, we have

\dim(S/I_{m})=\begin{cases}1,&m=1,\\[2.0pt]
\geq 2,&m\geq 2.\end{cases}

We recall that a standard graded
\displaystyle K
–algebra
\displaystyle R
is Cohen–Macaulay if
\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(R)=\dim(R).
For
\displaystyle R=S/I_{m}
, by Equation (
1
),
\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(S/I_{m})=1
, for all

\displaystyle m\geq 1
, while

\dim(S/I_{m})=1\quad\text{if }m=1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dim(S/I_{m})\geq 2\quad\text{if }m\geq 2.

The next theorem gives the exact height and dimension.

Theorem 13.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G_{m}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i])
, and let
\displaystyle I_{m}=I(G_{m})\subseteq S
. Then

\displaystyle\dim(S/I_{m})=\alpha(G_{m})=2^{2m-1}-2^{m}+1,

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{height}(I_{m})=2^{m}-2.

Proof.

The graph
\displaystyle G_{m}
is
\displaystyle C(2,2m)
. Applying the even case of Proposition
10.11
with
\displaystyle q=2
and
\displaystyle L=2m
gives

\alpha(G_{m})=2^{2m-1}-2^{m}+1.

For an edge ideal, the Krull dimension of the Stanley–Reisner quotient equals the independence number of the graph. Since
\displaystyle|V(G_{m})|=2^{2m-1}-1
, so the height is

\operatorname{height}(I_{m})=|V(G_{m})|-\alpha(G_{m})=2^{m}-2.

∎

The next theorem determines the Cohen–Macaulay cases.

Theorem 13.2
.

The quotient
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\displaystyle m=1
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle m=1
, then
\displaystyle G_{1}
has one vertex and no edge. Hence
\displaystyle I_{1}=(0)
and
\displaystyle S/I_{1}\cong K[x]
, which is Cohen–Macaulay.
Assume
\displaystyle m\geq 2
. Then by Theorem
12.2
,

\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{m})=2^{2m-1}-2.

Since
\displaystyle S
has
\displaystyle N=2^{2m-1}-1
variables, so Auslander–Buchsbaum implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{depth}(S/I_{m})=1.

On the other hand, Theprem

\dim(S/I_{m})=2^{2m-1}-2^{m}+1.

For

\operatorname{depth}(S/I_{m})=1<\dim(S/I_{m}),

so
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
is not Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

14
Hilbert series for
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]

The Hilbert series here is obtained twice, first from the independence polynomial and then from the corrected Betti numerator. Agreement of these two expressions gives a useful validation of the corrected global subtraction term in Theorem
12.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta_{m}=\operatorname{Ind}(G_{m})
be the independence complex of
\displaystyle G_{m}
. Since
\displaystyle I_{m}=I(G_{m})
, the quotient
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
is the Stanley–Reisner ring
\displaystyle K[\Delta_{m}]
. Let
\displaystyle n_{k}=|V_{k}|=2^{2m-k-1},
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq 2m-1.

By Proposition
10.12
, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle G_{m}
is

F_{G_{m}}(y)=1+\sum_{s=1}^{2m-1}A_{s}(y)\prod_{k=1}^{\min\{s-1,2m-s-1\}}(1+y)^{2^{2m-k-1}},

where

A_{s}(y)=\begin{cases}(1+y)^{2^{2m-s-1}}-1,&2s<2m,\\[2.0pt]
2^{2m-s-1}y,&2s\geq 2m.\end{cases}

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{m}}(t)=F_{G_{m}}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right).
Equivalently, if

\displaystyle F_{G_{m}}(y)=\sum_{r=0}^{\alpha(G_{m})}f_{r-1}y^{r},

then

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{m}}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}\sum_{r=0}^{\alpha(G_{m})}f_{r-1}t^{r}(1-t)^{N-r},\qquad N=2^{2m-1}-1.

The coefficients
\displaystyle f_{r-1}
can also be written directly by valuation-layer choices. If
\displaystyle a_{k}
is the number of chosen vertices from
\displaystyle V_{k}
, then

f_{r-1}=\sum\prod_{k=1}^{2m-1}\binom{2^{2m-k-1}}{a_{k}},

where the sum runs over all vectors
\displaystyle(a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m-1})
satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{2m-1}a_{k}=r,\qquad 0\leq a_{k}\leq 2^{2m-k-1},k+\ell<2m\quad\text{whenever }a_{k}>0\text{ and }a_{\ell}>0.

In particular,
\displaystyle a_{k}\leq 1
whenever
\displaystyle k\geq m
.

Remark 14.1
.

The Hilbert series may also be recovered from the minimal free resolution:

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{m}}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{r\geq 1}(-1)^{r}\beta_{r}(S/I_{m})t^{r+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

The shift is
\displaystyle r+1
, not
\displaystyle r+2
, because this formula is for the quotient
\displaystyle S/I_{m}
. This expression is often the fastest way to check a numerical Hilbert numerator once the corrected Betti numbers are known.

.

\displaystyle m=2
, we have
\displaystyle|V_{1}|=4
,
\displaystyle|V_{2}|=2
, and
\displaystyle|V_{3}|=1
. The independence polynomial is

F_{G_{2}}(y)=1+\bigl((1+y)^{4}-1\bigr)+2y(1+y)^{4}+y=1+7y+14y^{2}+16y^{3}+9y^{4}+2y^{5}.

Thus

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{2}}(t)=\frac{1-7t^{2}+16t^{3}-20t^{4}+15t^{5}-6t^{6}+t^{7}}{(1-t)^{7}}.

This agrees with the Betti numerator obtained from Example

:

1-7t^{2}+16t^{3}-20t^{4}+15t^{5}-6t^{6}+t^{7}.

15
Zero-divisor graphs of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})

This section shows that the truncated polynomial ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})
yields exactly the chain graph
\displaystyle C(p,c)
. Thus the corrected formula for prime powers is not special to integer residue rings, as it is a valuation-layer phenomenon for finite principal chain rings.

Let
\displaystyle p
be a prime number and
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{N}
with
\displaystyle c\geq 2
. Consider the ring
\displaystyle R_{p,c}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/\langle x^{c}\rangle\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}),
where
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
is the field with
\displaystyle p
elements, and let its zero divisor graph be
\displaystyle G_{p,c}=\Gamma(R_{p,c}).
The ring
\displaystyle R_{p,c}
is a standard example of a finite local principal ideal
(chain) ring. Every element
\displaystyle f(x)\in R_{p,c}
can be written uniquely as a polynomial of degree
\displaystyle<c
, that is,

f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{c-1}x^{c-1},\qquad a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}.

The ideal generated by the image of
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}=(x)\subset R_{p,c},
is the unique maximal ideal, and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{c}=(0)\neq\mathfrak{m}^{c-1}
. In particular,
\displaystyle R_{p,c}
is a local ring of length
\displaystyle c
with residue field

\displaystyle R_{p,c}/\mathfrak{m}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Every nonzero
\displaystyle f\in R_{p,c}
can be written uniquely in the form
\displaystyle f(x)=x^{k}u(x),
where
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq c-1
and
\displaystyle u(x)\in R_{p,c}
is a unit (that is,
\displaystyle u(0)\neq 0
). We
define the
\displaystyle x
–adic valuation by

v(f)=\begin{cases}k&\text{if }f(x)=x^{k}u(x)\text{ with }u\text{ a unit }(\text{or }u\in R_{p,c}^{\times}),\\[2.0pt]
+\infty&\text{if }f=0.\end{cases}

Then
\displaystyle f
is a unit if and only if
\displaystyle v(f)=0
, and
\displaystyle f
is a nonzero zero divisor if and only if

\displaystyle 1\leq v(f)\leq c-1
. For
\displaystyle k=0,1,\dots,c
, the powers of the maximal ideal are
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{k}=(x^{k}),
for
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq c,
and each
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{k}
consists of all equivalence classes of polynomials divisible by
\displaystyle x^{k}
.

The following lemma counts the valuation layers.

Lemma 15.1
.

For
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq c
,
\displaystyle|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|=p^{c-k}
, and for
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq c-1
, the set
\displaystyle V_{k}=\{f\in R_{p,c}\mid v(f)=k\}=\mathfrak{m}^{k}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}
has cardinality
\displaystyle|V_{k}|=p^{c-k-1}(p-1)
. In particular, the nonzero zero divisors of
\displaystyle R_{p,c}
are
\displaystyle Z^{*}(R_{p,c})=\bigcup_{k=1}^{c-1}V_{k}
and
\displaystyle|Z^{*}(R_{p,c})|=\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}p^{c-k-1}(p-1)=p^{c-1}-1.

Proof.

The ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{k}=(x^{k})
consists precisely of the classes of polynomials
of the form
\displaystyle x^{k}g(x)
with
\displaystyle\deg g\leq c-k-1
. So,
\displaystyle g(x)
has
\displaystyle c-k
coefficients, each with
\displaystyle p
choices, and hence
\displaystyle|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|=p^{c-k}
. Therefore, we have

|V_{k}|=|\mathfrak{m}^{k}|-|\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}|=p^{c-k}-p^{c-k-1}=p^{c-k-1}(p-1).

Summing from
\displaystyle k=1
to
\displaystyle c-1
, and then with substitution
\displaystyle j=c-k-1
, we obtain

|Z^{*}(R_{p,c})|=\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}p^{c-k-1}(p-1)=(p-1)\sum_{j=0}^{c-2}p^{j}=p^{c-1}-1.

∎

Let
\displaystyle f,g\in R_{p,c}
be nonzero zero divisors with
\displaystyle v(f)=k
and
\displaystyle v(g)=\ell
, for
\displaystyle 1\leq k,\ell\leq c-1
. The valuation is additive on products up to the nilpotency index
\displaystyle c
, so we have

v(fg)=\begin{cases}k+\ell&\text{if }k+\ell\leq c-1,\\
\geq c&\text{if }k+\ell\geq c.\end{cases}

Also, in the latter case
\displaystyle x^{c}\mid fg
, so
\displaystyle fg=0
in
\displaystyle R_{p,c}
. Equivalently, the following result gives the description of adjacency in
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
.

Lemma 15.2
.

Let
\displaystyle 1\leq k,\ell\leq c-1
and let
\displaystyle f\in V_{k}
,
\displaystyle g\in V_{\ell}
with
\displaystyle f\neq g
. Then

\displaystyle fg=0
if and only if
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq c.

Equivalently,

E(G_{p,c})=\bigl\{\{f,g\}\mid f\in V_{k},\ g\in V_{\ell},\ f\neq g,\ k+\ell\geq c\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle f=x^{k}u
and
\displaystyle g=x^{\ell}w
with
\displaystyle u,w\in R_{p,c}^{\times}
. Then

\displaystyle fg=x^{k+\ell}uw.

Since
\displaystyle uw
is a unit, this product is zero in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})
if and only if
\displaystyle x^{c}
divides
\displaystyle x^{k+\ell}
, which is equivalent to
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq c
.
∎

In particular, for
\displaystyle k\geq\lceil c/2\rceil
the induced subgraph on
\displaystyle V_{k}
is
complete, because if
\displaystyle f,g\in V_{k}
with
\displaystyle f\neq g
, then
\displaystyle k+\ell=2k\geq c
and

\displaystyle fg=0
. Consider the prime power ring
\displaystyle A=\mathbb{Z}/p^{c}\mathbb{Z}
. Every nonzero element

\displaystyle a\in A
can be written uniquely as
\displaystyle a=up^{k}
with
\displaystyle u
a unit and

\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq c-1
. Define the
\displaystyle p
–adic valuation
\displaystyle w
by
\displaystyle w(up^{k})=k
and

\displaystyle w(0)=+\infty
. The nonzero zero divisors in
\displaystyle A
are the elements with

\displaystyle 1\leq w(a)\leq c-1
. For
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq c-1
, consider

W_{k}=\{a\in A\mid w(a)=k\}.

Then it is clear that
\displaystyle|W_{k}|=p^{c-k-1}(p-1),1\leq k\leq c-1,
and for
\displaystyle a\in W_{k}
,
\displaystyle b\in W_{\ell}
with
\displaystyle a\neq b
, we obtain
\displaystyle ab=0
if and only if
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq c.
In other words, the zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(A)
has the same valuation layer sizes and the same adjacency rule as
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
.

The same layer sizes and edge rule of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/\langle x^{c}\rangle
occur in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/p^{c}\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 15.3
.

For every prime
\displaystyle p
and every integer
\displaystyle c\geq 2
, there is a graph isomorphism

\Psi_{p,c}:\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/\langle x^{c}\rangle)\longrightarrow\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/p^{c}\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A=\mathbb{Z}/p^{c}\mathbb{Z}
, and let
\displaystyle W_{k}
be the set of nonzero elements of
\displaystyle A
with
\displaystyle p
-adic valuation
\displaystyle k
. Then

\displaystyle|W_{k}|=p^{c-k-1}(p-1)=|V_{k}|

for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq c-1
. Choose a bijection
\displaystyle\psi_{k}:V_{k}\to W_{k}
for each
\displaystyle k
, and define

\Psi_{p,c}(f)=\psi_{k}(f)\quad\text{if }f\in V_{k}.

This is a bijection on the nonzero zero divisors.

If
\displaystyle f\in V_{k}
and
\displaystyle g\in V_{\ell}
with
\displaystyle f\neq g
, then Lemma
15.2
and the usual valuation rule in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/p^{c}\mathbb{Z}
give

fg=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k+\ell\geq c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Psi_{p,c}(f)\Psi_{p,c}(g)=0.

Thus
\displaystyle\{f,g\}
is an edge of
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
if and only if

\displaystyle\{\Psi_{p,c}(f),\Psi_{p,c}(g)\}
is an edge of
\displaystyle\Gamma(A)
, thereby it shows that

\displaystyle\Psi_{p,c}
is a graph isomorphism.
∎

Example 15.4
.

Let
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle c=3
. Then
\displaystyle R_{2,3}=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{3})
has two elements of valuation
\displaystyle 1
, namely
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle x+x^{2}
, and one element of valuation
\displaystyle 2
, namely
\displaystyle x^{2}
. The graph is the path
\displaystyle P_{3}
, with
\displaystyle x^{2}
adjacent to the two valuation-one vertices and no edge between them.

16
Cochordality and Betti numbers for
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})

The following results are the corrected chain-ring specialization of the type formula. They agree with the prime-power case of the corrected paper after replacing
\displaystyle q
by the prime
\displaystyle p
and the nilpotency index by
\displaystyle c
.

The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{p,c})
is the layered graph
\displaystyle C(p,c)
. The following statements are therefore immediate specializations of Section
10
.

Theorem 16.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G_{p,c}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}))
, where
\displaystyle p
is prime and
\displaystyle c\geq 2
. For
\displaystyle\lceil c/2\rceil\leq i\leq c-1
, define

s_{i}=\bigl(p^{i}-p^{c-i-1}-1,p^{i}-p^{c-i-1}-2,\ldots,p^{i}-p^{c-i}\bigr).

Then
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
is cochordal and has type list

(s_{c-1},s_{c-2},\ldots,s_{\lceil c/2\rceil}).

If a terminal zero occurs, it may be deleted without changing the graph or the Betti numbers.

Proof.

By Lemmas
15.1
and
15.2
, the graph
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
is
\displaystyle C(p,c)
. Therefore Corollary
10.3
gives the stated type list, and Lemma
10.9
gives the terminal-zero convention.
∎

The next theorem gives the Betti numbers of the quotient by the edge ideal.

Theorem 16.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G_{p,c}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}))
, let
\displaystyle N=|V(G_{p,c})|=p^{c-1}-1
, and let
\displaystyle I_{p,c}=I(G_{p,c})\subseteq S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
. For every
\displaystyle r\geq 1
,

\beta_{r}(S/I_{p,c})=\beta_{r,r+1}(S/I_{p,c})=\sum_{j=\lceil c/2\rceil}^{c-1}p^{c-j-1}(p-1)\binom{p^{j}-2}{r}-\binom{p^{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}-1}{r+1}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{p,c})=p^{c-1}-2.

If
\displaystyle I_{p,c}\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle I_{p,c}
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution and
\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(S/I_{p,c})=1
. The only case with
\displaystyle I_{p,c}=0
and
\displaystyle c\geq 2
is
\displaystyle(p,c)=(2,2)
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
10.10
with
\displaystyle q=p
and
\displaystyle L=c
. This gives the Betti formula, the projective dimension, and the linearity statement. The graph has no edge only if it has one vertex, because for
\displaystyle c\geq 2
the top layer
\displaystyle V_{c-1}
is adjacent to every other nonzero zero divisor and is a clique when it has more than one vertex. The equality
\displaystyle p^{c-1}-1=1
holds exactly when
\displaystyle(p,c)=(2,2)
.
∎

The following consequence is immediate.

Corollary 16.3
.

When
\displaystyle I_{p,c}\neq 0
, the graded Betti numbers of the edge ideal itself satisfy

\beta_{r,r+2}(I_{p,c})=\beta_{r+1,r+2}(S/I_{p,c})\quad\text{for }r\geq 0,

and all other graded Betti numbers of
\displaystyle I_{p,c}
vanish.

Proof.

The result follows from the short exact sequence
\displaystyle 0\to I_{p,c}\to S\to S/I_{p,c}\to 0
and the fact that
\displaystyle S/I_{p,c}
has nonzero graded Betti numbers only in bidegrees
\displaystyle(r,r+1)
.
∎

Example 16.4
.

Let
\displaystyle c=2
. Then all nonzero zero divisors have valuation
\displaystyle 1
, and any two distinct such elements multiply to zero. Hence

\displaystyle G_{p,2}\cong K_{p-1}.

The formula in Theorem
16.2
becomes

\beta_{r}(S/I_{p,2})=(p-1)\binom{p-2}{r}-\binom{p-1}{r+1}=r\binom{p-1}{r+1},

which is the usual Betti sequence of the quotient by the edge ideal of a complete graph.

Example 16.5
.

Let
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle c=4
. Then
\displaystyle G_{2,4}
is the same graph as
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{2}}[i])
in Example
12.4
. The Betti formula gives

\beta_{r}(S/I_{2,4})=2\binom{2}{r}+\binom{6}{r}-\binom{3}{r+1},

so

(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4},\beta_{5},\beta_{6})=(7,16,20,15,6,1).

This confirms the graph isomorphism between the two chain-ring presentations in this numerical case.

17
Cohen–Macaulayness and Hilbert series for
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c})

This final section completes the parallel with the Gaussian family. The quotient is Cohen–Macaulay exactly in the complete-graph case
\displaystyle c=2
, and the Hilbert series is governed by the independence polynomial of the chain graph
\displaystyle C(p,c)
.
With these notations, the valuation layers or subsets
\displaystyle V_{k}
(
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq c-1
) are defined as in
Lemma
15.1
, with
\displaystyle|V_{k}|=p^{c-k-1}(p-1),
and
\displaystyle|V(G_{p,c})|=p^{c-1}-1.

Let
\displaystyle I_{p,c}=I(G_{p,c})\subseteq S
, where
\displaystyle G_{p,c}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}))
and
\displaystyle S
has

\displaystyle N=p^{c-1}-1

variables. We first compute the exact dimension and height.

Theorem 17.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G_{p,c}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}))
, and let
\displaystyle I_{p,c}=I(G_{p,c})
. If
\displaystyle c=2a
, then

\dim(S/I_{p,c})=\alpha(G_{p,c})=p^{2a-1}-p^{a}+1,\qquad\operatorname{height}(I_{p,c})=p^{a}-2.

If
\displaystyle c=2a+1
, then

\dim(S/I_{p,c})=\alpha(G_{p,c})=p^{2a}-p^{a},\qquad\operatorname{height}(I_{p,c})=p^{a}-1.

Proof.

The graph
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
is
\displaystyle C(p,c)
by Lemmas
15.1
and
15.2
. Therefore Proposition
10.11
gives the displayed independence number and height. The equality
\displaystyle\dim(S/I_{p,c})=\alpha(G_{p,c})
follows because
\displaystyle S/I_{p,c}
is the Stanley–Reisner ring of the independence complex of
\displaystyle G_{p,c}
.
∎

The Cohen–Macaulay property is now determined exactly.

Theorem 17.2
.

For
\displaystyle c\geq 2
, the quotient
\displaystyle S/I_{p,c}
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\displaystyle c=2
. If one also includes
\displaystyle c=1
, then
\displaystyle R_{p,1}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
has no nonzero zero divisors and the corresponding quotient is trivially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

Assume first that
\displaystyle c=2
, then
\displaystyle G_{p,2}\cong K_{p-1}
. So its independence number is
\displaystyle 1
, and
\displaystyle\dim(S/I_{p,2})=1
. Also Theorem
16.2
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{p,2})=p-2.

Since
\displaystyle S
has
\displaystyle p-1
variables, so Auslander–Buchsbaum gives

\operatorname{depth}(S/I_{p,2})=(p-1)-(p-2)=1.

Thus depth equals dimension, and
\displaystyle S/I_{p,2}
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Now assume
\displaystyle c\geq 3
. Again by Theorem
16.2
, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{p,c})=p^{c-1}-2.

As
\displaystyle S
has
\displaystyle N=p^{c-1}-1
variables, we obtain

\operatorname{depth}(S/I_{p,c})=N-\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I_{p,c})=1.

On the other hand, Theorem
17.1
gives
\displaystyle\dim(S/I_{p,c})\geq 2
for every
\displaystyle c\geq 3
. Hence depth is strictly smaller than dimension, so
\displaystyle S/I_{p,c}
is not Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

We now compute the Hilbert series. Let
\displaystyle\Delta_{p,c}=\operatorname{Ind}(G_{p,c})
. Then
\displaystyle S/I_{p,c}=K[\Delta_{p,c}]
. Also, set

\displaystyle n_{k}=|V_{k}|=p^{c-k-1}(p-1),
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq c-1.
By Proposition
10.12
, the independence polynomial is

F_{G_{p,c}}(y)=1+\sum_{s=1}^{c-1}A_{s}(y)\prod_{k=1}^{\min\{s-1,c-s-1\}}(1+y)^{p^{c-k-1}(p-1)},

where

A_{s}(y)=\begin{cases}(1+y)^{p^{c-s-1}(p-1)}-1,&2s<c,\\[2.0pt]
p^{c-s-1}(p-1)y,&2s\geq c.\end{cases}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{p,c}}(t)=F_{G_{p,c}}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right).

If

\displaystyle F_{G_{p,c}}(y)=\sum_{r=0}^{\alpha(G_{p,c})}f_{r-1}y^{r},

then

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{p,c}}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{N}}\sum_{r=0}^{\alpha(G_{p,c})}f_{r-1}t^{r}(1-t)^{N-r},\qquad N=p^{c-1}-1.

Equivalently, for
\displaystyle r\geq 0
, the coefficient
\displaystyle f_{r-1}
is

f_{r-1}=\sum\prod_{k=1}^{c-1}\binom{p^{c-k-1}(p-1)}{a_{k}},

where the sum is over all vectors
\displaystyle(a_{1},\ldots,a_{c-1})
satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}a_{k}=r,\qquad 0\leq a_{k}\leq p^{c-k-1}(p-1),~k+\ell<c\quad\text{whenever }a_{k}>0\text{ and }a_{\ell}>0.

In particular, if
\displaystyle k\geq\lceil c/2\rceil
, then automatically
\displaystyle a_{k}\leq 1
.

Remark 17.3
.

The Betti-number expression for the Hilbert series is

\operatorname{Hilb}_{S/I_{p,c}}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{r\geq 1}(-1)^{r}\beta_{r}(S/I_{p,c})t^{r+1}}{(1-t)^{N}}.

This gives a useful check on computations obtained from the independence polynomial. The agreement depends on using the corrected Betti formula with the global subtraction term.

\displaystyle K_{p-1}

F_{G_{p,2}}(y)=1+(p-1)y.

Thus

H_{S/I_{p,2}}(t)=1+(p-1)\frac{t}{1-t}=\frac{1+(p-2)t}{1-t}.

For
\displaystyle p=3

H_{S/I_{3,2}}(t)=\frac{1+t}{1-t}=\frac{1-t^{2}}{(1-t)^{2}},

which agrees with
\displaystyle I_{3,2}=(x_{1}x_{2})\subset K[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

18
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we developed a uniform chain-ring framework for studying zero-divisor graphs through their valuation layers. The central object was the layered graph
\displaystyle C(q,L)
, whose adjacency relation is determined by the inequality
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq L
. This simple rule captures the zero-divisor graph of a finite chain ring with residue field of order
\displaystyle q
and nilpotency index
\displaystyle L
, and it allows the graph-theoretic and homological computations to be carried out without treating each ring element separately.

Using the cochordal constructible-system method, we proved that
\displaystyle C(q,L)
is cochordal and obtained an explicit type sequence. Substituting this type sequence into the corrected Dung and Vu
[
15
]
formula gave a closed expression for all graded Betti numbers of the quotient by the edge ideal. In particular, for
\displaystyle I=I(C(q,L))\subseteq S
, we obtained a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution whenever
\displaystyle I\neq 0
, together with the projective dimension
\displaystyle\operatorname{pd}_{S}(S/I)=q^{L-1}-2.

The correction term in the Betti formula is essential, as it records the global overlap created by the ordered star construction and prevents overcounting.

The general theory was applied first to the Gaussian quotient
\displaystyle R_{m}=\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i]\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]/(1+i)^{2m}.

Since
\displaystyle R_{m}
is a finite chain ring with residue field of size
\displaystyle 2
and nilpotency index
\displaystyle 2m
, its zero-divisor graph is precisely
\displaystyle C(2,2m)
. This identification gives the type sequence, the complete Betti table, the projective dimension, and the regularity of
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{m}))
. We also computed the independence number
\displaystyle\alpha(\Gamma(R_{m}))=2^{2m-1}-2^{m}+1
and height
\displaystyle\operatorname{height}I(\Gamma(R_{m}))=2^{m}-2.
Consequently,
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{m}))
is Cohen–Macaulay only for
\displaystyle m=1
.
The same method was then applied to the truncated polynomial rings
\displaystyle R_{p,c}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}).

Here the valuation layers are the sets
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{k}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}
, and their sizes are
\displaystyle p^{c-k-1}(p-1)
. The adjacency condition is again exactly
\displaystyle k+\ell\geq c
, so
\displaystyle\Gamma(R_{p,c})\cong C(p,c)
. This gives closed formulae for the graded Betti numbers, projective dimension, independence number, height, and Hilbert series. The Cohen–Macaulay classification is especially clean: for
\displaystyle c\geq 2
, the quotient
\displaystyle S/I(\Gamma(R_{p,c}))
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\displaystyle c=2
.
The graph
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}[i])
is
\displaystyle C(2,2m)
, while
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]/(x^{c}))
is
\displaystyle C(p,c)
. Thus both are threshold graphs with valuation weights, and their previously computed invariants are threshold-graph invariants as well.

The Hilbert-series computations provide an additional check on the Betti-number formulae. On one hand, the Hilbert series is obtained from the independence polynomial of the graph as

\displaystyle H_{S/I(G)}(t)=F_{G}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right).

On the other hand, the same series is recovered from the minimal free resolution:

H_{S/I(G)}(t)=\frac{1+\sum_{r\geq 1}(-1)^{r}\beta_{r}(S/I(G))t^{r+1}}{(1-t)^{|V(G)|}}.

The agreement between these two descriptions confirms the consistency of the corrected Betti expressions and the combinatorial description of the independence complex.

Several directions remain open. First, it would be useful to extend the method beyond chain rings to finite principal ideal rings with more than one maximal ideal, where the valuation-layer picture is replaced by a multi-indexed divisor structure. Second, one may study finer invariants of the independence complexes arising here, such as shellability, sequential Cohen–Macaulayness, and levelness. Third, the explicit type sequences suggest possible algorithms for computing Betti tables of larger classes of cochordal zero-divisor graphs without using Hochster’s formula directly. Finally, the comparison between graph isomorphism classes of zero-divisor graphs and ring-theoretic isomorphism classes remains a natural problem: the examples in this paper show that different rings may produce the same zero-divisor graph, but a systematic description of this phenomenon is still incomplete.
