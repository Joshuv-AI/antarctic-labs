Immobilization and characterization of bovine carbonic anhydrase enzyme on iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles: investigation of potential applicability in CO2 removal
Authors: Muhammet Firat, Şükrü Beydemir, Mesut Işik, Alev Akpinar Borazan, Ömer İrfan Küfrevioğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, carbonic anhydrase (CA) was purified from bovine erythrocytes using a Sepharose 4B-l-tyrosine sulfanilamide affinity chromatography method, achieving an 83.42-fold purification. The source of the enzyme is bovine blood erythrocytes, which were obtained from a slaughterhouse. Following purification, two different support materials (Fe₃O₄–NH₂ and Fe₃O₄@SiO₂–NH₂) were synthesized for enzyme immobilization, and sığır karbonik anhidraz (BCA) was immobilized via the covalent binding method. The support material prior to immobilisation and the enzyme-bound support material after immobilisation were characterised by FT-IR, SEM-EDX and VSM. The kinetic parameters of free BCA were determined as Km = 4.888 mM and Vmax = 3.081 µmol min−1 mL−1. For the immobilised enzyme, Km and Vmax values were 16.160 mM and 2.195 µmol min−1 mL−1 on support material I, and 9.369 mM and 1.810 µmol min−1 mL−1 on support material II, respectively. The operational stability of the immobilised enzymes was evaluated through 20 consecutive reuse cycles, during which support material I retained 86% of its initial activity, while support material II retained 73%. In contrast, the free enzyme could only be used once due to its loss of activity. Storage stability tests were performed by incubating the samples at 4 °C for 60 days. At the end of the incubation period, support material II maintained 60% of its initial activity, and support material I retained 49%, whereas the free enzyme preserved only 17% of its activity under identical conditions. Finally, the potential applicability of the immobilised BCA for CO₂ removal was investigated, demonstrating its promise as a stable and reusable biocatalyst for CO₂ capture applications.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available athttps://doi.org/10.1186/s13065-026-01828-8.Keywords: Carbonic anhydrase, Magnetic nanoparticles, Purification, Immobilization, Stability, Characterization, CO2 removal

Introduction

Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere are the primary driver of global warming and the associated disruption of Earth’s climate system. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, carbon dioxide (CO₂) derived from anthropogenic activities is the dominant contributor to this phenomenon. Combustion of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas accounts for nearly 89% of global anthropogenic CO₂ emissions. Data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) reveal that atmospheric CO₂ levels have risen by approximately 45%, from ~ 291 ppm in 1880 to 421 ppm in 2023, elevating global mean surface temperatures by at least 1 °C above pre-industrial levels [1]. Projections indicate that if fossil fuel consumption continues unabated, CO₂ concentrations will exceed 450 ppm by the end of this century [2]. Consequently, the development of efficient CO₂ capture and separation technologies has become a critical scientific and technological priority of the 21st century [3].

A broad range of physical, chemical, and biological approaches have been explored for CO₂ removal [4]. Among them, biocatalytic systems employing carbonic anhydrase (CA, EC 4.2.1.1) have emerged as particularly promising due to their high efficiency, specificity, and environmental compatibility [3, 5–8]. Carbonic anhydrase, a zinc-containing metalloenzyme that catalyzes the reversible hydration of CO₂, is ubiquitous across living organisms and essential to many physiological processes [1, 9]. Of its known isoforms, CA I and CA II are present in high concentrations in erythrocyte cytosol [10, 11]. Research has shown that slaughterhouse by-products, such as bovine blood and tissue waste, represent a sustainable source of high-value enzymes, including CA [12, 13]. The purification of bovine carbonic anhydrase (BCA) from discarded erythrocytes thus offers a dual benefit: enzyme recovery from biological waste and the transformation of low-value by-products into industrially relevant biocatalysts.

Despite their catalytic potential, free enzymes face major industrial limitations due to their solubility, low operational stability, and difficulty of reuse. As free enzymes are sensitive to variables in the catalytic environment, such as temperature and pH, a significant decrease in enzyme activity or complete inactivation may occur when conditions change. Therefore, the development of enzymes that are resistant to the harsh conditions of the reaction is a key issue. To overcome these challenges, researchers have investigated enzyme immobilisation using various carriers in recent years [12].

Enzyme immobilisation provides an effective route to overcome these challenges by enhancing catalytic stability, reusability, and recovery efficiency. However, alongside these advantages, certain potential limiting factors in enzyme immobilization such as low loading efficiency, high leaching rates and mass transfer resistance still remain [13].

The low stability and poor reusability of free CA under harsh conditions limit its industrial application [14]. Therefore, CA immobilisation has been recognised as a promising technology for improving its activity and stability. A review of studies reported in the literature indicates that immobilised CA exhibits better stability and reusability compared to free CA [15].

A wide variety of supports have been investigated for CA immobilisation [2, 14]. A significant proportion of the porous materials commonly used for immobilisation face challenges related to limited specific surface area, irregular pore size distribution, poor biocompatibility and a complex synthesis process [12].

Among these, surface-modified magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) stand out due to their large surface areas, ease of functionalisation and magnetic separability [15–17]. To improve immobilisation efficiency, MNPs are frequently functionalised with reactive moieties—such as amine, hydroxyl, carboxyl, or epoxy groups—that can form covalent bonds with nucleophilic residues of the enzyme (–NH₂, –OH, –SH) [16–18]. Coating MNPs with silica (SiO₂) further enhances stability and biocompatibility while providing a reactive surface for enzyme coupling [19, 20]. Silanisation with tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) enables the formation of silanol groups that can subsequently be modified using silane coupling agents such as 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES), introducing terminal –NH₂ groups that facilitate covalent enzyme attachment [21–23]. The presence of these amine groups significantly increases immobilisation efficiency compared to uncoated MNPs, where enzyme binding is largely governed by weak physical adsorption [24].

Among the various immobilisation strategies, covalent binding is preferred for industrial applications because it ensures enzyme retention and minimises leaching [25, 26]. The crosslinking agent glutaraldehyde (GA) is particularly effective in establishing strong covalent bonds between enzymes and support materials, yielding biocatalysts with high stability and durability [27–29]. GA acts as a bifunctional linker by introducing aldehyde groups that react with amino residues on both the enzyme and the functionalised carrier, forming stable imine linkages. This covalent network not only prevents enzyme detachment but may also enhance structural rigidity, improving overall catalytic performance.

In this study, BCA purified from bovine erythrocytes was immobilised via covalent attachment onto GA-activated support materials. Within this framework, the aim was to determine the enzyme loading ratio and to characterise the immobilized enzyme and free enzyme in terms of their optimum temperature, optimum pH, kinetic parameters (Km, Vmax) and stability (pH, thermal and storage). Finally, the potential applicability of the immobilized BCA for CO₂ capture and conversion was evaluated, highlighting its promise as a sustainable and reusable biocatalyst for mitigating anthropogenic CO₂ emissions.

Materials and methods

Purification of BCA

Blood sampling and haemolysate preparation

Bovine blood was obtained from Bilecik Municipality Ak Serhat Abattoir. In order to prevent blood clotting, 100 ml of blood was taken directly from the vein at the time of complete slaughter and 15 ml of acid citrase dextrose (ACD) solution used as anticoagulant was added. Blood samples were stored at 4 °C after collection and used for haemolysate within two days. Haemolysate was performed according to the method published in the literature [30].

Purification of BCA by affinity chromatography

Fifteen mL of the previously prepared homogenate was loaded onto a sepharose 4B-l-tyrosine sulfanilamide affinity chromatography column (Column diameter 1.3 cm, gel height 40 cm, elution rate 20 ml/h and fraction volume 3 ml). BCA enzyme was purified according to the published method [31]. Sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was used to control enzyme purity [32]. Protein was measured by the Bradford method using serum albumin as a standard [33].

Synthesising MNPs

Magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were synthesised in an open-air environment using the co-precipitation method. In the study, 7.5 g of FeCl₂·4 H₂O and 10.1 g of FeCl₃·6 H₂O were dissolved in 25 ml of water. 25 ml of NH₄OH (25%) was added to the solution, which was then stirred for 2 min at 700 rpm using a mechanical stirrer. It was observed that the solution turned black upon the addition of ammonium hydroxide. The resulting black precipitates were retained using strong neodyum magnets. The liquid portion was carefully removed using a pipette and discarded. In the subsequent step, the MNP’s were washed three times with 52 mL of deionised water and twice with 51 mL of ethanol, then filtered and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 2 h to form a powder [34, 35].

Functionalisation of MNPs

Two different support materials were synthesised by attaching different functional groups to MNPs.

Support material I (Fe3O4-NH2)

Amine group (NH2) was bound to MNPs using APTES (99%). 300 mg of dried and powdered MNPs were placed in 100 mL ethanol and 4 mL distilled water and dispersed homogenously by sonication for 15 min. Then 200 µl APTES was added to this mixture. The temperature of the mixture was increased to 70 °C and the reaction was continued for 18 h. After 18 h of reaction, APTES-bound magnetic nanoparticles were firstly separated from the mixture by centrifugation at 9000 rpm for 30 min. The MNPs obtained after centrifugation were separated by magnetic separation, washed 5 times with distilled water and dried [36].

Support material II (Fe3O4@SiO2-NH2)

For the preparation of silicon oxide coated MNPs, 4 g MNP was taken, mixed with 180 mL ethanol and 20 mL distilled water and sonicated for 30 min. Then 2 mL TEOS was added and sonicated for 15 min. 10 mL NH4OH was added dropwise to this mixture in a mixer. It was stirred at 750 rpm for 6 h. The resulting black precipitate was washed with ethanol and distilled water, collected with a magnet and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 4 h. 0.5 g of the powdered Fe3O4@SiO2 was taken, ethanol was added and sonicated for 30 min. Then 300 µl APTES was added and sonication was continued. It was washed with distilled water and ethanol and dried [37, 38].

Immobilisation of BCA

BCA was immobilised according to the method published on two different synthesised support materials [37].

Characterization

Characterization studies were carried out by FTIR, SEM and VSM to confirm the synthesis and immobilisation of MNPs. The magnetic properties of the obtained MNP before and after immobilisation were investigated at room temperature (298 K) with a VSM Oxford 3001 vibrating sample magnetometer. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements were performed to determine the functional groups on the surface of the support material and to verify the success of the enzyme immobilisation steps. FTIR was determined using a Perkin Elmer/Spectrum 100 spectrometer covering wave numbers between 400 and 4000 cm−1. Elemental properties were determined by EDX. SEM-EDX analysis was performed using a ZEISS/Supra 40 VP scanning electron microscope equipped with an EDX apparatus. Depending on the change in the morphological structure of the enzyme-bound support material, it was determined whether the enzyme bound to the surface or not.

Determination of pH, stability, and kinetic parameters

Determination of optimum and stable pH

In order to determine the optimum pH affecting the enzyme activity, immobilised CA and free CA enzyme concentrations were taken as equal. Enzyme activities were examined by esterase activity determination method in 1 M Tris-SO4 buffer solutions with pH between 7.0 and 9.0 and 1 M potassium phosphate (KH2PO4) buffer solutions with pH between 5.0 and 8.0. The optimum pH was determined as the value at which free and immobilised CA showed the highest activity. While determining the stable pH, immobilised CA and free CA enzyme were stored at 4 °C in 1 M Tris-SO4 buffer solutions with pH between 7.0 and 9.0 and 1 M potassium phosphate (KH2PO4) buffer solutions with pH between 5.0 and 8.0. Then, the pH at which the enzyme was stable was determined in the esterase activity measurement performed at 1 week intervals [40].

Determination of thermal stability and optimum temperature

The activities of free CA and immobilised CA were measured by esterase activity measurement method after incubation for a total of 80 min at 20 min intervals in pH 8, 1 M Tris-SO4 buffer at temperatures ranging from 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C and 70 °C. For the determination of the optimum temperature, esterase activity was determined by esterase activity measurement method using a water bath at the optimum pH of the enzyme previously determined by creating a temperature difference of 5 degrees between 25 and 70 °C [3].

Kinetic parameters

To investigate the effect of substrate amount on enzyme activity, 4 nitrophenylacetate was prepared at different concentrations (1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 mM). The activity was determined for each substrate concentration at optimum pH and optimum temperature. Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) and maximum reaction rate (Vmax) values were found for the enzymatic reaction by drawing Lineweaver–Burk graph with the obtained data [41].

Operational stability

400 µl of 0.05 M Tris-SO4 pH 7.4 buffer, 360 µl of 3 mM p-nitrophenyl acetate as substrate and 190 µl of purified water were added to 5 mg of enzyme. The shaken suspension solution was filtered and the absorbance was measured at 348 nm in a UV spectrophotometer at every 5 min interval. After immobilisation, between each cycle, the immobilised enzyme was magnetically detached with the help of a magnet and washed with phosphate buffer. This procedure was repeated 20 times for p-NPA hydrolysis of immobilised CA [40].

Storage stability

For the storage stability study, the samples were incubated with the enzyme loaded in 4.8 ml of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0) at 4 °C for 60 days. The activities of the catalysts for hydrolysis of p NPA were analysed for 60 days at 4 °C at 10-day intervals. The initial activity after immobilisation was assumed to be 100% and percentage stability was determined [3].

CO2 removal potential

A reaction mixture containing 0.9 g CaCl₂-2 H₂O and buffer solution was prepared. Enzyme was added to four different erlenmeyer flasks, one of which served as a control. To initiate the reaction, 60 ml of dissolved CO₂ gas (0 °C) was added to the mixtures, and the CO₂ removal potential was determined by monitoring the amount of CaCO₃ formed during the subsequent reaction at 10 °C. When the CO₂ solution was added to the erlenmeyer flask, it was immediately sealed with an airtight film and the mixture was left to stand for 2 h. The solution was then filtered and dried to measure the weight of the precipitated CaCO₃ [42].

Statistical studies

Data analysis and graphical visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA) software with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. The measurements were performed in 3 independent replicates. Descriptive statistical analyses were carried out, with the results presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), offering an indication of the variability associated with the mean values. The p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (n = X, mean ± SD).

MNPs were synthesised using the co-precipitation method in an open air environment. Magnetic nanoparticles were functionalised by attaching two different functional groups as described in “Functionalisation of MNPs” section. Characterization

SEM-EDX analysis

SEM images show that there is not much homogeneity and regularity in the shape and particle size of MNPs. In the images, it was determined that the particles were clustered due to the magnetic dipole moment (Fig. 3A). The surface of the functionalized MNP was found to change due to the silica coating (Fig. 3B). The surface is rough with high aggregation between crystals, especially in samples with TEOS. As a matter of fact, literature data show that SiO2 is amorphous and not spherical. This is thought to be due to the Van der Waals force between the particles. It is seen that the agglomeration decreases after surface modification (Fig. 3C). After immobilisation, an increase in surface roughness was observed (Fig. 3D and E). This confirms the successful binding of the enzyme to the support material.

SEM images (A MNP, B MNP-APTES-GA, C MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA, D MNP-APTES-GA-BCA, E MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA)

Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDX) performed with SEM images gives information about the elemental composition and shows whether immobilisation has occurred or not. In the EDX spectra of MNPs, peaks associated with Fe and O were obtained. This proved that the main component in MNPs is iron oxide formed by iron and oxygen (Fig. 4A). Approximately the same EDX spectra were obtained for Fe in functionalized MNPs (Fig. 4B and C). After immobilisation, S (sulphur) associated peaks were observed in the EDX spectra (Fig. 4D and E). It can be suggested that the S-related peaks are due to the amino acids methionine and cysteine in BCA. This finding proves that BCA was successfully immobilised on the surface of functionalized MNPs.

VSM analysis

The saturation magnetisations (Ms) of MNP-APTES and MNP-TEOS-APTES prior to immobilisation were measured as 59.040 emu/g and 59.680 emu/g, respectively (Fig. 6A). Prior to immobilisation, the MNPs exhibited negligible coercivity (Hc) and remanence, which are typical characteristics of superparamagnetic materials. Following immobilisation, the saturation magnetisations (Ms) for MNP-APTES-GA-BCA and MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA were measured as 54.342 emu/g and 55.332 emu/g, respectively. The results indicate a decrease in saturation magnetisation values following the immobilisation of the support materials (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, they exhibited negligible coercivity (Hc) and remanence, which are typical characteristics of superparamagnetic materials. The absence of hysteresis and residual magnetisation indicates that these particles are superparamagnetic in nature [47, 48].

Magnetic hysteresis loops (A before immobilisation, B after immobilisation)

In general, the saturation magnetisation (Ms) decreases due to the addition of non-magnetic organic layers following coating and, in particular, enzyme immobilisation. Consequently, in the VSM analysis conducted, the Ms values after immobilisation were lower than those before immobilisation. These results are consistent with those reported in other studies in the literature [2, 37, 49–51].

The magnetisation values showed that the immobilised enzymes can be easily separated from the reaction medium in the presence of an external magnetic field and that the redispersion of the catalysts can occur rapidly with a slight agitation when the magnetic field is removed. Therefore, this property can be used to remove immobilised magnetic nanoparticles from the reaction medium during reuse. Determination of pH, stability, and kinetic parameters

Kinetic properties

The kinetic parameters of free and immobilised BCA were determined by looking at the activity values at different substrate concentrations. Km and Vmax values of free BCA were 4.888 mM and 3.081 µmol min−1 mL−1, respectively, while Km and Vmax values of MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA were 9.369 mM and 1.810 µmol min−1 mL−1, respectively, and Km and Vmax values of MNP-APTES-GA-BCA were 16.160 mM and 2.195 µmol min−1 mL−1, respectively. Compared to free enzyme, immobilised enzymes showed an increase in Km value and a decrease in Vmax value (Table 2).

Km and Vmax values for free and immobilised BCA

When the studies in the literature are examined, it is observed that Km value of immobilised enzyme increases and Vmax value decreases. For example, in a study by Yadav et al. Km and Vmax values for free enzyme were found to be 1.594 mM and 1.307 µmol min−1 ml−1, respectively, and Km and Vmax values for immobilised CA were found to be 1.727 mM and 1.189 µmol min−1 ml−1, respectively [40]. In another study by Jing et al. Km and Vmax values of free CA were 6.091 mM and 0.091 µmol min−1 ml−1, respectively, while Km and Vmax values of immobilised CA were 8.077 mM and 0.027 µmol min−1 ml−1, respectively [49]. The reason for the increase in Km value and decrease in Vmax value in immobilised enzymes is thought to be due to the decrease in the affinity between the enzyme and the substrate as a result of the strong covalent bonding formed between the enzyme and the support material at the immobilisation stage, partially disrupting the conformational structure in the active site of the enzyme.

Optimum ve stabil pH

The optimum pH for both free and immobilised enzymes was determined as 9 (Fig. 7). According to these results, it can be said that the activity of BCA was not affected by the immobilisation process since the optimum pH value of free and immobilised enzymes was the same. The pH value at which the enzyme will show high activity in the immobilisation process varies depending on the chemical structure of the matrix and the immobilisation method. For example; Jing et al. found the optimum pH value of CA immobilised on magnetic poly (GMA-DVB) microspheres to be 8 [49].

Effect of pH on free and immobilised BCA activities

The pH at which the enzyme was stable was determined in esterase activity measurements performed one day apart for one week. Free and immobilised enzymes showed more stable activity in the pH 8–9 range. However, free BCA retained 78%, MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA 88% and MNP-APTES-GA-BCA 84% of its remaining activity at pH 9 (1.0 M Tris-SO4 buffer). Immobilised BCA enzymes had a higher percentage relative activity in the alkaline range compared to free BCA enzyme under the same buffer and same conditions. It is evaluated that this difference in stability may be due to the pH-dependent change in the three-dimensional conformational structure and immobilisation. The increase in the stability of immobilised BCA at alkaline pH is an important parameter for CO2 sequestration. In conclusion, immobilisation of BCA on MNPs increased the enzyme stability. In the studies reported in the literature [49, 52], the fact that the stable pH varies in the range of 8–10 depending on the CA source, support material and immobilisation method supports these findings.

Thermal stability and optimum temperature

For thermal stability, activities were measured by esterase activity measurement method at varying temperatures. The percentage remaining activity for free BCA was 100% at 30 °C, 100% at 40 °C, 93% at 50 °C, 12% at 60 °C and 11% at 70 °C (Fig. 8A); The remaining activity for MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA was 100% at 30 °C, 100% at 40 °C, 94% at 50 °C, 90% at 60 °C and 81% at 70 °C (Fig. 8B), and the remaining activity for MNP-APTES-GA-BCA was 100% at 30 °C, 100% at 40 °C, 81% at 50 °C, 63% at 60 °C and 67% at 70 °C (Fig. 8C). Although the activity of free BCA decreased to 11% at 60 and 70 °C, the remaining activity of immobilised BCA was maintained up to 81%. These results indicate that immobilised BCAs have a much better thermal stability than free BCA, especially at 60 and 70 °C. The results were shown as percentage relative activity time plots (Fig. 8).

The preference for covalent binding as a method reduced the conformational flexibility of the enzyme molecules, which resulted in the immobilised enzymes retaining a large proportion of their activity even at 70 °C. These results show that the active centre of the enzyme can be protected from temperature changes by the immobilisation procedure. This is of great importance especially for industrial applications. Because the thermal stability of 40–70 °C for CA is very important for the capture of CO2 in the flue gas without any additional cooling process. The results of the present study show better outcomes than those reported in other studies in the literature within certain temperature ranges. For example; Al-Dhrub et al. studied the thermal stability of free and immobilised hCA I at 30, 40 and 50 °C in the immobilisation of hCA I on amine functionalized magnetic nanoparticles. After 80 min incubation, the residual activities of the free enzyme were 79% at 30 °C, 56% at 40 °C and 24% at 50 °C, while the residual activities of the immobilised enzyme were 89% at 30 °C, 93% at 40 °C and 35% at 50 °C, respectively [37]. According to these results, the residual activity at 50 °C was 35%, while the residual activity at 50 °C was 94% in this study. In another study, Sahoo et al. immobilisation of carbonic anhydrase and an artificial Zn(II) complex on a magnetic support for biomimetic carbon dioxide sequestration, the thermal stability was measured after a 4 h incubation at various temperatures ranging from 30 °C to 70 °C. With increasing temperature, at 70 °C, immobilised CA retained 30% of its activity, while free CA lost 91% of its original activity [3].

The optimum temperature for free and immobilised BCA was determined as 45 °C (Fig. 9). Both showed the highest activity at 45 °C and lost their activity significantly at other temperature values. However, the effect of temperature on the enzyme activity of free CA was more pronounced than that of immobilised CA. The results proved that the immobilised enzyme preserves its conformational structure more easily at temperature. In addition, this situation should not be ignored in applications.

Effect of temperature on free and immobilized BCA activities

The optimum temperature value is in accordance with the studies in the literature. For example; Yadav et al. found the optimum temperature of immobilised CA as 45 °C in the immobilisation of CA on chitosan stabilised iron nanoparticles for carbonation reaction [40]. In another study, Jing et al. reported that the relative activity of CA immobilised on magnetic poly(GMA-DVB) microspheres and free CA first increased with increasing temperature and both reached a maximum at 45 °C [49].

Storage stability

The initial activity after immobilisation was assumed to be 100% and the remaining percentage relative activity at the end of 60 days was determined (Fig. 10). At the end of 60 days, MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA retained 60% of its activity, immobilised MNP-APTES-GA-BCA retained 49% of its activity, while free BCA retained only 17% of its activity under the same conditions. The results show a significant difference in the remaining activity between free and immobilised enzymes after 60 days of storage. This indicates that the immobilisation of BCA on magnetic nanoparticles by covalent binding significantly increases the storage stability of the enzyme. The immobilised enzymes, MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA and MNP-APTES-GA-BCA, have a wide potential in CO2 capture and various biotechnological applications and can be suggested to be used in these fields.

Storage stability of free and immobilized enzymes

The storage stability results are similar to the results of other studies reported in the literature. For example; Al-Duhrub et al. investigated the storage stability of human carbonic anhydrase I after immobilisation on amine-functionalised magnetic nanoparticles. The activity was determined in a 5-day period for 40 days. After 40 days of storage, free hCA I retained 50% of its original activity, while immobilised hCA I retained 89% of its original activity [37]. In another study, Yadav et al. immobilised carbonic anhydrase enzyme on chitosan stabilised iron nanoparticles for carbonation reaction and investigated its storage stability at room temperature. At the end of 20th day, they observed that immobilised CA showed 65% residual activity and free enzyme showed 43% residual activity [40].

Operational stability

When immobilised enzymes were reused 20 times for operational stability, MNP-TEOS-APTES-GA-BCA retained 73% of its activity and MNP-APTES-GA-BCA retained 86% of its activity (Fig. 11). In the immobilisation process, multiple reuse of an enzyme without losing its catalytic activity indicates that there is no leakage of the enzyme and the immobilisation process is successful. Considering that free enzymes can be used only once and enzyme production is costly, reuse of the enzyme many times in biotechnological applications will reduce the product cost. Loss of activity after each cycle may result from the loss of MNPs and leaching of the enzyme from MNPs [52]. When our current study is compared with similar studies in the literature in terms of operational stability, it stands out with its 86% retention of activity despite being reused 20 times. For example; in one study, rBhCA was immobilised on silanised MNPs. Immobilised rBhCA retained 50% of its activity after 22 reuses [52]. In another study; immobilisation of carbonic anhydrase on epoxy-functionalised magnetic polymer microspheres for CO2 capture was performed. The reusability of the enzyme remained approximately 47.6% of its initial activity after 6 cycles [49].

In conclusion, the use of BCA derived from waste blood presents a potentially viable biological approach to CO₂ removal that is economically viable. It is also anticipated that this work will contribute to and provide insights for future research.

The results are consistent with the reported studies. Yadav et al. investigated the immobilisation of carbonic anhydrase in alginate and its effect on the conversion of CO2 to calcite and found that the amount of carbonate precipitated in independent reactions using free and immobilised CA was almost equal [40]. Sharma et al. obtained calcium carbonate precipitation using chitosan-KOH immobilised P. fragi CA. They reported that more than 2-fold increase in sequestration potential was observed for the immobilised system compared to free enzyme [53].

As can be seen in Table 3, CaCO₃ formation was limited in the experiments performed without the enzyme and a little calcium carbonate precipitation was observed in the reaction medium. However, it was found that the amount of CaCO₃ formation increased significantly in the presence of free and especially immobilized carbonic anhydrase enzyme. This reveals that carbonic anhydrase enzyme significantly catalyzes the process of CO₂ hydration and subsequent carbonate formation. These findings support the potential use of these enzyme systems in environmentally sustainable chemistry applications and carbon capture and conversion technologies. In particular, the reusability and stability of immobilized enzyme systems offer important contributions to the economic and environmental sustainability of such processes.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates the sustainable purification and immobilisation of BCA obtained from slaughterhouse waste erythrocytes, highlighting its potential for industrial applications. By utilising waste bovine blood as an enzyme source, this approach not only contributes to environmental sustainability but also adds economic value through the recovery of high-performance biocatalysts.

In this study, BCA was successfully immobilised onto Fe₃O₄ MNPs functionalised with TEOS and APTES through covalent binding using GA. Immobilisation enhanced the kinetic and stability properties of the enzyme, significantly improving its catalytic performance. Compared with the free enzyme, immobilised BCA retained a higher relative activity across a broader pH range and exhibited remarkable storage stability, maintaining up to 60% of its activity after 60 days, while the free enzyme retained only 17% under identical conditions. In addition, the BCA enzyme immobilized on the second support material retained 86% of its initial activity after 20 repeated uses, demonstrating high operational stability.

The obtained results indicate that immobilization significantly enhanced the stability and reusability of the BCA enzyme, caused no substantial loss in its catalytic activity, and consequently improved its CO₂ capture capacity. Overall, the study introduces a sustainable, biologically based strategy for CO₂ capture using enzymes derived from industrial waste sources. The results are expected to provide valuable insight for future research on the development of eco-efficient enzyme-based systems aimed at mitigating CO₂ emissions in industrial processes.
