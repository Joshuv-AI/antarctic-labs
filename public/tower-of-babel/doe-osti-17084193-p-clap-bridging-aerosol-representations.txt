ABSTRACT
Aerosol effects on clouds and radiation depend on the distribution of particle size, chemical
composition, and mixing state, although this detail is difficult to retain at the spatial scales and
computational costs of Earth system models (ESMs). This project addresses this challenge through
coordinated advances in machine learning, performance-portable atmospheric chemistry, laboratory
evaluation, and particle transport. This report summarizes five machine-learning studies that
progress from compact generative aerosol representations and inference from partial observations
to population-linear embeddings, scale-covariant coagulation dynamics, and mixture-consistent
spatial coarse-graining. It also describes PartML, an ongoing effort to learn stochastic aerosol
process operators in a common latent representation. The TChem-atm contribution provides
multiphase chemistry on CPU and GPU architectures and demonstrates agreement with the existing
PartMC–CAMP implementation in idealized box-model tests. Rotating-drum experiments identify
aerosol composition, particle neutralization, and chamber configuration as important controls on
model–measurement agreement. Finally, the report documents progress toward applying PartMC
on E3SM grid boxes, including a transport test showing first-order horizontal convergence under
solid-body rotation, whilst identifying vertical verification and MPI integration as remaining tasks.
Together, these results establish components of a more detailed and computationally tractable
aerosol modeling capability, whereby demonstration of the complete coupled system remains future
work.

1. INTRODUCTION

Aerosol populations evolve through emissions, transport, coagulation, gas–particle exchange,
chemical aging, and removal, with each process changing the distribution of properties that governs
their interaction with clouds and radiation. The association of chemical species within individual
particles, commonly termed aerosol mixing state, is particularly important because two populations
with the same bulk composition can have different activation, absorption, and freezing behavior.
Particle-resolved models such as PartMC retain this information explicitly, providing a detailed
reference for assessing the approximations introduced by reduced aerosol representations [2, 3].
Retaining particle-level detail throughout an Earth system simulation is computationally demanding.
Conversely, a reduced model that reproduces selected diagnostics in isolated states may not remain
physically meaningful when populations are mixed, transported, or evolved repeatedly. This project
therefore pursued a coordinated set of capabilities: compact aerosol representations that respect
population structure, efficient numerical chemistry that can supply detailed reference calculations,
controlled experiments that test those calculations against measurements, and transport methods that
support particle populations on an atmospheric grid. These capabilities address different sources of
approximation and are evaluated separately in the chapters that follow.
The project’s primary method for creating new reduced representations is to leverage artificial
intelligence and machine learning for discovery of low-dimensional latent spaces, with computational
complexity similar to existing representations, but increased expressive capability that can retain
or reconstruct more of the particle-resolved information. We develop several architectures and
learning strategies in support of this goal, and a general shorthand for the project has become
“Physically-Constrained Learning for Aerosol Parameterization,” or P-CLAP.

1.1. Scientific approach

The common object across the project is a weighted population of aerosol particles. PartMC evolves
this population through stochastic particle operations and coupled chemistry. The Machine Learning
(ML) work compresses its size–composition information into a small number of coordinates and
learns either diagnostic readouts or process-specific evolution in those coordinates. TChem-atm
reduces the cost of the detailed chemistry calculations themselves. The experimental work tests
the assumptions needed to compare a well-mixed population model with measurements in a finite
chamber. The E3SM work addresses movement of populations between grid boxes, which is required
before a collection of detailed local aerosol calculations can act as a spatial model.
This organization also distinguishes three questions that can otherwise be conflated. Numerical
verification asks whether an algorithm reproduces a specified mathematical problem, such as an
analytically transported tracer. Model evaluation against experiments asks whether the physical

assumptions and experimental configuration explain observed aerosol evolution. Surrogate evaluation
asks whether a learned approximation reproduces the reference model, including the quantities and
variability relevant to the intended application. Strong performance on one of these questions does
not establish the others.

2.1. Scope and publication record

The ML contribution to P-CLAP addresses two related problems: how to represent a heterogeneous
aerosol population compactly while retaining the information needed for climate diagnostics, and
how to evolve that representation under the operations used by atmospheric models. The five studies
in Table 2-1 form a progression from generative compression to structured latent dynamics. PartML
is an additional manuscript in preparation that builds on this representation work to learn individual
stochastic process operators. The studies share scientific goals, but their datasets, diagnostic
definitions, latent dimensions, and computational benchmarks differ, whereby numerical errors and
speedups should therefore be interpreted within each study.

2.2. Compact generative aerosol representations

The first study investigates whether the aerosol information required for multiple diagnostics can
be compressed into a small learned state [4, 10]. The input is a binned representation of particle
number and speciated mass as functions of size, constructed from a PartMC–MOSAIC scenario
library. A variational autoencoder subsequently learns a low-dimensional latent distribution together
with a decoder that reconstructs the binned state. The supplied manuscript uses a library of 1,000
distinct 24-hour scenarios and demonstrates representations with ten or fewer latent coordinates,
reducing a state with hundreds of entries to a small fixed-dimensional vector.
The evaluation considers both direct reconstruction of the input distributions and downstream
aerosol diagnostics. The latter include CCN spectra, scattering and absorption coefficients, and
immersion-freezing frozen-fraction spectra. This distinction matters because a small error in a bulk
distribution does not necessarily imply a small error in a nonlinear diagnostic, especially when that
diagnostic depends on a low-abundance component. Here, CCN spectra were reconstructed most
readily, optical properties were more challenging, and frozen-fraction spectra were the most difficult
of the diagnostic groups considered. The reported hierarchy provides a useful guide for subsequent
model design: a representation should be evaluated against the scientific quantities it is expected to
carry, rather than only a generic reconstruction loss.
The supplied generative manuscript also examines pre-processing and model selection. Aerosol
variables span large dynamic ranges, so the transformation used before training changes which
perturbations are emphasized by the loss. The study develops a pre-processing optimization based
on robustness to imposed noise and evaluates generative realism through a sliced Wasserstein
comparison with held-out states. The latter is used to guide the weight on the variational regularization
term. These methods help assess whether a latent model produces plausible samples as well as
reconstructing individual inputs.
This work established the feasibility of compact, diagnostically useful aerosol representations. It
did not establish an exact inverse of the particle population, because binning already discards
particle-level associations, nor did it require the learned coordinates to commute with population
addition. Those limitations motivate the particle-set representation developed in AeroMELD.

2.4.3. Diagnostic fidelity and representational limits

The supplied AeroMELD study evaluates a ten-coordinate state, comprising one scale coordinate
and nine shape coordinates, against size-resolved mass and number distributions, CCN spectra,
optical coefficients, and freezing diagnostics. Its data split assigns entire scenarios to training or
testing, limiting leakage between temporally related snapshots. The manuscript reports 20,000
training populations and 5,000 testing populations for each 80–20 scenario split, with repeated splits
used to assess robustness.
The resulting embedding preserves the population algebra while supporting accurate diagnostic
reconstruction. In a Deep Sets pipeline, a nonlinear map is commonly applied after the particle
features are aggregated. AeroMELD places that nonlinearity in the diagnostic readout rather than
the stored additive state. Relative to that diagnostic architecture, retaining a linear population
aggregation therefore does not require discarding the nonlinear readout’s expressive capacity.
The remaining limitation is closure. A finite collection of learned moments is not generally sufficient
to identify an arbitrary particle distribution, and populations with the same encoded state can have

 20

different nonlinear process tendencies. The embedding provides a consistent state in which a closure
can be learned; the accuracy of that closure must be established empirically for each process and
population family.

2.6. Learning mixture consistency in nonlinear set encoders

The Maywald–Patel study asks whether mixture consistency can be induced through training when
a nonlinear post-aggregation map is retained in the encoder [8]. Its spatial test assembles particle
populations on a 16 × 16 fine lattice and averages them to a 2 × 2 coarse lattice. Each coarse
population is therefore a uniform mixture of 64 fine populations. Two paths are compared: aggregate
the encoded fine populations, or construct and encode the full coarse population. Their difference
measures whether the learned representation commutes with coarse-graining.
The training design separates a representation-level consistency loss from a mixture-output loss.
The former penalizes disagreement between the two encoded paths, where the latter improves
diagnostic predictions for mixed populations. Their effects are assessed through sweeps of loss
weights, the Dirichlet concentration used to sample mixture weights, and training mixture arity, with
ten seeds per configuration. A decomposition of diagnostic error separates the contribution of latent
inconsistency from the decoder’s own error.
In the reported nontrivial-mixture runs, a positive consistency loss reduces the median encoder gap
from approximately 106 –107 under non-mixture baselines to approximately 10−7 . All 160 reported
runs carrying the nontrivial consistency constraint show this collapse in the encoder gap. The
latent-mismatch contribution to diagnostic error falls with it, leaving the decoder error dominant.
Figure 2-1 shows the arity comparison with a population-linear encoder.
The study also fits an affine surrogate to the nonlinear outer map on sampled convex combinations
of held-out populations. The resulting affineness defect reaches approximately 4 × 10−16 in 134 of

2.7.7. Symmetric errors, held-out diagnostics, and the companion study

The manuscript additionally reports symmetric relative errors for run WM, which allow an indicative
comparison with the AeroMELD coagulation study [7]. Two features are visible only on this

 31

metric. The binned-number error at one hour, 0.150 at 𝑀 = 256, is essentially independent of
𝑀 (0.148 at 𝑀 = 4096) while its sampling reference falls from 0.023 to 0.005. The decoder
therefore misplaces the fine mode by approximately 15% however many particles it emits, which is
a systematic placement error that the set-level objective does not resolve. Second, the errors on the
dry mass distribution and composition, 0.28 and 0.27 at one hour, are nearly constant in horizon,
since coagulation conserves mass and persistence is almost exact for these quantities. On these
two blocks the 256-particle surrogate is nevertheless more accurate than four-replicate PartMC
at 250 particles (0.43 and 0.36) and at 4000 particles (0.31 and 0.24), because number-weighted
resampling carries the coarse mode in a fraction of a particle. On the number blocks the same
baselines are considerably more accurate than the surrogate.
Diagnostics withheld from training indicate what the latent state does not carry. The dry-volume-

fraction at 1.0% supersaturation, outside the scored band, has a CRPS of 5.1 × 10−2 compared with
1.2 × 10−2 for persistence. Composition is the part of the particle state that the decoder places least
well.
The comparison with Tang et al. is not controlled, since the two studies differ in horizon, environmental
range, particle number, species count, and decoding target. With that qualification, PartML is
competitive on total number within its horizon, with symmetric errors of 0.2% at one hour and 1.2%
at twelve, and is behind by factors of two to five on the size-distribution, mass, and composition
blocks and by factors of four to six on CCN activation. The former gap reflects the placement error
of a particle-level decoder, which a diagnostic-level decoder does not incur, and at 24 hours the
latter includes the over-prediction of number loss, which whole-trajectory training avoids.

2.7.8. Structural comparisons and computational cost

At matched shape dimension 𝑑 = 48, the population-linear encoder has a median mixture-consistency
defect of approximately 7.8 × 10−16 , compared with 0.63 for an unconstrained Deep Sets encoder
and 5.1 × 10−3 when a mixture-consistency penalty is added. The penalty substantially reduces the
defect but does not recover the exact algebraic identity. These defects are specific to the PartML
evaluation protocol and should not be compared numerically with absolute encoder gaps from the
separate coarse-graining study.
The predictive comparison is not uniformly in favor of the linear encoder. The unconstrained
nonlinear model has smaller number distribution errors, 0.145 and 0.126 at one and six hours,
whereas its one-hour number scale CRPS is larger, 1.73 × 10−3 , and its predicted mean-diameter
change has the wrong sign at both horizons. The penalized encoder recovers that sign but has a dry-
mass RMSE of 0.082 decades, compared with 0.029–0.043 across the linear-model initializations.
The differences in distribution accuracy are modest relative to the observed variability among linear-
model initializations. The strongest demonstrated advantage of the population linear representation
is its exact compatibility with population mixing, alongside competitive predictive performance.
On an Apple M2 Max CPU, the production model’s 32-member forecast costs approximately
9.8 × 10−3 s per one-hour application, excluding initial encoding, compared with 8.6 × 10−2 s for a
four-replicate PartMC forecast at 250 particles. Their one-hour distribution errors are comparable,

 32

0.176 and 0.178, giving approximately a ninefold cost reduction for the forecasts actually evaluated.
With four surrogate members, the cost falls to 1.2 × 10−3 s and NRMSE increases to 0.189,
corresponding to approximately a 70-fold cost reduction at equal ensemble size. One full-resolution
PartMC integration of the same interval costs 59 s, approximately 6 × 103 times the 32-member

The favorable cost comparison depends on decoder resolution and the required accuracy. For the

identifies 𝑀 = 256 with four members as the operating point, at approximately one fortieth of the
cost of the four-replicate 250-particle simulation, which is slightly more accurate at that horizon.
Improvements in particle placement and composition are therefore needed in addition to increasing
the number of generated particles.

2.7.9. Latent structure and process attribution

Since the encoder is population-linear, each latent coordinate is a fixed linear functional of the
population measure. Statements about the learned latent dynamics are therefore statements about
rates of change of population functionals and can be compared with physical expectations. This is
a second consequence of the linearity constraint, distinct from the exact mixing operations. The
revised manuscript includes a first analysis of this kind [9].
The analysis clusters latent trajectories into two regimes and compares them through trajectory
descriptors (initial, final and mean values, net change, linear trend, and excursion) of total number
𝑁, mass 𝑀, number-weighted mean logarithmic diameter, and the source-resolved number fractions.
The reference expectation is that of coagulation: a loss of number accompanied by growth in size,
and hence a negative correlation between log10 𝑁 and the mean of log10 𝐷. Figure 2-5 shows that
both regimes exhibit this signature in time, with falling number and increasing mean diameter from
a common initial state. The contrast between regimes is different. Relative to regime 1, regime 2 has
a larger excursion in log10 𝑁 (by 0.0090, a factor of approximately 1.02) and a lower net number
change (by 0.0064 decades), and also a lower final mean diameter (by 0.011 decades, a factor of
0.976) and lower final mass (by 0.044 decades, a factor of 0.905). Were the regimes separated by
coagulation strength alone, the regime with greater number loss would have the larger diameter. The
observed contrast, with lower 𝑁, 𝐷 and 𝑀 together, indicates that the separation reflects a coupled
process mixture.
Two further observations support this interpretation. The difference between the regimes’ Pearson
correlation matrices, shown in Figure 2-6, is small in the number–diameter entry (approximately
+0.03 as read from the figure), so neither regime has a markedly stronger coagulation-like coupling,
while the largest differences, of up to 0.24, involve the source fractions. The most discriminating
descriptors include the trend of one source fraction and the variability of another, with final source
fractions differing between regimes by 0.3 to 1.8 percentage points. Source composition determines
the population on which coagulation acts and covaries with emission and dilution in the scenario

 33

library, so a latent direction combining number loss with source-fraction change is a process-mixture
direction, within which coagulation is one component.
These findings are suggestive and not definitive. Most pairwise contrasts have modest effect sizes,
and their Benjamin–Hochberg adjusted 𝑝-values exceed conventional significance thresholds. The
supplied draft does not yet specify the clustering method, the number of trajectories per regime, or
whether 𝑀 denotes bulk or particle-scale mass, and these will be needed before the attribution can
be assessed quantitatively. The supportable conclusion is that the linear latent space is organized
by named aerosol quantities, principally number evolution and source composition, and that the
hypothesis of a single coagulation-only coordinate is directly testable in this representation and is not
supported by the present diagnostics. For P-CLAP, this provides a route to verifying learned operators
against process understanding, particularly once additional operators share the representation.

2.7.10. Implications and remaining work

PartML provides a common representation in which exact population operations can be combined
with a learned stochastic coagulation step. The revised results establish dry-volume conservation
to numerical precision through 24 compositions, repeated-application stability over the trained
horizons, useful prediction of total-number changes, and improvement on persistence over the
scenario length for total number, CCN activation, and the size distribution in turn. They also
identify the training-set size of the decoder as the control on particle placement and show that the
linear latent coordinates are open to physical interrogation. The remaining limitations concern the
systematic placement error of the fine mode, the accuracy of decoded composition and species mass,
uncertainty calibration, and the over-prediction of number loss beyond the trained horizon, for which
training on longer increments is the identified remedy. A number-weighted representation carrying
(𝑁, V) as its scale pair, which would separate the two effects of the hybrid weighting, also remains
to be tested. Extending the approach to a process suite will require training the representation on
intermediate states encountered during operator splitting and evaluating combinations of learned
and exact operators. The present evidence supports this architecture as a basis for that development,
while identifying the specific improvements needed before deployment in a coupled atmospheric
model.

2.8. Impact on aerosol research

The ML studies establish a common design principle: retain known population structure analytically
and learn the unresolved diagnostic or process dependence from detailed data. The compact generative
and conditional models establish what information can be retained or inferred, while AeroMELD
provides the additive coordinates required for mixing and transport. The coagulation studies
investigate complementary prognostic formulations, one as a concentration-scaled deterministic
ODE and the other as a finite-step stochastic transition with a particle-set decoder. Mixture-
consistency training quantifies the consequences of relaxing the structural constraint.
This progression provides an interface to the other project contributions. PartMC and TChem-atm
can supply detailed process data and references, in which the experimental work tests the validity

3. TCHEM-ATM: PERFORMANCE-PORTABLE MULTIPHASE

3.1. Contribution and scope

TChem-atm provides a performance-portable implementation of chemically detailed, multiphase
atmospheric chemistry. This chapter summarizes the published paper by Oscar H. Díaz-Ibarra,
Samuel G. Frederick, Jeffrey H. Curtis, Zachary D’Aquino, Peter A. Bosler, Lekha Patel, Cosmin Safta,
Matthew West, and Nicole Riemer, TChem-atm (v2.0.0): scalable performance-portable multiphase
atmospheric chemistry [11]. The implementation and numerical results are complementary to
the ML work: TChem-atm accelerates the detailed chemical calculation that defines a reference
trajectory, whereas an ML process surrogate approximates a selected evolution operator in a reduced
state.
Atmospheric chemistry produces coupled, stiff systems of ordinary differential equations. The
gas phase is shared by many aerosol particles or representative aerosol populations, and exchange
between phases couples their chemical evolution. As the number of aerosol particles and chemical
species increases, source-term evaluation and implicit time integration can become expensive.
TChem-atm addresses this cost through a portable parallel implementation and a choice of numerical
solvers that can be matched to the problem size.

3.2. Software architecture and multiphase state

TChem-atm combines the multiphase abstractions developed for CAMP with a Kokkos-based
implementation and the numerical infrastructure used by TChem and TINES. It is a standalone
atmospheric chemistry code, rather than a direct merger of the CAMP and TChem code bases.
The extension adds atmospheric mechanism parsing, gas–aerosol transfer kernels, and aerosol data
structures. TINES supplies numerical components shared with TChem, while SUNDIALS provides
additional time-integration options. Figure 3-1 summarizes the interfaces.
Chemical mechanisms are supplied through human-readable input files and parsed into structured
kinetic and aerosol-model objects. The state contains gas-phase concentrations and the chemical
composition associated with each computational particle. Schematically

3.3. Verification against PartMC–CAMP

The published numerical comparison uses a well-mixed PartMC box with constant temperature and
pressure. Gas-phase reactions use the CB05 mechanism, and gas–aerosol partitioning is included.
Coagulation and removal are disabled so that the chemistry and phase-exchange implementation
can be evaluated separately. The calculation starts with 103 computational particles and follows a
24-hour chemical evolution, comparing PartMC–CAMP with PartMC–TChem-atm on CPU and
GPU backends.
The comparisons include ozone, isoprene, and semivolatile products in the gas and aerosol phases.
With a relative solver tolerance of 10−6 for the accuracy comparison, the reported ℓ2 norms of
relative differences from PartMC–CAMP are 2.5 × 10−5 for gas species and 8.2 × 10−6 for aerosol
species. These accumulated solution differences should be distinguished from the local error
tolerance supplied to the time integrator. The time series remain close across the CPU, GPU, and
legacy calculations, supporting the consistency of the multiphase implementation in the tested
configuration.
This experiment is a numerical comparison of chemistry implementations. It does not include
the chamber loss processes in Chapter 4, the coagulation operators in Chapter 2, or the spatial
transport in Chapter 5. Isolating the chemical step is useful precisely because it makes discrepancies
attributable to a smaller set of numerical and interface choices.

4. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION OF AEROSOL POPULATION
 DYNAMICS

Source manuscript: When does a barrel behave like a box? Investigating experimental factors that
influence the quality of corresponding Monte Carlo simulations of aerosol population changes over
time in a drum. Samantha M. Kruse, Kyuhaeng Lee, Nicole Riemer, Matthew West, Lekha Patel,
and Ryan D. Davis [12].
Numerical atmospheric models rely heavily on experimental data for validation, yet discrepancies
often arise due to overlooked experimental factors. This study investigates how aerosol composition,
experimental configuration, and particle charge state influence the agreement between laboratory
aerosol measurements and PartMC Monte Carlo simulations. Experiments with sorbitol, raffinose,
and effloresced sodium chloride aerosol at low relative humidity reveal that aerosol morphology
affects coagulation behavior and model fidelity due to viscosity variations, with sorbitol yielding the
best model agreement. Rotating drum chambers significantly reduce experimental variability and
improve model matching, while static chambers lead to increased losses and greater disagreement
with models over time. Aerosol charge state is also critical, as non-neutralized aerosols develop
uneven size distributions that are not captured by PartMC, consistent with enhanced coagulation of
charged particles. These findings underscore the complexities between aerosol physicochemical
properties and experimental conditions, highlighting the necessity of carefully designed experiments
to enhance the accuracy and applicability of numerical atmospheric models, particularly for aerosol
processes under varying environmental conditions.

4.1. Introduction

With the recent rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML), there
has been a growing interest in leveraging these technologies to enhance atmospheric modeling. [13]
AI/ML techniques offer powerful tools for analyzing complex, high-dimensional datasets and can
potentially improve the accuracy and efficiency of predictions related to atmospheric processes
and climate dynamics by addressing challenges that traditional numerical models face due to
computational constraints and incomplete physical parameterizations. [14, 15, 16] However, for
these AI/ML models to “learn”, they must have a sufficient basis of training data. Numerical
atmospheric models are an ideal basis, as they have long been fundamental tools in meteorology and
climate science, providing insights into weather patterns, climate variability, and the interactions
between different components of the Earth system. [11, 17] The uncertainty associated with aerosol
representations in atmospheric models remains high relative to, e.g., gas phase chemistry. Due
to the compositional and structural variability of aerosols in comparison to gases, aerosols have
become recognized as one of the biggest unknown variables within Earth’s radiative budget. [18]

 45

Aerosols influence the scattering and absorption of solar radiation and act as cloud condensation
nuclei and ice nucleating particles, modulating cloud formation and precipitation processes. [19]
Over the past few decades, the inclusion of aerosols in numerical models has become recognized as
critical for accurately representing the Earth’s radiative budget. [20, 4] Despite these advances, a
major limitation of atmospheric models remains the validation and verification of their accuracy
against experimental data.
Ideally, model validation would rely on high-fidelity field measurements collected at multiple
sites and over extended time periods to capture the spatial and temporal variability of atmospheric
processes. However, practical constraints such as limited manpower, high operational costs,
and logistical challenges often restrict the availability and resolution of such datasets. [21, 22]
Furthermore, many field studies do not measure or report all parameters necessary for comprehensive
model evaluation, leading to gaps in the data that hinder the ability to fully constrain and improve
atmospheric models. An attractive alternative to field data is laboratory-generated data, which offers
controlled conditions where variables can be precisely manipulated and measured with high accuracy.
Laboratory experiments allow for systematic investigation of specific atmospheric processes, such as
aerosol formation, growth, and interfacial reactions, under reproducible conditions. [23] Controlled
environments reduce the uncertainties associated with natural variability and measurement noise
that often affect field observations. However, laboratory studies also have limitations. Experimental
artifacts can introduce biases that are not always accounted for in models. [24] Additionally, because
most experimental datasets are not originally designed with atmospheric modelers in mind, they
frequently lack measurements of critical variables necessary for accurate model reproduction and
validation, and studies that do often require a large amount of manpower (e.g. field studies). [25]
This disconnect underscores the need for improved integration between experimental design and
modeling requirements to enhance the utility of laboratory data in atmospheric science. This
relationship is demonstrated in Figure 4-1, where to have improved global climate models, there is a
need for better laboratory studies to build the models.

Here, we determine if common experimental variables, which are often presumed to have a

 46

negligible impact in small-scale chamber studies, impact the usability and match prediction of
PartMC, specifically in how an aerosol population changes over time from physical loss processes.
The effect of aerosol composition (and consequentially viscosity and phase state) was tested, to
determine how different types of coagulation based on shape factor of the primary aerosol influences
the predictions by PartMC. Sorbitol, raffinose, and sodium chloride were used at low relative
humidity to compare liquid, glassy, and crystalline states, respectively. [26, 27] Further, three
common experimental variables were tested to determine which, if any, increase or decrease the
match agreement between experiments and PartMC. Specifically, we tested chamber rotation, aerosol
neutralization, and method of introducing dilution gases to the chamber. Sorbitol had by far the
best match to PartMC of all three compositions, likely due to its low viscosity leading to rapid
and complete coalescence. This demonstrates that an aerosol system that undergoes coalescence
rather than agglomeration during coagulation will lead to lower experimental variability and a more
consistent, high-degree of match between experiments and PartMC. Although aerosol particles did
not have a deliberately-applied charge, using an aerosol neutralizer significantly improved the match
between PartMC and experiments. This indicates that the unknown charge distribution created
using common aerosol-generating techniques is influencing experimental outcomes. Additionally,
having a rotating chamber significantly reduces the variability in experiments and improves the
match to PartMC at longer timescales. Pressure drops caused by how dilution air is introduced did
not significantly impact the match to PartMC. This work contributes to a better understanding of
experimental factors that impact PartMC model results and improves their quality while enabling
scale-up of global atmospheric models to include more diverse aerosols, which ultimately enables
the applications of artificial intelligence to global atmospheric models.

4.2. Methods

In this section, we provivde experimental details of the experiments conducted against PartMC.

4.2.1. Drum experiments

The experimental setup for the drum experiments is shown in Figure 4-2. For the drum experiments,
solutions of sorbitol (99%, Sigma Aldrich), raffinose (≥98.0%, Sigma Aldrich), or sodium chloride
(99.0%, Fisher Scientific) were prepared at 100 ppm in water (18Ω, Millipore). These solutions
were aerosolized using an atomizer (Model 3076, TSI Inc.) operated at 25 psi with an oil-free air
compressor (0470442, Kobalt). These aerosols were dried using a diffusion dryer (AGS, Brechtel)
filled with Drierite (4 mesh, Sigma Aldrich). For some experiments, the aerosols were also
neutralized with an electrical aerosol neutralizer (1090, TSI Inc.) after drying. Then, the aerosols
were sent into a 40 L rotating drum (Biaera Technologies). Dilution air was introduced either by
pulling room air through a HEPA filter (6702-3600, Whatman), or by pushing air flow from the
compressor through the HEPA and into a mass flow controller (5 SLPM, Alicat) set to the total
pulling flow of the instruments post-drum. The experiments were either run at 0 rpm (static) or 2.3
rpm (rotating). The interior of the drum was equipped with a pressure, humidity, and temperature
logger (EL-SIE-6+, Lascar) for monitoring fluctuations during the experiments, and the data was
collected using EasyLog (Lascar). The outflow of the drum was measured using a scanning electrical

 47

mobility spectrometer (SMPS, TSI Inc.) which was a combination of an electrostatic classifier (EC,
Model 3082, TSI Inc.) and a condensation particle counter (CPC, Model 3750, TSI Inc.). The CPC
was operated with butanol (99.8%, Sigma Aldrich) as the condensate. The SMPS was operated
at 0.952 LPM, with a scan range of 8.2 to 310.6 nm and a scan rate of 2 minutes and 10 seconds.
Built-in multiple charge correction and diffusion correction were used for all samples. Data was
collected using the Aerosol Instrument Manager software (AIM, Version 10.3.1.0, TSI Inc.). During
the experiments, the aerosols were generated over a ten minute period, after which point the SMPS
was either pulling room air through a filter, or flow was being pushed from a mass flow controller
into the drum.

4.4. Discussion

The influence of aerosol composition had a significant effect on both PartMC match and experimental
variability, with sodium chloride performing the worst and sorbitol performing the best. Due to
the low relative humidity of the experiments, this is likely caused by different types of coagulation
processes. Sodium chloride and raffinose are both solids at 20% RH, with crystalline and amorphous
morphologies respectively. [28, 29] These morphologies lead to agglomeration, which introduces
variability in the shape factor. Additionally, the aerodynamic properties of the aerosols change
as a function of this shape factor, which is not accounted for in the SMPS data, leading to very
high standard deviations, especially in the larger size bins where the agglomerated aerosols are
present. Sorbitol however maintains liquid viscosity at lower relative humidities and undergoes
coalescence-based coagulation. This leads to a very consistent shape factor across the full experiment
leading to very low variability and better PartMC match.

Having the ability to rotate the chamber reduced experimental variability, and importantly, PartMC
could not match the static drum effectively at longer timescales. This improvement is likely due to
the altered flow dynamics within the drum. The primary benefit to using a rotating drum is that a
boundary layer grows in around the walls of the drum during rotation, which leads to more prolonged
aerosol suspension within the drum. These results match experimental work done previously. Huang
et al. 2019 found that for particles 1 𝜇m in size, a rotation rate between 2 and 3 rpm is ideal
for optimizing aerosol decay within the drum. [30] Both this work and the works of Huang et al.
2019 distinctly contrast that of Gruel et al. 1987, who concluded based on a numerical model
that the optimal rotation rates of a drum were independent of particle size. [31] Importantly, the
aerosols used by Huang et al. 2019 were neutralized prior to entering the drum, which may have
also improved their results. [30]
Neutralizing the aerosol was another experimental factor that had a large impact on the PartMC
agreement. When the sample aerosol was introduced to the drum without neutralization, it quickly
formed a bimodal distribution that remained present throughout the duration of the experiment,
while PartMC predicted that it would smooth out over time. The aerosol population also depleted
more quickly than its neutralized counterpart. There is evidence in the literature with similar
findings regarding coalescence behavior changes due to particle charge state. Ghosh et al. 2017
performed both models and experiments utilizing an electrical low-pressure impactor to investigate
the effects of charge on particle coagulation and found that bipolarly charged particles exhibited
significantly enhanced coagulation when compared to neutral particles. [32] Additionally, after
16 minutes, their model exhibited a similarly shaped bimodal aerosol distribution. This work is
also in agreement with a study by Simones and Loyalka, who found that charged carbonaceous
aerosols coagulated on faster timescales than model predictions. [33] These results may be enhanced
by the small chamber volume, where aerosol particle concentrations are much higher than what
may be found in the natural environment. However, current research has found that particle charge
state can significantly alter atmospheric coagulation processes as well. Quan et al. 2026 found
that electrostatic forces can reduce aerosol number concentrations by up to 64% in comparison to
Brownian coagulation alone. [34] Furthermore, He et al. 2020 studied meteorological data alongside
particle charge, and found that there are diurnal and seasonal shifts in net charge, with winter having
a higher net charge than summer, and daytime having a higher net charge than nighttime. [35] He et
al. 2020 concluded that some of the seasonal and diurnal variations in net particle charge state are
caused by fluctuations in atmospheric relative humidity. Indeed, Nomura et al. 2003 found that
when powder particles were triboelectrically charged, the level of net particle charge decreased with
increasing humidity. [36] However, this charging is not always consistent with relative humidity, and
may be compositionally dependent. Xu and Grosshans et al. 2023 found that electrostatic charge
on particles first increased with increasing relative humidity and then decreased with subsequent
relative humidity increases. [37] In a very different field of aerosol research, Kwok and Chan 2008
found that one brand of dry powder inhaler aerosols had the lowest net charge at 40% relative
humidity, which increased at both lower and higher relative humidity. [38]

 54

4.5. Conclusions

This study highlights the significant influence of aerosol composition, chamber dynamics, and
particle charge state on experimental variability and the agreement with the numerical model PartMC.
Among the aerosol types tested, sodium chloride exhibited the poorest model agreement and highest
variability, likely due to its solid crystalline morphology at low relative humidity (RH), which
promotes agglomeration and variable shape factors. In contrast, sorbitol, maintaining liquid-like
viscosity under similar conditions, readily coalesced, resulting in consistent shape factors, reduced
variability, and improved model agreement. The use of a rotating chamber markedly improved
experimental reproducibility and model matching by altering flow dynamics and sustaining aerosol
suspension through boundary layer formation, consistent with prior findings. These results contrast
with earlier numerical predictions, underscoring the importance of experimental validation. Aerosol
neutralization emerged as a critical factor, where non-neutralized aerosols formed persistent bimodal
size distributions and exhibited faster losses than predicted by PartMC, aligning with previous studies
demonstrating enhanced coagulation rates for charged particles. The impact of particle charge on
coagulation processes is further supported by recent atmospheric observations indicating diurnal
and seasonal variations in net particle charge influenced by relative humidity and composition.
One major consideration for both field and laboratory experiments that affect both aerosol charge
state and morphology is relative humidity. Based on this study and others, low relative humidity tends
to complicate analysis, through increased particle charge, particle agglomeration and subsequent
shape factor changes, or both. This is highly relevant to field studies, where data from low relative
humidity environments may be harder to utilize with numerical atmospheric models, such as arctic
studies as well as those conducted at high altitudes. One potential alternative may be to rehydrate
the aerosols prior to measurement; however, this may introduce further complications due to phase
state hysteresis of aerosol particles. [39] These issues can be partially mitigated by an aerosol
neutralizer, prior to measurement in the case of field work, and prior to interactions with other system
variables in the case of laboratory work. Ultimately, these findings emphasize the complex dynamics
between aerosol physicochemical properties and experimental setup, all of which must be carefully
considered to improve the fidelity of numerical atmospheric models and their representation of
atmospheric aerosols.

4.6. Impact on aerosol research

The chapter identifies a reproducible laboratory configuration for assessing the evolution of an aerosol
population while exposing conditions under which model–measurement agreement deteriorates.
Within the tested cases, sorbitol in a rotating drum with neutralization and pulled dilution flow
provides the strongest joint agreement in concentration and size distribution. A favorable aggregate
match statistic alone is insufficient when experimental variability is large, as the raffinose and
static-drum comparisons illustrate.
The non-neutralized and solid-particle cases identify priorities for examining charge effects,
morphology, chamber losses, and the mapping from modeled particle size to the mobility-based
measurement. These comparisons concern the reference physical model and the experiment, so they

 55

remain relevant even if a learned surrogate reproduces PartMC accurately. The supplied work does
not directly validate a trained ML surrogate against the drum measurements.

5. TOWARD PARTMC ON E3SM GRID BOXES

Previous sections and studies have validated PartMC and various AI/ML emulators based on it
against experiments and atmospheric measurement campaigns. However, these datasets are relatively
small, compared to the global atmosphere that the E3SM model is intended to simulate. It is well
known that the success of AI/ML methods depends both on having a large amount of data and, when
such AI/ML products are to be coupled within a larger simulation, that it is helpful that the data and
and the model have some degree of internal consistency and compatibility. With an eye toward an
ultimate goal of improving aerosol representations within global circulation models such as E3SM,
while maintaining their strict computational throughput requirements, in this section we develop the
capability to use E3SM as a driver to produce a large, high-fidelity PartMC data set that can be used
to train an E3SM-compatible emulator.
PartMC often requires 𝑂 (104 ) particles to accurately represent an aerosol parameterization in a
small control volume; coupling such a model directly to E3SM would mean simulating 𝑂 (104 )
particles per grid cell. A standard coarsely resolved climatology simulation with E3SM uses a
quadrilateral grid with 6 cubed sphere faces, each with 30 elements per cubed sphere edge, with 4
physics columns per element, and 72 levels per column, for a total of 𝑁𝑔 = 1.5552 × 106 grid cells.
Clearly, coupling PartMC directly to E3SM is not computationally practical for applications that
require ensembles of long time simulations. However, with E3SM’s emphasis on computational
performance, such direct coupling is feasible for short simulations, on the order of days rather than
decades. A small set of short simulations with E3SM driving a PartMC instance in each grid cell is
sufficient to provide a large, global training data archive. Such a data archive would conceivably
span the full set of atmospheric states than an E3SM-coupled PartMC emulator would encounter,
improving its value further, for E3SM specifically.
In this section, we describe our efforts to directly couple PartMC with E3SM for the purpose of
producing a reliable source of trustworthy training data. Two preliminary tasks must be completed
and then combined to achieve the direct coupling. First, PartMC and E3SM must be coupled at the
software level, with E3SM providing the atmospheric state data required to drive a PartMC instance
in each grid cell. Second, a new transport algorithm must be added that allows nearby grid cells’
instances of PartMC to be influenced by their neighbors, so that aerosol states correctly account for
the effects of wind and advection. Finally, existing E3SM aerosol emissions simulation procedures
must also be coupled with PartMC. Our developments are kept open source in the P-CLAP fork of
E3SM [40].

 57

5.1. Software coupling

Software coupling has several objectives. First, it must provide the ability to run an instance of
a PartMC box simulation for each E3SM grid cell. Second, it must communicate atmospheric
state data (pressure, temperature, and humidity, for example) from E3SM to PartMC, to drive
the stochastic aerosol parameterizations within. For these tasks we largely follow the workflow
established by a previous effort that coupled PartMC with the regional WRF model [17]. The
PartMC aerosol state is packed by columns and levels to support a similar distributed memory
parallel layout/communication pattern to the one already employed by E3SM.

5.2. Stochastic transport

Numerical transport algorithms account for advection, by wind, of suspended particles or gases within
the atmosphere, generally called tracers. A good numerical transport algorithm will be accurate
and preserve properties such as mass conservation and prevent the development of oscillations or
new extrema due to numerical error [41]. The current transport scheme in E3SM is a high-order
accurate, property-preserving pointwise semi-Lagrangian scheme called ISLET [42, 43].
ISLET deterministically transports tracer quantities that can be considered separately from, but
advected by, the simulated air mass. Similarly to the PartMC/WRF coupling effort [17], which
adapted WRF’s deterministic finite volume transport method to the stochastic setting required by
PartMC, we must adapt the semi-Lagrangian methods employed by E3SM to support stochastic
algorithms.
We revert back to a cell-integrated semi-Lagrangian method [41], previously implemented in
E3SM, instead of the pointwise interpolation scheme [43] that it currently uses. The cell-integrated
semi-Lagrangian algorithms will allow us to compute the percentage 𝑠𝑖 𝑗 (by mass) of source cell 𝑖 that
moves to destination cell 𝑗 over the course of one time step, for all 𝑖 ∈ [1, 𝑁𝑔 ]. The percentages 𝑠𝑖 𝑗
are deterministically computed by the integrated winds over a single time step interval 𝑡 ∈ [𝑡 𝑛 , 𝑡 𝑛+1 ].
To support the stochastic PartMC algorithms, at each time step, for each 𝑖, 𝑗 pair identified by the
advection step, 𝑠𝑖 𝑗 randomly selected particles from source cell 𝑖 are moved to destination cell 𝑗.
We compute horizontal transport separately from vertical transport, since the model uses two
different discretizations and parallel decompositions for its horizontal and vertical components. The
algorithms required to compute the horizontal components 𝑝𝑖 𝑗 for all 𝑖 are complicated by several
factors. First, since winds are solution-dependent and not known a priori, and because different
grid cells may reside on different computational ranks in a distributed computation, the transport
algorithm is inherently tied to the E3SM atmosphere model’s MPI implementation. Second, E3SM
uses a different grid for physical parameterizations such as PartMC, than it does for solving its
dynamics equations. Wind data lives on the dynamics grid, and the transport algorithms supported
by E3SM are part of its dynamics package, not its physical parameterization packages. In fact,
the dynamical core of E3SM is separately implemented, in software, from all of the physical
parameterizations used by E3SM, but our work requires us to bridge the two grids and the two
software paths to perform transport on the physics grid.

6. CONCLUSIONS AND REMAINING WORK

P-CLAP developed a set of complementary methods for retaining aerosol detail while reducing
the computational and experimental barriers to its use in atmospheric modeling. The machine-
learning studies demonstrated that a compact state can recover multiple climate-relevant aerosol
diagnostics, that partial observations can constrain an ensemble of plausible aerosol states, and that
a population-linear embedding can preserve the algebra of mixing and concentration changes. The
scale-covariant coagulation model extended this representation to stable prognostic evolution within
its evaluated scenario family, while the mixture-consistency study established both the potential and
the limitations of learning an additive representation through training. The ongoing PartML work
adds stochastic, process-specific transitions and a generative particle-set decoder, with preliminary
results that distinguish repeated-application stability from ensemble calibration.
TChem-atm provides a complementary improvement to the detailed reference model. Its multiphase
chemistry interface, Kokkos implementation, and selectable stiff solvers allow the same chemistry
formulation to execute on CPUs and GPUs. The published comparisons establish close agreement
with PartMC–CAMP for a controlled chemistry and partitioning problem and show that solver
choice becomes increasingly important as particle count grows. These results support more efficient
reference calculations without requiring the aerosol state to be compressed.
The rotating-drum experiments show why simulation fidelity must also be assessed against the
measurement process and experimental configuration. In the tested low-humidity cases, sorbitol
with a rotating drum, aerosol neutralization, and pulled dilution flow gave the most consistent joint
agreement in total concentration and size distribution. Different compositions and the absence of
neutralization produced discrepancies that cannot be resolved simply by improving a numerical
solver or training a more accurate surrogate of that solver. The experimental contribution therefore
identifies both useful reference cases and physical effects that need explicit treatment or further
measurement.
The PartMC–E3SM effort addresses the spatial component of this project. The supplied solid-body-
rotation test supports first-order horizontal convergence of the developing transport method. Vertical
verification and translation into a reviewed MPI implementation remain unfinished yet provides a
concrete advance in transport verification, though does not yet constitute a coupled PartMC–E3SM
aerosol simulation.

6.1. Implications for an integrated aerosol capability

The project results support a common interface between detailed and reduced aerosol models.
Detailed populations provide training and verification data, additive latent moments provide a compact
representation for mixing and transport, learned process operators provide local approximations to

 69

selected microphysical steps, and TChem-atm supplies performance-portable chemistry where a
detailed calculation remains necessary. Controlled experiments assess which parts of the reference
physics reproduce observed population evolution.
Several distinctions should be retained when these components are combined. Exact mixing in an
encoded state does not imply exact reconstruction of the complete particle distribution. Conservation
of mass in a reference coagulation calculation does not imply conservation by a generative decoder.
A fast batched surrogate and an accelerated chemistry kernel address different workloads, so their
speedups cannot be multiplied into an end-to-end climate-model estimate. Similarly, synthetic
conditional uncertainty does not establish uncertainty calibration for field measurements outside
the training distribution. These distinctions define the evidence required for the next stage of
development.

6.2. Remaining technical work

The most immediate experimental task is to complete the PartMC methods description so that
initialization, dilution, wall loss, stochastic sampling, and measurement comparison can be
reproduced. For PartML, the input-state diagnostic audit, decoder-sampling checks, benchmark
metadata, and latent-structure analysis should be completed before drawing stronger conclusions
about mass budgets, sample-count dependence, or physical process attribution. Additional process
operators and broader environmental tests are needed for both latent dynamics approaches.
For the spatial model, the next milestone is a reproducible vertical transport test followed by a
reviewed MPI implementation that preserves the intended particle weights and global budgets. A
small coupled grid experiment should then exercise particle transport and local aerosol evolution
together, with comparisons against both analytic transport cases and detailed box-model references.
Only after these stages will it be possible to quantify the accuracy, computational cost, and
conservation properties of a complete PartMC–E3SM or latent-aerosol configuration.
Taken together, the documented work establishes a foundation for aerosol models that retain more
mixing-state information at practical computational cost. Its strongest outcome is the combination
of mathematically structured representations, verified numerical components, and experiments that
expose the limits of the physical assumptions. The remaining work is to connect these components
and demonstrate their behavior under the repeated, coupled operations required by an atmospheric
model.
