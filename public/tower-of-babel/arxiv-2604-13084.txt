Complex Orthogonal Decomposition (C.O.D.)
using Python

Abstract




Abstract

This work presents the application of the
Complex Orthogonal Decomposition (C.O.D.)
to a simple spatio-temporal signal. C.O.D. has been introduced first in the article of B. Feeny, entitled "A Complex Orthogonal Decomposition for Wave Motion Analysis"
[
4
]
, published in the
Journal of Sound and Vibration
.

The purpose of this signal analysis method is to extract spatial and temporal modes out of a signal. This approach is especially suited to deal with oscillatory signals where phase information is important and where spatial forms are unknown.

We provide two theoretical chapters presenting the main mathematical concepts behind C.O.D. and a series of example (with associated
Python
scripts) to demonstrate the efficiency of the method and some characteristical features.

I
Motivation and presentation

The main idea of Complex Orthogonal Decomposition (C.O.D.) is to analyse a signal of time and space
s(t,x)
representing a physical quantity that oscillates. To introduce it, we look at a real example that demonstrates its purpose and how it works : fish swimming.

Indeed, many fishes have oscillatory motions when they swim, with definite frequencies linked to their species, their size or if they swim in schools or not for example
[
3
]
. However, this wave motion is not trivial : if we analyse their motion using the centreline of their body, researchers have shown that this oscillatory motion is not
spatially
periodical at all. It means that the spatial form of the wave is not trivial, as we can see on Fig.
1
. (b)-(e).

Figure 1:
Figure from Chen
et al.

[
3
]
- Fish body wave curve: (a) Diagram of the fish body wave curve. The blue area is the outline
of a BCF fish, and the red line segment is the fish body wave curve. (b)-(e) Fish body wave curves of four
different species of fish. Curves of different colors represent the body midline displacement of fish at 20 equally spaced time intervals during a single tail beat cycle.

Mathematically, it means that the signal
s(t,x)
(being the amplitude of oscillation of the fish,
t
being time and
x
a position along its centerline) does not decompose well on a 2D space-time Fourier basis. Consequently, the following decomposition

s(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}A_{j}\cos(k_{j}x-\omega_{j}t+\phi_{j})

(1)

cannot be truncated at relatively low index. The space Fourier basis do not represent well the motion in this case.

In this context, C.O.D. is the appropriate tool to analyse the signal. In a sense, it allows you to have your signal decomposed as :

s(t,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}T_{k}(t)\,X_{k}(x),

(2)

with
X_{k}
being spatially orthogonal to each other. In the case of the fish, it is interesting to see if some frequencies are associated with specific unknown body motions that would enhance the efficiency of the swim. Additionally, the C.O.D. provides a tool called the travelling index, a number that quantifies how much a given mode resembles a travelling wave versus a standing wave. It is typically an indicator that can give clues on the swim efficiency
[
2
]
.

From a mathematical point of view, the C.O.D. is not that different from the classical Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (P.O.D.) in terms of conceptual ideas
[
4
]
but is not used as much in studies. This paper aims at recalling the theory concerning Complex Orthogonal Decomposition, with applicative examples, and demonstrate its efficiency.

II
Spatio-temporal signal processing and C.O.D.

Let
s(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued spatio-temporal signal, smooth in both time
t\in\Omega_{t}
and space
x\in\Omega_{x}
.
\Omega_{t}=[0,T]
, with
T\in\mathbb{\overline{R^{+}}}
and
\Omega_{x}=[x_{lim_{1}},x_{lim_{2}}]
, with
(x_{lim_{1}},x_{lim_{2}})
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The core idea of C.O.D. is to express the real analytic signal as the real part of sum of separable complex spatio-temporal modes:

s(t,x)=\Re\left(\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}a_{j}(t)\,\phi_{j}(x)\right)

(3)

where:

•

\phi_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{C}
are orthonormal spatial modes,

•

a_{j}(t)\in\mathbb{C}
are complex temporal coefficients (also called complex ortogonal coordinates, C.O.C.
[
4
]
).

To do that, we need to have an equivalent complex signal. We use the Hilbert transform to achieve this goal.

1. Complex analytic signal and Hilbert transform

Given a real-valued signal
s(t,x)
, we define its
Hilbert transform
in time at each spatial position
x
. This transform captures the oscillatory structure of the signal in a way that separates amplitude and phase.

Formally, the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}\{s(t,x)\}
is defined by convolution in time
[
6
]
:

\widetilde{s}(t,x)=\mathcal{H}\{s\}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\Omega_{t}}\frac{s(\tau,x)}{t-\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(4)

where “p.v.” denotes the Cauchy principal value.

The complex analytic signal is then:

s_{c}(t,x)=s(t,x)+i\,\widetilde{s}(t,x).

(5)

Relation to the Fourier transform :

In the frequency domain, constructing the complex signal corresponds to suppressing the negative frequency components and doubling the positive ones. This is efficiently done using the (complex) Fourier transform.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}\{s\}(f,x)=\hat{s}(f,x)
be the temporal Fourier transform of
s(t,x)
. Then the complex part of the signal can be obtained via:

\hat{s_{c}}(f,x)=\begin{cases}2\hat{s}(f,x),&f>0,\\
\hat{s}(0,x),&f=0,\\
0,&f<0.\end{cases}

(6)

The complex analytic signal
s_{c}(t,x)
is then recovered by taking the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{s_{c}}(f,x)
:

s_{c}(t,x)=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{-1}\{\hat{s_{c}}(f,x)\}.

(7)

This frequency-domain filtering approach is equivalent to performing the Hilbert transform in the time domain, and is especially useful in numerical computations. It ensures that the resulting
s_{c}(t,x)
contains only positive frequencies, making it ideal for phase and envelope analysis.

2. Modal decomposition via C.O.D.

The spatial modes
\phi_{j}(x)
are obtained by solving the eigenvalue problem of the spatial covariance operator:

\mathcal{R}[f](x)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{x_{lim_{1}}}^{x_{lim_{2}}}s_{c}(t,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})^{*}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)s_{c}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(8)

where
∗
denotes complex conjugation.

This operator is Hermitian and positive semi-definite, ensuring a complete orthonormal set of eigenfunctions.

We call :

•

\lambda_{j}^{e}
the eigenvalues (that are real and positive, because of the positive semi-definite characteristics of the operator)
1
1

1

We use the exponent
e
to avoid any confusion with
\lambda
- the spatial wavelength.
.

•

\phi_{j}
the associated eigenvectors.

The temporal coefficients
a_{j}(t)
can be retrieved by projection:

a_{j}(t)=\int_{\Omega_{x}}s_{c}(t,x)\,\phi_{j}(x)^{*}\,dx

(9)

These properties follow from the fact that the spatial modes are eigenfunctions of a Hermitian (self-adjoint) covariance operator.

3. Reconstruction of the real signal

Once the complex signal
s_{c}(t,x)
has been decomposed as

s_{c}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}a_{j}(t)\,\phi_{j}(x),

(10)

where
a_{j}(t)
are complex-valued temporal coefficients and
\phi_{j}(x)
are complex-valued spatial modes (orthonormal in
x
), the original real-valued signal
s(t,x)
is reconstructed by taking the real part:

s(t,x)=\Re\left[s_{c}(t,x)\right]=\Re\left[\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}a_{j}(t)\,\phi_{j}(x)\right].

(11)

Expanding the real part explicitly, using
a_{j}(t)=a_{j}^{\Re}(t)+ia_{j}^{\Im}(t)
and
\phi_{j}(x)=\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)+i\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)
, we get:

s(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\left[a_{j}^{\Re}(t)\,\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)-a_{j}^{\Im}(t)\,\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\right].

(12)

Expressing the sum as products of real functions:

In general, it is
not
possible to express each term
\Re[a_{j}(t)\phi_{j}(x)]
as a single product of one function of time and one function of space unless either
a_{j}(t)
or
\phi_{j}(x)
is real.

However, we can rewrite the signal as a sum of products of real functions by defining:

T_{j}^{(1)}(t):=a_{j}^{\Re}(t),\quad X_{j}^{(1)}(x):=\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x),

(13)

T_{j}^{(2)}(t):=-a_{j}^{\Im}(t),\quad X_{j}^{(2)}(x):=\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x).

(14)

Then the real signal becomes:

s(t,x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left[T_{j}^{(1)}(t)\,X_{j}^{(1)}(x)+T_{j}^{(2)}(t)\,X_{j}^{(2)}(x)\right].

(15)

By reindexing, e.g., setting
k=2j-1
and
k=2j
, one can write:

s(t,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}T_{k}(t)\,X_{k}(x),

(16)

with all
T_{k}(t)
and
X_{k}(x)
real-valued functions. This expresses the signal as a sum of separable real modes.

4. Modal energies

The energy associated with each mode is given by the eigenvalues:

\lambda_{j}^{e}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|a_{j}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(17)

Modes are sorted so that
\lambda_{1}^{e}\geq\lambda_{2}^{e}\geq\cdots
, with the first few often capturing most of the signal energy. Using Parseval identity, we also have the link with the Power Spectral Density :

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|a_{j}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2\pi}{T}\int_{f_{min}}^{f_{max}}|\hat{a_{j}}|(\omega)^{2}\mathrm{d}\omega

(18)

5. Orthogonality and norm of the spatial modes

The spatial modes
\phi_{j}(x)
obtained through Complex Orthogonal Decomposition (C.O.D.) form an
orthonormal basis
in the space of square-integrable complex functions over the spatial domain.

Orthogonality:

For all
j\neq k
, the modes are orthogonal:

\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{x}}\phi_{j}(x)^{*}\phi_{k}(x)\,dx=0

(19)

with
∗
denoting the complex conjugate of the quantity.

Normalization:

Each mode is normalized to unit norm:

\|\phi_{j}\|^{2}=\int|\phi_{j}(x)|^{2}\,dx=1

(20)

This orthonormality ensures that the modal decomposition

s_{c}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}a_{j}(t)\,\phi_{j}(x)

(21)

is
unique
.

5. Travelling index

Each spatial mode
\mathbf{\phi}_{j}(x)
is a complex-valued function defined over the spatial domain
\Omega_{x}
. The goal of the travelling index is to quantify how much a given mode resembles a travelling wave versus a standing wave. This is achieved by defining a
travelling index

\alpha_{j}\in[0,1]
.

Let the
j
-th mode be written in terms of its real and imaginary components:

\mathbf{\phi}_{j}(x)=\Re[\mathbf{\phi}_{j}(x)]+i\Im[\mathbf{\phi}_{j}(x)]=\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)+i\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x),

(22)

We compute the complex matrix
W_{j}
and its Gram matrix
G_{j}
:

W_{j}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)&\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 2}

(23)

G_{j}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)\rangle&\langle\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\rangle\\
\langle\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)\rangle&\langle\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}

(24)

Then we define:

\alpha_{j}=\frac{1}{\kappa(W_{j})}=\frac{\sigma_{\min}}{\sigma_{\max}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{min}^{G}}{\lambda_{max}^{G}}}

(25)

where
\kappa(W)
is the condition number of the matrix
W
, and
\sigma_{\min}
,
\sigma_{\max}
are the smallest and largest singular values. In this context, the singular values of
W_{j}
are the square roots of
G_{j}
eigenvalues
\lambda^{G}

[
5
]
.

We use the quadratic formulas for the eigenvalues being the roots of the characteristic polynomial of the matrix since
G_{j}
matrixes has a 2 by 2 size
[
8
]
. The travelling index
\alpha_{j}
is then computed as :

\alpha_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{a+b-\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}{a+b+\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}}

(26)

•

\alpha_{j}=1
: the mode corresponds to a perfectly travelling wave (circular trace in the complex plane).

•

\alpha_{j}=0
: the mode is purely standing (real or imaginary only).

The travelling index thus provides a physically interpretable scalar measure of wave behavior within each mode extracted by C.O.D.

III
Discrete equivalent for C.O.D.

In reality, we often work with discrete signals coming from experiment. We consider a discretely sampled spatio-temporal signal represented by the matrix
\underline{\underline{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{x}}
, where:

•

N_{t}
is the number of time steps,

•

N_{x}
is the number of spatial points,

•

each row of
\underline{\underline{S}}
corresponds to a fixed time
t_{n}
,

•

each column corresponds to a fixed spatial location
x_{j}
.

In the following,
j\in[1,N_{x}]
and
n\in[1,N_{t}]
.

1. Complex signal matrix via Hilbert transform

To form the analytic complex signal, we apply a discrete Hilbert transform in the temporal direction (row-wise). For each spatial point
x_{j}
, we define the complex signal :

(\underline{s_{c}}){{}_{j}}=\underline{s}_{j}+i\,\mathcal{H}_{d}[\underline{s}_{j}],

(27)

where:

•

\underline{s}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times 1}
is the time signal at location
x_{j}
(i.e., column
j
of
\underline{\underline{S}}
),

•

\mathcal{H}_{d}
denotes the discrete Hilbert transform.

This yields the complex signal matrix
\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{x}}
:

\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}=\underline{\underline{S}}+i\,\widetilde{\underline{\underline{S}}},

(28)

where
\widetilde{\underline{\underline{S}}}
contains the Hilbert transforms of the columns of
\underline{\underline{S}}
. To get it, we use the same relation with the Fourier transform described in the continuous case, instead here one should use the Fast Fourier Transform to have discrete equivalent.

Relation to the FFT :

Let
\text{FFT}\{\underline{\underline{S}}\}=\widehat{\underline{\underline{S}}}
be the discrete temporal Fourier transform of the matrix
\underline{\underline{S}}
2
2

2

As written here, the complex FFT has to be shifted so that the frequency domain between
[-\frac{N_{t}}{2T},\frac{N_{t}}{2T}]
is mapped in order by
n\in[0,N_{t}]
. Then the complex part of the signal can be obtained via the following matrix:

(\widehat{\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}})_{nj}=\begin{cases}2\widehat{\underline{\underline{S}}}_{nj},&n>\lfloor N_{t}/2\rfloor,\\
\widehat{\underline{\underline{S}}}_{nj},&n=\lfloor N_{t}/2\rfloor,\\
0,&n<\lfloor N_{t}/2\rfloor.\end{cases}

(29)

The complex matrix
\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}
is then recovered by taking the complex inverse Fourier transform of
\widehat{\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}}
:

\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}=\text{FFT}^{-1}\{\widehat{\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}}\}.

(30)

2. Modal decomposition via C.O.D.

We now transpose
\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}
, resulting in:

\underline{\underline{Z}}=\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{t}},

(31)

We define the temporal covariance matrix:

\underline{\underline{R}}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\underline{\underline{Z}}\underline{\underline{Z}}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}},

(32)

which is Hermitian and positive semi-definite.
\underline{\underline{Z}}^{\dagger}
is the hermitian transpose of
\underline{\underline{Z}}
, so that
\underline{\underline{Z}}^{\dagger}=(\underline{\underline{Z}}^{\ast})^{T}

We solve the eigenvalue problem:

\underline{\underline{R}}\underline{\phi}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{e}\underline{\phi}_{j},

(33)

yielding:
- eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{e}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
representing the modal energies,
- eigenvectors
\underline{\phi}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times 1}
representing the spatial modes.

The temporal coefficients
\underline{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N_{t}}
are obtained via projection:

\underline{a}_{j}=\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{Z}}

(34)

or, equivalently, stacking the projections gives:

\underline{\underline{A}}=\underline{\underline{\Phi}}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{Z}},

(35)

where
\underline{\underline{\Phi}}=[\underline{\phi}_{1},\underline{\phi}_{2},\dots]\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}}
is the matrix of spatial modes and
\underline{\underline{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{t}}
contains the modal time series.

Thus, the dataset can be reconstructed as a sum of contributions:

\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}=\underline{\underline{A}}^{T}\underline{\underline{\Phi}}^{T}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\underline{a_{j}}^{T}\underline{\phi_{j}}^{T}

(36)

This decomposition is energy-preserving due to the orthonormality of the modes.

3. Reconstruction of the real matrix

To recover the real-valued signal
\underline{\underline{S}}
, take the real part of the transpose of
\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}
:

\underline{\underline{S}}\approx\Re[\underline{\underline{Z}}^{T}]=\Re[\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}].

(37)

4. Modal energies

Each eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
quantifies the energy of mode
j
:

\lambda_{j}^{e}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\|\underline{a}_{j}\|^{2}

(38)

The norm
\|\cdot\|
refers to the standard Euclidean norm (or
L^{2}
-norm) applied to complex vectors, defined by:

\|\underline{a}_{j}\|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}|a_{j}(t_{n})|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}|a_{jn}|^{2},

(39)

Modes are typically sorted by decreasing
\lambda^{e}_{j}
to identify the most energetic structures.

5. Orthogonality and norm of the spatial modes

We have the same discrete equivalent properties as for the continuous C.O.D.

Orthogonality:

The spatial modes
\underline{\phi}_{j}
form an orthonormal set in
\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times 1}
. For
j\neq k
, we have:

\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\underline{\phi}_{k}=0

(40)

Normalization:

Each mode is normalized to unit norm:

\|\underline{\phi}_{j}\|^{2}=\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\underline{\phi}_{j}=1

(41)

These properties arise from the Hermitian nature of the matrix
\underline{\underline{R}}
, and they ensure that the modes form a complete orthonormal basis for the spatial structure of the data.

6. Travelling Index

We write the
j
-th spatial mode as:

\underline{\phi}_{j}=\Re[\underline{\phi}_{j}]+i\Im[\underline{\phi}_{j}]=\underline{\phi_{j}^{\Re}}+i\underline{\phi_{j}^{\Im}}

(42)

We compute the complex matrix
\underline{\underline{W}}_{j}
and its Gram matrix
\underline{\underline{G}}_{j}
:

\underline{\underline{W}}_{j}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\phi_{j}^{\Re}}&\underline{\phi_{j}^{\Im}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times 2}

(43)

\underline{\underline{G}}_{j}(x)=\underline{\underline{W}}_{j}^{T}\underline{\underline{W}}_{j}

(44)

\underline{\underline{G}}_{j}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re})^{T}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re}&(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re})^{T}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im}\\
(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im})^{T}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re}&(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im})^{T}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}

(45)

Then we define:

\alpha_{j}=\frac{1}{\kappa(W_{j})}=\frac{\sigma_{\min}}{\sigma_{\max}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{min}^{G}}{\lambda_{max}^{G}}}

(46)

where
\kappa(W)
is the condition number of the matrix
W
, and
\sigma_{\min}
,
\sigma_{\max}
are the smallest and largest singular values. In this context, the singular values of
W_{j}
are the square roots of
G_{j}
’s eigenvalues
\lambda^{G}

[
5
]
.

We use the quadratic formulas for the eigenvalues being the roots of the characteristic polynomial of the matrix since
G_{j}
matrixes has a 2 by 2 size
[
8
]
. The travelling index
\alpha_{j}
is then computed as :

\alpha_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{a+b-\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}{a+b+\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}}

(47)

•

\alpha_{j}=1
: the mode corresponds to a perfectly travelling wave (circular trace in the complex plane).

•

\alpha_{j}=0
: the mode is purely standing (real or imaginary only).

This discrete formulation matches the practical implementation used in numerical algorithms and allows direct comparison between theoretical and computational characterizations of wave motion.

IV
Example n°1 : water waves in a tank

As an example, we’ll look at the motion of a fluid’s free surface—in simple terms, waves. We simulate fluid motion in a rectangular tank of width
L=400\,\mathrm{mm}
and water height
h=100\,\mathrm{mm}
. The surface motion (or wave) is described as the superposition of two standing or travelling wave modes, whose characteristics (amplitude, frequency, wavelength) are derived from the linear Airy wave theory
[
7
]
. In particular, we have the dispersion relation of the waves that link the pulsations and the wavelengths :

\omega_{n}^{2}=gk_{n}\tanh(k_{n}h)

(48)

with, in the case of sloshing the wavenumber being
[
7
]
:

k_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{n}}=\frac{n\pi}{L}

(49)

With this theoretical case, we aim at finding back what would give a 2D spatio-temporal FFT since here both space and time can be decomposed onto Fourier basis.

1. Signal Construction

The signal
s(x,t)
is defined as:

s(x,t)=s_{1}(x,t)+s_{2}(x,t)

(50)

with:

s_{i}(x,t)=A_{i}\left[\sin(\omega_{i}t)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{i}}\right)+\alpha_{i}\cos(\omega_{i}t)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{i}}\right)\right]

(51)

where:

•

x\in[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}]
,
t\in[0,T]
.

•

\lambda_{i}
are the wavelengths,
\omega_{i}
the angular frequencies.

•

\alpha_{1}\in[-1,1]
controls whether the first wave is standing or travelling. Here we only modify this parameter for the first mode. We will demonstrate that it is the travelling index of the first mode.

•

A_{i}
are amplitudes.

The signal is discretized in time and space, forming the matrix
\mathbf{S}
of size
N_{t}\times N_{x}
, with time samples along the rows and spatial samples along the columns.

We use numerically for this example:

•

L=400
mm,
A_{1}=15
,
A_{2}=4
(amplitudes in arbitrary units).

•

Mode
n_{1}=1
and
n_{2}=3
(sloshing modes).

•

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
(both components are stationnary)

•

N_{t}=1000
,
N_{x}=250
.

This example is derived in Python in

test_COD_ex_1.py
.

2. Some theoretical insights

We consider only
s_{1}(x,t)
to show the general idea of C.O.D., because here
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are orthogonal and well separated in frequency, which implies linearity of the C.O.D.

s_{1}(x,t)=A_{1}\left[\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)+\alpha_{1}\cos(\omega_{1}t)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\right]

(52)

This can be recognized as a combination that forms a travelling wave when
\alpha_{1}=1
or
-1
and a standing wave when
\alpha_{1}=0
. Our goal is to analyze the spatial structure after applying the Hilbert transform in time and relate the resulting complex field to the travelling index. Doing so, we will also compute the complex spatial component
\phi_{1}(x)
and the complex temporal coefficient
a_{1}(t)
so that :

s_{1}(x,t)=\Re[a_{1}(t)\phi_{1}(x)]

(53)

To achieve the decomposition, one need to compute the Hilbert transform
\widetilde{s}(x,t)
with respect to time
t
to obtain the complex signal:

s_{c,1}(x,t)=s_{1}(x,t)+i\,\widetilde{s_{1}}(x,t)

(54)

Recall the Hilbert transform of sinusoids in time:

\mathcal{H}[\sin(\omega t)]=-\cos(\omega t),\quad\mathcal{H}[\cos(\omega t)]=\sin(\omega t)

(55)

Applying linearity, we get:

\widetilde{s_{1}}(x,t)=-\cos(\omega_{1}t)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)+\alpha_{1}\sin(\omega_{1}t)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)

(56)

Therefore, the complex analytic signal is:

\displaystyle s_{c,1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=s_{1}(x,t)+i\widetilde{s_{1}}(x,t)

(57)

\displaystyle=A_{1}\left[\sin(\omega_{1}t)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)+\alpha_{1}\cos(\omega_{1}t)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+iA_{1}\left[-\cos(\omega_{1}t)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)+\alpha_{1}\sin(\omega_{1}t)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\right]

Group terms by
\sin(\omega_{1}t)
and
\cos(\omega_{1}t)
:

s_{c,1}(x,t)=A_{1}\left[\sin(\omega_{1}t)\bigg(\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)+i\alpha_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\bigg)+\cos(\omega_{1}t)\bigg(\alpha_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)-i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\bigg)\right]

(58)

Using Euler’s formula for the time part:

e^{i\omega_{1}t}=\cos(\omega_{1}t)+i\sin(\omega_{1}t)

(59)

we can rewrite the complex analytic signal as:

s_{c,1}(x,t))=A_{1}e^{i\omega_{1}t}\left[\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)-i\alpha_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)\right]

(60)

Therefore,

s_{c,1}(x,t)=a_{1}(t)\phi_{1}(x),

(61)

with

a_{1}(t)=A_{1}e^{i\omega_{1}t},\quad\phi_{1}(x)=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right)-i\alpha_{1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\right).

(62)

This confirms the decomposition of the complex analytic signal into a temporal coefficient and spatial mode encoding the travelling behavior through
\alpha_{1}
.

We of course have :

s_{1}(x,t)=\Re(a_{1}(t)\phi_{1}(x))

(63)

Reasoning by linearity will allow to do this also for
s_{2}
and therefore construct the full complex orthogonal decomposition of
s

Travelling Index :

We have as the spatial components of the component n°1 (same analysis can be done for component n°2):

\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)=\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr),\quad\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)=-\alpha_{1}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr)

We compute then the Gram matrix, being

G_{j}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)\rangle&\langle\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\rangle\\
\langle\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Re}(x)\rangle&\langle\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x),\phi_{j}^{\Im}(x)\rangle\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\
c&b\end{bmatrix}

Over one spatial period
\lambda_{1}
, compute

a=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}\sin^{2}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr)\,dx=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2},

(66)

b=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}\bigl(\alpha_{1}\cos\tfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\bigr)^{2}\,dx=\alpha_{1}^{2}\,\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2},

(67)

c=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}-\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr)\,\alpha_{1}\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr)\,dx=-\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}\tfrac{1}{2}\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\pi x}{\lambda_{1}}\Bigr)\,dx=0.

a
and
b

(70)

(71)

We demonstrated that

Let us consider the general form:

The Hilbert transform is:

\mathcal{H}[f](t)\approx-e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega t)

Appendice: non-uniform space grid

In experimental situations, the spatial sampling points are not always evenly spaced. In this part we extend the C.O.D. to non-uniform grids. To do so, we therefore consider a discretely sampled spatio-temporal signal represented by the matrix
\underline{\underline{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{x}}
, where:

•

N_{t}
is the number of time steps,

•

N_{x}
is the number of spatial points,

•

each row of
\underline{\underline{S}}
still corresponds to a fixed time
t_{n}
,

•

each column still corresponds to a fixed spatial location
x_{j}
, but the grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{x}}
is not necessarily uniform.

To correctly approximate continuous inner products and energy integrals, each spatial point
x_{j}
is associated with a positive quadrature weight
w_{j}
that represents the local spacing:

w_{j}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{2},&j=1,\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{x_{j+1}-x_{j-1}}{2},&2\leq j\leq N_{x}-1,\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{x_{N_{x}}-x_{N_{x}-1}}{2},&j=N_{x}.\end{cases}

(103)

The weights are collected into a diagonal matrix:

\underline{\underline{W}}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{N_{x}}).

1. Modal decomposition via C.O.D. on a non-uniform grid

We still construct the same way the analytic signal (see III.1), as well as the analytic signal matrix:

\underline{\underline{Z}}=\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{t}}.

(104)

To incorporate the non-uniform spacing, the spatial inner product between two complex spatial vectors

\underline{u},\underline{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}}

is defined as a weighted discrete quadrature:

\langle\underline{u},\underline{v}\rangle_{w}=\underline{u}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}w_{j}\,u_{j}^{\ast}v_{j}.

(105)

The corresponding temporal covariance matrix is:

\underline{\underline{R_{w}}}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\,\underline{\underline{Z}}\,\underline{\underline{W}}\,\underline{\underline{Z}}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}}.

(106)

The weighting matrix
\underline{\underline{W}}
ensures that spatial correlations are evaluated consistently with the non-uniform grid. The matrix
\underline{\underline{R_{w}}}
remains Hermitian and positive semi-definite.

We then solve the generalized eigenvalue problem:

\underline{\underline{R_{w}}}\,\underline{\phi}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{e}\,\underline{\phi}_{j},

(107)

yielding:

•

eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{e}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
(modal energies),

•

eigenvectors
\underline{\phi}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{x}\times 1}
(spatial modes).

The temporal coefficients are obtained by projection using the weighted inner product:

\underline{a}_{j}=\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\underline{Z}},

(108)

or equivalently,

\underline{\underline{A}}=\underline{\underline{\Phi}}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\underline{Z}},

(109)

with
\underline{\underline{\Phi}}=[\underline{\phi}_{1},\underline{\phi}_{2},\dots]
.

The reconstruction then reads:

\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}=(\underline{\underline{A}}^{T}\underline{\underline{\Phi}}^{T})\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}\underline{a}_{j}^{T}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{T}.

(110)

2. Modal energies and orthogonality

Each eigenvalue quantifies the weighted energy of the corresponding mode:

\lambda_{j}^{e}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\|\underline{a}_{j}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}|a_{j}(t_{n})|^{2}.

(111)

The spatial modes are orthonormal under the weighted inner product:

\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\phi}_{k}=\begin{cases}1,&j=k,\\
0,&j\neq k.\end{cases}

(112)

This ensures that energy is conserved consistently with the continuous-space formulation:

\int|s_{c}(x,t)|^{2}\,dx\;\approx\;\underline{s_{c}}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{s_{c}}.

3. Reconstruction of the real-valued field

To recover the physical real-valued field, we still take the real part:

\underline{\underline{S}}\approx\Re[\,\underline{\underline{S_{c}}}\,]=\Re[\,\underline{\underline{Z}}^{T}\,].

(113)

4. Travelling index on non-uniform grids

The computation of the travelling index for each mode remains unchanged, except that all spatial inner products involved in the Gram matrix are now weighted:

\underline{\underline{G}}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re})^{T}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re}&(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re})^{T}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im}\\[4.0pt]
(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im})^{T}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Re}&(\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im})^{T}\underline{\underline{W}}\underline{\phi}_{j}^{\Im}\end{bmatrix}.

(114)

The travelling index is still given by:

\alpha_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{a+b-\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}{a+b+\sqrt{(a-b)^{2}+4c^{2}}}},

(115)

with
a,b,c
computed from the weighted Gram matrix entries.

In summary, the non-uniform grid formulation preserves all properties of the standard discrete C.O.D., provided that the spatial weighting matrix
\underline{\underline{W}}
is systematically applied to every spatial inner product and correlation.
