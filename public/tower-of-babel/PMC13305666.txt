Changes in Arterial–Alveolar Oxygen Gradient and Mixed Venous Oxygen Saturations in Mechanical Thrombectomy for Pulmonary Embolism: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Adam D. Walsh, James Warner, Anna Dunn, Kabir Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLarge bore mechanical thrombectomy (LBMT) is an effective therapy for intermediate‐high risk PE, however, objective physiological endpoints to guide intraprocedural decision‐making remain poorly defined. The alveolar–arterial (A–a) oxygen gradient, mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2), and cardiac index (CI) may provide clinically useful insights during LBMT. We sought to determine if changes in alveolar–arterial gradients and estimated cardiac index were sufficiently consistent to be used to inform clinical decision making. This was a single‐center, prospective, pre–post cohort study of patients undergoing LBMT for intermediate‐high risk PE. Arterial and mixed venous blood gases were aspirated immediately before and after thrombectomy. CI was estimated via the indirect Fick method. Primary outcomes were changes in A–a gradient and mixed venous oxygen saturations; secondary outcomes included estimated CI and Modified Borg Dyspnea Scale. Sensitivity analyses excluded physiologically implausible outliers and accounted for intraprocedural hemoglobin changes. Twenty‐seven patients were included. LBMT was associated with a nonsignificant reduction in A–a gradient (−38.5 ± 129.9 mmHg, p = 0.052), which became significant after exclusion of outliers (−41.5 ± 62.9 mmHg, p = 0.0096). Mixed SvO2 decreased in most patients by 3.8% ± 7.3% (p = 0.015), corresponding to a modest reduction in estimated CI (−0.16 ± 0.67 L/min/m2, p = 0.032) which persisted after controlling for hemoglobin loss. Dyspnea scores improved in most patients with paired measurements. Acute improvements in oxygenation efficiency were reflected by reductions in A–a gradient, while paradoxical reductions in SvO2 and estimated CI suggest altered oxygen extraction ratio post‐procedure. These parameters may serve as novel intraprocedural physiological markers to complement thrombectomy endpoints.

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and the third leading cause of cardiovascular death after acute myocardial infarction and stroke [1]. In Australia, venous thromboembolism and PE have an annual incidence of 0.83 per 1000 population [2].

High and intermediate risk PE are associated with 30‐day mortality rates of 30% and 8%, respectively, however up to a third of patients have persisting dyspnea for months to years after the acute insult [3, 4]. Acute management ranges from anticoagulation to systemic thrombolysis or catheter‐directed therapies depending on risk stratification, typically using 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines [5]. Large bore mechanical thrombectomy (LBMT) has shown promising results in intermediate‐high and high risk PE, whereby a large caliber catheter is passed into the pulmonary arteries under fluoroscopic guidance in order to directly aspirate thrombi [6, 7, 8]. Patients may be lightly sedated in combination with local anesthesia at the femoral access site to avoid the risks of inducing general anesthesia in the presence of acute right ventricular dysfunction [7]. Systemic thrombolysis is generally reserved for hemodynamically unstable patients as it can be rapidly administered but carries a risk of catastrophic hemorrhage, particularly intracranial hemorrhage [9].

Emerging evidence such as the STORM‐PE trial suggests that LBMT may be superior in improving right ventricular function in intermediate‐high risk patients compared to anticoagulation alone [8]. The FlowTriever system for LBMT has been shown to have a low rate of major adverse events and improves mean pulmonary artery pressures [6]. However, decisions regarding conclusion of the procedure remains reliant on clinical assessment and fluoroscopy without objective physiological endpoints. Procedural duration is influenced by thrombus volume, pulmonary artery pressures, vital signs, symptomatic relief, suspected chronicity of thrombus, and anatomical location (central vs. peripheral) [10]. Pulmonary artery pressures may not return to normal values in patients with preexisting pulmonary disease and estimate baseline values are frequently unavailable. Prolonged procedures may be associated with cardiac injury or hemoptysis [6]. Objective assessment of right ventricular function and cardiac output usually requires invasive pulmonary artery catheterization, however, mixed venous blood can be aspirated from the thrombectomy catheter without deviation from procedural protocol allowing estimation of cardiac output using the indirect Fick method assuming fixed oxygen consumption [11].

Previous studies have shown a linear correlation between alveolar–arterial gradient and severity of PE in patients without pre‐existing cardiopulmonary disease [12]. The first study objective was to determine if calculation of the alveolar–arterial gradient could serve a useful tool in risk stratification and clinical decision making. Given recent evidence suggesting significant differences in cardiac index and mixed venous oxygen saturations, and the relative ease of intra‐procedural sampling, the second objective was to examine changes in these parameters before and after thrombectomy.

Methods

Study Design

This was an exploratory single‐center, prospective, single‐arm, pre–post cohort study. The study was designed to evaluate the acute physiological impact of large‐bore mechanical thrombectomy on oxygenation and hemodynamic parameters. Ethical approval was granted by the Barwon Health Human Ethics Committee.

Study Setting and Population

The study was conducted at University Hospital Geelong in Victoria, Australia. Patients were recruited between May 21, 2025 and December 12, 2025. Patients were identified through activation of a multidisciplinary Pulmonary Embolism Response Team. The Inari FlowTriever was utilized for all procedures and patients are routinely admitted to the intensive care unit post‐procedure.

All patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy for intermediate‐high risk pulmonary emboli at [Pulmonary Embolism Response Team] were eligible for inclusion in the study. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete medical records or did not have a paired arterial or mixed venous sample recorded, which would include any intraprocedural deaths. Patients were not excluded on the basis of pre‐existing cardiopulmonary disease to reflect the typical patient population undergoing thrombectomy. All patients undergoing thrombectomy are routinely scheduled for outpatient follow‐up at 4 weeks following hospital discharge.

Study Variables and Outcomes

Primary outcomes were changes in alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient and mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2). Secondary outcomes were changes in estimated cardiac index (CI) and changes in Modified Borg Dyspnea Scale (1–10). A fixed VO2 of 125 mL/min/m2 was assumed consistent with existing literature that utilizes the indirect Fick method (Equation 1) [13].

(1) CI=VO213.1∗Hb∗(SaO2−SvO2).

Descriptive variables collected included age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), use of thrombolysis or intubation, intensive care unit length of stay, and total hospital length of stay.

Data Collection and Measurement

Arterial blood gases were obtained via radial arterial catheterization prior to and after conclusion of the procedure for patient hemoglobin concentration, arterial oxygen, and carbon dioxide tension. Where clinically feasible, fraction of inspired oxygen remained constant for at least 10 min prior to collection and labeling of the ABG to ensure equilibrium between alveolar and arterial oxygen tension. Mixed venous blood gases were obtained from aspiration at the time of FlowTriever insertion into the pulmonary artery and at the conclusion of the procedure. Data were collected prospectively by the respiratory registrar at the time of procedure using a structured questionnaire and prelabeled blood gas syringe packs.

Method of delivery of oxygen was recorded as either room air, low flow or high flow nasal cannula, Hudson mask, non‐rebreather mask, endotracheal tube, or noninvasive ventilation. Where low flow devices were used, the fraction of inspired oxygen was estimated using a standardized scale (1 L/min = 0.24, increasing by 0.04 per liter to a maximum of 0.60) [14]. Alveolar–arterial gradients were calculated as continuous variables using the alveolar gas equation (Equation 2) with standard assumptions (atmospheric pressure 760 mmHg, water vapor pressure 47 mmHg, respiratory quotient 0.8).

Bias Mitigation

Hemoglobin concentrations were recorded from arterial blood gases consistently to avoid variance in comparisons between different instruments. All samples were processed using the same blood gas analyzer. A potential source of systematic bias was reliance on paper records of inspired fraction of oxygen and timing of blood gas results where accuracy is likely to reduce in the presence of more severe or unstable cases due to the high stress clinical environment. This would be expected to increase the variance of results and reduce statistical power without introducing directional bias.

Study Size

Power analysis was performed a priori. In the absence of prior literature, an expected mean difference in A–a gradient of 20 mmHg with a standard deviation of 30 mmHg was estimated, producing a sample of 20 patients which would provide 80% power at an α level of 0.05 with a paired t‐test. Anticipating a high attrition rate due to paper records, the study period was chosen to identify > 30 cases based on the procedural frequency.

Statistical Methods

Quantitative variables were analyzed as continuous data to maximize statistical power. Normality was assessed visually and by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used as appropriate. Due to skewed distributions, nonparametric binomial (exact) 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding values outside 1.5 times the interquartile range to account for physiologically implausible changes. Spurious values of recorded FiO2, particularly where insufficient time was allowed time for equilibration before blood gas sampling (e.g., transient administration of 100% oxygen for transfer), would be expected to cause large variability in estimated alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient.

To adjust for changes in hemoglobin concentration due to intraprocedural blood loss, a linear regression model was produced using hemoglobin difference as a predictor of change in mixed venous oxygen saturation. Model assumptions, including homoscedasticity and normality of residuals, were assessed using a residual‐versus‐fitted plot and a Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical significance was defined as a p value threshold of < 0.05. Analysis was performed using Stata software (StataNow/BE 19.5).

These were likely attributable to incorrect documentation of, or rapidly changing, fraction of inspired oxygen which would significantly affect estimated alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient and result in implausibly large differences in paired values. Discussion

Key Results

This pilot study identified several physiological changes that may be relevant to intraprocedural decision making in LBMT. After outlier exclusion, LBMT was associated with reductions in the alveolar–arterial gradient in intermediate‐high risk PE patients, which is the first time this parameter has been evaluated in a mechanical thrombectomy cohort. However, large variance in oxygen gradients and their changes limits immediate clinical utility, as accurate recording of inspired oxygen concentrations and blood gas timing is challenging in critically ill patients. The lack of statistical significance in the main analysis invites cautious interpretation of the arterial–alveolar gradient result. Notably, mixed central venous oxygen saturations decreased in the majority of patients, and thus the estimated cardiac index. This effect was significant after controlling for hemoglobin loss and contrasts prior studies.

Yuriditsky and colleagues demonstrated in a cohort of 97 mechanical thrombectomy patients that cardiac index may be reduced without meeting clinical criteria for shock, and that the central venous‐arterial carbon dioxide gradient could act as a surrogate marker for cardiac index calculated by the indirect Fick method [15]. Thrombectomy was associated with a significant improvement in cardiac index (mean difference 0.47 L/min/m2) and mixed venous O2 saturations (mean difference 5%). Improvements in estimated cardiac index were also demonstrated in the larger FLASH cohort although the effect was confined to patients with depressed cardiac indices (< 2 L/min/m2) [6]. In the EXTRACT‐PE study, an improvement in right‐to‐left ventricular ratio via computed tomography angiography was demonstrated; however, this was assessed over a 48 h interval rather than in the acute procedural phase [16].

Several physiological mechanisms may account for the observed changes in mixed venous oxygen saturations. Changes in mixed venous oxygen saturations reflect changes in the oxygen extraction ratio of all tissues and, when arterial oxygen saturations are maintained near 100% by supplemental oxygen, are unlikely to be affected by lung pathology. Reductions in SvO2 should therefore reflect either a reduced cardiac output or increased systemic oxygen extraction. Given the established effect thrombectomy has on pulmonary artery pressures by unloading right ventricular afterload, reductions in cardiac output appear unlikely; however published data on objective changes in cardiac output via thermodilution techniques remain lacking. Changes in oxygen extraction may reflect redistribution of blood to previously vasoconstricted tissue beds during a state of severe shock and a relief of “oxygen debt,” which has been described in populations with hemorrhagic shock and correlates with subsequent organ dysfunction [17]. Correlating changes in SvO2 with serum lactate may be a useful marker to determine if oxygen debt plays a role in worsening SvO2 in the immediate post‐procedure period. Reperfusion injury of ischemic pulmonary tissue releasing inflammatory cytokines into the systemic circulation may also contribute to increased metabolic demand and oxygen extraction ratio of peripheral tissues. Considering a reduction in markers of a systemic inflammatory response such as heart rate was demonstrated in the FLASH cohort, this mechanism appears less likely [6].

A key advantage of this study over large all‐comer registries such as FLASH and PEERLESS is the single‐center design. The absence of clinical standards on duration and conclusion of mechanical thrombectomy introduces significant heterogeneity with variance of local protocols [7]. Local protocols at our center have been described by Dunn and colleagues and the use of consistent location, staff, and equipment may reduce the influence of unmeasured confounders [10].

From a clinical perspective, assessing the appropriate level of patient improvement before procedural termination remains challenging. Angiographic clot burden and pulmonary artery pressures are easily obtained and remain the most commonly utilized measures, however, these may not accurately reflect true physiological improvement in the setting of severe right ventricular dysfunction and uncertain thrombus age. The findings in this study suggest that patients undergo acute changes in both their alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient and their systemic oxygen extraction ratio, which can be readily quantified at the bedside and may be used as a marker of resolving subclinical shock. Given the large observed variability and exploratory nature of these findings, further validation in a larger cohort is required before any integration into procedural decision‐making.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, there was a high exclusion rate, which may be attributed to reliance on written data collection methods in a high acuity clinical environment. Rapidly changing oxygen requirements are difficult to correlate precisely with timing of arterial blood gas aspiration and may take up to 10 min for oxygen tension to equilibrate with inspired fraction of oxygen [18]. More severe cases necessitating urgent intervention may have been excluded preferentially, however, review of excluded patients did not reveal a consistent pattern of greater illness severity. A small sample size precluded meaningful sensitivity analyses for comparison to the FLASH cohort in patients with a reduced baseline cardiac index. The sampling method was effectively a form of convenience sampling due to the exploratory nature of the study and time constraints associated with studying an infrequent procedure at this center.

Estimation of CI by the indirect Fick method makes a number of assumptions which may not be valid in this population. The assumed oxygen consumption value of 125 mL/min/m2 assumes an adult sitting at rest, whereas many patients undergoing the procedure have significant physiological derangement, such as compensatory tachycardia or systemic inflammatory response from infarcted pulmonary tissue. While absolute cardiac index values may be systematically misestimated, pre‐ and post‐procedural values would be expected to be affected proportionally and preserve the direction of effect. Cardiac output indexed to body surface area was not calculated to avoid introducing further assumptions.

Dyspnea scale changes may have been affected by anesthetic medications intraprocedurally. Of note, no patients reported a worsened dyspnea scale. These results were consistent with the FLASH cohort although a different scale was utilized (modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale) [6].

Additionally, there was no control arm in this study. Contemporary clinical practice has increasingly favored intervention over anticoagulation alone since the introduction of thrombectomy considering the established safety profile and demonstrated benefit [7]. Our center does not provide catheter‐directed thrombolysis as a direct comparator therapy. Considering these limitations, the findings of this exploratory should be regarded as hypothesis generating and interpreted within the methodological constraints outlined.

Current practice of basing mechanical thrombectomy endpoints on visual clot burden and mean pulmonary artery pressures is subject to multiple confounders such as age of clot and presence of right ventricular failure. This study demonstrates for the first time that there is a significant change in alveolar–arterial gradients for intermediate‐high risk patients undergoing LBMT that may be used in conjunction with current methods of determining a procedure endpoint. Paradoxical changes in mixed venous saturations and estimated cardiac index potentially reflect increased oxygen extraction of previously hypoperfused tissue beds, which may represent a resolution of a shock state in the acute phase and signal procedural success. These markers are rapidly calculated at the bedside using standard blood gas analyzers during thrombectomy without necessitating additional invasive procedures.

The ability to generalize study findings is limited by the single‐center design and the significant attrition rate. The reduction in mixed venous saturations and cardiac index supports a potential difference in response to thrombectomy depending on baseline cardiac index, considering previous studies have shown no difference in the overall population but an improvement when the baseline is reduced. Larger studies would be required to accurately quantify this difference and likely necessitate a multicenter design in Australia due to the infrequency of the procedure.
