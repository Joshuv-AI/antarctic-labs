Difficult intravenous access (DIVA) in the adult population: an umbrella review
Authors: Alba Torné-Ruiz, Maria Delgado, Sara Moreno, Alex Corral-Núñez, Tania Cemeli, Judith Roca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo synthesise evidence from systematic reviews on difficult intravenous access (DIVA) in adults, focusing on definitions, diagnostic criteria, risk factors and clinical interventions to guide clinical practice and health policy.DesignUmbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses.SettingAny clinical setting involving adult patients requiring peripheral venous access (including hospital, emergency and outpatient care).Data sourcesA systematic search was performed in PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane, Scopus and Web of Science in July 2025.Eligibility criteriaSystematic reviews and meta-analyses published from 2014 to 2025 that addressed DIVA in the adult population were included. Introduction

The placement of a peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) is a common technique in hospital care although it is not always successful, which leads to repeated punctures and alterations in the peripheral vascular system of patients, among other complications.1 This situation is recurring in paediatric, adult and geriatric populations,2 negatively impacting the safety of the patient and healthcare efficiency.

The term DIVA (difficult intravenous access) refers to individuals who present a significant difficulty in venous access. The definitions of DIVA are heterogeneous, and there are very few clinical prediction rules and reliable and well-validated algorithms for the insertion of the PIVC.3 4 This terminological and clinical variability makes the early detection of and the systematic approach to people with DIVA more difficult, leading to a great fragmentation in the evidence. In addition, it makes it difficult to precisely know the real incidence of the DIVA phenomenon in clinical settings. Some studies5 6 have determined incidences between 17% and 59.3%, according to the context and population analysed, being much higher in complex patients.

Being identified as a patient with DIVA implies a significant impact on the quality of the care, with important clinical and psychosocial consequences. These individuals tend to suffer from multiple failed intravenous catheterisation attempts, delays in diagnoses and treatments and an increased use of invasive devices such as central catheters, with the consequent increase in the risk of infections and other complications.7 In addition, this situation creates anxiety, fear and frustration in individuals who have a PIVC,2 and also implies a higher workload, consumption of resources and healthcare costs.8

To mitigate these negative consequences and improve the experience of the patient, many strategies have been explored to improve the treatment of a person with DIVA. Ultrasound-guided cannulation is one of the most studied techniques, showing promising results in terms of efficacy and user satisfaction.9 10 Likewise, some recent qualitative studies have delved into the experience of the patient during these procedures, underlining the importance of more humanised care centred on their perceptions.11 Nevertheless, the evidence on the compared efficacy and the standardised implementation of this evidence still requires a more rigorous synthesis.

Despite the growing body of evidence that addresses the concept of a DIVA patient from multiple clinical and contextual perspectives, the information is often diverse and fragmented, which is aggravated due to the absence of an agreed-on operational definition and standardised clinical criteria for its identification. Thus, considering that the amount of available evidence has reached a sufficient level of maturity, with multiple systematic reviews that address different aspects of the DIVA phenomenon, conducting an umbrella review was considered opportune. The methodological approach of synthesising evidence is ideal for overcoming the dispersion and the inconsistency of the findings. This type of second-order review uses a rigorous methodology to combine, compare and critically assess previous systematic reviews, allowing for the identification of areas of convergence as well as gaps in the evidence, thus offering a broader and more grounded view that can serve as a guide for clinical practice and future research.12

Given the above, the main aim of this study was to synthesise the existing evidence on systematic reviews about DIVA in the adult population, addressing its diagnostic definition, the associated risk factors and the reported clinical interventions, in order to identify areas of consensus and lacks of knowledge that could guide clinical practice.

Methodology

This study followed the methodological guidelines from the Joanna Briggs Institute on Umbrella Reviews13 and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) checklist (Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network,14 online supplemental material 1), with relevant items from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement applied where appropriate.15 The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251084947).

Search strategy

The search was performed in five databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane, Scopus and WOS-Core Collection) in July 2025. The search strategy was designed to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses about DIVA. The MeSH descriptors such as Catheterisation, Peripheral and Review Literature were used. The strategy was created by an expert documentalist (CC) along with three researchers (MD, JR and AT-R), and the formulas used are detailed in online supplemental material 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following were included: systematic literature reviews, with or without meta-analysis, published from January 2014 to July 2025, centred on adults or a mixed population in healthcare settings. No language restrictions were applied. The following were excluded: non-systematised narrative reviews, primary studies, meeting abstracts or other documents that were not peer-reviewed. The publication date limit (2014–2025) was due to the need to guarantee the clinical relevance, in a context in which more operational definitions emerged, as well as validated scales and technologies such as ultrasound, which were lacking in previous studies.

The research question was: What evidence do systematic reviews synthesise regarding diagnostic conceptualisation, associated clinical factors and clinical interventions aimed at adults with difficult intravenous access (DIVA)?

Data selection

The study selection was performed in two phases by two independent researchers (MD and AC-N) with the Rayyan platform (https://www.rayyan.ai/). First, the titles and abstracts were evaluated, followed by the complete texts of the studies that were preselected for the final inclusion. The discrepancies were solved through consensus or with the intervention from a third reviewer (AT-R).

Data collection

Two reviewers independently compiled the data (TCS and JR), which was verified by a third reviewer (AT-R). The following characteristics were extracted: (1) general characteristics (author, year, country), (2) methodological characteristics (objective, databases, keywords, included studies, risk) and (3) substantive characteristics (main results and conclusions).

Data synthesis

During the process of data extraction and synthesis, the research team (MD, JR, TCS, AC-N, SM and AT-R) established three analytical dimensions, based on the conceptual framework, and coherent with the aims of the study:

Definition and diagnostic criteria of DIVA: it examines how DIVA is conceptualised in the literature to improve comparability and clinical application.

These dimensions facilitated the identification of conceptual patterns and divergences between reviews. In addition, the degree of overlap was assessed (Corrected Covered Area, CCA) between the primary studies included in the reviews to verify validity.16

Study quality

The risk of bias in the reviews included was assessed by two independent reviewers through the use of the ROBIS (Risk Of Bias In Systematic Reviews) tool.17 The discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the research team.

The ROBIS tool is composed of five domains: (1) eligibility of the study criteria, (2) identification and selection of studies, (3) data collection and assessment of quality, (4) synthesis and presentation of the results and (5) overall judgement of risk of bias. Each domain was assessed through signalling questions with standardised response options: ‘yes’, ‘probably yes’, ‘probably no’, ‘no’, ‘not informed’ or ‘not applicable’. The risk of bias in each domain was scored as low, uncertain or high, as a function of the number and type of answers.

Based on these results, a global assessment of risk was established for each review, as low, moderate or high, following the criteria proposed by Melo et al,18 who applied this tool with a comparable methodological approach.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Studies selected

A total of 210 studies were identified in scientific databases, of which 165 were eliminated due to duplicity. After screening according to title, abstract and complete text, 38 were excluded for not meeting the criteria or for not focusing on a population with DIVA. Thus, a total of seven studies were included in this review (figure 1).

Characteristics of the studies included

Online supplemental material 3 shows the general, methodological and substantive characteristics of the seven reviews included. Of these, six corresponded to systematic reviews and a single review included a meta-analysis.19 Most of the publications were written in English, with the exception of a review written in Italian.20

With respect to the origin of the reviews, these came from the USA (n=2), Italy (n=2), Spain (n=1), the UK (n=1) and Australia (n=1). On their part, the primary studies included in four of the reviews that specified the country or region of origin19,23 encompassed diverse geographical areas, including Australia, Europe, North America, the Middle East, East Asia and the Caribbean (figure 2).

Geographical distribution of the primary studies.

Four reviews specified the healthcare context analysed,1922,24 with the hospital setting being predominant and with a notable presence of emergency, surgery and intensive care services. The populations studied mainly corresponded to hospitalised adults, although a review included a mixed sample.21 It was observed that the information about the basic characteristics of the participants was heterogeneous and in general limited, so it was not possible to describe it in a detailed manner.

As a set, the reviews included a total of 102 primary studies. The calculated CCA was 6.36%, which indicates a moderate overlap between reviews.

Risk analysis of the included studies

Four reviews were categorised as having a low overall risk of bias,19 21 22 24 one with a moderate risk23 and two with a high risk of bias.20 25 The main methodological concerns were related to the identification and selection of studies, the limited description of the complementary searches, the lack of the formal assessment of bias in some reviews and the predominance of narrative synthesis methods. In turn, most of the reviews showed a low concern in the eligibility criteria, the collection of data and study appraisal, which partially support the solidity of the synthesised evidence (table 1, figure 3).

Discussion

This umbrella review combines the available evidence from seven systematic reviews on individuals with DIVA, allowing for the structured identification and interpretation of three key dimensions: the diagnostic definition of DIVA, the factors of clinical risk and the assessment methods, as well as the clinical interventions implemented to address it. This three-sided perspective provides a comprehensive view that contributes to the consolidation of existing knowledge, the identification of important gaps in knowledge and the guiding of clinical practice and future research.

Although they share some elements, the diagnostic conceptualisation of DIVA and the identification of risk factors are distinct analytical dimensions. On the one hand, the conceptualisation refers to the clinical, technical and operational criteria used to define a person with DIVA. The main conclusion of the diagnostic dimension is the great conceptual heterogeneity. Our findings show the existence of a set of 15 criteria to define DIVA. Elements such as the presence of two or more failed attempts, the absence of visible or palpable veins and a history of previous difficult access are underlined as key markers in the early detection of individuals with DIVA.19,2225 These criteria represent the operational consensus with a higher frequency in the literature. Other related elements include the necessary use of support techniques for the visualisation or identification of intravenous access, such as ultrasound, infrared or transillumination.19 21 22 These elements coincide with the most recent reviews published after July 2025.26

In turn, the risk factors encompass clinical, anatomical or contextual characteristics that increase the probability that a patient may have DIVA. These are variables such as BMI indices in both extremes or the presence of specific pathological conditions (kidney disease, diabetes or history of chemotherapy, among others).1 4 In addition, Rodríguez-Calero et al.19 underline the relevance of the lack of vein palpability and visibility, as well as obesity and a history of DIVA, factors that were repeatedly pointed out by clinicians as early indicators of DIVA. These findings reinforce the need to consider not only procedural and anatomical criteria but also personal clinical characteristics when assessing risk, in agreement with other authors such as Sweeny et al.27

From a diagnostic approach, the results show more than 16 tools for anticipating DIVA,22 23 25 which reflects the effort of the scientific community for standardising the assessment of risk. Nevertheless, most lack an exhaustive validation, underlining the A-DICAVE scale22 23 due to its content validity, inter-evaluator reliability and clinical applicability, in agreement with the study by Salleras-Duran et al.28 Along this line, Nannini et al26 showed A-DIVA, EA-DIVA or the DIVA Clinical Predictor Tool as instruments with high levels of precision. Despite the availability of these validated scales, their clinical adoption is low, pointing to a gap in the transfer of knowledge to clinical practice.

Nevertheless, the literature is inconsistent. In the study by Bahl et al,22 82 potential factors for the identification of DIVA were discovered, of which only 10 were shown to be stable, which underlines the complexity and heterogeneity of the phenomenon, as well as the lack of data. These divergences could explain the low adoption of DIVA assessment instruments in clinical practice,22 29 in agreement with other studies,29 as they are perceived as not very practical and needing an inefficient time investment as compared with repeated attempts of intravenous access. As a result, the identification of patients with DIVA tends to be somewhat late, after various failed attempts, and many healthcare centres still lack formal mechanisms for its detection and treatment.29 30 In agreement with the findings from this review, it is necessary to promote an approach centred on the early detection of risk,31 32 supported by effective communication between health professionals and patients, and on the active participation of the patient. This change requires integrating key elements such as the anticipation of risk, the making of shared decisions, and the use of tools based on the best evidence possible.

This change in the management of DIVA, according to the finding from this study, indicates four types of interventions: organisational, technological, clinical and educational. The organisational interventions identified were centred on the implementation of escalation protocols, the early referral to specialised staff, the establishment of a maximum of two attempts per operator, and the individual management of intravenous access,1921,23 25 measures that coincide with that described in other studies.27 Although the Infusion Nurses Society recommends limiting the number of attempts per professional to two,33 this practice not always responds to clinical criteria, but tends to be conditioned by structural factors, such as a lack of trained staff or the absence of formal guidelines.29 These limitations can delay an adequate referral and prolong the sequence of failed attempts, with important clinical consequences, such as a delay in the treatment and the establishment of a diagnosis.34 In this context, the systematic recording of DIVA risk in the clinical history becomes especially important. In fact, some authors29 have proposed the incorporation of computerised alert systems that automate this identification and activate standardised care protocols, to improve the coordination between teams and to reduce the variability in clinical practice.

On the other hand, some studies19 21 25 on organisational interventions underlined the value of specialised teams, such as infusion therapy groups or critical care nurses, who act as expert leads in the treatment of patients with DIVA. Although their implementation is not homogeneous among health centres, the availability of specialised teams is a highly efficient clinical resource, associated with better quality care and a lower rate of complications.34 35 Their presence facilitates a rapid and specialised response, reinforcing the continuity and safety of care.30

With respect to technological interventions and the use of devices, ultrasound was consolidated as a more effective and validated tool to optimise venous access in patients with DIVA.19 21 23 25 Recent evidence11 36 also shows significant improvements in the success rate after the first attempt, in the experience of the patient, and the reduction in the amount of time needed for the procedure. Nevertheless, its generalised implementation is limited by the availability of teams and the need for specialised training.37 38 In contrast, other technologies, such as infrared or transilluminator devices, have shown scarce clinical efficacy, especially in adults, and their use is mainly centred on paediatric patients.39 40 The results from the present review, supported by the current literature, underline the importance of having an adequate infrastructure and strategies that facilitate the effective integration of technology in the management of patients with DIVA.

The clinical interventions that emerged from this review were centred on practices focused on improving the experience of individuals with DIVA, through strategies directed to reduce pain, anxiety and complications derived from the procedure.20 23 25 This approach coincides with the recent literature,11 41 which emphasises the importance of a communicative approach that utilises clear language, promotes the participation of the patient, and generates a calm environment, which favours a more positive experience of the procedure. In spite of this, studies that specifically address the perception of patients are still scarce.

The management of pain appears as a critical aspect, although it has been little explored. While some findings suggest that there are no significant differences in pain between conventional and ultrasound-guided techniques,42 others highlight that the behaviour of the health professional and the quality of communication can have an important impact that may be even stronger than the type of technique used.41 As a whole, the results underline the value of the therapeutic relationship and the need to continue researching the subjective and emotional component of clinical care among users and contexts related to DIVA.

Lastly, with respect to the educational interventions, the findings from this study22 24 and other recent evidence34 show that structured training significantly improves the technical skills needed for venous cannulation, although the impact of the competences of the clinician is still assessed in a heterogeneous and limited manner.19 In complex contexts such as DIVA, specific training not only optimises the technique but also contributes to the reduction in frustration and the strengthening of professional confidence,38 reinforcing the need for specialised training programmes and clinical support structures.

Clinical implications and future lines of research

The findings from the umbrella review support the need for structured and anticipatory clinical models to address DIVA in adults, by integrating organisational, technological, clinical and educational interventions. The organisational interventions include protocols of escalation, early identification and referrals to specialised teams; the technological ones are centred on the early use of echography; the clinical ones prioritise the reduction of pain and anxiety through patient-centred care; and the educational ones underline the specific training of the professionals to improve competences and care safety.

From a clinical perspective, the diagnostic conceptualisation of DIVA is mainly characterised by failed repeated attempts at peripheral venous cannulation (two or more), the absence of visible or palpable veins, and a history of difficulty, frequently requiring advanced techniques or specialised support to achieve a successful cannulation.

Likewise, the results underline the importance of care based on effective communication, shared decisions and minimisation of physical and emotional impacts of the procedure, especially in complex care contexts. Lastly, future studies should prioritise clinical validation and implementation of DIVA diagnostic tools and structured care pathways in diverse healthcare contexts and in a manner tailored to their specific needs, as well as the assessment of organisational models and results reported by the patients themselves.

Limitations

A moderate overlap was observed between the primary studies included (with a CCA of 6.36%). Although the level of coincidence does not compromise the validity nor the overall interpretation of the results, it must be considered when assessing the independence of the evidence synthesised. This finding also reflects a certain degree of maturity of the topic in the literature about individuals with DIVA.

In addition, a large heterogeneity was observed in the DIVA definitions, the results evaluated and the clinical contexts included, which limited the comparability between studies and hindered a quantitative synthesis. Lastly, the differences in the methodological quality of the reviews included could have influenced the findings.

Conclusions

The present scientific review identifies key similarities in the criteria used to define DIVA, especially highlighting the presence of ≥2 failed attempts, the absence of visible or palpable veins and a documented clinical history of difficulty. However, the lack of standardisation in these criteria limits their uniform application in clinical practice.

The findings underline that treating patients with DIVA transcends the technical or procedural component and calls for a structured and multidimensional approach.
