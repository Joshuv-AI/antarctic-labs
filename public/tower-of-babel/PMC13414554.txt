Human lungs maintain tissue-resident memory T cells against a broad spectrum of pathogens
Authors: Vicente Fajardo-Rosas, Aquib Ehtram, Isabel Marchand-Casas, Serena J. Chee, Monalisa Mondal, Celia Ferrández Alaminos, Francisco Emmanuel Castañeda-Castro, Zubin Roy, Lindsey Chudley, Michael Shackcloth, Judith Cave, Aiman Alzetani, Edwin Woo, Elizabeth Phillips, Simon Mallal, Alba Grifoni, Benjamin J. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells are critical for frontline immunity, yet they undergo rapid attrition in the mouse lung. Whether this paradigm applies to humans has remained unknown. Here we present a comprehensive analysis of T cells from human lungs, characterizing the prevalence and properties of lung TRM cells specific to a broad spectrum of pathogens. Using a T cell receptor-guided approach that integrates single-cell transcriptomics with paired T cell receptor repertoire profiling, we mapped the pathogen specificity of more than 87,000 lung T cells across 40 individuals, the majority of whom harbored TRM cells specific to multiple pathogens. We confirmed that a large fraction of lung TRM clones persist in the lung for many months to years. Thus, in contrast to those of mice, human lungs retain a stable and varied pool of pathogen-specific TRM cells, suggesting that strategies to bolster these responses could provide durable protection against severe lung infections.

Main

While serving their primary function in gas exchange, lungs are exposed to a broad spectrum of airborne pathogens capable of causing life-threatening infections1, with such infections accounting for more than 2 million deaths worldwide each year2. Protection from infections is provided by epithelial cells, pneumocytes and macrophages lining the airways and alveoli, which contribute to innate immune responses against inhaled pathogens1. In addition, adaptive memory immune responses generated during primary infections provide durable protection upon re-exposure. Multiple studies in mouse models of infections at barrier sites, including the lung, have highlighted tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) as a key cell type orchestrating protective responses during reinfection and heterologous infections3–5.

TRM cells generated in the mouse lung have been reported to decline rapidly over time, in contrast to the long-lived TRM cells in other organs, including the skin, liver and intestines4,6,7. Thus, it has been proposed that the lung-draining lymph nodes (LLN) serve as a reservoir of TRM cells that migrate retrogradely from the lung to maintain lung TRM cell responses8,9. The selective decline of TRM cells in the mouse lung is considered to offer host benefit by balancing better gas-exchange function with organismal fitness against infections4. These observations in mouse models have raised the question of whether TRM cells in human lungs similarly undergo rapid attrition, which could limit their capacity to provide durable memory responses to reinfection. This would also have major implications for vaccination approaches, in which generation of durable lung TRM cell responses is critical for long-term, effective protection against the targeted pathogen5,10,11.

Although TRM cells have been reported in the human lung12–17, as have pathogen-specific TRM cells18–24, previous studies have been limited by small cohort sizes, low cell yields and restricted pathogen coverage, leaving unresolved the question of whether the mouse paradigm of short-lived lung TRM and LLN-based maintenance applies to humans. Some studies have attempted to enumerate lung T cells and TRM cells that recognize epitopes from respiratory viruses such as influenza A virus (IAV)19,21,24, SARS-CoV-2 (refs. 22,23) and respiratory syncytial virus18,24 and herpesviruses (namely, the human cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)). However, many of these studies employed peptide megapool (MP)-based stimulation coupled with intracellular cytokine staining to assess the frequency of pathogen-specific T cells in the human lung18,22–24; this approach is likely to be confounded by false positives due to nonspecific or bystander activation of CD8+ T cells, which can occur owing to cytokine-mediated activation of memory CD8+ T cells25. Thus, such studies may overestimate the frequency of pathogen-specific cells unless there are adequate controls in place, for example, irrelevant peptide pools, addition of cytokine blockers or brefeldin for the entire duration of stimulation, or additional T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing to exclude bystander activation, as clonotypes of such cells will be detected under different peptide stimulation conditions. Moreover, previous efforts to identify such cells in tissue have lacked the throughput to support comparisons of different pathogen-specific T cell populations within an individual18–20,22,23. As a result, our understanding of how pathogen-specific T cell memory is distributed, shaped and maintained at mucosal sites in humans has remained limited. Thus, there is a need for high-resolution, large-scale approaches capable of capturing diverse pathogen-specific TRM cell populations for comparison within and across individuals.

To overcome these challenges, we profiled large numbers of T cells from the human lung and LLN to characterize the prevalence, clonal sharing and properties of lung TRM cells specific to a broad range of pathogens. We developed an integrative strategy that leverages the TCR as a molecular barcode of antigen specificity. By probing the lung TCR repertoires with (i) donor-matched pathogen-specific TCRs derived from blood, (ii) publicly available CD8+ TCRs and (iii) a newly generated TCR resource for CD4+ T cells, we were able to impute the pathogen specificity of a substantial fraction of lung T cells from 40 individuals. This strategy enabled us to characterize a large number of pathogen-specific T cells in the human lung, while also providing an extensive resource of CD4+ TCRs specific to common human pathogens. The scale and resolution of our datasets allowed robust enumeration and phenotypic comparisons of pathogen-specific T cells within and across anatomic sites. We observed that respiratory-pathogen-specific TRM clones persisted in the human lungs over many months to years and displayed limited clonal sharing with T cells in the LLN.

Human lungs harbor an oligoclonal pool of memory T cells

We obtained uninvolved lung tissue and LLN samples from 40 individuals who were clinically free of recent infections and deemed medically fit to undergo surgical lung resection for early-stage lung cancer (hereafter referred to as the lung cohort; Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a). The advanced age of the lung cohort (median 73 years, range 61–83; Supplementary Table 1) offered an opportunity to characterize the residual memory T cell populations in the lung that are shaped by lifelong exposure to pathogens and vaccine antigens. As each TCR is highly specific for an MHC-bound peptide epitope, its identity can be used to map the specificities of T cells to particular pathogens or host antigens. We first quantified the frequency of T cells with identical TCR clonotypes to assess their clonal abundance. This analysis provided a measure of antigen-specific clonal accumulation within the lung and LLN. The LLN compartment, as well as capturing cells that are trafficked from the lungs, largely serves as a surrogate for the pool of recirculating naive and memory T cells from the blood, thereby allowing comparison between lung tissue-resident and circulating memory populations. In contrast to the LLN, we observed a substantially higher ratio of T cells to TCR clonotypes in the lung—that is, clonal accumulation (Fig. 1b)—with the top 10% of the clonotypes accounting for 51% of CD4+ and 70% of CD8+ T cells in the lung (median across individuals; Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1d). In some individuals, as few as ten TCR clonotypes dominated the lung TCR repertoire (Fig. 1d), indicating preferential accumulation of certain antigen-specific memory T clones in the human lung. This selective clonal expansion of a restricted pool of memory T cells in the lung was supported by a high Gini index26 (Fig. 1e), consistent with pronounced oligoclonality and significantly reduced TCR repertoire diversity of lung T cells relative to LLN T cells (Fig. 1d–f and Extended Data Fig. 1e). Notably, a substantial fraction of highly expanded TCR clonotypes (clone size ≥ 10 cells) identified in the lung were not detected in the LLN TCR repertoires (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1f), suggesting that these clonotypes may correspond to lung-resident T cells that do not recirculate to the LLN. The Morisita–Horn similarity index (0, no overlap; 1, complete overlap), which accounts for both number and relative abundance of shared clonotypes in lung and LLN TCR repertoires27, remained low even when equal numbers of T cells were analyzed in each compartment and across all lung regions or LLNs examined (Fig. 1h and Extended Data Fig. 1g–j). Our reanalysis of the TCR repertoire from lung and LLN from an independent cohort of individuals without cancer showed patterns concordant with our results for clonal expansion and TCR sharing17 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, these findings indicate that the human lung harbors a highly expanded and oligoclonal pool of memory T cells, even in the absence of recent infections.

Selective clonal expansion of TRM cells in human lungs

If TRM cells with highly expanded, low-diversity TCR repertoires were abundant in the lung, this would imply that multiple antigen-specific TRM populations are preferentially retained in the human lung, in contrast to observations in mice, in which pathogen-specific lung TRM cells have been reported to decline rapidly over time4,6,7. Based on transcriptional profiles, we quantified different T cell subsets and found that TRM cells were the dominant CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subset in the human lung, regardless of the lung region analyzed (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 2a–e and Supplementary Table 3). These lung TRM cells displayed a high degree of clonal expansion and a high Gini index (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2f); notably, the top 10% of their TCR clonotypes comprised more than 46% and 50% of T cells in the CD4+ and CD8+ TRM subsets, respectively (median across individuals) (Fig. 2c). To assess whether recirculating T cell subsets in the lung, such as central memory T (TCM) or effector memory T (TEM) cells, contributed to the pool of antigen-specific TRM cells, we assessed clonal sharing of the highly expanded TCR clonotypes (clone size ≥ 10 cells) among different lung T cell subsets. Although most highly expanded TCR clonotypes of TRM cells were shared with other lung T cell subsets (Extended Data Fig. 2g), a substantial fraction (~50%) exhibited phenotypic bias, with preferential expansion (>2-fold) in the TRM subset relative to other subsets (Fig. 2d). Notably, this phenotypic bias of TCR clonotypes was also observed for all other lung T cells subsets (Fig. 2d), indicating that TCR clonotypes segregate according to T cell phenotype, as previously reported28. Even among the TCR clonotypes biased to the TRM phenotype, we observed significant clonal expansion and restricted diversity (Extended Data Fig. 2h,i). This suggests that a subset of highly expanded TRM clones are largely lung resident and independent of the TEM and TCM subsets, which can recirculate and potentially intermix with the circulating memory T cell pool28.

Mouse models have supported a role of the LLN as a major reservoir of pathogen-specific TRM cells that otherwise undergo rapid attrition in the mouse lung8,9. To assess whether TRM cells in the LLN were also a major reservoir of TRM cells in the human lung, we compared TCR clonotypes of lung TRM cells with T cells in their corresponding LLN. First, as reported in mouse models8, we observed both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with tissue-residency features (that is, TRM cells) in all the LLN stations analyzed (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 3a–e and Supplementary Table 1). Although these LLN TRM cells represented a relatively small fraction of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3e), their TCRs displayed high clonal expansion and their TCR repertoires displayed low diversity compared to other LLN T cell subsets (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 3f). Compared to other LLN T cell subsets, a large fraction (median ≥ 50% across donors for both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) of LLN TRM TCR clonotypes were clonally related to lung T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), indicating potential trafficking of TRM cells between these two compartments. However, the converse was not true, with a major fraction (median ≥80% across donors for both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) of the lung TRM TCR clonotypes not detected in the LLN T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Furthermore, among the highly expanded TCR clonotypes shared between the lung and LLN, those biased toward the TRM phenotype were significantly more abundant in the lung than in the LLN (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 4c–e).

We showed that TCR clonotypes biased to recirculating non-TRM subsets in the lung (as positive controls), especially those biased to the GZMK+ subset, displayed substantial sharing with CD8+ T cells in the LLN (Fig. 2g–i and Extended Data Fig. 4c–g). These results suggest that despite sufficient sample size and cell numbers to sensitively detect clonal sharing between the two anatomical compartments, lung TRM clones display substantially less sharing than GZMK+ subset-biased clones (Fig. 2h and Extended Data Fig. 4e). Even when we restricted our analysis to clonal sharing involving TRM-biased clones in the lung and TRM cells in the LLN, we identified several instances in which the top two most abundant lung TRM-biased clones were not shared, whereas less abundant donor-matched clones (for example, those ranked third or lower) were nonetheless shared with LLN cells (Extended Data Fig. 4h and Supplementary Table 4). These results suggest that the small size of the TRM subset in the LLN does not preclude sensitive detection of clonotype sharing between TRM cells in the lungs and LLN.

Reanalysis of published single-cell datasets from lung and LLN of individuals without cancer16,17 showed concordance with the lung T cell subsets identified in our cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Table 2). However, low lung T cell numbers (median 194 cells; Supplementary Fig. 1a) or small cohort size (n = 2; ref. 16) in these datasets limited the sensitivity of our analysis to detect significant differences in clonotype sharing between lung TRM and recirculating non-TRM subsets with LLN T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Taken together, our results suggest that the LLN is unlikely to represent the major reservoir of antigen-specific TRM cells in the human lung. Further quantitative studies considering the absolute frequencies and TCR repertoires of TRM cells across all lung-draining nodal stations will be necessary to confirm the minimal role of LLNs in the maintenance of TRM cells in human lungs.

TCR-guided imputation of pathogen specificity to lung T cells

Second, we utilized TCR specificity information generated independent of our cohort. We compiled 146,465 TCRs with known pathogen specificity from publicly available databases30–33, focusing mainly on 6 common human pathogens (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Table 5). As the published TCR datasets almost entirely comprised MHC class I-restricted epitopes recognized by TCRs of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 4c), we experimentally generated an additional large-scale resource of TCRs expressed on CD4+ T cells that recognized MHC class II-bound epitopes. Utilizing a large resource of PBMCs collected from an independent cohort of 61 healthy individuals (hereafter, the TCR–resource cohort; Supplementary Table 1), we performed a short-term antigen-reactive T cell enrichment (ARTE) assay34 to capture ex vivo T cells reactive to MPs targeting 10 common human pathogens and vaccine antigens and then generated full-length paired α and β chain TCR sequences for >800,000 putative pathogen-specific T cells (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 2). Employing a statistical framework, we assigned pathogen specificities to each TCR sequence, resulting in a curated reference of 45,353 TCRs of CD4+ T cells that were specific to common human pathogens (Fig. 3e,g, Supplementary Fig. 4d–f and Supplementary Table 5). More than 99% of these TCRs had not previously been reported in the published databases, and ~96% of the 384 overlapping TCRs showed concordant pathogen specificity (Supplementary Table 5). To experimentally validate this approach, we expressed 18 of these newly reported TCRs in the T reporter cell line and demonstrated dose-dependent activation following stimulation with corresponding pathogen peptide MPs (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 4g–i and Supplementary Table 5). Finally, we used these datasets of TCRs with known pathogen specificities to infer the pathogens recognized by the lung TCRs that we originally sequenced (integrative approach in Fig. 3d and Methods). Thereby, we imputed the pathogen specificity of ~11% and 15% of all lung CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, respectively (range 4–70%; Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 6). Overall, our findings indicate that a sizable fraction of memory T cells in the human lung display specificity to common human pathogens.

Pathogen-specific CD4+ TRM cells are prevalent in human lungs

Based on the TCR sequences of lung and LLN CD4+ T cells, we estimated the frequency of a broad range of pathogen-specific T cells. Several of the pathogen-specific TCR clonotypes showed substantial clonal expansion in the lungs compared with the LLN, confirming the presence of expanded populations of pathogen-specific CD4+ memory T cells in the human lung (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). For all five of the common respiratory viruses examined (SARS-CoV-2, IAV, parainfluenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus and metapneumovirus), we detected memory CD4+ T cells in the majority of individuals in our cohort, with frequencies ranging between ~0.1% and ~56% among all lung CD4+ T cells across donors and pathogens (median ~0.7%; Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 6b). Comparable frequencies (median ~0.9%, range 0.1–21%) were also observed for CD4+ T cells specific to the common herpesviruses (CMV and EBV). We then focused on lung CD4+ T cells specific to the Bordetella pertussis bacterium, which causes whooping cough, differentiating vaccine and nonvaccine antigens using two separate peptide MPs35: one targeted antigens included in the acellular pertussis (aP) vaccine, and the other encompassed the top 170 immunodominant non-aP vaccine epitopes. Lung memory CD4+ T cells specific to both types of antigen were detected, with relatively higher frequencies for those targeting vaccine antigens (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Phenotypic analysis of pathogen-specific lung CD4+ T cells revealed that a large fraction exhibited features of TRM cells (Fig. 4b,c), regardless of the lung region under assessment (Extended Data Fig. 6d). As expected, several highly expanded respiratory-pathogen-specific TRM-biased clones in the lungs showed limited or no sharing with the LLN (Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). The frequencies of pathogen-specific TRM cells varied substantially across donors (Fig. 4c), with no preferential enrichment for any particular pathogen (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 6g) and no significant associations detected with age, sex, body mass index, smoking or vaccination status (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 6h). However, the prevalence of pathogen-specific CD4+ TRM cells was strongly correlated with the overall abundance of CD4+ TRM cells (Fig. 4e). Although the sample size (40 donors) limited the statistical power of the analysis to definitively assess associations with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) types, donors carrying the HLA-DQB1*03:01 allele showed a trend toward higher frequencies of lung CD4+ TRM cells compared to other donors (Extended Data Fig. 6i and Supplementary Table 7). Together, these findings suggest that donor-intrinsic factors governing TRM cell generation and retention may have a key role in shaping the abundance of pathogen-specific CD4+ TRM cells in the lung.

Although pathogen specificity did not influence the preferential acquisition of a TRM phenotype, it did affect the generation of CD4+ TEM cells (cytotoxic T lymphocytes; CTLs), indicating that certain pathogens, such as CMV, preferentially drive CD4+ CTL accumulation in the lungs (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 6f,g). Notably, the experimentally validated CMV-specific TCRs comprising more than 250 lung CD4+ T cells also showed the CD4+ CTL phenotype in the lung (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k), consistent with previous reports describing this as the predominant phenotype among circulating CMV-specific CD4+ T cells37,38.

Pathogen-specific TRM cells did not cluster according to pathogen specificity; instead, many instances of donor-specific clusters of cells were observed (Fig. 4f). These results suggest that donor-intrinsic factors have a dominant role in shaping the transcriptional properties of pathogen-specific TRM cells, as well as in shaping their abundance. Transcripts encoding key effector cytokines TNF and IFNγ, which are known to be constitutively expressed by CD4+ TRM cells39, also showed no major differences among distinct pathogen-specific TRM cells (Fig. 4g). Finally, we reasoned that if pathogen-specific TRM cells were to decline rapidly after infection, we would find TRM cell responses only to those from recent exposures. Contrary to this expectation, many individuals harbored substantial numbers of TRM cells specific to multiple respiratory viruses (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 6b), all of which are unlikely to reflect recent infections. Taken together, these findings suggest that the human lung serves as a long-term reservoir of pathogen-specific CD4+ TRM cells generated by past infections.

Subclustering of CD8+ TRM cells identified a subset enriched for expression of transcripts encoding HLA-DR and other class II MHC receptors (Fig. 5e), a feature typically associated with recently activated effector T cells41. As individuals in our cohort reported no recent infections, and 39 of the 40 had not received a vaccination in the preceding 90 days (Supplementary Table 1), this finding suggests that the HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM subset may accumulate in the lung independent of recent TCR activation, consistent with previous observations in circulating HLA-DR+ CD8+ T cells from both HIV-1-infected and HIV-1-uninfected individuals42,43. The HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM subset was enriched for transcripts encoding transcription factors BATF and CSL (encoded by RBPJ) (Fig. 5f), which have been linked to the development and maintenance of TRM cells44–46. Notably, Notch signaling also contributes to constitutive expression of IFNγ by TRM cells44 and, accordingly, IFNG transcripts were significantly increased in the HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM subset compared to the other CD8+ TRM cells (Fig. 5f). Other TRM-associated molecules whose transcripts were upregulated in the HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM subset included the CD103 integrin α-subunit (encoded by ITGAE)3,44 and fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5)47 (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of a lung TRM gene signature in pathogen-specific HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM cells, suggesting enrichment of tissue-residency features (Extended Data Fig. 7k).

The frequency of these pathogen-specific HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM cells varied substantially across donors, with no preferential enrichment for any particular pathogen (Fig. 5g) and no significant associations detected with the assessed host factors except age, which was positively associated with the frequency of SARS-CoV-2-specific HLA-DRhi TRM cells (Fig. 5d). This suggests that other as-yet-unidentified donor factors may shape interindividual variability in this TRM subset. Overall, these results indicate that the human lung harbors a population of pathogen-specific HLA-DRhi CD8+ TRM cells that exhibit several features associated with enhanced tissue residency and functional features.

Persistence of lung TRM cells in humans

Lung T cells with TRM-biased TCR clonotypes at baseline largely maintained their tissue-resident phenotype and showed significant correlations in relative abundance across longitudinal samples, suggesting cell-intrinsic properties that sustain long-term phenotypic and clonal stability (Fig. 6e,f and Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). Notably, the expression of genes downstream of TCR signaling (TNFRSF9 and ENTPD1) did not differ significantly between the persistent and nonpersistent TRM clones (Extended Data Fig. 9f and Supplementary Table 7), suggesting that ongoing TCR signaling is unlikely to account for their long-term persistence.

Consistent with our observations in the cross-sectional lung samples (Figs. 4b,c and 5c), many individuals in the longitudinal cohort harbored substantial numbers of respiratory-pathogen-specific TRM cells (Fig. 6g, Extended Data Fig. 9g and Supplementary Table 6). Most importantly, we demonstrated that a large fraction of these pathogen-specific TRM cells shared TCR clonotypes across longitudinal samples from the same individuals, indicating persistence over months to years (Extended Data Fig. 9h,i). For example, in donor X04 and X06, more than 50% of highly expanded pathogen-specific TRM clones at baseline, including CD4+ TRM clones specific to multiple respiratory viruses, were detected in longitudinally collected paired lung samples (Extended Data Fig. 9i,j and Supplementary Table 6). Overall, our analysis of longitudinal samples confirmed that TRM clones, including those specific to common human pathogens, persist in the human lung.

Discussion

Our study supports the concept that the human lung serves as a reservoir of pathogen-specific TRM cells. Memory T cells in the lung exhibited a high degree of clonal expansion compared with the LLN, and, in many individuals, a few TCR clonotypes dominated the repertoire, reflecting preferential accumulation of certain antigen-specific memory T cells. A substantial fraction of these cells were specific to common human pathogens, and highly expanded TRM-biased TCR clonotypes exhibited restricted diversity, suggesting that they are largely tissue resident and not clonally related to other lung T cell subsets that reflect the circulating T cell pool. Several individuals harbored substantial TRM populations specific to multiple respiratory viruses even in the absence of recent infection. Finally, longitudinal analysis of surgically resected lung tissue samples from the same donors revealed that a large fraction of TRM clones persisted for months to years. Overall, these observations indicate that unlike in mouse models, in which lung TRM cells wane6,7 and the LLN may serve as a principal reservoir8,9, the human lung can act as a long-term reservoir for pathogen-specific TRM cells. This highlights fundamental differences in the maintenance of TRM populations between species and indicates a need for caution when extrapolating findings from mouse models of lung infection and vaccination to humans. Unlike animal studies, studies involving living humans cannot be fully quantitative, as it is not ethically possible to obtain the entire lung and all its LLNs, nor is it technically possible to extract all cells with 100% efficiency. However, as we analyzed more than one million T cells uniformly processed from a large number of donor-matched lungs and LLNs, this study provides a foundational resource for understanding the properties, trafficking and persistence of a wide range of pathogen-specific lung TRM cells in a real-world setting.

We speculate that anatomical differences in the bronchial and bronchiolar structure between humans and mice could account for the observed differences in lung TRM density. This is based on previous studies of human lungs in which the majority of the CD8+ TRM cells (CD103+ cells) were observed to be present in the bronchial/bronchiolar tissue (nongas-exchange compartment) and not the alveolar tissue, the gas-exchange compartment48,49. Therefore, the accumulation and persistence of TRM cells in the human lungs (bronchial compartment) may not adversely affect the gas-exchange function of the alveolar compartment, which is the claim made for the mouse lung. Notably, anatomical studies have shown that there are substantially more bronchial/bronchiolar divisions in the human lung compared to the mouse lung (>21 divisions versus ~13 divisions), such that there is substantially more bronchial/bronchiolar surface area in the human lungs50,51. Given that specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice cohoused with pet-store ‘dirty’ mice showed increased frequency of TRM cells in the lungs and many other organs compared to SPF mice52, it is also likely that the natural microbial exposures in humans may explain part of the observed differences compared with laboratory SPF mice.

Several findings from our study indicate important caveats in extrapolating conclusions from previous studies regarding the persistence of respiratory-pathogen-specific TRM cells in the human lungs4,6,7. First, a substantial fraction of lung T cells consists of recirculating non-TRM subsets. This includes T cells specific to a wide range of pathogens (Figs. 4a and 5a), which implies that the results of studies that fail to discriminate TRM cells in the total or pathogen-specific lung T cell populations12,53 are likely to be confounded by recirculating T cell subsets. Second, viruses capable of establishing persistent infections, such as EBV and CMV, give rise to large TRM clones in the lungs and, in some individuals, comprise a substantial fraction of the pathogen-specific TRM compartment (Figs. 4c and 5c and Extended Data Figs. 6f and 7h). This finding has important implications for the interpretation of previous studies13,15–17, as the mere detection of TRM cells in the lungs, in the absence of antigen-specific resolution, cannot be assumed to reflect durable TRM populations generated by acute respiratory infections. Rather, persistent viral infections may also shape the composition and apparent longevity of the lung TRM pool. Third, our imputation-based approach to define the spectrum of virus-specific T cells in the lungs revealed that a single pathogen, such as SARS-CoV-2 or IAV, could generate both TRM-biased and non-TRM-biased clones within the same individual (Extended Data Figs. 6f and 7h and Supplementary Table 6). In certain individuals, the responses were skewed toward non-TRM-biased clones, highlighting substantial intraindividual heterogeneity in tissue residency (Extended Data Fig. 7h). These findings suggest that previous studies18–21 that have relied on tetramer staining of a single viral epitope may have underestimated the diversity of the T cell repertoire and misrepresented the prevalence of tissue-resident populations, potentially yielding a biased view of pathogen-specific immunity in the lung. Finally, our reanalysis of published datasets16,17, particularly our comparison of TCR repertoires across different T cell subsets in lung and LLN, demonstrated that the large number of donors and higher T cell counts in our study reduced sampling biases and improved the sensitivity of clonal sharing analyses compared with previous work.

Although our dataset was large in terms of cellular resolution and depth, the size of the overall study cohort remained modest (n = 40 donors). Expanding the cohort will be important for exploration of the roles of genetic and host factors that influence the magnitude of pathogen-specific lung TRM cells. The underlying diagnosis of lung cancer may influence the nature of memory T cell responses in the uninvolved lung tissue, although our reanalysis of the single-cell transcriptomes of lung T cells from a cohort of cancer-free individuals showed concordant results. Another limitation was that the study cohort primarily reflected the baseline state of lung tissue, as most donors were infection-free and had not been recently vaccinated at the time of sample collection. Although study of acutely infected individuals is not feasible owing to the requirement for surgical tissue resection, individuals undergoing surgery after recent vaccination could provide an opportunity to examine vaccine-elicited T cell responses in the lungs. Overall, our study demonstrates the power of TCR-based imputation to uncover pathogen-specific T cell responses in the human lung, establishing a framework to systematically map antigen-specific tissue-resident immunity across the human body, with implications for understanding infection, vaccination and immune-mediated disease in humans.

Methods

Human samples

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the South Central – Hampshire A Research Ethics Committee (14/SC/0186) and the Ethics Committee of La Jolla Institute for Immunology (LJI). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection. Individuals did not receive compensation for participation in this study.

Two cohorts of a total of 46 individuals undergoing standard-of-care surgical resection for primary lung nonsmall-cell lung cancer at Liverpool Heart and Chest NHS Foundation Trust Hospital and University Hospital Southampton NHS Foundation Trust were enrolled. The cross-sectional (lung) cohort (n = 40) included 16 men and 24 women, all of white British ethnicity, with a median age of 73 years (range 61–83). The longitudinal cohort comprised 6 individuals who either required completion surgery or developed recurrence after their initial surgery and therefore required a second lung resection several months to years later (median 35 weeks, range 7–304). The cohort included 3 men and 3 women, all of white British ethnicity, with a median age of 71.5 years (range 67–78). Clinical and demographic metadata are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Lung tissue, along with matched peripheral blood and LLN (the latter was available for the lung cohort only), was collected intraoperatively. Following lobectomy, the surgical team followed a standardized protocol to collect the macroscopically uninvolved, nontumor-bearing regions of the lung parenchyma. To ensure that assessment of the surgical tumor margin was not compromised, the uninvolved lung tissue was typically obtained at least 5 cm away from the tumor margin. Although lung tissues were obtained from different lung lobes (Extended Data Fig. 1a), the lung parenchyma was uniformly resected to ensure consistent representation of small airways and alveoli in all cases. The lung tissue volume obtained was dependent on the tumor size and the resected lobe, with a median volume of 3 cm3 (range 1.2–15 cm3) (Supplementary Table 1). Lung tissues were processed immediately by dicing in <1-mm3 pieces and cryopreserved in multiple cryovials.

The surgical team obtained tumor-uninvolved lymph nodes that drained the resected lung lobe. In this study, LLN refers specifically to the hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes (per International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer nomenclature)that constitute the direct lymphatic drainage pathway of the resected lung lobe. As detailed in Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1, LLNs were obtained from stations 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11. The specific LLN stations collected were not identical across all donors, as they were anatomically matched to the noninvolved nodes draining the resected lung lobe. In the majority of the patients (n = 23), the subcarinal or hilar nodes (stations 7 and 10, respectively) were obtained. In other cases, station selection depended on the resected lobe and lack of tumor involvement; for example, subaortic nodes (station 5; n = 3) were analyzed for patients undergoing left lower lobe resections, and right paratracheal nodes (station 4; n = 2) were analyzed for some of the patients undergoing right upper lobe resections. The size of resected lymph nodes ranged from 0.2 cm3 to 0.9 cm3 (median 0.4 cm3). All samples were immediately processed by dicing into small pieces and cryopreserved in freezing medium (90% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide) before downstream processing.

For the TCR–resource cohort, PBMCs were obtained from a total of 61 healthy donors through the San Diego Blood Bank between December 2021 and February 2022. Each donor provided ~8 ml of leukoreduction system whole-blood collection material. Donor ages ranged from 27 to 79 years (median 65). Of the 61 donors, 47 were male, 12 were female, and 2 had unspecified sex information. No clinical or immunological abnormalities were reported for these donors at the time of collection. PBMCs were isolated and cryopreserved according to standard protocols for downstream immunological assays.

HLA typing was performed for class I (HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C) and class II (HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRB3/4/5, HLA-DQA1/DQB1 and HLA-DPB1) alleles in blood samples from the lung cohort. Typing was carried out by an ASHI-accredited laboratory at Murdoch University, as described in ref. 55.

Sample processing

Cryopreserved human lung tissue and LLN samples were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for ~5 min until only a small ice fragment remained, followed by washing with FBS and dilution in MACS buffer. The thawed tissue suspension was filtered through a 70-µm strainer; retained tissue pieces were collected and, in the case of lung tissue, finely diced using a scalpel. The minced tissue was transferred to a gentleMACS C tube (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-096-334) and supplemented with RPMI, after which enzymatic digestion was initiated by addition of DNase I (Sigma, D4527-200K) and Liberase (Sigma, 5401127001). The sample was subjected to mechanical and enzymatic dissociation using an OctoDissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) set at 37 °C for 15 min. Following dissociation, the cell suspension was filtered through a MACS SmartStrainer, with gentle pressing of residual tissue to maximize cell yield. For the single-cell analysis of circulating memory T cells (longitudinal cohort), cryopreserved PBMCs (n = 6) were thawed and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. The resulting cell suspensions (across all sample types) were centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in MACS buffer containing human FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi, 130059901), followed by addition of Cell-hashtag TotalSeq-C antibody (1 μg per condition) to enable hashtag-oligonucleotide-based sample deconvolution at the analysis stage. Cells were then stained at 4 °C for 30 min with a panel of antibodies, as listed in Supplementary Table 8. After washing, the cells were underlaid with FBS and centrifuged again. The final cell pellet was resuspended in cold MACS buffer supplemented with DAPI for viability staining, and cells were sorted for CD3+ T cells (from 40 cross-sectional lung and 8 LLN samples) or CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (from the 6 longitudinal lung and PBMC samples and the 28 LLN samples) on a FACSAria Fusion Cell Sorter (Becton Dickinson) and processed for downstream single-cell sequencing. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v.10).

Cell isolation and single-cell RNA sequencing with the 10x platform

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) library preparation was performed alongside TCR repertoire immune profiling and cell surface protein detection to enable hashtag-oligonucleotide-based sample demultiplexing following the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

Sequencing reads from scRNA-seq data were demultiplexed using Cell Ranger (v.7.1.0, 10x Genomics) with the mkfastq module and aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome (prebuilt reference 2020-A) with the count module. This generated unique molecular identifier (UMI)-collapsed gene expression matrices for 79 single-cell libraries (Supplementary Table 2). Independent gene expression libraries were aggregated into combined datasets using the aggr module, yielding four datasets (Supplementary Table 2): (i) lung CD3+ T cells from 40 donors (lung cohort), (ii) LLN CD3+ T cells from 38 donors (lung cohort), (iii) blood CD3+ T cells from 6 donors at baseline (longitudinal cohort) and (iv) lung CD3+ T cells from 12 longitudinal samples across 6 donors (longitudinal cohort) and 8 unrelated samples that were pooled in the same 10x libraries. Two donors (L034 and L040) were excluded from downstream analyses because of the very low numbers of LLN T cells (<100) recovered, resulting in an LLN dataset comprising 36 donors. Unrelated samples were removed.

Aggregated datasets were processed in R (v.3.6.1) using Seurat (v.3.2.3)56. Genes expressed in fewer than 0.1% of cells were excluded. To minimize doublets and eliminate low-quality transcriptomes while allowing variability in the distribution of UMI and gene counts, we excluded cells that did not meet the following quality control criteria: 500 ≤ UMI count ≤10,000; 400 ≤ gene count ≤3,000; and mitochondrial UMI percentage ≤15%. Data normalization was performed using NormalizeData with default parameters. Highly variable genes were identified with the FindVariableFeatures function using the vst method and default settings; selection was restricted to genes with a mean expression >0.01 and contributing 12% (CD8+ LLN dataset), 25% (blood datasets) or 30% (lung and CD4+ LLN datasets) of the standardized variance. Gene-wise scaling was performed using ScaleData, with regression of total UMI counts and mitochondrial UMI percentages (vars.to.regress argument), and default settings for other arguments. Although fluorescence-activated cell sorting batches showed similar quality-control-related feature variability within each aggregated dataset (Extended Data Fig. 1b), potential batch effects were further minimized using Harmony57 (RunHarmony; v.0.1.1). Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis, followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embedding using RunUMAP with the top 40 PCs, 15 neighbors (n.neighbors = 15) and a minimum distance of 0.1 (min.dist = 0.1). Unsupervised clustering was conducted with FindNeighbors and FindClusters, with a defined resolution, 30 principal components specified per iteration and all other parameters left unchanged. For the last iteration, a resolution parameter between 0.1 and 1 (by steps of 0.1) was selected for each dataset, defined as the value that maximized separation of expected T cell subsets while minimizing overclustering.

Subsets of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were defined through iterative rounds of clustering and phenotype-guided filtering. In each round, contaminant clusters were reassigned to the appropriate lineage-specific dataset for further refinement. A total of 16, 14 and 6 rounds of deconvolution were performed for lung, LLN and blood T cells, respectively, after which no lineage-contaminating clusters could be discerned.

To ensure consistency in cell subset annotation and UMAP embeddings across lung datasets, CD4+ and CD8+ lung T cell datasets from the longitudinal cohort (75,567 and 81,803 cells, respectively) were mapped onto larger reference datasets from the lung cohort (370,233 and 227,800 cells for CD4+ and CD8+, respectively) using Seurat’s reference mapping workflow for scRNA-seq data56. To minimize cohort-specific variation in TCR V and J gene usage, TCR genes were excluded from the set of highly variable genes, followed by recalculation of principal components in the reference datasets (RunPCA, using the same parameters as above). The recommended workflow was then applied as follows: (i) transfer anchors were identified using FindTransferAnchors (reduction = ‘pcaproject’, reference.reduction = ‘pca’, dims = 1:40); (ii) UMAP embeddings for the reference datasets were recomputed to reflect the updated highly variable gene set (RunUMAP, using the same parameters as above, with return.model = TRUE); and (iii) longitudinal datasets were mapped and annotated using MapQuery (reference.reduction = ‘pca’, reduction.model = ‘umap’). This procedure was performed separately for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells following lineage-specific deconvolution, as described above.

Expression levels of PTPRC isoforms (CD45RO and CD45RA) were quantified using IDEIS58 on each scRNA-seq library, using the corresponding cell barcodes from Cell Ranger’s cell calling algorithm as a barcode whitelist, with all other parameters left unchanged.

Single-cell TCR sequence data analysis

Reads from TCR repertoire immune profiling data were processed using Cell Ranger vdj (v.7.1.0) with the prebuilt GRCh38 reference (vdj-GRCh38-alts-ensembl-7.1.0) and aggregated following previously described workflows34,59. For the lung cohort, one aggregated dataset was generated for lung CD3+ T cells (n = 40 libraries, one per donor) and another for LLN CD3+ T cells (n = 21 libraries spanning 36 donors). For the TCR–resource cohort, 45 libraries derived from pathogen-reactive CD4+ T cells were aggregated into a single TCR dataset. For the longitudinal cohort, one aggregated dataset was generated for lung CD3+ T cells (n = 16 libraries spanning 12 samples across 6 donors) and another for blood CD3+ T cells (n = 2 libraries spanning 6 samples). Metrics from individual TCR libraries are presented in Supplementary Table 2. For single-cell TCR data, a TCR clonotype was defined as a unique set of CDR3 α and β chain sequences and the corresponding V and J gene segments.

TCR repertoire diversity was quantified using the Gini index (repDiversity, immunarch26 v.0.9) based on the TCR clonotype definition above, for each donor. TCR repertoires comprised either all TCR clonotypes or those from a given T cell subset, defined by unbiased clustering or phenotype bias (Supplementary Methods). Indices were provided only for TCR repertoires with ≥50 T cells.

Similarity of pairs of donor-matched TCR repertoires was quantified using the Morisita–Horn index (index_morisita, tabula60 v.3.3.2), calculated based on relative frequencies of clonotypes in each repertoire. The union of clonotypes across the two repertoires was used as the universe for each calculation. TCR repertoires were defined as described above, and figure panels specify whether clustering (cell subset) or phenotype bias subset was used as a filtering criterion. Reference values, to be used as positive controls representing maximal similarity between lung and LLN TCR repertoires, were obtained by comparing the two halves of a random split of the combined repertoires for each donor. Cell numbers were normalized to the minimum across donor-matched samples. Reference values were calculated separately for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, yielding a CD4 and a CD8 reference control, respectively.

Individual strategies to assign pathogen specificities to T cells

We developed three complementary strategies to assign pathogen specificities to lung T cells on the basis of their TCRs, as follows.

The donor-matched, circulating, antigen-specific TCR-based (‘donor-matched’) approach relied on pathogen specificities derived from the peptide MP-induced in vitro proliferation assay conducted on donor-matched blood samples (‘assay’ TCRs). CDR3 amino acid sequences from lung TCR clonotypes were compared against those from the assay TCRs, matched by chain, donor and T cell lineage. When a lung TCR clonotype matched an assay TCR with either the CDR3 of the α or β chain alone, its pathogen specificity was directly assigned. When both chains matched, we determined whether the two chains yielded concordant or conflicting specificities. Among all lung TCR clonotypes with a specificity provided by the donor-matched approach, ~16% (585 of 3,656) and ~20% (183 of 906) matched both chains for CD4+ and CD8+ TCRs, respectively. Of these, the majority (~89% (517 of 585) and 96% (176 of 183) of CD4+ and CD8+ TCRs, respectively) showed concordant specificities between the α and the β chains (Supplementary Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 6). Given that the α and the β chain sequences were obtained from independent TCR sequencing experiments for the same sample, the very high concordance between the α and β chains linked to a given pathogen-specific TCR, indicated high specificity of our donor-matched assignment approach.

In rare cases of conflicting assignments, which comprised only 68 CD4+ and 7 CD8+ lung TCR clonotypes (accounting for <1% of all the lung T cells predicted to be reactive to the common pathogens tested), priority was given to the specificity determined by the β chain, ensuring single, unambiguous specificity assignments. Notably, only TCR matches with shared V and J gene segments were considered. To determine the relative specificity when using a single β chain versus an α chain in resolving conflicting assignments, we used our in-house-generated large-scale resource of full-length paired α and β chain TCR sequences specific to ten common human pathogens as a positive control. We estimated the frequency with which single α or β chains from full-length aβ TCRs specific to a given pathogen were also observed in full-length TCRs annotated as specific to other pathogens (nonspecific). For each chain type, we quantified the conflict rate, that is, the proportion of cases in which the same chain appeared in TCRs annotated with different pathogen specificities. The frequency of β chain conflicts was significantly lower than that of α chain conflicts, when TCRs specific to each pathogen were separately tested against each other pathogen (P < 0.0001; Extended Data Fig. 5c). The overall conflict rate for all paired TCRs tested was lower for β chain compared to α chain conflicts (median of 10.3% versus ~17.5%). These results were consistent with those obtained using previously published TCR-based prediction approaches61,62, which showed that the TCR β chain provided a stronger determinant of antigen specificity without diminishing the predictive contribution of the TCR α chain.

The donor-unmatched, reference-match-based (‘reference match’) approach was based on perfect matches and quasi-matches between the lung TCR clonotypes and TCR clonotypes present in the CD4+ and CD8+ TCR reference sets described in the Supplementary Methods. A perfect match was defined as an identical CDR3 amino acid sequence and V and J gene segment for either chain (α or β). A quasi-match was defined as an equally long CDR3 sequence containing exactly one amino acid mismatch, with identical V and J gene segments. Matches and quasi-matches were restricted by chain and T cell lineage, such that lung CD4+ T cells were compared only against the CD4+ TCR reference set and lung CD8+ T cells against the CD8+ TCR reference set. Quasi-matches were attempted only for TCR clonotypes that already had a perfect match for one TCR chain. This process yielded five types of match between lung TCR clonotypes and reference TCR clonotypes: (i) two-chain perfect match, (ii) β-only perfect match, (iii) α-only perfect match, (iv) perfect β match with α quasi-match and (v) perfect α match with β quasi-match (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Except for the α-only perfect matches, all types were considered when assigning pathogen specificity to lung TCR clonotypes. Notably, two-chain matches (types i, iv and v) could involve only one TCR clonotype (perfect match) or multiple TCR clonotypes in the reference set, and the latter could imply concordant (unambiguous) or conflicting (ambiguous) specificities. In cases of ambiguous assignment, priority was given to the specificity dictated by the β chain to ensure an unambiguous call.

Integrative strategy to assign pathogen specificities to T cells

This strategy incorporated individual results from the three approaches described above, with priority assigned in the following order: (1) reference match strategy, (2) donor-matched strategy and (3) UCM strategy. Numbers of TCR clonotypes with assignments by each individual approach within the integrative approach are provided in Extended Data Fig. 5f.
