Development and validation of machine learning models for early diagnosis of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in pediatric Epstein–Barr virus infection
Authors: Yingying Ye, Yuqin Li, Yuewen Su, Meng Cao, Jiaxue Liu, Jiaying Ding, Shaoyan Hu, Jianmei Tian, Weifang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo establish a machine learning (ML) model for the early diagnosis of Epstein-Barr virus-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (EBV-HLH) based on clinical features and to utilize the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method to interpret the ML model, thereby providing reliable factors for diagnosing EBV-HLH.MethodsWe collected clinical data from 1,026 children with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection who were hospitalized at Children’s Hospital of Soochow University from October 2017 to September 2024. First, we compared the clinical data of Epstein-Barr virus-associated infectious mononucleosis (EBV-IM) and EBV-HLH through univariate analysis and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to select key features for machine learning training. Subsequently, we applied six machine learning algorithms and logistic regression to build diagnostic models, and the optimal model was selected based on multiple evaluation metrics. Finally, we utilized the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) algorithm to clarify the importance of variables in the model to facilitate its application in clinical settings.ResultsAmong the six machine learning models and logistic regression evaluated, the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model demonstrated the strongest discrimination ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.9775, sensitivity of 0.9461 and specificity of 0.9784. The SHAP analysis indicated that the most important predictors for EBV-HLH were D-dimer, cervical lymphadenopathy (CLA), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and CD3 + CD4+ T cells.ConclusionsThe XGBoost model demonstrated excellent predictive performance for the early identification of EBV-HLH in children. Compared with other models, it achieved higher sensitivity and may serve as a promising decision-support tool pending external validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03658-z.

Introduction

EBV is a widespread herpesvirus, with over 90% of the world’s population expected to be infected by adulthood [1]. Among symptomatic primary EBV infections, approximately 70% present as infectious mononucleosis [2]. In contrast, EBV-HLH is relatively rare. Although both conditions may present with similar non-specific symptoms, such as fever and hepatosplenomegaly in the early stages, infectious mononucleosis typically resolves within a few weeks and is considered a self-limiting disease with a favorable prognosis [3]. However, EBV-HLH is a rare and potentially life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by cytokine storm syndrome resulting from immune dysregulation. Because the prognosis of EBV-HLH is strongly influenced by the lymphocyte lineage infected by EBV, high-risk subtypes are associated with a poor outcome, with a reported 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 25.1% [4]. Therefore, early identification and treatment of patients with EBV-HLH are crucial for improving prognosis and potentially saving lives.

With the development of artificial intelligence, ML technology has been widely applied in the diagnosis and prognosis of clinical diseases. ML has garnered increasing attention in the medical field, particularly in the early prediction of COVID-19 severity and cancer prognosis, achieving remarkable results [5, 6]. Additionally, machine learning has proven successful in diagnosing two diseases with similar symptoms that are challenging for clinicians to differentiate: early multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children and Kawasaki disease [7]. The clinical symptoms and signs of EBV-IM and EBV-HLH are similar in the early stages, lacking specificity. Furthermore, the diagnostic criteria for EBV-HLH involve a series of time-consuming and labor-intensive tests. Early diagnosis and intervention for EBV-HLH are crucial [8–10]. Currently, the early diagnosis of EBV-HLH relies on the identification of individual clinical factors or the construction of models using traditional statistical methods [11, 12]. however, a systematic and interpretable predictive model remains limited.

This study aimed to develop and internally validate a machine learning-based model for the early identification of EBV-HLH in children. Only routinely available clinical and laboratory variables were included to enhance clinical applicability. Model interpretability was addressed using SHAP, enabling interpretation of model predictions and quantification of feature importance. Specifically, SHAP facilitates ranking of predictors based on their contribution to the model output [13]. By integrating routinely collected clinical data, this study provides a decision-support tool for the early identification of EBV-HLH in children with EBV infection.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This study included children with EBV infection who were hospitalized at Children’s Hospital of Soochow University from October 2017 to September 2024. Those admitted from October 2017 to September 2022 were assigned to the training set, and those admitted from October 2022 to September 2024 were assigned to the test set. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 940 patients with EBV-IM and 86 patients with EBV-HLH were included. We collected clinical information from the electronic medical records of enrolled patients, documenting age, sex, duration of fever, presence of rash, cervical lymphadenopathy, and ultrasonographic findings of the liver and spleen. Additionally, we recorded the first laboratory test results after admission, which included complete blood count, ferritin levels, biochemical parameters, humoral immune parameters, coagulation profiles, and lymphocyte subsets. All predictive variables were obtained within 24 h of hospitalization, prior to the establishment of a definitive EBV-HLH diagnosis and before the initiation of HLH-specific treatment.

Patients who were discharged against medical advice or had incomplete key clinical or laboratory data were excluded. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (2023CS219). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants.

Diagnostic criteria

EBV-IM can be diagnosed when any one of the following two criteria is met [9]: (1) Clinically diagnosed cases: meet any three of the following clinical manifestations and any non-specific laboratory test; (2) Confirmed cases: any three of the following clinical manifestations and any laboratory evidence of primary EBV infection was met. 1. Clinical manifestations were defined as: (a) fever; (b) sore throat; (c) Enlarged cervical lymph nodes; (d) Hepatomegaly; (e) Splenomegaly; (f) Eyelid edema. 2. Laboratory evidence of primary EBV infection was defined as: (a) positive anti-VCA-IgM and anti-VCA-IgG antibodies and negative anti-EBNA-IgG; (b) Single positive anti-VCA-IgG antibody with low affinity. 3.Non-specific laboratory tests were defined as: (a) the proportion of atypical lymphocytes in peripheral blood ≥ 0.10; (b) Peripheral blood lymphocyte proportion > 0.50 or absolute lymphocyte count > 5.0 × 109/L in children over 6 years of age.

The diagnosis of EBV-HLH should meet the following two criteria [10] : (1) HLH Diagnostic Criteria: According to the HLH-2004 protocol, HLH can be diagnosed if five of the following eight criteria are met: (a) Fever. (b) Splenomegaly. (c) Cytopenia, defined as a reduction in at least two of the three peripheral blood lineages: hemoglobin < 90 g/L (< 100 g/L in infants under 4 weeks of age), platelet count < 100 × 109/L, and neutrophil count < 1.0 × 109/L. (d) Hypertriglyceridemia (fasting triglycerides ≥ 3.0 mmol/L) and/or hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen ≤ 1.5 g/L). (e) Hemophagocytosis in the bone marrow, spleen, or lymph nodes without evidence of malignancy. (f) Decreased or absent natural killer (NK) cell activity. (g) Serum ferritin ≥ 500 µg/L. (h) Soluble CD25 (sCD25) ≥ 2.4 × 103 U/L (normal values may vary by laboratory). (2) Evidence of EBV Infection: One of the following criteria must be met: (a) Serological antibody testing indicates primary acute EBV infection (refer to the diagnostic basis for infectious mononucleosis). (b) Molecular biological methods, including PCR, in situ hybridization, and Southern blot, detect EBV in serum, bone marrow, lymph nodes, or other affected tissues. This includes positive serum or plasma EBV-DNA, positive EBV-EBER in affected tissues via in situ hybridization, or positive EBV-LMP1 immunohistochemical staining.

Selection of predictive factors

By comparing the clinical characteristics and laboratory variables of EBV-IM and EBV-HLH, we selected variables with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). We employed the LASSO regression to identify important predictors and eliminate redundant features [14]. This approach facilitates feature selection and helps improve model generalizability by reducing the risk of overfitting.

Model development and performance evaluation

The training set was used for model development, whereas the test set served to evaluate model generalizability. Six commonly used machine learning algorithms were developed, including k-nearest neighbors (KNN), Naive Bayes (NB), support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). Logistic regression (LR) was included as the baseline clinical prediction model for comparison. The overall study workflow is presented in Fig. 1. Within the training set, five-fold cross-validation combined with hyperparameter optimization was performed to identify the optimal parameter configuration for each model. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively to the training set, and class weighting was incorporated into applicable models. For each model, the optimal prediction threshold was determined by the point with the highest Youden index on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve derived from the training set and was subsequently applied to the test set for performance evaluation.

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using the AUC, area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC), accuracy (ACC), balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR−). The model demonstrating the best overall performance was selected as the final prediction model. The calibration performance of the final model was assessed using calibration curves and the Brier score. Calibration curves were generated from 1,000 bootstrap resamples to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. The Brier score quantified the consistency between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, with lower values indicating better calibration. The clinical utility of the final model was further evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA). DCA assesses clinical utility by comparing the net clinical benefit of model-guided intervention strategies with the default strategies of treating all patients or treating none across a range of threshold probabilities. The threshold probability represents the minimum predicted risk above which additional diagnostic or therapeutic intervention would be considered clinically justified [15].

Model explanation

To improve the transparency of machine learning algorithms and enhance clinical interpretation, we evaluated the model and used the SHAP algorithm[13] to clarify its predictions. This method ranks the importance of predictive factors and provides clear outputs. By addressing the "black box" issue, this approach helps clinicians understand the reasons behind model decisions, increasing trust and usability in practical applications.

Statistical analysis

According to the diagnosis, we divided the children into the EBV-IM group and the EBV-HLH group. We analyzed continuous variables using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Since the continuous variables did not conform to a normal distribution, we applied non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test) and presented the results as median and interquartile range (M [P25, P75]). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n [%]) and were compared using the chi-square test. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We constructed the predictive model by implementing six machine learning algorithms. This study used SPSS 27 and Python 3.10.0 for data analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1,026 children with EBV infection were included in the study. Patients admitted between October 2017 and September 2022 were assigned to the training set (n = 752), whereas those admitted between October 2022 and September 2024 were assigned to the test set (n = 274). In the training set, 321 patients (42.7%) were male and 431 (57.3%) were female, with a median age of 4.94 years (interquartile range: 2.23–7.65 years). Among these patients, 693 were diagnosed with EBV-IM and 59 with EBV-HLH. As shown in Table 1, no significant differences were observed in age or sex distribution between the two groups (P > 0.10). The analysis of clinical manifestations and laboratory examination results between the EBV-IM and EBV-HLH groups is shown in Table S1. Compared to the EBV-IM group, children with EBV-HLH had a longer duration of fever (P < 0.05). Additionally, EBV-IM children had a higher likelihood of cervical lymphadenopathy (P < 0.05), while EBV-HLH children showed a greater probability of rash and hepatosplenomegaly (P < 0.05). Most laboratory features differed significantly between the two groups, except for creatinine (CREA) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT).

Feature selection

To reduce the risk of inclusion bias and diagnostic circularity, variables included in the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria and closely related indicators were excluded before feature selection. These variables comprised white blood cell count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte percentage, neutrophil percentage, hemoglobin, platelet count, ferritin, triglycerides, fibrinogen, splenomegaly, and fever-related indicators. LASSO regression was subsequently applied to the remaining 26 clinical features in the training set. Using five-fold cross-validation, the optimal regularization parameter (lambda) was identified as 0.0082 (Fig. 2a and b). Based on this lambda value, five key clinical features with the greatest discriminative value for EBV-HLH were selected and incorporated into model training. These features included D-dimer, LDH, GGT, percentage of CD3 + CD4+ T cells, and CLA.

Machine learning model construction

We developed six machine learning models and a conventional LR model using five selected predictive variables for training and prediction. To address class imbalance, the SMOTE was applied to the training data for oversampling. In addition, class-weighting strategies were incorporated where applicable. A five-fold cross-validation approach combined with hyperparameter optimization was used to determine the optimal parameter settings for each model (Table S2). The discriminative performance of all models is presented in Table S3 and Fig. 3. Among all evaluated models, XGBoost achieved the highest discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.9775 and a precision–recall AUC (PR-AUC) of 0.9042. Compared with the LR model, XGBoost demonstrated higher sensitivity (94.6% vs. 81.0%) and a lower negative likelihood ratio (0.055 vs. 0.195), indicating improved performance in identifying and ruling out EBV-HLH cases. Given its superior overall performance, XGBoost was selected as the final model for subsequent interpretation and validation.

The calibration performance of the XGBoost model was evaluated using calibration curves and the Brier score. As shown in Fig. 4a, the calibration curve based on 1,000 bootstrap resamples closely aligned with the 45° reference line, indicating good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. The Brier score was 0.0224 (95% CI: 0.0086–0.0442), demonstrating good overall calibration performance. DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the models by quantifying net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. As shown in Fig. 4b, the “treat-none” strategy yielded a net benefit of zero across all thresholds, whereas the “treat-all” strategy provided net benefit only when the threshold probability was below 18%. In contrast, the XGBoost model achieved higher net benefit than the “treat-none” strategy across the entire range (0%-88%) and outperformed the “treat-all” strategy within a threshold range of 20%-88%. These results suggest that, across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities, the XGBoost model may provide superior net clinical benefit for individualized decision-making in EBV-HLH screening.

Model explanation

To help clinicians understand and apply machine learning models, we used the SHAP method to explain the output results of the final model by calculating the importance of variables. The SHAP summary diagram provides a global interpretation of the model and describes its overall functionality. As shown in Fig. 5a and b, the average SHAP value evaluates the contribution of features to the model. D-dimer had the strongest predictive value, followed by LDH. The horizontal axis (SHAP value) indicates the positive and negative impacts of each feature on the prediction result. A positive SHAP value indicates a positive impact, while a negative SHAP value indicates a negative impact. Increased D-dimer positively influences the prediction, driving it toward EBV-HLH, whereas increased CLA negatively influences the prediction, pushing it toward EBV-IM. SHAP dependence plots help illustrate how individual features affect the output of the prediction model. SHAP values greater than 0 correspond to a higher risk of EBV-HLH, while values less than 0 indicate a lower risk. As shown in Fig. 5c, D-dimer ≥ 3105.18 µg/L, LDH ≥ 801.66 U/L, percentage of CD3 + CD4+ T cells ≥ 29.3%, GGT ≥ 142.36 U/L, and absence of cervical lymphadenopathy were associated with an increased predicted risk of EBV-HLH.

Local explanations analyze how predictions apply to specific individuals by integrating personalized input data. Figure 5d illustrates the predictions for two different children. The colors indicate the positive and negative influences of the characteristics on the prediction results. The red sections represent features that increase the probability of EBV-HLH, while the blue sections show features that decrease this probability. The length of each section reflects the impact of the feature on the prediction; longer sections indicate a greater influence. Figure 5d-Ⅰ shows a child with EBV-IM. According to the prediction model, all five clinical features for this child reduce the likelihood of EBV-HLH. Based on this information, the model predicts that the child is at low risk for EBV-HLH. Figure 5d-Ⅱ shows a child with EBV-HLH. The prediction model identifies four features, such as D-dimer, LDH, GGT, and percentage of CD3 + CD4+ T cells, that increase the probability of EBV-HLH. The length of the sections indicates that D-dimer has the largest impact, followed by LDH. Therefore, based on this information, the model predicts a high risk of EBV-HLH for this child.

Discussion

This study was based on routine laboratory indicators available at the early stage of hospitalization in pediatric patients with EBV infection. Six machine learning models and a logistic regression model were developed and compared. Among all evaluated models, XGBoost showed the best overall performance. Logistic regression also demonstrated good discriminative ability, while XGBoost achieved higher sensitivity and a lower negative likelihood ratio. Given that EBV-HLH is a rapidly progressive disease associated with poor outcomes, reducing the risk of missed diagnosis is clinically important, making the higher sensitivity of XGBoost advantageous in early risk screening settings. SHAP analysis further identified the most important predictors in the model, including D-dimer, LDH, CD3⁺CD4⁺ T-cell percentage, GGT, and cervical lymphadenopathy. These findings suggest that machine learning methods based on routinely available early hospitalization data may help identify pediatric patients with EBV infection who are at high risk of developing EBV-HLH.

Previous studies have attempted to develop early identification models for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). A study based on data from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University constructed a predictive model using multivariable logistic regression and achieved good diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.953) by integrating soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sCD25) and other clinical indicators [16]. sCD25 is an important component of the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria [10]. However, in the present cohort, a substantial proportion of patients lacked sCD25 measurements. To avoid a marked reduction in sample size and potential selection bias caused by missing data, this variable was not included in model development. A multicenter study from the Netherlands enrolled 165 patients with suspected HLH and applied partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and principal component analysis (PCA) to identify a minimal set of variables for early decision support, achieving an AUC of 0.95 [17]. Consistent with previous studies, the present work also focused on early-stage risk identification, while further exploring the application value of machine learning algorithms in the diagnosis of EBV-HLH.

The results indicated that D-dimer was the most important predictor in the present study, with elevated levels reflecting a marked imbalance between the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems in patients with EBV-HLH [18]. Previous studies have shown that children with HLH commonly present with significant coagulation abnormalities, including prolonged PT and APTT, as well as markedly elevated D-dimer levels. Coagulation dysfunction in infection-associated HLH is generally more severe than that observed in other subtypes [19]. Further studies have shown that D-dimer levels are markedly elevated in patients with HLH complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and are closely associated with bleeding events and poor clinical outcomes [20]. From a mechanistic perspective, during the development of EBV-HLH, persistently activated macrophages and T lymphocytes release large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6. These cytokines trigger the coagulation cascade and cause endothelial injury, ultimately leading to widespread microthrombus formation [21, 22]. Meanwhile, activation of the fibrinolytic system occurs to clear these microthrombi, resulting in increased production of D-dimer [23]. Therefore, D-dimer not only reflects activation of coagulation and enhanced fibrinolysis, but also serves as a sensitive marker of systemic inflammatory burden and endothelial injury. Previous studies have further shown that elevated D-dimer levels are associated with increased disease severity and higher mortality risk in HLH [24]. Overall, D-dimer levels have clinical value in both the diagnosis and prognosis of EBV-HLH, warranting further investigation to clarify their role in patient outcomes.

In addition to D-dimer, LDH and GGT were also identified as important predictors. LDH is a key biomarker of cellular injury and tissue necrosis, and its elevation mainly reflects extensive cell damage caused by sustained immune hyperactivation and cytokine storm in EBV-HLH, including injury to lymphocytes, monocyte–macrophage cells, and parenchymal cells in organs such as the liver [25]. In addition, LDH is a core component of the endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX), and its elevation has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, microvascular injury, and poor clinical outcomes [26]. Because EBV-HLH is frequently accompanied by multi-organ involvement and systemic inflammatory responses, LDH not only reflects the extent of tissue injury but also partially indicates the systemic severity of the disease [27]. Overall, elevated LDH levels are closely associated with disease activity and severity in EBV-HLH. Therefore, LDH was identified as an important predictor in the present model and may provide valuable information for the early identification of EBV-HLH. GGT is a commonly used cholestasis-related hepatic enzyme and also demonstrated strong predictive value in the present study. GGT not only reflects hepatobiliary involvement but is also considered to be associated with oxidative stress and an intensified systemic inflammatory response. In EBV-HLH, sustained immune activation and inflammation can lead to hepatic injury and cholestasis, thereby resulting in elevated GGT levels [28].

In the present study, the percentage of CD4 + T cells was identified as an important predictor of EBV-HLH. SHAP analysis further indicated that an elevated CD4 + T-cell percentage was associated with an increased risk of EBV-HLH. They contribute to humoral immune responses, indirectly activate macrophages, and directly suppress inflammation [29]. EBV-HLH can lead to persistent activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. This excessive immune activation results in the release of large amounts of cytokines and induces abnormal proliferation of immune cells, thereby leading to an increased percentage of CD4⁺ T cells [21]. Additionally, we found that the incidence of cervical lymph node enlargement in children with EBV-IM was higher than in those with EBV-HLH. When the EB virus invades the body, it triggers a local inflammatory response, leading to the transport of pathogens to the lymph nodes and initiating a series of immune reactions that result in lymph node enlargement. However, in children with EBV-HLH, the excessive activation of the immune system causes a systemic inflammatory response that is not limited to local lymph nodes, which may explain the lack of significant lymph node enlargement in these patients [30].

The study has several strengths. First, the model was developed using only routine clinical and laboratory variables available within 24 h after hospital admission, allowing early risk assessment before completion of the full HLH diagnostic workup. Second, the SHAP algorithm was applied to interpret model predictions, improving model transparency and partially addressing the “black-box” limitation of machine learning models [31]. Third, a comprehensive evaluation was performed using multiple metrics, including discrimination performance, calibration, and decision curve analysis, to assess the model from different perspectives. The present study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively limited sample size, which may introduce selection bias and restrict the generalizability of the model. Second, although the XGBoost model demonstrated good performance in internal validation, simpler predictive models may offer advantages in clinical implementation. Further studies are needed to compare the clinical utility of different modeling approaches. In addition, external validation was not performed in the present study, and the applicability of the model across different regions and healthcare settings remains to be evaluated. Although SHAP analysis was used to improve model interpretability, the stability of feature importance rankings was not assessed using bootstrap resampling. Therefore, these findings require further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Finally, a prototype prediction tool was developed based on the final XGBoost model to demonstrate its potential clinical applicability. However, this tool is intended for research purposes only, has not been publicly deployed, and should not be used as an independent clinical decision-making tool prior to adequate external validation.

Future studies should not only focus on external validation but also expand data dimensions and modeling frameworks. The current model was developed using routine clinical and laboratory variables available within 24 h of hospitalization. Future work could further integrate standardized nursing data from electronic health records to better capture subtle clinical changes and nursing response patterns during early disease progression. Previous studies have shown that standardized nursing data can identify patient patterns associated with ICU transfer and clinical deterioration, highlighting its potential value in early warning systems [32]. Therefore, future predictive models for EBV-HLH could integrate laboratory parameters, clinical signs, and nursing data to develop a multimodal framework, thereby improving early identification performance and real-world applicability [33]. In addition, advanced machine learning architectures incorporating attention mechanisms could be explored to further improve model performance, robustness, and clinical applicability [34].

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the XGBoost model is a promising tool for the early identification of EBV-HLH. To validate these findings, multicenter studies and large-scale prospective research should be conducted.
