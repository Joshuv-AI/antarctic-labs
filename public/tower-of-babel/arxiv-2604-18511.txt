An adaptive discretization algorithm for locally optimal experimental design with constraints
Jochen Schmid, Philipp Seufert, Jan Schwientek, Tobias Seidel, Karl-Heinz Küfer Fraunhofer Institute for Industrial Mathematics (ITWM), 67663 Kaiserslautern, Germany jochen.schmid@itwm.fraunhofer.de

Abstract
We develop a novel iterative algorithm for locally optimal experimental design under constraints, like budget or performance constraints. It is an adaptive discretization algorithm. In every iteration, a discretized version of the constrained-design problem is solved and then the discretization is adaptively refined by adding an approximate violator of a suitable sufficient ε \varepsilon -optimality condition for the current design. We prove that with ε = 0 \varepsilon=0 , our algorithm converges to an optimal design and that with ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , our algorithm finitely terminates at an ε \varepsilon -optimal design. Compared to the existing algorithms on constrained experimental design, our algorithm comes with considerably less computational effort because the nonlinear subproblems in our algorithm have a smaller dimension and have to be solved only approximately and only in selected iterations (typically the last few). Additionally, our algorithm covers a considerably larger class of constraints. We demonstrate the good convergence properties of the algorithm on experimental design problems from chemical engineering that feature time and yield constraints.

Introduction

A large variety of scientific and industrial applications nowadays rely heavily on mathematical models for prediction and optimization. In particular, this is true for chemical process engineering. It is crucial, of course, that the employed models are sufficiently precise. In order to obtain precise models, one typically performs experiments and collects data, to which the model is then fitted. Yet, performing experiments is usually time- and cost-intensive. It is therefore important to find experimental plans, or designs, which are maximally informative, that is, lead to the highest possible model precision. Coming up with such maximally informative experimental designs is the core task of unconstrained optimal experimental design, and there is a tremendous amount of literature on such unconstrained design problems. In the first step, a saddle point of a discretized version of the constrained design problem (

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi(X^{k})}\Psi_{0}(\xi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{i}(\xi)\leq 0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}\text{ and }\Psi_{i}(\xi)=0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)\geq-\varepsilon\qquad(x\in X).

(1.5)

In analogy to unconstrained design, this sensitivity function, or more precisely, the function

In the first version of our algorithm – called the special algorithm in the following – the point

Setting and preliminaries

In this section, we formally introduce the constrained design problems considered in this paper, establish their solvability as well as sufficient and necessary optimality conditions for their solutions.
In this paper, we consider constrained locally optimal experimental design problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi(X)}\Psi_{0}(\xi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{i}(\xi)\leq 0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}\text{ and }\Psi_{i}(\xi)=0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}},

2.1
Solvability of the constrained design problem

In this section, we establish the existence of optimal solutions for (

Condition 2.1
.

(ii)

With these continuity and lower semicontinuity assumptions and the compactness of

Theorem 2.2
.

If Condition

Proof.

It immediately follows by the lower semicontinuity of the inequality constraint functions and the continuity of the equality constraint functions that

for (

\displaystyle g_{i}(x):=\Psi_{i}(\delta_{x})\qquad(x\in X)

(2.14)

for
i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
It is then straightforward to verify from the assumed continuity of
\Psi_{i}:\Xi(X)\to\mathbb{R}
(Condition
2.1
(iii)) that
g_{i}\in C(X,\mathbb{R})
for every
i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}
. It is further clear by the assumed affinity of
\Psi_{i}
(Condition
2.3
(iii)) that

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi)=\Psi_{i}\bigg(\sum_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\xi}\lambda_{x}\delta_{x}\bigg)=\sum_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\xi}\lambda_{x}\Psi_{i}(\delta_{x})=\int_{X}g_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)

(2.15)

holds true for every
\xi=\sum_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\xi}\lambda_{x}\delta_{x}\in\Xi(X)
with finite support. Since the probability measures of finite support are dense in
\Xi(X)
(Theorem II.6.3 of
[
32
]
), the equality (
2.15
) extends to arbitrary
\xi\in\Xi(X)
by continuity of
\Psi_{i}
. So, (
2.12
) is proven, and with (
2.12
) at hand, (
2.13
) and the continuity of
\psi_{i}
are trivial to prove.
∎

We now show that for design criteria and constraint functions of the form from Condition
2.4
(with the non-continuous
g_{i}
additionally assumed to take only finitely many values), there exists an optimal solution to (
2.1
) with finite support. We also give an upper bound
d
on the number of support points, see (
2.30
) below.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
f_{1}:X\to V_{1}
is a continuous function and
f_{2}:X\to V_{2}
is a (componentwise) lower semicontinuous function with finitely many values, where
X
is a compact metric space and
V_{1}
is a
d_{1}
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{R}
and
V_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
with some
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for every
\xi^{*}\in\Xi(X)
, there exists a design
\eta^{*}\in\Xi(X)
with at most
d_{1}+d_{2}+1
support points such that

\displaystyle\int_{X}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}=\int_{X}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{X}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}\leq\int_{X}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}.

(2.16)

Proof.

In the entire proof, we write
f(x):=(f_{1}(x),f_{2}(x))
and
V:=V_{1}\times V_{2}
. Since
f_{1}
is bounded and
f_{2}
takes only finitely many values, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a finite cover
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of the range
f(X)
that is of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{C_{1}\times\{c_{2}\}:C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{1n}\text{ and }c_{2}\in f_{2}(X)\}

(2.17)

and that consists of pairwise disjoint measurable sets whose diameters go to
0
as
n\to\infty
, that is,

\displaystyle\sup_{C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{1n}}\operatorname{diam}(C_{1})\longrightarrow 0\qquad(n\to\infty).

(2.18)

In order to see this, we can take an arbitrary coordinate mapping (linear isomorphism)
\kappa:V_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, choose
R\in(0,\infty)
so large that
\kappa(f_{1}(X))\subset[-R,R)^{d_{1}}
and define
\mathcal{C}_{1n}
to be the following finite cover of
f_{1}(X)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1n}:=\Big\{\kappa^{-1}(Q):Q=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d_{1}}[-R+\frac{k_{i}}{2^{n}}2R,-R+\frac{k_{i}+1}{2^{n}}2R)\text{ with }k_{1},\dots,k_{d_{1}}\in\{0,\dots,2^{n}-1\}\Big\}.

With the covers
\mathcal{C}_{n}
at hand, we now define the functions
f^{n}:X\to V
by

\displaystyle f^{n}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\text{ s.t. }f^{-1}(C)\neq\emptyset}f(x^{n}_{C})\cdot\mathrm{I}_{f^{-1}(C)},

(2.19)

where
x^{n}_{C}
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
with
f^{-1}(C)\neq\emptyset
is chosen such that

\displaystyle f(x_{C}^{n})\in C.

(2.20)

Since
f
is measurable by assumption and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
consists of finitely many measurable sets by construction,
f^{n}
is a measurable function with finitely many values. Since, moreover, the sets in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and cover
f(X)
, for every
x\in X
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a unique element
C:=C_{f(x)}^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
such that
C\ni f(x)
, in short:

\displaystyle C_{f(x)}^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{f(x)}^{n}\ni f(x).

(2.21)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle f^{n}(x)=f(x^{n}_{C_{f(x)}^{n}})\qquad(x\in X,n\in\mathbb{N})

(2.22)

and, in particular,

\displaystyle f^{n}(X)\subset f(X)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{x\in X,n\in\mathbb{N}}|f_{1}^{n}(x)|\leq\sup_{x\in X}|f_{1}(x)|<\infty.

(2.23)

Combining (
2.20
) (
2.21
) and (
2.22
), we further conclude that for given
x\in X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
the values
f^{n}(x)
and
f(x)
both belong to
C_{f(x)}^{n}=C_{1f(x)}^{n}\times\{c_{2f(x)}^{n}\}
. And therefore, for every
x\in X
we have

\displaystyle|f_{1}^{n}(x)-f_{1}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{diam}C_{1f(x)}^{n}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(n\to\infty)

(2.24)

\displaystyle f_{2}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{2f(x)}^{n}=f_{2}(x)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})

(2.25)

by virtue of (
2.18
).
With these preparations at hand, we can now conclude the proof of the lemma. As an immediate consequence of the finiteness of
f^{n}(X)
and of (
2.23
.a), we see that

\displaystyle\int_{X}f^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\in\operatorname{conv}f(X)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

(2.26)

And so, by Carathéodory’s theorem, we conclude that
\int_{X}f^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be written as a convex combination of at most
d:=\dim V+1=d_{1}+d_{2}+1
elements from
f(X)
, that is, there are
x_{1}^{n},\dots,x_{d}^{n}\in X
and
\lambda_{1}^{n},\dots,\lambda_{d}^{n}\in[0,1]
with

\displaystyle\int_{X}f^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{n}f(x_{i}^{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{n}=1\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

(2.27)

As a consequence of (
2.23
.b) and (
2.24
) and (
2.25
), we further see by the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\int_{X}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}f_{1}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{X}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}=\int_{X}f_{2}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

(2.28)

Combining now (
2.27
) with (
2.28
) and passing to a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that the sequences
(x_{i}^{n_{k}})
and
(\lambda_{i}^{n_{k}})
become convergent, we conclude by the continuity of
f_{1}
and the (componentwise) lower semicontinuity of
f_{2}
that

\displaystyle\int_{X}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}f_{1}(x_{i}^{*})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{X}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}f_{2}(x_{i}^{*}),

(2.29)

where
\lambda_{i}^{*}
and
x_{i}^{*}
denote the limits of
(x_{i}^{n_{k}})
and
(\lambda_{i}^{n_{k}})
, of course.
Setting
\eta^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{*}\delta_{x_{i}^{*}}
, the assertion of the lemma is then clear by (
2.27
.b) and (
2.29
).
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose that Condition
2.4
is satisfied and that, in addition, the functions
g_{i}
for
i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq,2}}
take only finitely many values. Suppose further that
\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)\neq\emptyset
and let
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
. Then the design problem (
2.1
) has an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution with at most

\displaystyle d:=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{d_{i}(d_{i}+1)}{2}+|I_{2}|+|I_{3}|+1

(2.30)

support points, where
I_{1}
is a minimal subset of
\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq,1}}
with
\{m_{i}:i\in I_{1}\}=\{m_{i}:i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq,1}}\}
and where
I_{2}:=I_{\mathrm{ineq,2}}
and
I_{3}:=I_{\mathrm{eq}}
.

Proof.

Since
\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)\neq\emptyset
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution
\xi^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
to (
2.1
) by virtue of Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
2.5
. We now define
V_{\mathrm{c}}:=\bigtimes_{i\in I_{1}}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}^{I_{3}}
and
V_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}}
and let

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{c}}(x):=\big((m_{i}(x))_{i\in I_{1}},(g_{i}(x))_{i\in I_{3}})\big)\in V_{\mathrm{c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{\mathrm{nc}}(x):=(g_{i}(x))_{i\in I_{2}}\in V_{\mathrm{nc}}

(2.31)

for
x\in X
. It is then clear by our assumptions that
f_{\mathrm{c}}:X\to V_{\mathrm{c}}
is a continuous function and that
f_{\mathrm{nc}}:X\to V_{\mathrm{nc}}
is a lower semicontinuous function with finitely many values. So, by Lemma
2.7
, there exists a design
\eta^{*}\in\Xi(X)
with at most

\displaystyle\dim V_{\mathrm{c}}+\dim V_{\mathrm{nc}}+1=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{d_{i}(d_{i}+1)}{2}+|I_{2}|+|I_{3}|+1=d

(2.32)

support points and with

\displaystyle\int_{X}f_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}=\int_{X}f_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{X}f_{\mathrm{nc}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}\leq\int_{X}f_{\mathrm{nc}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}.

(2.33)

As a consequence of (
2.31
) and (
2.33
), we conclude that

\displaystyle M_{i}(\eta^{*})=M_{i}(\xi^{*})\qquad(i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq,1}}),\qquad\int_{X}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}\leq\int_{X}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq,2}}),

(2.34)

\displaystyle\int_{X}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}=\int_{X}g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{*}\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}),

(2.35)

where for the first equality we used that for every
i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq,1}}
there is an
i_{0}\in I_{1}
with
m_{i}=m_{i_{0}}
. Since
\xi^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
as an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution is in particular feasible for (
2.1
), it follows from (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) that

\displaystyle\eta^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{0}(\eta^{*})=\Psi_{0}(\xi^{*}).

(2.36)

And therefore
\eta^{*}
is an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution to (
2.1
) which by (
2.32
) has at most
d
support points, as desired.
∎

2.2
Characterization of optimal designs

In this section, we characterize the solutions of (
2.1
) in terms of saddle points of (
2.1
), that is, saddle points of the Lagrange function of (
2.1
).

2.2.1
Sufficient optimality conditions

In this paragraph, we show that every saddle point
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})
of (
2.1
) gives rise to a solution
\xi^{*}
of (
2.1
) (Lemma
2.9
). Additionally, we establish the sufficient
\varepsilon
-optimality condition indicated in the introduction (Corollary
2.11
).
Whenever Condition
2.1
is satisfied, we will denote the Lagrange function of (
2.1
) by
L
. In other words, the function
L:\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle L(\xi,\lambda):=\Psi_{0}(\xi)+\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad((\xi,\lambda)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda),

(2.37)

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X):=\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{0}\cap\bigcap_{i\in I}\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda:=[0,\infty)^{I_{\mathrm{ineq}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{I_{\mathrm{eq}}}.

(2.38)

In view of the definition (
2.2
) of
\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)=\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{0}\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X).

(2.39)

As usual, a saddle point of (
2.1
) (or of
L
) is defined to be a point
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle L(\xi^{*},\lambda)\leq L(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})\leq L(\xi,\lambda^{*})

(2.40)

for every
\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and every
\lambda\in\Lambda
. It is straightforward to show and well-known that every saddle point of (
2.1
) gives rise to a solution of (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that Condition
2.1
is satisfied and that
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda
is a saddle point of (
2.1
). Then
\xi^{*}
is a solution of (
2.1
) satisfying the complementarity condition

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{*}\Psi_{i}(\xi^{*})=0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}).

(2.41)

Proof.

We can argue in exactly the same way as in
[
28
]
(Theorem 5.3.1 and 5.3.2) or in
[
14
]
(Theorem 2), for instance.
∎

Whenever both Condition
2.1
and Condition
2.3
are satisfied, we write
\psi^{L}
for the function
\psi^{L}:\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda\times X\to\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x):=\psi_{0}(\xi,x)+\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\qquad((\xi,\lambda,x)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda\times X).

(2.42)

It is straightforward to verify that
\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times X\ni(\xi,x)\mapsto\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x)
is the sensitivity function of
L(\cdot,\lambda)|_{\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)}
w.r.t.
\Xi(X)
for every
\lambda\in\Lambda
. In this context, it should be noticed, however, that
X\ni x\mapsto\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x)
is not continuous in general. After all, Condition
2.3
allows the sensitivity functions of the inequality constraint functions to be discontinuous. Specifically, writing

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\{i\in I:\psi_{i}\text{ is continuous}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\{i\in I:\psi_{i}\text{ is not continuous}\}

(2.43)

for the subsets of
I
for which the sensitivity function
\psi_{i}
of
\Psi_{i}|_{\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}}
is continuous or discontinuous, respectively, we see that
I=I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}\cup I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}
and, by Condition
2.3
and Lemma
2.6
, we also see that

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{eq}}\subset I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}\subset I_{\mathrm{ineq}},

(2.44)

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(\xi,x)=g_{i}(x)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad((\xi,x)\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}\times X\text{ and }i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}).

(2.45)

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
are satisfied. Then

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\eta)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\geq\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)

(2.46)

for every
\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}
and
\eta\in\Xi(X)
for every
i\in\{0\}\cup I
. In particular,

\displaystyle L(\eta,\lambda)-L(\xi,\lambda)\geq\int_{X}\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)

(2.47)

for every
\xi,\eta\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and every
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Additionally,
\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
is directionally open in the following sense: for every
\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and every
\eta\in\Xi(X)
, there exists an
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
such that
\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
for all
\alpha\in[0,\alpha^{*}]
. And finally,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x)\in\mathbb{R}\qquad((\xi,\lambda)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\times\Lambda).

(2.48)

Proof.

Since
\Psi_{i}
is convex for every
i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq}}
and even affine for every
i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}
, we see that

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi))-\Psi_{i}(\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\big(\Psi_{i}(\eta)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\big)\qquad(i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(2.49)

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi))-\Psi_{i}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\alpha\big(\Psi_{i}(\eta)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\big)\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}})

(2.50)

for all
\xi,\eta\in\Xi(X)
. Since, moreover,
\Psi_{i}|_{\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}}
is directionally differentiable w.r.t.
\Xi(X)
with sensitivity function
\psi_{i}
(Condition
2.3
and Lemma
2.6
), it follows from (
2.49
) and (
2.50
) that

\displaystyle\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{i}(\eta)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad(\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}\text{ and }i\in\{0\}\cup I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(2.51)

\displaystyle\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{i}(\eta)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad(\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}\text{ and }i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}})

(2.52)

for every
\eta\in\Xi(X)
. In view of (
2.38
), (
2.42
), (
2.51
) and (
2.52
), the assertions (
2.46
) and (
2.47
) are now clear.
We now move on to prove the directional openness of
\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. So, let
\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and let
\eta\in\Xi(X)
be given. We then know from Condition
2.3
and Lemma
2.6
that for every
i\in\{0\}\cup I
the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\frac{\Psi_{i}(\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi))-\Psi_{i}(\xi)}{\alpha}=\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\in\mathbb{R}

(2.53)

exists in
\mathbb{R}
and therefore
\Psi_{i}(\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi))
must be an element of
\mathbb{R}
for
\alpha
close enough to
0
. In other words,
\xi+\alpha(\eta-\xi)\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
for
\alpha
close enough to
0
, as desired.
It remains to prove the finiteness assertion (
2.48
). So, let
\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Since
X
is compact and non-empty (Condition
2.1
) and since the sensitivity functions
\psi_{i}
are continuous for
i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}\cup\{0\}
(Condition
2.3
), we obtain the boundedness

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}|\psi_{i}(\xi,x)|=\max_{x\in X}|\psi_{i}(\xi,x)|<\infty\qquad(i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}\cup\{0\}).

(2.54)

Since the sensitivity functions
\psi_{i}
for
i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}
are of the form (
2.45
) with bounded measurable functions
g_{i}
(Condition
2.3
), we also obtain the boundedness

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}|\psi_{i}(\xi,x)|<\infty\qquad(i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}).

(2.55)

In view of (
2.54
) and (
2.55
), it is now clear that
X\ni x\mapsto\psi^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x)
is bounded. In particular, it is lower bounded and thus the assertion (
2.48
) is proved.
∎

With the help of the previous lemma, we can now establish an important sufficient condition for the
\varepsilon
-optimality of a design
\xi^{*}
, namely in terms of the infimum of the sensitivity function
X\ni x\mapsto\psi^{L}(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*},x)
. (As has been pointed out after the definition (
2.42
), the sensitivity function is not continuous in general and therefore the infimum is not a minimum in general.)

Corollary 2.11
.

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
are satisfied. Suppose further that

\displaystyle\xi^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda

(2.56)

and
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
are such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{*}\Psi_{i}(\xi^{*})=0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*},x)\geq-\varepsilon.

(2.57)

Then
\xi^{*}
is an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution of (
2.1
). In particular,
\xi^{*}
is an
\varepsilon^{*}
-optimal solution of (
2.1
) with
\varepsilon^{*}:=|\inf_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*},x)|
.

Proof.

In view of (
2.56
.a) and of the solvability theorem (Theorem
2.2
), there exists an (exactly) optimal design
\eta^{*}
with
\eta^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
. In short,

\displaystyle\eta^{*}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{0}(\eta^{*})=\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)}\Psi_{0}(\xi).

(2.58)

With the help of (
2.47
), it now follows by (
2.56
)-(
2.58
) that

\displaystyle-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*},x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta^{*}(x)\leq L(\eta^{*},\lambda^{*})-L(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(\eta^{*})+\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}}\lambda_{i}^{*}\Psi_{i}(\eta^{*})-\Psi_{0}(\xi^{*})\leq\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)}\Psi_{0}(\xi)-\Psi_{0}(\xi^{*}).

(2.59)

In view of (
2.56
.a) and (
2.2.1
), it is now clear that
\xi^{*}
is an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution, as desired.
Applying the first part of the assertion with
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon^{*}:=|\inf_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*},x)|\in[0,\infty)
(Lemma
2.10
), we finally see that
\xi^{*}
is also an
\varepsilon^{*}
-optimal solution, as desired.
∎

2.2.2
A necessary optimality condition

In this paragraph, we show that under a suitable regularity condition, the converse implication of Lemma
2.9
holds true as well, that is, every solution
\xi^{*}
gives rise to a saddle point
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})
of (
2.1
) (Theorem
2.15
). We will use this implication to ensure that all the discretized design problems occurring in our algorithm really have a saddle point. See the proof of Lemma
3.4
below. Compared to the Robinson-Zowe-Kurcyusz regularity condition
[
36
,
51
]
used in
[

We call a subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
– with
J
a finite index set –
one-sided w.r.t.
0
iff there is a vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle Z\subset\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{J}:\nu^{\top}w\geq 0\}.

(2.60)

In other words, a subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
is one-sided w.r.t.
0
iff
Z
is completely contained in one of the two closed sides (half-spaces) of a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{J}
that passes through
0
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose
h:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{J}
is a mapping from some set
Y
to
\mathbb{R}^{J}
with some finite set
J\neq\emptyset
and let
Z:=h(Y)
be the range of
h
.

(i)

If
Z
is not one-sided w.r.t.
0
, then the component mappings
\{h_{j}:j\in J\}
are linearly independent.

(ii)

If
Z
has a non-empty intersection with the interior of every orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
, then
Z
is not one-sided w.r.t.
0
. In particular,
Z
is not one-sided w.r.t.
0
if

lies in the interior of
Z
.

Proof.

Assertion (i) immediately follows by the definitions of linear dependence and of one-sidedness w.r.t.
0
.
Assertion (ii) easily follows by the definition as well. Indeed, let
Z
have a non-empty intersection with the interior of every orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{J}
and assume, for the sake of argument, that
Z
is one-sided w.r.t.
0
. It then follows that there is a
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\nu^{\top}z\geq 0
for all

. In particular,

\displaystyle J^{*}:=\{j\in J:\nu_{j}\neq 0\}\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq\nu^{\top}z=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}z_{j}=\sum_{j\in J^{*}}\nu_{j}z_{j}\qquad(z\in Z).

(2.61)

Choose now a
z^{*}\in Z
that belongs to the interior of an orthant opposite from

or, more precisely, a point
z^{*}
such that

\displaystyle z^{*}\in Z\qquad\text{and}\qquad z^{*}_{j}\neq 0\quad(j\in J)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{sgn}(z^{*}_{j})=-\operatorname{sgn}(\nu_{j})\quad(j\in J^{*}),

(2.62)

where

denotes the sign of the non-zero real number

into (

As a first step, we show that the sets

\displaystyle C:=\big\{\big(\partial_{\eta-\xi^{*}}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{*})+\alpha,\,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{ineq}}(\xi^{*})+\partial_{\eta-\xi^{*}}\Psi_{\mathrm{ineq}}(\xi^{*})+\beta,\Psi_{\mathrm{eq}}(\eta)\big):

Since

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Psi_{i}(\xi^{*}+t(\hat{\eta}-\xi^{*}))}{t}=\partial_{\hat{\eta}-\xi^{*}}\Psi_{i}(\xi^{*})=-\hat{\beta}_{i}<0

3
Adaptive discretization algorithm

In this section, we formally introduce our algorithms to compute optimal experimental designs for (
2.1
).
As already indicated in the introduction, our algorithms are adaptive discretization algorithms which in every iteration proceed in two steps.
In the first step, a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
of the discretized design problem

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{k})}\Psi_{0}(\xi)

(3.1)

is computed, where
X^{k}
is the current discretization of the design space
X
. In view of the finiteness of
X^{k}
, every design
\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{k})
is of the form
\xi=\sum_{x\in X}w_{x}\delta_{x}
and thus, by the convexity and affinity of the objective and constraint functions, the discretized design problem (
3.1
) is a finite-dimensional (namely,
|X^{k}|
-dimensional) convex optimization problem, which is equivalent to the convex weight optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{w\in\Delta(X^{k})}\tilde{\Psi}_{0}^{k}(w)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\tilde{\Psi}_{i}^{k}(w)\leq 0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}\text{ and }\tilde{\Psi}_{i}^{k}(w)=0\text{ for all }i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}

(3.2)

with
\Delta(X^{k}):=\{w\in[0,\infty)^{X^{k}}:\sum_{x\in X^{k}}w_{x}=1\}
and
\tilde{\Psi}_{i}^{k}(w):=\Psi_{i}(\sum_{x\in X^{k}}w_{x}\delta_{x})
for
i\in I\cup\{0\}
.
In the second step, the discretization is updated in an adaptive manner. Specifically, the new discretization

\displaystyle X^{k+1}=X^{k}\cup\{x^{k}\}

(3.3)

arises from the old one by adding one point, namely an approximate violator
x^{k}
of the sufficient
\varepsilon
-optimality condition (
2.57
.b) in the sense of (
1.6
).
In the first version of our algorithm – the special algorithm – we set the optimality tolerance
\varepsilon=0
in (
2.57
.b) and compute
x^{k}
as a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution of the sensitivity minimization problem (
1.7
). As usual, this means that

\displaystyle x^{k}\in X\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x^{k})\leq\inf_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)+\underline{\delta}_{k}

(3.4)

or, in other words, that
\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x^{k})-\underline{\delta}_{k}
is a lower bound for the sensitivity function
X\ni x\mapsto\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)
at
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
.
In the case where this lower bound is larger than or equal to
0
, the current iterate
\xi^{k}
already is an optimal design for (
2.1
) (Corollary
2.11
) and we can terminate the iteration. In the opposite case, we continue the iteration with the new discretization (
3.3
).

Algorithm 3.1
.

Input: a non-empty finite subset
X^{0}
of
X
and tolerances
\underline{\delta}_{k}\in(0,\infty)
. Initialize
k=0
and proceed in the following steps.

1.

Compute a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})\in\Xi(X^{k})\times\Lambda
of the discretized optimal design problem (
3.1
)

2.

Compute a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution
x^{k}\in X
of the sensitivity minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x).

(3.5)

If
\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x^{k})-\underline{\delta}_{k}<0
, then set
X^{k+1}:=X^{k}\cup\{x^{k}\}
and return to Step 1 with
k
replaced by
k+1
. If
\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x^{k})-\underline{\delta}_{k}\geq 0
, then terminate.

In almost all applications, the sensitivity minimization problem (
3.5
) is a nonlinear optimization problem so that its approximate solution is computationally expensive, in general. Also, the special algorithm above is not guaranteed to terminate after finitely many iterations.
In the second version of our algorithm – the general algorithm – we therefore confine the approximate solution of the sensitivity minimization problems to as few iterations as possible and, moreover, we work with positive optimality tolerances
\varepsilon>0
to guarantee finite termination.
Specifically, in every iteration we first search for an arbitrary violator of the sufficient
\varepsilon
-optimality condition (
2.57
.b) at
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
, that is, for a point
x^{k}\in X
with

\displaystyle\psi^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x^{k})<-\varepsilon.

(3.6)

We can use an arbitrary finite search routine for this purpose, typically a multistart local search. And only if this search routine finds no violator, do we have to compute
x^{k}
as a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution of (
3.5
) as before (to be sure that at termination we have an
\varepsilon
-optimal design for (
2.1
)).

Algorithm 3.2
.

Input: a non-empty finite subset
X^{0}
of
X
, tolerances
\underline{\delta}_{k}\in(0,\infty)
, a tolerance
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
. Initialize
k=0
and proceed in the following steps.

1.

Compute a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})\in\Xi(X^{k})\times\Lambda
of the discretized optimal design problem (

)

2.

Search for a violator of the
\varepsilon
-optimality condition (

(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
, that is, for a point in the set

, using an arbitrary finite search routine.

–

If the search routine finds a point

, then set

and return to Step 1 with

replaced by

.

–

If the search routine finds no point in

, then compute a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution

of the sensitivity minimization problem

, then set

. If

is a special case of Algorithm

. In order to see this, simply set

.

) (that is, with

3.1
Well-definedness

In order for our algorithms to be well-defined, we need to impose Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
, of course, because otherwise the sensitivity function
\psi^{L}
used in the second step of our algorithms would not even be defined. In addition to that, we need to ensure

•

the existence of a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
for every discretized design problem

•

the existence of a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution
x^{k}
for every sensitivity minimization problem

occurring in the course of the algorithms, because otherwise the first step and the second step of our algorithms would not be well-defined. As we will see in the next lemma, there is a simple sufficient condition for the above requirements, formulated solely in terms of the initial discretized design problem. Indeed, all we need for the above requirements to be satisfied is a strictly feasible point
\eta^{0}
for the initial discretized design problem
\mathrm{CD}(X^{0})
with
\eta^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{0}
and the non-one-sidedness of
\Psi_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Xi(X^{0}))
. As has been shown above (Lemma
2.14
), this non-one-sidedness of
\Psi_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Xi(X^{0}))
is implied by the non-one-sidedness of
g_{\mathrm{eq}}(X^{0})
w.r.t.
0
.

Condition 3.3
.

(i)

\mathrm{CD}(X^{0})
has a strictly feasible point
\eta^{0}
with
\Psi_{0}(\eta^{0})<\infty
. In other words, there exists an

\displaystyle\eta^{0}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X^{0})\quad\text{with}\quad\Psi_{i}(\eta^{0})<0\quad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{i}(\eta^{0})=0\quad(i\in I_{\mathrm{eq}}).

(3.8)

(ii)

\Psi_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Xi(X^{0}))
is not one-sided w.r.t.
0
.

It should be noticed that if the original problem (
2.1
) has a strictly feasible point
\xi^{*}
with
\Psi_{0}(\xi^{*})<\infty
, then – in the situation of Corollary
2.8
– there also is a finite subset
X^{0}
such that the discretized version
\mathrm{CD}(X^{0})
has a strictly feasible point
\eta^{0}
with
\Psi_{0}(\eta^{0})<\infty
. Indeed, this immediately follows by the proof of Corollary
2.8
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
are satisfied. Suppose further that
\underline{\delta}_{k}\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
are tolerances and
X^{0}\neq\emptyset
is a finite subset of
X
such that Condition
3.3
is satisfied. If Algorithm
3.2
is initialized with such tolerances
\underline{\delta}_{k},\varepsilon
and such an initial discretization
X^{0}
, then the algorithm is well-defined, that is, for every iteration index
k
,

(i)

the discretized design problem (
3.1
) has a saddle point, and

(ii)

the sensitivity minimization problem (
3.7
) has a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution.

Proof.

Suppose that Algorithm
3.2
is initialized with tolerances
\underline{\delta}_{k},\varepsilon
and an initial discretization
X^{0}
as described above. Also, let
K
denote the set of iteration indices.

(i) We first show that for every
k\in K
the discretized design problem
\mathrm{CD}(X^{k})
has a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
.
In order to do so, we show that the assumptions of our saddle-point existence result (Theorem
2.15
) are satisfied with
X
replaced by
X^{k}
. So, let
k\in K
be an arbitrary iteration index of the algorithm.
We first observe that Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
are also satisfied with
X
replaced by any closed subset
X^{*}\neq\emptyset
of
X
. In particular, Condition
2.1
is satisfied for the finite (hence closed) subset
X^{*}:=X^{k}
(which is non-empty because
X^{k}\supset X^{0}\neq\emptyset
).
We further observe that

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X^{k})\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{k})\supset\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X^{0})\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{0})\neq\emptyset

(3.9)

because
X^{k}\supset X^{0}
by the definition of our algorithm and because
\eta^{0}
, as a strictly feasible point of
\mathrm{CD}(X^{0})
with
\eta^{0}\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{0}
, belongs to
\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X^{0})\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{0})
. In view of these observations, it follows by the solvability result (Theorem
2.2
) with
X
replaced by
X^{k}
that the problem
\mathrm{CD}(X^{k})
has a solution
\xi^{k}
.
It further follows by the proof of Lemma
2.14
(with
X^{*}:=X^{0}
and
X^{**}:=X^{k}
) that Condition
2.13
is satisfied with
X
replaced by
X^{k}
.
Consequently, by the saddle-point existence result (Theorem
2.15
) with
X
replaced by
X^{k}
, we wee that there exists a multiplier
\lambda^{k}\in\Lambda
such that
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
is a saddle point of
\mathrm{CD}(X^{k})
. In particular,
\mathrm{CD}(X^{k})
has a saddle point, as desired.

(ii) We now show that for every
k\in K
the sensitivity minimization problem (
3.7
) has a
\underline{\delta}_{k}
-approximate solution.
So, let
k\in K
be an arbitrary iteration index. Since the discretized design problem (
3.1
) has a saddle point
(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k})
by part (i) and since

3.2.1
Algorithm by Cook and Fedorov

In
[
12
]
(Section 2.2), the following algorithm is proposed to solve optimal design problems (
2.1
) with affine inequality constraints
\Psi_{i}
with
\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}=\Xi(X)
. Specifically, the setting considered there can be described as the special case of Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
where

\begin{gathered}I=I_{\mathrm{ineq}},\qquad I_{\mathrm{eq}}=\emptyset,\qquad I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}=\emptyset,\qquad\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)=\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{0}\\
\Psi_{i}(\xi):=\int_{X}g_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\qquad(\xi\in\Xi(X))\end{gathered}

(3.13)

for continuous functions
g_{i}:X\to\mathbb{R}
for
i\in I
. In essence, this algorithm repeatedly solves the linear descent-direction problems

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\Xi(X)}\partial_{\eta-\xi^{k}}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{i}(\eta)\leq 0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(3.14)

of the original problem and exploits the fact that – by the affinity of the constraints – each of these problems has a solution with at most
|I_{\mathrm{ineq}}|+1
support points (Note 1 of
[
12
]
). So, by restricting the search space of (
3.14
) to designs
\eta
with
|\operatorname{supp}\eta|\leq|I_{\mathrm{ineq}}|+1
, the infinite-dimensional linear optimization problem can be replaced by a finite-dimensional (nonlinear) problem, namely (
3.16
) below. In this context, we abbreviate
m:=|I_{\mathrm{ineq}}|
and denote the
m
-dimensional standard probability simplex by

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}:=\{w\in[0,\infty)^{m+1}:w_{1}+\dotsb+w_{m+1}=1\}.

(3.15)

Algorithm 3.5
.

Input: a design
\xi^{0}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
.
With this input at hand, set
k=0
and proceed in the following steps:

1.

Compute a solution
\eta^{k}\in\Xi(X)
of the linear descent-direction problem (
3.14
) with
|\operatorname{supp}\eta^{k}|\leq m+1
. In order to do so, compute a solution
(\tilde{x}^{k},w^{k})=(x^{k,1},\dots,x^{k,m+1},w^{k})\in X^{m+1}\times\Delta_{m}
of the nonlinear optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{(\tilde{x},w)\in X^{m+1}\times\Delta_{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}w_{j}\psi_{0}(\xi^{k},x^{j})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}w_{j}g_{i}(x^{j})\leq 0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(3.16)

and then set
\eta^{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}w_{j}^{k}\delta_{x^{k,j}}
.

2.

Compute a solution
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1]
of the convex step-size problem

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k}+\alpha(\eta^{k}-\xi^{k})).

(3.17)

In case the minimal value
\partial_{\eta^{k}-\xi^{k}}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})
of (
3.14
) or, equivalently, of (
3.16
) is non-negative, then terminate. In the opposite case, update the design by setting

\displaystyle\xi^{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_{k})\xi^{k}+\alpha_{k}\eta^{k}

(3.18)

and return to Step 1 with
k
replaced by
k+1
.

It is straightforward to verify that the iterates
\xi^{k}
of the above algorithm are all feasible for (
2.1
) and belong to
\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)
, in short:
\xi^{k}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
for all iteration indices
k
.
It is also not difficult to prove that in case of termination, the algorithm terminates at a solution of (
2.1
). Indeed, this follows from the easily established fact that for
\xi^{k}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
, the non-negativity of the optimal value of (
3.14
) implies the optimality of
\xi^{k}
for (
2.1
).

Concerning convergence of the algorithm, the following result is established in
[
12
]
(Theorem 7): In the aforementioned special case (
3.13
) of Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
, the criterion values
\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})
converge monotonically decreasingly to the optimal value of (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})\searrow\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)}\Psi_{0}(\xi)\qquad(k\to\infty),

(3.19)

provided that a suitable second-order strengthening of the directional differentiability assumption (
2.7
) holds true for
\Psi_{0}
.

Algorithm by Gaivoronski

In
[
20
]
(Section 5), the following algorithm is proposed to solve optimal design problems (
2.1
) with convex inequality constraints
\Psi_{i}
with
\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}=\Xi(X)
. Specifically, the setting considered there can be described as the special case of Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
where

\displaystyle I=I_{\mathrm{ineq}},\qquad I_{\mathrm{eq}}=\emptyset,\qquad I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}=\emptyset,\qquad\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X)=\Xi(X).

(3.20)

In essence, this algorithm repeatedly solves discretized versions of the linearized descent-direction problems

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\Xi(X)}\partial_{\eta-\xi^{k}}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k})+\partial_{\eta-\xi^{k}}\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k})\leq 0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(3.21)

of the original problem and exploits the fact that – under a strict feasibility condition – the optimal value
v^{k}
of (
3.21
) is equal to the optimal value of the minimax problem

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in[0,\infty)^{m}}\min_{x\in X}\underline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda,x)

(3.22)

with
m:=|I_{\mathrm{ineq}}|
(Theorem 1 of
[
20
]
).
In this problem,
\underline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the integral kernel of the Lagrange function of (
3.21
), that is,

\displaystyle\underline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda,x):=\psi_{0}(\xi^{k},x)+\sum_{i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}}}\lambda_{i}(\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k})+\psi_{i}(\xi^{k},x))\qquad((\lambda,x)\in[0,\infty)^{m}\times X).

(3.23)

In order to solve (
3.22
), an iterative discretization scheme for
X
is proposed in
[
20
]
, which then leads to the following algorithm (Section 5 of
[
20
]
).

Algorithm 3.6
.

Input: a design
\xi^{0}\in\Xi(X)
, a multiplier
\lambda^{0}\in[0,\infty)^{m}
, a finite subset
X^{0}
of
X
, and step sizes
\alpha_{k}\in(0,\infty)
with
\alpha_{k}\longrightarrow 0
and with
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty
.
With these inputs at hand, set
k=0
and proceed in the following steps:

1.

Compute a solution
x^{k}\in X
of the discretization updating problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}\underline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)

(3.24)

and update the discretization by setting
X^{k+1}:=X^{k}\cup\{x^{k}\}.

2.

Compute a solution
\eta^{k}\in\Xi(X^{k+1})
of the discretized linearized descent-direction problem

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\Xi(X^{k+1})}\int_{X}\psi_{0}(\xi^{k},x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k})+\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi^{k},x)\,\mathrm{d}\eta(x)\leq 0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(3.25)

and a solution
\lambda^{k+1}\in[0,\infty)^{m}
of the optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in[0,\infty)^{m}}\min_{x\in X^{k+1}}\underline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda,x),

(3.26)

which is easily seen to be the dual optimization problem of (

).

\displaystyle\xi^{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_{k})\xi^{k}+\alpha_{k}\eta^{k}

(3.27)

and return to Step 1 with
k
replaced by
k+1
.

It is important to notice that the iterates
\xi^{k}
of the above algorithm are generally not feasible for (
2.1
), even if the initial design
\xi^{0}
is chosen to be feasible. In fact, feasibility is guaranteed only in the limit

or, more precisely, only for the accumulation points of the iterate sequence

(Theorem 3 in
[

]
and the remark following its proof). It is therefore important that the algorithm does not terminate, which is why it does not feature a termination condition.

[

]

) of Conditions

and

, the criterion values

converge to the optimal value of (

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})\longrightarrow\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)}\Psi_{0}(\xi)\qquad(k\to\infty),

(3.28)

provided that (i) there exists an
\eta^{0}\in\Xi(X)
such that
\Psi_{i}(\eta^{0})<0
for all

and that (ii) the multiplier sequence

generated by the algorithm is bounded. As a sufficient condition for this boundedness assumption, the affinity of all constraint functions

is mentioned in

. In the case of non-affine constraint functions, however, no sufficient conditions for the boundedness of the multiplier sequence are given in

.

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi):=\int_{X}g_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\qquad(\xi\in\Xi(X))

for

.

In essence, the algorithm from

]
repeatedly computes suitable approximate solutions of the steepest descent-direction problems

\displaystyle\min_{\eta\in\Xi(X,\mathbb{R})}\partial_{\eta}\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\xi^{k}+\eta\in\Xi(X,[0,\infty))\text{ and }\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k}+\eta)=a_{i}\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,m+1\})

(3.32)

in the space of signed measures, where
I_{\mathrm{eq}}=\{1,\dots,m\}
and
\Psi_{m+1}(\xi):=\xi(X)
and
a_{m+1}:=1
. In doing so, a characterization of steepest descent directions for (
3.29
) is exploited (Theorem 4.1 of
[
30
]
). Also, the termination criterion of the algorithm is inspired by the following necessary optimality condition for (
3.29
): if
\xi^{k}
is a solution of (
3.29
) that satisfies the Robinson-Zowe-Kurcyusz regularity condition, then there exists a multiplier
\lambda^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
such that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)=0\text{ for $\xi^{k}$-a.e. }x\in X

(3.33)

(Theorem 2.1 of
[
30
]
). In the above relations,
\overline{\psi}^{L}
denotes the integral kernel of the directional derivatives – or, in other words, the signed sensitivity function – of the Lagrange function of (
3.29
),
that is,

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi,\lambda,x):=\overline{\psi}_{0}(\xi,x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\lambda_{i}g_{i}(x)\qquad((\xi,\lambda,x)\in\Xi(X,\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\times X).

(3.34)

Algorithm 3.7
.

Input: a design
\xi^{0}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X)
with finite support and optimality tolerances
\delta,\delta_{\operatorname{supp}}\in(0,\infty)
.
With these inputs at hand, set
k=0
and proceed in the following steps:

1.

Compute an approximate solution
\eta^{k}
of the descent-direction problem (
3.32
). In order to do so, compute a solution
\tilde{x}^{k}=(x^{k,1},\dots,x^{k,m+1})\in X^{m+1}
of the nonlinear optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{x}\in X^{m+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}w_{j}(\tilde{x})\overline{\psi}_{0}(\xi^{k},x^{j})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad w_{j}(\tilde{x})\geq 0\qquad(j\in\{1,\dots,m+1\})

(3.35)

and then set
\eta^{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}w_{j}(x^{k})\delta_{x^{k,j}}
,
where the following abbreviations have been used:

\displaystyle w_{j}(\tilde{x}):=e_{j}^{\top}G(\tilde{x})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{m+1}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G(\tilde{x}):=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}(x^{1})&\dots&g_{1}(x^{m+1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{m+1}(x^{1})&\dots&g_{m+1}(x^{m+1})\end{pmatrix}

for
\tilde{x}=(x^{1},\dots,x^{m+1})\in X^{m+1}
with
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
the
j
th canonical unit vector and with
g_{m+1}(y):=1
for
y\in X
.

2.

Compute a solution
\lambda^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
of the quadratically convex least-squares problem

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}\sum_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\xi^{k}}(\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda,x))^{2}

(3.36)

inspired by (
3.33
.b).
In case

\displaystyle\xi^{k}\Big(\Big\{x\in X:\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)-\min_{x\in X}\overline{\psi}^{L}(\xi^{k},\lambda^{k},x)>\delta\Big\}\Big)<\delta_{\operatorname{supp}}

(3.37)

and
\Psi_{0}((1-\alpha)\xi^{k}+\alpha\eta^{k})\geq\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
, terminate. In the opposite case, update the design by setting

\displaystyle\xi^{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_{k})\xi^{k}+\alpha_{k}\eta^{k},

(3.38)

where
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1]
is any step size with
\Psi_{0}((1-\alpha_{k})\xi^{k}+\alpha_{k}\eta^{k})<\Psi_{0}(\xi^{k})
, and then return to Step 1 with
k
replaced by
k+1
.

It is straightforward to verify that the iterates
\xi^{k}
of the above algorithm are all feasible for (
3.29
). In this context, it should be noticed, however, that the inverse of the matrix
G(\tilde{x})
used in (
3.35
) does not exist in general,
contrary to what is stated before (6.1) in
[
30
]
. (Indeed, let
X:=[0,(m+2)\pi]
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
g_{i}:=\cos(i\cdot)|_{X}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
g_{m+1}:=1
. Since
g_{1},\dots,g_{m+1}
when restricted to
[0,2\pi]
form an orthogonal system, the functions
g_{1},\dots,g_{m+1}
are linearly independent on
X
, as required before (2.4) of
[
30
]
. Setting
x^{j}:=\pi/2+j\pi\in X
and
\tilde{x}:=(x^{1},\dots,x^{m+1})
, however, we see that the
i
th row of
G(\tilde{x})
is identically zero for every odd index
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. And therefore,
G(\tilde{x})
is not invertible.)

Concerning convergence of the algorithm, not much is said in
[
30
]
. Indeed, the algorithm is illustrated and validated by means of several interesting examples in
[
30
]
(Section 7), which go beyond experimental design (but all of them feature convex objective functions as in experimental design). It should be pointed out, though, that no general convergence or termination results are proved or even formulated in
[
30
]
. In fact, at the very end of
[
31
]
, it is emphasized that an additional analysis is necessary to ensure that the algorithm really does converge to a solution of (
3.29
).

4
Convergence and termination results

In this section, we establish convergence and finite termination results for our adaptive discretization algorithm. Specifically, we show that the iterate sequence of the special algorithm (Algorithm
3.1
) accumulates at an optimal design for (
2.1
) and that the iterate sequence of the general algorithm (Algorithm
3.2
), in turn, terminates at an
\varepsilon
-optimal design. We begin with two preparatory lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
and
2.3
are satisfied and as in (
2.43
) let
I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}
denote the subset of
I
for which the sensitivity function
\psi_{i}
is continuous or non-continuous, respectively. Then

(i)

for
i\in\{0\}\cup I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}
, the objective or constraint function
\Psi_{i}|_{\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}}
, respectively, is continuous

(ii)

for
i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}
, the constraint function
\Psi_{i}|_{\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi)=\int_{X}g_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\qquad(\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}).

(4.1)

Proof.

(i) Suppose that
i\in\{0\}\cup I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}
and let
\xi^{n},\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}
with
\xi^{n}\longrightarrow\xi
as
n\to\infty
.
It then follows
by Lemma
2.10
that

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\xi^{n})-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\leq-\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi^{n},x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{i}(\xi)-\Psi_{i}(\xi^{n})\leq-\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{n}(x)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle|\Psi_{i}(\xi^{n})-\Psi_{i}(\xi)|\leq\max\bigg\{-\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi^{n},x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x),-\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi^{n}(x)\bigg\}

(4.2)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\psi_{i}(\cdot,x)
is continuous for every
x\in X
and since
\psi_{i}(\xi,\cdot)
is a bounded continuous function by Condition
2.3
(i) or, respectively, by the definition of
I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{c}}
, we conclude with the dominated convergence theorem that the right-hand side of (
4.2
) converges to

\displaystyle\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)=\partial_{\xi-\xi}\Psi_{i}(\xi)=0

(4.3)

as
n\to\infty
. So, the same is true for the left-hand side of (
4.2
), whence
\Psi_{i}(\xi^{n})\longrightarrow\Psi_{i}(\xi)
as
n\to\infty
, as desired.

(ii) Suppose that
i\in I^{\mathrm{sensi}}_{\mathrm{nc}}
. It then follows by Condition
2.3
that there exists a bounded measurable function such that

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(\xi,x)=g_{i}(x)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad((\xi,x)\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}\times X).

(4.4)

In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{\xi-\xi}\Psi_{i}(\xi)=\int_{X}\psi_{i}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)=\int_{X}g_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(x)-\Psi_{i}(\xi)\qquad(\xi\in\operatorname{dom}\Psi_{i}),

(4.5)

which is the desired conclusion (
4.1
).
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
and

(\xi^{k})
,

\displaystyle\xi^{k}\in\Xi_{\mathrm{fin}}(X^{k})\cap\Xi_{\mathrm{feas}}(X^{k})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{i}^{k}\Psi_{i}(\xi^{k})=0\qquad(i\in I_{\mathrm{ineq}})

(4.6)

for every iteration index

.

(i) Since

(4.18)

Since

5.2
Applications with a reaction kinetics model

In this section, we consider experimental design problems for a reaction kinetics model
[
43
,
44
]
from chemical engineering. It describes a chain of chemical reactions

A\rightleftharpoons B\rightarrow C

(5.33)

involving three components
A,B,C
, where
B
is the target product and
C
is a waste product. Accordingly, we are interested in experimental designs leading to a sufficiently high target product return after a sufficiently short time span. As in
[
43
]
, we assume that the forward reactions in (
5.33
) have reaction order
2
, that the backward reaction in (
5.33
) has reaction order
1
, and that the rate constants
k_{i}
of all three kinetic reactions depend on the temperature
T
according to the Arrhenius relation

\displaystyle k_{i}(T):=\alpha_{i}\exp\left(-E_{i}/(RT)\right),

(5.34)

where
R:=$1.986\text{\,}\mathrm{cal}$
is the universal gas constant and
\alpha_{i}
and
E_{i}
denote the pre-exponential factor and the activation energy of the
i
th reaction, respectively. We denote the molar composition of the reactive mixture by

\displaystyle s:=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}):=(a,b,c),

(5.35)

that is,
a,b
and
c
represent the molar fractions of the components
A,B
and
C
.
As a consequence of the above model assumptions, the evolution of the reactive system can be described by a system of ordinary differential equations of the form

\displaystyle\dot{s}(t)=g(s(t),T,\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad s(0)=s_{0}

(5.36)

where the right-hand side
g:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined by

\displaystyle g_{1}(s,T,\theta)

\displaystyle:=-k_{1}(T,\theta)s_{1}^{2}+k_{3}(T,\theta)s_{2}

(5.37)

\displaystyle g_{2}(s,T,\theta)

\displaystyle:=k_{1}(T,\theta)s_{1}^{2}-k_{2}(T,\theta)s_{2}^{2}-k_{3}(T,\theta)s_{2}

(5.38)

\displaystyle g_{3}(s,T,\theta)

\displaystyle:=k_{2}(T,\theta)s_{2}^{2}

(5.39)

and where
k_{i}(T,\theta):=k_{i}(T)
is the model from (
5.34
) and the model parameters
\theta
are given by

\displaystyle\theta:=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},E_{1},E_{2},E_{3})

(5.40)

the pre-exponential factors and the activation energies of the three reactions.
As the input quantities defining individual experiments we take

\displaystyle x:=(t_{\mathrm{m}},s_{0},T)\in\mathcal{X}:=[0,t_{\mathrm{f}}]\times\mathcal{S}_{0}\times\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{5},

(5.41)

that is, the measurement time
t_{\mathrm{m}}
(measured in
\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
), the initial molar composition
s_{0}=(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})
(measured in
\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}
), and the temperature
T
(measured in
\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
). We take the final time to be
t_{\mathrm{f}}:=10
and the sets of possible initial compositions and of possible temperatures to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}:=\big\{(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})\in[0.5,1.0]\times[0.1,0.7]^{2}:a_{0}+b_{0}+c_{0}=1\big\},

(5.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}:=[300,700].

(5.43)

What we are interested in here is the prediction of the composition
s
of the mixture at the measurement time
t_{\mathrm{m}}
as a function of the aforementioned input quantities
x
and model paremeters
\theta
. In other words, the output quantity of interest is
y:=s
and the prediction model of interest is the function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by

\displaystyle f(x,\theta):=s(t_{\mathrm{m}},s_{0},T,\theta)=s(x,\theta),

(5.44)

where
s(\cdot,s_{0},T,\theta):[0,t_{\mathrm{f}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the solution of the initial-value problem (
5.36
) for given initial composition
s_{0}
, temperature
T
, and model parameters
\theta
.
As already pointed above, we take the design space
X
to be a large finite subset of
\mathcal{X}
, namely the uniform grid

\displaystyle X:=\big\{(t_{\mathrm{m}},s_{0},T)

\displaystyle\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,10\}\times\{0.5,0.51,0.52,\ldots,1.0\}\times\{0.1,0.11,0.12,\ldots,0.7\}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\{300,301,302,\ldots,700\}:a_{0}+b_{0}+c_{0}=1\big\}\subset\mathcal{X}.

(5.45)

consisting of
1\,988\,960
grid points in
\mathcal{X}
. Also, the reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}
and the covariance matrix
\varsigma(x)
of the measurement errors we define in the same way as
[
43
]
, namely

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}:=\left(\overline{\alpha}_{1},\overline{\alpha}_{2},\overline{\alpha}_{3},\overline{E}_{1},\overline{E}_{2},\overline{E}_{3}\right):=(0.7,0.2,0.1,1000,1000,1000),

(5.46)

\displaystyle\varsigma(x):=\frac{1}{100}\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}(x,\overline{\theta})&0&0\\
0&s_{2}(x,\overline{\theta})&0\\
0&0&s_{3}(x,\overline{\theta})\end{pmatrix}.

(5.47)

In contrast to the exponential growth model (
5.4
), the reaction kinetics model (
5.44
) is not explicitly defined by a closed-form expression but implicitly defined by the solution
s(\cdot,s_{0},T,\theta)
of the initial-value problem (
5.36
). Accordingly, the Jacobians
D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
and the one-point information matrices
m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta})
from (
5.2
) are not explicitly defined either. In order to compute them, we exploit the well-known fact that the differentiated solution
t\mapsto D_{\theta}s(t,s_{0},T,\overline{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 6}
is the solution of the linearized initial-value problem

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{\theta}(t)=D_{s}g\big(s(t,s_{0},T,\overline{\theta}),T,\overline{\theta}\big)s_{\theta}(t)+D_{\theta}g\big(s(t,s_{0},T,\overline{\theta}),T,\overline{\theta}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad s_{\theta}(0)=0

(5.48)

(Corollary 9.3 of
[
1
]
), whose right-hand side depends on the solution of the initial-value problem (
5.36
). Instead of solving (
5.36
) and (
5.48
) sequentially, we solve them simultaneously, that is, we solve the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{s}(t)\\
\dot{s}_{\theta}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g(s(t),T,\overline{\theta})\\
D_{s}g\big(s(t),T,\overline{\theta}\big)s_{\theta}(t)+D_{\theta}g\big(s(t),T,\overline{\theta}\big)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}s(0)\\
s_{\theta}(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(5.49)

In order to do so, we use the solution method
\mathrm{solve\_ivp}
from the scipy.integrate package
[
46
]
. With the solution
t\mapsto(s(t),s_{\theta}(t))
of (
5.49
) at hand, we then obtain the required Jacobians and one-point information matrices as
D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})=s_{\theta}(t_{\mathrm{m}})
and

\displaystyle m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta})=100\cdot s_{\theta}(t_{\mathrm{m}})^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(s_{1}(t_{\mathrm{m}}),s_{2}(t_{\mathrm{m}}),s_{3}(t_{\mathrm{m}}))^{-1}s_{\theta}(t_{\mathrm{m}})

(5.50)

for all design points
x\in X
.

2.1
and
2.3

2.5
. Condition

B
at time
