Gut microbiota-mediated neuroinflammation following stroke: mechanisms and intervention strategies
Authors: Lili Teng, Minmin Wu, Wenjing Song, Qingmei Wang, Xun Luo, Luwen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is a complex and highly heterogeneous acute cerebrovascular disease characterized by high incidence, disability rates, and mortality. The occurrence of a stroke is the result of a series of chain reactions. Neuroinflammation, as a core component of the pathological process of stroke, serves as the dynamic hub of this chain of reactions. It acts both as a driving force in disease progression and a key regulator of functional recovery. Increasing evidence indicates that the gut microbiota has a dynamic coupling relationship with the neuroinflammatory pathological processes involved in stroke. This review aimed to explore the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota influences neuroinflammation following stroke and to evaluate its therapeutic potential in stroke.Main bodyFollowing a stroke, up to 50% of patients have pathophysiological changes in the gastrointestinal tract, which are closely associated with delayed recovery, increased mortality, and worsened neurological outcomes. The gut microbiota can modulate post-stroke neuroinflammation through multiple pathways, including immune regulation (innate and adaptive immunity), neural signal transmission (vagus nerve and enteric nervous system), neuroendocrine transmission (hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis), and gut microbiota metabolite pathways. This “central-peripheral-central” feedback loop is considered a potential intervention target. In this review, we summarized the characteristics of gut microbiota alterations following stroke, elucidated its mechanisms of action in neuroinflammation, and explored gut microbiota-based intervention strategies and their limitations. Finally, we proposed directions to provide new insights and references for clinical translation for the prevention and treatment of stroke.ConclusionsThe gut microbiota plays a key role in post-stroke pathology. The gut microbiota modulates the onset and progression of post-stroke neuroinflammation through multidimensional mechanisms via the “immune-neuro-endocrine” feedback pathway at multiple levels. Interventions targeting the gut microbiota can not only suppress excessive inflammation but also repair neural networks, ultimately improving functional outcomes after stroke.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Stroke is an acute brain tissue injury caused by alterations in cerebral blood supply, primarily encompassing ischemic stroke (IS) and hemorrhagic stroke [1]. Starting at the age of 25, the lifetime risk of experiencing a stroke globally is approximately 25% [2]. According to a report by the World Stroke Organization, global stroke-related losses are approximately US$890 billion, imposing a heavy burden on public health [3]. Currently, recombinant tissue plasminogen activator remains the only drug approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of stroke. Owing to the limited treatment window, only approximately 10% of patients qualify for treatment [4]. Even when treatment is available, up to 50% of patients have a sub-optimal outcome. Therefore, identifying safe and effective therapeutic targets for stroke is an urgent priority. As a significant pathological characteristic of neurological disorders such as stroke, neuroinflammation not only plays a dominant role during the acute stage but also influences the restoration of neural function during the subsequent recovery stage [5]. Recent studies have shown that the gut microbiota is a crucial target in the onset and progression of stroke-related neuroinflammation, offering a new perspective for explaining immune dysregulation and functional impairment after stroke.

The gastrointestinal tract is the largest immune organ, housing over 70% of immune cells [6]. When a stroke occurs, these immune cells are mobilized and migrate to the central nervous system (CNS), where they participate in immune regulation. The gut microbiota is a key regulator of intestinal and brain development and plays a crucial role throughout this process [7]. In recent years, the concept of the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) has significantly enriched the role of the gut microbiota. The MGBA is a dynamic bidirectional communication network between the gut and brain, mediated by the gut microbiota and its metabolites through immune, nervous, and neuroendocrine systems [8]. The gut microbiota can transmit signals to the CNS via the MGBA, thereby regulating neuroinflammation following a stroke and participating in the process of neural repair.

We conducted a detailed literature search of multiple databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, with the aim of providing a comprehensive review of current research progress. Use combinations of topic-related keywords such as “gut microbiota,” “stroke,” “neuroinflammation,” “inflammation,” “immunity,” and “gut-brain axis.” The search included various types of studies from 2015 to 2025, including original research, clinical trials, and review papers. We excluded conference abstracts, studies for which the full text was unavailable, studies unrelated to the research topic, and duplicate studies.

In this study, we aimed to provide a comprehensive review of current research progress, emphasizing the multidimensional mechanisms through which the gut microbiota regulates neuroinflammation after stroke through the immune, nervous, and neuroendocrine systems. Building on this foundation, we also discussed the microbiota-targeted interventions, the challenges facing their clinical translation, and future research directions to offer new insights into the prevention and treatment of stroke.

Stroke and neuroinflammation

CNS inflammation

Neuroinflammation is a central component of the pathological process in stroke. Its presence and development are not a static “on/off” phenomenon, but rather a highly dynamic, phased process involving multicellular coordination (Fig. 1). The acute stage has the most intense inflammatory response and represents a critical period for the progression of brain injury. Following a stroke, the oxygen and glucose supply fail to meet the metabolic demands of the brain, leading to local neuronal injury or death [9]. Damage-associated molecular patterns secreted by injured or dead cells rapidly initiate innate immune responses through pattern recognition receptors that activate microglia and astrocytes to trigger inflammatory cascades that severely compromise the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [10]. Damage to the BBB permits peripheral immune cells to continuously invade and accumulate within the CNS, thereby triggering a potent inflammatory storm [11]. Simultaneously, impaired meningeal lymphatic drainage prevents the timely clearance of antigens and inflammatory mediators to cervical lymph nodes, hindering the relocation of local inflammation [12]. This leads to the accumulation of waste products and inflammatory mediators, thereby exacerbating localized edema and compromising subsequent repair processes.

Central nervous system inflammation following stroke. Neuroinflammation following stroke is a dynamic process involving the coordinated participation of multiple cell types, primarily comprising three stages: the acute stage, the subacute stage, and the chronic stage. The acute stage of inflammation exhibits the most intense response, characterized by damage to the blood-brain barrier and rapid activation of microglia and astrocytes, which release inflammatory mediators and exacerbate neuronal injury. The subacute stage represents a transitional stage characterized by the persistent infiltration of peripheral immune cells and the anti-inflammatory conversion of microglia and astrocytes, which promote neural repair. During the chronic stage, the inflammatory response gradually subsides, but a persistent state of chronic inflammation may remain, hindering the process of nerve repair

The subacute stage is a transitional period characterized by persistent and intricate dynamic changes in neuroinflammation. Microglia and astrocytes gradually transition towards anti-inflammatory phenotypes, secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factors, which promote tissue regeneration and synaptic remodeling [13]. At this stage, monocyte-derived macrophages transition into an anti-inflammatory phenotype, which enhances the anti-inflammatory response [14]. As inflammation subsides and tissue edema resolves, the exchange between interstitial fluid and cerebrospinal fluid is partially restored. This improves lymphatic drainage and facilitates the clearance of inflammatory products and extracellular debris [15]. However, a chronic inflammatory condition may persist during the chronic stage. For instance, microglia and astrocytes that remain chronically activated continuously release inflammatory mediators, reactive oxygen species, and chemokines, leading to chronic neurotoxicity, neuronal loss, and brain atrophy [16]. Lymphocyte infiltration, particularly T cells, persists during the late phase of cerebral ischemia [17]. CD8+ T and CD4+ T cells are persistently recruited to the ipsilateral hemisphere, thereby impairing the recruitment and functional recovery of other immune cells in the brain, and initiating a delayed immune response [18, 19]. Concurrently, prolonged dysfunction of lymphatic drainage may lead to a sustained imbalance between the central and peripheral immune systems, thereby triggering secondary neurodegenerative events. This low-grade persistent inflammation may exacerbate chronic neurodegenerative changes and white matter lesions, increasing the risk of recurrent stroke [20].

To sum up, abnormally activated immune cells and the excessive release of inflammatory factors are the core drivers of neuroinflammatory damage following a stroke. Treatment should not be limited to vascular recanalization alone, but should instead involve a comprehensive therapeutic strategy that combines anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective approaches. Moreover, the temporal dynamics and the dual nature of inflammation must be considered to avoid risks of infections and complications associated with extensive immunosuppressive interventions.

Inflammation of the intestinal system

Following a stroke, up to 50% of patients develop gastrointestinal complications, including dysphagia, constipation, intestinal leakage, and even enteric sepsis [21]. This indicates that tissue damage caused by stroke is not confined to the brain but also induces alterations in the gastrointestinal tract and its microbiota. However, little is known about how these changes participate in the pathophysiological processes of CNS disease and how they influence functional outcomes (Fig. 2).

Post-stroke inflammatory changes in the gut are primarily characterized by activation of enteric glial cells, damage to enteric neurons, elevated levels of local inflammatory factors, compromised intestinal barrier function, and disruption of the intestinal microenvironment

The intestinal barrier is central to physiological homeostasis, ensuring the efficient transport of nutrients from the intestinal lumen into the circulation, while simultaneously preventing pathogens, toxins, and other harmful substances from penetrating the barrier, thereby protecting the host from infection [22]. Within 2 h of stroke onset, the expression of intestinal ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-4 decreases, leading to impaired mucosal barrier function and intestinal permeability [23]. Impaired mucus layer formation allows gut bacteria to directly contact intestinal epithelial cells, triggering immune activation and bacterial translocation, thereby increasing the risk of post-stroke infection [24]. Three days after stroke onset, intestinal CD45+ and CD11c+ immune cells migrate to the brain and meninges, exacerbating the inflammatory response, resulting in long-term learning and memory impairments [16]. γδ T cells in the intestinal epithelium also migrate to the meninges via the peripheral circulation [25]. By secreting IL-17, they induce increased chemokine production in the brain parenchyma, which exacerbates ischemic neuroinflammation. In summary, the migration of intestinal immune cells may be a key factor in inducing immune activation and neuroinflammation following a stroke.

Gut microbiota translocation is another key factor in triggering systemic neuroinflammation. Within 3 h after stroke onset, ileal permeability briefly peaks, making it easier for Escherichia coli and fecal Enterococci to translocate and spread to peripheral tissues, thereby promoting infection and a systemic inflammatory response [26]. Another animal study also showed that intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) induced pathological stimulation caused phasic fluctuations in gut and lung microbial diversity, and that by day 7, the lung microbiota had structurally converged with the gut microbiota [27]. Moreover, bacterial translocation induced by stroke can also affect peripheral tissues such as the liver, spleen, and mesenteric lymph nodes [28].

Characteristics of the altered gut microbiota following stroke

The human gut microbiota contains approximately one hundred trillion bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes, collectively encoding over three million genes, functioning as a “second genome” [29]. At the phylum level, the gut microbiota is primarily composed of the Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia, with Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes accounting for approximately 90% of all microbial communities [30]. The composition of the gut microbiota is dynamically changing, affected by multiple internal and external environmental factors, including genetic background, metabolism, early microbial exposure, dietary habits, and lifestyle [31]. These microbial communities maintain the dynamic equilibrium of the intestinal microenvironment through complex interactions; when this balance is disrupted, it may adversely affect the host. Multiple studies have revealed that after stroke onset, patients exhibit significant alterations in α-diversity, β-diversity, and the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio. Distinct structural characteristics have also emerged across different stroke subtypes and developmental stages, which suggests that the composition and function of the gut microbiota undergo dynamic changes during the pathological process of stroke. Clinical evidence

Studies have indicated that alteration of the gut microbiota in patients with stroke is mainly manifested by upregulation of Akkermansia and Enterococcus, and downregulation of Prevotella, Roseburia, and Faecalibacterium [38, 43–46]. Akkermansia primarily colonizes the mucus layer of the colon. Akkermansia uses mucin as a carbon and nitrogen source, deriving energy from its degradation to maintain intestinal ecological balance [47]. However, excessive degradation of mucus compromises the intestinal barrier and induces inflammatory responses. Silveira-Nunes et al. [48] found that an abnormal increase in Akkermansia abundance in patients with hypertension is closely associated with a pro-inflammatory shift in the immune profile, which may exacerbate susceptibility to stroke by modulating systemic inflammatory responses. Compared to patients without post-stroke depression (PSD), patients with PSD have a significantly increased relative abundance of Streptococcus, Akkermansia, and Barnesiella, and these genera were positively correlated with the severity of depression [49]. Overgrowth of Akkermansia and Lactobacillaceae has also been observed in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [45]. This suggests that Akkermansia can serve as a potential microbial biomarker for stroke.

Prevotella typically colonizes the intestinal mucus layer and plays a crucial role in maintaining gut health and supporting host metabolism. An observational study revealed that patients with AIS in a hypercoagulable state exhibit significant gut microbiota dysbiosis; in particular, the abundance of Alistipes and Prevotella is closely associated with the coagulation status of patients [50]. Another study found that a reduction in Prevotella levels was closely associated with disease severity and inflammation in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and was associated with an increased risk of subsequent stroke-associated pneumonia. This suggests that changes in Prevotella levels can serve as a biomarker for predicting poor outcomes in ICH and the risk of stroke-associated pneumonia [38]. It is noteworthy that the role of the gut microbiota exhibits bidirectionality, and whether it exerts beneficial or harmful effects may depend on the disease context and the properties of the microbiota. Therefore, when using targeted microbiome therapeutic strategies, the individual etiology of each patient and the complexity of their disease should be comprehensively considered.

Preclinical evidence

Relevant preclinical studies (Table 2) have further confirmed that disruptions in the gut microbiota and metabolites play a key role in the pathological progression following stroke. Following a stroke, the number of opportunistic anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae increases in the gut, while the number of fermenting anaerobic bacteria and bile acid and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) levels decrease, inducing alterations in gut immunity and metabolism that exacerbate local inflammation [51]. E. coli can also accelerate systemic inflammation following IS by affecting immune-related pathways and serum inflammatory cytokines [52]. These studies suggest that quantitative monitoring of the abundance of these microbial communities and related inflammatory markers may offer a potential strategy for stratifying the risk of neuroinflammation following stroke. Supplementation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SCGB 1 fermented and B. subtilis SCDB 291 chungkookjang can increase the abundance of Lactobacillus, Bacillus, and Akkermansia in the cecum, enhance propionate and butyrate metabolism as well as glucose metabolism, reduce lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis, and alleviate brain dysfunction [53]. In addition, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) administration can reduce infarct size and improve cognitive function in stroke mice by inhibiting hippocampal microglial activation and neuronal apoptosis [54]. In summary, these research findings provide a theoretical basis for developing a dual-targeted therapeutic framework that simultaneously suppresses peripheral inflammatory triggers through a “bottom-up” approach while blocking CNS inflammatory responses through a “top-down” approach.

Gut microbiota mediates neuroinflammation following stroke

Immune-mediated pathway

Extensive evidence indicates a complex bidirectional interaction between the immune system and the gut microbiota, proposing the integrated concept of the “microbiome-gut-immune-brain axis” [62, 63]. The gut microbiota regulates signaling between the intestine and the CNS by coordinating innate and adaptive immunity, thereby maintaining a dynamic equilibrium between the two systems. This mechanism is crucial for the progression of neuroinflammation following a stroke.

Innate immune response

The innate immune system, as the first line of defense against injury, plays a key role in the regulation of ischemic-hypoxic brain injury via the MGBA. First, the gut microbiota is essential for the development and functional maturation of microglia. A microbe-deficient environment leads to defects in the maturation, differentiation, and function of microglia, and impairs their ability to rapidly respond to external immune threats; whereas, complex gut microbiota transplantation can partially restore their function [64]. Huang et al. [65] found that with the participation of the gut microbiota, Acorus tatarinowii oils can mediate phenotypic conversion of microglia following IS, suppressing the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors including TNF-α, IL-17, and IFN-γ to achieve neuroprotection. Conversely, under aseptic conditions, the phenotype of microglia becomes abnormal, which leads to impaired microglial-neuronal communication, reduced functional connectivity within neuronal networks, and ultimately affects the recovery of neural function following cerebral ischemia [66].

In addition, the gut microbiota is also capable of affecting ischemic brain injury by regulating the activation of peripheral immune cells [67]. For example, Benakis et al. [68] found that gut dysbiosis can affect the balance of T-cell subsets by regulating dendritic cell activity, thereby disrupting the immune homeostasis of the small intestine and exacerbating ischemic brain injury. After colonizing the microbiota in germ-free mice with stroke, neutrophils expressed high levels of the maturation markers Ly6G, LFA-1, and CXCR4, indicating that the gut microbiota can exacerbate sensorimotor deficits by systemically modulating neutrophil activation [55]. These studies suggest that treatment strategies for brain injury should not only focus on “local neuroprotection” but also include “comprehensive interventions targeting the MGBA.” Clinically, gut microecological modulation strategies may be considered to optimize central immune cell phenotypes by improving the intestinal microenvironment, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for neural repair.

Adaptive immune response

Adaptive immunity and innate immunity play a synergistic role in inflammation surveillance. A deficiency in the gut microbiota can trigger systemic immunosuppression following a stroke, leading to an imbalance in T-cell and B-cell homeostasis in tissues such as the Peyer’s patches in the small intestine, the spleen, and the mesenteric lymph nodes, which in turn significantly increases mortality and worsens prognosis [69]. For example, Singh et al. [70] found that post-stroke gut microbiota dysbiosis can impair neurological and gastrointestinal function by activating monocytes/macrophages and T cells; meanwhile, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors can reverse this dysbiosis and modulate altered T cell homeostasis. In lymphocyte-deficient mice, this neuroprotective effect by the gut microbiota was absent [71]. This indicates that the immune system serves as the mediating bridge by which the gut microbiota modulates functional outcomes following a stroke. In mice with diabetic stroke, sodium butyrate (NaB) can exert neuroprotective effects by modulating the peripheral and intracerebral Treg/TGF-β axis through mimicking the anti-inflammatory actions of a healthy gut microbiota [72]. This provides a novel translational medical possibility for neurological rehabilitation in patients with diabetic stroke.

BBB damage

The BBB, as a crucial functional barrier between the circulatory system and the CNS, plays a role in mediating the effects of the gut microbiota on the central immune system [73]. Germ-free mice manifest high BBB permeability from the onset of intrauterine development, indicating that the communication between the gut microbiota and the BBB may be initiated prenatally and sustained across the life course [74]. Cai et al. [75] found that, in pathological conditions such as ICH, an imbalance in the gut microbiota activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to increased vascular permeability and impaired BBB integrity, which ultimately exacerbates secondary white matter damage. Conversely, supplementation with Parabacteroides distasonis can increase beneficial bacteria in rats with AIS, inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway, reduce inflammatory cytokines, and repair damage to the BBB [58]. At the same time, colonization with Clostridium butyricum or Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, or supplementing with NaB, can also improve BBB permeability and promote neurogenesis by enhancing the expression of tight junction protein [76]. These data indicate that the BBB plays a central role in the regulation of CNS immune homeostasis by the gut microbiota, and that mediating the protective effects of gut microbiota on the BBB may represent a potential therapeutic target for post-stroke rehabilitation.

Neural-mediated pathway

Sympathetic nerve

Within the gastrointestinal tract, sympathetic nerve fibers interact with multiple layers of the intestine, the myenteric plexus, submucosal plexus, and mucosa, through various nerve plexuses, participating in the regulation of intestinal motility, secretion, and immune responses [77]. The sympathetic nerve can also directly regulate the activity of intestinal epithelial cells, whereas blocking the sympathetic nerve inhibits the proliferation of intestinal stem cells [78]. The gut microbiota and its metabolites also influence sympathetic nerve activity. For instance, acetate derived from the microbiota can suppress efferent sympathetic nerve activity in spontaneously hypertensive rats, while also modulating the morphology and function of microglia and astrocytes, with antihypertensive effects [79]. Butyrate treatment can also alleviate inflammatory responses in the paraventricular nucleus by inhibiting sympathetic nerve activation, thereby significantly improving cardiac function in rats with heart failure [80]. These findings indicate that there is a bidirectional regulatory coupling mechanism between the gut microbiota and the sympathetic nerve, and that this interaction plays a crucial part in the development of inflammatory diseases.

Vagus nerve (VN)

The parasympathetic nerve fibers in the intestine consist of the VN and serve as the key pathway for communication along the MGBA. The vagal afferent fibers connect to the brain by receiving signals from microbial metabolites [81, 82]. In the context of inflammation, intestinal immune cells can activate vagal sensory neurons, projecting peripheral inflammatory signals to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem, thereby influencing neuroimmune responses [83]. In a bilateral subdiaphragmatic vagotomy experiment, Jin et al. [84] observed that LPS-induced neural activity in the caudal nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem was abolished. This further confirms the central role of the VN-nucleus of the solitary tract immunoregulatory immune axis in modulating peripheral inflammatory responses. Vagotomy can also lead to the loss of post-stroke intestinal Fut2-dependent fucosylation and failure of gut microbiota remodeling, thereby exacerbating systemic inflammatory responses [85]. Meanwhile, vagal afferent and efferent fibers synergistically participate in the inflammatory reflex. After the information transmitted by vagal afferent fibers is integrated in the CNS, vagal efferent fibers are activated to initiate cholinergic signaling, thereby enabling precise regulation of peripheral macrophages and immune cells [86]. Borovikova et al. [87] found that applying constant-voltage pulse stimulation to the vagal efferent fibers effectively prevented LPS-induced septic shock. This therapeutic effect was associated with the significant inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine release following the distal release of ACh by VN.

Based on the key regulatory role of the VN in the neuroimmune network. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has shown great potential in the treatment of stroke. The administration of VNS during the acute stage of IS can significantly improve gastrointestinal dysfunction, alleviate damage to the BBB and the colonic barrier, and thereby reduce the systemic inflammatory response [88]. VNS can also reduce brain inflammation by inhibiting the stimulation of neuroglial cells and cerebral endothelial cells by LPS [89]. It is worth noting that in the IS model following VN sectioning, the natural compounds β-citronellol and β-caryophyllene can enhance non-vagal gut-brain communication by modulating microbial metabolites and intestinal barrier integrity [90]. This suggests that these natural compounds can play a compensatory role when VN function is impaired, providing new potential targets for interventions against neuroinflammation following stroke.

Enteric nervous system (ENS)

The ENS consists of neurons and enteric glial cells (EGCs) and is capable of carrying out a variety of complex regulatory activities independently of the CNS [91]. In the context of stroke, the ENS exhibits regional susceptibility to ischemia, specifically manifested as varying degrees of loss of myenteric neurons in the colon and ileum [92]. These pathological changes compromise the integrity of tight junctions, promoting the translocation of microbiota and inflammatory signals, which in turn exacerbate systemic neuroinflammation.

The gut microbiota is essential for maintaining the homeostasis of the ENS. Microbiota depletion leads to defects in EGC dynamics and the loss of mucosal EGCs, thereby resulting in impaired intestinal peristalsis and increased intestinal permeability [93, 94]. These defects can be reversed after recolonization of the gut microbiota, highlighting the critical role of the EGCs/microbiota axis in maintaining the development and homeostasis of the ENS. Sun et al. [95] found that white kidney bean intervention can restore gut microbiota and metabolic homeostasis, inhibit EGC activation, and regulate Th1/Th17/Treg cells activity, thereby ultimately maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis. In mice with PSD, there was increased macrophage infiltration and EGCs damage in the ileal mucosa, while icariside II can mitigate inflammatory responses by targeting the Sirt6/NF-κB pathway, thereby protecting the enteric nervous network [96]. In summary, these research findings may provide a strong theoretical basis for the role of the gut microbiota in ENS damage and neuroinflammation following a stroke.

The hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis

The HPA axis serves as an integrative hub in maintaining homeostasis and the stress response. Stroke-induced lesions in the frontal and medial temporal lobes can disrupt the negative feedback mechanism of the HPA axis, leading to its sustained activation and exacerbating neuroinflammation [97]. An imbalance in the gut microbiota may participate in this process.

Early exposure to LPS can exert long-term effects on the HPA axis, particularly in adulthood, as evidenced by adrenocorticotropic hormone and corticosterone responses, and weakened glucocorticoid negative feedback inhibition [98]. In contrast, a combined intervention with Bacillus coagulans and Clostridium butyricum can reduce serum adrenocorticotropic hormone levels by regulating gut microbiota dysbiosis, thereby improving depressive and anxious behaviors [99]. These studies indicate that targeting the gut microbiota to modulate the HPA axis could be a promising strategy for improving functional outcomes following a stroke.

In addition to the HPA axis, multiple signaling molecules originating in the gut, such as serotonin (5-HT), are also involved in the progression of stroke. In mice with PSD, an imbalance in the gut microbiota was significantly associated with 5-HT, glutamate, and dopamine in the striatum; antibiotic treatment and FMT can effectively reverse this condition and suppress microglial activation [100]. In addition, acupuncture can effectively increase 5-HT and BDNF levels by regulating the composition of the gut microbiota, thereby effectively alleviating PSD [101]. In summary, the crosstalk between the gut microbiota and 5-HT may represent an important therapeutic target for PSD, warranting further in-depth investigation.

Gut microbiota metabolites-mediated pathway

SCFAs

SCFAs are significant metabolites of the gut microbiota. After being absorbed by colonic epithelial cells, some of them provide energy to the host cells, while the remainder enter the bloodstream via the portal vein, influencing brain function [102, 103]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that SCFAs are closely associated with various neurological disorders. For example, post-stroke cognitive impairment-associated gut microbiota can exacerbate hippocampal neuronal apoptosis and thalamic Aβ deposition by influencing LPS levels and the intestinal inflammatory responses [41]. Meanwhile, supplementation with NaB can reverse these injuries. Following a stroke, NaB exerted neuroprotective effects in delayed post-stroke treatment through a bidirectional mechanism [104]. In the early acute stage, it inhibited inflammatory factors and oxidative stress to protect brain tissue; and in the late acute stage, it increased the expression of IGF-1 in peripheral tissues to promote neural repair. In addition, NaB treatment has shown potential to prevent the loss of neuroblasts and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells in hypoxic-ischemic newborn rats, although its effect on neurogenesis at this stage is limited [105]. In summary, SCFAs participate in regulating neuroinflammation and repair processes following stroke through the MGBA; their molecular mechanisms of action and clinical translation value warrant further investigation.

Bile acids

Bile acids are biotransformed by the gut microbiota into secondary bile acids, which are subsequently recycled via the enterohepatic circulation. Secondary bile acids primarily include UDCA and deoxycholic acid. In the context of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI), dysbiosis of the gut microbiota may trigger abnormalities in bile acid metabolism, affecting the intestinal inflammatory response and barrier function [106]. However, combined treatment with UDCA and jasmonic acid can reduce infarct volume by regulating the p53 pathway, thereby improving focal CIRI [107]. Supplementation with UDCA can also alleviate morphological changes in hippocampal microglia following MCAO by regulating the TGR5/PKA pathway, thereby improving neural function [60]. Furthermore, the use of deoxycholic acid as a hydrophilic carrier in gene therapy can significantly reduce the toxicity of conventional carriers and enhance penetration of the BBB, offering greater therapeutic benefits in terms of reducing infarct volume and the number of apoptotic cells [108]. This suggests that bile acids may have broad application potential in the treatment of cerebrovascular diseases and drug delivery.

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)

TMAO is a metabolite produced by the gut microbiota that enters the bloodstream after being oxidized by the liver [109]. Multiple studies have shown that TMAO levels are closely associated with stroke risk factors, severity, adverse functional outcomes, and mortality. For example, Skye et al. [110] found that transplanting a microbial community with high levels of TMAO into recipient mice induced increased platelet reactivity and thrombosis, thereby increasing susceptibility to stroke. In IS, TMAO can also induce reactive astrocyte proliferation and gliosis through the Smurf2/ALK5 signaling pathway [111]. It is worth noting that, although high levels of TMAO induced by a long-term choline-rich diet can exacerbate the inflammatory response following ICH, its significant impact on the extent of brain injury remains unclear [112]. This may be related to insufficient exposure to TMAO in the brain, reduced food intake, and the short half-life of TMAO. This suggests that TMAO may play a role as a metabolic marker associated with the inflammatory response following ICH; however, the current evidence is insufficient to demonstrate that it significantly exacerbates brain injury. Further research is needed to explore its potential utility in prognostic assessment and personalized interventions following ICH.

Multi-mechanism interactions

Immune regulation, neural signaling, neuroendocrine signaling, and gut microbiota metabolites are key pathways mediating the signaling between the gut microbiota and neuroinflammation following a stroke (Fig. 3). These pathways do not operate independently but are interdependent and complementary, thereby forming an extensive and complex network.

In neural pathways, the gut microbiota influences central and intestinal immune homeostasis through pathways such as the sympathetic nerve, vagus nerve, and enteric nervous system. Post-stroke gut microenvironment imbalance also disrupts the negative feedback mechanism of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to abnormal release of adrenocorticotropic hormone and corticotropin-releasing factor, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation. Gut microbiota metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and bile acids serve as crucial signaling molecules in the microbiota-gut-brain axis and are also key factors in inducing neuroinflammation

First, the gut microbiota and its metabolites drive neuroinflammation through various mechanisms. Pro-inflammatory signaling molecules represented by LPS can cross the impaired intestinal barrier after cerebral ischemia and enter the circulatory system, inducing systemic inflammation; they may further traverse the BBB to activate microglia/macrophages and endothelial cells in the peri-infarct region of the CNS, thereby ultimately exacerbating brain injury [113, 114]. Specifically, LPS can influence the neuroendocrine system by binding to LPS-binding protein and activating the TLR4/MyD88 signaling pathway, leading to the release of corticosterone and subsequently triggering neuroinflammation [115]. LPS can also induce peripheral-central immune crosstalk by influencing the activation of astrocytes and dendritic cells, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [116]. However, SCFAs exert the opposite regulatory effect. SCFAs can not only indirectly influence microglial function by modulating peripheral lymphocytes and related inflammatory factor signaling, but also directly act on central microglia to alleviate ischemia-hypoxia-induced brain inflammation [117, 118]. NaB treatment can also improve anxiety and social deficits in LPS-exposed offspring by influencing epigenetic regulation of the HPA axis [119].

Second, central hypoxic-ischemic stimulation influences intestinal homeostasis through a positive feedback mechanism. After a stroke, the sympathetic nervous system becomes overactive and, by releasing norepinephrine, impairs the function of intestinal goblet cells and mucin secretion, thereby disrupting the intestinal microenvironment [120]. Following IS, excessive activation of the sympathetic nerve can also induce ischemic hypoxia injury in the gut, resulting in intestinal barrier dysfunction, microbiota translocation, and migration of intestinal immune cells, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [121]. At the same time, pathological changes associated with IS can trigger excessive release of glucocorticoids by the HPA axis, which attacks the gut, leading to metabolic abnormalities in the microbiota, damage to the intestinal mucosal structure, and disruption of tight junctions in endothelial cells, thereby increasing paracellular permeability [122].

In summary, this demonstrates that the immune, nervous, and neuroendocrine systems, along with metabolites, form a tightly interconnected network characterized by immune-metabolic coupling and neuro-immune coupling. An in-depth analysis of the multilevel, dynamic interactions among these signaling mechanisms is essential for developing targeted intervention strategies to effectively regulate post-stroke neuroinflammation.

Intervention strategies to target the gut microbiota

Dietary restructuring

Diet is one of the critical primary preventive measures for stroke [123]. Increasing evidence suggests that modifications in dietary composition, particularly the variety of foods and the proportion of nutritional components, can influence the pathophysiological processes of stroke by regulating intestinal homeostasis (Fig. 4).

Intervention strategies targeting the gut microbiota. Modifying dietary composition, probiotics/prebiotics/synbiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and Chinese herbal medicine can influence the composition and structure of gut microbiota and their metabolites, which effectively promote the colonization of beneficial bacteria, suppress neuroinflammation, and improve stroke outcomes

A plant-based diet is a dietary pattern that primarily relies on a wide variety of plant-based foods for energy and nutrients, and is closely associated with functional outcomes following stroke. For example, resveratrol can effectively suppress neuroinflammation following stroke by regulating the immune balance of Th17/Tregs and Th1/Th2 cells in the small intestine through the gut microbiota [124]. Intake of corn silk water extract can prevent neuronal cell death and post-stroke hyperglycemia through modulating oxidative stress and β-cell mass via the MGBA [125]. In focal CIRI diabetic rats, Dioscorea polystachya ‘yam gruel’ can suppress oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by regulating the relative abundance of probiotics and the level of SCFAs, ultimately improving cognitive function [126].

High-fat diets, in particular those rich in saturated fats and cholesterol, often lead to gut microbiota dysbiosis. A prolonged 45% high-fat diet can disrupt the gut microbiota, leading to elevated levels of serum inflammatory cytokines and an increase in the volume of cerebral infarcts [127]. However, a diet rich in unsaturated fatty acids can significantly reduce the severity of cerebral infarction following focal cerebral ischemia and improve motor and neurological function [128]. Treatment with omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids combined with docosahexaenoic acid can also effectively promote cognitive recovery after stroke by promoting neurogenesis and inhibiting glial scarring [129].

The ketogenic diet is a high-fat, low-carbohydrate dietary pattern, but it differs significantly from a high-fat diet in terms of diet composition and effectiveness. The ketogenic diet can significantly change the structure of the gut microbiota and protect mitochondrial DNA in the cerebral cortex of mice with IS by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathways [130]. In mice with IS, administration of β-hydroxybutyric acid in drinking water reduced astrocyte activation and inflammatory marker secretion, and accelerated sensorimotor and cognitive function [131]. These research findings demonstrate that the ketogenic diet is effective in the treatment of stroke; however, the potential mechanism by which it improves stroke outcomes through modulation of the gut microbiota requires further exploration.

In conclusion, improving dietary structure and adopting healthy eating habits can effectively regulate the gut microbiota, playing a positive role in stroke prevention and reducing the risk of recurrence.

Probiotics/prebiotics/synbiotics

Probiotics

Probiotics are organic supplements containing live non-pathogenic strains of bacteria, and common strains include Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [132]. Multiple clinical studies have confirmed the vital role of probiotics in stroke management and rehabilitation, particularly in the context of the MGBA.

Bifidobacterium compound preparations can improve intestinal barrier function in patients with severe IS, modulate immune balance, thereby reducing the risk of secondary infections, and promoting neurological recovery [133]. Zhong et al. [134] discovered that probiotics combined with enteral nutrition improved balance to the gut microbiota, suppressed the expression of inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10, and shortened the length of hospital stays and bedrest periods. Notably, the use of probiotics in specific high-risk cohorts, including neonates, patients with cancer, individuals with diabetes, and individuals after organ transplantation, may lead to serious infections, including pneumonia, endocarditis, and septicemia [135]. This may be because these individuals have weakened immune defenses and severely compromised intestinal barriers, making them more susceptible to probiotic-associated bacterial translocation and opportunistic infections, which can lead to serious complications. Therefore, a thorough risk and benefit assessment should be conducted before considering probiotic supplementation to maximize its effectiveness.

Prebiotics

Prebiotics, which act as substrates that selectively promote the activity of beneficial microorganisms, are present in a variety of common foods, particularly whole grains, fruits, and vegetables [62]. Prebiotics positively affect human health through various mechanisms, such as regulating lipid metabolism, maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and stimulating the growth of intestinal cells.

Polysaccharides from Polygoni Multiflori Radix Praeparata can significantly improve the metabolism of SCFAs and amino acids, increase the proliferation of a beneficial gut microbiota, improve intestinal homeostasis, thereby ultimately alleviating MCAO-induced cerebral and intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury [136]. Lian et al. [137] showed that oral administration of Puerariae Lobatae Radix-resistant starch significantly improved brain injury and intestinal barrier dysfunction by enriching the abundance of Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium and increasing levels of the gut metabolite melatonin. Through further studies, they discovered that melatonin mitigates brain injury by mediating the co-occurrence patterns of the gut microbiota. This mechanism emphasizes the importance of the gut microbiota and its metabolites as potential targets for stroke.

Synbiotics

Synbiotics integrate the advantages of probiotics and prebiotics, not only enhancing probiotic survival rates and activity but also improving the gut microecological balance and immune responses. Numerous studies have shown that the combined use of probiotics and prebiotics yields more significant results than the administration of either agent individually. Synbiotic treatment can increase the relative abundance of Prevotella, Lactobacillus, Rhamnosus, and Bacteroides and enhance intestinal barrier function, thereby alleviating MCAO-induced neuronal dysplasia [138]. Supplementation with Enterococcus faecalis and agave inulin, for 14 days post-stroke, remarkably reduced TNF-α levels, decreased neurological deficits, and ultimately improved memory and learning abilities [139]. Although these studies indicate that synbiotic supplements have significant neuroprotective effects, future clinical translation must focus on evaluating individualized responses, given the complexity of the human gut microbiota and interindividual differences.

FMT

FMT is a therapy that restores gut microbiota balance by transferring fecal samples from healthy donors to the recipients. Through FMT, the healthy microbiota in the recipient can rapidly proliferate and replace the dysbiotic microbiota, enhancing intestinal metabolic capacity and immune function [140]. Currently, FMT has demonstrated potential therapeutic effects in the treatment of stroke. For instance, a study has found that the gut microbiota of type-2 diabetes exacerbates brain injury following IS [141]. Conversely, transplantation of NaB-treated fecal microbiota can enhance intestinal barrier function and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. In mice with permanent MCAO, transplantation of atorvastatin-treated fecal microbiota can effectively correct gut dysbiosis and modulate intestinal immunity, suggesting that FMT has anti-inflammatory effects comparable to oral administration of atorvastatin [142].

The efficacy of FMT is significantly influenced by the age of the donor. Prevotella and Akkermansia dominate in young mice, whereas the relative abundance of these two genera gradually decreases in older mice [143]. These age-related changes in the gut microbiota provide a potential pathological basis for health issues during aging. Transplanting the gut microbiota of aged mice into young mice caused the intestinal environment to age, thereby increasing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and mortality following IS [144]. However, transferring the fecal microbiota of young mice via gavage can restore the disrupted microbial environment, suppress IL-17 expression in the gut, serum, and brain tissue, and thereby alleviate CIRI in aged mice [145]. This indicates that “fecal age” influences intestinal homeostasis and the susceptibility of the host to immune decline. Therefore, selecting healthy, young donor samples for FMT is more effective in improving the gut microbial environment and restoring overall function.

Sex may be another key factor influencing the efficacy of FMT. An animal study on stroke has also confirmed that male mice have pronounced changes in the abundance of Turicibacter and Ruminococcaceae, whereas female mice have substantial shifts in the abundance of Akkermansia, Alistipes, Faecalibaculum, and Ruminococcus [146]. Compared to colonizing with a microbiota from males, supplementing with a microbiota from females can positively modulate post-stroke functional outcomes, including an improved survival rate, reduced infarct volume, and reduced inflammation level [147]. These studies indicate that the gut microbiota dynamically responds to stroke-induced effects in a sex-specific manner. Future studies should further explore the specific mechanisms linking sex, the gut microbiota, and stroke recovery.

Chinese herbal medicine

Due to its multi-component and multi-targeted nature, Chinese herbal medicine provides distinct advantages in the prevention and treatment of neurological disorders. A growing body of research indicates that Chinese herbal monomers and compound formulas have significant therapeutic potential at different stages of stroke by regulating the gut microbiota. Preliminary treatment with rhubarb can improve the gut microbiota structure, reduce cerebral edema, while improving cerebral blood flow perfusion and histopathological changes in brain regions following IS [148]. It is worth noting that the therapeutic effects of rhubarb have a clear dose-dependent relationship, suggesting that in clinical practice, consideration should be given to optimizing the dosage to avoid excessive amounts that could cause the drug to shift from being therapeutic to causing harm. Treatment with baicalin can significantly restore plasma levels of TMAO and clusterin, suppress neuroinflammation, and improve hippocampal neuronal plasticity after CIRI [149]. In the absence of the gut microbiota, the neuroprotective effects of baicalin were significantly attenuated. These studies indicate that a healthy gut microbiome plays a vital part in drug metabolism and the manifestation of drug efficacy.

Traditional Chinese medicine formulas, which typically consist of multiple herbal ingredients containing various active components, can regulate numerous pathological processes related to stroke. In a rat model of acute lung injury complicated by ICH, Tongfu Xingshen capsules can significantly alleviate brain injury by alleviating gut microbiota dysbiosis and sphingolipid metabolism disorders, while also demonstrating positive effects on pneumonia and lung injury [150]. Oral administration of Xiaoyaosan can improve depression-like behaviors in mice with PSD by regulating the gut microbiota and metabolites, and influencing the expression of P2X7R/NLRP3 inflammasome [151].

In summary, Chinese herbal medicine contributes to the onset and progression of stroke by activating the MGBA. In-depth studies into key microbiota and their causal relationships with inflammatory and neuroprotective pathways provide a strong theoretical basis for promoting the precision application of Chinese herbal medicine in the comprehensive treatment of stroke.

Antibiotic therapy

Antibiotics are a class of drugs used to treat bacterial infections and help people stay healthy. Antibiotics represent the most direct and effective means of influencing the structure and function of the gastrointestinal microbiota [140]. Under specific conditions, antibiotics may positively affect stroke treatment by modulating the gut microbiota. An animal study has shown that early administration of minocycline following CIRI can enhance the expression of tight junction proteins and activate neuroprotective phenotype microglia/macrophages, and thereby significantly reshape brain networks [152]. Another animal study also confirmed that treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics before endothelin-1-induced ischemia can alter the composition of the gut microbiota and modulate systemic and cerebral inflammation via the peripheral circulation, thereby improving stroke outcomes [153].

However, some clinical studies have also indicated that antibiotics may have potential adverse effects on functional outcomes following a stroke (Table 3). Preventive ceftriaxone treatment in patients with acute stroke did not significantly improve functional outcomes, nor did it shorten the length of hospital stays or reduce in-hospital or follow-up mortality [154, 155]. In elderly patients with moderate-to-severe to severe acute stroke, prophylactic ceftriaxone did not improve functional outcomes or reduce the incidence of pneumonia within 7 days after stroke. Kalra et al. [156] found that prophylactic antibiotics did not substantially decrease the incidence of pneumonia in patients with post-stroke dysphagia and may cause adverse events, such as prolonged hospital stays, delayed diagnosis, and drug-resistant infections. The administration of intravenous minocycline for stroke within 24 h of onset did not reduce mortality or dependency rates at 90 days of follow-up [157]. These findings may be attributed to the complex mechanisms underlying post-stroke infections, particularly pneumonia, which can not be cured simply through antibiotics. Additionally, some patients may already have had aspiration or latent infections at admission or in the early stages of stroke, which delays the timing of preventive interventions, and the antibiotics used may also have inherent limitations. Therefore, in clinical practice, high-quality stroke unit care and early identification of complications should be emphasized, and the necessity of antibiotic use should be carefully assessed.

Relevant contradictory findings regarding the gut microbiota

Choline diet-induced high serum levels of TMAO did not enhance the expression of P38-MAPK, MyD88, HMGB1, or IL-1β at 24 h.

Choline diet-induced high serum levels of TMAO did not exacerbate acute or long-term histologic damage or neurologic deficits after ICH.

This study did not support that TMAO is a promising target for ICH therapy.

In particular, the individuals under neonatal stages and/or those with some clinical conditions, including malignancies, leaky gut, diabetes mellitus, and post-organ transplant convalescence, are unlikely to benefit from probiotics.

Some probiotic strains might take advantage of the weak immunity in these vulnerable populations and transform into opportunistic pathogens, engendering life-threatening pneumonia, endocarditis, and sepsis.

The unregulated and rampant use of probiotics potentially increases the risk of plasmid-mediated antibiotic resistance transfer to the gut infectious pathogens.

Prophylactic antimicrobial therapy did not improve functional outcomes in adults with acute stroke, nor did it shorten hospital stays or reduce mortality during hospitalization or follow-up.

This study did not support the use of preventive antibiotics in adults with acute stroke.

In patients aged 66 years or older, prophylactic use of ceftriaxone in the first 4 days following IS or ICH did not result in improvement of functional outcome or a reduction in the risk of death.

Preventive antibiotics failed to reduce the rate of pneumonia in the first 7 days.

Prophylactic antibiotics did not reduce the incidence of post-stroke pneumonia or mortality in patients with acute stroke with dysphagia.

Prophylactic antibiotics might increase the length of hospital stays and lead to poor outcomes in patients after stroke with dysphagia.

Stem cell therapy

Stem cell therapy is a treatment that uses the self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities to promote the repair of damaged tissues or organs. Stem cell therapy, represented by mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells, not only holds tremendous therapeutic effects in regenerative medicine but can also modulate immune responses by influencing the function of the gut microbiota [158]. As an emerging treatment strategy, stem cell therapy has garnered widespread attention in the clinical treatment of stroke (Table 4).

MSCs are multipotent cells derived from adult tissues, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord. With the involvement of the gut microbiota, MSC-derived exosomal miR-150-3p can suppress inflammatory responses and cell death via the TRAF6/NF-κB axis, thereby improving neurological damage following ICH [159]. Bone marrow MSC therapy can increase the abundance of bacteria capable of producing SCFAs and Lactobacillus, suppress neuroinflammation, and improve balance and coordination in rats with stroke [160]. In pigs with IS, combined treatment with induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neural stem cells and Tanshinone IIA-loaded nanoparticles significantly elevated the SCFA level throughout the study period [21]. This change was not only associated with reduced jejunal inflammation and improved intestinal integrity, but also with decreased brain lesion volume.

In summary, the crosstalk between stem cell therapy and the gut microbiota demonstrates broad potential in the treatment of stroke, and further high-quality studies are required to validate the safety and efficacy of its clinical translation.

Limitations and challenges in microbiome research

Limitations and challenges in microbiome research

Although existing research has confirmed that MGBA plays a key role in the onset and progression of neuroinflammation during stroke, there are still many limitations in this field that hinder its clinical translation. First, the choice of experimental animal models and their inherent biological limitations significantly affect the reliability of research findings. Currently, germ-free animal models and antibiotic-treated models are key tools for studying the mechanisms of action of the gut microbiota. However, due to the lack of microbial stimulation during growth and development, germ-free animals exhibit delayed development of the immune system, organs, and the nervous system, resulting in significant differences from normal animals in terms of physiological functions and pathological responses. Although antibiotic treatment models are easy to create, they also have inherent limitations. On the one hand, antibiotic treatment cannot eliminate gut microbiota, which may lead to the proliferation of certain drug-resistant bacteria and opportunistic pathogens once competitive pressure is reduced, thereby triggering systemic inflammation [161]. On the other hand, antibiotic treatment may produce off-target effects that affect mitochondrial function, the neuroendocrine system, and the immune system, making it difficult to distinguish whether research findings are driven by microbiota depletion or off-target effects [162].

Second, interspecies differences limit the generalizability of the research findings for clinical translation. Currently, most studies on MGBA are based on rodents; however, there are significant differences between rodents and humans in terms of intestinal anatomy, microbial composition, and immune response patterns; this suggests that the protective effects observed in animal models may fail to replicate in clinical trials due to unsuccessful colonization competition or antagonism from the host metabolic microenvironment. Meanwhile, patients with stroke often suffer from complications such as hypertension, diabetes, and pneumonia, whereas animal models typically involve a single condition, failing to fully replicate the complexity of clinical cases. Furthermore, the progression of neuroinflammation following a stroke is highly dynamic and irreversible; its cascade of reactions unfolds rapidly within a very short time window, which determines whether neurological function can be restored. However, intervention strategies targeting the gut microbiota suffer from a “time lag” in their mechanisms of action, making it difficult to deliver precise, synchronized treatment within the golden window of opportunity for neuroinflammation.

Finally, the challenges of standardizing experimental protocols in microbiome research affect the reproducibility of the studies. Currently, the lack of uniform standards regarding sample collection timing, methods, and storage may introduce batch effects, leading to false results. Differences in DNA extraction methods and the choice of sequencing platforms can also make it difficult to directly compare data across studies, and may even lead to conflicting conclusions. In addition, the microbiome of laboratory animals is extremely sensitive and highly plastic; even minor changes in the external environment can alter the composition and abundance of microbiota within the animals, which in turn affects the reproducibility of experimental data [163].

Prospects for future research

In summary, future research should focus on overcoming these challenges and bridging the gap between basic research and clinical practice, thereby advancing the translation of precision medicine. First, humanized microbiota animal models, organoid-on-a-chip co-culture systems, and comorbidity models should be employed to simulate complex clinical pathological states. These approaches can mitigate the impact of model limitations and species differences on mechanistic studies, thereby facilitating the translation of findings from animal models into human research. First, by utilizing animal models with humanized gut microbiota and organoid co-culture systems to simulate complex clinical pathological conditions, we can minimize the impact of model limitations and species differences on mechanistic studies, thereby facilitating the translation of findings from animal models to human research. Second, by integrating multi-omics and single-cell sequencing data, we can identify the precise molecular trajectories by which gut-derived signals modulate neuroinflammation, ultimately establishing a dynamic regulatory network linking the gut microbiota and neuroinflammation. In addition, promoting the establishment of standardized experimental protocols covering the entire process, including sample collection, processing, and microbiome sequencing and analysis, can reduce heterogeneity across studies and improve data quality and reproducibility. Finally, through large-scale, multicenter, long-term prospective clinical studies, it is possible to establish a deep correlation between the baseline gut microbiome profiles of patients, comorbidities, and treatment responses, thereby identifying personalized gut microbiome intervention strategies and achieving precision medicine.

Conclusion

The gut microbiota has emerged as a pivotal entry point for understanding the onset, progression, and intervention of neuroinflammation following a stroke. This review elucidated a complex bidirectional regulatory network, namely the MGBA. Through this network, gut microbiota dysbiosis can regulate neuroinflammation via the immune, nervous, and neuroendocrine systems and gut microbiota metabolites. On one hand, gut dysbiosis and subsequent metabolic aberrations can exacerbate systemic inflammation and promote central inflammation. On the other hand, cerebral hypoxic-ischemic injury, excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the HPA axis, and impaired intestinal barrier function can further disrupt the gut microenvironment, thereby establishing a vicious cycle of “brain injury-gut dysbiosis-inflammation amplification.” Consequently, the gut microbiota should be regarded as a key regulator within the entire pathological chain of post-stroke neuroinflammation, rather than a mere bystander in the pathological process.

In terms of intervention strategies, dietary interventions, probiotics/prebiotics, FMT, and Chinese herbal medicine have shown some neuroprotective potential. By restoring gut microbial homeostasis, fortifying the intestinal barrier, and modulating immune cell activity, these approaches can mitigate post-stroke neuroinflammation and facilitate the restoration of neural functional networks. Nevertheless, the current body of evidence remains heavily reliant on preclinical animal models. Human studies, being predominantly observational, have highlighted the correlation between gut dysbiosis and stroke outcomes but fall short of elucidating a definitive causal link. Consequently, future research should prioritize the development of animal models with greater pathophysiological fidelity and the implementation of standardized experimental protocols to explore the causal relationships between key gut microbiota and their metabolites and neuroinflammatory pathways. At the same time, leveraging multi-omics integration and conducting large-scale, multicenter clinical trials is imperative to precisely evaluate the safety, efficacy, and optimal therapeutic window of targeted microbiota interventions. Deciphering the intricate bidirectional crosstalk of the MGBA will provide critical support for developing new noninvasive therapies to alleviate neuroinflammation and improve functional recovery in patients with stroke.
