Comparative genomic analysis of clinically relevant human skin-associated fungi
Authors: Sofie Agerbæk, Knud Nor Nielsen, Julie B K Sølberg, Ying Marlene Zhang, Zahra Akil Meften Al-Badran, Marc Stegger, Sonja Kabatnik, Matthias Mann, Rachel A Clark, Ditte M L Saunte, Alberto Santos, Marianne Bengtson Løvendorf, Beatrice Dyring-Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal skin infections represent a significant global health burden, affecting approximately one billion people annually. Despite their prevalence and major global health impact, the molecular mechanisms underlying pathogenicity remain largely uncharacterized. Here we present the genomes of 66 fungal strains, representing highly prevalent and clinically relevant species associated with human skin infections. Using comparative genomics, we investigate whether the taxonomic groups in this collection of skin fungi differ in their genome architecture, metabolic capacity, secreted enzyme repertoires, adhesin content, and predicted virulence determinants. These analyses reveal substantial variation in genome size and gene contents, indicating genome compaction occurred as the fungi transitioned from free-living to host-associated lifestyles. We report two non-hybrid strains of Trichosporon ovoides, the causative agent of white piedra. Our analyses reveal substantial differences in metabolic adaptations across skin-associated fungi, corresponding to body-site and nutrient niches. Significant differences are also observed in the distribution of virulence factors and adhesins, which are imperative for biofilm formation and antifungal resistance. We discuss metabolic adaptation and virulence mechanisms revealed by our data in the context of clinical presentations, highlighting shared and lineage-specific signatures of adaptations. Together, these insights advance our understanding of skin-associated fungi and their mechanisms of infection.Subject terms: Infection, Fungal pathogenesis, Clinical microbiology, Phylogenomics

Introduction

Superficial fungal infections (SFIs) represent common dermatological conditions, impacting approximately one billion people worldwide1. With an estimated lifetime risk of 20–25%, these infections consistently rank among the top ten most prevalent skin diseases and include common diseases such as ringworm and pityriasis versicolor in a broad range of niches on the human body (Fig. 1). While specific fungal pathogens show distinct geographical distributions, the predominant causative organisms continue to persist worldwide. The primary etiological agents of SFIs belong to three major groups: molds of the family Arthrodermataceae (dermatophytes), yeasts of the order Saccharomycetales, and yeasts of the genus Malassezia2. Many of these fungi are part of the normal human skin microbiome, and especially Malassezia spp. are abundant in sebum-rich areas such as the scalp, face, and upper trunk3.

Despite their significant clinical importance and worldwide distribution, several skin-associated fungi lack comprehensive genome sequencing data, and for species with available information, many reference genomes are either incomplete or unavailable in public databases. Furthermore, available genomic data frequently originates from single isolates of unknown origin, failing to capture host intraspecies variation.

Diagnosis of superficial mycoses currently presents significant challenges due to their diverse yet overlapping clinical manifestations, which commonly include erythema, inflammation, pruritus, pigmentation abnormalities, hair loss, and desquamation1. Diagnostic analysis typically begins with patient history assessment and clinical evaluation, followed by direct microscopic examination of specimens (skin/nail scrapings or hair) using potassium hydroxide preparation and time-consuming culture-based methodologies for definitive identification. Increasingly, PCR-based molecular techniques targeting ribosomal RNA or Internal Transcribed Spacer regions are used as alternatives to microscopy and culture because they offer superior sensitivity, specificity, and faster turnaround times. However, these advanced methods are generally limited to high-income settings, whereas conventional approaches remain the mainstay in lower-income communities due to cost and infrastructure constraints4.

Next-generation sequencing technologies have revolutionized our ability to study microbial genomes over the last decade5. Still, many clinically relevant, globally prevalent fungi remain poorly characterized at the genomic level. The limited availability of well-annotated genomes for skin-associated fungi has hindered comparative genomic analyses that could reveal evolutionary adaptations to the skin environment, virulence mechanisms, and the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

Despite these limitations, recent reports have revealed important differences in adaptation processes and niche specialization across species of skin-associated fungi. Different requirements for energy sources, salt tolerance, and water availability lead to distinct body-site preferences; for instance, lipophilic Malassezia fungi tend to inhabit sebum-rich skin regions where they cause conditions including pityriasis versicolor and seborrheic dermatitis6. Virulence mechanisms also differ across skin-associated fungi, particularly with respect to the adhesin types they express to facilitate host cell attachment and the enzymes they secrete to assimilate energy or invade tissue7–9. These differences underline the high degree of variance in host specificity and tissue tropism observed among skin-associated fungi, contributing to their diverse clinical manifestations. While genomic studies on Candida spp. have shed light on the role of adhesin diversity and virulence factors10,11, there are limited data to support comparative genomics across skin-associated fungi, despite their high prevalence.

In this study, we present genome-scale data for a collection of 48 strains of clinically relevant fungi associated with SFIs. In combination with genomes of other clinically relevant species and non-pathogenic comparison species acquired from online databases, the full dataset includes 66 strains, including 32 fungal species with the highest global prevalence in human skin infections. Using comparative genomic analyses, we investigated whether skin-associated genera and families differ significantly in (i) metabolic gene content, (ii) predicted secreted enzyme repertoires, (iii) adhesin distribution, and (iv) virulence-associated gene abundance. By comparing the presence and copy-number variation of genes associated with these traits, we aimed to determine whether distinct clinical presentations and tissue tropisms reflect measurable genomic divergence. By providing a genomic resource of clinically relevant skin-associated fungi, this work establishes a framework for elucidating fungal pathogenesis underlying SFI-associated diseases and for guiding the development of targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Results

Comparative genomic analysis of clinically relevant fungal species reveals non-hybrid strains of Trichosporon ovoides

To establish a genomic framework covering the most common fungi associated with SFIs in humans, we reviewed the literature to identify the most prevalent and thereby clinically relevant species worldwide (details in Methods). Our analysis identified ten predominant genera: Candida, Epidermophyton, Hortaea, Malassezia, Microsporum, Nakaseomyces, Nannizzia, Pichia, Trichophyton, and Trichosporon. Within these genera, we identified 20 species commonly associated with human superficial mycoses, representing diverse taxonomic groups and infection sites (Fig. 1). To ensure clinical relevance, we targeted strains isolated from human skin or skin-related infections when available. The final cohort comprised 48 distinct fungal strains (Supplementary Data 1) cultured and sequenced using Illumina technology. To expand our cohort and enable species comparisons, we included genomes from 12 additional clinically relevant skin-associated fungal species, alongside 3 non-pathogenic, and 3 pathogenic non skin-associated fungal species (Supplementary Data 1).

The fungal genomes exhibited considerable size variation, ranging from below 8 Mbp in Malassezia spp. to over 50 Mbp in H. werneckii. Among dermatophytes, we observed similar genome sizes and gene numbers, with genome sizes ranging from 21.6 Mbp in T. rubrum to 24.5 Mbp in T. tonsurans. Some H. werneckii and M. furfur strains displayed a notably larger genome ( ~ 50 Mbp), consistent with previously reported genomic hybridizations12,13 (Fig. 2a).

Interestingly, two strains of T. ovoides (CBS7612 and CBS4098) exhibited genomes approximately half the length of a third strain (CBS9430), with the latter corresponding in size to the genomes available on NCBI genomes14. Previous reports argued that T. ovoides (and T. coremiiforme) are natural hybrids, with reported genome sizes of 42 Mbp15,16, while other members of this genus, T. asahii and T. inkin are documented non-hybrids. To validate our results, which instead support the presence of non-hybrid T. ovoides strains, we performed Average Nucleotide Identity analysis (OrthoANIu17) comparing our three isolates with reference genomes of each ovoides (GenBank Accession ID: GCA_040368805.1), coremiiforme (GCA_039545355.1), asahii (GCA_000293215.1), and inkin (GCA_040365635.1). Our three samples had > 97.5% identity with the ovoides reference, and less than 85.5% identity with the three other species, confirming their classification as T. ovoides.

We assessed the quality and completeness of the assemblies using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Ortholog analysis against the fungi db10 database (BUSCO18). The majority of genomes demonstrated high completeness scores with few missing orthologs, while some cases showed missing and fragmented percentages above 20% (Fig. 2b). The stand-alone cases were likely due to short-read mis-assemblies resulting from the high-repeat genomes characteristic of these fungi, while the high proportion observed across all Malassezia spp. was more likely due to loss of genes as part of host-adaptation and pathogenicity13. Duplication percentages above 50% were observed in the hybrid genomes of T. ovoides, H. werneckii, and M. furfur. Taken together, we assembled 48 genomes of clinically relevant skin-associated fungi, discovering previously undocumented non-hybrid strains of T. ovoides, the causative agent of white piedra.

Core- and species-specific gene families in clinically relevant fungal species

To identify orthologous genes across fungal strains, we performed ortholog clustering using OrthoFinder19. Analysis of 455,479 genes across all samples yielded 18,207 orthogroups (OGs), comprising 98.2% of the genes. Of these, 871 OGs were conserved across all samples while 1018 OGs were strain-specific (Supplementary Data 2). Species-specific OG distribution varied markedly from zero in many multi-sample species, such as C. dubliniensis and M. audouinii to 1729 in H. werneckii.

We identified no OGs shared exclusively across all skin-associated species (excluding the outgroups). The absence of a common genomic signature linked to pathogenicity was expected due to the large evolutionary distance and independent evolution of pathogenicity in these diverse fungi.

We investigated the evolutionary relationships between these fungal species by constructing a maximum likelihood phylogeny based on 160 OGs with all samples present, with sequence alignment performed using MAFFT20 and tree inference using IQ-TREE21 (Fig. 3a). The inferred phylogeny was consistent with the expected taxonomy. The dermatophytes in the T. mentagrophytes species complex (T. mentagrophytes, T. interdigitale, and T. indotineae) displayed non-monophyletic relationships, likely reflecting the ancient evolutionary splits represented across the tree. These deep splits occurred over short evolutionary timeframes, complicating phylogenetic inference. The split between T. mentagrophytes, T. interdigitale, and T. indotineae is evolutionarily very recent, with host-switching (from zoophilic to anthropophilic) and changes in clinical presentation and virulence caused by antifungal resistance being the main drivers for the separation22.

Analyses of family-, genus-, and species-specific OGs revealed patterns consistent with evolutionary relationships (Fig. 3a). The closely related dermatophytes showed very few species-specific and genus-specific OGs. Regarding the broader phylogenetic tree, we observed more order-specific OGs within N. glabratus, P. kudriavzevii, and S. cerevisiae compared to Candida spp., which had high proportions of genus-specific OGs. This aligns with the more ancient split between these genera, compared to e.g., Microsporum from the rest of the Arthrodermataceae. Among Basidiomycota, M. furfur displayed more species-specific OGs than the other more closely related Malassezia spp. The phylogenetically distant Hortaea and Trichosporon showed the highest number of species-specific OGs. Since no close relatives of these species were included, these OGs may reflect genus- or family-level specificity rather than species-specific traits.

While 871 OGs were shared across all species, 1853 OGs contained at least one representative of each genus (Fig. 3b). For the multi-species genera, Candida possessed the largest OG set with 1225 OGs containing only genes from Candida spp., followed by Malassezia spp. with 829 OGs (Fig. 3b). At the higher taxonomic level, 220 OGs contained only genera of the Saccharomycetales, while 210 OGs contained only Arthrodermataceae genera. Together, these analyses reveal both the conservation of core gene families essential for fungal biology and the emergence of species-specific gene sets that potentially contribute to the unique adaptive capabilities of these fungi closely associated with SFIs.

Metabolic adaptation revealed by functional genomic analysis

We next combined functional annotations of constituent genes within each OG. Genes were annotated with eggNOG mapper23 and Interproscan24 with several databases, including Cluster of Orthologous Genes (COG), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Enzyme Commission number (EC) (see Methods for details). We compared KEGG and EC functional categories across species (Fig. 4). For KEGG categories, we found significant differences among genera and families in all categories except carbohydrate metabolism (P ≤ 0.018, Supplementary Data 3, 4). Similar results were found across EC hydrolase subcategories, with significant differences across all categories among both genera and families (P ≤ 0.038, Supplementary Data 5, 6). Some single-species genera and families, including the outgroups, showed large deviations, but pairwise comparisons were not statistically robust due to limited replication.

Consistent with their reliance on host keratin and structural skin proteins, Arthrodermataceae showed significantly higher proportions of amino acid metabolism-associated genes compared with all other families (Saccharomycetaceae: P = 0.001, Malasseziaceae: P = 0.001, and Debaryomycetaceae: P = 0.002). Their saprobic relative, C. queenslandicum, showed a higher proportion of genes in the energy and amino acid metabolism categories, along with fewer in the lipid and nucleotide metabolism category.

Differences in the lipid and nucleotide metabolism category were also observed among the Saccharomycetaceae and Debaryomycetaceae, where the former had significantly expanded lipid metabolism (P < 0.001). This divergent evolution between families is not apparent in terms of clinical presentation and body-site niches. Still, these variations have potential molecular importance since they may allow the utilization of different energy sources in competitive environments.

Free-living species A. fumigatus and H. werneckii contained higher proportions of KEGG-categorized carbohydrate metabolism genes, supported by more glycosylases and fewer peptide and acid anhydride targeting hydrolases. The Malassezia spp. exhibited the lowest levels of glycosylases, with significantly fewer than the dermatophytes on both family (P = 0.014) and genus level (Microsporum: P = 0.026 and Trichophyton: P = 0.004). They also had significantly fewer enzymes targeting carbon-nitrogen-bonds than Trichophyton spp. (P = 0.032), and significantly more acid anhydrides than Candida spp. and Trichophyton spp. (P = 0.010 and P = 0.036). This aligns with previously reported gene loss, particularly in carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, as these species evolved to depend on host lipids as their primary carbon source13. Surprisingly, we found the Malassezia spp. to have significantly less KEGG-categorized lipid-targeting than the Candida spp. (P = 0.010). Indicating that although Malassezia spp. are obligately lipid-dependent, extensive gene loss associated with host specialization may have reduced overall lipolytic diversity, whereas Candida spp. retain a broader enzymatic repertoire that includes substantial lipid-degrading capacity.

In conclusion, our functional analysis highlights distinct metabolic profiles between taxonomic groups, providing insight into different adaptations to the skin environment.

Distribution of secreted enzymes reflects species-specific host adaptation mechanisms to the skin

To further understand how different fungi adapt to living on human skin, we analyzed the enzymes they secrete using SignalP25. This analysis yielded 608 OGs containing a total of 19,977 genes, including 239 OGs with no functional annotation (Supplementary Data 7). The proportion of predicted secreted genes differed significantly across species (F-test: F(37, 28) = 26.3, P < 0.001). In pairwise comparisons, certain species had significantly different proportions than most others, such as the Malassezia spp., Candida spp. and N. glabratus, which were all significantly different from all other species except each other (P ≤ 0.005), and H. werneckii against all other species (P ≤ 0.041; Supplementary Data 8). This pattern largely followed the taxonomical distance between species, with no clear distinction within closely related genera and families such as the dermatophytes.

We focused on genes potentially involved in energy assimilation from the host, such as protein, carbohydrate, and lipid-degrading enzymes (Fig. 5). Many of these enzymes may also play roles in pathogenesis and virulence7. We found significant differences among genera for the lipid category (χ2 (15) = 81.8, P < 0.001) and carbohydrate category (χ2(15) = 27.4, P = 0.026), while in pairwise comparisons, we found differences within most gene groups across all categories (Supplementary Data 9).

Dermatophytes showed enrichment in multiple types of secreted proteases, consistent with their keratinophilic nature, where they rely on the degradation of skin and nail proteins as a carbon source. We found significantly greater numbers of metalloprotease and subtilisin, which are enzymes that are involved in keratin hydrolysis26, in all dermatophyte genera (except Nannizzia), compared to H. werneckii, T. ovoides, Candida spp., and Malassezia spp. (P ≤ 0.023, Supplementary Data 10). Similarly, the number of fungalysin-annotated genes in dermatophytes was greater compared with all other samples, including their non-pathogenic relative, C. queenslandicum, however, formal statistical testing was not feasible for fungalysin and other sparse categories due to zero inflation across multiple genera, which precluded robust model fitting. For metalloproteases and subtilisins, we did not detect significant differences between dermatophytes and C. queenslandicum, where sufficient gene counts enabled statistical comparison. Together, these findings suggest that while certain keratin-associated proteases appear expanded in dermatophytes, not all protease families uniformly distinguish pathobiont from closely related non-pathogenic taxa. In contrast, the lung pathogens H. capsulatum and A. fumigatus had fewer gene copies of both metalloproteases and subtilisin, suggesting that the expansion of these enzymes occurred following the evolutionary divergence of these species. The degradation of structural proteins in the skin is relevant to the pathogenicity and virulence of keratinophilic fungi during infection and invasion, as it activates the host immune system and leads to an inflammatory response27. In contrast, Saccharomycetales spp., Malassezia spp., and T. ovoides generally exhibited lower copy numbers of secreted protein-targeting enzymes, apart from a genus-specific cysteine proteinase in Malassezia spp. and an aspartyl protease. The aspartyl proteases were significantly more abundant in Malassezia spp. compared with most other genera, including Trichophyton spp. (P < 0.001) and Candida spp. (P = 0.005). Saccharomycetales spp. were also significantly enriched in aspartyl proteases compared with most other genera apart from Malassezia (Supplementary Data 10). Secreted aspartyl proteases (Saps) are known to mediate disease progression through adhesion to host cells, macrophage modulation, and complement cascade disruption in Candida spp.28. We identifed a single OG of Saps in Saccharomycetales, which was most expanded with 21 copies in C. orthopsilosis, a species in which the Saps have not yet been functionally described. In Malassezia spp. Saps are also reported to contribute to inflammation and to play a role in host-cell adhesion. Moreover, a recent study proposed an allergenic role for these proteases29.

Across the carbohydrate-targeting secreted genes, we observed numerous significant pairwise differences among the genera (Supplementary Data 11). Carbohydrates are present on the skin surface in the form of glycolipids and glycoproteins. Breakdown of external polysaccharides is central to energy assimilation for most fungi and is involved in pathogenesis and host immune activation in plant-pathogens30. Increased growth and infections by Candida spp. in patients with diabetes have been proposed to be exacerbated by the hyperglycemia-associated increases in sugar availability31. Consistent with this, we observed enrichment in sugar metabolism in Candida spp. compared with the dermatophyte genera and Malassezia spp. (P < 0.003), reflecting their broader metabolic flexibility and ability to exploit host-derived carbohydrates.

The free-living species H. werneckii and A. fumigatus showed significantly higher numbers of glycosidases, lytic transglycolase, LPMO, and cellulose degradation compared with various of the other species (Supplementary Data 11), consistent with their dependence on plant matter degradation. The host-dependent Trichophyton spp. still contain genes involved in the degradation of plant material, pointing to a gradual evolution where gene loss can be a slow process. In contrast, the lack of such enzymes in Malassezia spp. may reflect the host-adaptation these species have undergone, involving genome compaction and a shift to lipid-dependent metabolism32.

We observe significantly higher copy numbers of lipase and phospholipase genes in Malassezia spp. and Candida spp., compared with most of the other genera (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 12). Lipolytic activity plays a significant role in both growth and virulence of skin fungi33. Lipid-dependent Malassezia spp. use external lipids as a central energy source due to their lack of a fatty acid synthase13, while skin disorders associated with Malassezia spp. are suggested to be induced by the breakdown of skin surface lipids into free fatty acids34. Our analysis confirmed the absence of fatty acid synthetase genes in all Malassezia spp. while identifying these genes in all other examined fungi (Supplementary Data 13).

The observed lower numbers of lipolytic enzymes in P. kudriavzevii and N. glabratus have been previously reported35, suggesting a more recent expansion of these genes in the Candida spp. Compared with Trichophyton spp. and Microsporum spp., the Candida spp. were also enriched in phospholipases (P < 0.001), enzymes associated with host cell penetration and lysis. As phospholipase expression and secretion is strain-specific36, higher copy numbers may contribute to more virulent strains. Surprisingly, we also identified phospholipases in H. werneckii, although, in a previous study, phospholipase activity was not detected in functional assays, which was suggested to contribute to the species’ non-invasive lifestyle37. The phospholipases and sphingomyelinases that we identified in H. werneckii may target plant sphingolipids, which would align with the non-invasive nature in animal tissue. However, multiple secreted OGs annotated with sphingomyelinase (SMase) activity were identified across most species, with Malassezia and Candida yeasts containing significantly higher counts than Trichophyton spp. and Microsporum spp. (P < 0.001). Although SMase-D proteins function as potent virulence factors in organisms such as bacteria and spiders38,39, their role in fungi remains unclear. To date, no studies have demonstrated extracellular SMase activity in fungal systems, despite SMase-D genes being reported in several pathogenic species40,41. In the skin-associated fungi, these SMases could be virulence factors, but they may also have endogenous roles such as membrane remodeling.

The variety and abundance of secreted enzymes are essential for the adaptation and pathogenesis of the fungi. These skin-associated fungi exhibit significantly different genetic adaptations, demonstrating how different energy assimilation strategies can sustain life within the same niche, yet result in varying levels of invasiveness and distinct clinical presentations.

Insights into infection through adhesion mechanisms

An essential factor in the pathogenicity and virulence of skin-residing fungi is their ability to adhere to host tissue and abiotic surfaces such as catheters. Adhesion proteins are also involved in flocculation, the formation of large surface-attached fungal communities42. Most known adhesins are large proteins anchored by covalent binding from a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) modification to membrane glucan, such that their extracellular N-terminal domain can partake in protein-ligand interactions with various host-cell surface substrates10. We used NetGPI v1.143 to predict which of the secreted OGs had GPI-anchor sites. In addition, we did string matching to find adhesin-related genes missing the secretion or GPI anchor site (see Methods). A total of 57 OGs containing 679 genes were predicted as having GPI-anchor sites (Supplementary Data 14), and another 50 OGs were categorized as adhesins based on their annotation. The highest numbers of potential adhesins were predicted for C. tropicalis, and the fewest were identified in Malassezia spp. (Fig. 6). One well-described adhesin family is the agglutinin-like sequence family, whose primary function is adherence to surfaces and which plays a key role in virulence in mice44. We identified agglutinin-like domains in four OGs, which contained between 2 and 15 gene copies across the samples (Fig. 6). These were enriched in Candida spp., especially C. albicans and C. tropicalis. Twelve OGs were annotated as Flocculin (FLO) with genes present in all samples, and again enriched in the Candida spp. The FLO family is diverse and involved in flocculation, biofilm formation, and the switch to filamentous growth, which aids invasion and affects pathogenicity45,46. The Hyr/Iff family is another family of adhesins expressed during hyphal formation, involved in resistance to phagocyte killing, and required for normal cell wall structure10,47. We found two Hyr1-annotated and one Iff11-annotated OG only present in the Candida spp. and N. glabratus, with most copies in C. tropicalis and C. metapsilosis, respectively. None of the Hyr1 annotated OGs are shared between these two genera, which indicates that substantial divergence and potential gene family expansion occurred after their evolutionary split. This pattern aligns with functional divergence, as Hyr1 remains important for the pathogenicity of N. glabratus despite its inability to form true hyphae48. Previous studies have shown different cell wall attachment mechanisms for some adhesins, such as those containing a C-terminal lectin-like GLEYA domain49. We annotated these domains in N. glabratus and P. kudriavzevii. The GPI-anchored OGs in the dermatophytes included a cysteine desulfurase and LysM domain-containing genes, for which copy numbers ranged from one in E. floccosum to four copies in Microsporum spp. and some Trichophyton spp. Previous reports established the enrichment of the LysM domain in dermatophytes and proposed a link between this domain and pathogenicity and virulence, mediated through host immune evasion and keratin degradation50,51. The functional mechanism involves sequestration of chitin in the fungal cell wall, thereby preventing its recognition as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern and subsequent degradation by host chitinases52.

Annotation terms among the rest of the predicted GPI-anchored adhesins included PIR, fasciclin, hydrophilin, and Ecm33, while many were unannotated. Some of these could represent previously undescribed adhesins, especially in less researched non-Saccharomycetales species. Adhesins play an important role in infection and in biofilm formation, which leads to heightened virulence by antifungal resistance and increased mortality53. While sparse knowledge is available on biofilm formation outside the Saccharomycetales, species within dermatophytes, Malassezia spp., and Trichosporon spp. have been shown to form biofilms in vitro54–57. This points to the need for further research into adhesins in a broader set of fungal species, as gaining insights into the mechanics of infection and biofilm formation can be instrumental in improving antifungal therapies and combating rising resistance.

Analysis of genes linked to pathogenicity and virulence

Beyond adhesins, the comparative genomic analysis identified diverse virulence-associated genes, some detected across all samples, like AbiEii toxin and polymorphic toxin system, and others confined to certain groups (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 15). These genes facilitate pathogenicity through invasion mechanisms, immune modulation, and the interplay with resident skin microbiota. Contrary to the expectation that increased pathogenicity would correlate with expanded and unique virulence gene repertoires, we did not observe higher numbers of virulence-associated genes in pathogenic or pathobiontic species compared with non-pathogenic outgroups (Malassezia spp. vs. R. toruloides, T. ovoides vs. R. toruloides, dermatophytes vs. C. queenslandicum, H. werneckii vs. C. queenslandicum, and Candida spp., N. glabratus, and P. kudriavzevii vs. S. cerevisiae, Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 16). Notably, R. toruloides actually carried significantly more putative virulence factors compared with Malassezia spp. (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 0.56, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.36–0.88, P = 0.008), and C. queenslandicum carried more putative virulence factors than H. werneckii (OR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.36–0.91, P = 0.013). No significant difference was observed between dermatophytes and C. queenslandicum (OR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.52–1.16, P = 0.203), between T. ovoides and R. toruloides (OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.43–1.16, P = 0.139), or between pathobiont Saccharomycetales (Candida spp., N. glabratus, and P. kudriavzevii) and S. cerevisiae (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 0.70–1.71, P = 0.831). This suggests that many virulence-associated genes identified in our study represent broadly conserved stress-response and environmental adaptation genes shared across diverse fungal lineages, rather than gene determinants uniquely expanded in pathogenic species. The absence of enrichment in more virulent or clinically invasive taxa instead points toward other potential drivers of phenotypic differences, such as differences in gene regulation. Notably, this interpretation is constrained by current annotation biases toward well-studied species, meaning lineage-specific or previously uncharacterized virulence factors or mechanisms may remain undetected.

Among the identified putative virulence-associated genes, we observed multiple OGs involved in secondary metabolite synthesis and regulation that are homologous to known toxin-associated pathways. These included putative aflatoxin producing polyketide synthases, which had more gene copies in Microsporum spp. and A. fumigatus. While aflatoxin is known to be produced by the plant-pathogenic species of Aspergillus, human-pathogen A. fumigatus does contain partial gene clusters that include a related polyketide synthase producing a metabolite with T-cell antiproliferative activity58. A homologous gene cluster to the one in A. fumigatus was found among dermatophytes, which could mean these secondary metabolites play a role in host infection by mediating immune modulation59. We also detected UstYa-like proteins (mycotoxin biosynthesis), which in other fungi catalyze the maturation of mycotoxins such as ustiloxin60, AbiEii-like toxins, known effectors in bacterial toxin–antitoxin systems61, and Tse2-like proteins related to contact-dependent inhibition toxins62. Although no functional studies have demonstrated production or secretion of these toxins in skin-pathogenic fungi, the co-occurrence of several toxin domains suggests these fungi may retain a broader, largely unexplored repertoire of bioactive secondary metabolites that could play a role in host skin infections.

Stress response mechanisms represent important virulence determinants enabling fungi to withstand host immune defenses. Our analysis identified multiple OGs associated with the stress-response, including osmotic stress-response, cell wall integrity, and thermal stress response (Fig. 6). CFEM domain-containing genes were notably abundant in Candida spp. These are well-known to be involved in pathogenesis through stress-response pathways and iron-acquisition from host heme during invasion63,64.

Finally, several potential modulators of fungal-bacterial interactions within the skin microbiome were identified. Gene families such as the polymorphic toxin system, arthropod defensins, along with the toxins discussed above, have been reported to exert antibacterial effects in other organisms61,65–67, suggesting that fungi may secrete these products to compete with co-resident bacteria. In addition, the killer toxin-resistant proteins likely mediate defense against killer toxins secreted by competing fungi68.

Collectively, this systematic characterization of virulence determinants across multiple fungal lineages establishes a molecular framework for understanding pathogenesis mechanisms, which may depend more on gene regulation than on lineage-specific gene expansion or acquisition.

Discussion

In this study, we cultured and sequenced 48 fungal strains across ten genera, focusing on clinically relevant fungi that cause prevalent SFIs and pose a significant health burden globally. To provide a broader comparative context, we included an additional 12 clinically relevant species and six outgroup species in the analyses. Through comparative genomic analyses, we tested whether taxonomic groups of skin-associated fungi differ significantly in metabolic capacity, secreted enzyme repertoires, adhesin content, and virulence-associated gene abundance. Our results support the hypothesis that skin-associated fungal genera exhibit distinct metabolic and secretory adaptations consistent with their ecological niches and tissue tropism, while overall virulence gene counts did not distinguish more virulent from less virulent species. Our comparative genetic analyses, therefore, provide insights into fungal adaptation and function, including predicting secretomes and virulence factors across diverse species.

We found high intra-genus similarity in the distribution of gene functions and notable differences between genera, including differences in predicted secreted metabolic gene products that agree with the body-site or environmental niches of the fungi. Our analyses revealed a range of adaptations to life on human skin, from host-lipid dependency in commensal Malassezia spp. to an abundance of plant-degrading enzymes in free-living H. werneckii. We also identified adhesins, which were abundant, especially in Candida spp., together with many previously undescribed adhesins that are potentially important for infection and biofilm formation in newly examined fungi. Finally, we compared predicted secreted gene products involved in virulence, which revealed group-specific immune-evasion, stress-response and competition factors. Collectively, our findings reveal genomic mechanisms underlying adaptation, pathogenicity, and virulence in skin-infecting fungi.

We report two previously undescribed strains of Trichosporon ovoides with small genomes, suggestive of non-hybrid origins. This finding is significant as hybridization has been reported in multiple human pathogenic fungi, such as species within Cryptococcus, Malassezia and Candida, and is likely to be an important evolutionary mechanism that contributes to the emergence of virulent traits69–71. Although hybridization may initially reduce fitness due to genetic incompatibility among two diverged lineages, overcoming these incompatibilities or experiencing hybrid vigor can enable the highly adaptable fungal genomes to undergo significant phenotypic shifts, driving pathogenicity and fitness. The coexistence of hybrid and non-hybrid lineages within the Trichosporon genus presents a valuable opportunity to investigate the genomic determinants of virulence through comparative approaches. By contrasting the non-hybrid strains identified in this study with known hybrid lineages, we can potentially disentangle the specific genomic features that contribute to pathogenicity.

We observed a consistent pattern of reduced genomes in the host-associated species compared to the mainly free-living species. For instance, Malassezia spp. underwent gene loss and genome compaction as it developed host dependency13. Compared with the mainly host-associated Candida spp. and dermatophytes, extensive genome compaction in Malassezia spp. could indicate a more ancient host-switch event. Host switching is a well-known process in skin-associated fungi and is believed to have given rise to the highly virulent strain T. indotineae as a predominantly anthropophilic clonal offshoot of the zoophilic T. mentagrophytes22. This emerging pathogen, which has recently been detected beyond its endemic regions72, highlights the role of host switching in the emergence of new, potentially drug-resistant, skin-infecting fungi.

Our analysis of the metabolic enzyme landscape in fungal species revealed marked differences between taxonomic groups. This might reflect adaptation after a change in available energy sources in the host, which leads to loss of genes degrading nutrients that are not present in the new niche. We discovered proportionally fewer carbohydrate-targeting enzymes and more hydrolases in Malassezia spp. than other skin-associated fungi. This pattern, in combination with the absence of a fatty acid synthase gene, is likely central to the shift towards lipid-dependent commensals34. In dermatophytes, carbohydrate-targeting enzymes are less abundant and protein-degrading enzymes are more abundant compared with closely related free-living fungi50. The metabolic adaptations in the genome are likely to be a consequence of dermatophytes becoming dependent on host proteins as a carbon source. The secretion of metabolic enzymes is also vital for the pathogenicity of the skin-associated fungi, as the breakdown of host molecules is integral to the clinical manifestations. The breakdown of lipids by Malassezia spp. on the skin surface is usually asymptomatic. However, an overly high fungal load can lead to excessive release of azelaic acid, which is believed to affect the skin’s melanocytes and induce the pigmentation changes observed in pityriasis versicolor34,73. Examination of other genomic features will bring more detailed insights into adaptation in skin-associated fungi, such as analyses of gene expression and evolutionary rates.

Upon invasion, adhesins play essential roles in the initial adherence to host cells by binding host cell surface structures and in the formation of biofilm by mediating cell-cell adhesion and aggregation74. We identified varying numbers of adhesins across skin-associated fungi, with a high proportion of undescribed proteins in all taxa except Saccharomycetales. This aligns with available research on adhesins, which has primarily focused on C. albicans and other Saccharomycetales75. Biofilm formation is clinically important, as it provides protection against the host immune system and antifungal medicine and severely complicates infections76. Besides Saccharomycetales, the ability to form biofilms has also been observed in Malassezia spp., dermatophytes and Trichosporon spp.54–57. Further investigations into the implications of biofilm formation for infection could also have consequences for the treatment of conditions like chronic onychomycosis, which are prevalent and difficult to treat77.

In addition to GPI-anchored genes, we also identified multiple adhesin annotations which were not predicted as secreted or carrying GPI-anchors. This could be an effect of the tandem repeats and genomic positions close to the telomeres, which are characteristic of adhesins78, making complete assembly of short-read data especially challenging. Alternative surface attachment mechanisms might also be employed by the fungi, such as seen in the N. glabratus Epa adhesins that lack the GPI-anchor attachment signal74. Ultimately, we present many potential adhesin genes across the skin-associated fungi, but only a few of them are well described and functionally understood. Our data will allow future assays to illuminate the function and effect on adherence of these genes, thus expanding the current understanding of adhesions and biofilm formation.

While our study provides insights into the genomics of skin-associated fungi, several limitations should be acknowledged. Short-read sequencing technology potentially results in fragmented contigs and incomplete resolution of repetitive regions and may have affected our ability to fully characterize certain genomic elements, particularly in complex regions, copy number variants, or those containing repetitive sequences. In addition, the functional annotation of many genes remains incomplete due to the limited reference databases for fungal species, especially those that are understudied. Consequently, some metabolically active or virulence-associated genes may not have been recognized or accurately classified in our comparative analyses, and fragmented gene models could have led to underestimation of gene copy numbers in specific functional categories. This limitation is particularly relevant to our hypothesis testing, as statistical comparisons of metabolic capacity, secreted enzymes, and virulence-associated gene abundance rely on existing annotations and predicted gene models. Many genes were assigned putative functions based solely on sequence homology, and a significant number remained annotated as hypothetical proteins with unknown functions. While the gap in functional characterization limits our understanding of species-specific adaptations and virulence mechanisms, our data should drive such work forward and provide specific avenues for future virulence studies.

It is also important to note that the presence of genes does not necessarily translate to protein expression or functional activity. As observed in morphological studies, many fungal species exhibit different morphologies based on external factors despite similar genomic content79. Morphogenesis, which is essential to the pathogenicity of, for example, Candida spp. and Malassezia spp.80,81, is regulated by complex gene expression networks rather than simply gene presence or absence. This disconnect between genotype and phenotype complicates our ability to draw direct conclusions about pathogenicity or morphological characteristics from genomic data alone. Despite these challenges, our study expands the genomic resources available for these understudied fungal pathogens, and our comparative analyses highlight gene families with interesting, potential functions, which represent promising candidates for future functional follow-up studies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that while skin-infecting fungi employ diverse pathogenic mechanisms, their genomic adaptations provide a window into both their evolutionary history and potential clinical vulnerabilities. Our findings support the hypothesis that distinct fungal lineages have undergone niche-specific metabolic and adhesion-related adaptations to the human skin environment, whereas differences in overall virulence gene abundance do not explain variation in pathogenicity. These results suggest that pathogenic success in superficial fungal infections reflects ecological specialization and regulatory adaptation, rather than the simple expansion of classical virulence determinant genes. By integrating comparative genomics across major taxonomic groups, we highlight how evolutionary divergence has shaped distinct strategies for nutrient acquisition, host interaction, and persistence on the skin. Our findings provide a foundation for future functional genomics and proteomics studies, which can now focus, for example, on expression pattern analysis during colonization and infection.

Methods

No ethics approval was required for this study as all research was conducted on fungal organisms and publicly available genomic data, which fall outside the scope of institutional and national ethics review requirements.

Fungal reference samples

Based on global reviews on the epidemiology of superficial fungal skin and nail infections published prior to 2018, we identified a group of skin-relevant fungal species (commensal and pathogenic, n = 20)82,83. To investigate the inter- and intraspecies diversity of the genomes, up to four different strains of each species were obtained from the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute biobank (Westerdijk, Utrecht, The Netherlands) and Statens Serum Institut (Copenhagen, Denmark). To obtain enough fungal biomass to perform whole genome sequencing (WGS), we cultured the fungi at the Department of Dermatology and Allergy, Herlev-Gentofte Hospital, Hellerup, Denmark, on appropriate media (Supplementary Data 1). All fungal samples were incubated at 28 °C for up to 21 days, depending on their growth rate, and harvested at the stationary phase. All samples were transferred to vials using an inoculation loop or curette and kept at − 80 °C until use.

Whole genome sequencing

Colony material was transferred to 15 mL tubes, including 2-3 mL lysis buffer (NucliSENS easyMag, bioMérieux Nordic, Gothenburg, Sweden) and incubated up to three days. Subsequently, all material was separated in three bead beating tubes containing 1.4 mm molecular-biology-grade zirconium beads (OPS Diagnostics, Lebanon, NJ, USA) and homogenized for 2 minutes at 30 Hz using a Vortex-Genie 2 (Scientific Industries, Inc., Bohemia, New York, USA), followed by centrifugation for 5 minutes at 16,000 × g. Lysates (up to 3 mL) were then subjected to the automated NucliSENS easyMag platform, using 100 µL silica and with an output volume of 60 µL. Double-stranded DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit meter and the dsDNA Quantification Assay (both Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, Denmark). DNA concentrations varied, and although a starting concentration of > 10 ng/µL was sought, several samples yielded lower concentrations but were successfully processed through WGS.

Libraries were constructed, and sequencing was performed using the Nextera XT Kit (Illumina, Little Chesterford, United Kingdom) and 300-cycle kits on the NextSeq 550 (Illumina) platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality control of the obtained sequencing data was conducted using bifrost (https://doi//github.com/ssi-dk/bifrost) to ensure adequate sequencing depth, species verification and identify contamination issues.

Data processing

Preprocessing and quality assessment of the Illumina sequencing data were conducted using fastp (v0.23.2, https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) with the following parameters ““--trim_front1 4 --trim_tail1 2 --trim_front2 4 --trim_tail2 2 --cut_right --cut_right_window_size 10 --length_required 80 --trim_poly_g”.

We performed de novo assembly using SPAdes v3.15.5 with the “--isolate” option84, followed by polishing with Pilon85. Assembly stats were summarized using Quast v5.2.086, and completeness was evaluated using BUSCO v5.6.118 with the closest available lineages. K-mer analysis was performed using Jellyfish v1.1.1187, Genomescope v2, and Smudgeplot88 on the sequencing reads. On a few inconsistent assemblies, we performed haplotig purging with purge_haplotig v1.1.289.

Assemblies were annotated using funannotate v1.8.190. Funannotate first masks repetitive regions of the genome with RepeatMasker91 and creates ab initio gene prediction consensus models with EVidenceModeler92 after training Augustus v3.392 using the BUSCO18 fungi_odb10 data set and GeneMark93. Exon locations were inferred from protein alignments against the SwissProt94 database with BLASTX and exonerate v2.4.095.

We performed Average Nucleotide Identity analysis using OrthoANIu17 comparing our three Trichosporon ovoides (CBS7612, CBS4098 and CBS9430) assemblies in an all-vs-all approach to other different Trichosporon species acquired from NCBI genome14: ovoides (GCA_040368805.1), coremiiforme (GCA_039545355.1), asahii (GCA_000293215.1) and inkin (GCA_040365635.1).

Inclusion of additional genomes from online databases

To expand the cohort for comparative analysis, we retrieved additional fungal genomes from NCBI (accession numbers and other information provided in Supplementary Data 1). These included more skin-associated species, such as Candida spp., Malassezia spp., and Trichophyton spp. For a broader context, we also incorporated three related non-pathogenic fungal species, understood here as species rarely seen involved in human skin infections: Rhodotorula toruloides, serving as a relative of the basidiomycota, Chrysosporium queenslandicum, serving as a relative of the dermatophytes and H. werneckii, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, serving as a relative of the pathobiont Saccharomycetales (Candida spp., P. kudriavzevii, and N. glabratus). Further three related pathogenic, non–skin-infecting fungal species were included: basidiomycete Cryptococcus neoformans, and ascomycetes: Aspergillus fumigatus, and Histoplasma capsulatum (Supplementary Data 1).

Genes were predicted for these assemblies with funannotate v1.8.1590 as explained above, with the exception of another Augustus version: v3.596.

All annotated genomes were assessed using BUSCO v5.6.116 with the fungi_odb10 database.

Functional annotation

All gene sets were functionally annotated using a combination of eggNOG-mapper23 and Interproscan24, combined using the annotate wrapper from funannotate88. Interproscan v5.70-102 was run with databases: Superfamily97 and PANTHER98, and eggNOG-mapper v2.1.13 with the databases: COG, KEGG, Pfam, GO, EC, and eggNOG orthology data23,99–102. Signalp v6.025 was used on all gene sets to predict the presence of a secretion signal peptide. On genes with predicted signal peptides, TMHMM v2.0103 was used to predict the presence of transmembrane helices and NetGPI v1.143 was used to predict the presence of glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors that anchor the protein to the cell membrane. All annotations for all genes in all genomes were combined in a single annotation table.

Orthologous gene analysis

Annotated genomes were analyzed using OrthoFinder v2.5.519, to identify shared orthologous genes and resolve the phylogeny of the species by multiple sequence alignment using MAFFT v720 and maximum likelihood tree inference using IQ-tree v3.0.121. Post-processing of results was carried out using R v4.4 and Python v3.11. Orthogroup distribution in samples given in the Hierarchical Orthogroup N0 output file was analyzed and summarized at various taxonomic levels.

Functional annotations from the contained genes were assigned to each OG. An important assumption of an orthogroup is the conservation of the function of genes; thus, the functional description for each orthogroup was given by the most common description of genes within, which were not “hypothetical protein”. For OGs with no eggNOG description assigned, any IPR, Pfam, COG or EC terms were used to obtain a functional description when possible.

OGs with at least one third of their genes predicted to be secreted by Signalp, and fewer than 70% of those Signalp-positive genes predicted as having one or more transmembrane helices by TMHMM, were considered secreted for gene copy number comparison.

Predicted secreted OGs were appointed to different categories pertaining to metabolism based on description string matching and manual curation (string matching terms given in Supplementary Data 17). OGs with virulence and adhesion-related functions were identified both by string matching with following manual curation in the full dataset (secreted and non-secreted) and from the predicted GPI-anchored adhesins from NetGPI v1.143. Gene counts for each OG in multi-species samples were summarized at the species level by taking the rounded average of copy numbers. Hybrid strains were kept separate from the non-hybrid strains of the same species. Z-scores of copy numbers were calculated and capped at −2 / + 2 to improve visualization, since the hybrid strains (mainly Hortaea werneckii) skewed the color scale undesirably.

Statistical testing

Proportions of genes categorized in KEGG pathways and EC hydrolase sub-categories (EC 3.x) were visualized and analyzed at different taxonomical levels. Differences in proportions across genera (n = 16) and families (n = 11) in each category were assessed using two-tailed Kruskal-Wallis tests. Then, for significant categories, pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) P-value correction.

The number of predicted secreted OGs per species (n = 38) were analyzed using generalized linear models (GLMs) with a quasibinomial distribution to account for overdispersion in the data. Species was included as the explanatory variable. No additional covariates were included in the model. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons among species were performed using estimated marginal means (EMMs) derived from the fitted model. P-values were corrected with BH.

Gene copy numbers across metabolic categories among genera (n = 16) were analyzed using GLMs with a negative binomial distribution. Genus was included as the explanatory variable. The total number of predicted secreted genes per genome was included as a logarithmic offset to account for variation in secretome size and was not treated as a covariate for hypothesis testing. Model significance was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests with χ2 statistics. Analyses were repeated separately for each gene group within each metabolic category. Resulting P values were adjusted using the BH correction. For significant gene-groups, pairwise comparisons were performed using EMMs, with BH correction applied to P-values.

Proportions of predicted putative adhesins (both string-matched OGs and predicted GPI-anchored OGs) per secretome were compared among genera (n = 16) using a two-sided Kruskal-Wallis test. Pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Mann-Whitney U tests) in a one-versus-all manner. P-values were adjusted using the BH correction.

Enrichment of putative virulence-associated OGs in pathobionts relative to non-pathogenic outgroups was assessed using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test on gene presence/absence data. Cumulative presences were summed across all samples within each pathobiont group and compared with the respective outgroup. The following comparisons were performed: (i) Malassezia spp. versus R. toruloides, (ii) T. ovoides versus R. toruloides, (iii) Candida spp., P. kudriavzevii, and N. glabratus versus S. cerevisiae, (iv) dermatophytes versus C. queenslandicum, and (v) H. werneckii versus C. queenslandicum.

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.1.
