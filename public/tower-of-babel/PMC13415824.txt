Recycling pharmaceutical waste (vancomycin) as sustainable corrosion inhibition for API 5 L X60 carbon steel in 1 M hydrochloric acid: a combined electrochemistry and theoretical study
Authors: Amira A E Satti, Ayman A O Younes, Mai M A H Shanab, Seham Hassan Bashir, Rabeea Mustafa Ali Daoub, Mariem M Motawea, Mohamed E Eissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepurposing expired vancomycin drug as an effective corrosion inhibitor for API 5 L X60 carbon steel (X60-CS) in 1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl). The pharmaceutical drugs that have expired are included in this category; they are a waste type that is usually burned rather than recycled. It’s interesting to note that certain drug compounds exhibit characteristics that prevent corrosion in a variety of metals and corrosive liquids. This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate vancomycin based on concentration on X60-CS in acidic environments. A variety of techniques were employed to assess the corrosion-mitigating properties of vancomycin. To assess vancomycin’s remarkable performance, we used extensive procedures, including weight loss (WL) and sophisticated electrochemical methods such as potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). By increasing vancomycin concentration, the inhibition efficiency will increase. The outcomes are remarkable: 94.2% (weight loss), 92.3% (polarization), and 95.3% (EIS) were the best inhibitory efficiencies attained at 298 K. With a good fit, vancomycin follows the Langmuir isotherm. The data obtained for activation energy increased from (50.82–80.35 kJ mol⁻¹), providing physical adsorption mechanisms in the interaction of the vancomycin inhibitor with the X60-CS surface. PDP results indicate that the corrosion current density (Icorr) decreases from 244 to 54 µA cm⁻² at inhibitor concentrations of 20 and 100 ppm, respectively. Along with weight-loss studies, surface analysis was carried out using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX), and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and vancomycin adsorption on the X60-CS surface was evidenced. The quantum chemical parameters (ELUMO, EHOMO, and E) have a strong relationship with the protection efficiency of the investigated drug. The vancomycin inhibitor conformational adsorption modification on the iron surface was also predicted using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Results gained for all techniques used are in good agreement.Keywords: Expired vancomycin, X60-CS, Corrosion inhibition, Hydrochloric acid, AdsorptionSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Carbon steel is one of the most widely used metals in offshore applications due to its favorable mechanical properties, cost-effectiveness, and ease of fabrication. Acid solutions are commonly used in various industrial processes to remove rust and scale1. Corrosion inhibitors are widely used as a corrosion control approach due to their simplicity of application, adaptability to complex geometries, and immediate protection. The presence of heteroatoms (O, N, S) and π-electron systems in these compounds can lead to adsorption onto metal surfaces, thereby forming protective films that suppress corrosion processes2.

Several effective techniques exist for controlling corrosion, with corrosion inhibitors being the most widely used method for protecting metals and alloys3. Corrosion inhibitors play a critical role in minimizing or preventing corrosion when added at low concentrations to a corrosive environment. They work by forming a protective film on the metal surface, effectively blocking direct contact between the metal and corrosive agents. These inhibitors are classified based on their origin (organic or inorganic) and their method of production (synthetic or naturally occurring)4. Organic inhibitors have been widely used to protect metals from corrosion in aggressive acidic environments, such as during acid pickling and cleaning processes5. Notably, organic compounds containing sulfur (S), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) atoms have demonstrated effectiveness as corrosion inhibitors for metals, especially active ones6–9. The efficacy of these compounds in inhibiting corrosion has been linked to their molecular structure, size, mass, the presence of heteroatoms, and their adsorptive properties10. Furthermore, under specific conditions, the electronic structure of organic inhibitors plays a crucial role in their ability to prevent corrosion. These inhibitors affect the kinetics of the electrochemical reactions involved in corrosion, thereby altering how the metal dissolves in acids. Current evidence suggests that most organic inhibitors function by adsorbing onto the metal’s surface. Numerous studies have focused on determining the adsorptive behavior of various compounds at the electrode/solution interface11,12. The effectiveness of expired Dextromethorphan HBr (EDM-HBr) as a corrosion inhibitor for carbon steel in an acidic environment. The results demonstrated its significant potential, achieving an inhibitory efficiency of 95.3% at 600 mg l–1 at 298 K 13. Further research by Li-Mao Chen et al.14 demonstrated that butylphthalide drugs effectively inhibit mild steel corrosion in 1 M HCl, achieving inhibition efficiency exceeding 94% at relatively lower dosage, with efficiency increasing with concentration. Separately, studies using various methods, including weight loss (WL), investigated butylphthalide drugs as a mild steel corrosion inhibitor in 1 M hydrochloric acid. These WL results indicated that the effectiveness of the butylphthalide drug in protecting mild steel at room temperature increased with concentration.

In a separate study15, the corrosion inhibition of carbon steel in aqueous hydrochloric acid by sulfamethoxazole drug was investigated using both experimental and theoretical methods. Tafel plot analysis indicated that sulfamethoxazole adsorption significantly reduced the corrosion current of carbon steel in this aggressive medium, demonstrating a high corrosion inhibition effectiveness of up to 95.3% at a concentration of 0.005 M and a temperature of 298 K.

Narasimha and Leena. 16 explored the Niraparib drug as a biocompatible, eco-friendly inhibitor of mild steel in HCl medium, achieving the highest inhibitory efficiency. Strong adsorption and the development of a durable protective coating on the metal surface are confirmed by the reduced Mean Square Displacement (MSD) values, indicating limited mobility of the inhibitor molecules. The anti-corrosion effect of Fluconazole on a carbon steel alloy in 1 M hydrochloric acid was confirmed17, with maximum inhibition yields of approximately 97.6% recorded at 1.6 g/L at ambient temperatures. The values of Gibbs free energy ranged from − 11.85 to − 17.75 kJ/mol. Similarly, Sumit Chauhan et al. 18 demonstrated the use of expired favipiravir and hydroxychloroquine sulfate for the protection of carbon steel against corrosion in HCl solutions. Potentiodynamic polarization tests indicated that both expired drugs are mixed-type inhibitors, achieving maximum inhibition efficiencies of 92.61% for Favipiravir and 96.55% for Hydroxychloroquine sulfate at 500 ppm and 303 K.

The adsorption and corrosion inhibition of mild steel in 0.5 M HCl solution by 5-(4-Dimethylaminobenzylidene) rhodanine (DABRh) were examined using electrochemical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) techniques. At 0.1 mM and 298 K, DABRh achieved an inhibition efficiency of 92.6% via the polarization method19.

Another study aimed to correlate the inhibition efficiency of Nifedipine on API 5 L X-52 steel in hydrochloric acid with its molecular electronic properties. Nifedipine demonstrated an 80.3% inhibition efficiency after 25 days of immersion at 500 ppm20. More recently, Anadebe et al. 21 explored the use of expired salbutamol as an anticorrosion additive for mild steel in oilfield acidizing fluid. Their research, employing weight loss, potentiodynamics, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, showed maximum inhibition efficiencies of 80%, 89%, and 84%, respectively, at a concentration of 0.4 g/L and a temperature of 323 K.

Separately, furosemide was explored as an inhibitor for carbon steel corrosion in 1.0 M HCl. Polarization methods showed it achieved an 87.6% inhibition efficiency at 300 ppm and 298 K22. The corrosion protection of carbon steel in 3.0 M phosphoric acid by expired lansoprazole and rabeprazole was investigated using Tafel polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). For lansoprazole, inhibition efficiency decreased from 92.9% to 69.3% as the temperature increased from 298 to 328 K23.

One study investigated the use of expired Ampicillin to protect carbon steel (CS) in an acid environment (H2SO4 solution)24. While a significant number of organic compounds have been investigated as corrosion inhibitors, many are unfortunately costly and pose health risks, with their toxic properties restricting their practical applications. Consequently, a crucial objective remains: to identify affordable and non-hazardous inhibitors for metal corrosion protection. In light of this, several researchers have explored the effects of non-toxic organic compounds and drugs on metal corrosion in acidic environments25–27.

API 5 L X60 carbon steel, widely used in pipeline transportation, is particularly susceptible to severe corrosion in hydrochloric acid (HCl) media, which is commonly employed for acidification, descaling, and cleaning operations. Conventional corrosion inhibitors, while effective, often rely on synthetic organic compounds that are costly, toxic, and environmentally persistent. The growing emphasis on green chemistry and circular economy principles has intensified the search for sustainable, low-cost molecular structures that feature heteroatoms (oxygen and nitrogen) and multiple double bonds, and it was also selected as a corrosion inhibitor due to its environmentally friendly properties. However, the corrosion-inhibition potential of recycled vancomycin derived from pharmaceutical waste has not been explored. Furthermore, the disposal of expired or unused vancomycin poses environmental risks, including the proliferation of antibiotic resistance and ecotoxicity. Repurposing this waste as a corrosion inhibitor offers a dual benefit: waste valorization and corrosion protection.

This study introduces, for the first time, the use of recycled pharmaceutical waste vancomycin as a sustainable corrosion inhibitor for API 5 L X60 carbon steel in 1 M HCl. In summary, this study presents a paradigm shift from synthetic chemical inhibitors to waste-derived sustainable alternatives. By demonstrating that recycled vancomycin, otherwise destined for environmental contamination, can serve as an effective, high-performance corrosion inhibitor, we not only address a critical industrial need but also establish a new circular economic pathway for pharmaceutical waste. The key advancements include: high inhibition efficiency, recycled vancomycin achieves up to 94.2% inhibition efficiency at 100 ppm in 1 M HCl, mixed-type inhibition (the inhibitor shifts Ecorr by < 85 mV), and waste-to-value paradigm (establishes a proof-of-concept for recycling antibiotic waste as high-performance corrosion inhibitor, addressing both environmental pollution and industrial corrosion challenges).To comprehensively assess its capabilities, the research incorporated a range of theoretical calculations, including Density Functional Theory (DFT) and Monte Carlo (MC) simulations.

Materials and methods

Materials

API 5 L X60 carbon steel specimens, measuring 2 cm x 2 cm x 0.2 cm, were prepared for weight loss experiments and surface analysis. The chemical composition was determined by optical emission spectrometry (OES) using a vacuum spectrometer according to ASTM E41528. The analysis revealed the following composition (wt%): C 0.14, Mn 1.04, Si 0.25, Cr 0.07, Mo 0.08, V 0.03, Cu 0.03, Ni 0.05, Al 0.026, P 0.014, S 0.011, Ti 0.015, Nb 0.001, and Fe balance. The material conforms to API 5 L X60 grade specifications29,30. Prior to each measurement, the steel samples were prepared as follows: first, the surfaces were abraded with SiC abrasive paper of grit sizes 300, 600, 800, 1000, and 1200; next, the samples were rinsed with acetone and isopropanol; and lastly, they were dried with dry air. It is important to note that the metal surface was polished with 1-µm alumina until a mirror finish was achieved, exclusively for the surface analysis tests, following the abrading procedure. The corrosive media is 1 M (37%) HCl, which was created by dilution with bidistilled water.

A stock solution of vancomycin (100 mL) was initially prepared by weighing one gram of the drug and dissolving it in one liter of doubly distilled water. The final concentrations used in the experiment—specifically 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ppm—were subsequently prepared via dilution from this stock solution. Methods

Weights loss (WL)

To ensure a consistently smooth X60-CS surface, the samples were meticulously abraded with every sheet of varying grit sizes, from 300 to 1200. Following abrasion, then air-dried. The prepared X60-CS coupons were subsequently stored in a vacuum desiccator to preserve their state for upcoming corrosion measurements31. The coupons were then suspended in 100 mL beakers using glass hooks. Corrosive solutions with and without varying concentrations of the drug were introduced into the beakers. After a specified duration, the coupons were removed, washed with distilled water and acetone, dried, and weighed. Weight loss (ΔW) was calculated as the difference between the initial and final weights. The duration of immersion was measured at different temperatures. The duration of this experiment is merely three hours. WL tests were performed in triplicate (three times) under identical conditions. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the result data, the average WL value from these three trials was measured and utilized in subsequent calculations. All experiments were achieved under aerated and static conditions. Notably, the standard deviation across the three parallel experiments remained consistently below 5%, indicating highly reproducible measurements. Electrochemical studies

Electrochemical measurements were carried out in a 100 mL cell fitted with the usual three-electrode setup. A saturated calomel electrode was used as the reference electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and the working electrode was composed of X60-CS. All measurements were recorded using a Gamry Reference 3000 Potentiostat/ Galvanostat/ZRA. To assemble the employed electrode, a small X60-CS piece (1 cm × 1 cm × 0.2 cm) was fabricated by welding to a copper rod and insulating it with a glass tube, leaving only one surface exposed to the solution. Experiments were carried out at room temperature (about 298 K) in 1 M HCl, both with and without vancomycin at several concentrations33. Before any electrochemical run, the electrode was left in the solution for 30 min to allow the open-circuit potential to stabilize.

Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP)

The corrosion response was examined using potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). For the PDP curves, the potential was swept from − 250 mV vs. SCE to + 250 mV relative to OCP at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s⁻¹. These scans were used to obtain the corrosion potential (Ecorr) and current density (icorr) under steady-state conditions, and the data were analyzed using Tafel extrapolation. This non-destructive method confirms the inhibitor’s adsorption on the metal surface. Theoretical calculations

A quantum-chemical simulation elucidated the adsorption mechanism. The calculated quantum chemical features were correlated with the observed inhibitory effectiveness of the corrosion inhibitor under investigation. The inhibition efficiency (IE) of vancomycin was determined by calculating various electronic and thermodynamic parameters, including adsorption energy, the energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (EHOMO), the energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO), and the energy gap (ΔE). Additionally, a Monte Carlo simulation was employed to investigate the preferred adsorption configurations of vancomycin molecules on the adsorption sites of the periodic X60-CS structure. Possible adsorption sites were pinpointed through MC searches, which employed a simulated annealing algorithm to explore various substrate adsorbate configurations. The MC simulations were conducted within a periodic slab model box composed of super-unit cells, with a 20 Å vacuum layer to prevent interactions between the periodic units. Each supercell of the Fe(110) surface was covered with a single inhibitor molecule. The compass force field was used to model the behavior of the vancomycin molecule on the X60-CS surface during adsorption, including the interaction mechanisms between the molecule and the metal surface. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) technique

Using an “Alpha II Bruker ATR spectrophotometer, the FTIR spectra of the pure vancomycin and X60-CS specimens dipped for a day in 1 M HCl with the maximum focus were recorded. For this analysis, the highest concentration of vancomycin in 1 M HCl, with X60-CS added for 3 hours, was used.

Atomic force microscope (AFM) technique

The technique you’re describing is AFM, specifically using the Pico SPM2100 AFM device. AFM is a powerful tool for studying surfaces at an atomic level. It is used to identify the morphological characteristics of the outer surface of X60-CS, which become rough due to corrosion or inhibitor adsorption, causing the material to depart from its optimum shape. This method involves immersing X60-CS metal in 1 M HCl for 3 h, both with and without expired medication. After treatment, the samples were removed, quickly rinsed with distilled water, and dried.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analyses

The surface analysis and elemental composition of both corroded X60-CS samples in the absence and presence of inhibitor were analyzed to get a clearer idea of how the steel surface changed after exposure to the acid solution, without and with the presence of the inhibitor. The microscope used (JEOL JSM-5500, Japan) was equipped with Energy-Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), allowing both surface features and elemental composition to be examined simultaneously. A working distance of 10 mm and an accelerating voltage of 20 kV were used for the analysis. EDX measurements are conducted by selecting specific, localized regions of a sample to determine its elemental composition. These measurements can be performed at individual spots, along a line, or across a 2D area. The spatial resolution of these measurements depends on the interaction volume of the electron beam with the sample, which is typically a few microns in scanning electron microscopy (SEM). This analysis revealed the elemental composition and surface distribution of the X60CS samples. The observed reduction in corrosion-related elements in the presence of vancomycin highlights its effectiveness as a corrosion inhibitor. The steel specimens were kept in 1 M HCl for three hours under the same conditions used in the WL measurements. After eliminating them from the solution, the samples were soaked quickly, left to dry in air, and examined without any additional treatment.

The inhibitor functions by blocking active sites on the steel surface. Figure 7 shows that the inhibitor affects both the anodic and cathodic polarization curves, indicating mixed-type inhibition. EIS measurements

To study the corrosion activity at the interface, we utilized electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), a well-established non-destructive method. Prior to recording the EIS spectra, the X60-CS electrode was allowed to stabilize in the solution until a steady-state condition was reached. Stabilization of the system was verified by tracking the open-circuit potential (EOCP) of X60-CS as a function of immersion time, both in the presence and absence of the inhibitor. An examination of the Nyquist plots (Fig. 8a) shows that each spectrum consists of a single semicircle, and this characteristic shape was preserved regardless of whether inhibitors were present in the solution.

The Nyquist (a) and Bode (b) plots indicate the dissolving behavior of X60-CS in 1 M HCl solution, both in the absence and in the presence of different concentrations of the vancomycin inhibitor at 298 K.

The presence of a semicircular arc in the Nyquist plots indicates that the corrosion of X60-CS is under charge-transfer control. Crucially, the introduction of an inhibitor alters the kinetics of this process without modifying the underlying mechanism52. This is further supported by the observation that inhibitor-treated samples yielded semicircles with larger diameters than untreated samples, indicative of enhanced corrosion resistance. This increase in impedance correlates with the formation of a protective layer on the steel surface, a finding consistent with the polarization measurements.

To provide a more comprehensive analysis, bode plots were constructed to examine the influence of varying vancomycin concentrations on the electrode’s impedance behavior (Fig. 8b). These plots illustrate the relationship between the impedance modulus (Zmod), the phase angle, and the applied frequency. Finally, the impedance response of the X60-CS electrode was quantitatively interpreted by fitting the experimental data to the equivalent electrical circuit model depicted in Fig. 9. The Nyquist plots (Fig. 8a) unequivocally display a single capacitive loop for the X60-CS electrode across all conditions. Notably, the diameter of this capacitive arc is substantially larger in the presence of vancomycin compared to the uninhibited 1 M HCl solution. In practice, the CPE exponent “n” can vary between 0 and 1, influenced by factors such as electrode surface roughness, heterogeneity, and the local dielectric constant. In the present study, the “n” values recorded for the uninhibited 1 M HCl solution were notably lower than those observed for the inhibited systems. Furthermore, “n” exhibited a progressive increase with rising inhibitor concentration. This trend suggests that inhibitor molecules adsorb uniformly onto the X60-CS surface, leading to a more homogeneous surface. Conversely, the lower “n” value for the blank (Table 5) indicates surface irregularities resulting from corrosion-induced roughening of the X60-CS electrode. Theoretical calculations

Quantum chemical calculations

Vancomycin was evaluated as a potential corrosion inhibitor for X60 carbon steel (X60-CS) in 1 M HCl solution using quantum-chemical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT). The adsorption process can occur via two mechanisms: the inhibitor molecule can either donate electrons to the metal’s unoccupied d-orbitals or accept electrons from the metal. The energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (EHOMO) represents the molecule’s ability to donate electrons, while the energy of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO) reflects its capacity to accept electrons63 as shown in Fig. 14. The energy gap (ΔE) is defined as the difference between ELUMO and EHOMO. Various electronic properties, including EHOMO, ELUMO, ΔE, dipole moment (µ in Debye), ionization potential, electron affinity, chemical potential, electronegativity, chemical hardness and softness, as well as electrophilicity and nucleophilicity, were calculated. Mechanism of corrosion inhibition

Table 2 demonstrates that the computed E*a values, which rise from 50.82 to 80.35 kJ mol-1 in the absence of vancomycin compared to its presence, suggest that physisorption is the main method by which the antibiotic binds to X60-CS in a 1 M HCl solution. This conclusion is reinforced by data showing that higher temperatures are associated with lower inhibition efficiency. Chemical adsorption is achieved through the formation of coordination complexes, wherein these groups donate lone pairs of electrons to the vacant orbitals of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions72. The X60-CS surface tends to become positively charged due to the excess of hydrogen ions in 1 M aqueous HCl. Chloride ions from the HCl solution are attracted to and adsorbed onto the positively charged X60-CS monolayer via electrostatic interactions, forming a first protective layer via physisorption (Fig. 16). vancomycin molecules become protonated due to the high H+ ion concentration in the acidic solution. The protonated vancomycin molecules interact with the pre-adsorbed chloride ions on the steel surface. In this primarily electrostatic interaction, the negatively charged chloride ions attract the positively charged vancomycin molecules, forming an electrostatic adsorption layer. Furthermore, the inhibitor’s substantial size and molecular weight enhance corrosion mitigation by enabling it to cover a large portion of the metallic surface73,74.
