Repurposing therapies in junctional epidermolysis bullosa: Oral ivermectin therapy associated with improved wound healing and reduced pruritus
Authors: Brandon Tan, Alena Yang, Dédée F Murrell
Published: 2026

Our understanding of epidermolysis bullosa (EB) has significantly evolved. Splicing mutations, often associated with intermediate disease severity, occur when nucleotide changes at exon-intron borders disrupt normal splicing, leading to exon skipping and truncated but partially functional proteins being produced.1 Over 21 genes with over 1000 different mutations have been implicated in EB pathophysiology, most encoding structural proteins critical for cell-to-cell adhesion and skin integrity. Genes widely expressed beyond the skin and mucous membranes, when mutated, may lead to extracutaneous tissue and organ involvement with significant morbidity. Phenotypic variability exists in both autosomal dominant and recessive EB, where differential splicing is considered a contributory factor.2

Since the 1980s, ivermectin has been established as a highly effective antiparasitic for various conditions including generalized crusted scabies,3 even in EB patients,4,5 where standard topical treatments may be ineffective or inappropriate. Recent studies have supported an additional immunomodulatory action of ivermectin that suppresses inflammatory processes in humans. Inhibition of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of the activated B (NF-κB) pathway leads to reduced liposaccharide-induced production of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6), integrin- β6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha. Ivermectin also inhibits signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, which usually upregulates proinflammatory markers in macrophages.3 In rosacea, a chronic progressive inflammatory dermatosis, IL-17 appears central to its pathogenesis by causing production of IL-1 and tumor necrosis factor alpha via the NF-κB pathway,6 alongside increased cutaneous Demodex mites. This supports a dual role of ivermectin—both as an effective immunomodulator and antiparasitic—in reducing skin inflammation and providing symptomatic relief in rosacea.3 Interestingly, preclinical studies have demonstrated modulation of multiple molecular targets—including cancer-related epigenetic deregulators (SIN3A/3B), RNA helicase, chloride channel receptors, purinergic receptors, crucial cell signaling cascades, and even stem cell pathways—with ivermectin use, prompting interest in its repurposing as antitumor therapy.7

We present a 31-year-old man of Afghan heritage, born to consanguineous parents, with intermediate generalized junctional EB. Genetic studies revealed in-frame deletion between exon 9 and intron 9 of LAMC2 (encoding gamma subunit of laminin 332) and homozygous COL17A1 mutations at exon 52, confirming reduced expression of 2 functionally related components of the hemidesmosome-anchoring filament complex.8 Oral ivermectin was commenced in February 2021 (200 mcg/kg twice weekly) for maggot infestation of chronic foot wounds. Within 3 months, no maggots were observed and reduction in pruritus and chronic wound odor associated with colonizing Gram-negative organisms were noted. Remarkably, with continued therapy, an 8% reduction in damage score on the Epidermolysis Bullosa Disease Activity and Scarring Index1—the most comprehensive and reliable EB-specific scoring system—was observed within 4 months, with a further 51% reduction by 1 year (Fig 1). This improvement sustained for 20 months, correlating clinically with improved wound re-epithelialization (Fig 2) and globally reduced pruritus. Additionally, a transient 21% to 42% reduction in Epidermolysis Bullosa Disease Activity and Scarring Index activity scores (active bullae and/or erosions) was observed in the first year of treatment (Fig 1).

Trend of Epidermolysis Bullosa Disease Activity and Scarring Index (EBDASI) damage (dashed line) and activity (solid line) scores performed every 3-6 months from August 2020 to December 2023 (maximum damage score = 230, maximum activity score = 276).

Serial clinical images of the patient’s chronic leg wounds demonstrating improved wound re-epithelialization from time of commencement of oral ivermectin (February 2021, left), at 4 months of therapy (June 2021, middle), and after 1 year of therapy (April 2022, right).

In this era of developing gene-targeted EB therapies, ivermectin may be repurposed for therapeutic benefits, predominantly in reducing pruritus through antiparasitic and anti-inflammatory actions. Although the evidence appears preliminary, we postulate that ivermectin may also influence spliceosome activity—analogous to antisense oligonucleotide-mediated exon skipping in novel gene therapies9—promoting increased full-length expression of key proteins such as laminins A3/B3/C2 and collagen VII, thereby reducing skin fragility in EB patients. In addition to the abovementioned anti-inflammatory effects of ivermectin, 1 study showed that ivermectin inhibits kallikrein 5 (KLK5) gene expression in the epidermis, which reduces inflammation in rosacea by preventing excessive production of cathelicidin antimicrobial peptides such as LL-37, previously demonstrated in skin of patients with rosacea.10 As the KLK5 protein regulates normal skin cell turnover by breaking down key structural proteins (corneodesmosomal components such as desmoglein 1), we postulate that blocking the KLK5 gene may have an additional downstream effect of reduced epidermal and mucosal shedding. Overall, our anecdotal case supports the well-documented anti-inflammatory actions of ivermectin in EB, and highlights the need for further studies evaluating ivermectin as a potential modifier of RNA splicing.
