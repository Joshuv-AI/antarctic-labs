Uncovering COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and intent in 15 developing countries: a multi-level, clustered analysis of six months of repeated, cross-sectional data
Authors: Cristina A. Smith, Daniel Handysides, Alan Cupino, Anna Nelson, W. Lawrence Beeson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 vaccination hesitancy is antithetical to preventing communicable disease. The study’s aim was to determine social ecological factors associated with vaccination acceptance / intent in select developing countries.MethodsTo examine vaccine acceptance / intent in fifteen developing countries, data was sourced from the Global COVID-19 Trends and Impacts Survey and analyzed with a multi-level, clustered, cross-sectional design. Weighted Generalized Additive Modeling (GAM) with 1,000 stratified cluster bootstraps mitigated intra-cluster correlation and temporal dependencies.ResultsStatistically significant linear variables included those age 55 + (OR = 1.204, 95% CI [1.095, 1.324], p < 0.001) and identification as female (OR = 1.538, 95% CI [1.448, 1.630], p < 0.001), which were responsible for increased odds of vaccine acceptance / intent. Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects (EDF = 2.50, p < 0.001) demonstrated a nonlinear decline in vaccine acceptance / intent. Time (Month) (EDF = 1.94, p < 0.001) was associated with a concave parabolic trend. Cultural Principal Components (PCs) 1–3, respectively, (EDF = 3.97, p < 0.001). EDF = 4.87, p < 0.001), and (EDF = 4.95, p < 0.001) demonstrated oscillating patterns of vaccine acceptance / intent depending upon cultural score direction and magnitude. All PCs demonstrated lower levels of vaccination acceptance / intent with no definitive cultural leaning. There was, however, a push-pull effect among all three dimensions, demonstrating PCs either drove or suppressed vaccination acceptance / intent.ConclusionsVaccine decision-making complexities are associated with cultural and individual influences. Such findings could inform future vaccine campaign strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13739-z.

Background

One consequence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic was the newly-created vaccine became a source of fear for some [1]. Such a reaction exemplifies vaccine hesitancy, which can be directed at specific vaccines – a reality manifested during the COVID-19 pandemic [2, 3]. While wealthy countries began to benefit from a vaccine in December 2020, developing countries awaited vaccine supplies, on average, 90 days longer [4]; however, when vaccines did arrive, hesitant attitudes towards them also affected their use [5, 6]. In fact, Nikoloski and colleagues reported low vaccination rates in low-income countries even while adequate supplies existed, which was not an uncommon pattern in low-income countries worldwide [7–9]. This occurred in an environment in which a program called COVAX attempted to create more access in low-income countries; though, it did not always adequately or evenly distribute the vaccines to LMICs [10, 11]. Some examined reasons why vaccine hesitancy existed. In fact, two studies retrospectively classified findings at the social ecological model (SEM) levels, demonstrating various individual, group, and societal influences could affect COVID-19 vaccine decision-making [12, 13]. The following section introduces the COVID-19 pandemic circumstances; the social ecological model; a relevant cultural model; and some of those individual, group, and societal-level findings.

Introduction

COVID-19 was a highly communicable disease that quickly reached pandemic proportions with many variants of differing communicability [14]; while vaccines provided hope for lessening the spread of disease, different versions of the vaccine had varying degrees of efficacy [15]. Two theoretical models can explain some of the circumstances around vaccine hesitancy. First, the social ecological model by Urie Bronfenbrenner explains human behavior through individual and societal interactions. While the model is imperfect, as it has been criticized for definitional fluidity between levels [16], the model levels were definitionally revised by Krug et al. and used in recent vaccine hesitancy research by Chait et al. [17, 18]. Thus, for definitional clarity of levels, we synthesized and applied Krug et al. and Chait and colleagues’ understandings of each level. This helped us to differentiate between the nuances of the second (relational) and third (community) levels. Specifically, they indicate that the relational level was meant to represent people with whom one has regular contact, while the community level can be representative of more distal relationships. The highest level—the societal level—can be represented by culture [17, 18].

A second model— Geert Hofstede’s six dimensions of culture—was included to quantify the concept of culture at the societal level of the social ecological model. This specific cultural model was used because others have implicated Hofstede dimensions as explanatory of COVID-19 cases, deaths, and vaccination rates [19–23]. By using this model, our research findings can add to the already existent vaccine literature. The Hofstede model includes the following six dimensions that describe cultural traits on a continuum: Power Distance, Individualism vs. Collectivism, Uncertainty Avoidance, Long-Term Orientation, Indulgence vs. Restraint, and Motivation towards Achievement and Success (previously Masculinity vs. Femininity) [24, 25]. These dimensions are operationally defined in Table 1 of the “Methods” section.

Existent literature has identified multiple factors in connection with COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in low-and-middle income countries (LMICs), representing various levels of the social ecological model. At the individual level, vaccine side effects have been implicated in reducing COVID-19 vaccination [12, 13], and individual demographics like age have been often associated with vaccination intention or rejection, with some studies reporting older age groups as being more hesitant, while other studies demonstrated the opposite effect [7, 21]. COVID-19 vaccination may also have been viewed as favorable in countries with high population density, as high population density was correlated with COVID-19 deaths [21]. Band wagoning behaviors of vaccine acceptance were also reported among friends and family [12, 13]. Tangentially, Lewis and Duch found personal perceptions in the form of perceived susceptibility were correlated with vaccination decision-making [24]. In fact, according to Lun and colleagues, one of the “community” factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination was the level of viral rampancy in the community [13]. However, preventive behaviors were also found to be relaxed when around individuals with whom one was well-acquainted compared to those with whom one was not familiar [26]. Finally, societal-level findings have demonstrated that each of Hofstede’s six dimensions of culture is associated with COVID-19 spread, including cases and deaths [26]. While many studies implicated the Hofstede cultural dimension of Individualism as among the most important associations with increased COVID-19 vaccination or pandemic impact, not all did. In fact, some demonstrated the involvement of other Hofstede dimensions besides Individualism [19–22].

This study was prompted in response to pleas for more studies using the SEM and the current lack of health research in low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) [27–31]. Studies have rarely looked at simultaneous analyses of cultural and behavioral variables using the social ecological model as a guide to study effects on vaccine hesitancy in multiple countries. Many studies may avoid the use of the social ecological model in research because of its complexity and difficulty in defining levels. While one study examined COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in terms of a version of the social ecological model in Indonesia, variable terms did not include culture as a societal-level determinant, and study results were exclusive to that country [18]. This study attempts to build on that study of Chait et al. and fill the void in social ecological model vaccine hesitancy research in four major ways. First, we target our study not at vaccine hesitancy but understanding population-level vaccine acceptance or willingness. Second, we examine trends using a Generalized Additive Model to quantitatively analyze secondary, pooled, repeated cross-sectional data from the Global COVID-19 Trends and Impacts Survey [32]. Third, we utilize a version of the social ecological model as an a priori theoretical model to examine individual and societal level effects on vaccine acceptance or willingness in fifteen low-income countries. Fourth, we utilize Hofstede cultural variables to understand the overarching cultural effects on vaccination acceptance or willingness. Our aim was to ascertain which SEM variables contributed most to increased and reduced vaccine acceptance / intent in developing countries during the pandemic. The following research questions are the focus of this study:

The importance of answering these questions lies in the concept of designing successful vaccine campaigns and educational strategies in the event of future pandemics. In other words, questions like, Should the vaccine campaign include group settings? Should the campaign focus on messaging that is culturally relevant? need to be explored. By answering some of these questions, vaccine campaigns and education strategies could be more productive in some LMICs.

Methods

Data

Secondary data were collected from multiple sources, including datasets stratified by age and gender from the Global COVID-19 Trends and Impacts Survey (CTIS) v1.0 Contingency Tables; these tables were aggregated by country and month [32–34]. With the assistance of Facebook / Meta, the University of Maryland and Carnegie Mellon University recruited international participants via the platform who were 18 or older and who concurred with informed consent procedures [35]. The survey was comprised of repeated, cross-sectional surveys, gathered into public-access, aggregate, monthly data [36]. This prompted the Loma Linda University Institutional Review Board (IRB # 5240449) to classify this study as non-human subjects research. The survey allowed participants one submission per survey round, but within one to five months those individuals could be re-surveyed [37, 38].

Variables

Each variable was chosen based on theoretical constructs previously discussed. Variables included Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects, Percent Time Spent Outside of Household (those spending at least 1 day), Percent Community COVID, and Hofstede dimensions of culture. The dependent variable, Percent Vaccine Acceptance / Intent is specifically defined as the weighted percentage of individuals responding that they have had a COVID-19 vaccine, had an appointment to receive one, or would be willing to vaccinate if given the chance. All percentage variables were gleaned from the global CTIS [34]. All data from the CTIS was a percentage of the population responding in the affirmative to the relevant survey question, weighted for nonresponse, the likelihood of survey respondents being re-sampled, country population sizes, and represented the pooling of daily or weekly samples; weekly weights were added in November 2021, which was our second month of study [35]. The survey versions included 11, 12, and 13 [38], which had no changes for these variables during the period of study [38, 39].

Each variable is connected to a level of the theoretical model, which is represented below in Fig. 1. Because theoretical levels can be conflated, it is important to point out that the variable for Percent Community COVID and Percent Spent Time Outside of the Household could each belong to the community level. However, it was decided to use Percent Time Spent Outside of the Household to represent the interpersonal level, as the Global CTIS survey specifically included requests for information regarding if the time were spent at workplaces or schools in the last 24 h. Thus, according to theory, spending time with peers is an example of a more regular relationship than simply knowing someone in the community with symptoms of COVID-19 [17].

Hofstede’s six dimensions of culture variables were extracted from the Culture Factor Group’s Country Comparison Tool. To ensure the cultural scores’ historical accuracy, the Wayback Machine from the Internet Archive https://web.archive.org/web/changes/https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison [40] was checked to definitively verify data did not change during the months and years of study, which was a methodology suggested by The Culture Factor Group [16]. While in many instances historical data on the Wayback Machine may be unreliable, the Hofstede dimensions were stable over many years, so scores were not likely to have changed. All variables are operationally defined in Table 1, while Table 2 provides detailed data harmonization techniques.

Country inclusion criteria

Countries were assessed for several inclusion criteria before being utilized in the final analyses. First, each country chosen had to have information for all six Hofstede’s dimensions available. A second inclusion criteria was that each country had to meet the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Official Development Assistance (ODA) funding criteria because these lists parse out which countries belong to the categories of low- and middle-income [41, 42]. We chose to include countries defined as both low-and-high middle income because even though high middle-income is distinguished from the low-middle income category, they both belong to the same broad middle-income category. Thus, their categorization in the broader realm of “middle-income” is suggestive of the potential lack of resources and financial capital to support mass-scale vaccination efforts that could be more plausibly supported in strictly high-income counterparts. Countries also had to have weighted data available from the Global COVID-19 Trends and Impacts Survey (CTIS) [34] with at least 500 people in the total sample size [35].

While the Global COVID-19 Trends and Impacts Survey (CTIS) was conducted between April 23, 2020 to June 25, 2022 by the University of Maryland and Carnegie Mellon University [33, 37], October 1, 2021 through March 31, 2022 were chosen as the months of focus for this study. October 1, 2021 was chosen as the start date of the study to ensure each country had already achieved at least one initial COVAX shipment (a program meant for developing countries to receive COVID-19 vaccines), which was checked against the GAVI website of vaccine rollouts [43] or searched via Internet using the following Boolean phrase: “who [descriptor] region twitter + first COVAX shipment in [country name].” This period of study was also chosen because it was when all variables of interest had available data, and these months represented dates prior to the study’s phasing out of data weighting [33]. Fig. 2 summarizes the decision-making process for country inclusion. Supplement 1 summarizes the sample sizes of the countries included in the analyses.

Data harmonization

Data preparation

For this multi-level, clustered, cross-sectional design, all data were prepared and analyzed using R, version 4.4.0 using the mcgv package for the GAM. Gemini (Gemini 3 Flash) and a GAM statistical manual provided guidance for handling the bounded, nonlinear data present in this dataset. Gemini (Gemini 3 Flash) assisted with R-code generation, which was manually verified by C.S. All R code is available at Supplement 2. Demographic variables were one-hot dummy-coded, using 18–24 and Male as reference groups for age and gender, respectively. Data was re-categorized for age and gender using R based on the one-hot point coding system to ensure all countries had data represented in each age group. All missing data were coded as 999, and 999 was set as NA in R. Final age groups were Age 25 to 34 (young adult), 35 to 54 (middle-aged), and 55+ (older adult) with the reference as age 18–24. Please see the data harmonization table, Table 2, to determine which age groups were combined. This combining methodology allowed us to keep age and gender as granular as possible to accurately represent each category, and it also helped to prevent convergence errors during the bootstrapping iterations. Unanswered (NA) questions were categorized as the refused groups, but because the age refused category and the combined gender refused/ other category had so few respondents overall that it caused bootstraps to not converge, the final GAM model was based on data that filtered out these respondents. Figure 3 below presents the final sample size and its derivation. Months were re-categorized to consecutive numbers (i.e.,1, 2, 3…) so that R could more easily determine the months were consecutive. There was also a dummy code added to demonstrate values for October 2021 and after October 2021 because October 2021 was prior to the implementation of combined daily and weekly weighting in the data. This extra dummy variable assisted with determining if changes in weighting methodologies possibly affected estimations.

Regarding data availability, most data were intact, but data for the variable Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects had 38.9% missingness. All other variables besides Percent Spent Time Outside of the Household (7.12% missingness) had percentage data missingness < 1%. In an attempt to reduce selection bias or power loss, missing data were addressed via Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), using Predictive Mean Matching (m = 5). Model diagnostics and structural checks were sourced from a base model composed of unspecified splines except for time, which should not exceed five knots (only 6 months of data); no transformation of the dependent variable; and data filtered for excluded age and gender groups. All final estimates were reported from 1,000 stratified cluster-robust bootstrapped iterations pooled from the five imputed datasets, taking 200 successful convergences from each of the five imputed datasets.

Preliminary testing of assumptions

Prior to analysis, we checked the Pearson’s correlation for multicollinearity between variables across all five imputed datasets. There was clear collinearity between some of the Hofstede dimensions and the male and female group. Please see Supplement 3 for the Pearson’s correlation chart. Linearity for all non-dummy coded terms was tested on one of the imputed datasets, and a Loess plot was used to demonstrate the non-linearity of each continuous term, including month. Please refer to Supplement 4, for the Loess plots. Because data was bounded by percentages, this was the likely culprit for the extreme nonlinearity found in the data. This, however, was not the only obstacle present; to explore the diagnostics further, we used our base GAM model (described previously) to assess general attributes representing violations of assumptions for the GAM. The Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) charts from the base GAM demonstrated several spikes in time lags; thus, autocorrelation was identified. In addition, heteroskedasticity was identified along with ceiling effects in the scatterplot of residuals for the base model; heteroskedasticity was definitively diagnosed with the Breusch Pagan test, which had a p-value < 0.001. Some iterative diagnostic checks were conducted throughout the analyses. For example, concurvity was tested for spline specification of the smooth function to avoid over- or under-fitting the model. High concurvity was found, particularly between the Hofstede dimensions and the country random effect and the Principal Component Analyses (PCAs) for culture and time (month) during trial models. Table 3 summarizes testing of the raw data and the iterative process of testing concurvity, k-indices, residuals, and autocorrelation. Supplement 3 and Appendix A provide evidence of heteroskedasticity, nonlinearity, autocorrelation, and general diagnostics. For linear terms, diagnostics confirmed high VIF for several terms.

Remedies for violated assumptions / statistical analyses

Violated assumptions were addressed during the analyses. Each strategy employed was designed as a statistical buffer to mitigate issues specific to the data previously mentioned. First, a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) accounted for the repeated, cross-sectional nature of the data. To mitigate the issue of collinearity between the Hofstede dimensions, we decided to use Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and to address the issues of collinearity between males and females, we set males as the reference group to eliminate its effects from the actual analyses. Ceiling effects were also addressed with the Smithson-Verkulien squeeze prior to precision weighting because it keeps values bounded between 0 and 100 (e.g., percentages) from gathering at exactly 0 and 100. Standard errors were then transformed to the link (logit) scale using the Delta Method to ensure that uncertainty was appropriately represented for the non-linear variables. Although the original data had multi-stage weighting of responses, we used a precision- weighted (inverse-weighted) GAM with Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation to ensure countries with more respondents were recognized as having higher precision. All GAM default settings were kept (no double penalty or customized gamma settings were instituted) other than the penalization provided by REML because these penalties and customizable settings led to non-convergence from singularity issues in bootstraps. To account for within-country effects, a country random effect was trialed, but there was persistent high concurvity between country and cultural variables. Thus, to model these country-level differences, we employed stratified cluster bootstrapping (by country) pooled across five imputed datasets to account for not only within-country correlations but also heteroskedasticity, ceiling effects, and the overall nested data structure. In fact, we performed a pooled stratified cluster robust bootstrap (re-sampling at the country level) until 200 successful convergences were achieved for each of the five imputed datasets using the “while” loop; when successfully reaching 1000 bootstraps total, the pooled distribution of final estimates were created. Such a strategy helped protect the small sample size from unstable estimations across all iterations and helped with autocorrelation. K dimensions were also trialed, but because the model continually broke with high k dimensions, we kept the dimensions as low as possible (less complexity) in order to keep just enough flexibility in the k dimension to capture the nonlinearity. Main model results

Residual diagnostics showed a highly stable distribution across all five imputed datasets. The pooled deviance residuals exhibited a mean kurtosis of 3.69 (SD < 0.001), indicating that the model’s error distribution was approximately normal with slight leptokurtosis. These are all consistent with appropriate Gaussian GAM assumptions. Kurtosis across all five datasets for the model registered at approximately 3.69 within 0.02 standard deviations. The total concurvity was at 0.972, which was improved from most models at 0.98 -1.0. While concurvity remained at 1.0 for the culture PCs, this was expected, as culture essentially was acting as a proxy for country information; this should not have led to improper estimates, however, because standard errors were low based on confidence intervals, and all coefficients, EDFs, and p-values were consistent even across stratified cluster bootstraps equal to 1000. This suggests appropriate model fit.

Statistically significant results (linear terms)

Results from the final GAM indicated those age 55 + (OR = 1.204, 95% CI [1.095, 1.324], p < 0.001) and those who identified as females (OR = 1.538, 95% CI [1.448, 1.630], p < 0.001), were statistically significant linear terms. Both demographics demonstrated increased odds of vaccination acceptance or intent relative to their respective reference groups of 18-24-year-olds and males. Specifically, those who were age 55 + had a 20% increase in odds of vaccination acceptance / intent, while being female rather than male was associated with 54% increased odds of vaccine acceptance or intent in this particular sample size.

Statistically non-significant results (linear terms)

While several demographics accounted for directional trends, these were not statistically significant. Specifically, identifying with the age group of 25-to-34 compared to 18–24 (OR = 0.929), 95% CI [0.852, 1.017], p = 0.11) was associated with decreased odds of vaccination acceptance / intent. Belonging to the age group 35 to 54 compared to 18–24 (OR = 1.049, 95% CI [0.965, 1.128], p = 0.24) was associated with an increased odds of vaccination acceptance or intent, which also was non-statistically significant. Note that starting at age 35 to 44, this was the age group in which vaccination acceptance / intent started to trend upwards. The variable for Pre-Post November 2021 was not statistically significant in this model, which would suggest changes in weighting methodology had no effect, but in later diagnostics, methodological shifts were statistically significant. This suggests the months of October and November may be affected by statistical weighting methodology changes.

Smooth term statistical significance

Regarding non-linear effects, the three composite cultural dimensions: Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects and Month emerged as statistically significant in the final analyses when using stratified cluster robust standard errors across five imputed datasets. The nonlinearity of the patterns was obvious based on the EDFs above 1, for most of the terms: Culture PC1 (EDF = 3.97, p < 0.001); Culture PC2 (EDF = 4.87, p < 0.001); Culture PC3 (EDF = 4.95, p < 0.001); Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects (EDF = 2.50, p < 0.001); and Month (EDF = 1.93, p < 0.001). Percent Community COVID (EDF = 1.0003, p = 0.719) and Percent Spent Time Outside of the Household (EDF = 1.005, p = 0.114) emerged as nearly linear. Detailed explanation of cultural score

Because the composite scores for the cultural component combined each of the six Hofstede dimensions of culture into three Principal Components, we decided to further investigate the meaning of the composite scores and each Hofstede dimension’s relationship to vaccine acceptance / intent. To do so, we de-constructed each composite score into its factor loadings, using pooled results across all five imputed datasets. We begin here by examining the individual PC distributions from the GAM. Then, we discuss the bar chart created to demonstrate how each PC score affected vaccine acceptance / intent. The bar chart was created by placing all values at the constant except culture, which was the pooled mean cultural score; this was then projected onto pooled PCA loadings. By doing this, we were able to determine which cultural PCs were most associated with increased or decreased vaccine acceptance / intent in each country of study and to determine synergistic patterns.

Three distinct cultural traits were discovered: the “Modern Individualist,” the “Cautious Collectivist,” and the “Disciplined Collectivist.” Please see Figs. 8 and 9 below and Table 7 below. According to factor loadings, the “Modern Individualist” culture is comprised of people, on average, seeking less social structure (low PD), more fun (high INDULG), and ambitiously (high MTAS) seeking short-term goals and rewards (low LTO). In cultures described as “Cautious Collectivist,” their populous tends to behave cautiously (high UA), but still seeks short-term goals and rewards (low LTO), and focuses on the well-being of the group instead of self (Collectivism). The “Disciplined Collectivist” prefers social structure (high PD), focuses on the group (high Collectivism), and seeks long-term goals and rewards (high LTO) in a risky manner (low UA).

For those cultures that met the criteria of the PC1 (“Modern Individualist”), at negative scores (low Individualism, low INDULG, and low MTAS), vaccine acceptance / intent started out low and constantly rose in vaccine acceptance / intent; as negative scores reached the global average (not negative or positive), vaccination acceptance / intent dipped. However, when reaching positive scores (high Individualism, high Indulgence, and high MTAS) (i.e., cultures that are higher in PC1) the higher the positive scores, the higher the levels of vaccine acceptance / intent than the global average. Cultures characterized by negative culture scores on PC2 (“Cautious Collectivist”), mean these cultures tends to be prone to risk-taking and more Individualistic; these cultures started out with low vaccination acceptance / intent, followed a sharp upward linear trend until reaching lower vaccine acceptance / intent at the global average. Positive scores (more collectivism and caution) were associated with another almost equivalent resurgence in vaccination. In other words, at extreme negative scores (risky, individualistic cultures), vaccination acceptance / intent started out low and drastically surged, and as it reached moderate scores closer to 0, vaccination drastically dipped. At positive scores (the cautious, collectivist), vaccine acceptance / intent drastically resurged to higher percentages then started to dip again. The final component, PC3 (the “Disciplined Collectivist”), is characterized by high vaccination scores at extreme negative scores (less social hierarchy [low PD] and more Individualism) followed by a slight dip in vaccination acceptance / intent until reaching an almost equivalent high of vaccine acceptance / intent at negative scores closer to 0 (the global average); when reaching scores at 0 (the global average), there is a sharp, steady decline in vaccination acceptance / intent followed by a sharp steady rise, at extremely low positive scores and high positive scores. This U-shaped distribution on the final curve indicates cultures scoring very high or low positive scores (the classic “Disciplined Collectivist”) tend to have high vaccination acceptance / intent and less vaccine acceptance / intent at the moderate positive scores. Please See Fig. 7 above.

In sum, these findings are complex and suggest the directionality and magnitude of the score matters, but because the elements are present to some degree in all countries, there is a push-pull type of relationship. In this sample size, there was no universal axiom applicable. In fact, there was a resistance between opposing factors in which some factors at times increased vaccination acceptance / intent, while at others, they decreased vaccine acceptance / intent. What this demonstrates is a highly complex, nonlinear association among various cultural factors dependent upon the directionality and magnitude of the cultural scores. Overwhelmingly, though, the Modern Individualist and Cautious Collectivist dimensions drove the decrease in vaccine / acceptance intent in this particular iteration of the imputed sample size, as demonstrated in Fig. 10 below. In this particular plot, the “Disciplined Collectivist” dimension was characterized by typically increasing vaccination percentage. Depending on randomization of the imputations, different elements were drivers or suppressors of vaccine acceptance / intent, suggesting cultural shifts are not stagnant and could benefit vaccine campaigns if they are regularly assessed. This constant push-pull effect could explain the reason for nonlinearity (and the highly complex relationships) because at times certain cultural influences may have been more dominant than others with varying effects. Thus, having clear cultural leanings away from cultural ambiguity (the global average) demonstrates associations with high or low vaccine acceptance / intent. However, it must be emphasized that this is not to be a universal axiom; this study is subject to ecological fallacy and a low sample size of only 15 countries. While these results suggest that cultural “clarity”—or strong alignment with a specific cultural profile—is associated with higher or lower vaccine intent, these patterns should be viewed as a framework for understanding these country trends rather than a generalizable finding. Finally, it should serve to remind people that culture is complex and vaccine education may benefit from addressing all of these cultural themes when clear cultural leanings are present.

The only variables with wide ranges include PC 2, Percent Spent Time Outside of the Household, and Percent Fear of Vaccine Side Effects with lower bounds of 1, suggesting these specific variables may be country-specific findings.

A second check of robustness was used to account for the stratified cluster design (N = 15 countries). Specifically, cluster-robust standard errors (HC2) were calculated using a linear approximation of the model. The primary cultural and behavioral predictors did not exhibit significant linear trends. However, because the Jackknife analysis confirmed highly non-linear relationships (mean EDFs of approximately 2–5) for these variables, the lack of linear significance further justifies the use of a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) to capture the complex, nonlinear drivers and suppressors of vaccine acceptance / intent. While the weighting methodological transition (the Method Jump variable) was not a primary driver in the pooled GAM, it emerged as a highly significant predictor (p < 0.01) in the cluster-robust covariance analysis. This suggests that while the cultural smooth terms capture the nuanced variance in vaccine acceptance / intent, there is a statistically significant baseline shift attributable to the survey methodology change for weighting that is identified when accounting for country-level clustering.

Finally, the permutation and F-tests for stability were examined. The permutation test examines if the original country data is more accurate than the shuffling of data by country (permutations); if the original data is more accurate it means the model is creating accurate predictions. Because of the small sample size, restricted permutations testing was used, and ultimately, demonstrated an inability to differentiate if the geographic findings were real or due to stochastic (random) clustering. In other words, these results are specific to these countries and not able to be applied universally, indicating lack of generalizability. F-test stability checks further demonstrated that reordering rows, without regard to within-country clustering, produced higher F-statistics, further verifying that these results are not generalizable but specific to these 15 countries.

While the model diagnostics suggested limitations, the model did exhibit high post-hoc statistical power, evidenced by the narrowness of the bootstrapped confidence intervals and the stability of the effect sizes for the cultural PCs across 1,000 iterations. PC1 and PC3 demonstrated the highest effect sizes of f2 = 0.24 and f2 = 0.31, respectively.

Discussion

Chen and Biswas [23] found specific Hofstede dimensions had varying associations with COVID-19 cases and deaths, and Bellio and colleagues found that when vaccine messages were tailored to dominant components of the culture, COVID-19 vaccine campaigns were more successful [44]. In this particular study, our results demonstrated that culture is one of the most important factors associated with having been vaccinated for COVID-19, having had an appointment for vaccination, or having been willing to vaccinate if the opportunity arose. Although no maxim could be derived because there is a constant push-pull between these three dimensions, in all countries, having scores at the global average demonstrated low vaccine acceptance / intent. This suggests an open opportunity in less culturally-defined countries to use “culturally appropriate” canned, standardized global health messaging materials because there is no clear, dominant cultural driver. However, in cultures where cultural scores are definitively positive and negative and have clearly defined magnitudes, it may be useful to target the multiple components of cultural dimensions and tailor health education materials with those specific cultural components in mind. In fact, the dominance of the cultural dimensions present and whether or not they were positive or negative, were associated with increases and decreases in vaccine acceptance / intent. Although cultural factors’ predominance and effect on vaccination acceptance / intent changed depending on the pooled imputation used, the three themes emerged as either drivers or suppressors of vaccine intention. This suggests one of two ways of strategizing vaccine education campaign: (1) target the suppressors of vaccination acceptance / intent or (2) target the drivers of increased vaccine acceptance / intent. For example, if a country has high Modern Individualist tendencies that reduce vaccination, the characteristics of Individualism, ambition (MTAS), Indulgence (desire for fun) could be targeted to help increase vaccine acceptance / intent. Messaging could focus on personal reasons for vaccination like being able to get out in public again and enjoy fun activities. Furthermore, if Cautious Collectivism is present and decreasing vaccination, these characteristics could be targeted to increase vaccine acceptance / intent. For example, a message about the known safety of the vaccine and the unknown virulence and uncertainty of the actual virus as well as the vaccine’s usefulness for the community could be helpful. Alternatively, if Disciplined Collectivism (high PD [respectful of social hierarchy] and collectivism) increased vaccination acceptance / intent, a strategy for targeting this element could be to have a well-respected leader give a message detailing how vaccination could be good for all members of society. Thus, multiple strategies could be used to target the suppressors and drivers of vaccination based on cultural findings and the cultural atmosphere at the time. While we did find some correlative results between culture and COVID-19 vaccine intentions and acceptance, these should be interpreted with caution because the base dimensions were at their limits in some cases. Even though we attempted to account for bias, self-response survey data can skew reality, especially when social desirability bias is present [36]. Finally, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive because we had a sample size of only fifteen countries and robustness checks suggested inability to generalize results to countries beyond the ones of study. Furthermore, results may be subject to ecological fallacy in which individual-level intention-behavior gap influences are not fully captured. Clearly, though, culture has some type of complex association with vaccination decision-making that should be explored further.

In our study, adults over the age of 18 to 24 and 25 to 34 tended to have higher odds of vaccine acceptance / intent. Such results align with those of one study that found varying effects of age on vaccination beliefs and patterns; in fact, the youngest generations tended to be more vaccine-hesitant than generations before them [45]. Potentially young adults may have been less likely to vaccinate due to fear of side effects and embracing misinformation, while older age groups may have believed they were more likely to contract COVID-19, potentially inducing older adults to vaccinate [45]. This suggests that potentially younger age groups need to receive more COVID-19 vaccine education. Due to low sample sizes in the age groups and filtering out of non-response age categories, we had to combine categories, which affected the ability to determine which age groups were individually responsible for vaccine acceptance / intent. Instead, we lumped many age groups together, potentially missing nuances between age groups, which limits our ability to determine granular age category effects on vaccine acceptance / intent.

In terms of gender identification, there were conflicting findings in the literature between men’s and women’s acceptance of the vaccine [46–48], but we found that women compared to men had higher odds of having been vaccinated, having had an appointment to vaccinate, or having responded they would be willing to vaccinate, if given the chance. Men, then, potentially should be included more prevalently in vaccine education campaigns in these countries. These findings should be juxtaposed by the limitation that we have a small sample size of only fifteen countries, and the sample size of Facebook / Meta participants may have been over or under-sampled. Thus, results may not be representative of developing countries as a whole. Furthermore, in our study we filtered out individuals who identified as other or refused to disclose their gender identity due to low population sample sizes. This limits our ability to glean information about these groups. Other studies have broached the topic of mistrust of the healthcare community, fear of COVID-19 vaccine side effects, and access barriers in LGBTQ populations. However, for some LGBTQ groups, there was a large percentage reportedly desiring vaccination, though this referenced study occurred in high income countries [49].

In our study, having reported spending at least one day outside of the household was associated with a statistically significant, almost perfectly linear, decrease in the odds of vaccination acceptance / intention. While desiring to be out and about in society may have induced some to vaccinate [48] initially, it is also possible that those venturing out in society during the midst of the pandemic may have been more prone to risk-taking [50]. This behavioral profile may have led these individuals to accept the risk of non-vaccination even while increasing their public exposure to the virus.

Knowing someone in the community with symptoms of COVID-19 was associated with initially higher vaccine acceptance or intent, followed by decreased vaccine acceptance / intent. Because the pattern followed an initial increase in vaccine acceptance / intent, followed by an almost linear decrease in vaccine acceptance / intent, this is suggestive of changes in perceived susceptibility. In fact, in one European study, perceived susceptibility started out high during the pandemic and eventually declined, particularly between 2021 and 2022 [51]. Because our study covers the same years of study, it is possible our study is detecting a similar trend in these countries of study. These results should also be interpreted with caution, though, because they are only representative of the countries of study, and there were statistical limitations to the findings’ generalizability.

Fear of vaccine side effects has been consistently associated with less vaccination in many studies [7, 52, 53]; we found that fear of vaccine side effects was not initially associated with low vaccine acceptance / intent; however, as more people reported fear of vaccine side effects, vaccination acceptance / intent declined significantly. Populations reporting more people with fear of vaccination side effects may have had concerns about some of the mild and severe adverse reactions reported [54], and there could be a band wagoning effect of people deciding not to vaccinate because others in their family or friend groups did not [12, 13]. However, data at the population level may not capture these individual nuances. Furthermore, because there was high missingness of data that was imputed and an inability to generalize findings, there may be an even stronger correlation than we found here. Such high missingness may also suggest social desirability bias was involved.

The effect of month was statistically significantly associated with vaccine acceptance / intent. This may be due to people changing their perceived susceptibility over time [51]. It is also important to note here that the methodological change in weighting affected the statistical reliability for the month of October, as reported in the F-statistics and parametric testing performed post-analysis. Still, because the month was statistically significant, it could mean that these LMICs were getting more access to COVAX as the program became more established, thus allowing people more of an opportunity to obtain the vaccine. This could mean that vaccine acceptance / willingness had not changed due to a decision-making process but because of accessibility.

Finally, high deviance explained at 91.05% is likely due to some of the strong influences of culture, high concurvity, and the small sample size that limited our ability to raise the knots to allow for more complexity. However, the necessity of limiting basis functions (k) to prevent mathematical instability in a small-cluster environment, combined with observed high concurvity among predictors, suggests that the model is highly optimized for this specific geographical snapshot. Consequently, while the internal predictive power is near-total, the potential for model misspecification remains, and the results should be viewed as a “case study” of these 15 countries rather than a universally applicable set of axioms.

Strengths & limitations

This study benefits from rigorous inclusion criteria and the use of multi-month cross-sectional data, providing a broad temporal view of vaccine acceptance / intent. A significant strength is the intentional focus on LMICs, a demographic often underrepresented in global health literature [31]. Methodologically, the use of stratified cluster bootstrapping in the Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) and statistically significant associations across the majority of bootstrapped iterations, including the cultural PCs, demonstrate model strengths. Furthermore, the Jackknife methodology of robustness checks demonstrate the model was not sensitive to outliers, though, major limitations still exist.

In fact, several limitations must be noted. First, cross-sectional data cannot denote causality. Second, despite the use of precision weighting, the potential for sampling or response bias remains [36], which may be further complicated by digital data collection favoring individuals with higher technology access. Furthermore, the change in methodological weighting between October and November is problematic. Social desirability bias may have been involved, which is particularly questionable in the variable for fear of vaccine side effects due to the need for a large portion of imputation. While rigorous inclusion criteria ensured all countries had initial access to COVAX, generalizability cannot be realized for large swaths of the developing country or for all regions of the world, especially for categories of individuals that were filtered out of final analyses. Additionally, concurvity remained high in the final modeling, particularly for time and the cultural PCs, which demonstrates there is more complexity to the spline than could be captured; however, we kept the splines at low base dimensions to stabilize results from a small sample size and the need for improved interpretability. Finally, there was variability of confidence intervals for some terms, suggesting magnitudes may vary significantly by country.

Conclusions

While individual-level and group factors of the SEM may be significant determinants of behavior, culture also has a strong influence on vaccination acceptance and intent in these 15 countries of study. Analysis of the Hofstede dimensions indicated that being vaccinated, having an appointment, or being will to vaccinate, if given the chance, may be viewed differently in these countries, depending upon the cultural filter. Culture emerged as a complex phenomenon in this model, suggesting that simply choosing “culturally acceptable” canned material may not be the best for all vaccine intervention campaigns. Instead, looking at each country’s culture holistically could provide more guidance. Specifically, in countries where the scores are moderate, standard vaccine education may be acceptable due to neutral cultural leanings, but in countries with cultural scores above or below the global average, each Hofstede cultural component could be individually examined and materials tailored to match the dominant cultural perspectives in that country. Culture, then, should not be overlooked by public health professionals in vaccine education campaigns. Implementation of culturally salient messaging and community engagement (i.e., including components tailored to specific cultures) may aid in increasing vaccination acceptance and intent in the countries of study. Future research should continue to incorporate the SEM into vaccine research with higher sample sizes for generalizability; future studies should also test if other relevant SEM variables (including culture) show similar support for the involvement of higher levels of the SEM in vaccination acceptance and intent.
