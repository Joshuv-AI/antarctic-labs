Beyond elimination; addressing challenges in maintaining the malaria elimination status in Suriname
Authors: Stephen Vreden, Hedley Cairo, Gabriela Sanches, Anfernee Neus, Malti Adhin, Marthelise Eersel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuriname has been certified malaria-free by WHO on June 30th 2025. In the post-elimination era, malaria is still requiring full attention of the public health system. This is in particular due to the malaria burden in neighbouring countries and the intense migration across borders related to small scale artisanal gold mining, which poses a risk for re-establishment of malaria into the country.AnalysisThroughout the process of achieving certification of malaria elimination, the National Malaria Program implemented a range of strategies to maintain the zero transmission status. This manuscript describes and explains these strategies, highlighting the recent addition of individual targeted drug administration and presumptive antimalarial treatment. In the context of maintaining the elimination status of Suriname, the regional malaria situation is presented and the importance of a regional strategy in the ongoing fight against malaria addressed.ConclusionSuriname is extremely susceptible for re-establishment of malaria. The National Malaria Elimination Task Force implemented innovative strategies, adapted to the dynamics of malaria importation. Next to these national actions, it is pivotal that the malaria situation in the Guiana Shield is addressed with a regional effort.

Introduction

Historically, Suriname had one of the highest malaria burdens in the Americas, but dramatic reductions in transmission since the early 2000 s culminated in its certification as malaria-free by WHO on June 30, 2025. [1, 2]. From the year 1996 to date, the Government of Suriname has appointed a malaria technical oversight committee, initially named the Malaria Board and renamed Malaria Elimination Task Force (METF) in 2018. Over the past 30 years, this committee has provided guidance to the NMP in measures towards control and eventually elimination of malaria. At the time of certification, local malaria transmission had already been prevented for almost four years. However, during this period, an average of about 60 import cases were diagnosed annually from over 10,000 microscopy slides (from proactive, reactive and passive case detection) that are examined each year. There is not a one-size-fits-all approach to address trans-border malaria. Multiple factors, including social and economic development, weak surveillance and response systems, poor access to health services, differences in national malaria policies and treatment-seeking behaviors can contribute to malaria importation [3].

Description of the national and regional malaria situation

Suriname is an upper middle-income country in the Guiana Shield of South America and borders Brazil, French Guiana and Guyana [4] (Fig. 1). More than 90% of the country is covered by rainforest. The country had a population of 626,028 people in 2023, mainly living in the coastal region, which has been free of malaria transmission for over 60 years. [5]. As indicated in Fig. 2, this region stretches southwards into about 80 km from the coast. An estimated 52,000 Indigenous and Tribal people live in the forested interior in numerous villages. Health provision to these people is provided by Medical Mission, an NGO that is supported by the Government [6]. Major efforts in ensuring the availability of early diagnosis and prompt treatment (EDPT), along with ample distribution of Insecticide-treated nets (ITN), resulted in malaria elimination in most of these villages more than a decade before the national elimination was achieved. In the interior of the country, there are also about 20,000 people, mainly mobile migrants, working in ASGM [7]. In order to control, and ultimately eliminate malaria in these populations, the NMP implemented a Malaria Service Deliverers (MSD) network, which is further described below. (Fig. 1). Malaria importation originates primarily in neighboring countries and is mainly related to the migrant gold mining community, with incidental cases in indigenous populations crossing the border for social or religious events. Among these migrant populations involved in gold mining, malaria is often diagnosed in receptive areas and occurs under the following circumstances: (1) relapses of P. vivax malaria occurring in people who experienced their previous malaria episode in a neighbouring country; (2) people who are travel companions of positively tested migrants but who present with symptoms and seek testing and treatment days to weeks days afterwards; (3) people whose rapid diagnostic test (RDT) is negative but whose positive microscopic result becomes available days afterwards. These gaps in detection pose a significant threat to re-establishment of malaria, since cases remain undetected by the public health systems for days to weeks. Migrants may enter the country in the coastal region, but if they don’t have symptoms at that time may not seek malaria consultation before entering the gold mines in the forest. In order to effectively address the challenge of preventing re-establishment and safeguarding the elimination status, the METF is continuously refining its strategies. This manuscript describes the post-elimination situation in Suriname, outlining its challenges and the approaches, including the recent introduction of two new interventions.

Malaria in neighboring countries

The vast majority (> 90%) of imported cases originates from neighboring countries (Table 1). Therefore, a brief description overview of the malaria epidemiology in the bordering countries is provided.

Brazil

The Brazilian portion of the Guiana Shield is located in the northernmost part of the country, primarily spanning the states of Roraima, Pará, and Amapá. These Brazilian states are the main contributors to cross-border malaria in the region, with transmission primarily related to gold mining and, to a lesser extent the movement of Indigenous populations. Brazil has intensified its efforts to eliminate P. falciparum (P.f.) malaria in particular, by expanding the access to diagnosis (through expanding RDT use), and treatment. Also, by implementing the use of tafenoquine, even for children, which may add to the decrease of the overall malaria burden. The total number of malaria cases, of those caused by P.f. and by P.v. decreased in the year 2025 by 15% and 30.7% and 11.1%, respectively, compared to 2024. This decrease has also been observed in the municipalities of the Guiana Shield, with a reduction of total cases by 21% in the same period. [8].

French Guiana

To the East of Suriname lies French Guiana, an overseas territory of France. With only a few hundred malaria cases annually in recent years, it is aspiring to eliminate malaria in the short term. Malaria cases in the country are mainly diagnosed in remote settlements of Indigenous populations and in the areas where mobile migrants are involved in illegal gold mining activities. The illegality of the latter creates an obstacle, not only for timely diagnosis and treatment to these populations, but also for adequate malaria surveillance, which is essential for monitoring and documenting the process of elimination [9].

Guyana

Guyana, west of Suriname in the Guiana Shield, remains in the malaria control phase, with transmission concentrated in forested interior regions and linked to artisanal gold mining and cross-border movement from Brazil and Venezuela. Strengthened surveillance, expanded access to diagnosis through microscopy and RDTs, and targeted interventions in high-risk communities have contributed to a decline in malaria cases. In 2025, the Annual Parasite Index fell from 230 in 2023 to about 100, with overall cases decreasing compared to 2024. Transmission is driven by Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax, the latter requiring radical cure strategies including tafenoquine. These measures support Guyana’s commitment to further reduce malaria incidence by 75% by 2030. [10].

MSD network

Previously, the mobile and migrant populations in Suriname, mainly those working in ASGM, had locally, no access to malaria diagnosis and treatment (nor to any health care) in the remote regions where they worked. To address this, the NMP recruited members of these communities and trained them in distributing ITNs and providing malaria diagnosis and treatment to their peers under the supervision and guidance of health professionals. These certified malaria workers are referred to as malaria service deliverers (MSDs). This initiative of an MSD network fostered community ownership and helped reduce (language) barriers to malaria care among under served and mobile populations, thus increasing community engagement and service uptake. It proved to be pivotal in early detection of malaria cases, significantly reduced the habit of taking antimalarial medication without diagnosis and helped to prevent re-establishment [11]. The fact that annually nearly 50% of all imported cases are diagnosed by MSDs in the interior, underscores the relevance of this network.

Outreach

Gold miners from the mobile and migrant populations often spend a few days in the capital Paramaribo, either on their way from bordering countries to the gold mines, or returning from the interior for shopping and leisure. In the areas where they typically reside, the NMP has implemented outreach activities, twice per month, providing opportunities for malaria screening. Additionally, staff members at some of the hotels commonly frequented by miners, have been trained to work as ‘urban MSDs' diagnosing the majority of cases in the capital. These outreach activities in the capital may further contribute to early detection and therefore to prevention of malaria re-establishment.

Screening facilities at borders and strategic places

In addition to official border posts, several informal crossing points exist where people enter Suriname from neighboring countries. At these locations the NMP has established free malaria testing and treatment facilities. However, these facilities remain under-utilized as testing is not mandatory upon entrance, and therefore, only a fraction of travelers is reached.

Prevention of P. vivax relapses

In the year 2025, 44% of imported P.v. cases were based on a relapse and 52% was diagnosed in receptive regions (Table 2). This further underlines the significant risk of re-establishment after importation. To address the problem of P.v. relapses, the METF implemented three treatment measures:

Re-treatment

During the last P.v. outbreak (2019–2021) in two adjacent Indigenous villages in the rainforest of Suriname, a single clonal lineage was identified throughout the outbreak, emphasizing the need for interventions specifically targeting relapses. It was observed that even if primaquine was given under supervision for 14 days, the outbreak could not be terminated. Only after it was decided to implement re-treatment with a full course of chloroquine and primaquine, relapses and transmission could be ended [12]. Since then, the policy for re-treatment of all P.v. cases after six weeks, was implemented.

Radical cure

It is a well-known fact that P.v. infections may cause relapses, even if the schizonticidal treatment has been adequately completed, which quite often is not the case [13]. Therefore, it was decided to offer re-treatment to travelers with a history of P.v. malaria within the previous nine months, even if they have negative testing results at that time. In order to clearly differentiate this re-treatment from the one in which the initial diagnosis and treatment were done in Suriname, it was designated as “radical cure”.

Tafenoquine

The use of a single dose of tafenoquine eliminates the problem of poor adherence to the 14-day primaquine regimen, especially if the single dose of the drug is administered under direct observation. Its use however, requires confirmation of an adequate glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity. Ample experience with the use of point-of-care G6PD testing was already available in the country, due to the CUREMA study, that was initiated in the year 2022, together with researchers from Brazil and French Guiana. [14]. The NMP expanded its use by procuring additional testing devices. With support of the Ministry of Health in Brazil, donating a stock of tafenoquine, Suriname could initiate the use of tafenoquine co-administered with chloroquine as first line treatment of P.v. malaria in March 2025. Patients in whom a deficiency or intermediate G6PD activity level is found, are still treated with primaquine and chloroquine.

Malakit

The inclusion of Malakit in the national strategy, after its proven efficacy in a multi-country study [15], was intended to reduce the import of malaria cases from bordering countries and territories. In short, Malakit consists of three RDTs, a full course of artemether/lumefantrine, 15 mg of primaquine, paracetamol, and visual instructions on performing and interpreting the RDT and taking the medication. Prior to receiving the kit, aspiring users have to pass a one-on-one practical training in its use. The goal of Malakit was primarily to provide treatment to the gold mining populations that entered the French Guiana forest (for illegal mining activities), where there is no access to diagnosis and treatment. This transnational project (involving Brazil, French Guiana and Suriname) resulted in significant decreases of malaria cases (especially P.f.) in miners returning to Suriname and Brazil from the French Guiana forest and was subsequently included in Suriname’s national malaria strategy and expanded to miners travelling from Suriname to Guyana and Brazil. However, this strategy is not adequate for use within countries that already have or are striving for malaria elimination, since it precludes adequate malaria surveillance and a timely outbreak response. This is because cases are not reported in real time, thus preventing case investigation and subsequent action.

Reactive case detection (RACD)

Whenever a malaria case is detected in a vulnerable area, the NMP conducts a case investigation to assess the potential for local transmission. The results of this case investigation, will guide the decision if and when screening of people in that area is indicated. The purpose of RACDs in Suriname is to interrupt transmission by identifying and treating infections surrounding a known index case. Depending on the circumstances (remoteness, high risk of transmission, unavailability of on-site microscopy), it may be decided to offer targeted drug administration.

Molecular surveillance

Since 2009, the University Institute for Biomedical Sciences (MWI) has established a national malaria gene bank in Suriname that serves as a repository for Plasmodium samples. Dried blood spots from all confirmed malaria cases are stored and linked to key identifiers, including Plasmodium species, location of diagnosis and the presumed origin of infection [16]. This standardized gene bank enables retrospective molecular malaria research, including investigations of the emergence and spread of antimalarial drug resistance and parasite phylogenetics, while also supporting molecular origin tracking of isolates.

Genetic markers associated with antimalarial drug resistance are assessed in imported Plasmodium falciparum cases, whenever feasible. This molecular surveillance adds an additional layer of monitoring to inform and strengthen interventions aimed at preventing not only the re-establishment of malaria but also the potential introduction and spread of drug-resistant parasite strains.

Genetic profiling of Plasmodium vivax cases can support the identification of potential outbreak sources, particularly when travel history is unclear or absent. Moreover, molecular confirmation of an imported case may reduce the need for costly reactive case detection missions to the interior, thereby improving the cost-effectiveness of response efforts. A similar approach has been implemented in the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea [17].

Newly implemented strategies

Despite systematic implementation of the strategies outlined above, the proportion of imported cases detected in vulnerable places did not decline during the four years of zero local transmission. Several factors may account for this finding. First, the MSD network, which plays an important role in early detection and treatment of cases diagnosed in vulnerable areas, thus helping to prevent re-establishment, is not consistently present in all of the numerous gold mining settlements. Nevertheless, maintaining and even expanding this network, is a key factor in Suriname’s plan for the maintenance of malaria elimination. Second, RACD is an intervention that, due to its high cost, is not sustainable. Also, since it is carried out at a specific point in time, introduced cases that appear days before or even weeks after the screening event may be missed. Another limitation is, that the RDT which is currently being used in Suriname has a sub-optimal performance and microscopy results are not always available on the same day. In the year 2025, 20% of all microscopically confirmed positive P.v. cases had a negative RDT result (Table 2). A similar poor performance of the same, WHO recommended RDT brand has been reported in a recently published study in Myanmar [18]. Since microscopy results may take some days to become available, this may cause patients, especially from mobile populations travelling to the interior with a parasitemia. In order to address the limitations of the current strategies, the NMP implemented two additional ones, being individual Target Drug Administration (iTDA) and Presumptive antimalarial treatment (PrAT). These strategies include offering treatment to individuals in whom no malaria diagnosis has been confirmed. Individuals may benefit from this, since a possible malaria episode in a place where no nearby treatment facility is available may be avoided and the risk of transmission be reduced. Although the latter not necessarily bears an individual benefit, it must be observed that the same goes for mass and targeted drug administration (MDA/TDA), which, based on the tolerability of antimalarial drugs and the usefulness in preventing malaria outbreaks, are both endorsed by WHO.[19].

Individual targeted drug administration (iTDA)

“Targeted Drug Administration (TDA) is a form of chemo-prevention involving the provision of a full course of an antimalarial medicine to individuals who are at increased risk of malaria infection compared to the general population”. This intervention is conditionally recommended by WHO [19]. In Suriname, migrants who are diagnosed with malaria, often have one or more travel companions, who potentially are also infected, but may have moved on and be out of reach when they develop malaria symptoms. People diagnosed with malaria are therefore always encouraged to refer their travel companions for malaria screening.

Although malaria diagnosis in Suriname is always done by both RDT and microscopy, the result of the latter, may not be available on the same day. Taking into account the unreliability of the RDT, travel companions, with a negative RDT result, and traveling onwards, are offered malaria treatment consisting of chloroquine plus tafenoquine, provided that theirG6PD activity level permits use of the latter. In case of a low G6PD activity level, they will be offered arthemeter/lumefantrine together with a 14-day primaquine regimen. This treatment approach is based on the fact that the vast majority of malaria cases in this region are caused by P.v. Although chloroquine plus tafenoquine is not the treatment of choice for P.f., it will reduce parasitemia and therefore prevent serious sequelae of infection with this species [20]. Combining artemether/lumefantrine with tafenoquine is not an option, since the latter needs to be co-administered with chloroquine, to be effective [21]. People getting treatment are informed that they will be called to inform them about the microscopy result and get additional treatment if needed. As this new treatment policy is about offering medication to individuals, rather than to groups of people at risk of malaria, as recommended by WHO, it is referred to as ‘individual targeted drug administration’ (iTDA).

Presumptive antimalarial treatment (PrAT)

Due to the sub-optimal performance of currently available RDTs, it is not safe to rely entirely on their result, especially not in suspected cases. Again, this may be problematic for people who cannot wait for microscopy results, but will be travelling onwards. Besides the advice to consult a physician in order to explore other causes for their symptoms, these people, coming from an area of malaria transmission, having malaria symptoms and a negative RDT result, with microscopy results pending, are offered presumptive antimalarial treatment, (the same schedule as for iTAD), if they will be travelling onwards before the microscopy result becomes available.

Regional strategy

In addition to these national actions, addressing malaria in the Guyana Shield requires strong regional collaboration and coordinated regional action, given the high mobility of populations and the cross-border nature of malaria transmission. In 2025, PAHO initiated a transnational technical board, including malaria experts from Brazil, French Guiana, Guyana, Suriname and Venezuela, to initiate and coordinate regional activities in the fight against malaria. This board aims to strengthen cross-border collaboration, particularly through the exchange of surveillance information and the implementation of joint interventions to reduce the malaria burden across the region.

Discussion

The vision of WHO and the global malaria community is a world free of malaria. Malaria elimination is critical to saving lives, boosting economies, and preventing parasite resistance to drugs. Therefore, once malaria elimination has been achieved in a specific region, it is imperative to safeguard this achievement with sustained vigilance and commitment. At the same time, every possible effort must be directed toward expanding the area of elimination.

Following the achievement of malaria elimination, the task of the METF shifted to maintaining the interrupted indigenous transmission. The availability of literature on countries’ strategies to maintain malaria elimination is very limited. Sri Lanka, having achieved elimination in 2016, developed a resilient system combining ongoing surveillance, early diagnosis and prompt treatment (EDPT), thorough case investigation, but also the continued involvement of health and other government authorities and NGOs. This integrated approach enables swift detection and containment of imported cases, preventing the resurgence of local transmission. [22]. Similar approaches were implemented after the People’s Republic of China’s great achievement of being certified malaria-free in 2021. [23]. Suriname is the first country in the Amazon region to obtain malaria-free certification. The strategies for this achievement were targeted to the local epidemiology and mainly included:1 Free EDPT availability even in the most remote areas,2 Free distribution and replacement of ITNs3 Detailed case investigation followed by reactive case detection when needed4 Ongoing investment in malaria awareness5 Involvement of stakeholders.

Malaria elimination in a country with porous borders, surrounded by regions with ongoing malaria transmission, with ASGM as the driver of malaria, leaves the country extremely susceptible for re-introduction. This highlights the importance of sustained vigilance and continuous consideration of innovative strategies, adapted to the dynamics of imported malaria. The new strategies presented in this manuscript, which are both conditionally recommended by WHO for elimination settings, have already demonstrated to be effective in identifying and treating cases that might otherwise be left untreated and cause re-establishment of malaria. [19].

Conclusion

Suriname is extremely susceptible for re-establishment of malaria. This requires permanent alertness of the health care system and other stakeholders, which in itself is challenging in a country where malaria has become almost history. The METF implemented innovative strategies, adapted to the dynamics of imported malaria. Next to these national actions, it is pivotal that the malaria situation in the Guiana Shield is addressed with a regional effort. The availability of funds for a regional approach is a prerequisite for expanding regional malaria elimination and safeguarding the elimination status in Suriname, that has only been achieved after a decades long struggle and investment from the National government and the Global Fund of over 20 million US Dollars. Beyond protecting Suriname, sustained regional malaria elimination will also generate significant public health benefits across the Guiana Shield, including a reduced collective malaria disease burden, prevention of avoidable deaths, and decreased school and work absenteeism, thereby improving overall social and economic productivity in the region.
