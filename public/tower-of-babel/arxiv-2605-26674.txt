1.
Introduction

The odometer semigroup arises naturally at the interface of combinatorial semigroup theory, topological dynamics, and noncommutative operator algebras. For an integer
n\geq 1
, the
odometer semigroup

\mathcal{O}_{n}
is the semigroup with generators

w,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
satisfying

wv_{i}=\begin{cases}v_{i+1},&1\leq i\leq n-1,\\
v_{1}w,&i=n.\end{cases}

It is also known as the adding-machine semigroup. This semigroup is closely related to the positive Baumslag–Solitar monoid
BS(1,n)^{+}
; see, for instance,
[
16
]
. It also admits descriptions in terms of Zappa–Szép products
[
18
]
.

The representation theory of semigroups and their associated

C^{*}
-algebras forms an important theme in operator theory, with
connections to the work of Cuntz
[
10
]
, Nica
[
27
]
, and Pimsner
[
29
]
. In the case of the odometer semigroup, related structures also appear in the study of topological subshifts, boundary quotient constructions, and scale-invariant dynamical systems
[
5
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
. The dynamical origin of the subject is the classical topological odometer. Given integers
k_{i}\geq 2
, the odometer acts on the compact product space
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{k_{i}}

by addition of the sequence
(1,0,0,\ldots)
according to the usual carry rule
[
16
]
; see also
[
4
,
7
,
12
]
. Odometer-type dynamical systems and their variants have appeared in the study of Toeplitz algebras
[
8
]
, von Neumann algebras
[
15
]
,
C^{*}
-algebras
[
34
]
, and semigroup
C^{*}
-algebras
[
6
,
24
,
25
,
27
]
.

In
[
19
]
, the representation theory of the odometer semigroup was developed in the setting of vector-valued full Fock spaces. More precisely, Fock representations of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
were classified in terms of operator-valued symbols. For each
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, an associated odometer map
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
was introduced. It was shown that, if
S^{\mathcal{E}}
denotes the standard row
isometry of left creation operators on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
, then a pair
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
is a Fock representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
if and only if there exists a uniquely determined symbol

L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
such that

W=W_{L}.

Thus the classification of Fock representations is reduced to the study of the corresponding operator
W_{L}
. While
[
19
]
established the existence of these maps and characterized the conditions under which they give rise to isometric, unitary, and Nica-covariant representations, the intrinsic geometric and operator-theoretic structure of
W_{L}
remained to be investigated.

The construction of
W_{L}
is reminiscent of the symbol calculus for noncommutative Toeplitz operators developed by Popescu
[
33
]
. Nevertheless, unlike the Toeplitz setting, the symbol in the odometer framework
is allowed to vary throughout the entire Fock space, leading to a substantially
richer structure. Motivated by the concluding remarks of
[
19
]
, the purpose of the present paper is to undertake a systematic study of the geometry, function-theoretic model, and operator-theoretic properties of odometer maps.

The central point of the paper is that an odometer map, although defined on
a noncommutative Fock space and governed by a noncommutative carrying rule,
contains a canonical single-variable analytic Toeplitz component. This
allows one to transfer questions about isometry, invertibility, norm,
Fredholmness, and defect multiplicity to the associated analytic symbol.

The first main result of the paper is a canonical block decomposition of

W_{L}
, together with a Hardy space realization of its Toeplitz-type block. In Section
3
, we introduce two canonical closed subspaces

\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
, which yield the orthogonal
decompositions

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}.

With respect to these decompositions,
W_{L}
admits the upper triangular block
representation

W_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
0&W_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

We observe that
W_{11}
is unitary. Consequently, whenever

\mathcal{E}\neq\{0\}
and
n\geq 2
, the odometer map
W_{L}
cannot be compact. For
n=1
, we show that
the only compact odometer maps are the zero maps
. This mirrors the corresponding compactness phenomenon for Toeplitz operators. The off-diagonal block
W_{12}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}
is either zero or has infinite rank. We refer to
W_{22}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
as the
Toeplitz-type operator
associated with
W_{L}
, and we define its
Toeplitz-type realization
on a vector-valued Hardy space. A key result of the paper shows that this realization is unitarily equivalent to the analytic Toeplitz operator

M_{\Theta}\quad\text{on }H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}),

where the symbol
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})

is uniquely determined by
L
. We also present a collection of explicit examples illustrating how different choices of the symbol
L
give rise to concrete
analytic symbols
\Theta
, including constant symbols, shift-type symbols, and Blaschke-product symbols.

For
n=1
, the odometer map is essentially an analytic Toeplitz-type
operator. In contrast, for
n\geq 2
, the canonical block decomposition
measures the deviation of
W_{L}
from the classical Toeplitz framework. In the special case
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
,

W_{L}
becomes the direct sum of a unitary operator and a Toeplitz-type
operator. Moreover, the Toeplitz-type realization of the principal block translates
questions about the multivariable noncommutative Fock space operator
W_{L}

into questions about the single-variable analytic symbol
\Theta
. This brings
Hardy space operator theory and
H^{\infty}
-function methods into the analysis
of odometer representations and establishes a direct connection with the
classical framework of analytic Toeplitz operators and Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş dilation
theory
[
26
,
28
,
30
]
.

In Section
4
, we apply this decomposition to study
operator-theoretic properties of
W_{L}
. First, we prove a
Douglas-type factorization theorem for odometer maps; see
Subsection
4.1
. By bridging the noncommutative geometry of the free Fock space with the classical Hardy algebra, we unlock a novel, purely operator-theoretic mechanism for such factorizations. More precisely, we
show that factorization at the level of symbols is equivalent to a
corresponding factorization of the associated odometer maps. Namely, if
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, then

\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
if and only if there exists a bounded operator
\Gamma\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
satisfying

\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\subseteq\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma.

Equivalently, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{L_{1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{L_{2}}}

Under the geometric assumptions
L_{i}\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp},\,i=1,2
, the multivariable Douglas factorization of the odometer maps descends into a single-variable analytic factorization of their associated analytic symbols. If

\Theta_{i}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
are the analytic symbols associated with
L_{i}
, then
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
implies the existence of
\Theta_{3}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\Theta_{1}(z)=\Theta_{2}(z)\Theta_{3}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

where
\Theta_{3}
is a constant operator-valued function given by
\Theta_{3}(z)\equiv C
. Here
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
satisfies
L_{1}=L_{2}C.
Thus the Fock-space factorization of odometer maps induces a single-variable analytic multiplier factorization of the corresponding Hardy space symbols.

Second, the Hardy space realization yields characterizations of several
basic operator-theoretic properties of
W_{L}
. In
Subsection
4.2
, we give new characterizations of the isometry and unitarity of
W_{L}
in terms of the associated analytic symbol
\Theta
, yielding shorter and more conceptual proofs of the corresponding criteria from
[
19
]
. We also obtain a new invertibility criterion:
W_{L}
is invertible precisely when
\Theta
is invertible in
H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
. Furthermore, we illustrate by examples the distinction between unitarity and invertibility in terms of the analytic symbol.

In Subsection
4.3
, we obtain norm formulae. For an arbitrary symbol
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, it was shown in
[
19
, Remark 2.4]
that
\|L\|\leq\|W_{L}\|\leq\|L\|+1.
Under the natural assumption

L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, we obtain the exact norm

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\},

where
\Theta
is the analytic symbol associated with the Toeplitz-type block of
W_{L}
. In particular, if
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, then
\|W_{L}\|=1
and hence the standard Sz.-Nagy–Foias dilation-theoretic consequences apply, including the existence of a unitary dilation and a contractive functional calculus for the disk algebra. Under a stronger geometric hypothesis, the above formula further reduces to

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|L\|\}.

A second main theme of the paper is the adjoint of an odometer map, detailed in Section
6
. Due to the highly noncommutative and symbol-dependent action of the odometer map, explicitly resolving its adjoint on the full Fock space requires a careful
analysis of the underlying odometer combinatorics. While
[
19
]
extracted an adjoint formula strictly under the rigid assumption that
W_{L}
is an isometry, we overcome these hurdles to derive a closed-form expression for
W_{L}^{*}
for
every
bounded symbol
L
. This formula provides a useful technical tool for the subsequent analysis. As a consequence, we recover the previously known isometric adjoint formula as a special case, thereby substantially extending the earlier theory.

Section
7
is devoted to consequences of the adjoint formula. The main object in this part is the space
\mathcal{E}_{L}
defined as

\mathcal{E}_{L}=\overline{\text{span}}\{e_{1}^{\otimes m}\otimes\eta:m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\eta\in\mathcal{E}\}\ominus\overline{\text{span}}\{e_{1}^{\otimes p}\otimes L\zeta:p\geq 1,\zeta\in\mathcal{E}\}.

We show that the position of
L\mathcal{E}
inside
\mathcal{E}_{L}

controls the isometric and Fredholm structure of the odometer map. For an isometry
W_{L}
, we prove the wandering subspace for the shift part in its Wold decomposition is identified with the defect space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
:

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}.

The following example shows that for an isometric odometer map,
\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})
is precisely the multiplicity of the shift part of
W_{L}
. Moreover, this defect space is reflected in the associated analytic Toeplitz operator
M_{\Theta}
. In particular, the multiplicity of the shift part of
W_{L}
is encoded by the associated analytic symbol:

\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}=U_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}),\qquad\operatorname{mult}(W_{L})=\operatorname{mult}(M_{\Theta}).

For an isometric odometer map
W_{L}
, the case
\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})=0
characterizes unitary maps via the Wold decomposition, and gives a shorter proof of
[
19
, Theorem 4.4]
. The condition
\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty

is equivalent both to the Fredholmness of
W_{L}
and to the essential normality of
W_{L}
. In this case,

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\,\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Under the same finite-defect hypothesis, we prove a Coburn-type spectral theorem:
For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, the operator
W_{L}-\lambda I
is Fredholm and

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L}-\lambda I)=-\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Furthermore, in this case, if
W_{L}
is a proper isometry, then its essential spectrum is the unit circle. We also illustrate, through examples, that the dimension of the defect space coincides with the absolute value of the corresponding Fredholm index.
Finally, the adjoint formula yields a necessary condition for
hyponormality: if
W_{L}
is hyponormal, then

\|L\eta\|\geq\|\eta\|,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

We illustrate through an example that this condition is not sufficient.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains the necessary preliminaries on full Fock spaces, odometer maps,
and vector-valued Hardy spaces. Section
3
establishes the canonical block decomposition of the odometer map and the Hardy space realization of its Toeplitz-type block. Section
4
develops applications of the block decomposition, including Douglas-type factorization, characterizations of isometric, unitary, and invertible
odometer maps, and norm formulae. Section
5
contains examples illustrating how the analytic symbol
\Theta
can be computed explicitly from the defining
symbol
L
. These examples also illustrate the characterization criteria for
W_{L}
to be an isometry, a unitary operator, or an invertible operator. In Section
6
, we compute explicitly the adjoint of an arbitrary bounded odometer map. Section
7
uses this formula to analyze

\ker W_{L}^{*}
, the Wold decomposition, Fredholmness, essential normality,
Coburn-type spectral phenomena, and hyponormality.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we fix notation and collect standard results that will be used throughout the paper. For background on operator theory, Hardy spaces, and
C^{*}
-algebras, we refer the reader to
[
1
,
14
,
26
]
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space. Throughout this paper, all Hilbert spaces are assumed to be separable and over
\mathbb{C}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, we write
P_{\mathcal{M}}
for the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{M}
. An operator
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
normal
if

N^{*}N=NN^{*}.

An operator
V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called an
isometry
if

V^{*}V=I.

An isometry
V
is called
pure
if
V^{*m}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{\mathrm{SOT}}0.

An isometry
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
unitary
if it is
surjective; equivalently,

U^{*}U=UU^{*}=I.

We now recall the classical vector-valued Hardy space model together with the basic operator-theoretic notions that will be used repeatedly in the sequel. For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, let

H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})=\left\{f(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}\eta_{m}:\eta_{m}\in\mathcal{E},\;\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\|\eta_{m}\|^{2}<\infty\right\}

denote the
\mathcal{E}
-valued Hardy space on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
.
Equipped with the norm
\|f\|^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\|\eta_{m}\|^{2},

it is a Hilbert space. The unilateral shift on
H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
is the multiplication operator

M_{z}f(z)=zf(z),\qquad f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}).

We define

\displaystyle H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle=\left\{\Theta:\mathbb{D}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})\,:\,\Theta\text{ is analytic and }\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}\|\Theta(z)\|<\infty\right\}.

For an operator-valued bounded analytic function
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D}),

the corresponding analytic Toeplitz operator is defined by

M_{\Theta}f(z)=\Theta(z)f(z),\qquad f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}).

It is well known that
\|M_{\Theta}\|=\|\Theta\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{|z|<1}\|\Theta(z)\|.
Moreover, a bounded operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}))
is an analytic Toeplitz operator if and only if

M_{z}T=TM_{z}.

Equivalently,

\{M_{z}\}^{\prime}=\{M_{\Theta}:\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})\}.

The multiplication operator
M_{\Theta}:H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
is an isometry if and only if
\Theta
is
inner
, that is,

\Theta(\zeta)^{*}\Theta(\zeta)=I_{\mathcal{E}}\quad\text{for a.e. }\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}.

Moreover,
M_{\Theta}
is unitary if and only if
\Theta
is a constant unitary operator-valued function.

A noncommutative analogue of the Hardy space is the full Fock space, which plays a central role in the theory of Cuntz–Pimsner algebras and semigroup crossed products
[
11
,
27
,
29
]
. The full Fock space over
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is denoted by

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{C}\Omega\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k},

where
\Omega
is the vacuum vector. For a word

\mu=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{k}\in F_{n}^{+}
, we write
e_{\mu}=e_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{k}},

and
e_{\emptyset}=\Omega
. Then
\{e_{\mu}:\mu\in F_{n}^{+}\}

is the canonical orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}
. The length of a word
\mu
is denoted by
|\mu|
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we abbreviate
e_{k}^{\otimes m}
by
e_{k}^{m}
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, the
i
-th creation operator
S_{i}
on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}
is defined by

S_{i}f=e_{i}\otimes f.

More generally, for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, we write
S^{\mathcal{E}}=(S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}},\ldots,S_{n}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})

for the amplified left creation tuple on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
. This is a pure row isometry; that is, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mu|=m\\
\mu\in F_{n}^{+}\end{subarray}}\|(S_{\mu}^{*}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f\|^{2}=0,

where
S_{\mu}=S_{\mu_{1}}\cdots S_{\mu_{m}}
whenever
\mu=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{m}\in F_{n}^{+}

[
32
, Remark 1.1]
. These creation operators play a fundamental role in noncommutative operator theory and arise naturally in free analytic models
[
17
,
31
]
.

Our main focus is on odometer maps associated with Fock representations of the odometer semigroup. For a natural number
n\geq 1
, the odometer semigroup
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is generated by
\{w,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, subject to the relations

wv_{i}=\begin{cases}v_{i+1},&1\leq i\leq n-1,\\
v_{1}w,&i=n.\end{cases}

A representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
on
\mathcal{H}
consists of a pair
(W,T)
, where
T=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})

is a row operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),

such that

WT_{i}=\begin{cases}T_{i+1},&i=1,\ldots,n-1,\\[4.0pt]
T_{1}W,&i=n.\end{cases}

When
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
and
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
is a representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, we call
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
a
Fock representation
. If, in addition,
W
is an isometry (respectively, a unitary or a contraction), then
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
is called an
isometric
(respectively,
unitary
or
contractive
) Fock representation.

It was shown in
[
19
, Theorem 2.2]
that
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
is a Fock representation if and only if there exists
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})

such that

W=W_{L},

where
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
is given by

W_{L}(\Omega\otimes\eta)=L\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E},

and for
e_{\mu}=e_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{m}}\in F_{n}^{+},\,\mu\neq\emptyset,

define

(2.1)

W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes\eta)=\begin{cases}e_{\mu_{1}+1}\otimes e_{\mu_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{m}}\otimes\eta,&\mu_{1}\neq n,\\[6.0pt]
e_{1}\otimes e_{\mu_{2}+1}\otimes e_{\mu_{3}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{m}}\otimes\eta,&\mu_{1}=n,\ \mu_{2}\neq n,\\[6.0pt]
e_{1}^{2}\otimes e_{\mu_{3}+1}\otimes e_{\mu_{4}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{m}}\otimes\eta,&\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=n,\ \mu_{3}\neq n,\\[6.0pt]
\vdots\\[4.0pt]
e_{1}^{m}\otimes L\eta,&\mu_{1}=\cdots=\mu_{m}=n.\end{cases}

Moreover, the symbol
L
is uniquely determined, and
\|L\|\leq\|W_{L}\|\leq\|L\|+1.

The operator
W_{L}
is called the
odometer map
with symbol
L
. A notable feature of this construction is that the role of symbols is analogous to that in the theory of noncommutative Toeplitz operators; see
[
33
]
and
[
19
, Remark 2.4]
.

The structure of
W_{L}
was investigated under additional assumptions such as isometry, unitarity, and Nica-covariance in
[
19
]
.
Let
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
be an isometric Fock representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. We say that
(W,S^{\mathcal{E}})
is a Nica-covariant representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
if

W^{*}(S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})=(S_{n}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})W^{*}.

For a symbol
L
, we define the subspace
\mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
as

(2.2)

\mathcal{E}_{L}=\overline{\text{span}}\{e_{1}^{m}\otimes\eta:m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\eta\in\mathcal{E}\}\ominus\overline{\text{span}}\{e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\zeta:p\geq 1,\zeta\in\mathcal{E}\}.

The following structural characterizations were obtained in
[
19
, Theorem 4.4]
. For convenience, we recall the relevant result below.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a Hilbert space, and let
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
. Then

(1)

(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
is an isometric representation if and only if
L
is an isometry and
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}
.

(2)

An isometric Fock representation
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
is unitary if and only if

L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
.

(3)

For an isometric symbol
L
, the map
W_{L}
is unitary if and only if
L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
.

A central role in the sequel will be played by an upper triangular block decomposition of the odometer map
W_{L}
.
We shall use compactness in the analysis of the off-diagonal block. An operator
K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is compact if, whenever
x_{n}\to 0
weakly in
\mathcal{H}
, one has
\|Kx_{n}\|\to 0.

The ideal of compact operators on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
. We then apply the preceding block decomposition of
W_{L}
to investigate factorization properties of odometer maps using Douglas’ range inclusion theorem (cf.
[
13
]
).

Theorem 2.2

(Douglas’ lemma)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{3})
and

B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3})
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathrm{ran}A\subseteq\mathrm{ran}B
;

(2)

there exists
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
such that

A=BC;

(3)

there exists
\lambda>0
such that

AA^{*}\leq\lambda BB^{*}.

We also compute the adjoint of the odometer map.
If
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
, then
A^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
is determined by

\langle Ax,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\langle x,A^{*}y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}},\qquad x\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\ y\in\mathcal{H}_{2}.

We then use the explicit formula for
W_{L}^{*}
to derive several
operator-theoretic consequences of the odometer map. The first consequence is a description of the kernel of
W_{L}^{*}
. Recall that, for a bounded operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
(\mathrm{ran}T)^{\perp}=\ker T^{*}.

Theorem 2.3

(von Neumann–Wold decomposition)
.

Let
V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an isometry. Then there exist orthogonal reducing subspaces
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{s}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{u}
,
where

\mathcal{H}_{s}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}V^{k}(\ker V^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{u}=\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}V^{k}\mathcal{H},

such that

(1)

\left.V\right|_{\mathcal{H}_{s}}
is a pure isometry;

(2)

\left.V\right|_{\mathcal{H}_{u}}
is unitary.

The space
\ker V^{*}
is the wandering subspace for the shift part of
V
.
We define the multiplicity of the unilateral shift part in the Wold
decomposition of
V
by

\operatorname{mult}(V):=\dim\ker V^{*}.

We shall also use the following standard Fredholm terminology
(cf.
[
2
]
). An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called

upper semi-Fredholm
if
\dim\ker T<\infty
and
\mathrm{ran}T\text{ is closed}.

It is called
lower semi-Fredholm
if
\dim\ker T^{*}<\infty
and
\mathrm{ran}T\text{ is closed}.

The operator
T
is called
semi-Fredholm
if it is either upper
semi-Fredholm or lower semi-Fredholm.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
Fredholm
if

\dim\ker T<\infty,\qquad\mathrm{ran}T\text{ is closed},\qquad\dim\ker T^{*}<\infty.

In this case, the Fredholm index of
T
is defined by

\operatorname{ind}(T)=\dim\ker T-\dim\ker T^{*}.

We shall use the fact that the Fredholm index is locally constant on the
semi-Fredholm set.

We shall also recall the notion of essential normality and the essential spectrum. An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be essentially normal if its self-commutator is compact, that is,

T^{*}T-TT^{*}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}).

For an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the essential spectrum is defined by

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:T-\lambda I\text{ is not Fredholm}\}.

Equivalently,
\lambda\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)
precisely when
T-\lambda I
is Fredholm. We shall combine this with the local constancy of the Fredholm index on the semi-Fredholm set to obtain a Coburn-type spectral result for odometer maps.

Finally, we shall use the following elementary characterization of hyponormality. An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
hyponormal
if
T^{*}T\geq TT^{*},
or equivalently,

\|T^{*}x\|\leq\|Tx\|,\qquad x\in\mathcal{H}.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
subnormal
if there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{H}
and a normal operator
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that
\mathcal{H}
is invariant for
N
and

T=\left.N\right|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Every subnormal operator is hyponormal. Therefore, any obstruction to hyponormality is also an obstruction to subnormality.

3.
Block decomposition and Hardy space realization

Throughout the paper,
\mathcal{E}
denotes a Hilbert space. For
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
denote the corresponding odometer map as defined by (
2.1
). Let
\{e_{\mu}:\mu\in F_{n}^{+}\}
be the canonical orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\mu\in F_{n}^{+}:\ |\mu|\geq 1\text{ and }\mu_{i}\neq 1\text{ for some }i\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\mu\in F_{n}^{+}:\ |\mu|\geq 1\text{ and }\mu_{i}\neq n\text{ for some }i\}.

We define

(3.1)

\mathcal{M}=\overline{\rm span}\{e_{\mu}\otimes\xi:\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{0},\,\xi\in\mathcal{E}\},\quad\mathcal{N}=\overline{\rm span}\{e_{\mu}\otimes\xi:\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0},\,\xi\in\mathcal{E}\}.

It follows immediately that

(3.2)

\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\overline{\rm span}\{e_{1}^{m}\otimes\xi:m\geq 0,\,\xi\in\mathcal{E}\},\quad\mathcal{N}^{\perp}=\overline{\rm span}\{e_{n}^{m}\otimes\xi:m\geq 0,\,\xi\in\mathcal{E}\},

and hence we obtain the orthogonal decompositions

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}.

Let
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Let
k
be the smallest index such that
\gamma_{k}\neq 1
, and set

e_{\gamma-1}=e_{n}^{k-1}\otimes e_{\gamma_{k}-1}\otimes e_{\gamma_{k+1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\gamma_{m}}.

For
\mu=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{m}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
, let
k
be the smallest index such that
\mu_{k}\neq n
, and set

(3.3)

e_{\mu+1}=e_{1}^{k-1}\otimes e_{\mu_{k}+1}\otimes e_{\mu_{k+1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{m}}.

Equivalently, for every
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
,

W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes\xi)=e_{\mu+1}\otimes\xi.

Note that the odometer map satisfies
W_{L}(\mathcal{N})=\mathcal{M}.

Let

W_{11}:=\left.W_{L}\right|_{\mathcal{N}},\quad W_{12}:=\left.P_{\mathcal{M}}W_{L}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}},\quad W_{22}:=\left.P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}W_{L}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}.

Then, with respect to the orthogonal decompositions
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, the operator
W_{L}:\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}

admits the following block matrix representation:

(3.4)

W_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
0&W_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

This block decomposition of
W_{L}
provides a convenient framework for the analysis of its geometric and operator-theoretic properties.
Much of the analysis in the subsequent sections will rely on this decomposition and the interaction between the blocks
W_{11}
,
W_{12}
, and
W_{22}
.

Observe that the restriction
W_{11}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{M}
is unitary. Since

\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}
are infinite-dimensional whenever
n\geq 2
and

\mathcal{E}\neq\{0\}
, it follows that
W_{L}
cannot be compact for
n\geq 2
.
Thus, the only remaining case in which compactness can occur is
n=1
.
The following proposition gives equivalent criteria for compactness in this case.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{E}\neq\{0\}
and
n=1
. Then an odometer map

W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
is compact if and only if

W_{L}=0
.

Proof.

If
W_{L}=0
, then
W_{L}
is trivially compact.
Conversely, assume that
W_{L}
is compact. Since
n=1
, we have

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}=\{0\}
.
Thus
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}^{\perp}.

Suppose
L\neq 0
. Then there exists

\eta\in\mathcal{E}
such that
L\eta\neq 0
. For
p\geq 0
, set

y_{p}=e_{1}^{p}\otimes\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}.

The vectors
\{y_{p}\}_{p\geq 0}
are mutually orthogonal, and hence

y_{p}\to 0
weakly as
p\to\infty
. Since
W_{L}
is compact, it follows
that
\|W_{L}y_{p}\|\to 0.

On the other hand, by the definition of the odometer map for
n=1
,
W_{L}y_{p}=(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta.

Since
S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}}
is an isometry, we obtain

\|W_{L}y_{p}\|=\|L\eta\|>0,\qquad p\geq 0.

This contradicts
\|W_{L}y_{p}\|\to 0
.
Therefore
L=0
, and hence
W_{L}=0.

∎

Since
W_{11}
is unitary, the non-unitary features of
W_{L}
are governed by the remaining blocks

W_{12}
and
W_{22}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be an odometer map with block decomposition (
3.4
). Then

W_{12}=\left.W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}}L)}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}},\qquad W_{22}=\left.W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L)}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}.

Proof.

It suffices to verify the identities on the dense spanning set
\{e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta:p\geq 0,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}

of
\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
. Let
p\geq 0
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
. By definition of the odometer map,
W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\eta.

Hence

P_{\mathcal{M}}W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=P_{\mathcal{M}}(e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\eta)=e_{1}^{p}\otimes P_{\mathcal{M}}L\eta=W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}}L)}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta).

Therefore,
W_{12}=\left.W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}}L)}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}.

A similar computation yields
W_{22}=\left.W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L)}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}.

∎

We next investigate the compactness properties of the off-diagonal block
W_{12}
. The following proposition shows that compactness of
W_{12}
is highly restrictive and, in fact, forces
W_{12}
to vanish identically.

Proposition 3.3
.

Consider the off-diagonal block
W_{12}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}
appearing in the block decomposition (
3.4
). Then

W_{12}\text{ is compact}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W_{12}=0.

Proof.

If
n=1,
then
W_{12}=0
. Therefore let
n\geq 2
. Assume that
W_{12}
is compact. Fix
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
and consider the sequence
y_{p}:=e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp},\,p\geq 0.

The vectors
\{y_{p}\}_{p\geq 0}
are orthogonal, and hence
y_{p}\to 0
weakly as
p\to\infty
. Compactness of
W_{12}
implies
\|W_{12}y_{p}\|\to 0.
Notice that

W_{12}y_{p}=W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}}L)}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=e_{1}^{p}\otimes P_{\mathcal{M}}L\eta=(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})P_{\mathcal{M}}L\eta.

Since
S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}}
is an isometry for each
p
, we obtain

\lim_{p\to\infty}\|W_{12}y_{p}\|=\|P_{\mathcal{M}}L\eta\|=0.

As
\eta
was arbitrary,
P_{\mathcal{M}}L=0
, that is,
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
.
Then clearly
W_{12}=\left.W_{(P_{\mathcal{M}}L)}\right|_{\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}=0.
The converse direction is immediate, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

The operator
W_{12}
is either zero or has infinite rank.

Proof.

If
W_{12}
has finite rank, then it is compact. By Proposition
3.3
, this forces
W_{12}=0.

Therefore, if
W_{12}\neq 0
, it must have infinite rank.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
(for example, when
W_{12}
is compact or when
W_{L}
is an isometry), then
W_{12}=0
. Consequently, the decomposition (
3.4
) reduces to the block-diagonal form
W_{L}=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}.

We now turn to the analysis of the second diagonal block
W_{22}
. To this end, consider the canonical unitaries
U_{1}:\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}),
and
U_{n}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}),

defined by

U_{1}(e_{1}^{p}\otimes\eta)=z^{p}\eta=U_{n}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta),\qquad p\geq 0,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Theorem 3.6
.

For
r\geq 0
, let

L_{r}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
be defined by
L_{r}:=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L.

Then

U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}=M_{\Theta}\quad\text{on }H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}),

where
M_{\Theta}
is the analytic Toeplitz operator with uniquely determined
symbol
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
given by

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{r}.

Proof.

For
p\geq 0
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,

U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}(z^{p}\eta)=U_{1}W_{22}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=U_{1}(e_{1}^{p}\otimes P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L\eta).

Therefore,

M_{z}(U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*})(z^{p}\eta)=U_{1}(e_{1}^{p+1}\otimes P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L\eta)=U_{1}W_{22}(e_{n}^{p+1}\otimes\eta)=(U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*})M_{z}(z^{p}\eta).

Since vector-valued polynomials are dense in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
, it follows that
U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}\in\{M_{z}\}^{\prime}.

Therefore, there exists a unique
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})

such that

U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}=M_{\Theta}.

To identify the symbol
\Theta
, fix
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
and consider the constant function
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}),
defined by
f(z)\equiv\eta.

As
L_{r}\eta
is the coefficient of
e_{1}^{r}
in the Fourier expansion of
L\eta
, we can write

(3.5)

P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L\eta=\sum_{r\geq 0}e_{1}^{r}\otimes L_{r}\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Therefore,

\Theta(z)\eta=(M_{\Theta}f)(z)=(U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}f)(z)=U_{1}(P_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}L\eta)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{r}\eta.

Hence
\Theta(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{r}.

∎

Definition 3.7
.

In view of Theorem
3.6
, we call
W_{22}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp}

the
Toeplitz-type operator
associated with
W_{L}
, or simply the

Toeplitz-type operator
when
W_{L}
is clear from the context.

Henceforth, we
identify
U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}
with the multiplication operator
M_{\Theta}
and call

\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
the
analytic symbol
associated
with
W_{L}
.

Let
V:\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
be the canonical unitary defined by
V(e_{1}^{p}\otimes\eta)=e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta,\,p\geq 0,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}.
Then

W_{22}V=U_{1}^{*}M_{\Theta}U_{1}.

The operator
W_{22}V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M}^{\perp})
is called the
Toeplitz-type realization
of
W_{L}
.

Combining the preceding results, we arrive at the first fundamental result: the structural decomposition of the odometer map.

Theorem 3.8
.

With respect to the orthogonal decompositions
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp},

the odometer map
W_{L}
admits the block decomposition

W_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
0&W_{22}\end{pmatrix},

where

(1)

W_{11}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{M}
is unitary;

(2)

W_{12}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}
is either zero or has infinite rank;

(3)

W_{22}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
is the Toeplitz-type operator
associated with
W_{L}
, and its Toeplitz-type realization is unitarily
equivalent to the analytic Toeplitz operator
M_{\Theta}
on

H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
,
where
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
is uniquely determined by
L
:

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{r},\qquad L_{r}=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L.

In Section
5
, we present several examples in which the associated analytic symbol is computed explicitly from a given symbol
L
, using Theorems
3.6
and
3.8
.

4.
Applications of the Block Decomposition

In this section, we apply the block decomposition from
Theorem
3.8
to investigate the operator-theoretic
properties of the odometer map
W_{L}
.

4.1.
Factorization of odometer maps

The block matrix structure of the odometer map enables a precise transfer of factorization properties from the level of defining symbols to the full Fock space. In particular, the following result applies Douglas’ lemma
[
13
]
to characterize when one odometer map factors through another in terms of their defining symbols.
First, assume that
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
. By Douglas’ lemma

[
13
]
, there exists an operator
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
such that

L_{1}=L_{2}C.

We fix such an operator
C
throughout this section, and
show that this factorization at the level of symbols is
equivalent to a corresponding factorization of the odometer maps.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
. Then
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
if and only if there exists a bounded operator
\Gamma\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})

satisfying

\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\subseteq\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E},\text{ and }W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma.

Further, if
W_{L_{2}}
is an isometry, then
W_{L_{1}}
is an isometry if and only if
\Gamma
is an isometry.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
.
Define
\Gamma_{C}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
by

\Gamma_{C}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=e_{n}^{p}\otimes C\eta,\qquad p\geq 0,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Since
\mathcal{N}^{\perp}=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\mathcal{E})
,
\Gamma_{C}
is a bounded operator with
\|\Gamma_{C}\|=\|C\|
. With respect to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp},
we define

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\mathcal{N}}&0\\
0&\Gamma_{C}\end{pmatrix}.

By construction,
\Gamma
is bounded with norm
\|\Gamma\|=\max\{1,\|C\|\}
. For any
\eta\in\mathcal{E},\,\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\eta)=\Omega\otimes C\eta\in\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
implies

\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\subseteq\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

We now verify the factorization
W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma
. For any
x\in\mathcal{N}
, identifying
x
with
x\oplus 0
, we have
\Gamma x=x
. Because the action of any odometer map on the space
\mathcal{N}
is completely independent of the symbol, we obtain

W_{L_{1}}x=W_{L_{2}}x=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma x,\qquad x\in\mathcal{N}.

Now let
e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
. Then

W_{L_{2}}\Gamma(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=W_{L_{2}}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes C\eta)=e_{1}^{p}\otimes L_{2}C\eta=e_{1}^{p}\otimes L_{1}\eta=W_{L_{1}}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta).

Since the operators agree on both
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
, we conclude that
W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma
.

Conversely, let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be such that
\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\subseteq\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
and
W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma
. Because the closed subspace
\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
is naturally isomorphic to
\mathcal{E}
, the restriction of
\Gamma
to this subspace defines a unique bounded operator
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
such that

\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\eta)=\Omega\otimes C\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Now

L_{1}\eta=W_{L_{1}}(\Omega\otimes\eta)=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma(\Omega\otimes\eta)=W_{L_{2}}(\Omega\otimes C\eta)=L_{2}C\eta.

Therefore,
L_{1}=L_{2}C
, and hence
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
.

Finally, assume that
W_{L_{2}}
is an isometry. Since
W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma
, we have

W_{L_{1}}^{*}W_{L_{1}}=\Gamma^{*}W_{L_{2}}^{*}W_{L_{2}}\Gamma=\Gamma^{*}\Gamma.

Therefore,
W_{L_{1}}^{*}W_{L_{1}}=I
if and only if
\Gamma^{*}\Gamma=I
.
∎

The preceding factorization theorem also has a natural Hardy space
interpretation. In the theory of vector-valued Hardy spaces, factorization problems for operator-valued analytic functions, particularly Schur-class and inner
functions, form a highly nontrivial part of the subject. Such factorization problems are closely related to Leech’s theorem

[
22
]
and to range inclusion problems for Toeplitz and Hankel operators on vector-valued Hardy spaces
[
3
]
. They also arise naturally in the Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş dilation-theoretic framework; see
[
26
, Chapter 5]
.

Under the geometric assumptions
L_{i}\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, the
Douglas-type factorization of odometer maps provides a bridge between
Fock-space operator theory and classical Hardy space multiplier factorization.
The following corollary shows that range inclusion for Fock-space symbols
gives rise to analytic factorization of the corresponding Hardy space
multipliers.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
L_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
for
i=1,2
be bounded symbols satisfying
L_{i}\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and let
\Theta_{i}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
be their associated analytic symbols.
If
\mathrm{ran}L_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{ran}L_{2}
, then there exists
\Theta_{3}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\Theta_{1}(z)=\Theta_{2}(z)\Theta_{3}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

where
\Theta_{3}
is a constant operator-valued function given by
\Theta_{3}(z)\equiv C
.

Proof.

Let
W_{L_{i}}
admit the block decomposition:

W_{L_{i}}=\begin{pmatrix}W^{(i)}_{11}&W^{(i)}_{12}\\
0&W^{(i)}_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad i=1,2.

Since
L_{i}\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, we have
W_{12}^{(1)}=W_{12}^{(2)}=0
. Moreover, the block
W_{11}^{(i)}
is independent of the symbol. Hence, setting
W_{11}:=W_{11}^{(1)}=W_{11}^{(2)},
we obtain
W_{L_{i}}=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}^{(i)}
for
i=1,2.

By Theorem
4.1
,
W_{L_{1}}=W_{L_{2}}\Gamma
, where
\Gamma=I_{\mathcal{N}}\oplus\Gamma_{C}
. Therefore,

W_{11}\oplus W_{22}^{(1)}=(W_{11}\oplus W_{22}^{(2)})(I_{\mathcal{N}}\oplus\Gamma_{C})=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}^{(2)}\Gamma_{C},

and hence,
W_{22}^{(1)}=W_{22}^{(2)}\Gamma_{C}.

By Theorem
3.6
,
M_{\Theta_{i}}=U_{1}W_{22}^{(i)}U_{n}^{*}
for
i=1,2.

Hence,

M_{\Theta_{1}}=M_{\Theta_{2}}(U_{n}\Gamma_{C}U_{n}^{*}).

Consider
U_{n}\Gamma_{C}U_{n}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}))
. For
p\geq 0
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,

U_{n}\Gamma_{C}U_{n}^{*}(z^{p}\eta)=U_{n}\Gamma_{C}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=U_{n}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes C\eta)=z^{p}C\eta.

Therefore,
U_{n}\Gamma_{C}U_{n}^{*}=I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C
. Notice that

\displaystyle(I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C)M_{z}(z^{p}\eta)=z^{p+1}C\eta=M_{z}(I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C)(z^{p}\eta).

Since the operators coincide on a dense subset, it follows that
(I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C)M_{z}=M_{z}(I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C)
. Hence there exists a unique symbol
\Theta_{3}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
such that
I_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\otimes C=M_{\Theta_{3}}
. Therefore
M_{\Theta_{1}}=M_{\Theta_{2}}M_{\Theta_{3}},

completing the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The conclusion of Corollary
4.2

also follows directly.
We get

L_{1,r}=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L_{1}=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L_{2}C=L_{2,r}C,\qquad r\geq 0.

Therefore, by Theorem
3.6
,

\Theta_{1}(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{1,r}=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}L_{2,r}C=\Theta_{2}(z)C,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

4.2.
Characterizations

Now, using the block decomposition (
3.4
), we obtain unified characterizations of odometer maps that are isometric,
unitary, or invertible in terms of the associated analytic symbol,
thereby refining
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})

and let
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})

be the analytic symbol associated with
W_{L}.

Then

(1)

W_{L}
is an isometry if and only if
L(\mathcal{E})\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and
\Theta
is inner.

(2)

W_{L}
is unitary if and only if
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}

and
\Theta
is a constant unitary operator-valued function.

(3)

W_{L}
is invertible if and only if
\Theta
is invertible in

H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

(1)

Suppose that
W_{L}
is an isometry. Pick
\eta,\gamma\in\mathcal{E}
, and let
e_{\mu}\otimes\gamma\in\mathcal{M}
. By the definition of the odometer map, there exists a unique preimage
e_{\mu-1}\otimes\gamma\in\mathcal{N}
such that
W_{L}(e_{\mu-1}\otimes\gamma)=e_{\mu}\otimes\gamma
.
Since
W_{L}
is an isometry, we have

\langle L\eta,\,e_{\mu}\otimes\gamma\rangle=\langle W_{L}(\Omega\otimes\eta),\,W_{L}(e_{\mu-1}\otimes\gamma)\rangle=\langle\Omega\otimes\eta,\,e_{\mu-1}\otimes\gamma\rangle=0,

where the final equality holds because
e_{\mu-1}\otimes\gamma\in\mathcal{N}
, and hence is orthogonal to the vacuum space. Hence,
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and
W_{12}=0
. Therefore,
W_{L}=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}
. Since
W_{11}
is unitary, it follows that
W_{22}
is an isometry. As
U_{1}
and
U_{n}
are unitaries, Theorem
3.6
implies

M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}=U_{n}W_{22}^{*}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}=I_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})},

which shows that
M_{\Theta}
is an isometry. Equivalently,
\Theta
is inner.

Conversely, if
L(\mathcal{E})\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
then
W_{12}=0.
Since
\Theta
is inner, the Toeplitz operator
M_{\Theta}
is an isometry. Therefore,
W_{22}=U_{1}^{*}M_{\Theta}U_{n}
is an isometry. Because
W_{11}
is unitary, the diagonal block operator
W_{L}=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}
is an isometry.

(2)

By (1) above
W_{L}
is unitary if
and only if
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
and
M_{\Theta}
is unitary, which holds if and only if
\Theta
is
a constant unitary operator-valued function.

(3)

Since
M_{\Theta}=U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*},
we have
W_{22}\text{ is invertible}
if and only if
M_{\Theta}\text{ is invertible}.

Equivalently,
\Theta
is invertible in

H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
, that is,

\Theta^{-1}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
.
Assume first that
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
is invertible. Define
X:\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
by

X:=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}^{*}&-\,W_{11}^{*}W_{12}W_{22}^{-1}\\
0&W_{22}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
W_{11}
is unitary, a direct computation gives

W_{L}X=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}&W_{12}\\
0&W_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}^{*}&-\,W_{11}^{*}W_{12}W_{22}^{-1}\\
0&W_{22}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\mathcal{M}}&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}\end{pmatrix}.

That is,
W_{L}X=I_{\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}
. A similar computation implies
XW_{L}=I_{\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}}.

Thus
X=W_{L}^{-1}
, and hence
W_{L}
is invertible.

Conversely, assume that
W_{L}
is invertible. We prove that
W_{22}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
is both surjective and injective.
First, let
y\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
. Since
W_{L}
is onto, there exists
u\oplus v\in\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
such that
W_{L}(u\oplus v)=0\oplus y.

Equivalently,

W_{11}u+W_{12}v=0,\qquad W_{22}v=y.

Hence
y\in\mathrm{ran}W_{22}
, so
W_{22}
is surjective.
Next, let
v\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
satisfy
W_{22}v=0
. Since
W_{11}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{M}
is unitary, there exists
u\in\mathcal{N}
such that
W_{11}u=-\,W_{12}v.

Then

W_{L}(u\oplus v)=\big(W_{11}u+W_{12}v\big)\oplus W_{22}v=0\oplus 0.

As
W_{L}
is injective, it follows that
u=0
and
v=0
. Hence
\ker W_{22}=\{0\}
. Since
W_{22}:\mathcal{N}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
is a bounded bijection between Hilbert spaces, the bounded inverse theorem implies that
W_{22}^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M}^{\perp},\mathcal{N}^{\perp})
.
Consequently
\Theta
is invertible.

∎

The following example shows that invertibility of
W_{L}
does not force the
associated analytic symbol
\Theta
to be a constant unitary
operator-valued function, in contrast to the unitary case.

Example 4.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
and fix a scalar
a\in\mathbb{C}
with
0<|a|<1
. Define

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
by

L\eta=\Omega\otimes\eta+a\,e_{1}\otimes\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

The associated coefficient operators are

L_{0}=I_{\mathcal{E}},\qquad L_{1}=aI_{\mathcal{E}},\qquad L_{r}=0\quad(r\geq 2).

Hence the associated analytic symbol is

\Theta(z)=I_{\mathcal{E}}+azI_{\mathcal{E}}=(1+az)I_{\mathcal{E}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Since
0<|a|<1
, the scalar function
1+az
has no zeros in
\mathbb{D}
, and
\frac{1}{1+az}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Therefore

\Theta^{-1}(z)=\frac{1}{1+az}I_{\mathcal{E}}\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D}).

Thus
\Theta
is invertible in

H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
. By
Theorem
4.4
,
W_{L}
is invertible.

On the other hand,
\Theta
is not a constant unitary operator-valued
function, since
a\neq 0
. Hence
W_{L}
is not unitary. Indeed, for any nonzero

\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,

\|W_{L}(\Omega\otimes\eta)\|^{2}=\|L\eta\|^{2}=\|\Omega\otimes\eta+a\,e_{1}\otimes\eta\|^{2}=(1+|a|^{2})\|\eta\|^{2}>\|\eta\|^{2}.

Thus
W_{L}
is not an isometry, and consequently
W_{L}
is not unitary.

4.3.
Norm of the Odometer Map

We compute the norm of
W_{L}
. For an arbitrary bounded symbol
L
, it is known that
\|L\|\leq\|W_{L}\|\leq\|L\|+1

(cf.
[
19
, Remark 2.4]
). Although these inequalities provide useful general bounds, they do not yield an exact norm formula. We show that, under the additional assumption
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp},

the preceding theorem yields an exact norm formula. Let
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}
denote the norm of
\Theta
in
H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be the odometer map with
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and let
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})

be the associated analytic symbol. Then

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\}.

Proof.

Since
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
,
W_{12}=0.

Hence the block decomposition (
3.4
) reduces to
W_{L}=W_{11}\oplus W_{22}
,
and therefore

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{\|W_{11}\|,\|W_{22}\|\}.

Since
M_{\Theta}=U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}
and
U_{1},U_{n}
are unitaries, we have

\|W_{22}\|=\|M_{\Theta}\|=\|\Theta\|_{\infty}.

Since
W_{11}
is unitary, we obtain

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\}.

∎

Notice that, if
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, then for every
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,
L\eta=W_{L}(\Omega\otimes\eta)=W_{22}(\Omega\otimes\eta).

Consequently,
\|L\eta\|\leq\|W_{22}\|\,\|\eta\|,

and hence Proposition
4.6
implies

\|L\|\leq\|W_{22}\|=\|\Theta\|_{\infty}.

Under the stronger geometric hypothesis
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L},
we prove that
\|L\|=\|\Theta\|_{\infty},

and therefore the norm of
W_{L}
is completely determined by the norm of
L
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
satisfy
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}
.
Then

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|L\|\}.

Proof.

Let
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
denote the analytic symbol associated with
W_{L}
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, Proposition
4.6
implies
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}=\|W_{22}\|
. Thus, it suffices to prove that
\|W_{22}\|=\|L\|
. We already know
\|L\|\leq\|W_{22}\|
. For the reverse inequality, let
f=\sum_{p=0}^{N}e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta_{p}\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
be a finite sum, where
\eta_{p}\in\mathcal{E}
. Then
W_{22}f=\sum_{p=0}^{N}e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\eta_{p}.

As
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}
, we get
L\mathcal{E}\perp(S_{1}^{k}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\mathcal{E}
for all
k\geq 1
. Since
S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}}
is an isometry, it follows that

\|W_{22}f\|^{2}=\sum_{p=0}^{N}\|e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\eta_{p}\|^{2}=\sum_{p=0}^{N}\|L\eta_{p}\|^{2}\leq\|L\|^{2}\sum_{p=0}^{N}\|\eta_{p}\|^{2}=\|L\|^{2}\|f\|^{2}.

Since finite sums of this form are dense in
\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
, this estimate extends by continuity to all of
\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
, establishing that
\|W_{22}\|\leq\|L\|
, and hence
\|W_{22}\|=\|L\|
.
∎

The following example shows that the assumption

L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}
in Proposition
4.7

cannot be weakened to the condition

L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
alone in order to obtain
\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|L\|\}.

Example 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{C}
, and define
L:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}
by

L(1)=\Omega+e_{1}.

Then
\|L\|=\|\Omega+e_{1}\|=\sqrt{2}.

The associated analytic symbol is

\Theta(z)=1+z,

and hence
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}=\sup_{|z|<1}|1+z|=2.

Therefore, by Proposition
4.6
,

\|W_{L}\|=\max\{1,\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\}=2\neq\max\{1,\|L\|\}=\sqrt{2}.

Remark 4.9
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be an odometer map satisfying

L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and let
\Theta
be its associated analytic
symbol. Then the spectral radius of
W_{L}
satisfies
r(W_{L})\leq\max\{1,\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\}.

In particular, if
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, the exact norm evaluates to
\|W_{L}\|=1
, making
W_{L}
a contraction. Consequently,
W_{L}
admits a unitary dilation in a larger Hilbert space (cf.
[
26
,
28
,
30
]
). Moreover, by von Neumann’s inequality,

\|p(W_{L})\|\leq\sup_{|z|\leq 1}|p(z)|

for every polynomial
p
. Thus
W_{L}
admits a contractive functional calculus for the disk algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
. If, in addition,
W_{L}
is absolutely continuous in the sense of Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş, then this calculus extends to the usual

H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
-functional calculus; namely, for every
f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
,

f(W_{L})=\operatorname{SOT}\!-\!\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f_{r}(W_{L}),\qquad f_{r}(z)=f(rz).

5.
Examples

In this section, we present a collection of examples illustrating the
structural decomposition in Theorem
3.8
and the
characterization results in Theorem
4.4
.
These examples show how different choices of the symbol
L
give rise to
explicit analytic symbols
\Theta
associated with
W_{L}
. Recall that, for
r\geq 0
,
L_{r}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
is defined by
L_{r}:=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L.

Example 5.1
.

Let

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
be defined by

L(\eta)=e_{1}\otimes\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Then

L_{1}=I_{\mathcal{E}},
and
L_{r}=0,\,\forall r\neq 1.

Therefore,

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{r}L_{r}=zI_{\mathcal{E}},\qquad\text{and }\qquad M_{\Theta}=M_{z}\quad\text{on }H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}).

We verify directly that
U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}=M_{z}.
For
p\geq 0
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
, consider
e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta\in\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
. Then

U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}(z^{p}\eta)=U_{1}W_{22}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=U_{1}(e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{1}\otimes\eta)=z^{p+1}\eta=M_{z}(z^{p}\eta).

Since the linear span of
\{z^{p}\eta:p\geq 0,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}
is dense in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
,
we get the required equality. Since
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
and
\Theta(z)=zI_{\mathcal{E}}
is inner,
Theorem
4.4
implies that
W_{L}
is an
isometry.
The constant symbol arises in the special case: If
L(\eta)=\Omega\otimes\eta
, then
\Theta(z)=I_{\mathcal{E}}.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space with orthonormal basis

\{h_{p}\}_{p=0}^{\infty}
.
Define
L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
by

L(h_{p})=e_{1}^{p}\otimes h_{p},\quad p\geq 0.

For each
r\geq 0
,

L_{r}h_{p}=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})(e_{1}^{p}\otimes h_{p})=\delta_{r,p}h_{p}.

Therefore,
L_{r}=P_{r},

where
P_{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}h_{r}
, and

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{r}P_{r}.

Moreover, for each
p\geq 0
,
\Theta(z)h_{p}=z^{p}h_{p}.

Hence

M_{\Theta}(f(z)h_{p})=z^{p}f(z)h_{p},\qquad f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

With respect to the orthogonal decomposition
H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{p=0}^{\infty}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})\otimes h_{p},

we obtain

M_{\Theta}=\bigoplus_{p=0}^{\infty}M_{z^{p}}.

A straightforward computation shows
U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}=M_{\Theta}.

Theorem
2.1
(1) guarantees
W_{L}
is an isometry. Consequently,
\Theta
must be inner by Theorem
4.4
. Indeed, for

\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, the boundary value of
\Theta
is given by
\Theta(\zeta)h_{p}=\zeta^{p}h_{p},\,p\geq 0.

Therefore,
\|\Theta(\zeta)h_{p}\|=\|h_{p}\|,\,p\geq 0,

and hence

\Theta(\zeta)^{*}\Theta(\zeta)=I_{\mathcal{E}},\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Example 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a Hilbert space, and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{D}
. Define the
finite Blaschke product

B(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{z-a_{j}}{1-\overline{a_{j}}z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Write its Taylor expansion at the origin as
B(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}z^{r},\,b_{r}\in\mathbb{C}.

Since
B\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, we have
\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}|b_{r}|^{2}=\|B\|_{H^{2}}^{2}<\infty.
For each
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
, the vectors
\{e_{1}^{r}\otimes\eta:r\geq 0\}
are
mutually orthogonal, and the series

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}e_{1}^{r}\otimes\eta
converges in norm in

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
.
Hence we define

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
by

L\eta=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}\,e_{1}^{r}\otimes\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Moreover,
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
, as
\|L\eta\|^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}|b_{r}|^{2}\|\eta\|^{2}=\|B\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|\eta\|^{2}.

For each
r\geq 0
, the coefficient operators
L_{r}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
are given by

L_{r}=(\langle e_{1}^{r},\cdot\rangle\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L=b_{r}I_{\mathcal{E}}.

Hence, the associated operator-valued symbol is

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{r}L_{r}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}z^{r}I_{\mathcal{E}}=B(z)I_{\mathcal{E}}.

Since
B
is a finite Blaschke product, it is inner. Therefore

\Theta(\zeta)^{*}\Theta(\zeta)=|B(\zeta)|^{2}I_{\mathcal{E}}=I_{\mathcal{E}}\quad\text{for a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Thus
\Theta
is an inner operator-valued function. Clearly
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
. Consequently, by
Theorem
4.4
, the corresponding
odometer map
W_{L}
is an isometry.
This yields a family of explicit isometric odometer maps parameterized by the
zeros
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
of the finite Blaschke product.

Remark 5.4
.

(1)

In the special case
m=1
, the Blaschke product reduces to
B(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z},\,a\in\mathbb{D}.

A direct computation of its Taylor coefficients yields

b_{0}=-a,\qquad b_{r}=(1-|a|^{2})\overline{a}^{\,r-1},\quad r\geq 1.

Define
L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
by

L\eta=-a\,\Omega\otimes\eta+(1-|a|^{2})\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\overline{a}^{\,r-1}\,e_{1}^{r}\otimes\eta.

The associated analytic symbol
\Theta(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z}\,I_{\mathcal{E}}
is inner and hence belongs to the Schur class. The corresponding odometer map
W_{L}
is an isometry.
This provides a concrete illustration of Theorem
4.4
, showing that the isometric nature of
W_{L}
is encoded by the inner Toeplitz symbol.

(2)

Consider Example 7.4 of
[
19
]
, where
\omega=\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2},
and

L:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}
is given by

L(\lambda)=\lambda(\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}c_{p}e_{1}^{p})\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp},

where
c_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}+3}},
and
c_{p}=c_{0}\,\omega^{p-1},\,p\geq 1.

Since
c_{0}=-\omega=1-\omega^{2},

a straightforward computation yields

\Theta(z)=c_{0}+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}c_{0}\omega^{p-1}z^{p}=-\omega+(1-\omega^{2})\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\omega^{p-1}z^{p}=\frac{z-\omega}{1-\omega z}.

Example 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
with its standard orthonormal basis

\{h_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, and let

S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
denote the unilateral shift.
Define a bounded symbol
L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}

by

L\eta=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{r+1}}\,e_{1}^{r}\otimes S^{r}\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Since
S
is an isometry, we have

\|L\eta\|^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{r+1}}\|S^{r}\eta\|^{2}=\left(\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{r+1}}\right)\|\eta\|^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\|\eta\|^{2}.

Hence
L
is bounded.
The coefficient operators are given by

L_{r}=\frac{1}{2^{r+1}}S^{r},\qquad r\geq 0.

Consequently, the associated operator-valued symbol is

\Theta(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}z^{r}L_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{z}{2}S\right)^{r}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\frac{z}{2}S\right)^{-1},

where
\|S\|=1
and
|z|<1
imply the series converges in operator norm.

Thus
\Theta\in H^{\infty}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}(\mathbb{D})
is a
resolvent-type operator-valued analytic function, and the Toeplitz operator

M_{\Theta}
provides an explicit model in which the Hardy-space shift
interacts with the unilateral shift on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
.
However,
\Theta
is not inner. Indeed, for
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, the radial
boundary value satisfies
\Theta(\zeta)h_{1}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{r}}{2^{r+1}}S^{r}h_{1}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{r}}{2^{r+1}}h_{r+1}.

Therefore

\|\Theta(\zeta)h_{1}\|^{2}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{r+1}}=\frac{1}{3}\neq 1=\|h_{1}\|^{2}.

Hence
\Theta(\zeta)^{*}\Theta(\zeta)\neq I_{\mathcal{E}}
.
This provides an example of a Toeplitz operator that is not an isometry. consequently, by
Theorem
4.4
,
W_{L}
is not an isometry.

6.
The adjoint of the odometer map

To analyze the spectral properties and algebraic structure associated with the odometer map, explicit knowledge of the adjoint operator
W_{L}^{*}
is required. While
[
19
, Proposition 4.1]
established an adjoint formula in the
isometric case, we derive here, by a direct inner-product computation, an
explicit formula for the adjoint of an arbitrary bounded odometer map
W_{L}
.
This general formula provides further insight into the structure of bounded
odometer maps. We first introduce some notation that will aid in the computation of the adjoint.

Every
e_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}
can be written uniquely as
e_{\gamma}=e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{\gamma^{\prime}},
where
p\geq 0
and either
|\gamma^{\prime}|=0
or
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}\neq 1
. In this case, we define
\gamma(1):=p
, and for
0\leq m\leq p,
we define

(6.1)

e_{\gamma^{(m)}}:=e_{1}^{p-m}\otimes e_{\gamma^{\prime}}.

Let
\{h_{s}\}_{s\in\Lambda}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{E}
,
where
\Lambda
is finite or countable.
For each
q\in\Lambda
, let

c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}:=\left\langle Lh_{q},e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}\right\rangle,\qquad\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\ s\in\Lambda.

With respect to the orthonormal basis

\{e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}:\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\ s\in\Lambda\}
of

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
, we have the Fourier expansion

(6.2)

Lh_{q}=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\,s\in\Lambda}c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}\,e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}.

We first record the action of
L^{*}
on basis vectors.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
be a bounded linear operator.
Then, for every
\mu\in F_{n}^{+}
and
s\in\Lambda
, we have

L^{*}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s})=\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}}\,h_{q}.

Proof.

For any
q\in\Lambda
, consider the Fourier series expansion (
6.2
) of
Lh_{q}
.
It follows that

\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}}=\langle e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s},Lh_{q}\rangle=\langle L^{*}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}),h_{q}\rangle.

Expanding
L^{*}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s})
with respect to the orthonormal basis
\{h_{q}\}_{q\in\Lambda}
of
\mathcal{E}
, we obtain

L^{*}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s})=\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\langle L^{*}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}),h_{q}\rangle h_{q}=\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}}\,h_{q}.

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be the odometer map with symbol
L
.
Then, for every
\gamma\in F_{n}^{+}
and
l\in\Lambda
, we have

(6.3)

\begin{split}W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l})&=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)\,(e_{\gamma-1}\otimes h_{l})+\sum_{p=0}^{\gamma(1)}\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}\,(e_{n}^{p}\otimes h_{q})\\
&=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)(e_{\gamma-1}\otimes h_{l})+\sum^{\gamma(1)}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{\gamma^{(p)}}\otimes h_{l}),\\
\end{split}

where
\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
denotes the characteristic function of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Proof.

With respect to the orthonormal basis
\{e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q}:\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\,q\in\Lambda\}

of

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
, we have

\displaystyle W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l})=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\,q\in\Lambda}\langle W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l}),e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q}\rangle\,e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q}=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},\,q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q})\rangle\,e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q}.

Using the decomposition

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
,
we get

(6.4)

W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0},q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle e_{\mu}\otimes{h_{q}}+\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}.

We simplify the coefficients in the first summand. Since
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
, it follows from (
3.3
) that

\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes h_{q})\rangle=\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},e_{\mu+1}\otimes h_{q}\rangle=\delta_{\gamma,\mu+1}\delta_{l,q}.

Notice that
\mu+1\in\mathcal{M}_{0}.
Therefore
if
e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l}\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, then the above inner product vanishes. Therefore, the first summand reduces to

\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{0},q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{\mu}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle e_{\mu}\otimes{h_{q}}=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)(e_{\gamma-1}\otimes h_{l}).

To simplify the second summand, we first observe that

\displaystyle W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes h_{q})=e_{1}^{p}\otimes Lh_{q}=e_{1}^{p}\otimes\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},s\in\Lambda}c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},s\in\Lambda}c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}.

We can write
e_{\gamma}=e_{1}^{\gamma(1)}\otimes e_{\gamma^{\prime}}.
If
p>\gamma(1),
then
\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}\rangle=0.
So let
0\leq p\leq\gamma(1).

\displaystyle\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+},s\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,s}}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{\mu}\otimes h_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+}}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,l}}\langle e_{1}^{\gamma(1)}\otimes e_{\gamma^{\prime}},e_{1}^{p}\otimes e_{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in F_{n}^{+}}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\mu,l}}\langle e_{\gamma^{(p)}},e_{\mu}\rangle,\qquad[\text{by }\eqref{eq:e_gamma_power_m}]

\displaystyle=\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq p\leq\gamma(1),q\in\Lambda}\langle e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l},W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}})\rangle e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq p\leq\gamma(1),q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}\,e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}.

Substituting these values in (
6.4
), we get

W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l})=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)(e_{\gamma-1}\otimes h_{l})+\sum_{0\leq p\leq\gamma(1),q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}\,e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}.

Lemma
6.1
implies that

\sum_{0\leq p\leq\gamma(1),q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}\,e_{n}^{p}\otimes{h_{q}}=\sum^{\gamma(1)}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{\gamma^{(p)},l}}\,{h_{q}}=\sum^{\gamma(1)}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{\gamma^{(p)}}\otimes h_{l}).

Therefore,

W_{L}^{*}(e_{\gamma}\otimes h_{l})=\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)(e_{\gamma-1}\otimes h_{l})+\sum^{\gamma(1)}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{\gamma^{(p)}}\otimes h_{l}).

This completes the proof.
∎

We now record two useful consequences of the general adjoint formula.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be the odometer map with symbol
L
.

(1)

For
2\leq k\leq n
,
m\geq 1
, and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,

(6.5)

W_{L}^{*}(e_{k}^{m}\otimes\eta)=e_{k-1}\otimes e_{k}^{(m-1)}\otimes\eta+\Omega\otimes L^{*}(e_{k}^{m}\otimes\eta).

(2)

For any
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp},

W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{p\geq 0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f.

Proof.

(1)

Here
e_{\gamma}=e_{k}^{m}
. Since
k\geq 2
, we have
\chi_{\mathcal{M}_{0}}(\gamma)=1
, so the first summand in (
6.3
) becomes

e_{k-1}\otimes e_{k}^{(m-1)}\otimes\eta
.
For the second summand, since
\gamma(1)=0
, only the term
p=0
occurs.

\sum_{0\leq p\leq\gamma(1)}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{\gamma^{(p)}}\otimes\eta)=\Omega\otimes L^{*}(e_{k}^{m}\otimes\eta),

which completes the proof.

(2)

First suppose that
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
is a finite linear combination, say
f=\sum a_{m,l}\,e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l}.
By Theorem
6.2
, we have

W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{m,l}a_{m,l}\,W_{L}^{*}(e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l})=\sum_{m,l}a_{m,l}\sum_{p=0}^{m}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l}).

Reindexing these finite sums according to
p
, we obtain

W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{p\geq 0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes\left(\sum_{m\geq p}\sum_{l}a_{m,l}\,L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l})\right).

For any
p\geq 0
,

L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=L^{*}\big(\sum_{m\geq p}\sum_{l}a_{m,l}\,e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l}\big)=\sum_{m\geq p}\sum_{l}a_{m,l}\,L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l}).

Therefore

W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{p\geq 0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f.

Since the identity holds on a dense subspace and
W_{L}^{*}
is bounded, the
series on the right converges in norm to
W_{L}^{*}f
for every

f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
.

∎

Using Corollary
6.3
, we recover the adjoint formula from

[
19
, Proposition 4.1]
in the isometric case.
First, in view of the expansion (
6.2
), we adopt the following notation. For
p\geq 0
, we set

c^{h_{q}}_{p,s}:=\langle Lh_{q},\,e_{1}^{p}\otimes h_{s}\rangle.

Corollary 6.4
.

For

L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
,
if the odometer map
W_{L}
is an isometry, then the adjoint of
W_{L}
is given by

W_{L}^{*}f=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{m}\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{m-p,l}}\,(e_{n}^{p}\otimes h_{q})=\sum^{m}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l}),\quad\text{if }f=e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l},\\[5.69054pt]
e_{n}^{m}\otimes e_{\mu_{1}-1}\otimes e_{\mu_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{k}}\otimes h_{l},\quad\text{if }f=e_{1}^{m}\otimes e_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{k}}\otimes h_{l},\,k\geq 1,\text{ and }\mu_{1}>1,\end{cases}

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
l\in\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
f=e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l}
. Since
(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0,\,\forall p\geq m+1,

Corollary
6.3
gives

W_{L}^{*}(e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l})=\sum^{m}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l}).

Lemma
6.1
implies
L^{*}(e_{1}^{m-p}\otimes h_{l})=\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{{m-p},l}}\,h_{q}
, and hence

W_{L}^{*}(e_{1}^{m}\otimes h_{l})=\sum^{m}_{p=0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{{m-p},l}}\,h_{q}=\sum^{m}_{p=0}\sum_{q\in\Lambda}\overline{c^{h_{q}}_{{m-p},l}}\,(e_{n}^{p}\otimes h_{q}).

Next, let
f=e_{1}^{m}\otimes e_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{k}}\otimes h_{l}\text{ with }\mu_{1}>1.

Then
W_{L}(e_{n}^{m}\otimes e_{\mu_{1}-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\mu_{k}}\otimes h_{l})=f,
and as
W_{L}
is an isometry, the
equality follows.
∎

7.
Applications of the adjoint formula

In this section, we use the explicit formula for
W_{L}^{*}
to derive several
operator-theoretic consequences for odometer maps.
We first identify the kernel of
W_{L}^{*}
and relate it to the defect space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
in the isometric case. This leads to consequences for the Wold decomposition,
Fredholmness, essential normality, and a Coburn-type spectral theorem. We end with a necessary condition for hyponormality, showing that this condition is not sufficient in general.

7.1.
Kernel structure and Wold decomposition

The kernel of
W_{L}^{*}
plays a central role in the structural analysis of the odometer map. We compute
\ker W_{L}^{*}
explicitly and show that, in the isometric case, this adjoint kernel precisely measures the orthogonal gap between the space
\mathcal{E}_{L}
and the range of the symbol
L
.
Equivalently, the defect space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
is the wandering
subspace for the shift part in the Wold decomposition of
W_{L}
. Hence its
dimension gives the multiplicity of the shift summand and, in the
Fredholm setting, determines the Fredholm index of
W_{L}
. This parallels classical Toeplitz theory, where the Fredholm index of a
Fredholm Toeplitz operator is encoded by the topological data of its symbol.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be an odometer map with symbol
L
. Then the kernel of its adjoint is given by

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\ker W_{22}^{*}=\{f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}:L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0\text{ for all }p\geq 0\}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\cap(L\mathcal{E})^{\perp}.

Moreover, if
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
is an isometric representation, then

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}.

Proof.

From the block decomposition of
W_{L}
in (
3.4
), the adjoint
W_{L}^{*}:\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{N}^{\perp}

is given by

W_{L}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{11}^{*}&0\\
W_{12}^{*}&W_{22}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
satisfy
W_{L}^{*}(h_{1},h_{2})=0.

Then

W_{11}^{*}h_{1}=0,\qquad W_{12}^{*}h_{1}+W_{22}^{*}h_{2}=0.

Since
W_{11}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{M}
is unitary, its adjoint
W_{11}^{*}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}

is also unitary. Therefore,
h_{1}=0,

which further implies
W_{22}^{*}h_{2}=0.

Thus

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\{(0,h_{2}):h_{2}\in\ker W_{22}^{*}\}.

Identifying
\{0\}\oplus\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
with
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, we obtain

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\ker W_{22}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}.

Now let
f\in\ker W_{L}^{*}
. Since
\ker W_{L}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, Corollary
6.3
yields

0=W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{p\geq 0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f.

As the subspaces
e_{n}^{p}\otimes\mathcal{E}
,
p\geq 0
, are mutually orthogonal, it follows that

L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0\qquad\text{for all }p\geq 0.

Hence

\ker W_{L}^{*}\subseteq\{f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}:L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0\text{ for all }p\geq 0\}.

Conversely, let
f\in\{g\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}:L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})g=0\text{ for all }p\geq 0\}.

Since
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, Corollary
6.3
implies
W_{L}^{*}f=\sum_{p\geq 0}e_{n}^{p}\otimes L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0.

Therefore

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\{f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}:L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f=0\text{ for all }p\geq 0\},

proving the required equality. This is equivalent to

\langle L^{*}(S_{1}^{*p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})f,\zeta\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=\langle f,(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\zeta\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}}=0,\quad\forall\,\zeta\in\mathcal{E},\,p\geq 0.

For
p=0
, this gives
f\perp L\mathcal{E}
. Therefore,
f\in(L\mathcal{E})^{\perp}.

For each
p\geq 1
, it follows that
f\perp(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\mathcal{E}.

Moreover, since
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{L}
in (
2.2
) implies

\ker W_{L}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}\cap(L\mathcal{E})^{\perp}.

Conversely, if
f\in\mathcal{E}_{L}\cap(L\mathcal{E})^{\perp}
, then by definition
f\perp(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\mathcal{E}

for all
p\geq 0.
Therefore, Corollary
6.3
implies
W_{L}^{*}f=0,
and hence

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\cap(L\mathcal{E})^{\perp}.

Finally, suppose
W_{L}
is an isometry. Theorem
2.1
implies
L
is an isometry and
L\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{L}.
Therefore,
L\mathcal{E}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{L}
. Consequently,

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}.

∎

The following example illustrates the preceding proposition by showing that, for an isometric odometer map, the multiplicity of the shift part of
W_{L}
is determined by the dimension of the defect space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
.

Example 7.2
.

Let

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
be given by

L\eta=e_{1}\otimes\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Then, for
p\geq 0
,
W_{L}(e_{n}^{p}\otimes\eta)=e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\eta=e_{1}^{p+1}\otimes\eta.

Hence the range of
W_{L}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
is
\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_{1}^{m}\otimes\eta:m\geq 1,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}.

Then
W_{L}
is an isometry, and

\ker W_{L}^{*}=(\operatorname{Ran}W_{L})^{\perp}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

For
p\geq 1
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta=e_{1}^{p+1}\otimes\eta.

Hence
\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta:p\geq 1,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}=\bigoplus_{j\geq 2}e_{1}^{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}.

Therefore

\mathcal{E}_{L}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\ominus\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta:p\geq 1,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}=(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\oplus(e_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}).

Consequently,

\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}=\big((\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\oplus(e_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E})\big)\ominus(e_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E})=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

Hence,

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E},\text{ and }\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=\dim\mathcal{E}.

Furthermore, using (
2.1
), one checks that
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}W_{L}^{m}(\ker W_{L}^{*}).

Therefore,
W_{L}\cong M_{z}
on
H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
.
Consequently,
\sigma(W_{L})=\sigma(M_{z})=\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

More generally, fix
k\geq 1
, and let

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
be given by
L\eta=e_{1}^{k}\otimes\eta,\,\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Then for
p\geq 1
,
\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta:p\geq 1,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\}=\bigoplus_{j\geq k+1}e_{1}^{j}\otimes\mathcal{E},
and hence

\mathcal{E}_{L}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\ominus\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta:p\geq 1,\eta\in\mathcal{E}\}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k}e_{1}^{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}.

Consequently,

\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}e_{1}^{j}\otimes\mathcal{E},\,\text{ and }\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=k\dim\mathcal{E}.

Again, using (
2.1
), one checks that
W_{L}
is a pure isometry
and is unitarily equivalent to the unilateral shift of multiplicity

k\dim\mathcal{E}
.

We next obtain another characterization of the unitarity of
W_{L}
, which recovers
[
19
, Theorem 4.4]
as a direct consequence of the preceding proposition.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
be an isometric Fock representation of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. Then the
odometer map
W_{L}
is unitary if and only if
L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

Proof.

Assume first that
W_{L}
is unitary. Then
\ker W_{L}^{*}=\{0\}.

By Proposition
7.1
, this is equivalent to
\mathcal{E}_{L}=L\mathcal{E}.
By the definition of

\mathcal{E}_{L}
, we obtain the orthogonal sum

\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=L\mathcal{E}\oplus\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\zeta:\ p\geq 1,\ \zeta\in\mathcal{E}\}=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\mathcal{E},

as for
q>p\geq 0
,
L\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\perp\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{r}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta:\ r\geq 1,\ \eta\in\mathcal{E}\}
implies

\left\langle(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\zeta,(S_{1}^{q}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta\right\rangle=\left\langle L\zeta,(S_{1}^{q-p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\eta\right\rangle=0.

Thus
L\mathcal{E}
is a generating wandering subspace for

\left.S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}}\right|_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}
. On the other hand,
\left.S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}}\right|_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}

is the unilateral shift on
\mathcal{M}^{\perp},

whose wandering subspace is precisely

\ker\left(\left.(S_{1}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})^{*}\right|_{\mathcal{M}^{\perp}}\right)=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

By uniqueness of the wandering subspace in the Wold decomposition, we obtain

L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.

Conversely, suppose that
L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
. Then
\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(S_{1}^{p}\otimes I_{\mathcal{E}})L\mathcal{E}:\ p\geq 1\}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_{1}^{p}\otimes\mathcal{E}:\ p\geq 1\}.

Hence, by the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{L}
,

\mathcal{E}_{L}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\ominus\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_{1}^{p}\otimes\mathcal{E}:\ p\geq 1\}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}=L\mathcal{E}.

Thus
\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}=\{0\}.

Since
W_{L}
is an isometry, it follows that
W_{L}
is unitary.
∎

By Proposition
7.1
, the shift part in the Wold decomposition of an isometric map
W_{L}
on

\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
is given by

\mathcal{H}_{s}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{L}^{k}(\ker W_{L}^{*})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{L}^{k}(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Moreover, since
W_{L}
is an isometry, Theorem
4.4

implies that the associated multiplication operator

M_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D}))
is also an isometry. Its corresponding
pure shift part is therefore

\mathcal{H}_{s}^{\Theta}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{\Theta}^{k}(\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}).

The following result shows that the multiplicity of the shift part of
W_{L}

is encoded by the associated analytic Toeplitz operator
M_{\Theta}
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
be an isometric representation. Then

\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}=U_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}),\,\text{and }\operatorname{mult}(W_{L})=\operatorname{mult}(M_{\Theta}).

Proof.

Theorem
3.6
implies that
M_{\Theta}=U_{1}W_{22}U_{n}^{*}
, and hence
M_{\Theta}^{*}=U_{n}W_{22}^{*}U_{1}^{*}
. Let
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
f\in\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}
if and only if

U_{n}W_{22}^{*}U_{1}^{*}f=0\iff W_{22}^{*}(U_{1}^{*}f)=0\iff U_{1}^{*}f\in\ker W_{22}^{*}.

By Proposition
7.1
, we know
\ker W_{22}^{*}=\ker W_{L}^{*}=\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
, and
U_{1}:\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{D})
is a unitary operator, we obtain

\ker M_{\Theta}^{*}=U_{1}(\ker W_{22}^{*})=U_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Therefore,

\operatorname{mult}(M_{\Theta})=\dim(\ker M_{\Theta}^{*})=\dim\big(U_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})\big)=\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})=\dim(\ker W_{L}^{*})=\operatorname{mult}(W_{L}).

∎

7.2.
Fredholm theory

Corollary
7.3
corresponds to the vanishing of the defect space. More generally, finite-dimensional defect characterizes the Fredholm and essential normality properties of
W_{L}
.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
be an isometric representation. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

W_{L}
is Fredholm;

(2)

W_{L}
is essentially normal;

(3)

\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty
.

In this case,

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\,\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Proof.

We first show that
(2)
and
(3)
are equivalent. Since
W_{L}
is an isometry,

[W_{L}^{*},W_{L}]=W_{L}^{*}W_{L}-W_{L}W_{L}^{*}=I-W_{L}W_{L}^{*}=P_{\ker W_{L}^{*}}.

It follows that
W_{L}
is essentially normal if and only if
P_{\ker W_{L}^{*}}
is compact. Since an orthogonal projection is compact if and only if its range is finite-dimensional, using Proposition
7.1
, we conclude that

W_{L}\text{ is essentially normal }\iff\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty.

This proves the equivalence of
(2)
and
(3)
.

Next, since
W_{L}
is an isometry, we have
\ker W_{L}=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{ran}W_{L}
is closed. Thus
W_{L}
is Fredholm if and only if
\dim(\mathrm{ran}W_{L})^{\perp}=\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}<\infty.
Therefore

W_{L}\text{ is Fredholm }\iff\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty.

This proves the equivalence of
(1)
and
(3)
.

Finally, if these equivalent conditions hold, then the Fredholm index of
W_{L}
is

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=\dim\ker W_{L}-\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=-\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=-\,\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

∎

The following example illustrates that the Fredholmness of the odometer map is completely determined by the dimension of the defect space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}
.

Example 7.6
.

Consider the symbol
L
from Example
7.2
, given by
L\eta=e_{1}\otimes\eta,\,\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

As computed there,
\ker W_{L}^{*}=\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}.
Hence
W_{L}
is Fredholm if and only if
\dim\mathcal{E}<\infty
. But
\dim\mathcal{E}=\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).
Thus this example verifies Proposition
7.5
. Moreover,

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\dim(\ker W_{L}^{*})=-\dim\mathcal{E}.

In particular, if
\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then
W_{L}
is Fredholm
and
\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-m,

the same index as the classical vector-valued unilateral shift of multiplicity

m
on
H^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}(\mathbb{D})
.

The next example exhibits a nontrivial family of isometric odometer maps for which the defect space, Fredholmness, and Fredholm index can be computed explicitly.

Example 7.7
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
be an orthogonal projection, and define

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E},\qquad L\eta=\Omega\otimes(I-P)\eta+e_{1}\otimes P\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Then
L
is an isometry, since for every
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
,

\|L\eta\|^{2}=\|(I-P)\eta\|^{2}+\|P\eta\|^{2}=\|\eta\|^{2}.

We now compute
\mathcal{E}_{L}
.
Since
e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\zeta=e_{1}^{p}\otimes(I-P)\zeta+e_{1}^{(p+1)}\otimes P\zeta,

it follows that

\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_{1}^{p}\otimes L\zeta:\ p\geq 1,\ \zeta\in\mathcal{E}\}=\left(\bigoplus_{m\geq 1}e_{1}^{m}\otimes(I-P)\mathcal{E}\right)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{m\geq 2}e_{1}^{m}\otimes P\mathcal{E}\right).

Therefore, by (
2.2
),
\mathcal{E}_{L}=(\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E})\oplus(e_{1}\otimes P\mathcal{E}),
and by Theorem
2.1
,
(W_{L},S^{\mathcal{E}})
is an isometric representation.
On the other hand,
L\mathcal{E}=(\Omega\otimes(I-P)\mathcal{E})\oplus(e_{1}\otimes P\mathcal{E}).

Hence
\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}=\Omega\otimes P\mathcal{E}.

Consequently,

\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})=\dim(P\mathcal{E})=\mathrm{rank}P.

It now follows from Proposition
7.5
that
W_{L}
is Fredholm if and only if
\mathrm{rank}P<\infty
, and in that case

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\,\mathrm{rank}P.

7.3.
Spectral consequences and a Coburn-type theorem

A classical consequence of Coburn’s theorem states that if
T
is a proper isometric Toeplitz operator of finite multiplicity, then its essential spectrum is the unit circle. Moreover, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, one has (cf.
[
9
]
):

\operatorname{ind}(T-\lambda I)=-\dim\ker T^{*},\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{D}.

Inspired by this classical result, we use our earlier characterization of Fredholmness to establish a similar spectral theorem for odometer maps on the free Fock space.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
W_{L}
be an isometric odometer map and suppose that
\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty.

Then, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, the operator
W_{L}-\lambda I
is Fredholm and

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L}-\lambda I)=-\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=-\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

If, in addition,
W_{L}
is not unitary, then
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(W_{L})=\mathbb{T}.

Proof.

As
\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty,

Proposition
7.5
yields that
W_{L}
is Fredholm with
\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E}).

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Since
W_{L}
is an isometry, the reverse triangle inequality implies

\|(W_{L}-\lambda I)x\|\geq\|W_{L}x\|-|\lambda|\|x\|=(1-|\lambda|)\|x\|.

Since
|\lambda|<1
, it follows that
W_{L}-\lambda I
is bounded below. In particular, its kernel is trivial and its range is closed. Therefore,
W_{L}-\lambda I
is semi-Fredholm for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Now the map

\lambda\mapsto W_{L}-\lambda I

is continuous in the operator norm, and its image lies in the set of semi-Fredholm operators. Since the index is locally constant on the semi-Fredholm set and
\mathbb{D}
is connected, we conclude that

\operatorname{ind}(W_{L}-\lambda I)=\operatorname{ind}(W_{L})=-\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

Since
\ker(W_{L}-\lambda I)=\{0\}
and the index is finite, it follows that the cokernel is finite-dimensional. Hence
W_{L}-\lambda I
is Fredholm for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. This proves the first assertion.

Consequently
\mathbb{D}\cap\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(W_{L})=\varnothing.

Since
W_{L}
is an isometry,
\sigma(W_{L})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Consequently,
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(W_{L})\subseteq\mathbb{T}.

Now assume, in addition, that
W_{L}
is not unitary. Then
W_{L}
is a proper
isometry. By the Wold decomposition,
W_{L}\cong U\oplus S_{d},

where
U
is unitary and
S_{d}
is the unilateral shift of multiplicity

0<d=\dim\ker W_{L}^{*}=\dim(\mathcal{E}_{L}\ominus L\mathcal{E})<\infty.

The essential spectrum of the unilateral shift of finite positive multiplicity
is
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{T}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(S_{d})\subseteq\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(U\oplus S_{d})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(W_{L}).

Combining both inclusions
we obtain

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(W_{L})=\mathbb{T}.

∎

7.4.
Hyponormality

Theorem 7.9
.

Assume
n\geq 2
. Let
W_{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E})
be an odometer map with symbol
L
. If
W_{L}
is a hyponormal operator, then

\|L\eta\|\geq\|\eta\|\quad\text{for all }\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Consequently, if
\|L\|<1
or if
L
has a non-trivial kernel, then

W_{L}
cannot be hyponormal and, consequently, cannot be subnormal.

Proof.

Let
W_{L}
be hyponormal. This requires
\|W_{L}x\|\geq\|W_{L}^{*}x\|
for all
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
. Fix
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
and consider
x=e_{n}\otimes\eta
.
By the definition of the odometer map,
W_{L}(e_{n}\otimes\eta)=e_{1}\otimes L\eta
, and hence

\|W_{L}x\|=\|e_{1}\otimes L\eta\|=\|L\eta\|.

To evaluate the adjoint action on
x
, we apply the explicit formula from Corollary
6.3
(1) with
k=n
and
m=1
:

W_{L}^{*}(e_{n}\otimes\eta)=e_{n-1}\otimes\eta+\Omega\otimes L^{*}(e_{n}\otimes\eta).

Since
n\geq 2
, the vector
e_{n-1}
is a word of length one, and hence

e_{n-1}\otimes\eta\perp\Omega\otimes\mathcal{E}
. Therefore,

\|W_{L}^{*}x\|^{2}=\|e_{n-1}\otimes\eta\|^{2}+\|\Omega\otimes L^{*}(e_{n}\otimes\eta)\|^{2}=\|\eta\|^{2}+\|L^{*}(e_{n}\otimes\eta)\|^{2}.

This implies
\|W_{L}^{*}x\|^{2}\geq\|\eta\|^{2}
. Enforcing the hyponormality condition
\|W_{L}x\|\geq\|W_{L}^{*}x\|
yields
\|L\eta\|\geq\|\eta\|
, completing the proof.
∎

We now show that the lower-bound condition in Theorem
7.9

is necessary but not sufficient for hyponormality.

Example 7.10
.

Assume
n\geq 2
, and fix a unit vector
h\in\mathcal{E}
. Define

L:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}_{n}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{E}
by

L\eta=\Omega\otimes\eta+e_{1}\otimes\eta,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Then

\|L\eta\|=\sqrt{2}\,\|\eta\|\geq\|\eta\|,\qquad\eta\in\mathcal{E}.

Thus
L
satisfies the necessary lower bound condition.
We show that
W_{L}
is not hyponormal. Let
x=e_{1}\otimes h
. Then

W_{L}x=e_{2}\otimes h,\qquad\|W_{L}x\|=1.

On the other hand, the general adjoint formula gives

W_{L}^{*}x=\Omega\otimes h+e_{n}\otimes h.

Hence

\|W_{L}^{*}x\|^{2}=2>1=\|W_{L}x\|^{2}.

Therefore,
W_{L}
is not hyponormal.
