Thiostrepton potentiates chemotherapy-induced cell death in cisplatin-resistant SKOV3 cells
Authors: Farzaneh Motafeghi, Ehsan Ghassemi Barghi, Jafar Gholami Gharab, Parham Mortazavi, Fahimeh Ramezani Tehrani, Sorour Ashari, Nasrin Ghassemi Barghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the main problems in the treatment of ovarian cancer is resistance to the main drug (cisplatin). Thiostrepton (TST), a thiazole antibiotic, helps weaken cancer cells and enhance the efficacy of platinum-based therapy. This study examines the combination of TST and cisplatin in the SKOV3/CDDP model of resistance, focusing on associated changes in oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, genotoxicity, apoptosis, inflammatory mediators, and Wnt/β-catenin output.MethodsSKOV3/CDDP cells were treated with cisplatin at an IC50 concentration, TST at concentrations (1, 2.5, 5 µM), and the combination of these two substances for 24 h. Cell viability, intracellular energy, and membrane damage were measured by MTT and ATP assays and LDH release, respectively. Apoptosis was assessed using caspase-3/7 and TUNEL activity.DNA damage was analyzed using the Comet and CBMN assays. Oxidative stress and antioxidant defense were assessed by measuring ROS, MDA, GSH/GSSG ratio, and SOD, catalase, and GPx activities. Mitochondrial dysfunction (ΔΨm, NAD+/NADH), cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β), and Wnt/β-catenin target genes (c-MYC, AXIN2) were evaluated.ResultsThe combination of cisplatin and TST was much more potent than when either was used alone. This combination increased oxidative stress processes, increased DNA damage, and decreased antioxidant capacity in cancer cells. Inflammatory cytokine levels were also increased, and c-MYC and AXIN2 expression were reduced, which was accompanied by the attenuation of β-catenin-dependent transcription.ConclusionOverall, TST increased the sensitivity of resistant ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin. Its anticancer effect was enhanced by oxidative-mitochondrial stress and DNA damage pathways, along with reduced Wnt/β-catenin transcription.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Cisplatin, Thiostrepton, Chemoresistance, Drug resistance, Ovarian cancer, SKOV3

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a clinical challenge for women worldwide. Mortality from this cancer is associated with various factors, including the aggressive nature of the disease, resistance to chemotherapy, including platinum-based drugs, and diagnosis at late stages of the disease. Although the initial response to first-line drugs, such as cisplatin, which acts by inducing DNA crosslinking followed by apoptosis, is favorable, most patients eventually develop resistance to these drugs [1, 2]. Drug resistance is a complex process driven by multifactorial mechanisms, including DNA repair, reduced drug accumulation, glutathione-mediated detoxification, and inhibition of apoptosis, which activate survival pathways. Therefore, identifying new strategies to overcome this drug resistance is important to improve disease outcomes and patient survival [3–6].

Current approved treatments for ovarian cancer include cytoreductive surgery, platinum/taxane-based chemotherapy, antiangiogenic therapy such as bevacizumab, PARP inhibitors, and biomarker-based agents such as mirotuximab and surovatensin for platinum-resistant patients with folate receptor alpha. Platinum/taxane chemotherapy remains the first-line treatment and offers a high response rate. However, most patients with the disease relapse and develop resistance to platinum therapy. Bevacizumab improves disease progression by targeting angiogenesis, but patient responses are generally modest and may be associated with vascular toxicity. PARP inhibitors significantly improve maintenance therapy, particularly in BRCA-mutated tumors or tumors with homologous recombination defects; however, their efficacy is biomarker-dependent, and resistance may develop. Mirotuximab surovatensin is an important option for FRα-positive platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, although its use is limited to antigen-positive tumors and may be associated with ocular toxicity. These limitations highlight the ongoing need to identify additional agents and alternative molecular pathways capable of overcoming chemoresistance in ovarian cancer [7–9].

Cells that have become resistant to treatment maintain their resistance in several ways [4]. One of the most important ways cells escape death is by tolerating or repairing DNA damage when drugs such as cisplatin damage them [10]. Further research has also shown that oxidative stress and long-term inflammation play important roles in the development and maintenance of resistance to platinum drugs [11]. Cisplatin-induced cell death in cancer cells is mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, these cells neutralize this damage by utilizing antioxidant defense systems such as glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). At the same time, peritumor inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IL-1β), contributes to cancer cell growth and escape. This cycle makes cell death less likely and drug resistance more persistent [12, 13].

Abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is another important reason why cancer cells become resistant to platinum drugs such as cisplatin. The purpose of this pathway is to control cell growth, change cell shape, and maintain stem cells. However, when it is activated excessively or incorrectly, it works in the cancer cell’s favor. In this case, β-catenin enters the cell nucleus in excess and activates genes such as c-MYC and AXIN2, which can promote the growth and survival of cancer cells. As a result, the cancer cell multiplies more, gains the ability to spread and migrate, becomes resistant to cell death (apoptosis), and is more likely to relapse. For this reason, this pathway could be an important target for treatment. For this reason, this pathway could be an important target for treatment [14, 15].

Given that drug resistance has multiple causes and that several factors contribute to it, such as oxidative damage, inflammation, DNA repair, and cancer growth pathways, it is better to develop treatments that target multiple pathways simultaneously.

Herbal medicines, plant-derived bioactive substances, and natural compounds have also been considered as potential adjuvant therapies to overcome cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. Various studies have reported several phytochemicals that enhance platinum sensitivity in preclinical models of ovarian cancer by modulating oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammatory signaling, and drug resistance pathways. These include curcumin, resveratrol, quercetin, artemisinin, and oleanolic acid. For example, studies have shown that curcumin can sensitize SKOV3/CDDP cells to cisplatin. The combination of cisplatin with artemisinin or oleanolic acid has also been investigated in cisplatin-resistant ovarian tumor models. However, due to low or poor bioavailability and inadequate clinical outcomes, the clinical application of many herbal medicines remains limited, requiring the identification of multifunctional agents and targetable pathways for refractory ovarian cancer [16–19].

Thiostrepton, a natural thiazole antibiotic, has attracted attention as an anticancer agent. Thiostrepton (TST) was initially identified for its antibacterial activity, which selectively inhibits the transcription factor FOXM1. This transcription factor, FOXM1, is often overexpressed in chemotherapy-resistant tumors and regulates genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, DNA repair, and the oxidative stress response [20, 21]. As a result, TST, by targeting FOXM1, induces apoptosis and suppresses the proliferation of tumor and resistant cells. Results also indicate that TST may modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, reduce antioxidant defense, and affect inflammatory signaling pathways [22, 23].

Thus, this study aims to investigate TST as a chemosensitizer in cisplatin-resistant SKOV3 ovarian cancer cells, focusing on its effects on cellular viability, apoptosis, oxidative and inflammatory responses, DNA damage, and key molecular signaling pathways involved in chemoresistance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Cisplatin and thiostrepton were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, USA, and all experimental chemicals were of analytical grade.

Cell culture

The cisplatin-resistant SKOV3/CDDP cells were obtained from the Pasteur Institute in Iran and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and sub-cultured at 70–80% confluency using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. Only cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments.

This study was funded by the Pharmaceutical Sciences Research Center (PSRC), Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. (Grant Number:1404-11-109-94429 and IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1404.119)

Treatment protocol

SKOV3/CDDP ovarian cancer cells were subjected to various treatments to evaluate the cytotoxic, genotoxic, oxidative stress, inflammatory, and molecular effects of Cisplatin (Cis), Thiostrepton (TST), and their combination. For experimental treatments, cells were seeded into appropriate culture plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The robust cisplatin-resistant phenotype of the SKOV3/CDDP cell line has been extensively validated in previous studies, which demonstrate an IC50 for cisplatin consistently exceeding 40 µM, significantly higher than that of the sensitive parental SKOV3 cells [24, 25].

Therefore, this established line was used exclusively to investigate the chemosensitizing mechanisms of thiostrepton. The following groups were established and treated for 24 h:

Control Group: Cells treated with 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as vehicle control

Cisplatin alone: 1 µM, 2.5 µM, and 5 µM

Cisplatin alone: 1 µM, 2.5 µM, and 5 µM

Thiostrepton alone: 1 µM, 2.5 µM, and 5 µM

Thiostrepton alone: 1 µM, 2.5 µM, and 5 µM

Combination Therapy:

The concentration range of cisplatin (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) was specifically selected as a sub-lethal, low-dose regimen. These concentrations are well below the established half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) typically reported for the highly resistant SKOV3/CDDP cell line (often > 30–40 µM), allowing for the accurate evaluation of chemosensitization without overwhelming baseline toxicity [25, 26]. Similarly, the chosen concentrations of thiostrepton (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) align with previous in vitro studies demonstrating its effective suppression of FOXM1 transcriptional activity and induction of apoptosis in ovarian and other cancer models within this low micromolar range [27, 28].

Cisplatin and thiostrepton were freshly prepared and diluted in culture medium prior to use. For combination groups, both compounds were added simultaneously to ensure co-exposure. All treatments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. After the 24-hour incubation period, cells were harvested and subjected to a subsequent assay.

MTT cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined by the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay. Briefly, 4 × 104 SKOV3/CDDP cells were plated in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. After 24 h of treatment with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination, the medium was removed. Then 100 µL of fresh culture medium containing 0.5 mg/mL MTT was added to each well. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 h to allow the formation of formazan crystals. After incubation, the MTT-containing medium was removed, and the formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 570–630 nm. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and statistical validity, and the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) was calculated for each treatment group [29, 30].

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Plasma membrane integrity was assessed using a commercial LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 4 × 104 SKOV3/CDDP cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were subsequently treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or both for a period of 24 h. At the end of the treatment, 50 µL of culture supernatant from each well was transferred into a new 96-well plate, and an equal volume of the kit’s reaction mix was added. The plates were then incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm with 620 nm as a reference using a microplate reader. Parallel wells were used for lysis with the kit-provided lysis buffer to measure total LDH release. LDH release in treated cells was reported as a percentage of total LDH to assess cytotoxicity. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± SEM [31, 32].

ATP content assay

Intracellular ATP levels were measured using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions. SKOV3/CDDP cells were seeded at 4 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After treatment, plates were equilibrated to room temperature for 30 min, and an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well. Plates were gently shaken for 2 min to induce cell lysis, and luminescence was measured after 10 min using a microplate luminometer. ATP content was expressed as relative luminescence units (RLU) compared with untreated control cells. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± SEM [33].

Caspase-3/7 activity assay

Apoptotic signaling was assessed using the Caspase-Glo® 3/7 Assay (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions. SKOV3/CDDP cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 4 × 104 cells/well and incubated overnight. Cells were then treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After treatment, 100 µL of Caspase-Glo 3/7 reagent was added to each well containing 100 µL of culture medium. Plates were gently shaken for 30 s and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Luminescence was measured using a microplate luminometer. Caspase-3/7 activity was expressed as fold change relative to untreated control cells. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± SEM [34].

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cell death in SKOV3/CDDP cells was evaluated using the Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay to detect DNA strand breaks characteristic of apoptosis. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, and 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, and 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche). Briefly, cells were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and fluorescein-labeled dUTP for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing with PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI to determine the total number of cells.

Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets for fluorescein (Ex/Em: 488/520 nm) and DAPI. For each well, five randomly selected fields were captured at identical exposure settings. The number of TUNEL-positive cells (green fluorescence) and the total number of nuclei (DAPI-stained) were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Alkaline comet assay (single-cell gel electrophoresis)

DNA strand breaks were evaluated using the alkaline Comet assay following standard procedures. SKOV3/CDDP cells were treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After treatment, cells were harvested, suspended in PBS, and mixed with low-melting-point agarose (0.7%), then layered onto pre-coated microscope slides. Slides were immersed in cold lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris, 1% Triton X-100, pH 10) and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C to lyse cells and remove membranes and proteins. Subsequently, slides were equilibrated in alkaline electrophoresis buffer (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, pH > 13) for 20 min to allow DNA unwinding. Electrophoresis was performed at 25 V and 300 mA for 20 min under alkaline conditions.

Following electrophoresis, slides were neutralized with 0.4 M Tris buffer (pH 7.5), stained with SYBR Green, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. DNA damage was quantified as tail moment or percentage of DNA in tail per cell using automated image analysis software (e.g., Comet Assay IV or CASP). All treatments were conducted in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± SEM [36, 37].

Cytokinesis-Block Micronucleus (CBMN) assay

The CBMN assay was performed to evaluate chromosomal damage and micronucleus (MN) formation in SKOV3/CDDP cells. Cells were treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After treatment, cytochalasin B (3 µg/mL) was added to the culture medium to block cytokinesis and promote the formation of binucleated cells. Cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. Following incubation, cells were harvested, fixed in methanol: acetic acid (3:1), and stained with DAPI (for fluorescence microscopy) or Giemsa (for light microscopy). The frequency of micronuclei was determined in 1,000 binucleated cells per treatment group using automated image analysis software.

Measurement of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

To quantify the production of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), cells were incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H₂DCFDA). SKOV3/CDDP cells were plated in 96-well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) and treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, and 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, and 5 µM), or a combination of both, for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated with 10 µM H₂DCFDA in serum-free media for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence intensity was recorded at excitation 485 nm and emission 535 nm with a microplate reader. ROS levels were determined as a percentage of untreated control cells [40, 41].

Reduced glutathione (GSH) assay

The intracellular redox state was assessed by quantifying reduced glutathione (GSH) and calculating the GSH/GSSG ratio using a commercial GSH/GSSG quantification kit (colorimetric or fluorometric; e.g., Cayman Chemical). After 24-hour treatment, cells were lysed in ice-cold buffer and deproteinized according to the kit protocol. Total and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were measured spectrophotometrically, and reduced GSH was normalized to total protein concentration. The GSH/GSSG ratio was used as an indicator of oxidative balance [35, 42].

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity

The SOD activity was determined with a colorimetric SOD assay kit (e.g., Cayman Chemical). The method is based on SOD-mediated inhibition of superoxide’s ability to reduce a tetrazolium salt to a formazan dye. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. SOD activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein (U/mg protein), normalized according to the total protein using the Bradford assay [43, 44].

Catalase (CAT) activity

Catalase (CAT) activity was evaluated using a commercial catalase activity assay kit (e.g., Cayman Chemical). The assay quantifies the rate of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) decomposition by catalase, where residual H₂O₂ reacts with a chromogenic substrate to yield a measurable product at 540 nm. Absorbance was recorded with a microplate reader, and CAT activity was expressed as U/mg protein [45].

Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx) activity

The activity of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) was measured using a GPx assay kit (Cayman Chemical), which involved GPx-catalyzed oxidation of GSH to GSSG in the presence of cumene hydroperoxide. The concurrent conversion of NADPH to NADP⁺ was accepted as a measure of GPx activity and monitored at 340 nm with a spectrophotometer. The activity was normalized to total protein and expressed as mU/mg protein [45].

Lipid peroxidation (malondialdehyde, MDA) assay

Lipid peroxidation was evaluated by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) formation using the Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances (TBARS) method. After 24 h of treatment, cells were lysed, mixed with the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent, and heated at 95 °C for 30 min. The resulting MDA–TBA adduct was measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm. MDA levels were expressed as nmol per mg protein [46, 47].

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) assay

The evaluation of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in SKOV3/CDDP cells was done with the use of the lipophilic cationic fluorescent dye 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DiOC₆), which is enriched in mitochondria with intact membrane potential. The cells were treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination and incubated for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to rinse away any residual treatments, and then incubated with 40 nM DiOC₆ in serum-free media for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark.

After incubation, cells were rinsed gently with PBS, detached with a trypsin-EDTA solution, and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (e.g., BD FACSCanto™ II) using 488 nm excitation and 530 nm emission (FITC channel). A decrease in DiOC₆ fluorescence intensity was interpreted as mitochondrial depolarization. A minimum of 10,000 events per sample was collected, and flow cytometry data were subsequently analyzed using FlowJo software. Results were expressed as a percentage of loss of ΔΨm. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and data were summarized as mean ± SEM for visualization [48, 49].

Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD⁺/NADH) ratio

To determine mitochondrial metabolic activity and cellular redox balance, we measured the NAD⁺/NADH ratio. After 24 h of treatment with Cisplatin, Thiostrepton, or their combination, cell lines SKOV3/CDDP cells were collected, lysed, and assessed using a commercial NAD⁺/NADH quantification kit (for example, Abcam or Cayman Chemical), following manufacturer instructions. Total NAD (NAD⁺ + NADH) was measured spectrophotometrically, and the reduced NADH was measured; the NAD⁺/NADH ratio was calculated to determine mitochondrial function and oxidative metabolic status. All analyses were performed in triplicate, and data is expressed as mean ± SEM [50].

Cytokine quantification by ELISA (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-1β)

To evaluate the inflammatory response induced by treatment, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were quantified in culture supernatants. SKOV3/CDDP cells were treated with Cisplatin (1, 2.5, 5 µM), Thiostrepton (1, 2.5, 5 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After incubation, culture media were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) were measured using commercial human ELISA kits (e.g., Nanjing Jincheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated for each cytokine and expressed as pg/mL. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± SEM [51].

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

To assay AXIN2 and c-Myc expression, total RNA was extracted from SKOV3/CDDP cells using a total RNA extraction kit from Yekta Tajhiz Azma Company, Iran, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nanodrop and agarose gel electrophoresis were used to assess the quantitative and qualitative properties of the extracted RNA. A commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Company, Iran) was used to synthesize cDNA from total RNA. Then, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a StepOne Plus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the endogenous control gene. The used primer sequences are as follows: AXIN2: Forward: 5′-TCAAGACGGTGCTTACCTGT-3′, Reverse: 5′-TTGCAGGCAAACCAGAAGTC-3′, c-Myc: Forward: 5′-GGC TCC TGG CAA AAG GTC A-3′, Reverse: 5′-CTG CGT AGT TGT GCT GAT GT-3′, GAPDH: Forward: 5′-GGTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACA-3′, Reverse: 5′-GTGAGGGTCTCTCTCTTCCT-3′. The 2−△△CT method was used to determine relative expressions of target genes [52, 53].

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences among treatment groups were evaluated using an ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed specifically by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test. All experimental data were derived from three independent experiments to ensure robust reproducibility and statistical validity. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [54]. To minimize observer bias, strict blinding procedures were implemented during data acquisition and analysis. Specifically, the quantitative scoring of imaging-based assays (including Comet and TUNEL assays) and the extraction of raw colorimetric/fluorometric data were performed by an independent investigator blinded to the treatment allocations. Statistical differences among treatment groups were evaluated using an ordinary one-way.

Results

Effect of cisplatin, thiostrepton, and their combination on SKOV3/CDDP cell viability

The cytotoxic effects of Cisplatin (Cis), Thiostrepton (TST), and their combination on SKOV3/CDDP ovarian cancer cells were evaluated using the MTT assay (Fig. 1). Treatment with increasing concentrations of Cis (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) or TST (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) for 24 h resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability compared with the untreated control group (p < 0.01–0.0001). Notably, the combination treatments (Cis + TST at 1 + 1, 2.5 + 2.5, and 5 + 5 µM) produced a more pronounced decrease in cell viability than either agent alone (p < 0.01–0.0001), indicating a potential potentiated cytotoxic interaction between Cis and TST. The highest cytotoxic effect was observed in the Cis + TST (5 + 5 µM) group, reducing cell viability to approximately 60% of the control. These findings suggest that thiostrepton enhances the cytotoxic efficacy of Cisplatin in resistant SKOV3/CDDP cells.

Effect of cisplatin, thiostrepton, and their combination on LDH Release in SKOV3/CDDP cells

To evaluate the effect of Cisplatin (Cis) and Thiostrepton (TST) on plasma membrane integrity in SKOV3/CDDP cells, LDH release was measured following 24 h of treatment. As shown in Fig. 2, Cisplatin induced a dose-dependent increase in LDH leakage compared to the control group, indicating enhanced cytotoxicity. Treatment with thiostrepton alone also caused a moderate but significant elevation in LDH release at higher concentrations. Notably, co-treatment with Cisplatin and Thiostrepton further augmented LDH leakage compared to either drug alone, suggesting a potentiated effect on cell membrane damage. The combination of Cis 5 µM and TST 5 µM produced the highest LDH release, confirming a marked increase in cytotoxicity (p < 0.0001) relative to single-agent treatments.

Effect of cisplatin, thiostrepton, and their combination on intracellular ATP levels

The impact of Cisplatin (Cis), Thiostrepton (TST), and their combination on cellular energy metabolism was evaluated by measuring intracellular ATP levels in SKOV3/CDDP ovarian cancer cells using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Fig. 3). Treatment with increasing concentrations of Cis (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) for 24 h resulted in a significant, dose-dependent reduction in ATP levels compared with the untreated control group (***p < 0.001–****p < 0.0001). Similarly, TST treatment (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) also led to a marked decrease in ATP content in a concentration-dependent manner (***p < 0.001–****p < 0.0001).

Effect of cisplatin, thiostrepton, and their combination on Caspase-3/7 activity

To evaluate the induction of apoptosis, Caspase-3/7 activity was measured in SKOV3/CDDP ovarian cancer cells following treatment with Cisplatin (Cis), Thiostrepton (TST), and their combination using the Caspase-Glo® 3/7 Assay (Promega) (Fig. 4). Exposure to increasing concentrations of Cis (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) for 24 h significantly increased Caspase-3/7 activity compared with the untreated control (**p < 0.01–****p < 0.0001), indicating activation of apoptotic pathways. Similarly, TST treatment (1, 2.5, and 5 µM) also resulted in a dose-dependent elevation of Caspase-3/7 activity (*p < 0.05–****p < 0.0001).

Discussion

Drug resistance is one of the most common problems in platinum-based cancer treatment, leading to disease relapse. The results of this study show that thiostrepton (TST) effectively sensitizes cisplatin-resistant SKOV3/CDDP cells to cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity.

The SKOV3/CDDP cell line is a well-established and validated model for the study of treatment-resistant ovarian cancer. Since the main differences between this resistant cell line and its sensitive parental line have been previously investigated and established in many studies [26, 55], using this cell line allowed us to investigate different mechanisms by which TST overcomes multifactorial resistance, rather than performing a large comparative screen.

Results of MTT, LDH, and ATP assays showed that TST significantly reduced cell viability and metabolic activity while compromising membrane integrity. The combination of TST and cisplatin also induced apoptosis, caspase-3/7 activation, and increased TUNEL staining compared with single-agent treatments. These results are consistent with previous studies. Studies have shown that thiazole antibiotics can overcome resistance in chemotherapy-resistant cells by lowering the apoptosis threshold and enhancing intrinsic death pathways [56, 57]. TST switches SKOV3/CDDP cells from resistance to a platinum-induced death state by disrupting cellular ATP and activating Caspase-3/7.

To fully validate the results and precisely determine the mechanism of action, this study simultaneously examined several different factors and biological pathways. The increase in oxidative stress factors (ROS and MDA) along with the decrease in antioxidant capacity (SOD, CAT, GPx (GSH/GSSG) is evidence of intracellular toxicity and inhibition of drug resistance. Also, to demonstrate that cell death is apoptotic (not due to random or infectious causes), all stages of this process were examined step by step. First, mitochondrial dysfunction and a decrease in membrane potential and intracellular energy production were observed, then caspase activity was examined, and finally, DNA degradation and fragmentation occurred. This sequence shows that the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis has been activated through mitochondria [58, 59]. The severe decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential also confirms that the cell has entered the irreversible phase of programmed cell death and that cell death-inducing factors (cytochrome c) are being released [60]. Finally, the coordination of all these results definitively proves that this drug weakens cancer cells.

The results of this study showed that the increase in LDH leakage is probably due to “secondary necrosis”: cells that initially enter apoptosis lose their membranes over time and, in the absence of cell clearance, undergo destruction [61, 62]. Accordingly, TST acts through two simultaneous pathways: first, it stops the growth and proliferation of cancer cells, and then, by activating the mitochondrial-dependent pathway, it induces programmed cell death. This process probably occurs through the reduction of cell-protective proteins, such as BCL-2 and Survivin, which ultimately leads to the release of cytochrome c and the initiation of the apoptotic cascade. In the subsequent stages, the cells enter secondary necrosis.

Importantly, although the initial decrease in cell metabolic activity (as measured by MTT and ATP) was not numerically severe, these early changes were decisive for the resistant SKOV3/CDDP cells. These cells have a high threshold for entering apoptosis, so even a moderate disruption in energy production can trigger an extensive, irreversible chain of cell death. This was fully confirmed by a sharp increase in the final markers of apoptosis, including activation of caspases 3 and 7, extensive DNA damage, and a positive TUNEL test. As a result, an initial and relatively mild metabolic disorder ultimately led to genetic collapse and irreversible death of the cancer cells. This finding suggests that combining TST with cisplatin can produce a significant anticancer effect even at low doses.

The therapeutic utility of TST in clinical practice is controversial and of great importance due to its specific pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. This substance is hydrophobic and does not dissolve well in water. For this reason, in some studies, its encapsulated form in micelles has been used to improve its delivery to the tumor. In animal models of breast and liver cancer, encapsulated TST was able to accumulate in tumor tissue; the highest accumulation was observed about 4 h after injection and continued for at least 24 h. The results showed that encapsulated TST reduced tumor growth, accompanied by decreased FOXM1 activity and increased signs of apoptosis in tumor tissue. Also, in an animal model of pancreatic cancer, TST inhibited tumor growth and reduced the expression of proteins such as FOXM1, STAT3/p-STAT3, GPX4, and Ki67. These results suggest that TST likely acts by disrupting oxidative balance and activating pathways associated with cell death. All findings from the above studies are consistent with the results of the present study; in this study, exposure of TST to resistant SKOV3/CDDP cells also increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. However, before clinical use of TST in ovarian cancer, more studies are still needed, including: detailed investigation of the drug’s behavior in the body, its distribution in tissues, determination of effective and safe doses and concentrations, and confirmation of its effects in animal models of ovarian cancer [63, 64].

One of the most important mechanisms that make resistant cells more sensitive is the inhibition of FOXM1 and the Wnt/β-catenin-dependent pathway. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays an important role in maintaining the characteristics of cancer stem cells, increasing cell migration and invasion (EMT), and enhancing DNA repair.

All of these factors make cancer cells more resistant to cisplatin. Therefore, when TST inhibits the biological pathways associated with drug resistance, the cell’s ability to survive, repair DNA damage, and resist chemotherapy is reduced, and the cells become more sensitive to cisplatin again [65, 66].

In platinum-resistant ovarian cancer models, activation of Wnt/β-catenin was associated with increased tumor sphere formation and greater resistance to cisplatin. Studies have shown that reducing β-catenin activity, either through genetic inhibition or by using drugs that inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, can reduce the number of cancer-initiating cells and increase the sensitivity of resistant cells to cisplatin.In addition, some small-molecule inhibitors, such as CWP232291, have shown antitumor effects in ovarian cancer models, including cisplatin-resistant lines and patient-derived organoids.These findings suggest that reducing Wnt/β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activity can attenuate pathways associated with survival and drug resistance. This is also consistent with the results of the present study; Because a decrease in the expression of AXIN2 and c-MYC genes was observed in treated cells, indicating inhibition of this resistance pathway [67, 68].

In this study, we did not directly examine FOXM1 and β-catenin protein levels or their nuclear localization. However, reduced AXIN2 gene expression is considered a valid indicator of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. AXIN2 is one of the most direct transcriptional targets of this pathway and is used to measure β-catenin/TCF activity [69]. Therefore, its reduction in expression indicates that the activity of this pathway is reduced in cells. The reduction of c-MYC (another downstream target of this pathway) also strengthens this result, indicating that the transcriptional output of Wnt/β-catenin is generally suppressed. Even without direct measurement of FOXM1 or β-catenin at the protein level, the gene data indirectly indicate that TST reduced the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which plays an important role in the drug resistance of ovarian cancer cells [56, 69].

After TST treatment, cell proliferation was reduced, cell death (apoptosis) was increased, and cisplatin resistance was reduced. All of these events are consistent with the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in ovarian cancer cells. Thus, the cells’ behavior suggests that this pathway is effectively shut down. Importantly, the protein FOXM1 plays a key role in the function and entry of β-catenin into the nucleus. Therefore, when FOXM1 is suppressed, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is effectively shut down [69–71].

The selection of Thiostrepton (TST) was predicated on its well-documented pharmacological role as an inhibitor of the FOXM1 transcriptional network [56, 57]. The strong downregulation of downstream target genes, such as AXIN2 and c-MYC, provides functional confirmation of this pathway’s silencing. Also, TST was previously known to inhibit the FOXM1 transcriptional network, so this study focused more on the ultimate biological consequences rather than reexamining early details such as underlying protein pathways or protein degradation [69, 71].

When the FOXM1/Wnt axis is inhibited by TST, the capacity of cancer cells to repair DNA is reduced. As a result, the combination of TST and cisplatin causes increased genotoxic stress (with increased micronucleus formation in the CBMN assay and DNA breaks in the Comet assay). The main reason is that FOXM1 normally keeps key DNA repair genes active. When TST suppresses this pathway, the DNA repair system fails, and cells cannot repair cisplatin-induced damage, which can progress to severe damage and cell death. Overall, inhibition of FOXM1 renders resistant cells unable to repair their DNA and vulnerable to chemotherapy [72].

The main effect of TST extends beyond cell growth to include the cellular oxidative balance. Resistant cancer cells often have a strong defense system that neutralizes the free radicals (ROS) produced by chemotherapy. This defense system includes antioxidants such as SOD, catalase, and GPx, as well as the GSH/GSSG balance [73]. Importantly, FOXM1 enhances the enzymatic antioxidant defense system, including manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) and catalase, making the cell more resistant to oxidative stress [74]. In this study, TST (especially in combination with cisplatin) disrupted this defense system. The results showed that the cell’s antioxidant balance was disrupted, the GSH/GSSG ratio decreased, and the activities of defense enzymes (SOD, CAT, GPx) decreased. As a result, ROS accumulated strongly inside the cell, causing extensive damage to membrane lipids (lipid peroxidation or MDA).

This oxidative stress was directly associated with severe mitochondrial damage. Combined treatment with TST and cisplatin resulted in a sharp decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and the NAD+/NADH ratio, indicating disruption of the cell’s energy production chain and metabolic collapse [60]. At the same time, large amounts of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IL-1β, were released into the cellular environment. Although these cytokines can usually support tumor survival, in this study, their sharp increase coincided with metabolic damage and extensive apoptosis. For this reason, the researchers believe that this increase is more indicative of a terminal stress response in the cells caused by excessive ROS accumulation [75]. However, the precise molecular pathways of this inflammatory response, such as the possible activation of NF-κB, were not directly investigated in this study and require further investigation. Overall, the results suggest that TST acts through multiple simultaneous pathways, including increased cytotoxicity, inhibition of the FOXM1/Wnt pathway, severe disruption of oxidative balance, and mitochondrial damage.

Currently, clinical studies of thiostrepton (TST) are limited. It is not yet approved as an anticancer drug, and its efficacy, safety, side effects, and drug interactions in humans are not fully understood. However, a thiostrepton-based compound, RSO-021, is being studied in a Phase 1/2 clinical trial in patients with malignant pleural effusions caused by mesothelioma and certain advanced tumors. This suggests that there is clinical interest in thiostrepton-based therapies, but there is not yet enough evidence to demonstrate their safety and broad efficacy in humans. Another important point is that no clinical study has yet examined the combination of TST with cisplatin in humans. Therefore, it is still unclear how the two drugs interact in the body, and hypotheses are being put forward that the combination may increase toxicity, exacerbate oxidative or mitochondrial damage, or damage normal tissues. For this reason, more preclinical and human studies are needed before this combination can be introduced into clinical therapy [76].

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the promising results, this study had several important limitations. First, the experiments were performed only on a cisplatin-resistant cell line (SKOV3/CDDP) and were not compared with other ovarian cancer models or sensitive primary cells. Second, whether TST is toxic only to cancer cells or may also harm normal ovarian cells and non-cancerous cells. Third, although the reduction in AXIN2 and c-MYC indicated inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, direct examination of FOXM1 and β-catenin proteins, β-catenin nuclear entry, and actual pathway activity were not performed. Fourth, additional experiments were not performed to establish the mechanisms’ dependence definitively; for example, it was not investigated whether the TST effect would be reduced if ROS were neutralized or caspases were inhibited. Finally, the drug’s behavior in the body, the dose that can be used in humans, the efficacy in animal models, and systemic toxicity were also not evaluated.

Conclusion

This study showed that thiostrepton (TST) sensitizes resistant ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin through multiple pathways. By increasing oxidative stress and DNA damage, inhibiting the FOXM1/Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and disrupting cellular oxidative and inflammatory balance, it weakened the main mechanisms of drug resistance and increased cell death. The results of this study suggest that TST could be considered as a promising adjuvant therapy alongside cisplatin. It is also possible that using this compound could reduce the required cisplatin dose, thereby reducing severe chemotherapy side effects while maintaining or even enhancing anticancer activity. However, this study was only conducted in vitro and on cells. It is not yet known what dose of TST is achievable and safe in humans. Therefore, further research on animal models, patient-derived cells, drug pharmacokinetics, and safety is needed before this approach can be introduced into clinical trials.
