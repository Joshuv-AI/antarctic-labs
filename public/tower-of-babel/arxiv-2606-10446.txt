Proportionality from Sampled Approvals
Gregory Kehne

Abstract
How much voter input is necessary in order to ensure representation in multiwinner elections? If voters are randomly selected from an underlying population, how many draws are necessary to find a proportional committee of k k candidates, with high probability? Sample-based adaptations of standard multiwinner voting rules that satisfy the justified representation (JR) proportionality axiom use O ~ ​ ( k 5 ​ log ⁡ m / δ ) \tilde{O}(k^{5}\log\nicefrac{{m}}{{\delta}}) sampled approval ballots over m m candidates, where δ \delta is a probability of failure and O ~ \tilde{O} suppresses 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( k ) {\mathsf{polylog}}(k) factors. We present a rule for which the sample complexity of JR-family proportional committee selection is O ~ ​ ( k 4 ​ log ⁡ m / δ ) \tilde{O}(k^{4}\log\nicefrac{{m}}{{\delta}}) . This separates the sample complexity of JR from that of the natural corresponding additive approximation to the voter coverage (Chamberlin-Courant) objective, which we show requires Θ ⁡ ( k 5 ​ log ⁡ m / δ ) \Theta(k^{5}\log\nicefrac{{m}}{{\delta}}) samples. For lower bounds, we present a family of instances with m , 1 / δ ∈ poly ( k ) m,\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}}\in\poly(k) for which Ω ⁡ ( k 3 ) \Omega(k^{3}) sampled ballots are necessary in order to identify a JR committee. We also show a dependence on log ⁡ m \log m is necessary. This lower bound is versatile, and also applies to Hare proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC) for ranked ballots. Unfortunately, no number of sampled ballots suffices to satisfy the slightly stronger Droop JR and Droop PSC axioms with high probability. But mild relaxations of JR require fewer samples, as do the beyond-worst-case domains and actual approval preferences we evaluate.



1
Introduction

We set out to determine how much voter input is required in order to identify representative outcomes in multiwinner elections.
If voters are randomly selected from an electorate, how many draws are necessary to find a committee that is proportional
with respect to the underlying electorate
, with high probability, and how does this requirement increase with the winning committee size?

This question is particularly well motivated in settings such as participatory budgeting and polling, where participation is costly and alternative spaces are complex.
The aims of promoting high and representative rates of participation and providing the information and context to voters sufficient to elicit meaningful preference data are often in tension with one another, and it can be difficult to know how to resolve these tradeoffs in practice.
A better understanding of how many randomly selected voters or respondents are necessary, as indexed by the aims of the social choice process, is useful for efficiently obtaining more meaningful and informed preference inputs.
This is part of the recipe for the success of citizens’ assemblies in producing population-representative recommendations on complex policy issues: randomly selecting a limited number of participants enables them to be brought together to both deliberate and learn about the issues they are asked to provide recommendations on—and to be paid for the effort.

Even within high-stakes and conventional applications of multiwinner approval voting to committee elections and to participatory budgeting, sample complexity is a natural criterion by which to both differentiate between rules and guide the design of new rules.
Participatory budgeting rules that are more sample-efficient in producing proportional outcomes could be expected to be more robust to changes in turnout or to misspecification of voter preferences, as measured by the axiomatic guarantees that motivate their design and deployment
50
.

Establishing the sample complexity of existing rules is also a theoretical step towards understanding when voter turnout in a multiwinner election becomes low enough that the proportionality guarantees of the outcome can become compromised, as a function of the committee size
k
.
In particular, if each voter has an independent probability of participation, then an election designer might hope to identify a proportional outcome with respect to the underlying population, as weighted by their ex ante turnout probabilities.
In this case, the sample complexity of the voting rule used determines necessary and sufficient bounds on the turnout required for satisfying a given proportionality axiom, as a function of the axiom and the size of the winning committee.

This shift in focus from producing the outcome of a specific rule to ensuring the outcome directly satisfies axiomatic properties is also aligned with and motivated by the emerging application domains of proportional representation.
Prominent recent applications include clustering in metric spaces
17

15
,
fairly choosing collections of matchings
8
,
selecting central nodes in a network
48
,
and models of online deliberation that entail choosing representative subsets from vast spaces of alternatives that contain many near-clones
32
;
26
.
In each of these domains, proportionality remains an important goal; but the choices a particular rule makes between (near-)clones may be inconsequential, and it may be infeasible to target the outcome of a particular rule if it requires knowing every voter’s approval of every candidate in order to choose a winning committee.

Whether conducting public opinion polling, organizing a citizens’ assembly, shortlisting a large slate of alternatives, or modeling stochastic voter turnout, attaining outcomes that are proportionally representative
of the underlying voter population
is a natural goal.
To do so, we must understand the sample complexity of our axioms: how many sampled voter preferences are necessary and sufficient for their satisfaction?

1.2
Related Work

This work draws from and builds upon a few areas of research within computational social choice.

Sortition and Sampling in Social Choice

The question of how many randomly chosen voters are needed to make collective decisions while representing the underlying population is central to sortition and the design of citizens’ assemblies.
Most recent theoretical and practical work on sortition uses a formulation that is, in some sense, dual to ours.
For a fixed number of individuals, representativeness constraints are placed on the outcome of the participant sampling procedure, and the sampling procedure is made maximally uniform subject to these constraints
28
;
27
.

Other recent work models sortition as proceeding via uniformly random selection, and studies the extent to which this selection satisfies definitions of proportional representation of metric preferences
20
and other demographic, metric, and welfarist objectives
12
.
The sample complexity questions
12
ask are close to ours in spirit.
For instance, they bound the extent and likelihood that a budget allocation in the approximate core for the sampled voters is in the (less) approximate core for the population.
Materially, the most similar prior results are due to
17
.
They work in the clustering setting with the related axiom of
proportional fairness
(PF), and (informally) they show that
\tilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})
samples suffice to find a
k
-subset satisfying PF with high probability, provided the proportionality quota is increased by an
\varepsilon
proportion of the population.

A distinct line of work proposes to find proportional committees in large-scale multiwinner approval voting instances by sampling voters i.i.d. but sequentially, and making adaptive and sparse queries of voter preferences
32
;
11
.
As their queries are sparse they require
\Omega(m)
sampled voters, and obtain incomparable bounds.
This has also inspired generative approaches to constructing slates of proportionally representative opinion statements
26
;
9
, where access to textual representations of all voters’ preferences is assumed.
Working in a similar model to that of
32
,
45
study the number of voter-candidate approvals necessary to implement polynomial-time approximations to the coverage objective.

Learning Axioms and Rules from Samples

Prior work has studied both approval-based multiwinner voting rules and proportionality axioms from the probably-approximately correct (PAC) learning framework.

14
showed committee scoring rules and sequential Thiele rules have polynomial sample complexity, and
62
both extended these results and considered the sample complexity of proportionality axioms such as
JR
.
This was in the course of developing a unified theory of many ABC voting rules and axioms that, like us, takes profiles to be distributions over approval sets.
This line of work is distinct in that their samples are pairs
(E,W)
from an unknown distribution over elections and committees output by an unknown rule, and the PAC guarantees are with respect to this profile distribution.

Stability and Robustness of Multiwinner Rules

Another area of research seeks to characterize how changes to approval profiles affect the outputs of various rules, or degrade the axiomatic guarantees of rules’ outputs.
Within this area, closest to our perspective is perhaps
(
42

Beyond the Droop quota,

2.1
Election Models

Throughout we will use
candidates
and

interchangeably, as well as

and

.
We consider a finite set
\mathcal{C}
of candidates.
Our aim is to output a committee

In approval-based committee (ABC) elections, each voter

Our focus is the setting where the voter population is large; as such, we view the population as a distribution over approvals
\mathcal{P}\in\Delta(2^{\mathcal{C}})

An

is then fully defined by the tuple
E=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P},k)

Let

denote the proportion of the population with approval set

.
Then a sample of
t

\mathcal{P}^{t}

For a discrete population

n

Although drawing

Furthermore, recent work in metric settings shows that samples without replacement are more informative

A typical ABC rule

receives the full preferences of the population and outputs a committee in

for a specified committee size

By contrast, our interest is in

t

Our goal will be for such

In general, a multiwinner approval voting axiom

specifies a set of feasible output committees for each input preference profile.
(We will introduce specific axioms shortly.)
We can now formalize what it means for a sample-based rule to satisfy an axiom.

Definition 2.1

(Axiom satisfaction w.h.p.)

We will say that a rule

(t,\delta)

Further,

This means that regardless of the population profile, the approvals

Rules

are more sample-efficient for

2.2
Sample Complexity

At a high level, if we have an algorithmic task defined with respect to a distribution over data, then it makes sense to ask whether this task can be accomplished by an algorithm given some number of samples from this distribution, and if so, how the best-possible expected performance or likelihood of succeeding at this task improves with the number of samples.
An influential framework for understanding such problems is probably approximately correct (PAC) learning
61

h^{*}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
of instances
\mathcal{X}
. Given labelled samples
(x,h^{*}(x))
from some unknown instance distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the goal is then to choose an
h\in\mathcal{H}
from some larger class, which is likely to agree with
h^{*}
on a
1-\varepsilon
fraction of future samples
x\sim\mathcal{D}
.
This admits myriad generalizations, for instance by replacing agreement with
h^{*}
by more general error metrics
34
.

A simple such problem is that of distinguishing a Bernoulli source with rate
p+\varepsilon
from
m-1
bad sources with rate at most
p
, given a uniform number of samples from each of the distributions.
1
1

1

Note that this is distinct from best-arm identification in the bandit setting, where adaptive sampling of indices is allowed.

The number of samples needed to identify the good source with high probability is well understood.
It coincides with that of the more general problem of selecting a source which is within
\varepsilon
of optimal, which will be useful to us.

Fact 2.2

(
\varepsilon
-Maximum Bernoulli sources)

Given probabilities
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})

\mathcal{D}\coloneq\prod\Bern(p_{i})

If

From this perspective, we should approach axiom satisfaction (
Definition

2.1
) by viewing each
k

as a hypothesis, and from the sample identifying a

This is what

But for axioms in the

and its relationship to our objective (axiom satisfaction) directly.
In particular, our objective is not characterized by approximately minimizing any linear function over the voters.
This situates us in a broader line of work on combinatorial optimization from samples, which includes finding approximately influence-maximizing sets of nodes on networks
52

2.2.1
Sampling with vs. without Replacement

We consider sampling with replacement, even though sampling without replacement is more closely aligned to our motivation
12
.
This is for convenience, but we argue sample complexity in the two models is asymptotically equivalent for our parameter regime of interest.
For a finite population, sampling with and without replacement coincide when the number of samples is bounded away from the total population size.

Observation 2.3
.

Suppose voter distribution
\mathcal{D}
is uniform over a finite population
[n]
.
If
t
and
[n]
are known, then
t
samples without replacement (w.o.r.) can simulate
t
samples with replacement (w.r.).
If the

are distinguishable, then for any

then

So if we are interested in

samples
without
replacement, then samples
with
replacement are weaker by at most a constant, and lower bounds carry over directly.

Warmup: Sampling for Chamberlin-Courant and Voter Coverage

JR
is a relaxation of the coverage guarantee provided by the Chamberlin-Courant rule for approval ballots, which returns the committee of
k
candidates which maximizes the number of voters who approve of at least one chosen candidate.
In the approval setting, it is immediate that the Chamberlin-Courant rule is equivalent to
Max k-Coverage
, the problem of choosing
k
sets from a set family so as to maximize the cardinality of their union
56
.

For a given committee, satisfying
JR
can be seen as a certificate of (approximate) local optimality with respect to voter coverage, while Chamberlin-Courant-winning committees are globally optimal with respect to voter coverage.
Viewed this way, the fact that Chamberlin-Courant-winning committees satisfy
JR
is somewhat intuitive, and can be formalized via an exchange argument.

Observation 3.1
.

If
W
is a Chamberlin-Courant winning committee, then
W
satisfies Droop
JR
.

In the same sense, (Hare)
JR
is a relaxation of approximately optimal global voter coverage:

Observation 3.2
.

Let
W^{*}
be a Chamberlin-Courant winner for the approval election
E
.
For any committee
W
, if
\cvg(W)>\cvg(W^{*})-\nicefrac{{1}}{{k^{2}}}
, then
W
satisfies
JR
for
E
.

One benefit of this relaxation from coverage to
JR
is computational tractability.
The sequential greedy algorithm efficiently finds a (Droop)
JR
committee in polynomial time, while obtaining better than a multiplicative
(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{e}})
-approximation to the
Max k-Coverage
(i.e. the Chamberlin-Courant) objective is NP-hard
24
, and under suitable assumptions this problem remains inapproximable even for small
k

The most natural way to construct sample-efficient rules for the

We begin by briefly presenting the sample complexity of the natural sample versions of known rules.

4.1
Adaptations of Existing Rules

When designing a sample-based variant of a coverage-based rule, we must ensure that enough samples are used so that any inaccuracy in which candidate or set of candidates is chosen falls safely within the slack provided by the difference between the Hare and Droop quotas.

By way of illustration, suppose we implement the greedy rule for

2

Therefore, it is sufficient to choose a candidate approximating the maximum coverage to (additive) extent

For

Lemma 5.6

There exist constants

Proof.

As we observed in the proof of
Theorem

5.3
, for a given
\mathcal{J}_{i}\in\mathcal{J}
the distribution of
\bar{n}
, the histogram of the supporters of candidates
j\in[k+1]
from the sample
\mathcal{A}=(A^{1},\ldots,A^{t})
, follows a multinomial distribution
M_{k,\varepsilon,i,t}=\Multin(t;p_{\downarrow},\ldots,p_{\uparrow},\ldots,p_{\downarrow})
, where for ease of notation we let
p_{\downarrow}\coloneq\nicefrac{{1}}{{k+1}}-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{k}}
and
p_{\uparrow}\coloneq\nicefrac{{1}}{{k+1}}+\varepsilon
.
The failure event for
f^{*}
is precisely that
\bar{n}_{i}\leq\min_{j\neq i}\bar{n}_{j}
when the unknown distribution is
\mathcal{J}_{i}
.
We will pick a threshold
\tau
as a function of
k
and
\varepsilon
, and lower bound the failure probability by

\displaystyle\probability_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\sim[k+1]\\
\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{i}^{t}\end{subarray}}\left(f^{*}(\mathcal{A})\not\in\text{JR}{}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{J}_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i}\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{i}^{t}}\left(\bar{n}_{i}\leq\min_{j\neq i}\bar{n}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{1}^{t}}\left(\bar{n}_{1}\leq\min_{j\neq 1}\bar{n}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{1}^{t}}\left(\bar{n}_{1}\leq\tau\>\land\>\tau\leq\min_{j\geq 2}\bar{n}_{j}\right).

(5.1)

This proceeds in two steps.
First, we show that these events are positively correlated; therefore our failure event is lower bounded by the product of their individual probabilities.
Then we lower bound the probability of each event.

Positive correlation can be shown via Chebyshev’s sum inequality.
For fixed
\tau
, let
\Gamma_{t}
denote the event that
\bar{n}_{1}<\tau
over
t
samples
\mathcal{A}
, and let
\Lambda_{t}
denote the event that
\min_{j\geq 2}\bar{n}_{j}\geq\tau
over
\mathcal{A}
.
Then

\displaystyle\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{1}^{t}}\left(\Gamma_{t}\>\land\>\Lambda_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}\probability(\Lambda_{t}\>\land\>t^{\prime}\leq\tau\>\>\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})\cdot\probability(\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}}\probability(\Lambda_{t}\>\>\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})\cdot\1\{t^{\prime}\leq\tau\}\cdot\probability(\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t^{\prime}}\probability(\Lambda_{t}\>\>\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})\cdot\probability(\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{t^{\prime}}\1\{t^{\prime}\leq\tau\}\cdot\probability(\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\probability(\Lambda_{t})\cdot\probability(\Gamma_{t}).

Here our invocation of Chebyshev’s sum inequality required that both
\probability(\Lambda_{t}\>\>\bar{n}_{1}=t^{\prime})
and
\1\{t^{\prime}\leq\tau\}
are decreasing in

.
This is clearly true for the latter; for the former it holds because for fixed
t

according to
\Multin(t-t^{\prime};p_{\downarrow},\ldots,p_{\downarrow})
.
As

increases the number of samples to distribute decreases, and so
\min_{j\geq 2}\bar{n}_{j}
will decrease also.

With this in hand, we turn to lower bounding each probability.
Recall (or observe) that for multinomials, each individual coordinate follows a binomial distribution; for
(\bar{n}_{1},\ldots,\bar{n}_{k+1})\sim\Multin(t;p_{1},\ldots,p_{k+1})

is distributed according to
\Bin(t,p_{j})

This will permit us to use binomial anticoncentration to lower bound
\probability(\Gamma_{t})
.

Before doing this, we address
\probability(\Lambda_{t})
.
Unfortunately, the negative association of multinomial coefficients works against us in our effort to lower bound
\probability(\Lambda_{t})
in terms of the behavior of its constituent binomial parts.
We instead resort to a union bound, and find

\displaystyle\probability(\Lambda_{t})=\probability(\land_{j\geq 2}\bar{n}_{j}\geq\tau)=1-\probability(\lor_{j\geq 2}\bar{n}_{j}<\tau)\geq 1-\sum_{j\geq 2}\probability(\bar{n}_{2}\leq\tau)=1-k\cdot\probability(X\leq\tau),

(5.2)

where

.
From the standard Chernoff lower tail bound, we can derive that
\probability(X\leq\tau)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]-\tau)^{2}}{2\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]}\right)
.
Upper bounding this by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2k}}
and plugging it into (

), we find that
\probability(\Lambda_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}
so long as

\tau\geq t\cdot p_{\downarrow}-\left(2t\cdot p_{\downarrow}\cdot\log 2k\right)^{1/2}.

(5.3)

We now turn to lower bounding
\probability(\Gamma_{t})
.
For this we will use standard forms of binomial anti-concentration in the lower tail e.g.
(
63
, Equation 5)
.
In particular, for

we have

\displaystyle\probability(\Gamma_{t})>\probability(B=\tau)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{8\tau(t-\tau)}}\cdot\exp\left(-t\dkl\left(\tau/t\>\|\>p_{\uparrow}\right)\right)

From here, we seek to upper bound this KL divergence.
Since

-divergence, we can start with

and so in order for

a sufficient condition is

Improved Bounds for Structured Domains

Having established worst-case upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity of satisfying multiwinner proportionality axioms, it is natural to ask whether the landscape changes if we restrict our attention to structured families of election instances, and if so, how.

7
Implications for Ranked Ballots

Although our main focus is on approval-based committee voting, our primary lower bound is versatile enough to imply sample complexity lower bounds on finding proportional committees in the setting of multiwinner voting based on ranked preferences.
In this domain, a standard criterion is that of solid coalition proportionality, due to

\nicefrac{{1}}{{k+1}}

In our notation, a multiwinner election from ranked preferences is again given by

Definition 7.1

(Proportionality for solid coalitions)

For

8.2
Discussion of Results

All of the rules that provably satisfy the axioms we consider appear to exhibit similar convergence rates in the number of sampled approvals needed to do so, and converge significantly more quickly than our worst-case or even beyond-worst-case guarantees would imply.
Note that since the same sets of samples were used for all rules, their absolute performances may jointly correlate with how informative the sampled ballots for a given value of
t
are.
It is also possible that these real approval instances are not challenging enough to discern between reasonable rules.

With that said, it is interesting to note the performance of rules on axioms they are not guaranteed to satisfy.

SeqPAV
performs remarkably well (and often indistinguishably from
PAV
) for both
JR
and
EJR+
, while
CC
with regards to
EJR+
is surprisingly poor on all instances considered, even in many cases relative to
AV
.
One possible explanation is that sets of multiple candidates with high but highly correlated support are prevalent.
Finally, it bears noting that
MES
often appears to require more samples than the score-based rules.
This could provide some indication that share-based approaches are less sample-efficient than score-based approaches to satisfying
JR
.

The strong empirical performance of all tested proportional rules on these elections could indicate that PB and other real-world approval instances exhibit other structure which explains the tractability of identifying proportional outcomes from samples.
If so, it remains to establish whether the beyond-worst-case results in
Section

6
characterize these instances, or whether other parameterizations provide better theoretical alignment to these empirical results.

9
Conclusion

In this work we have attempted to characterize the number of sampled ballots necessary to satisfy standard proportionality axioms in approval-based committee voting.
We have presented new lower bounds for
JR
as well as upper bounds for significant strengthenings, and we asymptotically separate the sample complexity of these axiom satisfaction problems from the sample complexity of their natural coverage counterparts.

The most immediate outstanding question is the correct dependence on
k
, and we do not have strong reason to believe either of the present bounds is tight.
A better understanding of the correct dependence could also help establish whether the tradeoff we observe between sample and computational complexity among the rules we consider is inherent to the problem of proportional committee selection.
Structured domains also merit further study.
On the one hand, our lower bound is simple enough that it holds in many traditional restricted domains of interest.
On the other, the empirical tractability of the problem invites new characterizations of when and how our lower bound can be circumvented.

The proportionality axioms considered here enjoy a wide range of refinements, generalizations, and applications.
Within the
JR
family, we have not established upper bounds for axioms that are incomparable with
EJR+
such as
BJR
and
FPJR
.
Moving towards the most immediate applications, participatory budgeting project selection from sampled approvals and committee voting from ranked ballots can be readily studied experimentally but were unaddressed here.
Developing rules that exhibit strong sample complexity performance and guarantees for these settings could be of practical significance, and constitutes a natural direction for future work.

Therefore it suffices to show this difference is nonnegative.
We do this by observing that

follows the multinomial distribution
\Multin(p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{i,k+1})
for each

for

\displaystyle\frac{\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\sigma(\ell)}^{t}}\left(\bar{n}(\mathcal{A})=\bar{n}^{\bot}\right)}{\probability_{\mathcal{A}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\sigma(1)}^{t}}\left(\bar{n}(\mathcal{A})=\bar{n}^{\bot}\right)}

Hence the rule
f^{\prime}
has strictly smaller error than

, a contradiction.
We conclude that for
\mathcal{J}_{i}\sim\mathcal{J}
, the

-optimal rule always chooses all but the sample-support-minimizing candidate as its output.

Proof of

.

Let
T=[k^{2}]
be the voter types and let
S\subseteq T
be the voter types observed within the sample
\mathcal{A}
, and let
\bar{S}\coloneq T\setminus S
.
If
t\leq k^{d}/2
then necessarily
\lvert S\rvert\leq\lvert T\rvert/2
.
Each candidate
c\in\mathcal{C}
has an incidence
S_{c}=\{A\in T:c\in A\}
on the sampled set of types and likewise on
\bar{S}
, with
\lvert S_{c}\rvert+\lvert\bar{S}_{c}\rvert=k^{d-1}
.

Suppose that on these samples
f^{*}
chooses a collection of winning candidates
W
with observed incidences
S_{W}\coloneq\{S_{c}\}_{c\in W}
and unobserved incidences
\bar{S}_{W}
.
Let
{\mathsf{W}}|_{S_{W}}
be the collection of all
W\in{\mathsf{W}}
that share these observed incidences.
Since
f^{*}
cannot distinguish between them from
\mathcal{A}
alone, all
W\in{\mathsf{W}}|_{S_{W}}
are equally likely to be its output.
But since every
k
-subset of voter types corresponds to a candidate, the if
W\sim{\mathsf{W}}|_{S_{W}}
on
\bar{S}
then the incidences of each of the
c\in W
on
\bar{S}
are independent random variables.
If
r_{c}\coloneq k-\lvert S_{c}\rvert
for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
, then the expected number of voters in
\bar{S}
approving of at least one candidate in
W
is given by

\lvert\{A\in T:A\cap W\neq\emptyset\}\rvert=\sum_{A\in T}\1\{A\cap W\neq\emptyset\}=\sum_{A\in T}R_{A},

Where
R_{A}\coloneq\lor_{c\in W}R_{A,c}
is the event that
A\in T
is in the incidence of
c
.
For each
A
, the
R_{A,c}
over all
c
are independent events, with
\probability_{W}\left(R_{A,c}\right)=\frac{r_{c}}{\lvert\bar{S}\rvert}\leq r_{c}\frac{2}{k^{d}}
.
Since they are independent,

\probability_{W}\left(R_{A}\right)=1-\probability(\land_{c}\neg R_{A,c})=1-\prod_{c}\left(1-\probability(R_{A,c})\right)\leq 1-\exp\left(-2\sum_{c}r_{c}\frac{2}{k^{d}}\right)\leq 1-e^{-4},

provided
4r_{c}\leq k^{d}
(e.g. if
k\geq 4
).
This then holds since
1-x\geq e^{-2x}
for
x\in[0,1/2]
and since
\sum_{c\in W}r_{c}\leq k^{d}
.
Therefore there is at least a constant chance of each voter type
A\in\bar{S}
remaining uncovered by the choice of
W
.
Since the events
\{R_{A}\}_{A\in\bar{S}}
are negatively associated, the number of uncovered
A
will exhibit strong concentration around its mean
\lvert\bar{S}\rvert\probability_{W}\left(R_{A}\right)
.

\bar{S}
not incident to any

. Then by Hoeffding,

For
k

\lvert S\rvert\cdot\frac{1}{2e^{4}}\geq\frac{k^{d}}{4e^{4}}\geq k
, this means even the
W
chosen by
f^{*}
will fail
JR
with high probability.
This concludes the proof.

Proof of
Proposition

6.3
.

We start with the first property.
Given a budget of
\tilde{O}(k^{3})
samples, we can run
Algorithm

2
with a minimal tolerance of
\varepsilon=k^{-3/2}
; let us call the resulting committee
W
, and suppose that none of the failure events bounded by
\delta
occur over the course of the run of
Algorithm

2
.

The remainder of this proof consists of analyzing the structure of
\mathcal{P}
under the conditions that
W
is a successful output according to
Algorithm

2
but nevertheless fails to satisfy
JR
.
If
W
fails
JR
, then there is some
c^{\prime}\not\in W
with marginal coverage at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}
.
In particular, since coverage is submodular, for all
c\in W
we have

\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}\leq\cvg(W\cup\{c^{\prime}\})-\cvg(W)\leq\cvg(W\setminus\{c\}\cup\{c^{\prime}\})-\cvg(W\setminus\{c\}).

On the other hand, if
W
is a successful output of
Algorithm

2
then no pivots with improvement better than
k^{-3/2}
remain: therefore for all
c\in W
,

k^{-3/2}\geq\cvg(W\setminus\{c\}\cup\{c^{\prime}\})=\cvg(W\setminus\{c\}\cup\{c^{\prime}\})-\cvg(W\setminus\{c\})-\left(\cvg(W)-\cvg(W\setminus\{c\})\right).

Combining these inequalities yields

\cvg(W)-\cvg(W\setminus\{c\})\geq\frac{1}{k}-k^{-3/2}.

This establishes the first property.

Finally, the first property implies the second: if all candidates in
W
have exclusive support at least
\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}
, then the probability
A\sim\mathcal{P}
is not an exclusive supporter is at most

1-\sum_{c\in W}\probability_{A\sim\mathcal{P}}\left(A\cap W=\{c\}\right)\leq 1-k\left(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\right)=k^{-1/2}.

This completes the proof.
