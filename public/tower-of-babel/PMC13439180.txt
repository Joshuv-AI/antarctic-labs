Creation of a computational space with model-free metasurface neural network
Authors: Mingfei Song, Chao Qian, Gaoang Wang, Cheng Li, Fang Yuan, Ali Momeni, Romain Fleury, Hongsheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Physical neural networks (PNNs) are neural-like computational frameworks that exploit the intrinsic dynamics of physical media to achieve ultrafast and energy-efficient information processing. However, the complex and strongly-coupled physical nature of PNNs in disordered environments makes them resistant to accurate differentiable modeling. Here, we propose a concept of computational space that empowers the chaotic environment itself with computational capabilities. This space constitutes a large-scale, model-agnostic PNN through distributed intelligent metasurfaces. To enable effective training, we develop a fully-forward learning framework that estimates zeroth-order gradients from in-situ measurable electromagnetic feedback, thereby circumventing the rigorous modeling requirements of conventional backpropagation. In experiments, we construct such computational space that achieves recognition accuracies of 97% for alphabetic characters and 99% for numeric patterns. Furthermore, the space exhibits the functionalities of enhanced focusing under disordered scattering conditions and reliable human position localization. This emerging paradigm of self-evolving physical intelligence holds potential for advancing embodied intelligence, autonomous cyber–physical systems, and next-generation human–machine interaction, marking a fundamental transition from computing the physics to computing with physics.

Introduction

Driven by the growing hardware demands from pervasive intelligence, physical neural networks (PNNs) have recently attracted considerable attention as a novel computational paradigm in which computation and learning emerge directly from the dynamics of physical substrates1,2. PNNs leverage intrinsic physical phenomena, such as wave propagation, interference, multipath coupling, and nonlinear material responses3–7, to construct computational isomorphs of artificial neural networks (ANNs) in optical, electromagnetic, acoustic, and mechanical media8–13. By outsourcing information processing to the underlying physics, PNNs inherently provide massive parallelism, ultrafast throughput, and exceptional energy efficiency, surpassing the limitations of conventional digital processors that must discretize and simulate these dynamics14–16. In recent years, remarkable versatility of PNNs—ranging from diffractive optical networks17,18 and neuromorphic metasurfaces19–21 to analog electromagnetic computing systems22,23—have demonstrated impressive capabilities in real-time imaging24,25, wavefront control26–28, adaptive sensing29, and spatiotemporal pattern recognition30. More broadly, PNNs perform computation directly through the evolution of physical parameters, offering unique advantages in specialized, physics-intensive scenarios. For example, PNNs could enhance satellite imaging by extracting critical boundary information31, serve as analog front-ends for quantum or weak-signal detection where digital interference must be minimized under strict noise constraints32,33, and strengthen geophysical and seismic sensing through continuous-field processing that exposes hidden structural signatures from complex subsurface reflections34. Furthermore, PNN-enabled environments could advance next-generation radar perception35, electromagnetic interference mitigation36, and astronomical radio observation37 through dynamic beam shaping and real-time physical reconfiguration38,39. With these attributes, PNNs establish a promising pathway toward fast, efficient, and physically embedded intelligence capable of interacting seamlessly with complex analog environments.

Despite these considerable advances, the practical implementation of large-scale, trainable PNNs remains fundamentally constrained by the difficulty of establishing accurate, differentiable models for analog physical systems in situ. Unlike digital neural networks, where neuron activations, weights, and gradients are explicitly defined, PNNs rely on complex wave-mediated interactions that, especially in complex environments, lack a stable, one-to-one correspondence between physical parameters and computational behavior. This inherent broken-isomorphism makes it extremely challenging to construct accurate differentiable models or digital twins that faithfully capture the underlying physics40,41. As a consequence, most existing PNN implementations depend on digital backpropagation, external optimization loops, or pre-characterized system responses, which preclude autonomous learning in real-world settings42–45. Furthermore, physical nonlinearities, environmental perturbations, multipath scattering, and hardware imperfections introduce systematic discrepancies between modeled predictions and actual system outputs, making simulated gradients deviate significantly from physical gradients. This challenge becomes particularly severe in spatially distributed environments, where high-order scattering, environmental uncertainty, and continuously evolving boundary conditions further obscure the relationship between controllable parameters and computational outcomes. In light of these, these limitations impede the scalability, robustness, and adaptability of state-of-the-art PNNs, underscoring the necessity for a new paradigm that supports in situ, model-free learning based on direct physical measurements rather than analytical computations.

In this work, we endow the physical environment itself with computational intelligence by introducing a concept of computational space, a physical neural network in which an open environment is deliberately engineered to perform computation and learning through its intrinsic electromagnetic dynamics. We instantiate a highly entangled, wave-based computational space using multiple computational metasurfaces (comp-metas). To enable autonomous adaptation, we develop a model-free, fully-forward learning framework that extracts zeroth-order gradients directly from physical perturbations and measurable responses, without invoking analytical gradients or digital twins. We experimentally implement this framework in free space by deploying four comp-metas around the region of interest, and demonstrate its versatility across diverse tasks. The system adaptively focuses electromagnetic energy in complex scattering scenarios, recognizes alphabetic and numeric patterns with accuracies of 97% and 99%, respectively, and robustly localizes human positions across subdivided spatial grids. Unlike conventional PNN implementations confined to isolated devices or predefined architectures, computational space distributes learning and inference across the environment itself, enabling computation to emerge collectively from spatially coupled physical interactions. These results establish the computational space as a scalable and self-evolving platform for physical intelligence, pointing toward a new computing paradigm in which computation emerges directly from the environment’s intrinsic physical dynamics rather than from externally imposed digital processing.

Results

Conceptual illustration of computational space

Building upon the concept of a self-contained PNN introduced above, we realize a computational space that is a physical environment deliberately engineered to perform complex computational tasks through electromagnetic interactions, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Ambient electromagnetic waves act simultaneously as both information carriers and agents of computation. Their interaction with objects or human bodies generates scattering fields that sensitively encode detailed geometric, positional, and postural information. The resultant information-rich scattering fields permeate the environment, creating a dynamic data layer that continuously reflects and communicates the physical context of the scene. In this framework, the scattering field is not merely a passive carrier of information, but an active computational medium whose distributed interactions continuously transform environmental inputs into task-relevant physical representations. To exploit the rich information encoded in the scattering field, we distribute comp-metas throughout the environment, creating a large population of physically coupled “neurons” that mimic the distributed-node philosophy of ANNs46. Each unit cell of a comp-meta operates as a tunable scattering node, electromagnetically coupled with all others through electromagnetic wave propagation, giving rise to a densely interconnected PNN in which computation emerges from multipath transformations and wave interference.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the computational space.The environment is endowed with the ability to perform in situ learning and adaptive optimization through its intrinsic physical wave dynamics. The environment is endowed with the ability to perform in situ learning and adaptive optimization through its intrinsic physical wave dynamics. Ambient electromagnetic waves interact with surrounding objects and human subjects, generating information-rich scattering fields that encode both physical and semantic information. Multiple computational metasurfaces (comp-metas) are distributed throughout the space, each acting as a tunable “neuron” to form a PNN through mutual electromagnetic interactions. By adaptively shaping and interpreting the scattered fields, the computational space executes diverse electromagnetic functions, including object recognition, spatial localization, and signal enhancement, directly in the wave domain without converting signals into electronic signals. This intelligent, wave-driven environment is therefore termed a computational space.

By reconfiguring comp-metas configurations, the computational space can shape and interpret the embedded scattering field to accomplish a variety of in-situ functions, such as object recognition, localization of individuals within the environment, and adaptive reshaping of electromagnetic fields to enhance wireless communication performance. All processing occurs directly in the wave domain without converting electromagnetic signals into slower electronic forms, allowing real-time, low-latency, and energy-efficient physical computation. In this way, an ordinary space becomes an intelligent wave-driven system capable of sensing, processing, and adapting to its surroundings through the physics of electromagnetic interactions. Beyond these demonstrated applications, such computational spaces hold profound potential for scenarios in which digital processors struggle with bandwidth and latency constraints, including autonomous driving47,48, and integrated sensing-and-communication systems49,50, where joint beamforming and inference must operate at microsecond timescales. As a general-purpose physical computing substrate, computational spaces pave the way for a new generation of intelligent environments that seamlessly integrate sensing, computation, and actuation.

Model-free fully-forward training framework

The scattering field generated by an object serves as the input signal to the computational space, where the trainable physical parameters correspond to the discrete phase states of each unit cell on the comp-metas. To optimize these parameters, the framework performs end-to-end physical training without analytical gradients or backpropagation, relying solely on experimentally measured feedback. Numerical simulations of object recognition

To validate the feasibility of the proposed model-free fully-forward training framework, full-wave electromagnetic simulations were performed within a modeled computational space. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the system consists of four independently controlled comp-metas positioned in free space. Each metasurface comprises a 7 × 7 array of unit cells, with each cell measuring 30 × 30 mm² and operating at 5.0 GHz. As shown in Fig. 3b, each unit cell integrates two voltage-controlled PIN diodes on its front and back surfaces, allowing precise manipulation of its reflection phase. Discussion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a physical computational space that supports end-to-end learning entirely within the electromagnetic environment. In contrast to conventional inverse-design or simulation-driven approaches that rely on analytical gradients or differentiable digital twins, our system implements a fully physical, closed-loop optimization process through a model-free, forward-only training framework. By extracting optimization cues directly from measured scattering responses, the computational space autonomously adjusts its physical degrees of freedom without prior modeling, thereby overcoming long-standing challenges in scalability, modeling fidelity, and in situ adaptability. Experimental demonstrations, including wave focusing under multipath scattering, high-accuracy recognition of alphanumeric patterns, and robust human-position localization, confirm that the computational space operates as an active learning medium. It can sense, interpret, and sculpt electromagnetic fields to perform functional computation purely through intrinsic physical interactions. This work also represents a significant advance beyond our recent invisible space termed as MetaSeeker52, evolving it into an actively trainable, large-scale computational platform.

Looking ahead, our work opens a promising avenue toward general physical intelligence in disordered environments. By incorporating a broader set of programmable physical parameters and extending the learning mechanism across multiple wave domains, future computational spaces may achieve multi-task, multi-frequency, and even multi-physics learning, thus enabling unified perception and adaptive control across electromagnetic, acoustic, and mechanical media. Our work establishes a new paradigm for physical intelligence by transforming the ambient environment into an active computational space, offering new possibilities for future smart-home devices, adaptive sensing, and embodied intelligent systems.

Threshold determination for the localization experiment

The predefined thresholds used for the three bits were determined from measurements taken at the initial stage of the experiment. Specifically, we recorded the transmission coefficients at probes A, B, and C while the object was positioned sequentially at all eight predefined grid locations. We compute the median of the eight measurements for each probe. These median values are then adopted as the fixed predefined thresholds S’A, S’B, S’C.
