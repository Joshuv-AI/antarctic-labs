EGID: A Comprehensive Multi-label Endoscopic Image Dataset for Gastritis Classification
Authors: Zeyu Li, Yingying Fang, Yiming Song, Haotian Bai, Tianhao Li, Yaqi Wang, Qingwei Zhang, Xiaobo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic atrophic gastritis is a well-recognized precancerous condition, emphasizing the need for accurate endoscopic classification. However, existing endoscopic datasets lack comprehensive multi-dimensional annotations for systematic gastritis classification. We present the Endoscopic Gastritis Image Dataset (EGID), comprising 5,883 high-quality white-light endoscopic images from 229 patients retrospectively collected at Renji Hospital, Shanghai, using the Olympus CV-290 system between March and November 2024. Each patient’s images are systematically annotated across four independent clinical dimensions: H. pylori infection status, presence of gastric atrophy, distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type. H. pylori infection status was determined based on rapid urease test results, whereas the three image-based endoscopic dimensions were independently assessed by two experienced endoscopists, with consensus adjudication by a senior gastroenterologist for discordant cases. The image-based annotation dimensions showed excellent inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s Kappa: 0.947–0.970). EGID provides the first publicly available multi-label endoscopic gastritis dataset, enabling development of artificial intelligence (AI)-based classification systems and serving as an educational resource for clinical training in gastritis diagnosis and cancer risk stratification.

Background & Summary

Gastritis, a common inflammatory condition of the gastric mucosa affecting millions worldwide, represents a critical clinical concern due to its role in gastric carcinogenesis1–3. Chronic gastritis, particularly when associated with Helicobacter pylori infection, follows a well-established progression pathway known as the Correa cascade—advancing sequentially from non-atrophic gastritis to multifocal atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately to gastric adenocarcinoma4,5. Given that gastric cancer remains the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally6, accurate identification and characterization of gastritis patterns through endoscopy has become increasingly important for cancer risk stratification and early intervention. The presence, extent, and distribution of gastric atrophy serve as critical indicators for determining cancer risk and guiding clinical management decisions, including H. pylori eradication therapy and endoscopic surveillance strategies7. However, gastritis presents various endoscopic appearances that can be challenging to classify and diagnose accurately8,9.

Multiple classification systems have been developed to standardize gastritis assessment. The Sydney System, an internationally accepted classification framework, provides a structured approach to categorizing gastritis based on topography, morphology, and etiology10. Building upon this foundation, the Operative Link for Gastritis Assessment (OLGA) and Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia (OLGIM) staging systems were introduced to stratify gastric cancer risk based on the extent and severity of atrophy or intestinal metaplasia11,12. The Kyoto classification emphasized endoscopic features associated with H. pylori infection status and gastric cancer risk8. Despite these standardized frameworks, gastritis diagnosis remains complex due to the need for multi-dimensional classification encompassing H. pylori infection status, presence and distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type, each carrying distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications. This multi-faceted assessment requirement poses significant challenges for consistent and accurate diagnosis, particularly in settings where specialized expertise may be limited.

Artificial intelligence offers the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and consistency in gastrointestinal endoscopy. AI systems utilizing deep convolutional neural networks have demonstrated expert-level performance in detecting gastric pathologies, including early gastric cancer and precancerous lesions13–15. However, robust AI model development critically depends on the availability of large-scale, comprehensively annotated datasets that capture the full spectrum of disease presentations across multiple classification dimensions. The creation of such datasets faces substantial challenges, including the labor-intensive nature of multi-dimensional annotation requiring specialized expertise, the time-consuming process of achieving expert consensus on complex cases, and the difficulty in assembling sufficiently large and diverse patient cohorts. These barriers have significantly limited the availability of suitable training data for AI algorithm development in gastritis classification.

While several public endoscopic datasets exist (Table 1), they present limitations for systematic gastritis classification. The IM and GA Benchmark dataset, although containing a large number of images, focuses primarily on intestinal metaplasia and gastric atrophy without comprehensive multi-dimensional classification16. The El Salvador Atlas of Gastrointestinal Video Endoscopy for gastritis includes only chronic gastritis examination videos without systematic classification across multiple clinical dimensions17. The Gastrolab Image Gallery contains merely a limited collection of H. pylori-associated gastritis images, lacking representation of autoimmune gastritis18. The GastroHUN dataset, while extensive, primarily targets anatomical landmark recognition, with gastritis diagnosis and H. pylori infection status provided only as auxiliary metadata rather than structured classification labels19. To our knowledge, none of these existing datasets provide comprehensive multi-label annotations that simultaneously capture H. pylori infection status, presence of atrophy, distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type. To address this gap, we present the Endoscopic Gastritis Image Dataset (EGID), a comprehensive collection of 5,883 high-quality white-light endoscopic images from 229 patients with systematic multi-label annotations across four clinically relevant classification dimensions. Each patient’s images are annotated across four independent perspectives: H. pylori infection status (positive vs negative), presence of atrophy (atrophic vs non-atrophic), distribution of atrophy (corpus-predominant, antrum-predominant, antrum and corpus, or normal), and gastritis type (type A gastritis, type B gastritis, or normal). These four classification dimensions were specifically selected based on their critical clinical implications for patient management and cancer risk assessment. The presence and distribution of gastric atrophy serve as key indicators for gastric cancer risk stratification, with corpus-predominant atrophy associated with different cancer risks compared to antrum-predominant patterns20,21. The distinction between Type A gastritis (autoimmune gastritis) and Type B gastritis (H. pylori-associated gastritis) is fundamental for determining appropriate management strategies, as Type A gastritis requires monitoring for pernicious anemia and neuroendocrine tumors, while Type B gastritis necessitates H. pylori eradication to prevent progression22,23. Current H. pylori infection status directly influences treatment decisions and surveillance intervals8,24. This multi-label approach enables comprehensive characterization of gastritis patterns from multiple clinical perspectives, providing flexibility for various research and clinical applications.

EGID offers multiple potential applications. First, it provides a comprehensive reference for endoscopists to better understand and differentiate various gastritis patterns, particularly in distinguishing between Type B and Type A gastritis—a clinically challenging differentiation with distinct treatment implications. Second, it serves as a valuable educational resource for training medical professionals in endoscopic diagnosis of gastritis. Most importantly, it provides a structured multi-label dataset for developing and validating AI algorithms for automated gastritis classification across multiple clinically relevant dimensions, which could potentially improve diagnostic accuracy and promote standardization in clinical practice. EGID combines clinically confirmed H. pylori infection status based on rapid urease test (RUT) results with expert-reviewed image-based annotations for gastric atrophy, atrophy distribution, and gastritis type. Discordant image-based annotations were resolved through expert consensus adjudication, supporting the reliability of the final dataset labels. This structured labeling strategy, combined with comprehensive representation of gastritis patterns, makes EGID a valuable resource for both clinical education and AI development in gastroenterology.

Data collection and patient selection

The dataset originates from the Endoscopy Center of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China. Gastroscopic images were retrospectively collected from 229 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy between March 2024 and November 2024. All endoscopic examinations were performed using the Olympus CV-290 endoscopy system (Olympus Medical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) under standard white-light imaging mode. Multiple images were systematically captured from each patient during endoscopic examination to document characteristic mucosal features across different gastric regions. Images of the gastric cardia and pylorus were specifically excluded to maintain standardization and focus on regions where gastritis patterns are most reliably assessed. All images from a single patient share identical classification labels across the four dimensions, as multi-dimensional classification is determined based on the overall endoscopic findings and confirmed diagnostic status of each patient.

Patients were retrospectively selected to ensure comprehensive representation of gastritis patterns across four key classification dimensions. Patients were included if they were aged 18 years or older, had completed upper gastrointestinal endoscopy examination, and had high-quality endoscopic images available documenting relevant gastric regions. Patients were excluded if they had gastric cancer, active gastrointestinal bleeding, peptic ulcers or polyps, previous gastric surgery, or poor image quality. The number of images per patient varied according to the number of representative high-quality images available from each examination. For each patient, images were selected to capture characteristic mucosal appearances across relevant gastric regions, while excluding images with poor focus, inadequate illumination, excessive mucus or bubbles, insufficient mucosal visualization, or non-target regions such as the gastric cardia and pylorus.

Ethics approval and privacy protection

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital (approval number: LY2024-166-B-AMD-01). As data collection was conducted retrospectively and neither interfered with patient care nor compromised information security, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

To ensure de-identification, all personally identifiable information was stripped from image metadata. Files were renamed using a standardized anonymization scheme with sequential numerical identifiers, ensuring complete anonymization while maintaining the association between images from the same patient.

Image standardization and processing

Following retrospective collection, all images underwent systematic processing to ensure quality and standardization. Quality screening was performed to exclude images with poor technical quality, including excessive blur, inadequate lighting, significant artifacts, or insufficient mucosal visualization. Only images with clear visualization of gastric mucosa, optimal focus, and minimal obscuring factors such as residual gastric contents, bubbles, or mucus were retained. For format standardization, the left sidebar containing timestamps, patient identifiers, and equipment information was systematically removed from all images to ensure complete de-identification and format uniformity. All selected images were saved in BMP format to preserve image quality without compression artifacts. No additional pre-processing, color adjustment, or data augmentation was performed to maintain the authenticity of the original endoscopic appearance.

Annotation process and quality control

The annotation process followed a rigorous classification system based on standardized endoscopic features. Each patient was assigned labels across four clinically relevant dimensions.

H. pylori infection status: active H. pylori infection was determined by positive RUT results25. Characteristic endoscopic features included disappearance of regular arrangement of collecting venules, diffuse mucosal erythema, and mucosal nodularity26.

Gastric atrophy: atrophic gastritis was identified by characteristic endoscopic features including mucosal thinning with increased transparency, enhanced visibility of the submucosal vascular pattern, pale or whitish discoloration of the mucosa, and loss or flattening of gastric rugal folds7. When intestinal metaplasia was present, it appeared as white slightly raised patches with light blue crests and white opaque fields27.

Type A gastritis: type A gastritis was diagnosed based on atrophic changes predominantly involving the gastric corpus, with characteristic sparing of the gastric antrum7.

Type B gastritis: type B gastritis was identified by atrophic changes initially involving the gastric antrum and incisura angularis, with variable progression to the gastric corpus, representing different stages of H. pylori-associated gastritis progression7.

Two experienced endoscopists (Physician A and Physician B), each with 15 years of clinical experience, independently reviewed all patients’ images and assigned labels for the three image-based endoscopic dimensions, including presence of atrophy, distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type. H. pylori infection status was determined based on RUT results and was therefore excluded from inter-rater agreement analysis. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, demonstrating excellent consistency according to Landis & Koch criteria28: presence of atrophy (κ = 0.947, 95% CI: 0.901–0.993), gastritis type classification (κ = 0.961, 95% CI: 0.927–0.995), and distribution of atrophy (κ = 0.970, 95% CI: 0.944–0.996). Initial disagreement occurred in 5 of 229 patients, corresponding to 124 of 5,883 images (Fig. 3). Cases with discordant annotations were reviewed through collaborative discussion involving both primary annotators and a senior gastroenterologist (Physician C) with over 30 years of experience in gastrointestinal endoscopy. The final dataset labels represent either the concordant initial assessments from Physicians A and B, or the consensus decision reached through three-physician collaborative review for discordant cases.Fig. 3Confusion matrices for inter-rater agreement across three image-based endoscopic annotation dimensions. Data Records

The complete EGID dataset has been deposited on Figshare29,30. The dataset comprises 5,883 high-quality white-light gastroscopic images from 229 patients, organized according to a multi-label classification system across four independent clinical dimensions.

The dataset employs a multi-label hierarchical structure where each patient is simultaneously classified across the four dimensions described in the Methods section. The root directory contains four main category folders representing H. pylori infection status, presence of atrophy, distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type. Each dimension folder contains subdirectories for specific classification categories: H. pylori infection status includes positive (n = 104) and negative (n = 125) subfolders; presence of atrophy includes atrophic (n = 165) and non-atrophic (n = 64) subfolders; distribution of atrophy includes corpus-predominant (n = 31), antrum-predominant (n = 74), antrum and corpus (n = 60), and normal (n = 64) subfolders; gastritis type includes Type A (n = 31), Type B (n = 134), and normal (n = 64) subfolders. Individual patient folders are nested within these classification subdirectories.

Patient folders are identified using sequential numerical codes (001–229), with all patient identifiers fully anonymized. Image files are saved in BMP format following the naming pattern [Patient ID]([Image_Number]).bmp (e.g., 001(1).bmp, 001(2).bmp). The left sidebar containing timestamps and equipment information has been removed from all images to ensure complete de-identification. Each patient contributed multiple endoscopic images, with a median of 24 images per patient (interquartile range: 19–30; range: 6–108). The mean number of images per patient was 25.69 ± 10.75.

The dataset includes a comprehensive metadata file (metadata.xlsx) located in the root directory, containing classification labels for all 229 patients across six fields: Patient_ID (001-229), Number of images, H_pylori_infection_status (positive/negative), Presence_of_atrophy (atrophic/non-atrophic), Distribution_of_atrophy (corpus-predominant/antrum-predominant/antrum and corpus/normal), and Gastritis_type (type A/type B/normal). This metadata file enables users to identify quickly patients meeting specific clinical criteria and facilitates batch processing for machine learning applications. The multi-label structure of this dataset enables flexible data access for various research applications, including single-dimension binary classification, multi-class classification within individual dimensions, or comprehensive multi-label classification across all four dimensions simultaneously.

Technical Validation

To verify the technical quality of the Endoscopic Gastritis Image Dataset and assess whether it is suitable for multi-task automatic gastritis analysis, we built a systematic multi-task deep learning baseline model based on the current public data organization scheme, and conducted a comparative evaluation of four classical backbones under a unified experimental setting. This section focuses on the experimental setup and result analysis, providing objective evidence for the reliability of the dataset.

Multi-task experimental setup

The dataset is organized according to four tasks: H. pylori infection status (H_pylori_infection_status), presence of atrophy (Presence_of_atrophy), distribution of atrophy (Distribution_of_atrophy), and gastritis type (Gastritis_type). Each patient has consistent labels across all four tasks, and the lowest-level folder corresponds to the patient ID. We first took the intersection of patient IDs across the four tasks and retained only those patients with complete annotations for all four labels, resulting in a multi-task subset containing 229 patients and 5,883 images.

To avoid data leakage caused by multiple images from the same patient, data splitting for technical validation was performed at the patient level rather than at the image level. All images from a given patient were assigned exclusively to one of the training, validation, or independent test cohorts. Given the class imbalance across several label dimensions, particularly corpus-predominant atrophy and type A gastritis, we adopted a patient-level multi-label stratified splitting strategy that simultaneously considered all four clinical label dimensions. The final split consisted of 161 patients in the training cohort, 34 patients in the validation cohort, and 34 patients in the independent test cohort, corresponding to 4,065, 853, and 965 images, respectively (Table 2). The validation cohort was used for model selection and early stopping, whereas the independent test cohort was held out for final performance evaluation. No patient overlap existed among the three cohorts. Because each patient contributed multiple images, image-level predictions were first obtained for all test images and then aggregated to derive patient-level performance, which served as the primary evaluation result. For patient-level evaluation, image-level softmax probabilities were averaged across all images from the same patient for each task, and the class with the highest aggregated probability was used as the final patient-level prediction. This aggregation strategy ensured that each patient contributed only once to the patient-level test metrics, regardless of the number of available images.

In terms of network architecture, we constructed a multi-task framework consisting of a shared feature extraction backbone and four task-specific heads. Each endoscopic image is first passed through the backbone to obtain a shared feature vector, which is then fed into four independent fully connected classification heads to predict the labels of the four tasks. All four tasks use cross-entropy loss, and the overall training loss is the simple average of the four task losses, so that all tasks are jointly optimized.

To evaluate the learnability of the dataset under different network architectures, we compared four commonly used ImageNet-pretrained backbones under exactly the same data split and training strategy: ResNet18, ResNet50, EfficientNet-B0, and DenseNet121. All models were initialized with official ImageNet-pretrained weights and fine-tuned on our dataset. During training, we applied standard data augmentation (such as random cropping, random flipping, and color jittering) to improve generalization. We trained for at most 25 epochs (ResNet50, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet121) or 125 epochs (ResNet18) and adopted an early stopping strategy: if the multi-task mean accuracy on the validation set did not improve for 5 consecutive epochs, training was terminated early and the model checkpoint with the best validation performance was saved. Throughout training, we recorded, for each epoch, the training/validation loss, multi-task mean accuracy, and the individual accuracy for each of the four tasks.

During training, the multi-task mean validation accuracy, defined as the arithmetic mean of the four task accuracies, was used only to monitor convergence and select the best checkpoint. Final model performance was evaluated separately on the independent test cohort. We reported accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, AUROC, and AUPRC for each task. Balanced accuracy and macro-F1 were included to account for class imbalance. For multi-class tasks, AUROC and AUPRC were calculated using a one-vs-rest strategy and macro-averaged across classes. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were estimated using 1,000 patient-level bootstrap resampling iterations on the independent test cohort.

Performance comparison of different backbones

The overall patient-level independent test performance of the four backbone networks is summarized in Table 4. DenseNet121 achieved the best overall performance among the evaluated backbones, with a mean patient-level accuracy of 83.82%, mean balanced accuracy of 81.74%, mean macro-F1 of 82.99%, mean AUROC of 93.60%, and mean AUPRC of 88.78% across the four tasks. ResNet50 showed the second-best overall performance, followed by ResNet18 and EfficientNet-B0.

At the task level, the binary H. pylori infection status task achieved the most stable performance across backbones, whereas the multi-class distribution of atrophy task was more challenging. For the best-performing DenseNet121 backbone, patient-level accuracies were 94.12%, 82.35%, 73.53%, and 85.29% for H. pylori infection status, presence of atrophy, distribution of atrophy, and gastritis type, respectively. The corresponding AUROC values were 99.30%, 89.78%, 90.85%, and 94.21%, and the corresponding AUPRC values were 99.22%, 78.18%, 85.77%, and 91.94%. These results indicate that EGID contains learnable visual signals across all four label dimensions, while fine-grained multi-class phenotypes remain more challenging than binary classification tasks.

The independent patient-level test protocol provides a more conservative estimate of model performance by strictly separating model selection from final evaluation and ensuring that each patient contributes only once to the patient-level test metrics, regardless of the number of images. These results support the technical usability of EGID as a patient-level multi-task benchmark dataset, while also showing that fine-grained multi-class gastritis phenotypes remain challenging.

For the H. pylori infection status task, most patients were correctly classified, producing a clear diagonal pattern in the confusion matrix. The presence-of-atrophy task also showed relatively stable performance, although some non-atrophic cases were misclassified as atrophic or vice versa. For the distribution-of-atrophy task, errors were more frequent than in the binary tasks, reflecting the greater difficulty of distinguishing fine-grained atrophy patterns from endoscopic images. Overall, binary tasks showed clearer decision boundaries than fine-grained multi-class tasks. This pattern is consistent with the clinical difficulty of differentiating subtle endoscopic phenotypes and supports the use of EGID as a technically reliable but challenging multi-task benchmark.

Taken together, the technical validation demonstrates that EGID supports strict patient-level multi-task evaluation under an independent test protocol. The baseline experiments were not intended to establish a clinically optimized diagnostic model, but rather to verify dataset usability, label learnability, and technical consistency. The strong AUROC and AUPRC values achieved by DenseNet121 across the four tasks indicate that the dataset contains meaningful visual signals, while the lower performance in fine-grained multi-class tasks highlights its value as a challenging benchmark for future method development. Therefore, EGID can be regarded as a technically reliable and structurally well-organized public resource for research on H. pylori detection, gastric atrophy assessment, and gastritis classification based on endoscopic images.

Usage Notes

The dataset can be used for single-task classification, multi-class classification, or multi-label multi-task learning. For AI-based studies, users should perform data splitting at the patient level rather than at the image level, because each patient contributes multiple endoscopic images with shared patient-level labels. All images from the same patient should be assigned exclusively to one subset to avoid patient-level data leakage. The patient-level train/validation/test split provided in this study can be used as a reference protocol for reproducible benchmarking. Users should note that this dataset represents an Asian population from a single center, which should be considered when generalizing findings to other populations or clinical settings. Although EGID provides multi-label annotations across clinically relevant gastritis dimensions, its size remains relatively limited for image-level AI model development. Further expansion with more patients, images, centers, and endoscopy systems would better support AI model training, validation, and generalizability assessment. Users should cite this dataset and the associated publication when publishing research based on this resource. Feedback on dataset utility and potential improvements is welcome to support continuous refinement.
