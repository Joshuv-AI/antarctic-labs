USNDP Mission
The mission of the United States Nuclear Data Program (USNDP) is to provide current, accurate,
authoritative data for workers in pure and applied areas of nuclear science and engineering. This is
accomplished primarily through the compilation, evaluation, dissemination, and archiving of extensive
nuclear datasets. USNDP also addresses gaps in the data, through targeted experimental studies and the
use of theoretical models.

I. Introduction

The US Nuclear Data Program (USNDP) Annual Report for Fiscal Year 2025 summarizes the work of USNDP
for the period of October 1, 2024 through September 30, 2025, with respect to the Work Plan for FY 2025
that was prepared in 2024. The Work Plan and Final Report for USNDP are prepared for the DOE Office of
Science, Office of Nuclear Physics. The support for the nuclear data activity from sources outside the US
Nuclear Data Program is summarized in the staffing table and Appendix A. This leverage amounts to
about 22.8 FTE scientific, to be compared with 21.9 FTEs at USNDP units funded by the DOE Office of
Science, Office of Nuclear Physics. Since it is often difficult to separate accomplishments funded by
various sources, some of the work reported in the present report was accomplished with nuclear data
program support leveraged by other funding.

FY 2025 was the 25th year in which the USNDP has operated under a Work Plan developed by the program
participants. The program continued to carry out important work in support of the DOE mission. The work
balances the ongoing collecting, analyzing, and archiving of nuclear physics information critical to basic
nuclear research and to the development and improvement of nuclear technologies with the electronic
distribution of this information to users in a timely and easily accessible manner. The present section of
the report consists of activity summaries for the major components of the USNDP. This is followed by an
updated staff level assignment table that reflects the final distribution of effort among the tasks carried
out during FY 2025. Then, we continue with the detailed status of work performed during FY 2025.

1. Compilations: The sum of the new entries added to the USNDP bibliographic (NSR - articles) and

 nuclear databases. For ENSDF, it is the number of evaluated nuclides, while for ENDF, it is the number

 submitted. The number of ENSDF evaluations remains well below the number needed, about 340, to
 evaluate each of the ENSDF nuclides on average every 10 years.

 Data retrieval is defined as a request for data from any of the databases that receives a result. Total
 pages accessed is not tallied.

 remained relatively stable in the last few years. A list of all peer reviewed journal articles published in
 FY25 given in the Appendix C.
5. Invited talks: The number of presentations given at the explicit invitation of the organizers of
 conferences, symposia, workshops, and training courses. The number of invited talks decreased from
 last year's value.

II. Network Coordination and Data Dissemination
The National Nuclear Data Center (NNDC) continues to serve as the core facility of the U.S. Nuclear Data
Program (USNDP). It has the main responsibility for national and international coordination, database
maintenance, and data dissemination. However, other program participants are also involved in
coordination and dissemination activities.

National and International Coordination

The NNDC serves as the secretariat for the US Nuclear Data Program and has prepared the Work Plan for
this fiscal year in cooperation with the members of the Coordinating Committee. The NNDC Head serves
as the chair of the USNDP Coordinating Committee, which consists of the Principal Investigators from each
of the participating group and chairs the annual meeting. This year, the USNDP Annual Meeting was held
at Duke University at the Triangle University Nuclear Laboratory from 1-4 October. A representative from
LANL chairs the Nuclear Reaction Data Working Group, and a representative from TUNL chairs the Nuclear
Structure Working Group.

In FY25, coinciding with the beginning of the new administration, the USNDP did not hold an annual
Budget Briefing.

The NNDC serves as the focal point for U.S. collaboration in international nuclear data activities. This
collaboration continued both in nuclear structure and decay data (Network of Nuclear Structure and
Decay Data Evaluators, NSDD) and reaction data (NEA Working Party on International Nuclear Data
Evaluation, WPEC, and Network of Nuclear Reaction Data Centers, NRDC).

The NNDC also chairs the Cross Section Evaluation Working Group (CSEWG), which produces the ENDF/B
evaluated nuclear data library for nuclear science and applied nuclear technology use. The 2024 CSEWG
Meeting was hosted by BNL from 4-8 November, 2024. This meeting focused on validation of the ENDF/B-
VIII.1 data which was released in the previous FY. The NNDC also hosted the Reactor Graphite Workshop
from 8-9 July, 2025, to address issues in reactor graphite ENDF evaluations, important for next several
advance reactor concepts.

The USNDP also continues to play a leading role in the annual Workshop for Applied Nuclear Data
Activities (WANDA) conference series. The USNDP chaired a full day session on “Data Preservation & Data
Workflows” and a half day session on “Nuclear Data Prioritization for Fusion” during the February 2025
WANDA meeting.

USNDP Databases

NNDC maintains the collaboration GitLab collaboration platform (git.ndc.bnl.gov) in order to facilitate
data and codes development and keep track of changes. The NNDC maintains seven nuclear physics
databases for USNDP, which were updated continuously this fiscal year with new and revised information
from efforts of the NNDC, USNDP and international collaborators. Distributions of all or parts of these
databases have been made to national and international collaborators as scheduled.

III. Nuclear Structure and Decay Data
The nuclear structure working group emphasizes the evaluation of measured nuclear structure and
decay properties for all isotopes. These data are maintained at the NNDC in the Evaluated Nuclear
Structure Data File (ENSDF). Production of ENSDF is an international effort operating under the auspices
of the IAEA Nuclear Structure Decay Data (NSDD) network. ENSDF is an important source of information
for derivative databases and applications, including NuDat, Nuclear Wallet Cards, RIPL, MIRD and
ENDF/B. Evaluations are published as peer-reviewed articles in the Nuclear Data Sheets. The Nuclear
Science Reference (NSR) and Experimental Unevaluated Nuclear Structure Data List (XUNDL) databases
have been kept current. The combination of ENSDF and XUNDL databases represents a nearly complete
literature coverage of experimental nuclear structure data, which is a salient feature of these databases.

Status of ENSDF

ENSDF evaluations of 155 nuclides were submitted for inclusion into the database. These evaluations
included recommended properties for 10,155 Levels and 16,575 Gammas. 11 mass chains were
reviewed by the network for consideration for publication in the Nuclear Data Sheets journal. Three
new ENSDF evaluators were trained in FY25, one from MSU and two from LLNL.

Status of XUNDL

Based on regular scanning of nuclear physics journals, the USNDP contributed 640 datasets to XUNDL.
The project to compile and carry out physics checks on the data from nuclear structure and decay data
manuscripts, as well as some reaction papers, submitted to Phys. Rev. C and European Physical Journal A
continues.

Status of NSR

Historically, the NSR project involved contributions from NNDC staff members and external contractors.
In FY 2025, the external contractors were eliminated at Brookhaven, and the NSR manager worked to fill
this void. Despite these loses, 2,539 articles were added to the NSR database. Combined efforts of R.
Arcilla, B. Shu and B. Pritychenko are exploring various automation strategies including Java-based
systems for NSR entry preparation for 30 major nuclear physics and engineering journals, webscraping
of several other journals, and work on using Large Language Models. USNDP contributions are from B.
Pritychenko (manager), C. Dunn, B. Shu, and R. Arcilla (NNDC), with international input from L.
Vrapcenjak (IAEA).

Status of ENSDF Codes

The core legacy ENSDF codes used throughout the evaluation workflow have been modernized by Jun
Chen of the MSU data center into a suite of Java programs offering enhanced functionality and new
features. Under his leadership, the MSU center continues to maintain and improve these existing Java
codes while also developing new analysis and utility tools. A recent milestone is the development of the
Java-based EvaluationToolkit, which consolidates all ENSDF Java codes into a single, integrated interface
to automate and streamline the evaluation workflow.

In addition, a significant new initiative at the MSU data center is exploring the incorporation of Artificial
Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML) into the workflow. This project is led by Lijie Sun, a new
evaluator at the center. An AI-assisted ENSDF formatting tool has been developed under this initiative
and is already in active use for his evaluations, with ongoing improvements. Efforts are being made to
make a package of this tool available for and easily used by other evaluators in the near future, in
conjunction with the use of existing Java ENSDF codes.

The active code suite includes the McMaster-MSU JAVA-NDS code for producing print-ready documents
and ENSDF data retrievals; the ConsistencyCheck toolkit for ensuring evaluation consistency; Java-RULER
for calculating transition strengths with an improved Monte Carlo error propagation; GLSC (Gamma to
Level Scheme Computation), which replaces legacy codes for fitting gammas and calculating emission
probabilities; and the AME-NUBASE viewer for customized retrieval and automatic Q-value updates.
Additional essential utilities include KeynumberCheck and FormatCheck for data validation, the
Excel2ENSDF converter for data compilation, the RadiationReport and LogftCalculator for decay dataset
analysis, and the AlphaHF code for alpha decay calculations.

Efforts to develop new utility and analysis codes are ongoing, focusing on further streamlining,
automating, and accelerating the evaluation workflow to enhance overall efficiency and productivity. A
complete list and detailed descriptions of these codes are provided in Appendix B – USNDP Data and
Code Products.

IV. Nuclear Reaction Data
The nuclear reaction data effort focuses on evaluation of nuclear reaction data and the related
measurement and compilation activities. USNDP also makes important contributions to nuclear reaction
model code development and improvement of reaction cross-section standards.

Status of ENDF

devoted to complementary tasks. Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) associated with the data in the
ENDF/B-VIII.1, for the library as a whole and for each individual sublibrary,
were minted pointing to a landing page developed and maintained by the

to what could, and should, potentially be part of a future ENDF release,
likely to be labeled as ENDF/B-IX.0. Among these efforts we can highlight
the organization of the 1st Reactor Graphite (ReGra) Workshop, on July 8-9, 2025, aimed to discuss data,
modeling and validation associated with the evaluation of thermal neutron scattering law (TSL) on
graphite employed in reactor applications. A laboratory report (BNL-229060-2025-INRE) was published
documenting the presentations and discussions.

Reaction Theory

FY25 theory development at LANL highlights fully microscopic approaches to neutron-induced reactions,
pre-equilibrium processes, and nuclear data evaluation. Key advances include the development of the
non-iterative Finite Amplitude Method (FAM) combined with QRPA to calculate neutron inelastic
scattering for both discrete and continuum states without adjustable parameters, successfully
reproducing experimental data for 208Pb and extending to pre-equilibrium neutron emission. The work
also introduces a random-matrix (GOE S-matrix) framework to smoothly connect resolved resonance,
unresolved resonance, and Hauser–Feshbach regions, enabling realistic cross-section distributions, along
with progress on revisiting GEANIE gamma-ray data and separating reaction and fission contributions. In
addition, the LACE (Los Alamos CoH-based Evaluation) framework is presented as an automated, code-
driven approach to producing comprehensive evaluated nuclear data libraries, with future perspectives
emphasizing improved pre-equilibrium modeling, uncertainty treatment in the URR, and applications to
fusion and photonuclear reactions.

LLNL’s recent progress emphasizes in neutron cross section evaluation, with a focus on uncertainty
quantification, machine-learning methods, and actinide evaluations. Key advances include forward
propagation of reaction-model parameter uncertainties to produce activation cross sections with

USNDP FY2025 Report Page 10

covariances, validation of the YAHFC reaction code against experimental data, and the development of
AI/ML approaches—such as graph neural networks and Gaussian process models—to predict cross
sections and associated uncertainties, including extrapolation beyond measured regions. The work also
highlights the near-completion of the n+239Pu evaluation, combining re-fitted optical model
parameters, GMAP-based fission analyses incorporating fissionTPC data, Monte Carlo–based covariance
generation, and extensive validation against benchmarks, establishing a robust, uncertainty-aware
evaluation framework for nuclear data applications.

Reaction Measurements

New cross-section measurements for the 68Zn(n,p)68Cu and 68Zn(n,α)65Ni reactions were reported at
neutron energies from 4 to 12 MeV, addressing major data gaps and long-standing discrepancies in the
existing experimental literature. Using the LENZ detector system at the LANSCE/WNR facility in LANL and
pulse-shape discrimination in silicon detectors, differential and total cross sections were deduced with
well-quantified uncertainties, providing the first experimental data for the (n,p) channel below 12 MeV
and resolving inconsistencies in the (n,α) data in this energy region. The new measurements show
significant deviations from evaluated nuclear data libraries, motivating updated Hauser–Feshbach
calculations with modified optical model and level-density parameters that achieve improved
agreement across elastic, inelastic, capture, and charged-particle emission channels, thereby
strengthening the nuclear data foundation for fusion, accelerator-driven systems, and related
applications.

Further progress was made in a machine-learning–aided pulse shape discrimination method for particle
identification in silicon semiconductor detectors, combining detailed physics-based simulations with
supervised learning to overcome the limitations of traditional ΔE–E techniques for low-energy charged
particles. Realistic simulations of signal generation—including charge transport, bias-voltage effects, and
preamplifier low-pass filtering—successfully reproduce key features of measured proton and α-particle
waveforms from the LENZ instrument at LANSCE in LANL. Using derivatives of these simulated
waveforms as training data, a Random Forest classifier trained solely on simulations achieves better
than 95% accuracy when applied to experimental data, demonstrating that ML-based pulse shape
analysis can enhance detection efficiency, recover otherwise discarded events, and provide a robust,
scalable approach for analyzing scarce or challenging nuclear reaction data. This effort achieved high
particle-identification accuracy, and outlined future plans for radioactive-target development, expanded
LANSCE measurements, and broader application of AI/ML techniques to enhance scarce or damaged
nuclear data.

The Photon Strength Function (PSF) database at LBNL was updated as part of its annual revision cycle by
the end of 2025, incorporating 53 newly assessed data files for 13 nuclei, along with quality indicators
for Oslo-method data and outlier analyses to identify problematic data or models. The Nuclear Level
Density (NLD) database effort began in 2025 as part of a Coordinated Research Project, with
dissemination planned for 2028, and includes LBNL/UCB responsibilities such as compiling and assessing
NLD data and comparing models to experimental results. In addition, a four-year funded USNDP

evaluator traineeship has been established to support training in quasi-continuum nuclear data
evaluation.

Nuclear Astrophysics

The astrophysical impact was investigated using newly measured 56Ni(n,p)56Co reaction rates on νp-
process nucleosynthesis in proton-rich outflows from core-collapse supernovae, incorporating
experimental data from the LENZ program at LANSCE together with updated rates for several other key
reactions. By implementing the experimentally constrained 56Ni(n,p)56Co rate and its uncertainties into
network calculations across multiple hydrodynamic trajectories, the study shows that this reaction acts
as a major bottleneck, with a higher rate terminating the νp-process earlier and significantly reducing
the production of light p-nuclei such as 92,94Mo and 96,98Ru. Additional updates to reactions including
the triple-α process, 7Be(α,γ)11C, 10B(α,n)13N, 10B(α,p)13C, and 59Cu(p,α)56Ni further demonstrate
how nuclear physics uncertainties propagate into abundance predictions, highlighting the critical need
for improved experimental constraints and coordinated advances in both nuclear data and supernova
modeling to resolve persistent discrepancies in p-nuclei production.

 LLNL
 Livermore Laboratory has had a program for nuclear data for almost 60 years, and this has been often
 independent of the national ENDF library format. In that time, LLNL has made its own ENDL libraries in
 format designed for punched cards, and it has its own groups of evaluators leading to revisions about
 twice per decade. In the 70s the ENDL libraries were described by a comprehensive series of 20
 descriptive volumes published under the label of UCRL-50400.

 Because this original ENDL format was less flexible than the ENDF format, in the recent decade we
 have had some incentive to make more modern data structure, and this led us to designing, coding and
 translating the new Generalized Nuclear Data Structure (GNDS). This GNDS method is now maintained
 internationally by WPEC, and it is used internally at LLNL for all stages of the nuclear pipeline from
 decay models, data storage, translation, testing and processing for transport codes. It is the most
 comprehensive method for interchange of nuclear data and is becoming widely adopted as the
 preferred future standard.

 The nuclear data that LLNL uses comes from a variety of sources. We examine existing libraries to
 determine which provides the best description of nuclear cross sections, and often use our own
 models for neutron reactions and decay processes. We have optimized our own Hauser-Feshbach
 models for neutron reactions on a wide variety of fission fragments and other nuclides of use for
 radiochemistry. As one of the first labs to comprehensively transport both neutrons and charged
 particles (isotopes of hydrogen and helium) we have a comprehensive library of low-energy charged-
 particle evaluations, with particular attention to exothermic reactions on targets up to lithium
 isotopes.

 In the last 15 years the experimental groups at LLNL have been using the indirect ‘surrogate’ method
 to measure cross-sections for which direct detection is unavailable, and the results have been made
 into GNDS evaluations to determine the effects of the new cross-sections. We test all evaluations by
 comparison with the standard database of critical-assembly measurements and also by comparison
 with the pulsed-sphere measurements once performed at LLNL. New critical assemblies are being
 created, measured and modeled. The national collaboration to use a Time Projection Chamber to
 measure actinide and standard cross-sections is being led by LLNL experimentalists. We have used
 both ENDL and ENDF processed libraries in transport codes for programmatic work.

 The USNDP provides a small contribution to the LLNL nuclear data team to enable the sharing of in-
 house data products with the national community. Our GNDS work is funded outside USNDP and has
 provided thorough checks of the ENDF libraries submitted by various groups.
