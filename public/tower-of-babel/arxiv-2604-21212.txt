Legal Infrastructure Organizes Eviction: Evidence from Philadelphia
Marios Papamichalis, Regina Ruane

Abstract
We examine how legal infrastructure organizes eviction in Philadelphia. Using 755,004 Philadelphia landlord–tenant court records filed from 1969 to 2022, we show that eviction is concentrated most strongly among plaintiff-side attorneys. In a typical year, the 10 most active plaintiff attorneys, about 3–4% of active plaintiff attorneys, handle 82.0% of represented cases. Filing is also highly routinized. It is largely same-plaintiff filing, concentrated at the same addresses, and reproduced through recurring plaintiff–attorney–property combinations. Eviction, in short, is organized through repeat actors and repeat places. Specialist attorney plaintiff-side counsel changes how cases are handled inside that system. When plaintiffs adopt specialist attorney counsel, filings rise and repeated use of the same addresses increases, although those filing-margin shifts appear to reflect broader reorganization around counsel entry. In stronger within-plaintiff and within-plaintiff–property comparisons, specialist attorney counsel is associated with fewer judgments by agreement, a lower fee share, and much less lockout-trigger language, with weaker evidence for default and downstream enforcement. That structure extends into the courtroom. Court is not a neutral stage: judges and repeated lawyer pairings shape default, agreement, enforcement, and settlement terms. Overall, eviction is organized through a concentrated plaintiff-side bar, repeat places, structured courtroom relationships, and the production of contracts and debt inside court.



Significance Statement

Eviction is usually measured as a filing, but a filing is only one stage in a longer legal process. Court records make it possible to observe who drives that process, where it repeats, how cases move through court, how agreements are written, and how debt is added to possession claims. This paper does two things. First, it maps eviction as a concentrated legal infrastructure organized through a plaintiff-side bar, recurring plaintiff–attorney servicing relations, repeated properties, and standardized contractual forms. Second, it identifies a narrower mechanism inside that infrastructure: when plaintiffs adopt specialist plaintiff-side counsel, conditional case processing shifts, especially in bargaining, fee burden, and lockout-trigger language. This shifts the focus of eviction from isolated filings to the legal intermediaries, repeated organizational pathways, and reusable settlement forms that structure displacement at scale.

1
Introduction

This paper investigates a set of foundational but still empirically under-specified questions about eviction in Philadelphia. We study how plaintiffs, plaintiff attorneys, defense attorneys, judges, properties, and tenants are connected inside the eviction process. We ask the following questions. To what extent is eviction concentrated among property owners, as opposed to the attorneys who represent them? Are filings mainly driven by landlords themselves, or by the lawyers they use? Do plaintiffs who hire the same attorney begin to file in similar ways? Do the same landlords and the same buildings return to court again and again? When an address is filed on repeatedly, do those cases end differently? Are outcomes shaped by judges and by repeated encounters between the same landlord and tenant lawyers? Does the court itself function as a site of debt generation through fees and costs added to possession claims? And once a case enters court, how are agreement terms and the path to enforcement shaped? A narrower causal question follows from those descriptive questions: when plaintiffs adopt specialist plaintiff-side counsel, does filing behavior or conditional case processing change?

We find that eviction is organized through legal infrastructure. A small set of plaintiff attorneys handles a very large share of cases, and plaintiffs are closely tied to those attorneys. Filing is routinized through repetition: the same plaintiff–attorney–property combinations appear again and again. Courtroom outcomes are also shaped by repeated relationships. Judges are linked unevenly to particular plaintiffs and attorneys, and repeated lawyer pairings produce different mixes of default, agreement, and enforcement. Additional profiling shows that most repeat triads are portfolio-embedded: the plaintiff–attorney pair typically spans multiple addresses, and the triad operates as an address-level implementation of that broader servicing relation rather than as an isolated single-address pipeline. Finally, eviction court governs not only possession but also contracts and debt. Attorneys shape the terms of agreements, repeated actors and properties shape fees and awards, and the court produces monetary burdens alongside displacement. In the agreement-text module, however, the strongest clause-level regularities are overwhelmingly template-driven, indicating governance through reusable forms rather than purely discretionary attorney drafting.

The paper also advances a narrower quasi-causal claim inside that broader institutional account. Using lagged specialist-counsel adoption, stacked plaintiff-month event studies, and within-plaintiff and within-plaintiff–property case comparisons, we estimate whether entry into specialist plaintiff-side counsel changes how plaintiffs use eviction court and how filed cases are processed. That claim is intentionally narrower than a general claim that legal infrastructure “causes eviction.” It concerns one mechanism inside the infrastructure, concentrated plaintiff-side legal intermediation, and the evidence is strongest for bargaining, contract design, and fee composition rather than for every downstream legal stage.

The paper’s principal contribution is therefore twofold. First, it re-specifies eviction as legal infrastructure. Rather than treating filings as isolated landlord reactions to tenant breach, the paper shows that eviction is routed through a concentrated plaintiff-side bar, repeated across routinized attorneys, judges and plaintiffs, processed within a structured courtroom field, and governed through contract and debt as well as possession. Second, it identifies one mechanism inside that infrastructure. The Philadelphia court record on evictions makes it possible not only to map that institutional architecture, but also to estimate how specialist plaintiff-side counsel changes the processing of filed cases.

3
Data, measurement windows, and empirical strategy

3.1
Data

The analysis draws on 755,004 landlord–tenant case records from the Philadelphia Municipal Court, spanning filing years 1969–2022. Restricting to residential cases yields the main analytic universe of 747,125 filings. Each filing may contain information on filing date, party identities, attorney representation, judge, address, procedural events, monetary claims and awards, free-text agreement fields, writ issuance, and service of the alias writ of possession. Throughout the paper, “served writ” refers to service of the alias writ and is used as the principal enforcement indicator.

The paper studies several nested universes within that record. Plaintiff- and attorney-concentration analyses use the years in which plaintiff-side attorney coverage is substantively informative. Cross-side attorney-pair analyses use the both-represented, both-attorney-named universe (
n=26{,}259
). Fee analyses use the modern fee universe, where award-side fee components are substantively informative. The JBA text module uses 220,622 non-empty agreement texts. Judge, dyad, triad, and tenant-history modules are estimated on the subsets of residential cases for which the relevant identifiers are observed.

Coverage is therefore not uniform across modules. Plaintiff-side attorney names are principally informative among represented cases; fee variables are most reliable in the modern period; and JBA text is observed for a large but selected subset of agreement cases. The analysis is organized around those observation boundaries rather than treating all fields as if they were equally informative over the full filing window. Because the extract is administrative rather than sample-based, the descriptive statistics reported below are population summaries of the observed court record rather than survey-weighted estimates.

The relevant implication is that 1969–2022 is an archival horizon, not a single uniformly informative analytic window. In a coverage audit of usable-field shares, the stable windows begin in 1983 for plaintiff-side attorney names, 1999 for cross-side attorney pairs, 2005 for fee fields, 1976 for non-empty JBA text, and 1988 for attorney-identified JBA text. The paper therefore distinguishes the full docket from the narrower windows on which particular actor, fee, and contract claims rest.

3.3
Core measures

The paper uses four families of measures, each designed to capture a distinct dimension of the eviction process.

Legal infrastructure and concentration.

Plaintiff and attorney concentration are summarized annually using two complementary indices. For year
y
, the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index is

HHI_{y}=\sum_{e}s_{ey}^{2},

and the top-10 filing share is

Top10Share_{y}=\sum_{e\in T10(y)}s_{ey},

where
s_{ey}
is entity
e
’s filing share in year
y
, and
T10(y)
is the set of the ten most active entities in that year. Plaintiff dependence on counsel is additionally summarized through plaintiff-specific top-1 attorney shares, top-attorney entry timing, within-plaintiff attorney switches, and shared-counsel clustering.

Repeated units.

A unique address identifier is constructed from cleaned address strings, with sequential fallbacks to rounded latitude–longitude coordinates and then ZIP Code
\times
census-tract combinations. For each filing
i
, we compute the count of prior filings at the same address within the preceding 365-day window and assign the case to an ordinal churn category
\{0,1,2,3\text{--}5,6+\}
. Same-plaintiff same-address repetition, plaintiff–attorney repetition, and plaintiff–attorney–property triads are constructed from linked identifiers. Tenant recurrence is measured through repeated tenant-name appearances, repeated same-address appearances, and repeated same-plaintiff appearances. We also construct indicators for whether a case involves a tenant with no prior observed eviction history.

Courtroom governance.

Judge sorting is summarized through top-
k
judge–actor contingency tables and chi-square association tests. Judge outcome regimes, judge fee regimes, and judge-specific JBA variation are estimated from residualized pooled ANOVA designs. Repeated attorney-pair exposure is measured descriptively and through pair-level regime tests. Bargaining-field versus adjudication-field comparisons are evaluated through relative model fit in the JBA text sample.

Contract and debt governance.

For each case
i
with a positive total monetary award, fee share is defined as

FeeShare_{i}=\frac{AttorneyFees_{i}+Costs_{i}+OtherFees_{i}}{TotalAward_{i}}.

As a supplementary measure, the paper computes the ratio of total award to amount sought when both quantities are positive, capturing the divergence between the amount originally claimed and the amount ultimately imposed. For JBA cases with available text, agreement and order fields are concatenated and clause indicators are extracted using regular-expression matching, including move-out language, payment-plan provisions, explicit deadlines, lockout triggers, and waiver provisions. These features are aggregated into a composite strictness index. Downstream agreement-governance models additionally track same-address refiling, no recorded satisfaction, served writ, and breach-related outcomes.

3.4
Empirical strategy

The empirical strategy is hybrid. The paper’s main objective remains to characterize how eviction is organized as a legal process, and most claims are descriptive or associational. At the same time,
Section
4.1
estimates a narrower quasi-causal mechanism: whether plaintiff adoption of specialist plaintiff-side counsel changes filing behavior and conditional case processing. The strongest descriptive claims are advanced where the data approximate the observed universe of filed cases. Model-based analyses are used to clarify mechanism, compare organizational levels, and document institutional heterogeneity.

For the quasi-causal module, specialist plaintiff-side counsel is defined using lagged plaintiff-side attorney activity: an attorney is coded specialist in year
y
if the attorney falls in the prior-year top decile of represented plaintiff-side filing volume, with robustness to prior-year top-10 and top-5% definitions. Let
A_{p}
denote plaintiff
p
’s first month of specialist-counsel adoption. Filing-margin estimates use stacked event studies of the form

Y_{pst}=\alpha_{ps}+\lambda_{ts}+\sum_{k\neq-1}\beta_{k}\mathbf{1}\{t-A_{p}=k\}+\varepsilon_{pst},

where
\alpha_{ps}
are plaintiff-by-stack fixed effects and
\lambda_{ts}
are calendar-month-by-stack fixed effects. Conditional-on-filing estimates use

Y_{ipt}=\alpha_{p}+\lambda_{t}+\beta Specialist_{ipt}+X_{ipt}\gamma+\varepsilon_{ipt},

and, in the strongest design,

Y_{ipt}=\alpha_{p\times property}+\lambda_{t}+\beta Specialist_{ipt}+X_{ipt}\gamma+\varepsilon_{ipt}.

We report joint lead tests and post-adoption composition checks for the event studies. Accordingly, filing-margin estimates are interpreted as quasi-causal only to the extent supported by those diagnostics, whereas within-plaintiff and especially within-plaintiff–property estimates are treated as the paper’s strongest evidence on the specialist-counsel mechanism.

The four results sections each rely on a distinct but related empirical design.
Section
4.1
combines annual concentration summaries and plaintiff-year dependence measures with a specialist-counsel adoption module composed of stacked plaintiff-month event studies, adoption-window diagnostics, within-plaintiff case models, within-plaintiff–property case models, first-touch heterogeneity tests, and robustness checks.
Section
4.2
combines repeated-address decompositions, tenant-history summaries, repeated-unit regressions, and residualized ANOVAs comparing attorneys, plaintiffs, addresses, plaintiff–attorney pairs, and plaintiff–attorney–property triads.
Section
4.3
uses judge–actor sorting tables, pooled judge regime tests, repeated attorney-pair regime comparisons, and bargaining-field versus adjudication-field fit comparisons.
Section
4.4
uses pooled attorney JBA-style tests, fee-driver ANOVAs across actors and repeated units, fee-outcome regressions, and downstream agreement-governance models.

Binary outcomes are estimated with linear probability models. Continuous outcomes such as strictness, fee share, and award/request ratios are estimated with OLS. Sorting questions are evaluated with chi-square association tests. Actor- and unit-level heterogeneity is summarized with residualized pooled ANOVA designs and
\eta^{2}
effect sizes so that attorneys, plaintiffs, addresses, plaintiff–attorney pairs, and triads can be compared on a common scale. Where the paper uses stacked adoption designs, within-plaintiff specifications, or within-plaintiff–property specifications, those estimates are interpreted as the paper’s narrow quasi-causal evidence on specialist counsel. The paper’s central contribution, however, lies in documenting the institutional organization of eviction—who concentrates filings, through which repeated units those filings are routinized, how courtroom governance is structured, and how contracts and debt are layered onto possession claims.

4.1
Concentrated plaintiff-side legal infrastructure

The strongest concentration in eviction lies in the plaintiff-side legal bar, not in the plaintiff population. Across 1983–2022, the mean top-10 filing share is 0.820 for plaintiff attorneys and 0.147 for plaintiffs, while the corresponding mean HHI values are 0.106 and 0.007. This gap survives denominator alignment. When plaintiff concentration is recomputed on the same represented and named-attorney universe used for attorney concentration, the mean plaintiff top-10 share rises only to 0.205, still far below the attorney value of 0.820. 4.2
Routinized production through repeated units

Repeated filing is not a small tail of the docket. In the residential filing window from 1969–2022, 50.6% of cases arise at addresses with at least one prior filing in the preceding year, and 24.6% arise at addresses with six or more prior filings. These repeated addresses do not simply experience more court activity. They also move through the court differently. The default rate rises from 45.9% at churn 0 to 57.9% at churn 6+, while the JBA rate declines from 32.5% to 23.3%. By contrast, writ issuance remains comparatively stable across churn categories, and served-writ rates are actually lower at the highest-churn addresses than at addresses with no prior filings. Repeated-address filing is therefore associated less with intensified enforcement than with a distinct procedural pathway that sorts cases toward default and away from negotiated resolution.

Those gradients are not driven only by unusually large or otherwise atypical properties. In the controlled churn models, which add address-scale proxies, amount sought, representation, housing status, ZIP fixed effects, and year fixed effects, churn 6+ remains associated with a 4.86 percentage-point increase in default (
p<0.001
) and a 7.88 percentage-point reduction in JBA (
p<0.001
) relative to churn 0. The same address is therefore not just returning to court more often. It is being processed through a different and more default-oriented channel (

4.3
Courtroom governance

Court outcomes are not distributed randomly across judges and lawyers. They are structured by who appears before whom, and by repeated encounters between the same plaintiff and defense attorneys. These results are based on the judge-linked residential sample of 54,273 cases observed within the broader 1969–2022 residential file, together with the represented dyad sample of 26,259 both-represented and both-attorney-named cases.

4.4
Contract and debt governance

Eviction court governs not only possession, but also contracts and debt. The JBA text universe contains 220,622 non-empty agreement texts, of which 84,099 attorney-identified texts enter the pooled attorney harshness ANOVA. 5
Discussion, conclusion, and limitations

5.1
Principal contribution

The paper’s central contribution is to show that eviction is organized through legal infrastructure. The Philadelphia court record reveals four linked features of that infrastructure. First, the strongest concentration in the system lies in the plaintiff-side legal bar rather than in the plaintiff population. Second, repeated filing is produced through repeated operational units, especially same-plaintiff repeat-address cases and recurring plaintiff–attorney–property triads that usually reflect portfolio-embedded servicing relations rather than single-address monopolies. Third, courtroom outcomes are structured by judges, repeated attorney pairings, and the courtroom relationships through which cases are bargained and enforced, although the text-based dyad comparison is too sparse for generalizable fit claims. Fourth, eviction court governs not only possession, but also contracts and debt through agreement terms, fees, costs, and monetary awards; in the agreement-text module, the strongest clause-level regularities are overwhelmingly template-driven.

Taken together, these findings recast eviction as more than a response to nonpayment or lease violation. Eviction appears instead as a structured legal process that organizes filing, bargaining, enforcement, and monetary extraction at scale. The plaintiff-side bar is the system’s main organizational bottleneck; repeated properties and portfolio-embedded triads are its clearest operational units; courtroom governance is relational rather than neutral and interchangeable; and standardized forms are a core instrument through which contract and debt are layered onto possession claims.

5.2
Policy implications

This interpretation changes the relevant object of intervention. If the clearest repeated operational object is often a portfolio-embedded plaintiff–attorney servicing relation, then regulation should track plaintiff–attorney portfolios as well as single buildings. If the largest clause differences are carried by reusable forms, then template review, form-document regulation, and oversight of standardized settlement language become as important as actor-specific sanctions. The quasi-causal results sharpen that implication: if specialist counsel changes bargaining frequency, fee composition, and contractual enforcement language even within the same plaintiff or the same plaintiff–property unit, then plaintiff-side legal intermediation is not merely a correlate of scale; it is itself a policy-relevant mechanism. If concentrated legal intermediaries organize throughput, then interventions aimed only at landlords will miss an important institutional layer. If repeated filing is strongly same-plaintiff and same-address, then repeated filing itself becomes a measurable target of regulation. If fee extraction is structured through repeated actors and properties, then debt regulation and fee caps become part of housing-stability policy rather than an ancillary concern. And if agreement terms vary systematically with attorney-linked forms and the repeated legal settings in which those forms are deployed, then settlement content should be treated as an object of governance rather than as a procedural black box.

5.3
Epistemic scope and limitations

The paper does not claim strong causal identification for every actor or legal stage. Its causal claim is narrower. The quasi-causal module concerns one mechanism inside the broader legal infrastructure: plaintiff adoption of specialist plaintiff-side counsel. Even there, the evidence is stronger for conditional case processing than for filing-margin responses. The stacked plaintiff-month event studies show substantial post-adoption shifts, but joint lead tests are non-flat for filing count, repeat-address share, default, and JBA, and several composition measures also change around adoption. Those estimates are therefore interpreted as adoption-linked reorganization rather than clean treatment effects on plaintiff throughput.

A second scope condition concerns measurement and model support rather than identification alone. The paper does not treat the full 1969–2022 archive as a single uniform measurement regime: actor-rich and fee-rich modules rely on later stable windows, which are now reported explicitly rather than implied by the archival horizon. Likewise, the largest attorney-associated differences in agreement language are largely template effects, and the text-based attorney-pair comparison is too sparse to support generalizable holdout performance. Those revisions do not negate the organizational claims; they discipline their scope.

By contrast, the within-plaintiff and within-plaintiff–property specifications provide more credible evidence on how filed cases are processed once specialist counsel is used. Still, those designs remain observational. They do not eliminate all time-varying plaintiff strategy, unobserved portfolio changes, or measurement error in attorney assignment and settlement text. The judge results continue to document structured courtroom governance rather than randomized judge effects, and the dyad results continue to describe repeated bargaining relationships rather than universal pair-specific treatment effects. The paper’s main claim is therefore two-tiered: descriptive for the system as a whole, and quasi-causal for the narrower specialist-counsel mechanism.

These limitations do not weaken the paper’s contribution. They clarify it. The paper documents the organizational architecture through which eviction is concentrated, routinized, governed, and monetized, and it identifies one mechanism inside that architecture through which specialist legal intermediation changes bargaining, contract design, and fee burden.

Supplementary Information

This supplement reports additional analyses that support both the main paper’s system-level claim that eviction in Philadelphia is organized through legal infrastructure and its narrower quasi-causal claim about specialist plaintiff-side counsel. In addition to the descriptive modules reported in the main text, it includes stacked adoption event studies, adoption-window composition diagnostics, within-plaintiff and within-plaintiff–property case models, first-touch heterogeneity tests, and robustness checks for alternative lagged specialist definitions. These supplementary results are used to distinguish adoption-linked reorganization on the filing margin from the more credible within-unit evidence on conditional case processing.

Unless otherwise noted, the full court-record extract contains 755,004 landlord–tenant cases filed between 1969 and 2022, of which 747,125 are residential. Modules are estimated on narrower universes when the relevant identifiers or text fields are observed. The plaintiff-attorney concentration modules rely on 1983–2022 filing years, the both-represented and both-attorney-named universe contains 26,259 cases, the JBA text universe contains 220,622 non-empty texts, and the fee modules are substantively informative in the modern period, with the stable coverage window beginning in 2005. All descriptive quantities are summaries of the observed administrative record, while model-based analyses are interpreted associationally unless the design explicitly compares the same plaintiff or the same plaintiff–property unit over time.

A supplementary coverage audit sharpens that point. Appendix D
JBA and fee governance as system consequences

D1
Agreement-text validity and contract governance

This section treats JBA and fee outcomes as system consequences rather than as the deeper bargaining and dyadic objects reserved for the companion courtroom paper. It reports the prevalence of agreement-text features, the non-monotone but persistently elevated fee series, the structure of fee extraction across actors and repeated units, and the way debt-heavy cases sort away from negotiated resolution.

The JBA text universe remains large and informative. Among 220,622 non-empty agreement texts, deadline language appears in 26.3% of cases, move-out language in 13.9%, lockout-trigger language in 9.6%, payment-plan language in 15.1%, and waiver language in 5.3%. The mean strictness score is 0.742. The additive strictness index remains highly aligned with a PCA-based alternative (correlation
r=0.722
), and the first principal component explains 30.6% of clause-feature variance. The agreement-text measures therefore capture a real and stable contractual dimension of the docket (
Table
D1
;
Figure
D1
).

A second validity issue is whether large attorney
\eta^{2}
values in JBA text reflect discretionary style or template reuse. The template audit strongly supports the latter. Raw attorney
\eta^{2}
equals 0.1697 for strictness, 0.3665 for waiver, 0.3029 for lockout-trigger language, 0.1352 for payment-plan language, and 0.0690 for move-out language. But normalized agreement-template identifiers explain far more of the same variation: raw template
\eta^{2}
is 0.9315 for strictness, 0.8968 for waiver, 0.9298 for lockout-trigger language, 0.9561 for payment-plan language, and 0.9159 for move-out language. Once template fixed effects are absorbed, residual attorney
\eta^{2}
falls to 0.0022, 0.0041, 0.0024, 0.0014, and 0.0015, respectively. The implication is that the largest attorney-associated clause differences are mostly governance through reusable forms rather than generic attorney style.

The fee series remains elevated through the modern period, but it is not monotone. Mean fee share rises from 1.4% in 2004 to 9.1% in 2010, remains 8.2% in 2019, rises to 13.5% in 2021, and then falls to 4.1% in 2022. Award-to-request ratios exceed unity in every modern year shown, indicating that court-imposed debt frequently exceeds the amount originally sought. The point is not a simple upward trend.
