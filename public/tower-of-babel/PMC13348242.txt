Association between nephrolithiasis and kidney disease progression in patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hongran Moon, Yong-Chul Kim, Hayne Cho Park, Taek Min Kim, Sung Yong Cho, Kook-Hwan Oh, Curie Ahn, Hyunjin Ryu, Yun Kyu Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNephrolithiasis is a common complication in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), yet its impact on long-term kidney outcomes remains uncertain. Although experimental and retrospective studies suggest a potential role of kidney stones in accelerating disease progression, prospective evidence based on imaging-confirmed nephrolithiasis is limited.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 410 patients with ADPKD from the HOPE-PKD cohort who underwent baseline abdominal computed tomography (CT) within 1 year of enrollment. Nephrolithiasis was defined as radiopaque lesions located within the collecting system, whereas cyst wall calcifications were excluded based on established CT criteria. The primary outcome was initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT) due to kidney failure. The secondary outcome was a composite of ≥50% decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate, doubling of serum creatinine, or initiation of RRT. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed with adjustment for demographic, metabolic, genetic, and imaging-based risk factors, including imaging-based risk stratification.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 5.18 years, 51 (12.4%) and 96 (23.4%) participants reached the primary and secondary endpoints, respectively. After adjustment for demographic, metabolic, and disease severity factors, the stone group showed a significantly higher risk of both the primary [Hazard ratio (HR) 2.84; 95% Confidence interval (CI) 1.26–6.38; P = .012] and secondary (HR 1.83; 95% CI 1.03–3.27; P = .041) endpoints compared with the no-stone group. Associations appear to be more pronounced in males, individuals with higher body mass index, worse baseline kidney function, larger kidney volume, and PKD1 genotype.ConclusionsImaging-confirmed nephrolithiasis is independently associated with adverse kidney outcomes in patients with ADPKD. Early detection and targeted management of kidney stones may improve risk stratification and kidney prognosis in this population.Keywords: autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, kidney failure, nephrolithiasis, prognosis, risk stratification

INTRODUCTION

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common inherited kidney disorder and a leading cause of kidney failure [1]. Disease progression in ADPKD is heterogeneous and influenced by genetic background, total kidney volume (TKV), and metabolic factors. In addition to cyst-related complications, nephrolithiasis is a frequent but often underrecognized comorbidity in this population [2–5].

The prevalence of nephrolithiasis in patients with ADPKD is substantially higher than in the general population, likely due to structural distortion of the kidney parenchyma, urinary stasis, and metabolic abnormalities [6, 7]. In non-ADPKD populations, kidney stones are well established as a risk factor for chronic kidney disease progression and kidney failure [8, 9]. However, the independent contribution of nephrolithiasis to kidney function deterioration in ADPKD remains uncertain.

Existing evidence suggests a potential link between nephrolithiasis and disease progression in ADPKD, but remains limited. Experimental studies have demonstrated that increased renal crystal burden can directly accelerate cyst growth and functional decline, providing biological plausibility for a contributory role of nephrolithiasis in ADPKD progression [10]. Clinical evidence, however, is largely derived from retrospective analyses. A post hoc analysis of the OVERTURE trial reported a greater decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and an increased risk of kidney failure among patients with a history of kidney stones [11], while a retrospective cohort study showed that even asymptomatic nephrolithiasis was independently associated with more rapid disease progression [12]. Despite these observations, longitudinal human studies with imaging-confirmed stone definitions and comprehensive adjustment for key prognostic factors remain scarce.

Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) is the most accurate, reference-standard imaging modality for detecting nephrolithiasis [13], yet stone assessment is not routinely incorporated into ADPKD risk evaluation. Although the PROPKD score, a validated prognostic tool for predicting kidney outcomes in ADPKD, incorporates early ‘urologic events,’ these are defined as gross hematuria/cyst hemorrhage, cyst infection, or cyst-related flank pain, and imaging-confirmed nephrolithiasis is not explicitly included as a prognostic variable in commonly used tools [14]. Prospective studies using imaging-confirmed stone definitions and kidney endpoints are therefore needed to clarify the clinical significance of nephrolithiasis in ADPKD.

In this study, we investigated the association between CT-confirmed nephrolithiasis and kidney outcomes in a prospective ADPKD cohort. By comprehensively adjusting for demographic, metabolic, genetic, and imaging-based risk factors, we aimed to determine whether nephrolithiasis independently predicts kidney disease progression in ADPKD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This study was conducted within the prospective Cohort for Genotype–Phenotype Correlation in Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (HOPE-PKD), a multicenter cohort designed to investigate the natural history of ADPKD in Korea [15]. The HOPE-PKD registry was initiated in 2009; however, during the early phase of the registry, imaging studies were performed according to clinical indication, and a standardized CT-based volumetric imaging protocol had not yet been implemented. In April 2011, a dedicated CT protocol for standardized assessment of nephrolithiasis and volumetric measurement of height-adjusted total kidney volume (htTKV) was established at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH). Therefore, the present study represents a single-center CT-imaged subset of the broader HOPE-PKD registry, including patients enrolled at SNUH between April 2011 and February 2016 who had CT imaging available within ±1 year of enrollment.

Among patients enrolled at SNUH between April 2011 and February 2016, 496 individuals were initially screened. Patients without an abdominal CT scan performed within one year before or after enrollment (n = 78) and those lost to follow-up (n = 8) were excluded. The final study population consisted of 410 patients, who were prospectively followed until March 2022 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart depicting the selection of study subjects. Among the 496 patients recruited in the HOPE-PKD cohort at Seoul National University Hospital, individuals who did not receive an abdominal CT scan within 1 year of enrollment or were lost to follow-up (n = 86) were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 410 patients. Patients were classified as either having nephrolithiasis (stone group, n = 80) or no urinary tract stones (no-stone group, n = 330), according to CT results.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of SNUH (IRB No. 1205-112-411), and all participants provided written informed consent. All participants were tolvaptan-naïve at baseline, as tolvaptan was not available for clinical use at SNUH during the enrollment period.

Imaging assessment and definition of nephrolithiasis

Baseline abdominal CT scans were reviewed by an experienced abdominal imaging specialist under the supervision of board-certified radiologists. Radiopaque lesions located within the collecting system, including the papilla, calyx, renal pelvis, ureter, or bladder, were classified as nephrolithiasis, whereas calcifications located along cyst walls or outside the collecting system were considered cyst calcifications. When lesion location was ambiguous, discrete or nodular round lesions were classified as stones, whereas curvilinear peripheral calcifications or milk-of-calcium appearances were classified as cyst calcifications, in accordance with previously described radiologic criteria [16].

Based on CT findings, patients were categorized into a stone group and a no-stone group.

Clinical variables and laboratory measurements

Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and blood pressure. Laboratory parameters assessed at baseline comprised eGFR, hemoglobin, serum uric acid, phosphorus, calcium, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, and total cholesterol. The plasma metabolic parameters were selected a priori based on their clinical relevance to nephrolithiasis and kidney disease progression, their availability as standardized baseline laboratory measurements in the cohort, and their role as potential confounders associated with kidney outcomes. The eGFR was calculated using the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation for non-Black individuals [17]. Urinary indices included spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and protein-to-creatinine ratio.

Imaging-based risk stratification and genetic analysis

The Mayo imaging classification was determined using established criteria, which stratify typical ADPKD into classes 1A–1E based on age and htTKV, with class 2 designated for atypical morphology [18]. Genetic analysis of PKD1 and PKD2 was performed using targeted exome sequencing combined with Sanger sequencing for PKD1 exon 1 and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, as previously described [15, 19]. Genetic variants were categorized as PKD1 protein-truncating, PKD1 non-truncating, PKD2 variants, or no mutation detected.

Quantification of htTKV was performed using baseline CT images. Kidney areas were measured on each contiguous axial slice using semi-automated, slice-by-slice segmentation with ImageJ software (version 1.5a; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). TKV was then calculated by summing the products of slice thickness and measured areas across all slices, in accordance with stereological volumetric principles. htTKV was obtained by dividing TKV by the patient’s height (in meters) and was used for subsequent analyses. This approach is consistent with previously described volumetric methods in ADPKD [20].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT), including hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation, due to the development of kidney failure. The secondary outcome was a composite of ≥50% decline in eGFR from baseline, doubling of serum creatinine, or initiation of RRT. In addition, the annual eGFR slope was calculated using serial outpatient eGFR measurements, with a minimum of four measurements per patient.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed by visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on whether their distribution was approximately normal. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Associations between nephrolithiasis and kidney outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Sequential models included an unadjusted model; Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 2 additionally adjusted for baseline eGFR and serum uric acid, phosphorus, and calcium; Model 3 further adjusted for comorbidities of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and genotype; and Model 4 additionally adjusted for Mayo imaging classification. The proportional hazards assumption was formally assessed by testing a time-dependent interaction between nephrolithiasis status and log-transformed follow-up time. When evidence of non-proportional hazards was identified, additional time-stratified Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed using outcome-specific cut-points based on the median follow-up time.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model-based adjusted survival curves were generated based on the fully adjusted Cox regression model.

Annual eGFR slope was analyzed using generalized linear mixed models with random intercepts and slopes. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, BMI, baseline eGFR, genotype, Mayo imaging classification, and htTKV. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0).

Kidney outcomes

During a median follow-up of 5.18 years (interquartile range, 3.41–8.03), 51 patients (12.4%) progressed to kidney failure requiring RRT, and 96 patients (23.4%) experienced the composite kidney outcome (Table 2). The incidence of the primary outcome did not differ significantly between groups in unadjusted analyses. Consistent with this, nephrolithiasis was not significantly associated with the primary outcome in the unadjusted Cox model or after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI (Model 1). After further adjustment for baseline kidney function and metabolic variables (Model 2), nephrolithiasis became significantly associated with adverse kidney outcomes, and this association remained significant in subsequent models. The HR changed little after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, but increased more noticeably after further adjustment for baseline kidney function and metabolic variables, suggesting that the association between nephrolithiasis and kidney outcomes became more apparent after accounting for baseline disease severity. Additional adjustment for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, genotype (Model 3), and Mayo imaging (Model 4) classification resulted in only modest further changes. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the stone group had a significantly higher risk of progression to kidney failure compared with the no-stone group (HR 2.84, 95% CI 1.26–6.38; P = .012) (Table 3).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

In sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with typical ADPKD morphology (Mayo classes 1A–1E), the association between nephrolithiasis and adverse kidney outcomes remained consistent (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Explorative subgroup analyses suggested that the association was more pronounced in certain high-risk subgroups, including males, patients with higher BMI, reduced baseline eGFR, increased htTKV, and PKD1 genotype (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort study of 410 patients with ADPKD, nephrolithiasis was not significantly associated with kidney outcomes in unadjusted analyses, but was associated with a higher risk of progression to kidney failure after multivariable adjustment. For the primary outcome, this association emerged after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, baseline kidney function, and metabolic variables (Model 2), and remained significant in the more fully adjusted models (Models 3 and 4). In the fully adjusted model including age, sex, BMI, baseline eGFR, uric acid, phosphorus, calcium, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, genotype, and Mayo imaging classification, nephrolithiasis was associated with an approximately 3-fold higher risk of kidney failure. For the secondary outcome, the association was not statistically significant in the unadjusted or partially adjusted models, but became significant in the model 3 and fully adjusted model (model 4). This association was more pronounced among patients with established high-risk features, including male sex, higher BMI, reduced kidney function, increased htTKV, and PKD1 genotype. To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study to demonstrate nephrolithiasis as an independent risk factor for kidney disease progression in ADPKD using CT-based stone classification.

In the general population, nephrolithiasis has been consistently associated with an increased risk of incident chronic kidney disease, kidney failure, and sustained declines in eGFR [7–9]. Large epidemiologic studies have reported a 30%–50% higher risk of kidney failure among individuals with kidney stones compared with those without stones, regardless of baseline kidney function or comorbid conditions [9]. Similarly, among patients with established chronic kidney disease, nephrolithiasis has been linked to a more pronounced decline in kidney function and an increased likelihood of dialysis initiation [8]. These findings underscore the pathophysiological impact of recurrent obstruction, infection, and tubular injury associated with stone disease in accelerating renal deterioration.

Although the association between nephrolithiasis and adverse kidney outcomes is well established in the general population, patients with ADPKD are at particularly high risk for stone formation. A meta-analysis by Kalatharan et al. demonstrated that the prevalence of nephrolithiasis in ADPKD ranged from 3% to 59%, exceeding that observed in unaffected family members [2]. Despite this high prevalence, studies specifically evaluating the impact of nephrolithiasis on kidney outcomes in ADPKD remain limited. A post hoc analysis of the OVERTURE trial suggested that a prior history of kidney stones was associated with more rapid disease progression [11], while a subsequent retrospective cohort study reported that even asymptomatic nephrolithiasis detected incidentally by CT was associated with accelerated kidney function decline [12]. However, these studies were limited by retrospective or post hoc designs, reliance on self-reported or chart-based stone history, and incomplete adjustment for key prognostic factors such as genotype and htTKV, raising concerns regarding misclassification and residual confounding.

Our study advances current knowledge by prospectively evaluating a well-characterized ADPKD cohort using CT-based definitions of nephrolithiasis at enrollmentt and assessing definitive kidney outcomes over extended follow-up. Interestingly, the association between nephrolithiasis and kidney outcomes was not evident in unadjusted analyses but became apparent after multivariable adjustment. Sequential modeling showed that adjustment for age, sex, and BMI had minimal impact, whereas further adjustment for baseline kidney function and metabolic variables, including eGFR, serum uric acid, phosphorus, and calcium, resulted in a more pronounced increase in the HR. This pattern suggests that baseline disease severity and metabolic factors may have obscured the underlying association in unadjusted analyses, and that appropriate adjustment allowed the independent contribution of nephrolithiasis to be more clearly identified. Further adjustment for comorbidities, genotype, and Mayo imaging classification resulted in only modest additional changes in the HR. Given that Mayo imaging classification reflects underlying structural disease severity in ADPKD, its inclusion in the fully adjusted model may introduce potential overadjustment or collider bias. Therefore, the fully adjusted estimates should be interpreted with caution, although the overall consistency of the findings across models supports a robust association. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that the association may be more pronounced in certain higher-risk subgroups, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Interestingly, eGFR slope did not differ significantly between the stone and no-stone groups, despite the significant associations observed for time-to-event kidney outcomes. This apparent discrepancy may reflect fundamental differences between these outcome measures. While eGFR slope represents an average rate of kidney function decline over time, time-to-event outcomes capture clinically meaningful thresholds or acute deteriorations in kidney function. In our study, eGFR slope was estimated based on repeated outpatient measurements [median number of measurements: 20 (IQR 12–24)] over a median follow-up duration of 5.2 years, which may not fully capture intermittent or acute declines in kidney function. In contrast, time-to-event outcomes may be more sensitive to the impact of episodic events.

This interpretation is further supported by the observed time-dependent association, which may reflect a greater contribution of nephrolithiasis to early or episodic kidney injury. While a potential contribution to long-term disease progression cannot be excluded, such effects may be less apparent in later stages due to the dominant influence of cyst growth and structural disease progression in ADPKD. In addition, the lack of adjustment for time-varying confounders in the slope analysis may have further limited our ability to detect differences between groups, as transient exposures or events may not be adequately reflected in longitudinal slope estimates.

Several mechanisms may explain the heightened prevalence of nephrolithiasis in ADPKD and its contribution to disease progression. Cyst expansion disrupts kidney architecture, impairs urinary concentration, and promotes urinary stasis, thereby increasing susceptibility to stone formation [21–23]. An association between TKV and nephrolithiasis has also been reported, supporting this structural hypothesis [22]. In addition, metabolic abnormalities such as hypocitraturia, hyperoxaluria, hyperuricosuria, and acidic urine are frequently observed in ADPKD [21, 24]. Experimental studies have further demonstrated that calcium oxalate crystal deposition can induce tubular dilatation and accelerate cystogenesis, acting as a potential ‘third-hit’ factor in disease progression [10].

From a clinical perspective, nephrolithiasis in ADPKD may warrant careful attention, rather than being considered entirely benign or incidental. Because routine imaging in ADPKD relies primarily on ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging, kidney stones—most reliably detected by CT—may be underrecognized and undertreated. Our findings suggest that CT-detected nephrolithiasis may identify patients at higher risk of adverse kidney outcomes in ADPKD; however, prospective studies are needed to determine whether targeted CT evaluation or proactive stone prevention strategies can improve risk stratification or modify kidney disease progression. In addition, emerging evidence indicates that tolvaptan therapy may reduce urinary lithogenesis, suggesting that its renoprotective effects may extend beyond suppression of cyst growth to include prevention of stone-related injury [25].

These observations raise the possibility that nephrolithiasis captures aspects of disease risk not fully reflected by conventional clinical or imaging markers. Our findings should be interpreted in the context of established prognostic models in ADPKD, including the PROPKD score. The PROPKD framework incorporates early urologic events as part of clinical risk stratification, whereas the present study specifically evaluated baseline CT-defined nephrolithiasis [14]. In this context, nephrolithiasis may represent a complementary clinical marker, although its incremental prognostic value beyond established risk factors remains to be determined. Prospective studies are warranted to assess whether the incorporation of nephrolithiasis into current risk stratification frameworks improves the prediction of kidney outcomes in ADPKD.

This study has limitations. First, nephrolithiasis was defined based on baseline CT performed within ±1 year of enrollment, and changes in stone status during follow-up were not captured. Therefore, our exposure likely reflects a baseline stone phenotype (i.e. stone diathesis) rather than time-updated stone events. This study was designed to evaluate the clinical relevance of nephrolithiasis detected on baseline CT imaging, including asymptomatic stones. Second, urinary biochemical parameters and stone composition data were unavailable, which limited our ability to more precisely characterize stone physiology. Third, serial htTKV measurements were not available, precluding assessment of longitudinal changes in imaging-based disease burden. These limitations may have introduced exposure misclassification, and our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality. In addition, although tolvaptan was not used during the enrollment period at our institution, its use gradually began in Korea after approval in December 2015, initially as non-reimbursed therapy [26]. Therefore, exposure during follow-up cannot be completely excluded. Because tolvaptan is now widely used in patients with rapidly progressive ADPKD, our findings may not be fully generalizable to contemporary ADPKD populations. Whether nephrolithiasis has similar clinical implications in patients treated with tolvaptan requires further investigation. Nevertheless, the strengths of this study include its prospective design, CT-based verification of nephrolithiasis, long-term follow-up, and comprehensive adjustment for genetic and imaging-based prognostic variables.

In conclusion, nephrolithiasis is independently associated with an increased risk of kidney failure in patients with ADPKD, particularly among high-risk subgroups, and its detection and management may provide an opportunity to refine risk assessment and delay kidney disease progression.
