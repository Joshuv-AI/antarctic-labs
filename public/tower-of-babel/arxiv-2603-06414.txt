Long-time behaviour of a nonlocal stochastic fractional reaction–diffusion equation arising in tumour dynamics Thanks: Corresponding author: N.I. Kavallaris ( nikos.kavallaris@kau.se )
Nikos I. Kavallaris, Subramani Sankar, Manil T. Mohan, Christos V. Nikolopoulos, Shanmugasundaram Karthikeyan

Abstract
. We introduce a stochastic nonlocal reaction–diffusion model arising in tumour dynamics. Spatial dispersal is described by the fractional Laplacian, accounting for anomalous diffusion and long–range relocation events. The system is perturbed by multiplicative fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst parameter H > 1 / 2 H>1/2 , which we interpret as temporally correlated fluctuations in the tumour microenvironment and host response. We first establish well–posedness and identify parameter regimes leading to global–in–time solutions or finite–time blow–up under general multiplicative fractional noise. We then focus on linear multiplicative noise and, via a Doss–Sussmann transformation, derive sharper results: explicit lower and upper bounds for the blow–up time together with quantitative estimates of the blow–up probability, clarifying how noise intensity can accelerate progression or, on favourable paths, enhance suppression consistent with extinction (loss of viability). Finally, one–dimensional simulations illustrate the interplay between anomalous diffusion, fractional noise, and the nonlocal reaction mechanism in shaping the long–time dynamics.

1.
Introduction

The main aim of this work is to analyse the long–time dynamics of the following
nonlocal stochastic fractional reaction–diffusion equation driven by fractional
noise:

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} du(t,x)-\Delta_{\alpha}u(t,x)\,dt&=\Bigg[\delta\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy+\gamma u(t,x)-\beta u^{p}(t,x)\Bigg]dt+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\,dB^{H}(t),\quad x\in D,\ t>0,\\
u(0,x)&=f(x),\quad x\in D,\\
u(t,x)&=0,\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here
\Delta_{\alpha}:=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
,
0<\alpha\leq 2
, denotes the
fractional Laplacian (see Section
3
for the precise definition and the
functional-analytic setting), and
B^{H}=(B^{H}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a fractional
Brownian motion with Hurst index
H\in[1/2,1)
defined on a filtered probability
space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
.
The parameters
\beta,\delta,\gamma>0
and
p,q>1
are fixed, the initial datum

f
is assumed to be nonnegative and not identically zero (e.g.
f\in C^{2}(D)
),
and the noise coefficient
\sigma(\cdot)
is a prescribed function, see again Section
3
for more details.

The investigation of (
1.1
) is motivated by tumour dynamics, where
u(t,x)
counts for tumour density. Nonlocal
diffusion operators such as
\Delta_{\alpha}
arise as continuum limits of
random walks with heavy-tailed jump distributions and provide effective
macroscopic descriptions of Lévy-type (superdiffusive) transport observed in
biological systems; see, for instance,

[
16
,
65
,
84
,
90
]
.
Moreover, the multiplicative perturbation
\sigma(u(t,x))\,dB^{H}(t)
models
temporally correlated environmental fluctuations (long-memory noise), which may
represent persistent variability in growth conditions, immune pressure, or
microenvironmental support; cf.
[
56
]
.
A more detailed discussion of the link between (
1.1
) and metastatic tumour
dynamics is provided in Section
2
. From a mathematical viewpoint, our goal is to characterise parameter regimes in
which the solution to (
1.1
) exists globally in time and, possibly,
decays to the trivial state (interpreted biologically as tumour extinction),
versus regimes in which the solution exhibits finite–time blow–up, corresponding
to a loss of regulatory control and uncontrolled tumour growth.

From a theoretical standpoint, blow–up and nonexistence phenomena play a
fundamental role in the qualitative analysis of nonlinear evolution equations
and are equally valuable from an applied viewpoint. Classical contributions by
Kaplan
[
47
]
and Fujita
[
38
,
39
]
initiated systematic
methods for proving finite–time blow–up for broad classes of nonlinear
parabolic problems, and these ideas have since inspired a large body of work in the deterministic setting.

In the stochastic setting the analytical picture is substantially more subtle and is still developing. In contrast to deterministic parabolic equations-whose evolution is entirely prescribed by the initial datum-solutions to SPDEs are shaped by the interaction between nonlinear reaction mechanisms and random fluctuations. This interplay may either accelerate or inhibit singularity formation, shift critical thresholds separating global existence from blow–up, and produce genuinely pathwise phenomena with no deterministic analogue. These features, together with applications in physics, biology, and finance, have motivated a growing literature on rigorous blow–up criteria, quantitative bounds on explosion times, and estimates of blow–up probabilities for nonlinear SPDEs.

Within this direction, Chow
[
20
,
21
]
studied finite–time blow–up in the mean
L^{p}
-sense for semilinear parabolic equations driven by space–time noise, adapting Kaplan’s eigenfunction method
[
47
]
. Variants of this strategy were subsequently developed for nonlocal semilinear models by Kavallaris and collaborators
[
50
,
52
]
. Related eigenfunction-based arguments have also been employed in the presence of anomalous diffusion, including fractional Laplacian operators; see Wang
[
86
]
.

For linear multiplicative noise that is purely time dependent, Dozzi and coauthors
[
25
,
26
,
54
]
analysed the long–time behaviour of semilinear parabolic SPDEs using a Doss–Sussmann type transformation. This device reduces the original SPDE to a pathwise random parabolic PDE, thereby allowing one to adapt deterministic blow–up techniques in the spirit of Fujita
[
38
,
39
]
and Kaplan
[
47
]
to obtain estimates for the blow–up time. In addition, in
[
25
,
26
,
54
]
lower and upper bounds for the blow–up probability are derived through estimates of exponential functionals of Brownian motion; see, for instance,
[
64
,
74
,
88
]
. Subsequently, related ideas were extended to coupled systems
[
41
,
58
,
75
]
and to nonlocal stochastic models
[
52
,
60
]
. Concerning semilinear SPDEs driven by fractional Brownian motion, Dozzi and collaborators
[
27
]
established two–sided bounds for the explosion time of a semilinear equation perturbed by a one–dimensional fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}=(B^{H}(t))_{t\geq 0}
with Hurst index
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
. They also provided sufficient conditions ensuring either finite–time blow–up or global existence, together with quantitative estimates for the probability of finite–time explosion. More recently, Dung
[
31
]
developed an alternative approach based on tail estimates for exponential functionals of fractional Brownian motion, leading to explicit upper and lower bounds on the finite–time blow–up probability in terms of the Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. This approach was again extended to case of coupled systems
[
76
]
and nonlocal equations
[
77
]
.

To place the present work in context, we briefly recall several contributions on stochastic parabolic equations involving fractional diffusion. Chang and Lee
[
15
]
studied regularity properties for SPDEs driven by a fractional Laplacian, while Liu and Tudor
[
62
]
established existence and uniqueness of mild solutions for stochastic fractional heat equations. Related well–posedness results for space–time fractional SPDEs on bounded domains were obtained by Foondun, Mijena and Nane
[
35
]
. These papers provide a solid analytical foundation for stochastic evolution equations with nonlocal generators, but they are primarily concerned with existence, uniqueness, and regularity, rather than with sharp long–time scenarios such as extinction versus finite–time blow–up in the presence of nonlocal reactions and temporally correlated noise.

On the other hand, quantitative blow–up analysis has been developed mainly in settings with classical diffusion and Brownian forcing. In the Brownian case (
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
) with the standard Laplacian (
\alpha=2
), Liang and Zhao
[
60
]
investigated finite–time blow–up for a nonlocal stochastic reaction–diffusion equation by means of a random transformation reducing the SPDE to a pathwise random PDE, from which bounds on the blow–up time and estimates of the blow–up probability can be derived. For related semilinear models with
\alpha=2
and simplified reaction structures (e.g. particular choices of parameters such as
\delta=1
and vanishing linear terms), blow–up criteria and probability estimates were obtained in
[
86
]
, and more recently in
[
77
]
for variants with different nonlocal terms.

The above literature indicates a clear gap: a systematic blow–up/extinction theory for nonlocal reaction–diffusion equations combining fractional spatial dispersal (
0<\alpha<2
) with multiplicative fractional Brownian forcing (
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
) is still largely missing. The model (
1.1
) is tailored to this regime and brings together three strongly interacting features:
(i)
anomalous Lévy–type diffusion via the fractional Laplacian, whose analysis relies on Dirichlet forms, heat-kernel and spectral estimates, and nonlocal maximum/comparison principles
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
24
,
55
]
; (ii) a genuinely nonlocal reaction term through the spatial integral,
[
51
]
, which induces global coupling and modifies classical Kaplan–Fujita blow–up mechanisms and their stochastic extensions
[
20
,
21
,
38
,
39
,
47
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
60
,
86
]
; and (iii) long–memory multiplicative noise driven by fBm, requiring a pathwise (Young/rough) integration framework and producing persistent excursions that may shift stability thresholds and alter blow–up times and probabilities
[
63
,
66
,
67
,
68
,
83
,
91
]
. Consequently, (
1.1
) serves as a natural and challenging testbed at the intersection of modern nonlocal PDE theory and stochastic analysis, and the goal of this paper is to develop a detailed long–time dynamical picture-global existence versus finite–time blow–up, two–sided blow–up time bounds, and quantitative blow–up probability estimates-thereby extending the Brownian/
\alpha=2
framework of
[
60
]
to the fractional diffusion/fractional noise setting.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we discuss the
biological motivation for the fractional nonlocal stochastic model
(
1.1
). Section
3
collects the analytical preliminaries and the
functional framework. In Section
4
we study the long–time dynamics
under general multiplicative fractional noise for
\frac{1}{2}<H<1
, identifying parameter regimes leading to
global-in-time solutions or finite–time blow–up (Theorem
4.1
),
as well as almost sure exponential decay in a strongly dissipative setting
(Theorem
4.6
). Section
5
focuses on linear
multiplicative noise: using a Doss–Sussmann type transformation we reduce
(
1.1
) to a random PDE and derive two-sided bounds for the blow–up time
(Theorems
5.8
and
5.12
), estimates on the blow–up rate
(Theorem
5.16
), and quantitative lower bounds for the
blow–up probability (Theorem
5.20
). In Section
6
we address
the special case
H\in(\tfrac{3}{4},1)
, where
B^{H}
is equivalent in law to a
standard Brownian motion, leading to simplified probability bounds
(Theorems
6.3
and
6.4
). The paper concludes with a numerical
study of (
1.1
) in Section
7
based on a finite-difference discretisation, illustrating
the interplay between anomalous diffusion, fractional noise, and the nonlocal
reaction term in the resulting long–time dynamics.

2.
Motivation

Apart from its intrinsic mathematical significance, equation (
1.1
) is also of interest due to its connection with cancer dynamics, as explained in detail below. In this framework,
u(t,x)
denotes the tumour cell density at time
t>0
and position
x\in D
within a biological tissue, and model (
1.1
) describes its evolution under specific biological conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=-\,\underbrace{(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u}_{\text{\scriptsize L\'{e}vy migration}}+\overbrace{\delta\!\int_{D}u^{q}(y,t)\,dy}^{\text{\scriptsize nonlocal signalling}}+\overbrace{\gamma u}^{\text{\scriptsize proliferation}}-\underbrace{\beta u^{p}}_{\text{\scriptsize crowding / therapy}}+\overbrace{\sigma(u)\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}(t)}^{\text{\scriptsize fBm noise}}.

The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
represents long-range Lévy-type tumour cell migration and invasion along heterogeneous tissue structures, in particular for metastatic cancer cells, consistent with experimental and modelling evidence for anomalous motility and nonlocal cancer cell dispersal
[
16
,
45
,
84
,
85
]
, see also Fig.
1
.
The nonlocal integral term
\delta\int_{D}u^{q}(x)dx
models domain-wide signalling or systemic feedback such as cytokines, growth factors or immune mediators, in line with nonlocal reaction–diffusion descriptions of biomedical signalling and immunological interactions
[
7
,
40
,
53
]
.
Local reaction dynamics
(\gamma u-\beta u^{p})
describe proliferation constrained by crowding, competition, or therapy, as in classical and nonlocal tumour reaction–diffusion models
[
59
,
85
]
.
The multiplicative fractional Brownian motion noise
\sigma(u)\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}
captures temporally correlated fluctuations in vascularisation, oxygenation, or immune pressure, generalising earlier stochastic cancer models driven by (white) Brownian noise to the long-memory setting
[
33
,
37
,
56
,
80
]
. Finally, the Dirichlet exterior condition encodes a hostile
environment outside
D
(e.g. resection boundary or lethal habitat).

This formulation integrates
two cutting-edge ingredients that, to our knowledge, have not been combined in a single continuum tumour model
:
(i) nonlocal spatial and mean-field couplings (capturing systemic signalling and long-range migration), extending the growing body of nonlocal reaction-diffusion models in biomedical applications and cancer invasion
[
7
,
16
,
40
]
;
and (ii) stochastic forcing with long-range temporal correlations (fractional Brownian noise), which goes beyond the classical white-noise perturbations considered in tumour SPDEs and stochastic ODE models
[
33
,
37
,
56
,
80
]
.
Their interplay raises fundamentally new questions on tumour dynamics that go beyond classical local PDE models without memory, nonlocality or correlated noise.

Mathematically, the model contributes to the emerging theory of nonlocal SPDEs with memory and fractional noise, sitting at the intersection of spatial fractional diffusion limits
[
65
]
, nonlocal reaction–diffusion equations in biomedicine
[
7
,
40
]
, and stochastic tumour growth models
[
37
,
80
]
.
Biologically, it clarifies conditions under which tumour growth remains bounded (therapy success, strong competition or inhibitory signalling) versus regimes leading to blow–up (uncontrolled systemic proliferation), complementing recent fractional and stochastic extensions of tumour and tumour–immune models
[
1
,
42
,
59
,
87
]
.
This dichotomy provides a rigorous framework to interpret the onset of aggressive cancer dynamics and the potential stabilising role of spatial nonlocality, memory and correlated fluctuations.

3.
Mathematical Preliminaries

Notation and function spaces

We work in the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(D),

equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{2}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}|u(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}}\right)^{1/2},

and inner product

\langle u,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{D}u(x)\,v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad u,v\in\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(D).

Also
\|u\|_{p}
stands for the norm of
L^{p}(D)
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty.

The canonical duality pairing between
H
and its dual is denoted by

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. For
u\in\mathcal{H}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
we write

u(t,\varphi):=\langle u(t),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{D}u(t,x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

We denote by

V:=H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)

the energy space associated with the fractional Laplacian
\Delta_{\alpha}
with Dirichlet exterior boundary conditions. The space
V
is a Hilbert space endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{H^{\alpha/2}(D)}^{2}:=\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\int_{D}\int_{D}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad u\in H^{\alpha/2}(D),

and corresponding inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
.

Throughout this work we will also use the Banach space

\mathcal{B}^{\nu,2}\big([0,t],\mathcal{H}\big),

which consists of all measurable functions
u:[0,t]\to L^{2}(D)
for which the norm

\|u\|_{\nu,2}^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{s\in[0,t]}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{2}}}\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\|u(\cdot,s)-u(\cdot,r)\|_{2}}{(s-r)^{\nu+1}}\,\mathrm{d}r}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s

is finite, i.e.
\|u\|_{\nu,2}<+\infty
, for
0<\nu<\tfrac{1}{2}
, cf.
[
92
]
.

Fractional Laplacian and relevant structures

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, be a bounded domain with
C^{2}
boundary and
let
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Throughout the paper we denote by

\Delta_{\alpha}:=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}

the fractional Laplacian of order
\alpha
with exterior Dirichlet
condition. For sufficiently regular functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we use
the pointwise singular integral representation

(3.1)

(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u(x)=C_{d,\alpha}\,\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
C_{d,\alpha}>0
is a normalising constant and
\mathrm{P.V.}
denotes the
Cauchy principal value. On the domain
D
we consider the operator with
homogeneous Dirichlet exterior condition,

u=0\quad\text{a.e.\ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,

understood in the usual weak (energy) sense via the space

H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)
; see, e.g.,
[
55
]
.

Semigroup operator

It is well known that
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
is the infinitesimal generator of
the rotationally symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose transition semigroup

(T_{t}f)(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[f(X_{t})\big],\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

acts as a convolution operator with a radially symmetric density

p_{t}(x-y)
; see, for instance,

[
55
]
. In this probabilistic framework, the
Dirichlet
fractional Laplacian on
D

is realised as the generator of the process killed upon exiting
D
. More
precisely, if

\tau_{D}:=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}\notin D\},

then

(S_{\alpha}(t)f)(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[f(X_{t})\,\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{D}\}}\big],\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in D,

defines a strongly continuous, sub-Markovian, self-adjoint contraction semigroup
on
H
, whose generator is the (self-adjoint realisation of)
\Delta_{\alpha}

with zero exterior condition; see, for example,

[
34
,
55
,
79
]
.

Variational structure and spectrum

We denote by
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
the Dirichlet form of the Dirichlet fractional
Laplacian
A_{\alpha}=-\Delta_{\alpha}
on
L^{2}(D)
. For
u
extended by
0
outside
D
,

(3.2)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u,u):=\frac{C_{d,\alpha}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,

with domain

Dom(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}):=H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)=\big\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):u=0\text{ a.e.\ on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,\ \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u,u)<\infty\big\}.

Then
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
is a densely defined, closed, symmetric, positive form and

A_{\alpha}
is the associated nonnegative self-adjoint operator; see, e.g.,

[
55
,
79
]
.

Since
D
is bounded,
A_{\alpha}
has compact resolvent and thus a purely discrete
spectrum

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{k}\to\infty,

with corresponding real eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
forming an orthonormal
basis of
\mathcal{H}
.

The eigenvalues admit the standard variational characterisations:

(3.3)

\lambda_{1}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u,u)}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{k}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)\\
\dim F=k\end{subarray}}\ \sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in F\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u,u)}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}},\ k\geq 2.

In particular, for any
u\in H^{\alpha/2}_{0}(D)
the Poincaré-type bound

(3.4)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u,u)\geq\lambda_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}

holds.

Also, the

first eigenpair

(\lambda_{1},\phi_{1})

satisfying:

(3.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{1}(x)&=\lambda_{1}\varphi(x),\ \ x\in D,\\
\varphi_{1}(x)&=0,\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,\end{aligned}}}\right.

is simple and strictly positive in

D
in the following sense: any eigenfunction associated with
\lambda_{1}
does
not change sign, and there exists a representative such that

\phi_{1}(x)>0,\quad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in D,\qquad\phi_{1}(x)=0,\quad\text{for a.e.\ }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,

see
[
46
,
78
]
.
This is a consequence of the variational characterisation of
\lambda_{1},
cf. (
3.3
),
combined with the strong maximum principle and Harnack-type inequalities for

\Delta_{\alpha}
; see, among many others,

[
46
,
78
]
.
For definiteness, we fix the normalisation

(3.6)

\phi_{1}>0\text{ in }D,\qquad\int_{D}\phi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1.

Stochastic integral, Itô’s formula and Malliavin calculus

Throughout this work we consider a filtered probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
that supports
a one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
(B^{H}(t))_{t\geq 0}
with Hurst
index
H\in(1/2,1)
, so that Young or pathwise stochastic integrals with
respect to
B^{H}
are well-defined; see, e.g.,

[
63
,
68
,
83
]
.

Stochastic Integral and Itô’s formula

We assume that the diffusion term
\sigma(\cdot)
is non-negative and satisfies

(3.7)

\sigma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\quad\sigma(0)=0,\quad|\sigma(r)-\sigma(s)|\leq L_{\sigma}|r-s|,\quad|\sigma(r)|\leq C_{\sigma}(1+r),\quad\forall r,s\geq 0,

for some constants
L_{\sigma},C_{\sigma}>0
.

Under condition (
3.7
) and the requirement that
u\in\mathcal{B}^{\nu,2}\bigl([0,t],\mathcal{H}\bigr)
for some
\nu\in(1-H,1/2)

we guarantee that the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(u(s))dB^{H}(s)
exists as a generalized Stieltjes integral in the Young sense (see
[
69
, Proposition 1]
and
[
92
]
).

In the case of fBm Itô’s formula is given by the following:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
66
, Lemma 2.7.1]
)
.

Let
(B^{H}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be an fBm with
H\in(1/2,1),\ F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}).
Then for any
t>0,

\displaystyle F(B^{H}(t))=F(0)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(B^{H}(s))dB^{H}(s).

Malliavin calculus

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the space of step functions on the interval
[0,T]
, and define
\mathcal{H}
as the closure of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the inner product

\langle\mathbf{1}_{[0,s]},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=R^{H}(s,t),

where
R^{H}(s,t)
denotes the auto-covariance function given by

\displaystyle R_{H}(t,s):=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[B^{H}(t)B^{H}(s)}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{2H}+t^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}}}\right).

We begin by defining the map
\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}\mapsto B_{t}^{H}=B^{H}(t)
on
\mathcal{S}
and then extend it to an isometry from
\mathcal{H}
into the Gaussian space
\mathcal{H}_{1}(B_{t}^{H})
generated by the fractional Brownian motion. This isometry is denoted by
\phi\mapsto B_{t}^{H}(\phi)
.
We now proceed to define the Malliavin derivative
D
with respect to fBM. Let us consider a smooth cylindrical functional of the form
F=f(B_{t}^{H}(\phi))
, where
\phi\in\mathcal{H}
and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. The Malliavin derivative
\mathcal{D}F
is an
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variable defined via the duality relation

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}F,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=f^{\prime}(B_{t}^{H}(\phi))\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}f\big(B_{t}^{H}(\phi)+\epsilon\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)}}\right|_{\epsilon=0},

which can be understood as a generalization of the directional derivative along the paths of fBm.

The operator
\mathcal{D}
is closable as a mapping from
L^{p}(\Omega)
into
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
for any
p\geq 1
, and it allows for the construction of Sobolev-type spaces on the Wiener space. Among these, the space
{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}
is of particular importance. It is defined as the closure of the set of smooth cylindrical random variables with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|F\|_{{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}[|F|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{D}F\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}]}}\right)^{1/2}.

In our framework, the Malliavin derivative will be interpreted as a stochastic process
\{\mathcal{D}_{t}F:\,t\in[0,T]\}
(see
[
67
]
, Section 1.2.1 and Chapter 5).

A useful tail estimate used in a vital manner across the manuscript is given below:

Lemma 3.2
.

[
30
, Lemma 2.1]

Let
Z
be a centered random variable in
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
. Assume that there exists a non-random constant
M_{0}
such that

\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{D}_{r}Z)^{2}dr\leq M_{0}^{2},\ \mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.s.}

Then the following estimate for tail probabilities holds:

\mathbb{P}(|Z|\geq x)\leq 2e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2M_{0}^{2}}},\ x>0.

Concepts of solutions

In this subsection we recall the notions of weak and mild solutions for the
stochastic evolution equation (
1.1
) driven by the fractional Laplacian
with Dirichlet exterior condition.

Definition 3.3

(Weak solution)
.

Let

\tau:\Omega\to(0,\infty]
be a stopping time.
A process

u:\Omega\times[0,\tau)\to\mathcal{H},\qquad(t,\omega)\mapsto u(t,\omega),

is called a
weak solution
to (
1.1
) on
[0,\tau)
if:

(i)

u
is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
–adapted and has almost surely
continuous trajectories in
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.

u\in C([0,\tau);\mathcal{H}),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

and the nonlinearities are integrable in the sense that

u\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}\big([0,\tau);L^{p}(D)\big)\cap L^{q}_{\rm loc}\big([0,\tau);L^{q}(D)\big),\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

for the exponents
p,q
in (
1.1
). Moreover, for every
\varphi\in V
the
scalar processes

t\mapsto u(t,\varphi):=\langle u(t),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad t\mapsto\langle\sigma(u(t)),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

are almost surely continuous on
[0,\tau)
and Hölder continuous of some order

\theta>1-H
on compact subintervals of
[0,\tau)
, so that the Young integral
with respect to
B_{t}^{H}
is well defined.

(ii)

For every test function
\varphi\in V
and every
t\in(0,\tau)
, the
following identity holds:

\displaystyle u(t,\varphi)

\displaystyle=u(0,\varphi)+\int_{0}^{t}u\big(s,(A_{\alpha}+\gamma I)\varphi\big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\delta\int_{0}^{t}\Bigg(\int_{D}u^{q}(s,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigg)\Bigg(\int_{D}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigg)\mathrm{d}s-\beta\int_{0}^{t}\langle u^{p}(s),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\mathrm{d}s

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\sigma(u(s)),\varphi\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}(s),\quad\mathbb{P}\mbox{–a.s.,}

where
u^{p}(s)
and
u^{q}(s)
are understood pointwise in
x\in D
and extended
by
0
outside
D
,
I
is the identity on
\mathcal{H}
, and the last integral is taken
in the Young (pathwise Riemann–Stieltjes) sense using the Hölder regularity
from
(i)
.

In particular, (
(ii)
) is the weak formulation of
(
1.1
) with homogeneous Dirichlet exterior condition.

This notion is the natural variational/weak formulation in the spirit of

[
23
,
71
]
adapted to the
nonlocal operator
A_{\alpha}
and the fractional noise, cf.
[
63
,
83
]
.

Definition 3.4

(Mild solution)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(D)
and let
(S_{\alpha}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the
C_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathcal{H}

generated by the Dirichlet fractional Laplacian
A_{\alpha}=-\Delta_{\alpha}

(cf. Section
3
).
Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
be a bounded, non-negative initial datum and let

\tau:\Omega\to(0,\infty]
be a stopping time.

An
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
–adapted process

u:\Omega\times[0,\tau)\to\mathcal{H}

is called a
mild solution
to (
1.1
) on
[0,\tau)
if:

(i)

u
has almost surely continuous trajectories in
\mathcal{H}
, and

u\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}\big([0,\tau);L^{p}(D)\big)\cap L^{q}_{\mathrm{loc}}\big([0,\tau);L^{q}(D)\big),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

so that all Bochner integrals below are well defined. Moreover, for every

\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
the scalar process

t\mapsto\langle S_{\alpha}(t-\cdot)\sigma(u(\cdot)),\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

is almost surely Hölder continuous of some order
\theta>1-H
on compact
subintervals of
[0,\tau)
, so that the Young (pathwise) stochastic integral
with respect to
B_{t}^{H}
is well defined.

(ii)

For every
t\in(0,\tau)
the following
mild formulation
holds

\mathbb{P}
–a.s. in
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=S_{\alpha}(t)f+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\Bigg[\delta\Bigg(\int_{D}u^{q}(r,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigg)\mathbf{1}_{D}+\gamma u(r)-\beta u^{p}(r)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}r

(3.9)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\sigma\big(u(r)\big)\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}(r),

where
\mathbf{1}_{D}
denotes the constant function
x\mapsto 1
on
D
, the
powers
u^{p}
and
u^{q}
are taken pointwise in
x\in D
and extended by
0

outside
D
, and the last term is a stochastic convolution in
\mathcal{H}
with respect
to
B_{t}^{H}
, defined pathwise in the Young sense (cf.
[
63
,
83
]
).

The above notions of weak (Definition
3.3
) and mild
(Definition
3.4
) solutions are consistent with the standard
evolution-equation framework for semilinear SPDEs in Hilbert spaces (see, e.g.,

[
23
,
71
]
) and with the
existing literature on equations driven by fractional Brownian motion,
cf.
[
63
,
83
]
.

Equivalence of weak and mild formulations

Under the above regularity assumptions and suitable Lipschitz and polynomial
growth conditions, cf. (
3.7
), on the nonlinearities

u\mapsto u^{p},\qquad u\mapsto u^{q},\qquad u\mapsto\sigma(u),

weak and mild solutions of (
1.1
) on
[0,\tau)
are equivalent.

More precisely, one has:

•

Any mild solution in the sense of Definition
3.4
is a
weak solution in the sense of Definition
3.3
. This follows
by testing the mild formulation (
3.9
) against

\varphi\in V
, using the semigroup identity

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\varphi=A_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\varphi,

and integrating by parts in time, together with standard stochastic Fubini
arguments for Young integrals; see, e.g.,

[
23
, Chapter 4]
,
[
71
, Chapter 3]
.

•

Conversely, if
u(\cdot,\cdot)
is a weak solution and the semigroup
(S_{\alpha}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is analytic and contractive on
H
(which holds for the Dirichlet fractional Laplacian, cf.
[
34
,
79
]
), then Duhamel’s
formula and the variation-of-constants method yield the mild representation
(
3.9
) from the weak formulation
(
(ii)
); see, for instance,

[
43
, Proposition 3.7]
and
[
23
, Chapter 5]
in similar settings.

The following lemma will be useful for further sections in the sequel.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
28
, Lemma 1]
Let
(B^{H}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
0<H<1
defined on a probability space
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}}}\right).

(i)

If
\nu>0,
then

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{B^{H}(s)-\nu s}ds<\infty}}\right)=1.

(ii)

If
\nu<0,
then

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{B^{H}(s)-\nu s}ds=\infty}}\right)=1.

4.
General Noise: global existence vs finite–time blow–up

We begin by analysing the long-time behaviour in the presence of a general
multiplicative fractional perturbation
\sigma(u)\,dB_{t}^{H}
, that is, we study
the nonlocal reaction–diffusion model (
1.1
). Motivated by the deterministic
counterpart (cf.
[
72
, Theorem 43.1]
), we establish the following two results.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
f\in L^{\infty}(D)
,
f\geq 0
, and the hypotheses (
3.7
) on
\sigma
. Then, for almost every
\omega
, there exists a unique
nonnegative mild solution

u(\omega,\cdot)\in C\big([0,T_{\max}(\omega));L^{\infty}(D)\big)

of (
1.1
), satisfying (
3.9
).
The solution enjoys a pathwise comparison principle:
if
f\leq g
a.e. in
D
, and
v
is the solution with initial data
g
, then

u(\cdot,t,\omega)\leq v(\cdot,t,\omega)\quad\text{for all }t<T_{\max}(\omega),\ \text{a.s.}

Moreover:

(i)

If
\beta=0
,
\delta>0
,
q>1
, and
f\not\equiv 0
, then blow–up occurs
in finite–time
\tau
with strictly positive probability.

(ii)

If
\beta>0
and
p>q
, then solutions are global almost surely:

T_{\max}(\omega)=+\infty
for almost every
\omega
.

(iii)

If
\beta>0
,
\delta>0
and
0<p<q
, then sufficiently large initial data
lead to blow–up in finite–time with strictly positive probability. More
precisely, there exists
R>0
such that if

y_{0}:=\int_{D}f(x)\phi_{1}(x)\,dx\geq R,

then the corresponding solution satisfies

T_{\max}(\omega)<\infty
with strictly positive probability.

Proof.

(Existence, uniqueness, positivity, comparison).

Fix
\omega
and regard
t\mapsto B^{H}_{t}(\omega)
as a deterministic
Hölder continuous path of exponent
\gamma<H
.
For
X=L^{\infty}(D)
, consider the map

\mathcal{T}(u)(t):=S_{\alpha}(t)f+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-\tau F(u(\tau))\,d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\sigma(u(\tau))\,dB^{H}_{\tau},

where

F(u):=\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}dx\Big)+\gamma u-\beta u^{p}.

The semigroup
S_{\alpha}
is analytic and contractive on
\mathcal{H}
and

L^{\infty}(D)
, and
F
is locally Lipschitz on
L^{\infty}(D)
.
By (
3.7
),
\sigma
is globally Lipschitz and of at most linear growth,
hence
t\mapsto\sigma(u(t))
is Hölder continuous in
L^{\infty}(D)
whenever

t\mapsto u(t)
. For
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
and such integrands,
Young integration yields a well-defined stochastic convolution

\int_{0}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\sigma(u(\tau))\,dB^{H}_{\tau}
in
L^{\infty}(D)
with
the usual estimates
(cf.
[
91
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
68
, Theorem 3.1]
,
and the abstract SPDE frameworks in

[
63
, Theorems 3.1–3.2]
,

[
57
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
73
,
83
]
).

For
T>0
small enough, standard fixed-point arguments in

C([0,T];L^{\infty}(D))
show that
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction on a ball,
yielding a unique mild solution on
[0,T]
. Iteration gives existence on

[0,T_{\max}(\omega))
for some maximal
T_{\max}(\omega)\in(0,\infty]
.

Positivity and comparison follow from the order-preserving properties of

S_{\alpha}
and of the drift
F
: if
u\geq 0
, then
F(u)
is increasing in
u

for
\delta,\beta\geq 0
and
\phi_{1}>0
, and
\sigma
satisfies
\sigma(0)=0

with
\sigma(r)\geq 0
, for
r\geq 0
. The nonlocal operator
-\Delta_{\alpha}

with zero exterior data enjoys weak and strong maximum principles and a
comparison principle, see e.g. the weak/strong maximum principles for the
fractional Laplacian in
[
12
, Theorems 3.9–3.10]
and the
comparison results in their Section 3.3.
This yields preservation of positivity and pathwise comparison for the mild
problem by standard monotone iteration.

(i)

(Blow–up for
\beta=0
).

Assume
\beta=0
,
\delta>0
,
q>1
. Test (
1.1
) against the positive
first eigenfunction
\phi_{1}
of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
:

y(t):=\int_{D}u(t,x)\phi_{1}(x)\,dx.

Using
-\Delta_{\alpha}\phi_{1}=\lambda_{1}\phi_{1}
and integrating by parts,

\displaystyle dy(t)+\lambda_{1}y(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy\Big)\int_{D}\phi_{1}(x)\,dx\,dt+\gamma y(t)\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB^{H}_{t},

where

\tilde{\sigma}(y(t)):=\int_{D}\sigma(u(t,x))\phi_{1}(x)\,dx,

which inherits local Lipschitz and linear growth from
\sigma,
cf. (
3.7
).
By Hölder inequality, (
3.6
) and positivity of
\phi_{1}
, there exists
c_{q}>0
such that

\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy\geq c_{q}\,y(t)^{q},

so

dy(t)+(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)\,dt\geq\delta c_{q}y(t)^{q}\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB^{H}_{t}.

For large
y
, the term
\delta c_{q}y^{q}
dominates the linear term

(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y
. Since
B^{H}_{t}
has variance of order
t^{2H}
and
satisfies a law of the iterated logarithm (hence
|B^{H}_{t}|=o(t)
a.s.),
we have sublinear growth in time (see e.g.
[
66
]
).

We now apply an Osgood-type comparison argument. Consider the
deterministic integral equation

z(t)=z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\delta c_{q}z(s)^{q}-(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)z(s)\big)\,ds+g(t),

with a perturbation
g
satisfying

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\inf_{0\leq h\leq 1}\big(g(t+h)-g(t)\big)=+\infty,

(as is the case for paths of Brownian motion and, more generally,
for fractional Brownian motion; cf. the Osgood criteria in

[
36
, Theorem 2.1]
.
The classical Osgood test says that explosion in finite–time occurs iff

\int^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{q}}<\infty
, which holds for
q>1
.
By a comparison theorem for integral equations (see again

[
36
]
),
y
dominates such a
z
and thus
blows up in finite–time with strictly positive probability.

(ii)

(Global existence for
\beta>0
,
p>q
).

Assume now
\beta>0
and
p>q
. Testing as above and using Hölder
inequalities and (
3.6
) there exist constants
c_{p},c_{q}>0
such that

(4.1)

\int_{D}u^{p}(t,x)\phi_{1}(x)\,dx\geq c_{p}y(t)^{p},\qquad\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy\leq c_{q}y(t)^{q}.

We obtain

dy(t)+(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)\,dt\leq\delta c_{q}y(t)^{q}\,dt-\beta c_{p}y(t)^{p}\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB^{H}_{t}.

For large
y
, the negative term
-\beta c_{p}y^{p}
dominates

\delta c_{q}y^{q}
since
p>q
, yielding a pathwise upper bound by a
nonexplosive ODE. Using again an Osgood-type comparison (now in the
nonexplosive direction), one excludes finite–time blow–up and obtains
global existence. The sublinear growth of
B^{H}_{t}
and the linear growth of

\tilde{\sigma}
prevent the noise from cancelling this dissipativity
(see also the SPDE versions of Osgood criteria in
[
36
]
).

(iii)

(Blow–up for
\beta>0
,
0<p<q
, large data).

Assume
\beta>0
,
\delta>0
and
0<p<q
. Let
\phi_{1}>0
be the first
Dirichlet eigenfunction of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
and set

y(t):=\int_{D}u(t,x)\phi_{1}(x)\,dx.

Testing (
1.1
) against
\phi_{1}
and arguing as in
(i)
yields

dy(t)+(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)\,dt\geq\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy\Big)\!\int_{D}\phi_{1}(x)\,dx\,dt-\beta\int_{D}u^{p}(t,x)\phi_{1}(x)\,dx\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB_{t}^{H},

with
\tilde{\sigma}(y)=\int_{D}\sigma(u)\phi_{1}dx
of at most linear growth.
By the same Hölder/eigenfunction bounds used above, cf. (
4.1
), the above yields

(4.2)

dy(t)\geq\Big(\delta c_{q}y(t)^{q}-\beta c_{p}y(t)^{p}-(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)\Big)\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB_{t}^{H}.

Since
q>p
and
q>1
, choose
R>0
such that for all
r\geq R
,

\delta c_{q}r^{q}-\beta c_{p}r^{p}-(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)r\geq\tfrac{\delta c_{q}}{2}\,r^{q}.

Therefore, on the event
\{y(t)\geq R\ \text{for }t\in[0,\tau]\}
we have

dy(t)\geq\tfrac{\delta c_{q}}{2}y(t)^{q}\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dB_{t}^{H},

and an Osgood-type comparison for integral inequalities with additive
perturbations (cf.
[
36
]
) implies that if

y_{0}=\int_{D}f(x)\phi_{1}(x)dx\geq R
is large enough, then
y
(hence
u
) blows up in
finite–time with strictly positive probability, because

\int^{\infty}s^{-q}\,ds<\infty
for
q>1
.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

(i)

Fractional vs. classical diffusion and the blow–up time.

In the eigenfunction reduction used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, the diffusion enters only
through the linear term
(\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}-\gamma)y(t)
, where

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\alpha}

(with zero exterior condition). Replacing
-\Delta_{\alpha}
by the classical
Laplacian
-\Delta
corresponds to
\alpha=2
and yields the damping

(\lambda_{1}^{(2)}-\gamma)y(t)
.

In the blow–up regimes (i) and (iii), for large
y
the scalar inequality has
the schematic form

dy(t)\ \gtrsim\ \delta\,y(t)^{q}\,dt\;-\;(\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}-\gamma)\,y(t)\,dt\;+\;\text{noise}.

Thus, a
larger
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
increases the linear
stabilising effect and therefore
delays
blow–up (or raises the size of
the initial datum needed to trigger blow–up), whereas a
smaller

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
weakens this effect and typically
accelerates

blow–up.

A quantitative comparison between fractional and classical diffusion follows
from the eigenvalue bounds for the killed symmetric
\alpha
-stable generator:
if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
D
and

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
that of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
, then

c\,(\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2}\ \leq\ \lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}\ \leq\ (\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2},

for some
c=c(D)\in(0,1]
, see
[
18
, (1.6) and Remark 3.5(i)]
.
Consequently, relative to the classical case
\alpha=2
, the linear damping
rate changes from
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}-\gamma
to

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}-\gamma\approx(\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2}-\gamma
.
Since the blow–up mechanism is governed by the competition between the
superlinear growth
\delta y^{q}
and the linear stabilisation

(\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}-\gamma)y
, this comparison shows how fractional
diffusion may either delay or accelerate blow–up
depending on the size
of

\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
(equivalently, the geometry/scale of
D
):

•

if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}>1
(typically
small, strongly confined domains
,
which in a tumour setting may be interpreted as
smaller tumours
or an
early-stage lesion occupying a limited region), then

(\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2}<\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
for
\alpha<2
. Hence replacing
classical diffusion by anomalous (fractional) diffusion reduces the linear
stabilisation
(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y
in the eigenfunction inequality, i.e. the
fractional Laplacian provides
weaker effective damping
than the Laplacian.
In this regime, anomalous dispersal (heavy-tailed jumps/long-range spreading)
therefore tends to
accelerate
the onset of explosive growth compared to
the classical diffusion model.

•

if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}<1
(typically
large domains
, which may be
interpreted as
larger tumours
occupying a larger spatial region), then

(\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2}>\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
for
\alpha<2
. In this case the
fractional Laplacian yields
stronger
linear stabilisation than the
classical Laplacian at the level of the principal mode, so anomalous diffusion
tends to
delay
blow–up (or raise the threshold for explosive behaviour)
relative to the classical diffusion model.

In all cases, the eigenvalue comparison affects the time scale and thresholds
but not the qualitative blow–up/global dichotomy driven by the exponents.

(ii)

Impact of the fractional noise and comparison with the Brownian case.

Since
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the stochastic integral with respect to
B^{H}
is treated
pathwise in the Young sense. The noise enters the reduced inequality through
the term
\tilde{\sigma}(y(t)\,dB_{t}^{H}
, where
\tilde{\sigma}(y)=\int_{D}\sigma(u)\phi_{1}\,dx
.
A key common feature with the standard Brownian case is that sample paths are

sublinear
in time: for fractional Brownian motion one has

|B_{t}^{H}|=o(t)
a.s. (Lemma
4.5
), while for Brownian motion

|W_{t}|=o(t)
a.s. by the law of the iterated logarithm. Consequently, in both
settings the stochastic forcing grows strictly slower than the linear
dissipation term
(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)
on long time scales, and the drift
dominance at large amplitudes is determined by the polynomial terms

\delta y^{q}
and
-\beta y^{p}
.

Regarding
finite–time blow–up
, the comparison principles based on
Osgood-type criteria apply in both cases but the relevant integration theory
differs:

•

For
Brownian noise
(
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
), the eigenfunction projection
typically yields an Itô SDE of the form

dy(t)=\big(\delta c_{q}y(t)^{q}-\beta c_{p}y(t)^{p}-(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)y(t)\big)\,dt+\tilde{\sigma}(y(t))\,dW_{t},

and one uses Itô calculus together with comparison/Osgood-type blow–up tests
for SPDEs/SDEs (see
[
36
]
and references therein). In this
framework, the quadratic variation may contribute additional effective drift
terms for specific choices of
\tilde{\sigma}
(e.g. linear multiplicative
noise), which can shift thresholds or rates.

•

For
fractional Brownian noise
(
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
), the projected
equation is interpreted pathwise as a Young (or rough) differential equation,
with no Itô correction term. The blow–up mechanism in cases (i) and (iii) is
therefore closer to a deterministic ODE with a continuous perturbation:
for large
y
, the superlinear term
\delta y^{q}
dominates the lower-order
terms and the perturbation
\tilde{\sigma}(y)\,B_{t}^{H}
; the Osgood-type integral
comparison for perturbed integral inequalities remains applicable

[
36
]
, yielding blow–up with strictly positive
probability for sufficiently large data when
q>1
.

In contrast, in the dissipative regime
p>q
(case (ii)), the higher-power
local damping
-\beta y^{p}
dominates the source at large amplitudes, and the
same comparison framework (Brownian Itô or fractional Young) yields a
nonexplosive upper bound and hence global existence.

Remark 4.3

(Biological interpretation: explosive tumour progression)
.

Theorem
4.1
singles out regimes in which the model predicts

runaway tumour progression
in finite–time. In biological terms, blow–up
represents an uncontrolled escalation of tumour burden, used in tumour-growth
modelling as a proxy for catastrophic progression or therapy failure when
regulatory mechanisms cannot compensate increasing tumour-promoting feedback;
see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.

Two clinically meaningful explosive scenarios emerge:
(i) if
\beta=0
, there is no effective density-dependent kill (no therapy or
ineffective clearance), so superlinear tumour-promoting feedback can drive
abrupt progression even from nontrivial initial burden; (iii) even when

\beta>0
, if the kill mechanism grows too slowly with burden (
0<p<q
), then
large initial tumours can enter a runaway regime, reflecting a
mismatch

between treatment intensity and accelerating systemic support.

The multiplicative fractional noise
\sigma(u)\,dB_{t}^{H}
can be interpreted as
temporally correlated environmental heterogeneity (e.g. fluctuating hypoxia,
immune pressure, or treatment adherence). The statement “with positive
probability” indicates a
risk
of explosive progression: adverse
realisations of the environment may precipitate rapid deterioration, while other
realisations may delay it, a familiar phenomenon in stochastic population and
tumour models
[
2
,
44
]
. Finally, blow–up also signals that
additional biological mechanisms (resource limitation, necrosis, saturation of
angiogenesis/therapy) would be needed to regularise the model at very high
densities
[
4
]
.

Remark 4.4

(Noise and (non-)eradication of blow–up: deterministic vs. stochastic dynamics)
.

Theorem
4.1
clarifies that, in our setting, multiplicative
fractional noise
does not eradicate
the blow–up mechanisms already
present in the deterministic model, and it can in fact
promote
blow–up in
supercritical regimes.

Deterministic benchmark.

When
\sigma\equiv 0
, (
1.1
) reduces to a nonlocal reaction–diffusion PDE.
In particular:

•

if
\beta=0
and
\delta>0
, then the nonlocal source
\delta\int_{D}u^{q}dx

with
q>1
yields finite–time blow–up (classically, via Kaplan/Fujita-type
testing or Osgood criteria);

•

if
\beta>0
and
p>q
, the damping term
-\beta u^{p}
dominates the
nonlocal growth and solutions are global (subcritical regime);

•

if
\beta>0
and
0<p<q
, sufficiently large data trigger blow–up
(supercritical regime).

Stochastic case.

The conclusions in Theorem
4.1
show that adding
multiplicative fBm does not reverse these phase portraits:

(i)

In the
no-damping
case
\beta=0
, blow–up still occurs with
strictly positive probability; the noise cannot compensate for the absence of
a stabilising mechanism.

(ii)

In the
subcritical
case
\beta>0
,
p>q
, global existence
holds almost surely: the dissipativity is strong enough to control the
stochastic perturbation under (
3.7
).

(iii)

In the
supercritical
case
\beta>0
,
0<p<q
, large initial
mass still yields blow–up with strictly positive probability; the noise does not
remove the superlinear amplification.

Heuristic mechanism and biological interpretation.

Projecting onto the first eigenfunction,

y(t)=\int_{D}u(t)\phi_{1}\,dx,

leads to a
scalar inequality of the form (
4.2
) in which the drift
contains competing powers
y^{q}
(growth) and
-y^{p}
(damping), while the noise
appears through a multiplicative term
\tilde{\sigma}(y)\,dB_{t}^{H}
. In tumour
dynamics this term models temporally correlated microenvironmental variability
(e.g. fluctuating nutrient supply, immune surveillance, vascular remodelling,
or therapy efficacy) that scales with the current tumour burden. Along sample
paths with sustained favourable fluctuations, the multiplicative forcing can
amplify the growth phase and increase the likelihood (or accelerate the onset)
of blow–up in supercritical regimes, i.e. it may
hinder eradication
.
Conversely, in the subcritical regime
p>q
the deterministic dissipation is
dominant, and under the Lipschitz/linear-growth constraint (
3.7
) the noise
cannot destabilise the dynamics, so extinction/persistence outcomes remain
qualitatively unchanged.

Related Osgood-type comparison principles for explosive SPDEs (and for
integral equations with stochastic perturbations) can be found in

[
36
]
; see also the classical deterministic blow–up tests of
Kaplan–Fujita type
[
38
,
39
,
47
]
.

To establish exponential decay of global-in-time solutions, and hence interpret
the dynamics as tumour extinction in the biological setting, we first record
the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.5

(Subexponential growth of fractional Brownian motion)
.

Let
(B_{t}^{H})_{t\geq 0}
be a fractional Brownian motion with
H\in(0,1)
.
Almost surely, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists

T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
such that

|B_{t}^{H}(\omega)|\leq\varepsilon t,\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega).

In particular, for every
c>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

e^{\pm cB_{t}^{H}(\omega)}\leq e^{\varepsilon t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega).

Proof.

Fractional Brownian motion has variance
\mathrm{Var}(B_{t}^{H})=t^{2H}

and satisfies a law of the iterated logarithm; in particular,

|B_{t}^{H}|=o(t)
as
t\to\infty
almost surely, see for instance

[
66
, Chapter 1]
.
Thus for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
T_{\varepsilon}
such that

|B_{t}^{H}|\leq\varepsilon t
for
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}
. The bound on

e^{\pm cB_{t}^{H}}
follows immediately.
∎

Theorem 4.6

(A.s. exponential decay for
p>q
and strong dissipation)
.

Assume
\beta>0
,
p>q
, and the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
.
Assume further that
\gamma<\lambda_{1}
and that
\beta
is sufficiently large
with respect to
\delta,\gamma
(so that the deterministic problem is
subcritical) and the noise term is rather weak (take
L_{\sigma}
in (
3.7
) small enough). Then almost surely there exist random

T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
and
C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
such that, for any

\varepsilon\in(0,(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)/4)
,

\|u(t,\omega)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\,e^{-\big(\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-2\varepsilon\big)t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega).

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)/4\bigr)
. We split the proof in
three steps.

Step 1: Exponential decay of
\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}
.

By the fractional Itô/energy formula given in Theorem
3.1

(for
p>q
), the
L^{2}
–energy of
u
satisfies

(4.3)

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}=-2\,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(u(t),u(t))+2\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)\,dy\Big)\|u(t)\|_{1}+2\gamma\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}-2\beta\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}+2\big(\sigma(u(t)),u(t)\big)\dot{B}_{t}^{H},

where
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
is the Dirichlet form associated with

-\Delta_{\alpha},
defined by (
3.2
). Taking expectations and using (
3.4
) together with

\mathbb{E}\big[(\sigma(u(t)),u(t))\dot{B}_{t}^{H}\big]\leq C(H,\sigma)\,\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2},

we obtain

(4.4)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq-2(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma))\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+2\delta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\!\int_{D}u^{q}dx\Big)\|u\|_{1}\Big]-2\beta\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{p+1}^{p+1}.

On the bounded domain
D
, Hölder’s inequalities give

\int_{D}|u|^{q}dx=\|u\|_{q}^{q}\leq|D|^{\frac{p+1-q}{p+1}}\|u\|_{p+1}^{q},\qquad\|u\|_{1}\leq|D|^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\|u\|_{p+1},

so that

\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}dx\Big)\|u\|_{1}\leq|D|\,\|u\|_{p+1}^{q+1}.

Substituting this into (
4.4
) yields

(4.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq-2(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma))\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+2\delta|D|\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{p+1}^{q+1}-2\beta\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{p+1}^{p+1}.

We now perform the splitting argument (large/small
\|u\|_{p+1}
) directly.
Set

M(t):=\|u(t)\|_{p+1}.

Large-
M
regime.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1)
and choose
\beta>0
(depending on
\delta,|D|,p,q,\eta
)
so large that there exists
R_{\beta}>0
with

(4.6)

\delta|D|\,r^{q+1}\leq\eta\beta\,r^{p+1},\quad\text{for all }r\geq R_{\beta}.

Equivalently,

r^{p-q}\geq\frac{\delta|D|}{\eta\beta},\qquad R_{\beta}:=\Big(\frac{\delta|D|}{\eta\beta}\Big)^{\!1/(p-q)}.

Since
p>q
, such
R_{\beta}
exists for any fixed
\eta\in(0,1)
when
\beta

is large enough. Then, whenever
M(t)\geq R_{\beta}
, we have

\delta|D|\,M(t)^{q+1}-\beta M(t)^{p+1}\leq(\eta\beta-\beta)M(t)^{p+1}=-(1-\eta)\beta\,M(t)^{p+1}.

Small-
M
regime.

If
M(t)<R_{\beta}
, then

M(t)^{q+1}\leq R_{\beta}^{q-1}M(t)^{2}.

On the other hand,
L^{p+1}(D)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(D)
(since
p+1>2
) implies

M(t)=\|u(t)\|_{p+1}\leq C_{D,p}\|u(t)\|_{2},

so that

\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{q+1}=M(t)^{q+1}\leq R_{\beta}^{q-1}C_{D,p}^{2}\,\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}.

Combining the two regimes.

The previous two estimates can be summarised as: there exists a constant

K_{\beta}>0
such that, for all
t\geq 0
,

(4.7)

\delta|D|\,\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{q+1}-\beta\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}\leq K_{\beta}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2},

with

K_{\beta}:=\delta|D|\,R_{\beta}^{q-1}C_{D,p}^{2}=\delta|D|\,C_{D,p}^{2}\Big(\frac{\delta|D|}{\eta\beta}\Big)^{\!\frac{q-1}{p-q}}.

Note that
K_{\beta}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
.

Substituting (
4.7
) into
(
4.5
) gives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq-2(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma))\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+2K_{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}=-2\big(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)-K_{\beta}\big)\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}.

Define

\kappa:=\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)-K_{\beta}.

Assume that
C(H,\sigma)<\lambda_{1}-\gamma
(which can be ensured by taking

L_{\sigma}
small enough). Since

K_{\beta}\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
, we can choose
\beta
so large that

K_{\beta}<\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)\big),

and hence

\kappa>\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)\big)>0.

Then

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq-2\kappa\,\mathbb{E}\|u\|_{2}^{2},

and Grönwall’s lemma yields a constant
C\geq 1
(depending on the data but
independent of
t
) such that

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\,e^{-2\kappa t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

This is the exponential mean-square
L^{2}
–decay estimate used in the
subsequent steps.

Step 2: Almost sure exponential decay in
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(D)
.

Fix any
\eta\in(0,\kappa)
; later we will choose
\eta
in relation to

\varepsilon
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Chebyshev’s inequality due to (
4.8
) gives

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|u(n)\|_{2}>e^{-(\kappa-\eta)n}\Big)\leq e^{2(\kappa-\eta)n}\,\mathbb{E}\|u(n)\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\,e^{2(\kappa-\eta)n}e^{-2\kappa n}=Ce^{-2\eta n}.

Hence

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\|u(n)\|_{2}>e^{-(\kappa-\eta)n}\Big)\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-2\eta n}<\infty.

By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, for almost every
\omega
there exists a random

N(\omega)
such that

\|u(n,\omega)\|_{2}\leq e^{-(\kappa-\eta)n},\quad\text{for all }n\geq N(\omega).

Since
t\mapsto\|u(t,\omega)\|_{2}
is continuous, we can interpolate between
integers and absorb multiplicative constants to obtain: there exist random

T_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)
and
C_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)
such that

(4.9)

\|u(t,\omega)\|_{2}\leq C_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)\,e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega).

Since
\eta\in(0,\kappa)
is arbitrary, we may (and do) choose it so that

(4.10)

\kappa-\eta>\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-\varepsilon.

Step 3: From
L^{2}
–decay to almost sure
L^{\infty}
–decay.

We now use the mild formulation

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=S_{\alpha}(t-s)u(s)+\int_{s}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\Big[\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}(r,y)\,dy\Big)+\gamma u(r)-\beta u^{p}(r)\Big]\,dr

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\sigma(u(r))\,dB_{r}^{H},

with
s=t-1
(for
t\geq 1
) and the smoothing estimate for the semigroup:
for some
\theta>0
and all
0<t-s\leq 1
,

(4.11)

\|S_{\alpha}(t-s)f\|_{\infty}\leq C(t-s)^{-\theta}\|f\|_{2},

see e.g.
[
11
,
17
]
.
We treat each term separately for
t\geq T_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)+1
.

(a) Linear term.

Using (
4.11
) with
s=t-1
and
(
4.9
),

\|S_{\alpha}(t-1)u(t-1,\omega)\|_{\infty}\leq C\|u(t-1,\omega)\|_{2}\leq CC_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)\,e^{-(\kappa-\eta)(t-1)}.

(b) Drift term.

For
r\in[t-1,t]
and
t
large, the drift

G(u(r)):=\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}(r,y)\,dy\Big)+\gamma u(r)-\beta u^{p}(r)

satisfies a pointwise bound of the form

\|G(u(r,\omega))\|_{2}\leq C\|u(r,\omega)\|_{2}+C\|u(r,\omega)\|_{p+1}^{p}\leq C_{\eta}^{(1)}(\omega)\,e^{-(\kappa-\eta)r},

where we used (
4.9
) and the embedding

L^{2}(D)\hookrightarrow L^{p+1}(D)
on
D
.
Then

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{t-1}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)G(u(r))\,dr\Big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t-1}^{t}\|S_{\alpha}(t-r)G(u(r))\|_{\infty}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{t-1}^{t}(t-r)^{-\theta}\|G(u(r))\|_{2}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq CC_{\eta}^{(1)}(\omega)\,e^{-(\kappa-\eta)(t-1)}\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\theta}\,ds.

Since
\theta<1
, the integral
\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\theta}\,ds
is finite and we
obtain

\Big\|\int_{t-1}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)G(u(r))\,dr\Big\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\eta}^{(2)}(\omega)\,e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}.

(c) Stochastic convolution.

For the Young stochastic integral on
[t-1,t]
we use that

B^{H}
is
\gamma
–Hölder for any
\gamma<H
and
\sigma
satisfies

|\sigma(r)|\leq C_{\sigma}(1+|r|),
cf. (
3.7
).
On the short interval
[t-1,t]
the Young estimate gives, for some

\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Big\|\int_{t-1}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\sigma(u(r))\,dB_{r}^{H}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq C(\omega)\,\sup_{r\in[t-1,t]}\|\sigma(u(r,\omega))\|_{2},

where
C(\omega)
depends on the Hölder norm of
B^{H}
on
[t-1,t]
but
not on
t
(by stationarity of increments). Using

|\sigma(r)|\leq C_{\sigma}(1+|r|)
and (
4.9
),

\sup_{r\in[t-1,t]}\|\sigma(u(r,\omega))\|_{2}\leq C_{\sigma}\Big(1+\sup_{r\in[t-1,t]}\|u(r,\omega)\|_{2}\Big)\leq C_{\sigma}\Big(1+C_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)e^{-(\kappa-\eta)(t-1)}\Big).

Thus we obtain a bound of the form

\Big\|\int_{t-1}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\sigma(u(r))\,dB_{r}^{H}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\eta}^{(3)}(\omega)\big(1+e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}\big).

To take into account the growth in
t
coming from the path
\omega
,
we use Lemma
4.5
, which states that for any

\varepsilon>0
there is
T_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\omega)
such that

|B_{t}^{H}(\omega)|\leq\varepsilon t,\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\omega).

Combining this with the continuity and stationarity of increments of
B^{H}

shows that
C_{\eta}^{(3)}(\omega)
may be chosen so that

C_{\eta}^{(3)}(\omega)\leq\tilde{C}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\,e^{\varepsilon t},

for
t
large enough. Hence for
t
large,

\Big\|\int_{t-1}^{t}S_{\alpha}(t-r)\sigma(u(r))\,dB_{r}^{H}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq\tilde{C}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\,e^{\varepsilon t}.

(d) Collecting the estimates.

Putting together (a), (b), and (c), we obtain for all

t\geq T_{\eta}(\omega):=T_{\eta}^{(0)}(\omega)+T_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\omega)+1
,

\|u(t,\omega)\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\eta}(\omega)\Big(e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}+e^{\varepsilon t}\Big),

for some random constant
C_{\eta}(\omega)
.

Recall that by (
4.10
),

\kappa-\eta>\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-\varepsilon,

and
\varepsilon<(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)/4
by assumption. In particular,

(\kappa-\eta)-\varepsilon>\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-2\varepsilon>0.

Thus the decaying term
e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}
dominates the subexponential
factor
e^{\varepsilon t}
:

e^{\varepsilon t}=e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}\,e^{(\kappa-\eta+\varepsilon)t}\leq e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}\,e^{(\kappa-\eta-\varepsilon+2\varepsilon)t}\leq e^{-(\kappa-\eta)t}\,e^{-(\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-2\varepsilon)t},

for
t
large, after adjusting constants. Absorbing all multiplicative
constants into a new random constant
C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
, we finally get

\|u(t,\omega)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\,e^{-\big(\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-2\varepsilon\big)t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega),

which is the desired almost sure exponential decay.
∎

Remark 4.7

(Biological interpretation: tumour extinction under strong therapy and weak environmental noise)
.

Proposition
4.6
has a clear extinction meaning:
under
strong dissipation
(
p>q
and
\beta
sufficiently large relative to

\delta,\gamma
) and
weak multiplicative environmental variability

(
L_{\sigma}
small), the tumour density decays to zero
almost surely
and

exponentially fast
in
L^{\infty}(D)
after a (random) transient time

T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
. In biological terms, this is a rigorous “tumour
eradication/extinction” scenario: for almost every realisation of environmental
fluctuations, the tumour burden eventually becomes arbitrarily small and keeps
shrinking at an exponential rate.

Moreover, the decay rate is controlled by the
spectral gap

\lambda_{1}-\gamma
: the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
-\Delta_{\alpha}

quantifies how strongly diffusion plus boundary losses suppress the population,
while
\gamma
quantifies net proliferation. Thus, larger
\lambda_{1}
(e.g. stronger effective diffusion/clearance or more confining domains) accelerates
extinction, whereas larger
\gamma
slows it down. The appearance of random
constants
C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
and
T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
is consistent with
heterogeneity: the same “therapy-dominant” regime produces extinction, but the
time-to-clearance and transient amplitude depend on the realised environmental
path. Connections between stochastic forcing and extinction/mean extinction times
are classical in population dynamics; see, e.g.,
[
44
,
82
]
.

Remark 4.8

(Deterministic benchmark, noise impact, and biological meaning)
.

Theorem
4.6
may be interpreted as a
robust
version of
the deterministic exponential stability obtained when the stochastic forcing is
absent.

Deterministic case.

If
\sigma\equiv 0
, the stochastic term in (
4.3
) vanishes and
(
4.5
) holds with
C(H,\sigma)=0
. Under the same
subcriticality assumptions (
p>q
,
\gamma<\lambda_{1}
, and
\beta
large so that

K_{\beta}
is small), one gets

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq-2(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-K_{\beta})\,\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2},\qquad t\geq 0,

hence

\|u(t)\|_{2}\leq\|u(0)\|_{2}\,e^{-(\lambda_{1}-\gamma-K_{\beta})t},

and, by semigroup smoothing, an analogous deterministic exponential decay in

L^{\infty}(D)
after a short transient. In the tumour interpretation, this
corresponds to
extinction/loss of viability
of the tumour cell density:
diffusion together with sufficiently strong local dissipation (
\beta u^{p}
)
overcomes the nonlocal proliferation drive.

Effect of multiplicative fBm noise.

When
\sigma\neq 0
, decay is weakened in two ways:

(i)

Reduced effective damping.

In (
4.5
), the stochastic term contributes a positive
quantity
C(H,\sigma)

\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}
, so the deterministic gap

\lambda_{1}-\gamma
is effectively replaced by
\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)
.
This explains the requirement that the noise be “weak” (small
L_{\sigma}
) so
that
\lambda_{1}-\gamma-C(H,\sigma)>0
.

(ii)

Random transients and rate loss.

Upgrading mean decay to an almost sure
L^{\infty}
estimate produces a random
prefactor
C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
and a random entrance time

T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
and forces a small loss in the exponent, yielding the
rate
\frac{\lambda_{1}-\gamma}{2}-2\varepsilon
in the theorem.

Biological interpretation (why stronger noise may prevent extinction).

Here the multiplicative term
\sigma(u)\,dB^{H}(t)
represents temporally
correlated fluctuations of the tumour microenvironment (e.g. variability in
nutrient/oxygen supply, immune pressure, vascular remodelling, or treatment
efficacy) that act proportionally to the current tumour burden. Larger noise
intensity amplifies intermittent
favourable episodes
during which the
effective net growth becomes less negative (or temporarily positive), allowing
the population to recover from low densities and thereby counteracting the
deterministic dissipation. Mathematically, this is reflected by the appearance
and growth of
C(H,\sigma)
: if
C(H,\sigma)
becomes comparable to

\lambda_{1}-\gamma
, the net damping can be neutralised, and the exponential
decay mechanism (tumour extinction) can no longer be guaranteed. In particular,
long-memory fluctuations (
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
) may sustain such favourable phases for
longer intervals, which further promotes persistence rather than extinction.

5.
Linear Fractional Multiplicative Noise

Henceforth, we focus on the case of the linear fractional multiplicative noise, that is we consider the problem:

(5.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} du(t,x)-\Delta_{\alpha}u(t,x)dt&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta\int_{D}u^{q}(t,y)dy+\gamma u(t,x)-\beta u^{p}(t,x)}}\right]dt+\sigma u(t,x)dB^{H}(t),\\
u(0,x)&=f(x),\ \ x\in D,\\
u(t,x)&=0,\ \ t>0,\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash D,\\
\end{aligned}}}\right.

where now
\sigma
is a positive constant. In that case a more delicate analysis could be delivered by making use of a Doss–Sussmann type transformation.

A Random PDE

Indeed, we consider of the random (Doss–Sussmann type) transformation

(5.2)

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\exp\{-\sigma B^{H}(t)\}u(t,x),\ t\geq 0,\ x\in D,

so that the equation
(
5.1
)
is transformed into the following random PDE:

(5.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{t}-\Delta_{\alpha}v(t,x)&=\gamma v(t,x)+\delta e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\int_{D}v^{q}(t,y)dy-\beta e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}v^{p}(t,x),\\
v(0,x)&=f(x),\ \ x\in D,\\
v(t,x)&=0,\ \ t>0,\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash D.\end{aligned}}}\right.

The following result provides an equivalence between the weak solution of the stochastic PDE (
1.1
) and that of the random PDE (
5.3
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u
be the weak solution of
(
5.1
)
. Then the function
v
defined by (
5.2
)
is a weak solution of the random PDE (
5.3
) and viceversa.

Proof.

By using Ito’s formula (see Theorem
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle e^{-\sigma B^{H}(t)}=1-\sigma\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\sigma B^{H}(s)}dB^{H}(s).

For any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
, the weak solution of the equation (
1.1
), as defined by Definition
3.3
, satisfies

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\tau_{\ast}
be defined by

(5.14)

\tau_{\ast}:=\inf\Bigg\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}\,\big\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\big\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}\,dr\geq\frac{1}{\delta|D|\,(q-1)\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}}\Bigg\}.

Then the stopping (blow–up) time
\tau_{b}
of problem (
5.3
)
satisfies

\tau_{\ast}\leq\tau_{b}.

Proof.

Fix
\omega\in\Omega
and work pathwise. In order to obtain an explicit upper
bound the stopping (blow–up) time
\tau_{b}
of problem (
5.3
), we introduce the auxiliary scalar function

\mathscr{G}
as the solution of a Bernoulli-type ordinary differential
equation.

Indeed, for
0<t<\tau_{\ast}
we define
\mathscr{G}:(0,\tau_{\ast})\to(0,\infty)
by

(5.15)

\mathscr{G}^{\prime}(t)=\delta|D|\,e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,\big\|e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\big\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\,\mathscr{G}^{q}(t),\qquad\mathscr{G}(0)=1.

This is a Bernoulli ODE with explicit solution. Solving (
5.15
) yields

(5.16)

\mathscr{G}(t)=\Bigg[1-(q-1)\delta|D|\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}\,\big\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\big\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\,dr\Bigg]^{-\frac{1}{q-1}},

which is well defined as long as the bracket in (
5.16
) remains
strictly positive. Accordingly,
\mathscr{G}
is defined on the maximal
interval
[0,\tau_{\ast})
, where
\tau_{\ast}
is given by (
5.14
).

Next, define the ordered set

\mathcal{M}:=\Big\{v\in C\!\big([0,\tau_{\ast});L^{\infty}(D)\big):\ 0\leq v(t,x)\leq e^{\gamma t}\,S_{\alpha}(t)\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(t)\Big\},

and the operator
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
by

(\mathcal{L}v)(t,x):=e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)f(x)+\delta\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)+\gamma(t-r)}\,S_{\alpha}(t-r)\Big(\int_{D}v(r,y)^{q}\,dy\Big)\,dr.

Since
S_{\alpha}(t)
is positivity preserving and
v\geq 0
, we have

(5.17)

e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)f(x)\leq(\mathcal{L}v)(t,x).

Moreover, if
v\in\mathcal{M}
, then

v(r,\cdot)\leq e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(r)
and hence

(5.18)

\int_{D}v(r,y)^{q}\,dy\leq|D|\,\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\|_{\infty}^{q}\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{q}\,\mathscr{G}(r)^{q}.

Using the semigroup property, (
5.18
) and the definition (
5.15
) of

, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}v)(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\|f\|_{\infty}+\delta|D|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}\,\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{q-1}\,\mathscr{G}(r)^{q}\,dr\;e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\|f\|_{\infty}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(t),

and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})\subset\mathcal{M}
by
(
5.17
)–(
5.19
).

Define the monotone Picard sequence

u^{(0)}(t,x):=e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)f(x),\qquad u^{(n)}:=\mathcal{L}u^{(n-1)},\quad n\geq 1.

By positivity of the integral term,
u^{(0)}\leq u^{(1)}
. If
u^{(n-1)}\leq u^{(n)}
,
then
(u^{(n-1)})^{q}\leq(u^{(n)})^{q}
and, since

is positivity preserving,

u^{(n)}=\mathcal{L}u^{(n-1)}\leq\mathcal{L}u^{(n)}=u^{(n+1)}.

Hence,
\{u^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 0}
is nondecreasing and bounded above by the envelope

e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(t)
, so it converges pointwise to
a limit
\tilde{u}(t,x)
for
0\leq t<\tau_{\ast}
. By monotone convergence,

\tilde{u}
satisfies
\tilde{u}=\mathcal{L}\tilde{u}
, i.e. it is a mild solution
of (
5.12
) (equivalently (

, and therefore

Finally, since

Combining this with

Remark 5.10

(Fractional diffusion/noise and the lower bound
\tau_{\ast}
)
.

Let
\tau_{\ast}^{(2,1/2)}
be the lower bound in
[
60
]
(classical diffusion

\alpha=2
and Brownian noise
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
), and let
\tau_{\ast}^{(\alpha,H)}
be
given by (
5.14
). Since
\tau_{\ast}
is the first time a nondecreasing
integral reaches a fixed threshold, a larger integrand yields a smaller

\tau_{\ast}
, and vice versa.

Effect of the fractional Laplacian.

For large times the semigroup decay is governed by the principal Dirichlet
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}(D)
of
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
, so larger

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
strengthens damping, reduces the integrand, and tends to

increase

\tau_{\ast}
. Using the comparison

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}\asymp(\lambda_{1}^{(2)})^{\alpha/2}
on convex domains

[
32
]
, the sign depends on the domain scale:
if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}>1
(small/confined
D
) then
\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}<\lambda_{1}^{(2)}

for
\alpha<2
, so fractional diffusion weakens damping and tends to

decrease

\tau_{\ast}
; if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}<1
(large
D
) the opposite trend
holds, and
\tau_{\ast}
tends to
increase
.

Effect of fractional Brownian noise.

Replacing
W_{t}
by
B_{t}^{H}
with
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
increases the typical magnitude and
persistence of excursions of the Gaussian weight
e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}

(cf.
\Var(B_{t}^{H})=t^{2H}
), which tends to
decrease

\tau_{\ast}
when

\sigma>0
and to
increase
it when
\sigma<0
(see, e.g.,

[
66
,
67
]
).

In summary, compared to
[
60
]
the net change in
\tau_{\ast}
is not
universally monotone: fractional diffusion may increase or decrease
\tau_{\ast}

depending on the domain scale, while fractional Brownian forcing typically
pushes
\tau_{\ast}
down for
\sigma>0.

Remark 5.11

(Effect of
\sigma
on the lower bound
\tau_{\ast}
)
.

Let
\tau_{\ast}(\sigma)
be defined by (
5.14
). For
\sigma=0
,

\tau_{\ast}(0)=\inf\Bigg\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\big\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}\,dr\geq\frac{1}{\delta|D|(q-1)\|f\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}}\Bigg\}.

Thus noise affects
\tau_{\ast}
only through the weight
e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}
.
Since
\tau_{\ast}
is a first hitting time of a nondecreasing integral, larger
values of
e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}
(on the relevant time window) make the
threshold reached sooner, hence
decrease

\tau_{\ast}
, while smaller values

increase
it. In particular, for
\sigma>0
sustained positive excursions
of
B^{H}
tend to yield
\tau_{\ast}(\sigma)\leq\tau_{\ast}(0)
, whereas sustained
negative excursions yield
\tau_{\ast}(\sigma)\geq\tau_{\ast}(0)
. For
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the persistence of excursions of
B^{H}

typically amplifies this effect; see, e.g.,
[
66
,
67
]
.

Upper bound for blow–up time
\tau_{b}

In this subsection we derive an explicit
upper bound

\tau^{\ast}
for the
blow–up time
\tau_{b}
under suitable assumptions. Let
t\in(0,\infty)
be a (fixed)
random time and choose
b>1
such that

(5.23)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} b^{\,q-p}\delta\,e^{-\sigma(q-p)B_{\ast}^{H}(t)}&\geq\frac{\beta M_{1}^{p}+\lambda_{1}M_{1}}{|D|^{\,1-q}}\geq\frac{\beta M_{1}^{p}}{|D|^{\,1-q}}\ \text{and}\\[1.99997pt]
b^{\,q-p}\delta\,e^{-\sigma(q-1)B_{\ast}^{H}(t)}&\geq\frac{2\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{p}}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{p+1}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{p}}}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Recall that
(\lambda_{1},\varphi_{1})
denotes the principal Dirichlet eigenpair of

-\Delta_{\alpha}
as in (
3.5
), normalised by (
3.6
), and

M_{1}:=\sup_{x\in D}\varphi_{1}(x)>0
while
B_{*}^{H}(t)
is given by (
5.6
). The next result then yields an upper bound
for the blow–up time of the solution
v(\cdot,\cdot)
to (
5.3
), and consequently for the
solution
u(\cdot,\cdot)
of (
1.1
).

Theorem 5.12
.

Assume that
p,q>1
with
p>q
. Let the initial datum satisfy

f(x)\geq b\,\varphi_{1}(x),\qquad x\in D,

and fix
b>1
such that the conditions in (
5.23
) hold. Then the blow–up time

\tau_{b}
of the solution satisfies
\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
, where

(5.24)

\tau^{\ast}:=\inf\Biggl\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}e^{\sigma(q-1)B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds\geq\frac{2\,J(0)^{\,1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}\Biggr\},

with

J(0):=\int_{D}f(x)\,\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx.

Proof.

Step 1: A stationary subsolution and a lower bound for
v
.

Fix
b>1
and define

\bar{v}(t,x):=b\,\varphi_{1}(x),\qquad t>0,\ x\in D,

and
\bar{v}(t,x)=0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D
. By assumption,

v(0,x)\geq f(x)\geq b\,\varphi_{1}(x)=\bar{v}(0,x),\qquad x\in D.

Consider the (random) PDE satisfied by
v
in (
5.3
). Writing the nonlinear
reaction part evaluated at
\bar{v}
as

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\delta e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\int_{D}\bar{v}(t,y)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}y-\beta e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\bar{v}(t,x)^{p}

(5.25)

\displaystyle=\delta e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,b^{q}\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\beta e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,b^{p}\varphi_{1}^{p}(x),

we first estimate the integral of
\varphi_{1}^{q}
. By Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\Bigg(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigg)^{\!\frac{1}{q}}\Big(\int_{D}1^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\!\frac{q-1}{q}}=\Bigg(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Bigg)^{\!\frac{1}{q}}|D|^{\frac{q-1}{q}},

which yields

\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\Big)^{q}|D|^{1-q}.

Under our normalisation
\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
, this implies

\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{q}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq|D|^{1-q}.

Using this in (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq\delta e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,b^{q}|D|^{1-q}-\beta e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,b^{p}\varphi_{1}^{p}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}b^{p}\Big[\delta e^{(q-p)\sigma B^{H}(t)}b^{q-p}|D|^{1-q}-\beta\varphi_{1}^{p}(x)\Big].

Noting that
B_{\ast}^{H}(t):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B^{H}(s)|
and using

e^{\pm\sigma B^{H}(t)}\geq e^{-\sigma B_{\ast}^{H}(t)}
, we further get

I_{1}(t,x)\geq e^{-(p-1)\sigma B_{\ast}^{H}(t)}b^{p}\Big[\delta e^{-(q-p)\sigma B_{\ast}^{H}(t)}b^{q-p}|D|^{1-q}-\beta M_{1}^{p}\Big],

recalling that
M_{1}:=\sup_{x\in D}\varphi_{1}(x)>0.

By the first inequality in (
5.23
), the quantity in brackets is bounded
from below by
\lambda_{1}b\,\varphi_{1}(x)
, and hence

I_{1}(t,x)\geq\lambda_{1}b\,\varphi_{1}(x),\qquad x\in D,\ t\geq 0.

On the other hand,

\frac{\partial\bar{v}(t,x)}{\partial t}-(\Delta_{\alpha}+\gamma)\bar{v}(t,x)=(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)b\varphi_{1}(x)\leq I_{1}(t,x)-\gamma\bar{v}(t,x)\leq I_{1}(t,x),

so
\bar{v}
is a subsolution of the random PDE satisfied by
v
(with the same
boundary condition and smaller initial data). By the comparison principle in
Proposition
5.7
, we conclude that

v(t,x)\geq\bar{v}(t,x)=b\,\varphi_{1}(x),\qquad x\in D,\ t\geq 0.

Step 2: Testing against the first eigenfunction.

Define

(5.26)

J(t):=\int_{D}v(t,x)\,\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Testing (
5.3
) against
\varphi_{1}
and using
-\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{1}=\lambda_{1}\varphi_{1}

yields

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}v_{t}(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)J(t)+\delta e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\Big(\int_{D}v(t,y)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}y\Big)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)-\beta e^{(p-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{D}v(t,y)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\Big(\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{q}{p}}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}.

Substituting this into the expression for
J^{\prime}(t)
gives

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(t)\geq(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)J(t)

(5.27)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\Big(\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{q}{p}}\Bigg[\delta\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}-\beta e^{(p-q)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\Big(\int_{D}v^{p}(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}\Bigg].

Step 3: Removing the damping term via (
5.23
).

Using
f\geq b\varphi_{1}
and the positivity of
v
, the comparison argument above
implies
v(t,x)\geq b\varphi_{1}(x)
and thus

\Big(\int_{D}v^{p}(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}\geq b^{p-q}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{p+1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}.

The second inequality in (
5.23
) is chosen precisely so that, after replacing

B^{H}
by
B^{H}_{\ast}
to control the exponential factor,

\beta e^{(p-q)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\Big(\int_{D}v^{p}(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}

on the time interval of interest. Hence the bracket in (
5
) is
bounded below by

\frac{\delta}{2}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}},

and we obtain

(5.28)

J^{\prime}(t)\geq(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)J(t)+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\Big(\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{q}{p}}.

Step 4: Jensen’s inequality and a Bernoulli-type differential inequality.

Since
\varphi_{1}\geq 0
and
\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\ \geq\ \Big(\int_{D}v(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{p}=J(t)^{p},

and therefore

\Big(\int_{D}v(t,x)^{p}\varphi_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{q}{p}}\ \geq\ J(t)^{q}.

Plugging this into (
5.28
) yields the Bernoulli-type inequality

(5.29)

J^{\prime}(t)\geq(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)J(t)+c_{0}\,e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,J(t)^{q},\qquad c_{0}:=\frac{\delta}{2}\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}.

Step 5: Comparison with the Bernoulli ODE and blow–up time.

Let
I(t)
solve the (random-coefficient) Bernoulli ODE

I^{\prime}(t)=(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)I(t)+c_{0}\,e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}\,I(t)^{q},\qquad I(0)=J(0).

Standard comparison for scalar differential inequalities implies
J(t)\geq I(t)

as long as both are finite (see, e.g.,
[
81
, Theorem 1.3]
). Solving this
Bernoulli equation explicitly gives

I(t)=e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)t}\Bigg[I(0)^{1-q}-(q-1)c_{0}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigg]^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}.

Thus
I(t)
blows up at the time
\tau^{\ast}
defined by

\tau^{\ast}:=\inf\Biggl\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}e^{\sigma(q-1)B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,\mathrm{d}s\geq\frac{I(0)^{1-q}}{(q-1)c_{0}}\Biggr\},

which is exactly (
5.24
). Since
J(t)\geq I(t)
, the explosion of
I
forces

J
to blow up no later than
\tau^{\ast}
. Therefore the solution
v
of (
5.3
)
(and hence
u
of (
1.1
)) cannot exist beyond
\tau_{b}
, with
\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
.
∎

Remark 5.13

(Fractional diffusion/noise and the upper bound
\tau^{\ast}
)
.

Let
\tau^{\ast}_{(2,1/2)}
denote the analogue of (
5.24
) in
[
60
]

(classical diffusion
\alpha=2
and Brownian noise
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
), and let

\tau^{\ast}_{(\alpha,H)}
be given by (
5.24
). Since
\tau^{\ast}
is the first
time a nondecreasing integral reaches a fixed threshold, a larger integrand in
(
5.24
) yields a smaller
\tau^{\ast}
, and vice versa.

Effect of the fractional Laplacian.

The semigroup damping in (
5.24
) is governed by the principal Dirichlet
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}(D)
of
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
: larger

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}
strengthens the factor
e^{(-\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}+\gamma)(q-1)s}
,
reduces the integrand, and thus tends to
increase

\tau^{\ast}
(later blow–up
upper bound). On convex domains one has the comparison

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}(D)\asymp(\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(D))^{\alpha/2}
(up to universal
constants), see
[
32
]
. Hence the direction depends on
the domain scale: if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(D)>1
(small/confined
D
) then typically

\lambda_{1}^{(\alpha)}<\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
for
\alpha<2
, so fractional diffusion
weakens damping and tends to
decrease

\tau^{\ast}
; if
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(D)<1

(large
D
), the opposite tendency holds and
\tau^{\ast}
tends to
increase
.

Effect of fractional Brownian noise.

Replacing Brownian motion by
B^{H}
with
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
changes the exponential weight

e^{\sigma(q-1)B^{H}(s)}
. Since
\Var(B^{H}(t))=t^{2H}
, larger
H
corresponds to
larger typical long-time excursions and stronger persistence, which tends to

decrease

\tau^{\ast}
when
\sigma>0
and to
increase
it when
\sigma<0
,
see, e.g.,
[
66
,
67
]
.

In summary, relative to
[
60
]
the net change in
\tau^{\ast}
is not
universally monotone: fractional diffusion may increase or decrease
\tau^{\ast}

depending on the confinement scale of
D
, while fractional Brownian forcing
typically pushes
\tau^{\ast}
down for
\sigma>0
.

Remark 5.14

(Effect of
\sigma
on the upper bound
\tau^{\ast}
)
.

Let
\tau^{\ast}(\sigma)
be defined by (
5.24
). Since
\tau^{\ast}(\sigma)
is
the first time a nondecreasing integral reaches a fixed threshold, a larger
integrand yields a smaller
\tau^{\ast}
, and vice versa. In particular, the
deterministic case
\sigma=0
gives

\tau^{\ast}(0)=\inf\Biggl\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds\geq\frac{2\,J(0)^{\,1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}\Biggr\}.

For
\sigma\neq 0
, the only change is the random weight

e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(s)}
in (
5.24
). Hence, for
\sigma>0
one has the
pathwise tendencies

B^{H}(\cdot)\geq 0\ \text{on the relevant window}\ \Rightarrow\ \tau^{\ast}(\sigma)\leq\tau^{\ast}(0),\qquad B^{H}(\cdot)\leq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \tau^{\ast}(\sigma)\geq\tau^{\ast}(0).

In short, relative to
\sigma=0
,
multiplicative noise may advance or delay the upper bound depending on the
realized sign/persistence of
B^{H}
; for
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
persistent excursions make
early threshold crossing more likely when
\sigma>0
(see, e.g.,

[
66
,
67
]
).

Remark 5.15
.

For initial data of the form

f(x)=b\,\varphi_{1}(x),\qquad x\in D,\qquad b>1,

and under the hypotheses ensuring the validity of the lower and upper estimates
(so that Theorem
5.8
yields
\tau_{\ast}\leq\tau_{b}
and Theorem
5.12

yields
\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
), the random times (
5.14
) and (
5.24
)
provide the
pathwise
bracketing

\tau_{\ast}\;\leq\;\tau_{b}\;\leq\;\tau^{\ast},\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

In particular,
\tau_{\ast}
is constructed from a
global
L^{\infty}
-control

through the fractional heat semigroup
(S_{\alpha}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, whereas
\tau^{\ast}

is obtained by
projection onto the principal mode

\varphi_{1}
and depends
explicitly on the spectral gap
\lambda_{1}
.

Specialization to
f=b\varphi_{1}
.

In this case
J(0)=\int_{D}f(x)\varphi_{1}(x)dx=b\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{2}(x)dx
, and thus the threshold
in (
5.24
) becomes

\frac{2\,J(0)^{\,1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}=\frac{2\,b^{\,1-q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{2}(x)dx}}\right)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}},

while the threshold in (
5.14
) reads

\frac{1}{\delta|D|(q-1)\|f\|_{\infty}^{q-1}}=\frac{1}{\delta|D|(q-1)\,b^{\,q-1}\|\varphi_{1}\|_{\infty}^{q-1}}.

Hence, both bounds scale as
b^{-(q-1)}
in the threshold: increasing the initial
amplitude
b
decreases the right-hand sides, so the defining integrals reach
their thresholds earlier and both
\tau_{\ast}
and
\tau^{\ast}
typically move

down
(shorter pre-blow–up window).

Comparison of the two integrands.

The lower bound (
5.14
) involves

e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(r)}\,\big\|e^{\gamma r}S_{\alpha}(r)\big\|_{\infty}^{q-1},

which captures
uniform
semigroup damping (hence depends on the full
nonlocal geometry). By contrast, the upper bound (
5.24
) involves

e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(s)}\,e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s},

which isolates the
principal Dirichlet mode
via
\lambda_{1}
and is
therefore sharper when the dynamics are dominated by
\varphi_{1}
.
Consequently, for eigenfunction initial data
b\varphi_{1}
one typically expects

\tau^{\ast}
to be closer to
\tau_{b}
, while
\tau_{\ast}
provides a more robust
(but possibly more conservative) guarantee that blow–up cannot occur before

\tau_{\ast}
.

In summary, for
f=b\varphi_{1}
the quantities (
5.14
)–(
5.24
) yield an
explicit random estimation interval
[\tau_{\ast},\tau^{\ast}]
for the blow–up time

\tau_{b}
, with
\tau_{\ast}
reflecting global
L^{\infty}
-semigroup control and

\tau^{\ast}
reflecting principal-eigenmode amplification.

Two–sided estimates for the blow–up rate in the maximum norm

We now quantify the growth of the solution in the maximum norm by combining

a priori upper envelope
on the guaranteed existence interval

[0,\tau_{\ast})
(Theorem
5.8
) with the
Bernoulli lower growth estimate

leading to the upper bound
\tau^{\ast}
(Theorem
5.12
).

Theorem 5.16

(Lower and upper bounds on
\|v(t)\|_{\infty}
)
.

Let
v
be the (pathwise) mild solution of (
5.3
) with blow–up time
\tau_{b}
.

(i) Upper envelope up to
\tau_{\ast}
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
5.8
, and let
\tau_{\ast}
be defined by
(
5.14
). Then, for all
0\leq t<\tau_{\ast}
,

(5.30)

\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq\big\|e^{\gamma t}S_{\alpha}(t)\big\|_{\infty\to\infty}\,\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(t),

where
\mathscr{G}
is given by (
5.16
). In particular,
the right-hand side diverges as
t\uparrow\tau_{\ast}
.

(ii) Bernoulli lower growth up to
\tau^{\ast}
.

Assume
p,q>1
with
p>q
, let
f\geq b\varphi_{1}
on
D
, and choose
b>1
so that
(
5.23
) holds. Set

J(0):=\int_{D}f(x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx,\qquad c_{0}:=\frac{\delta}{2}\Bigg(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,dx\Bigg)^{\frac{p-q}{p}},

and let
\tau^{\ast}
be defined by (
5.24
). Then, for all
0\leq t<\tau^{\ast}
,

(5.31)

\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\geq e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)t}\Bigg[J(0)^{1-q}-(q-1)c_{0}\!\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds\Bigg]^{-\frac{1}{q-1}},

and the right-hand side diverges as
t\uparrow\tau^{\ast}
.

Consequently,
\tau_{\ast}\leq\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
and, on the
common interval
[0,\tau_{\ast})
,
\|v(t)\|_{\infty}
is trapped between the explicit
lower bound (
5.31
) and the explicit upper envelope
(
5.30
).

Proof.

Step 1: proof of (
5.30
).

From Theorem
5.8
(inequality (
5.21
)) we have, for
0\leq t<\tau_{\ast}
,

0\leq v(t,x)\leq e^{\gamma t}\,(S_{\alpha}(t)\mathbf{1})(x)\,\|f\|_{\infty}\,\mathscr{G}(t),\qquad x\in D.

Taking the supremum over
x\in D
yields (
5.30
). The
blow–up of the right-hand side as
t\uparrow\tau_{\ast}
follows from the explicit
formula (
5.16
), since
\tau_{\ast}
is defined as the hitting time
of the critical level of the integral in the denominator.

Step 2: proof of (
5.31
).

As in the proof of Theorem
5.12
, the functional
J
satisfies the scalar
differential inequality

J^{\prime}(t)\geq(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)J(t)+c_{0}\,e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(t)}J(t)^{q}.

Let
I
solve the associated Bernoulli ODE with
I(0)=J(0)
. By comparison for
scalar differential inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
81
, Theorem 1.3]
),

J(t)\geq I(t)
on the common interval of existence, and solving the Bernoulli
equation gives

I(t)=e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)t}\Bigg[J(0)^{1-q}-(q-1)c_{0}\!\int_{0}^{t}e^{(q-1)\sigma B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds\Bigg]^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}.

Finally, since
\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx=1
and
\varphi_{1}\geq 0
,

J(t)=\int_{D}v(t,x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx\leq\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty},

hence
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\geq J(t)\geq I(t)
, which is exactly
(
5.31
). The divergence as
t\uparrow\tau^{\ast}
is built into
the definition (
5.24
).
∎

Remark 5.17

(Reading the two bounds as “rate information”)
.

The upper envelope (
5.30
) is an
a priori
control
valid up to the guaranteed time
\tau_{\ast}
(hence it provides an explicit
upper-growth profile on
[0,\tau_{\ast})
). The lower bound
(
5.31
) shows that, under the conditions of
Theorem
5.12
, the maximum norm must grow at least as fast as a Bernoulli
profile and cannot stay bounded up to
\tau^{\ast}
. Together, these estimates
yield a quantitative bracketing of the growth of
\|v(t)\|_{\infty}
before blow–up.

Blow–up probability

Next we quantify the
probability of blow–up
for the solution of
(
5.3
) (and therefore also for the original variable in (
1.1
)).
A key step is to control tail probabilities for exponential functionals of the
driving noise. More precisely, we shall obtain an upper bound for

(5.32)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\{-as+\sigma X_{s}\}\,ds<x\Bigg),

where
x>0
,
\sigma>0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and
(X_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is a continuous
stochastic process (in particular, this framework covers the choice

X_{s}=B_{s}^{H}
,
H\in(0,1)
).

To this end, we impose the following standing assumption on
X_{s}
.

Assumption 5.18
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-adapted, continuous process. We assume:

(A1)

The exponential moment functional is integrable:

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-as}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\sigma X_{s}}\big]\,ds<\infty.

(A2)

For every
t\geq 0
, the random variable
X_{t}
is Malliavin differentiable,
namely
X_{t}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
.

(A3)

There exist a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)=\infty
and,
for each
x>0
, a finite constant
M_{x}
(possibly depending on
x
) such that,
almost surely,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\int_{0}^{s}|D_{\theta}X_{s}|^{2}\,d\theta}{\big(\ln(x+1)+f(t)\big)^{2}}\ \leq\ M_{x}\ <\ \infty,

where
D_{\theta}X_{s}
stands for the Malliavin derivative of the stochastic process
X_{s},
cf. Section
3
.

Theorem 5.19
.

[
31
, Theorem 3.1]

Suppose that Assumption
5.18
holds. We have

(5.33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\{-as+\sigma X_{s}\}ds<x}}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\frac{(m_{x}-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}M_{x}}}}\right\},

where

\displaystyle m_{x}=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{t}\exp\{-as+\sigma X_{s}\}ds+1}}\right)+f(t)}{\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+1}}\right)+f(t)}}}\right]\geq 1.

The next result provides a quantitative estimate on the
probability of finite–time
blow–up
for (
1.1
) in the fractional Brownian regime
H\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
.
It is formulated in terms of the random upper bound
\tau^{\ast}
derived in
Theorem
5.12
, and yields an explicit lower bound for the blow–up
probability.

Theorem 5.20
.

Assume
\alpha_{1}>H
. Suppose
q>p>1
and fix initial
data
f\geq 0
satisfying
f(x)\geq b\,\varphi_{1}(x)
for some
b>1
, where

\varphi_{1}
denotes the principal Dirichlet eigenfunction of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
in

D
. Assume also that
b
is chosen so that (
5.23
) holds and let
\tau
be
the blow–up (maximal existence) time of (
1.1
). Then:

(i)

If
\lambda_{1}<\gamma
, then
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\ast}=\infty)=0
for any
nontrivial nonnegative solution of (
1.1
). In particular,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)=1,

that is, blow–up occurs in finite–time almost surely.

(ii)

If
\lambda_{1}>\gamma
, then the probability of blow–up admits the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)\geq 1

(5.34)

\displaystyle-\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\Bigg(\log\Bigg(\frac{2J(0)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}+1\Bigg)\Bigg)^{\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}-2}\Bigg(\frac{\alpha_{1}-H}{\alpha_{1}}\Bigg)^{2-\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}}\big(N(H)-1\big)^{2}\Bigg\},

where
\rho:=\sigma(q-1)
,

J(0):=\int_{D}f(x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx,

and
N(H)
is defined by

(5.35)

\displaystyle N(H):=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{\ln\!\Big(1+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\!\big\{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s-\tfrac{\rho^{2}}{2}s^{2H}+\rho B^{H}(s)\big\}\,ds\Big)+t^{\alpha_{1}}}{\ln\!\Big(\frac{2J(0)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}+1\Big)+t^{\alpha_{1}}}\Bigg].

Proof.

We prove the two assertions separately.

(i)

Case
\lambda_{1}<\gamma
.

Recall the law of the iterated logarithm for fractional Brownian motion
(see, e.g.,
[
5
]
): almost surely,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{B^{H}(t)}{t^{H}\sqrt{2\log\log t}}=-1,\qquad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{B^{H}(t)}{t^{H}\sqrt{2\log\log t}}=1.

In particular,
B^{H}(t)
attains arbitrarily large positive values along a
sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
.
Since
\gamma-\lambda_{1}>0
, the deterministic factor

e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}
grows exponentially in
s
, and therefore the
integral appearing in the definition of
\tau^{\ast}
(see (
5.24
)) diverges
almost surely as
t\to\infty
. Consequently,

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\ast}=\infty)=0.

By Theorem
5.12
, the maximal existence time
\tau_{b}
satisfies

\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
, hence
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)=1
, i.e. blow–up occurs in
finite–time almost surely for any nontrivial nonnegative solution.

(ii)

Case
\lambda_{1}>\gamma
.

By the definition of
\tau^{\ast}
and the fact that
\rho>0
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\ast}=\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho B^{H}(s)+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds<\frac{2J(0)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho B^{H}(s)-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}s^{2H}+(-\lambda_{1}+\gamma)(q-1)s}\,ds<\frac{2J(0)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}(x)^{\frac{q}{q-p}}\,dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}\Bigg),

where the last step uses the standard exponential normalization by

e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}s^{2H}}
.

Set

X_{t}:=-\frac{\rho}{2}\,t^{2H}+B^{H}(t),\quad\rho:=k(q-1),\quad a:=(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)(q-1),\quad x:=\frac{2J(0)^{1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\Big(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)dx\Big)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}.

Then the preceding probability is of the form (
5.32
):

\mathbb{P}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-as+\rho X_{s}}\,ds<x\Big).

We now verify that
X_{t}
satisfies Assumption
5.18
with the choice

f(t)=t^{\alpha_{1}}
,
\alpha_{1}>H
.

(A1).
Using
\Var(B^{H}(s))=s^{2H}
and

\mathbb{E}e^{\rho B^{H}(s)}=\exp\{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{2}s^{2H}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-as}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\rho X_{s}}\big]\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-as}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big\{\rho B^{H}(s)-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}s^{2H}\Big\}\Big]\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-as}\,ds=\frac{1}{a}<\infty,

since
a=(\lambda_{1}-\gamma)(q-1)>0
.

(A2).
Since
B^{H}(t)\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
for each
t\geq 0
, and
t^{2H}

is deterministic, it follows that
X_{t}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
.

(A3).
The Malliavin derivative satisfies

D_{\theta}X_{s}=D_{\theta}B^{H}(s)=K_{H}(s,\theta)
for
\theta\leq s
. Hence

\int_{0}^{s}|D_{\theta}X_{s}|^{2}\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{s}K_{H}(s,\theta)^{2}\,d\theta=\Var(B^{H}(s))=s^{2H},

and therefore

(5.36)

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\int_{0}^{s}|D_{\theta}X_{s}|^{2}\,d\theta=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}s^{2H}=t^{2H}.

With
f(t)=t^{\alpha_{1}}
and
\alpha_{1}>H
, the ratio in Assumption
5.18
(A3)
is almost surely bounded:

\sup_{t\geq 0}\frac{t^{2H}}{\big(\ln(x+1)+t^{\alpha_{1}}\big)^{2}}=:M_{H}<\infty,

and a direct optimisation yields

M_{H}=\Big(\frac{\alpha_{1}-H}{\alpha_{1}}\Big)^{2-\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}}\Big(\ln(x+1)\Big)^{\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}-2}.

Having verified Assumption
5.18
, we can apply Theorem
5.19
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\ast}=\infty)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\Big(\ln(x+1)\Big)^{\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}-2}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{1}-H}{\alpha_{1}}\Big)^{2-\frac{2H}{\alpha_{1}}}\big(N(H)-1\big)^{2}\Bigg\}.

Finally, since
\{\tau_{b}=\infty\}\subseteq\{\tau^{\ast}=\infty\}
(because

\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{\ast}
), we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)=1-\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}=\infty)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\ast}=\infty),

and substituting the previous bound yields exactly (
(ii)
).
This completes the proof.

∎

6.
The special case
\frac{3}{4}<H<1

In this section we focus on the case
\tfrac{3}{4}<H<1
. In this parameter range,
the Gaussian measures on
C([0,T])
induced by the fractional Brownian motion

B^{H}
and by a standard Brownian motion
W
are
equivalent
(mutually
absolutely continuous); see
[
19
, Theorem 1.7]
. Consequently, for
pathwise questions and blow–up/existence criteria that are stable under
equivalent changes of measure, it is convenient to work on a probability space
carrying a Brownian motion
W
and to view the fractional-noise model through a
Brownian representation.

In particular, introducing the stochastic exponential transformation

v(t,x):=\exp\{-\sigma W(t)\}\,u(t,x),\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in D,

eliminates the multiplicative Brownian term and converts (
1.1
) into the
following random (pathwise) parabolic problem with time-dependent coefficients:

(6.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{t}(t,x)-\Delta_{\alpha}v(t,x)&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}}\right)v(t,x)+\delta\,e^{(q-1)\sigma W(t)}\int_{D}v^{q}(t,y)\,dy-\beta\,e^{(p-1)\sigma W(t)}v^{p}(t,x),\\
v(t,x)&=0,\qquad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,\\
v(0,x)&=f(x),\qquad x\in D.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here, the correction term
-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}v
is the usual Itô contribution
arising from the product rule for
e^{-\sigma W(t)}u(t,\cdot)
.

Let

\vartheta:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}-\gamma,

so that the linear term in (
6.1
) can be written as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}}\right)v=-\vartheta\,v.

With this notation, all arguments developed in the previous sections apply to
(
6.1
) after the simple replacement
\gamma\mapsto-\vartheta
. In
particular, the same comparison principles and Osgood-type criteria yield
analogous existence and blow–up regimes, and the qualitative finite–time blow–up
statements remain the same as in the fractional setting
H\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
.

The next theorem formulates explicit lower bounds for the finite–time blow–up
of solutions to the Brownian-transformed random PDE (
6.1
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that
p,q>0
and let
f\in L^{\infty}(D)
satisfy
f\geq 0
. Define the
stopping time

(6.2)

\tau_{**}:=\inf\Bigg\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}\exp\!\big\{(q-1)\sigma W(r)\big\}\,\big\|e^{-\vartheta r}S_{\alpha}(r)\big\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}\,dr\geq\frac{1}{\delta|D|\,(q-1)\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}}\Bigg\}.

Let
\tau_{b}
be the
(maximal existence) blow–up time of the solution
v
to (
6.1
). Then

\tau_{**}\leq\tau_{b}.

The proof of Theorem
6.1
is a direct consequence of
Theorem
5.8
, applied to the transformed equation (
6.1
) after the
parameter substitution
\gamma\mapsto-\vartheta
(equivalently,

\gamma-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}=-\vartheta
) and with
B_{t}^{H}
replaced by the Brownian
motion
W_{t}
.

We recall that a random variable
X(\alpha_{2},\beta_{1})
is said to follow a

Gamma distribution
with shape parameter
\alpha_{2}>0
and scale parameter

\beta_{1}>0
if it admits the density
\widetilde{f}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}}
(cf.
[
58
]
)

\widetilde{f}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{1}}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{\beta_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}\exp\!\Big\{-\dfrac{x}{\beta_{1}}\Big\},&x\geq 0,\\[8.00003pt]
0,&x<0.\end{cases}

The following classical identity in law for exponential functionals of Brownian
motion will be used to derive a lower bound on the blow–up probability of solutions to
(
6.1
).

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\bar{\alpha}_{1}>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2(W(t)-\bar{\alpha}_{1}t)}\,dt\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle law}}{{=}}\ \frac{1}{2\,X(\bar{\alpha}_{1},1)},

where
(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion and

X(\bar{\alpha}_{1},1)
is a Gamma random variable with parameters

(\bar{\alpha}_{1},1)
. See, e.g.,
[
64
,
74
,
88
]
.

Let

W_{\ast}(t):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|W(s)|,\qquad t>0,

where
(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion. By the
classical maximal inequality for Brownian motion (see, e.g.,

[
48
, p. 96]
),
for any
A>0
and
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(W_{\ast}(t)\geq A\big)\leq\frac{4\sqrt{t}}{A\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{A^{2}}{2t}}}\right).

In particular,
W_{\ast}(t)<\infty
almost surely for every fixed
t>0
.

Fix a (possibly random)
t\in(0,\infty)
and choose
b>1
such that

(6.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} b^{\,q-p}\delta\,e^{-\sigma(q-p)W_{\ast}(t)}&\geq\frac{\beta M_{1}^{p}+(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)M_{1}}{|D|^{\,1-q}}\ \geq\ \frac{\beta M_{1}^{p}}{|D|^{\,1-q}},\ \text{and}\\[3.00003pt]
b^{\,q-p}\delta\,e^{-\sigma(q-1)W_{\ast}(t)}&\geq\frac{2\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{p}}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{p+1}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{q-p}{p}}},\end{aligned}}}\right.

recalling that
(\lambda_{1},\varphi_{1})
denotes the principal Dirichlet eigenpair of

-\Delta_{\alpha}
in
D
(normalised as in (
3.6
)) and

M_{1}:=\displaystyle\sup_{x\in D}\varphi_{1}(x)\in(0,\infty).

The next theorem, inspired by the analysis in
[
60
]
, provides (i) an almost sure upper bound on the blow–up time and
(ii) an explicit lower bound on the blow–up probability for the Brownian-driven
random PDE (
6.1
) (and hence, via the transformation, for (
1.1
) in the
Brownian-equivalent regime).

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose that
p,q>1
with
p>q
. Let the initial datum satisfy

f(x)\geq b\,\varphi_{1}(x)
for all
x\in D
, where
b>1
is chosen so that
(
6.3
) holds. Let
\tau_{b}
denote the (maximal existence) blow–up time of the
solution
v
to (
6.1
). Define

J(0):=\int_{D}f(x)\varphi_{1}(x)\,dx,\qquad\rho:=\sigma(q-1),\qquad\theta_{1}:=\frac{2(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)(q-1)}{\rho^{2}}.

Then
\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{**}
, where

\tau^{**}:=\inf\Biggl\{t\geq 0:\int_{0}^{t}\exp\!\big\{\sigma(q-1)W(s)-(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)(q-1)s\big\}\,ds\geq\frac{2J(0)^{\,1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}\Biggr\}.

Moreover, the blow–up probability admits the lower bound

(6.4)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}<\infty)=1-\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}<X(\theta_{1},1)\,\rho^{2}\,J(0)^{\,1-q}}}\right),

where
X(\theta_{1},1)
is a Gamma random variable (shape
\theta_{1}
, scale
1
).

Proof.

The upper bound
\tau_{b}\leq\tau^{**}
is obtained by repeating the eigenfunction
testing and comparison argument used in the proof of Theorem
5.12
, now
applied to (
6.1
) with the linear coefficient
-(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)
.
This yields the threshold functional
\tau^{**}
above.

To estimate
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}=\infty)
, note that

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}=\infty)=\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho W(s)-(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)(q-1)s}\,ds<\frac{2J(0)^{\,1-q}}{\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}}}}\right).

Using Brownian scaling,

\,2W(\tfrac{\rho^{2}}{4}s)\overset{law}{=}\rho W(s)
, and the change of variables

s=\tfrac{4t}{\rho^{2}}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho W(s)-(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)(q-1)s}\,ds\ \overset{law}{=}\ \frac{4}{\rho^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2(W(t)-\theta_{1}t)}\,dt,\quad\theta_{1}=\frac{2(\lambda_{1}+\vartheta)(q-1)}{\rho^{2}}.

Lemma
6.2
then gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2(W(t)-\theta_{1}t)}\,dt\ \overset{law}{=}\ \frac{1}{2X(\theta_{1},1)}.

Substituting this identity yields

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}=\infty)=\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta(q-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}\varphi_{1}^{\frac{q}{q-p}}(x)\,dx}}\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}<X(\theta_{1},1)\,\rho^{2}\,J(0)^{\,1-q}}}\right),

and therefore

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{b}<\infty)\geq\mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}<\infty)=1-\mathbb{P}(\tau^{**}=\infty)
leading to the desired estimate (
6.4
).
∎

The next theorem provides an explicit upper bound on the blow–up probability of
solutions to (
6.1
).

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume
p,q>1
with
p>q
and let
f\in L^{\infty}(D)
satisfy
f\geq 0
. Then

\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{b}<\infty\}\leq\int_{\widetilde{N}}^{\infty}h(y)\,dy,

where

\rho:=\sigma(q-1),\quad\widetilde{N}:=\frac{1}{\delta|D|(q-1)\|f\|_{\infty}^{\,q-1}},

and

h(y)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\rho^{2}y}}}\right)^{\frac{2\vartheta(q-1)}{\rho^{2}}}}{y\,\Gamma\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\vartheta(q-1)}{\rho^{2}}}}\right)}\exp\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2}{\rho^{2}y}}}\right).

Proof.

By the definition of the lower-bound stopping functional in the Brownian case,

\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{b}<\infty\}\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau_{**}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho W(s)-\vartheta(q-1)s}\,ds\geq\widetilde{N}}}\right).

Writing
W^{(\hat{\alpha})}(s):=W(s)-\hat{\alpha}s
with

\hat{\alpha}=\vartheta(q-1)/\rho
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho W(s)-\vartheta(q-1)s}\,ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\rho W^{(\hat{\alpha})}(s)}\,ds\ \overset{law}{=}\ \frac{4}{\rho^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2W^{(\nu)}(t)}\,dt,\quad\nu:=\frac{2\hat{\alpha}}{\rho}=\frac{2\vartheta(q-1)}{\rho^{2}}.

The exponential functional identity (see
[
88
, Ch. 6, Cor. 1.2]
)
yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2W^{(\nu)}(t)}\,dt\ \overset{law}{=}\ \frac{1}{2Z_{\nu}},

where
Z_{\nu}
is Gamma distributed with density

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}e^{-y}y^{\nu-1}\,dy
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{b}<\infty\}\leq\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{\rho^{2}Z_{\nu}}\geq\widetilde{N}}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z_{\nu}\leq\frac{2}{\rho^{2}\widetilde{N}}}}\right)=\int_{\widetilde{N}}^{\infty}h(y)\,dy,

with
h
as stated.
∎

7.
Numerical Results

In this section we present a numerical study of (
1.1
) in one space
dimension (
d=1
). Throughout, we impose homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on
the complement of the interval
D=[-1,1]
.

Finite-difference approximation

To approximate (
1.1
) numerically we employ a finite-difference
discretisation in space combined with a semi-implicit Euler scheme in time,
cf.
[
29
,
61
]
. Related strategies have also been used in
[
49
]
.

We discretise
[0,T]\times[-1,1]
by setting
t_{n}=n\Delta t
with

\Delta t=T/N
(
n=0,1,\dots,N
), and
x_{j}=-1+j\Delta x
with
\Delta x=2/M

(
j=0,1,\dots,M
). Let
U_{h}^{n}=(u_{0}^{n},u_{1}^{n},\dots,u_{M}^{n})
denote the grid
approximation of
u(t_{n},\cdot)
, and write

u_{h}^{n}:=(u_{1}^{n},\dots,u_{M-1}^{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1},

since the exterior Dirichlet condition yields
u_{0}^{n}=u_{M}^{n}=0
.

(1) Spatial discretisation of the fractional Laplacian.

We approximate the fractional Laplacian

\Delta_{\alpha}u=-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u,\qquad 0<\alpha\leq 2,

by the finite-difference method of
[
29
]
, which leads to a matrix
representation

(\Delta_{\alpha}u)(t_{n},\cdot)\ \approx\ A\,u_{h}^{n},

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times(M-1)}
has entries

A_{i,j}=C_{1,\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{M-1}\frac{(k+1)^{\chi}-(k-1)^{\chi}}{k^{\rho}}+\frac{(M+1)^{\chi}-(M-1)^{\chi}}{M^{\rho}}+\Big(2^{\chi}+\kappa_{\rho}-1\Big)+\frac{\chi}{\alpha M^{2\alpha}},&i=j,\\[11.00008pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{\big(|j-i|+1\big)^{\chi}-\big(|j-i|-1\big)^{\chi}}{2\,|j-i|^{\rho}},&\Big(j\neq i,\\
&j\neq i\pm 1\Big),\\[11.00008pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\Big(2^{\chi}+\kappa_{\rho}-1\Big),&j=i\pm 1,\end{array}}}\right.

with

\chi=\rho-2\alpha,\qquad\kappa_{\rho}=\begin{cases}1+2\alpha,&2\alpha\in(1,2),\\
1,&2\alpha=1,\end{cases}\qquad\rho\in(2\alpha,2].

In our simulations we take
\rho=1+\alpha
. We refer to
[
29
]
for the
derivation, admissible parameter ranges, and numerical considerations.

(2) Semi-implicit Euler time stepping.

Let the drift term

F(u):=\delta\Big(\int_{D}u^{q}dx\Big)+\gamma u-\beta u^{p}.

at time
t_{n}
be approximated by

F(u_{h}^{n})=\delta\,\mathcal{I}\!\big[(u_{h}^{n})^{q}\big]+\gamma u_{h}^{n}-\beta(u_{h}^{n})^{p},

where
\mathcal{I}[(u_{h}^{n})^{q}]
denotes the (replicated) numerical approximation
of the scalar integral
\int_{-1}^{1}u(t_{n},y)^{q}\,dy
, evaluated for instance via
Simpson’s rule and then placed into
\mathbb{R}^{M-1}
as a constant vector
(multiplying the all-ones vector).

For the stochastic forcing we write

B_{h}(t_{n}):=\sigma(u_{h}^{n})\,\big(k\,b_{s}^{H}(t_{n})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1},

where
b_{s}^{H}(t_{n})
denotes the discrete fractional Gaussian noise increment and

\sigma(\cdot)
is the chosen multiplicative coefficient (e.g.
\sigma(u)=\sigma u
).

Using a semi-implicit treatment of the diffusion (implicit in
A
and explicit
in the nonlinearities), the update reads

\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}=A\,u_{h}^{n+1}+F(u_{h}^{n})+B_{h}(t_{n}),

equivalently,

(I-\Delta t\,A)\,u_{h}^{n+1}=u_{h}^{n}+\Delta t\,F(u_{h}^{n})+\Delta t\,B_{h}(t_{n}).

(3) Sampling fractional Brownian motion.

Fractional Brownian motion paths are generated via the circulant embedding
method; see, e.g.,
[
61
, Chapter 6]
. In particular, for
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
the
increments reduce to those of a standard Brownian motion, and the discrete
noise can be represented by

b_{s}^{1/2}(t_{n})=\frac{\xi(t_{n+1})-\xi(t_{n})}{\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad b_{s}(t_{n})\sim N(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.},

where
\xi
is a standard Brownian motion.

Interpretation of Tables
1
–
5
.

(i)

Effect of the reaction exponent
q
(Table
1
).

Keeping
p=2
fixed, increasing
q
strengthens the nonlocal source term

\delta\int_{D}u^{q}
, which promotes faster growth at high amplitudes. This is
consistent with Table
1
: once
q
is sufficiently large (here near

q\approx 2
), the estimated blow–up probability within
[0,1]
increases and
then plateaus around
0.53
–
0.55
. Meanwhile, the conditional mean blow–up time
decreases, indicating that blow–up (when it occurs) tends to occur earlier for
larger
q
.

(ii)

Effect of temporal correlation
H
(Table
2
).

Table
2
suggests an overall increase in the blow–up probability as the
Hurst index
H
increases from
0.5
toward
1
. Since larger
H
corresponds to
more persistent increments of
B^{H}
, favourable growth phases can last longer,
which is compatible with both the increased probability and the decreasing mean
blow–up time.

(iii)

Effect of the noise intensity
\sigma
(Table
3
).

We choose
\delta
large enough so that the deterministic problem (
\sigma=0
)
blows up within the time horizon
[0,1]
. As
\sigma
increases, the estimated
blow–up probability over
[0,1]
decreases, indicating that stronger
multiplicative fluctuations generate more sample paths that are driven away from
the blow–up regime (or delay blow–up beyond
t=1
). We observe a sharp drop as
soon as
\sigma>0
, followed by a slight increase for larger
\sigma
; however,
the probability remains well below the deterministic value
1
. Moreover, among
those trajectories that do blow–up, the blow–up time tends to be smaller on
average, consistent with the fact that occasional large positive excursions of
the noise can trigger a more abrupt blow–up.

(iv)

Effect of initial amplitude (Table
4
).

Increasing the coefficient
c
raises the overall initial mass and drives the
system deeper into the unstable regime. Table
4
exhibits the expected
threshold-type behaviour: the blow–up probability increases rapidly toward
1

and the mean blow–up time decreases markedly.

(v)

Effect of the diffusion exponent
\alpha
(Table
5
).

Over the tested range, the blow–up probability and the first two moments of

\tau_{b}
vary only mildly. For this particular domain, horizon, and parameter
regime, the reaction and noise effects appear to dominate moderate changes in

\alpha
, so the influence of fractional diffusion on the reported blow–up
statistics is comparatively weak.

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

We split the proof into two parts.

Step 1. Weak maximum principle:
w\geq 0
in
\overline{D}_{T}
.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that
w
takes a negative value on

\overline{D}_{T}
, i.e.

m:=\inf_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}w<0.

By continuity, this infimum is attained at some point

(t_{0},x_{0})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The boundary and initial conditions imply that:

•

w(0,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in D
,

•

w(t,x)\geq 0
for all
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D)
.

Thus the negative minimum cannot lie at
t=0
or outside
D
, and we must have

(t_{0},x_{0})\in D_{T}
.

Exponential change of variable.

Let

M_{1}:=\|c_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(D_{T})},\qquad M_{2}:=\|c_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times D)},\qquad|D|:=\text{Lebesgue measure of }D.

Choose

\Lambda>M_{1}+M_{2}|D|,

and define

v(t,x):=e^{-\Lambda t}w(t,x).

Then
v
is bounded and continuous on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and attains a
negative global minimum

m_{v}:=\inf_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}v<0

at some point
(t_{1},x_{1})\in D_{T}
(since
w
and
v
have the same sign and the
same zero sets in time). Clearly,

v(t,x)\geq 0\quad\text{for }t=0\text{ or }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,

so the minimum is indeed attained in
D_{T}
.

We claim that
v
is a viscosity supersolution in
D_{T}
of

(8.1)

v_{t}(t,x)-d\,\Delta_{\alpha}v(t,x)\;\geq\;(c_{1}(t,x)-\Lambda)\,v(t,x)+\int_{D}c_{2}(t,y)\,v(t,y)\,dy.

Indeed, let
\varphi\in C^{1,2}
and suppose
v-\varphi
has a local minimum at

(t,x)\in D_{T}
with
(v-\varphi)(t,x)=0
. Define the test function

\psi(\tau,z):=e^{\Lambda\tau}\varphi(\tau,z),

and observe that
w-\psi
also has a local minimum at
(t,x)
:

(w-\psi)(\tau,z)=e^{\Lambda\tau}v(\tau,z)-e^{\Lambda\tau}\varphi(\tau,z)=e^{\Lambda\tau}(v-\varphi)(\tau,z).

Since
w
is a viscosity supersolution of (
5.9
), we have

\psi_{t}(t,x)-d\Delta_{\alpha}\psi(t,x)\;\geq\;c_{1}(t,x)w(t,x)+\int_{D}c_{2}(t,y)w(t,y)\,dy.

But
w=e^{\Lambda t}v
and

\psi_{t}(t,x)=\Lambda e^{\Lambda t}\varphi(t,x)+e^{\Lambda t}\varphi_{t}(t,x)=e^{\Lambda t}\big(\Lambda\varphi(t,x)+\varphi_{t}(t,x)\big),

\Delta_{\alpha}\psi(t,x)=e^{\Lambda t}\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi(t,x)

(using linearity and translation invariance of
\Delta_{\alpha}
). Dividing by
e^{\Lambda t}>0
, we get

\varphi_{t}(t,x)+\Lambda\varphi(t,x)-d\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi(t,x)\;\geq\;c_{1}(t,x)v(t,x)+\int_{D}c_{2}(t,y)v(t,y)\,dy.

Since
\varphi(t,x)=v(t,x)
at the touching point, this is exactly
(
8.1
) in viscosity sense. Thus
v
is a viscosity supersolution of
(
8.1
).

Contradiction at the global minimum.

Let
(t_{1},x_{1})\in D_{T}
be such that
v(t_{1},x_{1})=m_{v}=\min v<0
.
By continuity, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can define a quadratic test function

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=m_{v}+\varepsilon\big(|x-x_{1}|^{2}+(t-t_{1})^{2}\big)

so that
v-\varphi_{\varepsilon}
attains a local minimum
0
at
(t_{1},x_{1})
.
(Indeed
v(t_{1},x_{1})=m_{v}
and
v\geq m_{v}
everywhere, while

\varphi_{\varepsilon}>m_{v}
away from
(t_{1},x_{1})
for small enough
\varepsilon
.)

Since
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1,2}
, we may use it as test function in the
viscosity supersolution inequality (
8.1
) at
(t_{1},x_{1})
:

(\varphi_{\varepsilon})_{t}(t_{1},x_{1})-d\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})\;\geq\;(c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda)\,v(t_{1},x_{1})+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,v(t_{1},y)\,dy.

Now,

(\varphi_{\varepsilon})_{t}(t_{1},x_{1})=0,

and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},\cdot)
has a
strict global minimum
at

x_{1}
(since
|x-x_{1}|^{2}>0
for
x\neq x_{1}
). By the very definition of
the fractional Laplacian,

\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})=c_{d,s}\,\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})-\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},y)}{|x_{1}-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,dy,

and the integrand is strictly negative on a set of positive measure
(
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},y)>\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})
for
y\neq x_{1}
).
Hence

\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})>0,

and therefore

(\varphi_{\varepsilon})_{t}(t_{1},x_{1})-d\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})=-d\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})<0.

On the other hand, since
(t_{1},x_{1})
is a global minimum point of
v
,
we have
v(t_{1},y)\geq m_{v}
for all
y\in D
, and thus

\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,v(t_{1},y)\,dy\;\geq\;m_{v}\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,dy.

Therefore the right-hand side of (
8.1
) at
(t_{1},x_{1})
satisfies

\displaystyle(c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda)\,v(t_{1},x_{1})+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,v(t_{1},y)\,dy

\displaystyle\geq m_{v}\Big(c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,dy\Big).

By the choice of
\Lambda
and the bounds on
c_{1},c_{2}
,

c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,dy\leq M_{1}-\Lambda+M_{2}|D|<0,

so the factor in parentheses is strictly negative, and since
m_{v}<0
we get

m_{v}\Big(c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,dy\Big)>0.

Hence the right-hand side of (
8.1
) at
(t_{1},x_{1})
is strictly positive:

(c_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})-\Lambda)\,v(t_{1},x_{1})+\int_{D}c_{2}(t_{1},y)\,v(t_{1},y)\,dy>0.

This contradicts the viscosity inequality, which says that the left-hand side,
equal to
-d\Delta_{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},x_{1})
, must be greater or equal
to the right-hand side. Thus our assumption that
m_{v}<0
(equivalently
m<0
)
is false, and we conclude that

w(t,x)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in\overline{D}_{T}.

Step 2. Strong maximum principle: strict positivity in
D_{T}
if
w(0,\cdot)\not\equiv 0
.

Assume now that
w(0,\cdot)\not\equiv 0
in
D
and
w\geq 0
on
\overline{D}_{T}
.
Suppose, by contradiction, that there exists
(t_{*},x_{*})\in D_{T}
such that

w(t_{*},x_{*})=0.

Then
w
attains its global minimum
0
in the open set
D_{T}
.

By Step 1,
w
is a bounded, nonnegative viscosity supersolution of
(
5.9
), and hence of

w_{t}-d\Delta_{\alpha}w-c_{1}(t,x)w\;\geq\;\int_{D}c_{2}(t,y)\,w(t,y)\,dy\geq 0\quad\text{in }D_{T}.

That is,
w
is a viscosity supersolution of a linear, nonlocal parabolic equation
with a (possibly sign-changing) zeroth-order term and a nonnegative right-hand side.

For nonlocal parabolic equations with Lévy operators such as
(-\Delta)^{s}
and
bounded coefficients, a
strong maximum principle
for viscosity solutions
is known: if a bounded viscosity supersolution
u
of

u_{t}-\mathcal{L}[u]-a(t,x)u\geq 0

(in an open set) attains a nonnegative minimum at an interior point and satisfies
appropriate nonlocal boundary conditions, then either
u
is strictly positive
in the domain, or
u
is constant. For linear operators of fractional Laplacian type,
this is proved, for instance, in

[
9
,
10
,
22
,
46
]

(see also the general frameworks in

[
3
,
8
,
14
]
).

Applying such a strong maximum principle to
w
(or to
v=e^{-\Lambda t}w
),
we conclude that either:

•

w(t,x)>0
for all
(t,x)\in D_{T}
, or

•

w(t,x)\equiv 0
in
D_{T}
.

The second alternative is not compatible with the assumption that

w(0,\cdot)\not\equiv 0
in
D
and the continuity in time. Hence only the first
alternative can hold, and we obtain

w(t,x)>0\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in D_{T}.

This completes the proof.
∎
