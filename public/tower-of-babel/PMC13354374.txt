Comparative evaluation of hook plate and tension band wiring in the management of acromioclavicular dislocation: a retrospective study

Background

The acromioclavicular (AC) joint is located between the lateral end of the clavicle and the medial part of the acromion. The joint capsule is reinforced by a strong system of ligaments, and there is a fibrocartilaginous disc between the two bony surfaces[1]. AC joint dislocation (ACD) is a fairly common shoulder girdle injury, seen in about 9–12% of active adults[2]. In the majority of cases, this injury typically results from a direct fall on the shoulder tip with the arm in adduction[3]. Historically, the most commonly used classification is the one proposed by Allman and Tossy[4], which was later modified by Rockwood[5]. This classification divides AC dislocations into six types, depending on several factors increasing in severity and ligament injury from type 1 to type 6. The optimal treatment method for AC dislocations is widely controversial[6]; however, conservative treatment is commonly used for the management of types I and II ACD[7]. In contrast, the gold standard for Rockwood IV–VI lesions is surgical treatment[8]. The optimal management for type III remains an ongoing debate[9]. Numerous surgical techniques have been developed over the years, and the optimal operative treatment remains questionable[10]. The two most commonly performed techniques are tension band wiring (TBW) and hook plate (HP). The aim of this study is to evaluate the functional, clinical, and radiological results of patients with acute ACD graded from type 3 to type 5, according to the Rockwood classification, treated by two methods of stabilization: TBW and HP, in order to determine which has the best outcome and which is recommended for treatment.HIGHLIGHTSThis study compares the clinical outcomes of hook plate (HP) and tension band wiring (TBW) in acromioclavicular joint dislocation management.HP fixation showed faster functional recovery and higher Constant scores in early follow-up.TBW demonstrated comparable long-term outcomes, with fewer hardware-related complications.The incidence of postoperative complications was higher in the HP group due to implant irritation.Results support individualized surgical selection based on patients’ needs, activity levels, and surgeons’ expertise.

This retrospective comparative study has been reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines[11].

Patients and methods

A retrospective comparative study was conducted at our hospital between 2022 and 2024. Inclusion and exclusion criteria:

Inclusion criteria included adult patients (≥18 years) with acute ACD (within 2 weeks of injury), classified as Rockwood type III, IV, or V, who underwent surgical stabilization using either TBW or HP fixation and had a minimum follow-up of 6 months.

Exclusion criteria were chronic AC dislocations (>2 weeks from injury), previous shoulder surgery on the affected side, associated fractures of the clavicle or scapula, neurological injury of the upper limb, and patients lost to follow-up.

Patient file: The basic sample consisted of 34 patients divided into two groups: 12 patients treated with TBW and 22 patients treated with HP. Most patients were physically active males engaged in manual labor or sports-related activities. Relevant comorbidities, such as smoking, diabetes mellitus, and osteoporosis, were recorded from medical files; however, their low prevalence did not allow for meaningful subgroup analysis.

Method: A diagnosis was made based on clinical and X-ray findings, and then the indication for surgery was determined. During the clinical examination of the AC joint, we assessed horizontal and vertical instability of the lateral clavicle.

X-ray examination: The radiological examination of the shoulder girdle was performed for all patients. The assessment included anteroposterior (AP), Zanca, and stress radiographic views, as well as a computed tomography (CT) scan before surgery.

Statistical analysis: Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using an independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the normality distribution assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All diagnoses of ACD were established based on clinical examination and standardized radiological assessment, including AP, Zanca, and stress views, supplemented by CT when required. The diagnosis and Rockwood classification were made by experienced orthopedic surgeons.

Indication for surgery: All patients with Rockwood type IV and V injuries were treated surgically, as surgical management is widely accepted for these injury types. In patients with Rockwood type III injuries, the indication for surgery was based on functional demands, including activity level and occupational requirements, after clinical and radiological assessment. This approach reflects real-world clinical decision-making but may have introduced selection bias. All patients underwent surgery within 1–7 days. All patients in the study were operated on by experienced surgeons.

Operative techniques: All surgical procedures were performed by senior orthopedic surgeons with comparable experience in shoulder surgery, thereby minimizing potential performance and learning-curve bias.

All patients were placed in the beach-chair position, and a longitudinal skin incision was made to expose the lateral end of the clavicle and ACJ. In the TBW group, open reduction of dislocation was performed and fixated with two K-wires inserted trans-articularly through the joint. K-wires were inserted bicortically for more stability, parallel to each other, and the assembly was completed by inserting the wire loop, with every step performed under fluoroscopic control (Fig. 1A). In the HP group, a tunnel was made behind the ACJ in the subacromial space. The locking HP was then inserted into this tunnel, and the plate was fixed with regular 3.5 mm cortex screws and locking screws. The hook was bent parallel to the acromion to avoid subacromial erosion (Fig. 1B). Deltoid and trapezius muscle sutures were performed in all patients as a reconstruction of the dynamic stabilizers and for supraclavicular reinforcement. Postoperatively, the operated arm was supported with a sling. All patients followed a standardized rehabilitation protocol. Passive and assisted range-of-motion exercises were initiated after sling removal, followed by progressive strengthening exercises. Full return to activities was allowed after implant removal and radiological confirmation of stability. K-wires were removed after 6–8 weeks, and HPs were removed at 12–14 weeks. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Constant–Murley score, a validated and widely used shoulder-specific outcome measure that integrates subjective parameters (pain and activities of daily living) and objective parameters (range of motion and strength). Although outcome assessment was not blinded to the type of fixation, this reflects routine clinical practice and is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. The Constant score evaluations were conducted by orthopedic surgeons during routine outpatient follow-up visits[12].

Results

Clinical outcomes

All 34 patients were able to return to their pre-surgical occupation. The average operation time in total was shorter in the TBW group, with no statistical significance. The time during which the operated arm was supported with a sling was shorter in the HP group (around 2–4 weeks), which has statistical importance (P-value = 0.0001). There was no significant decrease in ROM in the affected side at the last follow-up in all patients. The functional results were measured using the Constant score 2 weeks after surgery, 4 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, and at the last follow-up 6 months after surgery. The average score increased from 58.2 at the first checkpoint to an average of 92.1 at the last follow-up in the TBW group, while in the HP group, the average score increased from 66.2 at the first checkpoint to an average of 94.2 at the last follow-up. There was statistical significance, with a P-value less than 0.05, in the first two periods of follow-up, with a higher Constant score in the HP group.

Although statistically significant differences in Constant scores were observed in favor of the HP group during early follow-up, these differences gradually diminished over time, resulting in comparable functional outcomes between the two groups at the final follow-up.

Radiological outcomes

We evaluated implant placement and joint position after surgery, and at every check point, the follow-up, up to the last one, which occurred 6 months after surgery.

X-ray findings after surgery: In 34 cases, the postoperative imaging of the AC joint position and implant was assessed as correct. In three cases, we found malposition of the K-wires, and in one case of Rockwood type 5, there was horizontal widening of the AC joint gap. Redislocation of 50–100% of the acromial width was evident on stress radiographs at the last follow-up in 2 patients, with no correlation to functional outcome.

Complication

Complications were classified as early or late, and as minor or major, depending on the timing of occurrence and the need for additional intervention or revision surgery.

Overall, we recorded complications in five patients in the TBW group (41.6%) and in five patients in the HP group (22.7%). Complications in the TBW group

Wound healing disorders occurred in one patient, and it was a superficial infection managed by dressings and antibiotic therapy. Deep infection of the K-wire was observed in one patient, treated with antibiotics and early metal removal (after 6 weeks), resulting in a final Constant score of 88. There was migration of K-wires in two cases by around 1 cm, which was monitored during routine follow-ups and did not influence the functional outcome. In one case, the K-wires broke after extreme physical effort (Fig. 2A), which was resolved by extraction in due time, with a final Constant score of 90. Complications in the HP group

In one case, there was a deep infection of the HP, treated with intravenous antibiotics and early plate extraction. Impingement syndrome occurred in two patients, which resolved after plate extraction and did not affect the final functional outcome. Acromial osteolysis was visible in the distal acromion in two patients (Fig. 2B); the extent of wear was small and correlated with longer plate retention.

Discussion

This study compared clinical and functional outcomes between two methods of management for acute AC dislocation. Both methods temporarily fix the acromion and the clavicle in order to ensure healing conditions for the ligaments and the delto-trapezius fascia, which is important for functional stability[13]. TBW is one of the most standard techniques, which has been used unchanged for a long time and yields good results when performed correctly[14]. The cause of relatively frequent complications is poor technical performance, which is reported in the literature to be in the range of 6−17%[13]. We have observed complications (infection, migration, or breakage of K-wires) in 41.6% of the patients. Choosing the correct thickness of the wires and the correct insertion point through the acromion could help prevent these complications. The duration of fixation and removal of K-wires is often reported in the literature as 6 weeks, which was very early in our experience. HP is a less challenging method and has become popular in practice over the past 20 years. While being more expensive, it is much more stable and allows for the possibility of early rehabilitation and weight-bearing[15]. Most patients could bear weight on the affected arm approximately 4 weeks after surgery. The main disadvantage of this method is the irritation in the subacromial space caused by the plate hook, which can lead to osteolysis or impingement and is mentioned in every study dealing with HPs[16]. The recommended extraction time is about 3 months after surgery[11,14], and in our study, we agree with this point of view. In our study, among the 34 patients, only four were female (11.7%), consistent with literature reports. In the literature, the incidence is much higher in males[17]. In our study, we used the Constant score for clinical and functional evaluation, as it has been used in the literature to evaluate treatment, with average values around 90[11,13]. We found similar results, with average values in the last follow-up being 92.1 in the TBW group and 94.2 in the HP group. Limitations of our study

First, patient assignment was not randomized, which is inherent in the retrospective design of the study and may have introduced selection bias; however, all consecutive patients presenting during the study period were included without selective exclusion. Second, the follow-up period was too short to analyze the incidence of arthritis in the AC joint, which may differ in long-term outcomes and therefore limits conclusions regarding degenerative changes. Third, we did not evaluate the AP translation of the AC joint via axial view after treatment and compare it with the normal contralateral side, which may have limited the radiological assessment of horizontal instability. Additionally, the study population reflects real-world clinical practice, including a predominance of male patients consistent with the known epidemiology of ACDs. We consider the absence of some data in the surgical protocol regarding the extent of traumatic changes in the AC joint and shoulder girdle to be a disadvantage related to the retrospective nature of data collection, which could not be analyzed in the results. Radiological assessment was performed as part of routine clinical practice without formal interobserver reliability analysis, which may represent a source of measurement bias.

The inclusion of Rockwood Type III injuries, based on functional demand rather than uniform criteria, may have introduced confounding variables and affected group comparability.

Functional outcome assessment using the Constant score was not blinded to the type of implant, which may have introduced assessment bias.

Conclusion

Both methods used in our study are adequate options for the management of acute AC dislocation, and while they differ in surgical technique, they yield comparable functional and clinical outcomes. While the TBW is a more cost-effective alternative, we found that the HP group had fewer complications and allowed early full range of motion, with the understanding that the plate should be removed within 3 months to prevent osteolysis and subacromial irritation.
