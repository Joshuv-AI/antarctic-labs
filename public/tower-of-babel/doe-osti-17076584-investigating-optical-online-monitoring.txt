 ABSTRACT

Volatile fission products released during used nuclear fuel reprocessing require online off-gas monitoring
to support treatment performance, process control, and regulatory needs. In fiscal year 2026, Oak Ridge
National Laboratory’s Iodine Off-gas Testing and Abatement Laboratory evaluated Raman spectroscopy
and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) as complementary tools for monitoring iodine in
dynamic sorbent testing. Ex situ Raman spectroscopy successfully identified iodine-reacted phases on
silver- and copper-based sorbents, but in situ Raman implementation was limited by probe and window
constraints that obscured key low-wavenumber features. LIBS detected gas-phase iodine in helium and
argon, with limits of detection of 3 and 5 ppm, respectively, but not in air at ≤100 ppm. Overall, the
results show that Raman spectroscopy and LIBS can provide complementary information for iodine
monitoring, but probe limitations and gas matrix effects must be addressed before either technique can be
deployed in complex reprocessing off-gas systems.

 1. INTRODUCTION

As fission occurs in a reactor, fission products are generated within the fuel meat and either stay in
solution or migrate to grain boundaries and interstitial spaces of the cladding. With the increasing interest
in domestic used nuclear fuel (UNF) recycling, greater attention is being given to the separation of fission
products during reprocessing. Many fission products are considered impurities that require separation via
reprocessing, and some may have value for radiopharmaceutical and industrial applications. During UNF
reprocessing, the volatile fission products evolve into the headspace and downstream piping, and this off-
gas requires treatment to capture radionuclides such as iodine-129, carbon-14, and krypton-85. The
evolution of capture technologies (TRL 2 → 6 or higher) necessitate a system that can track the
separation and fate of fission products to address regulatory and operational drivers. Online monitoring
techniques would satisfy these needs for activities across multiple technology readiness levels (TRLs)—
that is, at bench, laboratory, and pilot scales—to understand the effectiveness of off-gas treatment within
these systems and aid in technical maturation.

Sensor technology for gaseous systems remains limited compared to liquid or solid-state techniques.
Furthermore, for UNF recycling off-gas applications, all monitoring techniques can be complicated by
material compatibility, sample matrix complexity, high background radiation, and limited points of
access. Research and development efforts within the Materials Recovery and Waste Form Development
(MRWFD) Campaign have largely relied on recorded mass change1, 2 and gas chromatography–mass
spectrometry (GC-FID)3, 4 to measure iodine sorbent performance and breakthrough in real time.
However, both modalities present challenges when implemented at laboratory or industrial scale. Given
the advances in analytical methodologies, commercialization of automated online monitoring tools,
vendor-supported instrument customization, and decreased footprint of sensitive instrumentation, it has
become advantageous to consider additional sensors and techniques that can support these activities.

In fiscal year 2026, the primary goal of this effort was to test both alternative and supportive
spectroscopic techniques using current experiments to demonstrate proof-of-principle. At Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL), the Iodine Off-gas Testing and Abatement Laboratory (IOTA Lab)
investigated two online monitoring techniques—Raman spectroscopy (RS) and laser-induced breakdown
spectroscopy (LIBS)—as potential candidates for leveraging online monitoring tools within a dynamic
test system. This report summarizes our findings on the application of these techniques and discusses
their feasibility for larger-scale implementation.

2.1 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

RS is a powerful non-destructive optical spectroscopy technique that is a workhorse in materials
characterization. Raman spectra are considered molecular fingerprints because Raman signatures stem
from bonds in materials that exhibit a change in polarizability during vibrational oscillation. As a
scattering method, the selection rules for RS are different from those for infrared absorption spectroscopy;
thus, RS allows data to be collected in H2O-rich atmospheres. By taking advantage of the flexibility of
fiber optics to deliver laser excitation to the site of analysis and transmit light from collection optics back

 2

to the spectrometer, RS can be integrated into a wide range of systems. The use of gas-phase RS for
monitoring gas-phase molecular iodine (I2) in a static gas cell has been recently reported.5 In that study, a
532 nm laser excitation was used with chemometric modeling and resulted in limits of detection (LODs)
down to 6.7 × 10−5 torr. In 2022, ORNL used a commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) mobile Raman system
to determine iodine vapor concentrations, and although the fluorescence in this system prevented LOD
determination, discernable resonances were visible at > 5 ppm in a flowing cell.6 In the work documented
in this report, a Raman probe was custom-made to monitor the reactivity of iodine vapor with sorbent
materials (namely Ag- and Cu-based sorbents) over time while withstanding the corrosive nature of the
iodine vapor. More information on the instrumentation can be found in Appendix A.

2.1.1 Ex Situ Experiments

Prior to experiments with the Raman probe, spectra of three sorbents (pristine and following exposure to
iodine vapors) were acquired with a Renishaw InVia Raman microscope system. With the lowest
magnification objective (5×), Ag0400, Ag AC6120, and CuSiO2 were characterized; their spectra are
shown in Figure 2(A–C). As shown in Figure 2(A) and 2(B), one of the two E2 fundamental modes of β-
AgI was observed at 114 cm−1.7-9 In Figure 2(C), the green dashed lines indicate peaks from the iodine-
exposed CuSiO2, while the black dashed lines indicate peaks from the pristine CuSiO2. The pristine
CuSiO2 has two sharp peaks at 91 and 147 cm−1, and it has a broad band at 624 cm−1; these effects can be
assigned to the T2u, T1u(TO) and a combination of the T1u (TO) and T1u (LO) modes of Cu2O,
respectively.10, 11 The three main features in the iodine-exposed CuSiO2 spectrum at 125, 299, and
610 cm−1 are assigned to the TO mode of CuI and the Ag and Bg modes of CuO.12, 13

Raman spectra acquired by the custom Raman probe are shown in Figure 2(D–F). Due to the corrosivity
of iodine vapor, a sapphire window had to be incorporated at the end of the probe extension tube.
Sapphire has an inherent Raman spectrum, so the spectra reflected in Figure 2 represent those after the
subtraction of the sapphire background. Unfortunately, due to the truncated transmission window of the
Raman probe, the main peaks of interest—the AgI peak at 114 cm−1 and the CuI peak at 125 cm−1—are
unobservable. Discussions with the probe manufacturer are underway, as the probe was expected to allow
for observation of peaks down to 90 cm−1.

2.2 LASER-INDUCED BREAKDOWN SPECTROSCOPY

A complementary technique to RS is LIBS. Although LIBS is typically referenced as being
simultaneously sensitive to nearly all elements, its application to halogens is diminished. This lower
sensitivity is caused by challenges associated with the excitation of halogen elements related to their
ionization energies.14 Furthermore, the strongest emission peaks for most halogens fall in the deep
ultraviolet (UV) wavelengths that are difficult to measure outside of reduced pressures and vacuum
conditions.14 To the authors’ knowledge, only a few studies on the measurement of gas-phase iodine have
been performed using LIBS.15-18

LIBS is performed by focusing a pulsed laser into a sample stream to generate a microplasma. The optical
emissions of this plasma are then measured to provide an elemental spectrum regardless of molecular
form. Thus, tandem Raman/LIBS measurement modalities provide an understanding of molecular
speciation or concentrations and total elemental composition. Previous work at ORNL established a
mobile gas LIBS platform for monitoring various laboratory systems and demonstrated the platform’s use
for monitoring noble gases in a molten salt reactor in molten salt reactor (MSR) relevant off-gas stream
with good success.19-21 A traditional aqueous reprocessing off-gas streams differ significantly in many
ways to MSRs— including the presence of NOx and variations in off-gas compositions—further
investigation is needed to assess the feasibility of LIBS trace gas analysis in this flowsheet.

 3. CONCLUSION

The MRWFD Campaign has been assessing novel ways of recycling used fuel. The performance of these
methods is compared with standard nitric acid dissolution, followed by solvent extraction. However, the
results are generally obtained on grab samples or after the process has finished and limits informing
operators on experiments in progress. Applying online analytical methods to R&D experiments could
provide real-time information on system and sample performance as a function of time. Real-time data
would allow for mid-experiment corrections and insight, improve control of reaction parameters such as
temperature control or reagent addition, and complement the common practice of ex situ characterization.
An approach to developing the methodology for implementing online sensors involves first instrumenting
existing experimental systems that would benefit greatly from automated data processing. Thus, this work
served as a first step in demonstrating the value of evaluating online analytical techniques within existing
iodine off-gas test systems to advance monitoring capabilities for UNF reprocessing applications. RS
permitted the identification of iodine-reacted phases on silver- and copper-based sorbents, while LIBS
demonstrated complementary elemental sensitivity for gas-phase iodine in inert carrier gases. Both
techniques also revealed important implementation challenges in realistic process environments.
Specifically, probe design limitations, optical interferences, and strong gas matrix effects highlight the
need for additional instrument optimization before these methods can be reliably deployed for complex
reprocessing off-gas streams.

Progression from these initial experiments will continue with testing on relatively clean process streams
with increasing complexity to address the knowledge gaps identified above, establish the evidence needed
for further development, and justify continued funding. Thus, next steps toward implementing monitoring
systems in larger and more integrated processes should focus on continued deployment in existing
experimental platforms, such as ORNL’s IOTA and INL Idaho National Laboratory’s off-gas testbeds,
where the techniques can be refined under increasingly complex but controlled conditions while
establishing automated data collection and analysis workflows. Data from processing will be made
available to inform models of system performance. This would necessitate the involvement of modelers in
the design of the sensor network, as well as upfront consideration of data management.

Ultimately, the individual systems will be combined into an integrated off-gas system with multiple
sensor modalities, data management strategies, and modeling to generate real-time performance data.
Integrated sensor technology will support immediate evaluation of the off-gas and quick responses under
process upset conditions. Real-time data will be provided to industry partners, contributing to bridging the
gap between the campaign and industry needs. As a complete system is being developed, feedback from
these tests will be translated into the evolving off-gas design.

APPENDIX A. RAMAN EXPERIMENTS

 APPENDIX A. RAMAN EXPERIMENTS

Instrumental Methods

A custom Raman probe manufactured by InPhotonics, Inc. for an excitation wavelength of 785 nm is
shown in Figure 3. The stainless-steel probe body was clamped to the top opening of the oven and
produced a 5 mm diameter collimated beam. The Hastelloy extension sleeve extended from the probe into
the oven through the glass dome over the thin bed experimental set-up. A sapphire window was sealed
and welded to the end of the extension sleeve. The fiber optics were encased in stainless-steel with core
diameters of 105 and 200 µm for excitation and collection, respectively.

A Raman Monitoring System (RAMS™) from Spectra Solutions, Inc. was utilized for excitation and
collection of Raman spectra during sorbent monitoring experiments. The RAMS contained a 785 nm laser
with maximum power output set to 500 mW; the output power of the RAMS and Raman probe was
measured with a photodiode power sensor (Thorlabs S121C) and power meter console (Thorlabs
PM100D). The Raman scatter was dispersed through a high throughput volume phase holographic grating
and imaged onto a NIR enhanced back-illuminated CCD detector (2000 x 256 pixels). The system probed
a spectral range of 0-3010 cm-1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 for a 25 µm slit. SpectraSoft data acquisition
software was used for collecting Raman spectra. Custom written Python scripting was used for spectra
processing and analysis.

Experimental Methods

Ex-situ spectra acquired with the Renishaw InVia Raman microscope had the following parameters: 785
nm excitation, 700 cm-1 center wavenumber, 10 second acquisitions, 10 accumulations, 5x objective, pin
hole out, cosmic ray removal on. The laser power varied based on the different sorbent materials. The
pristine sorbents and the iodine exposed CuSiO2 were acquired with the laser power set to 5% while the
iodine exposed Ag0400 and Ag AC6120 (unknown loading %) were acquired with the laser power set to
0.5% and 1%, respectively.

Ex-situ spectra acquired with the RAMS™ and InPhotonics Raman probe were acquired with the probe
power at 326 mW, 10 second acquisitions, and the average of 10 accumulations. The probe was
positioned 2.5 cm inch above the samples.

APPENDIX B. LIBS EXPERIMENTS

 APPENDIX B. LIBS EXPERIMENTS

Experimental Methods for Gaseous Detection of Iodine

The mobile LIBS system was transported to the IOTA Laboratory across the US Department of Energy's
ORNL campus for measurements. Gas sample streams containing trace iodine (e.g., <100 ppm) were
made using high-purity helium (99.9%, Airgas), high-purity argon (99.9%, Airgas), and compressed air
carrier gases. The compressed air was passed through packed bed desiccant columns to remove excess
moisture, resulting in an average of 103 ppm H2O for the duration of the test. The iodine-bearing gases
were generated by passing the carrier gas through a temperature-controlled packed bed containing
elemental iodine set to 18 °C. All flow rates were controlled using mass flow controllers (SmartTrak,
Sierra Instruments). Iodine concentrations in the gas stream were controlled by varying the flow rate
through the packed bed and using a diluting gas at the bed outlet. The total flow rate to the LIBS
instrument was held constant at nominally 6.3 L min−1.

Instrumental Methods for Gaseous Detection of Iodine

LIBS measurements were performed using a customized gas LIBS system outfitted onto a mobile
platform. A nanosecond pulsed Nd:YAG laser (Nano-200LG, Litron) with a maximum pulse energy of
200 mJ per pulse was operated at 20 Hz. The laser was fired through a beam expansion module and then
focused through an angled window into a gas cell. A gas stream was flowed through the gas cell; a nozzle
was used to focus the gas precisely to the laser focal point. A larger-diameter tube was used as the gas cell
