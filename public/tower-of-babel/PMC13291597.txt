A teleost-specific oxygen–immunity axis where FIH activates NF-κB via competitive IκBα binding
Authors: Zhipeng Zhan, Mincong Liang, Yang Yu, Yangchi Cui, Zhimin Li, Weiqiang Pan, Ziyi Zhou, Lai Jiang, Yafeng Hou, Jian He, Kun Wu, Shaoping Weng, Jianguo He, Changjun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study uncovered an aquatic-specific oxygen–immunity axis in teleosts, in which the oxygen sensor FIH activated the NF-κB pathway not through hydroxylation but by competitively displacing p65 from IκBα. Data from cellular models, drfih–/– zebrafish, and infection assays revealed that this mechanism tuned inflammatory output and pathogen resistance under hypoxia. Structural modeling and domain-swap experiments explained why the interface was present in aquatic animals but lost in terrestrial vertebrates, linking environmental oxygen to lineage-specific immune control. These findings illuminated how vertebrates adapt to high pathogen loads in hypoxic aquatic environments and may inform prediction and intervention strategies for climate-driven hypoxia and aquaculture health.

Aquatic animals face heightened survival pressures compared with their terrestrial counterparts due to the dual challenges of hypoxia and greater pathogen exposure (1). Atmospheric oxygen is far more abundant than dissolved oxygen in water, and oxygen-saturated aquatic environments rarely contain more than 8 mg/L dissolved O2, a disparity compounded by reduced diffusion efficiency in water (2, 3). Climate-driven hypoxia further exacerbates respiratory limitations in aquatic animals, which already operate within narrower oxygen availability ranges than terrestrial lifeforms (4, 5). Concurrently, aquatic environments contain higher concentrations of bacteria and viruses (1012–1014 microorganisms/m3) than terrestrial air does (below 105 microorganisms/m3), exposing aquatic animals to greater pathogen pressure (6, 7). How aquatic animals, compared with terrestrial animals, adapt to hypoxia while simultaneously withstanding high pathogen pressure in microbe-rich environments remains largely unknown (8, 9).

Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling serves as a central mechanism for oxygen homeostasis by sensing changes in oxygen tension, and its dysregulation has been implicated in tumorigenesis and metabolic disorders (10, 11). Both factor inhibiting HIF (FIH; also known as hypoxia-inducible factor 1 subunit alpha inhibitor, HIF1AN) and HIF prolyl hydroxylase domain enzymes (PHDs) are 2-oxoglutarate and Fe(II)-dependent oxygenases involved in oxygen-dependent regulation of HIF transcriptional activity, but act via different mechanisms (12). FIH hydroxylates a specific asparagine residue in the C-terminal transactivation domain of HIF-1α, preventing its interaction with transcriptional coactivators p300/CBP, thereby negatively regulating HIF transcriptional activity (13, 14). Kinetic studies have established that FIH retains its catalytic activity at lower oxygen tensions compared with PHD enzymes, positioning it as a critical sensor under severe hypoxia (15, 16). Most evidence has focused on FIH-mediated hydroxylation in mammals, with limited investigation of nonmammalian species or hydroxylation-independent regulation.

NF-κB is a key intracellular signaling transducer that plays an essential role in antimicrobial and inflammatory responses (17). In the resting state, IκBα binds to the p65/p50 complex and retains NF-κB in the cytoplasm, inhibiting the transcriptional activity of NF-κB. Upon recognition of pathogens such as bacteria by cell surface receptors such as Toll-like receptors, the IκB kinase (IKK) complex is activated, leading to the degradation of IκBα and the release of NF-κB, which then translocates into the nucleus to initiate the transcription of target genes (18). NF-κB induces the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, as well as antimicrobial peptides such as defensins, enhancing the host’s ability to clear pathogens (19, 20). Extensive crosstalk between hypoxic and inflammatory signaling is well documented in mammals, where hypoxia can activate NF-κB to promote cell survival (21), and conversely, NF-κB can transcriptionally regulate HIF signaling (22). While hypoxia is known to activate NF-κB, whether FIH directly modulates this signaling cascade in teleosts remains to be elucidated.

The mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) is a freshwater teleost inhabiting Asian waters from the Zhujiang River system to the Amur River system and is an economically important aquaculture species widely farmed in China (23). Its aquaculture production in China reached approximately 540,000 tonnes in 2024, underscoring its substantial economic importance. With the intensification of mandarin fish production, high stocking density, excessive feeding, and suboptimal management can reduce dissolved oxygen, making hypoxic stress a recurrent challenge in culture systems (24). Bacterial diseases and other pathogenic pressures also remain major constraints on mandarin fish aquaculture and are closely linked to innate immune regulation (25). Phylogenetically, S. chuatsi belongs to the order Centrarchiformes, a broadly distributed acanthopterygian order that includes diverse freshwater and nearshore marine lineages (26). Thus, S. chuatsi provides an economically, physiologically, and phylogenetically relevant teleost model for investigating how hypoxia interfaces with inflammatory signaling.

In this study, hypoxia-induced upregulation of FIH was found to activate the NF-κB pathway and enhance inflammatory responses and antibacterial defense in teleosts through sequestration of IκBα. This effect occurred independently of FIH hydroxylase activity. The regulatory axis appeared to be conserved in the teleosts examined, but differed from that in mammals. Structural predictions further suggested that related competitive interfaces might occur in several aquatic taxa and an amphibian, but were likely absent from the terrestrial species examined. Collectively, these findings revealed a hydroxylase-independent mechanism, likely associated with aquatic lineages, that linked oxygen sensing to innate immunity, broadened the understanding of how aquatic animals cope with hypoxia, and had implications for vertebrate evolution, climate-driven hypoxia, and aquaculture health.

Results

FIH Positively Regulates NF-κB Under Hypoxia.

To elucidate the molecular underpinnings of the hypoxic response in teleosts, particularly regarding immune regulation, transcriptome sequencing was performed on total RNA extracted from MFF-1 cells (a fish cell line derived from S. chuatsi) cultured under hypoxia (3% O2) or normoxia (21% O2). Transcriptomic analysis identified a total of 1,057 differentially expressed genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). As anticipated, hypoxia relieved scFIH (S. chuatsi FIH)-mediated repression of HIF signaling and markedly upregulated HIF target genes. Concurrently, schif1an, the mandarin fish genome annotation corresponding to scfih, was significantly increased in the transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 1A). These results were further corroborated at both the mRNA and protein levels via RT–qPCR and Western blot analyses, respectively (Fig. 1 B and C). Subsequent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of pathways related to signal transduction, metabolism, disease development, and the immune system (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Further analysis and molecular experiments highlighted an upregulation of innate immune and inflammatory genes under hypoxia (Fig. 1 D and E).

To further elucidate the effects of hypoxia on immune and inflammatory signaling pathways, luciferase reporter assays were performed in MFF-1 cells. Results showed that hypoxia increased the activities of GAS, IFN-ω, IFN-α, IFN-γ, ISRE, NF-κB, and STAT3 reporters (Fig. 1F). Given the pivotal role of the NF-κB pathway in inflammation (17), subsequent analyses focused on its activation under hypoxia. Notably, NF-κB activation was progressively enhanced with decreasing oxygen levels (Fig. 1G). A hallmark of NF-κB pathway activation is the nuclear translocation of p65, a key subunit of the NF-κB complex. Due to the unavailability of antibodies specific for endogenous scp65 (S. chuatsi p65), an overexpression system was employed. It is noteworthy that the high levels of exogenous scp65 likely saturated the cytoplasmic retention capacity of endogenous scIκBα (S. chuatsi IκBα), resulting in partial nuclear translocation of scp65 under basal conditions. Consequently, under normoxia, scp65 was distributed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus in MFF-1 cells, whereas it predominantly accumulated in the nucleus under hypoxia (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). To corroborate these findings in a native context, parallel experiments were performed in ZEM2S cells (a fish cell line derived from Danio rerio) using antibodies against endogenous drp65 (D. rerio p65). Consistent with the overexpression data in S. chuatsi, endogenous drp65 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm under normoxia, but exhibited significant nuclear translocation upon hypoxic stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). These results indicated that hypoxia could activate the NF-κB pathway in vitro.

To evaluate the role of scFIH in inflammatory signaling, scfih overexpression and knockdown experiments were performed in MFF-1 cells. Under hypoxia, scfih overexpression robustly activated the NF-κB reporter (Fig. 1 H and I) and upregulated the expression of inflammatory genes, including scil-1β, scil-8, and sctnf-β (Fig. 1J). Conversely, siRNA-mediated knockdown of scfih in MFF-1 cells reduced NF-κB reporter activity (Fig. 1K) and the expression of inflammatory genes under hypoxia (Fig. 1L). Notably, although hypoxia naturally relieved scFIH-mediated repression of HIF signaling, scfih knockdown still induced a modest further upregulation of HIF target genes (scglut1, scvegfaa, and scldha) (Fig. 1M). These findings suggested that scFIH functioned as a positive regulator of the NF-κB pathway under hypoxia.

FIH Activates NF-κB by Competing With P65 for IκBα Binding Under Hypoxia.

The classical NF-κB pathway involves upstream signal stimulation, activation of the IKK complex, IκB phosphorylation, and subsequent NF-κB translocation to the nucleus (17, 18). To determine the target of scFIH in activating the NF-κB pathway, a panel of inhibitors was used to suppress the NF-κB pathway in scfih-overexpressing MFF-1 cells under hypoxia. Specifically, these included ST 2825 (10 μM), which inhibits MyD88-dependent activation (27), BAY 11-7082 (5 μM), which blocks IKK-mediated phosphorylation of IκBα (28), and (−)-DHMEQ (10 μM), which prevents the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (29) (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2 B and C, treatment with (−)-DHMEQ, but not ST 2825 or BAY 11-7082, significantly inhibited scFIH-induced NF-κB reporter activity and downstream gene expression. These results indicated that scFIH-mediated NF-κB activation under hypoxia was independent of scMyD88 (S. chuatsi MyD88) and canonical scIKK (S. chuatsi IKK)-mediated scIκBα phosphorylation, yet required nuclear translocation. These data suggested a mechanism in which scFIH likely facilitated the direct dissociation of NF-κB from scIκBα and its subsequent nuclear translocation. To further investigate this possibility, coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed in MFF-1 cells under hypoxia to examine the interactions of scFIH with the major downstream components of scIKK, including scIκBα, scp65, and scp50 (S. chuatsi p50). The results showed that scFIH interacted with scIκBα (Fig. 2D), but not with scp65 or scp50 (Fig. 2 E and F), consistent with previous reports describing an interaction between FIH and IκBα (30, 31). Indirect immunofluorescence assays revealed cytoplasmic colocalization of scFIH and scIκBα in MFF-1 cells under hypoxia (Fig. 2 G and H). Meanwhile, dual-luciferase assays in sciκbα-overexpressing MFF-1 cells showed that scfih overexpression moderately rescued the inhibitory effect of scIκBα on the NF-κB pathway under hypoxia (Fig. 2I). These results suggested a potential physical or functional interaction between scFIH and scIκBα under hypoxia.

To explore this interaction, AlphaFold3 was used to predict the binding sites and regions involved in the interaction between scIκBα and scFIH. The structural model revealed an extensive interaction surface involving both the JmjC domain and the C terminus of scFIH (Fig. 2J). Based on this multicontact prediction, the interaction was hypothesized to be multivalent. To test this, a series of scFIH truncation mutants was generated, including mutants lacking the N terminus, JmjC domain, or C terminus, as well as a construct containing the N terminus alone (Fig. 2K). Consistent with the AlphaFold3 prediction that distinct regions of scFIH, particularly the JmjC domain and C terminus, independently contribute to the binding interface, Co-IP assays in MFF-1 cells showed that deletion of any single domain was insufficient to abolish the interaction with scIκBα under hypoxia (Fig. 2 L–N). The ΔJ mutant retained the C terminus, and the ΔC mutant retained the JmjC domain, both of which were predicted to mediate contact with scIκBα. In contrast, the N-terminal fragment alone did not interact with scIκBα (Fig. 2O). Together, these findings supported a multivalent binding model in which scFIH associates with scIκBα through multiple structural elements, thereby conferring a robust interaction that is resistant to single-domain perturbation. These findings suggested that scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway by directly targeting scIκBα rather than scp65 or scp50 in MFF-1 cells under hypoxia, thereby promoting the dissociation and nuclear translocation of NF-κB independently of upstream kinase signaling.

To further investigate how scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway through interaction with scIκBα, three-dimensional structural predictions using AlphaFold3 suggested potential competitive binding among scIκBα, scFIH, and scp65 (Fig. 2P). Structural modeling predicted that both the Lys305 residue of scFIH and the Arg247 residue of scp65 interacted with the same Glu284 residue on scIκBα, suggesting a potential competitive binding mechanism (Fig. 2Q). Co-IP experiments in MFF-1 cells confirmed that reduced scFIH levels increased the binding of scIκBα to scp65 under hypoxia (Fig. 2R), indicating that scFIH weakened the scIκBα–scp65 interaction. Furthermore, the Lys305 residue of scFIH was identified as a key site mediating this competitive binding. The scFIH(K305A) mutant failed to activate the NF-κB reporter (Fig. 2S) or upregulate downstream gene expression compared with wild-type scFIH (Fig. 2T), and lost the ability to influence the scIκBα–scp65 interaction in MFF-1 cells under hypoxia (Fig. 2U), indicating that the Lys305 residue of scFIH was critical for competition with scp65 for scIκBα binding. Additionally, Co-IP analysis in MFF-1 cells revealed an interaction between the mutant scFIH and scp65 under hypoxia, suggesting that abolishing the competitive binding facilitated the assembly of a ternary complex among these three proteins (Fig. 2U). Collectively, these results suggested that scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway through competition with scp65 for scIκBα binding, mediated by its Lys305 residue under hypoxia, thereby releasing scp65 and promoting its nuclear translocation.

FIH Employs an Identical Competitive Mechanism to Activate NF-κB Under Normoxia.

Given that protein–protein interactions are fundamentally dictated by their structural conformations, which typically remain stable regardless of oxygen tension, it was reasoned that this competitive binding mechanism might not be exclusive to hypoxia. To assess this hypothesis, scfih overexpression and knockdown experiments were performed in MFF-1 cells under normoxia, yielding results consistent with the hypoxic observations. Specifically, scfih overexpression significantly activated the NF-κB reporter in MFF-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and upregulated the expression of inflammatory genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Conversely, scfih knockdown attenuated NF-κB reporter activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) and the transcription of these inflammatory genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), while still inducing a modest further upregulation of HIF target genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). In addition, scfih overexpression in MFF-1 cells promoted the translocation of scp65 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. In contrast, cells without scfih transfection exhibited cytoplasmic localization of scp65 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). These results indicated that under normoxia, FIH could also activate the NF-κB pathway.

Consistent with the hypoxic data, normoxic Co-IP and indirect immunofluorescence assays in MFF-1 cells demonstrated that scFIH and scIκBα still interacted (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G) and colocalized (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H and I). Additionally, after NF-κB pathway activation by phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 100 nM) (32), dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that overexpressing scfih in sciκbα-transfected MFF-1 cells similarly alleviated the scIκBα-mediated inhibition of the NF-κB pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S4J). The Lys305 residue also remained critical under normoxia. Normoxic Co-IP assays demonstrated that reduced scFIH levels enhanced the scIκBα–scp65 interaction in MFF-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4K). Conversely, the scFIH(K305A) mutant could neither activate the NF-κB pathway (SI Appendix, Fig. S4L) nor upregulate downstream genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4M), and it could not competitively modulate the scIκBα–scp65 interaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S4N). The protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX, 100 μg/mL) (33) and the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (10 μM) (34) were also used in MFF-1 cells to assess whether scFIH affected scIκBα degradation. Overexpression of scfih in MFF-1 cells significantly delayed the degradation of scIκBα compared with the control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4O). While treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 attenuated scIκBα degradation in both groups, scIκBα remained significantly more stable in scfih-overexpressing MFF-1 cells than in the control group under normoxia (SI Appendix, Fig. S4P). These findings indicated that scFIH did not promote scIκBα degradation to activate the NF-κB pathway. Instead, it substantially stabilized the protein. Furthermore, these results suggested that scIκBα might also undergo an uncharacterized nonproteasomal degradation process that may be restrained by scFIH. Collectively, these observations demonstrated that even under normoxia, scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway through competition with scp65 for scIκBα binding.

FIH contains a hydroxylase domain, while IκBα contains an ankyrin repeat domain that can be hydroxylated. Studies in mammalian cells have identified IκBα as a hydroxylation substrate of FIH. However, rigorous biophysical and cellular analyses have demonstrated that this hydroxylation does not alter IκBα folding, proteasomal degradation, or its binding affinity for the p65/p50 complex (31, 35). Consequently, FIH does not appear to influence the classical NF-κB pathway in mammals (36). To determine whether scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway in a hydroxylase-dependent manner under normoxia in teleosts, luciferase and RT–qPCR analyses were conducted in MFF-1 cells. A hydroxylase-inactive mutant of scFIH [scFIH(D202A)] was constructed and used for functional validation alongside wild-type scFIH (SI Appendix, Fig. S4Q). Overexpression of scfih(D202A) in MFF-1 cells, similar to wild-type scFIH, activated the NF-κB reporter (SI Appendix, Fig. S4R) and upregulated expression of NF-κB pathway target genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4S), indicating that scFIH activated the NF-κB pathway independently of its hydroxylase activity under normoxia.

FIH Deficiency Inhibits the Inflammatory Response and Antibacterial Immunity In Vivo.

To investigate the role of FIH in the inflammatory response in vivo, drfih (D. rerio fih)-deficient zebrafish (drfih–/–) were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout. Sequencing and genotyping analyses confirmed a 17-bp deletion in the drfih gene (Fig. 3A). This deletion induced a translational frameshift that led to a premature stop codon. The predicted product was a truncated 49-amino acid peptide that lacked approximately 300 amino acids, including the JmjC domain present in the wild-type protein (Fig. 3B).

To assess the impact of FIH deficiency on the inflammatory response, an acute inflammation model was established in zebrafish larvae based on previous studies (37). Three-day postfertilization larvae were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS). As shown in Fig. 3C, the survival rate of drfih–/– larvae exposed to LPS was higher than that of the drfih+/+ larvae. After 12 h of LPS exposure, compared with drfih+/+ larvae, drfih–/– larvae exhibited a milder inflammatory response, with no significant tail fin edema, tissue damage, or typical pericardial swelling associated with inflammation (Fig. 3D). RNA samples from larvae of different genotypes after LPS or PBS exposure were collected to assess the expression of inflammatory genes. The results showed that without exogenous stimulation, there was no significant difference in the expression of inflammatory genes. Following LPS challenge, wild-type larvae displayed a greater upregulation of inflammatory genes (dril-1β, dril-6, dril-8, and drtnf-α) compared with drfih-deficient larvae (Fig. 3E).

A moderate inflammatory response assists the host in combating bacterial infections, while an excessive inflammatory reaction triggered by a high bacterial load can result in host tissue damage and may ultimately lead to mortality (38, 39). To further validate the role of FIH in modulating the inflammatory response, adult zebrafish were intraperitoneally injected with Vibrio anguillarum to induce different levels of inflammation (40, 41). This design was used to test whether FIH promoted bacterial clearance at a low dose but exacerbated inflammatory injury at a high dose. Following challenge with an approximate LD50 of V. anguillarum (103 CFU), drfih+/+ zebrafish exhibited an overall higher survival rate compared with drfih–/– zebrafish, indicating that drfih-deficient individuals had a reduced ability to resist V. anguillarum infection (Fig. 3F). Following high-dose V. anguillarum challenge (106 CFU), the results showed that 12 h postinjection, the survival rate of drfih+/+ zebrafish dropped to 50%, while drfih–/– zebrafish showed only a 5% reduction in survival, with a final mortality rate lower than that of drfih+/+ zebrafish (Fig. 3G), indicating that drfih deficiency protected zebrafish from excessive inflammatory injury caused by severe bacterial challenge.

Previous studies have established that V. anguillarum infection induces robust inflammatory responses in the liver and spleen of zebrafish (42, 43). Since the accumulation of neutrophils is a hallmark of inflammation and infection (44), the liver was selected for histological examination because it is readily sampled and is a major target organ during infection. In the liver of drfih+/+ zebrafish 12 h after infection with 106 CFU of V. anguillarum per fish, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed an increase in neutrophils, indicating an inflammatory response, whereas no significant inflammation was observed in drfih–/– zebrafish (Fig. 3 H and I). RT–qPCR analyses revealed that drfih deficiency suppressed the upregulation of inflammatory genes such as dril-1β, dril-6, dril-8, and drtnf-α in the liver of drfih–/– zebrafish (Fig. 3J). Because V. anguillarum infection can induce systemic inflammation, inflammatory gene expression was also examined in brain tissue from zebrafish infected with the same infection dose by RT–qPCR. Consistent with the findings in the liver, the expression of multiple inflammatory cytokine genes was similarly suppressed in the brain (Fig. 3K). These findings suggested that FIH deficiency suppressed NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses and antibacterial immunity in vivo.

FIH Deficiency Suppresses Hypoxia-induced Activation of NF-κB In Vivo.

Given the established role of FIH in hypoxia responses, the impact of drfih deletion on hypoxic adaptation was examined in zebrafish. drfih-deficient zebrafish displayed distinct behavioral differences compared with their wild-type and heterozygous siblings in a hypoxic environment. Time-course observations revealed that during exposure to 5% O2, drfih+/+ and drfih+/– zebrafish progressively migrated to the water surface, exhibiting a sustained surfacing behavior after 10 h. In contrast, drfih–/– zebrafish remained distributed throughout the water column, suggesting enhanced hypoxia tolerance in drfih-deficient zebrafish (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Immunohistochemical analysis of liver and brain tissues showed marked nuclear translocation of drp65 in drfih+/+ zebrafish under hypoxia, with increased staining intensity indicating higher p65 levels, whereas this response was attenuated in drfih–/– zebrafish (Fig. 4 A–D). RT–qPCR analysis of the examined tissues revealed that wild-type zebrafish exhibited higher expression of inflammatory genes under hypoxia (Fig. 4E), supporting the observed nuclear translocation and increased expression of drp65. These results suggested that FIH enhanced activation of the NF-κB pathway in response to hypoxia in vivo.

Evolutionary Changes in IκBα May Underlie the Loss of Mammalian FIH Competitive Inhibition.

Previous studies have shown that the interaction between FIH and IκBα in mammalian cell lines does not affect NF-κB activation (31). To investigate whether mammals exhibit a similar competitive binding mechanism to that observed in teleosts, the corresponding assays were performed in HEK293T cells. Co-IP analysis revealed that hsFIH (Homo sapiens FIH) and hsIκBα (H. sapiens IκBα) interacted in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5A). In HEK293T cells, hypoxia activated both the NF-κB pathway and HIF signaling. However, hsfih overexpression selectively inhibited HIF signaling under hypoxia (Fig. 5B) but had no detectable effect on the NF-κB pathway under normoxia or hypoxia (Fig. 5C). Dual-luciferase assays also showed that hsfih overexpression reduced hypoxia response element (HRE) reporter activity (Fig. 5D) but did not affect NF-κB reporter activity in HEK293T cells under both normoxia and hypoxia (Fig. 5E). Co-IP analysis using graded amounts of hsFIH in HEK293T cells showed that the binding of hsp65 (H. sapiens p65) to hsIκBα was independent of hsFIH dosage (Fig. 5F). The results also suggested that hsFIH formed a complex with hsp65 and hsIκBα. Three-dimensional structural predictions did not identify putative competitive binding sites within the hsp65–hsIκBα–hsFIH protein complex (Fig. 5G). These analyses indicated that, unlike fish FIH, hsFIH did not exhibit competitive binding with hsIκBα. In contrast, it may form a relatively stable hsp65–hsIκBα–hsFIH complex, consistent with the outcome observed with the mutated scFIH.

Three-dimensional structural overlap analyses of homologous proteins showed that, compared with scIκBα, hsIκBα possessed an additional α-helix at the C terminus, which corresponded to the primary competitive binding region in fish (Fig. 5H). These differences at the C terminus of IκBα were therefore hypothesized to be critical for the loss of this mechanism in mammals. To test this hypothesis, a chimeric protein hs-scIκBα was constructed using overlap PCR technology, in which the 257 to 317 aa segment of hsIκBα was replaced with the 257 to 308 aa segment of scIκBα (Fig. 5I). Dual-luciferase assays with the chimeric protein revealed that both hsIκBα and hs-scIκBα reduced NF-κB reporter activity in HEK293T cells. Upon hsfih overexpression, the inhibitory effect of hs-scIκBα on the NF-κB reporter was partially relieved (Fig. 5J). The three-dimensional structures of hs-scIκBα in complex with hsFIH or hsp65 were predicted, revealing a competitive region among the three proteins (Fig. 5K). Co-IP assays in HEK293T cells showed that the binding of hsp65 to hs-scIκBα was affected by hsFIH dosage (Fig. 5L). These findings suggested that the C-terminal region of IκBα played a key role in the differences in competitive binding mechanisms.

To explore the similarities and differences, a phylogenetic analysis of IκBα was conducted, revealing interspecies variations alongside marked evolutionary conservation. Despite overall conservation, IκBα sequences clustered into distinct phylogenetic lineages (Fig. 6A). To further investigate the universality of the FIH–IκBα mechanism, putative competitive binding interfaces involving p65, IκBα, and FIH were modeled across diverse species. Structural predictions supported the existence of competitive binding in teleosts (D. rerio, S. chuatsi, and Salmo salar), the marine mammal Delphinapterus leucas, the aquatic crustacean Macrobrachium rosenbergii, and the amphibian Xenopus laevis. In contrast, homologous interfaces were not identified in the terrestrial reptile Anolis carolinensis, the bird Accipiter gentilis, or H. sapiens (Fig. 6B). Collectively, these predictions were consistent with an evolutionary adaptation potentially associated with aquatic environments.

Discussion

The escalating ecological crisis of global warming and aquatic deoxygenation is inextricably linked to the health of aquatic populations, primarily by exacerbating hypoxia-induced immune dysregulation. As dissolved oxygen levels decline, aquatic animals face a dual threat: metabolic restriction and increased susceptibility to pathogen invasion. To survive these concurrent challenges, teleosts may have evolved sophisticated regulatory mechanisms that maintain immune competence amid oxygen deprivation (45, 46). This study identified an oxygen–sensitive regulatory axis in fish that enhanced immunity and resistance to pathogen infection, with FIH acting as a critical modulator of NF-κB-mediated inflammation through competitive displacement of p65 from the IκBα inhibitory complex. Under hypoxia, elevated FIH expression disrupted the IκBα–p65 interaction and thereby potentiated inflammatory signaling. However, this mechanism appeared to be absent from the terrestrial species examined (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). This regulatory axis likely represented an adaptive strategy enabling aquatic animals to maintain immune competence in hypoxic environments, even when confronted with high pathogen exposure.

Emerging evidence indicates functional crosstalk between HIF signaling and the NF-κB pathway (47). While prior work had established a positive feedback loop between HIF-1α and NF-κB that amplifies cellular responses to combined inflammatory and hypoxic stress and had demonstrated that the regulation of NF-κB activity by PHD enzymes depends on hydroxylation of IKKβ, the role of FIH in NF-κB regulation had remained unclear (48, 49). Previous studies have shown that the asparaginyl hydroxylase activity of FIH is inhibited under hypoxia (50). In this study, FIH upregulation under hypoxia was shown to activate the NF-κB pathway through a hydroxylation-independent mechanism. In this pathway, structural modeling and mutational analyses suggested that the Lys305 residue of scFIH competed with the Arg247 residue of scp65 for engagement of Glu284 in scIκBα. As a result, reduced scp65–scIκBα binding permitted nuclear translocation of scp65 and subsequent activation of NF-κB. These results broadened FIH’s functional repertoire under both hypoxia and normoxia.

FIH is capable of catalyzing asparaginyl hydroxylation of IκBα and related ankyrin repeat-containing proteins. However, previous studies in mammalian systems have consistently demonstrated that such posttranslational modification does not directly activate or significantly modulate NF-κB pathway activity (30, 31). In mouse and zebrafish, FIH also hydroxylates IKKε, thereby dampening type I interferon signaling (47). The lack of NF-κB regulation by IκBα hydroxylation may reflect that FIH target sites lie outside IκBα’s core functional domains and thus do not perturb its inhibitory engagement with NF-κB. Interestingly, in mammalian systems, IκBα has been proposed to act as a sink that competitively sequesters FIH away from HIF-1α, thereby derepressing HIF-1 activity (51, 52). The findings revealed an evolutionarily divergent mechanism in teleosts, in which FIH actively sequestered IκBα to trigger innate immunity rather than being merely sequestered by IκBα to tune hypoxia responses. Nevertheless, protein–protein interactions are often multivalent and supported by auxiliary interfaces (exosites) that promote recruitment and positioning for catalysis (53, 54). Consistent with this, it was found that in fish, both the JmjC domain and the C terminus of FIH interacted with IκBα, and that binding sites outside the hydroxylation region competed with p65 for association with IκBα.

Structural analyses of IκBα orthologs revealed evolutionary divergence in the C-terminal domain architecture between teleosts and mammals, such as humans and mice. Previous studies have revealed that structural features can induce substantial conformational alterations (55, 56). Comparative modeling suggested that secondary structural elements in teleost IκBα created a binding interface for FIH. Experimental validation confirmed that this teleost-specific structural feature enabled FIH-mediated activation of the NF-κB pathway, whereas human IκBα lacked the requisite structural motifs for such interactions. It should be emphasized that although all aquatic species examined were predicted to possess competitive binding regions, not all of these predicted regions were associated with the C terminus of IκBα. These observations support a model of lineage-specific adaptation, in which aquatic animals may have evolved FIH-dependent immune modulation to counterbalance the dual challenges of hypoxic stress and microbial exposure.

Materials and Methods

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (No. SYSY-IACUC-2023-B0125). Zebrafish were maintained under standard conditions, and MFF-1 cells (57), ZEM2S cells, and HEK293T cells were cultured in their respective media. Cells and zebrafish were subjected to hypoxia in a controlled-atmosphere workstation. Plasmids encoding mandarin fish fih, iκbα, p65, and p50, as well as human fih, iκbα, and p65, together with selected mutant or chimeric derivatives, were generated by PCR-based cloning (58). These constructs were transfected into cells for overexpression or knockdown experiments, dual-luciferase reporter assays (59, 60), coimmunoprecipitation and Western blotting (61, 62), RT–qPCR (62, 63), and immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy (64), with S. chuatsi fih-targeting siRNAs included where indicated (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) (65). A drfih knockout zebrafish line was generated by CRISPR/Cas9, and inflammatory phenotypes were assessed in a larval lipopolysaccharide exposure model (37) and an adult V. anguillarum challenge model (66) by mortality, gene expression analysis, histology, and p65 immunohistochemistry, as appropriate. Transcriptomic profiling of hypoxia-treated MFF-1 cells was performed using RNA-seq, followed by differential expression and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses (67–72). The raw data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI GEO database under accession number GSE319394. AlphaFold3-based structural modeling (73) and phylogenetic analysis (74–76) were used to examine structural features and evolutionary relationships. Continuous N- or C-terminal regions longer than 50 amino acids with pLDDT scores below 70 were considered low-confidence regions and were excluded from the displayed structural models. Detailed protocols, statistical analyses, primer and siRNA sequences, plasmid information, antibodies, accession numbers, and bioinformatic parameters are provided in the Supplementary Information.
