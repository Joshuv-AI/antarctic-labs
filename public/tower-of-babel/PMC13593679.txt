Chrysophanol is associated with reduced inflammation and oxidative stress in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury
Authors: Xiaoxiao Li, Bingqing Ouyang, Mengdi Zhang, Haoran Sun, Hui Ye, Lijiao You, Weifang Yuan, Haikuo Wang, Hainan Yang, Kailang Xu, Ming Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a common and severe complication in critically ill patients with high mortality, characterized by a dysregulated inflammatory response and oxidative stress. Chrysophanol (CHR), a natural anthraquinone, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties in other septic conditions, but its role and mechanism in SA-AKI remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the protective effects of CHR against SA-AKI and the underlying mechanisms. Cell viability, inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IL-10), and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed after CHR pretreatment. An in vivo model was established using LPS method. Renal injury was evaluated through histopathology (H&E, Masson's, TUNEL staining), renal function (serum creatinine and BUN), and systemic inflammation (serum IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10). In vitro, LPS significantly reduced HK-2 cell viability and increased the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS. CHR pretreatment restored cell viability, suppressed the mRNA levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1, enhanced IL-10 expression, and reduced intracellular ROS levels. In vivo, LPS-induced mice exhibited severe renal pathological damage, apoptosis, collagen deposition, and elevated serum creatinine and BUN. CHR treatment attenuate these pathological changes, reduced renal injury scores and apoptosis, and improved renal function. Furthermore, CHR significantly decreased serum levels of pro-inflammatory IL-6 and TNF-α while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in LPS mice. Chrysophanol treatment is associated with improved cell survival, reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, and decreased oxidative stress in LPS-stimulated HK-2 cells and septic mice. These associations suggest a potential protective role of chrysophanol in SA-AKI, though the underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection (Evans et al. 2021; Desposito and Bascara 2024). Clinically, it occurs when an infection triggers an excessive and uncontrolled inflammatory response, leading to widespread tissue damage, multiple organ failure and is one of the leading causes of death among hospitalized patients (Angus and Poll 2013; Rudd et al. 2020). Sepsis is characterized by a dysregulated innate immune response to pathogenic infection, involving the transcription and release of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)−1, and IL-6, along with increased oxidative stress and activation of the complement system (Kuwabara et al. 2022; Kounatidis et al. 2024; Gotts and Matthay 2016).

Sepsis accounts for approximately 25–75% of all AKI cases among critically ill patients (Zarbock et al. 2023; Bagshaw et al. 2008; Uchino et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2020; Hoste et al. 2015). In sepsis, tissue injury arises not only from the direct effects of pathogenic invasion but also from the excessive and dysregulated inflammatory response of the host. Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is common in critically ill patients and is strongly correlated with poor clinical outcomes (Zarbock et al. 2023; Bagshaw et al. 2007). The underlying pathophysiology involves microcirculatory dysfunction, cellular metabolic reprogramming, and dysregulated inflammatory signaling (Zarbock et al. 2023; Shum et al. 2016). Moreover, compared with AKI of other causes, SA-AKI is associated with higher in-hospital mortality and longer hospitalization. The high incidence and poor prognosis of SA-AKI have attracted increasing attention in recent years.

In traditional Chinese medicine, natural products have long been used to treat diseases, and their bioactive metabolites have served as effective therapeutic agents for centuries. For instance, Moringa oleifera, a Moringaceae plant distributed across tropical and subtropical regions of Asia and Africa, possesses antioxidant, anti-quorum sensing, and DNA-protective activities (Singh et al. 2009). Similarly, TaoHe ChengQi Decoction (THCQD) protects against sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy by activating the Nrf2 pathway (Lu et al. 2024), while Babaodan (BBD) alleviates LPS-induced sepsis by inhibiting NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Li et al. 2022). In addition, the natural compound baicalein reduces sepsis-related inflammation and oxidative stress by modulating NF-κB signaling, with the strongest protective effect observed at 100 mg/kg pretreatment (Dicle et al. 2024).

Previous studies have shown that Chrysophanol (CHR) protects against sepsis-induced acute myocardial injury by reducing inflammation and apoptosis through regulation of the miR-27b-3p/PPARG axis (Zhao et al. 2022). However, its protective role in SA-AKI remains unclear. Therefore, this study conducted in vivo and in vitro experiments using LPS-stimulated HK-2 cells and a mouse model to further investigate the protective effects of Chrysophanol against SA-AKI.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Seventh People’s Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. All experimental procedures followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the same institution (Approval No.: 2024-AR-013).

Reagents

Chrysophanol was purchased from MeilunBio (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in DMSO. The human renal proximal tubular epithelial cell line (HK-2) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every other day. When cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were trypsinized and seeded into 6-well or 96-well plates for subsequent experiments.

Establishment of SA-AKI cell and co-culture models

HK-2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, resuspended, counted, and seeded into six-well plates. After 24 h of incubation to allow cell attachment, the cells were treated with 10 μg/mL LPS for 24 h to establish LPS-stimulated HK-2 cells. Chrysophanol was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve a final DMSO concentration of 0.1% (v/v) in all treatment groups, including the CHR-containing solutions. For in vitro experiments, the control group received an equal volume of vehicle (0.1% DMSO in culture medium). For in vivo experiments, the normal control (NC) and LPS groups received an equal volume of vehicle (0.1% DMSO in PBS) by gavage.

Cell viability assay

HK-2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. In the treatment groups, CHR (10, 30, or 50 μM) was added 30 min prior to LPS stimulation (10 μg/mL) and co-incubated with LPS-treated cells for 24 h. Cell viability was then determined using the CCK-8 assay, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (Thermo Labsystems, Waltham, MA, USA).

Measurement of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels in LPS-stimulated HK-2 cells were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HK-2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until reaching 60–80% confluence. In the treatment groups, CHR (10, 30, or 50 μM) was added 30 min before LPS stimulation (10 μg/mL). After treatment, DCFH-DA working solution was added to each well, and fluorescence was observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope. Cells in the positive control group displayed strong green fluorescence, while those in the negative control group showed weak fluorescence; differences in fluorescence intensity among groups reflected changes in intracellular ROS levels.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA from HK-2 cells in each group was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using the TaKaRa PrimeScript 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ™ SYBR® qPCR Master Mix following the recommended protocol. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method, and all experiments were conducted in triplicate. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Animal treatment with CHR

The sepsis mouse model was established using the LPS method. All mice used in this study were male. In a separate experimental setup, mice were randomly assigned to five groups (N = 6 per group): normal control (NC), LPS, and LPS + CHR (low, medium, and high dose) groups. Mice in the LPS group received an equal volume of solvent to the stomach for seven consecutive days, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (20 mg/kg) for 24 h to induce sepsis (Huang et al. 2020). Similarly, mice in the CHR-L, CHR-M, and CHR-H groups were administered CHR to the stomach at doses of 100, 150, or 300 mg/kg, respectively, for seven days before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (20 mg/kg) for 24 h. The LPS-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) model was established as previously described. After 24 h, all mice were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemistry and pathological assessment

Renal pathology was evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and TUNEL staining. Kidneys were collected 24 h after model induction, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFS), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. The sections were stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome for histopathological evaluation, while apoptosis was assessed using TUNEL staining. Histological injury was scored using a semiquantitative scale based on the percentage of cortical tubular damage: 0, no damage; 1, 1–25%; 2, 26–50%; 3, 51–75%; and 4, 76–100%. All H&E staining and data collection were performed in a blinded manner. A minimum of 10 high-power fields (400 ×) were scored for each of the mice.

Serum creatinine, BUN, and cytokine measurement

Renal function was evaluated by measuring serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) using commercial assay kits (WEIAOBIO, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, blood samples were collected 24 h after LPS and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min to obtain serum. Serum levels of creatinine and BUN were determined using ELISA kits from WEIAOBIO. In addition, serum TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-6 were measured using commercially available ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

The analysis demonstrated that a sample size of n = 6 provides 80% statistical power to detect a 30% difference in serum creatinine at an α level of 0.05, which corresponded to the anticipated effect size for our primary endpoint. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect of CHR on SA-AKI in mice

To further investigate the protective effect of CHR on SA-AKI, morphological and renal functional changes were evaluated in LPS-induced mice. Histological and functional alterations were consistent with the in vitro findings. The LPS group showed severe renal injury characterized by vacuolar degeneration and detachment of renal tubular epithelial cells. In addition, special staining methods, including Masson’s staining and TUNEL staining, were performed to further assess kidney injury in the mouse model. The results showed that CHR exerted a dose-dependent protective effect. Masson positive fibrotic areas (blue color, original magnification 200 ×) and TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells (yellow color) were gradually reduced with increasing CHR concentrations (Fig. 4A). CHR treatment protected SA-AKI model mice in a concentration-dependent manner, alleviating pathological features such as brush border loss and cast formation. Furthermore, renal injury scores and apoptosis rates were evaluated to assess renal function. Both parameters were significantly decreased following CHR treatment (Fig. 4B–C). Discussion

Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a common and severe complication in critically ill patients, accounting for approximately 45–70% of all AKI cases (Uchino et al. 2005; Hoste et al. 2015). It is characterized by a complex pathophysiology involving dysregulated inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular apoptosis, contributing to high mortality rates (Bagshaw et al. 2007; Yegenaga et al. 2004; Bouchard et al. 2015; Sureshbabu et al. 2018). The development of effective therapeutic agents to alleviate these pathological processes is of significant clinical importance.

In this study, we demonstrated that Chrysophanol (CHR), a natural anthraquinone, confers a protective effect against SA-AKI both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings indicate that CHR alleviates renal injury by enhancing cell viability, suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reducing oxidative stress, and inhibiting tubular cell apoptosis. The initial in vitro experiments provided strong evidence for the efficacy of CHR. LPS stimulation significantly reduced cell viability, which was markedly reversed by CHR in a concentration-dependent manner. This indicates that CHR directly protects renal tubular cells—the primary targets in AKI—from sepsis-induced cytotoxicity. We next explored the mechanisms underlying this protective effect. A characteristic of sepsis is the excessive release of pro-inflammatory mediators (Evans 2018; Rello et al. 2017; Póvoa et al. 2005; Delano and Ward 2016). Among them, CRP serves as a key marker of inflammation and infection, primarily induced by IL-6. Consistent with this, our data showed a dramatic upregulation of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in LPS-treated HK-2 cells. CHR treatment potently suppressed the mRNA expression of these cytokines while simultaneously upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. This shift in the inflammatory balance from a pro- to an anti-inflammatory state is crucial for controlling the "cytokine storm" and mitigating subsequent tissue damage. Furthermore, oxidative stress is a key driver of cellular injury in sepsis (Patoli et al. 2020; Tsuji et al. 2023; Sikora et al. 2023). Our results confirmed a substantial increase in intracellular ROS in HK-2 cells following LPS challenge. CHR exhibited a significantly reducing intracellular ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner. This antioxidant property likely contributes to the preservation of cellular integrity function, working in concert with its anti-inflammatory action to protect the renal tubules. To translate these in vitro findings into a more physiologically relevant context, we employed the well-established LPS model in mice. The in vivo results robustly corroborated our cellular data. LPS-induced mice exhibited classic features of SA-AKI, including elevated serum creatinine and BUN levels, severe renal tubular damage, increased collagen deposition, and widespread tubular cell apoptosis. CHR administration significantly ameliorated all these pathological alterations in a concentration-dependent fashion. The reduction in Masson's staining and TUNEL-positive cells specifically indicates that CHR attenuates renal collagen deposition and apoptosis in the acute phase of septic injury, which could have implications for long-term renal recovery. The systemic anti-inflammatory effect of CHR was also evident in vivo. Serum analysis revealed that CHR treatment significantly lowered the elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α while boosting the levels of IL-10 in septic mice. Previous research demonstrated that chrysophanol-loaded micelles (CLM) significantly enhanced the bioavailability of chrysophanol (approximately 3.4-fold), promoted favorable accumulation in the liver and brain, reduced renal injury markers, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim-1), and serum creatinine (SCr) (Gu et al. 2021). In another study, a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method was developed and validated to systematically investigate the pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and excretion of five rhubarb anthraquinones, including chrysophanol, in rats following oral administration of rhubarb extract. The results demonstrated rapid absorption, extensive tissue distribution, and predominant fecal excretion (Zhao et al. 2021). Similarly, another study investigated the tissue distribution of five rhubarb anthraquinones, including chrysophanol, in normal rats and CCl₄-induced liver-injured rats after 12 weeks of oral administration of rhubarb extract. The findings showed that free anthraquinones were distributed mainly in the liver, kidney, and spleen. Tissue concentrations were higher in normal rats than in model rats, and no significant accumulation was observed after 4 weeks of drug withdrawal (Fang et al. 2011).

Our findings are in agreement with the growing evidence highlighting the therapeutic potential of natural compounds in sepsis. Our study extends this protective role of CHR to the kidney, identifying it as a multi-organ protective agent in sepsis. The mechanisms we identified—suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress—align with known pathways implicated in SA-AKI (Kounatidis et al. 2024; Zarbock et al. 2023; Li et al. 2022). While our study did not elucidate the precise upstream molecular target of CHR, its efficacy in modulating these critical pathways highlights its potential as a promising candidate for further investigation. Nevertheless, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies are still required before any translational conclusions can be drawn.

The limitations of this study are as follows: (1) LPS-induced models primarily reflect endotoxemia rather than the complex polymicrobial sepsis observed in clinical settings. Therefore, future studies using cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) or other clinically relevant sepsis models are warranted to further validate our findings. (2) This study still requires further investigation using pathway inhibitors (e.g., NF-κB or Nrf2 inhibitors) or siRNA-mediated knockdown approaches to further validate the underlying mechanisms. (3) A larger cohorts should further strengthen the statistical robustness of the findings, particularly for secondary histological and biochemical endpoints. (4) Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data are lacking. The absence of these data represents a significant limitation when considering the therapeutic applicability of the compound. These results indicate that CHR treatment correlates with reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in SA-AKI models. However, whether CHR directly modulates specific upstream signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, Nrf2) to mediate these effects remains to be determined.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that Chrysophanol exerts a significant protective effect in SA-AKI. Its multifaceted actions, encompassing anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic effects, effectively attenuate renal dysfunction and histopathological damage in experimental models. These findings suggest that CHR holds promise as a potential therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of SA-AKI.
