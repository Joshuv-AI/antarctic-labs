Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of a point-of-care ultrasound program for the diagnosis of forearm fractures in rural Nepal: a mid-level provider qualitative study
Authors: Evan Weldon, Mandeep Pathak, Elijah Katz, Leland Perice, Sanjaya Bahadur Chand, Lauren McCabe, Samuel Nesemann, Justin Zhao, Myles Dworkin, Ramu Kharel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Nepal, fractures contribute an estimated 2.5 million disability-adjusted life years annually, with upper-extremity injuries representing the most common fracture type. While radiography is the diagnostic standard, access to X-ray machines and trained technicians is limited in rural Nepal, leading to delayed diagnosis and substantial patient costs. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a low-cost, portable imaging modality that has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing upper-extremity fractures and is well suited to resource-limited settings.MethodsSeventeen health assistants and auxiliary health workers, together called mid-level providers (MLPs), who attended a two-day ultrasound training program focused on forearm fractures were selected for this study. Participants were selected based on successful completion of the training and continued practice at Bayalpata Hospital. Participants were interviewed with a semistructured guide that assessed their perceptions of the training and their implementation of ultrasound in clinical practice. Thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo.ResultsSeventeen MLPs participated, representing a combined 126 years of clinical experience. Five major themes emerged: (1) positive perceptions of the training program, (2) frequent clinical application of fracture POCUS, (3) improved diagnostic confidence and autonomy, (4) barriers, including language and duty-time constraints, and (5) strong support for expansion to additional facilities and anatomic regions. Participants reported reduced reliance on radiography, faster delivery of care, and enthusiasm for further POCUS training.ConclusionMid-level providers successfully integrated forearm fracture POCUS into routine clinical practice following ultrasound training. MLPs reported confidence using POCUS for fracture diagnosis after a two-day, hands-on training session. These findings support the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the program and support the expansion of ultrasound training in rural Nepal, particularly in areas lacking X-ray access.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09598-6.

Introduction

Trauma is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, representing 10% of worldwide deaths, and 90% of them occur in low- or middle-income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. In Nepal, fractures lead to an estimated annual loss of 2.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [3]. Steep terrain and difficult road conditions contribute to the high prevalence of falls and motor vehicle incidents [4]. Upper extremities are the most common anatomical site of injury and fracture, representing 42% of all trauma in Nepal [5]. Radiographs are the diagnostic standard of care for upper-extremity fractures. However, access to X-ray machines and trained radiology technicians is limited, and traveling to hospitals with these capabilities is a major barrier for patients, who often face long travel times and associated costs [6]. Ultrasound represents a possible solution as a low-cost, mobile alternative to X-ray machines for fracture diagnosis that could prevent unnecessary referrals.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is an affordable and versatile imaging modality that is well suited to resource-limited settings [7]. While X-ray is the gold standard for the diagnosis of fractures, POCUS has shown comparable sensitivity and specificity [8]. Champagne et al. performed a meta-analysis and reported that the pooledsensitivity and specificity of POCUS for the diagnosis of upper-extremity fractures were 0.93 and 0.92, respectively [9]. However, ultrasound is user dependent, and proper training and experience are necessary to achieve diagnostic accuracy [10]. While physicians typically perform ultrasounds, other medical staff have been successfully trained on certain POCUS techniques [11].

Many countries facing shortages of physicians, especially in rural areas, have turned to health assistants (HAs) and auxiliary health workers (AHWs), collectively called mid-level providers (MLPs) here, to fill gaps in care. HA certification requires a three-year program and earns a level B license with the Nepal Health Professional Council (NHPC). AHWs have completed a Community Health Assistant course along with at least 18 months of clinical training [12]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recognized the importance of MLPs and advocates for their international recognition and furthering opportunities for professional development and training [13].

A pilot training was conducted in April of 2025 at Bayalpata Hospital in rural far-western Nepal with the goal of teaching MLPs in POCUS for diagnosing forearm fractures of the radius and ulna and assessing their accuracy. Preliminary unpublished results from our cohort show a high sensitivity of 92%, with ongoing data collection to be part of future study. Three months following the initial training program, this qualitative study aimed to assess (1) perceptions of the ultrasound training program, (2) application of skills in clinical practice, (3) barriers to implementation, (4) recommendations for training improvements, and (5) the feasibility of scaling the program. The findings can then infer the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the POCUS training.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study followed a qualitative descriptive design using semi-structured interviews to explore the implementation, impact, and areas for improvement in using POCUS training for the diagnosis of forearm fractures by MLPs at Bayalpata Hospital (BH). BH is a rural secondary-care, provincial hospital located in Achham District, far-western Nepal. BH cares for nearly 100,000 patients every year through its integrated healthcare model [14]. As part of an ongoing capacity-building initiative, MLPs who staff BH emergency department (n = 17) completed a structured musculoskeletal POCUS training program focused on forearm fracture diagnosis of the radius and ulna in adults and children [15]. The training consisted of a two-day curriculum combining didactic and hands-on instruction to teach a standardized six-view scanning protocol for fracture identification followed by post-training competency assessment. The training was taught in English with numerous translators, who themselves were doctors and proficient in the ultrasound technique, working to ensure understanding. This study is designed to qualitatively assess the training program.

Participants and sampling strategy

All of the 17 MLPs who completed the training program were approached to participate in interviews. Seventeen MLPs were recruited. In a presurvey administered before the course, 41% indicated that they had never used ultrasound before the course. Participants were selected across professional roles and years of clinical experience to provide diverse perspectives. Post training competency was assessed by a written exam and a practical test where a trainer used a standard checklist to ensure adequate performance of the POCUS evaluation.

Participation was voluntary, and all individuals provided verbal and written informed consent before data collection.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted between June 15th and July 1st using a standardized semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary File 1). The interview guide was developed in English and translated into Nepali. The interview guide was reviewed by multiple native Nepali, native English, and bilingual reviewers to ensure clarity and translation fidelity. Before the interview, demographic information, including job title, years of experience, and prior ultrasound experience, was collected. The interview guide included open-ended questions across five areas of interest: (1) perceptions of the ultrasound training program, (2) application of skills in clinical practice, (3) barriers to implementation, (4) recommendations for training improvements, and (5) the feasibility of scaling the POCUS program. Interviews were conducted in Nepali. Two interviewers, including a bilingual facilitator, were present for all interviews. Neither of the interviewers were involved in the training that was given in April. The interviews were conducted in a private medical office on the BH campus. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and lasted 20–45 min.

Data management and analysis

Audio recordings were reviewed and transcribed into English. Transcripts were de-identified and imported into NVIVO (Windows Version 15) for analysis. Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report. Coding was performed iteratively using a combination of inductive and deductive approaches. Deductive codes were based on the interview guide areas of interest, whereas inductive codes emerged from participant narratives. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts and met regularly to compare and reconcile coding decisions. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until a final codebook was developed and applied across all transcripts.

Reliability

Several methods were used to improve the rigor of the analysis. Credibility was supported by analyst triangulation and participant validation during interviews. Dependability and confirmability were increased through an audit trail documenting coding decisions, codebook evolution, and NVIVO queries. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting and participant characteristics. Progressive interviews reiterated common themes and data saturation was approached by the end of the 17th interview.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Nepal Health Research Council (Ref. No. 1861) and the Institutional Review Board at BrownHealth (Ref. No 2279491-2n). All participants provided written informed consent. Data was stored securely on encrypted, password-protected systems. Quotes presented in this manuscript have been anonymized to preserve participant privacy.

Results

Participants (n = 17) were either health assistants (HAs) or Auxiliary Health Workers (AHWs). Combined, they have 126 years of clinical experience, averaging 7.3 years per person. Over half (59%) had some ultrasound experience prior to the fracture training.

The themes are (1) perceptions of the ultrasound training program, (2) application of skills in clinical practice, (3) barriers to implementation, (4) recommendations for training improvements, and (5) the feasibility of scaling the program. Of these 5 themes, 17 subthemes and 60 codes were developed and applied across all interview transcripts. Theme 1: perceptions of the ultrasound training program

The general perceptions of the ultrasound training program, including the teaching and hands-on practice portions, were positive. Across n = 17 interviews, the perceived value of the training was “very valuable.” All 17 participants felt confident in performing the ultrasound skill after the training.The training was very good. In many rural and hilly areas around us, especially in forests and high-altitude regions, there is no proper transportation, and it takes a long time for trauma patients to reach the hospital. In such settings, this kind of training is extremely valuable. It might be one of the best ways to improve trauma care in remote areas. -- Participant 6.The sessions were well organized, with class presenters and translators available to help us understand everything clearly. The lecture slides had well-justified explanations and clear, colored images that made the concepts easier to grasp. -- Participant 9.

All participants (n = 17) endorsed substantial use of the ultrasound skill in the emergency room since the training session. Theme 3: barriers to implementation

When asked about ways to improve the training or implementation of the skill, participants commented on the length of class, duty time interference, and difficulty understanding the spoken language, English.

Regarding class length, n = 4 (23.5%) thought class was the right length, n = 1 (5%) thought class was too long, and n = 3 (17.6%) thought class was too short. Nine of the participants (53%) did not have an opinion regarding class length. Regarding duty time interference, n = 6 (35.3%) mentioned that having duty time at the same time as the training interfered with participation. Other participants either did not have duty time at the same time (n = 8, 47%) or did not feel like duty time interfered (n = 3, 17.6%). The most common barrier was language difficulties. In addition, n = 12 (70%) described some difficulty with understanding the training in English and n = 5 (30%) did not have any difficulty understanding the spoken language.At times, it was a bit difficult to participate fully, especially when patients came in emergency because I was on duty. However, we managed our time effectively and prioritized attending the training while also addressing urgent clinical needs. -- Participant 10.Yes, the spoken language was easy for me to understand. Some participants found it a bit difficult at first, but since we translated it into Nepali when needed, it wasn’t a problem overall. -- Participant 8.

Theme 4: recommendations for training improvements

The participants had a number of recommendations for improving the training, ranging from including lower-extremity fracture diagnosis to including a handbook or video guide. When asked, all participants (n = 17) said they would like lower-extremity training. A further 6 (35%) wanted special ultrasound training, such a EFAST and gynecologic assessment. An additional n = 8 (47%) thought a handbook or video guide would be helpful for long-term retention of material. Of note, n = 3 (17.6%) said they did not want a handbook, believing it would be overwhelming or unhelpful. The majority of participants (n = 15, 88%) believe that duplicating this ultrasound training at other hospitals in Nepal would be beneficial. Unprompted, many participants (n = 5, 29%) specifically mentioned performing the training at hospitals with no X-ray. They mentioned the benefits of having a portable and flexible imaging modality in resource settings where there are currently no fracture diagnosis methods. Discussion

This qualitative study evaluated a recently conducted POCUS program focused on upper extremity fracture diagnosis for MLPs in the setting of rural Nepali. Specifically, MLPs were taught how to identify pediatric and adult radius and ulna fractures. Using Proctor’s framework for the assessment of implementation outcomes, the findings of this qualitative study affirm the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the POCUS program [17]. Acceptability is inferred from the MLP’s high valuation of and positive attitude towards the training. Appropriateness is inferred from the endorsement of positive impact on clinical outcomes and the desire to duplicate the training in similar settings. Lastly, feasibility is inferred from the regular use of the skill and limited barriers to implementation.

Analysis of the interviews showed that the training greatly improved confidence in performing the skill after the training. The teaching and structure of the class were viewed positively. This is consistent with other short MLP POCUS training programs, which have also proven to be successful [17].

The need for POCUS training and equipment in rural, resource-limited settings was emphasized by the participants. POCUS was described as transformative for fracture diagnosis in clinical practice, allowing real-time bedside diagnosis, reducing reliance on X-ray, and preventing unnecessary referrals. This was important to MLPs, as BH has the only x-ray machine in the region. This is consistent with the literature, as nearly 3.2 billion people are estimated to lack access to imaging around the world [9. The training was regarded as suitable for participants, clinically relevant, and impactful for patient care. While this training was unique for its focus on fracture diagnosis, these findings echo other literature that assessed mid-level provider POCUS training in EFAST and obstetric exams and found the successful adoption of ultrasound skills and improved clinical outcomes for patients [17, 18].

Recommendations for training improvements further reinforced the need for continued POCUS education and ways to improve future delivery of training. Participants described the importance of increased hands-on practice, native-language instruction, and supplementary materials such as handbooks. These suggestions mirror best practices in medical education and highlight the ways in which POCUS curricula can be optimized to increase skill retention and engagement [19].

The need for broader POCUS education is evident, and future program development to fill this gap can be informed by this investigation into barriers to implementation and the potential for expansion. Some of the described barriers, including duty-time conflicts and language limitations, are common constraints in rural healthcare settings and show the importance of protected training time and locally adapted instruction. Despite challenges the majority of participants enthusiastically supported expanding POCUS training to more anatomic regions and to other hospitals, especially those lacking X-ray machines. These findings suggest that MLP-led ultrasound training was appropriate, acceptable and feasible in the context. Ultimately successful dissemination of POCUS programs could support the creation of decentralized rural trauma care systems that reduce unnecessary referrals, accelerate patient care, and reduce patient cost.

Future studies can investigate how ultrasound training of MLPs can be expanded to positively impact care in rural Nepal and similar austere environments. The MLPs were unanimous in wanting more POCUS training. This included how to diagnose lower-extremity fractures. Many believed that being able to identify fractures in areas that currently have no means of differentiating which trauma sequelae require higher care could have a dramatic effect on clinical outcomes and patient delays. For many patients in the far-west region, the journey to reach BH is 8 h or more and involves significant cost. Preventing unnecessary journeys by creating a network of ultrasound-trained MLPs in rural health posts could save significant resources.

This study had some limitations that must be considered. First, as a single-site study conducted at a rural hospital in far-western Nepal, the findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare settings. Second, while the interviews were not conducted by members of the training team, there may still have been social desirability bias, potentially leading participants to overreport positive experiences or underreport challenges. Third, the relatively small sample size (n = 17), while encompassing all available participants, limits the ability to assess nuanced differences across provider roles or experience levels. Additionally, as interviews were primarily conducted in Nepali and translated into English, subtle meanings or provider intentions may have been lost or altered during transcription and translation.

Conclusion

The results of this study highlight the promising role of mid-level providers in learning and implementing point-of-care ultrasound for the diagnosis of forearm fractures in a rural, resource-limited setting. Participants with limited POCUS experience reported confidence in performing the skill, improved diagnostic capabilities, and successful clinical integration following a two-day ultrasound training session. This study shows the overall acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of MLP based POCUS program in the setting of rural Nepal. Future work could include creating a focused implementation strategy, and development of longitudinal, context-specific ultrasound training models as part of broader strategies to improve musculoskeletal care in low-resource settings.
