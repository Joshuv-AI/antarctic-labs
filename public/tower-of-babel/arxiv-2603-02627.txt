Topological bounds on the dynamical growth rate of chemical reaction networks
Praful Gagrani, Jiwei Wang, Yannick De Decker, David Lacoste

Abstract
Growth and decay are system-level properties of chemical reaction networks (CRNs) relevant from prebiotic chemistry to cellular metabolism. Their properties are typically analyzed through the kinetics of particular models, which requires specification of the full set of kinetic laws and parameters. In this work, we derive stoichiometry-based constraints on the growth (or shrinkage) rate, in the balanced-growth regime of scalable CRNs. The resulting bounds are controlled by a topological quantity, the maximum amplification factor, defined via a von Neumann max–min problem over feasible fluxes as illustrated by numerical tests on random-network ensembles of CRNs. We argue for the relevance of our results in the context of origin of life studies, but also for designing synthetic chemical reaction networks.



Autocatalysis and positive feedback are ubiquitous in biochemical organization and in candidate prebiotic chemistries: they enable self-maintenance, replication-like amplification, and, in open settings, persistent growth

Eigen and Schuster 1977
;
Baum et al. 2023
;
Ameta et al. 2021
. Stoichiometric frameworks have recently made the identification of autocatalytic subnetworks operational via purely structural criteria and algorithms that identify minimal autocatalytic motifs and their embeddings inside large networks
Blokhuis et al. 2020
;
Gagrani et al. 2024
;
Golnik et al. 2025
;
Andersen et al. 2020
. This progress raises a natural quantitative question: once a network is topologically capable of amplification, what controls
how fast
it can grow or decay?

From a dynamical perspective, growth rates depend on nonlinear kinetics and on rate constants that can vary over orders of magnitude and are often poorly constrained. This creates a gap: topology-based notions of autocatalysis certify feasibility, while kinetics determines realized performance. Bridging this gap requires a mixed kinetic–topological metric that quantifies amplification strength. Prior structural results in mass-action settings exist in restricted regimes (e.g., special coupling or dilute limits)
Despons et al. 2024
;
Unterberger and Nghe 2022
;
Nandan et al. 2025
; here we identify a broad balanced-growth regime in which a single metric controls the achievable growth rate.

Maximum Amplification Factor—

A CRN with internal species set
\mathcal{S}
and reaction set
\mathcal{R}
is specified by nonnegative
|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{R}|
output and input matrices
(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})
, where
|\mathcal{S}|
and
|\mathcal{R}|
are the number of species and reactions in the networks, respectively. The associated stoichiometric matrix is then given by
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}^{+}-\mathbb{S}^{-}
. In this work, we do not assume reversible reactions and each column of the stoichiometric matrix corresponds to a unique one-way reaction.
We consider the dynamics

\dot{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbb{S}\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{n}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{n}
collects populations and
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{n})
denotes the one-way reaction fluxes. Independently of kinetics, we also consider nonnegative flux vectors
\mathbf{x}\geq 0
as arbitrary reaction fluxes. Given
\mathbf{x}
, the induced species-wise input and output rates are
\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}
, respectively (SM). The topological quantity introduced next is defined by optimizing over such fluxes.

We define the
Amplification Factor
(AF) of a flux vector
\mathbf{x}
as the species-wise minimum ratio of output to input rates

\alpha(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\;\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}}.

(2)

The
Maximum Amplification Factor
(MAF) is the maximal value of the amplification factor over feasible fluxes,

\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})\,=\,\max_{\mathbf{x}>\bm{0}}\,\alpha(\mathbf{x})\,=\,\max_{\mathbf{x}>\bm{0}}\;\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}},

(3)

with the convention that species with
(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}=0
do not constrain the minimum (they correspond to
+\infty
). Note that
\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}
denotes that
\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{0}
but
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Inspired by von Neumann’s technological expansion rate in input-output models of economics (
Neumann 1945
,
Sargent and Stachurski 2019
, Sec. 9.5 in
Gale 1989
), the MAF was introduced for hypergraphs in
Blanco et al. 2025
. From now on, we will denote the MAF with
\alpha
for clarity of presentation.

Autonomous and autocatalytic networks—
We say that a CRN is
autonomous
if every reaction has at least one reactant and at least one product and every species appears as a reactant in at least one reaction and as a product in at least one reaction. In practice, reactions that effectively create or remove matter are modeled through chemostatted species representing exchange with an environment. Given an arbitrary network, an autonomous subnetwork can be obtained by iteratively removing chemostatted species and species that appear only as reactants or only as products, together with reactions that
act as a source or sink. Thus, autonomous networks are natural candidates for the self-maintaining biochemical core of a cell, as they describe the internally closed reaction architecture required to sustain ongoing biochemical activity.

For autonomous networks, it can be shown that the MAF satisfies
0<\alpha<\infty
(SM). A CRN is
autocatalytic
if, in addition to being autonomous, there exists a reaction flux such that all species are net produced (SM). From Equation
3
, it follows immediately that
\alpha>1
for autocatalytic networks. In SM, we show that the MAF of reducible networks is determined by their irreducible component with the highest MAF; thus,
\alpha
acts as a “best-module” topological score. For a given network, efficient algorithms and their implementation for identifying irreducible autocatalytic subnetworks with the highest MAF can be found in
Blanco et al. 2025
.

A network is considered to be minimal with respect to a certain property if it does not contain any subnetwork that also verify the property. In particular, finding the MAF for minimal autonomous networks reduces to solving the generalized-eigenvalue problem of the form
\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}=\alpha\phantom{\cdot}\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x}.
Minimal autocatalytic networks, termed (autocatalytic)
cores
, in reversible networks are shown to be of five distinct types
Blokhuis et al. 2020
, displayed in Fig.
1
. These cores provide useful reference points for interpreting
\alpha
as an amplification strength (see Table
1
), yielding characteristic values such as
\sqrt{2}
(Type I and III), the golden ratio
(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
(Type IV) and
2
(Type V). Numerical approximations of the exact values are summarized in Table
1
. Since for minimal networks, Eq.
3
reduces to the generalized eigenvalue problem, the ‘dual’ autoinhibitory core resulting from reaction reversal exchanges
\mathbb{S}^{+}\leftrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{-}
has MAF
1/\alpha
. Note that, however, for an arbitrary network,
\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{-},\mathbb{S}^{+})\neq 1/\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})

Balanced growth in scalable reaction networks—

We consider the regime of balanced growth, which is typically used to study chemical attractors
Singh and Jain 2023
and metabolic networks
Dourado and Lercher 2020
.
For species population vector
\mathbf{n}
, we introduce the extensive quantity
\Omega=\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{n}
, where the vector
\mathbf{w}
quantifies the relative weights of the species.
Then, the relative population vector
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{n}/\Omega
is such that

\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{q}=1.

A choice of CRN kinetics
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y})
is a Class-1 system
Pandey et al. 2020
, or
scalable

Lin et al. 2020
, if it satisfies

\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y})/c=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y}/c),

for any
c>0
.
For scalable fluxes, the rescaled reaction flux
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{n})/\Omega=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})
only depends on
\mathbf{q}
.
Under this condition, the composition dynamics of the relative population on the simplex reads (see SM for details):

\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbb{S}\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})-\bigl(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbb{S}\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})\bigr)\mathbf{q}.

(4)

Assuming that (
4
) admits a stable fixed point
\mathbf{q}^{*}
, the population scale evolves exponentially,

\dot{\Omega}=\Lambda\,\Omega
, with balanced-growth rate
\Lambda
characterized by

\mathbb{S}\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})=\Lambda\,\mathbf{q}^{*},\qquad\Lambda=\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbb{S}\,\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}).

(5)

We refer to a system with
\Lambda>0
,
\Lambda=0
, and
\Lambda<0
, respectively, as a ‘growing’, ‘steady’, and ‘shrinking’ system.

Topological bounds on
\Lambda
—
Consider scalable mass-action kinetics with reaction rates

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{r}(\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=k_{r}\frac{1}{\Omega^{\|(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}\|-1}}\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{n}_{s}^{(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}},

(6)

where
k_{r}>0
is the rate constant associated with reaction
r
,

\|(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}\|=\sum_{s}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}
is the total order of the reactants.
In contrast to standard mass-action kinetics, where
\Omega
denotes a constant volume, which has a simple expression for ideal gas-phase systems
Gillespie 1992
, the choice
\Omega=\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{n}
is natural for modeling ideal fluids with
\mathbf{w}
encoding the relative weights of the species contributing to the total volume.

We now focus on autonomous scalable mass-action reaction networks with
\mathbf{w}\geq\bm{1}
that admit a stable fixed point
\mathbf{q}^{*}
in the positive orthant such that
\mathbf{q}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
. For such networks,

\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\phantom{\cdot}\mathbf{q},

where the kinetic–stoichiometric prefactor is

\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\;:=\;\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\;\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}\,k_{r}.

(7)

From this relation, we obtain a universal loose lower-bound
-\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}<\Lambda.

We then show that if the MAF of a network is not greater than one, the network must either be steady or shrinking, i.e.
\alpha\leq 1\implies\Lambda\leq 0
.
Finally, we show that growing networks satisfy

\;\Lambda\;\leq\;\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\,(\alpha-1).

Collecting the above relations in a single equation, we get the main result of this work

-\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\;<\;\Lambda\;\leq\;\max\left(0,\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\,(\alpha-1)\right).

(8)

The upper bound is saturated for autocatalytic networks with unimolecular inputs and equal rate constants. For these networks, furthermore,
(\alpha-1)
coincides with the largest eigenvalue of the stoichiometric matrix
\mathbb{S}
. Full details of these results, along with a worked example, are provided in the SM.

Equation (
8
) turns the MAF
\alpha
into a sharp, parameter-sparse descriptor of growth.
First, it yields a strict
decay certificate
for non-autocatalytic networks: if
\alpha\leq 1
, then
\Lambda\leq 0
independently of the rate constants and of the choice of stable fixed point.
Second, it imposes an explicit
upper bound
for autocatalytic networks: even under arbitrary kinetic tuning, the balanced growth rate cannot exceed
\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}(\alpha-1)
. While Eq. (
8
) implies that any growing network must be autocatalytic, i.e.
\Lambda>0\implies\alpha>1
, the converse does not hold: an autocatalytic network need not exhibit positive growth. In other words, the sign of
\Lambda
fundamentally depends on the kinetics when
\alpha>1
.

When bounds on rate constants
k_{r}
are available, Eq. (
8
) leads to a clear separation between stoichiometric and kinetic structure of the network. Indeed, the growth rate
\Lambda
, rescaled by
K=\max_{r}k_{r}
, is then bounded by quantities that depend only on the network topology:

-\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{1}\;<\;\frac{\Lambda}{K}\;\leq\;\,\max\,\!\bigl(0,\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{1}(\alpha-1)\bigr),

(9)

where
\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{1}=\max_{s}\sum_{r}(\mathbb{S}^{-})_{s}^{\,r}
.

Numerical tests on random autonomous networks—

We tested the bounds on randomly generated autonomous, unambiguous CRNs with
2\leq|\mathcal{S}|\leq 12
and
2\leq|\mathcal{R}|\leq 12
(SM).
We also vary the maximum reaction order for products,
M^{+}
, and reactants,
M^{-}
, where

M^{\pm}:=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{S}^{\pm})_{s}^{r}.

For each instance, we computed
\alpha
from (
3
) using computational implementations in
Sargent and Stachurski 2019
;
Blanco et al. 2025
. When necessary, we also used integration of the scalable composition dynamics to its stable fixed point
\mathbf{q}^{*}
following
Lin et al. 2020
, and extracted
\Lambda
from (
5
). Using
\delta\equiv\alpha-1
and
\tilde{\Lambda}\equiv\Lambda/\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}
, Equation
8
can be recast as

-1<\tilde{\Lambda}\leq\max(0,\delta).

(10)

Figure
2
confirms that all networks satisfy this bound. The majority of autocatalytic networks with (
\alpha>1
) are far from the limit
\delta\simeq 0
, and their growth rate stays far from the lower bound
\tilde{\Lambda}=-1
and is positive. Thus, these networks have a built-in tendency for growth.
The majority of autoinhibitory networks (
\alpha<1
), satisfy the empirical envelope

\delta\leq\tilde{\Lambda}.

This lower bound is much tighter than the one of Eq. (
8
), but it is
not universal
: it can fail.
In that case, one obtains the outliers shown in red in the right figure of Fig.
2
. As explained in SM, these outliers mainly appear in the region near
\delta\simeq 0^{-}
.

A striking feature of the left column of Fig.
2
for maximum reaction orders
M^{+}=M^{-}=2
is that the distribution of
\delta=\alpha-1
values concentrates on a discrete set of values.
In Fig.
3
, we have labeled the peaks in
\alpha
together with a representation of their core structure.
We observe that a large number of networks concentrate on the MAF value of type I or type III cores, which is
\sqrt{2}
or on the value 2, which corresponds to the MAF value of type V. A large degeneracy of networks is observed among networks with
\alpha\geq 1
, at values
\alpha=1
, and at
\alpha=2
, which coincides with the value of
M^{+}
. In SM, we show that the MAF
\alpha
satisfies
1/M^{-}\leq\alpha\leq M^{+}
. For higher reaction orders
M^{+}
and
M^{-}
, the peaks flatten out and the distribution approaches a continuous spectrum.

Discussion and outlook—

The bounds of Eq. (
8
) turn a purely structural quantity, the MAF
\alpha
, into a quantitative control parameter for balanced growth. Operationally, one can compute
\alpha
from stoichiometry
Blanco et al. 2025
;
Sargent and Stachurski 2019
;
De Martino et al. 2012
, then estimate
\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}
from input stoichiometries and rough kinetic scales, to obtain an upper bound on balanced growth.

An important observation is that, for a fixed topology, varying rate constants changes
\Lambda
continuously, within limits set by
\alpha
.
This suggests the following evolutionary scenario
for the improvement in chemical growth.
One route is kinetic tuning under fixed stoichiometric constraints, which moves
\Lambda
within the admissible interval while the MAF
\alpha
remains unchanged. The large degeneracy of networks observed for certain MAF values suggest the possibility of neutral evolution at fixed MAF value.

The second route is structural modification (adding/removing reactions, merging motifs, rewiring autocatalytic components), which changes
\alpha
itself and thereby shifts the admissible bound.
In evolutionary terms, this means a punctuated equilibrium with topological network changes playing the role of mutations that alter the admissible growth bound, while subsequent kinetic adaptation and neutral evolution adjust
\Lambda
within the available range
Qian and Bishop 2010
;
Solé et al. 2024
;
Jain and Krishna 2001
.

Beyond this scenario, our result has also practical implications for open chemical reactors because
in a continuously stirred reactor with dilution rate
d
, the population scale following a possible transient satisfies
\dot{\Omega}=(\Lambda-d)\,\Omega
.
Then, sustained growth requires
\Lambda>d
, therefore
\Lambda
acts as a dilution threshold above which the system is washed out.
Combined with Eq. (
8
), this yields a necessary structural condition for persistence against dilution:
even with optimal kinetic tuning, survival at dilution
d
is excluded unless
\alpha>1
and
d
lies below the corresponding growth bound
Kolchinsky 2025
.

In compartmentalized systems with a steady-composition, the
growth rate
\Lambda
controls the rate of change of any extensive quantity besides that of the global population
Sughiyama et al. 2022
. The
compartment volume is one such quantity, and indeed, experiments have shown that the compartment volume can inform on the autocatalytic growth of small confined chemical species and that it represents a proxy for fitness when coupling many such compartments together
Lu et al. 2024
.

These interpretations suggest several natural extensions of the present work.
First, it opens a route towards
network engineering
: using only knowledge of stoichiometry, one could design networks, that could potentially drive growth above a target threshold, or induce a robust decay for
\alpha\leq 1
(robust decay).
Pursuing this program requires composition rules for
\alpha
: which elementary network operations (adding a reaction, merging species, etc.) increase or decrease
\alpha

Sarkar and England 2019
;
Despons et al. 2025
, and by how much? For related results in the economics literature, see
Bapat et al. 1995
.
Such a set of design rules would make
\alpha
predictive under network modification and enable sharp statements about minimal structural changes needed to shift admissible growth bounds.

A second direction is to close the gap between topological capability (
\alpha
) and dynamical realization (the steady flux).
Identifying sufficient dynamical conditions that force the steady flux to approach MAF-optimal patterns would turn the universal bound into a quantitative estimate for restricted kinetic classes and clarify which motifs control performance. At the same time, an improved understanding of the connection between the stoichiometric description of CRNs and thermodynamics
Despons et al. 2024
could uncover functional organization principles in CRNs energetics. While
\alpha
is purely stoichiometric, sustained growth in open chemistry requires chemical work supplied by chemostats
Liang et al. 2024
;
Gagrani et al. 2025
;
Marehalli Srinivas et al. 2024
.
A natural extension is therefore to connect growth bounds to thermodynamic budgets stated in terms of chemostat chemical potentials, affinities, or dissipation rates.

Finally, scalable balanced growth is an asymptotic statement.
In finite populations, demographic noise and rare-event switching can induce extinctions or transitions between composition attractors even when a stable
\mathbf{q}^{*}
exists deterministically
Singh and Jain 2023
;
Lazarescu et al. 2019
.
Extending the framework to stochastic scalable kinetics
would quantify the reliability of a topologically permitted growth bounds and provide risk-aware structural criteria, such as expected persistence times under fluctuations.

3
that

\alpha\;=\;\max_{\mathbf{x}>\bm{0}}\;\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}}.

(S8)

Since, by property
(A2)
, every species is produced by at least one reaction, there exists a feasible flux
\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}
such that the numerator
\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}\gg\mathbf{0}
. Thus, for such a flux,
(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}/(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}>0
for every
s
and, in particular, the minimum over all species is greater than zero. Furthermore, by property
(A3)
, every reaction consumes and thus, for the same
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}
(as not all elements
(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}
can be simultaneously zero). Thus, there must exist at least one species
s
for which
(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}/(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}<\infty
which implies that minimum over all species must also be less than infinity. Since
\alpha
is the maximum over such values, it must also satisfy
0<\alpha<\infty
.

B3. MAF of reducible networks

Von Neumann in
Neumann 1945
and
Theorem 9.10 in
Gale 1989
prove that there exists a unique MAF for irreducible semi-autonomous networks. Since autonomous networks are also semi-autonomous, irreducible autonomous networks have a unique MAF.

We now turn to the case of reducible networks. If the network decomposes into disjoint irreducible components that do not exchange mass—equivalently, if
(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})
can be brought into block-triangular form after a suitable permutation—then the maximum amplification factor is simply the maximum of the MAFs of the individual irreducible blocks.

More generally, a reducible network may consist of an irreducible core whose species feed one or more downstream subnetworks. In this case, any flux through downstream reactions necessarily increases the consumption of species in the core without providing compensating production within it. As a result, activating downstream subnetworks cannot increase the uniform production-to-consumption ratio across species, and can only reduce the achievable amplification factor. Optimal fluxes therefore concentrate on the dominant irreducible component.

In this sense,
\alpha
functions as a “best-module” structural score and is determined solely by the irreducible component with the highest MAF.

B4. MAF of minimal networks

Observe from the second formulation of the MAF (Equation
S7
) that the optimal solution
\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}
for a CRN
(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})
satisfies

\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}\;\geq\;\alpha\,\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x}.

(S9)

While, in general, the above equation only holds as an inequality, the equality must be satisfied for some species and reactions otherwise
\alpha
would not be the maximum value that satisfies the inequality. Given the subnetwork
(\underline{\mathbb{S}}^{+},\underline{\mathbb{S}}^{-})
and
\underline{\mathbf{x}}
, we obtain the following
generalized eigenvalue problem
:

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbb{S}}^{+}\underline{\mathbf{x}}=\alpha\,\underline{\mathbb{S}}^{-}\underline{\mathbf{x}},

(S10)

with the constraint
\underline{\mathbf{x}}>\mathbf{0}
.
The relation between solutions of Eq. (
S7
) and the generalized eigenvalue problem is studied in Ref.
Thompson and Weil 1971
.

An autonomous network is
minimal
if there is no subnetwork that is also autonomous. Since there is no species that is never consumed, such a network must also be irreducible. It has been shown that minimal autonomous networks have an equal number of species and reactions
Gagrani et al. 2024
;
Blokhuis et al. 2020
;
Vassena and Stadler 2024
, and that their input matrix can be rearranged into a diagonal form. Clearly, then, the network is irreducible and
\mathbb{S}^{-}
is non-singular. Using the results in
Thompson and Weil 1971
, the problem of finding MAF reduces to the generalized eigenvalue problem of Eq. (
S10
), which further reduces to the standard eigenvalue problem

(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}=\alpha\,\mathbf{x},

(S11)

where
\alpha
is the highest eigenvalue of
(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{+}
with a corresponding nonnegative Perron-Frobenius eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
.

C. Composition dynamics

For species population
\mathbf{n}
, relative weight vector
\mathbf{w}>\mathbf{0}
, and scale
\Omega=\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{n}
, we define the relative population vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\Omega}=\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{n}}.

(S12)

Consider a scalable dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{n}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{n}(t)),

(S13)

that satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{n})}{\Omega}=\frac{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}\Omega)}{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}),

(S14)

Taking a time derivative of
\Omega
and using the change of coordinates readily yields

\dot{\Omega}=\Omega\,\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}).

(S15)

Substituting
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{q}\Omega
and using the chain-rule in Equation
S13
yields the composition equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})-(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}))\mathbf{q}.

(S16)

The fixed point of the above equation yields the relation stipulated in Equation
5
of the main text.

D. Bounds on the dynamic growth rate

D1. Assumptions

We assume an autonomous network under mass–action kinetics Eq. (
6
) with positive rate constants and the existence of at least one stable fixed point of Equation
S16
in the positive orthant
\mathbf{q}^{*}\gg\mathbf{0}
, ensuring that the growth is exponential with a rate defined by Eq. (
5
).

We also assume that the relative weight vector satisfy
\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{1}
, which simply means that the population of each species has a positive contribution in
\Omega
.
Since
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{q}=1
, it follows that
\mathbf{q}\leq 1
.

S0.2
D2. Kinetic-stoichiometric prefactor

From the form of the propensity of Eq. (
6
),

(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}))_{s}:=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}k_{r}\prod_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{q}_{t}^{(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{t}}.

(S17)

Given assumption
(A1)
, for each species
s
, there exists a reaction
r
such that
(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}\geq 1
.
For such a pair of
s
and
r
, we rewrite
\mathbf{q}^{(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}}=\mathbf{q}_{s}p_{r}^{s}(\mathbf{q})
, where
p_{r}^{s}(\mathbf{q})
is a monomial of the concentration coordinates. This yields,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}))_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}_{s}\sum_{r:(\mathbb{S}^{-})_{s}^{r}>0}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}k_{r}p^{s}_{r}(\mathbf{q}).

(S18)

Since all the concentrations are upper bounded by 1, for every
r
,
p^{s}_{r}(\mathbf{q})\leq 1
. Putting these together, we get

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}))_{s}\leq\mathbf{q}_{s}\sum_{r}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}k_{r}\leq\mathbf{q}_{s}\max_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{r}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{t}k_{r}\right).

(S19)

Using

\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\;:=\;\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\;\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}\,k_{r},

(S20)

we obtain the inequality

\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q})\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\phantom{\cdot}\mathbf{q}.

(S21)

S0.3
D3. Universal lower bound

By Equation
5
,
\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})=\Lambda\mathbf{q}^{*}
, for every species
s

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{(\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{\mathbf{q}^{*}_{s}}=\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}-(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{\mathbf{q}^{*}_{s}}.

(S22)

Since
\mathbf{q}^{*}
is in the positive-orthant,
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\gg\mathbf{0}
. Furthermore, by assumptions
(A2)
and
(A4)
, every reaction produces and every species is produced by some reaction, yielding
\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\gg\mathbf{0}
.
This means that

\displaystyle\Lambda>-\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{\mathbf{q}^{*}_{s}}\geq-\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k},

(S23)

where Eq. (
S21
) is used in the last line.

D4.
\alpha\leq 1
implies
\Lambda\leq 0

From the definitions of MAF, Equations
2
and
3
in the main text, we obtain the following implication and its contrapositive:

\displaystyle\exists\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}:

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x}\gg\mathbf{0}\implies 1<\alpha(\mathbf{x})\leq\alpha,

\displaystyle\nexists\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}:

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x}\gg\mathbf{0}\impliedby\alpha(\mathbf{x})\leq\alpha\leq 1.

(S24)

Since
\Lambda
is of the form
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x}
, we can see from the second line in Equation
S24
that
\alpha\leq 1\implies\Lambda\leq 0
, i.e. if the MAF of a network is not greater than one, the network must either be steady or shrinking.

D5. Upper bound on growing networks

For growing networks, for which
\Lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{\mathbf{q}^{*}_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{\left(\mathbb{S}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\right)_{s}}{\left(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\right)_{s}},

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\max_{\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\left(\max_{\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\left(\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})-1\right),

(S25)

where in the second line
\Lambda>0
and Equation
S21
are used, and the definition of MAF (Eq.
3
) is used to obtain the final line. Observe that, by assumptions
(A1)
and
(A3)
, every reaction consumes and every species is consumed by some reaction, yielding
\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}^{*})\gg\mathbf{0}
and a positive denominator for every
s
.

D6. Non-universal lower bound

For shrinking networks, for which
\Lambda<0
, then:

(\mathbb{S}^{+}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}<(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}.

(S26)

We also have that
\Lambda
can be expressed as:

\Lambda=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{q_{s}^{*}}=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{q_{s}^{*}}-\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{q_{s}^{*}}\right).

(S27)

Using the definition of the MAF
\alpha
, for the flux vector
\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*})
, we have:

\alpha\geq\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}=\alpha(\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*})).

(S28)

Defining a positive quantity

\epsilon:=\alpha-\alpha(\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*})),

(S29)

for each species
s
, we can write:

\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}\geq\alpha-\epsilon.

(S30)

Note that
\epsilon
tends to zero as
\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*})
as approaches the optimal flux vector.

Using the above inequality, we can write:

\Lambda\geq\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left((\alpha-\epsilon-1)\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}}{q_{s}^{*}}\right).

(S31)

Now using the inequality of Eq. (
S21
), namely
(\mathbb{S}^{-}\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*}))_{s}/q_{s}^{*}\leq\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}
, we have:

\Lambda\geq(\alpha-\epsilon-1)\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}.

(S32)

For a majority of random networks, we empirically find

\Lambda\geq(\alpha-1)\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}.

(S33)

This holds when
\jmath(\mathbf{q}^{*})
is sufficiently close to the optimal flux vector,
so that
\epsilon
is negligible. However, there are some networks, especially those for which
\alpha-1
is small, for which the above bound is broken. In other words, this lower bound is not universal. The networks that break the bound correspond to the outliers visible in Fig.
2
.

E. Examples and numerical tests

S0.4
E1. Unimolecular inputs

From the discussion in Section B3, for minimal autonomous networks with unimolecular inputs, the input matrix is the identity,
\mathbb{S}^{-}=\mathbb{I}
, and the generalized eigenvalue problem reduces to

\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}=\alpha\mathbf{x},

where
\alpha
is the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of
\mathbb{S}^{+}
. Furthermore, the same eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
satisfies

\mathbb{S}\mathbf{x}=(\mathbb{S}^{+}-\mathbb{I})\mathbf{x}=(\alpha-1)\mathbf{x},

(S34)

from which we see that
(\alpha-1)
is the largest eigenvalue of the stoichiometric matrix
\mathbb{S}
.

Moreover, from Equation
7
,

\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}=\max_{r}k_{r},

(S35)

so the kinetic–stoichiometric prefactor reduces to the maximum rate constant. Thus, in the case where all rate constants are equal,
k_{r}=k\ \forall r\in\mathcal{R}
, the inequality becomes an equality,

\Lambda=k(\alpha-1),

(S36)

and the fixed point
\mathbf{q}^{*}
is given by the Perron–Frobenius eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbb{S}
, normalized such that
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{x}=1
.

S0.5
E2. Worked example

Figure S1
:
The dynamical growth rate
\Lambda
is shown for different choices of the rate constants, together with an overlay of the quantity
\|\mathbb{S}^{-}\|_{k}\bigl(\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})-1\bigr)
for the type 1 autocatalytic core. In all cases, this quantity provides an upper bound on the growth rate
\Lambda
, and the bound is saturated when
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
.

Consider the CRN

r_{1}:\text{A}\to\text{B},\qquad r_{2}:\text{B}\to 2\text{A}.

Then (ordering species as (A,B) and reactions as (
r_{1},r_{2}
)),

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{+}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{-}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(S37)

Following Eq.
3
,

\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})=\max_{\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}}\min\left(\frac{2\mathbf{x}_{2}}{\mathbf{x}_{1}},\frac{\mathbf{x}_{1}}{\mathbf{x}_{2}}\right).

(S38)

For any
\mathbf{x}_{2}
, it is clear that the max-min is attained at
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\sqrt{2}\mathbf{x}_{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathbb{S}^{+},\mathbb{S}^{-})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}=[\sqrt{2},1]^{T}.

(S39)

Observe that
\alpha=\sqrt{2}
is simply the highest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{S}^{+}
, and
(\sqrt{2}-1)
is the highest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{S}
.

Under mass-action with
\mathbf{w}=[1,1]^{T}
(so
\mathbf{q}_{A}+\mathbf{q}_{B}=1
) and
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
, the growth rate solution satisfies

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}-1,

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}+1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+1}.

(S40)

For this assignment

, and from Equation

, we have the upper bound

(S41)

.

S0.6
E3. Numerical construction of the random networks

We construct a random stoichiometric matrix
\mathbb{S}\in\mathbb{Z}^{S\times R}
, where
S
and
R
respectively denote the number of species and reactions in the network. The integers
S
and
R
are randomly drawn in the set
[2,12]
, where
12
is chosen arbitrarily to restrict the maximum system size.

The stoichiometric matrix is decomposed in the output and input matrices
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}^{+}-\mathbb{S}^{-}
. We define the product and reactant
reaction order
, respectively, as

m_{r}^{+}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{S}^{+})^{r}_{s},\quad m_{r}^{-}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{S}^{-})^{r}_{s}.

(S42)

Let us also introduce a couple of integer parameters
(M^{+},M^{-})
referred to as the maximum forward (resp. backward) reaction order such that

m_{r}^{+}\leq M^{+},\qquad m_{r}^{-}\leq M^{-}\quad\text{for all }r\in\mathcal{R}.

(S43)

For each forward reaction
r
, the product reaction order
m_{r}^{+}
is sampled uniformly in the set
[1,M^{+}]
. Conditional on this value, we construct the column
(\mathbb{S}^{+})^{r}
by repeatedly sampling a species uniformly until the first constraint of Eq. (
S42
) is satisfied. We also make sure that the maxima
(M^{+},M^{-})
are reached by some reaction. This procedure yields the output matrix
\mathbb{S}^{+}
. Analogously, we also obtain the input matrix
\mathbb{S}^{-}
. The rate constants for a realization are picked from a uniform distribution between 0 and 1, and normalized by scaling the largest rate constant to 1.

Finally, only networks satisfying the
unambiguous
and
autonomous
conditions are retained. A network is unambiguous if no reaction shares a species between its reactant and product complexes. Although the theoretical results of this work do not require unambiguity, ambiguous reactions are autonomous in the repeated species. Such reactions therefore contain an irreducible autonomous subnetwork supported entirely on that species, which can generate trivial or structurally decoupled contributions to the MAF.
By excluding these cases, we focus on networks in which amplification arises from genuine inter-species structure rather than from embedded single-species self-coupling motifs.

E4. Bounds on the MAF

By definition of the reaction order, we have

\forall\,r:\;\sum_{s}(\mathbb{S}^{\pm})_{s}^{r}\leq M^{\pm}.

(S44)

By the definition of the reaction order and the property of autonomy (every reaction consumes and produces at least one species), we obtain the right and left inequalities, respectively, in the following equation:

\forall\,r:\;1\leq\sum_{s}(\mathbb{S}^{\pm})_{s}^{r}\leq M^{\pm}.

(S45)

Consider the quantity

Q(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\sum_{s,r}(\mathbb{S}^{+})_{s}^{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}}{\sum_{s,r}(\mathbb{S}^{-})_{s}^{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}}.

(S46)

By Eq.
S45
, we have

\sum_{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}\leq\sum_{s,r}(\mathbb{S}^{\pm})_{s}^{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}\leq M^{\pm}\sum_{r}\mathbf{x}_{r},

(S47)

which implies

\frac{1}{M^{-}}\leq Q(\mathbf{x})\leq M^{+}.

(S48)

Recall that the MAF
\alpha
is defined as the maximum over
\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{0}
of the minimum ratios
(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}/(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}
.
Suppose
\alpha>M^{+}
, and there exists
\mathbf{x}
such that
\forall\,s:\,(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}/(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}>M^{+}.

Clearly,

Q(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\sum_{s}(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x})_{s}}{\sum_{s}(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x})_{s}}>M^{+},

(S49)

which would violate the right-hand side of Eq.
S48
and yield a contradiction. Thus
\alpha\leq M^{+}
.

Now suppose
\alpha<1/M^{-}
and there exists
\mathbf{x}^{*}
such that

\min_{s}\frac{(\mathbb{S}^{+}\mathbf{x}^{*})_{s}}{(\mathbb{S}^{-}\mathbf{x}^{*})_{s}}<\frac{1}{M^{-}}.

(S50)

Following the earlier discussion around Eq.
S10
of the solution for
\alpha
, there is a set of species
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\geq 1
in an irreducible subnetwork of the CRN such that the minimum is attained simultaneously. Defining

Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime},r}(\mathbb{S}^{+})_{s}^{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime},r}(\mathbb{S}^{-})_{s}^{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}},

(S51)

and using the same arguments as before, we see that
\forall\,\mathbf{x}:\,1/M^{-}\leq Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
. However, if
\alpha<1/M^{-}
, then
Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{*})<1/M^{-}
, which violates the inequality for
Q^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
. Again we obtain a contradiction, and thus
\alpha\geq 1/M^{-}
.

Putting all the previously stated results together, we obtain

\frac{1}{M^{-}}\leq\alpha\leq M^{+}.

(S52)

In what follows, we present examples of networks that saturate these bounds. For ambiguous networks with
|\mathcal{S}|=1
, the lower and upper bounds are saturated by the following networks, respectively:

\{(M^{-})X\to X\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{X\to(M^{+})X\}.

(S53)

An unambiguous network with
M^{+}=2
and
|\mathcal{S}|=2
that saturates the upper bound is (the construction for the lower bound is analogous):

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle\to 2X_{2},

(S54)

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle\to 2X_{1}.

(S55)

Similarly, an unambiguous network with
M^{+}=2
and
|\mathcal{S}|=3
that saturates the upper bound is:

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle\to X_{2}+X_{3},

(S56)

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle\to X_{1}+X_{3},

(S57)

\displaystyle X_{3}

\displaystyle\to X_{1}+X_{2}.

(S58)

Observe that the last network is exactly the type V autocatalytic core.
