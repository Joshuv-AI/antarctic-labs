Effectiveness of computer game-based rehabilitation protocol on the health-related quality of life and glycosylated hemoglobin in elderly diabetics
Authors: Salima Mulla Bijapuri, Sanjay Tejraj Parmar, Sharmila Dudhani, Tony Szturm, Hemamalini Gururaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the greatest challenges in healthcare today and is evolving into a pandemic, due to significant comorbidity and mortality. Physical activity (PA) plays a crucial role for older adults by preventing or delaying functional and psychological decline from aging and comorbidities like diabetes, while also positively impacting their health-related quality of life (HR-QOL). Exergaming serves as an emerging form of exercise that combines technology, games, and leisure. Hence, this study determines the impact of computer game-based rehabilitation protocol (CGRP) on HR-QOL and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values in elderly diabetics.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A single-blinded randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted on 82 participants allocated into two groups (41each). Group A (control) received a conventional physiotherapy program, while Group B (experimental) underwent CGRP. Outcome measures, including HbA1c levels and HR-QOL, were assessed at baseline and after 12 weeks of rehabilitation. Within-group changes in HbA1c and World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF) domain scores were evaluated using paired t-tests, while between-group differences were analyzed with independent t-tests. A P-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.RESULTS:Significant reduction in HbA1c levels was observed in the experimental group (P < 0.00001) after the intervention, and a notable shift from poor to good diabetes control status was observed. Additionally, the QOL scores improved significantly in the experimental group (P < 0.00001).CONCLUSION:The current study demonstrated that CGRP is an effective intervention for improving glycemic control and HR-QOL in elderly individuals with T2DM. The dual-task nature of CGRP, which combines PA with cognitive engagement through interactive games, appears to offer a unique advantage over conventional interventions.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is presenting as one of the greatest challenges in healthcare today. It has evolved into a pandemic, due to significant comorbidity and mortality globally. It is projected that by 2050, nearly one fifth of India’s population will be aged 60 years or older, and a substantial portion of this group will be living with T2DM.[12] Diabetes, especially in the elderly, is often accompanied by long term complications such as peripheral neuropathy, leading to diminished sensation in the feet, impaired balance, and a heightened risk of falls. These factors collectively contribute to reduced independence and a lower HR QOL. Each year, diabetes alone causes over 1 million deaths, making it the ninth leading cause of mortality.[1] Individuals over the age of 65years experience falls at least once per year, with many of them experiencing repeated falls in future. Falls lead to some type of physical injury in 20–30% of cases, whereas 2–5% result in long hospital stay.[2] The psychological effects of falls in the elderly are often far more hazardous.[3] Consequently, elderly persons acquire a fear of falling and experience psychological distress. Furthermore, rehabilitation from fall related injuries generally necessitates a period of prolonged immobility,[4] which sets off a vicious process of greater dependency on daily activities and worsening of functional movements, impacting the quality of life.[5] Recent evidence suggests that HbA1c, the gold standard for glycemic management monitoring, can predict diabetes related complications. A 1% decrease in HbA1c reduces the risk of myocardial infarction by 14%, diabetes related mortality by 21% and microvascular complications by 37%. Maintaining HbA1c ≤7% can reduce long term complications by upto 76%.[6] Physical activity (PA) plays a crucial role for older adults by preventing or delaying functional and psychological decline caused by aging and comorbidities like diabetes, while also impacting health related quality of life (HR QOL). Despite its benefits, less than half of adults, including those with T2DM and prediabetes, engage in regular PA[7] due to factors like fatigue, access issues (e.g., rehabilitation or fitness centers), discomfort, inability to exercise outdoors because of adverse weather conditions, and other motivational and practical challenges.[8]

Conventional rehabilitation programs, although beneficial, often lack the engagement and motivation necessary for sustained participation, particularly among older adults. There is a pressing need for rehabilitation strategies that are not only physiologically effective but also cognitively stimulating and enjoyable, encouraging long term adherence. Exergaming serves as an emerging form of exercise that combines technology, games, and pleasure. In recent years, computer game based rehabilitation protocol (CGRP) utilizing commercial gaming consoles has been popular in both academic and clinical settings.[9] While video/computer games were originally designed for entertainment, there has been a shift toward creating interactive videogames, particularly for rehabilitation.[10] Theoretically, both virtual reality and CGRP have the advantage of teaching patients in goal oriented tasks, allowing for repetitive practice in an enhanced environment conducive to resolving cognitive and motor difficulties and acquiring new skills.[1011] Exergaming is a recent and widely used form of exercise that has the potential to enhance both mental and physical health. However, given how common exergames are, more research is necessary to determine how playing them affects users’ QOL in relation to their well being.[8] There is a pressing need for rehabilitation strategies that are not only physiologically effective but also cognitively stimulating and enjoyable, encouraging long term adherence.

Computer game based rehabilitation (CGBR) is an emerging, dual task approach that combines PA with interactive cognitive stimulation. In this model, patients simultaneously walk on a treadmill and engage in visually challenging games using motion sensing devices, promoting attention, coordination, and motor control. This approach aligns well with the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommendations for older adults, which emphasize atleast 150 min of moderate intensity PA per week.[3]

While CGBR has shown promising results in Western settings for conditions like stroke, Parkinson’s disease, and cerebral palsy,[45] its effectiveness among elderly Indians with T2DM remains underexplored. Given the cultural, infrastructural, and motivational differences in this population, it is critical to evaluate whether such interventions can be adapted and scaled in the Indian context. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effectiveness of a CGRP in improving glycemic control (HbA1c) and HR QOL in elderly individuals with T2DM. Figure 1 describes the theoretical framework.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A single-blinded randomized controlled trial (RCT), which is a part of the large research project, was conducted. Before participation, all individuals were provided with detailed information about the purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits of the study in a language they could understand. Informed consent was obtained in writing from each participant using a standardized consent form approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (SDMIEC: 87:2020). The form was administered and explained by the principal investigator in a one-on-one setting to ensure that participants had the opportunity to ask questions and clarify doubts. Participation was entirely voluntary, and individuals were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point without any consequence to the in routine care. Study participants and sampling is a separate subheading.

The participants of this study were community-dwelling elderly individuals aged between 60 and 75 years, diagnosed with T2DM for more than five years. Participants were recruited through screening camps and outreach activities conducted in collaboration with retired employees’ organizations and local health networks. Inclusion criteria required participants to have adequate visual and auditory function to engage with computer-based tasks, the ability to perform daily activities independently, and a mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score above 20,[12] indicating sufficient cognitive function for dual-task interventions. Individuals were excluded if they had uncontrolled hypertension, cardiopulmonary disorders, recent musculoskeletal or neurological surgeries, or active foot ulcers/cellulitis, as these conditions could interfere with exercise safety or participation.

A computer-generated random allocation sequence was prepared before the start of the study using QUICKCALCS software (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA) assigning numbers to either the CGRP group or the conventional physiotherapy group. Each group assignment was then placed inside sealed, opaque, and sequentially numbered envelopes. After agreeing to participate and obtaining assigned informed consent, participants were asked to select one envelope, which revealed their group allocation. This process ensured allocation concealment and minimized selection bias. The study followed a single-blinded design, where the outcome assessor was blinded to participant groupings throughout the intervention and analysis stages. Due to the nature of the interventions, physiotherapists and participants could not be blinded, but all assessments were standardized to ensure consistency across both groups. Participants continued the in routine medical management for T2DM throughout the study period. The Consolidated standards of reporting trials (CONSORT) flow diagram is illustrated in Figure 2.

Consolidated standards of reporting trials (CONSORT) diagram showing the flow of participants through each stage of a randomized trial

A total of 108 individuals were screened, of whom 82 met the eligibility criteria and consented to participate. These participants were then randomly assigned into two equal groups (n = 41) using a computer-generated random number list followed by an opaque envelope draw, ensuring unbiased allocation. Based on the eligibility criteria, 82 patients were recruited from camps and various retired organizations and allocated into two groups of 41 each by using a computer-generated randomization process. In Group A (control group), a detailed exercise protocol of a conventional physiotherapy program was performed, whereas in Group B (experimental group) CGRP was performed.

Data collection tool and technique

Socio-demographic information of the patients, consisting of name, age, sex, medical history, whether able to walk outdoors regularly, with or without assistive device for ambulation, self-reported history of falls, amount of home care assistance, strength and range of motion of all joints, current number of medicine prescriptions, and diabetic profile, was recorded. In Group A, the session consisted of a 5-min warm-up period, that was followed by 35 min of training program which included aerobic exercise (static bicycling), resistance and balance training consisting of five leg exercises (knee extension, knee flexion, hip adduction, hip abduction, and standing calf raise), chest press, shoulder press, abdominals and back extensors with the individualized workloads at 50–70% of 10 repetition maximum (RM) on the multiple station universal resistance training equipment (multigym). Each resistance training exercise was performed as two sets of 10–15 repetitions each. The intensity of training was set as prescribed according to the ACSM exercise prescription for the elderly guidelines.[13] If the patient comfortably lifted the weight with good form for 12 repetitions, rated 12–13 on the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale,[14] 5% was added for the next session. The session ended with 5 min of cool-down exercises. The exercise was progressed during the weeks of the training period, and the loads were 70–80% of the maximum. Balance exercises included side-leg raises, squats, standing hip extension and flexion, tandem standing, toe and heel stands, standing with eyes open and closed, and chairs it-to-stands without hand support.

Resistance training program

In Group B, initially, a 5-min warm-up period of dual-task balance exercises (standing on a sponge or balance disk) while playing simple visuo-tracking computer games was performed, followed by a training program that included treadmill walking, interspersed with rests accordingly, for a total of 35 min. A motion mouse secured to a head band enabled hands-free, responsive interaction with exercise or brain fitness games.[151617]

For each participant, various computer games focusing on goal-directed cognitive activities were selected from a pool of 20 games purchased from Big Fish Games (www.bigfishgames.com). The tasks consisted of tracking multiple targets, presence of distractors, speed accuracy requirements, visual search, working memory, and matching tasks activities. The patients were made to play the games while sitting and standing on a fixed surface before progressing with the rehabilitation. Patients walked on a level treadmill at 0.9m/s for 5 min for acclimatization. Cognitive tasks were initially performed standing on a stable surface, standing on compliant surfaces like foam and balance disk while playing simple visuo-tracking games ranging from moderate to difficult levels of visuo-cognitive games, followed by standing on a stationary treadmill, and once they developed good practice, they were made to walk on a treadmill, with support, and later without support. The goal-directed, computer-controlled movements offered an accessible way to track various cognitive events while performing complex motor tasks like walking. Participants used a standard optical mouse to play the computer game for 2 min while seated, familiarizing themselves with the cognitive task. Adequate rest was given in between the games. Participants gradually progressed through the training both physically and cognitively. Eventually, the games progressed to a difficult level, and the speed of the treadmill was increased. The treating physiotherapist adjusted the treadmill speed.[151617] The treadmill was equipped with safety side rails in easy reach. Once the patient managed to play the difficult level of games with increased speed, he/she reached the highest level of balance and gait training. The last 5 min was utilized as a cool-down period that involved treadmill walking without playing any games. During the sessions, participants were regularly asked to report their exertion levels. The therapist adjusted the workload (e.g., treadmill speed, game complexity, resistance levels) to maintain the effort within the moderate to somewhat hard range (RPE12–14), as recommended by the ACSM for elderly individuals.

Atleast one qualified and trained physiotherapist was assigned to each group, who explained to the patients about the procedure to play the games and do the other exercises, provided a demonstration, and helped patients practice and perform the training safely, whereas the principal investigator treated the patients in both groups. This CGRP tool uses an affordable, commercial motion-sensing mouse to interact and control computer games or applications. Patients attended a total of 36 scheduled sessions for 12 weeks (3 sessions per week) that were scheduled at different times of the day for both groups. Each session lasted for 45 min and included rest periods as needed. In both groups, patients were instructed to have their snacks as prescribed by the dietician 15 min before the exercise session. Vitals were tested before each exercise session. Ahead of the training, the maximal heart rate (MHR) of all the patients was calculated, and the exercise prescription for the elderly was set to moderate-vigorous of 40–60% of MHR. The target heart rate range was calculated using the Karvonen method: Target Heart Rate = [(Heart Rate max – Heart Rate rest) × (percent intensity) + Heart Rate rest].[18] The outcome measures were assessed on day one and at the end of 12 weeks of rehabilitation, which consisted of the effect on T2DM by measuring glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) through blood samples of the patients, and HR-QOL by using the World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire.[192021]

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was obtained in writing from each participant using a standardized consent form approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (SDMIEC: 87:2020).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences SPSS software (version 26.0) (IBM, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) summarized sociodemographic characteristics and outcome measures (HbA1c and WHOQOL-BREF scores) at baseline and post-intervention (12 weeks). Data normality was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Within-group changes in HbA1c and WHOQOL-BREF domain scores were evaluated using paired t-tests, while between-group differences were analyzed with independent t-tests. A P-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Both groups consisted of 24 males and 17 females, and the mean age of the patients in Group A and Group B was 65.44 ± 2.50 years and 65.59 ± 2.51 years, respectively. Additionally, the mean duration since diagnosis of T2DM was 11.15 ± 4.59 years and 11.17 ± 3.97 years, respectively. Discussion

The present study evaluated the effectiveness of a CGRP on HR-QOL and glycemic control in elderly individuals with T2DM. While both groups demonstrated improvements in QOL post-intervention, the experimental group receiving CGRP showed greater gains, particularly in the physical, psychological, and social domains of the WHOQOL-BREF. These findings suggest that the dual-task nature of CGRP, merging cognitive stimulation with PA, offers a unique advantage over conventional physiotherapy, which primarily targets physical function.

These outcomes are consistent with findings from Cacciata et al.,[8] who reported that exergaming interventions significantly improved overall QOL in older adults, particularly when the games were engaging and personalized. Similarly, Cicek et al.[22] demonstrated that interactive video games not only enhanced mobility but also improved mood and social engagement in elderly participants. The current study supports these observations, as participants in the CGRP group not only met PA guidelines but also expressed increased enjoyment and motivation during sessions, likely contributing to higher adherence.

Importantly, the social domain of QOL improved in the CGRP group. This aligns with findings from Lyubenova et al.,[23] who observed that serious games improved social participation and reduced feelings of isolation among elderly participants. These benefits are particularly meaningful in the Indian context, where elderly individuals with chronic illnesses like T2DM often face limitations in social engagement due to mobility impairments and fear of falling.

A key finding of this study was the significant improvement in the physical domain of HR-QOL in the CGRP group following 12 weeks of intervention. This domain reflects facets such as energy, mobility, activities of daily living, pain management, and sleep quality, all of which are commonly impaired in elderly individuals with T2DM due to chronic fatigue, neuropathy, and reduced physical capacity.

The dual-task nature of CGRP, combining treadmill walking with interactive visuo-cognitive games, appears to have played a vital role in enhancing physical endurance and functional mobility. Unlike conventional physiotherapy, which may become repetitive, CGRP offers goal-directed and adaptive tasks that challenge both motor and cognitive systems, potentially leading to greater neuromuscular engagement and motor relearning.

These findings are in agreement with a prior study by Gamito et al.,[24] who reported that exergame-based training improved not only muscle strength and balance but also motivation and fatigue levels, thereby improving the physical functioning of older adults.

Furthermore, the immersive and responsive environment of CGRP may reduce perceived exertion and enhance movement confidence, both of which are critical in elderly diabetics who often avoid PA due to fear of falling or fatigue. Improvement in physical function is particularly meaningful in this group, as it directly correlates with reduced dependency, better self-care, and overall independence, which are central goals in geriatric rehabilitation. Engaging in a regular PA is highly advised as it has been proven to enhance QOL and functional ability relating to health and is an effective intervention in minimizing several functional impairments associated with aging.[25] Additionally, as older adults are less inclined to participate in exercises, recommendations for older persons should place a focus on muscle-strengthening activities, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, reducing sedentary behaviour, and risk management to improve cardiovascular health and HR-QOL.[222324252627] However, in the current study, the use of CGRP could be a complementary and effective rehabilitation approach to ameliorate the social connectivity and improve the adherence to exercises and reduce healthcare costs, which can come along with uncontrolled diabetes and increased dependency in elderly people. Dual-tasking activities, such as walking and playing games simultaneously, give them a sense of accomplishment, confidence, and success as they proceed through the game’s stages at a steady pace while being vigilant and making CGBR an enjoyable and engaging activity. In practice, it has been noted that CGRP can enhance QOL; therefore, to motivate individuals to engage in health-promoting activities and enhance their QOL, CGRP may be considered in health-promotional and educational initiatives.

Moreover, the results highlighted that CGRP utilized in the experimental group is effective in improving the control status of T2DM when measured through HbA1c levels, as shown by the shift of the maximum number of patients from poor to good control with statistically significant results. The American Diabetes Association recommends HbA1c as an alternative to fasting blood glucose for diabetes diagnosis.[28] HbA1c is a key biomarker for long term glycaemic control, reflecting the glycemic history of the past 2–3 months and predicting the risk of long term diabetic complications.[28] Additionally, elevated levels of HbA1c serve as an independent risk factor for stroke and coronary heart disease in both diabetic and non diabetic individuals.[2829] Hence, the HbA1c test provides crucial information regarding diabetes related to its diagnosis and prognosis, making it a reliable biomarker.[28] It is observed that only less than half of adults, including prediabetic patients and patients with T2DM, participate in PA despite the health benefits associated with it.[27]The dual task activities, which included 45 min of treadmill walking (with appropriate rest intervals) and progressively increasing speed and inclination, were key to the significant improvements in HbA1c levels after 12 weeks. The study involved close observation for signs of hypoglycemia, continuous heart rate monitoring, and supervised clinical exercise sessions.[21] No changes were done in their regular medicines intake.

Aerobic activities are well established to enhance insulin sensitivity, improve glucose uptake by skeletal muscles, and reduce insulin resistance, thereby contributing to better glycaemic regulation. Similarly, resistance and strengthening exercises increase muscle mass and metabolic activity, which further support long-term glucose control. When performed regularly, even moderate-intensity exercise programs have been shown to produce clinically meaningful reductions in HbA1c in individuals with T2DM. While digital rehabilitation represents a promising future direction, the findings also reaffirm that conventional balance and gait exercises remain effective, especially for individuals with limited access to technology. However, such programs require longer duration, greater consistency, and sustained motivation to achieve comparable outcomes. However, there is a dearth of research regarding whether exergaming alone can provide clinically significant decreases in HbA1c and can lower the problems associated with T2DM. The results in this research showed a significant reduction in the HbA1c values in both groups after 12 weeks of rehabilitation. A recent analysis of high risk individuals for diabetes found that combining diet and PA significantly reduces or delays T2DM onset, rivalling pharmacotherapy. While metformin lowers risk by 31%, lifestyle interventions with PA and weight loss reduce it by 58%.[29] Moreover, this study reported that increased PA through CGRP resulted in similar outcomes in comparison to pharmacotherapy, without the side effects of anti diabetes medications. In terms of effectiveness, these non-pharmacological approaches perform comparably to drug-based treatments for averting diabetes onset. Hence, exergames could be a useful, appealing, and popular kind of exercise that caters to people who haven’t found conventional PA interesting.

At the end of the follow-up, dual-task activities involving treadmill walking and playing games had a lasting and positive effect. Group exercises enhanced social functions by encouraging communication among patients. Both groups showed a positive effect, with the CGRP group experiencing a more significant improvement, which was linked to increased confidence, range of motion, coordination, and balance. The choice of PA depends on personal preference, but CGBR is an excellent option as it involves intentional movements and allows for increasing difficulty levels to keep the activities challenging and adapted to the player’s abilities, allowing them to be suitable for long-term use at home.[30] Hence, this approach is indeed crucial for promoting active longevity in the Indian elderly population.

Conclusion

The current study demonstrated that CGRP is an effective intervention for improving HR-QOL and glycemic control (HbA1c) in elderly individuals with T2DM. CGRP led to a substantial shift in the HbA1c status of participants, with a greater number achieving good glycemic control post-intervention. Moreover, CGRP showed significant enhancements in the HR-QOL in comparison to the control group receiving conventional physiotherapy. The dual-task nature of CGRP, which combines PA with cognitive engagement through interactive games, appears to offer a unique advantage. This approach not only improved physical health outcomes, such as balance and coordination, but also enhanced mental well-being and social connectivity. The results suggest that incorporating CGRP into rehabilitation programs could provide a novel and enjoyable method to enhance adherence to PA, potentially reducing the long-term complications associated with diabetes and promoting overall QOL among the elderly.

The study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, limiting the generalizability of findings to other populations and settings. Participants required additional training to understand and operate the computer-based games, which may have influenced engagement and performance. The motivational effect and novelty of the games may have temporarily enhanced QOL, requiring further exploration to differentiate between true therapeutic effects and placebo-like engagement.

Future research should investigate the long-term effects of CGRP on diabetes management and explore its potential integration into standard care practices for elderly diabetic populations. Additionally, examining the scalability and feasibility of implementing such technology-driven interventions in various healthcare settings could provide further insights into their widespread applicability.
