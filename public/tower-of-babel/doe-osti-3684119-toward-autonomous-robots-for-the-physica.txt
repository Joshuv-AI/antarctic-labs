Abstract
Laboratory automation is challenging because lab environments are less structured and less
replicable than traditional factory environments. In manufacturing, robots often operate in low-
mix, high-volume settings where parts, fixtures, lighting, tools, and motions are standardized.
This makes fixed automation practical because the same task is repeated many times under
controlled conditions. Physical laboratory work is different. Labs are often high-mix, low-volume
environments where tasks change, setups are reconfigured, objects are small, and the number
of repetitions for any one task may be limited. A vial shifted by only a few millimeters or a holder
placed slightly differently, can compound into the stoppage of a costly autonomous workflow.
 This project explored whether learned closed-loop policies can improve robustness for
physical lab manipulation. Instead of relying only on open-loop record/replay, where the robot
repeats a fixed motion, we trained policies that react to live camera observations and robot
state. The work first used a simpler cube task to validate the training and rollout pipeline, then
applied the same closed-loop imitation learning approach to a lab-relevant vial handling task:

SO-101 Vial Handling Work Cell
We built an SO-101 work cell for evaluating closed-loop policies on a lab-relevant precision
handling task. The setup included an SO-101 follower arm, two RGB cameras, a vial staging
area, a vial holder, and a physical testbed with repeatable start and target locations. The robot
state and action spaces contained six values: five arm joints and the gripper position. The main
hardware setup is shown in Fig. 4A, and the leader/follower teleoperation arrangement used for
demonstration collection is shown in Fig. 1B.
 The cameras were important because the policy could only react to what it could
observe. One view (camera 1: top camera) captured the broader workspace layout, while
another view (camera 2: attached to robot) helped capture the grasp and insertion region.
During rollout, the policy used live camera frames and robot state to decide the next action,
rather than assuming the object and robot were always in the exact same position.

Custom Cylindrical Fit End Effector
The physical gripper design was a major component of making the vial task reliable. The
original gripper was modified with a custom cylindrical fit end effector designed around the vial
geometry, shown in Fig. 1A. The purpose of the attachment was not just to grip harder, but to
make the vial sit in a more repeatable position as the gripper closed.
 This mattered because the insertion phase was highly sensitive to alignment. If the vial
shifted or rotated differently inside the gripper from trial to trial, the learned policy had to
compensate for additional variation. By centering and stabilizing the vial mechanically, the
gripper reduced variation before insertion and made the policy's learning problem easier. This
was an important lesson from the project: reliable robot learning depends on both the model and
the physical design of the system.

Record, Audit, Train, Rollout, Evaluate
The project followed a record -> audit -> train -> rollout -> evaluate workflow. First, we recorded
human demonstration episodes through teleoperation. Each episode represented one complete
task attempt and stored synchronized camera frames, robot joint and gripper state,
demonstrated actions, timestamps, and task information. The demonstration loop is shown in
Fig. 2A.
 After recording, we audited the dataset before training. The audit step checked
metadata, episode counts, frame counts, camera streams, task labels, video layout, and
whether the dataset structure was consistent. This step was important because training on a
broken or inconsistent dataset can lead to wasted training time or misleading results.
 Training used many demonstration episodes so the policy could learn the relationship
between observations and actions. Rollout was the real test: the trained policy controlled the
physical robot from live observations. Finally, evaluation used repeated physical trials from fixed

Cube Validation Round
Before moving to vial manipulation, we used a 3D printed cube to isolate one key variable:
whether the learned policy could handle changes in object starting position. By using a simpler
object with consistent geometry, this round reduced the effects of grasp complexity, vial shape,
and insertion difficulty, allowing us to focus specifically on closed-loop adaptation to different
start locations. The conceptual cube setup and fixed testing locations are shown in Fig. 3B, and
the physical cube setup is shown in Fig. 3A.
 The cube ACT (Action Chunking Transformers) policy was trained on demonstration
episodes and evaluated across fixed physical start positions. Cube ACT succeeded in 26 out of
36 trials, or 72.2%, as shown in Fig. 3C. This result showed that a learned closed-loop policy
could use visual feedback and robot state to respond to object-position variation more
effectively than an open-loop replay script. By isolating starting-location variation in a simpler
task, the cube round validated the training and rollout workflow before applying it to the more
lab-relevant vial task.

Vial ACT: Applying Closed-Loop Control to a Lab Task
After the cube task validated the pipeline, we moved directly to vial handling. Vial handling had
greater relevance to laboratory automation because it involved a small cylindrical object, a
physical holder, and precision placement. The cylindrical shape reduced orientation sensitivity
compared with a cube, but the holder tolerance made accurate placement critical. The final vial
setup is shown in Fig. 4A.
 For the vial ACT experiment, we collected 175 vial demonstration episodes containing
51,106 frames. The ACT policy was trained on targeted vial demonstrations with consistent
camera views, task setup, and the custom gripper attachment. During rollout, ACT used live
observations to adjust the approach, grasp, transfer, and placement motion.
 The vial ACT policy achieved the strongest result of the project: 34 successful physical
rollouts out of 36 trials, or 94.4% accuracy, as shown in Fig. 4B. A trial counted as successful
only if the robot grasped the vial, moved it, and placed it into the holder without manual
correction. This result showed that closed-loop imitation learning could extend from a simple
validation task to a lab-relevant precision handling task.

 The vial ACT result also showed the value of collecting more targeted training data and
improving the physical setup. The custom gripper and larger task-specific dataset produced
cleaner grasps and insertions than earlier smaller-dataset attempts. While task differences also
mattered, the result supported the practical lesson that high-quality, task-specific
demonstrations generally improve closed-loop policy reliability.

Vial SmolVLA: Testing a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Policy
We also evaluated SmolVLA, a vision-language-action policy, on the vial task. The motivation
for testing SmolVLA was different from ACT. ACT is a strong practical baseline for focused
single-task imitation, while VLA models are more forward-looking for flexible lab automation
because they can condition on task or instruction text in addition to images and robot state.
 SmolVLA was trained and evaluated using the same general vial handling workflow. The
VLA policy achieved 31 successful physical rollouts out of 36 trials, or 91.1% accuracy, as
shown in Fig. 4B. This showed that a vision-language-action policy could be successfully
implemented on the SO-101 vial task. However, ACT remained slightly stronger for this focused
single-task evaluation.
 The VLA result remained important because it demonstrated a path toward more general
policies. Although ACT performed slightly better on the focused vial-placement task, SmolVLA
still achieved a high success rate while using a vision-language-action architecture that can
condition on task instructions as well as images and robot state. This showed that the SO-101
workflow was not limited to a single-task ACT policy; it could also support a policy class
designed for broader, instruction-conditioned behavior. For future lab automation settings,
where a robot may need to handle different objects, changing procedures, and varied task
descriptions, VLA models are better aligned with the long-term goal. The tradeoff observed
during this project was that VLA policies require more compute and are more sensitive to real-
time inference speed and camera latency, making ACT the stronger practical choice for this
specific single-task setup.

ACT vs VLA
ACT and VLA policies both output robot actions, but they have different strengths. ACT uses
camera images and robot state to predict actions. It is fast, stable, lower compute, and well
suited for one well-defined task when high-quality demonstrations are available. In this project,
ACT provided the best practical result for single-task vial placement, as shown by the 94.4% vial
success rate in Fig. 4B.
 VLA policies use camera images, robot state, and task or instruction text. This makes
them more flexible and better suited for varied tasks, objects, or language-conditioned behavior.
The tradeoff is that VLA models are heavier, slower to run, and more dependent on low-latency
sensing and inference. In this project, SmolVLA was a useful comparison and showed strong
promise, but ACT was the most reliable policy for the specific vial task.
 The important conclusion is not that VLA should replace ACT, or that ACT should
replace VLA. Instead, the results show that the best policy depends on the circumstance. ACT
is a strong choice when the task is focused, demonstrations are high quality, and efficient rollout
matters. VLA policies may be better suited for future settings where the robot must follow
different instructions, handle multiple objects, or generalize across a wider range of laboratory
tasks.

Camera Placement Lessons
Camera placement had a major effect on policy performance. A top-down camera view was
useful for seeing the overall workspace position, but it often lost important depth and contact
cues. During grasping or insertion, the robot arm or gripper could also occlude the object from
an overhead view.

 I reached the 45-degree camera conclusion by comparing policy behavior under similar
training conditions. With the oblique camera view, the robot’s grasping and insertion motions
appeared noticeably more confident and accurate. The object, gripper, and holder stayed visible
through more of the task, and the policy seemed to receive better visual information about
height, tilt, contact, and alignment. This made the 45-degree view more useful for manipulation
than a purely top-down view. A practical rule from the project is that if the camera view makes
the task hard for a human to teleoperate reliably, it will also be hard for an imitation policy.

Conclusions and Next Steps
This project built and evaluated a closed-loop SO-101 vial handling testbed with two RGB
cameras and a custom gripper attachment. Record/replay was brittle because it repeated fixed
motions without feedback. Learned policies improved robustness because they reacted to live
camera observations and robot state. The workflow shown in Fig. 2 connected demonstration
collection, dataset auditing, policy training, rollout, and evaluation into one complete process.
 The strongest result was the vial ACT policy, which achieved 34 out of 36 successful
physical rollouts, or 94.4%, on fixed-start evaluation. The SmolVLA policy also performed well,
reaching 31 out of 36 successful rollouts, or 91.1%. These results are summarized in Fig. 4B.
 The main takeaway is that reliable robot learning requires more than choosing a model.
Dataset quality, camera placement, physical gripper design, calibration, and real-time rollout
speed all affected performance. The custom gripper in Fig. 1A made the vial grasp more
repeatable, the workflow in Fig. 2 helped prevent poor datasets from entering training, the cube
result in Fig. 3C validated the closed-loop approach, and the vial results in Fig. 4B showed that
the method could succeed on a lab-relevant manipulation task.
 For future work, stronger GPU hardware and lower-latency camera and inference
pipelines would allow faster training, more reliable rollout, and a fairer evaluation of VLA models
for broader lab automation tasks. Additional targeted demonstrations could also improve
recovery from known failure modes and expand the system toward more flexible multi-object or
instruction-conditioned laboratory workflows.
