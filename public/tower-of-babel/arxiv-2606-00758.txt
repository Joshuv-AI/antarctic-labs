Abstract
In recent years, graph signal processing has emerged as a powerful framework at the intersection of signal processing and graph theory, providing tools for the analysis of signals defined on nodes while accounting for their relationships represented by edges. These tools have been successfully applied to various settings, including statistical hypothesis testing. In particular, non-parametric approaches based on surrogate generation have been proposed for signals on undirected graphs. However, they are yet to be extended to directed graphs. In this work, we first revisit the notion of stationary graph signals on directed graphs. Specifically, and through the eigendecomposition of the graph shift operator, we define directed graph wide-sense stationary signals. Then, we propose a new framework to generate surrogate graph signals that preserve covariance structure under stationarity assumptions. Null distributions of the test metric can then be constructed from these surrogates and serve as a reference for the empirical data. Finally, we provide guiding examples and an application on real data, in which we compare the performance of our framework with existing techniques for undirected graphs or based on naive permutation, demonstrating feasibility and superiority of the proposed approach.



I
Introduction

Graph signal processing (GSP) has emerged as a compelling methodology for managing data expressed on structured domains; e.g., in the context of social, biological, technological networks. GSP considers graphs and graph signals in a common framework to revisit classical signal processing operations
[
36
,
24
,
22
]
. In the probabilistic setting, where graph signals are considered as realizations of random processes, GSP is essentially limited to undirected graphs, which motivates this work to provide a framework to enable statistical hypothesis testing on directed graphs. We emphasize that, in contrast to the extensive body of work on directionality inference for causal graph discovery
[
9
,
28
,
35
,
1
]
, the present study addresses statistical hypothesis testing for assessing signals observed on a predefined graph. Several differences also exist in the type of graphs that each framework considers.

In conventional signal processing, a random process in the time domain is said to be weakly stationary when the first two moments, mean and autocorrelation, do not vary with respect to time. Equivalently, its covariance matrix is diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) basis
[
5
]
. The latter concept has been used for defining graph wide-sense stationarity (GWSS) for undirected graphs
[
14
,
26
,
21
]
; i.e., a random process on the graph is GWSS if and only if its covariance matrix is diagonalized by the eigenvectors of the graph shift operator (GSO), commonly the adjacency matrix or the graph Laplacian, that can be considered as the graph Fourier transform (GFT). Stationarity of graph signals has been leveraged in a variety of applications, notably Wiener-based graph signal denoising, the forecasting of graph signal timeseries and graph structure learning
[
8
,
16
,
11
,
6
]
. For non-parametric statistical testing, under the stationarity assumption, we proposed a scheme to generate surrogate graph signals
[
27
]
based on sign randomization of the GFT coefficients. For a chosen test metric, the value from the empirical data can then be compared against the null distribution obtained from surrogates.

Recently, the notion of stationarity has been extended to directed graphs
[
15
]
leading to directed GWSS (DGWSS). Here, the covariance matrix can be expressed as a left–right product of a block-diagonal matrix by the generalized eigenvector matrix of the GSO and its Hermitian transpose. However, this formulation relies on the Jordan Normal Form (JNF) of the GSO, which presents two major limitations: the JNF is numerically unstable and computationally inefficient. Yet, most graphs are strongly connected and thus come with a diagonalizable directed GSO
[
33
]
, and, if not, they can be made so by a minimal perturbation
[
32
]
. This provides a complex-valued eigenbasis, but that is not necessarily orthogonal. This allows to obtain meaningful phase information that can be leveraged for operations such as the graph Hilbert transform
[
7
,
43
]
. Surrogate generation on directed graphs has not yet been addressed, except for the special case of complex-valued Hermitian graphs
[
2
]
.

In this work, we first revisit the notion of directed graph wide sense stationarity (DGWSS) through the eigendecomposition of the GSO and extend properties from GWSS to DGWSS. Next, we study white noise (WN) on the graph, a particular type of stationary signal with flat power spectral density, highlighting some striking differences between WN on undirected and directed graphs. We then present the framework for non-parametric testing, leveraging our proposed surrogate generation scheme. Throughout all experiments, we will compare our framework against surrogates generated on the undirected graph, and surrogates from naive permutations, demonstrating that directionality is meaningful and useful to be accounted for. We also show results for experimental data from temperature measurements on a sensor network.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
III
, we introduce the directed graph signal processing framework. Then, in Sect.
IV
, we revisit the definition and properties of GWSS and its extension to directed graphs. We focus in Sect.
V
on white noise for directed graphs and discuss its differences with the undirected setting. Next, in Sect.
VI
, we present the proposed non-parametric testing framework together with the surrogate generation scheme. Numerical experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets are presented in Sect.
VII
, including temperature data on a sensor network. Finally, we conclude the paper and outline future research directions.

Graph Fourier Transform

III-A

Undirected Graph

Consider an undirected graph with
N
nodes represented by its symmetric adjacency matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}

\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\bar{{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T},

(1)

Applying the GFT brings the initial graph signal into the spectral domain, where spectral filtering can then be performed as the multiplication by a diagonal matrix

{\mathbf{y}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\bar{{\mathbf{G}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{\mathbf{x}}.

(2)

{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{J}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}

Stationarity of graph signals has been defined and studied on multiple occasions

13

IV-A

Undirected Graph Stationary

Let
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a random multivariate variable that we further on refer to as a random graph signal. We can then define the first- and second-order statistical moments of
{\mathbf{x}}
as the mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}[n]]
and the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}=\mathbb{E}[({{\mathbf{x}}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\mathbf{x}}})({{\mathbf{x}}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\mathbf{x}}})^{H}]
, respectively. Without loss of generality, we assume throughout the paper that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{0}}
. While
[
26
]
defines GWSS of
{\mathbf{x}}
through conditions on mean and covariance, they also provide the following equivalent definition.

Definition 1

(GWSS)
.

A random graph signal
{\mathbf{x}}
is GWSS on an undirected graph if and only if there exists
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
such that its covariance matrix can be factorized as

{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T},

(8)

where
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is diagonal. In particular, the graph power spectral density (PSD) of
{\mathbf{x}}
is defined as
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{h}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{h}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n]=\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n,n],

diagonal entries of
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
.

Remark
.

Furthermore, by rewriting Eq. (
1
), we obtain

\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}^{H}]

(9)

being the graph spectral covariance matrix. Now since
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is diagonal, the spectral components of
{\mathbf{x}}
are uncorrelated. When
{\mathbf{x}}
is not stationary,
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is still the graph spectral covariance, however, it is not diagonal anymore.

Given that the diagonal structure of
\hat{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is a key property of a GWSS. Characterizing how diagonal the graph spectral covariance matrix is, can be used to characterize the stationarity of the random variable. Following this, we define the “stationary level”
[
26
]
.

Definition 2

(GWSS Stationary Level)
.

For a random variable
{\mathbf{x}}
, its covariance matrix
{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, the associated graph spectral covariance matrix
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
and the PSD
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{h}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, the stationary level of
{\mathbf{x}}
is defined as

\bar{\kappa}({{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{||\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{h}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}}{||\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}||_{F}}.

(10)

In practice, we propose estimators for the covariance matrix and the graph spectral covariance matrix as follows. Given
K
realizations of the random graph signal
{\mathbf{x}}
, denoted as
{\mathbf{m}}_{k},k=1,\ldots,K
, we define the empirical covariance matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}
as

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{H},

(11)

and the empirical graph spectral covariance matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{m}}
as

\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{H}{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}\hat{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{H},

(12)

with
\hat{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}
being the GFT of
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}
. It follows that the empirical PSD is defined as
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{h}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}[k]=\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}[k,k]
. Furthermore , we obtain the empirical stationary level
\bar{\kappa}({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}})
by replacing
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
in Definition
2
with their empirical counterparts
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}
and
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}
, respectively.

IV-B

Directed Graph Stationary

We first remind the notion of GWSS processes on directed graph introduced by
[
15
]
. Then, we revisit the concept through the eigendecomposition of the graph shift operator instead of the JNF. We provide the definitions and properties of stationarity to directed graphs.

Definition 3

(GWSS - Jordan Decomposition)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{J}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}
be the Jordan Decomposition of the adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
. The authors of
[
15
]
state that a random graph signal is GWSS for the JNF of
{\mathbf{A}}
if and only if there is a Jordan block diagonal matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{{\mathbf{J}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
that relates to the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
as

{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{V}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}^{\raisebox{-2.29053pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbf{J}}$}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{V}}^{H}.

(13)

Reformulating this definition for
{\mathbf{A}}
diagonalizable leads to the generalized eigenvectors being replaced with the proper eigenvectors, and the block diagonal matrix turns into a regular diagonal matrix, which brings us to the following definition of DGWSS.

Definition 4

(DGWSS)
.

A random graph signal is Directed GWSS (DGWSS) if and only if there exists a diagonal matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
such that its covariance matrix can be expanded into:

{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{U}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}.

(14)

The diagonal entries of
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
contain the graph PSD
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n]=\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n,n].

(15)

Since
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is diagonal, the spectral components of
{\mathbf{x}}
are uncorrelated.

Given the definition of DGWSS, we revisit the following property of interest, enunciated by Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a spectral filter and
{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
a DGWSS random graph signal. The filtered random variable
{\mathbf{y}}
by the spectral filter
{\mathbf{G}}
remains DGWSS and its PSD is given by

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}[n]=|{\mathbf{G}}[n,n]|^{2}\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}[n].

Proof.

Given that
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}^{H}]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{H}]\,{\mathbf{U}}^{-H}{\mathbf{G}}^{H}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{U}}\Bigl({\mathbf{G}}\underbrace{{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-H}}_{=\;\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}}{\mathbf{G}}^{H}\Bigr){\mathbf{U}}^{H}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{U}}\bigl({\mathbf{G}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{G}}^{H}\bigr){\mathbf{U}}^{H}={\mathbf{U}}\bigl(|{\mathbf{G}}|^{2}\,\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\bigr){\mathbf{U}}^{H},

which shows that
{\mathbf{y}}
is DGWSS and yields the PSD.
∎

Similar to Definition
2
, we give the definition of stationarity level for DGWSS.

Definition 5

(DGWSS Stationary Level)
.

For a random variable
{\mathbf{x}}
, its covariance matrix
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, its associated graph spectral covariance matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
and the associated PSD
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, the stationary level is defined as

\kappa({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{||\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}}{||\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{x}}||_{F}}.

(16)

An estimate
\kappa({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}})
for an empirical signal can be obtained by plugging in the empirical spectral covariance matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{m}}}
and PSD
\hat{{\mathbf{h}}}_{{\mathbf{m}}}
.

Unlike the undirected setting, the directed GFT basis is generally non-orthogonal, implying that directed-graph WN is no longer spatially uncorrelated. Consequently, node signals exhibit graph-dependent variances and cross-covariances determined by the graph topology and edge orientation.

Fig.
1
illustrates the covariance structure of WN for two directed graphs. In the synthetic example (Fig.
1b
), nodes sharing common predecessor nodes exhibit increased covariance, producing visible block structures in the covariance matrix. Moreover, nodes with larger in-degree present larger variances. Similar behavior can also be observed in the real graph example (Fig.
1d
), where covariance and variance patterns reflect the directional organization of the graph.

These observations highlight a fundamental distinction between undirected and directed graph WN. While WN on undirected graphs is graph-independent, directed-graph WN intrinsically encodes topology-dependent spatial correlations through the non-orthogonality of the directed GFT basis. This distinction will later play an important role in interpreting stationary surrogate signals and the associated null hypothesis, since preserving graph spectral second-order statistics on directed graphs also implicitly preserves graph-dependent covariance structures.

V
White Noise on Graphs

An important family of stationary random graph signals is white noise (WN). While WN on undirected graphs has similar properties to conventional WN on the Euclidean domain, its extension to directed graphs comes with some surprising properties. We will consider some practical examples to illustrate these points.

V-A

White Noise on Undirected Graph

We consider a WN random graph signal
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined through its graph spectral covariance matrix being the identity matrix
[
37
, Chap. 8.10]
; i.e.,
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{I}}_{N}
.
For an undirected graph, the GFT is a real-valued orthonormal basis, and thus the covariance matrix of
{\mathbf{w}}
is also the identity matrix

{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{H}={\mathbf{I}}_{N}.

(17)

Therefore, the variance is uniform for all nodes and the cross variances are null. In the case of a Gaussian distribution, the nodal signals are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

V-B

White Noise on Directed Graph

The real-valued WN random graph signal
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is also defined through the diagonal graph spectral covariance matrix
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{I}}_{N}
, and thus the following covariance matrix is obtained:

{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}={\mathbf{U}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}.

(18)

It should be noted that, despite
{\mathbf{U}}
being a matrix of complex-valued eigenvectors,
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}
is a proper covariance matrix for
{\mathbf{w}}
, that it is real-valued and positive semi-definite as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

The matrix
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}
is real-valued and positive semi-definite.

Proof.

(Proof of realness) Rewrite
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}=\sum_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H}
with
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
the eigenvectors. Now by grouping the indices of real eigenvalues into the set
\mathcal{R}
and the indices of complex conjugate eigenvalues into the set
\mathcal{C}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H}+{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\star}{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\star}}^{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}}\Re({\mathbf{u}}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

Thus proving realness of
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}
.

(Proof of positive semi-definiteness) For any
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{H}{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}{\mathbf{x}}=\|{\mathbf{U}}^{H}{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0

thus proving positive semi-definiteness of
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}
.
∎

L
surrogates using the undirected graph structure are generated as follows:

{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\,\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}^{(l)}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{\mathbf{m}}.

(20)

An important result of sign-randomization is that the undirected-graph surrogates are realizations of a GWSS process.

Proposition 3
.

For an observed graph signal
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the undirected graph surrogates
{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}
are realizations of a GWSS.

Proof.

Let the surrogate random variable be
{\mathbf{z}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{\mathbf{m}}
. Its covariance equals

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}=\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{z}}^{H}\big]=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}\underbrace{\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}{\mathbf{m}}{\mathbf{m}}^{H}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}}_{=\;\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}}\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}^{T}\big]\,\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T},

where
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}^{T}\big]
. Since
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}
is diagonal with entries
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}[k,k]=\epsilon_{k}
(i.i.d. Rademacher), the
(n,m)
-entry of
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}[n,m]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}[n,n]\,\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}[n,m]\,\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}[m,m]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}\epsilon_{m}]\,\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}[n,m].

Because
\epsilon_{n}
are independent with
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}^{2}]=1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}\epsilon_{m}]=\delta_{n,m}
. Hence

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}[n,m]=\delta_{n,m}\,\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{m}}[n,m],

so
\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}
is diagonal. Therefore
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}\hat{\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{\mathbf{z}}\bar{{\mathbf{U}}}^{T}
has the form required by Definition
1
, and
{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}
is a realization of a GWSS process.
∎

VI-C

Directed Surrogate Graph Signals

Generalizing the sign randomization matrix, we present a phase randomization matrix
{\mathbf{R}}
for directed graph surrogates, defined as follows:

{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{j\Psi_{n}},&\Im(\lambda_{n})\neq 0,\\
\epsilon_{n},&\Im(\lambda_{n})=0,\end{array}\right.

(21)

with
\epsilon_{n}
being i.i.d Rademacher random variables and

\Psi_{k}\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}\mathcal{U}([-\pi,\pi])
. If a real signal is desired then for indices of conjugate pairs
k,l
, i.e where
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{l}^{*}
, we set
\Psi_{k}=-\Psi_{l}
.
In directed graphs, the eigenvectors are not necessarily orthogonal, and it is well known that due to non-orthogonality, Parseval’s theorem does not hold in general
[
32
]
. As such energy is in general not preserved when applying a phase randomization. Yet, under the null hypothesis of stationarity, the energy is preserved. To show this, we first show Proposition
4
.

Proposition 4
.

If
{\mathbf{x}}
is DGWSS, then
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}
is DGWSS and we have
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
1
, since
|{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]|^{2}=1
for
n=1,...,N
.
∎

Following Proposition
4
, notice that from
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}
, we have in particular
\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{z}})=\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}})
which equivalently yields
||{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}=||{\mathbf{z}}||_{2}
. Thus a phase-randomized DGWSS is also DGWSS and preserves energy.

Under null hypothesis, a measured signal
{\mathbf{m}}
and
K
instances of the phase randomization matrix
{\mathbf{R}}^{(l)},l=1,\dots,L
, then
L
directed graph surrogates are generated as follows:

{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{R}}^{(l)}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}.

(22)

In practice, the energy of
{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}
may differ from that of
{\mathbf{m}}
. Therefore, we normalize the surrogate by a factor
||{\mathbf{m}}||_{2}/||{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}||_{2}
.

Finally, we show once more that the directed-graph surrogates are realizations of a DGWSS process.

Proposition 5
.

For an observed graph signal
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the directed graph surrogate
{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}
is a realization of a DGWSS.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{\mathbf{m}}
the surrogate random variable associated to
{\mathbf{m}}^{(l)}
. The covariance matrix of
{\mathbf{z}}
is

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{z}}^{H}\big]={\mathbf{U}}\,\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{R}}\underbrace{{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}{{\mathbf{m}}{\mathbf{m}}}^{H}\,{\mathbf{U}}^{-H}}_{=\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\big]{\mathbf{U}}^{H}.

Define the spectral covariance

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{R}}\,\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{m}}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\big],\qquad{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{U}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}.

Since
{\mathbf{R}}
is diagonal,

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}[n,m]=\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]\,{\mathbf{R}}^{*}[m,m]\big]\,\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{m}}}[n,m].

Thus
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}
is diagonal once we show
\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]{\mathbf{R}}^{*}[m,m]\big]=\delta_{n,m}
. Each diagonal entry of
{\mathbf{R}}
is either a Rademacher variable
\epsilon
(real eigenvalue) or a random unit phase
e^{j\Psi}
(complex eigenvalue), with conjugate pairs sharing
\Psi_{l}=-\Psi_{k}
.
If
n=m
then
|{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]|=1
so the expectation equals
1
. For
n\neq m
, we have:

•

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}\epsilon_{m}]=0
(independent real signs);

•

\mathbb{E}[e^{j(\Psi_{n}-\Psi_{m})}]=0
(independent phases);

•

\mathbb{E}[e^{2j\Psi_{n}}]=0
(conjugate-phase pairs);

•

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}e^{-j\Psi_{m}}]=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{n}]\mathbb{E}[e^{-j\Psi_{m}}]=0
(real/complex).

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{R}}[n,n]\,{\mathbf{R}}^{*}[m,m]\big]=\delta_{n,m},

so that
\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}
is diagonal and
{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{U}}\hat{{\mathbf{H}}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}{\mathbf{U}}^{H}
. Thus
{\mathbf{z}}
is a DGWSS process.
∎

VI-D

Building Test Statistic Distributions with Surrogates

Once surrogates are generated, test statistics need to be defined in order to verify the null hypothesis. In this work, we show two simple statistics that can be easily translated to a wide range of applications.

VI-D
1
Nodal Value

From
K
observations
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
we generate
L
surrogate sets
\{{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{(l)},k=1,\ldots,K\}_{l=1}^{L}
. The average empirical node signal
{\mathbf{m}}[n]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}[n]
is compared to the null distribution

\Pi(n)=\left\{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{(l)}[n],\quad l=1,\ldots,L\right\},

(23)

identifying the
p
-value (one-tailed) under the null hypothesis as being
l^{\prime}/(L+1)
where
l^{\prime}
is the rank of
{\mathbf{m}}[n]
among the sorted values of
\Pi(n)
.

This test procedure allows to detect node singularities that deviate from stationarity. Since we test for each node, an appropriate discount needs to be applied on the p-value. One could for instance use a Bonferroni correction
[
10
]
dividing the significance level
\alpha
by
N
, but also implying that at least
N\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}\rceil
surrogates would need to be generated to make sure that the multiple-comparison adjusted significance level is reached.

VI-D
2
Covariance structure

Starting from
K
observations
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, we generate
L
surrogate sets
\{{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{(l)},k=1,\ldots,K\}_{l=1}^{L}
. We are interested in testing the relationship between pairs of nodes. To do so we compute the empirical covariance

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}\,{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{T}

(24)

and compare each entry
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}[n,m]
to the following null distribution

\Pi(n,m)=\left\{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{(l)}[n]\,{{\mathbf{m}}}_{k}^{(l)}[m],\quad l=1,\ldots,L\right\},

(25)

identifying the
p
-value (one-tailed) under the null hypothesis as being
l^{\prime}/(L+1)
where
l^{\prime}
is the rank of
{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}}[n,m]
among the sorted values of
\Pi(n,m)
.

As an immediate application of the previously introduced statistical tests, we detect nodal signals and covariances against a null of stationary graph signals. Then, in a more realistic setting, we consider timecourses generated from diffusion model

Detection of Non-Stationary Covariance Structure

We now consider the following signal generation model

(28)

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{x}}\;=\;{\mathbf{H}}_{{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}+{\mathbf{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}.

(29)

The signal realizations are denoted again as
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}

As the density increases, the undirected null becomes more appropriate, reducing false positive rate. \Delta

Application to Temperature Data on Sensor Network

Finally, we apply the framework to real-world temperature data collected in 40 meteorological stations in the North-Western Coast of France over the full year of 2023 (Fig.

), which yields
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}
,
k=1\,\ldots,354
, measurements after removing missing data. The graph is defined by associating nodes to stations. The edge weights derived from distance; i.e., for the edge

{\mathbf{p}}_{n}
represent the geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude) of node

[

,
34

15

[
32
]
, which is effectively adding 4 edges to a total of 149 edges. The undirected counterpart is obtained by symmetrizing the adjacency matrix
A
, yielding
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{T})
. The signal is further preprocessed by first centering over time, and then over nodes. This leads to a data matrix of size
40\times 354
. In Fig.
6c
, the spectral covariance matrix is shown for the full graph signals. The obtained stationary level is
\kappa({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}})\approx 0.41
,
\bar{\kappa}({\mathbf{H}}_{\mathbf{m}})\approx 0.49
, for respectively directed and undirected case showing that the temperature timecourses are non-stationary with respect to the directed graph and its undirected counterpart.

We now seek to detect irregularity in the measurements that leads to the aforementioned non-stationarity considering a null hypothesis that assumes stationarity. We generate
9999
surrogates per day to build a null distribution per node and per timepoint with initial significance level of
\alpha=5\%
which is adjusted for multiple comparisons. In Figs.
6a
and
6b

19

354

90
) while almost none of the inland stations do. Such irregularities are present all over the year especially during summer and winter. Moreover, we can observe from Figs.

While we have no ground truth, a tentative explanation for these results can be attributed to unmodeled marine influences affecting coastal stations. Owing to the high thermal inertia of the sea, coastal regions often exhibit temperatures that differ from other areas
[
31
]
, which is likely responsible for the increased number of detections that do not obey the stationarity assumptions. This effect is more pronounced for the directed graph, whereas the undirected graph may partially mitigate marine influences due to the spatial similarity among surrogate signals at neighboring coastal nodes.

VIII
Discussion & Outlook

We revisited and leveraged the concept of stationarity on directed graphs. First, we show that white noise on directed graphs comes with a graph-specific, non-diagonal covariance matrix. Second, we propose a novel method for non-parametric hypothesis testing that exploits phase randomization of signals in the graph spectral domain. Finally, we demonstrate, using various examples with synthetic and real data, the benefits of considering surrogates on directed graphs compared to those for undirected graphs or naive permutation that do not fully exploit the graph structure. Here, we highlight several points for extension and discussion that could further increase the applicability and impact of the proposed framework.

Our starting point is that the covariance matrix is diagonalized by the eigendecomposition of the GSO, rather than only block-diagonal as in previous work
[
15
]
. Therefore, the covariance structure can be summarized by a length-
N
PSD similar to the definition of GWSS. Moreover many real graphs are often strongly connected, enabling diagonalization of the GSO
[
33
]
. In cases where this is not possible, recent graph adjustment algorithms
[
32
,
39
]
may be applied, all in all to overcome the computational instability of the JNF. Most importantly, as demonstrated in recent work on the graph Hilbert transform
[
7
]
, the JNF prevents the emergence of phase. For instance, the GSO for a directed path does not lead to conjugate eigenvectors nor does it provide phase information that can be exploited for “shifting” operations. As a result, directional information encoded through phase shifts cannot be exploited by surrogate generation.

These considerations relate to the distinction with graphical causal models that impose acyclicity of the graph
[
25
]
. The rationale is that directed acyclic graphs allow non-ambiguous identification of statistical dependencies. For our framework, such graphs would lead to a non-diagonalizable GSO requiring adjustment as mentioned before. Specifically, the emergence of phase of the GFT coefficients, which is essential for the proposed randomization scheme, requires the presence of cycles in the graph
[
7
]
. Therefore, both frameworks come with their own assumptions and applications.

The proposed approach also differs fundamentally from the surrogate algorithm introduced in
[
2
]
. Although the authors employ a Hermitian Laplacian formulation, which to some extent can accommodate for directionality, they do not provide a constructive procedure to obtain a Hermitian Laplacian from a directed graph. The underlying construction relies on symmetric edge weights, with directionality encoded by the sign of the imaginary part which is selected by maximizing smoothness criterion on the observed signals. It is therefore, in general, not applicable to directed graphs. Importantly, the method in
[
2
]
is applied on complex-valued observations for which the notion of graph stationarity was not defined. As a result, the corresponding surrogates are not grounded in stationarity considerations and do not leverage the notion of directed graph stationarity. Furthermore, the surrogate signals in
[
2
]
are introduced for data augmentation purposes, rather than for statistical hypothesis testing, which is a central objective of the present work.

The framework has been devised for real-valued graph signals. However, since the definition of DGWSS extends to complex-valued random vectors, the methodology can be readily broadened to complex-valued data, both for measurements and surrogates. In that case, the test statistic needs to be adapted to deal with the complex nature of the signals; e.g., by considering separately the real and imaginary parts, or resort to circular statistics
[
17
]
.

In addition to testing nodal values or covariance structures, various other test metrics have been defined in
[
41
,
4
]
to measure chaotic behaviours of non linear systems, such as Lyapunov exponents. These statistics could be adapted to the graph setting and employed within our framework to test for non-linear behavior on graphs.

In this work, multiple-comparison effects are handled using standard correction procedures, such as the Bonferroni and FDR method, applied to the p-values obtained from the proposed surrogate-based nonparametric tests. In a similar way, recent advances in multiple hypothesis testing strategies on networks
[
18
]
could be used, exploiting the underlying graph structure to control for false discovery rate while improving detection power.

Additionally, one can observe that in our method, no explicit assumption with regards to the probability distribution of the random signal was made. However, similarly to phase-randomized surrogate generation for temporal signals
[
41
]
, the present approach implicitly tests observations against Gaussian distributed surrogates. This arises from the fact that phase randomization only preserves autocovariance, such that, when considering a large number of surrogates, the resulting phase-randomized surrogates are asymptotically Gaussian due to the central limit theorem
[
20
]
. As originally proposed in
[
41
]
, an amplitude-adjusted surrogate generation scheme can be employed to extend the null hypothesis to non-Gaussian processes.

Throughout this work, we employ the adjacency matrix as the GSO, following a previous study that introduced stationary process for directed graphs using the adjacency operator
[
15
]
. Nevertheless, the directed graph Laplacian operator could also be employed to characterize stationarity, in a manner analogous to the GWSS for undirected graph
[
26
]
. In both cases, the associated spectral representation relies on the eigen- or Jordan decomposition. By contrast, alternative decompositions based on singular-values
[
44
]
or optimized orthogonal bases
[
34
]
produce real-valued singular values and components, which hinders an immediate extension of the uniform phase randomization mechanism central to our framework. This observation would instead motivate the investigation of alternative matrix factorizations that possess spectral conjugate structures, such as the Schur decomposition
[
45
]
which comprise 2-by-2 matrix of complex conjugate eigenvalues pairs, or the polar decomposition
[
19
]
, which exploits a real-valued unitary factor whose eigenvalues lie on the unit circle and come in conjugate pairs. These factorizations may enable other types of randomizations that also leverage conjugate pairs.
