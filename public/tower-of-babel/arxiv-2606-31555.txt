Self-propulsion of a polaron with an oscillating coupling to its quantum bath Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jacopo Romano, Andrea Gambassi

Abstract
Motivated by the quest for active quantum matter, we investigate the dynamics of an impurity immersed in a quantum gas — a polaron — whose coupling to the surrounding medium is periodically modulated in time, alternating in sign. By integrating out the bath degrees of freedom, we derive an effective velocity-dependent drag force acting on the impurity. Above a critical modulation frequency, the corresponding drag coefficient becomes negative at low velocities, signaling the onset of self-propulsion. In the classical limit, we characterize this transition as a function of the modulation frequency and the bath chemical potential. We then compute the leading-order quantum corrections to the impurity dynamics and show that, while the transition remains robust, it can be suppressed by sufficiently precise measurements of the impurity position.

Polarons are quasi-particles that form when a particle interacts with a quantum bath, often described as a quantum field. The concept was originally introduced
to describe electron motion in solids, where lattice phonon excitations act as a bath

Sykes et al. 2009

The physical origin of this drag can be understood as follows. As the impurity moves through the bath, it distorts the surrounding medium, generating a wake of depleted (accumulated) density for repulsive (attractive) interactions. In either case, the wake exerts a backward force on the impurity, giving rise to a drag that opposes its motion. Suppose now that, at some instant, the impurity–bath coupling reverses sign on a timescale shorter than that required for the wake to relax the excess energy. The interaction with the pre-existing wake then temporarily switches from dragging to propelling the impurity. As the wake gradually adapts to the new coupling, the force eventually becomes dissipative again. In this work, we show that this simple mechanism can be harnessed to induce autonomous motion of the impurity by periodically reversing the sign of its interaction to reinstate the transient propelling regime.

In order to characterize the features of the resulting effective particle dynamics and highlight the possible emergence of activity, we consider its semiclassical limit.
We show that, at sufficiently high modulation frequencies, the (speed-dependent) mean drag over one modulation period becomes aligned with the direction of motion, corresponding to a negative drag coefficient for the particle.
This marks the onset of self-propulsion in a spontaneously selected direction,
which we characterize by determining the critical boundary for this transition and by computing the self-propulsion speed for sinusoidal modulations.
We also investigate the features of the wake, showing that in the self-propelling regime the point of maximal field perturbation can

We show that, while the self-propulsion transition is generically robust, it can be suppressed, at least at intermediate times, by the momentum uncertainty associated with preparing a spatially localized initial state. A similar suppression arises from frequent and sufficiently precise measurements of the impurity position, highlighting a fundamental distinction between classical and quantum self-propulsion.

Finally, we derive an expression for the velocity autocorrelator, highlighting the interplay between stochastic and quantum fluctuations in determining the mean-squared displacement (MSD) of the impurity.

Conclusions.

We have demonstrated that it is possible to induce self-propulsion of polarons (i.e., to generate
act
ive polar
ons
—
actons
) by periodically modulating the sign of their interaction with the surrounding quantum bath. The transition to this active behavior, described at the semiclassical level by an active Brownian motion, is influenced by the quantum fluctuations and occurs in generic spatial dimensionality.
Here we focused on the emergent semiclassical dynamics, but the phenomenon of activation discussed here should be robust against quantum fluctuations. An important direction for future work is indeed the investigation of the effective action beyond leading order in the quantum components
\bm{R}_{q}
. This would allow one to assess the role of quantum effects in the acton dynamics, both within and beyond the Markovian approximation.
Such an analysis would also clarify the influence of the bath statistics on self-propulsion, which is made inconsequential here by the semiclassical approximation.
An especially interesting direction for future work, also in view of possible experimental realizations, is to investigate an impurity coupled to a superfluid, where the interplay between self-propulsion and the Landau critical velocity may give rise to novel dynamical phenomena.
Another open question concerns the possibility of velocity-alignment interactions between actons mediated by their wake, potentially of nonreciprocal nature. Such interactions could have important consequences for their collective behavior and may lead to the emergence of collectively traveling states. Finally, we note the possibility to consider systems with a spin-dependent, oscillating bath-impurity coupling, which may give rise to self-propulsion aligned with the spin direction, rather than along a spontaneously selected direction. In light of these considerations, the present work opens multiple new avenues both for understanding and designing active motion at the quantum scales.

\langle\zeta^{i}(t)\zeta^{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{ij}(v^{*})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. Calling
\hat{\bm{e}}(t)
a vector of norm
1
aligned with
\bm{v}(t)
, we project the dynamics Eq. (
S23
) on its longitudinal and transverse components by applying to both sides of Eq. (
S23
)
\hat{\bm{e}}\,\cdot
and
-\hat{\bm{e}}\times\hat{\bm{e}}\times
respectively. Since the longitudinal component is damped by the friction, oscillations of
v
around
v^{*}
are negligible at sufficiently low
T
. For the longitudinal component instead we have:

m_{I}v^{*}\partial_{t}\hat{e}^{i}(t)=[\delta^{ij}-\hat{e}^{i}(t)\hat{e}^{j}(t)]\zeta^{j}(t).

(S24)

Defining
\eta^{i}(t)=1/(m_{I}v^{*})[\delta^{ij}-\hat{e}^{i}(t)\hat{e}^{j}(t)]\zeta^{i}(t)
and computing its correlator we obtain the dynamics reported in the main text.

S.IV
Perturbative self-energy of the acton and Dyson equation

The Green function
\bm{\mathcal{G}}
for the acton dynamics is a matrix-valued function with component:
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}(t_{1},t)=\frac{i}{2}\langle R_{\alpha}^{i}(t_{1})R_{\beta}^{j}(t)\rangle
, where the indices
\alpha,\,\beta=c,\,q
run through quantum/classical components while the
i,\,j
indices run through space dimensions. Its time evolution is described by the Dyson equation in terms of the unperturbed Green’s function
\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
for a free particle (
g=0
) and the self energy
\bm{\Sigma}
. The latter is defined as the sum of all the irreducible diagrams that can be obtained connecting the vertices induced by
S_{B}=S_{\rm{diss}}+S_{\rm{fluc}}
with the free propagator
\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
. In particular, the Dyson equation reads:
[\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{-1}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}]\,\circ\,\bm{\mathcal{G}}=\mathds{1}
, where the
\circ
denotes matrix product in the spatial and classical/quantum components and convolution in time, while the identity is given by
\mathds{1}^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta^{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. Finally, the inverse of the free propagator is:
(\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{0})_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}(t_{1},t)=-m_{I}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}\delta^{ij}\partial_{t_{1}}^{2}\delta(t_{1}-t)
, with:

\bm{\sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S25)

We compute the self-energy
\bm{\Sigma}
to first order in perturbation theory in
S_{B}
. At this order, all contributing diagrams contain a single interaction vertex and are therefore irreducible. Consequently, the self-energy can be written as:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\frac{\delta^{2}S_{B}/\hbar}{\delta R^{i}_{\alpha}(t_{1})\delta R^{j}_{\beta}(t_{2})}\right\rangle_{0},

(S26)

where
\langle...\rangle_{0}
indicates that the expectation value computed in the free theory. To evaluate (
S26
) we start by noticing that, due to causality:
\Sigma_{cc}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})=0
and
\Sigma_{cq}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})=\Sigma_{qc}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})^{*}
.
We compute now
\bm{\Sigma}_{qq}
: in the Markovian limit, the fluctuation action (
2
) is given by

S_{\rm{fluc}}=4i\hbar\int dtR_{q}^{i}(t)\,R_{q}^{j}(t)\,\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{ij}[\dot{R}_{c}(t)].

(S27)

Performing the functional derivative of (
S27
) with respect to
\bm{R}_{q}(t)
and
\bm{R}_{q}(t^{\prime})
we arrive to:

\bm{\Sigma}_{qq}(t_{1},t_{2})=-4i\langle\bar{\mathbb{D}}[\dot{R}_{c}(t_{1})]\rangle_{0}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2}).

(S28)

To compute the expectation value in (
S28
) we notice that, since the free action is quadratic and the initial condition is gaussian,
\dot{R}_{c}^{i}(t)
is gaussian at any time
t
. Moreover, since in the free theory momentum is conserved, the variance of
\dot{R}^{i}_{c}
is constant and related to the momentum variance by
\langle\dot{R}^{i}_{c}(t)^{2}\rangle=\sigma_{p}^{2}/m_{I}^{2}
.
Finally, since the initial momentum distribution is isotropic,
\Sigma^{ij}_{qq}
is also isotropic and we can write:
\Sigma^{ij}_{qq}(t_{1},t_{2})=-4iD_{R}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\delta^{ij}
, where
D_{R}
is defined according to the main text.

We then decompose
\bm{\Sigma}_{cq}
as
\bm{\Sigma}_{cq}=\bm{\Sigma}_{cq,d}+\bm{\Sigma}_{cq,f}
, where the contributions
\bm{\Sigma}_{cq,d}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{cq,f}
come respectively from the dissipative
S_{\rm{diss}}
and fluctuation part
S_{\rm{fluc}}
of the action
S_{B}
. The dissipative contribution can be computed directly in the Markovian limit, in which
S_{\rm{diss}}
becomes:

S_{\rm{diss}}=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,R^{i}_{q}(t)\bar{\gamma}(\dot{R}_{c}(t))\,\dot{R}_{c}^{i}(t).

(S29)

Then, using that for a generic functional
G
:
\delta G/(\delta R^{i}(t))=-\partial_{t}[\delta G/\delta\dot{R}^{i}(t)]
, we obtain:

\Sigma^{ij}_{qc,d}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\left\langle\frac{\partial[\dot{R}_{c}^{i}(t)\bar{\gamma}(\dot{R}_{c}(t))]}{\partial\dot{R}_{c}^{j}(t)}\right\rangle_{0}\partial_{t_{2}}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\delta^{ij}.

(S30)

The expectation value in (
S30
) can be computed with similar consideration as for
\langle\bar{\mathbb{D}}(\dot{R}_{c})\rangle_{0}
in Eq. (
S28
). This leads to:
\Sigma^{ij}_{qc,d}(t_{1},t_{2})=-\gamma_{R}\partial_{t_{2}}\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})
, with
\gamma_{R}
given by Eq. (
4
) of the main text. The evaluation
\bm{\Sigma}_{qc,f}
in the Markovian limit is more subtle, since direct calculation based on (
S27
) give rise to an ill-defined
\theta(0)
in the result. To avoid this ambiguity, one must instead compute
\bm{\Sigma}_{qc,f}
from the non-Markovian action in (
2
) and take the Markovian limit only at the end. Taking the functional derivative of Eq (
2
) w.r.t.
R_{q}^{i}(t_{1})
and
R_{c}^{j}(t_{2})
we find:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{qc,f}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})=2i\bigg[

\displaystyle\langle R_{c}^{l}(t_{1})R^{k}_{q}(t_{2})\rangle_{0}\langle\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}\mathfrak{D}^{ik}(\bm{R}_{c}(t_{2})-\bm{R}_{c}(t_{1}),t_{2},t_{1})\rangle_{0}

(S31)

\displaystyle-\int dt\langle R_{c}^{l}(t)R^{k}_{q}(t_{2})\rangle_{0}\langle\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}\mathfrak{D}^{ik}(\bm{R}_{c}(t_{2})-\bm{R}_{c}(t),t_{2},t)\rangle_{0}\,\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\bigg],

where we use Wick’s theorem to decompose the expectation value in (
S26
) as the product of two expectation values. In Markovian limit, we approximate
\bm{R}_{c}(t_{2})-\bm{R}_{c}(t_{1})\simeq\dot{\bm{R}}_{c}(t_{2})(t_{1}-t_{2})
consider only the average of (
S31
) w.r.t. oscillations of the coupling. We then apply
\Sigma_{qc,f}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})
on a test function
\mathcal{T}(t)
. Since
\mathfrak{D}
decays rapidly in
t_{1}-t_{2}
we can approximate
\mathcal{T}(t)=\mathcal{T}(t_{1})+\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(t_{1})(t_{1}-t_{2})
. The term
\propto g(t_{1})
vanishes and we obtain:

\int dt_{2}\Sigma_{qc,f}^{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})\mathcal{T}(t_{2})=-\frac{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(t_{1})}{m_{I}}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-t_{2})^{2}\langle\nabla^{j}\nabla^{k}\bar{\mathfrak{D}}^{ik}(\dot{\bm{R}}_{c}(t_{2})(t_{1}-t_{2}),t_{1}-t_{2})\rangle_{0},

(S32)

where we used
\langle R_{c}^{i}(t)R^{j}_{q}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{0}=i/(2m_{I})(t^{\prime}-t)\theta(t-t^{\prime})

(t_{1}-t_{2})\nabla^{i}=\partial/(\partial\dot{R}_{c}^{i}(t_{2}))

\Sigma^{ij}_{qc,f}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1}{m_{I}}\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{ik}}{\partial v^{j}\partial v^{k}}\right\rangle\partial_{t_{2}}\delta(t_{2}-t_{1})

S30

The response function
R^{ij}_{v}

R^{ij}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})=-\partial_{t}\mathcal{G}_{cq}^{ij}(t,t^{\prime})

m_{I}\partial_{t}R^{ij}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})+\gamma_{R}R^{ij}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta^{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(S34)

Solving (
