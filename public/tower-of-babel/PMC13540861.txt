Intravenous lidocaine reduces propofol requirements during endoscopic ultrasound: a randomized, double-blinded, controlled trial
Authors: Tongxuan Wang, Yu Wang, Zhongli Liao, Qi Chen, Xianzhang Zeng, Qianyun Pang, Aihua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePropofol as a sole sedative agent for endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) often causes respiratory and circulatory depression. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of intravenous lidocaine on propofol dose, propofol-related complications, patient safety and comfort during EUS.Patients and methodsEighty patients scheduled for EUS were randomly allocated to two groups. Lidocaine group (Group L, n = 40) received a 1.5 mg/kg lidocaine bolus followed by a 2 mg/kg/h infusion, while the control group (Group C, n = 40) received an equal volume of saline. Sedation was induced with 2 mg/kg propofol in both groups, followed by a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol at 4 mg/kg/h. 50 mg propofol was administered as needed, and the propofol infusion rate was also increased by 0.5 mg/kg/h. The primary outcome was total propofol dose.ResultsGroup L showed less total propofol dose (p = 0.007). In addition, maintenance (p = 0.018) and supplemental propofol doses (p = 0.016) were also reduced in Group L. Group L showed less incidence of intraoperative hypoxia, more stable hemodynamic profile, shorter induction and recovery times, reduced postoperative pain, higher patient and endoscopist satisfaction scores compared to Group C.ConclusionIntravenous lidocaine reduced total propofol dose during EUS. It also improved safety and procedural efficiency, although these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger studies.Trial registrationThe study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2400086658) on 07/08/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04018-0.Keywords: Lidocaine, Propofol, EUS, Complications, Satisfaction score

Introduction

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) has become an indispensable diagnostic and therapeutic tool in Gastroenterology. By integrating endoscopy with ultrasonography, EUS provides high-resolution imaging of the gastrointestinal wall and adjacent structures. EUS offers superior spatial resolution for detecting small lesions, staging gastrointestinal and pancreaticobiliary malignancies, and guiding fine-needle aspiration or therapeutic interventions [1, 2]. However, due to its prolonged examination time, intricate maneuvers, and higher patient tolerance requirements, EUS requires deeper sedation, which may increase the risk of sedation-related adverse events. Meanwhile, EUS often induces discomforts including cough, hiccup, nausea, and vomiting, which can compromise procedural stability, leading to incomplete examinations or complications [3, 4]. Guidelines from the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy(ASGE) emphasize the importance of tailoring sedation to ensure successful procedure completion, while also noting that cardiopulmonary events remain the leading cause of adverse outcomes during sedated endoscopic interventions [5]. For these reasons, adequate and stable sedation is essential to ensure procedural quality and patient comfort.

Propofol is widely used for gastrointestinal endoscopy because of its rapid onset, short recovery profile, and predictable sedative effect. Nevertheless, propofol lacks intrinsic analgesic effects and is often associated with injection pain, which may reduce patient satisfaction. Meanwhile, increasing doses of propofol may increase the risk of cardiopulmonary complications [6–8], highlighting the importance of maintaining a delicate balance between adequate sedation and patient safety.

To address these concerns, adjuvant drugs have been explored to reduce propofol requirements while maintaining sedation quality. Dexmedetomidine, for example, has been shown to decrease propofol dose, but its use may cause bradycardia and hypotension [9–11]. Similarly, opioids such as Sufentanil enhance sedation yet carry risks of respiratory depression and increase healthcare costs [12]. These trade-offs highlight the need for alternative adjuvants that can effectively reduce propofol demand without increasing perioperative risk.

Lidocaine has attracted attention as a potential adjuvant to propofol sedation. Beyond its well-known local anesthetic effects, it also exhibits systemic antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory, and anti-hyperalgesic properties [13]. Clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated that intravenous lidocaine can attenuate visceral and injection pain [14], and promote postoperative recovery [13]. While there is growing evidence supporting the benefits of intravenous lidocaine in various gastrointestinal endoscopy procedures such as colonoscopy and ERCP; however, studies specifically evaluating its use in EUS remain limited. We therefore designed a double-blinded, randomized controlled trial to investigate whether lidocaine could reduce propofol dose and improve safety and quality of sedation during EUS.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center, double-blinded, randomized controlled trial, conducted at Chongqing University Cancer Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing University Cancer Hospital (CZLS2023084-A-06), and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2400086658) on 07/08/2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was supported by the Shapingba District Technology Innovation Project (2025132). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Participants

From August 2024 to August 2025, 93 patients scheduled for painless EUS were enrolled. Only patients undergoing EUS examination were included, patients undergoing EUS-FNA or biopsy were excluded to avoid differences in procedure complexity. Inclusion criteria were: ASA I-III; BMI 18–30 kg/m2; STOP-Bang score ≤ 2. Exclusion criteria were: Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea Syndrome (OSAHS) or asthma; arrhythmia and atrioventricular block; allergy to lidocaine; severe hepatic, renal dysfunction or neurological dysfunction; chronic alcoholism, refusal to sign the informed consent.

Study protocol

Patients were randomly assigned to either lidocaine group (Group L) or control group (Group C) using a computer-generated random number table. The research assistant was responsible for randomization and preparation of study medications. The anesthesiologist performed sedation and intraoperative monitoring while remaining blinded to group allocation. Outcome assessment and data collection were performed by blinded evaluator. All patients fasted at least 8 h prior to the procedure. Patients were prepared in the operating room, where a peripheral venous catheter was inserted into the right upper limb, and they received nasal oxygen at 5 L/min while lying in the left lateral position. Baseline vital signs, including heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP)—including systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP)—electrocardiogram (ECG), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were monitored.

Group L received an intravenous bolus of lidocaine at 1.5 mg/kg, followed by a continuous infusion at 2 mg/kg/h; Group C received an equal volume of saline. Propofol was then administered at 2 mg/kg until Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score = 1 was achieved. Both groups received a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol at 4 mg/kg/h. Sedation depth was continuously assessed using the MOAA/S score throughout the procedure, if patients showed signs of inadequate sedation (MOAA/S score ≥ 3, pain-related facial expressions, or involuntary movement), an additional 50 mg propofol was administered as needed, and the propofol infusion rate was also increased by 0.5 mg/kg/h. After the EUS, patients were monitored in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) until fully awake. Hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg or MAP reduction ≥ 20% from baseline) was treated with 40 µg phenylephrine. Bradycardia (HR < 50 bpm) was treated with 0.5 mg of atropine. Hypoxia was managed according to the underlying clinical cause and severity. Airway interventions, including jaw thrust for airway obstruction, mask ventilation for hypo-ventilation, and endotracheal intubation when clinically necessary, were performed based on the patient’s respiratory status. The study protocol was discontinued if lidocaine toxicity or severe vital sign abnormalities occurred.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was total propofol dose. Secondary outcomes included induction dose, maintenance dose, supplemental dose, and the frequency of supplemental dosing; the incidence of sedation-related complications, including hypoxia, hypotension, bradycardia; cough, hiccups, nausea, and vomiting. Vital signs included BP, HR, and SpO2. These parameters were recorded at T0—T7 (T0: baseline; T1: 1 min; T2: 3 min; T3: 5 min; T4: 10 min; T5: 15 min; T6: 20 min; T7: 30 min after the start of the procedure). In cases where the procedure lasted longer than 30 min, vital-sign data was not recorded per protocol. The EUS procedure time was defined as the interval from the insertion of the endoscope into the oral cavity to its final removal; induction time was defined as the initial injection of propofol to MOAA/S = 1; recovery time was defined as the period between endoscope withdrawal and the patient’s return to full consciousness (patient opened eyes and was able to correctly state his/her name and age); PACU time was defined as the interval from the end of the procedure to discharge from the PACU to the ward. Postoperative pain was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Patient and endoscopist satisfaction scores with anesthesia was assessed using a 0 to 10 numerical scale, where 0 indicated extreme dissatisfaction and 10 indicated extreme satisfaction.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the CreateMed applet (Tianchuang Mingda, Beijing, China). based on the results of our pilot study, the total propofol dose during EUS was 329.01 ± 96.98 mg in Group C and 270.03 ± 80.00 mg in Group L. Assuming a two-sided α = 0.05 and statistical power of 0.8, the sample size was calculated to 36 patients in each group. Considering a loss to follow-up of 10%, 40 patients were required for each group.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted after excluding patients with missing data. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and parametric or non-parametric tests were applied accordingly. Variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normal distribution variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Continuous data were compared using Student’s t-test (parametric test) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric test). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and compared by Fisher’s exact test. For secondary outcomes, a Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized for data analysis, and GraphPad Prism 7.00 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used for graphical representation.

Of these, two had severe asthma; two had OSAHS, one had Mobitz type II atrioventricular block, one had severe hepatic dysfunction, and five declined to provide informed consent. Primary outcome

Compared with Group C, the total propofol dose was reduced by 58.60 mg in Group L (325.8 ± 107.40 mg vs. 267.2 ± 80.41 mg; mean difference: 58.60 mg; 95% CI (16.31, 100.8) mg; p = 0.007).

Secondary outcomes

The maintenance dose was reduced by 52.50 mg (201.30 ± 112.70 mg vs. 148.80 ± 78.39 mg, mean difference: 52.50 mg; 95% CI (9.24, 95.69) mg; p = 0.018). The supplemental dose was also significantly lower in Group L compared with Group C (95.0 ± 62.84 mg vs. 65.0 ± 44.14 mg, mean difference: 30.00 mg; 95% CI (5.83, 54.17) mg; p = 0.016). However, there was no significant difference in the induction dose and continuous infusion dose between the two groups (124.40 ± 21.68 mg vs. 118.40 ± 25.53 mg, mean difference: 6.03 mg; 95% CI (-4.52, 16.57) mg; p = 0.259 and 106.3 ± 66.04 mg vs. 83.8 ± 57.72 mg, mean difference: 22.47 mg; 95% CI (-5.14, 50.08) mg; p = 0.109; respectively). In addition, the frequency of supplemental dosing was lower in Group L than in Group C (1.90 ± 1.26 vs. 1.30 ± 0.88; mean difference: 0.60; 95% CI (0.12, 1.08); p = 0.016) (Table 2).

Immediately after recovery of consciousness, VAS pain scores were significantly lower in Group L than in Group C (2.43 ± 1.06 vs. 1.43 ± 0.81, mean difference: 1.00; 95% CI (0.58, 1.42); p < 0.0001).

Both patient and endoscopist satisfaction scores were also higher in Group L (10 [10–10] vs. 10 [8–10], p = 0.0002; 10 [10–10] vs. 9 [8–10], p < 0.0001, respectively). No cardiac arrhythmia occurred during the procedure, and no participant exhibited any signs of local anesthetic toxicity—such as dizziness, somnolence, blurred vision, perioral numbness, or metallic taste after awakening. Despite transient hypoxia and hypotension in some patients, no related complications occurred after the procedure, and all patients were safely transferred back to the ward.

Discussion

Our study indicates that intravenous lidocaine during EUS resulted in a reduction in total propofol dose, indicating a propofol-sparing effect. In addition, patients receiving lidocaine also showed improvements in sedation quality, procedural conditions, and recovery profiles. Importantly, these benefits were achieved without an increase in lidocaine related adverse events.

The propofol-sparing effect with lidocaine administration may be attributable to its multi-modal pharmacological actions. Lidocaine attenuates peripheral nociceptive transmission by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels and simultaneously exerts central antinociceptive effects by reducing spinal dorsal horn excitability and modulating NMDA receptor-mediated signaling [15, 16]. In addition, lidocaine has been shown to attenuate sympathetic activation and the stress response, thereby enhancing hemodynamic stability and reducing the sedative dose required to achieve adequate anesthesia [17]. These combined mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the reduction in propofol dose observed in our study.

With the increasing burden of upper gastrointestinal disorders, the clinical application of EUS has expanded considerably in recent years. Nevertheless, compared with conventional upper endoscopy, EUS requires deeper and more prolonged sedation due to its larger scope diameter and longer procedural duration, thereby increasing the risk of sedation-related complications. Lidocaine is widely used in clinical practice because of its safety, rapid onset, and versatility. Owing to its dual role as a local anesthetic and adjuvant in general anesthesia, intravenous lidocaine has been increasingly used for perioperative analgesia and sedation management. Importantly, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that intravenous lidocaine can reduce the propofol requirements during procedural sedation and decrease the incidence of sedation-related complications. Accumulating studies reported that intravenous lidocaine reduced total propofol dose, the incidence of hypoxia and postoperative pain in gastroscopy [18], colonoscopy [19], and ERCP [20], resulting in higher sedation quality and endoscopist satisfaction. However, limited studies have evaluated the safety and efficacy of intravenous lidocaine in EUS. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to demonstrate that intravenous lidocaine, as an adjuvant during EUS, is safe and effective, reducing total propofol dose, lowering the incidence of sedation-related complications. Our findings were consistent with prior studies. In painless colonoscopy, Li found that Intravenous lidocaine significantly reduced the frequency of oxygen desaturation and apnea episodes [21]. Ates reported that during ERCP, intravenous lidocaine effectively reduced propofol dose and recovery time [22]. According to our study, the difference between groups was primarily observed in supplemental propofol dose rather than continuous infusion dose, suggesting that the propofol-sparing effect of lidocaine appears to be mainly driven by reduced need for rescue dosing rather than baseline sedation requirements. This finding suggests that intravenous lidocaine may enhance patient’s tolerance to procedural stimulation during EUS, thereby reducing the need for supplemental propofol dosing. However, we observed no significant difference in the induction dose of propofol between the two groups. This may be because the induction dose of propofol was standardized at 2 mg/kg for all patients; resulting in no between-group differences in induction dose. Furthermore, an induction dose of 2 mg/kg is generally sufficient in clinical practice, and no supplemental propofol was required during induction in either group. We found no difference in PACU stay duration between the two groups. This may suggest that intravenous lidocaine does not affect the post-anesthesia recovery process, likely due to its short half-life and duration of action.

Hypoxia remains one of the common complications during gastrointestinal endoscopy under sedation, which increases morbidity, interrupts the procedure and reduces overall satisfaction of both patient and endoscopist. Our study observed that intravenous lidocaine reduced the incidence of hypoxia and the need for airway interventions during propofol-induced sedation. This improvement enhanced procedural safety and facilitated a smoother process, which may be clinically meaningful in the setting of EUS sedation. In contrast to hypoxia, the differences in hemodynamic parameters were relatively modest and transient, and these short-term variations were not associated with clinically observed cardiovascular complications, suggesting that statistically significant differences do not necessarily translate into clinical outcomes. Thus, these hemodynamic findings should be interpreted cautiously.

In our study, both patients and endoscopists in Group L reported higher satisfaction scores compared with those in Group C, suggesting that lidocaine-assisted propofol sedation not only enhances patient comfort but also facilitates a smoother procedural experience for the endoscopist. The lower satisfaction of patients in Group C was partially attributed to propofol-induced injection pain. This may be explained by the fact that lidocaine, as a widely used anesthetic agent, provides effective local anesthesia, and its intravenous administration has been shown to reduce pain responses at the injection site. Previous studies have demonstrated that intravenous lidocaine effectively alleviates propofol-induced injection pain, and when administered at a dose of 1.5 mg/kg, it provides analgesic effects through both local anesthetic action and central analgesia [14, 23–25]. Importantly, the PACU time did not differ between the two groups, indicating that intravenous lidocaine did not delay post-anesthesia monitoring. Taken together, these findings highlight the potential of intravenous lidocaine to improve both the quality and efficiency of EUS procedures.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The anesthetic depth was assessed using clinical sedation scales rather than objective monitoring tools such as BIS. Incorporation of BIS monitoring in future studies may allow a more objective comparison of sedation depth between groups. The structured propofol regimen in this study may limit generalizability to settings where propofol is administered using target-controlled infusion (TCI) or individualized titration approaches. Results may differ under TCI or BIS-guided sedation. The study was not powered to detect rare lidocaine-related toxicities. Therefore, the safety findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in higher-risk populations. In addition, only one lidocaine dosing regimen was evaluated in this study, and future studies with objective monitoring tools and larger sample sizes are warranted to further validate these findings and determine the optimal lidocaine dose for EUS sedation.

Conclusion

Intravenous lidocaine, as an adjunct to propofol sedation during EUS, reduced total propofol dose. Secondary findings suggested potential improvements in safety-related outcomes and procedural efficiency. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and selected population.
