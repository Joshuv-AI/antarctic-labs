Commissural and Coronary Alignment Strategies in Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: Success Rate and Risks
Authors: Clara Fernandez-Cordón, Ignacio Amat-Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Commissural alignment (CA) is becoming increasingly important in the lifetime management of younger patients with aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation, potentially influencing coronary access, valve-in-valve feasibility and haemodynamics. Interest in the topic has led to a growing body of evidence, valve design improvements and optimised implantation techniques. In this review, we summarise the clinical impact of CA, its success rate and limitations, stressing the potential role of coronary alignment as opposed to CA. We also describe the techniques to achieve CA with the latest iterations of the most used transcatheter aortic valve implantation devices.

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become the standard of care for a growing number of patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) across all surgical risks.1,2 With its expansion to younger individuals, there is a high probability that they may need a second TAVI procedure during their lifespan.3,4 Furthermore, the likelihood of presenting with coronary artery disease (CAD) requiring coronary re-access after TAVI will increase. In this evolving scenario, some aspects of the index TAVI procedure have become increasingly important in current practice. One of them is commissural alignment (CA) between the commissural posts of the transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) and the commissures of the native aortic valve. CA facilitates coronary re-access, reduces the risk of coronary obstruction and allows leaflet modification techniques in case of re-do TAVI; it is also believed to improve valve haemodynamics, potentially increasing durability. While CA is routinely performed in surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR), the alignment of the prosthetic and native commissures during TAVI is random unless specific CA techniques are implemented.5 CA also has pitfalls, especially in patients with certain anatomical features such as cusp asymmetry or coronary eccentricity. In these cases, coronary alignment (CoA) might be a better strategy to ensure coronary access.6

The aim of this review is to update and summarise the current evidence on CA and its impact and success rates, along with describing challenging scenarios in which CoA may be a better strategy. Device-specific techniques with the latest iterations of the most commonly used TAVI devices are described.

Definitions

In 2022, the ALIGN-TAVR consortium proposed a standardised definition of CA and other related concepts7 (Figure 1):

Cusp symmetry is defined according to the inter-commissural angle of the largest cusp. Cusps are considered symmetric when this angle is 120–125°, meaning that each one of the three cusps corresponds to approximately one-third of the sinus aorta circumference. Higher inter-commissural angle means one sinus will be significantly larger than the other two, increasing cusp asymmetry, which is staged as mild (125–130°), moderate (130–135°) or severe (>135°).

Commissural Alignment and Related Concepts

Clinical Impact and Reasons for Commissural Alignment

The lifetime management of young patients with AS treated with TAVI should consider TAV haemodynamics and durability, easy coronary re-access and the feasibility of redo TAVI minimising the risk of coronary obstruction. Transcatheter Aortic Valve Haemodynamics and Durability

In vitro studies have shown that CMA can impact fluid patterns in the aortic root, induce leaflet stress and reduce flow to the sinuses, therefore increasing flow stagnation.15–17 Simulated SAPIEN XT (Edwards) deployment in different orientations inside an aortic root model with severe AS, found that with increasing degrees of CMA there was also an increase in leaflet stress due to less expansion of the TAV.18 A bench model of valve-in-valve ACURATE neo (Boston Scientific) inside a Mitroflow surgical bioprosthesis (Sorin Group Canada) also showed increasing leaflet interaction and intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR) with growing degrees of CMA.19

Several studies have evaluated the impact of CA in vivo, trying to discern whether these mechanisms are sufficient to impact TAV haemodynamics and durability in clinical practice. The retrospective RESOLVE registry reported that CMA was associated with a higher rate of transvalvular gradient progression at 30 days in patients treated with the balloon-expandable (BE) intra-annular SAPIEN 3 valve.8 However, a more recent study with the same valve did not find differences in gradient progression or mortality at 5 years between aligned and misaligned valves.9 Another multicentric cohort of patients treated with supra-annular self-expandable (SE) devices (Evolut PRO/PRO+ [Medtronic] and ACURATE neo2) did report higher mean gradients and greater progression of gradients at 1 year in patients with moderate/severe CMA.10 A retrospective cohort of patients treated with the Portico (Abbott Vascular) TAV consistently showed higher mean gradients post-TAVI when severe CMA was present.11 Higher rates of postprocedural mild central AR and greater AR progression at 1 year in subjects with CMA have been reported as well.5,10 Despite these early signs of accelerated TAV degeneration, no criteria for structural valve deterioration were met and clinical events did not differ or were not reported in these studies. Prospective investigations and longer follow-up data are needed to confirm the true impact of these findings.

Hypo-attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) is a marker of subclinical leaflet thrombosis that can be detected by post-TAVI cardiac CT. Since one of the consequences of CMA in bench studies is blood stagnation within the aortic sinus, increased thrombogenicity and HALT have been proposed as potential mechanisms of the early valve degeneration seen in patients with CMA. Kahn et al. reported a 40% incidence of HALT in a cohort treated with SAPIEN 3 when CMA was present (versus 28% in those with optimal CA), although this was not statistically significant.20 Recently, Jung et al. found that patients with HALT had a greater angle between native and prosthetic commissures, and more frequent severe CMA (37% versus 20%), than patients without HALT.21 However, the association of HALT with CA and its impact on valve durability require further investigation.

It is important to note that most of the data supporting improved haemodynamic, lower rates of central AR and HALT in aligned valves are derived from studies with short-term follow-up. Whether this is really going to impact valve durability and long-term clinical outcomes is yet to be elucidated. The only published work with a 5-year follow-up of a cohort of patients treated with SAPIEN 3 valves failed to prove differences in gradient progression or mortality according to the degree of CA.9 This is an important gap in knowledge that should be emphasised.

Coronary Re-access after Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation

The prevalence of CAD in patients with severe AS undergoing TAVI ranges from 20% to 80%.22,23 Given this high prevalence and the expansion of TAVI to younger individuals, it is likely that the need for coronary angiography in patients with previous TAVI will increase.

Factors impacting coronary access after TAVI depend on patient anatomy, device characteristics and procedural aspects such as implantation height and CA. Surgeons performing SAVR align the native and prosthetic commissures by direct visualisation, achieving almost universal CA. However, in the early years of TAVI, the orientation of the valve was random, with only 20% of cases meeting criteria for CA.5 As the degree of CMA increases, it becomes more likely that one of the commissural posts stands in front of the coronary ostia (a situation known as coronary overlap), acting as a physical barrier that can hinder selective coronary access. TAV design also plays an important role in the feasibility of coronary access. SE valves with longer stent frames, supra-annular leaflets and smaller cell design can complicate coronary access; on the other hand, shorter-frame BE devices, intra-annular leaflets and open cells favour easier coronary cannulation. Ochiai et al. assessed the incidence of unfavourable coronary access features by post-procedural CT (defined as ostium below the skirt of the TAV, or in front of the commissural post above the skirt).24 The incidence of these features was 35% for the left coronary artery (LCA) and 26% for the right coronary artery (RCA) after Evolut R/PRO implantation, but only 16% for the LCA and 8% for the RCA after SAPIEN 3.

The RE-ACCESS study was the first prospective investigation on coronary access after TAVI.12 In patients treated with commercially available TAVI devices (Evolut R/PRO, SAPIEN 3/ULTRA, ACURATE neo and Portico) implanted without CA techniques, unsuccessful coronary cannulation occurred in 7.7% of subjects. Of note, all but one of these patients had received an Evolut device, and unsuccessful cannulation was 18% in the Evolut group. Higher implantation depth, TAV oversizing with respect to the aortic sinus, and Evolut TAV were independent predictors of unsuccessful cannulation.

The RE-ACCESS 2 study was then designed to evaluate predictors of unsuccessful coronary cannulation after TAVI, using supra-annular SE devices (Evolut PRO/PRO+ and ACURATE neo2) implanted with CA techniques.13 Unsuccessful cannulation occurred in 5.5% of all patients and 7.5% of patients receiving Evolut (six of seven cases occurred in patients receiving Evolut). Severe CMA significantly impaired coronary re-access, but only among Evolut recipients. Therefore, the open-cell design of ACURATE seemed to ensure coronary re-access even when CMA was present.

Similarly, the ALIGN-ACCESS study assessed the impact of CA in coronary access after TAVI with SAPIEN 3, Evolut PRO/R and ACURATE neo devices.14 Selective coronary access was higher for SAPIEN 3 than for aligned or misaligned SE supra-annular TAVs (95% versus 71% versus 46%) and cannulation was unfeasible in 11% of patients with misaligned SE devices. Misaligned SE valves, TAV oversizing with respect to the aortic sinus and lower sinus height were independent predictors of impaired cannulation.

The results of these studies highlight the importance of CA to facilitate coronary re-access, especially in younger patients with supra-annular longer-frame SE devices, who might require lifetime management of CAD. Although the reported success rate of coronary access after TAVI remains high, in challenging cases left radial or transfemoral access can be used, and catheter choice might need to be adapted. On the contrary, the implantation of an intra-annular low-frame TAV in a sub-coronary position does not usually require any modification of the coronary canulation technique.23 RCA cannulation is usually more demanding than LCA, with higher rates of non-selective ostial engagement and longer cannulation times.12,13

Coronary Obstruction Risk and Feasibility of Valve-in-valve Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation

Young patients treated with percutaneous or surgical bioprosthetic valves will likely require at least a second replacement during their lifespan. Valve-in-valve TAVI (both TAV-in-SAV and TAV-in-TAV) is feasible but exhibit high complexity and inherent challenges. One of the most feared complications is the acute impairment of coronary flow due to sinus sequestration or coronary obstruction, related to the neo-skirt formed by the leaflets of the first valve and the stent frame of the second. Understanding the 3D interactions between the valves, the aortic root and the coronary ostia is key to guiding preprocedural planning, avoiding coronary obstruction and preserving coronary access (Table 2). Several algorithms have aimed to assist in decision-making.6,25 A CT study of TAV-in-TAV with different combinations of Evolut and SAPIEN valves showed that coronary re-access was impaired in over one third of patients, but was more favourable when the first valve was a SAPIEN 3.26 When the TAV-in-TAV procedure is conducted emergently in the context of first valve embolisation, the probability of achieving CA of both prostheses drops to less than 10%.27

When the first valve is commissurally aligned, leaflet modification techniques such as bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) can effectively reduce the risks of coronary obstruction by splitting the leaflet located in front of the coronary ostia.28,29 However, if the first valve is misaligned, BASILICA will not be effective. A different technique for the prevention and bail-out of coronary obstruction is chimney stenting, which can be used with good acute results regardless of CA, but has potential pitfalls if subsequent coronary re-access is needed.30

Implantation Techniques and Success Rates With Current Devices

Current iterations of TAVs and their delivery systems incorporate device-specific radiopaque markers, aimed to locate the neo-commissures and guide CA techniques.

Self-expandable Valves

The basic principle of CA-oriented implantation techniques for SE devices relies on the adequate orientation of the TAV in the 3-cusps (3C) and cusp overlap (CO) coplanar fluoroscopic views. When the coronary cusps are symmetric, the native valve commissures are located 120° apart, and each of them is in front of the centre of the opposite cusp. In the CO view (usually right anterior oblique caudal), the right coronary cusp (RCC) and left coronary cusp (LCC) are superimposed to the right of the screen, leaving the non-coronary cusp (NCC) isolated to the left. This means the RCC-LCC commissure is located to the right of the screen. On the other hand, in the 3C view the RCC is in the middle of the screen, between the LCC to the right and the NCC to the left.

All current SE TAV devices incorporate radiopaque markers at the commissural posts that can identify when the valve is crimped and after expansion. To achieve CA, we should aim for a II-I configuration of the commissural markers in the CO view, and a I-I-I configuration in the 3C view. Evolut (Medtronic)

The Evolut TAV has a C-paddle in the outflow that identifies the position of one of the commissural posts. When the valve is crimped, this C-paddle is mounted 90° clockwise from the fluoroscopic hat-marker of the delivery system. The new iterations of the Medtronic TAV (Evolut FX and FX+) incorporate three additional dot markers in the inflow of the valve that are positioned at the site of the commissural posts to improve CA and also mark the optimal 3 mm implantation depth. The Evolut FX+ valve also has three open windows, four-times the size of the valve cells, that are located between the commissures at a height consistent with the average coronary take-off height obtained from the Evolut low-risk trial.3 If CA is obtained, and assuming cusp symmetry and absence of significant coronary eccentricity, there will be one window in front of each coronary ostia, facilitating coronary re-access. Although the open windows can be seen when the TAV is correctly aligned, according to the manufacturer they should not be used to guide the CA technique, which has remained unchanged from the prior iterations of Evolut.

Several studies have reported 86–92% rates of favourable CA (optimal CA or mild CMA) with the Evolut R/PRO devices using the flush port at 3 o’clock technique.14,31 First experience with the new Evolut FX device has reported even higher rates of CA, ranging between 93% and 96%, and significantly reduced risk of severe coronary overlap compared with the previous Evolut PRO+ (9% versus 23%).32,33 Although not endorsed by the manufacturer, 3 o’clock flush port insertion followed by rotation of the Evolut R/PRO device at the annulus plane as needed to achieve the desired commissural post orientation, led to 90% favourable CA in the COMALIGN trial.34

Navitor (Abbott)

The Navitor TAV has three radiopaque commissural posts that can be used to achieve CA. The latest iteration, Navitor Vision, incorporates three additional dot markers at the inflow of the valve to indicate the neo-commissures and the recommended valve implantation depth of 3 mm, in a similar way to the Evolut FX/FX+ device. Second, conversely, if in 3C view one of the posts is isolated to the right, counterclockwise rotation of the delivery should be applied. Third, if no post is isolated, the valve can be perfectly aligned (centre-front position of the post located in the middle) or severely misaligned (centre-back), and we will need the CO view to discern between the two. In case of misalignment, both clockwise or anticlockwise rotation can be applied to correct it.

After reaching the aortic annulus, valve orientation is assessed in 3C view. At this point, there are three possibilities.36 First, if one of the posts is isolated (slit-like appearance) to the left of the screen, clockwise rotation of the delivery should be applied. Then, CA can be confirmed in the 2-cusps view by achieving a II–I configuration of the posts. Second, conversely, if in 3C view one of the posts is isolated to the right, counterclockwise rotation of the delivery should be applied. In case of misalignment, both clockwise or anticlockwise rotation can be applied to correct it.

Of note, the valve should be rotated after retrieving it to the descending aorta, as the flexibility of the catheter does not allow for the rotation to transmit up to the aortic annulus.

Rotation must be done gently in 10° increments, allowing for the torque to translate. Then the operator must release their hands from the handle, so the torque dissipates. Once the orientation is correct, the valve can be advanced to the annulus plane and delivered in 3C view.

In an unselected population treated with ACURATE neo2, successful CA was achieved in 80% of cases, with 17% mild CMA, and only 3% moderate or severe CMA.37 Other studies have reported CA or mild CMA in 88–100% of cases.14,34 As yet, there are no reports on CA success rate with the new ACURATE Prime.

Other Self-expandable Devices

Similar techniques using the CO view and device-specific commissural radiopaque markers have been described for other less commonly used SE devices, such as Hydra (SMT) and ALLEGRA (Biosensors).38–40

The supra-annular ALLEGRA device has three T-markers in the outflow that are aligned opposite to the valve commissures, and six radiopaque gold markers where the leaflets are sutured. Recently, a CA technique developed in a beating-heart model and then applied in 10 patients proved 80% success rate in achieving optimal CA without safety concerns.40 The valve was inserted with the flush port at 12 o’clock. In the ascending aorta, the gold marker configuration should be I–II–II–I in 3C and II–II–II in CO view. Once deployed, angiographic evaluation showing I–I–I configuration of the T-markers in 3C view and II–I configuration in CO view would be indicative of successful CA.40

Balloon-expandable Devices

Commissural post radiopaque markers of BE devices cannot be adequately visualised when the valve is crimped, therefore the previously described techniques for SE valves are not applicable. CA techniques should be based on a patient-specific crimping orientation of the device, derived from a detailed assessment of the preprocedural CT.

Myval (Meril Life)

The Myval TAV is the first BE alternative to the Edwards device that has become commercially available. Santos-Martínez et al. were the first to describe a CA technique that could be used for any TAV device, including BE devices.41 The Accurate Commissural Alignment (ACA) technique uses preprocedural CT to obtain a patient-specific angle, starting at the RCA (0) that is pointing at 12 o’clock. This angle is used to crimp the valve with a certain degree of clockwise or counterclockwise rotation, which must be maintained during TAV advancement along the vasculature, allowing CA when the valve rotates in the aortic arch.41,42 The technique’s proof of concept was performed with in silico and bench testing simulations, followed by in vivo implantation of the ACURATE neo device in 11 patients, with successful CA in all.42 Its efficacy was then proved in vivo in 10 patients with the Myval BE device, achieving CA in six and mild CMA in the remaining four cases (although three cases had right coronary overlap attributed to coronary eccentricity).41

The second iteration of Myval, Myval Octacor, has two rows of octagon-shaped cells and three commissural posts located 120° apart. Similar to the ACA technique, the OctaAlign technique (developed by Meril Life) provides a patient-specific CT-derived clock angle to crimp the valve with one of the posts oriented to the mid-right coronary cusp. The delivery system must then be inserted with the logo of the handle facing up, and this position must be maintained while advancing through the vascular anatomy. Assuming a 180° rotation in the aortic arch, the TAV will change its orientation from anteroposterior to posteroanterior, and the commissural post crimped towards the mid-RCC will end up pointing backwards towards the NCC-LCC commissure, achieving CA (Figure 5). In case of bicuspid anatomy, the commissural post must be crimped towards the origin of the RCA instead.43–45 OctaAlign has been tested in 32 patients treated with Myval Octacor, including tricuspid and bicuspid anatomy. CA or only mild CMA was achieved in 70% (including 77% of the bicuspid), while severe CA was present in four patients.43 Both the ACA and OctaAlign techniques avoid the need to torque the system inside the body, potentially reducing the risk of vascular and embolic events.

SAPIEN

To date, no technique has proved to be effective in reducing the risk of CMA with the SAPIEN TAV. Although the previously described ACA technique could potentially be used with any TAV, no dedicated studies have been conducted with SAPIEN.42 In the ALIGN-TAVR and Low Risk TAVR studies, intentional crimping of the SAPIEN 3 valve at different orientations did not improve CA.31,46

Assessment Methods

The gold standard technique to assess CA after TAVI is post-procedural CT, but this is not routinely performed and is associated with unnecessary radiation and contrast administration. Fluoroscopic assessment of valve orientation after deployment is a more realistic approach in clinical practice and has shown relatively good concordance with CT. In the COMALIGN study, fluoroscopy showed an 86% accuracy to identify moderate to severe CMA with SE valves (ACURATE neo2, Evolut R/PRO and Portico).34 A method of assessing CA with the SAPIEN 3 valve has also been described, based on pre-procedural CT and intraprocedural aortography in CO and 3C views, with a strong correlation with post-TAVI CT.47

Challenging Scenarios, Limitations and Risks

Commissural Alignment versus Coronary Alignment

The rationale of CA greatly relies on symmetry. The geometry of TAV devices is symmetrical, with equal 120° angles between the three commissural posts and the nadir of the bioprosthetic leaflets located at 60° from each post. However, the geometry of the aortic root is not symmetrical: one cusp may be larger than the others (cusp asymmetry), or the coronary ostia might not be centred in the coronary cusp (coronary eccentricity). This is more prominent in bicuspid anatomies. Moderate to severe cusp asymmetry is present in 20% of tricuspid aortic valves and up to 65% of bicuspid aortic valves, the NCC being the more dominant. Moreover, moderate to severe coronary eccentricity occurs in 5.5% LCA versus 33% RCA in tricuspid anatomies and 6% LCA versus 23% RCA in bicuspid anatomies.48

In this imperfect real-life scenario, even optimal CA might not avoid some risk of coronary overlap. Some authors suggest that the focus should be on orienting the TAV commissural posts as far as possible from the coronary ostia. This is achieved by positioning one of the posts in the bisector of the angle between both coronary ostia – what is known as CoA. A coronary-aligned TAV would facilitate coronary re-access and ensure the feasibility and efficacy of BASILICA and other leaflet modification techniques. In practice, coronary ostia overlap view (as opposed to CO view) should be obtained by preprocedural CT analysis, and during valve implantation, positioning one of the commissural posts to the right of the screen in this view will lead to optimal CoA.6,48,49 Most techniques used for CA in BE valves can be adapted to achieve CoA using the coronary ostia overlap view (Figure 6).

Redondo et al. analysed the pre-TAVR CT of 100 patients and performed virtual valve implantation to simulate commissural and coronary alignment.6 Risk of coronary overlap was defined as the angle between the coronary ostia and the nearer TAV post. With simulated CA, the risk of coronary overlap was moderate (20–35°), or severe (<20°) in 27% and 5% of cases, respectively. Most coronary overlap cases involved the more eccentric RCA. However, when coronary overlap was simulated, the mean angle between the coronary ostia and the nearest post was 48° (equal for both coronary arteries as expected) and coronary overlap decreased significantly: 95% of cases had no risk, and the remaining 5% had moderate risk. The results of this simulation study are consistent with those of the DA VINCI prospective study, that included 34 patients with type 1BAV with right–left raphe that underwent TAVI with BE devices and CA techniques.50 Despite 86% rate of CA/mild CMA, moderate to severe coronary overlap was seen in 14% of RCA and 45% of LCA, hindering selective bilateral coronary cannulation in more than one-third of patients.

The optimal alignment strategy is yet to be elucidated. In patients with asymmetric features, including most bicuspid valves, CoA should probably be favoured to avoid significant coronary overlap. On the contrary, in symmetric anatomies both CA and CoA will have similar results in terms of valve orientation. It is unclear what constitutes an acceptable angle between the coronary ostia and the TAV posts, and a cut-off with clinical implications in coronary re-access, BASILICA procedures and valve haemodynamics has not been established. Future investigations are warranted to attempt to answer these questions.

Feasibility and Potential Risks

Not all TAV devices are made the same. Optimal rotation could not be applied at the level of the aortic root in 15% of Evolut R/PRO devices when compared to its contemporary ACURATE neo2 and Portico.34 The low metal-to-artery ratio of the ACURATE neo2 TAV has been shown to allow easy and safe rotation in the aortic annulus.37,51 On the contrary, and despite the improved flexibility of the last iteration of Evolut FX/FX+, the manufacturer currently recommends retrieving the valve to the descending aorta to torque it.

The potential risks of TAV rotation inside the body should be considered and balanced with the benefit of achieving optimal valve orientation, especially in older patients with significant atherosclerosis. To date, rotation manoeuvres appear safe, and they have not been shown to increase periprocedural ischaemic stroke or vascular complications.34,37 However, these cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, alternative accesses are increasingly used in patients with femoral arteries unsuitable for TAVI. Most CA techniques and studies have been performed using transfemoral access, and there are no recommendations regarding flush port insertion from the axillary or carotid route, nor safety reports in this scenario.

Future TAV iterations will continue to improve radiopaque markers’ visibility, delivery system flexibility and torqueability. At the same time, future investigations should continue to enhance implantation techniques, integrating these features with a personalised analysis of the pre-TAVI CT, with the goal of achieving optimal commissural or CoA for each patient and ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Optimal CA is one of the landmarks of current TAVI practice, especially in younger patients with longer life expectancy. CA facilitates coronary access after TAVI, and it is key to reducing the risk of coronary obstruction during valve-in-valve procedures, allowing the effective use of leaflet modification techniques. It also impacts valve haemodynamics, including gradient progression, AR and thrombogenicity, although the long-term impact on valve durability is yet to be established. Current TAV device design and improved implantation techniques have allowed for high rates of CA, especially for BE valves, although these are not always implemented in clinical practice. Individualised pre-TAVI CT planning to obtain patient-specific implant angles is a current need. This patient-specific approach, including the need for coronary over CA in asymmetric and bicuspid anatomies, leading to personalised alignment protocols, is the next step for continued improvement of clinical outcomes.
