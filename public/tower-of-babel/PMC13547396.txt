Overweight/obesity clustering and wealth differentials among indigenous households in Eastern India
Authors: Lucky Singh, Neha Shri, Shashi Kala Saroj, Jaya Singh Kshatri, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Overweight/obesity is an emerging public health concern among India’s tribal populations, yet evidence on its distribution and determinants remains limited. This study examined the prevalence, clustering, and socioeconomic inequalities in overweight/obesity among tribal adults in Odisha. Data from the Odisha Tribal Family and Health Survey (OTFHS, 2022–23) covering 17,976 adults aged ≥ 18 years from 8465 households across 13 tribal-dominated districts were analysed. Overweight/obesity was defined using Asian BMI cut-offs (≥ 23 kg/m2). Multilevel logistic regression assessed variations at individual, household, PSU, and district levels, while Wagstaff decomposition identified contributors to wealth-based inequality. Overall, 20.2% of tribal adults were overweight/obese, and 9.4% of households had all adult members overweight/obese. Higher clustering was observed in Balasore, Rayagada, Gajapati, and Deogarh districts, whereas Koraput and Mayurbhanj districts reported lower proportions. Household-level factors accounted for the largest variation in overweight/obesity (19%), followed by PSUs (9%) and districts (4%). Overweight/obesity was concentrated among wealthier households. Being married, male, non-PVTG, and residing in urban-dominated areas significantly contributed to wealth-related inequality. Household environments and changing dietary patterns play a major role in overweight/obesity among tribal adults in Odisha, indicating a growing risk of non-communicable diseases with the erosion of traditional lifestyles.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, adult overweight/ obesity has risen to alarming levels globally, where high Body Mass Index (BMI) contributed to 3.71 million deaths and 129 million disability-adjusted-life-years (DALY), in 20211. This increase is not only in high-income countries but also among the Low-Middle Income Countries (LMIC)2, where the dual burden of undernutrition and overnutrition exists. The rising prevalence of overweight/ obesity is a major driver of the epidemiological transition, shifting the disease burden from communicable and nutrition-related conditions toward non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancers3,4.

Reflecting this global trend, India is witnessing a rapid increase in the burden of malnutrition while undergoing rapid urbanization, lifestyle changes, a shift in dietary behavior, and increase in sedentary lifestyle. In 2021, India was home to 180 million people with overweight/ obesity, being one of the top three leading contributors to the overweight/ obesity, and is further projected to have 450 million people with overweight/ obesity by 20505. Data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS)-56 reveal an increasing prevalence of overweight/ obesity among men and women, both, across rural and urban areas. While research on overweight/obesity in India has primarily focused on urban and peri-urban settings, emerging evidence shows that rural and marginalized populations are also experiencing a gradual increase in overweight/ obesity rates7,8. Besides the individual risk of overweight/ obesity, researchers have highlighted the clustering of overweight/ obesity at the household level9.

In literature, overweight/ obesity among tribal population is not much explored in India. Since, tribal communities, are named as ‘Scheduled Tribes’ (ST), and constitutionally defind in Article 342(1) and Article 366(25), which comprise 8.6% of India’s total population, translating to 104.3 million people10. Historically, tribal populations have often been overlooked through the lens of food insecurity, poverty, and chronic undernutrition 11. However, recent studies indicate a shift, with overweight/ obesity and related metabolic disorders emerging even among these groups12,13. This transition is attributed to the mechanization of labour, acculturation to urban diets, and increasing proximity to urban centers, which have led to an increase in the penetration of overweight/ obesity14 among these tribal communities that have been once considered outreach groups. Evidence suggests that these historically marginalized and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations with limited exposure to modern lifestyles are at par with the other general population and have shown an alarmingly high prevalence of NCDs15 and overweight/ obesity. For instance, estimates provided by NFHS-5, indicated that in Odisha, during 2015–16, 5.5% tribal women, and 9.9% tribal men were overweight/obese 16, which rose to 8.7% and 12.5% respectively during 2019–216.

Odisha is home to one of the largest tribal populations in India, consisting of approximately one-fifth (22.9%) of the total state population. The state has 62 identified tribes, including 13 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)17. PVTGs represent some of the most marginalized communities, characterized by small populations, geographic isolation, and dependence on forest-based livelihoods. Traditionally, these communities, especially PVTGs, relied on nutrient-rich, diverse ecosystems, locally available foods, such as millets, pulses, and wild edibles. However, socioeconomic transitions, government food distribution systems emphasizing rice and wheat, and exposure to processed foods have led to a gradual erosion of these traditional dietary patterns 18. Despite this, tribal overweight/ obesity prevalence has been less focused on in the public health literature. Exposure to tobacco and alcohol among these indigenous populations also heightens their vulnerability to developing overweight/ obesity19.

The distribution of overweight/ obesity within tribal population is not uniform and may be shaped by household-level, community-level, and regional factors. Also, within tribal households, younger individuals are more likely to be exposed to westernized dietary patterns characterized by processed, calorie-dense foods due to economic integration and proximity to urban centers. At the same time, older members are more likely to adhere to traditional, nutrient-rich diets. This intergenerational difference may lead to variations in overweight/ obesity prevalence within the same household. These patterns are influenced by shared environmental exposures, dietary practices, socio-economic conditions, and cultural norms within tribal communities 14. Understanding household-level clustering of overweight/ obesity among tribal population is therefore essential, as such clustering may indicate localized risk environments that can accelerate the spread of overweight/ obesity-related health problems. Despite the growing body of research on overweight/ obesity trends in India, there remains limited evidence on how overweight/ obesity is distributed within and between tribal households that are undergoing rapid shifts in food environments and livelihoods. This study addresses this gap by examining the prevalence and clustering patterns of overweight/ obesity at the household level among tribal populations in Odisha. Additionally, since overweight/ obesity is considered a disease of affluence, wealth-based differences in the prevalence of overweight/ obesity among participants are also assessed.

Methods

Data source

This study used secondary data from the Odisha Tribal Family Health Survey (OTFHS), which was conducted between June 2022 and July 2023. This survey is the first of its kind to provide a community-based, comprehensive assessment of health indicators, exclusively for the tribal population of Odisha. A multi-stage random sampling design collected information from 40,921 tribals, selected from 10,230 households. All the samples belonged to the 53 enlisted tribal groups, including the 13 PVTGs, which resided in 341 clusters from 14 tribal-dominated districts of Odisha, namely Balasore, Deogarh, Gajapati, Kalahandi, Kandhamal, Keonjhar, Koraput, Malkangiri, Mayurbhanj, Nabarangpur, Nuapada, Rayagada, Sambalpur, and Sundergarh districts. This survey was conducted collaboratively by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR)–Regional Medical Research Centre (RMRC), Bhubaneswar (now known as the National Institute of Health Research [NIHR], Bhubaneswar), Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), Government of India; and funded by the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes Research and Training Institute (SCSTRTI), Odisha, Ministry of Tribal Affairs, Government of India. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Human Ethics Committee of ICMR-RMRC, Bhubaneswar (Approval No. ICMRRMRCB/IHEC-2021/68) and the Government of Odisha (Approval No. MS2-IV/04/2020/PI-1, Bhubaneswar). All study procedures were carried out in accordance with the 'ICMR- National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants’ and other relevant guidelines and regulations applicable to research involving vulnerable populations in India. Written informed consent was obtained from all the adult participants prior to data collection. For adolescent participants, written informed consent was obtained from the adolescents, along with assent from their parents or legal guardians. And for children, parental or guardian consent was obtained before the commencement of the interview20,21 .

OTFHS provides information on individual characteristics (age, sex, marital status, health, and nutrition) through an ‘individual-level’ questionnaire, and household information related to WASH, household wealth/ assets through a ‘Household-level’ questionnaire. Both questionnaires were translated into the ‘Odia’ language for better understanding. Every selected permanent resident of the notified tribes was pre-informed about the survey. However, those individuals who were bedridden and had recognizable cognitive impairments were excluded from the survey. Detailed information on data collection procedures, sampling design, and questionnaire can be obtained from the survey report 21. Additional contextual variables were obtained from the data of Census of India, 2011. Included variables are : (i) type of block (village, municipality, and outgrowth, notified area council and outgrowth, municipality, notified area council), (ii) distance from the nearest town, (iii) locality inhabitation among district (≤ 50% ST population, > 50% ST population), and (iv) proportion of urban population in the block. These variables were included to capture the role of geographic and socio-environmental factors in shaping dietary practices and overweight/ obesity risk.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome variable is overweight/ obesity, measured by the Body Mass Index (BMI). BMI is the human body weight ratio in kilograms (kg) per height square (m2), denoted as kg/m2. Here, the 'Asian BMI cut-off’ is taken to show individuals ‘overweight/ obesity’ with a BMI ‘equal or more than 23 kg/m2′22, since Asians have higher body fat percentage, greater abdominal adiposity, and increased risk of type- 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at a relatively lower BMI than Western populations. The Asian BMI-cut-offs are particularly relevant for tribal communities undergoing nutrition transition, as early detection of excess weight at lower- BMI thresholds can help prevent the onset of overweight/ obesityrelated co-morbidities.

To show the clustering of overweight/ obesity among the tribal population at household-level, households were further classified into three categories based on the number of overweight/ obese individuals within the household: (i) no adult member was overweight/ obese in the household, (ii) only one adult was overweight/ obese in the household, (iii) one or more members were overweight/ obese in the household.

Additionally, overweight/ obesity clusters were further divided by sex, for ‘adult male tribe’: (i) no adult male was overweight/obese (ii) one adult male was overweight/ obese (iii) all adult male were overweight/ obese in the family; and for ‘adult females’: (i) no adult female was overweight/obese (ii) one adult female was overweight/ obese (iii) all adult female were overweight/ obese in the family.

Statistical analysis

Bivariate analysis has been used to estimate the prevalence of overweight/ obesity by background and household & contextual level characteristics at the individual level, and chi-squared (χ2) tests are used to test the association between background characteristics and overweight/ obesity.

Multilevel logistics regression with random intercepts and fixed slopes at four levels (individual, household, PSU, and district-level) was used to estimate the four-level stratified heterogeneities in the overweight/ obesity using the ‘melogit’ command. The total effect has been calculated in the null- model without controlling for the confounding factors. Model-I estimates the variation at different levels when controlled for the household/ contextual factors (wealth quintiles, social groups, dietary practices, type of block, distance from the nearest town, locality of inhabitation, and percentage of urban population in the block), and Model-II further adjusted for individual factors (age, sex, marital status, years of schooling, and tobacco and alcohol use) and household & contextual level factors. Additional analyses

This study also determined the wealth-based inequality in the prevalence of overweight/ obesity among the tribal population using Wagstaff’s Concentration Index decomposition24 and helps to identify the contribution of individual, household, and contextual factors in the concentration of overweight/ obesity. Concentration index ranges between -1 to + 1, where a negative sign of one (-1) shows concentration of outcome in the ‘poorest’ wealth quintile and a positive sign of one (+1) shows concentration in the richest wealth quintile; however, zero (0) represents the perfect equality or no unequal distribution.

All these analyses are conducted using the 17th version of STATA software.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 shows the distribution of study participants and prevalence of overweight/ obesity by individual’s background, household, and contextual level characteristics among the selected samples. Nearly half (48.75%) of tribal adults were aged 18–39 years, 36.38% were in 40–59 years age-group, and 14.87% were 60 years or older. Most (75.34%) were currently married, 14.43% had never married, and 10.23% were in widowed, divorced, or separated category. Over half of tribal population (50.72%) had no formal schooling; 12.33% had only 1–5 years of education, whereas, 27.70% had 6–10 years, and 5.94% had 11- 12 years , and only 3.31% tribal population had 13 and above years of schooling. Similarly, alcohol consumption and tobacco use were reported by 67.41% and 51.48% of the total participants, respectively. Among them, one-third (34.48%) consumed aerated soft drinks, and 24.56% consumed fried foods regularly, whereas, 17.09% e meat, 10.47% eat fish or eggs, and 12.52% pulses or green vegetables on a daily or weekly basis; and only 0.87% consumed none of these items. More than half (52.49%) were in the poorest wealth quintile, and 26.15% in the middle quintile, with only 5.49% in the richest quintile, and 2.70% were from the richest groups. PVTGs accounted for 14.88% of the sample. Most households (81.99%) were in villages, 12.24% in ‘Notified Area Councils,’ and fewer than 5% in municipalities (3.6%) or Notified Area Council and Outgrowth (1.15%), and Municipality and Outgrowth (1.02%).

Prevalence of overweight/obesity among adult-tribal people by their individual, household, and contextual characteristics

One-fifth (20.23%) of the tribal individuals (≥ 18 years) were overweight/ obese, with a higher prevalence among males (22.86%) compared to females (17.86%). Married individuals (21.67%) had a greater prevalence than widowed/divorced/separated (16.34%) or unmarried (15.47%) counterparts. Overweight/obesity was more common among younger (18–39 years of age) (20.99%) and middle-aged (40–59 years of age) adults (21.84%) compared to older adults (60 and above years) (13.82%). By schooling years , highest prevalence of overweight/ obesity was found among individuals with 13 years and above schooling (30.08%), followed by those with 11–12 years of schooling (26.85%), and 6–10 years of schooling (25.03%).The prevalence was lower among individuals with no education (15.76%) or with 1–5 years of schooling (22.02%).

Richer (28.33%) and richest (24.02%) quintiles showed higher overweight/ obesity prevalence than middle (18.08%), poorer (15.83%), and poorest (10.6%) wealth quintiles. Compared to PVTGs (16.75%), other tribal groups had higher overweight/ obesity prevalence (20.84%).

Dietary patterns revealed higher overweight/ obesity prevalence among those who consumed fried foods (22.43%), meat (20.45%), aerated drinks (20.52%), and eggs/fish (20.13%) on a daily or weekly basis. Additionally, the geographical patterns indicated that households in ‘notified area council’ (23.89%) and ‘notified area council and outgrowth’ (21.46%) area households reported higher overweight/ obesity prevalence compared to village households (19.77%), municipalities (18.85%), and municipality outgrowths (16.85%). Similarly, higher overweight/ obesity prevalence was also observed in households located within 5 km (20.43%) or 21–90 km (21.29%) from the nearest town. At the district/block level, Scheduled Tribe (ST)-dominated districts (21.37%) and urban-populated blocks (20.85%) had showed higher burden of overweight/ obesity prevalence, compared to their counterparts.

Proportion of overweight/obese adult members among tribal households of Odisha (N = 8465).

Clustering of all adult overweight/ obese members was found highest among the households of the richest wealth quintile (14.3% households), while 25.39% households in the richest wealth quintile had all the male members overweight/ obese, and 15.57% households had all the females overweight/ obese. One-tenth of non-PVTGs households (9.47%) had all adult members being overweight/ obese, compared to 8.87% households among the PVTGs. Households with frequent (daily/weekly) fried foods, aerated drinks, meat, or eggs/fish had higher clustering of overweight/ obesity among all adults (11.28%, 8.99%, 8.98%, and 8.73%, respectively). In contrast, only 7.03% of households with overweight/obese adults reported regular consumption of vegetables and pulses. Clustering of all overweight/obese adults in households was highest in municipal areas (10.32%), followed by villages (9.5%) and (8.9%) in notified area councils. Around 9.3% of the households situated nearest to towns (5 km or less) had the overweight/ obesity among all members.

Overall 18.2% of the households had all males being overweight/ obese , and 12.2% of the households had all females being overweight/obese. Among households situated in urban-populated blocks, 11.1% had all adults overweight/ obese, whereas, only 8.7% of households in non-urban blocks had all-adults overweight/ obese. Clustering of all overweight/obese adults was slightly higher in ST-populated districts (9.6%) than in less ST-populated districts (8.6%).

Discussion

Clustering of overweight/ obesity in the household reflects the shared exposures and diet patterns that shape the nutrition-related health risk. This study attempts to understand the clustering of overweight/ obesity prevalence at the household level. It quantifies the contribution of different hierarchical-levels to unexplained variation in the prevalence of overweight/ obesity. In addition, this study identified the key factors contributing to wealth-related inequalities in overweight/obesity prevalence among the tribal households of Odisha. Our findings highlight that overweight/ obesity is not randomly distributed among the adult tribal population of Odisha but aggregated within the households, suggesting common dietary patterns, physical activity environments, and socioeconomic conditions among the household members.

Findings showed that around 20.23% of the tribal population was overweight/ obese. Although, the estimate is comparable to estimates reported for the general population 6, and tribal population in India 25,26, it is essential to note that these tribal communities have been historically considered protected from overweight/ obesity due to higher levels of physical activity and locally- sourced diets. However, the narrowing gap of overweight/ obesity between the tribal and the general population implies an increasing future burden of diet-related NCDs in . The observation that overweight/ obesity is significantly lower among PVTGs aligns with theoretical expectations and empirical findings from India.

PVTGs are among the most marginalized communities, with traditional lifestyle and health practices and geographical isolation, which support higher physical activity and minimally processed, locally sourced diets, and thus offer protective effects against overweight/ obesity27. For instance, an analysis of PVTGs in Central India reported that approximately half (51.4%) were underweight. In comparison, only 1.3% of Baiga men and 1.8% of women had BMI values above 25, indicating low overweight/ obesity prevalence28. Also, within the tribe, the health condition of PVTGs is reported to be worse, which increases their vulnerability to diseases such as tuberculosis. The higher prevalence of overweight/ obesity across all ethnic groups can be attributed to the increased fat consumption and decreased protein and energy consumption against the recommended dietary allowances29, where the scheduled castes and scheduled tribes have reportedly shown lower diet quality. Previous studies have demonstrated that as families get richer, they tend to consume more fat-dense food 29. A sex differential in the overweight/ obesity prevalence within the same households, with men bearing a greater burden of overweight/ obesity, is not new 12 and reflects the gap in food habits, where women usually have a lower diet diversity score as compared to men 30.

Similar to the observations at the national level, multilevel analysis shows that overweight/ obesity among tribal households is strongly clustered at the household and PSU levels, with smaller but notable variation at the district level9. Even after adjusting for individual, household, and contextual factors, substantial residual variance remained, especially at the household level, indicating the strong influence of shared family practices and community environments. The increase in district-level variance suggests unobserved contextual factors, such as local food environments and socioeconomic transitions, may be driving overweight/ obesity. The above finding aligns with literature demonstrating that spatial and macro-level contexts, including neighborhood wealth and state characteristics, exert an independent effect on overweight/ obesity risk beyond individual factors9,31.

Research indicates that dietary patterns and lifestyle factors are the risk factors for overweight/ obesity and other chronic diseases, such as high cholesterol level, hypertension, and type-2 diabetes among tribal communities as well 28. Our decomposition analysis revealed that marriage was one of the strongest contributors to wealth-based inequality in overweight/ obesity among tribal adults, which is in-line with broader patterns observed across India. Married couples often converge towards shared dietary and lifestyle patterns, including greater consumption of calorie-dense meals, reduced individual agency in food choice, and collective sedentary habits that increase mutual overweight/ obesity risk among household members 9. Another key driver, i.e., frequent consumption of fried foods, indicates the dietary shifts into tribal diets, eroding once-protective indigenous eating habits 30. Our study also demonstrated that residence in an urban locality is a substantive factor for higher overweight/ obesity prevalence, reflecting that proximity to towns and cities brings higher access to processed foods and less active lives, accelerating overweight/ obesity emergence among tribal populations. An Indian study reported that ultra-processed food consumption has penetrated all societal sections, cutting across socioeconomic groups 32. Together, these trends indicate that tribal communities are no longer epidemiologically isolated but are increasingly following and paralleling the overweight/ obesity trajectories seen in broader Indian society.

Sample distribution of the study, mostly consisted of young individuals aged 18–59 years without formal schooling. High overweight/ obesity prevalence among these age groups shows an early onset of nutrition transition in these marginalized communities. Younger adults are particularly vulnerable to poor eating habits, increased exposure to ultra-processed foods, and sedentary lifestyles, which are key drivers of overweight/ obesity risk. Findings revealed that households where all adult members are overweight/ obese were more common in non-PVTG (20.84%) households, Notified Area Council (23.89%), and Notified Area Council and Outgrowth (21.46%), high urban (20.85%) and tribal populations (21.37%), and greater wealth (28.33%). The higher overweight/ obesity prevalence among the wealthier and localities with higher urban population is due to the consumption of high-calorie diets like fried food , cold/ aerated drinks, canned products, refined sugar, a highly processed non-vegetarian diet, and red meat 33. Since these products are loaded with sugar, salt, as well as saturated and trans fats, like palm oil, which are significant risk factors for triglycerides, LDL (low-density lipoprotein), and fat deposition, as observed in tribal and urban populations,15. Findings show substantial geographical variability in terms of overweight/ obesity prevalence. For example, Balasore (18.83%), a non-tribal-dominated district, has the highest concentration of households where all adults are overweight/ obese.

On the contrary, a smaller percentage of households with all- adults overweight/ obese are observed in tribal dominated districts such as Koraput (4.96%), Mayurbhanj (3.96%), Nabarangpur (6.8%), and Malkangiri (6.95%) districts. This spatial differential represents that the prevalence of overweight/ obesity is higher in areas with urban and Western diet influence. The probable reason for such heterogeneity could be the availability and accessibility of high-calorie food and processed products availability and accessibility, which are more common in urban and peripheral areas 34.

This study also revealed that maximum variation in the overweight/ obesity prevalence was found at the household level (0.191), followed by the PSU level (0.09), after controlling for all the individual, household, and contextual factors. Furthermore, the decomposition analysis revealed that socioeconomic inequality in overweight/obesity was driven by adults aged 40–59 years, currently married men, individuals with higher educational-level (6–10 years or more years of schooling), tobacco users, and non-PVTG communities. Dietary factors, including regular consumption of fried foods and aerated drinks, along with urban residence (Notified Area Councils and urban outgrowths) and proximity to towns (11–90 km), also contributed substantially to the observed inequality.ing These key determinants need to be focused to tackle the hot-spots of the overweight/ obesity prevalence among the tribal population of Odisha.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides evidence of the burden of overweight/ obesity among tribal populations of Odisha, including PVTG and non-PVTG groups, who are rarely represented in large-scale national or state health surveys. Using a tribal focused, large dataset and multilevel modelling enabled us to capture the clustering of overweight/ obesity at household and contextual levels. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits causal interpretations of the observed associations. Second, the absence of detailed information on physical activity, genetic factors, occupational workload, and cultural dietary practices restricts a complete understanding of behavioural drivers of overweight/ obesity. Third, dietary exposures relied on self-reported frequency of food consumption at the household level, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias and mask individual dietary habits. Lastly, a predominance of younger adults and those without formal schooling in the sample may influence the observed prevalence patterns and limit generalizability to older or more educated tribal groups. Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable insights into the household-level clustering of overweight/ obesity among adult tribals and highlights essential directions for future health research.

Policy implications

The findings highlight the need to integrate tribal populations into India’s broader non-communicable disease prevention and control agenda 35. Districts with higher tribal concentrations and higher overweight/ obesity prevalence need interventions beyond standard nutrition programs, including awareness campaigns on diet, processed food consumption, and promoting traditional food habits and healthy lifestyles. Moreover, the clustering of overweight/ obesity at the household level highlights the need to adopt healthy food choices, including women who cook meals and men, who usually get rations and groceries. Following their traditional agriculture reliance, the government should facilitate an environment for producing and cultivating healthy and sustainable food 36, so that reliance on healthy food increases. Strengthening community-based platforms such as Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and tribal health workers can help deliver culturally appropriate messages. At the same time, district-level as well as block-level planning should prioritise preventive strategies accounting for the future chronic disease burden. Policies that recognise the diversity within tribal groups, such as PVTGs and non-PVTGs, can ensure more equitable health outcomes.

Conclusion

Aligned with the general population, there is a growing burden of overweight/ obesity among tribal adults in Odisha, influenced by socioeconomic and contextual factors. The clustering of overweight/ obesity within households and district-level heterogeneity highlights the role of shared food environments, dietary choices, and socioeconomic transitions in shaping health risks. Significantly, the protective effects of indigenous diets and lifestyles are diminishing, which could translate into a substantial burden of non-communicable diseases among tribal groups in the coming decades.
