Smooth linearization of nonautonomous dynamics under general dichotomic behaviour
Lucas Backes, Davor Dragičević, Wenmeng Zhang

Abstract
. The main purpose of this paper is to formulate new conditions for smooth linearization of nonautonomous systems with discrete and continuous time. Our results assume that the linear part admits a very general form of dichotomy known as μ \mu -dichotomy and that the associated μ \mu -dichotomy spectrum exhibits appropriate spectral gap and spectral band conditions. We observe that our notion of μ \mu -dichotomy encompasses the classical notions of exponential, polynomial and logarithmic dichotomies as very particular cases. In particular, our result is in sharp contrast to most of the previous results in the literature which assumed that the linear part admits an exponential dichotomy. Our techniques exploit the relationship between μ \mu -dichotomy and exponential dichotomy via a suitable reparametrization of time.

1.
Introduction

One of the most important notions in the theory of dynamical systems is that of (uniform) hyperbolicity. It plays a fundamental role in many situations, like in the study of stability, chaos, and bifurcations. The counterpart of this notion in the case of nonautonomous dynamical systems is the
exponential dichotomy
, a concept whose study dates back to the pioneering work of Perron
[
46
]
. In the discrete time case, we say that the nonautonomous dynamics defined by the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of invertible linear operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
admits an exponential dichotomy
if there exist constants
{K},\lambda>0
and a sequence of projections
(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for every
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
with
m>n
,
A_{n}P_{n}=P_{n+1}A_{n}
and

\displaystyle\|A_{m-1}\cdots A_{n}P_{n}\|\leq{K}e^{-\lambda(m-n)}\;\text{ and }\;\|A^{-1}_{n}\cdots A^{-1}_{m-1}Q_{m}\|\leq{K}e^{-\lambda(m-n)}

(1.1)

where
Q_{m}=\text{\rm Id}-P_{m}
.
As with hyperbolicity for autonomous systems, the notion of an exponential dichotomy has broad applications, including the study of invariant manifolds, invariant foliations, and normal forms
[
8
,
47
,
51
]
, and there exists extensive literature regarding this notion for both finite and infinite-dimensional systems (see, for example,
[
19
,
36
,
44
,
59
]
).

Even though the notion of an exponential dichotomy is of great importance in the study of nonautonomous systems, there are many important classes of systems that do not fit this framework.
In fact, due to the flexibility of the nonautonomous setting, it is easy to construct broad classes of systems which admit a splitting into stable and unstable directions, but with non necessarily exponential rates of contraction and expansion (see, for instance, Example
1
below). In this paper, we are interested in systems with this type of general behavior.
Given any strictly increasing sequence
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of positive numbers with
\mu_{0}=1
and such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mu_{n}=+\infty\;\text{ and }\;\lim_{n\to-\infty}\mu_{n}=0,

we say that
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
admits a
\mu
-dichotomy
if there exist constants and a sequence of projections as in the case of an exponential dichotomy such that, instead of satisfying (
1.1
), they satisfy

\displaystyle\|A_{m-1}\cdots A_{n}P_{n}\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{-\lambda}\;\text{ and }\;\|A^{-1}_{n}\cdots A^{-1}_{m-1}Q_{m}\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{-\lambda}

for
m\geq n
. Note that by taking
\mu_{n}=e^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we recover the notion of an exponential dichotomy. Other particular cases that are widely explored in the literature correspond to the case where the sequence
\mu
has a polynomial or logarithmic behavior. We will expand on this in the following sections.

In what follows, we are interested in studying the problem of smooth linearization of nonautonomous systems whose linear part admits a
\mu
-dichotomy. We will consider systems with both discrete and continuous time. This problem consists of finding a time dependent smooth change of coordinates sending the solutions of the nonlinear system into the solutions of the linear one. As a preliminary to our main results, we formulate smooth linearization results for one-sided systems whose linear part admits an exponential dichotomy; these are derived from existing results for two-sided systems
[
31
,
32
]
. Then, by combining these findings with a suitable time reparametrization (as explored in
[
29
]
), we obtain the desired results in the
\mu
-dichotomic context. A key aspect for the validity of our results is that the associated
\mu
-dichotomy spectrum
exhibits appropriate spectral gap and spectral band conditions.
The importance of our results stems from their broad generality. In fact, we are able to treat in a unified manner several important classes of systems for which results of this type were previously unavailable. We would like to point out the relevant works
[
9
,
40
]
dealing with nonautonomous linearization under
\mu
-dichotomies. However, these works do not deal with smooth or differentiable linearization.

The linearization problem described above is among the most fundamental ones in the theory of dynamical systems and has a long history (of which we do not attempt to provide a complete survey). In the context of autonomous dynamics, the problem of establishing sufficient conditions under which conjugacy exhibits higher regularity was first considered in the pioneering works of Sternberg
[
54
,
55
]
. In the 1950s, he proved that for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C^{k}
hyperbolic diffeomorphisms satisfying some non-resonance conditions up to order
k
admit a
C^{r}
linearization.
We also mention the fundamental works of Hartman
[
37
]
and Grobman
[
35
]
, who proved independently in the 1960s that
C^{1}
hyperbolic diffeomorphisms can be
C^{0}
linearized near the hyperbolic ﬁxed point.
This result shows that if one is only interested in the case
r=0
in Sternberg’s theorem, one can take
k=1
and, moreover, no non-resonance conditions are required (see Section
3
for details). We note that infinite-dimensional versions of this result were obtained independently by Palis
[
42
]
and Pugh
[
48
]
.

Palmer
[
43
]
established the first nonautonomous version of the Grobman-Hartman theorem for continuous-time dynamics, while a discrete-time version was subsequently formulated in
[
2
]
. Both results were obtained by assuming that the associated linear system admits a uniform exponential dichotomy.
The problem of
smooth
linearization for nonautonomous systems has been considered only recently. To our knowledge, the first results in this direction were obtained in
[
15
,
16
]
for uniformly and nonuniformly exponentially stable systems. Moreover, in
[
23
]
, the authors established a Sternberg-type theorem in the setting of a uniform exponential dichotomy with continuous time.
More relevant to our context are the works
[
31
,
32
]
(see also
[
28
]
) where
C^{1}
, differentiable (at 0) and Hölder linearization results were obtained in the setting of a nonuniform exponential dichotomy under some spectral gap and spectral band conditions. As previously emphasized, these results will play a central role in our proofs.

Finally, we mention recent results
[
5
,
14
,
17
,
38
]
dealing with the smooth linearization of nonautonomous systems in the absence of any kind of dichotomy, non-resonance or spectral gap assumptions. However, as observed in
[
5
]
, such results in the setting of
[
31
]
(and consequently also in the setting of the present paper) can fail to be applicable or could yield weaker results.

This paper is organized as follows. The first three sections are dedicated to discrete-time dynamical systems. More precisely, in Section
2
, we introduce the notions of strong
\mu
-dichotomy and the strong
\mu
-dichotomy spectrum, exploring the relationship between these concepts and exponential dichotomy via time reparametrization. In Section
3
, we present results concerning
C^{1}
, differentiable (at 0), and Hölder linearization under the assumption of an exponential dichotomy on the half-line. Section
4
is devoted to establishing the
C^{1}
, differentiable (at 0), and Hölder linearization results under the assumption of a
\mu
-dichotomy. At the end of Section
4
, we briefly explain how these results can be extended to the infinite-dimensional setting. Finally, in Section
5
, we provide continuous-time versions of the results established in Section
4
.
Appendix
A
serves as an addendum to the proof of the main results in
[
31
]
. In this section, we strengthen the properties of the conjugacies presented in the aforementioned work and provide an argument that was missing in the original paper.

Note:
We observe that a previous version of this paper was written in the particular case when the linear part of the system admits a (nonuniform) polynomial dichotomy. That version is available on ArXiv (
https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04804
) and will remain as a permanent preprint.

\mu
-dichotomy and dichotomy spectrum

In this section, we introduce the notions of
\mu
-dichotomy and
\mu
-dichotomy spectrum, followed by a discussion of their fundamental properties. We start by fixing some notation.

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of all natural numbers,
\mathbb{Z}
the set of integers,
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
the set of nonnegative integers, and
\mathbb{Z}^{-}
the set of nonpositive integers. Given
J\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}^{-},\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
and a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in J}
of invertible operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us consider the associated nonautonomous linear system

x_{n+1}=A_{n}x_{n},\quad n,n+1\in J

(2.1)

whose evolution family is given by

\mathcal{A}(m,n):=\begin{cases}A_{m-1}\cdots A_{n},&m>n;\\
\text{\rm Id},&m=n;\\
A_{m}^{-1}\cdots A_{n-1}^{-1},&m<n,\end{cases}

(2.2)

where
Id
denotes the identity operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.1.
Growth rates and
\mu
-dichotomy for discrete time dynamics

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\in J}
be a strictly increasing sequence of positive numbers with
\mu_{0}=1
and such that

•

for
J=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mu_{n}=+\infty;

(2.3)

•

for
J=\mathbb{Z}^{-}
,

\lim_{n\to-\infty}\mu_{n}=0;

(2.4)

•

for
J=\mathbb{Z}
, conditions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are satisfied.

Any sequence
\mu
satisfying these properties is called a
growth rate
.

Definition 1
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in J}
be a sequence of invertible operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
(A_{n})_{n\in J}
admits a
\mu
-dichotomy
if there exists a family of projections
(P_{n})_{n\in J}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constants
K,\lambda>0
such that

•

for
n,n+1\in J
,

A_{n}P_{n}=P_{n+1}A_{n};

(2.5)

•

for
m,n\in J
with
m\geq n
,

\|\mathcal{A}(m,n)P_{n}\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{-\lambda}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathcal{A}(n,m)Q_{m}\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{-\lambda}\quad

(2.6)

where
Q_{n}:=\text{\rm Id}-P_{n}
.

In addition, if there exists
a\geq\lambda
such that

\|\mathcal{A}(m,n)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathcal{A}(n,m)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}

(2.7)

for
m\geq n
, we say that
(A_{n})_{n\in J}
admits a
strong
\mu
-dichotomy
.

Remark 1
.

We observe that the notion of
\mu
-dichotomy generalizes several well-known notions of dichotomy. For example, in the case where

, by taking
\mu_{n}=e^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recover the notion of

(see
[
22
]
); by taking
\mu_{n}=1+n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recover the notion of

(see
[

,
26
,
27
]
); by taking
\mu_{n}=\ln(e+n)
,

, we recover the notion of
logarithmic dichotomy
(see
[
52
]
).

Remark 2
.

We emphasize that several versions of the
\mu
-dichotomy for both discrete- and continuous-time dynamics have already appeared in the literature, and various properties of these systems have already been investigated. For instance, there are studies addressing invariant manifolds

2.2.
Time rescaling

We now recall a time rescaling procedure described in
[
29
]
. Given a growth rate
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
, we can associate to it the continuous, strictly increasing function
\widetilde{\mu}\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
given by

\widetilde{\mu}(t)=\begin{cases}\mu_{n}&\text{ if }\ t=n\\
\mu_{n}+(t-n)(\mu_{n+1}-\mu_{n})&\text{ if }\ n<t<n+1\end{cases},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

One can easily check that this is an invertible function and that its inverse
{\widetilde{\mu}}^{-1}\colon[1,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is the continuous, strictly increasing function given by

\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(t)=\begin{cases}n&\text{ if }\ t=\mu_{n}\\
n+\dfrac{t-\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n+1}-\mu_{n}}&\text{ if }\ \mu_{n}<t<\mu_{n+1}\end{cases},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

Using these maps, we associate with the system (
2.1
) the family of invertible linear operators
(B^{\mu}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

B^{\mu}_{n}=\mathcal{A}\left(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\ \lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1\right),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}

(2.8)

and consider the nonautonomous linear system

y_{n+1}=B^{\mu}_{n}\,y_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.9)

It is easy to see that the evolution family associated to (
2.9
) is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}^{\mu}(m,n)=\mathcal{A}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{m-1})\rfloor+1,\,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1).\end{split}

We have the following relationship between systems (
2.1
) and (
2.9
).

Theorem 1

(Corollary 3.3 of
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a growth rate satisfying

\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\mu_{n}}\leq\theta,\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(2.10)

for some
\theta\geq 1
and consider a sequence of invertible linear operators
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which there exist
K,a>0
such that (
2.7
) is satisfied. Then, there exists
K^{\prime}>0
such that

\|\mathcal{B}^{\mu}(m,n)\|\leq K^{\prime}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathcal{B}^{\mu}(n,m)\|\leq K^{\prime}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}

for
m\geq n
. Moreover, the following assertions are equivalent:

i)

(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a
\mu
-dichotomy;

ii)

(B^{\mu}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
admits an exponential dichotomy

Remark 3
.

It is easy to see that exponential, polynomial and logarithmic growth rates (recall Remark
1

).

3.
Smooth linearization under exponential dichotomy

It is well known that
C^{0}
conjugacy in general does not preserve important dynamical properties such as characteristic directions and smoothness of invariant manifolds. Preserving these properties requires at least
C^{1}
conjugacy. In the 1970s, an effort was made by Belitskii in
[
10
]
to establish a
C^{1}
linearization of
C^{2}
hyperbolic diffeomorphisms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
under certain second-order non-resonance conditions. This result requires much weaker conditions on smoothness and non-resonance than the Sternberg’s theorem in the
C^{1}
case. Moreover, Hartman’s example given in
[
37
]
shows that Belitskii’s non-resonance conditions cannot be avoided.

Since the 1990s, two independent research directions emerged. In one direction, the goal was to extend Belitskii’s result to the infinite-dimensional setting, while the other direction was concerned with differentiable (at the fixed point
0
) linearization in the absence of non-resonance conditions.
Concerning the first direction of research, we mention the work
[
58
]
, which established
C^{1}
linearization result on Banach spaces under appropriate spectral gap and spectral band conditions for the linear part. This result was further extended to the nonautonomous setting in
[
31
]
. In the second direction, van Strien
[
56
]
claimed the linearization result for
C^{2}
diffeomorphisms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without any non-resonance conditions. The conclusion was that there exists a conjugacy which is simultaneously differentiable at 0 and Hölder
continuous near 0 (weaker than
C^{1}
smoothness). However, van Strien’s proof was found to be incorrect (see
[
49
]
). In
[
32
]
, the authors gave a correct proof of
van Strien’s result by using different methods.

In what follows, we present two linearization results for nonautonomous dynamics under the assumption that the linear part admits an exponential dichotomy. The first result (see Theorem
3
) gives conditions under which the conjugacies are
C^{1}
, while the second result (see Theorem
4
) concerns the case when the conjugacies are differentiable at
0
and locally Hölder
continuous. In contrast to the existing result in the literature, the novelty is that Theorems
3
and
4
are concerned with one-sided dynamics (which will make them applicable to the problem of linearization under
\mu
-dichotomic behavior).

3.1.
C^{1}
linearization under exponential dichotomy

We start by recalling that a strong exponential dichotomy is just a strong
\mu
-dichotomy, as described in Definition
1
, with the growth rate
\mu
given by
\mu_{n}=e^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathbb{B}=(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
be a sequence of invertible operators on

Suppose that
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}

a_{1}\leq b_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\leq b_{k}<0<a_{k+1}\leq b_{k+1}<\ldots<a_{r}\leq b_{r},

for some

\begin{cases}a_{k+1}-b_{k}>\max\{b_{r},-a_{1}\},\\
b_{i}-a_{i}\leq-b_{k}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k,\\
b_{i}-a_{i}\leq a_{k+1}\text{ for }k+1\leq i\leq r.\\
\end{cases}

(3.2)

Moreover, let
f_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that

\mathbb{R}^{d}=S_{r}\oplus V_{r},\ S_{r}=S_{r-1}\oplus V_{r-1},\ldots,S_{3}=S_{2}\oplus V_{2}.

Hence,

\mathbb{R}^{d}=V_{r}\oplus V_{r-1}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{2}\oplus S_{2}.

Define an operator
B
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle B\rvert_{S_{2}}=e^{a_{1}}\text{\rm Id}_{S_{2}},\ B\rvert_{V_{2}}=e^{a_{2}}\text{\rm Id}_{V_{2}},\ldots,B\rvert_{V_{r}}=e^{a_{r}}\text{\rm Id}_{V_{r}},

(3.5)

where
\text{\rm Id}_{V}
denotes the identity map on a subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover,
set
B_{n}:=B
for
n<0
and consider
\mathbb{B}^{\prime}:=(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}
be defined as in Definition
2
with
\mu_{n}=e^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
(recall Remark
4
).
We now claim that
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}=\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}
. As mentioned above,
S_{i}
does not depend on
c_{i}
. Thus, for any

\tilde{c}_{1}\in(-\infty,a_{1}),~~\tilde{c}_{i}\in(b_{i-1},a_{i})\text{ for }i\in\{2,\ldots,r\},\text{ and }\tilde{c}_{r+1}\in(b_{r},\infty),

we find that the sequence
(\frac{1}{e^{\tilde{c}_{i}}}B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
also admits a strong exponential dichotomy with projections
\tilde{P}_{n}^{i}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

\Ima\tilde{P}_{0}^{i}=S_{i}
. Moreover, it is easy to see from (
3.5
) that the sequence
\left(\frac{1}{e^{\tilde{c}_{i}}}B_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}}
admits a strong exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{Z}^{-}
with projections
\tilde{P}_{n}^{i}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}
such that
\Ker\tilde{P}_{0}^{-}=V_{i}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{r}
. Since

\mathbb{R}^{d}=S_{i}\oplus V_{i}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{r},

we conclude from the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 2.3]
that
(\frac{1}{e^{\tilde{c}_{i}}}B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
admits a strong exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{Z}
.
This implies that
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}
, i.e.,
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}\subset\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}
. On the other hand, we trivially have that
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}\subset\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}
, proving that the two spectra coincide, i.e.,

\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}^{\prime}}=\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}[a_{i},b_{i}].

The conclusion of the theorem now follows readily from Theorem
9
given in Appendix A (which is a corollary of
[
31
, Theorem 2]
).
∎

, we can also obtain the following result, where we remove the spectral gap condition, i.e., the first inequality of (
3.2
), and obtain the simultaneously differentiable (at 0) and Hölder linearization.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{B}=(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
is as in the statement of Theorem
3
and that
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}
has the form (
2.12
) and satisfies (

) and

b_{i}-a_{i}\leq-b_{k}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k\;\text{ and }\;b_{j}-a_{j}\leq a_{k+1}\text{ for }k+1\leq j\leq r.

(3.6)

Let
f_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

C^{1}

,

\displaystyle\|h_{n}(x)-h_{n}(y)\|\!\leq\!\tilde{L}\|x-y\|^{\alpha_{1}}\;\text{ and }\;\|h_{n}^{-1}(x)-h_{n}^{-1}(y)\|\!\leq\!\tilde{L}\|x-y\|^{\alpha_{1}}

for

satisfying

and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

exactly as in the proof of Theorem

. Then, it remains to apply
[

, Lemma 4]
to

(for

,

and

4.
Smooth linearization under
\mu
-dichotomy

In this section, we present our main results concerning the linearization under
\mu
-dichotomic behavior.

4.1.
C^{1}
linearization under
\mu
-dichotomy

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
be a growth rate that satisfies (
2.10
) for some
\theta\geq 1
and
\mathbb{A}=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of invertible linear operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that admits a strong
\mu
-dichotomy. Furthermore, suppose
\Sigma_{\mu D,\mathbb{A}}
has the form (
2.13
)
with
1\leq r\leq d
and that the numbers
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
satisfy (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).
Finally, let
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
C^{1}
maps
g_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the following:

•

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

g_{n}(0)=0\;\text{ and }\;Dg_{n}(0)=0;

(4.1)

•

there exists
c>0
such that

\|Dg_{n}(x)\|\leq c\frac{\mu_{n}^{\prime}}{\mu_{n}},\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$,}

(4.2)

where
\mu_{n}^{\prime}=\mu_{n+1}-\mu_{n}
;

•

there exists
M>0
such that

\|Dg_{n}(x)-Dg_{n}(y)\|\leq M\frac{\mu_{n}^{\prime}}{\mu_{n}}\|x-y\|,\quad\text{for $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(4.3)

Then, provided that
c
is sufficiently small, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms
\psi_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\psi_{n+1}\circ(A_{n}+g_{n})=A_{n}\circ\psi_{n},\;\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},

(4.4)

and there exist
T,\rho>0
so that

\|D\psi_{n}(x)\|\leq T\quad\text{and}\quad\|D\psi_{n}^{-1}(x)\|\leq T,

(4.5)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|x\|\leq\rho
.

Proof.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G_{n}:=A_{n}+g_{n}
. We start observing that, whenever
c>0
given by (
4.2
) is small enough,
G_{n}
is a bijection (a diffeomorphism, in fact) for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For this purpose, note that (
4.2
) implies that

\|g_{n}(x)-g_{n}(y)\|\leq c\frac{\mu_{n}^{\prime}}{\mu_{n}}\|x-y\|

(4.6)

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now, given
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we observe that the map
x\mapsto A_{n}^{-1}y-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x)
is a contraction on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Indeed, given
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, using (
2.7
), (
2.10
) and (
4.6
), we have that

\begin{split}\|A_{n}^{-1}y-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x_{1})-(A_{n}^{-1}y-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x_{2}))\|&=\|A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x_{1})-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x_{2})\|\\
&\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\|g_{n}(x_{1})-g_{n}(x_{2})\|\\
&\leq cK\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{n}^{\prime}}{\mu_{n}}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\\
&\leq cK\left(\frac{\mu_{n+1}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a+1}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\\
&\leq cK\theta^{a+1}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|.\end{split}

Therefore, provided that
cK\theta^{a+1}<1
, we see that

x\mapsto A_{n}^{-1}y-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x)
is a contraction and, in particular, has a unique fixed point. That is, there exists a unique
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
x=A_{n}^{-1}y-A_{n}^{-1}g_{n}(x)
, which is equivalent to
G_{n}(x)=A_{n}x+g_{n}(x)=y
. This shows that
G_{n}
is a bijection as claimed. In particular, we can consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(m,n):=\begin{cases}G_{m-1}\circ\ldots\circ G_{n},&m>n;\\
\text{\rm Id},&m=n;\\
G_{m}^{-1}\circ\ldots\circ G_{n-1}^{-1},&m<n.\end{cases}

Let
\mathbb{B}=(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=(B^{\mu}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence given by (
2.8
). By Theorem
1
, we have that
\mathbb{B}
admits an exponential dichotomy and satisfies (
2.11
).
Moreover, Theorem
2
gives
\Sigma_{ED,\mathbb{B}}=\Sigma_{\mu D,\mathbb{A}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by

f_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor}\mathcal{A}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,j+1)g_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)(x)).

(4.7)

Our objective now is to show that
\mathbb{B}
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3
so that we can translate the conclusions of that theorem into our setting. We start with some auxiliary observations.

Note that, by (
2.10
), for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have

\frac{\tilde{\mu}(t+1)}{\tilde{\mu}(t)}=\frac{\mu_{t+1}}{\mu_{t}}\leq\theta

and, for each
t\in[0,+\infty)\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have

\frac{\tilde{\mu}(t+1)}{\tilde{\mu}(t)}=\frac{\mu_{r+1}+(t-r)(\mu_{r+2}-\mu_{r+1})}{\mu_{r}+(t-r)(\mu_{r+1}-\mu_{r})}\leq\frac{\mu_{r+2}}{\mu_{r}}=\frac{\mu_{r+2}}{\mu_{r+1}}\frac{\mu_{r+1}}{\mu_{r}}\leq\theta^{2},

where
r=\lfloor t\rfloor\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Consequently, since
\theta\geq 1
, we conclude that

\frac{\tilde{\mu}(t+1)}{\tilde{\mu}(t)}\leq\theta^{2},\quad\text{for all}\ t\in[0,+\infty).

(4.8)

We also have the following useful estimate from
[
30
, Lemma 3.1]
:

\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\leq\theta\log\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right).

(4.9)

Next, we claim that there exists
\tilde{a}\geq a
such that

\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{\max(m,n)}}{\mu_{\min(m,n)}}\right)^{\tilde{a}},\quad\text{for $m,n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

(4.10)

Suppose initially that
m\geq n
. Observe that

\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)=\mathcal{A}(m,n)x+\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)g_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)),

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
m\geq n\geq 1
and thus

D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)=\mathcal{A}(m,n)+\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x).

(4.11)

Consequently, using (
2.7
) and (
4.2
), we get

\begin{split}\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)\|&\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}+Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j+1}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)\|\\
&\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}+Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)\|.\end{split}

Hence,

\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{m}}\right)^{a}\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)\|\leq K+Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{a}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)\|.

(4.12)

Now, in order to get the desired estimate, we need the following discrete-version of Gronwall’s lemma (see, e.g.
[
33
, Lemma 4.32]
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
(u_{m})_{m\geq n}
and
(z_{m})_{m\geq n}
are two nonnegative sequences that

u_{m}\leq K\{\alpha+\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}z_{j}u_{j}\}

for
m\geq n
.
Then

\displaystyle u_{m}\leq K\alpha\,e^{K\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}z_{j}},\quad\forall m\geq n.

Thus, applying Gronwall’s lemma to (
4.12
) and using (
4.9
) we get that

\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}e^{Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}}\leq K\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a+Kc\theta}.

Therefore, (
4.10
) holds with
\tilde{a}:=a+Kc\theta
. The case where
m<n
can be obtained similarly.

As a final auxiliary result, we need to estimate

\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(y)\|

for
m\geq n
. By (
4.11
) we have that

\begin{split}&D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(y)\\
&=\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)\\
&\phantom{=}-\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\\
&=\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))(D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))\\
&\phantom{=}-\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)(Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))-Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y).\end{split}

Thus, using (
2.7
) and (
4.2
), we get that

\begin{split}&\left\|\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))(D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))\right\|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left\|\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))(D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))\right\|\\
&\leq Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j+1}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\|\\
&\leq Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\|.\end{split}

Moreover, (
2.7
), (
4.3
), (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) imply that

\begin{split}&\left\|\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\mathcal{A}(m,j+1)(Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y))-Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x))D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\right\|\\
&\leq KM\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j+1}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\|\cdot\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\|\\
&\leq K^{3}M\|x-y\|\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\left(\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2\tilde{a}}\\
&\leq K^{3}M\|x-y\|\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2\tilde{a}}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\\
&\leq K^{3}M\theta\|x-y\|\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2\tilde{a}}\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{a}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right).\end{split}

Combining these observations, we conclude that, for
m\geq n
,

\begin{split}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{m}}\right)^{a}\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(y)\|&\leq K^{3}M\theta\|x-y\|\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2\tilde{a}}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)\\
&\phantom{\leq}+Kc\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\|D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(j,n)(y)\|.\end{split}

Thus, using Gronwall’s lemma once again, we get that

\begin{split}\|D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(m,n)(y)\|&\leq K^{3}M\theta\|x-y\|\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{3\tilde{a}+Kc\theta}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{m}}{\mu_{n}}\right)\\
\end{split}

(4.13)

for
m\geq n
.

We now estimate the size of
\|Df_{n}(x)\|
. Note that

\begin{split}&Df_{n}(x)=\\
&\sum_{j=\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor}\mathcal{A}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,j+1)Dg_{j}(\mathcal{G}(j,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)(x))D\mathcal{G}(j,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)(x).\end{split}

Thus, using (
2.7
), (
4.2
), (
4.9
), and (
4.10
), we get that

\begin{split}\|Df_{n}(x)\|&\leq K^{2}c\sum_{j=\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor}\left(\frac{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1}}{\mu_{j+1}}\right)^{a}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\left(\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}}\right)^{\tilde{a}}\\
&\leq K^{2}c\left(\frac{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1}}{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}}\right)^{a+\tilde{a}}\sum_{j=\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor}\frac{\mu_{j}^{\prime}}{\mu_{j}}\\
&\leq\theta K^{2}c\left(\frac{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1}}{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}}\right)^{a+\tilde{a}}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1}}{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}}\right)\\
&\leq c\theta^{1+2(a+\tilde{a})}K^{2}e^{a+\tilde{a}}\log(\theta^{2}e)\end{split}

where in the last inequality, we have used that (recall the properties of
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}
given in Section
2.2
and (
4.8
))

\begin{split}\frac{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1}}{\mu_{\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1}}\leq\frac{\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})+1)}{e^{n-1}}=\frac{\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})+1)}{\widetilde{\mu}(\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n}))}\ \frac{e^{n}}{e^{n-1}}\leq e\theta^{2}.\end{split}

(4.14)

Hence, there exists
C>0
such that

\|Df_{n}(x)\|\leq cC,\quad\text{for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

Finally, recalling (
4.7
), we have that

f_{n}=\mathcal{G}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)-\mathcal{A}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1).

(4.15)

Thus,

\begin{split}&Df_{n}(x)-Df_{n}(y)\\
&=D\mathcal{G}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)(x)-D\mathcal{G}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)(y).\end{split}

Consequently, by (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), there exists
\tilde{M}>0
such that

\|Df_{n}(x)-Df_{n}(y)\|\leq\tilde{M}\|x-y\|,\quad\text{for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

Combining the previous observations, we conclude that
\mathbb{B}
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3
. Thus, there is a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms
h_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

h_{n+1}\circ(B_{n}+f_{n})=B_{n}\circ h_{n}

(4.16)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, there exist
T_{1},\rho_{1}>0
such that

\|Dh_{n}(x)\|\leq T_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\|Dh_{n}^{-1}(x)\|\leq T_{1},

(4.17)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|x\|\leq\rho_{1}
.
In particular, recalling (
2.8
) and (
4.15
), we get that

h_{n+1}\circ\mathcal{G}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)=\mathcal{A}(\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n})\rfloor+1,\lfloor\widetilde{\mu}^{-1}(e^{n-1})\rfloor+1)\circ h_{n},

We now apply Theorem

.

5.

In this section, we present versions of Theorems

Let us consider a linear nonautonomous equation

x^{\prime}=A(t)x,\quad t\geq 1,

where

(5.2)

5.1.
Growth rates and
\mu
-dichotomies for continuous time dynamics

We say that a function

if it is strictly increasing and
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mu(t)=+\infty
. Moreover, if
\mu
is differentiable, we say that it is a

.

Definition 3
.

We say that (
5.1
) (or equivalently, that the evolution family
(T(t,s))_{t,s\geq 1}
) admits a
\mu
-dichotomy
if there exist
K,\lambda>0
and a family of projections
P(t)
,
t\geq 1
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the following properties hold:

•

for
t\geq s\geq 1
,

P(t)T(t,s)=T(t,s)P(s);

(5.3)

•

for
t\geq s
,

\|T(t,s)P(s)\|\leq K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\bigg)^{-\lambda},

(5.4)

and

\|T(s,t)Q(t)\|\leq K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\bigg)^{-\lambda},

(5.5)

where
Q(t)=\text{\rm Id}-P(t)
.

In addition, if there exists
a\geq\lambda
such that for
t\geq s
,

\|T(t,s)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\right)^{a}\quad\text{and}\quad\|T(s,t)\|\leq K\left(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\right)^{a},

(5.6)

we say that (
5.1
) admits a

\mu
-dichotomy
.

Analogously to the discrete time case, by taking
\mu(t)=e^{t}

(strong) exponential dichotomy
; by taking
\mu(t)=1+t

\mu(t)=\ln(e+t)

(strong) logarithmic dichotomy
.

In the next proposition, we present a relationship between the dynamics of (
5.1
) and a suitable discretization of it.

•

) admits a strong
\mu
-dichotomy;

•

Proof.

Taking

Similarly, (

\tau\in\mathbb{R}

x^{\prime}=\bigg(A(t)-\tau\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(t)}\text{\rm Id}\bigg)x,\quad t\geq 1

(5.10)

does not admit a strong
\mu
-dichotomy.
\Sigma_{\mu D,A(\cdot)}
is called the
strong
\mu
-dichotomy spectrum
of (
5.1
).

Given
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, let us consider

T_{\tau}(t,s)=\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\bigg)^{-\tau}T(t,s).

Then, differentiating with respect to
t
,

\begin{split}T_{\tau}(t,s)^{\prime}&=-\tau\left(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\right)^{-\tau-1}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(s)}T(t,s)+\left(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\right)^{-\tau}T(t,s)^{\prime}\\
&=\left(A(t)-\tau\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(t)}\text{\rm Id}\right)\left(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(s)}\right)^{-\tau}T(t,s)\\
&=\left(A(t)-\tau\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(t)}\text{\rm Id}\right)T_{\tau}(t,s),\\
\end{split}

and
T_{\tau}(t,s)
is the evolution family of (
5.10
). Moreover, we have that

T_{\tau}(n+1,n)=\bigg(\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\mu(n)}\bigg)^{-\tau}T(n+1,n),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Combining this observation with Proposition
1
, we see that, if
T(t,s)
satisfies (
5.6
) and
\mu
satisfies (
5.7
) for some
\theta\geq 1
, then the following properties are equivalent:

•

the sequence
\left(\left(\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\mu(n)}\right)^{-\tau}T(n+1,n)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not admit a strong
\mu
-dichotomy;

•

(
5.10
) does not admit a strong
\mu
-dichotomy.

The following result is a direct consequence of the above fact.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that there exist
K,a>0
such that (
5.6
) holds and that
\mu
satisfies (
5.7
) for some
\theta\geq 1
. Then

\Sigma_{\mu D,A(\cdot)}=\Sigma_{\mu D,\mathbb{A}},

where the sequence
\mathbb{A}=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is given by (
5.8
). In particular,
\Sigma_{\mu D,A(\cdot)}
is given by (
2.13
) with
1\leq r\leq d
and
a_{1}\leq b_{1}<a_{2}\leq\ldots<a_{r}\leq b_{r}
.

5.3.
C^{1}
linearization under
\mu
-dichotomy

The following is a version of Theorem
5
in the continuous time setting.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mu
be a differentiable growth rate satisfying (
5.7
) for some
\theta\geq 1
and suppose that (
5.1
) admits a strong
\mu
-dichotomy. Assume that
\Sigma_{\mu D,A(\cdot)}
is given by (
2.13
), where
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
satisfy (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).
Furthermore, suppose that
f\colon[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
C^{1}
map satisfying the following conditions:

•

for
t\geq 1
,

f(t,0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad D_{x}f(t,0)=0;

(5.11)

•

there exists
\eta>0
such that

\|D_{x}f(t,x)\|\leq\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(t)},\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$};

(5.12)

•

there exists
L>0
such that

\|D_{x}f(t,x)-D_{x}f(t,y)\|\leq L\frac{\mu^{\prime}(t)}{\mu(t)}\|x-y\|,\quad\text{for $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

(5.13)

Then, provided that
\eta
is sufficiently small, there exist
C^{1}
maps
H,G\colon[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the following properties:

i)

if
t\mapsto x(t)
is a solution of (
5.2
), then
t\mapsto H(t,x(t))
is a solution of (
5.1
);

ii)

if
t\mapsto x(t)
is a solution of (
5.1
), then
t\mapsto G(t,x(t))
is a solution of (
5.2
);

iii)

for
t\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

H(t,G(t,x))=x\quad\text{and}\quad G(t,H(t,x))=x;

iv)

there exist
R,\zeta>0
such that

\|D_{x}H(t,x)\|\leq R\;\text{ and }\;\|D_{x}G(t,x)\|\leq R,

for every
t\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\|x\|\leq\zeta
.

Proof.

The general idea of the proof consists of using the discretization of (
5.1
) given by (
5.8
) to obtain Theorem
7
as a consequence of Theorem
5
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by (
5.8
). Combining our assumptions with Proposition
1
and Corollary
1
, we easily see that
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
5
. Let
t\mapsto\varphi(t,t_{0};x_{0})
denote the solution of (
5.2
) satisfying
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
. Hence, by the variation of constants formula, we have that

\displaystyle\varphi(t,n;x)=T(t,n)x+\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))\,dr.

(5.14)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, set

g_{n}(x)=\varphi(n+1,n;x)-A_{n}x=\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))\,dr.

(5.15)

We will now verify that this sequence of
C^{1}
maps
g_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies all the assumptions of Theorem
5
.

It follows from (
5.11
) that
\varphi(t,t_{0};0)=0
, and thus
g_{n}(0)=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,

\begin{split}Dg_{n}(0)&=\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)D_{x}f(r,0)D_{x}\varphi(r,n;0)\,dr=0,\end{split}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We conclude that (
4.1
) is true.

Observe that

D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)=T(t,n)+\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\,dr

(5.16)

for
t\geq n
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, from (
5.6
), (
5.7
) and (
5.12
) we obtain that for every
t\in[n,n+1]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\begin{split}\lVert D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)\rVert&\leq K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(n)}\bigg)^{a}+\int_{n}^{t}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\lVert D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\rVert\,dr\\
&\leq K\theta^{a}+\int_{n}^{t}K\eta\theta^{a}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\lVert D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\rVert\,dr.\end{split}

Hence, from Gronwall’s lemma it follows that

\lVert D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)\rVert\leq K\theta^{a}e^{\int_{n}^{t}K\eta\theta^{a}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\,dr}
. Thus, since (using (
5.7
))

\int_{n}^{t}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\,dr\leq\ln\mu(n+1)-\ln\mu(n)\leq\ln\theta\leq\theta,

(5.17)

we get

\lVert D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)\rVert\leq\hat{M}

(5.18)

for every
t\in[n,n+1]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\hat{M}:=K\theta^{a}e^{K\eta\theta^{a+1}}
(which, in particular, is independent of
n
).
On the other hand, note that

Dg_{n}(x)=\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\,dr.

(5.19)

Then, combining (
5.6
), (
5.7
), (
5.12
), (
5.18
) with (
5.19
) we get

\begin{split}\lVert Dg_{n}(x)\rVert&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\hat{M}\,dr\\
&\leq\frac{K\hat{M}\theta^{a}\eta}{\mu(n)}\int_{n}^{n+1}\mu^{\prime}(r)dr=K\hat{M}\theta^{a}\eta\left(\frac{\mu(n+1)-\mu(n)}{\mu(n)}\right),\end{split}

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We conclude that (
4.2
) holds with
c:=K\hat{M}\theta^{a}\eta>0
, which can be made sufficiently small by taking
\eta
sufficiently small.

Next, we observe that (
5.16
) implies that

\begin{split}&D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(t,n;y)\\
&=\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\,dr\\
&\phantom{=}-\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;y))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\,dr\\
&=\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))(D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y))\,dr\\
&\phantom{=}+\int_{n}^{t}T(t,r)(D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))-D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;y)))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\,dr.\end{split}

Hence, it follows from (
5.6
), (
5.7
), (
5.12
), (
5.13
), (
5.17
) and (
5.18
) (together with the
mean-value theorem) that

\begin{split}&\lVert D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(t,n;y)\rVert\\
&\leq\int_{n}^{t}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}L\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\lVert\varphi(r,n;x)-\varphi(r,n;y)\rVert\hat{M}\,dr\\
&\phantom{=}+\int_{n}^{t}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(t)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\lVert D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\rVert\,dr\\
&\leq KL\theta^{a+1}\hat{M}^{2}\|x-y\|+\int_{n}^{t}K\theta^{a}\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\lVert D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\rVert\,dr,\end{split}

for
t\in[n,n+1]
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Gronwall’s inequality again, one can conclude that there exists
\check{M}\geq 1
such that

\lVert D_{x}\varphi(t,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(t,n;y)\rVert\leq\check{M}\lVert x-y\rVert,

(5.20)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[n,n+1]
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Moreover, since

\begin{split}&Dg_{n}(x)-Dg_{n}(y)\\
&=\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)\,dr\\
&\phantom{=}-\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;y))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\,dr\\
&=\int_{n}^{n+1}T(n+1,r)D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))(D_{x}\varphi(r,n;x)-D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y))\,dr\\
&\phantom{=}+\int_{n}^{n\!+\!1}T(n+1,r)(D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;x))\!-\!D_{x}f(r,\varphi(r,n;y)))D_{x}\varphi(r,n;y)\,dr,\\
\end{split}

we obtain from (
5.6
), (
5.7
), (
5.12
), (
5.13
), (
5.18
) and (
5.20
) (together with the mean-value
theorem) that

\begin{split}&\lVert Dg_{n}(x)-Dg_{n}(y)\rVert\\
&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}\eta\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\check{M}\lVert x-y\rVert\,dr\\
&\phantom{\leq}+\int_{n}^{n+1}K\bigg(\frac{\mu(n+1)}{\mu(r)}\bigg)^{a}L\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\hat{M}\lVert x-y\rVert\hat{M}\,dr\\
&\leq\frac{K\check{M}\theta^{a}\eta}{\mu(n)}\|x-y\|\int_{n}^{n+1}\mu^{\prime}(r)dr+\frac{KL\hat{M}^{2}\theta^{a}}{\mu(n)}\|x-y\|\int_{n}^{n+1}\mu^{\prime}(r)dr\\
&=\left(K\check{M}\theta^{a}\eta+KL\hat{M}^{2}\theta^{a}\right)\frac{\mu(n+1)-\mu(n)}{\mu(n)}\|x-y\|\\
\end{split}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we conclude that (
4.3
) holds, and, therefore, all the hypotheses of Theorem
5
are satisfied for
\mathbb{A}=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined above.

Thus, provided that
\eta
is sufficiently small, by Theorem
5
there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
C^{1}
-diffeomorphisms
\psi_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) (for some
T,\rho>0
).
We now set

H(t,x)=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,t;x))\;\text{ and }\;G(t,x)=\varphi(t,n;\psi_{n}^{-1}(T(n,t)x)),

(5.21)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[n,n+1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In order to verify that
H(t,x)
and
G(t,x)
satisfy properties i) and ii) of our statement, we see from (
5.15
) that

\varphi(n+1,n;x)=A_{n}x+g_{n}(x)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Combining this fact with (
4.4
) we get that

\begin{split}H(t,\varphi(t,1;x))&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,t;\varphi(t,1;x)))\\
&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,1;x)))\\
&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,n-1;\varphi(n-1,1;x)))\\
&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}((A_{n-1}+g_{n-1})(\varphi(n-1,1;x)))\\
&=T(t,n)(A_{n-1}\circ\psi_{n-1}(\varphi(n-1,1;x)))\\
&=T(t,n-1)\psi_{n-1}(\varphi(n-1,1;x)),\\
\end{split}

for every
t\in[n,n+1)
and
n>1
. The formula above also gives us that

T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,1;x)))=T(t,n-1)\psi_{n-1}(\varphi(n-1,1;x)).

Then, proceeding recursively, we can conclude that

H(t,\varphi(t,1;x))=T(t,1)\psi_{1}(x)

for
t\geq 1
. In particular,
H(t,\varphi(t,1;x))
is a solution of (
5.1
) which proves i). Similarly, we can prove that
G
satisfies property ii) of our statement.

Now, we observe that

\begin{split}H(t,G(t,x))&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,t;G(t,x)))\\
&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,t;\varphi(t,n;\psi_{n}^{-1}(T(n,t)x)))\\
&=T(t,n)\psi_{n}(\psi_{n}^{-1}(T(n,t)x))\\
&=T(t,n)T(n,t)x=x,\end{split}

for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in[n,n+1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we get
H(t,G(t,x))=x
for every
t\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Similarly, we can show that
G(t,H(t,x))=x
for every
t\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consequently, conclusion iii) of our statement also holds true. It remains to show that iv) is satisfied.

Proceeding as we did to obtain (
5.18
), we find that there exists
\hat{M}>0
such that

\|D_{x}\varphi(n,t;x)\|\leq\hat{M}

(5.22)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[n,n+1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, recalling that
\varphi(n,t;0)=0
(which is a consequence of (
5.11
)) and combining (
5.22
) with the mean-value theorem, we get

\|\varphi(n,t;x)\|\leq\hat{M}\|x\|.

(5.23)

Therefore, if
\|x\|\leq\frac{\rho}{\hat{M}}
we get that
\|\varphi(n,t;x)\|\leq\rho
for every
t\in[n,n+1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, since for every

t\geq 1
there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t\in[n,n+1)
, combining this observation with (
4.5
), (
5.6
), (
5.7
), (
5.22
) and the definition of
H
, we get that

\begin{split}\|D_{x}H(t,x)\|&\leq\|T(t,n)\|\|D_{x}\psi_{n}(\varphi(n,t;x))\|\|D_{x}\varphi(n,t;x)\|\\
&\leq K\theta^{a}T\hat{M},\end{split}

whenever
\|x\|\leq\frac{\rho}{\hat{M}}
. Similarly,

\begin{split}\|D_{x}G(t,x)\|&\leq K\theta^{a}T\hat{M},\\
\end{split}

for every
t\geq 1
whenever
\|x\|\leq\frac{\rho}{\hat{M}}
. Therefore, taking
R=K\theta^{a}T\hat{M}
and
\zeta=\frac{\rho}{\hat{M}}
, we obtain iv). This concludes the proof of Theorem
7
.
∎

We can proceed exactly as in the proof of Theorem

.

), (

) and (

), (

), (

), (

and

\begin{split}\lVert\varphi(n,t;x)-T(n,t)x\rVert&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}\lVert T(n,s)f(s,\varphi(n,s;x))\rVert\,ds\\
&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}K\theta^{a}\sup_{\delta\in(0,1)}\|D_{x}f(s,\delta\varphi(n,s;x))\|\|\varphi(n,s;x)\|\,ds\\
&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}K\theta^{a}L\frac{\mu^{\prime}(s)}{\mu(s)}\|\varphi(n,s;x)\|^{2}ds\\
&\leq\int_{n}^{n+1}K\theta^{a}L\frac{\mu^{\prime}(s)}{\mu(s)}\hat{M}^{2}\|x\|^{2}ds\leq K\theta^{a+1}L\hat{M}^{2}\lVert x\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

Combining these observations, we get that

\begin{split}H(t,x)&=x+o(\lVert x\rVert^{1+\varrho})\end{split}

as
\|x\|\to 0
(recall that
\varrho<1
). This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
9

A.1.
Foliations

Lemma 2
.

Take
\mathbf{x}\in Y_{\infty}
and

,

be such that

Proof.

We define
\mathbf{y}=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}
by

\begin{split}y_{n}&=\begin{cases}v_{n}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2})_{n}&n\neq k;\\
v_{k}^{1}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{k}&n=k\end{cases}\\
&=\begin{cases}v_{n}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2})_{n}&n\neq k;\\
v_{k}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{k}&n=k.\end{cases}\end{split}

Observe that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

we have

\begin{split}&(\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{y}))_{m}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(x_{m-n})-\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(v_{m-n}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2})_{m-n})&m\neq n+k;\\
\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(x_{m-n})-\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(v_{m-n}^{1}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{m-n})&m=n+k,\end{cases}\end{split}

where

\mathcal{F}(k,l):=\begin{cases}F_{k-1}\circ\ldots\circ F_{l}&k>l;\\
\text{\rm Id}&k=l,\end{cases}

and

Consequently,

\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{y})\|_{\infty}\leq\max\{\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{v}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2}))\|_{\infty},\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{v}^{1}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{1}))\|_{\infty}\}.

Thus, since

\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{s}(\mathbf{x})

\mathbf{v}\in Y_{\infty}^{s}

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}).

By projecting onto

. Hence,

v_{k}^{1}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{1})_{k}=y_{k}=v_{k}^{2}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2})_{k}=v_{k}^{1}+p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}^{2})_{k},

yielding the desired claim.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{i}\in Y_{\infty}
,
i=1,2
and
\mathbf{v}\in Y_{\infty}^{s}
be such that

for some

. Then

p(\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{v})_{k}=p(\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{v})_{k}.

Proof.

We define
\mathbf{y}=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}
by

y_{n}=\begin{cases}v_{n}+p(\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{v})_{n}&n\neq k;\\
v_{k}+p(\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{v})_{k}&n=k.\end{cases}

Then, using the notation introduced in the proof of Lemma
2
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\begin{split}&(\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{2})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{y}))_{m}\\
&=\begin{cases}\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(x_{m-n}^{2})-\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(v_{m-n}+p(\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{v})_{m-n})&m\neq n+k;\\
\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(x_{m-n}^{1})-\mathcal{F}(m,m-n)(v_{m-n}+p(\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{v})_{m-n})&m=n+k.\end{cases}\end{split}

Hence,

\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{2})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{y})\|_{\infty}\leq\max\{\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{2})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{v}+p(\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{v}))\|_{\infty},\|\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{x}^{1})-\mathbb{F}^{n}(\mathbf{v}+p(\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{v}))\|_{\infty}\},

Lemma 5

.

We define
\mathbb{F}_{-}\colon Y_{\infty}^{s}\to Y_{\infty}^{s}
and
\mathbb{F}_{+}\colon Y_{\infty}^{u}\to Y_{\infty}^{u}
by

\mathbb{F}_{-}:=\Pi^{s}\circ\mathbb{F}\circ(\text{\rm Id}_{Y_{\infty}^{s}}+g^{s})

and

\mathbb{F}_{+}:=\Pi^{u}\circ\mathbb{F}\circ(\text{\rm Id}_{Y_{\infty}^{u}}+g^{u}).

Then,
[
57
, Lemma 3.2]
gives that

\eta\circ\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{F}_{-}\circ\Pi^{s}+\mathbb{F}_{+}\circ\Pi^{u})\circ\eta

(A.12)

and

\mathbb{F}\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ(\mathbb{F}_{-}\circ\Pi^{s}+\mathbb{F}_{+}\circ\Pi^{u}).

We note that
\mathbb{F}_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n-1}
and
D\mathbb{F}_{-}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbb{A}_{-}^{\ast}:=\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\rvert_{Y_{\infty}^{s}}
and
D\mathbb{F}_{+}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbb{A}_{+}^{\ast}:=\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\rvert_{Y_{\infty}^{u}}
.

A.3.
Linearization of a contractive system

We now turn our attention to the linearization of contractive systems. We will follow the idea developed in
[
58
, Sections 5 and 7]
. The case of expanding systems can be treated in an analogous manner.

Let us assume that the linear part
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
admits an exponential contraction (that is, an exponential dichotomy with
P_{n}=\text{\rm Id}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
) and that the corresponding Sacker-Sell spectrum is of the form (recall Remark
7
)

a_{1}\leq b_{1}<a_{2}\leq b_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{k}\leq b_{k}<0.

A.3.1.
Local linearization

We can assume that
A_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
are of the block-diagonal form
A_{n}=\diag(A_{n}^{1},\ldots,A_{n}^{k})
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where the Sacker-Sell spectrum of
(A_{n}^{i})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is given by
[a_{i},b_{i}]
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
(apply
[
21
, Theorem 3.11]
to the standard dichotomy spectrum). Assume that the dimension of the block
A_{n}^{i}
is
d_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Notice that the decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
induces the decomposition

Y_{\infty}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}Y_{\infty}^{i},

where
Y_{\infty}^{i}
consists of all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}
with
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
for each
n
.

Now, the general strategy in the proof of the main result in
[
58
]
consists in finding a sequence of
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms
(\Phi_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
such that
\Phi_{i}
is a conjugacy between
\mathbb{F}_{i}
and
\mathbb{F}_{i-1}
where
\mathbb{F}_{k}=\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{F}_{0}=\mathbb{A}^{\ast}
and, for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, each
\mathbb{F}_{i}
has the form
\mathbb{F}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=(F_{n}^{i}(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}
with

F_{n}^{i}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}A^{1}_{n}x^{1}&+&f^{1}_{in}(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{k})\\
A^{2}_{n}x^{2}&+&f^{2}_{in}(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{k})\\
&\vdots&\\
A^{i}_{n}x^{i}&+&f^{i}_{in}(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{k})\\
A^{i+1}_{n}x^{i+1}&&\\
&\vdots&\\
A^{k}_{n}x^{k}&&\end{pmatrix}

where
x=(x^{1},x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})\in\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and
f^{j}_{in}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
. Then, considering the composition
\Phi:=\Phi_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi_{k}
, we get a conjugacy between
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{A}
. The objective in the sequel is to observe that
\Phi
has the property that its
n
-th coordinate depends only on the
n
-th coordinate of the argument.

As explained in the proof of
[
58
, Lemma 10]
, the induction step starts from
\mathbb{F}_{k}:=\mathbb{F}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{F}_{k}(\mathbf{x})=(F_{n-1}(x_{n-1}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}.

By writing a point in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a pair of
(u,v)
with
u\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
, we can write each
F_{n}
as

F_{n}(u,v)=(\diag(A_{n}^{1},\ldots,A_{n}^{k-1})u+\tilde{f}_{n}^{a}(u,v),A_{n}^{k}v+\tilde{f}_{n}^{b}(u,v)),

where
\tilde{f}_{n}^{*}=\pi^{*}\circ f_{n}
,
*=a,b
and
\pi^{a}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and
\pi^{b}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
are projections associated with the splitting
\mathbb{R}^{d}=(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
.

Consequently, each point
\mathbf{x}\in Y_{\infty}
can be written as
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
, where
\mathbf{u}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-1}Y_{\infty}^{i}
and
\mathbf{v}\in Y_{\infty}^{k}
and

\mathbb{F}_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=(F_{n-1}(u_{n-1},v_{n-1}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad\mathbf{u}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-1}Y_{\infty}^{i},\ \mathbf{v}\in Y_{\infty}^{k}.

Recall the map
\Psi_{k}
defined in
[
58
, Section 5]
which is given by

\Psi_{k}:=\lim_{m\to\infty}(\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{k})^{-m}\Pi^{b}\mathbb{F}_{k}^{m},

where
\Pi^{b}\colon Y_{\infty}\to Y_{\infty}^{k}
is the projection given by
Y_{\infty}\ni(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mapsto(\pi^{b}x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and
\mathbb{A}_{k}^{\ast}:=\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\rvert_{Y_{\infty}^{k}}
.
Since
\mathbb{F}_{k}^{m}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})_{n}
depends only on
(u_{n-m},v_{n-m})
and
((\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{k})^{-m}\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n+m}
, we conclude that
\Psi_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})_{n}
depends only on
(u_{n},v_{n})
. The same applies then to the map
\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=(\mathbf{u},\Psi_{k}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}))
introduced in the proof of
[
58
, Lemma 10]
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{k-1}
be given by
[
58
, Eq. (5.14)]
(for
\ell=k
):

\mathbb{F}_{k-1}:=\Phi_{k}\circ\mathbb{F}_{k}\circ\Phi_{k}^{-1},

defined locally around
\mathbf{0}\in Y_{\infty}
. Due to the property of
\Phi_{k}
mentioned above (which also holds for its local inverse),
\mathbb{F}_{k-1}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n-1}
.
Writing a point
\mathbf{x}
as a triple
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})
with
\mathbf{u}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-2}Y_{\infty}^{i}
,
\mathbf{v}\in Y_{\infty}^{k-1}
and
\mathbf{w}\in Y_{\infty}^{k}
, we have (see the proofs of
[
58
, Lemma 5 and Lemma 10]
) that

\begin{split}\mathbb{F}_{k-1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})&=(\diag(A_{n-1}^{1},\ldots,A_{n-1}^{k-2})u_{n-1}+\bar{f}_{n-1}^{a}(u_{n-1},v_{n-1},w_{n-1}),A_{n-1}^{k-1}v_{n-1}\\
&\quad+\bar{f}_{n-1}^{b}(u_{n-1},v_{n-1},w_{n-1}),A_{n-1}^{k}w_{n-1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\end{split}

where
\bar{f}_{n}^{a}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-2}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and
\bar{f}_{n}^{b}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{k-1}}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\bar{f}_{n}^{*}(0)=0
and
D\bar{f}_{n}^{*}(0)=0
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
*=a,b
.

Notice that the Sacker-Sell spectra of
(\diag(A_{n-1}^{1},\ldots,A_{n-1}^{k-2}))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
(A_{n-1}^{k-1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and
(A_{n}^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are given by
[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup\ldots\cup[a_{k-2},b_{k-2}]
,
[a_{k-1},b_{k-1}]
, and
[a_{k},b_{k}]
, respectively.

From the discussion in the preceding paragraph, we may assume that

\|D\mathbb{F}_{k-1}^{-1}(\mathbf{0})\rvert_{\mathcal{W}}\|\leq 1/(e^{a_{k}}-\delta)\quad\text{and}\quad\|D\mathbb{F}_{k-1}(\mathbf{0})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}\|\leq e^{b_{k-1}}+\delta,

with
\delta>0
sufficiently small where
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
are subspaces of
Y_{\infty}
consisting of sequences in
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k-2}\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d_{k-1}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
, respectively.

The idea is now to modify the nonlinear parts
of
\mathbb{F}_{k-1}
outside a neighborhood of
0
by modifying the nonlinear parts of each
(\mathbb{F}_{k-1})_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is, by modifying the maps
(u,v,w)\mapsto(\bar{f}_{n-1}^{a}(u,v,w),\bar{f}_{n-1}^{b}(u,v,w),0)
.
Then, for the associated modification
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}
(which coincides with
\mathbb{F}_{k-1}
on a neighborhood of
\mathbf{0}
), we can apply
[
24
, Theorem 2]
to
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}^{-1}
.The associated invariant manifolds will have the same structure as the invariant manifolds discussed in Section
A.1
,
that is, they can be represented as

\mathcal{M}=\{(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}):\ \mathbf{u}=h_{1}(\mathbf{w}),\ \mathbf{v}=h_{2}(\mathbf{w})\},

where
h_{i}(\mathbf{w})_{n}
depends only on
w_{n}
for
i=1,2
. Indeed, by
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]

\mathcal{M}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in Y_{\infty}:\ \sup_{m\geq 0}(\|\mathbb{F}_{k-1}^{-m}(\mathbf{x})\|q^{-m})<+\infty\right\},

for some
q>1
. Take now
\mathbf{w}^{i}
,
i=1,2
in
\mathcal{W}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
w_{k}^{1}=w_{k}^{2}
. We define
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in Y_{\infty}
by

x_{n}=\begin{cases}(h_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{1})_{n},h_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{1})_{n},w_{n}^{1})&n\neq k;\\
(h_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{2})_{n},h_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{2})_{n},w_{n}^{2})&n=k.\\
\end{cases}

Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2
, we find that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}
, which then easily implies that
h_{i}(\mathbf{w}^{1})_{k}=h_{i}(\mathbf{w}^{2})_{k}
for
i=1,2
. Hence,
h_{i}(\mathbf{w})_{n}
depends only on
w_{n}
for
i=1,2
.
Consequently,
\Theta
given by
[
58
, Eq. (5.21)]
has the property that
\Theta(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
. Let

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}:=\Theta\circ\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}\circ\Theta^{-1}.

By the previous discussion
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n-1}
.

Next, let

\tilde{\Psi}_{k-1}:=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{B}^{-m}\Pi^{v}\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{k-1}^{m},

where

(\mathbb{B}\mathbf{v})_{n}=A_{n-1}^{k-1}v_{n-1}.

Observe that
\tilde{\Psi}_{k-1}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
. The same comment applies to

\Psi_{k-1}:=\tilde{\Psi}_{k-1}\circ\Theta

and for

\Phi_{k-1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}):=(\mathbf{u},\Psi_{k-1}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}),\mathbf{w}).

We now set

\mathbb{F}_{k-2}:=\Phi_{k-1}\circ\mathbb{F}_{k-1}\circ\Phi_{k-1}^{-1}

and continue with the above procedure as indicated in the proof of
[
58
, Lemma 5]
to obtain
\Phi_{k-2}
up to
\Phi_{1}
. Moreover, as indicated in the proof of
[
58
, Lemma 10]
, the
local
linearization is given as the composition

\Phi:=\Phi_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{k},

where each
\Phi_{i}
,
i=1,...,k
, have the property that the
n
-th coordinate of the image depends only on the
n
-th coordinate of the argument. In particular,
\Phi
also has this property.

A.3.2.
From local to global linearization

Our objective now is to extend the local linearization
\Phi
from a neighborhood
U\subset Y_{\infty}
to the whole space
Y_{\infty}
. We start noticing that, by (
C2
), for
\eta
sufficiently small,
\mathbb{F}
is a contraction. Moreover, by (
3.3
),
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
. Thus, it is possible to choose a small neighborhood
U_{0}\subset U
of origin such that
\mathbb{F}(U_{0})\subset U_{0}
and

U_{0}\subset{\rm int}\,\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})\subset U
,
where
{\rm int}\,\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})
denotes the interior of the set
\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})
. In particular,

\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\circ\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{x})\;\text{ for every }\mathbf{x}\in U_{0}.

(A.13)

Then, define

X_{i}:=\mathbb{F}^{-(i+1)}(U_{0})\backslash\mathbb{F}^{-i}(U_{0}),\quad V_{0}:=\Phi(U_{0}),\quad Z_{i}:=(\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-(i+1)}(V_{0})\backslash(\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-i}(V_{0})

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
It is clear that

\displaystyle X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset,~~\forall i\neq j,\quad~~X_{i}\cap U_{0}=\emptyset,\quad

\displaystyle U_{0}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}X_{i}=Y_{\infty},~~\mathbb{F}^{-1}(X_{i})=X_{i+1}

and

\displaystyle Z_{i}\cap Z_{j}=\emptyset,~~\forall i\neq j,\quad~~Z_{i}\cap V_{0}=\emptyset,\quad

\displaystyle V_{0}\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}Z_{i}=Y_{\infty},~~(\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-1}(Z_{i})=Z_{i+1}.

Then we define the global conjugacy
\Phi_{*}\colon Y_{\infty}\to Y_{\infty}
by

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Phi(\mathbf{x}),&\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in U_{0},\\
((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-(i+1)}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{i+1})(\mathbf{x}),&\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in X_{i},~~\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.\end{array}\right.

In order to show that
\Phi_{*}
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism, we note that

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F})(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0}).

(A.14)

In fact, the above equality clearly holds for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})\setminus U_{0}=X_{0}
by the definition of
\Phi_{*}
. Moreover, for
\mathbf{x}\in U_{0}
, using (
A.13
), we have

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=\Phi(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F})(\mathbf{x})

which proves (
A.14
).
Similarly,

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-2}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{2})(\mathbf{x})\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-2}(U_{0}).

Indeed, the above equality holds for
\mathbf{x}\in X_{1}
by the definition of
\Phi_{*}
. Now, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})
, by (
A.14
), we know that

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F})(\mathbf{x}).

On the other hand, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-1}(U_{0})
we have that
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{x})\in U_{0}
. Thus, by (
A.13
) applied to
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{x})
,

\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F})(\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{x})).

Therefore, combining these two observations, we conclude that

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-2}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{2})(\mathbf{x})

as claimed.
Proceeding in the same manner, we find that

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-i}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{i})(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{-i}(U_{0}),\ \forall i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

(A.15)

This implies that
\Phi_{*}
is
C^{1}
on each of the open sets

\mathbb{F}^{-i}(U_{0})
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
whose union is the entire
Y_{\infty}
. Consequently,
\Phi_{*}
is
C^{1}
on
Y_{\infty}
. Moreover, by (
A.15
),

\Phi_{*}\circ\mathbb{F}^{-i}(U_{0})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-i}\circ\Phi)(U_{0})=(\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-i}(V_{0}).

Thus,
\Phi_{*}(X_{i})=Z_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
which implies that
\Phi_{*}\colon Y_{\infty}\to Y_{\infty}
is one-to-one and, therefore, a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism. Furthermore, it follows easily from the previous observations that
\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\circ\Phi_{\ast}=\Phi_{\ast}\circ\mathbb{F}
. In particular,
\Phi_{\ast}
is a (global)
C^{1}
linearization of
\mathbb{F}
.

Finally, we note that
\Phi_{*}
has the property that
\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
. Indeed, take
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in Y_{\infty}
with
x_{n}=y_{n}
and let
k\in\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
be such that
\mathbb{F}^{k}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbb{F}^{k}(\mathbf{y})
belong to
U_{0}
(recall that
\mathbb{F}
is a contraction with
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
). By (
A.15
), we have

\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-k}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{k})(\mathbf{x})\;\text{ and }\;\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{y})=((\mathbb{A}^{\ast})^{-k}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}^{k})(\mathbf{y}).

(A.16)

Thus, since
\mathbb{F}^{k}(\mathbf{x})_{n+k}=\mathbb{F}^{k}(\mathbf{y})_{n+k}
, and recalling that
\mathbb{A}
(resp.
\Phi
) have the property that the
j
-th coordinate of the image depends only on the
(j-1)
-th (resp.
j
-th) coordinate of the argument, it follows by (
A.16
) that
\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{x})_{n}=\Phi_{*}(\mathbf{y})_{n}
as claimed.

A.4.
Conclusion

Let
\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{-},\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{+},\mathbb{F}_{-}
and
\mathbb{F}_{+}
be as in the end of Section
A.2
. Note that, by (
C2
), for
\eta
sufficiently small,
\mathbb{F}_{-}
is a contractive system, while
\mathbb{F}_{+}
is an expanding system. Thus, from the discussion in Section
A.3
it follows that there exist
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms
\Phi_{-}\colon Y_{\infty}^{s}\to Y_{\infty}^{s}
and
\Phi_{+}\colon Y_{\infty}^{u}\to Y_{\infty}^{u}
such that

\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{-}\circ\Phi_{-}=\Phi_{-}\circ\mathbb{F}_{-}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{A}^{\ast}_{+}\circ\Phi_{+}=\Phi_{+}\circ\mathbb{F}_{+}.

In addition,
\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
.

Setting

\Phi:=(\Phi_{-}\circ\Pi^{s}+\Phi_{+}\circ\Pi^{u})\circ\eta,

(A.17)

where
\eta
is as in Section
A.2
, we find that
\Phi\colon Y_{\infty}\to Y_{\infty}
is a homeomorphism such that (recall (
A.12
))

\Phi\circ\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{A}^{\ast}\circ\Phi.

Moreover,
\Phi(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
once every object involved in the definition of
\Phi
has this property (as observed in Sections
A.2
and
A.3
). In addition,
\Phi
is of class
C^{1}
as
\Phi_{\pm}
and
\eta
are of class
C^{1}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
h_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by

h_{n}(v):=\Phi(\mathbf{v}^{n})_{n},

where
\mathbf{v}^{n}=(v_{m}^{n})_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is given by
v_{m}^{n}=0
for
m\neq n
and
v_{n}^{n}=v
. Note that, by construction, the conjugacy
\Phi
given by (
A.17
) coincides with the map
\Phi
used in the proof of Theorem
9
that comes from
[
31
, Appendix A]
. In particular,
h_{n}
given above coincide with
h_{n}
given in (
A.6
). Thus, all we have to do to finish the proof of Proposition
2
is to show that each
h_{n}
is a bijection. We start showing that
h_{n}
is injective.

Let
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
h_{n}(v)=h_{n}(w)
. Then
\Phi(\mathbf{v}^{n})_{n}=\Phi(\mathbf{w}^{n})_{n}
. On the other hand, since
v_{m}^{n}=w_{m}^{n}=0
for
m\neq n
, we have
\Phi(\mathbf{v}^{n})_{m}=\Phi(\mathbf{w}^{n})_{m}=0
for each
m\neq n
(recall that
\Phi(\mathbf{x})_{n}
depends only on
x_{n}
). Hence,
\Phi(\mathbf{v}^{n})=\Phi(\mathbf{w}^{n})
, and thus, since
\Phi
is injective,
\mathbf{v}^{n}=\mathbf{w}^{n}
. Consequently,
v=v_{n}^{n}=w_{n}^{n}=w
and
h_{n}
is injective. Let us now show that
h_{n}
in also onto. Take an arbitrary
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define
\mathbf{w}^{n}
as above. Then, since
\Phi
is onto, there is
\mathbf{y}\in Y_{\infty}
such that
\Phi(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{w}^{n}
. Thus, taking
v:=y_{n}
we have that
\Phi(\mathbf{y})_{n}=\Phi(\mathbf{v}^{n})_{n}
, which implies that
w=h_{n}(v)
and
h_{n}
is onto as claimed. This finishes the proof of Proposition
2
.

p

q

also
\eta
