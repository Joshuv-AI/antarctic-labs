Borane-assisted hydride addition and dihydrogen reductive elimination at iridium: a gateway to Ir(–i)/Ir(i) redox catalysis
Authors: Hajime Kameo, Younosuke Kawahara, Yoshihiro Shimoyama, Takuto Kambayashi, Hiroyuki Matsuzaka, Didier Bourissou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The σ-electron accepting character of the Z-type borane ligand enables hydride addition to the neutral Ir(i) carbonyl hydride complex 1 upon reaction with potassium alkoxide, concomitantly affording a ketone and an anionic Ir(i) complex 2 bearing two terminal hydride ligands. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction, T1 measurements, IR (νCO), XPS (Ir 4f7/2), and DFT analyses support a d8 Ir(i) assignment for 2. Thermolysis of 2 triggers H2 reductive elimination to give the structurally characterized tetrahedral Ir(–i) carbonyl complex 3 and exposure of 3 to H2 cleanly regenerates 2, substantiating a reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) oxidative addition/reductive elimination process. This process is amenable to catalysis, as illustrated by the dehydrogenation of a secondary alcohol, highlighting the potential of Lewis-acidic Z-type ligands to access unconventional low-valent redox chemistry at iridium.

Introduction

Reductive elimination (RE) is the key bond-forming and product-release elementary step in numerous transition-metal-catalyzed reactions.1,2 Its feasibility is strongly governed by both the geometry and electronic state of the metal center. Accordingly, RE is most commonly observed from complexes that feature favorable coordination environments and oxidation states. Recent studies have shown that Lewis acids (LAs) can assist RE through multiple, complementary modes. First, Lewis acids (LAs) can coordinate to a metal center as Z-type (σ-accepting) ligands. In doing so, they often increase steric congestion while simultaneously withdrawing σ-electron density from the metal, effects that can favor reductive elimination (RE).3–7 Second, LAs can promote RE by sequestering a strongly coordinating anionic co-ligand (often a halide) and thereby transiently removing it from the immediate coordination sphere of the metal.7b,8 Such ligand abstraction can open access to coordinatively unsaturated intermediates—sometimes formulated as cationic or zwitterionic species—that provide a more favorable pathway for RE.7b Such halide-abstraction strategies are particularly well documented in Au redox chemistry. For an overview of Au(i)/Au(iii) catalysis and Au(iii)-to-Au(i) RE, see ref. 8a, and for representative Au(iii) cases, see ref. 8b–d. Related microenvironment-based approaches can extend this concept beyond Au, enabling otherwise slow alkyl–alkyl RE from high-valent centers such as Pt(iv).8e In addition, LA assistance can arise without necessarily requiring persistent direct coordination to the metal center: by engaging a remote basic site, an LA can perturb the key bond-forming event and thus facilitate RE.9,10

Although LA assistance of RE has been supported by stoichiometric studies, its direct role in enabling catalysis has been demonstrated in only a limited number of cases. A catalytically relevant example is found in Ni(0)/Ni(ii) C–C cross-coupling systems employing Zn reagents.5 In these systems, the Zn component is argued to play a dual role: it serves as the coupling reagent while also associating with the Ni center as a σ-accepting (Z-type) Lewis acid, thereby promoting Ni(ii) → Ni(0) C–C reductive elimination.

Moreover, the LA-assisted RE scenarios discussed to date imply relatively common redox couples such as Ru(0)/Ru(ii),4 M(0)/M(ii) (M = Ni,5 Pd,6 Pt7), and Au(i)/Au(iii).8 In this context, besides favoring RE, the intrinsic σ-acceptor character of Lewis acids may enable to stabilize reduced, low-valent complexes and thereby expand the set of oxidation states that are practically accessible. As such, LA motifs may open the door to rare or even new redox cycles that involve unusually low oxidation states.

A proof-of-concept for such low-valent redox gateways was provided by Lu and co-workers in the catalytic hydrodefluorination of fluoroarenes using a heterobimetallic Rh–In platform (Fig. 1).11a This transformation likely proceeds through a Rh(–i)/Rh(i) redox cycle, and several catalytic intermediates have been characterized. The In-based metalloligand is proposed to stabilize the reduced Rh(–i) species responsible for the Ar–F bond activation. Subsequent work with a similar ligand platform showed that an anionic Rh–Ga complex can promote C–F bond cleavage under violet-light irradiation via photoinduced single-electron transfer, with a neutral Rh(0) intermediate detected by EPR.11b Moreover, TM–LA cooperativity has been very recently extended to a Co–Ga system, in which photodissociation of H2 generates a “naked” Co(–i) species that functions as a photoreductant for the hydrodefluorination of electron-rich fluoroarenes.11c Related Ni–B chemistry has been reported by Peters and co-workers, who demonstrated reversible H2 addition across a diphosphine–borane-supported Ni–B unit to give a hydride/borohydride species and further connected this reactivity to olefin hydrogenation catalysis.12a Subsequent work enabled the observation of a d10 Ni–H2 adduct as an intermediate in H–H oxidative addition across the Ni–B interaction.12b Collectively, these studies highlight the growing potential of Lewis-acidic metalloligands to access unusually low-valent reactivity. At the same time, they also underscore that well-defined, reversible redox interconversions based on H2 reductive elimination/oxidative addition remain rare, and that their direct connection to catalysis is still underdeveloped, particularly for iridium.

In this work, we explored the viability of Ir(–i)/Ir(i) redox cycling by leveraging a Lewis-acidic Z-type borane ligand. Given iridium's tendency to favor higher oxidation states (e.g., Ir(iii)), accessing and documenting a reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) couple with well-defined species is challenging. As detailed hereafter, borane (Z-type) coordination was found to enable hydride addition and dihydrogen reductive elimination at an Ir(i) center, thereby establishing a reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) redox couple. This process is amenable to catalysis, as substantiated in a proof-of-concept transformation, namely the dehydrogenation of secondary alcohols. The manner in which the Ir–B interaction promotes this unorthodox reactivity at iridium was analyzed by a combination of experimental and computational approaches.

Results and discussion

Hydride addition enabled by a DPB Z-type ligand: towards an octahedral anionic Ir(i) dihydride (2)

Z-type boranes can render electron-rich late-transition-metal centers sufficiently electrophilic to undergo coordination or addition of external Lewis bases and nucleophiles,13 thereby providing access to unusual anionic metalate species. In our previous work, DPB ligands enabled the addition of halides and cyanide to low-valent Pt(0) species to afford anionic Pt(0) complexes.13d On this basis, we examined whether the Lewis-acidic borane unit of the DPB ligand could similarly promote hydride addition to the Ir carbonyl hydride complex 1, (DPB)Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3).14 Gratifyingly, reaction of 1 with KH afforded the dihydrido iridium(i) complex [(DPB)IrH2(CO)][K(E)] (2a: E = [2.2.2]cryptand; 2b: E = dibenzo-18-crown-6) with concomitant PPh3 elimination (Scheme 1). A single hydride resonance at δ = −10.4 ppm integrating for 2H was observed, and formation of free PPh3 was confirmed by 31P NMR spectroscopy. These results indicate that DPB Z-type ligation drives hydride addition to selectively furnish the octahedral anionic Ir(i) dihydride 2.

Synthesis of anionic octahedral Ir(i) dihydrido complex 2.

In contrast to the borane-containing complex 1, the borane-free Ir(i) complex Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3)3 did not react with KH, even in the presence of 18-crown-6 or [2.2.2]cryptand, indicating that the enhanced electrophilicity imparted by the borane is essential for formation of the anionic species. Consistent with this interpretation, π-back donation to CO is significantly weakened by the borane ligand (νCO 1960 cm−1 for 1vs. 1920 cm−1 for Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3)3).15 Notably, the borane ligand also enables hydride addition using a secondary alcohol: reaction of 1 with 1-phenylethanol in the presence of KOtBu afforded 2 along with acetophenone, although the reaction reached a plateau (56% NMR yield; Fig. S5).

Single crystals of 2b were obtained by slow diffusion of n-octane into a THF solution, enabling X-ray diffraction analysis (Fig. 2a). The Ir center adopts an octahedral L3X2Z-type geometry with facial coordination of the DPB ligand. The two hydride ligands are mutually cis (H–Ir–H = 83(3)°). The long H⋯H separation (1.93 Å) is consistent with a classical dihydride, as further supported by T1 measurements (T1 = 0.325 s at −50 °C, 0.281 s at −55 °C, 0.322 s at −60 °C; Fig. S14). Hydride addition strengthens π-back donation to CO, shifting νCO from 1960 cm−1 (1)13 to 1910 cm−1 (2), comparable to that of Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3)3 (1920 cm−1).15 In addition, σ-donation to borane appears strengthened upon hydride addition: the Ir–B distance shortens from 2.444(7) Å in 1 (ref. 14) to 2.301(4) Å in 2b.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to probe the oxidation state of 2 (Fig. S16). The Ir 4f7/2 binding energy (60.6 eV) is close to those of reference Ir(i) complexes (61.0 eV for Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3)3; 60.7 eV for Ir(Cl)(N2)(PPh3)2)16 and distinct from Ir(iii) reference complexes (62.7 eV for fac-IrCl3(PMe2Ph)2;17a 62.3 eV for K3[Ir(CN)6];17b 63.1 eV for [IrI3(H2NCH2CH2NH2)]).17c Notably, even the halide-free anionic Ir(iii) cyanido complex K3[Ir(CN)6] exhibits a higher Ir 4f7/2 binding energy than 2b. Together with the νCO value and DFT/IBO analyses showing four doubly occupied 5d orbitals and a predominantly dative Ir → B interaction (Fig. S33), the XPS data support the d8 Ir(i) assignment of 2. To the best of our knowledge, 2 represents the first iridium(i) complex bearing two terminal hydride ligands; Ir(i) complexes with two bridging hydrides have been reported previously.18

H–H reductive elimination from Ir(i): towards an Ir(–i) complex (3) and reverse H2 oxidative addition

The cis arrangement of the two hydrides in 2 and the presence of the borane moiety at Ir led us to explore H2 reductive elimination at Ir(i) (Scheme 2). Thermolysis of 2a under reflux conditions selectively afforded the dehydrogenative product [(DPB)Ir(CO)][K(E)] (3a, E = [2.2.2]cryptand). As expected for generation of a more electron-rich Ir carbonyl species, H2 reductive elimination markedly reinforces π-back donation to CO (νCO = 1852 cm−1). Although direct oxidation-state comparison with [Ir(CO)4]− (1894 cm−1)19 and [Ir(CO)3(PPh3)]− (1926 and 1861 cm−1)20 is complicated by the different number of CO ligands and ancillary ligand environment, the unusually low νCO value nevertheless indicates a highly electron-rich, low-valent Ir carbonyl species. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis of 3a revealed a highly distorted tetrahedral Ir(–i) carbonyl complex (Fig. 2b). The Ir center is coordinated by two phosphine donors and one carbonyl ligand, while the borane/aryl fragment of the DPB ligand occupies the remaining coordination site through close Ir–B and Ir–Cipso(C2) contacts of 2.292(4) and 2.328(3) Å, respectively. These structural metrics are consistent with an η2-BC interaction21 in the solid state and directly confirm coordination of the BPh unit to the electron-rich Ir center, in a manner reminiscent of the isoelectronic (DPB)M(0) complexes (M = Ni,12b Pd,22 Pt13d). Importantly, exposing a THF solution of 3a to H2 immediately regenerated 2a in 95% NMR yield (Fig. S3), establishing a reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) oxidative addition/reductive elimination process.

DFT calculations support the feasibility of H2 elimination from 2 (Fig. 3). In line with the experimental data, the optimized structure of 2 features a long H⋯H distance (2.056 Å) typical of classical hydrides. H2 reductive elimination proceeds viaTS2/3 with a moderate Gibbs activation barrier (ΔG‡ 25.0 kcal mol−1) to afford the tetrahedral complex 3. The optimized structure displays close contacts between Ir and the BPh unit (Ir–B = 2.319 Å; Ir–Cipso = 2.288 Å), in good agreement with the experimentally observed Ir–B and Ir–Cipso distances of 2.292(4) and 2.328(3) Å in 3a. The computed structure shows somewhat greater slippage of the BPh unit toward an η3-BCC description, but both the experimental and optimized structures support the same key feature: coordination of the borane/aryl fragment to the highly electron-rich Ir center. Importantly, IBO analysis along the IRC for the 2 → 3 transformation visualizes the bond reorganization during H–H reductive elimination: the two σ(Ir–H) bonds are converted into the σ(H–H) bond and dIr upon crossing TS2/3, consistent with pairwise coupling of the cis hydrides. As expected for a tetrahedral, highly electron-rich species, IBO analysis confirms five occupied 5d orbitals (Fig. S34). The endergonic nature of H2 elimination (ΔG +11.4 kcal mol−1) is consistent with the facility of the reverse hydrogenation of 3. In contrast to Lu's Rh(–i)11 and Peters' Ni(0)12 systems, an η2-H2 intermediate was not located here, which may reflect the particularly electron-rich character of the Ir(–i) center.

Reactivity of the structurally characterized Ir(–i) complex 3: PPh3 coordination and protonation

The solid-state structure of 3a establishes a distorted tetrahedral Ir(–i) carbonyl complex in which the electron-rich Ir center interacts with the borane/aryl fragment of the DPB ligand through close Ir–B and Ir–Cipso contacts. To further probe the lability of this borane/aryl interaction and to trap the Ir(–i) state in a more conventional donor environment, PPh3 was added to 3a, affording the PPh3 adduct [(DPB)Ir(CO)(PPh3)][K(E)] (4a, E = [2.2.2]cryptand). The structure of 4a was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (Fig. 2c). Complex 4a can also be synthesized directly in good yield by reacting 1 with KH in the presence of [2.2.2]cryptand under reflux. In 4a, the Ir center is five-coordinate and adopts a trigonal-pyramidal geometry, with PPh3 located trans to the borane ligand (B–Ir–P = 178.88(9)°). The Ir–B distance (2.410(6) Å) is longer than in 2b, likely reflecting steric repulsion between CO and the phenyl substituent at boron. The νCO value of 4 (1846 cm−1 for 4b) is comparable to that of 3a, indicating that the oxidation state remains unchanged upon PPh3 coordination. The Ir 4f7/2 binding energies of 3b and 4b are 60.5 and 60.8 eV, respectively (Fig. S17 and S18), falling in the same low-binding-energy region as 2b and the anionic Ir carbonyl reference [PPN][Ir(CO)4] (61.0 eV; Fig. S21). These values are consistent with highly electron-rich, low-valent Ir environments. We note that XPS is not used here as a standalone method to distinguish between the Ir(i) and Ir(–i) assignments, because the Ir 4f7/2 binding energies of 2–4 differ only slightly. Instead, the Ir(–i) assignment of 3 and 4 is supported by the combined evidence from the low νCO values, reversible H2 addition to give 2, and DFT/IBO analyses showing five occupied 5d orbitals (Fig. S34 and S35).

The pronounced electron-rich character of 4a enables proton abstraction from 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol to selectively regenerate 1 (Fig. S6). These stoichiometric findings prompted us to explore whether the Ir(i) → Ir(–i) reductive elimination pathway could be applied to catalytic alcohol dehydrogenation (Fig. 4).23

Proof-of-concept Ir(–i)/Ir(i) catalysis

With the reversible 2 ⇄ 3 interconversion in hand, we next examined whether this low-valent redox gateway can be coupled to catalytic turnover in acceptorless alcohol dehydrogenation. Using 5 mol% of 1 and 25 mol% of KOtBu (Table 1), catalytic dehydrogenation of 1-phenylethanol was performed under reflux using a reflux condenser and afforded acetophenone in 36% GC yield (Entry 1). Notably, conducting the reaction in a sealed THF vessel decreased the yield to 19% (Entry 2), and performing the reaction under an H2 atmosphere essentially suppressed ketone formation (Entry 3). These observations are consistent with inhibition by H2 elimination/addition interconversion between 2 and 3. Consistent with an acceptorless scenario, formation of H2 was directly detected by 1H NMR spectroscopy in a J-Young tube in THF-d8, where a resonance at δ 4.55 appeared after the catalytic run (Fig. S11). Both the catalyst and the base are essential (Entries 4 and 5). The yield improved to 52% upon switching the solvent to 2-MeTHF (Entry 6), while 18-crown-6 had no significant effect (Entry 7). The borane-free complex Ir(H)(CO)(PPh3)3 showed negligible activity (<1%), and adding BPh3 did not improve the outcome (Entries 8 and 9), underscoring the essential role of the ambiphilic DPB ligand. Increasing the catalyst loading to 10 mol% improved the yield to 74% (entry 13), while further increasing the amount of KOtBu to 50 mol% afforded acetophenone in 86% yield under otherwise identical conditions. Complexes 2a, 3a, and 4a displayed comparable catalytic activity to 1 (Entries 10–12), consistent with a catalytic cycle involving reductive elimination from 2 (Fig. 4). Preliminary tests with other secondary alcohols gave only modest results, suggesting that the present system is sensitive to substrate structure (see SI for details).

DFT calculations were performed to examine the key steps mediated by anionic species in catalysis, namely reduction of 1 with potassium 1-phenylethoxide and the H2 reductive elimination (Fig. 5). After endergonic PPh3/(MeCH(Ph)OK)2 exchange (ΔG 4.5 kcal mol−1), β-hydride elimination proceeds with a moderate activation barrier (ΔG‡ 11.5 kcal mol−1), producing acetophenone and an anionic Ir(i) dihydrido intermediate A2. Formation of the experimentally obtained complex 2 involves isomerization of the Ir coordination sphere (the CO ligand shifts from cis to trans position to B). This isomerization proceeds in two steps, via reversible Ir to B hydride transfer, with the Ir(i) (borohydrido)hydride A3 as intermediate.24 Subsequent H2 reductive elimination from 2 requires the highest activation barrier in the computed profile (ΔG‡ 27.7 kcal mol−1 from 2KTHF), indicating that this step is the most energetically demanding process in the catalytic cycle. Additional calculations using more explicitly solvated [K(THF)]+ and [K(THF)2]+ models show that THF coordination to potassium does not lower the barrier for H2 reductive elimination, but instead makes this step less favorable (see Fig. S29 in the SI). Finally, PPh3 coordination (formation of 4) followed by protonation regenerates 1, completing the catalytic cycle. Thus, the calculations support the feasibility of Ir(–i)/Ir(i)-redox catalysis.

To further probe the catalytic mechanism, the reaction using 1 was monitored by 31P NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S13). After 4 h, dihydrido complex 2 was observed as the major species (43%), along with a minor amount of anionic Ir(–i) complex 4 (22%). This speciation aligns with the computed relatively high barrier for H–H reductive elimination as well as the experimentally observed relatively severe conditions required for H2 elimination, suggesting that catalytic turnover is accessed by engaging the reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) redox couple while H–H reductive elimination remains energetically demanding.

Conclusions

The σ-electron-accepting Z-type borane ligand enables hydride addition to the neutral Ir(i) carbonyl hydride 1 in the presence of a potassium alkoxide, concomitantly forming a ketone and the first octahedral anionic Ir(i) dihydride 2 bearing two terminal hydrides. The d8 Ir(i) assignment of 2 is supported by IR (νCO), XPS (Ir 4f7/2), and DFT analyses. The Ir(i) dihydride complex 2 undergoes H2 reductive elimination to furnish the tetrahedral Ir(–i) complex 3, whose solid-state structure confirms close Ir–B and Ir–Cipso contacts with the DPB ligand. The reverse hydrogenation regenerates 2, establishing a reversible Ir(–i)/Ir(i) oxidative addition/reductive elimination interconversion. This unconventional Ir(–i)/Ir(i) redox pathway is amenable to catalysis, as illustrated by the dehydrogenation of 1-phenylethanol as proof-of-concept. Notably, the 2 ⇄ 3 interconversion is established using well-defined chemical species and corroborated by complementary structural, spectroscopic, and computational evidence (X-ray diffraction, T1, XPS, and DFT/IBO), thereby clarifying the operative role of the Lewis-acidic motif in this cooperative TM–LA system. Ongoing efforts will explore broader reactivity enabled by Lewis-acidic ligands and leverage these insights toward low-valent redox catalysis.
