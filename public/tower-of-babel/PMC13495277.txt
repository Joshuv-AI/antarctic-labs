Investigation of the functional interaction between problematic behaviors, social interaction, and attentional orienting
Authors: Seonmi Yun, Cheolhwan Kim, Koeun Jung, Suk Won Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prior research has suggested that visual attention plays a critical role in social cognition and adaptive functioning. Building on these findings, the present study investigated whether similar patterns of visual attention are associated with social abilities in typically developing adults. Fifty participants completed a modified version of an attentional cuing task, the Adult Self-Report (ASR), and the Sociotype Questionnaire (SOCQ) to assess social functioning and behavior. Performance on the visual attention task showed that participants performed better when a cue indicating the target location was presented prior to the appearance of the task stimuli, demonstrating a classic cuing effect. Correlational analyses revealed a negative association between the magnitude of the cuing effect and ASR scores, suggesting that higher levels of self-reported maladaptive behaviors were linked to reduced attentional benefits. In contrast, SOCQ scores were positively correlated with the cuing effect. Additionally, a significant negative correlation between ASR and SOCQ scores indicated that individuals reporting more problem behaviors also experienced greater social difficulties. These findings add to the growing body of evidence linking visual attention capabilities to social adaptability. They suggest that attentional mechanisms may play a role in supporting social interactions, even in non-clinical populations. This study highlights the potential of visual attention as a cognitive factor underlying social functioning in typical development and opens avenues for future research beyond clinical contexts.

Visual attention is a cognitive process that enables individuals to selectively focus on relevant visual information while filtering out distractions, allowing for efficient navigation of complex environments. This process prioritizes key details such as movement, color, and socially significant cues like facial expressions and gaze direction. Within the attention network model of the human brain, attentional orienting refers to the mechanism that allocates processing resources toward spatial locations based on predictive cues [1]. Efficient attentional orienting facilitates adaptive behavior by guiding individuals toward goal-relevant information in dynamic contexts. This optimal control of attention plays an important role in social interaction as successful social adaptation depends on detecting, prioritizing, and integrating socially meaningful signals while suppressing irrelevant distractions.

This proposition is supported by evidence from clinical and subclinical populations that atypical visual orienting may be associated with social difficulties [2, 3]. Furthermore, such findings raise a broader theoretical question: whether variability in attentional orienting efficiency is related to social functioning beyond diagnostic boundaries. This possibility is consistent with contemporary models of psychopathology, which emphasize that psychological characteristics are distributed along continua across the population [4, 5]. Within this perspective, variability in attentional orienting efficiency may relate to social functioning even among individuals without clinical diagnoses. To examine this possibility, the present study investigated visual attentional control in a non-clinical adult sample and evaluated its association with social and behavioral functioning.

To address this issue, we used two tools to assess social competence in adults. First is the Adult Self-Report. The ASR test is a part of the Achenbach Empirically Based Assessment System (ASEBA) and includes items that evaluate adaptive functioning and associated problems [6, 7]. In 2003, Achenbach expanded the original young adult screening tool, initially designed for individuals aged 18-30, to include items suitable for screening adults up to age 59. Like other instruments in the ASEBA family, it is based on DSM-IV diagnostic categories. The ASR test is a validated tool for assessing adult psychopathology and is utilized for clinical and research purposes across mental health, forensic, counseling, and medical settings. In 2009, nationwide data were collected to standardize the Adult Self-Report in Korea, and the Korean version of the ASR was released in 2010 (ASEBA Korea, https://aseba.co.kr/). In the present study, ASR total scores were conceptualized as dimensional indicators of general maladaptive functioning rather than as markers of any specific diagnostic category.

In addition, we incorporated another questionnaire to explore a more detailed level of social interaction. This new instrument is the Socio-Type Questionnaire (SOCQ), developed by Marijuan and colleagues [8]. The SOCQ was created through a study examining social ties among Spanish adults (n=1,075) from diverse social and regional backgrounds. Data were collected from participants regarding the different types of social ties they maintain, such as family, friends, acquaintances, and work or school colleagues, along with information on how these ties are dynamically updated through face-to-face dialogue and other forms of interaction. The collected data were analyzed using correlational methods and a cross-sectional design, with the number of factors, consistency, and reliability of the resulting scale being assessed, along with identified correlations.

As the two primary assessments aimed to determine whether visual attention characteristics are related to problem behaviors and sociability in adults, we also evaluated participants’ visual attention control abilities. we used a Posner-style cuing paradigm, a foundational method for studying the allocation of spatial attention since its introduction in the 1970s [9]. In the classic task, an attentional cue is presented before the target, and the target subsequently appears either at the cued or uncued location after a brief delay. Better performance for validly cued targets is interpreted as evidence of more efficient spatial orienting. In the present study, however, we adapted this logic using the spatial configuration of the Attention Window task developed by Hüttermann and Memmert [10]. This modification was methodological in purpose. A standard Posner task with simple single-location displays would leave limited variability for detecting individual differences. By contrast, the multi-cluster, multi-eccentricity arrangement of the Attention Window paradigm creates a more demanding perceptual task while preserving the essential logic of spatial cueing. Thus, although the original Attention Window task was designed to assess attentional breadth by having participants count target stimuli, our modified version used its spatial configuration to examine attentional orienting efficiency. Specifically, participants detected target stimuli among distractors after presentation of a spatial cue indicating the likely target location. The performance advantage for validly cued relative to invalidly cued targets served as our index of attentional orienting efficiency. Then, we examined the relationship between this attentional control measure and participants’ scores on the ASR and SOCQ, aiming to understand how visual attention relates to behavioral and social functioning.

Critically, these links emerge even when the tasks used to measure attentional control do not involve social stimuli, suggesting that it is the efficiency of the control mechanism itself, rather than sensitivity to socially relevant content, that underlies the relationship with social functioning. From this perspective, attentional control may represent a domain-general cognitive control resources, including attentional regulation, predict social competence across development and in adulthood [11–13].

The present study takes this logic one step further by deliberately using simple, non-social geometric stimuli to measure spatial attentional orienting. This choice was intentional; by removing any social or affective content from the task, we aimed to isolate the most elementary form of attentional orienting, the ability to benefit from a spatially predictive cue, and test whether even this basic capacity relates to social and behavioral functioning in adults. The cuing effect (the accuracy advantage for validly cued over invalidly cued targets) serves as an index of how efficiently an individual exploits spatial information to orient attention. It reflects the benefit conferred by valid predictive cues rather than the cost of invalid ones, though the two are interrelated. If this low-level, content-free measure of orienting efficiency covaries with individual differences in social functioning and problem behaviors, it would provide compelling evidence that fundamental attentional mechanisms, not just higher-order social cognition, contribute to how people navigate their social world.

The present study builds upon our previous work examining visuospatial cueing differences associated with autistic-like traits in non-clinical adults [14]. However, the earlier publication primarily served as a theoretical and conceptual review article, in which an attentional orienting framework was proposed and preliminarily substantiated through a proof-of-concept experiment. The primary aim of that study was to establish the conceptual feasibility that domain-general attentional mechanisms may be linked to social functioning characteristics.

In contrast, the current study substantially extends this framework through a more comprehensive empirical investigation. Rather than focusing exclusively on autistic-like traits, the present study adopts a broader dimensional approach by examining general problem behaviors and social connectedness using the Adult Self-Report (ASR) and Sociotype Questionnaire (SOCQ). Furthermore, the current study includes subdimension-level analyses and Bayesian statistical approaches to more rigorously characterize the relationship between attentional orienting and broader dimensions of psychological and social functioning. Thus, the present work moves beyond the initial conceptual framework by providing expanded empirical evidence that attentional orienting is associated with broader patterns of social adaptability in typically developing adults.

Methods

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Specifically, all experimental protocols were approved by the Chungnam National University Institutional Review Board (202310-SB-181-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To be noted, this study was not registered as a clinical trial (Clinical trial number: not applicable).

Participants

A group of fifty adults (28 female, 22 male, age range: 19–25) took part in the study and received monetary compensation. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no difficulty in performing the task. The sample size was informed by an initial pilot phase conducted using the identical experimental protocol. In the pilot study (n = 24), we observed a correlation between ASR scores and the attentional cueing effect (r = .42), suggesting a medium-sized association. Based on this preliminary estimate, we planned to recruit approximately 50 participants to ensure stable estimation of correlational effects. Even though the sample size was determined a priori, considering that this is modest, we also report Bayes factors [15].

The pilot phase employed the same procedures and measures as the final study, and pilot participants were retained in the full dataset. Accordingly, the initial power calculation should be interpreted as a planning tool to guide recruitment rather than as an independent confirmatory power analysis. The final sample comprised 50 participants, and all reported analyses are based on the complete dataset. All participants completed behavioral measures and questionnaires to assess their level of social adaptation.

Stimuli & apparatus

Visual task

The experiment was programmed and run using Psychopy3 (https://www.psychopy.org/download.html). The visual task display in this study consisted of two clusters, each with four shapes in it (Fig. 1). The four shapes in each cluster were light and dark circles, and light and dark triangles, with the light triangle serving as the target stimulus. The clusters were positioned opposite each other in relation to the center of the screen. The four directions of their appearance were two diagonal lines, a horizontal line, and a vertical line.

Fig. 1Task stimuli and example trials of visual task

Task stimuli and example trials of visual task

The viewing distance was set to 30 cm. At a viewing distance of 30 cm, the maximum eccentricity of 25° corresponds to approximately 14 cm from central fixation. This falls well within the horizontal extent of a standard 24-inch monitor (~ 53 cm width), ensuring that all stimuli were fully visible on screen.

A small dot was presented and participants were asked to fixate on the dot. Two clusters of four shapes appeared equidistant from the fixation. Each cluster was 1.2 × 1.2 cm in size. The circles within each cluster had a diameter of 0.5 cm, and the triangles were isosceles, with a height of 0.5 cm and a base of 0.5 cm. Each cluster always contained four stimuli arranged in a 2 × 2 configuration. The target stimulus was a light grey triangle. On each trial, the number of target triangles within each cluster was determined by a predefined condition variable (2 for Position 1 and 2 for Position 2), yielding 0 to 4 targets per cluster. Across trials, the target count levels (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4) were presented equally often (20% each). The remaining items in the cluster were filled with distractors (light grey circle, dark grey circle, and dark grey triangle), which were randomly selected to complete the four-item cluster. All four items within each cluster were then randomly shuffled across the 2 × 2 positions. Following stimulus presentation, participants were not asked to make an immediate speeded detection response. Instead, after stimulus offset, two positions were randomly selected from within the stimulus clusters and probed sequentially on screen, with each queried location marked by an X. For each probe, participants indicated whether a light grey triangle had been present at that exact location by pressing f for “no” and j for “yes.”

Although eye movements were not directly recorded, several features of the task design reduce the likelihood that overt gaze shifts systematically influenced performance. The stimulus array was presented for 300 ms, which approaches the latency required to initiate voluntary saccades, thereby limiting the opportunity for participants to execute and utilize eye movements. Furthermore, for optimal task performance, foveating one location is not ideal because attention had to be distributed among two potential task locations.

Participants went through a short practice session including 10 ~ 20 trials to be familiarized with the task.

Before each task display, two circles were briefly presented for 200 ms at potential locations where the stimulus clusters would later appear. These circles served as attentional cues intended to direct participants’ focus. On 80% of the trials (valid trials), the cue locations corresponded to the meridian along which the stimulus clusters were subsequently presented. On the remaining 20% of trials (invalid trials), the target and other task stimuli appeared at locations that were not cued. In valid trials, the two pre-cue circles appeared at the same meridian as the subsequently presented stimulus clusters. In invalid trials, the cues were presented along a different meridian than the one in which the stimulus clusters appeared. In target-present trials, light grey triangles could appear in one or both clusters, independent of cue configuration. Therefore, in valid trials, the cues were always presented at the spatial locations of the two stimulus clusters. In contrast, during invalid trials, neither cue corresponded to the actual cluster location, meaning both cues were invalid.

Stimulus pairs were presented for 300 ms along one of four possible meridians: horizontal, vertical, and two diagonals separated by 45-degree angles. All stimulus clusters were positioned at equal distances from the center of the screen. The distance from fixation varied randomly between 5 and 25 degrees of visual angle, in increments of 5 degrees, across a total of 800 trials (40 trials per run, 20 runs in total).

Although the number of target stimuli (light grey triangles) varied across trials, this factor was not central to the aims of the current study and was therefore excluded from the data analysis. Importantly, the overall pattern of behavioral results did not differ based on the number of targets presented. A visual depiction of the experimental procedure is provided in Fig. 2.

Sociotype Questionnaire (SOCQ)

Following the visual task experiment, we administered the Sociotype Questionnaire (SOCQ), which consists of 32 items designed to evaluate the quality of participants’ relationships across four categories: family, friends, acquaintances, and study/workmates8. Each category was assessed through eight questions. Participants responded to items such as, ‘I speak and relate with my family’ and ‘The family members care about me’ in family category, ‘I have friends to tell and share problems.’ and ‘With the rush and the stress, I do not find time for my relations.’ in friends category, ‘I speak and relate satisfactorily with my peers.’ and ‘I feel valued by my peers.’ in study/workmates category, ‘It is easy for me to win support from acquaintances.’ and ‘Relations with my acquaintances are forced.’ in acquaintances category. To minimize the effects of response styles and central tendency bias, a 6-point Likert-type scale without a neutral midpoint was used, ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (always). Participants rated their level of agreement with each statement, with some items being reverse scored. Each subscale could yield a maximum of 40 points, with a total possible score of 160. Higher scores indicate greater perceived social integration. The global SOCQ score was computed as the sum across the four subscales. The global score represents the sum of responses across all categories.

The original English version of the SOCQ was developed and validated in a large adult sample (n = 1,075), demonstrating acceptable internal consistency and factorial validity [8]. For the present study, the questionnaire was translated into Korean, as no validated Korean version was available at the time of data collection. The translation was reviewed and refined through discussion among the authors and senior researchers to ensure conceptual and semantic equivalence with the original instrument. In the absence of a formally validated Korean version, we assessed scale reliability using Cronbach’s alpha, which was α = 0.872 for the full-scale SOCQ, indicating good internal consistency.

Adult self-report

Following the completion of the visual attention task and SOCQ questionnaire, participants completed the Adult Self-Report [6, 7]. The ASR consists of 131 problem behavior items rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, 2 = very true), along with an Adjustment Scale and a Substance Use Scale. The Problem Behavior Scale is composed of three subscales:

Syndrome scale

Measures internalizing problems (e.g., anxiety, depression, withdrawal, somatic complaints) and externalizing problems (e.g., aggressive behavior, rule-breaking, attention-seeking behavior), as well as thinking problems, attention problems, and others.

DSM-Oriented diagnostic scale

Assesses DSM-based disorders, including DSM Depressive Disorder, DSM Anxiety Disorder, DSM Somatic Symptom Disorder, DSM Avoidant Personality Disorder, DSM ADHD, and DSM Antisocial Personality Disorder.

Crisis questionnaire

Evaluates immediate crisis situations.

The core of the ASR is the 123-item problem behavior section, which asks respondents to rate on a three-point scale whether they have exhibited each described behavior as an adult in the past six months. Although the problem behavior items are numbered up to 123, item 56 consists of nine sub-items, bringing the total number of items to 131. The Adjustment Scale assesses social functioning in various areas, including friendships, spouse/partner relationships, family, work, and education. The Substance Use Scale evaluates behavioral problems related to smoking, alcohol, and drug use.

In the present study, we focused on the Total Problems score derived from the problem behavior items. This score reflects overall maladaptive functioning across multiple symptom domains. Importantly, ASR scores were conceptualized as dimensional indicators of general psychopathology rather than as measures of any specific diagnostic category.

Design and procedure

Participants completed a visual attention task in a quiet laboratory. The task was displayed on a 24-inch (approximately 60 cm diagonal, 16:9 aspect ratio) external LCD monitor connected to a laptop computer. Participants were seated at a viewing distance of approximately 30 cm. Each run consisted of 40 trials, with a total of 20 runs (800 trials in total). At the end of each run, participants were allowed to take a break and resumed the task when they felt ready. The experiment included two independent variables: cue type (valid vs. invalid) and target distance (five levels of eccentricity from central fixation). The primary dependent variable was response accuracy as a function of cue type. The task took approximately 40 min to complete. Following the visual attention task, participants completed the SOCQ and ASR questionnaires (Fig. 3).

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R package software (https://www.r-project.org). As a first step, the accuracy data of the visual task were analyzed to examine the effects of cue type and target distance on performance in the visual search task. A two-way repeated measures analysis of variance with cue type (valid vs. invalid) and target distances as within subject factors was applied to the accuracy data. Then, we first examined how the cueing effect (i.e., the accuracy difference between valid and invalid trials) was associated with participants’ global ASR scores. We then extended this analysis to examine how each ASR subdimension is related to the cueing effect. A parallel set of analyses was conducted using the global SOCQ scores and their respective subdimensions. To increase statistical power, we collapsed data from all different distance trials.

Results

The datasets are publicly available at https://osf.io/h259y/?view_only=5818fcd7068b4f90a5bce4a92d7bc04a. Overall accuracy was about 73.7% indicating that the task was attention-demanding and participants understood and performed the task appropriately.

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on the accuracy data from the visual search task, with cue type (valid vs. invalid) and target distance (five eccentricity levels) as within-subject factors. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of cue type, F(1, 49) = 145.6, p < .001, indicating higher accuracy on valid trials compared to invalid trials (see Fig. 4). A significant main effect of target distance was also observed, F(4, 196) = 99.56, p < .001, with accuracy decreasing as distance from fixation increased. The interaction between cue type and distance was significant, F(4, 196) = 19.7, p < .001. Because the primary research question concerned overall attentional orienting efficiency rather than distance-specific modulation, cuing effects were computed by collapsing across distance levels. The cuing effect was calculated by subtracting accuracy on invalid trials from accuracy on valid trials, such that higher values reflected greater attentional benefit from valid cues.

Analysis of reaction time (RT) data showed a similar pattern. There were significant main effects of cue type, F(1, 49) = 120.7, p < .001, and target distance, F(4, 196) = 24.87, p < .001, as well as a significant interaction between the two variables, F(4, 196) = 8.38, p < .001, suggesting that both speed and accuracy were modulated by cue validity and spatial position.

We then examined how the magnitude of the cuing effect was related to ASR scores (see Fig. 5). The cueing effect was calculated by subtracting accuracy on invalid trials from accuracy on valid trials, such that higher values reflected a greater attentional benefit from valid cues. A scatter plot was constructed with ASR total scores.

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between the magnitude of the accuracy-based cueing effect and ASR scores (see Fig. 5). A significant negative correlation was observed between ASR Total Problems scores and the cueing effect, r(48) = –0.44, p = .002, 95% CI [–0.63, –0.19], BF10 = 31.13. Thus, individuals reporting higher levels of overall problem behaviors demonstrated reduced attentional benefit from valid cues.

Comparable negative associations were observed across ASR subdimensions. The cueing effect was negatively correlated with DSM-Oriented scores, r(48) = –0.40, p = .004, 95% CI [–0.60, –0.14], Syndrome scores, r(48) = –0.42, p = .003, 95% CI [–0.62, –0.17], and Crisis Index scores, r(48) = –0.42, p = .003, 95% CI [–0.62, –0.17]. To account for multiple comparisons across ASR subdimensions, Bonferroni correction was applied (α = 0.05 / 4 = 0.025). All reported associations remained significant after correction. Collectively, these results indicate a robust inverse relationship between attentional orienting efficiency and dimensional indices of general psychological functioning within this sample.

A similar analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between attentional orienting and social connectedness, as measured by the SOCQ (Fig. 6). A significant positive correlation was observed between SOCQ total scores and the cueing effect, r(48) = 0.33, p = .019, 95% CI [0.05, 0.55], BF10 = 4.62. Thus, individuals reporting higher overall social connectedness demonstrated greater attentional benefit from valid cues.

We further examined associations with the four SOCQ subdimensions (family, friends, study/workmates, acquaintances). Among these, only the friendship subscale was significantly correlated with the cueing effect, r(48) = 0.42, p = .003, 95% CI [0.17, 0.62]. The remaining subdimensions were not significantly associated with cueing magnitude.

Although the primary analyses focused on accuracy-based measures of attentional orienting, we also conducted exploratory analyses on reaction time (RT) data to assess whether RT-based cueing effects exhibited similar associations with individual difference measures. As expected, participants responded faster to targets appearing at validly cued locations compared to invalid locations. However, correlation analyses revealed no significant associations between RT-based cueing effects and either ASR or SOCQ total scores. Because the task emphasized accurate detection of a faint target rather than response speed, accuracy was treated as the primary outcome measure. RT-based measures may reflect additional processes beyond perceptual discrimination, including motor execution latency and individual differences in response strategy, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously in the context of attentional orienting efficiency.

This dissociation between accuracy and RT findings may be attributed to the nature of the experimental task. Participants were instructed to detect the presence of a faint target (a light grey triangle), regardless of the number of stimuli, with no emphasis placed on response speed. As such, accuracy was the primary outcome measure, capturing perceptual discrimination and attentional allocation. In contrast, RT is more likely to be influenced by non-perceptual factors such as motor execution latency, decision confidence, and individual differences in speed–accuracy tradeoff strategies. Therefore, RT in this context may not offer a reliable or interpretable index of attentional cueing efficiency.

Finally, a significant negative correlation was found between ASR and SOCQ total scores, r(48) = –0.41, p < .005, BF10 = 16.07. This inverse relationship further supports the primary findings, indicating that atypical sensory responsiveness and perceived social connectedness are associated with attentional orienting in opposite directions.

Discussion

The present study examined whether individual differences in attentional orienting are dimensionally associated with variation in social functioning in a non-clinical adult sample. By examining the relationships between visual attention performance, ASR scores (problem behaviors), and SOCQ scores (social competence), we aimed to shed light on the connection between attentional mechanisms and social functioning.

Our findings revealed that participants performed significantly better in the valid cue condition than in the invalid cue condition, confirming the effectiveness of the attentional cue in guiding attention. Additionally, performance declined as the target’s distance from fixation increased, consistent with previous studies showing that peripheral targets require greater attentional resources for accurate detection [16]. Notably, the magnitude of the attentional cuing effect varied with ASR scores, such that the effect diminished as ASR scores increased. This finding suggests that higher levels of problem behavior (high ASR score) may be associated with differences in visual attentional orienting.

Conversely, SOCQ scores were positively correlated with the attentional cuing effect, meaning that individuals who reported stronger social ties demonstrated more effective attentional control. This finding suggests that greater social engagement may be associated with a more responsive and adaptive attentional system. Social interactions often require rapid shifts of attention to process dynamic visual cues, such as facial expressions and gestures. Thus, individuals with stronger social networks may have better-developed attentional mechanisms, allowing them to efficiently allocate visual attention in response to external cues. This implies that individuals with richer social interactions tend to exhibit more flexible and efficient attentional control.

To be noted, the psychometric properties of the Korean version of the SOCQ warrant consideration. While the original instrument was developed and validated with robust psychometric properties by Marijuan and colleagues, the Korean translation used in this study has not undergone formal cross-cultural psychometric validation. The internal consistency of the scale was acceptable in the current sample (α = 0.81), suggesting adequate reliability; however, the factor structure and convergent validity of the Korean SOCQ have not been formally examined. Future work should conduct a full cross-cultural validation of the Korean SOCQ — including confirmatory factor analysis and assessment of convergent and discriminant validity — before the scale is used more broadly in Korean-speaking populations.

Our results align with previous studies demonstrating that visual attention was associated with social performance in individuals with autism-like traits [2, 3, 17].

In line with these studies, the present findings extend prior work by demonstrating that attentional orienting is dimensionally associated with social functioning within a non-clinical adult population.

The negative correlation between ASR scores and the Sociotype Questionnaire (SOCQ) is also remarkable. This finding indicates that participants with higher ASR scores (indicating more problem behaviors) tend to have lower SOCQ scores, reflecting poorer social relationships. This is also broadly consistent with many previous studies emphasizing the close relationship between visual attention and social functioning [18–21].

Importantly, the present findings should also be considered in relation to our previous work examining visuospatial cueing differences associated with autistic-like traits in non-clinical adults [14]. Although that earlier publication addressed a related topic, it primarily served as a theoretical and conceptual review article in which an attentional orienting framework was proposed and preliminarily substantiated through a proof-of-concept experiment. The primary contribution of the earlier work was therefore conceptual, aiming to establish the feasibility of linking domain-general attentional mechanisms to autism-related social characteristics.

The current study substantially extends that framework in several important ways. First, rather than focusing specifically on autistic-like traits, the present study adopted a broader dimensional perspective by examining general problem behaviors and social connectedness using the ASR and SOCQ. Second, the current study investigated both maladaptive and adaptive dimensions of functioning, thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of the relationship between attentional orienting and social functioning. Third, we conducted subdimension-level analyses and Bayesian statistical analyses to provide a more rigorous and detailed assessment of these associations.

Taken together, these findings extend the earlier conceptual framework by demonstrating that attentional orienting is not only associated with autism-related characteristics, but may also reflect broader domain-general mechanisms underlying psychological functioning and social adaptability in the general adult population.

While the present study unveils the relationship between visual attention characteristics, level of problem behavior, and social functioning, this study has some limitations that warrant further investigation. First, this study had participants with a general adult population without clinical diagnoses, limiting its applicability to clinical groups. Future research involving individuals with significant problem behaviors is needed to determine whether similar relationships can be found at the clinical level. Second, related to the above, the relatively narrow age range of the sample may limit the generalizability of our findings to older adult populations. Attentional processes and social behavior can vary across the lifespan, and future research should examine whether the patterns observed here extend to older individuals. Additionally, the current study did not include measures of personality traits, which may serve as important mediating or moderating factors in the relationship between visual attention, sociability, and problem behaviors. Incorporating assessments of individual personality dimensions in future studies could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying these associations. Addressing these limitations will help clarify the extent to which our findings reflect broader patterns of cognitive and social functioning in diverse adult populations.

Third, although the current sample size of 50 participants was guided by a pilot-based power analysis, it remains modest for individual differences correlation research. Effect sizes estimated from pilot samples are known to frequently exceed those obtained in full studies, and the stability of the present correlations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. While the Bayes factors reported alongside the frequentist results (BF10 ranging from 4.62 to 31.13) provide additional evidence that the observed associations are unlikely to be spurious, replication in larger independent samples is necessary to confirm their robustness and to enable examination of potential moderating variables. Addressing these limitations will help clarify the extent to which our findings reflect broader patterns of cognitive and social functioning in diverse adult populations.

Taken together, the present findings suggest that individual differences in attentional orienting are dimensionally associated with variation in social functioning in a non-clinical adult sample [4, 5] These findings may inform future investigations exploring whether attentional processes contribute to broader patterns of social adaptation. Furthermore, future research may examine whether the present framework can be extended to clinical populations.
