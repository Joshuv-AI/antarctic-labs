Auto-Conditioned Frank-Wolfe Algorithms
Khanh-Hung Giang-Tran, Soroosh Shafiee, Nam Ho-Nguyen

Abstract
Frank-Wolfe methods are projection-free algorithms for constrained optimization whose practical performance often depends critically on the choice of step size. Classical closed-loop step-size rules typically require prior knowledge of a global smoothness constant, while line-search variants avoid this requirement at the cost of additional function evaluations and implementation overhead. In this paper, we develop a fully auto-conditioned framework for Frank-Wolfe-type methods. The framework replaces the global Lipschitz constant in closed-loop step sizes with a local Lipschitz estimator computed from first-order information along the iterates. We show that this abstraction captures several important projection-free subroutines, including standard Frank-Wolfe, Matching Pursuit, pairwise Frank-Wolfe, and away-step Frank-Wolfe. For the resulting general class of methods, we establish convergence to stationary points in the nonconvex setting and recover the standard sublinear convergence guarantees in the convex setting, without requiring prior knowledge of a global smoothness constant. We further show that, when specialized to particular Frank-Wolfe variants and combined with additional structural assumptions, the same auto-conditioned framework yields accelerated convergence rates. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed methods provide substantial practical improvements over line-search-based alternatives, highlighting the benefits of adapting to local curvature while retaining the simplicity of closed-loop step-size rules.

Introduction

Let
{\cal X}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a given feasible set, and consider the optimization problem

\min_{x\in{\cal X}}~f(x).

(
1
)

We denote
x^{\star}
to be an optimal solution to (
1
).
In this paper we are interested in settings where the feasible set
{\cal X}
is constructed via a dictionary
{\cal A}
, and the objective function satisfies standard smoothness assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Dictionary representation)
.

There exists a dictionary
{\cal A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
or
{\cal X}=\lin({\cal A})
where
\conv(\cdot)
denotes the convex hull of a set, and
\lin(\cdot)
denotes its linear span.
Moreover, the dictionary
{\cal A}
is compact set with diameter
D_{{\cal A}}<\infty
, that is,

\|x-y\|_{2}\leq D_{{\cal A}},\qquad\forall x,y\in{\cal A}.

Assumption 2

(Lipschitz gradient)
.

\nabla f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, that is,

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{2}\leq L\|x-y\|_{2},\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

In settings where
1
holds, a common consideration is to look for approximate solutions to
Eq.

1
that are
sparse
in the dictionary
{\cal A}
. For
\conv({\cal A})
and
\lin({\cal A})
, under
2
it is well-known that Frank-Wolfe and Matching Pursuit-type methods can find sparse solutions iteratively.

A key part of such methods is the selection of the stepsize, i.e., a parameter that governs how far we move away from the current iterate. A common choice is the classic closed-loop stepsize rule (also referred to as the short-step rule) based on the smoothness constant
L
. Namely, at iteration
t
, given
x_{t}\in{\cal X}
, one sets the stepsize to be

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}\leftarrow\argmin_{\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{t}^{\max}]}\left\{f(x_{t})-\gamma\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}+\frac{\gamma^{2}L\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right\}=\min\!\left\{\dfrac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L\lVert d_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}},\,\gamma_{t}^{\max}\right\},

(
2
)

where
\gamma_{t}^{\max}
the maximal admissible stepsize and
d_{t}
is the search direction; we will discuss how exactly these are set later.
For example, under the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe subroutine, one has
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=1
.
Note that the equality in
Eq.

2
follows from the fact that the search direction
d_{t}
will be chosen to ensure
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
.
Under appropriate conditions, a closed-loop stepsize rule can yield accelerated convergence guarantees compared to more conservative open-loop (i.e., fixed schedule) stepsizes. However, implementing it requires knowledge of
L
, and even when an estimate is available, its practical performance depends strongly on the quality of the estimate. That is, an overly large estimate typically leads to excessively small steps and therefore slow optimization progress.

An effective strategy to encourage less conservative closed-loop stepsizes is to use local smoothness parameters instead of the global constant
L
. While these are rarely available a priori, line search allows us to test different stepsizes to look for a sufficient decrease condition that would replicate the knowledge of such parameters. The disadvantage of this, however, is that each iteration may require multiple objective evaluations before an acceptable stepsize is found, which can substantially increase the per-iteration cost. In settings where these evaluations are expensive, the practical gains from taking larger steps may therefore be offset by the additional computational overhead of the line search procedure.

The main goal of this paper is to develop an auto-conditioned (line search-free) mechanism that improves this closed-loop rule by replacing the global parameter
L
with a local Lipschitz estimator computed from the current trial point, and provide convergence analyses for both Frank-Wolfe- and Matching Pursuit-type methods in a unified framework. To this end, for any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we define

\ell(x,y):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2\left|f(y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)\right|}{\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}},&x\neq y,\\[5.38193pt]
0,&x=y.\end{cases}

(
3
)

This quantity serves as a local estimate of the Lipschitz of
f
along the segment joining
x
and
y
, and it will be the basis for our auto-conditioned stepsize strategy.

The key contributions of this paper are summarized below.

\diamond

We propose a general abstract framework for auto-conditioned Frank-Wolfe methods based on a closed-loop step-size rule, where it uses the local Lipschitz estimator introduced in (
3
). We further show that several Frank-Wolfe variants, including the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe, standard Matching Pursuit, pairwise Frank-Wolfe, and away-step Frank-Wolfe subroutines, fit this abstraction.

\diamond

For this general framework, we establish convergence to stationary points in the nonconvex setting. In the convex setting, we recover the known sublinear convergence guarantees usually derived under prior knowledge of a global Lipschitz constant with closed-loop stepsizes, but now for a fully auto-conditioned method that uses only local Lipschitz estimates. We then show that acceleration is possible when the framework is specialized to particular subroutines. Namely, we establish accelerated convergence rates for the corresponding Frank-Wolfe variants under additional structural assumptions.

\diamond

Finally, our numerical results show that the proposed auto-conditioned approach is competitive with line-search alternatives and offers improvements in regimes where function evaluations are expensive.

Standard Matching Pursuit.

Pairwise Frank-Wolfe.

The pairwise Frank-Wolfe method updates the iterate by transferring mass from an active atom to a newly selected Frank-Wolfe atom, thereby reducing the zig-zagging behavior that often slows down the classical Frank-Wolfe method near the boundary of the feasible set

In this paper, we revisit the pairwise Frank-Wolfe method through an auto-conditioned scheme and show that it recovers the improved guarantees.

Away-Step Frank-Wolfe.

Away-step Frank-Wolfe, formalized by

We now introduce an adaptive variant of the classical closed-loop rule.

This yields an auto-conditioned framework that preserves the basic structure of Frank-Wolfe and Matching Pursuit methods while making the stepsize less sensitive to global smoothness estimates.

\{k_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is well defined.

Now fix
t\geq 0
. Since
k_{t}
is the
t
-th element of an increasing sequence of nonnegative integers, we clearly have
k_{t}\geq t
. On the other hand, we have
\left|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[k_{t}]\right|=t+1
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle k_{t}+1=\left|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[k_{t}]\right|+\left|{\cal I}_{\eta}^{c}\cap[k_{t}]\right|=(t+1)+\left|{\cal I}_{\eta}^{c}\cap[k_{t}]\right|\leq t+1+\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\!\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor.

Thus, the claim in (
7
) follows.
∎

The second requirement concerns the output of the subroutine.

Condition 2

(Subroutine admissibility)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, the following hold

(i)

The search direction satisfies
\|d_{t}\|_{2}\leq D_{{\cal A}}
and the whole segment generated by this direction remains feasible, that is,
x_{t}-\gamma d_{t}\in{\cal X}
for any
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{t}^{\max}]
.

(ii)

The set of good iterations
{\cal G}
defined in
Eq.

5
is infinite.

(iii)

If the function
f
is convex, then there exists a constant
R\geq 1
, independent of
t
, such that

\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}\geq\frac{f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})}{R},

where
x^{\star}
denotes an optimal solution of (
1
).

The next lemma collects the basic properties of
Section

2
that will be used repeatedly throughout the paper.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold, and that
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the sequence generated by
Section

2
. Under
1
and
2
, for every
t\geq 0
, the following hold.

(i)

The trial point and the next iterate satisfy

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1})\leq f(\bar{x}_{t+1})\leq f(x_{t})-\gamma_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}+\frac{L_{t+1}}{2}\gamma_{t}^{2}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(ii)

If
t\in{\cal I}_{\eta}
, then

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1})\leq f(x_{t})-\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\gamma_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}.

(iii)

If
t\in{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
, then

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1})\leq f(x_{t})-\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\min\left\{1,\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\right\}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}.

(iv)

If, in addition,
f
is convex and
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
, then

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})\geq f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star}).

Proof of
Lemma

4
.

We first prove
(i)
. By the update rule,

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\begin{cases}\bar{x}_{t+1},&\text{if }f(\bar{x}_{t+1})<f(x_{t}),\\
x_{t},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, in either case,
f(x_{t+1})\leq f(\bar{x}_{t+1})
.
Moreover, by definition
L_{t+1}\geq\ell(x_{t},\bar{x}_{t+1})
, implying

\displaystyle f(\bar{x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t})+\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(\bar{x}_{t+1}-x_{t})+\frac{L_{t+1}}{2}\|\bar{x}_{t+1}-x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

The claim then follows since, by construction,
\bar{x}_{t+1}-x_{t}=\gamma_{t}d_{t}
.

We now prove
(ii)
. By the definition of
\gamma_{t}
, we have
\gamma_{t}L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}
. Combining this with
(i)
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t})-\left(1-\frac{L_{t+1}}{2L_{t}}\right)\gamma_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}.

If
t\in{\cal I}_{\eta}
, then
L_{t+1}\leq\eta L_{t}
. Substituting this bound into the inequality above establishes the claim.

We next prove
(iii)
. If
t\in{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
, then either
\gamma_{t}^{\max}\geq 1
or
\gamma_{t}<\gamma_{t}^{\max}
.
If
\gamma_{t}^{\max}\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}=\min\left\{\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}},\gamma_{t}^{\max}\right\}\geq\min\left\{\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}},1\right\}.

If instead
\gamma_{t}<\gamma_{t}^{\max}
, then necessarily

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}=\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\geq\min\left\{\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}},1\right\}.

Hence, in both cases,

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}}{L_{t}\|d_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\right\}.

The claim then follows by combining this with
(ii)
.

Finally, if
f
is convex and
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
, then since
v_{t}
minimizes the linear functional
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}v
over
{\cal A}
and
x^{\star}\in\conv({\cal A})
, we have

\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})\geq\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-x^{\star})
.
By convexity of
f
, we additionally have
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-x^{\star})\geq f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})
. Combining these two establishes the claim.
∎

We now verify that several classic Frank-Wolfe direction-finding rules satisfy these admissibility conditions.
For each variant, we check
2
and then combine this verification with
Lemma

3
to obtain a quantitative lower bound on the cardinality of the index set
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
, which will be used later in the convex case.

2.1
Closed-Loop Frank-Wolfe Subroutine

We begin with the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe subroutine given in
Section

2
, where the feasible set is defined as
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\eta>1
. Under
1
and
2
, the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe subroutine in
Section

2
satisfies Conditions
2

(i)
and
(ii)
. More precisely, for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|\geq t+1-\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor.

(
8
)

If additionally
f
is convex, then
2

(iii)
holds with
R=1
.

Proof of
Lemma

5
.

Under
Section

2
, we have
d_{t}=x_{t}-v_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=1
.
Since
x_{t}\in\conv({\cal A})
and
v_{t}\in{\cal A}\subseteq\conv({\cal A})
, for any
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{t}^{\max}]=[0,1]
, we have
x_{t}-\gamma d_{t}=(1-\gamma)x_{t}+\gamma v_{t}\in\conv({\cal A}).

Moreover, because both
x_{t}
and

belong to
\conv({\cal A})
, and the diameter of
\conv({\cal A})
equals the diameter of
{\cal A}
, it follows that

Hence,
2

(i)
is satisfied.

Next, since
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=1
for every

, every iteration belongs to
{\cal G}
by
definition. Therefore,
{\cal G}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and in particular
2

(ii)
holds.
We now prove the cardinality bound.
Since
{\cal G}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we have
|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|=|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|
. The bound (
8
) now follows directly from
Lemma

3
, which implies that among the first
t+1
iterations, at most
\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\!\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor

{\cal I}_{\eta}
.

Finally, suppose that

is convex. Since here

,

4

(iv)
yields
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}=\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})\geq f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star}).
Thus,

(iii)
holds with

.
∎

2.2
Matching Pursuit Subroutine

We next consider the Matching Pursuit subroutine given in
Section

2
, where the feasible set is defined as
{\cal X}=\lin({\cal A})
.
We verify that this choice fits our abstract framework and then deduce a lower bound on the cardinality of
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
.
To do so, recall that given a set
{\cal A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
0\in\conv({\cal A})
, the associated atomic norm of a vector
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined by
\|x\|_{{\cal A}}:=\inf\{c>0:x\in c\cdot\conv({\cal A})\}
.

Assumption 3

(Regular dictionary)
.

The dictionary
{\cal A}
is symmetric around the origin, that is,
{\cal A}=-{\cal A}
. Furthermore, if
f
is convex, the initial level set has bounded atomic radius, that is,

\displaystyle R_{{\cal A}}=\max\left\{1,\;\sup\left\{\|x-x^{\star}\|_{{\cal A}}:x\in\lin({\cal A}),\,f(x)\leq f(x_{0})\right\}\right\}<\infty.

The symmetry part of
3
is without loss of generality for Matching Pursuit. Indeed, replacing
{\cal A}
by its symmetrization
\tilde{{\cal A}}:={\cal A}\cup(-{\cal A})
does not change the feasible set, since
\lin(\tilde{{\cal A}})=\lin({\cal A})
.
By contrast, the finiteness of
R_{{\cal A}}
is a regularity assumption on the initial level set. Nonetheless, this condition is only required to show that
2

(iii)
holds.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\eta>1
. Under
1
,
2
and
3
, the Matching Pursuit subroutine in
Section

2
satisfies Conditions
2

(i)
and
(ii)
.
More precisely, for any
t\geq 0
, the bound (
8
) holds for the MP subroutime as well. If additionally
f
is convex, then
2

(iii)
holds with
R=R_{{\cal A}}
.

Proof of
Lemma

6
.

Under
Section

2
, we have
d_{t}=-v_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=\infty.

Since
{\cal A}=-{\cal A}
,
-v_{t}\in{\cal A}
, and therefore
2\|v_{t}\|_{2}=\|v_{t}-(-v_{t})\|_{2}\leq D_{{\cal A}}
.
Thus, we have
\|d_{t}\|_{2}=\|v_{t}\|_{2}\leq D_{{\cal A}}.

Furthermore, for any
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
,
x_{t}-\gamma d_{t}=x_{t}+\gamma v_{t}\in\lin({\cal A})={\cal X}
, since both
x_{t}
and
v_{t}
belong to
\lin({\cal A})
. Thus,
2

(i)
holds.

Next, since
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=\infty\geq 1
for every
t\geq 0
, every iteration belongs to
{\cal G}
. Therefore,
{\cal G}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and in particular
2

(ii)
holds. The bound (
8
) now follows directly from
Lemma

3
.

Finally, suppose that
f
is convex. Since the acceptance rule in
Section

2
guarantees
f(x_{t})\leq f(x_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
,
3
implies
\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|_{{\cal A}}\leq R_{{\cal A}}
.
We thus have

\displaystyle f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\leq\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-x^{\star})\leq\Big(\max_{a\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}a\Big)\cdot\|x_{t}-x^{\star}\|_{{\cal A}}\leq R_{{\cal A}}\Big(\max_{a\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}a\Big),

where the first inequality follows from convexity of
f
and the second inequality follows from the polar inequality
[
51
, Theorem 15.1]
.
Since
{\cal A}=-{\cal A}
and
v_{t}\in\argmin_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}v
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{a\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}a=-\min_{a\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}a=-\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}v_{t}=\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}.

Combining the above inequalities yields
f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star})\leq R_{{\cal A}}\,\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}.

Thus,
2

(iii)
holds with
R=R_{{\cal A}}
.
∎

2.3
Pairwise Frank-Wolfe Subroutine

We next consider the pairwise Frank-Wolfe subroutine given in
Section

2
, where the feasible set is
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
.
This variant is naturally implemented by maintaining an active-set representation
x_{t}=\sum_{s\in{\cal A}}\alpha_{s,t}s
, where, by convention,
\alpha_{s,t}=0
for
s\notin{\cal S}_{t}
. The essential idea of
Section

2
is to “swap out” the worst performing atom of the current active set
s_{t}\in{\cal S}_{t}
, and swap in the most promising atom
v_{t}\in{\cal A}
.
This can be implemented recursively by updating the pair
({\cal S}_{t},\alpha_{\cdot,t})
from one iteration to the next. If the trial point is accepted, i.e.,
x_{t+1}=\bar{x}_{t+1}
, then the coefficients are updated according to

\displaystyle\alpha_{s,t+1}=\alpha_{s,t}-\gamma_{t}\,\mathds{1}_{\{s=s_{t}\}}+\gamma_{t}\,\mathds{1}_{\{s=v_{t}\}},\qquad\forall s\in{\cal A}.

If the trial point is rejected, i.e.,
x_{t+1}=x_{t}
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{s,t+1}=\alpha_{s,t},\qquad\forall s\in{\cal A}.

In all cases, the next active set is recovered via
{\cal S}_{t+1}=\{s\in{\cal A}:\alpha_{s,t+1}>0\}
. We now verify that this choice fits our abstract framework and then derive a lower bound on the cardinality of
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
under the following additional assumption.

Assumption 4

(Finite dictionary)
.

The set of atoms
{\cal A}
is finite.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\eta\in(1,2)
. Under
1
,
2
and
4
, the pairwise Frank-Wolfe subroutine in
Section

2
satisfies Conditions
2

(i)
and
(ii)
.
More precisely, for any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|\geq\frac{t+1}{3|{\cal A}|!+1}-\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor.

(
9
)

If
f
is additionally convex, then
2

(iii)
holds with
R=1
.

Proof of
Lemma

7
.

Under
Section

2
, we have
d_{t}=s_{t}-v_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\max}=\alpha_{s_{t},t}
. Since
s_{t},v_{t}\in{\cal A}
, we have
\|d_{t}\|_{2}=\|s_{t}-v_{t}\|_{2}\leq D_{{\cal A}}
.
Moreover, for any
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{t}^{\max}]=[0,\alpha_{s_{t},t}]
, writing
x_{t}=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}_{t}}\alpha_{s,t}s
,
we obtain
x_{t}-\gamma d_{t}=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}_{t}\setminus\{s_{t},v_{t}\}}\alpha_{s,t}s+(\alpha_{s_{t},t}-\gamma)s_{t}+(\alpha_{v_{t},t}+\gamma)v_{t}
, where we use the convention
\alpha_{v_{t},t}=0
if
v_{t}\notin{\cal S}_{t}
. All coefficients remain nonnegative and still sum to
1
, and therefore
x_{t}-\gamma d_{t}\in\conv({\cal A})={\cal X}.

Hence,
2

(i)
holds.

We next prove the lower bound in (
9
), from which
2

(ii)
will follow.
Define the set

\displaystyle{\cal H}:=\{k\geq 0:f(\bar{x}_{k+1})<f(x_{k})\}.

Let
\ell_{0}<\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{m}
be the elements of
{\cal H}\cap[t]
. We first aim to show

|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|\geq\frac{|{\cal H}\cap[t]|}{3|{\cal A}|!+1}.

(
10
)

Define
J_{t}:=\{j\in[m]:\ell_{j}\notin{\cal G}\}
.
If
J_{t}=\emptyset
, then
|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|=|{\cal H}\cap[t]|
, so (
10
) is immediate. Hence we may assume
J_{t}\neq\emptyset
.
Write
J_{t}=\bigcup_{r\in[R_{t}]}\{a_{r},a_{r}+1,\dots,b_{r}\}
as union of disjoint integer intervals,
where
a_{0}\leq b_{0}<a_{1}\leq b_{1}<\cdots<a_{R_{t}}\leq b_{R_{t}}
.
We claim

\displaystyle b_{r}-a_{r}+1\leq 3|{\cal A}|!,\qquad r\in[R_{t}].

Indeed, for every
j\in\{a_{r},\dots,b_{r}\}
, since
\ell_{j}\notin{\cal G}
, we have
\gamma_{\ell_{j}}^{\max}<1
and
\gamma_{\ell_{j}}=\gamma_{\ell_{j}}^{\max}=\alpha_{s_{\ell_{j}},\ell_{j}}
.
Thus, we have
|{\cal S}_{\ell_{j}}|\geq 2
, and the atom
s_{\ell_{j}}
is removed from the active set at the next iterate.
If
v_{\ell_{j}}\in{\cal S}_{\ell_{j}}
, then the active-set size decreases by
1
.
If
v_{\ell_{j}}\notin{\cal S}_{\ell_{j}}
, then the active-set size stays the same as
{\cal S}_{\ell_{j}+1}=({\cal S}_{\ell_{j}}\setminus\{s_{\ell_{j}}\})\cup\{v_{\ell_{j}}\}.

Since
\ell_{j}\in{\cal H}
, we also have
f(x_{\ell_{j}+1})<f(x_{\ell_{j}}),
so the same iterate cannot reappear.

Fix
c\in\{2,\dots,|{\cal A}|\}
. Whenever
|{\cal S}_{\ell_{j}}|=c
, the number of possible iterates is at most
{|{\cal A}|!}/{(|{\cal A}|-c)!}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle b_{r}-a_{r}+1\leq\sum_{b=2}^{|{\cal A}|}\frac{|{\cal A}|!}{(|{\cal A}|-c)!}=|{\cal A}|!\sum_{q=0}^{|{\cal A}|-2}\frac{1}{q!}<e|{\cal A}|!<3|{\cal A}|!,

as desired.

We next find a lower bound for
|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|
.
Since
{\cal S}_{0}=\{x_{0}\}
, the first element of
{\cal H}\cap[t]
belongs to
{\cal G}
. Hence
a_{0}\neq 0
, so
a_{r}-1\in[m]\setminus J_{t}
for every
r
, and these indices are distinct. Thus,
R_{t}+1\leq|[m]\setminus J_{t}|
.
Consequently, we have
|J_{t}|=\sum_{r\in[R_{t}]}(b_{r}-a_{r}+1)\leq 3|{\cal A}|!\,(R_{t}+1)\leq 3|{\cal A}|!\,|[m]\setminus J_{t}|.

Since

\displaystyle|[m]\setminus J_{t}|=|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|J_{t}|+|[m]\setminus J_{t}|=m+1=|{\cal H}\cap[t]|,

we conclude that
Eq.

10
holds.

We now show that
{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]\subseteq{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]
. Let
k\in{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]
. Since
k\notin{\cal G}
, we have
\gamma_{k}^{\max}<1
and
\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{k}^{\max}
.
If
k\notin{\cal H}
, then
f(\bar{x}_{k+1})\geq f(x_{k})=f(x_{k+1})
. By
Lemma

4

(i)
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq f(\bar{x}_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{L_{k+1}}{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}\|d_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{L_{k}\|d_{k}\|_{2}^{2}},\gamma_{k}^{\max}\right\}=\gamma_{k}^{\max}\implies\gamma_{k}L_{k}\|d_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k},

and therefore
0\leq f(\bar{x}_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq(\tfrac{L_{k+1}}{2}-L_{k})\gamma_{k}^{2}\|d_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence
L_{k+1}\geq 2L_{k}
, which contradicts
k\in{\cal I}_{\eta}
, since
\eta<2
. Therefore
k\in{\cal H}
.

It thus follows that
|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]|\leq|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]|
.
By (
10
), we have

\displaystyle|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]|=|{\cal H}\cap[t]|-|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|\leq 3|{\cal A}|!\,|{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]|.

Since
{\cal H}\cap{\cal G}\cap[t]\subseteq({\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t])\cup({\cal I}_{\eta}^{c}\cap[t]),

we obtain
|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]|\leq 3|{\cal A}|!\,(|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|+|{\cal I}_{\eta}^{c}\cap[t]|)
.

Using the first part of
Lemma

3
,

\displaystyle|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|\geq t+1-\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor\qquad\text{and}\qquad|{\cal I}_{\eta}^{c}\cap[t]|\leq\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor.

Since
|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|=|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|-|{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap{\cal G}^{c}\cap[t]|,
we have

|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|\geq t+1-\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor-3|{\cal A}|!\Bigl(|{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}\cap[t]|+\left\lfloor\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{L}{rL_{0}}\right)\right\rfloor\Bigr).

Rearranging yields (
9
). Since the right-hand side of (
9
) tends to
+\infty
as
t\to\infty
, it follows that the set
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
is infinite, and therefore
{\cal G}
is infinite as well. This proves
2

(ii)
.

Finally, suppose that
f
is convex. Since
x_{t}
is a convex combination of atoms in
{\cal S}_{t}
and
s_{t}
maximizes the linear functional
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}s
over
{\cal S}_{t}
, we have
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(s_{t}-x_{t})\geq 0
.
Using
Lemma

4

(iv)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}=\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(s_{t}-x_{t})+\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})\geq\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})\geq f(x_{t})-f(x^{\star}).

Hence,
2

(iii)
holds with
R=1
.
∎

2.4
Away-Step Frank-Wolfe Subroutine

We now consider the away-step Frank-Wolfe subroutine given in
Section

2
, where the feasible set is defined as
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
.
The away-step variant can be implemented recursively by carrying the pair
({\cal S}_{t},\alpha_{\cdot,t})
from one iteration to the next.
If the trial point is accepted, i.e.,
x_{t+1}=\bar{x}_{t+1}
, then an accepted Frank-Wolfe step gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{s,t+1}=(1-\gamma_{t})\alpha_{s,t}+\gamma_{t}\,\mathds{1}_{\{s=v_{t}\}},\qquad\forall s\in{\cal A},

whereas an accepted away step gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{s,t+1}=(1+\gamma_{t})\alpha_{s,t}-\gamma_{t}\,\mathds{1}_{\{s=s_{t}\}},\qquad\forall s\in{\cal A}.

If the trial point is rejected, i.e.,
x_{t+1}=x_{t}
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{s,t+1}=\alpha_{s,t},\qquad\forall s\in{\cal A}.

In all cases, the next active set is recovered from
{\cal S}_{t+1}=\{s\in{\cal A}:\alpha_{s,t+1}>0\}
.
We now verify that this choice fits our abstract framework and then deduce a lower bound on the cardinality of
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let

This section develops the convergence theory that is common to all admissible subroutines in our framework. More precisely, throughout this section we only assume

3.1
Nonconvex settings

In the nonconvex setting, the quantity
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}
is a natural stationarity measure for our framework. Indeed, when
{\cal X}=\conv({\cal A})
, a first-order stationary point
x\in{\cal X}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x)^{\top}(x-v)=0.

This is exactly the first-order optimality condition when the problem is convex. For the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe, away-step, and pairwise subroutines, one always has

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}\geq\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v_{t})=\max_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}(x_{t}-v).

Thus, controlling
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}
controls a valid first-order stationarity measure over
\conv({\cal A})
.

When
{\cal X}=\lin({\cal A})
, the corresponding first-order condition is

\displaystyle\max_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x)^{\top}v=0.

Under the Matching Pursuit subroutine, we have
d_{t}=-v_{t}
, and thus,
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}=-\min_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}v
.
Under the symmetry assumption
{\cal A}=-{\cal A}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}=\max_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}v.

Therefore, in both regimes, the quantity
\nabla f(x_{t})^{\top}d_{t}
provides a meaningful measure of first-order nonstationarity.

Using the lower bound above and

\displaystyle f(x_{h_{t}+1})-f(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{h_{t}})-f(x^{\star})-\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\min\left\{1,\frac{\alpha\|\nabla f(x_{h_{t}})\|_{2}}{8L}\right\}\bigl(f(x_{h_{t}})-f(x^{\star})\bigr).

Suppose now that

4.2
Acceleration under Matching Pursuit, Pairwise, and Away-Step Subroutines

We now turn to linear convergence under stronger assumptions for the Matching Pursuit, pairwise Frank-Wolfe, and away-step Frank-Wolfe subroutines.
In all three cases, the key additional ingredient is a quadratic error bound relating the objective gap to the square of the directional gap generated by the corresponding subroutine.
We first introduce the geometric quantities that appear in these bounds, and then state the main theorem of this subsection.

Definition 2

(Geometric strong convexity)
.

The geometric strong convexity constant
\mu_{\cal A}
of
f
over
\conv({\cal A})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{\cal A}:=\inf\left\{\frac{2\bigl(f(y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)\bigr)}{\gamma_{{\cal A}}(y,x)^{2}}:x,y\in\conv({\cal A}),\ \nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)<0\right\},

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\cal A}}(y,x):=\frac{-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)}{\nabla f(x)^{\top}(s_{{\cal A}}(x)-v_{{\cal A}}(x))},\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle v_{{\cal A}}(x)\in\argmin_{v\in{\cal A}}\nabla f(x)^{\top}v,

\displaystyle s_{{\cal A}}(x)\in\argmin\left\{\nabla f(x)^{\top}s:s\in\argmax_{u\in{\cal S}}\nabla f(x)^{\top}u,\ {\cal S}\in{\cal S}_{x}\right\},

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{x}:=\left\{{\cal S}\subseteq{\cal A}:x\text{ is a proper convex combination of all atoms in }{\cal S}\right\}.

Definition 3

(Minimal directional width)
.

The minimal directional width of
{\cal A}
is

\displaystyle w_{\cal A}:=\min_{d\in\lin({\cal A})\setminus\{0\}}\max_{z\in{\cal A}}\frac{z^{\top}d}{\|d\|_{2}}.

Under
7
and
4
, the constant
\mu_{\cal A}
is strictly positive by
[
28
, Theorem 6]
. Let
\{h_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the elements of
{\cal G}\cap{\cal I}_{\eta}
in increasing order.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
\{x_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the sequence generated by
Section

2
under one of the subroutines
Sections

2
,
2
and
2
, and let
\eta\in(1,2)
. Assume
1
,
2
and
7
together with Condition
1
. In addition, suppose
3
holds under the Matching Pursuit subroutine, and
4
holds under the pairwise and away-step subroutines. Then, for any

,

\displaystyle f(x_{h_{t}})-f(x^{\star})\leq\bigl(f(x_{0})-f(x^{\star})\bigr)\max\left\{1-\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\frac{1}{R},1-\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\frac{1}{GLD_{{\cal A}}^{2}}\right\}^{t},

where
G=1/(2\mu w_{\cal A}^{2})
for the Matching Pursuit subroutine,

for the pairwise Frank-Wolfe subroutine, and

for the away-step Frank-Wolfe subroutine.

relies on the following subroutine-specific quadratic error bounds. These estimates follow from

5
Numerical Results

We compare variants of Frank-Wolfe methods across five problem classes, organized into four algorithmic families: the closed-loop Frank-Wolfe method (
CFW
), the away-step Frank-Wolfe method (
AFW
), the pairwise Frank-Wolfe method (
PFW
), and the Matching Pursuit method (
MP
). For each family, we evaluate three stepsize rules: the classic variant that uses the global Lipschitz constant of the gradient (no prefix), the backtracking line-search in
[
46
]
(prefix
B-
), and our auto-conditioned scheme proposed in
Section

2
(prefix
AC-
). For reference, we also include the open-loop Frank-Wolfe method (
OFW
), corresponding to the Frank-Wolfe subroutine with
\gamma_{t}=2/(t+2)
in convex setting and
\gamma_{t}=1/\sqrt{t+1}
.
In the following, we first describe the problem formulations and datasets and then present the numerical results. To ensure reproducibility, all source codes are made available at
https://github.com/brucegiang/AC-FW
.

Dictionary Learning.

The goal of dictionary learning is to find a compact representation of a dataset
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Formally, we seek a dictionary
D=[d_{1}\cdots d_{p}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times p}
such that each data point
a_{i}
is well approximated by a linear combination of the columns of
D
. This leads to the problem

\displaystyle\min_{D,X}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|a_{i}-Dx_{i}\|_{2}^{2}:\begin{array}[]{l}D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times p},~X=[x_{1}\cdots x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n},\\[4.30554pt]
\|d_{j}\|_{2}\leq 1,~\forall j\in[p],\quad\|x_{i}\|_{1}\leq\beta,~\forall i\in[n]\end{array}\right\},

where
X
is the coefficient matrix. We generate the data as follows. We set
n=1000
,
m=500
, and
p=200
, and construct the dataset matrix
A=BC\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, where
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times p}
has entries drawn i.i.d. from the standard Gaussian distribution with columns rescaled to unit
\ell_{2}
-norm, and
C=\tfrac{1}{\|U\|_{2}\|V\|_{2}}UV^{\top}
with
U\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times l}
and
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times l}
also drawn i.i.d. from the standard Gaussian distribution and
l=50
. We test
\beta\in\{5,10,15\}
. The data generation follows the procedure in
[
9
,
19
]
. We implement all variants of the
CFW
algorithm, along with
OFW
, in the nonconvex setting.

Constrained Regularized Logistic Regression Problem.

In a binary classification setting, we are given a training dataset
{\cal D}^{\text{tr}}=\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the feature vector and
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the class label. The objective is to learn a predictor
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that, for a new instance
\hat{a}
, the predicted label is
\hat{b}=1
if
\hat{a}^{\top}x\geq 0
and
\hat{b}=0
otherwise. We consider the following optimization problem

\min_{\|x\|_{1}\leq\beta}~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\ell(a_{i}^{\top}x,b_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2},

where for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
, the logistic loss is given by
\ell(y,b):=\log(1+e^{y})-by
.
Following
[
46
]
, we evaluate our algorithms on the
RCV1.binary
dataset from the LIBSVM repository
[
12
]
and the
Madelon
dataset from the UCI repository
[
4
]
, and we set
\lambda=0.01
and
\beta\in\{5,10,15\}
. We implement all variants of
CFW
,
PFW
, and
AFW
, along with
OFW
, in the convex setting with a finite dictionary.

Unconstrained Regularized Logistic Regression Problem.

To evaluate the Matching Pursuit subroutine in
Section

2
, we also consider the following optimization problem

\min_{x\in\lin({\cal A})}~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\ell(a_{i}^{\top}x,b_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2},

where
{\cal A}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|x\|_{1}\leq\beta\}
and
\ell
denotes the logistic loss. Following
[
46
]
, we use the same datasets and parameter settings as in the constrained case. We implement all variants of
MP
in the convex setting.

Constrained Huber Regression Problem.

We consider the collaborative filtering problem
[
41
]

\displaystyle\min_{\|X\|_{*}\leq\beta}~\frac{1}{|{\cal I}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in{\cal I}}\ell_{\delta}(A_{ij}-X_{ij}),

where
{\cal I}\subseteq[m]\times[p]
is the set of observed entries of
A
, and
\ell_{\delta}
denotes the Huber loss with parameter
\delta>0
, defined as
\ell_{\delta}(a):=\tfrac{1}{2}a^{2}
if
|a|\leq\delta
and
\ell_{\delta}(a):=\delta(|a|-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta)
otherwise. Following
[
46
]
, we use the
MovieLens
dataset
[
21
]
with 1M movie ratings, and set
\delta=1
and

. We implement all variants of

Distributionally Robust Minimum Mean Square Error Estimation.

We consider the distributionally robust minimum mean square error estimation problem studied in
[
52
,
42
]
, which seeks an estimator of a signal
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
from an observation
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
under distributional uncertainty modeled by a Wasserstein ambiguity set around a nominal Gaussian distribution
\mathbb{P}={\cal N}_{d}(0,\Sigma)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
d=n+m
. As shown in
[
52
, Theorem 2.5]
, the resulting minimax problem admits the convex reformulation

\displaystyle\max\left\{\Tr\left[S_{xx}-S_{xy}S_{yy}^{-1}S_{yx}\right]:S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{xx}&S_{xy}\\
S_{yx}&S_{yy}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d},\ \Tr\left[S+\Sigma-2\left(\Sigma^{1/2}S\Sigma^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}\right]\leq\rho^{2}\right\},

where
\rho>0
is the Wasserstein radius.
Let
S^{\star}
denote the optimizer of the above problem. Given output
y
, the prediction is
\hat{x}=S^{\star}_{yx}(S_{yy}^{\star})^{-1}y
. Maybe surprising, the linear minimization oracle over the feasible set admits a quasi-closed solution due to
[
52
, Theorem 3.2]
.

We generate the data following
[
52
, Section 5.1]
. We aim to predict a signal
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4d/5}
from an observation
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d/5}
, with
d\in\{100,1000\}
.
We draw a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, and let
R
denotes the orthogonal matrices whose columns are the orthonormal eigenvectors of
A+A^{\top}
. We then define
\Sigma=R\Lambda R^{\top}
, where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix with entries drawn uniformly from
[0.1,10]^{d}
. The Wasserstein radius is set to
\rho=\sqrt{d}
. We implement all variants of
CFW
, along with
OFW
, in the convex setting with an infinite dictionary.

Summary of Numerical Results.

Figures
1
reports the Frank-Wolfe gap as a function of wall-clock time for all variants across the five problem classes described above. Across nearly all instances, the auto-conditioned variants (
AC-
) match or outperform their backtracking counterparts (
B-
), and both consistently improve on the classic variants that rely on a global Lipschitz constant and on the open-loop
OFW
baseline. The advantage of
AC-
over
B-
is most pronounced when objective evaluations are expensive. For example, on the dictionary learning,
MovieLens
, and distributionally robust MMSE estimation problems,
AC-CFW
reaches gap levels several orders of magnitude smaller than
B-CFW
within the same time budget, reflecting the per-iteration savings from avoiding repeated function evaluations during line search. On the logistic regression problems, where function evaluations are comparatively cheap, the gap between
AC-
and
B-
narrows, but the auto-conditioned variants remain competitive across all subroutines and choices of
\beta
. The plain variants and
OFW
, which do not adapt to local curvature, lag substantially in most instances and in some cases stall well above the levels reached by the adaptive methods. Overall, these results indicate that the auto-conditioned scheme delivers the benefits of adaptivity while retaining the simplicity and low per-iteration cost of closed-loop step-size rules. To ensure reproducibility, all
source codes are made available at
https://github.com/brucegiang/AC-FW
.
