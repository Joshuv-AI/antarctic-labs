On the paucity of lattice triangles
David Kurniadi Angdinata, Evan Chen, Ken Ono, Jiaxin Zhang, Jujian Zhang

Abstract
. A rational triangle T T (one whose angles are rational multiples of π \pi ) unfolds to a translation surface ( X T , ω T ) (X_{T},\omega_{T}) . The lattice triangle problem asks to classify those T T for which ( X T , ω T ) (X_{T},\omega_{T}) is a Veech (lattice) surface, which means that the SL 2 ​ ( ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}) -orbit of ( X T , ω T ) (X_{T},\omega_{T}) is closed in its stratum (so its projection to moduli space is a Teichmüller curve). The classification of lattice surfaces is deeply connected to the study of orbit

[
2

3

13

Mirzakhani and Wright provided a definitive geometric tool for this study

For the specific case of rational triangles, Larsen, Norton,
and Zykoski
[

succeeded in assembling this abstract geometric rank

This criterion, which we describe below,
reduces the lattice property to a check of modular arithmetic relations among
the triangle’s angles across the reduced residue system modulo

.

To state this reformulation, we require some notation.
Write

for the least nonnegative residue of

We also let

(3)

U_{n}\coloneq\{a\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}:\ \gcd(a,n)=1\}

be the reduced residue system modulo

.
Furthermore, a unit

is called

2a\not\equiv 2\pmod{n}.

Remark
.

If

is odd, then the unique non-usable unit is

.
If
n
is even, the solutions to

are

and

(Larsen–Norton–Zykoski, Proposition 2.1
[

3.
The sum
S(p,q)
and its Fourier expansion into Ramanujan sums

In this paper,
we’ll in fact attempt to find a usable

[ap]_{n}<[2p]_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[aq]_{n}<[2q]_{n}.

Since these are already two of the three inequalities in (

Define the indicator of the interval
I_{m}\coloneq\{1,2,\dots,m\}\subset\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

1_{I_{m}}(x)\coloneq\begin{cases}1,&1\leq[x]_{n}\leq m,\\

For

m_{p}\coloneq 2p-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{q}\coloneq 2q-1.

(We have

since we are assuming our triangle is obtuse.)
The main protagonist of our paper is the counting function

defined by

By definition,

counts the number of units

Our goal is to show that we typically have

A direct combinatorial approach is difficult due to the complex distribution of the units

Hence in the rest of this section we will rewrite

using discrete Fourier analysis to transform the problem into a sum
over the character group of

This transition allows us to exploit the orthogonality of characters and the
specific vanishing properties of Ramanujan sums to obtain the necessary bounds.

3.1.
Discrete Fourier coefficients of short intervals

For a function
f\colon\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
, define its (normalized) Fourier coefficients

\widehat{f}(k)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}f(x)\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ikx/n},\qquad k\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}.

Then classical Fourier inversion gives

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f}(k)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ikx/n}.

Lemma 3.1

(Fourier coefficients of an interval)
.

If
m\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
and
f=1_{I_{m}}
, then

\widehat{f}(0)=m/n
, and for
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, we have

|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq\frac{1}{2\,\min(k,n-k)}.

In particular, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq 1+\log n.

Proof.

The formula for
\widehat{f}(0)
is immediate.
For
k\neq 0
, we have

\widehat{f}(k)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ikx/n}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ik/n}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ikm/n}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ik/n}}.

Thus, we have

|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{2}{|1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ik/n}|}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{|\sin(\pi k/n)|}.

Using
\left\lvert\sin x\right\rvert\geq\frac{2}{\pi}\min(x,\pi-x)
on
[0,\pi]
gives

\left\lvert\sin(\pi k/n)\right\rvert\geq\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{n}\min(k,n-k)=\frac{2}{n}\min(k,n-k),

so

|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq\frac{1}{2\,\min(k,n-k)}.

Summing this bound over
k
and pairing
k
with
n-k
gives

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2\,\min(k,n-k)}=\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{t}.

For
n\geq 10
we have the standard estimate

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{t}\leq\log n
(and the claimed bound
may be checked directly for
2\leq n\leq 9
). Adding
|\widehat{f}(0)|=m/n\leq 1
proves the last claim.
∎

3.2.
Ramanujan sums

The Fourier estimates established in Lemma
3.1
provide control
over the indicator functions of the intervals
I_{m_{p}}
and
I_{m_{q}}

in the frequency domain.
However, because our counting function
S(p,q)
involves a sum
restricted to the units
a\in U_{n}
,
the resulting expansion naturally gives rise to exponential sums
over reduced residue systems.
These sums, known as Ramanujan sums, play a vital role in our ability
to detect number-theoretic obstructions.
In this subsection, we recall their formal properties and establish
specific vanishing conditions for prime powers,
which will eventually allow us to show that the error terms in our expansion
are small when the denominator
n
possesses a large prime factor.

For
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the Ramanujan sum (see Chapter 16 of
[
5
]
)

(6)

c_{n}(t)\coloneq\sum_{a\in U_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi iat/n}.

These sums can be explicitly evaluated.

Lemma 3.2

(Ramanujan sums at prime powers)
.

Let
p
be prime,
k\geq 1
, and write
v=v_{p}(t)
(with the convention
v_{p}(0)=\infty
).
Then

c_{p^{k}}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&v\leq k-2,\\
-\,p^{k-1},&v=k-1,\\
\varphi(p^{k})=p^{k}-p^{k-1},&v\geq k.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\zeta\coloneq\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i/p^{k}}
.
Write the sum over units as all residues minus those divisible by
p
:

c_{p^{k}}(t)=\sum_{a=0}^{p^{k}-1}\zeta^{at}\;-\;\sum_{b=0}^{p^{k-1}-1}\zeta^{(pb)t}.

The first geometric sum is
p^{k}
if
p^{k}\mid t
and
0
otherwise.
The second is
p^{k-1}
if
p^{k-1}\mid t
and
0
otherwise.
Therefore, we have:

•

If
p^{k-1}\nmid t
(i.e.
v\leq k-2
), both sums vanish, so
c_{p^{k}}(t)=0
.

•

If
p^{k-1}\mid t
but
p^{k}\nmid t
(i.e.
v=k-1
), then
c_{p^{k}}(t)=0-p^{k-1}=-p^{k-1}
.

•

If
p^{k}\mid t
(i.e.
v\geq k
), then
c_{p^{k}}(t)=p^{k}-p^{k-1}=\varphi(p^{k})
. ∎

Lemma 3.3

(Multiplicativity)
.

If
\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})=1
, then
c_{n_{1}n_{2}}(t)=c_{n_{1}}(t)c_{n_{2}}(t)
.

Proof.

This is standard and follows from the Chinese remainder theorem and the product
decomposition of reduced residue systems modulo coprime moduli.
∎

3.3.
The main term and error term for
S(p,q)

Having established the necessary bounds on the Fourier coefficients of interval
indicators and the properties of Ramanujan sums in the previous section,
we can now explicitly decompose
S(p,q)

into a dominant main term
M(p,q)
and a fluctuating error term
E(p,q)
.

Let
f_{p}\coloneq 1_{I_{m_{p}}}
and
f_{q}\coloneq 1_{I_{m_{q}}}
.
By Fourier inversion, we have

f_{p}(ap)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ikap/n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{q}(aq)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\ell aq/n}.

Plugging into (
5
) gives

S(p,q)=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\sum_{a\in U_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ia(kp+\ell q)/n}.

Thus, we have

(7)

S(p,q)=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{n-1}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\,c_{n}(kp+\ell q).

By splitting off the main term corresponding to
(k,\ell)=(0,0)
, we find that

M(p,q)\coloneq\widehat{f_{p}}(0)\widehat{f_{q}}(0)c_{n}(0)=\frac{m_{p}}{n}\cdot\frac{m_{q}}{n}\cdot\varphi(n)=\frac{(2p-1)(2q-1)}{n^{2}}\varphi(n).

In our work, we will need to control the remaining contribution, which we think of as the “error term”

(8)

E(p,q)\coloneq S(p,q)-M(p,q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k,\ell\leq n-1\\
(k,\ell)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\,c_{n}(kp+\ell q).

Remark

(A crude global bound on
E(p,q)
for comparison)
.

Before exploiting the specific arithmetic properties of the denominator
n
,
we first establish a baseline for the magnitude of the error term
E(p,q)
defined by (
8
).
By applying the uniform Fourier coefficient estimates from
Lemma
3.1
and the trivial bound for Ramanujan sums,
we obtain a global estimate that holds for all candidates in the hard window.
While this initial bound is too coarse to prove
S(p,q)\geq 5
for all cases,
it provides the necessary framework to show that the primary challenge lies in
controlling the sum over characters whose frequencies align with the prime power divisors of
n
.
Using
|c_{n}(t)|\leq\varphi(n)
and Lemma
3.1
, we have

(9)

|E(p,q)|\leq\varphi(n)\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{f_{p}}(k)|\Big)\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)|\Big)-M(p,q)\leq\varphi(n)(1+\log n)^{2}.

However, this crude bound is too weak for our purposes.
To achieve a meaningful saving in the error term
E(p,q)
,
we must exploit the specific arithmetic structure of the denominator
n
.
This is where the hypothesis on
P^{+}(n)
comes into play.

4.

The global bound derived in (

in sparse residue classes.

Specifically, in this section our goal is to prove the following result

c_{P^{\alpha}}(t)=0\quad\text{whenever}\quad v_{P}(t)\leq\alpha-2,

equivalently,

c_{P^{\alpha}}(t)=0\quad\text{unless}\quad P^{\alpha-1}\mid t.

(When
\alpha=1
this condition is vacuous, since
P^{\alpha-1}=1
.)
Hence, only pairs
(k,\ell)
satisfying

(10)

kp+\ell q\equiv 0\pmod{P_{0}}

can contribute to
E(p,q)
through the factor
c_{P^{\alpha}}(kp+\ell q)
.

We further split the remaining terms according to whether
P^{\alpha}
divides
kp+\ell q
:

E(p,q)=E_{P_{0}}(p,q)+E_{P^{\alpha}}(p,q),

where

E_{P_{0}}(p,q)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k,\ell\leq n-1\\
(k,\ell)\neq(0,0)\\
P_{0}\mid(kp+\ell q)\\
P^{\alpha}\nmid(kp+\ell q)\end{subarray}}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\,c_{n}(kp+\ell q),

and

E_{P^{\alpha}}(p,q)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k,\ell\leq n-1\\
(k,\ell)\neq(0,0)\\
P^{\alpha}\mid(kp+\ell q)\end{subarray}}\widehat{f_{p}}(k)\widehat{f_{q}}(\ell)\,c_{n}(kp+\ell q).

For terms in
E_{P_{0}}(p,q)
, we have
v_{P}(kp+\ell q)=\alpha-1
, so Lemma
3.2
gives

|c_{P^{\alpha}}(kp+\ell q)|=P^{\alpha-1}.

Therefore, we have (by Lemma
3.3
)

(11)

|c_{n}(kp+\ell q)|=|c_{P^{\alpha}}(kp+\ell q)\,c_{m}(kp+\ell q)|\leq P^{\alpha-1}\varphi(m)=\frac{\varphi(n)}{P-1}.

For terms in
E_{P^{\alpha}}(p,q)
, we use the trivial bound
|c_{n}(\cdot)|\leq\varphi(n)
.

4.2.
Bounding the restricted sum

The congruence condition (
10
) restricts
\ell
to a single residue
class modulo
P_{0}
once
k
is fixed, provided that
\gcd(q,P)=1
.
Similarly, the condition
P^{\alpha}\mid(kp+\ell q)
restricts
\ell
to a residue class modulo
P^{\alpha}
.
We use the following lemma to control the Fourier mass of short-interval indicators on such residue classes.

Lemma 4.2

(Fourier mass in a residue class)
.

Let
f=1_{I_{m}}
with
1\leq m\leq n-1
and let
d\mid n
.
For any residue class
b\pmod{d}
, choose the representative
b\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
and define

\Sigma_{f}(d;b)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\ell\leq n-1\\
\ell\equiv b\ (d)\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|.

Then we have the pointwise bounds

\Sigma_{f}(d;0)\leq\frac{m}{n}+\frac{1+\log(n/d)}{d},

and for
1\leq b\leq d-1
,

\Sigma_{f}(d;b)\leq\frac{1}{2b}+\frac{1}{2(d-b)}+\frac{1+\log(n/d)}{d}.

Proof.

Write
n=dN
where
N=n/d
, and write the elements of the residue class as

\ell=b+jd,\qquad j=0,1,\dots,N-1.

Let
J\coloneq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2d}\right\rfloor=\lfloor N/2\rfloor

and let
H_{J}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{j}

(with the convention
H_{0}\coloneq 0
).

By Lemma
3.1
, we have

|\widehat{f}(0)|=\frac{m}{n},\qquad|\widehat{f}(\ell)|\leq\frac{1}{2\min(\ell,n-\ell)}\quad(\ell\neq 0).

We split the sum defining
\Sigma_{f}(d;b)
into two ranges:
\ell\leq n/2
and
\ell>n/2
.

First range (
\ell\leq n/2
)
:
If
b=0
, then the term
\ell=0
contributes
m/n
. The remaining terms in this range are
\ell=jd
with

1\leq j\leq J
, hence we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\equiv 0\ (d)\\
1\leq\ell\leq n/2\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2jd}=\frac{H_{J}}{2d}.

If
1\leq b\leq d-1
, then the term
j=0
(i.e.
\ell=b
) contributes at most
1/(2b)
,
and for
j\geq 1
we have

b+jd\geq jd
, so we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\equiv b\ (d)\\
1\leq\ell\leq n/2\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|\leq\frac{1}{2b}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2(b+jd)}\leq\frac{1}{2b}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2jd}=\frac{1}{2b}+\frac{H_{J}}{2d}.

Second range (
\ell>n/2
)
:
Write
\ell=n-t
with
1\leq t<n/2
. Then
\min(\ell,n-\ell)=t
, and the congruence
\ell\equiv b\ (d)
becomes

t\equiv-b\ (d)
.
If
b=0
, then
t
runs over the positive multiples of
d
, so
t=jd
with
1\leq j\leq J
and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\equiv 0\ (d)\\
n/2<\ell\leq n-1\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2jd}=\frac{H_{J}}{2d}.

If
1\leq b\leq d-1
, then the smallest positive
t
with
t\equiv-b\ (d)
is
t_{0}=d-b
, contributing at most

1/(2(d-b))
, and the remaining terms have the form
t=t_{0}+jd\geq jd
for
j\geq 1
. Hence, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\equiv b\ (d)\\
n/2<\ell\leq n-1\end{subarray}}|\widehat{f}(\ell)|\leq\frac{1}{2(d-b)}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2(t_{0}+jd)}\leq\frac{1}{2(d-b)}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2jd}=\frac{1}{2(d-b)}+\frac{H_{J}}{2d}.

Combining the two ranges gives

\Sigma_{f}(d;0)\leq\frac{m}{n}+\frac{H_{J}}{d},\qquad\Sigma_{f}(d;b)\leq\frac{1}{2b}+\frac{1}{2(d-b)}+\frac{H_{J}}{d}\quad(1\leq b\leq d-1).

Finally, using the standard bound
H_{J}\leq 1+\log(1+J)\leq 1+\log(N)=1+\log(n/d)
completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
4.1

Recall the decomposition

from §

.

4.3.1.
A crude bound for

.

Using (

|E_{P_{0}}(p,q)|\leq\frac{\varphi(n)}{P-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{f_{p}}(k)|\,\Sigma_{f_{q}}(P_{0};b_{k}),

\gcd(q,P)=1

For every residue class

|E_{P_{0}}(p,q)|\leq\frac{\varphi(n)}{P-1}(1+\log n)^{2}.

Reduction of

to a residue-class sum.

Set

. Using
|c_{n}(\cdot)|\leq\varphi(n)

|E_{P^{\alpha}}(p,q)|\leq\varphi(n)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{f_{p}}(k)|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\ell\leq n-1\\

If

\sum_{1\leq j\leq n/d-1}|\widehat{f_{q}}(jd)|

By Lemma

If

4.3.3.
Averaging over the unit

.

Assume

and let

denote the inverse of

modulo

Set

. Define the weight

and set

By Lemma
3.1
, for every

, hence

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}|\widehat{f_{p}}(k)|\,\Sigma_{f_{q}}(d;uk)\ \leq\ S(u).

We now give an estimate on the average value of

.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\frac{1}{|U_{d}|}\sum_{u\in U_{d}}S(u)\leq\frac{7(1+\log n)^{2}}{d}.

Proof.

For
b\neq 0
, Lemma
4.2
and
\frac{1}{2b}+\frac{1}{2(d-b)}\leq\frac{1}{\min(b,d-b)}
give

\Sigma_{f_{q}}(d;b)\leq\frac{1}{\min(b,d-b)}+\frac{1+\log(n/d)}{d}.

Fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
with

, write

with

and
\gcd(k^{\prime},P)=1
.
As

\frac{1}{|U_{d}|}\sum_{u\in U_{d}}\frac{1}{\min([uk]_{d},d-[uk]_{d})}=\frac{1}{\varphi(P^{\alpha-v})}\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}/P^{\alpha-v}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\frac{1}{P^{v}\min(t,P^{\alpha-v}-t)}\leq\frac{4(1+\log n)}{d},

\sum_{t\leq M}\frac{1}{t}\leq 1+\log M\leq 1+\log n

, then

\Sigma_{f_{q}}(d;0)\leq\frac{m_{q}}{n}+\frac{1+\log(n/d)}{d}\leq 1+\frac{1+\log(n/d)}{d}.

Moreover, applying Lemma

We now split the sum

For

\frac{1}{|U_{d}|}\sum_{u\in U_{d}}\Sigma_{f_{q}}(d;uk)\leq\frac{5(1+\log n)}{d},

hence

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2

In this section, we provide the proof of our main quantitative result.
We begin by restating Theorem
1.1
as Theorem
5.1
below.
This version is a more technical formulation of the result meant to align
explicitly with the arithmetic setup and error estimates established in the previous two sections.
By comparing the growth of the main term against the suppressed error term,
we show that the existence of a large prime factor in
n
is sufficient to
guarantee a violation of the rank obstruction for almost all candidates.

Theorem 5.1
.

As
n\rightarrow+\infty
in
\Omega^{+}
(see (
2
)),
all but an
o(1)
proportion of pairs
(p,q)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}

with
\gcd(p,q,n)=1
and
\gcd(q,P)=1
have

S(p,q)\geq 5.

In particular, for such
(p,q)
there exists a
usable
unit
a\in U_{n}
satisfying

[ap]_{n}<[2p]_{n}
and
[aq]_{n}<[2q]_{n}
,
so Proposition
2.1
implies that the corresponding triangle is not a lattice triangle.

The idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1

is to exclude two exceptional regions of
(p,q)
.
First, Proposition
4.1
excludes a set of residue
classes modulo
d=P^{\alpha}
of relative size
\ll 1/R
.
Taking
R\coloneq\lceil\log n\rceil

ensures that the corresponding arithmetic exceptional region

\mathcal{E}_{n}
has density

(16)

\frac{\#\mathcal{E}_{n}}{\#\mathcal{T}_{n}}\ll\frac{1}{P^{+}(n)}+\frac{1}{R}=o(1).

With the choice
R=\lceil\log n\rceil
, the error bound from
Proposition
4.1
becomes

|E(p,q)|\ll\varphi(n)\frac{(1+\log n)^{2}R}{P^{+}(n)}\ll\varphi(n)\frac{(1+\log n)^{3}}{P^{+}(n)}.

Additionally, we remove a geometric region

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:(2p-1)(2q-1)\leq n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}\},

where the main term is too small to dominate the error term.
As shown in the proof, this region also has density
0
.

We now flesh out the details in the sketch.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Write
n=P^{\alpha}m
with
\alpha\coloneq v_{P}(n)\geq 1
and
\gcd(P,m)=1
, and set

d\coloneq P^{\alpha}
.
Let
R\coloneq\lceil\log n\rceil
.

5.1.1.
Discarding a negligible exceptional set of pairs.

Fix
q
with
\gcd(q,P)=1
. By Proposition
4.1
, there exists
a set

\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

with

|\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)|\leq\frac{\varphi(d)}{R}

such that the error bound in Proposition
4.1

holds whenever

p\bmod d\notin\mathcal{B}_{q}(R).

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the set of pairs
(p,q)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}

with
\gcd(q,P)=1
for which either
P\mid p
or

p\bmod d\in\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)
.

For fixed
q
, the constraint
p+q<n/2
restricts
p
to an interval
of length
<n
. The condition
P\mid p
contributes at most
n/P

values of
p
, while the residue condition

p\bmod d\in\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)
contributes at most

\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)|}{\varphi(d)}n+O(1)\leq\frac{n}{R}+O(1)

values of
p
. Thus

\#\{p:(p,q)\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\}\ll\frac{n}{P}+\frac{n}{R}.

Summing over all admissible
q
(there are
\ll n
choices) yields

\#\mathcal{E}_{n}\ll n^{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{P}+\frac{1}{R}\Bigr).

Since the triangular region

\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:p,q>0,\ p+q<n/2\}

contains
\asymp n^{2}
lattice points, we obtain

\frac{\#\mathcal{E}_{n}}{\#\mathcal{T}_{n}}\ll\frac{1}{P}+\frac{1}{R}.

Because
P\to\infty
along the sequence under consideration
and
R=\lceil\log n\rceil\to\infty
, this ratio tends to
0
.
Hence the exceptional region
\mathcal{E}_{n}
has density
0
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

5.1.2.
Removing a second negligible region.

Define

(17)

\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:\ (2p-1)(2q-1)\leq n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}\}\}

Since
2p-1\geq p
and
2q-1\geq q
, the defining condition of

\mathcal{C}_{n}
implies

pq\leq n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}.

Hence

\mathcal{C}_{n}\subseteq\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:\ pq\leq n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}\}.

A standard divisor bound yields

\#\{(p,q):pq\leq X\}\ll X\log X.

Applying this with
X=n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}
gives

\#\mathcal{C}_{n}\ll n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}\log n.

Since the triangular region
\mathcal{T}_{n}
contains
\asymp n^{2}

lattice points, it follows that

\frac{\#\mathcal{C}_{n}}{\#\mathcal{T}_{n}}\ll n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}\log n\to 0.

Thus
\mathcal{C}_{n}
occupies a negligible proportion of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

5.1.3.
Main term dominates the error term.

It therefore suffices to prove
S(p,q)\geq 5

for pairs
(p,q)\notin\mathcal{E}_{n}\cup\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
For such pairs we have

S(p,q)=M(p,q)+E(p,q),\qquad M(p,q)=\frac{(2p-1)(2q-1)}{n^{2}}\varphi(n).

Since
(p,q)\notin\mathcal{E}_{n}
, then
\gcd(p,P)=1
and
p\bmod d\notin\mathcal{B}_{q}(R)
,
so Proposition
4.1
yields

(18)

|E(p,q)|\leq 9R\,\varphi(n)\frac{(1+\log n)^{2}}{P},

As we have
R=\lceil\log n\rceil\leq 1+\log n
and
P\geq n^{\frac{1}{\log\log n}}
, the bound (
18
) implies

(19)

|E(p,q)|\leq 9\,\varphi(n)n^{-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}(1+\log n)^{3}.

Thus, we have

S(p,q)\geq\varphi(n)\left(\frac{(2p-1)(2q-1)}{n^{2}}-9n^{-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}(1+\log n)^{3}\right)

Since
(p,q)\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}
we have

(2p-1)(2q-1)>n^{2-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}.

Since
n^{1/(2\log\log n)}
grows faster than any power of
\log n
,

9n^{-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}(1+\log n)^{3}=o(n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}).

In particular, for sufficiently large
n
,

9n^{-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}(1+\log n)^{3}<\frac{1}{2}n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}

and hence

S(p,q)\geq\varphi(n)\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}-9n^{-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}(1+\log n)^{3}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\varphi(n)n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}.

Finally, since
\varphi(n)\gg n/\log\log n
, the quantity

\varphi(n)n^{-\frac{1}{2\log\log(n)}}
tends to infinity as
n\to\infty
.
Hence
S(p,q)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. In particular,

S(p,q)\geq 5

for all sufficiently large
n
.

5.1.4.
Existence of a usable unit.

If
S(p,q)\geq 5
, then there exists at least one unit

a\in U_{n}
satisfying

[ap]_{n}<[2p]_{n},\qquad[aq]_{n}<[2q]_{n}.

Proposition
2.1
then implies that the corresponding triangle
is not a lattice triangle.

5.2.
Exceptional set where
P\mid q

To deduce Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
5.1
,
we briefly justify that restricting to
\gcd(q,P)=1
costs negligible density in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

Indeed, the number of
q
in
[0,n/2]
divisible by
P
is at most
n/P\leq n^{1-\frac{1}{\log\log n}}
,
whereas the full range of
q
is
\gg n
.
Thus, the excluded pairs are
O(n^{2-\frac{1}{\log\log n}})
among
|\mathcal{H}_{n}|\asymp n^{2}
, which is
o(n^{2})
.

5.3.
A density 1 corollary over denominators

While Theorem
5.1
establishes a powerful obstruction for denominators
possessing a sufficiently large prime factor,
its full utility is realized by considering the statistical distribution of such factors across all integers.
By invoking results from smooth-number theory,
we can demonstrate that the conditions of our main theorem are satisfied for a
set of denominators of natural density 1.
This leads to the Corollary
1.2
which confirms that the
Mirzakhani–Wright rank obstruction rules out almost all candidates in the hard
window for a set of denominators of natural density
1
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

By smooth-number theory (Dickman–de Bruijn theory as developed by Hildebrandt and Tenenbaum
[
6
]
),
for any function
y(n)
with
y(n)\to\infty
sufficiently slowly,
the set of
y(n)
-smooth integers has natural density
0
.
Equivalently, for a set of integers
n
of natural density
1
we have

P^{+}(n)\geq\exp\!\Big(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\Big).

Applying Theorem
5.1
to those
n
yields the corollary.
∎

6.
AxiomProver’s autonomous Lean verification

We provide context for this project as well as the protocol used for Lean
formalization and verification.
K.O. gave a draft of the proof of some relevant analytic number theory estimates.
We asked whether AxiomProver
can autoformalize these proofs, offering an example of AI assistance in mathematical research.
What did we learn? We found that AxiomProver could both correct,
as well as autoformalize, K.O.’s draft.
In particular, the draft contained some correctable mistakes which did not invalidate the core argument.

To be precise, for
\eta>0
, we define the truncated region

\mathcal{H}_{n}(\eta)\coloneq\{(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 1}:\ \ \min(p,q)>\eta n,\ p+q<\tfrac{n}{2},\ \gcd(p,q,n)=1\}.

AxiomProver autoformalized the following theorem,
given a natural language statement and an informal proof.

Theorem 6.1

(Analytic engine: a lower bound for
S(p,q)
)
.

If
\eta\in(0,1/6)
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, then we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\rightarrow+\infty\\
P^{+}(n)\geq n^{\theta}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\left\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(\eta)\ :\ \gcd\!\big(q,P^{+}(n)\big)=1\ \ and\ \ S(p,q)<5\right\}|}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}(\eta)|}=0.

To make the scope precise, we emphasize that the AI system was not asked to reprove deep external
theorems in the theory of translation surfaces, Veech theory,
and the Mirzakhani-Wright rank obstruction theorem and its number theoretic
formulation Proposition
2.1
.
Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
1.2
follow from these
facts and Theorem
6.1
.

Compared to Theorem
5.1
, Theorem
6.1
has additional parameters
\eta
and
\theta
.
In fact, Theorem
6.1
was originally the main theorem in K.O.’s draft.
Once AxiomProver had formally verified Theorem
6.1
,
the authors further optimized the proof strategy to eliminate
\eta
and
\theta
with the same core argument.
This is done by replacing
\theta
with
\frac{1}{\log\log n}

and then replacing the region
\min(p,q)<\eta n

with the region
\mathcal{C}_{n}
defined in (
17
) in Section
5
,
while leaving Sections
3
and
4
intact.
Thus, from a mathematical standpoint,
the authors do not consider Theorem
6.1
and Theorem
5.1

to be essentially different from each other.

6.1.
AxiomProver Protocol

Here we describe the protocol we employed using AxiomProver to

6.1
in Lean (see

11
,
14
]
),
the main engine in the paper.

Process

The formal proofs provided in this work were developed and verified using Lean
4.26.0
.
Compatibility with earlier or later versions is not guaranteed due to the
evolving nature of the Lean 4 compiler and its core libraries.
The relevant files are all posted in the following repository:

https://github.com/AxiomMath/lattice-triangle

The input files were

•

problem.tex
, the problem statement in natural language

•

a configuration file
.environment
that contains the single line

lean-4.26.0

which specifies to AxiomProver which version of Lean should be used.

•

a markdown file
task.md
that contains the single line

State and formalize the main theorem.

•

informal_proof.tex
, a draft of the proof provided by K.O. (which had some correctable mistakes)

Given these two files,

•

•

, a complete Lean 4.26.0 formalization of the proof.

After AxiomProver generated a solution, the human authors wrote this paper
(without the use of AI) for human readers.
At first glance, the proofs found by AxiomProver do not resemble the narrative presented in this paper.
Turning a Lean file into a human-readable proof is difficult
because Lean is written as code for a type-checker.
