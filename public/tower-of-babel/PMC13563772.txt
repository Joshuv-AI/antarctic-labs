Use of 3D printed coracoid models for preoperative planning in the Latarjet procedure: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Vitor La Banca, Fernando Luiz Affonso Fonseca, Ana Victória Palagi Viganó, Luiz Giglio, Rodrigo Pereira Amarante, Henrique Schaffhausser, Guilherme Henrique Vieira Lima, Luiz Henrique Oliveira Almeida, Luis Gustavo Prata Nascimento, Joel Murachovsky, Roberto Yukio Ikemoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Latarjet procedure is effective for treating recurrent anterior shoulder instability, but graft-related complications may occur. Accurate preoperative planning is essential to minimize these complications. Three-dimensional (3D) printed models may support surgical planning; however, their role in Latarjet surgery remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether coracoid dimensions measured on 3D-printed models differ from intraoperative measurements and whether their use influences surgical decision-making.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 25 patients underwent computed tomography based 3D printing of the scapula. Coracoid length, width, and thickness were measured on the 3D-printed models and compared with intraoperative pre- and post-osteotomy measurements using precision calipers. Surgeons also subjectively rated the usefulness of the 3D models for surgical planning.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in coracoid thickness among the three measurement time points (p = 0.6956). Significant differences were found for coracoid length (p = 0.0005) and width (p = 0.02) between the 3D model and pre-osteotomy measurements, while no differences were observed between pre- and post-osteotomy measurements. The models were rated as “very helpful” in 28% of cases, “somewhat helpful” in 52%, and “not helpful” in 20%. In three cases, preoperative planning with the 3D model resulted in a change in fixation strategy.ConclusionThree-dimensional printed coracoid models provide reliable estimation of coracoid thickness and may assist in preoperative planning in selected Latarjet procedures, particularly when graft adequacy or fixation strategy is uncertain. Although statistically significant differences in length and width were observed, the models were considered helpful in most cases.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: Latarjet, Shoulder instability, Three-dimensional printing, Coracoid process, Preoperative planning, Anterior shoulder instability

Introduction

The Latarjet procedure is a reliable surgical technique for treating recurrent anterior shoulder instability, particularly in patients with high functional demands or significant anterior glenoid bone loss [1–3]. Compared to soft tissue procedures such as Bankart repair, the Latarjet procedure presents superior shoulder stabilization in specific populations, including patients with glenoid bone loss above 13–20%, those with previous failed soft tissue procedures, or those with bipolar bone loss involving ‘off-track’ Hill-Sachs lesions. This technique has consistently demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes in these groups, characterized by lower recurrence rates and significantly higher rates of return to sports and daily activities [4, 5]. However, the overall complication rate has been reported to be as high as 16% [6], encompassing neurovascular injuries, graft- or fixation-related issues, and persistent instability [7, 8]. Graft-specific complications such as fracture, nonunion, or malposition occur in approximately 1.9% to 3.6% of cases, with intraoperative coracoid graft fracture being a particular concern [6], especially with the increasingly advocated arthroscopic Latarjet, with some studies reporting fracture rates of up to 7% in this technique [9].

Effective preoperative planning is widely recognized as critical for minimizing such complications in orthopedic surgery. High-resolution imaging, particularly three-dimensional computed tomography, plays a key role in assessing glenoid bone loss and planning graft placement while also allowing for a more accurate evaluation of coracoid morphology [10, 11], which can vary significantly among individuals [12, 13]. However, interpreting coracoid dimensions using standard imaging modalities presents challenges due to the complex three-dimensional orientation of the coracoid process, anatomical variability, and the superimposition of surrounding structures [11, 14]. These factors, combined with the limitations of two-dimensional imaging, make it difficult to precisely evaluate key characteristics such as curvature, thickness, and length, potentially compromising the surgeon’s ability to anticipate intraoperative challenges.

In recent years, three-dimensional (3D) printed bone models have emerged as an innovative tool in orthopedic surgical planning [15]. Derived from high-resolution imaging data, these models offer accurate, patient-specific anatomical representations that can be physically manipulated, allowing for detailed preoperative assessments and surgical simulations. Unlike virtual reconstructions, 3D printed models provide a tangible understanding of spatial relationships, potentially enhancing surgical decision-making [16, 17]. Despite their growing use in different orthopedic procedures [16–20], the application of 3D printed models in planning the Latarjet procedure, particularly for evaluating graft size and guiding osteotomy remains underexplored.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the utility and reliability of three-dimensional (3D) printed scapular models for preoperative planning in the Latarjet procedure. The primary research question was whether coracoid dimensions measured on 3D-printed models differ from intraoperative measurements. A secondary research question was whether preoperative planning using 3D-printed models could influence surgical decision-making, particularly with respect to osteotomy planning and fixation strategy. We hypothesized that coracoid measurements obtained from 3D models would closely approximate those of the native anatomy, except for graft length, which was expected to differ between the planned and intraoperatively obtained graft, as some difficulty in determining the point of osteotomy was expected. In addition, we hypothesized that preoperative planning with 3D models would be considered useful by surgeons in at least half of the cases.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at a single-center hospital between 2023 and 2025 and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [21]. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Centro Universitario FMABC Institutional Review Board (IRB) (CAAE: 49430521.1.0000.0082) prior to the initiation of the study. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. Participant confidentiality and data protection were ensured throughout the research process.

Participants

Participants consisted of patients with diagnosis of anterior shoulder instability who were indicated for surgical treatment by the Latarjet procedure. Inclusion criteria encompassed all skeletally mature individuals. Exclusion criteria included a history of acute or previous coracoid fracture, prior surgical intervention involving direct manipulation of the coracoid process (such as coracoplasty, coracoid transfer procedures, or acromioclavicular joint stabilization procedures requiring coracoid instrumentation), or any form of scapular dysplasia. Those conditions were excluded from the current study to ensure sample homogeneity for the primary purpose of anatomical validation. Procedures not involving the coracoid, such as arthroscopic labral repair (Bankart procedure), were not considered exclusion criteria, as they were not expected to affect coracoid morphology or measurement.

Image processing and 3D printing

All participants underwent CT scanning of the affected shoulder. The following minimum parameters were used to obtain the CT images: slice thickness of up to 1 mm, isotropic voxel, and availability of a bone reconstruction algorithm (‘bone window’), as recommended by the RSNA (Radiological Society of North America – 3D Printing SIG) [22]. DICOM files were processed using InVesalius software (CTI Renato Archer, Brazil) for 3D reconstruction. Image segmentation was performed in MeshMixer (Autodesk Inc., USA), isolating the coracoid and glenoid. The segmented models were then prepared for printing in CURA software (Ultimaker, Netherlands) and printed using an Ender-5 Pro 3D printer (Creality, China) with PLA filament (1.75 mm).

Measurement collection

Coracoid dimensions were assessed at three time points: preoperatively on the 3D-printed model, intraoperatively before osteotomy (pre-osteotomy), and intraoperatively after osteotomy (post-osteotomy). Coracoid process measurements were obtained on the 3D printed models by the operating surgeon using a precision caliper with 0.02 mm resolution (MTX, China). The following anatomical dimensions were assessed using standardized landmarks. Coracoid length was defined as the distance from the tip of the coracoid to the planned osteotomy point at the ‘elbow’ of the coracoid, where its undersurface changes from a horizontal to a vertical direction [23] (Fig. 1A). The width was defined as the maximum distance between the medial and lateral cortices of the coracoid [15, 24] (Fig. 1B). Finally, the thickness was measured as the maximum distance between the superior and inferior cortices of the process [15–24] (Fig. 1C).

Two weeks later, patients underwent an open Latarjet procedure according to a previously described technique [25]. Intraoperative measurements of the native coracoid were obtained in two phases: first, before osteotomy (pre-osteotomy), and second, after osteotomy (post-osteotomy). These measurements were performed using a second identical caliper. Width and thickness followed the same anatomical references as used on the 3D models. Length was measured as the distance from the palpable tip of the coracoid to the site where the osteotomy was planned (pre-osteotomy) (Fig. 2) and then to the point where it has been performed [26] (post-osteotomy) (Fig. 3). No other changes were made to the standard open Latarjet surgical technique [25] employed in all procedures.

The group of examiners was formed by 6 different orthopedic surgeons who were fellowship trained in shoulder surgery with a mean 7,7 ± 1,6 years of surgical practice, with a mean volume of 6,5 ± 5.5 Latarjet procedures per year, were independent from the study team and were not involved in study conception, data analysis, or manuscript preparation. Although they were aware of the use of 3D models in surgical planning, they were not informed of the specific study hypotheses or expected outcomes. Prior to study initiation, they received standardized training in the measurement protocols as previously described, with practice sessions conducted using independent 3D models to ensure methodological consistency.

Variables

Demographic variables include age, gender, affected side, weight, and height. Primary outcome variables were coracoid process length, width, and thickness measured preoperatively on the 3D model, and intraoperatively before and after osteotomy.

Potential source of measurement bias

Potential sources of measurement bias include manual caliper-based measurements, which are subject to intra-observer variability and limited precision, particularly for millimeter-level differences. To mitigate this, all measurements were performed using standardized anatomical landmarks, identical instruments, and predefined protocols across all measurement time points.

Sample size and statistical methods

A priori power analysis based on comparable 3D models to native bone studies [27] indicated a minimum sample size of 23 patients to ensure 80% power and a 95% confidence interval. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test along with skewness and kurtosis. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA was used to compare groups, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. If normality assumptions were violated, Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were used. Intra-observer reliability was assessed for coracoid measurements obtained at three predefined time points: pre-osteotomy, post-osteotomy, and 3D model assessment. Reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [28], applying a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements.

3D model utility and planning observations

After surgery, operating surgeons answered a structured questionnaire to evaluate the utility of the 3D models using three categories: “Very helpful,” “Somewhat helpful,” or “Not helpful.”. Additionally, they were asked to make any observations regarding the use of the 3D models on the case and to mention if there were any modifications to a previous surgical plan following the 3D model evaluation.

Thickness

No statistically significant differences were observed in coracoid thickness among the three measurement conditions (p = 0.6956). Mean thickness values were comparable between the 3D-printed models, pre-osteotomy, and post-osteotomy measurements.

Length

Significant differences were observed in coracoid length across the three measurement conditions (p = 0.0005). Post hoc analysis demonstrated a significant difference between measurements obtained from the 3D-printed models and intraoperative pre-osteotomy measurements. No statistically significant differences were observed between pre-osteotomy and post-osteotomy measurements or between post-osteotomy measurements and the 3D models.

Width

Coracoid width also differed significantly across measurement conditions (p = 0.02). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference between 3D model measurements and intraoperative pre-osteotomy measurements. No significant differences were identified between pre-osteotomy and post-osteotomy measurements or between post-osteotomy measurements and 3D models.

Coracoid width demonstrated moderate agreement between pre- and post-osteotomy measurements and between post-osteotomy and 3D model measurements, with lower agreement between pre-osteotomy and 3D model assessments. Coracoid thickness showed the highest reliability, with moderate to good agreement between pre- and post-osteotomy measurements and lower, though acceptable, agreement in comparisons involving the 3D models.

Utility of 3D models and surgical planning

Surgeons rated the 3D-printed models as “very helpful” in 28% of cases, “somewhat helpful” in 52% of cases, and “not helpful” in 20% of cases. In three cases, preoperative assessment of the 3D model led to a modification of the planned fixation strategy, including changes in screw diameter or number of screws used. Discussion

The present study evaluated the utility and reliability of three-dimensional (3D) printed scapular models for preoperative planning of the Latarjet procedure. Addressing the primary research question, our findings demonstrated no statistically significant differences for most coracoid graft dimensions, with discrepancies observed in length and width between the 3D models and intraoperative pre-osteotomy measurements. These results partially support our hypothesis and suggest that 3D-printed models provide a reasonably accurate, albeit imperfect, estimation of native coracoid dimensions. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as intra-observer reliability was limited across most measurement comparisons, particularly for coracoid length and width, which may reduce the consistency and practical applicability of these measurements.

Differences in coracoid length and width between the 3D models and pre-osteotomy measurements may be explained by both technical and anatomical factors. From a technical perspective, surgeons may not be able to reproduce the osteotomy line exactly as planned on the 3D model while the coracoid remains in situ, even when using consistent anatomical reference points. This limitation highlights the potential value of more precise planning tools, such as patient-specific instrumentation, which has been previously described [29, 30]. From an anatomical standpoint, soft tissue insertions, including the conjoint tendon, pectoralis minor, and coracoacromial ligament, as well as the proximity of the clavicle and coracoclavicular ligaments, may obscure bony landmarks and reduce measurement reproducibility, contributing to the observed differences between 3D models and pre-osteotomy measurements. The observed discrepancies specifically observed in coracoid width may be attributed to the technical difficulty of achieving precise bone-to-bone caliper contact through overlying soft tissues, further compounded by the somewhat cylindrical and irregular morphology of the coracoid process, which limits the reproducibility of manual measurements until the graft is fully isolated post-osteotomy.

Importantly, although statistically significant, the observed discrepancies were within a range of 2–3 mm. Such differences are likely to be of limited clinical relevance and unlikely to meaningfully influence surgical outcomes or complication rates, particularly in cases involving larger grafts. This interpretation is supported by the intra-rater reliability analysis, in which coracoid thickness demonstrated the highest consistency, whereas length and width showed greater variability, especially in comparisons involving post-osteotomy and 3D model measurements. As reliability was assessed using absolute agreement, some of this variability may also reflect inherent limitations of manual caliper measurements, further supporting the notion that small millimeter-level differences may not be clinically meaningful.

From a practical standpoint, this interpretation was reflected in surgical decision-making. Modifications in fixation strategy, such as opting for single-screw fixation, were observed only in a case with a markedly reduced graft length of 17 mm, which deviat14urgical planning may occur primarily in cases with clearly diminished graft dimensions, rather than because of minor measurement discrepancies. In this context, the absolute precision of individual measurements may be less critical than the ability of the 3D model to provide a reliable, patient-specific anatomical representation that supports intraoperative decision-making. Thus, even with small dimensional differences, these models may retain meaningful clinical utility as an adjunct for anticipating graft adequacy and guiding fixation strategy.

In contrast, coracoid thickness and post-osteotomy graft dimensions were more reliably reproduced between native bone and 3D-printed models. This may be explained by the increased freedom of graft manipulation after osteotomy, allowing for more consistent measurement. This finding is particularly relevant given that reduced graft thickness has been associated with an increased risk of graft-related complications, including fracture, fixation failure, or insufficient glenoid reconstruction [31]. In this context, accurate preoperative assessment of graft thickness may be especially valuable, and 3D-printed models may serve as a useful adjunct during surgical planning.

Regarding the secondary research question, surgeons rated the 3D models as helpful (52%) or very helpful (28%) in most cases, particularly in challenging scenarios involving screw selection or fixation strategy. Although these assessments were subjective, they suggest that 3D-printed models may offer added value in selected cases, even when conventional imaging modalities such as two-dimensional CT scans are available. It should be acknowledged, however, that despite the surgeons being experienced and independent from the research team, they were aware of the study objectives, and thus these subjective evaluations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, it is the authors ’experience that 3D printed models may be particularly useful in smaller patients or in situations where the feasibility of double-screw fixation or adequate graft size is uncertain.

Some limitations of this study should be considered. This was a single-center, cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size; however, this is acceptable given the exploratory, innovative, and therefore initial nature of the investigation on this topic. All measurements were obtained manually using calipers, a method that is inherently subject to human variability but was intentionally selected to reflect intraoperative conditions rather than laboratory based or digital measurement environments. Measurements were performed by a single observer; although the inclusion of multiple observers would have been desirable, this was avoided minimizing potential bias during intraoperative data collection, where blinding was not feasible. Furthermore, this study did not compare 3D-printed models with other preoperative planning modalities, such as conventional 3D CT measurements or intraoperative navigation systems, and did not assess clinical outcomes, complication rates, or cost-effectiveness. A direct comparison, particularly with CT-based measurements, could provide further insight into the relative accuracy and added value of 3D printing in preoperative planning. These aspects remain beyond this initial report’s scope and warrant further investigation.

Within these limitations, this study is, to our knowledge, the first to evaluate the role of 3D printed models in Latarjet surgical planning with direct intraoperative correlation of coracoid graft dimensions. Our findings support further research into the use of 3D printing for Latarjet procedures, particularly in specific technical variations such as arthroscopic Latarjet, where access to the coracoid is more limited [32], or congruent-arc Latarjet techniques, in which graft dimensions are of heightened importance [33]. Future applications may also include patient-specific instrumentation, mixed-reality planning tools [34], and the use of 3D models for surgeon and patient education [35, 36].

Conclusion

Three-dimensional printed scapular models provided a reasonable and reliable estimation of coracoid graft dimensions for preoperative planning of the Latarjet procedure. Differences found were small and primarily affected graft length and width, while coracoid thickness showed higher reproducibility. However, intra-observer reliability was poor in several measurement comparisons, particularly for coracoid length, which may limit the consistency and reproducibility of these measurements. Importantly, regarding the secondary objective of this study, these models were considered helpful in most cases and were able to influence surgical decision-making in selected scenarios, supporting their potential clinical utility. These models may represent a useful adjunct to conventional imaging in selected cases, warranting further investigation of their clinical impact.
