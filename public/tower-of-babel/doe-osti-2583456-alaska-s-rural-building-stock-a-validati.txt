Executive Summary
The availability of accurate national data on demographics, building stock, and energy use is vital for
modeling residential buildings and evaluating energy affordability and resilience strategies. However,
rural and Indigenous populations, including those in rural Alaska, are typically underrepresented in these
datasets. ResStock primarily gathers data from national surveys and energy audits that tend to focus on
more populated areas, resulting in limited coverage and accuracy for remote and underserved
communities. These rural Alaskan communities face unique challenges because of their remote
locations, severe weather conditions, and limited access to resources, which result in high energy
burden. This report examines how rural Alaskan communities are underrepresented in the ResStock™
housing characteristics and energy model and highlights the need for improved data to address their
unique housing and energy challenges.

We conducted a validation study considering ResStock, field data, and aerial and 3D-view data
collection (A3DDC) datasets. The validation process started by using the filter based down-selecting
approach on the ResStock building stock dataset. We selected rural Alaska boroughs and census areas
located in American Society of Heating, Refrigerating, and Air-Conditioning Engineers International
Energy Conservation Code Climate Zone 8 to ensure a more accurate and fair comparison with field
data, which was collected in Nome census area, a rural area located in Climate Zone 8.

While ResStock may accurately represent several characteristics of the building stock for rural Alaska,
we identified some differences among modeled, field data, and A3DDC datasets. The following building
characteristics have a high impact on modeled energy consumption and demonstrated large differences.
We summarize these differences and list possible actions to resolve differences for future iterations of
ResStock results:

 • Heating setpoints. The average heating setpoint in ResStock is lower than the average observed
 in the field data. Revisit distributions and consider higher setpoints, which could potentially
 address missing loads if found to be the case.
 • Toyo heating. Toyo heating is currently missing from ResStock, but prevalent in field data
 collected. Develop and include Toyo heating in future modeling for ResStock and EnergyPlus.
 • Natural gas. Natural gas is not readily available in rural Alaska apart from the North Slope.
 Remove natural gas as a water heater fuel type outside of North Slope County.
 • Foundation type. Field and A3DDC data show that 80% of dwellings have crawlspaces in rural
 Alaska, while ResStock shows only 28.7% of dwellings have crawlspaces and 40% of dwellings
 have basements. Revisit distributions and consider more crawlspaces than basements.
 • Infiltration rates. The average infiltration rate is 5.06 ACH50 in ResStock, approximately 30%
 lower than the average field data, 7.35 ACH50. Reevaluate the distribution to consider higher
 infiltration rates.
 • Exterior wall type. Field data has revealed the predominancy of wood and vinyl light colored
 walls, while ResStock shows a predominancy of wood and brick medium/dark colored walls.
 Include more vinyl and less brick, and revise wall color for greater proportion of light than dark.
 • Roof material. Field and A3DDC data show that 93% of dwellings have metal roofs in rural
 Alaska, while approximately 75% of dwellings in ResStock have shingles. Revise from majority
 shingles to majority metal roofing material.
 • Occupant count. Occupancy rates in field data are 2.85 persons/dwelling, compared to 2.20
 persons/dwelling in ResStock. Update to higher occupant count.

 • Building orientation. Field and A3DDC shows approximately 35% of dwellings are south
 facing, while ResStock follows a normal distribution. Update to represent a higher proportion of
 south-facing than relatively equal south- and north-facing.
The findings suggest that updating ResStock’s input data could better represent rural Alaskan buildings
and their energy consumption. ResStock can be used to identify the residential building energy
consumption and energy efficient building improvements.

Motivation
Understanding the building stock in a specific community or region can help develop more accurate
energy models to set targets for specific energy efficiency measures. By analyzing housing
characteristics such as construction materials, heating systems, and insulation levels, researchers and
policymakers can estimate home energy use and identify opportunities for housing improvements. As
energy affordability efforts rise in the United States, researchers can identify energy efficiency
measures. Tailoring these measures can reduce energy consumption and lower energy bills.

Accurate, updated, and detailed nationwide data on demographics, building stock, and energy data
provide foundational information needed to model viable energy efficient technologies in residential
buildings. However, this data is not always available with the same level of quality and detail for all
populations. Rural and Indigenous populations, including American Indian and Alaska Native
communities, are often underrepresented in publicly available datasets (Evans-Romaysa et al. 2022).
Rural communities —like rural Alaskan communities—face several housing-related challenges because
of their remote locations, extreme weather conditions, and limited access to construction materials and
skilled labor for repairs, maintenance, and new home construction (Robinson 2021, Hossain 2016).
Additional challenges include high costs of heating fuels and electricity, resulting in a significant energy
burden for households in these communities (Robinson 2021, Hossain 2016, U.S. Department of
Agriculture 2017). In this report, we focus on the representation of residential building characteristics in
rural Alaskan communities within the national housing stock model, ResStock.

Background
Publicly available datasets contain partial information on residential buildings in Alaska. These datasets
include the Residential Energy Consumption Survey public-use microdata (U.S. Energy Information
Administration, 2023), the American Community Survey public-use microdata (U.S. Census Bureau,
2023), and the American Housing Survey (U.S. Census Bureau, 2021). These household surveys collect
data from a nationally representative sample of housing units and include household demographics,
energy use patterns, and housing unit characteristics, among others. Other surveys dedicated to
collecting housing data specific to Alaska include the Anchorage Housing Survey (NeighborWorks
Alaska Organization, 2022), surveys conducted by Tribal Organizations, and several others.
Additionally, the Alaska Retrofit Information System (ARIS), an online database platform developed by
the Alaska Housing Finance Corporation, also contains housing data from Alaskan households and is
based on energy ratings and building assessments conducted during evaluations of energy performance
(Alaska Housing Finance Corporation, 2018, Alaska Housing Finance Corporation 2022).

The ARIS data contains information on fuel use, heating equipment type and efficiency, infiltration rates
measured by air changes per hour at 50 pascals pressure difference (ACH50), construction and
insulation materials, and several other housing characteristics. This detailed dataset can be used as a
benchmark tool because it provides a thorough understanding of the building stock in Alaska, which can
help to support informed decision-making for energy policies (Alaska Housing Finance Corporation
2022, Alaska Housing Finance Corporation 2015). However, several challenges can arise when using
ARIS data, which include an uneven regional representation and a high representation of households
that have participated in energy efficiency programs or assessments. These structural data collection
challenges limit the representation of housing conditions across the state, which can vary greatly. ARIS

 1

data has informed Alaska-specific housing characteristics, including the model ResStock 3.3.0 (National
Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2024).

ResStock is a residential buildings characteristic and energy analysis tool that combines extensive public
and private data sources, statistical sampling, and detailed sub-hourly building simulations, among other
modeling outputs. The model generates high granularity and accuracy in representing the varied housing
stock and the effects of building technologies across different communities (National Renewable Energy
Laboratory, 2021). Integrating ARIS data into ResStock supported an Alaska-specific building stock
analysis, but how revised ResStock building characteristics compares to field and remotely-sense data
remains unclear.

To address this gap, we conducted a validation study to assess the representation of rural Alaska housing
stock in the ResStock database using field data. While collecting field data can be expensive and
resource-intensive—requiring field surveys, measurements, and inspections—it also provides a direct
benchmark against which model predictions or survey data can be compared, which increases
confidence in the results. We compared field data collected in two rural communities on housing
characteristics to ResStock housing stock for rural Alaska. This validation study also provides an
opportunity to ensure that rural and Indigenous populations are more accurately represented in
ResStock. Ultimately, this method promotes greater trust in data-driven decisions, leading to improved
strategies for energy security and affordability in the residential sector.

Objectives
Primary Objective: Validate the accuracy and reliability of a set of input distributions into the
ResStock building stock models for rural Alaska using field and aerial and 3D-view data collection
(A3DDC) from small Alaskan rural communities.

Secondary Objectives: Suggest improvements to the ResStock building stock models based on
validation results.

ResStock
ResStock is an analysis tool that helps states, municipalities, utilities, and other stakeholders identify the
impact of electrification and energy efficiency upgrades. It uses a statistical sampling methodology to
provide a probabilistic representation of the housing stock characteristics in the United States (National
Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2016). To develop this statistical representation, it uses a variety of
public and private data sources, including several nationally distributed surveys. The ARIS dataset was
integrated into ResStock to improve Alaska-specific housing characteristics (National Renewable
Energy Laboratory, 2024), which processed 80,000 records from the ARIS database after the year 2008.
The records contain detailed building characteristics and energy use across all census areas in Alaska
and informed the development of 1,269 dwelling models in ResStock.

Field Data
Field data was collected by the Michigan State University and Iowa State University teams as part of
“NNA Research: Collaborative Research: Responding to Energy Insecurity in Arctic Housing Using a
Community-Based Participatory Research” project (National Science Foundation 2022) in two rural
communities in Alaska, Nome and Unalakleet. These two communities are located on the western coast
and belong to the Nome census area. A summary of population, housing units, and number of homes
assessed in each community is presented in Table 2, and Figure 1 shows a map of Alaska with the
location of these two communities.

We conducted semi-structured interviews with homeowners and energy assessments over four trips. The
semi-structured interviews gathered information on household size, house age, and thermostat settings
for both winter and summer. Field data collection followed a Michigan State and Iowa State Universities
Institutional Review Board protocols and occurred from 2021 to 2024. Energy assessments focused on
gathering detailed housing characteristics, including building orientation, foundation type, materials and
colors of walls and roofs, heating systems, water heater equipment, energy sources, building dimensions,
and infiltration rates. A digital energy assessment guide was developed within Fastfield™ (Quickbase,
2023), a mobile data collection and analytics tool enabling direct collection of data on the site’s housing
characteristics, equipment specifications, and environmental conditions. Infiltration rates were measured
using blower door tests performed in each assessed home. During the energy assessments, long-term
indoor air quality sensors were installed. These sensors measure indoor temperature, relative humidity,
and several other indoor air quality indicators.

As indicated in Table 2, 48 and 43 homes were assessed in Unalakleet and Nome, respectively. The
sample of housing units assessed in Unalakleet can be considered representative of the community
(Guillante et al 2024). The sample of housing units was not randomly selected, because homeowners
interested in participating signed up voluntarily. Data collection took place from 2021 to 2024. We
collaborated with local tribe leaders, including members from the Native Village of Unalakleet and
Village of Solomon, and we also utilized radio announcements for outreach. This approach tried to
ensure a broad representation across different housing types and demographics within each community,

We selected these areas in ResStock to ensure a fair and accurate comparison with field data collected in
rural areas within Climate Zone 8, specifically in the Nome census area. After the down-selection
approach in ResStock, we cleaned and organized the datasets to ensure uniform nomenclature, with
some variable options combined. For instance, for this study, under the variable “Windows,” we
aggregated options such as “Double Clear Metal,” “Double Clear Non-Metal,” and “Double, Low-E”
into a single category named “Double pane.” In the case of “Double, Clear, Non-metal, Storm,” these
were grouped with “Triple pane” windows. The same procedure was applied to “Roof material,” where
options such as “Wood, Asphalt, and Composition Shingles” were consolidated and named “Shingles.”
Similarly, for “Foundation type,” “Unvented and Vented Crawlspaces” were merged into “Crawlspace,”
and “Unheated and Heated Basements” were combined into “Basement.”

With the datasets cleaned and organized, we selected a total of 17 variables—11 categorical and 6
numerical—from ResStock and the field data for comparison. We selected these variables because of
their compatibility across datasets and their potential impact on building energy simulations. For the
categorical variables, we considered the following: building type, building orientation, heating fuel type,
heating system type, water heater fuel type, window type, exterior wall type, exterior wall color,
foundation type, roof material, and vacancy status. For the numerical variables, we selected the
following: floor area, number of stories, vintage, infiltration (ACH50), heating setpoint, and number of
occupants. We performed descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation) to compare these
quantitative characteristics. We used only four variables for the A3DDC because of its data collection
limitations: building orientation, foundation type, roof material, and floor area. We visualized and
compared the data using the Python packages 'matplotlib' for plotting, 'numpy' for numerical operations,
and 'pandas' for data manipulation. Additionally, we employed the 'tabulate' package to present the
results in a clean, readable table format.

Results and Analysis
We compared ResStock building stock data relative to the field and A3DDC data across the following
building characteristics: building type, building orientation, water heater fuel type, heating fuel type,
heating system type, window type, exterior wall type, exterior wall color, foundation type, roof material,

 7

vacancy status, floor area, number of stories, vintage, infiltration (ACH50), heating setpoint, and
number of occupants. Findings are often reported in percentages, which support comparisons across
datasets, despite varying sample sizes (Dlugosz et al 2006).

Building Type
Five different building types are included in ResStock: single-family detached and attached, multifamily
with 2–4 units, multifamily with 5+ units, and mobile homes. The base data for this characteristic comes
from U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Information Administration (EIA)’s Residential Energy
Consumption Survey at the state-level. For the field data, three different types were observed: single-
family detached, single-family attached, and multifamily with 5+ units. The most common building type
across both datasets is single-family detached homes (75.7% in ResStock and 84.6% in the field data),
as shown in Figure 4. Overall, both datasets show alignment, indicating that ResStock effectively
represents the distribution of building types within these rural communities.

Water Heater Fuel Type
Figure 6 shows the comparison results for water heater fuel type between ResStock and the field data.
The base data for this characteristic comes from the U.S. EIA 2020 Residential Energy Consumption
Survey (RECS) microdata and for Alaska, the distribution is based on Alaska Retrofit Information
System (2008 to 2022) maintained by Alaska Housing Finance Corporation. The main source used by
water heaters in rural Alaska according to both datasets is oil. However, the field data reveals a higher
percentage of households with fuel oil water heaters (82.4%) compared to ResStock (53.9%). Electricity
is the second most common source of heating in both datasets, and in this case, ResStock states a higher
percentage compared to the field data. ResStock also contains households that use natural gas, propane,
and other fuel as an energy source for water heaters. These households are in five census areas and
boroughs, including the North Slope (4 buildings), Nome (2 buildings), Yukon (2 buildings), Bethel (1
building), and Southeast Fairbanks (1 building). According to the Alaska Department of Natural
Resources Division of Oil and Gas (Alaska Department of Natural Resources Division of Oil & Gas,
2023) and the National Pipeline Mapping System Public Viewer (U.S. Department of Transportation,
2003), only the North Slope borough contains natural gas pipelines. Therefore, natural gas could be the
primary energy source for water heaters in this region, but this may not be the case for other regions.
While ResStock includes information from an Alaskan database, it may still underrepresent households
with fuel oil water heaters and overrepresent those with natural gas water heaters. This discrepancy
could arise because, although rural Alaskan communities predominantly rely on fuel oil for water
heating, larger cities in Alaska tend to use natural gas. As a result, the weighting of urban areas in the
dataset may skew the overall representation toward natural gas systems.

Heating Fuel and System Type
The heating system and fuel type comparison is illustrated in Figure 7(a) and Figure 7(b), respectively.
The base data for heating fuel and heating system type is derived from the U.S. EIA 2020 Residential
Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) microdata. For Alaska, the distribution is based on the Alaska
Retrofit Information System (2008 to 2022) maintained by Alaska Housing Finance Corporation. The
field data reveals that there are two main heating system types, fuel oil boilers and direct-vent heaters
(Toyostoves). When compared with ResStock, the number of fuel oil boilers aligns well with the field
data (65.2% in ResStock and 60.5% in the field data) whereas Toyostoves, which account for 38.5% in
the field data, are not represented in the ResStock dataset. This absence can be associated with the
limited capability of current energy simulation tools used in ResStock to account for direct-vent heaters
such as Toyostoves.

Regarding the heating sources used by households in rural Alaska, oil is the primary heating source
observed in both datasets (84.3% in ResStock and 98.9% in the field data). While these communities
rely on oil as the main source of heating, some households located in the North Slope borough also use
natural gas, which is facilitated by the presence of natural gas pipelines—the only rural area in Alaska
with such infrastructure. The North Slope area contains six of the 100 largest oil fields and one of the
100 largest natural gas fields in the United States (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2024).

Accurate information on heating systems and sources plays an important role because heating represents
a significant cost for homes in Alaska. In Anchorage, the main source of heating is natural gas; in rural
communities, oil is the primary source of heating (Kohler and Schutt 2012). Having a dataset in
ResStock that accurately represents these communities allows for testing different upgrades and energy
efficiency measures, as well as helping community leaders make more informed decisions based on their
unique heating challenges.

Figure 8 presents the types of windows between datasets and their respective percentages. The base data

most observed window types being double pane. However, according to the field data, 17.6% of homes
had triple-pane windows, whereas ResStock accounts for 9.6%. These findings could be associated with
the fact that some of the assessed homes in the field data were retrofitted with more energy-efficient

Exterior Walls Type and Color
Figure 9(a) and Figure 9(b) show the results of exterior wall finish type and color, respectively, between
the two datasets. Regarding exterior wall finish type, wood is predominant in both ResStock (56.5%)
and the field data (51.6%). However, the field data indicates nearly 40% of homes have vinyl as the
exterior wall finish material, whereas ResStock only accounts for 4.3% using this material. These results
suggest an underrepresentation of vinyl exterior wall finishes and potentially an overrepresentation of

 11

brick and concrete walls without attached finish materials in ResStock. The ResStock base data for this
characteristic comes from the Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD) database, at the
individual parcel level.

We also observed a discrepancy in exterior wall colors between the two datasets. While the field data
reveals more households with light-colored exterior walls, ResStock contains a higher percentage of
medium- and dark-colored walls, followed by brick and concrete. Exterior wall materials and colors can
affect the amount of heat absorption. In heating-dominated climates, darker-colored exteriors generally
absorb more heat from the sun compared to lighter colors, and that can help increase the indoor
temperatures in buildings and affect energy simulation results (Fernandez-Antolin, del-Río, and
Gonzalez-Lezcano 2019).

Foundation Type
The different foundation types and their respective percentages are illustrated in Figure 10. The sample
counts and sample weights from ResStock are constructed using U.S. EIA 2009 Residential Energy
Consumption Survey (RECS) microdata. The results show an agreement between the field data and
A3DDC, with a predominance of crawlspaces (82.4% and 80.7%, respectively), followed by slab on
grade (11.0% in the field data and 18.5% in A3DDC). When comparing these results with ResStock,
ResStock shows that many buildings have basements (40.0%). Considering that 80% of Alaska is
underlain by permafrost, or permanently frozen ground, one of the greatest challenges is building
foundations that can remain stable over time (Cold Climate Housing Research Center n.d.). Therefore,
the most common foundation types observed in rural Alaska include crawlspaces, slab-on-grade, and
“permafrost foundations” such as triodetic foundations or driven pilings. The results suggest that there
may be an inaccurate distribution of foundation types across these communities in the ResStock dataset.

Vacancy Status
Regarding vacancy status, both datasets, the field data and ResStock, show a larger number of buildings
as occupied, 67.8% and 91.2%, respectively. The remaining homes, 32.2% (ResStock) and 8.8% (field
data) are considered unoccupied. The ResStock base data for vacancy status comes from the 2019-5yrs
Public Use Microdata Samples (PUMS). This could explain the observed discrepancy, as the PUMS
dataset may not account for vacancy characteristics specific to Alaska, particularly rural Alaskan
communities. Additionally, in Alaska, there are significant differences in vacancy patterns between
urban areas, such as Anchorage and Fairbanks, and rural regions. Rural Alaskan communities often
experience higher vacancy rates due to factors such as seasonal occupancy, economic challenges, and
limited housing stock turnover. However, these nuances may not be well-represented in national datasets
such as PUMS, which aggregate data across broader geographic regions and lack sufficient granularity
for rural and remote areas.

Heating Setpoint and Indoor Temperature
Figure 16 shows the comparison between heating setpoints (ResStock) and the average indoor

temperatures were used to compare heating setpoints because temperature is usually regulated by the
heating system. Results from the field data reveal a tendency toward higher temperatures inside homes
in rural Alaska. Most homes (40.4%) had average indoor temperatures in the range of 70–74°F. In
contrast, the ResStock dataset reports many homes with heating setpoints around 55–59°F. The base
data for heating setpoint in ResStock comes from the U.S. EIA 2020 Residential Energy Consumption
Survey (RECS) microdata. All homes reported with temperatures ranging from 55 to 59°F in ResStock
are unoccupied (vacancy status = vacant). When these vacant homes are removed from the dataset, the
resulting distribution of temperatures for the remaining occupied homes becomes more representative of
typical residential heating patterns, but still the field data shows higher heating temperatures being set by
households in rural Alaskan communities compared to ResStock. The average heating setpoint for

Conclusions
Data on rural areas and Indigenous populations has been limited, leading to their underrepresentation in
nationwide datasets. Access to this information is important for accurately modeling residential

 18

buildings and identifying energy-efficient solutions across diverse populations and building types
throughout the United States, including those in typically underrepresented areas. Based on that, this
validation study aimed to identify similarities and differences across ResStock, the field data, and
A3DDC datasets in rural areas in Alaska. ResStock is an excellent tool for modeling stock and testing
energy efficiency upgrades, and it has significantly improved since the integration of ARIS data for the
state of Alaska. A summary of the findings is reported below, along with possible next steps and
implications for revisions described in the following section.

Among the categorical variables we analyzed, our findings suggest that ResStock may be
underrepresenting south-facing buildings and overrepresenting north-facing buildings. Regarding
heating equipment, Toyostoves account for 38.5% in the field data but are not represented in the
ResStock dataset, possibly because of the limited capability of current energy simulation tools used in
ResStock to account for direct-vent heaters such as Toyostoves. The field data also revealed a higher
prevalence of vinyl exterior wall finish materials and lighter-colored walls compared to the ResStock
dataset. Additionally, the field data shows a higher percentage of crawlspaces as the foundation type,
whereas ResStock contains more buildings with basements. Because 80% of Alaska is underlain by
permafrost, the presence of basements in homes is unlikely. Roof material is another characteristic that
differs between datasets, with ResStock indicating more shingled roofs and the field data showing more
metal roofs.

Among the numerical variables, infiltration rates are shown to be lower in ResStock compared to the
field data. This could be attributed to the fact that ARIS data, integrated into ResStock, includes mostly
data on homes that participated in weatherization programs, potentially resulting in more energy-
efficient features being represented. Heating setpoints also report lower values in ResStock compared to
the field data. A similar trend is observed for the number of occupants, where ResStock reports lower
averages of persons per household.

Next Steps and Implication for Revisions
Based on this validation study, we identified some differences between modeled, field data, and A3DDC
datasets. The following building characteristics have a high impact on modeled energy consumption and
demonstrated large differences:

 • Heating setpoints. The average heating setpoint in ResStock is lower than the average observed
 in the field data. Revisit distributions and consider higher setpoints, which could potentially
 address missing loads if found to be the case.
 • Toyo heating. Toyo heating is currently missing from ResStock, but prevalent in field data
 collected. Develop and include Toyo heating in future modeling for ResStock and EnergyPlus.
 • Natural gas. Natural gas is not readily available in rural Alaska apart from the North Slope.
 Remove natural gas as a water heater fuel type outside of North Slope County.
 • Foundation type. Field and A3DDC data show that 80% of dwellings have crawlspaces in rural
 Alaska, while ResStock shows only 28.7% of dwellings have crawlspaces and 40% of dwellings
 have basements. Revisit distributions and consider more crawlspaces than basements.
 • Infiltration rates. The average infiltration rate is 5.06 ACH50 in ResStock, approximately 30%
 lower than the average field data, 7.35 ACH50. Reevaluate the distribution to consider higher
 infiltration rates.

 19

 • Exterior wall type. Field data has revealed the predominancy of wood and vinyl light colored
 walls, while ResStock shows a predominancy of wood and brick medium/dark colored walls.
 Include more vinyl and less brick, and revise wall color for greater proportion of light than dark.
 • Roof material. Field and A3DDC data show that 93% of dwellings have metal roofs in rural
 Alaska, while approximately 75% of dwellings in ResStock have shingles. Revise from majority
 shingles to majority metal roofing material.
 • Occupant count. Occupancy rates in field data are 2.85 persons/dwelling, compared to 2.20
 persons/dwelling in ResStock. Update to higher occupant count.
 • Building orientation. Field and A3DDC shows approximately 35% of dwellings are south
 facing, while ResStock follows a normal distribution. Update to represent a higher proportion of
 south-facing than relatively equal south- and north-facing.
The findings suggest that the probability logic in ResStock could be revised to better represent the
building stock in rural areas in Alaska. ResStock can be used to identify effective upgrades or energy
efficiency improvements to address specific challenges faced by these communities. Additionally, it will
help community leaders make more informed decisions regarding investments and solutions.
