An 82-Year-Old Man Presenting With Syncope in the Setting of Atrial Fibrillation With Slow Ventricular Response Underwent LBBAP Implantation

An 82-year-old man presenting with syncope in the setting of atrial fibrillation with slow ventricular response underwent LBBAP implantation histopathological analysis of an incidental myocardial fragment adherent to the pacing lead helix unexpectedly revealed ATTR-CM, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for cardiomyopathies in contemporary electrophysiology practice. ATTR-CM = transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis; ECG = electrocardiogram; LBBAP = left bundle branch area pacing; LV = left ventricular.

Transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CM) is an infiltrative cardiomyopathy caused by myocardial deposition of misfolded transthyretin fibrils.1 Once considered rare, ATTR-CM is now recognized as a relatively common condition in elderly patients, particularly those presenting with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, left ventricular hypertrophy, and conduction system disease.2 Despite growing awareness and the availability of disease-modifying therapies, diagnosis is frequently delayed.1

Conduction system pacing has emerged as a physiological pacing strategy requiring deep septal lead implantation.3 During deep septal implantation, when lead repositioning is required, small myocardial fragments may occasionally be found adherent to the lead helix.4 We report a unique case in which such an incidental finding led to the histological diagnosis of ATTR-CM.

History of Presentation

An 82-year-old patient was referred to our institution for recurrent presyncope and a recent syncopal episode. Twelve-lead electrocardiography showed permanent atrial fibrillation with slow ventricular response.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome surgically treated approximately 15 years earlier. No previous diagnosis of cardiomyopathy or systemic amyloidosis was known.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed left ventricular systolic function at the lower limits of normal (ejection fraction: 50%) without regional wall motion abnormalities, mild aortic stenosis without other significant valvular disease, moderate concentric left ventricular hypertrophy (interventricular septum thickness: 13 mm), and a small circumferential pericardial effusion.

Management

Given an anticipated ventricular pacing burden exceeding 20%, a dual-chamber pacemaker with left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) was selected. Venous access was obtained via fluoroscopically guided puncture of the left axillary vein. A Medtronic SelectSecure 3830 lead was delivered using a C315HIS sheath. Septal mapping was performed by pacing the right ventricular septum, observing a W-shaped QRS pattern in lead V1 with discordant polarity in leads II and III.

The lead was advanced into the interventricular septum with approximately 10 clockwise rotations, achieving an estimated penetration depth of 10 to 11 mm. Electrical parameters after the initial deployment were unsatisfactory, prompting lead extraction and repositioning. Upon lead withdrawal, a macroscopically visible fragment of myocardial tissue was observed adherent to the helix (Figure 1). In light of the patient's clinical features, the tissue was preserved and sent for histopathological examination.

A small fragment of myocardial tissue is visible adherent to the exposed screw-in helix of a Medtronic SelectSecure 3830 lumenless pacing lead.

The lead was subsequently repositioned using the same technique, achieving optimal electrical parameters and evidence of left bundle branch capture, including an R-R′ pattern in lead V1, left ventricular activation time of 71 ms, V1-V6 interpeak interval of 52 ms, QRS duration of 110 ms, impedance of 770 Ω, sensing of 12 mV, and pacing threshold of 1.1 V at 0.4 ms.

Follow-Up

Histological examination of the retrieved tissue fragment, likely originating from the subendocardial interventricular septum—a myocardial region frequently involved in cardiac amyloid deposition5—revealed small areas of extracellular amorphous material that stained positive with Congo red and exhibited apple-green birefringence under polarized light. Subsequent immunohistochemical analysis using an amyloid typing kit demonstrated transthyretin positivity, confirming ATTR amyloid deposition within myocardial tissue (Figure 2).

Amorphous foreign material compatible with device polymer coating (“guidewire coating”), likely derived from the pacing lead, is also visible (asterisk) (Congo red stain; original magnification ×20). (B) The same specimen examined under polarized light demonstrating apple-green birefringence within the nodular deposit (boxed area), confirming the presence of amyloid (Congo red stain; original magnification ×20). (D) Immunohistochemical staining with anti–transthyretin antibodies showing positive immunoreactivity within the amyloid deposits, consistent with transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis; the asterisk indicates foreign material compatible with device polymer coating (original magnification ×40).

Bone scintigraphy with a bone-avid tracer demonstrated intense cardiac uptake corresponding to Perugini grade 3. Serum and urine studies showed no evidence of a monoclonal component, and transthyretin genetic testing was negative, supporting the diagnosis of wild-type ATTR-CM. Disease-specific therapy with tafamidis was subsequently initiated.

Discussion

ATTR-CM is an increasingly recognized cardiomyopathy in elderly patients and is frequently associated with involvement of the cardiac conduction system.2 Bradyarrhythmias, atrioventricular conduction disturbances, and atrial arrhythmias commonly occur during the disease course and may require permanent pacing.1 Amyloid infiltration of the conduction system contributes to progressive electrical dysfunction and increasing ventricular pacing burden over time.6,7

Within this clinical background, physiological pacing strategies such as LBBAP are increasingly adopted in patients with an anticipated high ventricular pacing burden.8 At the same time, the diagnostic approach to ATTR-CM has evolved substantially over recent years. In contemporary practice, diagnosis is frequently achieved through noninvasive algorithms, whereas histological confirmation is generally reserved for selected cases.1

Against this evolving clinical and diagnostic landscape, the present case provides an instructive perspective. Although in retrospect, several clinical red flags for ATTR-CM were already present before pacemaker implantation, the patient had not previously undergone specific evaluation for a possible infiltrative cardiomyopathy. The diagnosis ultimately emerged from an incidental finding during routine pacemaker implantation, whose significance became apparent when interpreted within the appropriate clinical context. The retrieval of a myocardial fragment adherent to the pacing lead helix was unplanned; however, its potential relevance was recognized in light of the patient's clinical phenotype, prompting histological evaluation.

This case illustrates how ATTR-CM may remain unrecognized until an opportunity for diagnosis is consciously identified. It further supports the notion that the disease may be more prevalent than commonly assumed and that recognition often depends less on complex investigations than on disease awareness and clinical context. In this regard, familiarity with cardiomyopathies represents an essential component of contemporary cardiology practice rather than a purely subspecialty domain.

Electrophysiologists may occupy a particularly favorable position within this diagnostic pathway. Rhythm and conduction disturbances are frequent clinical features of ATTR-CM and often represent the reason for initial specialist evaluation.9 Consequently, electrophysiologists are frequently among the first clinicians to encounter these patients, sometimes before the development of overt heart failure. This perspective offers both responsibility and opportunity: Integrating cardiomyopathy awareness into electrophysiology practice may facilitate earlier disease recognition and appropriate referral within multidisciplinary care pathways.

The importance of this approach is further emphasized in the current therapeutic era. Strengthening cross-disciplinary integration between electrophysiology and cardiomyopathy expertise is essential to ensure that patients can benefit from timely, targeted treatments.

Conclusions

ATTR-CM may be diagnosed through unexpected pathways. In rare circumstances, myocardial tissue adherent to an LBBAP lead can provide decisive histological information. This case reinforces the importance of disease awareness and interdisciplinary collaboration between electrophysiology and cardiomyopathy specialists.
