Causal associations between antibody-mediated immune responses to infectious agents and cardiovascular inflammatory diseases: A Mendelian randomization study based on UK Biobank and FinnGen
Authors: Qi Huang, Wanqian Pan, Shikun Sun, Weixiang Chen, Jia Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA two-sample Mendelian randomization study was performed to evaluate causal associations between 46 pathogen-specific antibodies and six cardiovascular inflammatory diseases: myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis, coronary atherosclerosis (CAS), polyarteritis nodosa (PAN), and giant cell arteritis (GCA). Antibody data came from UK Biobank, diseases data came from FinnGen. Instrumental variables were selected using strict (p < 5 × 10−8) and lenient threshold (p < 5 × 10−6). Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to control FDR; FDR‑adjusted q < 0.05 defined “robust associations.” Under strict threshold, robust associations were observed for HHV-7 U14 antibody levels with myocarditis (OR = 2.049, 95% CI: 1.325–3.168, p = .001, FDR = 0.044), EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels with CAS (OR = 0.909, 95% CI: 0.866–0.954, p = 1.00E-04, FDR = 0.001), and EBV EA-D antibody levels with GCA (OR = 0.410, 95% CI: 0.247–0.680, p = .001, FDR = 0.007). Under lenient threshold, EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels (OR = 0.924, 95% CI: 0.889–0.960, p = 5.03E-05, FDR = 0.012) and anti-Helicobacter pylori IgG seropositivity (OR = 1.050, 95% CI: 1.021–1.080, p = .001, FDR = 0.047) were robustly associated with CAS. No significant horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected, and reverse MR did not support reverse causation. This study provides genetic evidence for causal associations between pathogen‑specific antibodies with myocarditis, CAS and GCA. Associations identified under strict threshold represent more robust evidence, whereas those under lenient threshold are hypothesis-generating. Validation in more rigorous designs and non-European populations is warranted.

Introduction

Cardiovascular inflammatory diseases encompass a spectrum of immune-mediated pathological processes leading to cardiac and vascular injury, including myocarditis, pericarditis, accelerated atherosclerosis, and systemic vasculitis such as polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) and giant cell arteritis (GCA).1,2 These diseases are significant contributors to heart failure, myocardial infarction, aneurysm formation, and sudden death, imposing a substantial global health burden.3 While traditional risk factors (including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking) are well-established, evidence suggests that pathogen infections and the persistent immune responses they elicit may play roles in the initiation and progression of cardiovascular inflammation.4–6

Various pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and parasites, have been implicated in cardiovascular inflammation.7–10 These infectious agents may contribute through mechanisms such as direct tissue damage, triggering autoimmunity, inducing chronic low-grade inflammation, or promoting endothelial dysfunction.11,12 Serum levels of pathogen-specific antibodies serve as biomarkers of past or persistent infection and may reflect the state of the host immune response.13 However, observational studies are unable to reliably distinguish causal associations from confounding factors.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, effectively minimizing confounding bias.14 This study employed MR analysis to systematically evaluate the causal associations between 46 antibody-mediated immune responses to infectious agents and the risks of myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis, coronary atherosclerosis (CAS), PAN, and GCA.

Methods and materials

Data sources

Exposure data

Genetic instruments for antibody-mediated immune responses were derived from GWAS conducted by Butler-Laporte et al.16 This study performed large-scale serological profiling of antibody responses against 13 infectious pathogens, using data from the UK Biobank cohort. A total of 46 antibody-related phenotypes were defined, comprising 15 seropositivity traits and 31 quantitative antibody level traits (refer to Table S1).

Instrumental variable selection

For the exposure data, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) exhibiting genome-wide significant associations (p < 5 × 10−8) were initially selected as IVs. However, for certain exposures, no SNPs met this stringent criterion. To ensure an adequate number of IVs for each phenotype, the significance threshold was relaxed to p < 5 × 10−6, based on previous research.17 MR analysis was conducted using the conventional threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) when at least one genome-wide significant SNP was available. Subsequently, a clumping procedure was performed (r2 < 0.001, clumping window = 10,000 kb) to mitigate the effects of linkage disequilibrium. The strength of the SNPs was evaluated using the F-statistic; SNPs with an F-statistic > 10 were retained for MR analysis to minimize weak instrument bias. The F-statistic was calculated as F = [R2 (n − k − 1)] / [k (1 − R2)], where R2 represents the variance explained by the IV, n is the sample size, and k denotes the number of SNPs.18 The number of SNPs used for each exposure-outcome pair and their individual F-statistics are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. IVs showing a stronger association with the outcome than the exposure were removed using Steiger filtering.19

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.4.2) using packages including “TwoSampleMR” and “MR-PRESSO.”

For exposures with only one IV, the Wald ratio method was used to estimate the causal association.20 For exposures with multiple IVs, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode methods were employed. The IVW method was prioritized for causal estimation, with other methods serving as supplements.21 Causal effect sizes are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Results from IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode were considered consistent if the effect direction (positive or negative) aligned. To control the false discovery rate (FDR), the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used for multiple comparisons. Specifically, the p-values obtained from m tests are sorted in ascending order and denoted as P(i). For each p-value, the corresponding Q-value is calculated as Q(i) = [m × P(i)] / i, and then compared Q-value with α. Under the lenient threshold, for each cardiovascular inflammatory disease, FDR correction was applied independently to a family of 230 tests, corresponding to the combination of 46 antibody traits × 5 MR methods. Under the strict threshold, FDR correction was applied only to the family of tests for which valid results were actually obtained, rather than to all theoretically possible combinations. In order to distinguish between associations those remain significant after FDR correction (q < 0.05) and those are only nominally significant (p < .05 but q ≥ 0.05), only FDR significant findings are described as “robust associations,” while nominally significant findings are referred as “suggestive associations.”

To assess robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed, and horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO. A non-zero intercept in MR-Egger regression (p > .05 indicates no pleiotropy) and significant outliers detected by MR-PRESSO suggested pleiotropy.22 Cochran’s Q test was used to evaluate heterogeneity among IVs, with a P-value greater than 0.05 suggesting no significant heterogeneity.19 Leave-one-out analysis evaluated whether causal estimates were driven by single influential SNPs.

Results

FDR correction was applied separately for each cardiovascular inflammatory disease. Under the lenient threshold, for each disease, the correction was performed across a family of 230 tests (46 antibody traits × 5 MR methods). Under the strict threshold, the numbers of tests included in FDR correction varied by disease: 35 for myocarditis, 35 for pericarditis, 34 for endocarditis, 35 for CAS, 35 for PAN, and 25 for GCA (detailed in Tables S4–S15).

When the genome-wide significance threshold for IV selection of antibody traits was set at p < 5 × 10−8, robust associations were observed for human herpes virus (HHV) 7 U14 antibody levels with myocarditis (OR = 2.049, 95% CI: 1.325–3.168, p = .001, FDR = 0.044); Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) EBNA-1 antibody levels (OR = 0.909, 95% CI: 0.866–0.954, p = 1.00E-04, FDR = 0.001) and anti-varicella zoster virus (VZV) IgG seropositivity (OR = 1.115, 95% CI: 1.058–1.175, p = 4.77E-05, FDR = 0.001) with CAS; EBV EA-D antibody levels (OR = 0.410, 95% CI: 0.247–0.680, p = .001, FDR = 0.007) and anti-Merkel cell polyomavirus IgG seropositivity (OR = 1.595, 95% CI: 1.304–1.952, p = 5.66E-06, FDR = 1.42E-04) with GCA. Among these, heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests for the associations of HHV-7 U14 antibody levels with myocarditis, EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels with CAS, and EBV EA-D antibody levels with GCA were non-significant (p > .05). In the MR analysis results with sufficient SNPs, funnel plots indicated no significant heterogeneity. Leave-one-out analyses indicated that no individual SNP had a dominant effect on the causal estimates (refer to Figures. S1–S24). However, when the genome-wide significance threshold for IV selection of antibody traits was set at p < 5 × 10−6, scatter plots showed that the results for the association between helicobacter pylori CagA antibodies and myocarditis (refer to Figure S15), as well as between anti-Toxoplasma gondii IgG seropositivity and CAS (refer to Figure S23), were inconsistent in the direction of effect estimates across the five MR analysis methods used.

Reverse MR analysis found no significant causal effects of cardiovascular inflammatory diseases on pathogen-derived antibody levels (refer to Table S39).

Among the FDR‑significant associations, those also passed all sensitivity analyses (no significant heterogeneity as indicated by Cochran’s Q test p > .05, no significant horizontal pleiotropy as indicated by MR‑Egger intercept p > .05, and consistent directions of effect across all MR methods) are defined as “sensitivity‑robust associations.” These include: HHV‑7 U14 antibody levels with myocarditis under the strict threshold; EBV EBNA‑1 antibody levels with CAS under both the strict and lenient thresholds; EBV EA‑D antibody levels with GCA under the strict threshold; and anti‑Helicobacter pylori IgG seropositivity with CAS under the lenient threshold. Other FDR‑significant associations that showed potential issues in one or more sensitivity tests are reported as “FDR‑significant but not fully robust.”

Based on the above analyses, under both IV selection thresholds for antibody traits (p < 5 × 10−8 and p < 5 × 10−6), EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels maintained a robust negative association with CAS, which is the main finding of this study. Under the stringent IV selection threshold, among results with a sufficient number of SNPs, HHV-7 U14 antibody levels with myocarditis, and EBV EA-D antibody levels with GCA also showed robust associations. At the relaxed threshold, anti‑Helicobacter pylori IgG seropositivity exhibited a robust positive association with CAS. No significant horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected for these associations, and reverse MR analysis did not support causal effects of cardiovascular inflammatory diseases on antibody levels, reinforcing the reliability of the causal direction. However, several nominally significant associations did not reach the robust threshold after FDR correction and should be considered suggestive findings, requiring validation in larger or independent cohorts.

Discussion

The present study employed two IV selection thresholds to balance statistical power and reliability. Associations reaching FDR significance under the strict threshold (p < 5 × 10−8) are considered more robust, whereas those significant only under the lenient threshold (p < 5 × 10−6) should be interpreted with greater caution. The relaxed selection criteria may introduce weak instrument bias and increase the risk of false-positive findings; therefore, these associations are considered hypothesis-generating and require independent replication.

Our findings highlight the potential role of EBV-related immune responses in cardiovascular inflammatory diseases. EBV is a widely prevalent herpesvirus that establishes lifelong latency and has been implicated in various autoimmune and inflammatory diseases.23 Previous studies have suggested that EBV infection may lead to cardiovascular complications such as coronary artery dilation, coronary artery aneurysm, myocarditis, acute myocardial infarction, valvular heart disease, heart failure, and pulmonary arterial hypertension.24 These pathological changes arise from direct viral toxicity and immune system responses. Evidence of direct EBV injury comes from viral gene detection in endomyocardial samples.25 However, autopsies of patients with EBV‑associated myocarditis have revealed severe inflammatory infiltration, whereas electron microscopy showed no direct invasion of cardiomyocytes, suggesting that the injury may not be caused by direct viral damage.6,26 Currently, more evidence indicates that EBV‑related cardiovascular involvement is an indirect injury mediated by immune responses. Pathological studies have found that EBV‑associated coronary artery injury is caused by lymphocytic vasculitis, and EBV‑related vasculitis involves local inflammatory cell infiltration, cytotoxic damage, and cytokine secretion.8

EBNA-1 is a core protein for maintaining EBV latent infection; its glycine-alanine repeat domain inhibits the proteasomal degradation of its own antigens, impairs MHC class I antigen presentation, and enables evasion of CD8+ T cell immune surveillance.27 Our study found a genetically predicted negative association between elevated levels of EBV EBNA‑1 antibodies and the risk of CAS. This result may be related to cross‑protective immune responses generated during EBV infection, such as the induction of regulatory B‑cell or T‑cell responses, which in turn suppress chronic inflammation associated with atherosclerosis.28,29 An alternative explanation is that elevated levels of EBNA‑1 antibodies may reflect effective immune control of latent EBV infection by the host, thereby reducing vascular endothelial injury and chronic inflammation induced by viral reactivation.30 Maaike J. M. et al. found that virus‑specific CD8+ T cells enriched in atherosclerotic plaques are not activated through antigen‑specific interactions, suggesting the existence of antigen‑independent immunomodulatory mechanisms.31 Baumeier et al. showed that EBV lytic transcripts are significantly associated with the severity of myocardial inflammation in patients with heart failure, indicating that EBV lytic reactivation may be involved in inflammatory heart disease.32

The negative association between EBV EA‑D (lytic early antigen) antibodies and GCA observed in this study supports the notion that effective control of EBV reactivation may reduce immune‑mediated damage to the vascular wall. Giardina et al. reported a case of chronic active EBV infection complicated by giant cell temporal arteritis, further suggesting a potential link between EBV and GCA.33 Furthermore, Shimizu et al. found that EBV EA‑IgG titers are positively associated with atherosclerosis only in individuals with low genetic susceptibility for angiogenic capacity, indicating that the host genetic background modifies this association.34 Taken together, these findings suggest that the impact of EBV‑related immune responses on cardiovascular inflammation may be associated with the balance between viral latency and lytic reactivation as well as host genetic factors; however, the underlying mechanisms require experimental validation.

HHV‑7 is a ubiquitous human beta-herpesvirus. Primary infection often occurs during childhood, after which the virus establishes lifelong latency or persistent low‑level replication in CD4+ T lymphocytes and salivary glands.35 In this study, a robust positive association was observed between HHV‑7 U14 antibody levels and the risk of myocarditis. Although HHV‑7 is generally considered to have low pathogenicity, clinical case reports have linked it to myocarditis. Ozdemir et al. first reported that HHV‑7 may be a causative pathogen of acute myocarditis in children, detecting HHV‑7 sequences by PCR in four patients, two of whom progressed to dilated cardiomyopathy.36 Okabe et al. described a case of fulminant myocarditis caused by HHV‑7 infection in a 22‑month‑old girl, who survived but with severe neurological sequelae, and HHV‑7 DNA was detected in myocardial tissue.37 These case reports suggest that HHV‑7 has cardiac tropism, although its pathogenic mechanism remains unclear.

Studies have indicated that HHV‑7 can replicate in CD4+ T lymphocytes and may disrupt local immune responses by using viral proteins such as U12 and U51 as chemokine receptors, thereby diverting chemokines from their natural ligands.38,39 Furthermore, virus‑infected cells may trigger autoimmune responses through bystander activation or epitope spreading, inducing apoptosis of neighboring bystander cells, a process that may involve the TNF‑related apoptosis‑inducing ligand (TRAIL) signaling pathway.40 Immune evasion proteins encoded by HHV‑7, such as U21, can downregulate MHC class I and class II proteins expression to avoid cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)‑mediated killing of infected cells, thereby allowing viral persistence or recurrent reactivation, which may indirectly contribute to chronicity of myocardial inflammation.41–44 However, it should be emphasized that the antibodies measured in this study are serum levels directed against the HHV‑7 U14 protein, reflecting the host’s immune response status to viral infection rather than direct evidence of viral presence or active replication, and the specific mechanism underlying this association remains to be elucidated. Moreover, because myocarditis is a disease with high etiological heterogeneity and HHV‑7 antibodies have an extremely high seroprevalence in the general population, the genetic association observed in this study cannot establish causality.45 Therefore, the results of this study should be considered hypothesis‑generating and need to be validated in independent cohorts, as well as further elucidated by viral detection in myocardial tissue and functional experiments.

In our study, the positive association between anti‑Helicobacter pylori IgG seropositivity and risk of CAS (OR = 1.050) reached a robust level under the lenient IV selection threshold. This effect size is similar to that reported by Zhang et al. in their MR study.46 Mechanistically, Helicobacter pylori may promote atherosclerosis by inducing systemic low‑grade inflammation, directly damaging the vascular wall, and exacerbating risk factors including hypertension, diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia.47 Meta‑analyses showed that Helicobacter pylori infection significantly increases carotid intima‑media thickness and pulse wave velocity, with a more pronounced effect in individuals infected with cytotoxin-associated protein A (CagA)-positive strains.48,49 CagA and vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA) are major virulence factors of helicobacter pylori. CagA can be transported to bloodstream and vascular endothelial cells via exosomes, induce osteogenic phenotype switching and calcification of coronary artery smooth muscle cells, and also promote the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby contributing to endothelial injury.50 VacA may reduce nitric oxide levels, leading to impaired endothelial function.47 Anti-Helicobacter pylori IgG may exacerbate the inflammatory response by forming circulating immune complexes, consequently increasing the risk of CAS. Although the genetic findings of this study support a potential causal role of Helicobacter pylori in CAS, the effect size is extremely small, and the more lenient threshold may introduce weak instrument bias; therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Several associations reached FDR significance but failed at least one sensitivity test. For example, the association between anti-Merkel cell polyomavirus IgG seropositivity and GCA (strict threshold) could not be tested for heterogeneity or pleiotropy due to an insufficient number of SNPs; thus, no strong mechanistic interpretation is offered. Similarly, the association between anti-VZV IgG seropositivity and CAS (strict threshold) is based on a single SNP, precluding heterogeneity and pleiotropy testing. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require independent replication.

In addition, recent clinical observations of rare myocarditis, pericarditis, and other cardiovascular inflammatory events following COVID‑19 vaccination have received attention. These events are thought to be related to vaccine‑induced immune responses.51,52 However, the antibodies analyzed in this study are pathogen‑specific antibodies directed against natural infection, and the MR design was used to assess genetically predicted long‑term immune exposure, which is fundamentally different from the acute immune response following vaccination. Therefore, the results of this study should not be directly extrapolated to vaccine‑related cardiovascular events.

The following limitations should be considered. First, for some antibody traits, no instrumental variables were available under the strict genome‑wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8); therefore, a more lenient threshold (p < 5 × 10−6) was used to obtain a sufficient number of SNPs. Although the F‑statistics were all >10, weak instrument bias may still be introduced, particularly for exposure – outcome pairs with a small number of IVs. Second, a large number of hypothesis tests were performed in this study. Although the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for FDR correction, some nominally significant associations may still be false positives. Accordingly, associations that remained significant after FDR correction were defined as “robust associations,” whereas the others were defined as “suggestive associations” to distinguish the strength of evidence. Third, horizontal pleiotropy is an inherent limitation of MR analysis. Although MR‑Egger intercept and MR‑PRESSO tests did not indicate significant pleiotropy, these tests have limited power when the number of IVs is small and cannot completely rule out pleiotropy through unknown pathways. Fourth, both exposure and outcome data were derived from European populations (UK Biobank and FinnGen), so the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups is unclear. Given the differences in pathogen prevalence, immune response profiles, and cardiovascular disease risk across ethnic groups, the extrapolation of the results is limited. Fifth, antibody level data were measured at a single time point and cannot capture dynamic changes within individuals. Moreover, serum antibody levels as exposures only represent the host’s immune response status to past or persistent infection and are not directly equivalent to the infection itself. Sixth, this study did not examine differences in disease severity or subtypes (e.g., myocarditis with preserved ejection fraction versus myocarditis with reduced ejection fraction), which should be explored in future studies. Seventh, MR analysis reflects the association between lifelong genetically determined susceptibility to antibody levels and disease risk; it cannot capture dynamic fluctuations in antibody levels over time or their temporal relationship with disease onset. Finally, although the MR results suggest potential causal relationships, these require further validation, and the underlying biological mechanisms need to be elucidated by in‑depth experimental studies.

Conclusion

This study provides genetic evidence from MR analysis supporting a negative causal association between EBV EBNA‑1 antibody levels and the risk of CAS, as well as a potential positive causal association between anti‑Helicobacter pylori IgG seropositivity and the risk of CAS. Furthermore, robust causal associations were observed between HHV‑7 U14 antibodies and myocarditis, and between EBV EA‑D antibodies and GCA. These findings suggest that pathogen‑specific antibody‑mediated immune responses may exert bidirectional regulatory effects in inflammatory cardiovascular diseases, although the effect sizes are modest and the results are subject to potential biases such as pleiotropy, weak instrument bias, and population generalizability. Future studies should adopt more stringent instrumental variable selection criteria, integrate multi‑omics data, and validate the findings in non‑European populations to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms and clinical implications.
