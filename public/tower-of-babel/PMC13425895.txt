Comment on “Complications and Survival of Teeth Restored With Crowns Following Crown Lengthening: A Retrospective Cohort Study”
Authors: Hui-Nan Wu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the retrospective cohort study by Alhabashi and colleagues examining long-term outcomes of teeth restored with full-coverage crowns following surgical crown lengthening (CLP). The study provides a relatively large cohort with follow-up extending to 10 years and reports both survival and complication outcomes. The reported survival rates (96.8% at 5 years and 95% at 10 years) appear higher than those typically described in comparable long-term cohort studies.1 While this may be partly explained by case selection and treatment setting, a few methodological issues may influence how these results should be interpreted.

Baseline periodontal bone level, particularly initial alveolar bone defect depth, was not clearly incorporated as an independent covariate in the multivariable model.2 Considering its well-established role in periodontal prognosis, the absence of this parameter may result in residual confounding and limit the robustness of risk adjustment across heterogeneous baseline conditions. The interpretation of “regular dental attendance” is also not straightforward. Without a clear definition of supportive periodontal treatment, this variable likely captures a mixture of behavioural, socioeconomic, and compliance-related factors rather than a standardized recall protocol.3 As such, it is difficult to disentangle whether the observed association reflects true treatment effect or underlying patient-related differences.

In addition, defining survival solely as tooth retention provides only a partial view of long-term treatment performance. A considerable proportion of retained teeth still experienced biologically or prosthetically relevant complications, suggesting that survival alone may overestimate overall clinical success. A composite endpoint incorporating complication burden or retreatment need might have provided a more clinically informative measure of outcome stability.

From a statistical standpoint, selecting variables based on univariate screening may have restricted the inclusion of clinically relevant confounders. This approach is also sensitive to sample size and may affect model stability when outcome events are limited. In addition, it increases the risk that important prognostic factors are excluded solely due to lack of univariate significance, rather than true lack of association. Importantly, although survival outcomes were reported separately at 5 and 10 years, no formal statistical comparison between these time points was performed. As a result, it remains unclear whether outcomes were truly stable over time or whether subtle late differences in survival trajectories or complication accumulation may exist. This limits interpretation of the temporal dynamics of treatment success.

Finally, multiple teeth within the same patient appear to have been analysed without accounting for clustering, which may lead to underestimated variance and artificially narrow confidence intervals. The use of logistic regression also does not fully take advantage of the time-to-event structure inherent in a 10-year follow-up, where censoring and varying observation periods could meaningfully influence estimates.4

Overall, the study provides useful long-term data in a clinically relevant field, but the above considerations may be relevant when interpreting the magnitude and temporal stability of the reported survival outcomes.
