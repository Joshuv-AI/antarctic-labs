ABSTRACT
Reanalysis of genomic data in rare disease is highly effective in increasing diagnostic yields but remains limited by manual approaches. Automation and optimization for high specificity will be necessary to ensure scalability, adoption and sustainability of iterative reanalysis. We developed Talos, an open-source tool that automates variant prioritization by integrating dynamically updated gene−disease and variant-level evidence with inheritance-aware filtering and validated its performance using data from 1,089 individuals with rare disease. Trio-based analysis identified 90% of known diagnoses, returning 1.3 variants per case on average. Variant burden reduced to one variant per 200 cases on iterative monthly reanalysis. Application to an unselected cohort of 4,735 undiagnosed individuals identified 241 diagnoses (5.1% yield): 78 (32%) due to new gene−disease relationships, 54 (22%) due to new variant-level evidence and 109 (45%) due to improved analysis strategies. Our automated, iterative reanalysis model demonstrates the feasibility of delivering frequent, systematic reanalysis at scale.

Main

Genomic testing has transformed rare disease diagnosis, with many more families receiving a timely and accurate molecular diagnosis to guide clinical care1–3. However, more than 50% of individuals remain undiagnosed after the initial test4. Unlike most other diagnostic investigations, genomic data can be stored and repeatedly reanalyzed, and such reanalysis has proven highly effective in delivering new diagnoses over time. A meta-analysis of 29 studies comprising 9,419 undiagnosed patients with Mendelian disorders reported an increase in diagnostic yield of 10% (95% confidence interval: 6−13%) after a median time of approximately 24 months5. Several research studies have delivered reanalysis in large cohorts, using different models. The Deciphering Developmental Disorders study performed cohort-wide, automated reanalysis between 2014 and 2018, incorporating updated annotations and pipeline improvements, resulting in an additional 13% increase in diagnostic yield in a cohort of 1,133 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders6. The Exomiser variant priorization tool has been applied to 24,015 unsolved rare disease cases from the 100,000 Genomes Project, increasing diagnostic yield by 2% but requiring extensive manual review and tool optimization to increase efficiency7. Most recently, the Solve-RD Consortium demonstrated the feasibility of scaling manual case-based reanalysis in a cohort of 6,004 families, using a collaborative network approach combined with dedicated infrastructure, with an 8.4% increase in diagnostic yield8.

These studies have illuminated the principal sources of new diagnoses: emerging knowledge of disease-associated genes and variants and improvements in analysis processes9. The knowledge base for genomic interpretation is constantly evolving. Each year, hundreds of novel gene−disease associations and thousands of new disease-associated variants are identified and deposited in public databases10–12, directly affecting diagnostic yields. The bioinformatic tools used in analysis also continue to mature. Diagnostic pipelines increasingly incorporate analysis for a broader range of variant types, including copy number variants (CNVs), structural variants (SVs) and short tandem repeats (STRs), resulting in more diagnoses.

Reanalysis is supported at a policy level13–15 yet, in practice, is limited by complex clinical and laboratory processes, lack of reimbursement and workforce shortages16,17. A very low proportion of undiagnosed individuals currently benefit from reanalysis, and access is inequitable, primarily driven by highly motivated clinicians, laboratories and patients16,18. Without these inconsistent factors to drive reanalysis, patients remain undiagnosed, which, in turn, may directly impact the quality and cost of clinical care. Meanwhile, the volume of stored genomic data that would benefit from reanalysis continues to grow year on year.

Automation has been proposed as a possible solution, potentially enabling reanalysis to be scaled equitably to thousands of datasets and to be performed frequently. Clinical and laboratory workforce attitudes toward automation are largely positive16, with models incorporating automation starting to emerge19–24. These efforts have used a mix of proprietary tools19, in-house solutions22–24 or case-by-case submission to external analysis portals20, all of which limit broader adoption.

Key questions remain about how to implement reanalysis at scale in both research and clinical practice. In any model, tradeoffs around sensitivity, specificity, acceptable variant burden, frequency of reanalysis and degree of automation would be required to balance the added strain on resources while optimizing diagnostic outcomes25,26. Hence, evaluations of the performance and workload implications of different models in large, unselected cohorts are needed to inform future policy and practice.

Results

Talos is an open-source tool designed for iterative reanalysis of genomic data in rare disease (Fig. 1), intended to be run periodically as new gene−disease associations and variant classifications emerge, returning only variants with newly actionable evidence. We assessed the performance of Talos in validation cohorts that had previously undergone manual analysis in clinical and research settings. Assessing Talos performance

We assessed the performance of Talos using two previously analyzed cohorts that included both diagnosed and undiagnosed individuals. These cohorts were selected to enable benchmarking against manual analysis and to evaluate performance with and without parental data. The cohorts had previously undergone a range of clinical and research analyses, which together contributed to the set of known diagnoses used for benchmarking. Each cohort was reprocessed from raw sequencing data using a standardized pipeline incorporating Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) for short variant calling, GATK-SV for SV detection in genomes and GATK-gCNV for CNV analysis in exomes (Methods). The performance of Talos was evaluated against the set of variants detected by this pipeline.

Acute Care Genomics cohort

We first applied Talos to the Acute Care Genomics (ACG) cohort, in which ultra-rapid clinical genomic testing had been performed as a first-tier test in 401 critically ill infants and children between 2018 and 2022 as part of a national Australian study1,27. Sequencing was performed as exomes (n = 115) or genomes (n = 286), with most cases analyzed as trios (378/401, 94%; Fig. 2). The most common reasons for referral were seizures and neonatal hypotonia. Over 50% of probands were <1 month of age at the time of testing. Of the 20 missed diagnoses, eight (40%) involved moderate or low impact variants that lacked supporting information in ClinVar, all in recessive genes, and another four resulted from incomplete penetrance (Extended Data Table 1). These variants were not returned by Talos due to its intentionally conservative filtering strategy. During the original manual analysis, these variants had been classified as likely pathogenic using a mixture of criteria, including being in trans with another pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant, having a highly specific phenotype match or having supportive functional studies1.

Given the frequent use of singleton testing in both research and clinical settings, we next assessed the performance of Talos in the absence of parental data. To simulate singleton analysis, we removed parental data from all 401 probands in the ACG cohort, including those originally tested as duos and not included in the trio analysis above. Talos returned a median of 1.8 variants per singleton and identified 170 (82%) of the 208 achievable diagnoses (Table 1 and Extended Data Table 2). The decline in diagnostic performance compared to trios was primarily due to 16 missense variants in dominant genes, where de novo status was required to support likely pathogenic classification. When phenotype-based filtering was applied, the median number of candidate variants reduced to 1.2 per singleton, with 160 diagnoses identified (77%; Table 1) (Extended Data Table 3).

When all ACG families were considered (trios and non-trios), Talos identified 186 of 208 diagnoses (89%), returning a median of 1.3 candidate variants per family (Table 1). Phenotype-based filtering reduced the median number of candidate variants per family to 1.0 and correctly identified 173 diagnoses (83%).

Rare Genomes Project cohort

The Rare Genomes Project (RGP) cohort includes families with rare disease recruited through a remote study model launched in 2018, enabling direct partnership with families and advocacy groups to enroll participants from all 50 US states and Puerto Rico. Most had already undergone clinical genetic testing with negative or inconclusive results. For this study, we analyzed data from 688 RGP-enrolled families (298 singletons and 390 trios) with genome sequencing performed by the Broad Institute prior to September 2022. At enrollment, probands ranged in age from 7 months to 82 years, with a median age of 26 years (Fig. 2). Prior analysis identified 106 molecular diagnoses, the diagnostic yield of 15.4% reflective of a cohort with previous uninformative clinical testing.

Among the 106 prior diagnoses in the RGP cohort, 98 were due to SNVs or indels, 15 were CNVs or SVs, two were mitochondrial variants, eight involved STRs and three were detected using gene-specific callers. Inheritance patterns included 58 diagnoses attributed to autosomal dominant conditions (29 de novo; four inherited, including one with incomplete penetrance; and 25 with unknown inheritance due to absence of parental data), 34 attributed to autosomal recessive conditions (23 compound heterozygous and 11 homozygous), 13 attributed to X-linked conditions (10 de novo and three inherited or unconfirmed) and two attributed to conditions with mitochondrial inheritance.

Of the 57 trios with known diagnoses in the RGP cohort, pathogenic variants were detectable within the GATK/GATK-SV/GATK-gCNV pipeline for 54 (95%), with the remaining two diagnoses attributable to one STR and one CNV in SMN1 (Table 1). Talos returned a median of 1.3 candidate variants per trio for manual review (range, 0−12). These candidates included 47 of the 54 diagnoses possible (87%). Stricter filtering using high-level phenotype matching (Methods) reduced the number of candidate diagnoses per family to 0.9 and correctly identified 45 diagnoses (83%; Table 1).

In the absence of parental data (simulating singleton analysis), Talos returned a median of 2.2 candidate variants per singleton and identified 81 (87%) of the 93 possible diagnoses (Table 1). When phenotype-based filtering was applied, the median number of candidate variants reduced to 1.1 per singleton, with 78 diagnoses identified (84%) (Extended Data Table 4).

Across all 688 RGP families, Talos identified 80 of 93 possible diagnoses (86%), returning a median of 1.6 candidate variants per family (Table 1). When phenotype-based filtering was applied, the median number of candidate variants dropped to 0.8 per family, correctly identifying 77 diagnoses (83%).

Comparison with other tools

To contextualize these findings and evaluate how Talos compares to existing automated variant prioritization pipelines, we benchmarked this aspect of its performance against Exomiser, a widely used tool28. SNVs and indel call sets from the ACG cohort were analyzed using Exomiser v.14 with default configuration.

In the ACG cohort trios, 194 families had known diagnoses attributable to SNVs or indels. When all variants prioritized by Exomiser were considered, it successfully identified the causative variant in 172 families (89%), with the lowest-ranked diagnosis at 32. Performance decreased when limiting the number of variants reviewed per family. The causative variant was identified in 164 families (85%) when only the top 10 ranked variants were considered, in 157 families (81%) when limited to the top five and in 127 families (65%) when only the highest-ranked variants were reviewed.

Because outcomes are paired within the same families, we used McNemarʼs exact test on discordant pairs, defined as families where Talos identified the causative variant but Exomiser did not versus families where Exomiser identified the causative variant but Talos did not. This showed no significant difference at the all-variants and top 10 settings (Talos-only 12 versus Exomiser-only 12, P = 1; 18 versus 10, P = 0.18, respectively) but favored Talos at top five and top one (25 versus 10, P = 0.017; 53 versus 8, P < 0.0001, respectively).

These results suggest that Talos and Exomiser have similar sensitivity for SNVs and indels, although their design strategies differ. Although Exomiser relies on ranking and returns a broader list, Talos is optimized to return a smaller number of highly specific unranked candidate variants per family. The specific variants identified by each tool differed (Extended Data Fig. 1).

Iterative reanalysis in research and clinical datasets

We next evaluated real-world utility through deployment of Talos at scale in a large, unselected cohort of 4,735 individuals who had undergone prior genomic testing but remained undiagnosed (Fig. 2). This included 962 pediatric and adult research participants recruited as part of Australian Genomics rare disease flagship studies investigating the diagnostic utility of genomic testing across a range of clinical indications29. Genomic sequencing was performed by clinically accredited laboratories between 2017 and 2022 and was largely undertaken as singletons (n = 739, 76%), with average diagnostic yield of 33%, varying between 17% and 54% based on clinical indication for testing29. We also included 3,773 pediatric and adult patients referred for genomic testing to a single diagnostic laboratory (Victorian Clinical Genetics Services) between 2019 and 2022, who remained without a full diagnosis after initial testing.

The full cohort of 4,735 individuals included 3,739 who had exome sequencing and 996 with genome sequencing. Most were analyzed as singletons (n = 3,737), with 998 probands sequenced as part of a trio. One-third were children under 5 years of age at the time of the original test (n = 1,440; 30%). The most common reasons for referral were neurodevelopmental disorders (n = 1,662; 35%), followed by cardiac (n = 997; 21%), renal (n = 695; 15%) and neurological (n = 416; 9%) conditions. Comprehensive genomic analysis had previously been undertaken in 2,086 individuals (44%), whereas the remaining 2,649 (56%) had analysis restricted to virtual panels based on clinical indication (for example, cardiac or renal panels; Fig. 2).

Review of Talos output identified 268 likely causative variants consistent with 241 new diagnoses in 238 individuals (5.1% additional yield; Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). All likely causative variants were subsequently classified as P/LP by accredited clinical laboratories. Of the variants identified, 168 (62%) were coding SNVs and indels, 32 (12%) were noncoding SNVs and indels and 69 (26%) were CNVs and SVs. More than half of the additional diagnoses resulted from changes in knowledge about either gene−disease relationships (n = 78; 32%) or variant−disease relationships (n = 54; 22%). The remaining 109 diagnoses (45%) comprised variants that could have potentially been identified as part of the original analysis. Analysis for variant types not included in the original test, such as CNVs and SVs, identified 69 additional diagnoses, including CNVs ranging in size from 0.6 kilobases (kb) to 1.8 megabases (Mb) and one translocation transecting the PKD2 gene in an individual with cystic renal disease. Of the 68 CNVs, 63 (93%) were small intragenic deletions considered to be below the resolution of standard chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) (200 kb). Other variants were not reported during the original diagnostic analysis either due to overly restricting analysis based on phenotype (n = 27) or based on filtering criteria (n = 10), or they were missed due to human error (n = 10).

Of the 78 diagnoses attributed to new gene−disease relationships, 46 (59%) were not yet curated in Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) at the time of reanalysis, and 11 (14%) were due to new modes of inheritance reported for well-established disease genes, such as ALPK3 and IFT140.

Diagnostic yield from reanalysis was similar across clinical indications: 5.2% additional yield (86/1665) for neurodevelopmental disorders, 5.3% (53/997) for cardiac disorders and 5.9% (41/695) for renal disorders. Sources of new diagnoses varied by clinical indication for testing. New gene−disease associations and newly detected CNVs accounted for 78% of new diagnoses in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders, whereas variant reclassification accounted for 58% of new diagnoses in individuals with cardiac disorders (Fig. 3b).

The diagnostic yield from reanalysis of exome cases was 4.8% (180/3,741) compared to 6.1% (61/996) in genome cases. Of the diagnoses obtained in the genome cohort, six (10.5%) were only tractable due to availability of genome data (four noncoding variants in RNU4-2, one deep intronic variant in MRPL39 and one chromosomal translocation).

Diagnostic yield varied based on time elapsed since the original analysis, with 8.3% additional yield in individuals undergoing diagnostic testing in 2019 (73/879), 6.4% in 2020 (68/1,068), 3.2% in 2021 (42/1,319) and 4.0% in 2022 (49/1,234) (Fig. 3c).

Twenty-nine cycles of iterative monthly reanalysis were performed. Cohorts entered the reanalysis program at different timepoints (Fig. 3a), with all cohorts undergoing at least three cycles of iterative reanalysis. Whereas 221 diagnoses (92%) were identified as part of the initial cycle of reanalysis upon entry into the program, 20 diagnoses (8%) were obtained as part of iterative monthly cycles. All were due to new information about gene−disease relationships. During iterative reanalysis, Talos only returns candidate variants where the supporting evidence has changed since the previous round, such as updates to ClinVar or PanelApp Australia annotations. As a result, later cycles returned an average of five new candidate variants per 1,000 cases. The average time between new knowledge becoming publicly available and a new diagnosis identified was 32 days, with the shortest being 1 day. Computational and resource requirements

Talos is designed to run on a single machine or virtual machine and is readily deployable on both local high-performance computing (HPC) clusters and cloud environments. All analyses described in this study were performed on a single virtual machine with 16 cores and 64 GB of memory. On Google Cloud Platform, the one-time cost of running the variant annotation workflow for 1,000 genomes from single-sample variant call formats (VCFs) was $11.25 USD (Extended Data Table 5). Running the variant filtering and prioritization workflow on the same cohort cost $0.21 AUD per run, equivalent to $1.65 USD annually if reanalysis were performed monthly. Discussion

We introduce Talos, a publicly available tool for automated reanalysis of genomic data in research and clinical settings. Given that clinical and laboratory resources are the principal barrier to systematic reanalysis at scale16, we optimized Talos for high specificity to increase scalability, acceptability and ease of adoption and conducted performance evaluation in two independent validation cohorts, totaling 1,089 probands. When deployed in a large, unselected cohort of previously undiagnosed individuals with rare disease, Talos identified new diagnoses in 5.1% while returning a median of 1.2 candidate variants per family on initial analysis. This 5.1% yield is cohort specific and will vary across settings depending on case mix, prior workup and time since initial analysis. Variant burden decreased markedly on iterative cycles, addressing sustainability concerns while shortening the time between new information becoming publicly available and diagnosis. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of delivering frequent, systematic reanalysis at scale.

Consistent with previous manual reanalysis studies5,9, just over half of new diagnoses were due to new information about gene−disease and variant−disease relationships, highlighting the critical importance of rapidly and openly sharing knowledge in improving diagnostic outcomes30. Integrating new knowledge into automated pipelines requires careful balancing between quality of evidence against sensitivity and timeliness. We leveraged the PanelApp Australia platform, as the monthly frequency of updates11 supports rapid incorporation of emerging gene−disease relationships, with the median time from publication of new information to diagnosis in this cohort being just 32 days.

The structured curation of evidence for gene−disease associations, including the use of standardized terms for mode of inheritance (MOI), further refined variant filtering, while automating virtual panel assignment to support phenotype matching further decreased variant burden.

The use of ClinVar similarly poses challenges relating to quality of evidence and the dynamic nature of the database, with thousands of new entries added each year. Although efforts are underway to curate content and resolve discrepant ratings between submitters and ClinGen expert panels31,32, we elected to use the star rating system as a threshold that denotes entries with sufficient evidence to trigger manual review. Our ClinvArbitration module further downweights entries from outlier submitters and prioritizes variants where the balance of evidence had shifted toward pathogenicity over time. The module can be deployed independently—for example, to support the periodic reevaluation of variants identified through targeted sequencing, including in cancer predisposition testing, where the clinical implications of variant reclassification can be profound33,34.

Notably, just under half of the diagnoses identified by Talos were potentially identifiable during the original analysis, highlighting the importance of cohort-level reanalysis in quality assurance and continuous improvement. Genomic analysis approaches continue to evolve. Overly restrictive clinical and laboratory practices based on virtual panels, quality metrics, definition of target regions and inheritance filters have repeatedly been identified as sources of missed diagnoses35–39. Furthermore, the integration of analysis for additional variant types, in particular CNVs and STRs, as part of clinically accredited pipelines is increasing40,41. Automated reanalysis of stored data from historical cohorts thus allows patients and research participants to benefit from current best-practice analysis methods, exemplified here by the large number of new diagnoses identified by CNV analysis on exome data.

With scalable, systematic reanalysis now technically feasible, questions of policy, professional practice guidance and funding models must be addressed. Although reanalysis is supported at a policy level13–15, clinical and laboratory practice based on manual models is highly variable, occurring at low volumes and resulting in inequity of access16,17. Automated models offer a consistent and efficient process to identify a large proportion of additional diagnoses over time17. The process can be communicated clearly to clinicians and, in turn, to patients and families, setting expectations, including as part of consent and result return discussions. Although many consent forms and supporting documents refer to result reinterpretation over time16,42, clinicians, laboratories and families would benefit from the clarity on timing, initiation and process of reanalysis that an automated, cohort-based approach brings43.

Although the automated model described here scales, simplifies and standardizes reanalysis17, skilled manual review and iterative communication between laboratories and referring clinicians44 remain central to responsible implementation16. The performance evaluation against manual analysis and benchmarking against other automated analysis tools presented here will aid development of evidence-based professional guidance on reanalysis. Automated and manual approaches should be considered complementary: automated pipelines such as Talos enable systematic, frequent, low-burden reanalysis across large cohorts, whereas manual reanalysis remains essential in specific contexts such as evolving clinical features and needs.

Beyond clinical considerations, automated reanalysis models raise implementation questions about infrastructure and bioinformatics capability. Cohort-based models rely on economies of scale to optimize benefit. However, legal and logistical barriers may prevent centralized data aggregation, limiting feasibility in some jurisdictions. This may disadvantage underresourced laboratories and worsen inequitable access. Health economic evaluations will be critical to inform funding models and policy decisions.

We recognize several important limitations to our approach. Iterative reanalysis was performed only on individuals who remained undiagnosed or partially diagnosed after their initial test. A small but significant number of individuals who initially receive a diagnosis have the diagnosis retracted over time as gene−disease and variant−disease associations are refuted. Automated reanalysis pipelines can potentially have an important role to play in ensuring accuracy of diagnoses over time, including identification of new information that results in variant downgrades. At present, Talos reappraises genomic data using only the clinical information provided at the time of the original test request. However, clinical features can evolve, and automating updates to clinical information—for example, by directly mining electronic medical records—may improve performance. Other areas that may benefit from further automation include direct literature mining for new information associating variants and genes to disease45.

Although Talos is currently optimized for diagnostic use, it can be modified to support discovery research—for example, by using lists of candidate genes to identify additional cases to support novel and emerging gene−disease associations. Deployment in additional settings that require scalability, such as primary diagnostic analysis and population screening, will be important to assess wider user needs and operational performance. Talos is also compatible with long-read sequencing data provided in VCF format, offering a further avenue for future application.

In summary, we demonstrate the feasibility of iterative automated reanalysis in rare disease, scaled to thousands of datasets, and provide an open-source tool to enable broad adoption. Our work forms a solid foundation for the integration of machine learning and artificial intelligence approaches and has potential for broad applicability to support timely and equitable genomic care.

Talos variant annotation workflow

This is a lightweight workflow designed for rapid and efficient annotation of variants from large cohorts. It is intended to be run once per cohort and label each variant with predicted transcript consequence and annotations from sources that do not evolve rapidly.

Transcript consequence annotations are provided by BCFtools/csq for SNV/indels and SVAnnotate for CNV/SVs using Ensembl v.133 gene models. Each variant is labeled with observed population allele frequencies from gnomAD v.4 using echtvar. The workflow outputs all variants in Hail MatrixTable format for efficient processing in the variant prioritization workflow.

Talos variant logic modules

Talos variant logic modules each implement a specific rule-based decision tree representing a distinct set of evidence that indicates that a variant should be considered as potentially causative. Modules are applied to each variant independently. Variants that meet a moduleʼs criteria are annotated with the corresponding label. Module labels are not mutually exclusive; variants may carry multiple labels. Variants that do not match any module are excluded from downstream analyses.

The set of variant logic modules applied in a given analysis is configurable. Each module can be assigned either ‘primary’ or ‘supporting’ significance within the run configuration file. Variants annotated by a primary module are eligible to be reported as candidates in both dominant and recessive disease genes. By contrast, variants annotated only by supporting modules can contribute to diagnoses in recessive genes but only as a second hit alongside a variant with primary-level evidence. Homozygous variants annotated only with supporting labels are excluded from analysis.

Talos phenotype matching

Talos uses patient-supplied HPO terms to highlight variants whose genes are plausibly related to the observed phenotype. By default, Talos tests whether any patient HPO term is similar to the HPO terms associated with a gene or with a disease-specific gene panel, and it flags such genes as phenotype matched in the report. This signal is advisory by default. Talos does not exclude variants on the basis of phenotype but surfaces potential matches to draw attention for review. Users may optionally enable a strict phenotype filter that retains only variants in phenotype-matched genes or panels, which increases specificity and reduces reviewer burden. The sensitivity tradeoff for this mode will depend on the completeness and accuracy of patient phenotyping and on how comprehensively HPO terms capture the phenotypic scope of each gene.

Talos uses three strategies to identify genes associated with phenotypes observed in a specific patient. Each strategy is intentionally inclusive, such that if one or more of a patient’s phenotypes is matched to a given gene, then that gene is considered a ‘phenotype match’ for the family.

Patient phenotype to gene matching

Direct fuzzy matching of patient HPO terms with the HPO associated with a specific gene is performed using the Semsimian48 tool to calculate pairwise semantic similarity scores. If any pair of HPO terms has an Ancestor Information Content score above the configurable threshold (default = 14), the gene is considered a phenotype match for the patient.

Patient phenotype to gene panel matching

PanelApp Australia contains 261 gene panels, each related to a specific disease or group of disorders. These panels have been annotated with one or more high-level HPO terms representative of the group of disorders. For each gene panel, if one or more panel HPO terms either match or are ancestors of a patient HPO term, then the patient is considered a phenotype match for that panel and all of the genes in that panel.

Cohort to gene panel matching

If the cohort being analyzed consists of patients of a specific disease group (for example, mitochondrial disorders), one or more gene panels can be applied as a ‘phenotype match’ to all families.

Allele frequency filtering

The Talos variant prioritization workflow filters variants based on their observed allele counts or frequencies. Thresholds are configurable and tailored to the expected MOI. Two sources of allele frequency are used in filtering: population allele frequencies from gnomAD v.4 and cohort allele frequencies calculated from the dataset under analysis. When the cohort is sufficiently large, cohort-specific frequencies are useful for removing common pipeline-specific artifacts that may not be present in gnomAD. To support more permissive filtering, separate thresholds can be specified for variants previously reported in ClinVar as P/LP.

ClinVar summarization using ClinvArbitration

The ClinVar database maintains an aggregate classification score for each variant based on the classifications provided by each submitter. However, due to the consensus-based logic used to determine the aggregate classification, many variants with a clear majority of P/LP submissions are labeled as ‘Conflicting classifications of pathogenicity’ due to a minority of ‘Uncertain’ submissions. To generate ClinVar variant aggregate classifications better suited to the needs of automated variant prioritization, we developed an alternative aggregation approach implemented in a tool called ClinvArbitration.

The ClinvArbitration algorithm uses a modified ‘majority wins’ strategy to resolve conflicts. Where more recent submissions exist, ClinvArbitration will also disregard ClinVar submissions made before the widespread use of modern ACMG variant curation guidelines53.

ClinvArbitration allows for variant submissions from specific submitters to be excluded during processing. To prevent circular logic during the evaluation of Talos, this feature was used to exclude any ClinVar submissions made by the laboratories that analyzed the evaluation cohorts in this study. Specifically, the submitters ‘Broad Center for Mendelian Genomics (Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard), Broad CMG’ and ‘Laboratory for Molecular Medicine; Mass General Brigham Personalized Medicine’ were excluded from ClinvArbitration summaries used to analyze the RGP cohort, and the submitter ‘Victorian Clinical Genetics Services, Murdoch Children’s Research Institute’ was excluded from ClinvArbitration summaries used to analyze all other cohorts.

ClinvArbitration is run for each monthly release of ClinVar. The open-source software and monthly data releases are available from https://github.com/populationgenomics/ClinvArbitration.

Variant calling

SNV calling was performed using GATK variant calling best-practices workflows. For the RGP cohort, genome sequencing data were processed through a pipeline based on Picard, using base quality score recalibration and local realignment at known indels. The BWA aligner54 was used for mapping reads to the human genome build 38. SNVs and indels are jointly called across all samples using the GATK HaplotypeCaller package55 v.4.0. Default filters were applied to SNV and indel calls using the GATK Variant Quality Score Recalibration (VQSR) approach. Genome sequencing data were additionally processed to detect SVs using GATK-SV52.

All remaining cohorts were reprocessed from FASTQs by the Center for Population Genomics using DRAGMAP v.1.3.0 and GATK v.4.2.6.1. SV calling of genomes was performed using the GATK-SV structural variation discovery pipeline v.0.28.4. CNV calling in exomes was performed using GATK-gCNV v.4.5.0.0 (ref. 56).

Exomiser comparison

We benchmarked Talos against Exomiser (v.14, default configuration) on the same ACG trios with known SNV or indel diagnoses. Success for Talos was defined as the causative variant being returned under Talos default settings. Success for Exomiser was defined as the causative variant ranking within the top 1, top 5 or top 10 variants per family, plus an unconstrained ‘all’ condition. Because outcomes are paired within the same families, we compared tools using McNemarʼs exact test applied to the discordant pairs (Talos-only versus Exomiser-only) at each threshold, reporting two-sided P values. This test is appropriate for paired binary data and conditions on the total number of discordant outcomes, which controls for case-level heterogeneity and removes ties where both tools either succeed or fail. We used the exact version because the number of discordant pairs was modest. Tests that assume independent samples, such as two-sample proportion tests or Fisherʼs exact test, are not suitable for these paired comparisons.

Talos outputs

Due to the diverse workflows used for manual review and curation of variants across laboratories, the primary output of Talos is a single machine-readable file in JSON format. The output file contains a list of all variants meeting the criteria to be considered potentially causative, a summary of the evidence supporting each variant and metadata describing the configuration used to perform the analysis. The JSON output file can be transformed into a format suitable for integration into the curation workflow of a specific laboratory. The default Talos workflow contains a JSON to HTML report generator script as a reference implementation.

Variant confirmation and reporting

Variants found by the reanalysis but not identifiable by the original clinical laboratory using currently clinically accredited tools on existing short-read data were orthogonally confirmed by a range of methods. CNVs were generally confirmed by high-resolution chromosome single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarray (hrCMA, GDA-Cyto v.1; Illumina), if at least three informative probes were available. CNVs not identifiable by hrCMA and other complex variants (for example, inversions) were confirmed using long-read adaptive sampling on a Promethion 24 sequencer using R10.4.1 flow cells and V14 ligation and sequencing chemistry (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). In one case, insufficient remaining DNA necessitated long-range polymerase chain reaction (PCR). One reciprocal translocation was confirmed using clinical G-banded chromosome metaphase karyotyping.

All variants identified as being likely diagnostic through manual review of Talos output, including those undergoing orthogonal validation, were forwarded to the relevant clinical laboratory for assessment. Variants were assessed using clinically accredited processes for variant classification and reporting, based on ACMG guidelines, and reported to the relevant clinician where a new diagnosis was identified.

Statistics and reproducibility

This study evaluated the performance of an automated reanalysis tool in pre-existing rare disease cohorts. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Cohort sizes reflect the full set of eligible individuals available from each contributing study: the two validation cohorts (ACG, n = 401 probands; RGP, n = 688 families) comprised all individuals from the respective studies with genome or exome sequencing data of sufficient quality for reprocessing through the standardized pipeline; the prospective reanalysis cohort (n = 4,735) comprised all undiagnosed or partially diagnosed individuals meeting the inclusion criteria above. No data were excluded from the analyses; individuals harboring diagnostic variants in classes not reliably detected by the standardized pipeline (for example, STR expansions, low-level mosaic variants and retrotransposon insertions) were retained and are reported as missed diagnoses were applicable. The experiments were not randomized; the study used a benchmarking design in observational cohorts rather than a randomized comparison. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment; for the validation cohorts, prior diagnoses were known at the time of evaluation to enable benchmarking against manual analysis, although Talos itself is a deterministic, automated pipeline whose output is generated without investigator input. To support reproducibility, Talos is open-source and packaged within a fixed software environment (Docker, Nextflow), with all configuration files used in this study provided with the code (https://github.com/populationgenomics/talos). External annotation sources (PanelApp Australia, ClinVar and gnomAD) are versioned at runtime, enabling exact reproduction of any individual reanalysis cycle.
