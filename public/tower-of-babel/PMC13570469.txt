The Rintala Advanced Modification (RAM) Flap: A Novel Technique for Off‐Centre Nasal Reconstruction
Authors: Ramy Awadalla, Derek Davies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe traditional Rintala flap is associated with visible forehead scarring and glabellar deformities. We describe the Rintala Advanced Modification (RAM) flap, a novel technique that restricts the flap below the eyebrow line and employs asymmetric Burow's triangles for off‐centre defects. Based on 20 patients with nasal basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas, the RAM flap demonstrates superior aesthetic outcomes with no glabellar complications or flap tip necrosis.

Introduction

Nasal reconstruction following excision of non‐melanoma skin cancers presents significant challenges for dermatologic surgeons. Basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) and squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) commonly occur on the nasal dorsum, requiring reconstructive techniques that balance oncological safety with optimal aesthetic outcomes [1]. The Rintala flap, first described in 1969, has been an option for midline nasal defect reconstruction, utilising glabellar and forehead skin laxity to provide an excellent tissue match [2].

However, the traditional Rintala flap has significant limitations. The flap often extends above the eyebrow line onto the forehead, creating conspicuous horizontal scarring [3]. Advancement of the flap can lead to glabellar hump from tissue bunching, glabellar drop from flap ptosis, eyebrow distortion and, most importantly, risk of flap tip necrosis [3]. These complications frequently impact patient satisfaction and may require secondary revision procedures [3].

Previous modifications have described shorter advancement distances and periosteal stay sutures to mitigate some of these issues [4]. We developed the Rintala Advanced Modification (RAM) flap to address these limitations. This technique incorporates strategic design modifications that eliminate possible complications while expanding the range of defects suitable for reconstruction [5]. We present our technique and clinical experience with 20 consecutive patients (Table 1).

Main Text

Patient Series

Between April 2024 and September 2025, we treated 20 consecutive patients with histologically confirmed BCCs (n = 16) and SCCs (n = 4) on the supra‐tip and nasal tip anatomical subunits. All lesions were excised with appropriate surgical margins, and resulting defects were reconstructed using the RAM flap technique. Patient ages ranged from 55 to 83 years (mean 67 years), with 12 males and 8 females. Defects ranged from 8 to 20 mm in diameter. Ten defects were positioned midline (0°–5° from the nasal centreline) and the other ten defects were off‐centre (10°–20° from centreline).

It is important to note that our series of RAM flap repairs successfully addressed defects located across multiple anatomical subunits, including the nasal supra‐tip and nasal tip.

Surgical Technique

Step 1: Defect Assessment

The defect is carefully measured, and its position relative to the nasal midline is determined. The RAM flap is suitable for defects up to 15 mm diameter for female patients and up to 20 mm diameter for male patients located within 20° of the nasal centreline (Figure 1). The angle from the centreline determines whether symmetric or asymmetric Burow's triangles will be designed.

RAM flap design demonstrating gender‐specific defect size limits (females 8–15 mm; males 8–20 mm) and strict limitation of the flap below the inferior eyebrow line.

Step 2: Flap Design

A rectangular advancement flap is designed from the glabellar region, extending inferiorly to encompass the defect. The critical design principle is that the superior border of the flap is marked strictly below the inferior line of the eyebrows, ensuring no incisions extend onto the forehead. The flap length is typically 1.5 to 2.5 times the defect diameter.

For midline defects (0°–5° from centreline), symmetric Burow's triangles of equal size are designed on either side of the flap base, positioned below the eyebrows. For off‐centre defects (5°–20° from centreline), asymmetric Burow's triangles are designed. Importantly, the larger Burow's triangle is positioned on the side contralateral to the defect (Figures 2 and 3). This counterintuitive design compensates for the lateral vector of tissue movement as the flap advances toward the off‐centre defect, creating balanced closure that maintains glabellar symmetry and prevents eyebrow distortion [6] (demonstrated in Figures 4 and 5).

Step 3: Flap Elevation

The flap is elevated in the supra‐periosteal plane, which provides robust blood supply mainly from bilateral supratrochlear arteries and possibly from dorsal nasal arteries depending on the level of the superior border of the flap, which minimises ischemia and flap tip necrosis risks [2]. Care must be taken to preserve the supratrochlear vessels superiorly, which provide primary blood supply to the flap [7]. The glabellar region is widely undermined to allow tension‐free advancement. If additional length is needed, transverse relaxation incisions can be made in the galea aponeurosis, although this is rarely necessary with proper design.

Step 4: Flap Advancement and Fixation

The flap is advanced into the defect, and the Burow's triangles are excised. Deep sutures are placed to secure the flap to the nasal periosteum—this fixation is mandatory and represents a key technical innovation. Multiple 4–0 absorbable sutures are placed at the level of the nasal periosteum for centre defects and upper lateral cartilages for off‐centre defects to prevent flap ptosis and maintain glabellar contour. This deep fixation provides stable, long‐lasting results that resist gravitational sagging.

Step 5: Layered Closure

The deep dermis is approximated with 5–0 absorbable sutures to reduce tension on the skin closure. The skin is closed with 5–0/6–0 non‐absorbable sutures using a simple interrupted technique. The Burow's triangle sites are closed with particular attention to achieving precise skin edge alignment to minimise scarring. Sutures are typically removed at 7–10 days.

Key Technical Modifications

The RAM flap differs from the traditional Rintala flap and its previously described modifications in three fundamental ways. First, while shorter advancement distances have been proposed [4], the RAM flap's strict limitation of the flap below the inferior eyebrow line is a core, non‐negotiable design principle that completely eliminates visible forehead scarring and enhances flap survival by providing a robust blood supply from the supratrochlear arteries. By keeping all incisions within the glabellar region, scars are hidden in natural creases and shadows [7].

Second, the strategic use of asymmetric Burow's triangles for off‐centre defects is a novel concept not previously described [4] that expands the technique's applicability. Traditional Rintala flaps use symmetric triangles, which work well for midline defects but create unequal tension distribution for lateral defects, causing eyebrow distortion and glabellar asymmetry [2]. In the RAM flap, positioning the larger triangle contralateral to the defect balances tissue movement and maintains facial symmetry [6].

Third, while stay sutures have been described [4], the RAM flap's mandatory deep periosteal fixation is a cornerstone of the technique that prevents glabellar hump and drop complications. Unlike a single stay suture, the RAM flap employs multiple anchoring sutures at both the nasal periosteum and upper lateral cartilages, providing a multi‐point fixation system that prevents downward gravitational forces that could otherwise translate into alar distortion [3].

Clinical Outcomes

All 20 patients achieved successful reconstruction with no flap necrosis or partial flap loss. One patient (5%) developed minor wound dehiscence at a Burow's triangle closure site, which healed by secondary intention without requiring revision. No patients developed glabellar hump or glabellar drop during follow‐up (range 3–18 months, mean 9 months). All patients reported satisfaction with aesthetic outcomes, and no patient requested secondary revision surgery.

At follow‐up, scars were well‐healed and inconspicuous, hidden within natural creases below the eyebrows. Eyebrow position remained symmetric in all cases. No patients developed eyelid ptosis, medial canthal webbing, or asymmetric eyebrow position at the Burow's triangle closure sites. Furthermore, no cases of permanent nasal tip elevation or alar rim notching were observed across the full range of defect sizes (8–20 mm) and lateralities (0°–20°). Transient, mild nasal tip elevation was noted in three cases with larger midline defects (Figures 6 and 7), which fully resolved upon suture removal at Days 7–10.

We consider an intraoperative acute nasal tip elevation of 3–6 mm for female patients and 4–9 mm for male patients to be acceptable. This acceptable range is highly dependent on the individual patient's preoperative nasolabial angle (the angle between the philtrum and the nasal tip) and overall nose size. In our experience, elevation within these parameters fully resolves upon suture removal at Days 7–10, with the nasal tip returning to a cosmetically satisfying, normal anatomical position as the tissues relax (demonstrated in Figures 6 and 7).

RAM Flap Technical Considerations

Regarding the potential for periorbital complications from asymmetric Burow's triangles, no eyelid ptosis or medial canthal webbing was observed in our series. The supra‐periosteal dissection plane and incision placement well medial to the canthal structures, combined with mandatory deep periosteal fixation, appear to mitigate this risk. Nonetheless, this remains an important consideration, particularly for defects with high degrees of laterality, and warrants ongoing surveillance.

The potential concern of alar notching, this is particularly relevant for off‐centre defects, where the oblique vector of advancement places asymmetric forces on the alar rim; Therefore, the asymmetrical burrows triangles are playing great role to even the tension on both sides and achieve tension‐relieving mechanisms, distributing closure tension along the lateral incision lines rather than concentrating it at the distal flap tip(demonstrated in Figures 4 and 5).

Indications and Contraindications

The RAM flap is ideally suited for the supra‐tip and nasal tip anatomical subunits defects following BCC or SCC excision, with defect sizes 8–15 mm diameter for females and 8–20 mm for males, within 20° of the nasal centreline, in patients with adequate glabellar and forehead skin laxity who prioritise aesthetic outcomes. For defects approaching the maximum recommended size (15 mm ♀‐ 20 mm ♂), patients should be explicitly consented for the potential risk of alar margin distortion, including nasal tip elevation or alar rim notching, although this complication was not observed in our series.

Contraindications include defects larger than 20 mm diameter (consider paramedian forehead flap or bilateral myocutaneous Island Pedicle Flap) [8], defects beyond 20° from centreline (consider nasolabial or cheek advancement flaps), columella defects (may require axial blood supply modifications [4]), tight forehead skin and previous forehead surgery with scarring.

Comparison With Alternative Techniques

For small defects (less than 8 mm), primary closure or rhombic flaps may be preferable. For very large defects, the paramedian forehead flap remains the gold standard [8]. The RAM flap occupies a specific niche for intermediate‐sized defects (8–20 mm) on the nasal dorsum, offering advantages over primary closure (which may cause excessive tension) and forehead flaps (which require two stages and create forehead scars) [8].

Compared to bilobed or nasolabial flaps; despite the bilobed flap's use of adjacent same texture nasal skin, it is limited by tissue availability for larger defects and carries a recognised high risk of pin‐cushioning deformity [9]. The nasolabial flap often utilises thicker cheek skin with a different texture and thickness [10]. The RAM flap advances glabellar skin and upper nasal dorsum skin, which share similar sebaceous density, thickness and texture with the lower nasal dorsum and nasal tip skin, providing an excellent match and a reliable, single‐stage repair with excellent aesthetic integration [5].

The bilateral myocutaneous island pedicle flap is another excellent alternative for these defects. It offers a robust musculocutaneous blood supply, an identical tissue match (utilising adjacent dorsal skin), and no nasal tip elevation when executed. Each flap carries its own distinct risk profile: the RAM flap is associated with transient nasal tip elevation that fully resolves following suture removal, while the nasalis myocutaneous island pedicle flap carries a minor risk of pin‐cushioning, which is significantly lower than that associated with the standard subcutaneous island pedicle flap [11]. The choice between these two techniques should therefore be guided by defect location, size and the individual patient's anatomy, as both represent reliable and cosmetically acceptable reconstructive options for this anatomical region.

Conclusion

The RAM flap is a novel surgical technique offering significant advantages over the traditional Rintala flap for nasal reconstruction following non‐melanoma skin cancer excision [2]. The key innovations—strict limitation below the inferior eyebrow line, strategic use of asymmetric Burow's triangles for off‐centre defects, and mandatory periosteal fixation—result in superior aesthetic outcomes with minimal complications. Based on our experience with 20 patients, the RAM flap is safe, reliable and versatile for reconstructing the supra‐tip and nasal tip anatomical subunits defects up to 15–20 mm diameter within 20° of the nasal centreline. By eliminating visible forehead scarring and preventing glabellar hump or drop and without the risk of flap tip necrosis, the RAM flap achieves excellent patient satisfaction and represents a valuable addition to the dermatologic surgeon's armamentarium for managing nasal skin cancers [1].
