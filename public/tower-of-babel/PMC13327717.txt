Impact of an Intensive Lifestyle Camp on Anthropometry and Quality of Life in Vulnerable Children: A 3-Year Follow-Up of a Nonrandomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Dorthe Dalstrup Pauls, Loa Clausen, Lea Brader, Kajsa Järvholm, Henrik Støvring, Jens Meldgaard Bruun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe prevalence of overweight and obesity in children and adolescents continues to rise, and there is a lack of long-term follow-up to clarify health and well-being trajectories into adolescence and adulthood. This study investigated anthropometry and quality of life (QoL) trajectories in 7–14-year-old vulnerable children 3 years after attending a lifestyle camp.MethodsVulnerable children referred to a 10-week multicomponent lifestyle camp due to overweight or obesity, and/or psychosocial issues were enrolled. Anthropometry was assessed using bioelectric impedance and QoL using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory. Mixed-effects models were performed to assess longitudinal changes in anthropometry and QoL and explore if improvements achieved during the lifestyle intervention predicted long-term outcomes.ResultsIn total, 222 participants (12.3 ± 1.4 years) were included, of which 126 (56%) participated in the 3-year follow-up (15.4 ± 1.3 years). Of those, 91 (72%) had anthropometry assessed, and the majority (83%) of participants were still living with overweight/obesity. Body mass index standard deviation score (BMI-SDS) was lower at 3 years (2.00 standard deviation [SD] ± 1.1) compared to baseline (2.48 SD ± 0.9). QoL remained higher at the 3-year follow-up compared to before the intervention. Compared to end-of-treatment, girls had an increase in BMI-SDS (0.37 SD [95% CI 0.26; 0.48]) and body fat (5.62% [95% CI 4.36; 6.88]) at the 3-year follow-up, while boys had a decrease in body fat (−2.25% [95% CI –3.87; −0.62]) but no change in BMI-SDS. Overall, QoL decreased (total QoL −8.33 [95% CI –10.73; −5.94]) during the post-intervention phase.ConclusionThe lifestyle camp, a child-centered intensive short-term multicomponent intervention focused on well-being, peer interaction, and social belonging, may be a promising treatment strategy for improving health and well-being long-term in vulnerable children, although the improvements attenuate across time. Future research should explore the potential to rethink treatment strategies and investigate whether continuous long-term support may help sustain improvements further throughout adolescence.

Introduction

The transition from childhood to adolescence, and adolescence itself, is a vulnerable period and a critical time for shaping behavioral strategies, social identity, health status, and well-being in adulthood [1]. It is well established that children and adolescents with overweight and obesity more often experience lower self-esteem, bullying, stigmatization, loneliness, and lower quality of life (QoL) compared to peers with normal weight [2–4]. The causal direction between psychological challenges and overweight and obesity is still uncertain [4]. Large population-based studies demonstrate that the association between overweight/obesity and reduced QoL emerges around 8–9 years of age and strengthens throughout adolescence [5, 6], emphasizing the importance of preventive action and effective strategies to ensure a healthy weight development and the overall well-being of children and adolescents.

Despite multisectoral efforts, the prevalence of overweight and obesity continues to rise [7–9]. If current trends continue, by 2035, two in five 5–18-year-olds will be living with overweight or obesity [10], increasing the risk of impaired QoL and the development of noncommunicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes and coronary heart disease in adulthood [11–13].

Outpatient multicomponent lifestyle interventions are the recommended first-line treatment for children and adolescents with overweight and obesity [14]. In general, lifestyle interventions vary greatly in duration, type, and setting, with evidence suggesting that interventions with higher contact hours (≥26 h) and those that include physical activity sessions as part of the intervention show larger effects on body mass index (BMI) [15, 16]. Few longitudinal studies have demonstrated sustained, long-term (3–8 years) improvements in BMI standard deviation score (BMI-SDS), health behaviors, and QoL following lifestyle interventions [17–20] while randomized controlled trials are limited by a lack of long-term follow-up beyond 2 years [21, 22].

The effect of short lifestyle camps (2–6 weeks) to promote health and well-being in children and adolescents with overweight and obesity has been explored in a few studies, showing short-term improvements in BMI, fat mass, blood pressure, cardiovascular and physical fitness, depressive symptoms, and self-esteem [23–27]. A structured 3-week camp including nutritional training and physical exercise was more successful in treatment of overweight and obesity in 7–12-year-olds compared to a conventional outpatient hospital program, resulting in a 2 month longer maintenance of weight loss and greater improvements in dietary intake and physical activity [28]. However, to accurately evaluate the impact of lifestyle interventions, more long-term follow-up studies are needed to clarify health and well-being trajectories into adolescence and adulthood.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate post-intervention trajectories related to anthropometry and QoL in 7–14-year-old vulnerable children, predominantly with overweight and obesity, 3 years after attending a 10-week multicomponent lifestyle camp [29, 30]. The secondary aim was to assess whether improvements achieved during the lifestyle intervention predicted long-term anthropometric and QoL outcomes.

We hypothesize that participants, compared to end-of-treatment, will show increased body weight, height, BMI-SDS, fat mass, and fat-free mass 3 years post-intervention, partly due to growth and a diminished intervention effect. However, BMI-SDS and QoL are not expected to return to pre-intervention levels. Furthermore, greater initial improvements in BMI-SDS, fat mass, and QoL during the intervention are hypothesized to predict more favorable outcomes at the 3-year follow-up.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The present study is a 3-year follow-up of a non-randomized clinical trial. The clinical trial investigated the effect of a higher protein diet in children with overweight and obesity attending a 10-week multicomponent lifestyle intervention. Moreover, the trial aimed to explore overall short- and long-term changes in anthropometry, QoL, eating behavior traits, disordered eating behaviors, and dietary intake. Changes over 3 years in eating behavior traits, disordered eating behaviors, and dietary intake will be reported in future publications. The study design and 1-year outcomes were reported in detail earlier [29, 30]. In short, changes in anthropometry were similar between groups (higher protein or not) after the intervention why participants were pooled for the present analyses.

Participants were 7–14-year-olds referred to the camps by their general practitioner due to overweight or obesity and/or psychosocial issues such as loneliness, unhappiness, and/or social and family-related problems. Each child’s referral (application) was evaluated independently by the organization running the camps, with poor well-being being the primary indication for acceptance. As reported in our previous paper, the majority of children referred to the camp had overweight or obesity [29]. However, this was not a requirement for referral, and participants were included regardless of pre-intervention weight status. For the present study, exclusion criteria were having an eating disorder, requiring a special diet, having a disease, e.g., diabetes or liver diseases, or not completing at least 7 weeks of camp.

The camps were free of charge and ongoing for ten consecutive weeks. All participants attended school at camp 2 to 3 h each day. The camp staff were social workers specialized in childcare, initiating physical and social activities. Educated kitchen staff prepared meals aligning to the Danish dietary guidelines [31] and educated children about healthy meals. Participants went home over the weekends. Parents were invited to four family educational days – one before, two during, and one after the camp – to gain insight into camp life and participate in social and health-related activities with their children. After the camp, participants returned home with support provided by healthcare professionals from the home municipality, if needed. Support from healthcare professionals could include regular meetings to monitor anthropometry, discuss dietary or physical activity habits, or assist participants in initiating leisure activities. As part of the present study, participants returned to the camp for a 1- and 3-year follow-up assessment.

Measurements

Anthropometry

Camp staff collected anthropometry during the first and last week of lifestyle camp and at the 1- and 3-year follow-up. Due to the daily routines at the camp and the scheduling of individual meetings with each family for the 1- and 3-year follow-ups, anthropometry could not be measured at a consistent time of day. Camp staff were instructed to measure body weight (kg), body fat (%), and skeletal muscle mass (kg) in all participants wearing light clothing and no shoes using bioelectric impedance (InBody model 270, Hopkins Medical Products, Grand Rapids, MI, USA). Height (m) was measured using a fixed wall measuring tape. The primary outcome, BMI-SDS, was calculated using World Health Organization AnthroPlus software, and a BMI-SDS ≤1 standard deviation (SD) was defined as normal weight, >1 SD as overweight, and >2 SD as obesity [32].

Questionnaires

Participants and their parents filled in questionnaires before camp, immediately after camp, and at the 1- and 3-year follow-up via the REDCap.org database hosted at Aarhus University. Background characteristics, including participant sex and age, parental education, household income, diseases, and current daily occupation, were collected before camp and/or at the 3-year follow-up assessment, with parents being the main respondent.

QoL was measured using the Danish version of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0 (PedsQL 4.0) [33]. The PedsQL consists of 23 items answered on a five-point Likert scale. Responses are summed up to a total QoL score, and subscales for physical, emotional, social, and school functioning. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a higher QoL. Participants were the main respondents of the PedsQL but were encouraged to complete it with assistance from their parents if needed. The internal reliability of the PedsQL including all subscales were within acceptable range or above, across all time points (Cronbach’s alpha (α) = 0.69–0.91). Reliability was lowest for the subscale school functioning (α = 0.69–0.79) (online suppl. S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552702).

Statistical Analysis

In descriptive analyses, continuous data are presented as mean ± SD and as absolute numbers and percentages (%) for categorical data. Unadjusted mixed-effects models were performed to investigate mean changes in continuous outcomes. The mixed-effects models included time as a fixed effect and a random effect for each participant. Due to lack of studies exploring sex differences in response to lifestyle interventions [34] and considering the differences in change in body composition between girls and boys during this 3-year period due to puberty, sex-stratified analyses were performed. Moreover, age-stratified analyses (<12 vs. ≥12 years) were performed for anthropometric measures. Changes from 10 weeks to 3 years were reported as mean changes with 95% CI. A sensitivity analysis, including only participants with complete data, was performed to investigate the robustness of the primary outcome (i.e., BMI-SDS) using similar analysis.

A mixed-effects model was also used to examine the association between changes in BMI-SDS, body fat, and QoL, respectively, during camp and long-term outcomes, including an interaction between time and changes. A random effect for each participant was included to account for repeated measurements. Average marginal effects were computed to estimate the expected outcome at the 1- and 3-year follow-up. In all mixed-effect models, available data were used without imputation.

Dropout analyses were conducted using t-tests to examine whether children lost-to-follow-up at 3 years differed from those who remained in the study with respect to baseline characteristics. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, USA) with a p value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

The clinical trial was preregistered at clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT04522921). The longitudinal part of this study was preregistered at OSF Registries (www.osf.io), registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/MPE4Z. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as online supplementary material.

Results

The study included 222 participants (12.3 ± 1.4 years) at baseline, and 126 (56%) participated in the 3-year follow-up (15.4 ± 1.3 years) of whom 124 (98%) answered the PedsQL, and 91 (72%) returned to the camp for assessment of anthropometry (shown in Fig. 1). Slightly more girls than boys participated at each time point (56% girls at 3 years), and throughout the study, the majority of participants (83% at 3 years) were living with overweight or obesity. Most participants (83%) were in their final year of primary school during the 3-year follow-up (Table 1) (online suppl. S2).

Anthropometry

Girls had a significant increase in BMI-SDS (0.37 SD [95% CI 0.26; 0.48]) and body fat (5.62% [95% CI 4.36; 6.88]) at 3 years compared to end-of-treatment, whereas boys had a significant decrease in body fat (−2.25% [95% CI –3.87; −0.62]) but no significant change in BMI-SDS (shown in Fig. 2) (online suppl. S4+S5). Among girls, changes were similar across age groups (<12 vs. ≥12 years). For the entire sample, BMI-SDS was lower at the 3-year follow-up (2.00 SD ± 1.1) compared to baseline (2.48 SD ± 0.9) (shown in Table 2), and changes were similar across age groups (<12 vs. ≥12 years). Sensitivity analysis, including only participants with complete data, showed similar results (online suppl. S3). Overall, 48% sustained a BMI-SDS reduction of >0.25 SD at the 3-year follow-up compared to before camp. Anthropometry and quality of life trajectories following lifestyle camp

Quality of Life

Participants had a decrease in total QoL (−8.33 [95% CI −10.73; −5.94]) and all QoL subscales at the 3-year follow-up compared to end-of-treatment, and the sex-stratified analyses were similar. Girls reported greatest reduction in school functioning (−16.09 [95% CI −20.7; −11.49]) and emotional functioning (−12.49 [95% CI −17.24; −7.73]), whereas boys reported greatest reduction in school functioning (−7.82 [95% CI –13.56; −2.08]) and physical functioning (−4.33 [95% CI –7.79; −0.88]). However, QoL scores remained higher at the 3-year follow-up compared to before lifestyle camp (Table 2) (online suppl. S4+S5). In those with normal weight at baseline, only physical functioning declined from end-of-treatment to 3 years, though it remained similar to baseline levels.

Relationship between Improvements during Lifestyle Intervention and Long-Term Outcomes

Participants with a greater decrease in BMI-SDS and body fat during camp presented with a lower body fat at the 1- and 3-year follow-up. On average, a one-unit greater decrease in BMI-SDS during camp led to an additional 9.06% (95% CI −14.50; −3.62) and 8.36% (95% CI –14.12; −2.61) body fat reduction at the 1- and 3-year follow-up, respectively. Further, participants with a greater decrease in BMI-SDS during camp had higher emotional (14.14 [95% CI 2.48; 25.80]) and social functioning (18.53 [95% CI 8.07; 29.00]) at the 1-year follow-up, while participants with a greater decrease in body fat during camp reported a minor increase in total QoL, emotional and social functioning at 1 year. Changes in BMI-SDS and body fat during camp had no effect on QoL 3 years later (Table 3).

Participants with a greater increase in emotional functioning during camp presented with a slightly higher BMI-SDS at the 1- and 3-year follow-up and a higher body fat at 3 years. Additionally, a greater increase in emotional functioning during camp led to marginally higher scores on social functioning at 1 year (0.17 [95% CI 0.02; 0.32]), but not at 3 years. Finally, participants experiencing a greater increase in school functioning during camp had a slightly lower body fat at the 1- and 3-year follow-up (Table 3).

Dropout analyses revealed no significant baseline differences in age, sex, BMI-SDS, parental household income, and QoL between participants who completed the 3-year follow-up and those who did not. However, there was a nonsignificant trend, indicating that dropouts had a slightly higher BMI-SDS at baseline (p = 0.05) (online suppl. S6).

Discussion

The improvements achieved during the lifestyle camp were attenuated at the 3-year follow-up compared to immediately after the intervention, with participants showing an increase in all anthropometric measures and a decline in QoL. Still, compared to baseline, both anthropometry and QoL were improved 3 years after camp but due to the absence of a control group, we can only speculate whether the observed long-term changes are attributable to camp participation. Changes in anthropometrics during the intervention were the strongest predictors of beneficial long-term changes in anthropometry and QoL.

The substantial reduction in BMI-SDS during this short-term intervention, with partially sustained improvements 3 years later, is comparable to findings from a combined 1-year inpatient-outpatient study by Adam et al. [19]. Compared to that study, we observed a 0.13-unit greater decrease in BMI-SDS during the intervention period, while the increase in BMI-SDS at the 3-year follow-up was slightly higher in our study (+0.08 BMI-SDS). In the study by Adam et al. [19], the in-patient program was shorter (6 weeks) compared to the present study but followed by a 10.5-month out-patient program, which may partly explain the observed differences.

A BMI-SDS reduction of more than 0.25 SD is a suggested marker for clinical improvements in cardiovascular risk factors [35]. In the present study, nearly half of participants (48%) had such a reduction after 3 years, while typically less than 10% of children and adolescents undergoing lifestyle interventions maintain meaningful improvements several years’ post-intervention [36]. Our findings align with the findings from Adam et al. [19], showing that 34% sustained a BMI-SDS reduction of more than 0.20 SD 3 years later.

Limited and conflicting evidence exists regarding sex differences in response to lifestyle interventions [34]. In the present study, girls appeared most challenged in sustaining long-term anthropometric improvements. Although changes were similar across age groups, sex differences may be influenced by pubertal development, which was not assessed.

We can only speculate on why improvements in anthropometry were sustained for a substantial number of participants 3 years after the intervention. The lifestyle camp may be an effective approach, but additional interventions during the follow-up period or other unknown individual or environmental factors may also have influenced these outcomes. According to the clinical practice guidelines published by the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2023 on childhood obesity treatment, a child-focused, family-centered, multidisciplinary intervention should be initiated as early and intensively as possible [37]. Similarly, family-based multicomponent behavioral interventions addressing behavior change are recommended in most countries [14, 38, 39]. The recommended target for childhood obesity treatment is more than 26 h of engagement over a 3- to 12-month period [40], and the lifestyle camp setting clearly met the target of 26 h of engagement. However, it did not meet the recommended duration.

Guidelines also recommend the involvement of a multidisciplinary team of experts [40]. In the present study, the lifestyle camps were led by social workers who focused on well-being, peer interaction, friendships, and daily healthy routines. They were supported by schoolteachers and kitchen staff preparing meals according to guidelines. Thus, our findings may suggest that an intensive, short-term child-centered multicomponent intervention delivered by social workers, and focused on well-being, peer interaction, and social belonging may serve as an important complement to traditional care. Especially metabolically healthy children and adolescents, who do not require specialized medical care, may benefit from interventions that more directly target the psychological/psychosocial challenges associated with overweight and obesity [41, 42]. As most camp participants are living with overweight or obesity, closer collaboration with healthcare professionals may however be beneficial to identify psychological factors contributing to the development and maintenance of overweight and obesity, e.g., disordered eating behaviors, depression, anxiety, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [43–46].

Current evidence and recommendations also support parental involvement as essential for achieving long-term success in childhood obesity treatment [37, 47]. In the lifestyle camp setting, parents participated sporadically, and it can thus be speculated whether increased parental involvement would enhance long-term outcomes even further. Future studies could also evaluate whether continued support from camp staff or extended peer-to-peer interaction could be effective.

Even though a decline in QoL was observed at the 3-year follow-up compared to end-of-treatment, QoL scores remained significantly higher than at baseline, consistent with previous findings [19]. However, when compared to a community sample of Danish 12–14-year-olds, all QoL scores were lower in the present sample [48], indicating a continued vulnerability and the need for ongoing support.

Those with a greater decrease in BMI-SDS during camp maintained a healthier body composition at the 3-year follow-up and also appeared to have higher emotional and social functioning 1 year after the intervention, emphasizing the continued need to support vulnerable children and adolescents in achieving weight loss within a supportive and caring environment. Due to the small sample size and the homogeneity of participants with respect to socioeconomic status, we were unable to examine whether baseline characteristics influenced intervention effects or predicted long-term outcomes, which remains an important area for future research.

Limitations

Although this study presents long-term effects of a real-life intervention, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, dropouts had numerically, although nonsignificant higher BMI-SDS at baseline, which may indicate selection bias and influence the results. Thus, children attending the follow-up assessment may be those who demonstrated the greatest progress following the intervention. Second, the study is limited by the lack of a control group. A comparison with a similar vulnerable cohort who did not participate in the camp would yield important knowledge about the added value of a camp setting relative to no intervention or standard care. Third, puberty status was not assessed in the present study, which may affect changes in body composition, especially in girls. Fourth, the low number of participants with normal weight did not allow assessments of effect differences across weight classes.

Conclusion

The observed improvements in anthropometry and QoL after a 10-week lifestyle camp were partially sustained 3 years later. Although some decline occurred between end-of-treatment and the 3-year follow-up, these findings may suggest that an intensive, short-term child-centered multicomponent intervention focused on well-being, peer interaction, and social belonging could serve as a promising treatment strategy for vulnerable children with overweight and obesity. The findings highlight the need to rethink obesity treatment strategies for children and adolescents. Moreover, the study underscores the ongoing need for professional support to promote healthy weight trajectories and overall well-being throughout adolescence in vulnerable populations.
