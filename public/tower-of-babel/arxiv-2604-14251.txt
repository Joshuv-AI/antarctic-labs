Calibrate-Then-Delegate: Safety Monitoring with Risk and Budget Guarantees via Model Cascades
Edoardo Pona, Milad Kazemi, Mehran Hosseini, Yali Du, David Watson, Osvaldo Simeone, Nicola Paoletti

Abstract
Monitoring LLM safety at scale requires balancing cost and accuracy: a cheap latent-space probe can screen every input, but hard cases should be escalated to a more expensive expert. Existing cascades delegate based on probe uncertainty, but uncertainty is a poor proxy for delegation benefit, as it ignores whether the expert would actually correct the error. To address this problem, we introduce Calibrate-Then-Delegate (CTD), a model-cascade approach that provides probabilistic guarantees on the computation cost while enabling instance-level (streaming) decisions. CTD builds on a novel delegation value (DV) probe , a lightweight model operating on the same internal representations as the safety probe that directly predicts the benefit of escalation. To enforce budget constraints, CTD calibrates a threshold on the DV signal using held-out data via multiple hypothesis testing, yielding finite-sample guarantees on the delegation rate. Evaluated on four safety datasets, CTD consistently outperforms uncertainty-based delegation at every budget level, avoids harmful over-delegation, and adapts budget allocation to input difficulty without requiring group labels.

Introduction

As large language models are deployed across a growing range of applications, safety monitoring
has become a core component of responsible deployment.
Latent-space probes offer a promising solution: by training a small classifier on the internal
activations of the deployed model, they provide safety scores at near-zero inference cost.
Empirically, activation-based probes have been shown to be effective in settings including the detection of strategically deceptive inputs
(
13
)
, bias and toxicity
(
24
)
, hallucinations
(
12
)
, sensitivity to trojans
(
21
)
, and refusal
(
3
;
25
)
.
However, when deployed alone, latent probes may be unreliable: they can track spurious correlations (e.g., confusing morally charged content with dishonesty)
(
13
)
and are vulnerable to obfuscation attacks
(
6
)
.

This has motivated the introduction of
model cascades

(
22
;
14
)
, which pair a cheap model (e.g., a latent probe) with a
more capable but expensive expert (e.g., a larger LLM classifier or a chain-of-thought monitor), coordinated by a
delegation rule
that determines when to escalate a given input.
Notably,
22
instantiate this approach for detecting
high-stakes
inputs, i.e. queries that may lead to interactions that compromise safety.
In their approach, the probe scores a fixed pool of inputs and forwards the top-
k
most uncertain ones to the expert. This has two limitations:
batch-level routing
, since delegation decisions require the full pool and cannot be made as inputs arrive; and a
fixed delegation quota
, since the top-
k
policy always escalates exactly
k
inputs regardless of whether the expert would actually help.

In this work, we introduce
Calibrate-Then-Delegate (CTD)
, a model-cascade approach that calibrates delegation rules to achieve provable risk and budget control.
As shown in figure
1
, our approach combines a cheap safety probe
\rho
and an expensive expert
\epsilon
, and defines an instance-level delegation rule that decides, for each input and without batch context, whether to invoke the expert by comparing a delegation signal against a threshold. This threshold is calibrated on held-out data following the Learn-then-Test (LTT) procedure
(
2
)
to provide finite-sample, PAC-style guarantees: with high probability, the fraction of escalated inputs does not exceed a prescribed budget
\alpha
, while maximising the cascade’s safety performance.

A key question is how to construct an effective delegation signal.
22
use the safety probe’s predictive uncertainty, based on the intuition that we should escalate to the expert when the probe cannot be trusted. However, this signal is agnostic to the expert, treating all uncertain inputs equally, whether or not the expert would actually correct the probe’s prediction.
Delegating in the latter case is detrimental to both safety and computation cost. Moreover, the probe’s uncertainty may itself be miscalibrated (e.g., confident on incorrect predictions and uncertain on correct ones). To address this, we introduce the notion of
delegation value
(DV), which quantifies, for an input
x
with label
y
, the benefit of escalation as the margin
P_{\epsilon}(y\mid x)-P_{\rho}(y\mid x)
. We then propose
DV probes
, latent probes trained to predict this quantity, yielding a more informative and target-aligned delegation signal.

We make three contributions.
(1) We propose
Calibrate-Then-Delegate
(CTD), a model-cascade method with finite-sample guarantees on both delegation rate and safety performance.
(2) We introduce DV probes, an auxiliary latent probe that estimates the per-input benefit of delegation, enabling more targeted delegation than probe uncertainty alone.
(3) We evaluate CTD on four safety datasets from the benchmark of
22
, demonstrating that our method dominates uncertainty-based routing at every budget level, with particularly large gains when the expert is weaker than the probe (up to
+7\%
AUC and
+9\%
accuracy with a strong expert, and
+11\%
AUC and
+19\%
accuracy with a weak expert). Moreover, it prevents over-delegation when this would harm accuracy, and allocates the computation budget adaptively based on the input difficulty.

2
Problem Definition

Model Cascade.

We consider a two-stage safety monitoring cascade
operating on inputs
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
with binary safety labels
y\in\{0,1\}
, where
y=0
denotes safe and
y=1
unsafe. The pairs
(x,y)
are drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
P_{XY}
.
As shown in figure
1
the system encompasses the following components:

\bullet

Linear probe
\rho
, a logistic regression model trained on the internal activations
z(x)\in{\mathcal{Z}}
of the monitored LLM. It produces a safety score
\rho(z(x))\in[0,1]
. We write
\rho(x)
as shorthand when the latent map is clear from context.

\bullet

Expert
\epsilon
, a more capable but expensive model, e.g., a
large LLM that operates directly on
x
which returns a safety score
\epsilon(x)\in[0,1]
.

\bullet

Delegation policy

\pi_{\lambda}
, a binary decision rule that routes each input to either the probe or the expert. The policy’s decision depends on the hyperparameters
\lambda
, which typically correspond to thresholds applied to a given delegation signal.

Given these components, the final cascade output becomes:

s_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}\epsilon(x)&\text{if }\pi_{\lambda}(x)=1\quad\text{(call expert)},\\
\rho(x)&\text{if }\pi_{\lambda}(x)=0\quad\text{(probe only)}.\end{cases}

(1)

When an input is delegated (
\pi_{\lambda}(x)=1
), one could alternatively combine the probe and expert predictions (e.g., by averaging) instead of exclusively switching to the expert.

We remark that the delegation policy in (
1
) applies to each input independently. In
contrast,
22
require
access to a data batch before making any routing decisions, which precludes streaming settings where inputs arrive sequentially.
That’s because their approach forwards a fixed number of inputs to the expert, those inputs scoring within the central
k\%
of the batch’s delegation scores (
\epsilon
’s predictive uncertainty in their case).

Design Objectives.
We seek a delegation policy
\pi_{\lambda}
that formally controls the budget
R_{\text{B}}
, i.e., the fraction of inputs delegated to the expert, while maximising the performance
R_{\text{P}}
of the probe-expert cascade
s_{\lambda}
on the safety monitoring task. Both
R_{\text{B}}
and
R_{\text{P}}
depend on the policy’s parameters
\lambda
and are formally defined as follows:

R_{\text{B}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}\!\big[\pi_{\lambda}(X)\big],\qquad R_{\text{P}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P_{XY}}\!\big[{\mathcal{L}}(s_{\lambda}(X),Y)\big],

(2)

where
P_{X}
is the marginal input distribution obtained from
P_{XY}
, and
{\mathcal{L}}:[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a loss function of choice measuring the safety prediction quality of the cascade, like the misclassification rate. We refer to
R_{\text{B}}
and
R_{\text{P}}
as
risks
.

Problem Statement.

We assume access to a set of calibration datapoints
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
generated i.i.d. from the data distribution
P_{XY}
.
Given a finite set of candidate policy parameters
\Lambda
, a delegation budget level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
, find a parameter
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda
that solves the problem

\lambda^{*}\in\argmin_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\;\hat{R}_{\text{P}}(\lambda)\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad\Pr_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}}\!(R_{\text{B}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha)\geq 1-\delta,

(3)

where
\hat{R}_{\text{P}}(\lambda)
is an estimate of the performance risk
R_{\text{P}}
obtained from a held-out dataset, and the probability is over the randomness of the calibration set.
Note that the formulation (
3
) imposes a formal probabilistic constraint on the budget risk, while making a best effort at maximising performance, i.e., minimising the safety monitoring loss.

3
Background: Learn-then-Test

Learn-then-Test (LTT;
2
) is a post-hoc calibration framework aimed at
selecting a hyperparameter
\lambda
from a finite candidate set
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{T}\}
such that a risk function
R(\lambda)
is provably controlled using a finite-sample calibration dataset.
The key idea is to reframe calibration as
multiple hypothesis testing
: instead of minimising risk,
we ask which candidates can be
certified
as risk-controlling.

Setup.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a held-out calibration set drawn i.i.d. from distribution
P_{XY}
, independent of the learned predictor.
Given a risk level
\alpha\in(0,1)
and a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
, we
associate to each hyperparameter
\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda
the null hypothesis

H_{j}:R(\lambda_{j})>\alpha,

(4)

and seek the set of hyper-parameters
\Lambda^{*}
whose rejection satisfies the condition

\Pr_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}}\left(\forall\lambda\in\Lambda^{*}:R(\lambda)\leq\alpha\right)\geq 1-\delta

(5)

P-values.

For each
H_{j}
, LTT obtains a valid
p
-value
p_{j}
, i.e. a random variable satisfying the inequality
\Pr(p_{j}\leq t\mid H_{j})\leq t
for all
t\in[0,1]
under
the null
H_{j}
.
When the risk is the expectation of a Bernoulli loss, as is our budget risk
R_{\text{B}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[\pi_{\lambda}(X)]
in (
2
), we use the one-sided binomial
p
-value, i.e., the cumulative distribution function of a binomial random variable with parameters
(n,\alpha)
evaluated at
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{\lambda_{j}}(x_{i})

(
2
)
. Because the binomial test is uniformly most powerful, this choice maximises power (sensitivity) for any fixed level
\delta

(
18
)
.

Fixed-Sequence Testing.

To satisfy the guarantees of Eq.
5
, we need testing procedures that control the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER), i.e., the probability of incorrectly rejecting at least one true null hypothesis among the candidate thresholds, at level
\delta
.
Bonferroni correction (i.e., testing all hypotheses at level
\delta/|\Lambda|
) controls FWER but is over-conservative when the risk function
R(\lambda)
is monotone in
\lambda
. In such cases, LTT employs
fixed sequence testing

(
7
)
, in which the hypotheses are ordered from the most to the least conservative (i.e., from those most likely to be rejected to those least likely) and then tested in this order. The testing procedure stops at the first index
j
such that the hypothesis
H_{j}
can’t be rejected at level
\delta
, i.e., such that
p_{j}\geq\delta
, and returns the set of parameters
\Lambda^{*}=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{j-1}\}
corresponding to the hypotheses that were rejected at level
\delta
. Crucially, such a procedure controls the FWER (so satisfying (
5
)) while preserving the full power of each individual test.

4
Calibrate-Then-Delegate

While the safety probe’s uncertainty is a natural signal for delegation, it has two key limitations.
First, safety probes are known to be miscalibrated
(
22
)
, that is, their confidence scores do not reliably reflect the probability of a correct prediction. This is why
22
resort to ranking inputs in a batch by uncertainty rather than thresholding on absolute scores.
Second, uncertainty is entirely agnostic to the expert’s performance: a highly uncertain example where the expert also fails provides no benefit from delegation, while an overconfident but wrong prediction might benefit greatly.

Our CTD approach addresses these limitations by introducing
delegation value (DV) probes
, a lightweight model that explicitly estimates the
benefit of delegation
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, CTD then defines its delegation policy by calibrating the DV probe via LTT to enforce strict budget constraints, while applying a Pareto filtering step
(
17
)
to prevent delegation from harming the accuracy of the overall safety monitoring cascade. We remark that we use three disjoint datasets for training the safety probe, training the DV probe, and calibration.

4.1
Delegation-Value Probes

We define the
delegation value
(DV) of an input
x
with true class
y
as the marginal benefit of invoking the expert on
x
:

v(x,y)=P_{\epsilon}(y\mid x)-P_{\rho}(y\mid x),

(6)

where
P_{\rho}(y\mid x)
and
P_{\epsilon}(y\mid x)
are the probabilities assigned to the
correct class by the probe and expert, respectively.
When
v(x,y)>0
, delegation steers the prediction toward the correct label; when
v(x,y)<0
, delegation is harmful. While alternative definitions of
v(x,y)
are possible (see Appendix
A
), the form in (
6
) directly quantifies how much more likely the expert is to predict the correct label than the probe, yielding a richer signal that binary or ternary indicators of which model to prefer.

For a collection of points
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, we also define the
effective delegation capacity
as the proportion of points with positive delegation value,
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}[v(x_{i},y_{i})>0]
.
This quantity provides a natural upper bound on useful delegation: allocating a budget beyond this level necessarily includes inputs for which delegation is harmful.

Since
v(x,y)
depends on the unknown label
y
, it cannot be computed at inference time. We therefore introduce
DV probes
, auxiliary models
d(x)
trained to predict the delegation value from latent representations
z(x)
. Concretely, we fit a ridge regression model
d(x)=w_{v}^{\top}z(x)+b_{v}
using targets
v(x_{i},y_{i})
computed offline from probe and expert outputs.

4.2
Calibration via LTT and Pareto Filtering

Using our DV probe
d(x)
, the CTD delegation policy applies the threshold-based selection rule
\pi_{\lambda}(x)=d(x)>\lambda
.
CTD calibrates the threshold
\lambda
to satisfy the budget constraint, while achieving strong safety monitoring performance as per problem (
3
).

Since the design problem (
3
) involves both budget and performance risks, prior to the testing procedure, we restrict attention to promising candidates on the budget-performance plane using a
Pareto filtering
step
(
17
)
. Specifically, using a held-out split
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{est}}
, we evaluate the empirical risks
\hat{R}_{\text{B}}(\lambda)
and
\hat{R}_{\text{P}}(\lambda)
and retain only thresholds that are not dominated in both objectives. This filtering step provides two key benefits. First, by discarding dominated thresholds before testing, it concentrates statistical power on promising candidates; second, thresholds that would yield over-delegation are filtered out before they can be selected, ensuring the final policy delegates only when it is empirically beneficial to do so.

Let
\Lambda^{\prime}
be the set of Pareto-filtered thresholds. Note that the budget risk
R_{\text{B}}(\lambda)
is monotonically decreasing in
\lambda
, with lower thresholds delegating more inputs, i.e., yielding more aggressive policies. Accordingly, after ordering these thresholds from conservative to aggressive, we apply fixed sequence testing (Section
2
) using a calibration dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}
and return the set
\Lambda^{*}\subseteq\Lambda^{\prime}
of risk budget-controlling parameters, i.e., satisfying the probabilistic constraint in problem (
5
). Finally, we select from the set
\Lambda^{*}
the parameter
\lambda^{*}
yielding the best empirical performance risk
\hat{R}_{\text{P}}
as estimated during the Pareto-filtering step. The resulting threshold
\lambda^{*}
thus provides a solution to the design problem (
3
).

5
Results

5.1
Experimental Setup

We follow the experimental setup of
22
. The safety probe is trained on their synthetic dataset (8,000 examples of high- and low-stakes queries generated by GPT-4o and filtered for label quality).
For evaluation we use four of their class-balanced datasets, covering a range of domains: Anthropic HH
(
5
)
(online harm; 500/500 dev/test), MTSamples
(
9
)
(clinical notes; 278/500), MTS-Dialog
(
8
)
(doctor-patient dialogue; 274/86), and ToolACE
(
19
)
(tool-use interactions; 328/500), for a total of 1,380 dev and 1,586 test examples.
The dev split is used for DV probe training, and the test split is divided equally into calibration (for threshold selection) and evaluation.
Within the calibration set, 30% is allocated to the optimisation split
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{est}}
and 70% to the hypothesis-testing split
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}
.

The safety probe is a logistic regression on mean-pooled activations from layer 11 of Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, selected as the best-performing layer on the dev set.
We evaluate the cascade with two experts: a
strong
expert (Gemma-3-27B-IT) that substantially outperforms the probe, and a
weak
expert (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, the same model from which activations are extracted) that often performs worse than the probe.

We sweep 20 budget levels
\alpha\in[0.05,0.95]
using a confidence of
1-\delta=0.9
for the probabilistic guarantees. We define the set of admissible parameters
\Lambda
using a grid of 100 points.
For Pareto testing calibration, we use the accuracy error as the performance risk
\hat{R}_{\text{P}}=1-\text{accuracy}
. Results are qualitatively similar when using AUROC error (
1-\text{AUROC}
) as the performance risk instead; we report these in Appendix
E
.

Methods.

We evaluate five delegation strategies, varying the delegation signal (DV probe vs. probe uncertainty) and the delegation mechanism (calibrated threshold vs. batched top-
k
). The strategies are:

CTD
, our full method, using a calibrated threshold policy on the DV score
d(x)
;
Unc. (uncertainty) calibrated
, a calibrated threshold policy based on the proximity-to-median uncertainty score
-|\hat{F}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}}(\rho(x))-0.5|
of
22
, where
\hat{F}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{cal}}}
is the empirical CDF of probe scores on the calibration set;

DV top-
k
, a batched top-
k
delegation strategy based on the DV score
d(x)
, and

Unc. top-
k
: the batched top-
k
baseline from
22
based on the proximity-to-median score. As a performance upper bound, we also consider

Oracle top-
k
, a batched top-
k
policy based on the ground-truth delegation value
v(x,y)
. For all batched configurations, we report results at batch size
B=128
. We show that results are qualitatively similar across different batch sizes in Appendix
B
.

5.3
Overall Cascade Performance

Strong Expert.

We first evaluate the cascade with a strong expert (Gemma-3-27B-IT), with the results shown in Figure
3
. To facilitate comparisons between uncertainty and DV probes, we first compare two batched top-
k
cascades that differ only in their delegation signal: DV top-
k
(orange, dashed) vs. Unc. top-
k
(blue, dashed).
DV top-
k
dominates Unc. top-
k
at every budget fraction, for both area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and accuracy, demonstrating that the DV probe
d(x)
captures a more informative delegation signal than uncertainty alone.
Note that all top-
k
methods are forced to use their full allocated budget, so their performance degrades once the budget exceeds the effective delegation capability.

CTD (orange, solid) layers Pareto-testing calibration on top of the DV signal, adding two properties.
First, it allows delegation decisions to be made on each individual point, without batch-level statistics, making it applicable in streaming settings. Despite this, it maintains probabilistic budget-control guarantees and closely tracks the DV top-
k
performance across budget ranges.
Second, beyond the effective delegation capability, it
plateaus
instead of degrading: the Pareto filter discards thresholds that would over-delegate, so the cascade never harms performance regardless of the allowed budget.
Unlike the other methods, at the highest budget levels, CTD settles above the expert-only performance. Finally, Unc. calibrated (blue, solid) plateaus where Unc. top-
k
degrades, confirming that robustness to over-delegation comes from calibration, not from the choice of signal.

Weak Expert.

As shown in Figure
4
, when the expert (Llama-3.2-1B) is weak compared to the original probe
\rho(x)
, there may still be instances where delegation is beneficial, but they are few and harder to identify. As a result, the performance of expert-only inference is
worse
than the probe-only inference.
The top-
k
methods are, by construction, very prone to over-delegation, since the effective delegation capacity of this cascade is very low.
CTD, on the other hand, uses the DV probe signal to identify the few instances where invoking the expert is beneficial, and then automatically caps delegation to prevent over-delegation (at approx 40% of the available budget), ensuring that performance never degrades below the probe-only baseline even with a weak expert.
Performance at higher budget levels is vastly superior to the top-
k
and expert-only methods.

Related Work

Safety monitoring with latent probes.

Linear probes on LLM activations have been used to detect toxicity
(
24
)
,
hallucinations
(
12
)
, sleeper agents
(
21
)
,
and strategic deception
(
13
)
, but are unreliable alone
due to spurious correlations and obfuscation attacks
(
13
;
6
)
.

22
address this by cascading a probe with an LLM expert via a
batch-level top-
k
policy; CTD removes that design’s batch-routing and fixed-quota limitations.

4
similarly pair probes with conformal calibration for safety,
but abstain from generation rather than escalating to an expert.

Calibrated cascades and risk-controlled delegation.

CTD builds on LTT
(
2
)
, which frames
threshold selection as multiple-hypothesis testing with PAC-style FWER control, and
Pareto testing
(
17
)
, which extends this to simultaneous multi-objective control.

15
apply fixed-sequence
testing to cascaded judges to guarantee human-agreement rates, and

16
characterise the optimal two-threshold structure of
weak-strong verification policies.

LLM routing and model cascades.

Several works address inference cost-quality trade-offs by routing queries across
models: FrugalGPT
(
10
)
learns cascade strategies matching the best
individual model at up to 98% cost reduction; AutoMix
(
1
)
adds
self-verification and POMDP-based routing; RouteLLM
(
23
)
trains
routers from human preference data;
11
derive a
theoretically optimal framework unifying routing and cascading; and

20
composes heterogeneous signals for mixture-of-modality
deployments.

7
Conclusion

We introduced Calibrate-Then-Delegate (CTD), a model-cascade framework for LLM safety monitoring that replaces uncertainty-based delegation with a learned delegation value signal and provides probabilistic guarantees on the delegation rate by calibrating the delegation threshold via multiple hypothesis testing.
CTD outperforms uncertainty-based delegation at every budget level, with gains of up to
+7.9\%
AUC with a strong expert and
+11.0\%
with a weak expert, while automatically preventing harmful over-delegation.
The DV probe incurs negligible cost, as it reuses the same activations as the safety probe, and the calibrated threshold enables streaming deployment without relying on batch-level statistics.

Appendix A
Binary Delegation Value formulation

An alternative to the continuous delegation value (Equation
6
) is a binary indicator that captures only cases where delegation flips the hard prediction from wrong to right:

v_{\text{bin}}(x,y)=\mathds{1}\!\big[\text{probe wrong}\;\wedge\;\text{expert correct}\big].

(7)

When
v_{\text{bin}}(x,y)=1
, delegation corrects the outcome; when
v_{\text{bin}}(x,y)=0
, delegation does not help—either both are correct, both are wrong, or the probe is already correct.
This formulation is aligned primarily with accuracy: the oracle ranking by
v_{\text{bin}}
optimises for flipping wrong-to-right predictions, but treats all such flips equally regardless of the magnitude of score improvement.
In particular, it does not directly optimise AUC, since AUC depends on the
ordering
of scores, not just binary correctness.
The continuous formulation
v(x,y)=P_{\epsilon}(y\mid x)-P_{\rho}(y\mid x)
captures partial improvements and is better aligned with both AUROC and accuracy.

Table
2
and Figure
6
compare the two formulations empirically using DV top-
k
delegation at
B=128
.
With the strong expert, the continuous DV probe matches or exceeds the binary variant in AUC at every budget level (up to
+1.2
pp at
\alpha=0.50
), while accuracy is comparable.
The advantage is more pronounced with the weak expert, where continuous DV improves both AUC (
+2.6
pp) and accuracy (
+3.8
pp) at
\alpha=0.50
.
The gap widens at higher budgets because the continuous formulation provides a finer-grained ranking: it distinguishes large score improvements from small ones, allowing the top-
k
set to prioritise examples where delegation helps most.

Appendix D
Empirical validation of budget guarantees

We empirically validate the PAC budget guarantee
\Pr(R_{\text{B}}(\lambda^{*})\leq\alpha)\geq 1-\delta
by repeating the calibration procedure across 500 independent random splits of the test data into calibration and evaluation subsets. For each trial, we calibrate the threshold on the calibration split (using either budget-only LTT or Pareto-filtered LTT) and measure the realised delegation rate on the held-out evaluation split. Figure
11
shows the results at
\alpha=0.3
and
\delta=0.1
. Budget-only LTT is near-tight: its violation rate (8.6% with the strong expert, 6.6% with the weak) closely tracks
\delta
. Pareto-filtered LTT is more conservative (3.0% and 4.0% respectively), because the Pareto filter restricts the candidate set by discarding thresholds that would hurt performance. With a weak expert, delegation is rarely beneficial, so the Pareto filter prunes more aggressively and realised rates fall further below
\alpha
.

Right

(6.6% and 4.0%), with the Pareto filter adapting its conservatism to the limited effective delegation capacity.
