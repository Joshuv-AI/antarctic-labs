1 Background and Introduction
This report addresses relevant simulations, experiments, and supplementary efforts that demonstrate
satisfaction of deliverables for the Sensors in Environments: Accelerometers (SE-A) testbed. For
the reader’s convenience, a summary of work is included at the start of the report. The report is
then separated into four main sections: an experimental efforts overview, analysis efforts overview,
supplemental efforts overview, and a results summary. Notable achievements leading to satisfaction
of work package (WP) deliverables are summarized and lessons learned throughout the project are
included at the end.

1.1 TRUST Overview
The objective of the Delivery Environments (DE) Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty in Simulations
and Tests (TRUST) project is to quantify and help increase confidence in specific areas of computa-
tional and experimental capabilities that are applicable to the development, on-target assessment,
and qualification of current and future delivery environments. The TRUST project consists of five
"low-complexity", single-feature testbeds used to conduct experiments with accompanying models
and simulations. Each of the five single feature testbeds aims to isolate an engineering feature or
behavior of interest, then work towards improving the fundamental engineering understanding of
that feature. Additionally, the single feature testbeds are used to identify capability development
needs that can help reduce model and experimental uncertainty.

TRUST includes five testbeds and their associated engineering analysis baseline models (EABMs):

  1. Contact Thermal Conductivity (CTC)

  2. Nonlinear Dynamics (ND)

  3. Sensors in Environments: Accelerometers (SE-A)

  4. Sensors in Environments: Fiber Optic Displacement Gauges (SE-FOD)

  5. Sensors in Environments: Thermocouples (SE-TC)

1.2 Sensors in Environments: Accelerometers Testbed
The Sensors in Environments: Accelerometers testbed is a collaboration between the Advanced En-
gineering Analysis Group (E-13) and the Test Engineering Group (V-14) that first began in fiscal
year 20 (FY20). The testbed design was motivated by the need to better characterize and propagate
the uncertainty in experimental techniques used by V-14 in mission-like environments. Discussions
with subject matter experts (SMEs) identified instrumentation as the best candidate for single fea-
ture testbeds addressing experimental uncertainty sources [1]. As such, this project seeks to build
experimental and simulation experience in V-14 and E-13, respectively, and to quantify experimen-
tal and analysis uncertainty in modal tests with testbeds targeted at instrumentation uncertainty
quantification. Specifically, primary questions that aim to be answered include how does the ac-
celerometer mounting method affect test measurements, where should accelerometers be mounted to
obtain relevant test results, and what modeling efforts are needed to inform modal tests and vibration
environments? For these sources of uncertainty, outside sources of influence that contribute to the

ter mass, sensitivity, and placement. Past and current efforts [2][3][4][5][6][7][8] aim to address the

1.2.1 Deliverables
Each existing TRUST testbed is at a different level of maturity and has its own unique deliverables.
This will be discussed more in Section 1.3, but SE-A is planned to transition to a "completed"
status at the end of FY25. As such, efforts this FY focused on fulfilling the remaining objectives
in the multi-year status plan, finishing incomplete work, and presenting completed work for review
within and outside the lab. The FY25 deliverables for SE-A are:

  1. Finishing the uncertainty quantification (UQ) of the experimental data to build confidence
 and quantify uncertainty in experimental testing techniques related to modal tests in V-14.

  2. Refining the explicit simulation models to build confidence and quantify uncertainty in simu-
 lation and modeling efforts related to dynamic, explicit analyses in E-13.

  3. Seeking internal and external peer review to assess the readiness of SE-A for completion based
 on current complexity.

  4. Documenting all completed work and workflows for historical archiving and easy reference.

2.1 Updating Experimental Mode Shapes
Data collected from experiments in FY24 consisted of modal parameters for the etched aluminum
hemisphere up to 1600 Hz. Within this bandwidth there exists 12 mode shapes and, out of these 12
modes shapes, 5 are symmetric meaning their deformation shape is the same but one is orthogonal
to the other. An example of this is shown in Figure 1.

Assuming free-free boundary conditions, these mode shapes would appear at the same frequency,
which is what is seen in the finite element simulations. However, perfect boundary conditions do
not exist in the experimental setup. As a result, the frequencies drift apart because of damping
effects. This creates a unique challenge when developing analyses for determining uncertainty.

Previous data processing only considered the symmetric modes that had the most prominent peak
and ignored their orthogonal counterparts, thus leading to a reduced number of modes selected.
While this did not inherently affect the results, it is considered bad practice as the ignored modes
are real and should be included in the analysis. The modes should be considered as unique and ana-
lyzed separate from one another. Previous Bayesian analyses had trouble separating the symmetric
modes between the peaks of the bimodal distribution because the symmetric modes were not being
considered as unique. See Section 3.1 for a more detailed description of the bimodal distribution
problem.

 Figure 1: Mode shapes 4 and 5 for the hemisphere.

Page 4 Updating Experimental Mode Shapes

While trying to understand how to incorporate the symmetric modes into the Bayesian analysis, it
was brought to the attention of the team that the experimental mode shapes have the potential to
"swap" between frequencies. Take Figure 1, which shows the experimentally obtained mode shapes
for modes 4 and 5, as an example. If the mode shapes were to "swap", then the shape occurring
at mode 4 (408.4 Hz) would occur at mode 5 (422.1 Hz) and vice versa. To verify whether this
was occurring, each test file needed to be updated to include all 12 mode shapes and each mode
shape needed to be qualitatively analyzed across 33 tests. To do this, a tracking table with rows
consisting of the test file IDs and columns consisting of the mode number was established [12]. An
image capturing each mode shape at its maximum displacement for each test was uploaded in order
to be visually compared across all tests. From this, it was determined the experimental symmetric
mode shapes were not swapping between frequencies across tests and were in fact “locked” in place,
likely because of the added mass of the six accelerometers used in the experiments. See Section 3.2
for information on how this impacted the implicit hemisphere simulations.

3 Analysis Efforts
This section discusses developments in analyzing, modeling, and simulating the TRUST SE-A
testbed. Note that much of the background detail will be omitted since it was already covered
in detail in the FY24 report [8]. Interested and newer readers are advised to read that paper prior
to this one. First, the bimodal distribution problem will be briefly discussed before transitioning
into the "unlocked" simulation mode shapes and how to resolve them. Then, there will be a review
of the efforts to create a cross-MAC matrix between the simulations and experiments. Finally, the
updated explicit hemisphere simulations and all of the different modeling techniques implemented
will be covered. All modeling and simulations efforts took place in Abaqus 2024 [13].

3.1 The Bimodal Distribution Problem
This topic is the focus of the FY25 SE-A WESJ paper [14] and presentation [15]. Readers are
advised to read that paper for an in-depth overview, but a brief summary will be given here. Es-
sentially, if an analyst attempts to perform Bayesian inference [16] on modal data obtained from
experiments on axisymmetric structures, all identified mode shapes must be treated as their own
individual mode shape, including the coupled symmetric mode shapes that have nearly identical
natural frequencies and only differ in their orientation around the axis of symmetry. These sym-
metric mode shapes appear in FRFs as closely spaced peaks, as seen in Figure 2, which can make
automated peak-finding difficult and invalid by grouping the wrong mode shapes together.

Should this occur, like it did in FY24 for SE-A [8], the collected natural frequencies for each
symmetric mode pair will form a bimodal distribution where each peak corresponds to one of the
symmetric modes. When a Bayesian inference is conducted on that data, the final posterior will end
in-between the two peaks, falsely indicating that the most likely natural frequency for that mode
is one that was never observed in the experiment. By decoupling the symmetric mode pairs and
treating each symmetric mode shape as its own individual mode shape, the distributions for each
mode’s natural frequency will revert to Gaussian and the Bayesian posterior will encompass the
observed data. An example of the confounded Bayesian results and the corrected version for both
symmetric mode pairs are shown in Figure 3.

3.2 Unlocked Simulation Mode Shapes
From discussion with SMEs in V-14, it was learned that symmetric mode shapes inherent to axisym-
metric structures can experience a "swapping" behavior if they are not "locked" in place during the
experiment. In repeat, identical iterations of the same experiment, symmetric mode shapes can swap
their near-identical natural frequencies, making it difficult to reliably track those mode shapes. This
same swapping behavior was observed in identical, repeat implicit hemisphere simulations within
a *FREQUENCY step. For experiments, the modes can be locked into place by introducing an
external mass to the test body to break symmetry. So long as the added mass does not signifi-
cantly impact the modal outputs, the mode shapes should be repeatable. Typically, the mass of
the attached accelerometers enforces enough asymmetry to ensure this, which was the case for SE-A.

This same approach was used in the simulations by creating reference points (RPs) where the
accelerometers would be located and tying them to the respective nodes on the model. These refer-
ence points were then assigned a mass of 1 gram to represent one of the types of accelerometers used
in the experiments [17], which resulted in 6 grams total added to the model. To confirm the mode
shapes were locked, six identical simulations were conducted with the exact same command line ar-
guments and their mode shapes and natural frequencies were compared with each other. The mode
shapes and natural frequencies matched across every simulation, indicating the modes were now
successfully locked. Afterwards, a comparison was made between the mass-loaded simulation and
the non-mass-loaded (nominal) simulation to see what effects the mass-loading has on the model, if
any [18]. The mode shapes were identical in SE-A’s frequency range of interest (0-1200 Hz) and up
to 2800 Hz, but higher frequencies saw slight distortions or "ripple" patterns along the rim of the
hemisphere where the mass-loaded RPs were located. These effects became more exacerbated as the
frequency increased, which shows that mass-loading can distort the mode shapes of the simulations
but mostly in the higher frequency ranges. Intertwined with the distortion of the mode shapes
are differences in the natural frequencies. Figures 4 and 5 show the absolute and percent relative
differences, respectively, of the mass-loaded and nominal natural frequencies. Note that both sets
of simulations found the same number of modes and that the nominal frequencies were taken as the
reference values when calculating the percent relative difference.

Generally, the lower frequency modes see smaller differences than the higher frequency modes when
mass-loading is used. The differences in the natural frequencies can be as much as 25-30 Hz, but
when looking at the differences as a percentage, they are all almost less than 1%. However, it should
be noted that these behaviors are likely all model and mesh dependent. The hemisphere is not very
large and weighs ≈5 kg (≈11 lbm). It is possible that if this study were repeated on a larger model
with more interacting parts, the behaviors would change. Similarly, the density of the model’s mesh
affects how rigid it is, which then affects the mode shapes and natural frequencies. A finer mesh
would decrease the model’s rigidity and possibly increase the differences in the mode shapes and
natural frequencies.

It is worth noting that the LADSS project studied the effects of mass-loading on modal param-
eters of a rectangular aluminum plate using Digital Image Correlation (DIC). More information
on that project can be found in Section 5.2 and their paper [19], but they observed a 3% shift in
the natural frequencies with and without accelerometer mass in their DIC experiments, which is a
bigger shift than what the SE-A simulations observed (≈1%). Their plate weighed ≈0.47 kg (≈1
lbm)—significantly lighter than the hemisphere—and the increased shift in the natural frequencies
supports the mass-loading effects being dependent on the mass of the structure. The 4.53 kg (≈10
lbm) difference between the two structures resulted in a 2% shift difference in their natural frequen-
cies, or about 1% for every 5 lbm.

Note that all results moving forward involving the implicit hemisphere simulation is with the non-
mass-loaded model.

3.3 Cross-MAC Matrix Efforts
In order to quantitatively assess the dynamic behavior of the hemisphere in the simulations, a cross-
MAC matrix between the simulation and experimental data was pursued this FY. By comparing
the simulated mode shapes against the experimental mode shapes, the resulting MAC matrix would
help confirm if the simulated model was an accurate representation of the real structure and its
experimental set-up. To make this comparison, the data must be equivalent between simulation
and experiment, meaning the MAC matrix had to be calculated from all acceleration data or all
displacement data. The experiments recorded accelerations while Abaqus output displacements.
It was decided to integrate the experimental acceleration data to obtain displacements because
they’re more "literal" and comprehensible than accelerations are. The experimentalists were able
to integrate and re-export the data from their testing software, creating new compliance FRF data
[20]. Before attempting to create the cross-MAC, a self-MAC was created from the compliance FRF
data to prove the feasibility of the task. That self-MAC, which should be identity and can be seen
in Figure 6, is nowhere close to identity.

Cross-MAC Matrix Efforts Page 9

 Figure 6: MAC matrix with poor quality created from compliance FRF data.

The reason for such poor quality was because the automated peak-finding method that was pre-
viously implemented is unable to find every peak in each FRF out of the hundreds that one test
generates, which the MAC requires. It’s capable of finding the majority, but some are inevitably
missed. Peaks that were missed were replaced with a value of NaN, which are the source of the
columns in the off-diagonals of the matrix. Automating the peak-finding is necessary for the thou-
sands of FRFs across every test conducted in FY24 and updating the method to be even more
robust was not feasible with the remaining time in the FY. As such, this pursuit was abandoned
in favor of generating the FRFs from the acceleration data, which was the original method and
makes the peak-finding much more accurate. The indices of the peaks found in the original FRFs
were recorded then the displacement data was indexed at those indices to create a new self-MAC.
Peaks that were missed at a specific node/hammer hit location were taken from the next nearest
node or accelerometer. This greatly helped to ensure all necessary data points to create the MAC
were captured. Compared to the self-MAC from the compliance FRF data, the self-MAC from the
indexed displacement data in Figure 7 is much closer to identity, though still not perfect.

3.4 Explicit Hemisphere Simulation Efforts
The primary objective of developing a dynamic, explicit FEM of the hemisphere was to answer the
question of what modeling efforts are needed to inform modal tests and vibration environments?,
specifically for more complicated and nonlinear systems. The hemisphere is a simple structure, but
with profuse test data. There is opportunity to validate the dynamic response of the hemisphere in
the explicit simulations, which would increase confidence in the methodology behind building those
models (selecting BCs, filtering, timestepping, etc.). Afterwards, when the time comes to build a
nonlinear system that necessitates an explicit simulation, we would be more confident in it having
validated the methodology with SE-A.

The dynamic response of the hemisphere during testing cannot be captured in an implicit model
so efforts were made to refine the explicit model created last FY in order to better capture the
dynamic response of the test article. This section will provide an overview of the model, detail
those modeling efforts and the simulation’s runtime, and discuss future work for what can be done
to continue investigating the dynamic response of the model.

3.4.1 Model Overview
The geometry and mesh were based on the implicit hemisphere FEM, which was modeled as a
perfectly free, linear elastic, undamped system. In reality, this is not accurate so the explicit model
includes the fishing lines (one of the boundary conditions used in the series of experiments con-
ducted in FY24) in order to replicate the experimental testing conditions. These fishing lines were
constrained at their ends (where they would be attached to some fixturing to suspend the hemi-
sphere) and were coupled to the hemisphere with a continuum distributing coupling type. This
coupling method was based on work done by a Q-18 engineer who modeled fishing lines on a plate
to represent a "free-free" shaker test [21]. From speaking with him and after reviewing the model,
he recommended the same approach be taken for the hemisphere. The explicit hemisphere model
is shown in Figure 10.

The hemisphere is made of aluminum 6061-T6 [22] and the fishing lines are HDPE Densetec [23].
The nominal material properties of both are tabulated in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. All parts
were meshed in Abaqus 2023. Information about the model’s mesh can be found in Table 3.

3.4.2 Model Calibration
The experiments conducted in FY24 consisted of hitting the hemisphere 5 times at 36 different
locations that comprise the geometry of the test article in the testing software. Those 5 hits
would then get averaged for future post-processing. If one of the hammer hit locations had an
accelerometer, the hits would occur around the hemisphere. One hammer hit location could take
anywhere between 20-30 seconds and though that data is available, the simulation length would
grow untenable with a lot of "dead" time in between hammer hits. To mitigate this, the explicit
simulations focused on only the first hit within the first 0.1 seconds of the experiment. The force
data from the experiment was passed to the model in the form of an amplitude input deck, and the
model was loaded in the same location as the experiment. The direction of the force was towards
the center of the hemisphere (the origin of the global coordinate system). Figure 13 shows the input
force data from the experiment.

Page 16 Model Calibration

 Figure 13: Explicit hemisphere loading amplitude curve.

Simulation results were pulled from the elements where accelerometers would be located and are the
focus for all comparisons. All hammer hit locations occurred at the nodes were the accelerometers
were located. Those results were generated from history outputs, all with the antialiasing option in
effect. The simulations were sampled at a rate of 200,000 Hz in order to ensure resolution quality
while the experiments were sampled at 3,200 Hz. Even though the results are being pulled from the
elements where the accelerometers are located, effectively mimicking accelerometer data, Abaqus
Explicit will only give those outputs in the global coordinate system. In order to obtain data from
the local coordinate system of each node, a unit normal vector that is always pointing to the center
of the hemisphere is first calculated. Then tangent vectors that make a right-handed orthonormal
basis are calculated using that unit normal vector. With those, a transformation matrix is populated
and applied to all of the global accelerations to transform them to local accelerations. These vectors
were confirmed to match with the experimentalists’ local coordinate systems, which can be seen in
Figure 14.

The following list is a summary of all efforts made in calibrating the model results to the experimental
results. The goals were to match as close as possible to the acceleration response, FRF curves, PSD
curves, and modal parameters.

 • Running the simulations with both antialiasing and aliasing.

 – No significant differences in the results.

 • Parameter study of the alpha damping term in the aluminum material card ranging between
 0.001 and 0.1. Also applying damping at the step definition.

 – No significant differences in the results, which is undesirable. After each hit, the experi-
 mentalists stop the hemisphere’s ringing by pinching the structure, effectively damping.
 Even with the damped load data being applied, the hemisphere continues to ring and
 oscillate from the initial excitation.

 • Impact locations at every accelerometer location.

 – The simulation results are very sensitive to impact location and which "accelerometer" is
 being sampled. One preliminary prediction was applying the load at a given accelerom-
 eter node would result in nosier data at that node and quieter data at an accelerometer
 node farther away, but that wasn’t always the case.

 • Including gravity in the simulations.

 – Adding gravity created exceedingly high and unrealistic peak accelerations for some of the
 accelerometer nodes that would affect the creation of the FRFs. For other accelerometer
 nodes, there was no clear correlation for making the data nosier or smoother.
Page 18 Model Calibration

 – It was observed that the fishing lines would stretch under the effects of gravity in the
 simulation, physically lowering the hemisphere up to 10 mm which is unrealistic. This
 indicates how the fishing lines are modeled need to be more closely looked at since they
 should be rigid.

 • Running a "free-free" model with no fishing lines or boundary conditions.

 – No significant differences in the results.

 • Removing the fishing lines and constraining the hemisphere where the fishing lines are located
 (only in the Y direction).

 – This generated lots of noise in the Y and and Z directions, and left almost no response
 in the X directions.

3.4.3 Model Future Work
Overall, the most significant factors affecting the explicit simulation results are the fishing lines/boundary
conditions, the impact location, and which accelerometer node is being sampled from. Future work
should be focused primarily on updating how the fishing lines are modeled and checking to make
sure they do not stretch under the effects of gravity. After that, the focus should shift to studying
the acceleration response at each node where an accelerometer is located. To keep things tractable,
only one node should be excited with the input load until the output responses match more closely
to the experiments. The following list outlines planned future work for the explicit model if SE-A
was not wrapping up this FY.

 • Update the orientation of the fishing lines in the model to match their angled position in the
 experiments.

 • Make sure the fishing lines are not tensile only.

 • Filter the acceleration data for both the simulations and experiments since they are noisy in
 general.

 • Apply a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the acceleration data for comparison.

 • Physically model the accelerometers and average the responses of each element using the
 ACOM output variable.

 • Continue investigating damping in the model through the many damping options Abaqus
 offers.

 • Make comparisons between other experiments using fishing lines.

 • Model other boundary conditions like the bungee cords and piano wire.

 • Recreate the mode shapes from the displacements in the explicit simulations for comparison.

4.6 Explicit Simulations
Throughout the course of working on the explicit simulations, the simulation acceleration time-
histories were never able to match qualitatively the experiments’. The oscillatory behavior is cap-
tured, but the amplitudes and peak accelerations consistently differ. Additionally, the explicit
simulations do not dampen out like the experiments do since there is nothing to stop their ringing.
The response in the x-direction is the one that is most damped while the response in the z-direction
is the least damped, though this is dependent on which node is being impacted and which accelerom-
eter node is being sampled. Figure 50 shows the acceleration time-history response for one of the
accelerometer nodes from the explicit simulations.

as seen in Figure 51. Though the explicit simulation FRFs are distinct and have clear peaks with

5 Supplemental Efforts: Los Alamos Dynamics Summer School

5.1 Los Alamos Dynamics Summer School
In FY25, an SE-A inspired project was used in the LADSS program. The LADSS program is a
Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL)-run summer school where a select number of students
work on dynamics related projects over a 10-week period. This particular project involved iden-
tifying mode shapes using DIC—a contactless optical technique used to measure full-field surface
displacements—in order to determine the feasibility of DIC as a replacement to standard accelerom-
eter measurement methods. DIC works by capturing high-resolution images of a speckled surface
before and after deformation. By measuring the shift in the pixel intensity patterns, DIC can track
the movement of individual points on the surface of an object.

The students were tasked with:

  1. Developing an analysis workflow to determine the modal parameters of an aluminum plate
 using analytical models and modal analysis in finite element software.

  2. Characterizing the modal parameters and sources of uncertainty from the analytical and FEM
 simulations.

  3. Performing preliminary static tests using DIC to determine the feasibility of extracting dis-
 placements from obtained images.

  4. Post-processing the static DIC data using Correlated Solution’s VIC 3D to demonstrate the
 extraction of displacements.

  5. Performing hammer impact tests using both DIC and accelerometers.

  6. Determining the maximum achievable frequency that the cameras could capture.

  7. Comparing the modal parameters from the analytical model, FEM simulations, accelerometer
 data, and the DIC data, as well as reporting error metrics between the different methods.

In this study, the students proved that DIC is a valid measurement technique for determining modal
properties up to 5000 Hz and that DIC can be performed successfully on modal impact tests in which
all modes within a defined frequency range are excited at once. A more detailed description of the
work performed and the results can be found in LA-UR-25-29447 [19]. The LADSS program requires
the students to produce a conference paper for IMAC by the end of the program. Their paper was
accepted and the students will be presenting their work at IMAC XLIV in Palm Springs California
in 2026.

6.3 Lessons Learned
Here, lessons learned throughout SE-A’s lifetime are discussed.

  1. It is possible to efficiently optimize sensor placements from simulations in order to provide
 reliable and relevant test data.

 • For modal tests, the frequency range of interest, number of available sensors, and sensor
 placement availability are the most important factors to consider when trying to optimize
 the sensor placements. There will usually be a tradeoff between frequency range of
 interest and number of available sensors, as well as testing time, that will determine the
 results of the optimization. In general, one sensor per mode is recommended though
 the features of interest of some modes can overlap, thus reducing the number of sensors
 needed.
Page 52 Lessons Learned

 • These optimized sensor placements can be verified using the MAC both before and after
 testing.
 • This optimization method is independent of the size or complexity of the test article,
 making it highly adaptable to more complicated and relevant structures with the only
 tradeoffs being optimization time.
 • Supports milestone grading criteria through improved system modeling to
 inform testing.

  2. Keeping track of all hammer hits is necessary when trying to remove rejected hammer hits
 from modal impact hammer test data.

 • Supports milestone grading criteria through methods for data handling.

  3. All mode shapes of a structure, including the symmetric modes, need to be considered their
 own unique mode shape for data post-processing, though this isn’t strictly necessary for sensor
 optimization.

 • Supports milestone grading criteria through methods for data handling.

  4. The symmetric mode shapes of axisymmetric structures need to be confirmed "locked" to
 ensure repeatability and reliability of results. This can typically be achieved through mass-
 loading induced by sensors.

 • This concept also applies to FEA simulations and is proven in Section 3.2 [18].
 • Supports milestone grading criteria through guidelines for testing, test set-
 up, and new modeling techniques.

  5. Modeling appropriate boundary conditions in dynamic, explicit simulations is key to recreating
 and eventually predicting experimental results from modal impact hammer tests.

 • These simulations are often hampered by long runtimes caused by high sampling of data.
 Utilization of high performance computing (HPC) is recommended for these simulations.

  6. Sensor placement and mass-induced loading from the sensors proved to have the most signif-
 icant effects on the modal parameters. Sensor sensitivity shifted the natural frequencies, but
 to a lesser degree. The sensor mounting interface had minimal effect on the modal parameters
 and piano wire was the only BC that significantly shifted the modal parameters while the
 others were mostly in agreement with each other.

 • Boundary conditions have the highest impact when comparing experiments to a simulated
 free-free environment and are large contributors to the impacts of the other categories
 of uncertainty. Without more experiments focused on isolating these differences, it is
 difficult to provide reasoning for which categories of uncertainty had more impact than
 the others.
 • Many of the trends and behaviors identified in SE-A are largely test-article dependent.
 Mass-loading from heavier accelerometers had the most significant effect on the range of
 possible modal parameters, but this is heavily dependent on the size and complexity of
 the test article when compared to the size and number of accelerometers used.
Lessons Learned Page 53

 • In terms of the hemisphere and which categories of uncertainty contributed the most
 to the spread of its modal parameters, the descending order of importance would be
 accelerometer mass > accelerometer placement > accelerometer sensitivity/boundary
 conditions > mounting interface.
 • Supports milestone grading criteria through guidelines for test set-up.

  7. Creating an appropriately sized mesh geometry in Testlab is necessary to avoid mode shape
 distortions.

 • Too coarse of a mesh size will result in degradation of the mode shapes due to spatial
 aliasing. Too fine of a mesh will generally result in longer test times. It is best to find a
 balance between mode shape resolution and testing time.
 • Supports milestone grading criteria through guidelines for test set-up.

  8. It is important to select an appropriate hammer tip that will impart enough energy into the
 unit under test (UUT) without overloading the accelerometers.

 • The hammer tip can be used as a coarse adjustment for input excitation.
 • Supports milestone grading criteria through guidelines for test set-up.

  9. The accelerometers must be securely attached to the UUT. If the accelerometers are not
 securely attached, they will introduce noise into the data.

 • Supports milestone grading criteria through guidelines for test set-up.

 10. Poor cable management could lead to added noise in the data.
