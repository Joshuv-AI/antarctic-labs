Surgeon workload in supervised robotic colorectal surgery: a surgical task load index study
Authors: Yasuhiro Ishiyama, Yasumitsu Hirano, Sohei Akuta, Yume Minagawa, Akihito Nakanishi, Yusuke Nishi, Hisashi Hayashi, Takatsugu Fujii, Hirofumi Sugita, Chikashi Hiranuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobot-assisted colorectal surgery (RAS) is increasingly adopted; however, surgeon workload during supervised training and its determinants remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a single-center study combining a prospective surgical task load index (Surgical-TLX) assessment and a retrospective cohort analysis (October 2023–August 2025). Junior surgeons were defined as non-Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery (JSES)–certified surgeons who had < 10 robot-assisted colorectal cases as the primary operator and were within 8 years after graduation. All junior cases were performed under direct supervision by JSES-certified surgeons throughout the procedure. The primary outcome was Surgical-TLX workload (six domains). Secondary outcomes included operative time, console time, blood loss, Clavien–Dindo complications, length of stay, and pathological findings.ResultsSurgical-TLX was assessed in 72 cases by 10 surgeons. Compared with JSES-certified surgeons, junior surgeons had higher mental, temporal, task complexity, and situational awareness workload. In multivariable models, high temporal burden was associated with advanced tumor factors (T ≥ 3 and N-positive disease). Operative and console times were longer for junior surgeons, whereas blood loss, postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ 2 or ≥ 3), length of stay, and pathological outcomes were comparable.ConclusionsUnder structured supervision, RAS can be implemented safely for junior surgeons despite higher perceived workload. Surgical-TLX may help visualize cognitive burden and inform graded autonomy and supervisory strategies, particularly for advanced tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12979-z.

Robot-assisted surgery (RAS) is rapidly expanding in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment with high-precision manipulation and minimal invasiveness, demonstrating advantages over conventional laparoscopic surgery. RAS contributes to reduced intraoperative blood loss and shorter postoperative recovery [1, 2]. Furthermore, Hamamoto et al. argued that, although operative costs are higher for RAS, the total medical costs remain comparable owing to reduced postoperative complications [3].

However, whether RAS can be safely performed by less-experienced surgeons or those with limited laparoscopic experience remains to be fully elucidated. In Japan, the certification system of the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery (JSES) is well-established, and certified surgeons possess substantial laparoscopic experience and technical proficiency [4]. In clinical practice, improved outcomes in laparoscopic colorectal surgery when JSES-certified surgeons participate have been reported [5, 6]. Conversely, some reports suggest that laparoscopic experience is not essential for RAS; however, clinical data examining this remain limited [7, 8] and no studies have compared surgeons’ stress levels. In modern surgical practice, surgeon workload and stress have become important issues because they may directly influence operative performance, decision-making, and patient safety. Excessive workload can reduce attentional capacity, impair situational awareness, delay intraoperative decision-making, and potentially increase the risk of technical errors. These concerns may be particularly relevant in robotic surgery, where the surgeon operates from a console, is physically separated from the patient and bedside team, and must integrate visual information, instrument control, communication, and procedural decision-making in a highly technology-dependent environment.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on intraoperative workload and stress during robotic surgery. A systematic review of robot-assisted radical prostatectomy highlighted the importance of evaluating surgeons’ intraoperative workload in robotic procedures. In addition, stress assessment using the Senhance robotic surgical system demonstrated the feasibility and clinical relevance of evaluating stress entities in robotic surgery. However, data regarding surgeon workload in robot-assisted colorectal surgery, particularly in supervised training settings involving junior surgeons, remain limited [9, 10].

We hypothesized that robot-assisted colorectal surgery could be performed safely by junior surgeons within a structured and supervised educational environment, while surgeon workload may differ according to experience level. Our study objective was to compare surgeon workload and short-term outcomes of robot-assisted colorectal surgery between JSES-certified surgeons and junior surgeons under direct supervision, and to explore clinical and surgeon-related factors associated with high workload.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a hybrid design incorporating a prospective observational study (Surgical-TLX) and a retrospective cohort study (clinical outcomes) conducted at a single institution. Between October 2023 and August 2025, 384 RAS procedures were performed for CRC using the da Vinci Xi system at the International Medical Center of Saitama Medical University. We analyzed 195 consecutive cases, excluding those performed by surgeons with more than 50 RAS cases. The patients were divided into the JSES-certified (n = 94 cases) and junior (n = 101 cases) groups. For the Surgical-TLX assessment, 72 cases were prospectively collected from the above population, including only those who could complete self-assessment within 24 h postoperatively and consented to study participation. Surgeons were categorized according to their JSES certification status (certified vs. non-certified). The primary outcome was surgeon workload (Surgical-TLX six-domain scores), and secondary outcomes included operative time, console time, blood loss, postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo classification [11]), length of hospital stay, and pathological findings.

Junior surgeons were defined as surgeons who were not certified by the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery (JSES), had performed fewer than 10 robot-assisted colorectal procedures as the primary operator, and were within 8 years of graduation from medical school. All procedures performed by junior surgeons were conducted under direct supervision of JSES-certified surgeons, who were present throughout the operation and provided guidance as needed to ensure patient safety. Supervision was delivered according to a standardized institutional training framework, with consistent supervisory presence and guidance principles applied across junior cases.

The primary surgeon was defined as the surgeon who performed the majority of the procedure at the console. Supervising surgeons intervened only when necessary to ensure safety.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion was primary tumor resection for CRC using the da Vinci Xi system. The exclusion criteria were obstructive CRC, cases requiring combined resection of other organs, those with lateral lymph node dissection, and those performed by surgeons with > 50 RAS procedures. As the learning curve for robotic surgery is reported to plateau at 25–49 cases, surgeons with 50 or more cases were excluded [12, 13].

Primary outcome

Surgeon stress assessment (Surgical-TLX)

The original validated surgical task load index (Surgical-TLX), a surgery-specific workload assessment tool based on the NASA-TLX, was used without modification to evaluate intraoperative surgeon workload [14]. No robotic surgery-specific items were added, and no changes were made to the domain structure, scoring method, or weighting procedure. The Surgical-TLX quantifies six domains: mental demands, physical demands, temporal demands, task complexity, situational awareness, and distraction. Each surgeon rated these domains on a visual analog scale ranging from 0 (“very low”) to 20 (“very high”). For the weighting procedure, these raw ratings were converted to a 0–100 scale according to the original Surgical-TLX scoring method. In the present study, the original version was applied to robot-assisted colorectal surgery to assess intraoperative workload while preserving the validity of the established instrument.

Weighting followed the “Weighted rating” and “Pairwise comparisons” procedures shown in Appendix 1. Briefly, pairwise comparisons were performed among the six domains, resulting in 15 comparisons. The number of times each domain was selected as the more important contributor to workload was counted as its weighting score, ranging from 0 to 5. The weighted score for each domain was calculated by multiplying the converted rating score, ranging from 0 to 100, by the weighting score. The Overall Stress score was then calculated by summing the weighted scores across all six domains and dividing the total by 15. Therefore, the final Overall Stress score is expressed on a 0–100 scale rather than as a simple 0–20 raw score.

Secondary endpoints

Patient characteristics (age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, tumor location, and preoperative TNM stage), operative findings (operative time, blood loss, intraoperative complications, postoperative complications, and postoperative length of stay), and postoperative pathological findings were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was determined assuming that a 10-point difference in Surgical-TLX scores between groups would be clinically meaningful. Previous studies have reported that a 10-point difference is clinically significant [13]. Assuming a standard deviation of 15 points based on prior literature and pilot data, a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, and statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated using a two-sample t-test. Accordingly, a minimum of 36 patients per group (72 patients total) was determined necessary to detect a 10-point difference in Surgical-TLX scores between groups.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables as medians and categorical variables as n (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Effect sizes were reported with median differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Hodges–Lehmann method for continuous variables, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI for categorical variables, alongside p-values.

For multivariable analysis, linear regression was constructed with Surgical-TLX domain scores (continuous) as dependent variables, and logistic regression models were constructed with high burden in each domain (predefined as above the third quartile) as dependent variables. Logistic regression was also used for primary clinical outcomes (Clavien–Dindo ≥ 2 and ≥ 3), calculating adjusted odds ratios and 95% CI. All models included the following predefined covariates: age, sex, BMI, tumor location (colon/rectum), T stage (T ≥ 3), N stage ( ±), operative procedure, operative time, time of day (morning/afternoon), history of laparotomy, and surgeon group (JSES-certified/junior). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using JMP Pro 19 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Determinants of high Surgical-TLX burden (upper quartile)

We then evaluated factors associated with high workload in each Surgical-TLX domain. High workload was defined as a domain score above the upper quartile. In this analysis, high temporal burden was defined as a temporal demand score > 52, and high situational awareness burden was defined as a situational awareness score > 47. High temporal burden was significantly associated with advanced tumor factors, including T factor (T ≥ 3: OR 5.44, 95% CI 1.02–43.2, p = 0.04) and N factor (N-positive disease: OR 11.6, 95% CI 1.86–71.8, p = 0.0086) (Table 2). Other variables, including age > 75 years, sex, BMI > 25, tumor location, operative time, and time of day, were not significantly associated with high temporal or situational awareness burden. Discussion

This study examined whether proficiency in laparoscopic surgery influenced robot-assisted surgical outcomes. The junior group experienced significantly higher surgeon stress in the mental, temporal, task, and situational awareness domains. Junior surgeons experienced significantly higher workload across multiple Surgical-TLX domains. Under direct supervision by JSES-certified surgeons, short-term outcomes were comparable between groups, suggesting that supervised robotic training can be implemented safely while acknowledging the increased cognitive burden in less-experienced surgeons.

The NASA-TLX has been validated for assessing mental workload during laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and is associated with increased postoperative complications and mortality, proving to be useful as a surgical evaluation tool [15]. Furthermore, several reports have evaluated RAS using the NASA-TLX [16–18].

NASA-TLX is useful for assessing surgeon workload in pediatric robotic surgery, suggesting that it is a valuable assessment tool for robotic surgery [17]. Additionally, since surgeons operate from a console during robotic surgery, spatially separated from the operative field, unlike in laparoscopic or open surgery, visualizing the workload using standardized metrics, such as NASA-TLX, is useful for both educational design and safety assessment [19].

Stress assessment using Surgical-TLX is valuable for a detailed understanding of the mental and physical burdens faced by surgeons during surgery [14]. Our results suggest that junior surgeons experienced a significantly higher burden than JSES-certified surgeons in the mental, temporal, task, and situational awareness domains. The mental aspect reflects a stronger element of solo surgery in robotic surgery than that in laparoscopic surgery, revealing the impact of limited surgical experience. Regarding temporal burden, the actual operative time was prolonged, suggesting that stress was related to extended surgery duration. For task and situational awareness, although robotic manipulation can compensate for technical aspects, decision-making regarding traction direction and tissue handling may reflect differences in surgical experience.

From an educational perspective, our findings support the concept of graded autonomy in robotic colorectal surgery. While junior surgeons can achieve comparable short-term outcomes under direct supervision, the significantly higher mental, temporal, task-complexity, and situational-awareness burdens suggest that autonomy should be increased stepwise according to case complexity and trainee readiness. In particular, advanced tumors (T ≥ 3 and/or N-positive disease) were associated with higher temporal burden, indicating that these cases may require closer supervision and/or selective allocation of critical steps to experienced surgeons.

Excessive workload may negatively affect performance by reducing attentional capacity, delaying intraoperative decision-making, and impairing situational awareness. Repeated exposure to high workload may also contribute to mental and physical fatigue, reduce learning efficiency, and increase the risk of burnout among junior surgeons. Therefore, incorporating workload assessment into robotic surgery training programs may help balance patient safety, trainee development, and surgeon well-being, thereby supporting workforce sustainability [20, 21].

Surgical-TLX may also serve as a practical “intervention trigger” during training programs. For example, consistently high temporal or situational awareness scores could prompt supervisors to adjust case selection, increase intraoperative guidance, allocate challenging phases to the supervisor, or provide targeted simulation and video-based feedback before the next case. Thus, beyond measuring workload retrospectively, Surgical-TLX may function as a structured feedback loop to optimize supervision intensity, guide graded autonomy, and support safe progression toward independent performance.

This study also examined the clinical factors associated with high burden in each Surgical-TLX domain. The results suggested a strong association between temporal burden and oncological stage, increasing with lymph node metastasis and T ≥ 3 tumors.

Advanced cancer increases intraoperative decision-making and technical options and prolongs operative time and increases the possibility of conversion to open surgery, consistent with clinical experience [22, 23].

For situational awareness burden, the influence of lymph node metastasis and surgical factors was suggested. Lymph node metastasis has been reported as an independent factor for increased difficulty, which supports our findings [24]. As situational awareness largely depends on surgical experience, junior surgeons might have been more affected. This may explain why junior surgeon status was identified as a risk factor.

Herein, the operative and console times were shorter in the JSES-certified group; however, blood loss, postoperative length of stay, and postoperative complications showed no significant differences between the groups. These findings are consistent with those of Noh et al. who reported no differences in postoperative complications between experienced and inexperienced surgeons [25]. However, their finding of shorter operative time for inexperienced surgeons differed from ours. Our study also examined console time, enabling an evaluation specific to robotic manipulations. Surgical experience may influence the operative time. Conversely, for laparoscopic surgery, JSES-certified surgeons have fewer complications and shorter operative times [6], suggesting that operative time may be influenced by experience with both laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size for clinical outcome analysis. Second, the workload assessment of TLX surgeons was a subjective measure that could not completely exclude observer bias. However, the sample size required for the primary analysis met predetermined assumptions. Third, we did not examine the learning curve effects. Particularly, junior surgeons might improve their skills through experience, potentially introducing residual confounding factors in estimating between-group differences and determinants of high burden. However, no clear decrease in the overall NASA-TLX burden with increasing case numbers has been reported, suggesting that this was not essential [17]. Additionally, to minimize learning curve effects, we only compared surgeons with moderate experience, suggesting minimal learning curve influence. Finally, because Surgical-TLX data were prospectively collected from a subset of the clinical cohort, selection bias was possible. Another limitation is that Surgical-TLX was not originally developed specifically for robotic surgery. In this study, we used the original validated version without modification to preserve the established domain structure and scoring method. Although recent studies have supported the importance of workload and stress assessment in robotic surgery, further studies are needed to validate Surgical-TLX specifically in robot-assisted colorectal surgery and to examine its reliability across different robotic platforms and procedures.

In conclusion, junior surgeons experienced significantly higher workload during robot-assisted colorectal surgery, particularly in the mental, temporal, task complexity, and situational awareness domains. Because all junior cases were performed under direct supervision by JSES-certified surgeons, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting surgeon workload within a supervised training environment rather than independent performance. Surgical-TLX may provide a useful framework to visualize cognitive burden and to guide case selection and graded autonomy during robotic surgical training.
