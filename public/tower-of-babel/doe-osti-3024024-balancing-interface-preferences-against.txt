Overview
STRUDEL is an open source project enabling teams to plan and create user-centered software
for science communities. One facet of this project is the STRUDEL Planning Framework, a tool
to help science projects plan their software development and integrate UX methodologies.
Based on years of collaboration with scientific software projects, the STRUDEL team developed
a typology of these projects. The typology divided up their characteristics into categories like
science domain, primary purpose, roles and composition of the project team, team member
turnover, size of user base, UI types, and common user task flows. Our hope for the STRUDEL
Planning Framework was that by understanding the meaningful dimensions of a software
project, we would be able offer tailored, expert guidance to PIs and research software engineers
(RSEs) new to user experience work.

Developing the Planning Framework involved iterating through multiple designs. We first
prototyped the tool as a quiz that asked users a short series of questions about their software
project and returned advice on how to begin incorporating UX methods into their work and what
that might mean for their development process. After gathering feedback on this prototype and
recognizing the need to offer more customization in our guidance, we pivoted to a new interface:
a custom GPT called STRUDEL Bot. While initially our greatest challenge was in delivering
properly tailored advice, this change in approach meant that our primary concern
became ensuring the trustworthiness of that information.

Below we describe in more detail the process of developing and evaluating STRUDEL Bot as
well as our current strategy for its continued stewardship, ensuring it delivers relevant, high
quality, custom advice.

Quiz Based Approaches
Our vision was tidy and clear: we would quiz users about their project, and depending on the
characteristics they reported, we would offer different advice. We imagined offering project
timelines, information on what kind of contributors would be needed, and information on how to
conduct UX work with the resources they had on hand.

We first worked to determine what we should ask users, narrowing the STRUDEL typology to a
minimal set of questions that would allow us to offer tailored advice. However, we found that
without extremely specific information about users’ software projects, our ability to comment was
limited. Absent details from users, our advice was often the same: communicate your value
proposition, use what data you have on hand to learn about the issues your users have, use
interviews/usability testing/heuristic analysis to get more targeted data, iterate on solutions, and
improve your documentation. The extent of data collection we would recommend, or the
documentation we might propose may differ somewhat, but, on the whole, our advice could not
be effectively tailored without far more specific information from users.

Conversely, asking more questions posed a different problem. The more characteristics we
attended to, the more burdensome the process would be for users and the more complex our
system would need to be. We could not simply collate a list of recommendations, one for each
project characteristic; our advice had to make sense as a whole and we thus had to consider
how response A would interact with responses B, C, D, etc.

Ultimately, after iterating through several prototypes with various degrees of logic incorporated,
we opted to gather feedback and see what strengths and weaknesses potential users saw in
our tool. We talked with eight participants representing software projects of various sizes and
multiple organizations (including a national lab and multiple universities). Interviewees took the
planning framework quiz and reviewed their output (1-3 pages of text) prior to our feedback
sessions. Participants included: one professor who writes code, one research software
engineering consultant, one academic research software engineer, three Lawrence Berkeley
National Lab employees, and four managers.

A key finding from our interviews was the routine appraisal that some of the advice we offered
was of real value, but that it felt too generic. Information on Nielsen’s heuristics for usability
design (presented in the advice as “design principles”) was particularly popular and interviewees

had specific colleagues in mind to share the advice with. Participants did not want to send it to
colleagues with user experience training though, thinking it to be information they would already
know. It was also clear that if the advice were better tailored to the participants’ circumstances,
then it could provide more value. One manager, for example, noted that while some research
projects might want guidance on staffing and project timelines for UX work, the advice she
wanted would be exclusively about what “this one developer or their manager can do.”

When discussing the possibility of using an interactive AI agent to deliver the advice, some
participants expressed interest, noting it would make a useful supplement to documentation and
could be more engaging and specific. One participant appreciated that an interactive interface
might reduce the “wall of text” that we had provided in our quiz output. However, others
expressed “a little bot fatigue” and some concern that they would be unable to recognize faulty
advice. Participants also sought skimmability and easy sharing with others.

Turning to Generative AI
While we revised our prototypes, generative AI tools took off. These tools would not only be able
to tailor advice like we wanted but they could create other materials like interview scripts that we
had initially assumed would have to be created by users (though with our advice to guide them).
Moreover, generative AI offered a way to avoid sending users a deluge of information to sift
through: rather than one long read out covering everything they might need to know, they could
receive some advice and then ask for more of the type that they needed. Despite having heard
some reservations about AI agents during feedback sessions, AI offered solutions and
opportunities not available otherwise and we moved ahead with a new prototype that we called
STRUDEL Bot.

This pivot marked a key change in our project. We had been dealing with one problem: “How do
we tailor high quality but generic advice without creating a complex system that requires
onerous user input?” Now we had to address a new one: “How do we know the advice
STRUDEL Bot gives is high quality?” Previously we had not been concerned with the quality of
our recommendations, only their specificity and how best to deliver them. By offering advice
through a generative AI tool, we solved the complexity and delivery questions but were left with
issues of trust and accuracy.

STRUDEL Bot’s construction
We first prototyped STRUDEL Bot by simply giving detailed prompts to a generative AI tool,
telling it to assume the role of an expert on UX and STRUDEL. After evaluating its plausibility as
an implementation of our planning framework, we moved on to developing STRUDEL Bot as a
custom GPT made available to users for free through ChatGPT’s GPT Store.

The GPT is configured through a set of instructions and files attached as additional knowledge
for the bot. We supplied the bot with several websites and repositories the STRUDEL project

 4

manages1 as well as tutorials our team created to teach people user research methods (e.g., a
tutorial on interviewing). We also included the advice we had written for our earlier quiz-based
implementations of the planning framework and the STRUDEL typology. Additionally, in the
instructions, we gave a list of assumptions that the bot can make (e.g., “Users have little
experience conducting UX work”) and gave guidelines around when to make certain
recommendations (e.g. “Avoid recommending surveys due to the likelihood that they will be
poorly constructed. If a user really wants to conduct a survey, give tips on how to ensure
questions are well phrased, scales are appropriate, the length isn't onerous, and no participants
are coerced into responding.”).

STRUDEL Bot is instructed to ask users a set of questions based on the STRUDEL typology
and to reference the STRUDEL project’s documentation and resources before responding to a
prompt. Through trial and error we refined the instructions, making changes to the list of
assumptions, the number of questions STRUDEL Bot should ask users before responding, the
resources the bot could reference, and adding additional guidelines to ensure the bot effectively
delivered information. We sought to optimize the number of questions so that users would not
be fatigued when answering and so that initial outputs would be highly relevant, making it less
likely users would need to request a correction.

Evaluating STRUDEL Bot
As we prototyped STRUDEL Bot we continuously compared our implementation to ChatGPT
5.2’s responses to a set of prompts. To avoid bias from previous conversations, Temporary Chat
was used for testing. Three prompts were repeatedly used:
 1.​ Create a project plan that estimates how many people, with what skills, need to work and
 for how long to achieve the project goal quoted below. Also describe what kind of user
 experience work would be useful and provide an estimated timeline to completion with
 steps along the way. Write this concisely so that I can include it in a funding proposal;
 excluding the timeline, I have 0.5 a page available to describe this work.

 variants to any synthetic biologist, we will create a modular software stack, with a Python
 API (e.g., using FastAPI) at its base upon which we will build higher level interface
 components (e.g. REST APIs and/or web-based GUIs with ReactJS as for GUIs in other
 projects; or Python CLIs or Jupyter Notebook UIs) for integration with existing BER
 capabilities and endpoints. AI agents will be able to leverage the APIs in coordination
 with core AI capabilities and query to develop sophisticated and automated knowledge
 discovery workflows. Since humans will need to use these same interfaces, we will
 de-risk design and development with targeted user experience (UX) research, an
 approach we have successfully used in other multi-disciplinary projects.”

We took notes on the differences between STRUDEL Bot and ChatGPT 5.2’s responses.
Evaluation was less straightforward than anticipated, largely because generative AI models are
non-deterministic and thus we were tempted to point out trends before any could truly be
recognized. For instance, our first comparison featured STRUDEL Bot as implemented via a
detailed prompt. The baseline ChatGPT 5.2 response gave a much more aggressive timeline
when asked for one, estimating 18 person-months compared to STRUDEL Bot’s proposal 52.5
person-months. This difference aligned with our expectations that ChatGPT might be more likely
to propose timelines like those seen in industry settings. However, repeated prompts showed
that this pattern was not consistent—in other trials STRUDEL Bot offered lower estimates than
ChatGPT. It was important for us to note our confirmation bias as we continued evaluation.

What was consistently evident was that, by supplying STRUDEL Bot with our documentation
and instructions, we were able to refine the language and advice that the AI used so that it
aligned more with scientific settings. STRUDEL Bot, for example, repeatedly used the term
Research Software Engineer (RSE) while ChatGPT did not. We also successfully instructed
STRUDEL Bot to remind users that they should contact their IRB to discuss the possibility of
needing an ethics review. STRUDEL Bot also correctly noted that qualitative analysis will take
dramatically longer if users are seeking publication-level rigor rather than only generating
internal insights. The differences between STRUDEL Bot and the unconfigured ChatGPT were
sometimes subtle, but these contextually relevant details can help build trust with users and
ensure UX work goes smoothly.

Both STRUDEL Bot and ChatGPT offered reasonable suggestions for project plans, interview
protocols, and designing a CLI tool. Occasionally the outputs would include comments that were
not completely correct, a hazard for an educational resource or someone planning a project, but
these issues were relatively minor. For instance, when prompted to create an interview protocol,
STRUDEL Bot once noted that semi-structured interviews are recommended “when:

 ●​ You have a real system
 ●​ You want to improve workflows or prioritize UI changes”

These points are true, but these are not the only occasions where semi-structured interviews
are useful. Interviews are, in fact, a key user research method when planning projects, before a
real system is in place. We hypothesize that STRUDEL Bot was likely pulling from
documentation we supplied on rapid usability testing, a method that requires some kind of
system to interact with (prototype or production).

Next Steps
STRUDEL Bot has been released as a prototype version of our Planning Framework. As
STRUDEL Bot is improved, we will focus on the new challenge of ensuring the trustworthiness
and accuracy of responses. To stay relevant, the documentation we supply STRUDEL Bot must
be maintained as well. For example, while not necessary a few short years ago, useful UX
advice should now include commentary on how and when AI tools can be integrated.

Our current strategy is to develop more documentation and static educational resources, e.g.,
tip sheets and tutorials, and include these in STRUDEL Bot’s knowledge base. Because
STRUDEL Bot clearly draws from its current instructions and knowledge base, allowing it to
offer scientifically relevant advice, we anticipate that bolstered documentation will enable more
high quality responses. Another key aspect of improving STRUDEL Bot will be gathering
feedback from users: As we come to better understand the prompts users provide and the
issues they encounter, we will be able to iteratively improve the framework. At present, we have
a feedback form available for users to describe their experience and request the STRUDEL
team to reach out.

Conclusion
We implemented a planning framework for scientific software projects as an AI chatbot,
STRUDEL Bot. STRUDEL Bot asks users a series of questions about their software project,
using responses to provide customized advice on improving user experiences with scientific
software. This approach to offering guidance satisfies the STRUDEL team’s goals and
addresses feedback from users of an early prototype. STRUDEL Bot is able to provide tailored
advice relevant to the scientific context. It asks users questions before delivering that advice,
theoretically reducing the likelihood that a user needs to request corrections. However, by
leveraging an AI agent, we place a greater burden on users to evaluate the credibility of the
advice they receive and must work ourselves to continuously improve its knowledge base. While
not a codebase the STRUDEL team owns, we must still steward it.

As STRUDEL Bot said itself, it “remains an evolving prototype, but it demonstrates a practical
path toward delivering user experience guidance that is both accessible and tailored to scientific
software teams.”
