DNA metabarcoding reveals dietary niche partitioning among threatened Himalayan Ungulates based on chloroplast rbcL and nuclear ITS2 markers
Authors: Mohsin Javid, Khursheed Ahmad, Orus Ilyas, Affifullah Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiet is the most important axis of niche differentiation in ungulates and mediates coexistence, habitat requirements. Himalaya amplify dietary dynamics because seasonal snow cover and plant phenology decreases resource availability. In this study fecal DNA metabarcoding was used to quantify diet and the dietary niche separation among five sympatric wild ungulates Hangul (Cervus hanglu hanglu), Kashmir markhor (Capra falconeri cashmiriensis), Kashmir musk deer (Moschus cupreus), Himalayan goral (Naemorhedus goral), and Himalayan ibex (Capra sibirica). Fresh fecal samples were collected throughout the Kashmir Valley, western Himalaya across the elevational and habitat gradients. We amplified two plant barcodes (chloroplast rbcL and nuclear ITS2) and sequenced the amplicons using Oxford Nanopore. Diets were analyzed by relative read abundance, functional traits, dietary diversity, niche breadth, and interspecific overlap. Across the seasonal diet profiles, we detected 208 plant species from 174,871 assigned reads. Hangul summer diet exhibits the broadest diet among species (richness = 115 taxa, H′ = 4.48, Levins’ B = 64.7) with a diffuse core of many co-dominant taxa, indicating generalist mixed feeding during peak plant productivity. Musk deer during autumn showed the narrowest diet (richness = 50, B = 14.3), indicating strong seasonal concentration on a smaller set of plants. Pairwise dietary overlap (Pianka’s O) ranged from 0.136 to 0.985, showing both convergence and partitioning among the ungulate species. The results are consistent with a landscape-level coexistence model in which sympatric Kashmir ungulates share a broad forage base but segregate through differences in functional feeding (grazing vs. browsing tendencies), dominance structure, and seasonal shift. The study used composite species-season templates, the results are interpreted as landscape level diet profiles rather than individual or population level variance estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02554-4.

Introduction

Diet in ungulates is the most important and main axis of the ecological niche. It links consumers to vegetation dynamics through energy and nutrient acquisition constraints and the spatial distribution of edible biomass. In herbivores, these mechanisms link feeding decisions to migration and habitat selection. Animals locate places where suitable forage is accessible and safe to maximize the benefit ratio over cost. In sympatric ungulates, dietary niche segregation can reduce interspecific competition and facilitate coexistence., High diet similarity in resources limited landscape can intensify competitive interactions and increase sensitivity to habitat degradation and human land use [28, 34]. Seasonality also strengthens these processes in temperate ecosystems.

Across the Himalaya, snow cover, low temperatures, and plant phenology shape forage availability over seasons., This shift the relative accessibility of plant biomass in meadows, forest understories and woody browse in shrublands and forests. During the summer season, alpine and subalpine meadows provide abundant forbs and graminoids, forest edges and riparian corridors provide high-quality browse and understory herbs. But the competition with livestock is high along the alpine pastures with seasonal herders [19, 33]. In winter, herbaceous production declines and access to high-elevation pastures is restricted due to snow, so ungulates increase dependency on woody browse, evergreen foliage, bark, or residual forage at lower elevations and in sheltered topographic. These seasonal constraints develop specific diet shifts and interspecific niche partitioning through time and space. This leads to periods of relaxed competition during resource abundance and potential convergence during resource deficient winter [20].

Marker and primer choice is key and of more importance for metabarcoding performance. To study plants the Consortium for the Barcode of Life (CBOL) recommended two plastid loci (rbcL and matK) as core plant barcodes. They combine broad primer universality with provide useful taxonomic signal [8]. In diet metabarcoding -DNA is extracted from degraded fecal samples so we used loci and primer sets that can amplify reliably and produce amplicons which are compatible with downstream sequencing and retaining identification power. The chloroplast gene rbcL has consequently been widely adopted as a robust plant barcode and is supported by expanding reference libraries that improve assignment accuracy when rbcL is used alone or in multi-locus pipelines [5, 13, 17, 22]. Along with the chloroplast rbcL, complementary nuclear markers increase resolution [27] The ribosomal ITS2 region has been validated as a powerful barcode for plant identification and is frequently used to enhance taxonomic discrimination in mixed plant samples [9, 27]. Recent primer development has emphasized short-amplicon ITS2 primers with high universality and resolution for diet metabarcoding across tropical and temperate floras [24, 39]. Utility for ITS2 is adequate subjected to reference database because all loci are subject to amplification and recovery biases. Combining markers can reduce locus-specific blind spots and improve recovery of both dominant and minor diet components [5, 11]. Interpreting the sequence reads also requires care, read counts are informative but can be distorted by differential amplification efficiencies and biomass-to-DNA relationships, so they are best treated as semi-quantitative indices rather than direct measures of biomass intake [11].

In this study we apply a dual-marker (rbcL + ITS2) fecal DNA metabarcoding framework to characterize dietary niche structure of the Kashmir ungulate community. We use a composite sampling design pooled DNA of 24 fecal samples per species season. This helped derive landscape-level diet profiles that were logistically feasible and representative across a mountain landscape The study focus five threatened sympatric wild ungulate species, Hangul (Kashmir red deer), Kashmir markhor, Himalayan musk deer, Himalayan goral, and Himalayan ibex. The composite designs reduce the ability to estimate within-group variance, but provide robust seasonal output of dominant forage taxa and functional composition at species level that are directly relevant for landscape-level management and for testing hypotheses about community niche organization [4, 11, 29]. However, the composite design cannot estimate within-group variance, individual dietary variation, or formal population-level hypothesis tests; these outputs should therefore be interpreted as descriptive species-season summaries.

In this dual-marker framework, rbcL and ITS2 were interpreted as complementary rather than identical sources of evidence. rbcL was used primarily for broad plant recovery because of primer universality and stronger reference coverage, whereas ITS2 was used to improve taxonomic resolution among closely related taxa. When marker-level assignments differed, species-level names were treated conservatively and retained only when supported by database evidence and ecological plausibility; otherwise, the assignment was interpreted at genus or family level.

Diet in ungulates is the most important and main axis of the ecological niche, this is the first study in the western Himalaya over the diet of the ungulates. Hangul is the critical endangered species restricted to 100 sq. km area in Kashmir valley and has not been studied for important ecological parameters till now. Markhor, Musk deer, Ibex are also endangered species and diet of those species is also unknown to the conservationists. Conservation breeding programme of Hangul can be augmented and supported by this study in feeding animals in CBC (Conservation breeding centre) Kashmir.

We address three primary questions (1) What plant species and functional groups comprise each ungulate season diet profile, and how do diets shift seasonally within three range restricted species hangul, markhor, and musk deer (2) How do dietary diversity and niche breadth differ among wild ungulate species, and which profiles show concentrated core diets and broad generalism (3) What are the patterns of dietary overlap and niche partitioning among wild species, and how do these patterns relate to seasonal shifts and functional composition.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Kashmir Valley, in the western Himalaya India. The valley is located at the intersection of the Palearctic and Oriental biogeographic realms. It acts as a transitional zone, containing a mix of flora and fauna from both regions. The Kashmir Valley lies between latitude 33° and 35°N, and longitude 73° and 76°E and covers area of 16,000 km2. Valley is intermountain basin surrounded by the Pir Panjal Range to the south-west and the Greater Himalayan Range to the north-east. The valley floor lies at ~ 1,600 to 1,800 m a.s.l., while surrounding mountains rise to ~ 3,000 5,400 m. Jhelum River and its tributaries (Sindh, Lidder, Doodh Ganga) that traverse the valley floor are the source of water to the Kashmir agriculture and horticulture before exiting through a gorge in the Pir Panjal to the Pakistan. Our sampling was designed to capture this full elevational gradient from low plains ~ 1,580 m to alpine zones above ~ 5,100 m, encompassing multiple microclimatic zones and landscapes from temperate forests to alpine meadows. This elevational breadth provides strong seasonal contrasts in food availability and accessibility to the wild ungulates. Summer productivity is highest in meadows and forest understories, whereas winter snow cover decreases access to high-elevation pastures and limits herbaceous growth. This increases the importance of woody browse and low-elevation forage landscapes of wild ungulates (Fig. 1). The full 1,580-5,100 m gradient refers to the study-wide sampling frame; individual species were sampled within their occupied seasonal ranges, so elevation coverage was not identical among species. This design captured the major seasonal forage gradients available to the ungulate assemblage while avoiding livestock-dominated areas where possible.

Focal ungulates and seasonal sampling design

In this study we focused on five threatened sympatric wild ungulate species, Hangul (Kashmir red deer), Kashmir markhor, Himalayan musk deer, Himalayan goral, and Himalayan ibex. Seasonal sampling coverage differed among species based on the presence and absence of livestock, field logistics and species ecology. We preferred livestock free landscapes for each species to collect samples. Hangul and markhor samples were collected in summer from May to August and winter November to February. Musk deer were sampled in summer May to July, autumn August to October, and winter November to February. Goral and ibex samples were pooled across the entire sampling period (all available samples combined), producing one integrated diet profile per species without seasonal separation.

Fecal collection and composite sample preparation

Fresh fecal samples were collected non-invasively along trails, feeding areas, and other defecation sites across diverse habitat types and elevational zones. We followed established protocol to minimize contamination and maximize DNA integrity for diet metabarcoding [3, 23]. Each sample (fresh pellet group) was collected using sterile polyethylene gloves, placed in a sterile labeled container, and preserved in 90% lab-grade ethanol at -20 °C until processing. To obtain landscape-integrated diet representation for each species season group and keeping sequencing costs manageable, we used a DNA-level composite approach. The genomic DNA was extracted separately from each of 24 independent fecal samples per species season group. Equal quantities of DNA (100 µl) from those 24 extracts were then pooled to generate a single composite DNA template per group 2.4 ml. Pooling at the DNA stage rather than physically combining dried fecal material helps reduce biases associated with variable pellet size, moisture, and digestion state, and is consistent with metabarcoding studies showing that within sample heterogeneity can influence detected diet profiles and that pooling strategies can improve representativeness of group-level signals [1, 6, 18]. Fecal samples were collected with clear species attribution and Hangul, Ibex and Markhor have same size pellets but they use different landscapes and have no landscape overlap. Hangul samples were collected from Dachigam National Park and Tral Wildlife Sanctuary in both the landscapes livestock is strictly prohibited. Musk deer samples were collected throughout the landscape due to its valley wide distribution. Musk deer have different defecation behavior; they make latrine sites and defecate there for years. Markhor samples were collected from Kazinag National Park core zone which is under paramilitary control and no herders or livestock are permitted in the landscape. Ibex samples were collected from Gurez valley army restricted zones where livestock and herders are not permitted. Goral samples were collected in the Kazinag National park from animal sighted defecation sites they come down and graze in front of the lachipora village, I have observed the species feeding for multiple days.

Species attribution was based on direct sighting where available, species-specific pellet morphology, defecation behaviour, and spatial separation of species ranges. Hangul, markhor and ibex pellets can be similar in size, but their samples were collected from non-overlapping or strongly segregated landscapes; musk deer samples were supported by characteristic latrine-site behaviour. Each species-season composite contained DNA extracted separately from 24 fresh pellet groups before pooling, but individuals were not genotyped and repeated use of a site by the same individual could not be excluded. Only fresh pellet groups were retained, and heavily dried or decomposed pellets were excluded; because reads were analysed after DNA-level pooling, the effect of freshness on read number could not be modelled statistically.

Method of extraction

Cambrian automated DNA extraction (MANTA platform). DNA from stool samples was extracted using Cambrian’s automated extraction method on the MANTA platform. Stool samples were received in tubes containing stabilization buffer and mixed thoroughly using a pipette. An aliquot (500 µl) was transferred to a 2 ml microcentrifuge tube using a cut tip. STL (300 µl) was added and the tube was vortexed for 30 s, then incubated at 56 °C for 10 min with shaking (400 rpm). Four to five stainless-steel beads were added and bead-beating was performed for 5 min in pulses. The sample was vortexed at maximum speed for 40 s followed by a 20 s rest, then centrifuged at 14,500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a clean 2 ml microcentrifuge tube and the pellet was discarded. STP (200 µl) was added to the supernatant and vortexed for 30 s, incubated at 56 °C for 10 min with shaking (400 rpm), then chilled on ice for 5 min and centrifuged again at 14,500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a clean 2 ml microcentrifuge tube and purification and elution were performed following the MANTA standard procedure using pre-filled cartridges. DNA quality was assessed using NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific; NanoDrop One) and Agilent TapeStation, and DNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Cat. no. Q32854) (Annexure I).

Extraction and QC metrics (Nanodrop ratios, TapeStation integrity assessment, and Qubit concentrations) for the composite DNA templates are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (Annexure I).

To maximize plant detection and reduce locus-specific amplification bias, we amplified two widely used plant DNA barcode regions. The chloroplast rbcL locus which is a core plant barcode with broad universality and strong reference coverage and the nuclear ribosomal ITS2 region that provide higher taxonomic resolution among closely related plant species [8, 9]. For ITS2-based herbivore diet metabarcoding, short-amplicon primer sets have been developed to improve amplification success and taxonomic resolution from degraded fecal DNA across broad floras [24]. Multi-locus strategies by combining ITS2 and rbcL are used to improve overall plant detection and read confidence (Supplementary Table S2) [41]. PCR amplifications were performed separately for each locus for each composite sample, and the resulting amplicons were indexed and prepared for sequencing following the standard Oxford Nanopore native barcoding amplicon library preparation protocol [26, 37]. Indexed libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Oxford Nanopore platform. The two loci were therefore used to reduce locus-specific blind spots: rbcL favoured recovery and comparability, while ITS2 contributed finer taxonomic resolution where reference coverage was adequate.

PCR master-mix compositions and cycling conditions are detailed for rbcL (Tables S3-S4) and ITS (Tables S5-S6). Lane details for representative agarose gels are given in Supplementary Tables S7-S8 (rbcL) and S18 (ITS), with gel images shown in Supplementary Figures S1-S3. Native barcoding library preparation reaction compositions (end-prep, barcode ligation, and adapter ligation) and barcode yield/Qubit summaries are provided in Tables S9-S12, and the overall library-preparation workflow is shown in Supplementary Figure S4.

Bioinformatic processing and taxonomic assignment

The generated Nanopore amplicon reads were base called and demultiplexed through Dorado (v0.7.2; [26]). Read length and the quality distributions were checked with NanoStat [10] from the NanoPack toolkit. Adapter trimming and read pre-processing were conducted with Porechop [14, 40]. Adapter and barcode ligations are provided in Tables S10-S11. This removes Nanopore adapter sequences from read termini and splits reads containing internal adapter signatures. Reads containing internal adapters were split into separate segments to ease chimeric sequences [12, 40]. To reduce sequence redundancy and develop representative diet sequences for further downstream ecological analysis, reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% identity using VSEARCH [32]. OTU clustering breaks highly similar sequences into operational units, improve computational efficiency and stabilizing diversity estimates by minimizing inflation from sequencing noise [32]. Representative OTU sequences were classified taxonomically using Kraken2 against the NCBI nucleotide database [42]. Kraken2 assigns taxonomy based on exact k-mer matches and a lowest common ancestor approach to resolve ambiguous hits [42]. Taxonomic output reports were imported into Pavian for interactive inspection and visualization [7]. According the research questions of this study which focus on plant diet, we filtered Pavian outputs to retain only Eukaryota, Plantae assignments and excluding non-plant sequences, and the plant-only taxon table was used for all downstream diet analyses and visualizations [7, 38, 42].

We demultiplexed Nanopore reads using Dorado v0.7.2, inspected read quality with NanoStat, removed adapters with Porechop, and split reads containing internal adapter signatures because these can represent putative chimeric reads. We did not include an independent mock-community benchmark, Illumina validation subset, or separate de novo chimera-screening step; therefore, we state this limitation explicitly rather than inferring validation beyond the available pipeline.

Across the 14 locus-level libraries, we generated 1,962,853 raw reads, of which 1,944,992 reads remained after adapter trimming and preprocessing, representing 99.1% read retention. After 97% VSEARCH OTU clustering, 1,180,083 reads were retained. The ITS libraries contributed 910,394 raw reads, 899,535 processed reads, and 688,426 clustered reads, whereas the rbcL libraries contributed 1,052,459 raw reads, 1,045,457 processed reads, and 491,657 clustered reads.

Marker-specific Kraken2 classification rates ranged from 65.4% to 89.8% for ITS libraries, with a weighted mean of 78.6% of clustered reads, and from 85.4% to 97.0% for rbcL libraries, with a weighted mean of 92.8% of clustered reads. We used NanoStat for quality inspection; however, because a final numerical mean post-preprocessing. We interpreted species-level assignments cautiously because Nanopore reads can retain residual insertion and deletion errors, and public sequence databases may incompletely represent Himalayan flora, particularly among closely related congeners.

To make fair comparisons among the composite species-season diet profiles, we worked with relative read abundance (RRA) rather than raw read counts, because sequencing yield and PCR performance varied between profiles. We summarized diet composition visually in two ways: (i) stacked bar plots showing the share of reads assigned to each plant growth form, and (ii) heatmaps highlighting the most abundant taxa across profiles. To show how reads were distributed across taxonomic levels, we also produced Sankey-style flow diagrams in Pavian [7]. All statistical analyses were carried out in R [31] using the vegan package [25]. We calculated alpha diversity using Shannon’s index (H′; [35]) and Simpson’s index [36], derived evenness as Pielou’s J = H′ / ln(richness), and summarized diet breadth with Levins’ standardized niche breadth (B; [21]). Differences in diet composition among species were assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, and rarefaction curves were used to check whether sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the plant diversity represented in each composite profile. Because each species-season group was represented by one DNA composite, diversity, breadth, overlap and NMDS outputs were interpreted descriptively; no individual-level variance or replicated hypothesis testing was inferred from the composite profiles.

Results

The composite diet profiles show species specific and seasonal differences in diet diversity and breadth. Hangul and Kashmir musk deer had the broadest summer diets, each detecting 115 plant taxa. They also showed the highest diversity (H′ ≈ 4.38, 4.48) and the widest niche breadth in summer(B = 54.69, 64.70). They used mixed food plants, with 71 being strongly dominant for Hangul. In winter, Hangul’s diet contracted to 61 taxa (B = 47.83), and the number of taxa making up most of the diet also fell, with 45 core taxa. This shift is consistent with a seasonal change toward fewer key foods. Musk deer showed the strongest seasonal tightening; niche breadth dropped sharply in autumn (B = 14.27) and remained relatively low in winter (B = 21.45). This suggests they rely more on a smaller set of plants outside summer. Markhor diets were comparatively narrow in both seasons, with 37 to 39 taxa (B = 34, 36) and small core diets of 28 to 30 taxa. This indicates more focused feeding. In the pooled samples, ibex had a broader and more diverse diet than goral, with ibex at 89 taxa (B = 40.61) and goral at 53 taxa (B = 24.99). This shows that ibex used a wider variety of plants in the combined dataset (Table 1, Supplementary Table S20). Functional composition of diets

Forbs and other non-graminoid plants made up a large part of all profiles, ranging from about 35.5% to 68.3% of reads. This shows a strong reliance on herbaceous plants within the group. The graminoid (grass and sedge) content was between 4.3% and 26.4% of reads, while shrubs accounted for 10.1% to 21.8%, and tree browse ranged from 6.2% to 24.9%. These trends reflect differences between species and seasonal changes in diet. Goral had the highest graminoid proportion at 26.4%, which aligns with more grazing in open areas. In contrast, musk deer in autumn had a very high forb component at 68.3% and a low contribution from trees and climbers. Climbers and vines played a notable role in several profiles, contributing up to about 12–13% in the summer diets of Hangul and musk deer. This supports the use of forest-edge and riparian areas where climbing plants flourish. Growth-form composition varied markedly among the diet profiles (Fig. 2; Table 2, and Supplementary Table S21).

All ungulates relied heavily on forbs and herbs, but there were clear differences in other types of food. Goral and the summer diets of Hangul and musk deer had higher graminoid contributions, indicating greater use of grasses and open herb layers. Markhor and Hangul winter diets included more shrubs and trees, with markhor winter having about 38.7% combined shrubs and trees, and Hangul winter about 41.1%. Musk deer showed a marked increase in woody browse during winter, with shrubs at 21.75% and trees at 24.90%, compared with 10.09% shrubs and 8.93% trees in autumn. Climbers consistently made up about 12–13% of the summer diets for Hangul and musk deer, suggesting use of forest edges and understory vines during the growing season.

Dominant taxa and seasonal changes

We found a variety of plant types in the diet, with the most common types changing by season and species. Species like Epilobium cylindricum (a willowherb) and Chamaenerion angustifolium (fireweed) were dominant in the autumn diet of musk deer, making up about 34% of autumn reads. These plants were almost absent in other profiles. In comparison, Malva neglecta (mallow) appeared in every profile, reaching particularly high levels in ibex (10.6%) and appearing in many others. Poa annua and Celtis spp. (hackberry) were prominent in the summer diets of Hangul and musk deer; Poa annua 4% in both profiles. Betula utilis (Himalayan birch) and other woody plants such as Rhododendron anthopogon became more significant in the winter diet of musk deer, with birch making up about 14.4% and rhododendron about 10.2% of reads. These patterns show clear seasonal changes. Ungulates take advantage of fleshy forbs such as willowherbs during late summer and autumn, but rely on woody plants such as birch and rhododendron during winter shortages. The study also indicates that Urtica dioica and Urtica ardens were regularly eaten by several species, suggesting that they are common forage resources, while other taxa were specific to particular profiles. Each ungulate species along the different diet profiles showed important seasonal changes in feeding. For Hangul during summer, the top contributors were Poa annua (4.58% of reads) and Celtis spp. (3.88%). They were followed by the forb Cardamine macrophylla (large bittercress, 3.27%), the grass Digitaria cillaris (2.93%), and the fig Ficus palmata (2.90%) (Table 3). The top 15 taxa for each profile are provided in Supplementary Table S16. Hangul’s summer diet had a very diffuse core, with 71 taxa forming 80% of cumulative reads, indicating low dominance by any single species. By winter, Hangul’s diet changed dramatically. Richness dropped to 61 taxa, and the core 80% diet shrank to 45 taxa, showing higher dominance. Vicia sativa subsp. nigra (7.32%) became the top item. Two nettles, Urtica dioica (3.59%) and Urtica ardens (3.20%), along with Malva neglecta (2.84%) and Betula utilis (2.38%), were prominent diet items. Hangul increased shrub and tree browsing in winter, making up about 41% of the diet (Table 2), while graminoids fell to 8.46%. Only 24 plant taxa were shared between Hangul’s summer and winter diets, leading to a low Jaccard similarity of 0.158, indicating about 84% turnover of taxa (Supplementary Table S13). Pianka’s overlap index between Hangul’s seasons was O = 0.352 (Fig. 5), showing moderate dietary differentiation. These results show that Hangul tracks seasonal resource availability rather than simply dropping rare taxa in winter. Many dominant summer forbs essentially disappeared, replaced by different woody and hardy plants in winter. In markhor, summer diet was mixed but featured a strong forb component (48.35%), along with substantial shrub (14.05%) and tree (18.65%) contributions. Richness was 39 taxa, and niche breadth was B = 36.27 (Table 1). Dominant summer taxa included Urtica ardens (4.99%) and Urtica dioica (4.86%), Malva neglecta (4.23%), Betula utilis (3.84%), Ficus palmata (3.35%), and smaller portions of Cardamine macrophylla, Aegilops tauschii, Bistorta vacciniifolia, Prunus cornuta, and Ziziphus oxyphylla (about 2.5–2.8%) (Table 3; Supplementary Table S16). In winter, markhor diet had more woody components (shrubs 21.16%, trees 17.50%), while graminoids declined to 10.05% (Table 2). Richness dropped slightly to 37, and niche breadth to B = 34.22, showing a mild increase in dominance structure compared to summer. Markhor’s seasonal diet shift was less extreme than Hangul’s. The summer-winter Jaccard similarity was 0.490, much higher than Hangul’s 0.158, and Pianka’s overlap was O = 0.740 (Fig. 5), indicating that markhor used a more consistent set of resources throughout the year. In fact, markhor’s top winter taxa were largely similar to summer’s: Urtica dioica (5.04%) and Urtica ardens (4.74%) remained top items, Bistorta vacciniifolia (4.60%) and Vicia sativa subsp. nigra (4.56%) rose in rank, while Malva neglecta (3.75%) remained important. However, markhor showed some winter-specific increases, including Hedera nepalensis, Viburnum grandiflorum, and Hippophae rhamnoides. These seasonal changes were moderate compared to Hangul or musk deer (Table 2).

Musk deer diet showed the strongest seasonal shifts. Summer was broad and diverse (115 taxa; H′ = 4.384; B = 54.69), resembling Hangul’s summer, with a mix of forbs (35.48%), graminoids (17.19%), shrubs (14.64%), trees (19.40%), and climbers (12.77%) (Table 2). Top summer taxa mirrored those of Hangul: Vitis spp. (5.31%), Celtis spp. (4.51%), Cardamine macrophylla (3.56%), Digitaria cillaris (3.35%), Herpetospermum pedunculosum (3.26%), Eragrostis multiflora (3.23%), Ficus palmata (3.19%), Poa annua (3.16%), Ziziphus oxyphylla (3.00%), and Solanum nigrum (2.57%). By autumn, however, musk deer diets narrowed drastically; richness dropped to 50 taxa and B = 14.27, and the profile became mostly forb-dominated (68.28% forbs, with shrubs and trees below 20% combined; Table 2). The autumn diet was mainly made up of two herbaceous species, Epilobium cylindricum at 19.95% and Chamaenerion angustifolium at 14.43%, together accounting for over a third of reads. No other taxa exceeded about 2.6% in autumn, with the next highest being Urtica dioica (2.57%), Urtica ardens (2.41%), and Malva neglecta (2.08%). This indicates that musk deer focus heavily on a few succulent forb species in the post-monsoon period, likely taking advantage of fast-growing forbs. During winter, musk deer switched to a browse-heavy diet, with shrubs at 21.75% and trees at 24.90%, while graminoids fell to 4.31% (Table 2). Richness remained relatively low (46 taxa), and niche breadth was low (B = 21.45). The main winter foods were Betula utilis (birch) at 14.40% and Rhododendron anthopogon at 10.19%, together almost a quarter of the diet, showing heavy reliance on woody evergreen and deciduous browsing. Other winter items included Malva neglecta (4.17%), Thymus linearis (4.07%), Bergenia ciliata (3.47%), Heracleum candicans (3.44%), and the ever-present nettles Urtica spp. (each about 3%). Seasonal turnover in musk deer was extreme: only 18 taxa overlapped between summer and winter diets (Jaccard = 0.126; Supplementary Table S13), and Pianka’s overlap was only O = 0.161 (Fig. 5), indicating almost complete reordering of diet from the growing season to winter. The specific shifts were marked: birch and rhododendron increased by 14.4 and 10.2% points, respectively, from summer to winter, while summer favorites such as Vitis spp., Celtis australis, Digitaria cillaris, Eragrostis multiflora and Solanum nigrum declined. This shows that musk deer follow a distinct seasonal diet, focusing on palatable forbs in autumn and switching to available woody forage in winter.

For goral and ibex, we have single pooled profiles that combine their diet across seasons due to fewer samples. Goral had a higher graminoid proportion (26.4%) than most other profiles, consistent with its grazing habits in open grassy areas and meadow edges. Goral’s diet richness was 53 and niche breadth B = 24.99. The dominant items in the goral diet were grasses and forbs from open spaces Themeda anathera a tall grass, (12.86%) and Bistorta vacciniifolia a herb, (10.16%) were top, followed by Verbascum thapsus, mullein, (4.13%), Origanum vulgare (2.96%), Urtica dioica (2.71%), Urtica ardens (2.37%), Hedera nepalensis (2.10%), Viburnum grandiflorum (1.99%), Malva neglecta (1.96%), and Fragaria nubicola (1.95%). These shows that goral feeds in mixed scrub grassland habitats grasses, low forbs, some vines like Hedera. The pooled ibex diet featured a forb-heavy composition (57.1% forbs) with significant shrub (19.6%) and tree (11.3%) parts. Richness was relatively high at 89 taxa, and niche breadth was broad (B = 40.61).

The common ibex dietary plants included Malva neglecta (10.63%), Urtica ardens (4.81%), Ficus palmata (wild fig, 4.45%), Stellaria media (chickweed, 3.66%), Spergularia rubra (sand spurrey, 2.24%), Urtica dioica (2.06%), Rosa beggeriana (wild rose, 1.82%), Boehmeria virgata (a nettle relative, 1.81%), Hedera nepalensis (1.53%), and Veronica spp. (1.44%). This shows that ibex, adapted to rocky cliffs, also eats a variety of forbs including ruderal herbs such as Malva, Stellaria, and Veronica, plus shrubs such as wild rose, fig and some climbers. Pairwise dietary overlaps, measured by Pianka’s index O (on a 0 1 scale), varied widely across the profiles (Fig. 5; see also Supplementary Table S17 and S23). The highest overlaps were generally seen between profiles within the same species or season. For example, Hangul (Summer) and Musk deer (Summer) had O = 0.985, showing they shared a similar herb-rich diet. Markhor (Summer) compared to markhor (Winter) had O = 0.740, indicating that markhor’s diet remained similar between seasons. Hangul (Winter) vs. markhor (Winter) was also high (O = 0.721), as was Hangul (Winter) vs. markhor (Summer) (0.720). This suggests that Hangul’s winter diet, which includes woody plants, overlaps significantly with markhor diets in general. Other relatively high overlaps (O ~ 0.59 to 0.66) happened between ibex and markhor and between goral and markhor in some comparisons. In contrast, the lowest overlaps were usually found between profiles with very different dietary focuses. Goral (pooled) vs. Hangul (Summer) was very low (O = 0.136) since goral’s diet, rich in grasses, shares little with Hangul’s wide mix of forbs and browse in summer. Goral vs. musk deer summer was similarly low (0.140). Musk deer winter vs. Hangul summer scored 0.154, and musk deer winter vs. musk deer summer only 0.161. Other low combinations (O ~ 0.17 to 0.26) involved musk deer autumn, which specializes in forbs, contrasting with either Hangul summer or goral or ibex, etc. (Fig. 5 for full matrix). Generally, high overlaps were more common among the summer profiles of larger species (Hangul, markhor, musk deer), which all took advantage of varied herbaceous communities. Low overlaps occurred when comparing a profile that specializes in forbs (musk deer autumn) with one that is heavy on browse or grasses (goral or Hangul summer) or when comparing pooled profiles (goral, ibex) to a seasonally specialized profile.

Dietary niche breadth and dominance structure

Diet profiles varied markedly in their focus on a small number of plant species compared to their distribution across wild ungulates. The size of the core diet, which reflects the number of taxa needed to make up 80% of total reads, showed a wide range among profiles (Table 1). Some profiles had as few as 28 to 33 taxa markhor in winter, musk deer in winter and autumn, while others reached up to 71 taxa Hangul in summer. Larger core diet suggest consumption spread across many plant species, meaning no single taxon dominates. In contrast, smaller core diets indicate heavy reliance on a few taxa. These dominance patterns were reflected in the Levins’ niche breadth values B (Table 1). Profiles with broader diet (high B) corresponded to larger core diet, meaning many taxa contributed, while profiles with narrower diet (low B) had smaller core sets. This structure is ecologically significant. Narrow diets that depend heavily on a few key plants may make a species more vulnerable to changes or losses in those plants or habitats. A more generalized diet, however, could help protect against such changes. Hangul (Summer) had the highest H′ (~ 4.48) reflecting a very diverse diet, whereas Musk deer (Autumn) had the lowest H′ (~ 3.40), indicating a diet dominated by few species. Intermediate values were observed for other profiles. Higher H′ generally corresponded to larger niche breadth and core diet size, whereas low H′ profiles had one or two taxa accounting for a large fraction of diet. Shannon diversity (Fig. 6) provides a numerical perspective on diet diversity. Hangul summer had the highest H′ among all profiles. This finding is consistent with its having the greatest number of taxa and a very even distribution of those taxa (Table 1). In contrast, musk deer autumn recorded the lowest H′. This reflects a diet mainly made up of a few forb species, which results in lower evenness. Most other profiles showed H′ values between 3.5 and 4.2, suggesting diet with fairly high diversity but some dominant items. Evenness (Pielou’s J) values (Table 1) confirm that markhor summer and winter diet were extremely even (J ≈ 0.99). This means no single taxon dominated those diets. On the other hand, musk deer autumn had the lowest evenness (J = 0.870), indicating that one or two taxa made up a large portion of the diet.

Dietary alpha diversity (Shannon’s H′) for each ungulate profile

Levins’ B is a standardized index, ranging from 0 to 100 in our calculation, that measures how many resources (taxa) are used and how evenly they are distributed. Hangul (Summer) had the highest niche breadth at approximately 64.7, which indicates a very broad diet. In contrast, Musk deer (Autumn) had the lowest at around 14.3, showing a specialized diet. Other profiles fell between these two extremes. This pattern closely follows the results from Shannon diversity, highlighting clear differences in diet breadth among the ungulates. Levins’ niche breadth values (Fig. 7) ranked the profiles similarly. Hangul summer and musk deer summer had the broadest niche breadths, around 55 to 65. They were followed by Hangul winter and ibex, which had breadths of about 40 to 48. Markhor and musk deer winter had values between 21 and 36, while musk deer autumn had the lowest at around 14. The relationship between B and diversity metrics is expected because both are influenced by how evenly diet is spread across many versus few food items. These results point out that Hangul (summer) and musk deer (summer) are clear generalists in this group, consuming a wide variety of plants with relatively low dominance. On the other hand, musk deer (autumn) acts as an extreme specialist, relying on a limited range of forbs. Other profiles like goral, ibex, and markhor sit at intermediate positions.

The DCA/CA and functional CCA ordinations revealed clear dietary separation among ungulate profiles based on plant functional composition, with the first two axes explaining a substantial proportion of total variation. In the unconstrained DCA/CA ordination, Axis 1 explained 35.949% of the inertia and Axis 2 explained 17.475%, giving a cumulative explanation of 53.424%, while the functional CCA showed stronger constrained structure, with Axis 1 explaining 43.718% and Axis 2 explaining 19.859%, together accounting for 63.577% of the constrained inertia (Table 4). The DCA/CA site scores showed that Hangul Summer and Musk deer Summer clustered strongly on the negative side of Axis 1, with scores of -0.637 and − 0.620, respectively, indicating high similarity in summer diet composition; in contrast, Musk deer Autumn, Musk deer Winter, Goral and Ibex were positioned on the positive side of Axis 1, showing clear functional dietary divergence (Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table S28). Along Axis 2, Musk deer Autumn was strongly separated with a high positive score of 1.351, whereas Ibex showed strong negative separation with a score of − 1.432, suggesting distinct seasonal and species-specific dietary strategies (Supplementary Table S28). Growth-form centroids further explained this separation: Forb/Herb and Shrub were positioned on the positive side of Axis 1, whereas Climber/Vine, Tree, Graminoid and Other occurred on the negative side, indicating that Axis 1 primarily represents a gradient from woody/climber and graminoid resources toward forb-herb and shrub-based diets (Supplementary Table S29 and Fig. 9). The CCA vector correlations supported this interpretation, with Shrub showing the strongest positive association with CCA1 (0.898), followed by Forb/Herb (0.705), while Climber/Vine (-0.871), Tree (-0.683) and Graminoid (-0.515) were negatively associated with CCA1 (Table S30). CCA2 separated profiles mainly along a forb-graminoid gradient, with Forb/Herb positively correlated with CCA2 (0.617) and Graminoid negatively correlated (-0.626), explaining the strong upward position of Musk deer autumn and the downward separation of goral in the constrained ordination (Supplementary Table S30 and Fig. 9). Overall, these ordinations indicate that ungulate dietary niches are structured by both species’ identity and seasonal shifts, with summer Hangul and Musk deer showing close dietary overlap, while autumn/winter Musk deer, Goral, Ibex and winter Markhor occupy more distinct functional dietary spaces driven by differential use of herbs, shrubs, trees, climbers and graminoids.

Discussion

Using a dual-marker (rbcL and ITS2) fecal DNA metabarcoding approach with Oxford Nanopore sequencing, we provide a landscape-level view of diets and dietary niche structure for a group of ungulates living together in the western Himalaya. Our results show that ungulates are mixed feeders at the landscape level, consuming both herbaceous and woody plants [15, 16]. However, their diet differs significantly in composition, dominance, and seasonal adaptability. These dietary differences may help explain how multiple ungulate species coexist in an area where their habitats and plant communities overlap [28, 34]. Functional variation among the species is evident from their growth-form profiles. Forbs and herbs made up most of their diets, indicating the high availability and nutritional value of herbaceous plants in Himalayan meadows and forest understories during the growing season. However, the consumption of grasses varied significantly.

Goral showed a preference for grazing, consuming a considerable amount of grass, while Hangul and musk deer had much lower levels of grasses in their winter diets, aligning with their browsed-heavy profiles during snow cover [15, 16]. In winter, shrubs and trees became more significant for seasonally sampled species like Hangul, markhor, and musk deer, reflecting the limited availability of soft herbaceous forage under snow. Climbing plants were prominent in several summer diets, suggesting that species like Hangul and musk deer frequently feed in edge and riparian areas where these plants are plentiful in summer. Hangul (Kashmir red deer) showed particularly diverse diets in summer, with high species variety and the largest niche breadth among all profiles. Its core diet consisted of 71 taxa, which made up 80% of its intake. This varied diet shows that Hangul takes advantage of many plants in summer, rather than focusing on just a few, which is typical behavior for generalist mixed feeders during peak plant growth [15, 16]. In winter, Hangul’s diet shifted to more browsing: its species richness and niche breadth decreased, becoming more dominated by woody shrubs and trees, which together made up about 40% of its diet, while grasses became a minor component. The significant seasonal turnover, with only about 16% of taxa shared between summer and winter, suggests that Hangul actively tracks seasonal resource availability. This means Hangul shifts to different plants as the seasons and snow conditions change.

From a conservation standpoint, this pattern indicates that Hangul’s survival relies on a connected mix of seasonal habitats: forb-rich meadows and forest openings in summer, and lower-elevation shrublands or evergreen forests in winter. Losing either habitat could be harmful, as Hangul needs to move between these seasonal resource areas [2]. Markhor diets were more stable across seasons than those of Hangul or musk deer. Although markhor increase its woody browse in winter, the overall composition of its diet remained similar between summer and winter, with an overlap of about 0.74. This suggests that markhor consistently uses specific shrub-dominated slopes or open forests throughout the year, reducing seasonal dietary changes. Its ability to access browse resources, like evergreen shrubs and oak scrub, in winter likely protects it from drastic diet shifts. Therefore, conservation actions should focus on maintaining healthy shrubland habitats and browse availability on rocky slopes and open woodlands to ensure that winter forage remains accessible, even under snow. Musk deer exhibited the most significant seasonal diet change of all the species. Its summer diet was broad and shared similarities with Hangul’s summer diet, both using diverse forbs.

This overlap suggests that these two species may converge in summer when resources are abundant. However, musk deer’s autumn diet became highly specialized, mainly consisting of a narrow set of forbs, particularly Epilobium species. This change could reflect musk deer exploiting a late-season surge of high-quality herbs after the monsoon, possibly in particular microhabitats. By winter, musk deer shifted almost entirely to woody browse, especially birch and rhododendron. These dietary patterns indicate that musk deer might prefer distinct seasonal habitats: alpine meadows or openings rich in ephemeral forbs in autumn and dense understory or subalpine scrub with evergreens in winter. Protecting these post-monsoon herb-rich areas, like subalpine clearings where fireweed and willowherb thrive, as well as winter browse stands (birch/rhododendron forests), is crucial for musk deer survival. At the community level, our findings suggest both dietary overlap and niche partitioning [28, 34]. The high overlap among some summer profiles, such as those of Hangul and musk deer, indicates that during the productive season, these ungulates may rely on a shared, abundant food source from rich herbaceous communities. This overlap could allow them to occupy the same space without significant competition since food is plentiful. However, this could become problematic if summer forage decreases, perhaps due to overgrazing by livestock or drought. In contrast, the very low overlaps seen between certain diets, like goral versus Hangul in summer or musk deer in winter versus Hangul in summer, indicate strong partitioning. This often relates to differences in feeding strategies or seasons. For example, goral, which grazes in grasslands, had little dietary overlap with Hangul, a mixed feeder that browses in forests and edges [15, 16]. Musk deer also had minimal overlap with Hangul during summer.

This pattern of high overlap during resource-rich times and partitioning during diet changes aligns with a model of dynamic niche partitioning [28, 34]. Coexistence seems to be facilitated not by strict separation, but by flexible foraging and timing. In other words, ungulates in Kashmir share a broad foraging base when resources are plentiful, minimizing competition, and differentiate their diet when necessary, such as in winter when each relies on different specific resources or spaces for refuge. Interestingly, our metabarcoding found DNA from several edge- and disturbance-tolerant wild taxa in the diet, such as Celtis australis, Cardamine macrophylla, Aegilops tauschii, and Ziziphus oxyphylla, particularly in Hangul and markhor during summer. This suggests that these ungulates use forest-edge, scrub, open-slope, and meadow-margin forage rather than indicating direct dependence on cultivated crops. The repeated detections across multiple samples show that use of these natural edge resources is not a rare occurrence.

Ungulates may increasingly use edge habitats when natural forage is limited; however, the revised taxa do not require interpretation as direct crop feeding. On the other hand, natural shrublands, forest margins, rocky slopes, and wild herb layers may provide crucial forage during times when natural forage is scarce, especially late winter or early spring. Therefore, management strategies should prioritize maintaining native forage habitats and reducing livestock pressure. Protecting natural winter forage areas and applying conflict-prevention measures only where field evidence confirms crop use would help maintain connectivity and keep animals in safer foraging zones.

This study establishes a landscape level baseline of what these ungulates eat and which plant functional groups matter, providing actionable information for management. In summary, the ungulate community in the Kashmir Valley shares a wide range of forage overall, yet shows clear differences in dietary structure that likely support coexistence. Coexistence is aided by functional differences some species or seasons lean more toward grazing while others browse [15, 16, 30]. Seasonal dietary switching also helps reduce direct competition during critical times [28, 34]. Additionally, differences in dominance structures occur; some species are generalists while others focus on specific resources. These factors indicate that conservation and management strategies need to consider seasonal variations. Protecting only summer meadows without ensuring access to winter browse, or vice versa, would leave the full annual needs of these herbivores unmet especially for Hangul, which needs year-round habitat connections between summer and winter ranges, and for musk deer, which undergo significant dietary changes.

DNA metabarcoding can be combined with other ecological data to improve conservation planning. For example, merging diet analysis with spatial data, such as GPS tracking or camera-trap occupancy, could show where each species meets its dietary needs and identify critical foraging hotspots. Understanding the spatial overlap with livestock grazing would help design grazing practices that reduce competition for food, like adjusting livestock timing to avoid conflicts during key ungulate feeding times. Ongoing or future human impacts, such as expanding agriculture or climate-driven changes in vegetation, could change the diet landscape. Regular metabarcoding monitoring can provide early warnings by tracking changes in diet composition or diversity over time, such as declines in certain forage types or shifts toward less-preferred foods. The supplementary tables and plant lists we provide serve as an inventory of forage plants for these species, which can guide habitat restoration efforts, like planting or encouraging the growth of important forage species in key areas. Essentially, our study emphasizes specific forage resources and niche differences that conservation efforts should consider, reinforcing that effective management of this ungulate group must account for the functional ecology of diet and its seasonal changes.

Limitations of the composite design and taxonomic assignment

The composite design is a strength for obtaining broad species-level seasonal profiles across a difficult Himalayan landscape, but it also limits inference. It cannot estimate individual dietary variance, quantify uncertainty within a species-season group. Therefore, the diversity, overlap and ordination results should be interpreted as landscape-level diet summaries rather than population-level inference. Future work should sequence individual fecal replicates, include temporal replication within seasons, and validate a subset of assignments using mock communities or Illumina sequencing.

Conservation and management implications

Authorities need to conserve and restore summer meadows that are rich in forbs and have openings in forest understories. These areas support diverse diets and mixed feeding during the growing season. They provide the essential forbs and soft grasses that species like Hangul and musk deer rely on in summer. Management should keep alpine meadows and forest glades productive by preventing overgrazing and maintaining natural fire cycles. It is important to maintain winter browse belts, shrublands, and browse-rich forest edges at lower and mid elevations. These habitats should remain accessible under snow and provide critical woody forage, such as birch, willow, and rhododendron. Protecting and improving these winter refuges through reforestation or planting native browse species will help species like Hangul, markhor, and musk deer survive the harsh winter months. Regulating livestock grazing intensity and seasonal timing in key wild ungulate forage areas will help reduce the depletion of the herb layer in summer and browsing pressure in winter. Possible actions include creating grazing-free zones or seasonal closures in the core areas of national parks. This is especially important during late summer when wild ungulates are fattening up and in late winter when forage is scarce.

Sustainable grazing practices that minimize competition, such as staggering grazing away from sensitive seasons or areas, will benefit wild herbivores. Furthermore, we must maintain habitat connectivity across different elevations and aspects. This allows ungulates to track seasonal forage patterns and migrate between summer and winter ranges. We need to protect corridors that link low-elevation wintering areas to high-elevation summer ranges from fragmentation. This can be achieved by avoiding development or roads in critical corridor zones. Functional connectivity will help prevent animals from having to move to less optimal or conflict-prone areas, such as agricultural fields, when natural forage is available elsewhere. By focusing on these priorities, we can protect both the diversity of summer resources and the availability of winter resources, reduce the impact of human livestock, and enable movement. This management approach will better secure the year-round foraging needs of this group of animals. This is especially urgent for the Hangul, whose small remaining population needs secure summer meadows in Dachigam and connected access to nearby winter browse areas. The musk deer is also at risk, as it is very sensitive to disruptions in its specialized seasonal habitats.
