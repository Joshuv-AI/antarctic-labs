Multiple Mellin-Barnes integrals in Schwinger-DeWitt technique
A. O. Barvinsky, A. E. Kalugin, W. Wachowski

Abstract
We consider off-diagonal asymptotic series for integral kernels of functions of Laplace-type operators on curved backgrounds. These expansions are obtained by applying integral transforms to the DeWitt series for the heat kernel of the corresponding operator and thus represent a DeWitt-type series in the heat kernel coefficients with the coefficients of this expansion (which we call basis kernels) being some hypergeometric-type functions of the Synge world function. Basis kernels of certain class of operator functions were found previously in terms of N N -fold Mellin-Barnes integrals. In this paper we study series representations of the corresponding Mellin-Barnes integrals in both non-resonant and resonant cases and suggest a physical interpretation for the emerging series, which is related to the UV and IR properties of operator functions.

Consider a spacetime
\mathcal{M}
—
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with metric
g_{ab}
and Levi-Civita connection
\nabla_{a}
. Let
\hat{F}(\nabla)
be a Laplace-type operator (i.e., minimal of 2nd order) acting on sections of a vector bundle over
\mathcal{M}
:

\hat{F}(\nabla)=\hat{1}\Box+\hat{P},

(1.1)

where
\Box=-g^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
is the covariant Laplacian, hats denote the matrix structure in the fibers, and
\hat{P}=\hat{P}(x)
is an arbitrary matrix (a “potential term”). The heat kernel for
\hat{F}(\nabla)
is defined as the integral kernel of the operator exponential:

\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\tau\hat{F})\,\delta(x,x^{\prime}),

(1.2)

where
\delta(x,x^{\prime})
is the invariant delta function. The heat kernel is a two-point (i.e., depending on two points
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, as well as on the additional proper time parameter
\tau
) matrix-valued function. A remarkable fact
DeWitt 1965
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
is that there is an asymptotic DeWitt’s expansion of the heat kernel in the ultraviolet (UV) limit
\tau\to 0

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|\,x,x^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\!B_{\frac{d}{2}-n}(\tau,\sigma)\cdot\hat{a}_{n}[F|x,x^{\prime}],

(1.3)

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma)=\frac{\tau^{-\alpha}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\!\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2\tau}\right),

(1.4)

where
\sigma=\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
is the Synge world function, and
\hat{a}_{m}[F|x,x^{\prime}]
are off-diagonal heat kernel (or HaMiDeW) coefficients.

We include the quasiclassical Pauli-Van Vleck-Morette determinant
\Delta^{1/2}(x,x^{\prime})
, which is usually written explicitly, in coefficients

.
Coincidence limits
\nabla_{b_{1}}\cdots\nabla_{b_{k}}\hat{a}_{n}\big|_{x=x^{\prime}}
are local invariants of mass dimension
2n+k

\hat{P}
, the Riemann tensor, the curvature in the bundle, and covariant derivatives thereof
DeWitt 1965
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
;
Gilkey 1975
;
Gilkey 1995
. They play a fundamental role in quantum field theory (QFT) in curved spacetime, since they determine the one-loop effective action of the theory with the wave operator
\hat{F}(\nabla)
.

However, in applications (in particular, for studying theories with non-minimal wave operators
Barvinsky and Kalugin 2024
;
Barvinsky et al. 2025
), it is often necessary to work not only with the operator exponential
\exp(-\tau\hat{F})
, but also with operator functions
f(\hat{F})
of a more complex form, for example,
\exp(-\tau\hat{F})/\hat{F}^{\mu}
. Therefore, we would like to obtain a representation for their integral kernels,

\hat{K}\big[f(F)\big|x,x^{\prime}\big]=f(\hat{F})\,\delta(x,x^{\prime}),

(1.5)

as a functional series, similar to (
1.3
)-(
1.4
). To do this, it suffices to note that any operator function
f(\hat{F})
can be written as an integral transform of the operator exponential
\exp(-\tau\hat{F})

\displaystyle f(\hat{F})=\mathfrak{L}_{f}\,e^{-\tau\hat{F}}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,f^{*}(\tau)\,e^{-\tau\hat{F}},

(1.6)

\displaystyle f^{*}(\tau)=\int\limits_{C}\frac{d\lambda}{2\pi i}\;f(\lambda)\,e^{\tau\lambda},

(1.7)

which should be understood as the Laplace transform from the scalar variable
\tau
to the operator variable
\hat{F}
. Now we apply the integral transform
\mathfrak{L}_{f}
term-by-term to the DeWitt expansion (
1.3
), and obtain the desired expansion

\displaystyle\hat{K}\big[f(F)\big|x,x^{\prime}\big]\simeq\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{B}_{\frac{d}{2}-n}[f|\sigma]\cdot\hat{a}_{n}[F|x,x^{\prime}],

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]=\mathfrak{L}_{f}B_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,f^{*}(\tau)\,B_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma).

(1.9)

The transforms we performed are possible because we work outside the coincidence limit
x=x^{\prime}
, thus avoiding the singularities that arise it it.
Off-diagonality
, which is essentially a form of regularization by separating points, is a key ingredient of our approach, allowing us to flexibly use the powerful apparatus of integral transforms.

A remarkable property of the expansion (
1.8
) is that it explicitly distinguishes the data of two types: whatever the function
f
, information about it is contained in the scalar functions
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
(
1.9
), which we call
basis kernels
. And all the information about the geometry of the spacetime
\mathcal{M}
and the vector bundle, as well as the specific form of the operator
\hat{F}(\nabla)
, is still encoded in the familiar HaMiDeW coefficients
\hat{a}_{n}[F|x,x^{\prime}]
. This property is an off-diagonal generalization of the phenomenon called ”functoriality” in the literature
Gilkey 1975
;
Gilkey 1995
, so we call it
off-diagonal functoriality
.

An important point, crucial for our strategy, is the following: as we explain in detail in
Barvinsky et al. 2026a
;
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, since the expansion (
1.8
) is obtained by term-by-term integration of the DeWitt expansion (
1.3
), which is valid only in the UV limit
\tau\to 0
, it is not in fact a total expansion of the integral kernel
\hat{K}\big[f(F)\big|x,x^{\prime}\big]
, but only its UV half (which we express by using the
\simeq
sign instead of equality in (
1.8
)). To obtain the second, infrared (IR) half of the total expansion, we would have to similarly integrate term-by-term the asymptotic expansion of the heat kernel
\hat{K}_{F}(\tau|x,x^{\prime})
in the opposite IR limit
\tau\to\infty
(finding which, however, is a much more difficult problem).

In other words, the same point can be expressed as follows: as is well known, according to the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, the order of summation and integration can be changed only if all intermediate sums and integrals converge absolutely. In our case, neither one nor the other holds: the DeWitt expansion (
1.3
) is an asymptotic (divergent) series, but in addition, the integral in (
1.9
) for sufficiently small
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha
can diverge at the IR limit
\tau=\infty
, and therefore requires some additional regularization. The expressions (
1.8
)-(
1.9
) imply regularization by means of analytic continuation: if the integral converges in a certain region of its parameters, then the analytic continuation of the resulting expression beyond this region yields the regularized value of the divergent integral.

But we can do in another way: add a massive term to the operator
\hat{F}\mapsto\hat{F}+m^{2}
, and take it into account “nonperturbatively” in the prefactor. This means that in the DeWitt expansion (
1.3
), the function
B_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma)
(
1.4
) should be replaced by the expression

W_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma,m^{2})=\frac{\tau^{-\alpha}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2\tau}-\tau m^{2}\right).

(1.10)

After this, the integral transform
\mathfrak{L}_{f}
(
1.6
) acting term-by-term leads to the following modified expansion

\hat{K}\big[f(F+m^{2})\big|x,x^{\prime}\big]\simeq\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{W}_{\frac{d}{2}-n}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]\cdot\hat{a}_{n}[F|x,x^{\prime}].

(1.11)

It is in a new family of functions

\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]=\mathfrak{L}_{f}W_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma,m^{2}),

(1.12)

which we call
complete massive kernels
. Note that the presence of an exponentially decaying factor
\exp(-\tau m^{2})
in
W_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma,m^{2})
(
1.10
) ensures good convergence of the integral in (
1.12
) at the IR limit
\tau=\infty
. Accordingly, the functions
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
are well defined for all values of the parameter
\alpha
.

In
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
we used a simple but effective technique of integral transforms to compute the basis
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
and complete massive
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
kernels for operator functions
f(\hat{F})
of three types important for applications, namely for
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})/\hat{F}^{\mu}
,
1/(\hat{F}^{\mu}+\lambda)
, and finally for
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})/(\hat{F}^{\mu}+\lambda)
. It turned out that for these operator functions (as well as for many others of interest), the corresponding kernels can be universally represented as
N
-fold MB integrals, where
N
is one less than the number of dimensional parameters in the problem under consideration.

In this paper, which is a continuation of
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, we take the MB integrals obtained there as a starting point and examine their properties in detail in both non-resonant and resonant cases in Secs.
4
and
5
, respectively. Before discussing these specific examples, however, we need to examine the general properties of multiple MB integrals in greater detail, which will be done in the preliminary Sec.
2
.

2
Elements of MB integral theory

Multiple MB integrals will be defined as a multiple inverse Mellin transform. In this regard, it is useful to recall some properties of Mellin transforms, which will be done in Subsec.
2.1
. Next, in Subsec.
2.2
, we give the precise definition and discuss some subtle questions about it. Finally, Subsec.
2.3
will be devoted to series representations of MB integrals.

2.1
Mellin transforms

Let some function
H(z)
be absolutely integrable on any interval that is a proper subset of the positive semi-axis
(0,+\infty)
, and
H(z)\sim z^{-a}
for
z\to 0
, and
H(z)\sim z^{-b}
for
z\to\infty
(where
a,b
can be equal to
\pm\infty
which means that
H(z)
grows or decreases faster than any power). Then, obviously, the integral

h(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z^{s-1}H(z),

(2.1)

converges at the lower limit
z=0
for any complex parameter
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s>a
and at the upper limit
z=\infty
for any
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<b
. Consequently, if
a<b
, the integral (
2.1
) converges absolutely for any
s
lying in a vertical strip
a<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<b
, which is called the
fundamental strip
of the function
H(z)
. Then the integral (
2.1
) defines a holomorphic function
h(s)
on it, which can be analytically continued beyond the fundamental strip and is called the
direct Mellin transform
of the function
H(z)
.

Then the Mellin inversion theorem states that the integral

H(z)=\int\limits_{w-i\infty}^{w+i\infty}\frac{ds}{2\pi i}\,z^{-s}h(s),

(2.2)

where the integration is over a vertical straight contour lying in the fundamental strip
a<w<b
, recovers the original function
H(z)
.

Here it is necessary to note two very important circumstances. First, in contrast to the Fourier transforms more familiar to physicists, the direct and inverse Mellin transforms are fundamentally asymmetric. The direct Mellin transform (
2.1
), when it is defined at all, is always unique: for each function
H(z)
its fundamental strip and (if it is not empty) the Mellin image
h(s)
in it are uniquely defined. On the contrary, its inverse (
2.2
) is ambiguous: if we are given some function
h(s)
that has several vertical analyticity strips
a_{k}<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<b_{k}
(separated by some singularities), then the integrals (
2.2
) in which the vertical contours lie in these strips will define different functions
H_{k}(z)
(differing from each other by residues in the singularities of the function
h(s)
).

Let us illustrate this with a simple example: consider the usual exponential
H(z)=e^{-z}
. We can see that the integral (
2.1
) for it exactly coincides with (
A.1
), whence it follows that the fundamental strip coincides with the half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s>0
, and the Mellin image is the gamma function
h(s)=\Gamma(s)
. However, the gamma function
\Gamma(s)
, in addition to the half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s>0
, has an infinite number of other analyticity strips
-n<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<-n+1
. And if we calculate the integral (
2.2
) with a contour lying in such a strip, then obviously the result will differ by residues at the
n
poles of the function
\Gamma(s)
lying to the right of this contour. The result is the function obtained from the exponential by subtracting the first
n
terms of its Taylor series

H_{n}(z)=e^{-z}-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-z)^{k}}{k!}.

(2.3)

We have
H_{n}(z)\sim z^{n}
at
z\to 0
and
H_{n}(z)\sim z^{n-1}
at
z\to\infty
, so the integral (
2.1
) is convergent only for
-n<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<-n+1
. Therefore it is the fundamental strip for
H_{n}(z)
. And all these functions
H_{n}(z)
have the same Mellin image
\Gamma(s)
.

Secondly, for some functions
H(z)
the direct Mellin transform may be not defined at all in the sense that the integral (
2.1
) diverges for all values of the parameter
s
, and the fundamental strip of the function
H(z)
is empty. However, even in this case the function
H(z)
can be represented as an integral of the form (
2.2
), the integration in which will now be not over a straight vertical contour, but over its deformation that bypasses the singularities of the function
h(s)
. Moreover, if the original function
H_{\alpha}(z)
depends on some external parameter
\alpha
, and in some range of
\alpha
the integral (
2.1
) diverges everywhere, then its “Mellin image” will still be given by the same function
h_{\alpha}(s)
, continued from the region of
\alpha
in which the Mellin transform is well defined.

Another simple illustrative example: consider the function

H_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{(1+z)^{\alpha}},

(2.4)

where
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
. It is easy to verify that for
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha>0
this function has a fundamental strip
0<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits s<\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha
, inside which the integral (
2.1
) converges and generates the following Mellin image

h_{\alpha}(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dz\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)z^{s-1}}{(1+z)^{\alpha}}=\Gamma(s)\Gamma(\alpha-s).

(2.5)

This function can be analytically continued to the entire complex plane except for the points
s=-n
and
s=\alpha+n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where the gamma functions have poles. Then the original function can be reconstructed by the formula (
2.2
) with a straight contour lying in the fundamental strip (see Fig.
1(a)
).

However, for
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha<0
the integral (
2.1
) diverges for any values of
s
—the fundamental strip is empty, and the direct Mellin transform is not defined. Nevertheless, even in this case the original function can be represented by an integral of the form (
2.2
)

H_{\alpha}(z)=\int\limits_{C}\frac{ds\,z^{-s}}{2\pi i}\Gamma(s)\Gamma(\alpha-s),

(2.6)

where the integration contour
C
is now not straight, but curved and separating the poles of the gamma functions
\Gamma(s)
and
\Gamma(\alpha-s)
(see Fig.
1(b)
).

A completely analogous situation occurs in the much more general case of the well-known Fox
H
-functions
H_{p,q}^{m,n}\!\left[z\left|\,\begin{smallmatrix}(a,A)\\
(b,B)\end{smallmatrix}\right.\right]
, for which the Mellin image has the form

h(s)=\frac{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma(b_{i}+B_{i}s)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(1-a_{j}-A_{j}s)}{\prod\limits_{i=m+1}^{q}\Gamma(1-b_{i}-B_{i}s)\prod\limits_{j=n+1}^{p}\Gamma(a_{j}+A_{j}s)},

(2.7)

where
A_{j}
and
B_{i}
are positive, and the corresponding arrangement of poles is shown schematically in Figure
1(c)
. If the leftward- and rightward-running poles do not coalesce, it is always possible to draw a contour
C
that would separate them. Clearly, the parameters
a_{j}
and
b_{i}
may be such that this contour cannot be straightened. In this case, the integral (
2.1
) will diverge for any value of
s
. However, by continuously varying the parameters
a_{j}
and
b_{i}
, we can always pull the poles apart so that a non-empty fundamental strip appears. And in this strip, the integral (
2.1
) will reproduce the expression (
2.7
).

This observation is a rigorous justification of the procedure we use for regularizing divergent integrals by analytic continuation with respect to a parameter.

2.2
Multiple MB integrals

N
-fold MB integrals define a class special functions of hypergeometric type of
N
complex variables. Suppose that in addition to
N
, we are also given another integer
m
. We will assume that the index
a
runs from 1 to
N
, and the index
i
runs from 1 to
m
. Suppose we are given data of the three following types:

•

vector
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{i})
of length
m
with nonzero integer components;

•

(N\times m)
-matrix
A=(A_{i}^{a})
with real components, it can be decomposed into
m
column vectors
\bm{A}_{i}=(A_{i}^{a})
, which must be nonzero
\bm{A}_{i}\neq 0
;

•

finally, a vector
\bm{a}=(a_{i})
of length
m
with arbitrary complex components.

First, we define a function that is the product of
m
gamma factors with arguments linear in
N
complex variables
\bm{s}=(s_{a})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
:

h\left(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\bm{s}\right)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma_{i},\qquad\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma^{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{s}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}-a_{i}),

(2.8)

where
\bm{s}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}=s_{a}A_{i}^{a}
. Then a
N
-fold MB integral will look like

H\left(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\bm{z}\right)=\int\limits_{C}\frac{d^{N}s}{(2\pi i)^{N}}\,h(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\bm{s})\,\bm{z}^{-\bm{s}},

(2.9)

where
\bm{z}=(z_{a})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\bm{z}^{-\bm{s}}=\exp(-\bm{s}\cdot\ln\bm{z})
for
|\arg z_{a}|<\pi
.
3
3

3

Here and below for functions such as
\ln
,
|\ldots|
,
\arg
, etc. we agree to assume that
f(\bm{z})=\big(f(z_{1}),\ldots,f(z_{N})\big)
.
The integration here is over some
N
-cycle
C
, which will be characterized in detail below.

First of all, we note that the Mellin image
h(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\bm{z})
(
2.8
) does not depend on the order of the gamma factors
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma^{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{s}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}-a_{i})
. This means that the MB integral
H(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\bm{z})
does not change under a simultaneous permutation of the components of the vectors
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{a}
and the columns of the matrix
A
(just as its value does not change under a simultaneous permutation of the arguments
\bm{z}
and the rows of the matrix
A
). Since the role of the gamma factor
\Gamma_{i}
is determined by the sign of the parameter
\alpha_{i}
, it is convenient to agree they are ordered such that the first
m_{+}
factors are with positive powers
\alpha_{i}>0
, followed by
m_{-}
factors with negative powers
\alpha_{i}<0
,
m_{+}+m_{-}=m
. We will also denote
\mathcal{A}_{+}=\{\bm{A}_{i}\mid\alpha_{i}>0\}
.

The singularities of the function
h(\bm{s})
(
2.8
) are determined by the singularities of the
m_{+}
gamma factors
\Gamma_{i}
for which
\alpha_{i}>0
. Each of these factors
\Gamma_{i}
generates an infinite family of polar hyperplanes of complex dimension
(N-1)

L_{i,n}=\{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\bm{s}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}=a_{i}-n\}.

(2.10)

The
N
-cycle
C
over which the integration (
2.9
) is performed is defined as follows: one can always choose the parameters
\bm{a}
such that a straight vertical contour
\bm{\gamma}+i\mathbb{R}^{N}
can be drawn such that for each
i=1,\ldots,m_{+}
it lies to the same side of all polar hyperplanes
L_{i,n}
(
2.10
) (i.e.
\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}>\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a_{i}
), then we choose it as
C
. If the parameters
\bm{a}
become such that a straight vertical contour with the specified property ceases to exist,
C
should be understood as a continuous deformation of
\bm{\gamma}+i\mathbb{R}^{N}
that does not intersect the polar hyperplanes
L_{i,n}
.

The multiple MB integral (
2.9
) can converge or diverge depending on the external parameter
\bm{z}
. The situation is governed by the following key invariant:

\varpi=\min_{\|\bm{x}\|=1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\,|\bm{x}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}|,

(2.11)

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Using the Stirling formula (
A.9
), it is easy to show
Zhdanov and Tsikh 1998
that for
\varpi\leq 0
the integral (
2.9
) diverges for any
\bm{z}
. If
\varpi>0
, it converges absolutely in the domain

\begin{split}U_{\varpi}=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}&\mid z_{a}\neq 0,\;\\
&|\arg z_{a}|<\pi,\;\|\arg\bm{z}\|<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\varpi\}\end{split}

(2.12)

and defines an analytic function in it, which can then be analytically extended from
U_{\varpi}
to all
\mathbb{C}^{N}
except a certain singular surface of lower dimension (for Feynman integrals, it is called a “Landau surface”).

For
\varpi>0
and for the
N
-fold MB integral (
2.9
) to be well-defined, it is necessary, in particular, that the number of gamma factors with positive powers be no less than the dimension
m_{+}\geq N
, and that
\mathcal{A}_{+}
contain at least one set of
N
vectors that forms a basis for the
\bm{s}
-space. For brevity, we will call such sets
clusters
and denote them by subsets
I\subset\{1,\ldots,m_{+}\}
.

Finally, we note that the linear structure of the arguments of the gamma factors
\Gamma_{i}
in (
2.8
) is preserved under general affine transformations of
\bm{s}
-space. That is, under shifts by an arbitrary complex vector
\bm{l}=(l_{a})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
\bm{s}\mapsto\bm{s}+\bm{l}
, and under arbitrary invertible linear transformations defined by a real matrix
M=(M_{b}^{a})
,
\bm{s}\mapsto\bm{s}M=s_{b}M_{a}^{b}
. This leads to the relations:

\displaystyle H(\alpha_{i},\bm{A}_{i},a_{i}|\,\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\bm{z}^{\bm{l}}\;H(\alpha_{i},\bm{A}_{i},a_{i}+\bm{l}\cdot\bm{A}_{i}|\,\bm{z})

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\det M\;H(\alpha_{i},M\bm{A}_{i},a_{i}|\,\bm{u}),

(2.14)

where
M\bm{A}_{i}=M_{b}^{a}A_{i}^{b}
and
\ln\bm{u}=M\ln\bm{z}
.

2.3
Series representations

Non-resonant and resonant cases.

We will assume that the coefficients of the matrix
A
introduced above (and so its column vectors
\bm{A}_{i}
) are fixed, while the complex parameters
\bm{a}=(a_{i})
are variable. For each cluster
I
, we denote
\bm{a}_{I}=(a_{i})_{i\in I}
. Analogously, by
A_{I}
we denote the square
(N\times N)
-matrix that results if we retain in
A
only the columns
\bm{A}_{i}
for which
i\in I
. Since they are linearly independent by definition,
\Delta_{I}=\det A_{I}\neq 0
. But then the
N
polar hyperplanes of
L_{i,n}
corresponding to a given cluster
I
will intersect at an infinite family of points, which we will call
simple poles
,

\bm{s}_{I}(\bm{n})=(\bm{a}_{I}-\bm{n})A_{I}^{-1},

(2.15)

where
A_{I}^{-1}
is the matrix inverse to
A_{I}
, and
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}
.

The following fundamental alternative holds:

•

In the generic
non-resonant
case (i.e., for almost all values of the parameter
\bm{a}
), the simple poles do not coincide with each other. In other words, there are no points at which more than
N
polar hyperplanes
L_{i,n}
intersect.

•

However, for some exceptional values of the parameter
\bm{a}
, several simple poles can coalesce, generating a point at which more than
N
polar hyperplanes
L_{i,n}
intersect. Such an exceptional case is called
resonant
.

Although the resonant case is exceptional, it is extremely important from a physical point of view. For example, dimensionally and analytically regularized Feynman integrals belong to the non-resonant case, but removing the regularization leads to the resonant case. On the other hand, the generic non-resonant case is much simpler, since then a multidimensional residue at a simple pole can be understood simply as repeatedly taking
N
ordinary 1-dimensional residues with respect to the variables
s_{a}
, and if the simple poles coalesce, this requires the use of the local Grothendieck residue. Moreover, the non-resonant case is in a sense more fundamental, since the resonant case can be always obtained from it by taking the appropriate limit with respect to the parameter
\bm{a}
. Therefore, it is correct to first consider the generic non-resonant case and only then move on to the resonant one.

Cluster series.

The study of the non-resonant case leads to a very important concept of a
cluster series
. We will call this the formal series obtained by summing the residues of the integrand in (
2.9
) at the simple poles
\bm{s}_{I}(\bm{n})
(
2.15
) generated by some given cluster
I
:

\mathcal{H}_{I}(\bm{z})=\sum\limits_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}}\Res\limits_{\bm{s}=\bm{s}_{I}(\bm{n})}\left(h(\bm{s})\,\bm{z}^{-\bm{s}}\right).

(2.16)

In the simplest case when
\alpha_{i}=1
for all
i\in I
, such cluster series reduces to the Horn series:

\mathcal{H}_{I}(\bm{z})=\Delta_{I}^{-1}\bm{u}^{-\bm{a}_{I}}\sum\limits_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}}c_{\bm{n}}\frac{(-\bm{u})^{\bm{n}}}{\bm{n}!},

(2.17)

where

\displaystyle\ln\bm{u}=A_{I}^{-1}\ln\bm{z},\quad\ln\bm{z}=A_{I}\ln\bm{u},

(2.18)

\displaystyle c_{\bm{n}}=\prod\limits_{i\notin I}\Gamma^{\alpha_{i}}(-\bm{n}\cdot\bm{B}_{i}-b_{i}),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{i}=A_{I}^{-1}\bm{A}_{i},\quad b_{i}=a_{i}-\bm{a}_{I}A_{I}^{-1}\bm{A}_{i}.

(2.20)

In the case when
\alpha_{i}=2,3,\ldots
for some
i\in I
, the corresponding factors
\Gamma_{i}
have poles of higher orders. Then we will have a generalized logarithmic series (containing, along with the powers
\bm{u}^{\bm{n}}
, also the powers of the logarithms
\ln u_{a}
) with polygamma functions
\psi^{(k)}(n_{i})
in its coefficients. Although deriving the corresponding expressions does not present any difficulty, they do not appear in this work and are rather cumbersome, so we will not present them here.

Reduction of a 2-fold MB integral to combination of cluster series.

Using a multivariate generalization of the residue theorem, an
N
-fold MB integral
H(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\,\bm{z})
(
2.9
) can be (generally speaking, ambiguously) represented as combinations of the above-introduced cluster series
\mathcal{H}_{I}(\bm{z})
(
2.16
).

In the trivial case
N=1
, the MB integral is simply the well-known Fox
H
-function (
2.7
), and there are only two representations: we can “close the contour”
C
either to the right or to the left and reduce the integral to the sum of residues at all leftward- or rightward-running poles, respectively, resulting in expansions near the points
z=0
or
z=\infty
.

In fact, the case
N=2
is also almost trivial in the sense that these representations can also be constructed without any difficulty. The corresponding algorithm was basically built in
Zhdanov and Tsikh 1998
. However, already for
N=3
, the situation becomes significantly more complex, and some subtle points and pitfalls arise.

However, in the present paper, only a single 3-fold integral appears, and one that does not encounter the difficulties noted. Therefore, we will limit ourselves to briefly presenting the algorithm for
N=2
without justification, and will not touch on the case
N>2
at all.

In the case
N=2
, each cluster is a pair of nonzero, non-collinear real vectors
\bm{A}_{i}
and
\bm{A}_{j}
on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which we will denote by
\{ij\}
. The conical hull of these vectors is a sector with an angle less than
\pi
, which we will denote by
S_{ij}
. Among all sectors defined by different clusters of vectors from
\mathcal{A}_{+}
, there exist minimal ones, i.e., ones such that no other vector from
\mathcal{A}_{+}
falls within them.

So, each such minimal sector
S
defines its own representation of a 2-fold MB integral
H(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\,\bm{z})
as the sum of cluster series
\mathcal{H}_{ij}(\bm{z})
, over all clusters
\{ij\}
such that the corresponding sectors
S_{ij}
contain the given minimal sector
S
:

H(\bm{\alpha_{i}},A,\bm{a}|\,\bm{z})\approx\sum\limits_{\{ij\}\colon S\subset S_{ij}}\mathcal{H}_{ij}(\bm{z}).

(2.21)

Convergence of series.

In the formula (
2.21
) above, we put the sign
\approx
instead of the equality sign, since until now we have not asked the question: do the cluster series
\mathcal{H}_{ij}(\bm{z})
on the right-hand side of (
2.21
) converge and, if so, for what
\bm{z}
? The general answer to this question is controlled by the vector parameter

\bm{A}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\alpha_{k}\bm{A}_{k},

(2.22)

and for the case
N=2
it is also very simple. The cluster series
\mathcal{H}_{ij}(\bm{u})
is absolutely convergent everywhere if the vector
\bm{A}
lies inside the sector
S_{ij}
4
4

4

It is important that the variable
\bm{u}
(
2.18
) is used here, not
\bm{z}
. Accordingly, the singularities with respect to the variables
z_{a}
lie either at infinity or at zero.
, and asymptotic (divergent) if
\bm{A}
lies outside of
S_{ij}
. If it lies exactly on the boundary of
S_{ij}
, then it converges in some region
U_{ij}
and has a singularity on its boundary.

Accordingly, only three cases are possible:

•

Unbalanced
case: the vector
\bm{A}\neq 0
lies in the minimal sector
S_{\mathrm{conv}}
. Then all cluster series in the (
2.21
) representation for this sector
S_{\mathrm{conv}}
will be absolutely convergent, and for any other minimal sector
S
, some of the cluster series in the corresponding (
2.21
) representation will be asymptotic.

•

Balanced
case:
\bm{A}=0
. Then, for each minimal sector
S
, the (
2.21
) representation is well-defined in its domain, which is the intersection of the corresponding domains
U_{ij}
, and these representations analytically continue each other.

•

Finally, in the intermediate case, the vector
\bm{A}\neq 0
lies exactly on the boundary of the two minimal sectors
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Then the corresponding representations (
2.21
) for these two sectors are each defined in their own domains
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
and analytically continue each other. And for any other sector
S
, the corresponding representation will contain asymptotic cluster series.

Pinches and the logarithmic case

So far, we have considered the generic non-resonant case, where the parameters
\bm{a}
are such that the simple poles
\bm{s}_{I}(\bm{n})
(
2.15
) do not coalesce. Then, their residues form cluster series (
2.16
), and different clusters do not interact with each other, leading to a sum over clusters (
2.21
).

However, for some values of the parameter
\bm{a}
, some of the coefficients in the cluster series (
2.16
) may become infinite. This situation always corresponds to a coalescence of several simple poles
\bm{s}_{I}(\bm{n})
(
2.15
) generated by different clusters
I
. Thus, the corresponding resonant case can be understood as an interaction between clusters. The following fundamental alternative is possible:

•

It may happen that the
N
-cycle
C
is pinched between coalescing simple poles. So this situation is called a
“pinch”
. Then the divergence of the coefficients in the cluster series (
2.16
) leads to the entire MB integral
H(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\,\bm{z})
becoming infinite, i.e. it has a pole as a function of the parameter
\bm{a}
.

•

If no pinch occurs, the coalescing simple poles form a higher order pole, the residue at which should be understood as a local Grothendieck residue. Its calculation leads to the appearance of logarithms, which is why this case is called
logarithmic
in the literature. Although divergences arise in the coefficients of the formal cluster series (
2.16
), they cancel each other out in such a way that the entire MB integral
H(\bm{\alpha},A,\bm{a}|\,\bm{z})
remains finite. This simply means that in the logarithmic case, clusters generating coalescing simple poles can no longer be considered independent.

3
General structure of basis and complete kernels

In this section, we consider the structure of basis
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
(
1.9
) and complete massive
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
(
1.12
) kernels. While the general statements presented here clarify the specific examples discussed later in Sec.
4
, they are “empirical” in nature in that they simply generalize observations on analytic expressions in these specific examples. The question of their rigorous justification and applicability is certainly of considerable interest, but it remains outside the scope here.

First of all, note that it is also convenient to introduce a third family of functions

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}[f|m^{2}]=\mathfrak{L}_{f}M_{\alpha}(\tau,m^{2}),

(3.1)

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}(\tau,m^{2})=\frac{\tau^{-\alpha}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\tau m^{2}\right).

(3.2)

We call them
complementary kernels
because their properties are indeed complementary in many aspects to those of the basis kernels
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
. In particular, if the integral (
1.9
) always converges well at the UV limit
\tau=0
, but can diverge at the IR limit
\tau=\infty
for sufficiently small
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha
, then the situation with the integral (
3.1
) is exactly the opposite: it converges well at the IR limit
\tau=\infty
, but can begin to diverge on the UV limit
\tau=0
for sufficiently large
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\alpha
(and in this case, too, it requires regularization via analytic continuation).

Next, we apply our trick with term-by-term integration of asymptotic expansions to the definition of complete massive kernels
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
(
1.12
). If in the expression
W_{\alpha}(\tau,\sigma,m^{2})
(
1.10
) we expand the exponential
\exp(-\tau m^{2})
into a power series corresponding to the UV limit
\tau\to 0
, apply the integral transform
\mathfrak{L}_{f}
(
1.6
) to it term-by-term, and use the definition (
1.9
), then we immediately obtain the expansion

\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]\xrightarrow{\text{UV}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-m^{2})^{k}}{k!}\,\mathbb{B}_{\alpha-k}[f|\sigma].

(3.3)

On the contrary, if in (
1.10
) we expand the exponential
\exp(-\sigma/2\tau)
into a power series corresponding to the IR limit
\tau\to\infty
, apply
\mathfrak{L}_{f}
(
1.6
) to it term-by-term, and use the definition of (
3.1
), we obtain a completely different expansion

\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]\xrightarrow{\text{IR}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\sigma/2)^{n}}{n!}\,\mathbb{M}_{\alpha+n}[f|m^{2}].

(3.4)

Although these transforms involve manipulations with divergent series and integrals, as we explained in
Barvinsky et al. 2026a
;
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, the remarkable fact is that the expressions (
3.3
)-(
3.4
) are not meaningless at all, but rather somehow determine the structure of the complete massive kernel
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
(
1.12
). In the simplest case of complex power
f(\hat{F})=\hat{F}^{-\mu}
, as we discussed in detail in
Barvinsky et al. 2026a
;
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
and will briefly reproduce below in Subsec.
4.1
, the complete massive kernel
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[F^{-\mu}|\sigma,m^{2}]
is exactly equal to the sum of the two series (
4.7
) corresponding to the expressions (
3.3
)-(
3.4
). In the general case of an arbitrary operator function
f(\hat{F})
, the situation is slightly more complicated.

By carefully comparing the series representations obtained for specific functions
f(\hat{F})
below in Sec.
4
, we can verify that the expression (
1.12
) is formed by series of three types: some of them are present simultaneously in both expansions (
3.3
)-(
3.4
)—we will call them
regular
; while others—we will call them
singular
—are present in only one of them. These, in turn, are clearly divided into those coming from the UV region, i.e., appearing only in the expansion (
3.3
), or from the IR region, i.e., appearing only in the expansion (
3.4
). We will denote the corresponding contributions generated by terms of these three types by the superscripts ‘reg’, ‘UV’, and ‘IR’.

It is also convenient, in basis
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
(
1.9
) and complementary
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}[f|m^{2}]
(
3.1
) kernels, to separate the contributions that generate regular and singular terms in complete kernels
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
(
1.12
). By definition, the basis kernel
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]
will contain only regular and UV singular contributions, while the complementary kernel
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}[f|m^{2}]
, conversely, will contain only regular and IR singular contributions. As a result, we obtain the following decompositions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}+\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}[f|m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}+\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}+\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}}+\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}}.

(3.7)

In this case, the corresponding singular parts will be related by the relations

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-m^{2})^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{B}_{\alpha-k}^{\text{UV}}[f|\sigma],

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\sigma/2)^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{M}_{\alpha+n}^{\text{IR}}[f|m^{2}],

(3.9)

and for the regular part of complete massive kernels, two types of relations will hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-m^{2})^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{B}_{\alpha-k}^{\text{reg}}[f|\sigma],

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\sigma/2)^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{M}_{\alpha+n}^{\text{reg}}[f|m^{2}].

(3.10)

It naturally arises a hypothesis that the compatibility of these relations must be ensured by the existence of some fourth family of objects
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}[f]
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|m^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-m^{2})^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{L}_{\alpha-k}[f],

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|\sigma]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\sigma/2)^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{L}_{\alpha+n}[f],

(3.12)

where
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}[f]
does not depend neither on
\sigma
nor
m^{2}
. We will call these objects the
regular kernels
. The specific examples discussed further in Sec.
4
confirm this assumption.

To summarize this discussion, we have learned how to reduce the “two-variable” function
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
to two “one-variable” functions
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}}[f|\sigma]
and
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}}[f|m^{2}]
and one “constant”
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}[f]
, which is a significant simplification.

The expansions (
3.10
)-(
3.12
) imply the following structure of limits for regular contributions:

\textstyle{\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m^{2}\to 0}
\scriptstyle{\sigma\to 0}
\textstyle{\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|\sigma]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sigma\to 0}
\textstyle{\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{reg}}[f|m^{2}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces.}
\scriptstyle{m^{2}\to 0}
\textstyle{\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}[f]}

(3.13)

At the same time, the coincidence limit
\sigma\to 0
for the ultraviolet part of the basis kernel
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}}[f|\sigma]
behaves simply as a power of
\sigma
. And similarly, the massless limit
m^{2}\to 0
for the infrared part of the complementary kernel
\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}}[f|m^{2}]
behaves simply as a power of
m^{2}
. That is, they either tend to infinity or vanish depending on the values of the parameter
\alpha
. It is for this reason that we call the different contributions “regular” and “singular”.

4.1
The complex power
\hat{F}^{-\mu}

First of all, let us recall that the basis and complementary kernels for the operator power

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\alpha}\!\big[F^{-\mu}\big|\sigma\big]

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{\alpha}\!\big[F^{-\mu}\big|m^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu-\alpha\right)}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(\mu)}\,m^{2(\alpha-\mu)}.

(4.2)

(It is convenient to think of them as trivial MB integrals “of

Further, the complete massive kernel for the power
\hat{F}^{-\mu}

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}\!\big[F^{-\mu}\big|\sigma,m^{2}\big]=\frac{2m^{2(\alpha-\mu)}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(\mu)}\;\mathcal{K}_{\alpha-\mu}(z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{-\alpha-1}\,\exp\left(-t-\frac{z}{t}\right),\quad z=\frac{\sigma m^{2}}{2}.

(4.4)

As we explained in detail in
Barvinsky et al. 2026a

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(z)=\int\limits_{C}\frac{ds}{2\pi i}\,z^{-s}\kappa_{\alpha}(s),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(s)\Gamma(s-\alpha).

(4.6)

Further, we will systematically use this notation throughout: we will use a capital base symbol for the MB integral (e.g.
\mathcal{K}
), and a lowercase base symbol for its Mellin image (e.g.

-fold inverse Mellin transform (

).

The Mellin image
\kappa_{\alpha}(s)
has two rows of leftward-running poles

and

,

. In the terminology of the general theory presented in Subsec.

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(z)
as a series for

4.4

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\text{IR}}(z)+\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\text{UV}}(z),

5
Resonant case example

In this concluding section we explicitly compute series representations for MB integrals in the resonant case which arises when several simple poles coalesce for some values of parameters
\bm{a}
in (

unless it is explicitly stated otherwise. As we have noted in Sec.
2.3
, the resonant case is of particular significance from the physical point of view, since, for example, parameter
\alpha
is defined as
\alpha=d/2-n
, where

corresponds to the ordinal number of the HaMiDeW coefficient, so, naturally, it must take integer values. However, as we showed in
Barvinsky et al. 2026b

As these pinches only arise in the resonant case, the non-resonant expressions serve as regularized versions of corresponding resonant series. For example, representations with
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}
can be interpreted as dimensional regularization of IR divergences. Consistency of this procedure with massive regularization was directly verified in
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, by comparing divergent and finite terms of resonant series for IR-divergent basis kernels
\mathbb{B}_{\alpha+\epsilon}[f|\sigma]
,
\epsilon\to 0
with massless limits of complete massless kernel resonant series
\mathbb{W}_{\alpha}[f|\sigma,m^{2}]
,
m^{2}\to 0
, for several examples of operator functions

. These explicit checks relied on series representations of certain double MB integrals in the resonant case, which were stated without a proper derivation. The goal of this section is to fill in this gap.

An important thing to note is that strictly speaking, there is no need to explicitly obtain resonant series representations separately, since due to the principle of continuity for local residue
Griffiths and Harris 1978
they can be obtained from the corresponding non-resonant series representations by taking the appropriate limit in the parameter space. Therefore, in some sense, the non-resonant case is more fundamental even though it does not directly correspond to any physical scenario. Despite this fact we choose to do a separate resonant computation anyway for reasons of completeness and additional verification. Moreover, in the computation below we make use of the local multidimensional residue, so this section might be useful to some readers who would like to familiarize themselves with this object.

The only series representation of resonant

5.1

Consider a double Mellin-Barnes integral with a straight contour

\displaystyle=\Res_{\bm{s}=\bm{0}}\frac{w(\bm{s})}{g_{1}(\bm{s})\dots g_{N}(\bm{s})}\det

At the same time, this also serves as an additional verification of our approach, in which we avoid separate consideration of the resonant series, since they can be obtained from the non-resonant series by the appropriate limit procedure even in cases when the final answer is singular due to pinches.

6
Conclusion

This marks the end to the series of papers
Barvinsky et al. 2026a
;
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, where we have developed a systematic method for obtaining off-diagonal expansions for integral kernels of functions of Laplace-type operators on curved background. The technique, based on the off-diagonal functoriality of the DeWitt expansion, yields asymptotic series in the heat coefficients of the operator with the basis kernels as coefficients of the series. Both basis and complete kernels are functions of the Synge world function are independent of the underlying geometric data and the exact nature of the Laplace-type operator itself. Hypergeometric nature of these functions makes it highly convenient to apply the formalism of
N
-fold Mellin-Barnes integrals to study them.

The developed method has direct applications to the development of heat kernel methods for nonminimal operators, necessary for studying various models with nonminimal wave operators. In general, it should be useful in many areas of theoretical physics and mathematics, in particular, the theory of pseudodifferential operators and its applications. It could also be of use in studying various theories, such as higher-derivative nonlocal gravities.

In the present paper we have obtained and analyzed series representations for basis and complete kernels for a family of Laplace-type operator family of functions
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})/(\hat{F}^{\mu}+\lambda)
in both resonant and non-resonant cases. We have shown that the former case, despite being physically more relevant is, in some sense, less fundamental than the latter. Indeed, the series representation in the resonant case can be obtained by taking the appropriate limit of the non-resonant series in the parameter space. And, as illustrated in the last Sect.
5
this limit procedure remains meaningful even if the resonant series bears singularities due to contour pinches in the corresponding Mellin-Barnes integrals.

Apart from the complete massive and basis kernels of the operator function
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})/(\hat{F}^{\mu}+\lambda)
, we have studied simpler operator functions such as the complex power
\hat{F}^{-\mu}
, the resolvent of the power
1/(\hat{F}^{\mu}+\lambda)
, the heat kernel of the power
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})
, and the hybrid kernel
\exp(-\tau\hat{F}^{\nu})/\hat{F}^{\mu}
. Using Mellin-Barnes representations for basis and complete massive kernels for these operator functions obtained in
Barvinsky et al. 2026b
, we have obtained series representations for these hypergeometric-type functions, which allowed us to explore and verify various limits which connect them. Apart from serving as a verification of the generalized functorial technique, calculating these limits allowed us to suggest direct physical meaning to the individual cluster series, which together comprise the complete series representation. We concluded that in general, the basis and complete kernels contain several types of terms, which in simple cases can be directly associated with the IR and UV limits.

Among the open problems related to the present generalized functorial technique we would like to specifically mention the computation of IR asymptotic series, at least in the simplest geometries. Another interesting direction for further research would be the complete classification of cluster series for multiple Mellin-Barnes integrals according to their physical meaning is of interest.

Among the possible generalizations of this approach we would like to mention two principal directions. Firstly, one can consider kernels of operators of non Laplace-type, be that nonminimal or higher-order ones. In both cases, the starting point of the functorial technique—the heat kernel—has a more complicated structure than a relatively simple DeWitt series, however, we believe that this generalization is possible. Another, much more difficult task is to obtain expansions for the kernels of functions of multiple operators. This problem is directly related to many areas of ongoing research including multiloop calculations on a curved background and is thus of great interest.

Regarding the technique of calculating series representations of
N
-fold Mellin-Barnes integrals, which we extensively use in the paper, we have encountered some subtleties with the method of
Ananthanarayan et al. 2021
for
N>2
. Although this does not affect any of the integrals we encountered, given the importance and scope of applications of MB integrals future work should focus on these issues.

Appendix A
Euler gamma function and its properties

Euler gamma function is defined using the well-known integral

\Gamma(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt,

(A.1)

absolutely converging for
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits z>0
. Integrating by parts, we prove the key relation

\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z),

(A.2)

from which it follows that the gamma function is a generalization of the factorial
\Gamma(n+1)=n!
, since (
A.1
) yields
\Gamma(1)=1
. On the other hand, using the relation (
A.2
) the gamma function can be analytically extended to the domain
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits z\leq 0
to a meromorphic function defined on the entire complex plane, except for simple poles at the points
z=-n
with residues

\Res_{z=-n}\Gamma(z)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}.

(A.3)

Important properties of the gamma function are the reflection formula

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)},

(A.4)

and the multiplication theorem

\prod\limits_{k=0}^{m-1}\Gamma\big(z+\tfrac{k}{m}\big)=(2\pi)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}m^{\frac{1}{2}-mz}\Gamma(mz),

(A.5)

which at
m=2
turns into the well-known Legendre duplication formula.

The well-known beta integral is expressed in a simple way through the gamma function:

B(x,y)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}dt=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}.

(A.6)

Another relation, often used in physics (in calculating Feynman integrals), has the form:

(x+y)^{-\alpha}=\frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int\limits_{C}\frac{ds}{2\pi i}\,\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(\alpha+s)\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{-s},

(A.7)

where the integration contour
C
separates leftward- and rightward-running poles.

A universal tool for analyzing asymptotic behavior of gamma function is the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\ln\Gamma(a+s)

\displaystyle\approx\ln\sqrt{2\pi}+\left(a+s-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln s-s

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k(k+1)}B_{k+1}(a)s^{-k},

(A.8)

for
|\,s|\,\to\infty
and
|\,\arg s|\,<\pi-\varepsilon
(here
B_{k}
are Bernoulli polynomials). Dropping the sum in the second line of this expansion yields the famous Stirling formula:

\Gamma(a+s)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,s^{a+s-\tfrac{1}{2}}\,e^{-s}\left(1+O(s^{-1})\right).

(A.9)
