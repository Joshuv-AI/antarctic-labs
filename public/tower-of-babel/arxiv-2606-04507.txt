Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation
Han Zhu, Chengkun Cai, Yuanfeng Song, Xing Chen, Sirui Han, Yike Guo

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have become increasingly adopted in daily applications, with deep research standing out as a particularly important capability. Unlike traditional question-answering (QA) tasks, deep research report generation lacks definitive ground-truth, making reward design inherently unverifiable and limiting effective reinforcement learning. Existing approaches mitigate this challenge with LLM-as-a-judge and query-dependent evaluation rubrics, but they still rely on static evaluators that cannot adapt their standards as the solver improves, leading to insufficient and eventually saturated optimization pressure. We address this limitation with a s elf-evolving co -evolutionary training framework for deep re search evaluation and generation ( SCORE ), which tightly couples an evaluator and a solver in a shared-parameter learning process. Rather than treating generation and evaluation as isolated modules, we leverage their intrinsic connection to enable joint improvement within a single shared-parameter model. To restrict this process, we introduce a meta-harness, which dynamically controls the evaluation environment based on solver performance, encouraging valid evaluation dimensions and sufficiently deep evaluator search. Extensive experiments on deep research benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvement in report generation quality, showing that co-evolving evaluation and generation is a promising direction for training open-ended research agents.

In a nutshell, our contributions are summarized as below:

We propose SCORE, an evaluator-solver self-play framework for training deep research agents with unverifiable rewards. This framework provide dynamic, multidimensional rewards to optimize the solver’s ability in open-ended report generation, constrained by a meta-harness.

•

We provide a theoretical analysis of SCORE under shared parameters. Our analysis clarifies the role of consistency in evaluator-side adaptation and characterizes the local optimization behavior of shared-parameter alternating updates.

•

We show that our method improves agent performance across multiple evaluation dimensions on deep research benchmarks, and provide a systematic analysis of how parameters affect training procedure under unverifiable rewards.

Methodology

:

3.1
Problem Setting

We study
deep research report generation
, where the goal is to learn a policy

\pi(r\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{q})

over candidate reports
r
for a given query
q
and environment
\mathcal{E}_{q}
. We assume that report quality is governed by a latent functional

Q(r;q,\mathcal{E}_{q}),

and the ideal objective is to assign more probability mass to reports with higher latent quality. Here,
q\in\mathcal{Q}
denotes a user query,
r\in\mathcal{R}
denotes a candidate long-form report, and
\mathcal{E}_{q}
denotes the query-specific evidence and evaluation environment.

Unlike QA-style tasks,
Q
is inherently
multi-dimensional
and
query-dependent
: a report may succeed or fail along different axes, such as factual support, evidence coverage, citation grounding, contradiction handling, uncertainty calibration, and discourse coherence. As a result, long-form report generation rarely admits a single reliable verifier or a fixed scalar reward that is both sufficiently informative and broadly applicable.

We therefore formulate deep research as a query-conditioned report optimization problem with an evaluation bottleneck. In particular, the central challenge in training deep research models is how to evaluate long-form reports in a query-specific and stable manner under limited supervision.

3.2
Overview of SCORE

To address this challenge, we propose SCORE, a retrieval-grounded and externally guided evaluator-solver framework for deep research report generation as shown in Figure
2
. SCORE contains four components:

1.

an external evidence environment that can be queried through search and retrieval tools,

2.

a fixed external
meta-harness
that shapes the query-specific evaluation environment,

3.

an
evaluator
that constructs query-conditioned rubrics and produces structured report assessments,

4.

a
solver
that interacts with the evidence environment, gathers supporting evidence, and generates reports under the resulting rubric.

A key design choice of SCORE is that the solver and evaluator are implemented as two functionally distinct roles over a
shared underlying actor
. This design is motivated by the substantial overlap between report generation and report evaluation, both of which require query understanding, evidence modeling, and discourse-level reasoning. We empirically examine this choice in our ablations Section
5.2
.

Training proceeds through sequential alternating updates on the shared actor: the solver is optimized using evaluator-derived report rewards, while the evaluator is optimized using rubric-level auxiliary signals. Both updates are regularized by KL divergence against a reference policy to limit excessive policy drift.

3.3
External Evidence and Query-Specific Environment

For each query
q
, SCORE interacts with an external evidence environment through search and retrieval tools. We denote by

D_{q}^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}(q)

the initial evidence obtained from this environment, where
\mathcal{R}
abstracts the retrieval interaction available to the system. In practice, this evidence environment may include web search, document retrieval, or other tool-mediated evidence sources.

Based on the initial evidence
D_{q}^{(0)}
, SCORE constructs a query-specific evaluation environment through a fixed external
meta-harness
:

\mathcal{E}_{q}=\mathrm{Harness}(q,D_{q}^{(0)},\mathcal{S}),

where
\mathcal{S}
denotes recent training statistics or historical signals available to the harness. The resulting environment
\mathcal{E}_{q}
specifies the admissible evaluation space for the current query, including candidate evaluation dimensions, structural constraints on valid rubrics, process requirements for evidence verification or comparison, and auxiliary task-specific guidance.

The meta-harness is external and non-trainable. It is implemented as a fixed external controller and is not optimized by gradient descent together with the actor. Its role is to shape the admissible evaluation space in which evaluator-solver interaction takes place.

3.4
Evaluator and Solver

Given query
q
and environment
\mathcal{E}_{q}
, the evaluator samples a query-conditioned rubric

(H_{q},w_{q})\sim\pi_{e}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{q}),

where
H_{q}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\}
denotes the selected evaluation dimensions and
w_{q}
their corresponding weights.

Conditioned on the query, the current evidence state, and the evaluator-selected rubric, the solver interacts with the external evidence environment to gather supporting information and generate a group of candidate reports

r_{1},\dots,r_{K}\sim\pi_{s}(\cdot\mid q,D_{q}^{s},H_{q},w_{q}),

where
D_{q}^{s}
denotes the evidence collected by the solver and
K
is the number of solver rollouts sampled for the same query during training.

For assessment, the evaluator may perform constrained supplementary retrieval or verification based on the query and the generated reports, producing its own evidence context
D_{q}^{e}
. It then produces structured judgments over candidate reports, including dimension-wise scoring, evidence support checking, contradiction analysis, and citation verification. These structured judgments are converted into solver-side learning signals.

3.5
Shared Actor and Training Signals

SCORE implements the solver and evaluator as two roles over a shared actor with parameters
\theta
. This design is motivated by the observation that report generation and report evaluation rely on overlapping latent capabilities, including query understanding, evidence modeling, and discourse-level reasoning. Under this view, generation and evaluation are not treated as fully separate functions, but as two role-specific mappings induced from a shared underlying representation. A shared actor therefore provides a common representational substrate for both roles, allowing evaluator-side adaptation to influence the same internal representations used by the solver. We empirically examine this design choice in Section
5.2
.

SCORE uses two distinct reward channels:

\underbrace{R_{s}(r;q,D_{q}^{e},H_{q},w_{q})}_{\text{solver-side report reward}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underbrace{R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q})}_{\text{evaluator-side auxiliary reward}}.

The solver-side reward supervises report generation, while the evaluator-side reward adapts the evaluator’s rubric-selection behavior.

Solver-side report reward.

For each generated report
r
, the evaluator produces a scalar report reward

R_{s}(r;q,D_{q}^{e},H_{q},w_{q}),

where
D_{q}^{e}
denotes the evidence context used by the evaluator for rubric-conditioned assessment,
H_{q}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\}
denotes the selected evaluation dimensions, and
w_{q}=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}
denotes their corresponding weights. This reward serves as the primary supervision signal for optimizing the solver.

In our implementation, the abstract report reward is instantiated as

R_{s}(r)=a(r)\cdot\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\,s_{j}(r;q,D_{q}^{e})\right),

where
a(r)
denotes a report-level validity or accuracy score, and
s_{j}(r;q,D_{q}^{e})
denotes the evaluator score of report
r
on dimension
h_{j}
under evaluator-side evidence context
D_{q}^{e}
. Intuitively, the solver is encouraged to generate reports that are both valid and strong along the evaluator-selected rubric dimensions.

Evaluator-side auxiliary reward.

To adapt the evaluator, SCORE introduces a rubric-level auxiliary reward based on inter-report consistency. For a fixed query and evaluator-selected rubric

\Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q}),

the solver generates
K
reports under the same query and evaluation setting. We define the raw evaluator-side auxiliary reward as

R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}),

where
C(\Gamma_{q})
is the empirical consistency score computed by aggregating pairwise agreement across the generated reports. In the current implementation,
C(\Gamma_{q})
is computed as the batch mean of pairwise agreement scores over extracted report conclusions.

Evaluator updates use an EMA-centered version of this signal,

A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t-1},

where
b_{t-1}
is the previous exponential moving average baseline of past auxiliary rewards. After computing the centered signal, the baseline is updated as

b_{t}=\beta b_{t-1}+(1-\beta)R_{e}(\Gamma_{q}),

where
\beta
is the EMA decay coefficient. Thus,
R_{e}
favors rubrics under which solver behavior is more reproducible across repeated rollouts, while
A_{e}
provides the actual lower-variance signal used for REINFORCE-style evaluator updates.

Overall, SCORE forms a two-channel training loop: evaluator-derived report rewards improve the solver, while consistency-based rubric-level rewards adapt the evaluator. The solver-side reward targets report quality under the selected rubric, whereas the evaluator-side reward favors rubrics that induce more stable and reproducible solver behavior. In this sense, consistency is used as a stability-oriented auxiliary signal for rubric adaptation rather than as a direct correctness criterion.

3.6
Sequential Alternating KL-Regularized Optimization

Given the two reward channels defined in Section
3.5
, training in SCORE proceeds through sequential alternating updates on the shared actor. Each step constructs a query-specific evaluation environment, generates rubric-conditioned reports, evaluates them under evaluator-side verification context, and then applies solver-side and evaluator-side updates to the shared parameters
\theta
.

The solver-side update uses evaluator-derived report rewards
R_{s}
, which are converted into group-normalized advantages and optimized with GRPO. The evaluator-side update uses the EMA-centered auxiliary reward
A_{e}
, derived from the raw consistency reward
R_{e}
, to update the rubric-selection policy with a REINFORCE-style objective. Although these updates are computed from different trajectories and rewards, both act on the same actor and are therefore coupled through the shared parameterization.

Both updates are regularized by KL divergence against a fixed reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. In our implementation, this KL term is applied at the actor-update level rather than as an explicit reward penalty. Thus, KL regularization is not treated as a third reward channel, but as an optimization regularizer that limits excessive policy drift under shared-actor learning.

At the analysis level, we denote the unregularized role-specific objectives by
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)
and
\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)
, respectively. The corresponding KL-regularized objectives can be written as

\mathcal{L}_{s}^{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),

\mathcal{L}_{e}^{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),

where
\lambda>0
is the KL regularization coefficient.

KL regularization serves two purposes in SCORE. First, it stabilizes alternating optimization by limiting abrupt policy drift under shared-role learning. Second, it helps maintain compatibility between solver-side and evaluator-side updates on the shared actor. In this way, SCORE combines evaluator-derived report rewards, consistency-based evaluator adaptation, external grounding, shared-role learning, and KL-regularized alternating optimization into a unified training framework for long-form report generation.

4
Theoretical Analysis

We analyze two properties of SCORE: the role of consistency as an evaluator-side auxiliary signal, and the first-order optimization behavior of shared-parameter KL-regularized alternating updates.

Assumptions.

We assume that: (A1) the analysis-level objectives
\mathcal{L}_{s}
and
\mathcal{L}_{e}
are
L
-smooth; (A2) the corresponding stochastic gradients are unbiased or approximately unbiased with bounded variance; and (A3) the stochastic gradients are almost surely bounded.

Consistency as rubric-induced reproducibility.

Let
\Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q})
denote the evaluator-selected rubric for query
q
. Suppose the solver generates
K
reports under the same query, evidence context, and rubric, and let

C(\Gamma_{q})

denote the resulting empirical consistency score obtained by aggregating pairwise agreement scores across these reports. In the current implementation, this is the mean pairwise consistency across generated reports. We define the raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal as

R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}),

and use the centered signal

A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t}

for evaluator updates, where
b_{t}
is an exponential moving average baseline.

Proposition 1

(Consistency estimates rubric-induced reproducibility)
.

Let
\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})
denote the population mean of the consistency metric under rubric
\Gamma_{q}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})].

Thus, the raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal estimates the expected reproducibility induced by rubric
\Gamma_{q}
under repeated solver rollouts.

Higher reproducibility suggests that the rubric induces a more stable and repeatable evaluation context for the solver, which makes it a useful auxiliary signal for evaluator-side adaptation. Centering by the EMA baseline reduces gradient variance without changing the underlying preference encoded by the raw signal. In SCORE, consistency is therefore used as a rubric-level auxiliary signal for evaluator-side adaptation.

First-order characterization of alternating updates.

Let the shared-actor updates be

\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta^{\prime}-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}),

where
\hat{g}_{s}
and
\hat{g}_{e}
are the stochastic gradients of the solver-side and evaluator-side objectives, respectively. Define the analysis surrogate

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),\qquad\alpha=\eta_{e}/\eta_{s},

where
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the fixed reference policy and
\lambda>0
is the KL regularization coefficient.

Proposition 2

(First-order characterization of alternating updates)
.

Under Assumptions (A1)–(A3), the expected one-step SCORE update satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{t})+\rho_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}).

This result shows that the shared-parameter solver-evaluator updates follow, to first order, a KL-regularized surrogate descent direction, with only a higher-order residual induced by sequential alternating updates. Detailed derivations are deferred to Appendix
F
.

Experimental Results

Main Results

As illustrated in Table
1
, SCORE demonstrates a substantial and robust improvement in analytical depth and valid citation across all agents. Basic reinforcement learning (RL) methods such as GRPO and DPO suffer from a catastrophic degradation in at lease one dimension, occasionally collapsing to exactly zero in Plan-and-Execute agents when integrating LLama-3.1-8B-Instruct. This phenomenon is driven by Lazy Likelihood Displacement (LLD), a condition where the likelihood of correct reasoning trajectories decays during tool-integrated RL, ultimately causing training collapse
[
7
]
. Furthermore, agent architectural magnify this instability, as decoupled long-horizon frameworks like Plan-and-Execute exhibit premature collapse and severe reward hacking compared to step-by-step ReAct policies
[
6
]
. In contrast, SCORE maintains stable performance by utilizing a co-evolving evaluator that provides dense, consistent constraints through dynamic evaluation rubrics. Notably, we observe a consistent decline in readability across nearly all training methods. We hypothesis this a fundamental trade-off because models are optimized to synthesize comprehensive analysis and rigorously cite evidence, the generated report inherently incorporates denser academic vocabulary and more complex logical structures, thereby sacrificing conversational simplicity for analytical rigor which might be similar to the trade-off between diversity and factuality
[
24
]
.

Case Study

As illustrated in Figure

in Appendix

, different types of research queries inherently require distinct evaluation criteria. For instance, legal queries demand absolute precision and strict citation of statutory texts, whereas open-ended questions require the aggregation of diverse perspectives to maintain fairness and objectivity. A generic, static rubric fails to capture these critical nuances, often assigning acceptable scores to reports that are superficially fluent but fundamentally flawed. In contrast, our adaptive evaluation framework dynamically introduces specialized evaluation dimensions tailored to each query. This mechanism ensures that the generated reports are accurately penalized or rewarded according to the specific rigor demanded by the task.

6
Conclusion

Generating comprehensive reports for deep research is a highly complex task, and the inherent difficulty of evaluating these open-ended reports severely hinders the training process. We observe a strong positive correlation between a model’s evaluation and generation capabilities. Based on this insight, we propose SCORE, a self-evolving training framework that jointly optimizes an evaluator and a solver. Because the entire training procedure fundamentally relies on unverifiable rewards, we introduce an external meta-harness to impose necessary environmental constraints on the evaluator. This module simultaneously guides the evaluator to actively explore a broader range of relevant evaluation dimensions throughout the training phase. Finally, experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves substantial performance improvements while requiring only a remarkably small amount of training data.

Appendix A
Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of our self-evolving framework, we acknowledge several limitations in our current study. First, the unique nature of deep research report generation demands exceptional instruction-following capabilities from the base language models. Models with weaker foundational abilities may struggle with dynamic rubrics generation and tool call. Second, our training paradigm relies heavily on entirely unverifiable rewards. This lack of ground-truth makes the model susceptible to policy collapse during the optimization process. Consequently, careful hyperparameter tuning and strict early stopping mechanisms are necessary to ensure training stability. Even though our framework successfully provides differentiated reward signals for specific criteria, aggressively optimizing for one dimension can sometimes lead to performance degradation in another. Balancing these competing objectives simultaneously remains a challenging problem for future exploration.

Appendix B
Future Work

According to our experiments, we observe that inherent trade-offs still exist during the training process for deep research report generation. We hypothesize that this issue is primarily caused by sparse reward signals resulting from exceptionally long agent trajectories. To address this challenge, future work will explore phased training paradigms. By decomposing the deep research generation process into distinct functional stages and assigning intermediate rewards, we aim to resolve the long-standing credit assignment problem that plagues end-to-end policy optimization.

Appendix F
Detailed Proofs

This appendix provides derivations for the results stated in Section
4
. Throughout, let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the shared actor parameters, and let

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)

denote the KL-regularized surrogate potential.

F.1
Derivation of Proposition
1

Recall that
\Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q})
denotes the evaluator-selected rubric for query
q
. Suppose the solver generates
K
reports under the same query, evidence context, and rubric. Let

c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q}),\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq K,

denote the pairwise agreement score between reports
r_{i}
and
r_{j}
under rubric
\Gamma_{q}
, and let

M=\binom{K}{2}

be the number of report pairs. In the current implementation, the empirical consistency score is defined as

C(\Gamma_{q})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q}).

The raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal is

R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}).

Let

\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q}):=\mathbb{E}[c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})]

denote the population mean of the consistency metric under rubric
\Gamma_{q}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[C(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})\right].

By linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mathbb{E}[c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})=\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q}).

Taking expectation over the induced rubric distribution yields

\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})].

This proves Proposition
1
.

In implementation, evaluator updates use the centered signal

A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t},

where
b_{t}
is an exponential moving average baseline. Since
b_{t}
is independent of the current sampled rubric action, subtracting it reduces gradient variance without changing the underlying expected preference encoded by
R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})
.

F.2
Derivation of Proposition
2

The shared-actor updates are

\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta^{\prime}-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).

Hence,

\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}=-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).

Taking conditional expectation with respect to
\theta_{t}
gives

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\mid\theta_{t}]-\eta_{e}\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})\mid\theta_{t}].

Under Assumption (A2),

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\mid\theta_{t}]\approx\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})\mid\theta_{t}]\approx\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).

Thus,

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]\approx-\eta_{s}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).

By Assumption (A1),
\mathcal{L}_{e}
is
L
-smooth, so

\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\|\leq L\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}\|.

From the first update,

\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}=-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),

and by Assumption (A3),
\|\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\|\leq G
. Therefore,

\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}\|\leq\eta_{s}G,

which implies

\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\|\leq L\eta_{s}G.

Hence we may write

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})+\delta_{t},\qquad\|\delta_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}).

Substituting this into the expected update yields

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\delta_{t}.

Using
\alpha=\eta_{e}/\eta_{s}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\bigr)+\rho_{t}^{(0)},

where

\rho_{t}^{(0)}=-\eta_{e}\delta_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}^{(0)}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}).

Including KL regularization against the fixed reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, we define

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).

The same argument then gives

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{t})+\rho_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}),

which proves Proposition
2
.

F.3
Summary

The above derivations establish two facts used in the main text:

1.

the raw evaluator-side consistency signal estimates the expected reproducibility induced by a rubric under repeated solver rollouts;

2.

the shared-parameter solver-evaluator alternating updates follow, to first order, a KL-regularized surrogate descent direction, with only a higher-order residual due to sequential updating.

Training Data Size

We apply different training data size in SCORE. While the co-evolving evaluator successfully stabilizes training on smaller datasets, increasing the data scale accelerates model collapse. The reason is both of the solver and evaluator rely on instinct reward rather than strictly verifiable external ground truth. With limited training data, this exact internal feedback effectively sharpens the model’s correct priors and provides necessary dense constraints. However, as the dataset size increases significantly, the solver progressively discovers blind spots in the evaluator and over-optimizes against these proxy rewards. This dynamic inevitably forces the optimization process to saturate, degrading the factual accuracy as the solver exploits the distributional limits of the proxy feedback. As illustrated by Figure
5
, the solver entropy collapses prematurely at large scales, followed by a classic rise-then-fall trajectory in the training rewards. This observation aligns perfectly with recent theoretical findings on unsupervised reinforcement learning, confirming that intrinsic reward mechanisms eventually amplify self-reinforcing hallucinations when exposed to extensive data
[
15
]
