The efficacy of different irrigant activation methods in the elimination of bacteria colonizing in dentin tubules
Authors: Şeyba Sara Mansuroğlu, Tamer Taşdemir, Tuba Dinçer, Celal Kurtuluş Buruk, Ümit Uzun, Tuğba Koşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this ex vivo study was to investigate the effects of 5 distinct irrigant activation systems on bacterial colonization within dentinal tubules utilizing confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).Materials and MethodsSixty extracted human mandibular premolars with single canals were prepared using resiprocal instruments. The teeth were embedded in acrylic resin blocks and the root canals were inoculated with Enterococcus faecalis. Root canals were irrigated using five different irrigant activation methods: standard needle irrigation, IrriFlex, EDDY, Endoactivator, and passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI). Horizontal sections, 1 ± 0.1 mm in thickness, were obtained at distances of 2, 5 and 8 mm from the apical end of the teeth. Sections were visualized using a CLSM. The penetration depth of dead bacteria was measured to evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of the irrigant activation systems. Statistical analysis was performed using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction.ResultsPUI showed significantly greater penetration depth of dead bacteria in both the coronal and apical thirds compared to the other experimental groups (p<0.05).ConclusionAlthough a statistically significant reduction in bacteria was observed in all experimental groups compared to the control, the canal disinfection was not complete in any of the groups. According to our results, standard needle irrigation had the lowest disinfection efficiency, while PUI demonstrated the highest among the evaluated activation methods.

Introduction

It is well known that bacterial infection is the main cause resulting in pulpal and periapical pathologies (1). However, achieving the absolute elimination of the microorganisms that infect this microenvironment (2). Inadequate eradication of bacteria from the root canal is indeed a significant challenge, which arises not only owing to the intricate anatomy of the endodontic space but also the development of bacteria into biofilms that are resistant to antimicrobial agents and the immune system (3, 4). Difficulties in the removal of bacteria and their by-products can lead to chronic inflammation or delays in the healing process (5).

The basis of successful root canal treatment follows a systematic approach that involves several critical steps including accurate diagnosis, a treatment plan based on the tooth's anatomy and structural configuration, debridement of the endodontic system, disinfection, and subsequent obturation. Complete debridement is almost impossible due to morphological features including the complexity of lateral and accessory canals, isthmuses, canal curvatures and irregularities in canal walls (6, 7). Ex vivo and clinical studies indicate that because of this intricate endodontic network, mechanical preparation alone is not sufficient for the complete removal of bacteria and by-products from all regions of the root canal. Therefore, the irrigation process has become particularly important (7).

The activation of irrigants during root canal preparation is recommended to remove intraradicular microorganisms and achieve 3D cleaning (8, 9). Sonics, ultrasonics, and lasers can activate the irrigants within the root canal (10, 11). Syringe and needle-based irrigation (SNI), using a 27 or 30 gauge (G) needle to access the apical third, represents the traditional method for root canal irrigation (12) and is widely accepted by dentists and endodontists (7).

The recently introduced side-vented irrigation needle, IrriFlex (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland), is made of polyethylene and features 2 lateral apertures just beneath the closed end for lateral fluid dispersion. The lateral apertures, which target the dentinal walls, ensure balanced irrigation, allowing the fluid to reach the coronal area at a consistent rate. In addition, IrriFlex has a unique design that reduces apical extrusion while allowing high lateral flow velocity to facilitate the efficient delivery of solutions to the coronal region. Its flexible shaft allows access to apical areas of curved roots without damaging the dentin walls. Designed with a 0.04 taper to adapt to the endodontic anatomy and navigate easily adjacent to the canal walls (13).

Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) effectively removes the bacterial aggregates from the canal through strong acoustic flow. This acoustic microstreaming disperses bacterial aggregates and eliminates debris from the root canal by inducing a constant flow of irrigant (9). Previous literature demonstrates that PUI exhibits higher efficacy for the eradication of debris and smear layers from root canals and isthmus compared to the SNI (11, 14).

EDDY (VDW, Munich, Germany) is a recent sonic device utilized for root canal activation, which is fabricated from a flexible polyamide-based material with a non-cutting tip design on a 25/.04 size. It is sterile and disposable. EDDY is activated by 5000-6000 Hz air pressure-driven oscillations provided by the air scaler (15). Similar to ultrasonic devices, EDDY generates cavitation and acoustic streaming in irrigants, enhancing the debridement and disinfection of the intricate root canal system (15, 16).

EndoActivator (EA) (Dentsply Tulsa, Tulsa, OK, USA) activates irrigants with sonic energy. The system comes with a contra-angle handpiece that generates sonic energy at various frequencies (33-167 Hz) and disposable flexible tips in three different dimensions (Yellow 15/.02, Red 25/.04, and Blue 35/.04). This cordless, battery-powered, device can vibrate 2.000-10.000 cycles/minute (15).

To the best of our knowledge, current endodontic literature lacks a comprehensive study that analyses and compares five different activation systems: SNI, IrriFlex, PUI, EDDY, and EA, regarding their efficacy in biofilm removal capacity of the irrigant. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to analyse the antimicrobial efficacy of different irrigation activation systems on infected root canals using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The null hypothesis was that no significant difference in the efficacy of the tested methods for eradicating E. faecalis biofilm on root canal walls.

Materials and Methods

Sample Size Calculation

Based on Hage et al. (17), G*Power (v3.1.9.4) indicated that nine samples per group (total n=54) were required (alpha error=0.05, beta error=0.20, and effect size=0.55). To compensate for a potential 10% data loss, the final sample size was adjusted to n=10 per group, totaling 60 specimens.

Sample selection and preparation

A total of 60 human-extracted single-rooted mandibular premolars were obtained from the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery and Periodontics departments at the Faculty of Dentistry, Karadeniz Technical University, Trabzon, Türkiye. The ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Karadeniz Technical University Faculty of Medicine with the decision number 2021-380. Teeth presenting with cracks or fractures under an 8× magnification dental operating microscope (Möller-Wedel Denta 300, Wedel, Germany) were excluded from further evaluation. Following soft tissues were mechanically removed, and the teeth were kept in 0.1% thymol (Tıb-Tek, Istanbul, Türkiye) until used in the experiment.

To standardize the specimens, the coronal portion of the teeth was removed using a diamond fissure bur no. 012 (Sunshine Diamonds, Langenhagen, Germany) attached to a high-speed handpiece, yielding roots of 18±1 mm in length from all specimens. The working length (WL) was determined by subtracting 1 mm from the length measured when a #10 K-file (Antaeos, VDW, Munich, Germany) was just visible at the apical foramen. Canal preparation was performed using T-Endo Must reciprocal instruments (Dentac, Istanbul, Türkiye) per the manufacturer's instructions. The 25/.06 and 40/.04 files were used by attaching an Ai motor (Woodpecker DTE, Guangxi, China) which was set to reciprocate at 160 CCWo and 40 CWo. Canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Cerkamed, Nisko, Poland) following each file change. Irrigation was conducted using 3 mL of distilled water, and the canals were dried using paper points (Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Study blocks were prepared by embedding the samples in acrylic resin. Upon the hardening of the resin blocks, the samples were placed in autoclavable plastic containers with lids and sterilized at 121 °C and 1 atm pressure for 15 min.

Infusion of the root canals with Enterococcus faecalis

E. faecalis bacteria American Type Cell Culture Collection 29212 (ATCC, Rockville, Maryland, USA) was inoculated into 50 mL medium containing brain-heart infusion (BHI; Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA) and 0.25% glucose and cultured for 24 h. Root canals received the E. faecalis suspension (1.5x108 colony/mL), adjusted according to McFarland no: 0.5 standards, using sterile insulin syringes and placed in a 15 mL tube containing 10 mL of bacterial growth medium. The samples were incubated at 37 °C and 100% humidity for 21-day to facilitate bacterial colonization of the canal wall and dentinal tubules. Every 3-day, 5 mL of culture medium was withdrawn, and 5 mL of fresh medium was added.

The activation of irrigants

The combination of EDTA and NaOCl, which is accepted as the irrigation protocol for disinfection of root canals and removal of smear layer, was also preferred in this study. The specimens were divided into 6 groups of 10 teeth each on a random basis.

Group 1 (SNI): A 30 G side-vented needle (Steri Irrigation Tips, Diadent, Canada) was attached to a 2.5 mL syringe (Ayset, Istanbul, Türkiye) and positioned 2 mm behind the WL. The canal was initially irrigated with 1 mL of 17% EDTA (Cerkamed) for 20 sec, repeated 3 times, followed by 1 mL of 2.5% NaOCl for 20 sec, and repeated 3 times. The irrigant was activated by gently oscillating the irrigation needle up and down without touching the root canal walls. Each irrigant was used in a volume of 3 mL and activated for a duration of 1 min.

Group 2 (EA): The root canal was first filled with 1 mL of 17% EDTA with the syringe and needle used in group 1. The 25/.04 red EA tip, attached to the EA head, was positioned 2 mm behind the WL and activated for 20 sec at a frequency of 10.000 cycles/min. This procedure was repeated 3 times, followed the same procedure using 2.5% NaOCl.

Group 3 (EDDY): The root canal was first filled with 1 mL of 17% EDTA with the syringe and needle used in group 1. The 25/.04 flexible polyamide tip, attached to the TA 200 head (Micron, TA 200 Airscaler, Ikegami, Otaku, Tokyo, Japanese) with a frequency of 6000 Hz, was positioned 2 mm behind the WL and activated for 20 sec. This procedure was repeated 3 times, followed by the same procedure using 2.5% NaOCl.

Group 4 (IrriFlex): The polypropylene IrriFlex tip with a 30 G diameter and 0.04 taper, attached to the syringe used in group 1, was placed into the canal 2 mm shorter than the WL and irrigated with 1 mL of 17% EDTA for 20 sec. This procedure was repeated 3 times. Irrigation was subsequently performed with 1 mL of 2.5% NaOCl for 20 sec, repeated 3 times. The solution was activated by gently oscillating the irrigation needle up and down without touching the canal walls.

Group 5 (PUI): The syringe and needle from group 1 were used to initially fill the root canal with 1 mL of 17% EDTA. The ultrasonic device (P5 Newtron XS, Satelec Acteon, Merignac, France) equipped with an IRR 20/25 stainless steel tip (Irrisafe, Satelec Acteon) was set to force 9 and positioned 2 mm behind the WL for 20-sec activation cycle. This cycle was performed 3 times, followed by the same procedure using 2.5% NaOCl.

Group 6 (Control): No irrigation procedure was applied to this group to see and verify the microbial biofilm layer formed by E. faecalis contamination in the root canals.

Sample preparation for CLSM

On each acrylic block, the 3rd, 6th, and 9th mm levels from the apical onwards, representing the apical, middle, and coronal third levels, were marked with an acetate pen. Horizontal sections approximately 1 ± 0.1 mm thick were obtained from the marked levels using a 0.3 mm thick diamond disc (Metkon) mounted on a micro-cutting device (Metkon Microcut 125, Bursa, Türkiye) under water cooling.

The LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Molecular Probes, Inc., Eugene, OR), was applied to each section according to the manufacturer's instructions. Live bacteria with intact cell membranes were visualized with SYTO 9 (green fluorescence) at an excitation/emission wavelength of 488 nm, and dead bacteria with damaged membranes were visualized with propidium iodide (PI-red fluorescence) at an excitation/emission wavelength of 543 nm using appropriate lasers under CLSM (Leica, TCS SP8 MP, Mannheim, Germany) at 20×2 magnification.

Evaluation of specimens by CLSM

CLSM images of the specimens were analyzed with Leica Application Suite-Advanced Fluorescence software (LAS AF, Leica Microsystems GmbH). The software measured the depth of red fluorescence from dead bacteria, penetrating into the dentinal tubules. The distance between the tubule lumen and the point where the red fluorescence ceased was measured and recorded (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was carried out using SPSS for Windows 17.0 software. The conformity of the data to a normal distribution was assessed by means of the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were calculated as median, minimum/maximum, mean, and standard deviation. Kruskal-Wallis test, Mann-Whitney U test and Bonferroni correction (critical p-value P < 0.017 for cross-sectional levels and critical p-value P < 0.01 for pairwise group comparisons) were used in statistical analyses. A level of P < 0.05 between groups was considered statistically significant.

Measurement of the penetration depth of dead cells by using CLSM image analysis program.

Results

Firstly, to verify that 21 days of incubation of E. faecalis was sufficient to infect dentin tubules and root canals, we conducted LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability assay on control samples in which no irrigation activation protocol was applied. The stained sections of the control group samples show that all sections of the root canal were effectively colonized by viable bacteria, as seen by the green color (Figure 2).

Different irrigation activation systems were analyzed and compared for their antibacterial efficacy using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability. As shown in Figure 1, the distance between the end of the dead bacteria (red) region and the beginning of the live bacteria (green) region was measured to quantify the efficacy of the activation methods. As expected, the penetration depth of dead cells showed a positive correlation with the efficacy of the activation method; lower disinfection efficacy resulted in a short penetration depth, and higher disinfection efficacy resulted in a greater penetration depth (Figure 2). The mean values of the penetration depth of dead cells across all groups are presented in Table 1.

When the maximum dentinal tubule penetration depth measurements of dead bacteria were compared, the PUI group exhibited significantly greater penetration depth in the coronal, and apical thirds than the other experimental groups (P < 0.05). When only the coronal third was considered, SNI group exhibited a significantly reduced penetration depth compared to the EA, PUI, EDDY, and IrriFlex groups (P < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between EA and EDDY groups (P> 0.05); however, both groups exhibited greater penetration depth compared to the IrriFlex group (P <0.05). In the middle third, SNI group exhibited a significantly reduced depth of penetration compared to the EA, PUI, EDDY, and IrriFlex groups (P < 0.05). No significant difference was detected between the EA and PUI groups (P > 0.05); however, EDDY and EA groups showed greater penetration depth compared to the IrriFlex group (P < 0.05) (Figure 3).

In the apical third, SNI showed significantly reduced penetration depth compared to the EA, PUI, EDDY and IrriFlex groups. The EA group exhibited significantly higher penetration depth than the IrriFlex group (P < 0.05). In all sections, the highest value of dead bacteria penetration depth was found in the coronal third of the PUI group (618.74 μm), while the lowest value was found in the apical third of the control group (49.28 μm). In intra-group comparisons based on section levels, the penetration depth in the coronal third was significantly higher than in the apical third for the SNI and IrriFlex groups (P < 0.01). In contrast, the penetration depth in the apical third was significantly lower in the PUI, EDDY, and EA groups compared to the other sections (P < 0.01) (Figure 3).

Discussion

This study compared the efficacy of different irrigation activation methods on canals contaminated with E. faecalis using the CLSM. The null hypothesis was rejected because there was a significant difference in the effect of different irrigation activation methods on bacterial colonization of dentinal tubules.

To ensure that the experimental conditions were as close as possible to those in the clinic, human-extracted mandibular premolars without caries, fractures or cracks were used in the study. Moreover, in this study, due to the advantages of reciprocal motion compared to rotation, canal preparation was performed with T-Endo Must files used with reciprocal motion (18). In previous studies (18), it was emphasized that the apical preparation diameter should be at least #40 for acoustic flow debridement in root canals (19). In accordance, in our study, all specimens were shaped with T-Endo Must reciprocal files up to a WL of 40/.04 files to ensure adequate irrigation and debridement.

E. faecalis represents a small proportion of the flora in untreated root canals; however, it plays an important role in the etiology of persistent periradicular lesions after root canal treatment (1, 20). Therefore, purpose of this evaluation, canals were intentionally contaminated with E. faecalis prior to the disinfection protocols.

In our study, different irrigation protocols were assessed via CLSM regarding their influence on bacterial colonization inside dentinal tubules. CLSM not only visualizes the bacteria in the biofilm structure but also allows us to distinguish between live (green) and dead (red) bacteria in infected dentin without damaging the biofilm (21).

Our findings align with several in vivo and in vitro reports documented in the literature, demonstrating that all the irrigating activation techniques assessed significantly decreased the bacteria capable of penetrating the dentinal tubules. According to our results, PUI showed the best antibacterial activity across all sections of the root canal. Also, numerous studies indicate that PUI activation following manual or rotary preparation significantly reduces bacterial counts and yields more effectively compared to SNI (11, 22). Cachovan et al. (23) conducted a study comparing the hydrodynamic system (RinsEndo), PUI (Piezo smart) and SNI, revealing that the antibacterial efficacy of both activation methods was higher than that of SNI. The superiority of PUI over other methods is the efficacy of high-powered ultrasound in disrupting bacterial biofilms, thereby enhancing the susceptibility of planktonic bacteria to the bactericidal activity of NaOCl. In addition, the resulting cavitation can temporarily weaken the bacterial cell membrane, increasing its permeability to NaOCl (24).

SNI showed less penetration depth of dead bacteria at each sectional level compared to the other experimental groups. This result may be due to the fact that the irrigation solution has the highest velocity when it leaves the irrigation needle and eventually the velocity decreases. The static irrigation reservoir of the conventional needle limits the distribution and cleansing efficacy of the irrigation solution within the root canal system (25). Therefore, we believe that the ineffectiveness of the SNI group in terms of disinfection is due to its low penetrating capacity into the dentinal tubules.

On the other hand, the EA group showed a lower penetration depth of dead bacteria compared to the PUI group and a higher penetration depth than the SNI and IrriFlex groups. In parallel with our study, Bago et al. (26) evaluated the antibacterial efficacy of SNI and EA in root canal using 5% NaOCl, demonstrating that activation with EA was more effective than SNI in reducing bacterial counts.

When we compared the effects of two sonic devices, EDDY and EA, on bacteria colonizing dentinal tubules, we found that EA and EDDY exhibited similar penetration depth values. A study investigating the antibacterial efficacy of EDDY, EA and SNI analysis using an approach similar to ours reported that EDDY and EA were more effective than SNI, while no significant difference between EA and EDDY, in line with our results (27).

When comparing the apical, middle, and coronal root sections in our study, the apical third showed significantly less dead bacterial penetration depth than the other sections in all groups. This aligns with the findings reported by Zeng et al. (28) showing that the investigated irrigation activation methods, SNI and EDDY, were less effective in the apical region compared to the coronal and middle regions. These results can be explained by the increasing number and size of dentin tubules from apical to coronal (29).

Based on the outcomes generated from this investigation, IrriFlex showed a higher penetration depth than the SNI at all third levels. IrriFlex showed lower penetration depth than PUI, EDDY and EA in the coronal and middle thirds, while in the apical segment, IrriFlex showed lower penetration depth than only EA and PUI.

This study has several limitations. First, the ex vivo conditions do not fully reflect the natural host response or biological fluid dynamics, which may restrict the extrapolation of these outcomes to clinical scenarios. Moreover, the use of a single bacterial species does not adequately represent the multispecies biofilm structure typically seen in clinical settings. Finally, potential operator variability in the activation procedures may have influenced the results. Considering these factors, the findings of this study should be supported by further research.

Conclusion

This study assessed the antimicrobial efficacy of five different irrigation activation methods on bacteria colonizing dentinal tubules. A statistically significant reduction in bacteria was observed in all experimental groups compared to the control; however, disinfection was not complete. Our results indicate that SNI had the lowest disinfection efficiency, while PUI demonstrated the highest among the techniques analyzed. The findings obtained from present laboratory study need to be supported by further clinical studies conducted with larger samples.
