Novel Utilization of a Preangulated, Osseointegrated Implant in a Case of Metacarpophalangeal Amputation: Design, Technique, and Rehabilitation Protocols Plus Functional Outcomes
Authors: Nicole Liddy, Malcolm N Hamilton-Hall, Sophia A Strike, Andrew Etheridge, Princess Filippi, William A Green, Brock A Lindsey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThumb amputations considerably impair hand function, yet many patients are ineligible for reimplantation. Osseointegration can greatly restore dexterity and sensation. This case presents the first reported thumb osseointegration operation in the U.S. and the first reported use of a preangulated thumb implant worldwide. A single-stage osseointegration procedure was performed without complication. Ten-week resonance frequency analysis demonstrated an Implant Stability Quotient of 61; no loosening occurred by 13 months. The patient demonstrated successful thumb-small finger opposition and reported considerable improvement in activities of daily living by 6 months. No implant complications or skin issues were observed. The patient’s Action Research Arm Test score increased by 28 points within 13 months, whereas her Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score decreased by 9% over this period. She also demonstrated sensation to heat/cold through her thumb implant. This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of single-stage thumb osseointegration using a preangulated implant head. Patient-reported satisfaction and clinical function improved markedly. A single-stage approach offers potential advantages in recovery time over traditional 2-stage protocols. This fascinating case establishes an explicit framework for single-stage osseointegration in digital reconstruction, including single-stage technique, implant selection, prosthetic design, and rehabilitation procedures. It also highlights the potential for this technology to improve outcomes for thumb amputees ineligible for replantation.Key words: Amputation, Osseointegration, Outcomes, Prosthetic reconstruction, Thumb

Digital amputation affects approximately 7.5 per 100,000 persons per year in the United States, with approximately 20% receiving thumb amputations.1 These injuries pose marked functional challenges, as the thumb contributes an estimated 40% to 50% of overall hand utility.2 Loss of thumb function results in substantial deficits in grip strength, fine motor control, and dexterity. Although early reimplantation remains the gold standard, up to 83.5% of patients are ineligible for this procedure on presentation.3 Alternate reconstructive or prosthetic options include microsurgical toe-to-hand transfers, bionic prostheses, and silicone vacuum prostheses, although these have varying degrees of inherent limitation.1 Osteoplastic options such as composite radial forearm flaps, iliac crest grafts (either devascularized or vascularized with groin flap), or free osseocutaneous fibular flaps can provide excellent function in appropriate candidates. Still, these too have significant risks for graft morbidity, reoperation, and graft resorption.4, 5, 6, 7 Osseointegration (OI) is an emerging alternative that allows for direct attachment of an external prosthesis to the skeleton by biologically anchoring an abutment into host bone.8

Although osseointegration was first described in the early 1960s, its application in the thumb remained unexplored until 1996, with the first successful thumb OI occurring in Sweden.9 This landmark success led to considerable advancement in research. Notably, patients with osseointegrated thumb implants consistently report return of vibration and position sensory feedback, termed “osseoperception.”10 Studies demonstrate 95% global hand function on the Sollerman test, excellent Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) outcomes, and superior grip strength in osseointegration compared with other reimplantation alternatives.1,10

This report presents the first known U.S. application of thumb OI and the first ever documented use of a preangulated OI implant in the hand, detailing surgical approach, postoperative integration, and functional outcomes. We believe that this case discussion and proposed rehabilitation protocol provides an explicit framework for osseointegration applications in the field of digital reconstruction.

Case Report

Initial presentation

We present a 66-year-old woman quadrilateral amputee who underwent multiple limb amputations due to septic ischemic compromise. Her amputations included bilateral transtibial amputations, left transradial amputation, and right partial hand amputation. The right-hand amputation involved thumb loss at the proximal phalanx, transmetacarpal amputation of the index finger, metacarpophalangeal disarticulations of the middle and ring fingers, and a proximal phalanx amputation of the small finger. Following initial amputation, she underwent right first web space deepening, which improved mobility but did not provide sufficient length for functional use (Fig. 1). At presentation, she was unable to use her right hand effectively and felt finger prostheses would not be functional without thumb opposition. Preoperative QuickDASH assessment demonstrated a 65.9% impairment level, reflecting pronounced functional disability.

Preoperative planning

The affected hand was evaluated using computed tomography. Imaging data were processed using Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine to Print software to generate a 3-dimensional (3D) model of bony anatomy (Fig. 2). This data were imported into AutoCAD for further modeling. The final model was 3D printed using Formlabs technology (Fig. 3). Using the 3D-printed model, inner cortical width was measured at 5.8 mm, whereas available bone length to the subchondral area was 28 mm. For secure fixation, SouthernImplants requires a minimum of 5.0–10 mm bone stock. The SouthernImplants Co-Axis 12° external hex implant was chosen for its preset angulation, optimizing thumb opposition and hand function while ensuring even force distribution. A temporary healing abutment was prepared to allow appropriate osseointegration at the titanium-bone interface after surgery; a permanent abutment was prepared for subsequent use.

Operative technique

The patient underwent general anesthesia. A longitudinal dorsal incision was made over the prior amputation site, with dorsal and palmar skin flaps elevated to expose underlying bone. The residual proximal phalanx was separated from the metacarpal head using a Freer elevator and removed with a rongeur to ensure a clean implantation interface. The medullary canal was accessed using a 2.0-mm Twist Drill, followed by sequential reaming to 5.0 mm under copious irrigation to prevent thermal necrosis. A 6.0 mm × 18 mm titanium osseointegration screw was inserted using a 1.22 Hex Driver, ensuring intimate cortical contact to optimize primary stability. A torque-limiting screwdriver seated the implant to approximately 30 Ncm final torque. The 12° angulated head was oriented toward the small finger to facilitate stable opposition and optimize hand function (Fig. 4A).

The inserted driver indicates the direction of angulation to the lesser fingers in reference to the Co-Axis external hex platform. B A photo of the patient’s right hand following soft tissue closure of the thumb incisional site. C A postoperative AP radiograph confirming appropriate medullary placement of the Co-Axis osseointegration implant.

Following implant placement, the insertion guide was removed, and the surgical site was irrigated. Dorsal and palmar skin flaps were repositioned, and a 6.0-mm skin punch created a transcutaneous opening for the implant abutment. The healing abutment was secured using a spanner wrench. Soft tissue closure was performed using 2-0 Vicryl and 3-0 nylon sutures (Fig. 4B). Skin flaps were prophylactically trimmed to prevent overpadding and minimize tissue mobility. The extremity was dressed with Xeroform and sterile dressing. Plain radiographs confirmed proper implant positioning (Fig. 4C).

Postoperative care and rehabilitation

Following single-stage surgery, the use of the thenar muscles was restricted for 2 weeks via splinting. Sutures were removed with stoma care instructions provided. Proper surgical site protection included twice-daily cleaning using soap, water, and a soft-bristled toothbrush to prevent biofilm formation. To promote successful osseointegration, the patient avoided shear stress and torque on the temporary abutment; risk activities included pushing to stand and performing supported walking with pressure applied to the thenar hand, or attempting to hold large objects between the radial aspects of her residual hands. For patients with an isolated thumb amputation, they would need to avoid grip/pinch in the surgical hand so as to minimize force transmission to the abutment. At 10 weeks, the temporary abutment was replaced with a permanent abutment in the clinic. Three assessments evaluated osseointegration: resonance frequency analysis yielded an Implant Stability Quotient of 61, indicating satisfactory stability; abutment torquing to 32–40 Ncm ensured secure fixation; and radiographic imaging confirmed proper placement.11 At 12 weeks, the abutment was retorqued, confirming stability.

With clearance for formal hand therapy 3 months after surgery, the patient began a gradual weight-loading and traction protocol using a training prosthesis. This staged approach facilitated systematic stress increase to encourage healing and promote functional use. Six visits over 4 months addressed weight-loading, traction, and functional training goals. Initial therapy consisted of axial loading through the abutment (50 g of force). Axial load goals increased by 50 g weekly for the first month, 100 g weekly for the second month, 200 g weekly for the third month, and 400 g weekly for the fourth month. Final axial load reached 2200 g (4.85 lbs).

Initial traction loading involved resting the thumb with the training prosthesis perpendicular to the floor for 30 seconds, with incremental increases to tolerate 10 minutes of continuous traction without discomfort. Perception of both heat and cold was confirmed through her abutment. The patient could sense hot water (150 °F) via the prosthetic after 10 seconds of immersion and ice water after 16 seconds. Six months after surgery, the patient could successfully hold weighted objects and write using an adaptive pen. Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) was also found to be 61 at this time. ISQ is a tool common to dental implantology, whereby the OI bone-implant interface stiffness is determined via radio frequency analysis. A probe is placed on the exposed abutment, providing a stability score ranging from 1 to 99; 60 is a common minimum cutoff to indicate mechanical stability, thus true bony ingrowth, in most dental OI practices.12

Prosthetic design/production

Following permanent abutment placement, impressions were made for a weight training device. During intake, periabutment tissues were assessed and cleansed. The Impression Coping tool, using vinyl polysiloxane, captured the residuum-abutment relationship. Following confirmation of osseointegration, physical and digital impressions facilitated restorative component design (Fig. 5A). Additional thumb intake data collected included goniometric measurement of intended flexion (55°), reposition distance (9.0 cm), opposition distance (6.3 cm), proximal-to-distal palmar-dorsal compression, and circumferential proximal-to-distal axis measurements. Structured light 3D surface scans generated phototexture mapping of the residuum. The final prosthetic surface was stained to match skin tone (Fig. 5B).

To improve stability, the distal abutment segment contained knurling for prosthesis retention (Fig. 5C). The abutment ridge possessed a novel magnetic attachment (provisional patent application 63/788,459) to provide additional stability and prevent implant slippage amid strong grip force application, all while remaining easily removable with intentional distraction force. Multiple diagnostic fitting sessions optimized thumb function and ensured utility alongside a separate, body-powered, ratcheting partial right-hand device affixed via Velcro straps (Fig. 5D). The thumb prototype was evaluated for flexion angle, silicone-to-tissue interface, retention mechanisms, and pinch/grip performance.

Assessment of function

Multiple outcome measures assessed digit function return as the patient progressed through postoperative weight-bearing milestones. At 3 and 6 months after surgery, the carpometacarpal joint range of motion measured 40° palmar abduction and 45° radial abduction. Functional grasp and pinch were enhanced by optimized prosthetic alignment. At 8 months, lateral pinch strength measured 4.67 lbs using a digital pinch gauge; grip strength remained below the measurable threshold on a Jamar dynamometer but is expected to improve with continued training.

The Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) and Box and Blocks Test evaluated overall hand function. The patient improved from a baseline ARAT score of 9 to 37 by 13 months after surgery. On the Box and Blocks test, she transferred 34 blocks in 1 minute at 13 months—approximately 53% of the age-adjusted normative value.13 Prior to osseointegration, she was functionally incapable of completing this test. The patient’s QuickDASH disability score improved from 70% at baseline to 64% at 13 months when using both thumb and index/middle finger prostheses—representing a 6% functional gain but only a raw score change from 42 to 39. However, the minimal clinically important difference of this test is approximately 16 points.14 With a thumb prosthesis alone, her score remained at 70% (raw score of 42), reflecting the importance of distal palmar tissue and additional fingers to support grip. The use of the thumb prosthesis alone improved hand tingling symptoms (severe to mild) and sleep disturbances (moderate to mild). The patient reported high satisfaction with prosthetic function. She manipulated various small objects, including small marbles, with her prosthetics. Although challenges remain with tasks like writing or using a mug without adaptive equipment, she expressed confidence in developing these skills through continued training. Discussion

The minority of reported thumb OI cases use single-stage protocols. Most reported upper-extremity osseointegration procedures in general use 2-stage approaches, where implant placement is followed by prolonged healing before abutment installation.1,15 Although this method allows better soft tissue control and reduced infection risk, it increases recovery time, surgical burden, and resource use.16 A single-stage approach was deemed appropriate given the patient’s favorable cortical bone thickness, robust soft tissue envelope, excellent primary stability, overall medical stability, and strong support system enabling regular follow-up.

Critically, the innovative use of a preangulated implant drastically reduces stress concentration contralateral to pressure application; biomechanical studies of incisors, which experience nonaxial forces similarly to the thumb, show that angulated implants require 3.5 times the applied force of unangulated counterparts to fracture.17, 18, 19, 20 This results in drastically reduced abutment and soft tissue stress, in addition to significantly higher torque before screw failure.21 These engineering principles translate well to the hand, particularly for functional alignment with residual digits during precision grip and pinch. To our knowledge, this is the first reported translation of this implant design into thumb OI despite its mechanically relevant impacts on the distribution of nonaxial forces. Implant force resilience is even more critical in the thumb than the incisors, as the metacarpophalangeal joint can experience over 600N of contact force alone when the hand applies only 10 kg of grip force to an object22; this does not consider the relevant shear and torque forces present at the metacarpophalangeal joint with grip. This far exceeds the maximum 250N of force experienced at the incisor during mastication of harder foods23 This theoretically will confer longevity benefits in thumb OI, but clinical studies are necessary to truly assess the biomechanical impact in such cases.

Several design features contributed to implant success. The moderately rough, Alumina-blasted surface of the Southern Implants Co-Axis implant increases bone-to-implant contact, promoting osseointegration.19,24,25 The gold-plated abutment screw system allowed slight deformation under torque, creating a component lock that improved preload; craniofacial implants have capitalized on such plastic deformation for decades to improve preload retention.26 The quad-head screw design enables higher torque values prior to failure, enhancing long-term stability.

A key design challenge was promoting the independent donning and doffing capability of the prosthetic, as the patient lacked contralateral hand function; this concern inspired the innovative, magnetic thumb retention design.

The patient demonstrated considerable functional recovery, particularly in tasks involving her new thumb and small finger—the most mobile residual digit. Although grip strength remained below measurable levels on the Jamar dynamometer, ongoing therapy is expected to yield further gains. Her ARAT score more than quadrupled by month 13, indicating enhanced grasp, grip, and gross movement of the hand.27 Her Box and Blocks Test performance, despite representing greater than 50% of the average score for her age, is impressive for a patient with longstanding quad-limb amputation.17 These functional gains corresponded with improved independence in daily activities and high subjective satisfaction.

However, her QuickDASH score showed only modest improvement from 70% to 64%, a 6% gain. Previous studies have criticized possible “floor” or “ceiling” effects when using this measure across various orthopedic pathologies.28,29 Many QuickDASH scoring criteria do not reflect the complex pathological context of our patient, underestimating her marked functional recovery. Scored tasks were heavily influenced by the presence of bilateral lower-limb and contralateral upper-limb amputations, making it difficult to isolate improvements in operative hand function. Several QuickDASH items—such as playing sports—are much more difficult for a quadruple amputee than the average QuickDASH user, limiting the instrument’s sensitivity in this context. In contrast, tasks such as gripping her forearm rollator became much easier with the thumb prosthesis alone. Although this positive change does not improve mobility limitations imposed by her bilateral above-knee amputations, the ability to securely grasp the rollator handles greatly enhances the safety and usability of the device.

The patient reported no adverse implant-related events at 13 months. Intraoperative thinning of soft tissue flaps to 2–3 mm and diligent postoperative stoma care likely contributed to successful skin-implant interface. Her main concern involved minor sweating under the silicone prosthesis after prolonged wear, which did not affect function. She preferred proprioceptive feedback provided by the silicone prosthesis over metal variants; we attribute enhanced sensation to subtle pressure fluctuations at the skin-silicone interface. Fine motor skill improvements, although not matching functional levels of a native hand, contributed to the patient’s increased confidence and sense of independence. Additionally, more complex functions—such as using a knife to cut food—may take years to fully relearn, particularly given the prolonged absence of native hand function following the patient’s initial amputations.

This case demonstrates that thumb osseointegration, using preangulated implants, is feasible and safe when performed with careful planning and multidisciplinary support. As the field advances, developing standardized guidelines for implant sizing, prosthetic considerations, torque thresholds, rehabilitation, and hygiene are critical. It is also crucial to consider the economic proposition of thumb OI in general and specifically using preangulated implants; the implant itself costs approximately $279 wholesale. The healing abutment costs $35 wholesale. The prototype retention system cost $1500 across multiple design iterations, whereas the silicone thumb prosthetic cost ∼$250. Although surgical cost comparisons will not be published here because of the author’s concerns of “cost fixing” for U.S., we can compare operative times as a surrogate for costs. Toe-to-hand transfers, as noted, are an option to restore hand function in cases of chronic thumb amputation (recognizing that our patient lacked toes in this specific case). However, such transfers have been described to take 11–13 hours of dedicated operative work.30,31 Our described thumb OI procedure had a length of 51 minutes from incision to closure and 97 minutes total in the OR, representing a considerably better OR/personnel resource utilization.

Comparative studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes between single- and 2-stage procedures and explore integration with emerging technologies such as myoelectric or nerve-driven prostheses. This case is also limited because of ∼1 year follow-up, whereas a 2019 study found that custom-designed implants had 57% success rates after a median of 9.5 years, although even at that time, standardized implant success was found to be 100%. Existing case studies, to our knowledge, also lack the ability to provide robust generalizations regarding population infection, implant failure/loosening, or periprosthetic fracture rates (especially in the United States). Still, this report presents a meaningful addition to the field of osseointegration, especially as the field shifts toward implants with greater concordance with native anatomy. We also describe a novel rehabilitation schedule, which we propose as a preliminary framework for thumb OI rehabilitation pending the development of evidence-based protocols. Our use of ISQ stability testing provides a simple, objective measurement of this implant that is already standard of care when using such an implant in dental OI. Thus, our work lays theoretical groundwork for safely expanding the role of osseointegration in digital prosthetic reconstruction.
