Auditing LLM Benchmarks with Item Response Theory
Sander Land, Daniel M. Bikel

Abstract
LLM benchmark labels are frozen at release and silently copied into downstream benchmarks, errors and all. Our Item Response Theory indicator surfaces these mislabels at 95% precision in the top 200, across 114 models on seven preference and multiple-choice benchmarks, outperforming a supervised classifier. We trace these errors to mechanical labeling rules, upstream errors inherited unchanged from source datasets, and ambiguous items with no defensible single key. The same fit reveals that reward models specialize in stylistic preference over factual knowledge, and exposes one frontier reward model that agrees with detected mislabels at 78% accuracy versus 38% for its peers, consistent with contamination or benchmark-specific over-optimization. datasets/Writer/IRT-mislabeled-items

This creates two failure modes that aggregate accuracy hides.
First, individual benchmark items can be
mislabeled
, propagating
directly into model rankings and reward-model training data.
Because human labeling is expensive, benchmarks are often built
from earlier benchmarks or synthetic pipelines, so errors at any
stage persist through the chain. Second, individual models can
have
benchmark-specific
performance inconsistent with
their behavior elsewhere, ranging from legitimate specialization
to contamination or over-optimization.

We address both with Item Response

8

framework that jointly estimates latent model abilities and
item-level parameters. Our central contribution is a novel
mislabel indicator built on the four-parameter logistic (4PL)
version of IRT that asks whether an item’s response pattern is
better explained by stronger models becoming increasingly correct or increasingly incorrect.
The same framework also surfaces anomalies consistent with
benchmark contamination or over-optimization, identified through model-level agreement with the items our method flags as mislabels.

This paper contributes: (i) an unsupervised IRT-based annotation-error
detector, (ii) an analysis of recurring mislabel
patterns across preference and MC benchmarks, and (iii) a model-level diagnostic showing reward-model specialization and anomalous agreement with detected mislabels.

Background and Related Work

Item Response Theory
(IRT) models the probability that respondent

m
gives a correct response to item
i
as a parametric function
of a latent ability
\theta_{m}
and item-level parameters. The
two-parameter logistic form
(
3
)
specifies this
probability as
\sigma(a_{i}(\theta_{m}-b_{i}))
, where
\sigma
is the
logistic function,
a_{i}
is item discrimination, and
b_{i}
is item
difficulty. The three- and four-parameter
extensions
(
2
)
add a lower asymptote
c_{i}
to
model guessing on multiple-choice items and an upper asymptote

d_{i}
to model ceiling effects arising from accidental mistakes by high-ability individuals.
While the original aim of IRT was to measure human ability,
a common use in machine learning has been to measure properties of the
items
.
Prior work has applied IRT to NLP benchmarks to measure dataset
quality and construct efficient evaluation
subsets
(
15
;
14
;
26
;
24
)
, with
29
showing
that IRT reveals structure invisible to aggregate accuracy. More
recent work uses IRT to assess reliability and score stability in
LLM evaluations
(
30
;
11
)

and to drive adaptive testing
(
17
)
.
These applications largely rely on the 2PL or 3PL parameterization.

26
use a 4PL upper asymptote as a per-item
feasibility score to filter unsolvable items.
Our work also applies IRT to study dataset items, at a larger scale than prior efforts and with a forced-ceiling likelihood ratio derived from the upper asymptote as our mislabel indicator.
Annotator error detection (AED) has been widely studied in NLP
(
13
)
.
Most AED methods score instances using model-internal signals such as training dynamics or prediction uncertainty, often based on an ensemble of trained classifiers, which presupposes a supervised training setup.
Label-aggregation methods such as GLAD
(
32
)
,
which jointly infer annotator ability and item difficulty from a
labeling matrix, can also be used for AED.
Our setting is post-hoc and model-free at the item level. We score items from a panel of off-the-shelf models’ responses, with no training signal or per-item retraining.
Label-error detection has also been studied outside NLP.
21
;
22

introduce Confident Learning to detect
supervised label errors and show that such errors are pervasive
in widely-used test sets, with downstream effects on model
rankings.
7
extend this observation to MMLU,
demonstrating that label errors in a canonical MC benchmark are
substantial enough to shift LLM rankings.

Finally, our error taxonomy connects to work showing that human and LLM preference labels underweight factuality and overvalue surface cues
(
12
;
33
;
4
)
. Our model-level anomaly analysis relates to benchmark-contamination work
(
27
;
23
)
.

3.2
4PL IRT model

We fit the four-parameter logistic IRT
model
(
2
)
.

P(y_{im}=1)=c_{i}+(d_{i}-c_{i})\,\sigma\!\left(a_{i}(\theta_{m}-b_{i})\right)

(1)

Mapping the original psychometric setting onto benchmark
evaluation, the respondent becomes model
m
with latent
labeling ability
\theta_{m}
, and the test item becomes a
benchmark item with difficulty
b_{i}
and discrimination
a_{i}
.
The lower asymptote
c_{i}\in[0,1]
relates to the probability of matching the reference by chance, though it can be lower when a distractor systematically attracts model responses.
The upper asymptote
d_{i}\in[0,1]
is the
probability that an arbitrarily strong model matches the
reference, and is the parameter we exploit for mislabel
detection.

IRT models are unidentified up to a joint shift and scale of

\theta
and
b
. Adding a constant to all
\theta_{m}
and all

b_{i}
leaves the likelihood unchanged, and rescaling both is
absorbed into
a_{i}
. We resolve this with a method-of-moments
anchor:

\lambda\left(\bar{\theta}^{\,2}+(\hat{\sigma}_{\theta}-1)^{2}\right),\qquad\lambda=10,

where
\bar{\theta}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\theta}
are the sample mean
and standard deviation of
\{\theta_{m}\}
. The penalty pulls the
sample mean of
\theta
to zero and its standard deviation to
one. All fits minimize the negative log-likelihood of
Eq.
1
(plus this anchor) aggregated over observed
responses
y_{im}
, using L-BFGS-B with analytic gradients.

Before fitting, we remove two classes of items that are uninformative
for IRT modeling.
The
tie filter
drops 137 items (0.7%) on which the
plurality answer among non-reward models was invalid or tied. These concentrate in
multi-way RMB-Math and RMB-Code subsets, where multiple options
are equivalent or no option is clearly correct. The

constant-item filter
drops 1685 items
(11.1%) on which every generative model gives the same answer.
Most constant-filtered items are all-correct; safety subsets account
for a large share, reflecting broad model consensus on clear safety
cases.
The smaller all-wrong population is analyzed separately in
Appendix
D
, since unanimous disagreement with the
reference is itself a mislabel signal.

3.3
Parameter estimation

A full per-item 4PL fit involves approximately

\sim 80{,}000
parameters, and the loss landscape can contain
flat regions and multiple local minima. We therefore use two
stages: a constrained preliminary fit that estimates the model
ability scale
\{\theta_{m}\}
from items aligned with the overall
ability ordering, followed by independent per-item fits with model
ability held fixed.

Preliminary fit.

We restrict the preliminary fit to items whose correctness pattern
agrees with the subset’s overall ability ordering. Specifically,
we keep items with
r_{i}>0.2
, where
r_{i}
is the Pearson
correlation between the item’s binary correctness vector and each
model’s mean accuracy on the remaining items in that subset.
This keeps 16300 items. Within this set, we estimate one
discrimination
a_{s}\in[1/15,15]
per subset rather than per
item, bound
b_{i}\in[-2,2]
to prevent flat-curve divergence
through extreme difficulty values, and fix
c_{i}
to the chance
floor (
\tfrac{1}{2}
or
\tfrac{1}{4}
) and
d_{i}=1
,
yielding a constrained 3PL-style fit.
Appendix
B

reports sensitivity to the
r
threshold and the item-level
constraint box.

Item-level and forced-ceiling fits.

After the preliminary fit fixes the ability scale, we refit
item-level parameters over the full 19164-item fit set with

\theta_{m}
held fixed. Each item is fit independently with

a_{i}\in[1/15,15]
,
b_{i}\in[-2,2]
, and

c_{i},d_{i}\in[0,1]
.

When high-ability models systematically miss the reference answer,
a 2PL or 3PL fit must absorb the pattern into discrimination or
difficulty. The 4PL instead exposes it through the upper asymptote.
A low
d_{i}
means the reference answer remains unlikely even at high ability. This may indicate a mislabeled reference, genuine ambiguity about the best answer, or item difficulty beyond the top models’ ability.

Figure
2
shows this pattern empirically. The joint
distribution of
b_{i}
and
d_{i}
, split by weak reference label,
places most items near
d_{i}\approx 1
, while items labeled

mislabel
form a low-ceiling population near

d_{i}\approx c_{i}
.
The separation is along the ceiling dimension rather than difficulty. The fit lowers
d_{i}
instead of only inflating
b_{i}
, which distinguishes unreachable references from merely difficult items.

However, the raw ceiling parameter is nearly bimodal. Of the
19164 items in the fit set, 151 have
d_{i}<10^{-3}

(effectively at the floor) and 12357 have
d_{i}>0.999

(effectively at the ceiling), leaving little ranking resolution
within either mode. We therefore convert the ceiling test into a
likelihood-ratio indicator.
For each item we independently refit
(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i})
under two
forced-ceiling hypotheses,
d_{i}=1
and
d_{i}=0
, and record

\Delta\ell_{i}=\ell_{i}(d_{i}=0)-\ell_{i}(d_{i}=1),

where
\ell_{i}(\cdot)
is the optimized average per-respondent
log-likelihood for item
i
under the specified ceiling.
Positive
\Delta\ell_{i}
means the response pattern is better explained by a
zero-ceiling fit, i.e. that ability inflates the probability of
the
non-reference
answer. Figure
3

illustrates this contrast.
We use this
\Delta\ell_{i}
score as our mislabel indicator.

Inspecting flagged items by hand
(Appendix
E
) surfaces three
recurring sources of label noise (Figure
4
).

The most common is construction and verification artifacts. Topic-fit
labels, format verifiers, older automatic judges, style-variant
templates, and by-construction error injection can all mark a response
correct for satisfying the benchmark construction rule rather than for
being the better answer. Many of these become letter-vs.-spirit
failures. For example, the chosen response satisfies a surface constraint, while
the rejected response better satisfies the prompt’s intent. RM-Bench’s style-variant template is a high-volume case. A forced-concise chosen response is paired with a detailed rejected response, so the brevity penalty can outweigh the injected flaw.

A second pattern is source errors inherited unchanged. GSM8K reasoning
errors, MMLU-Pro key issues, and original preference-label mistakes
reappear in downstream benchmarks, sometimes across many generated
variants, after the source label has been treated as settled.

A final group consists of items with no stable single best answer,
including duplicate options, convention-dependent answers, and
near-equivalent preference pairs. Such examples may be usable as
preference-training data, where noise averages over many examples, but
are poor benchmark items because each label is treated as ground truth
for model ranking. These cases concentrate toward the tail of the
ranking and explain much of the precision drop after the highest-ranked
items.

3.6
Detecting model-level anomalies

Subset-level ability deviations.

The fitted item parameters enable a separate question: which
models show subset-specific departures after their overall level
is factored out? We hold
(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i})
fixed and refit a
per-pair ability
\theta_{m,s}
for each (model, subset) pair
under a Gaussian prior toward the global
\theta_{m}
with precision

\lambda_{\theta}=0.1
. We then center within each model,

\Delta\theta_{m,s}=\theta_{m,s}-\bar{\theta}_{m}
, where

\bar{\theta}_{m}
is the model’s mean fitted ability across subsets.

Figure
5
shows the resulting structure.
Reward models cluster above their own mean on preference
benchmarks, especially RewardBench 2 and RM-Bench Safety, and
below their mean on factual MC benchmarks such as GSM and MATH.
This pattern suggests format-specific specialization, likely amplified
by model scale. In particular, reward models appear better calibrated to
preference-style comparisons than to factual MC answering. Their average

\bar{\theta}_{m}
therefore understates preference-benchmark performance
and overstates factual MC performance.
The deviations are consistent but modest, with typical

|\Delta\theta_{m,s}|
for reward models on preference subsets in
the 0.2–0.5 range.

Agreement with detected mislabels.

A sharper anomaly appears when we inspect which models agree with the
reference on items flagged as mislabeled.

On preference benchmarks, most reward models stay within the
non-reward distributions and show no systematic tendency to agree with
bad references.
The exception is Skywork-Reward V2 Llama 8B, a high-scoring reward model on the

RewardBench 2 leaderboard
. Its agreement with detected mislabels is far
outside both its peer group and the non-reward population.
The MC panel suggests the effect is preference-specific. On factual MC
tasks, reward models neither agree with mislabels at elevated rates nor
separate from the non-reward distribution.

Because the effect is concentrated in one reward model, it does not
look like a general reward-model tendency to exploit preference-label
artifacts. Two mechanisms remain plausible. First, accidental contamination:
Skywork-Reward V2 is trained on an unusually large 40M-pair mixture
drawn from public preference datasets
(
18
)
,
overlapping the ecosystem from which these benchmarks draw. Second,
benchmark-specific over-optimization: public benchmark releases can
shape subsequent synthetic preference data and model selection.
Response data alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms.

4
Discussion

Both IRT and label-error detection have a rich history in NLP, including some work that touches on both. Our contribution is the specific construction: a forced-ceiling likelihood ratio derived from the 4PL upper asymptote, which we show outperforms the baselines we test. The gain is not that simpler signals fail entirely. Many baselines, including a basic 4PL fit, recover some high-confidence errors. The forced-ceiling score is stronger because it avoids the spurious local minima that can affect a free 4PL fit, and preserves a ranked signal across the full review queue, more clearly highlighting the most suspicious items. Our two-stage pipeline fixes
\theta
before the item refits, so this ratio runs efficiently in parallel, and can be applied post-hoc to new benchmarks.

The practical requirement for applying this mislabel detection pipeline to new benchmarks is modest, requiring only the response matrix with per-item correctness for each model.
This is compatible with evaluation aggregators such as Every Eval Ever
(
5
)
, which supports both benchmark-level and item-level submissions.
Once item-level responses are available for a benchmark, maintainers can rerun the indicator as stronger models arrive, without new inference, and prioritize high-confidence items for review.
We deposit our
evaluation results, including per-item responses and model justifications, at the Every Eval Ever Datastore

The reward-model result also suggests a practical contamination check.
Agreement with detected mislabels is not proof of contamination, as a model may share a benchmark’s labeling heuristic for other reasons.
However, it is a useful screening signal. Skywork-Reward V2 Llama 8B is the
case study here, agreeing with
\Delta\ell_{i}
-flagged references at
78% on preference tasks, far outside peer
reward models and the non-reward distribution. When a model agrees
unusually often with references that stronger independent models
reject, teams should inspect overlap with public benchmark data,
synthetic-data recipes, and model-selection criteria before treating
leaderboard gains as general progress.

The flagged items also point, after manual review, to preventable
failures.
Many confirmed errors and ambiguous items come from construction rules
that reward proxies for response quality, source labels copied without
rechecking, or examples with no stable single best answer.
Benchmark builders can reduce these errors
by inspecting high-disagreement items before release, validating source
labels when converting or augmenting datasets, and avoiding mechanical labeling rules that confuse surface compliance with response quality.
After release, the same indicator gets stronger as more diverse models evaluate the benchmark, so newly suspicious items can be prioritized for review rather than silently copied into the next benchmark or training mixture.

5
Limitations

The main limitation of our evaluation is the weak reference labeling
procedure. We use GPT-5.4 (extra-high) as a label aggregator over
strong-model judgments and model-side justifications, so the labels
inherit some limitations of LLM-as-judge evaluation. Exhaustive human
adjudication of roughly 20k items is impractical; the weak labels are
therefore a scalable proxy for comparing ranking methods and
estimating top-of-queue precision, not definitive relabeling of every
benchmark item. In human review of the top flagged items
(Appendix
E
), we found that the
aggregator accurately detects many failures, but sometimes sides with
proponent-model reasoning and endorses the reference where

subjective
would be more accurate.

The forced-ceiling indicator depends on a panel of models with
sufficient ability spread to make
\theta
identifiable. With
114 models spanning 2023–2026, our panel is comfortably above
this threshold, but the indicator’s resolution would degrade on
benchmarks with fewer high-ability respondents. The per-item refit also
assumes the global
\theta
scale generalizes across subsets.
Benchmarks built from item distributions very different from those
studied here, or dominated by a single model family, may not reproduce
the precision numbers we report.

The Skywork-Reward V2 finding is limited by lack of access to its
training data and selection procedure. Skywork released training data
for an earlier generation of reward models, but not for V2, so we
cannot test overlap with the flagged benchmark items directly. High
agreement with detected mislabels is therefore a screening signal, not
proof of contamination or benchmark-specific over-optimization.

Appendix F
Task and Label-Aggregator Prompts

We use two prompt families for our evaluations: task prompts to
run the benchmarks themselves, and label-aggregator prompts to
assign weak reference labels.

Benchmark Task Prompts.

Generative models evaluate preference items using the markdown
prompt, and multiple-choice (MC) items using the direct
answer-selection prompt, both shown in Table
6
.
The table displays shortened, formatted examples from real
benchmark items; full evaluation runs dynamically substitute each
item’s specific conversation, candidates, question, and options.
Reward models, conversely, are not prompted with these templates.
For preference items, they directly score candidate completions
appended to the original conversation; for MC items, they score
answer options as assistant responses to the question.

Label-Aggregator Prompts.

To evaluate the unsupervised error detector, we use a separate
GPT-5.4 label aggregator to label candidate benchmark errors. For each item, the
aggregator receives the benchmark content along with up to three
high-performing model justifications supporting the reference
label, and up to three supporting the strongest alternative. The
aggregator is called at temperature
0
with JSON response formatting
enabled. The system and user prompts are shown in
Table
6
.

You are a helpful assistant in evaluating the quality of the responses for a given instruction. Your goal is to select the best response for the given instruction.

Here are some rules of the evaluation:

(1) If the instruction does not contain harmful content, prioritize instruction-following, helpfulness, accuracy, level of detail, and harmlessness.

(2) If the instruction contains harmful content, prioritize the harmlessness and safety of the response.

## Benchmark item

[USER]

If $\det \mathbf{A} = -1,$ then find $\det (\mathbf{7A}).$

-18a - 59

## Examples of models that AGREE with the label (picked A)

### Example 1

To find the determinant of $\mathbf{7A}$, we can use the property of determinants that says $\det(\mathbf{cA}) = cˆn\det(\mathbf{A})$, where $\mathbf{c}$ is a constant and $n$ is the number of rows (or columns) in the matrix. In this case, $n=2$, so:

$\det(\mathbf{7A}) = 7ˆ2\det(\mathbf{A}) = 49 \times (-1) = -49$

Answer: A

---

## Examples of models that DISAGREE with the label (picked D)

### Example 1

To find \(\det(7\mathbf{A})\) given that \(\det \mathbf{A} = -1\), we use the property of determinants for scalar multiplication:

For an \(n \times n\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and a scalar \(k\),

\[

\det(k\mathbf{A}) = kˆn \det(\mathbf{A}).

\]

Here, \(k = 7\) and \(\det(\mathbf{A}) = -1\). Therefore,

\[

\det(7\mathbf{A}) = 7ˆn \cdot (-1) = -7ˆn.

\]

Answer: D

---

## Your task

Classify this benchmark item. Output a JSON object with exactly these fields:

{

"summary_task": "One sentence describing what the task asks.",

"summary_ref": "One sentence describing what the A response does.",

"summary_alt": "One sentence describing what the D response does.",

"summary_ref_reason": "One sentence summarising why pro-label models prefer A.",

"summary_alt_reason": "One sentence summarising why pro-D models prefer it.",

"label_reason": "Two to three sentences explaining your verdict.",

"label": "<one of: mislabel | label_correct | subjective | unsure>"

}

Label definitions:

- mislabel: the benchmark label is wrong; the alternative response is objectively better.

- label_correct: the benchmark label is correct; the labelled response is objectively better.

- subjective: both responses are genuinely acceptable; the disagreement reflects a legitimate difference in standards (style, verbosity, formality, cultural norms), not an error. Either response could be labelled without being wrong.

- unsure: you cannot reach a confident verdict — the task is too hard or specialised for you to evaluate correctly, the evidence is insufficient or contradictory, or the reasoning provided is too thin to judge. Use sparingly; prefer a specific label when possible.
