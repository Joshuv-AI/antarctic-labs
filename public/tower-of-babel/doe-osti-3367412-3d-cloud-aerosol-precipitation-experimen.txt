1.0 Summary
 The objective of this campaign was to complement the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric
 Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s Cloud And Precipitation Experiment at kennaook
 (CAPE-k) in northwest Tasmania by deploying scanning, fixed pointing angle, and in situ aerosol, cloud,
 and precipitation remote-sensing instruments during the last six months of the experiment. These
 instruments provided a three-dimensional (3D) context to the second ARM Mobile Facility (AMF2)
 vertically pointing observations collected at the kennaook Cape Grim (KCG) site and captured a portion
 of the life cycle of the clouds before and after the passage over the AMF2 vertical column. These 3D
 cloud measurements will be used in the near future to 1) investigate how the vertical profiles of aerosol
 and cloud properties are representative of the broader area and how these properties evolve during the
 portion of the cloud life cycle captured by the scanning instruments, 2) quantify how accurate the cloud
 fraction derived with assumptions from vertically pointing observations are compared with direct 3D
 cloud fraction measurements, 3) evaluate cloud fraction and liquid water path in the Australian
 Community Climate and Earth-System Simulator–Convective-scale (ACCESS-C) forecast model, and
 4) evaluate aerosol and cloud products from the European Space Agency/Japanese Aerospace Exploration
 Agency (ESA/JAXA) Earth Cloud Aerosol Radiation Explorer (EarthCARE) mission using the scanning
 measurements, offering more chances of exact collocation, thereby complementing the statistical
 approach we are planning to employ with AMF2 observations for that same purpose.

2.0 Results
 The initial plan was to deploy the following instruments:
  • The Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) scanning W-band FMCW Doppler radar, the Bistatic Radar
 System for Atmospheric Studies (BASTA)
  • The University of Melbourne (UoM) scanning mini-micropulse lidar (miniMPL)
  • The BoM vertically pointing 355nm backscatter and depolarization lidar (RMAN-511 lidar from
 Leosphere)
  • The UoM UV-Vis Airyx Multi-Axis Differential Optical Absorption Spectrometer (MAXDOAS)
  • The University of Utah (UoU) 2-channel microwave radiometer (Radiometrics)
  • Two optical disdrometers belonging to BoM (Parsivel 2 and Eigenbrodt ODM470)
  • Three micro rain radars (1 MRR-PRO and 2 MRR-2).

 The daily instrument operational status has been summarized in a separate Excel spreadsheet.

 The scanning W-band radar and miniMPL were successfully deployed at some distance from the AMF2
 containers, next to the autosonde launcher location, while the BoM lidar has been installed near the
 Ka-band ARM Zenith Radar (KAZR) container. We thank the ARM technicians onsite for their
 invaluable help installing, monitoring, and maintaining these fragile instruments.

 1

The two BoM optical disdrometers were installed in the so-called "paddock", which sits half way between
the autosounder location and the ARM containers. They both operated continuously during that period,
and all data have been processed and are available.

The three micro rain radars were installed. The MRR-PRO was installed on top off one of the ARM
containers as part of another ARM campaign proposal earlier (AFC10277 – SPORT proposal) and
operated continuously. The two MRR2 instruments were installed at the "paddock", and oriented
perpendicularly to each other to capture two perpendicular components of the 3D wind field, and pointing
15 degrees off zenith, as is typical with wind profilers, to capture a sufficient projection of the horizontal
wind components. The transmitter of the two MRR2 unfortunately failed, so only two MRRs ended up
producing data.

The UoM MAXDOAS instrument was deployed between 2 June and 18 July 2025 for an intercomparison
with the permanent instrument already installed at the site (run by Steve Wilson at the University of
Wollongong). Comparisons indicated a good agreement.

Due to the age of the 2-channel UoU microwave radiometer, it was decided that it would only be
deployed in case the more advanced ARM 3-channel radiometers and microwave profilers failed. This
was not needed. Earlier quantitative comparisons from Professor Mace between this 2-channel microwave
radiometer and the advanced MVP300A microwave profiler demonstrated excellent consistency between
the two instruments.

The initial plan with the scanning instruments was to focus on describing the "baseline" sector – compass
directions from roughly 180-280 degrees true. Unfortunately, the azimuthal axle of the scanning W-band
radar failed during transport and the elevation axle also failed on the first day of operation after we left
the site, so the instrument got stuck in a fixed but known slanted position between 20250709 and
20250730 (22 days). The elevation axle was fixed during a visit on 20250731, so we operated in range-
height indicator (RHI) mode pointing within the azimuthal sector covered by the scanning lidar between
20250801 and 20250812. On that day, the elevation axle failed again and could not be repaired onsite, so
the ARM technicians secured the instrument in vertical position and it operated in this way between
20250813 and 20250910. We went onsite from the10th to the 12th of September 2025 to install a mini
version of BASTA (called miniBASTA) and readjust the verticality of BASTA. Acquisition started on
20250912 with both BASTA and mini BASTA in vertically pointing mode until 20251015.

 The scanning miniMPL lidar did collect data in the baseline sector between 20250716 and 20251015, but
 intermittently. Overall, 25 full days and 45 partial days of scanning lidar observations can be used from
 this short deployment to investigate the spatial distribution of Southern Ocean clouds in conjunction with
 the AMF2 observations.
