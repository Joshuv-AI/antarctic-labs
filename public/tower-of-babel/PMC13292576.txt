Spatial burden and self-managed safety in peritoneal dialysis: a qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews
Authors: Youngsu Kim, Heejung Choi, Gyu-Tae Shin, Heungsoo Kim, Min-Jeong Lee, Inwhee Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeritoneal dialysis is promoted for its spatial flexibility compared to in-center hemodialysis. However, this flexibility entails a significant relocation of clinical responsibility, requiring patients to manage infection control and medical logistics within non-institutional settings. While spatial factors—including housing conditions, storage capacity, and environmental cleanliness—are critical to modality sustainability, they are often reduced to logistical metrics in clinical consultations. This study investigates the concrete mechanisms through which patients evaluate and manage their physical environments to ensure clinical safety.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 43 adult patients receiving peritoneal dialysis at a tertiary university hospital in South Korea. Interviews explored how participants experienced and evaluated different spaces in relation to peritoneal dialysis. Data were analyzed inductively using thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants perceived space not as a static setting but as a managed clinical site, categorized into three domains. Hospitals represented delegated safety, where clinical rigor was entrusted to professionals. Homes functioned as sites of internalized safety, where patients exercised hygienic control by reconfiguring domestic routines to accommodate medical inventories. Spaces beyond the home revealed a paradox of spatial liberation: while peritoneal dialysis theoretically enables flexibility, the unpredictability of external environments often triggered rigorous risk assessments, leading to self-imposed spatial narrowing. Across all domains, the patient’s capacity for environmental control was the primary determinant of whether a space was deemed safe.ConclusionsThis study highlights that space is not a neutral backdrop to treatment, but a central dimension of the PD experience. Understanding how patients perceive and manage their environment is essential for meaningful modality discussions, realistic education, and patient-centered communication. Without addressing these spatial dynamics, PD risks being presented as flexible in principle while remaining constraining in practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05089-4.

Introduction

The shift toward patient-centered outcomes in nephrology highlights that the treatment environment is not a passive backdrop but a clinically consequential component of care [1, 2]. For dialysis patients, whose treatment is both frequent and long-term, these habituated spaces directly shape health, well-being, and daily rhythms.

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is often promoted as a home-based therapy offering greater lifestyle autonomy than in-center hemodialysis (HD) [3]. While HD confines patients to institutional settings, PD is recognized for being relatively more flexible in terms of time and space [4–8]. However, this flexibility requires a significant trade-off: it relocates the responsibility for infection control from clinical environments to the patient’s private domain, effectively internalizing clinical safety as a personal obligation.

In PD, “place” is a fundamental prerequisite for treatment. To perform dialysis safely, patients must reconfigure domestic areas into sterile medical sites. Despite its importance, existing literature often reduces space to logistical metrics—such as travel distance or storage capacity [9–11]—overlooking space as a lived experience requiring continuous negotiation [12, 13]. Furthermore, while frameworks such as the social determinants of health often treat the environment as a static background, they fail to capture how patients actively reconstruct their domestic spaces through continuous spatial labor [13]. Patients must actively evaluate environmental suitability, balancing cleanliness and privacy with the need to accommodate substantial medical inventories.

Emerging research indicates that PD patients assume primary responsibility for maintaining safety within domestic environments [14–16]. Yet, the specific criteria patients use to distinguish between safe and risky spaces remain under-researched [17–19]. Because these spatial contingencies are often absent from pre-dialysis counseling, patients may encounter unforeseen burdens—such as the psychological stress of managing contamination risks in social settings—only after initiating therapy.

This study examines how PD patients conceptualize and evaluate their environments. Rather than viewing space as a static setting, we conceptualize it as a dynamic environment requiring active assessment to ensure safety. By focusing on how patients differentiate between clinical, domestic, and public spheres, this study elucidates how patients’ environmental control and internalized responsibility inform everyday practices and treatment decisions. We argue that environmental fit is a determinant, rather than a peripheral factor, in sustaining life on dialysis. Unlike HD, which is conducted in controlled clinical settings, PD relies entirely on the patient’s management of their own environment; therefore, this study focuses exclusively on the experiences of PD patients.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative interpretive design underpinned by social constructivism, focusing on how spatial meanings are constructed through lived experience [20]. While the original study explored broad motivations for PD, spatiality emerged as a dominant inductive theme. Consequently, we employed an inductive thematic analysis to derive insights directly from the data [21], focusing on the patients’ spatial experiences rather than imposing pre-existing spatial theories.

Sample and setting

The study was conducted at a tertiary university hospital in South Korea. In this setting, PD care is delivered through a nurse-led education model. Follow-up involves outpatient clinic visits every 1–3 months. Most participants were self-caring, with 24 performing Continuous Ambulatory Peritoneal Dialysis (CAPD) and 19 using Automated Peritoneal Dialysis (APD), either independently or with minimal familial supervision.

Using purposive sampling, we recruited adult patients with at least six months of PD experience to ensure stabilized self-management routines. To achieve maximum variation, we targeted diversity in dialysis modality, prior HD experience, and employment status (Table 1). Of 58 patients screened, 15 declined due to time constraints or privacy concerns. The final sample of 43 participants (Table 2) included both CAPD and APD users, providing a comprehensive range of spatial experiences.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the participating university hospital (AJOUIRB-SB-2025-324). All participants received written and verbal information regarding the study aims, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality safeguards. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Supplementary Table 1).

Data collection

Data were collected from July to December 2025 through semi-structured, one-on-one face-to-face interviews. Patients were introduced to the study by nurses during their clinic visits; subsequently, the interviewer followed up with those who expressed interest to arrange the interviews. Prior to each session, participants completed a brief questionnaire on demographic and clinical characteristics, followed by a formal explanation of study aims and ethical rights. Written informed consent, including permission for audio recording, was obtained from all participants with an emphasis on the right to withdraw at any time. Interviews were conducted by a researcher with a PhD in social sciences, experienced in qualitative health research and independent of the clinical care team. This separation was maintained to minimize power imbalances and clinical authority bias. Interviews lasted 40–90 min and were held in a private room during participants’ routine clinic waiting periods. A flexible interview guide focused on environmental navigation, hygiene management, and travel experiences (Supplementary Table 2). All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using an inductive reflexive thematic analysis following the approach of Braun and Clarke [21], conducted collaboratively without the use of qualitative analysis software. Following an inductive thematic approach [22], analysis progressed through three stages:

Initial open coding: Three researchers independently identified segments related to spatial evaluation and infection control.Categorization: Codes were compared and grouped into subcategories.Theme refinement: Researchers held weekly consensus meetings to resolve interpretive discrepancies and define overarching themes (Table 3).

To ensure trustworthiness, investigator triangulation was utilized alongside a systematic record of all analytical decisions. Reflexivity was addressed by documenting researchers’ assumptions—specifically the shift from viewing PD as merely “home-based” to seeing it as “environmentally demanding”—throughout the iterative analysis. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was confirmed, where no new spatial concepts emerged from the data.

Results

Participants described three primary spatial contexts in relation to dialysis: the hospital, the home, and spaces outside the home. Across these settings, participants consistently evaluated space through the lens of clinical safety (Table 4), particularly in relation to infection risk, spatial management burden, and the ability to maintain hygienic control.

Theme 1. The hospital: delegated responsibility and institutional safety

Participants did not describe the hospital as a neutral site of treatment. Rather, it emerged as a therapeutic space imbued with institutional meanings, primarily associated with clinical safety, delegated obligations, and externally imposed structure. These spatial meanings shaped how participants understood hospital-based dialysis in contrast to home-based modalities.

While a small number of participants expressed reservations about the safety of hospital-based dialysis, citing concerns about cleanliness and shared equipment, such as “things that you don’t know who used” (P5, F, 53), most participants perceived the hospital as the safest environment for dialysis, particularly in relation to infection prevention. This sense of clinical safety was grounded not merely in the physical setting of the hospital, but in the presence of medical professionals and standardized institutional protocols. Within this space, responsibility for maintaining sterility and responding to complications was understood to be transferred from the patient to the clinical system. As one participant explained:

I do dialysis at home. But if it’s not done properly, there’s always the fear that bacteria can get in and cause peritonitis. For patients who receive dialysis in the hospital, you don’t have to worry about those things at all. Everything is taken care of the sterilization, the safety. In that sense, I think hospital-based dialysis is the safer option (P19, F, 66)

I do dialysis at home. But if it’s not done properly, there’s always the fear that bacteria can get in and cause peritonitis. For patients who receive dialysis in the hospital, you don’t have to worry about those things at all. Everything is taken care of the sterilization, the safety. In that sense, I think hospital-based dialysis is the safer option (P19, F, 66)

This delegation of responsibility was particularly salient among participants who anticipated age-related declines in their capacity for meticulous self-management. The hospital was regarded as offering reassurance in the context of bodily aging and diminishing self-reliance. One participant noted:

When you get older, it becomes harder to manage everything yourself. That’s why, for elderly people, I think going to the hospital and having the nurses do it is more comfortable (P15, M, 46)

When you get older, it becomes harder to manage everything yourself. That’s why, for elderly people, I think going to the hospital and having the nurses do it is more comfortable (P15, M, 46)

Participants also emphasized continuous clinical monitoring as a central feature of hospital-based safety. Bodily states were translated into numerical indicators—such as weight changes or laboratory values—which were interpreted and acted upon by healthcare professionals. As one participant described:

I went to a hospital that only does dialysis…everything was done through the machines. Now it all comes up on the computer. They can immediately see things like potassium being too high, and they respond right away. I thought it would be good for older people, because everything is monitored and checked (P33, F, 67)

They can immediately see things like potassium being too high, and they respond right away. I thought it would be good for older people, because everything is monitored and checked (P33, F, 67)

While the hospital was widely perceived as a safe therapeutic environment, participants also described it as a space characterized by rigid temporal constraints. HD required participants to align their bodies and daily routines with institutional schedules, often several times per week. This temporal structuring was experienced as a loss of flexibility and autonomy.

Theme 2. The home: internalized responsibility and environmental control

In contrast to the hospital, the home was defined as a therapeutic space in which safety was actively produced and maintained by patients themselves. Although medical professionals were not physically present, participants perceived the home as a site of internalized safety because it allowed them to exert hygienic control, restrict access to others, and adjust treatment practices to their own autonomy.

It’s the same at home. I pay a lot of attention to hygiene. When I’m doing dialysis at home, if anyone moves around, I’ll say something… There’s nothing safer than my own home, my own space. If there’s any dust or anything, I clean it all up myself (P35, F 51)

It’s the same at home. I pay a lot of attention to hygiene. When I’m doing dialysis at home, if anyone moves around, I’ll say something… There’s nothing safer than my own home, my own space. If there’s any dust or anything, I clean it all up myself (P35, F 51)

Participants did not conceptualize clinical safety as a discrete technical task performed at specific moments, but as a continuous spatial condition embedded in everyday life. At home, they believed they could maintain adequate hygiene without medical staff.

Although the home lacked clinical monitoring technologies and continuous professional surveillance, participants did not necessarily interpret this absence as increasing risk. Instead, clinical safety was understood in terms of spatial regulation—clean surfaces, limited movement of people, and familiarity with the space—rather than the presence of healthcare professionals. For many, the home was seen safer than public or semi-public environments precisely because access could be restricted to family members.

Some participants further described subdividing domestic space in order to sustain this sense of environment control. In these cases, specific rooms or areas were designated exclusively for dialysis and kept separate from shared family spaces to minimize potential contamination risks. As one participant noted:

I have my own room, so I go in there and set it up separately at home. I keep it separate, with my son’s room and my wife’s room, so since there are three rooms, I just use one as my medicine room (P2, M, 65)

I have my own room, so I go in there and set it up separately at home. I keep it separate, with my son’s room and my wife’s room, so since there are three rooms, I just use one as my medicine room (P2, M, 65)

Participants also described how dialysis equipment and supplies reshaped domestic space. In the South Korean context, domestic space is shaped by distinct housing types, most commonly apartments, detached houses, and multi-unit dwellings (villas). Apartments and detached houses generally offer more storage space than villas, whereas villas often have more limited spatial capacity (Table 2). These differences in housing conditions directly influenced participants’ ability to store dialysis supplies and organize treatment environments within the home. For participants living in smaller dwellings such as villas, spatial constraints posed a significant challenge:

Honestly, there are quite a lot of constraints in terms of space for storing the boxes. Since I have to stack around 30 to 35 boxes a month, I can’t keep them all inside the house. So I end up keeping some of them in the hallway. (P11, M, 26)

We have quite a lot of extra space at home, and since it’s just my wife and me living together, it’s not really inconvenient. (P43, M, 68)

While some couples performed treatment in shared bedrooms, the majority of participants utilized a dedicated private space within the home.

The home functioned as a space where safety did not depend on medical professionals, but on the patient’s ability to maintain environmental control. Home-based dialysis positioned clinical safety as a self-managed spatial practice rather than as a condition conferred by institutional care.

Theme 3. Outside the home: the paradox of flexibility and limited control

In contrast to the relative environmental stability of the hospital and the home, spaces outside the home were consistently described as unstable and difficult to manage for dialysis. Such environments were primarily evaluated in terms of infection risk, limited hygienic control, and uncertainty regarding emergency support.

While most participants expressed significant constraints in traveling, only one participant reported being able to travel relatively freely. However, her mobility remained structured around dialysis requirements: she traveled domestically with a large tent, which she perceived as a separate and controlled space, and reported experiencing similar difficulties during international travel as other participants. One participant with prior HD experience noted that while HD requires finding a hospital at the destination, both PD and HD involve significant—yet distinct—barriers to travel.

Travel accommodations were portrayed as only conditionally suitable for dialysis. The feasibility of travel depended less on geographical distance than on whether a private and hygienic environment could be secured.

I do it at a lodging … The thing I worry about most is finding a place that’s clean. It’s really a hassle (P23, M, 57)

I do it at a lodging … The thing I worry about most is finding a place that’s clean. It’s really a hassle (P23, M, 57)

Another participant emphasized the lack of acceptable spaces in public settings:

I wish there were places where the space was more accommodating. First of all, there has to be an appropriate space. It has to be clean above all. A public health center is at least clean, but ordinary rest areas or places like that are absolutely not an option (P32, F, 54)

Participants frequently stated that only a limited range of hotels were willing to accept deliveries of dialysis fluid, while many other accommodations declined to receive medical shipments. While some avoided conducting dialysis in non-domestic spaces such as cars, others carried out dialysis in improvised settings, including vehicles or outdoor environments, when appropriate accommodation could not be secured. Although technically feasible, these spaces were regarded as fundamentally inadequate, given the challenges of maintaining adequate hygiene and stable physical conditions.

Beyond logistical barriers, participants described preemptively limiting travel due to anticipated difficulties. Concerns about maintaining hygiene, adhering to treatment schedules, and managing potential complications in unfamiliar settings led some participants to avoid leaving home altogether. Together, these accounts indicate that spaces outside the home were shaped by overlapping infection risks and logistical constraints related to dialysis. In response, participants explained progressively narrowing their activity spaces, often limiting movement to environments where dialysis could be reliably managed.

Discussion

Delegated safety and internalized safety

Participants distinguished between delegated safety in hospital-based HD and internalized safety in PD. In HD, safety is institutionally produced; responsibility is delegated to standardized protocols and professional surveillance, allowing patients to relinquish vigilance to the clinical system [23–25]. Conversely, PD safety depends on the patient’s internalized responsibility to manage hygiene. This transition relocates responsibility from institutional oversight to self-governance [24–26]. While the hospital is a space pre-designed for safety, the home becomes a safe clinical environment only through the patient’s active exercise of environmental control.

For PD patients, the institutional advantages of hospital-based care were not the primary factor in modality choice, aligning with literature suggesting that clinical factors alone do not dictate these decisions [5, 27]. Although HD offers maximum clinical security, it often limits lifestyle autonomy through rigid schedules and physical immobilization [25, 26]. As long as peritonitis risks were manageable, participants prioritized personal autonomy in governing their routines and ensuring lifestyle continuity over absolute clinical security. Despite the burden of self-managed medical care, patients reported higher satisfaction by maintaining independence at home [28].

Clinical safety was ultimately evaluated through the lens of spatial controllability. Environments were perceived as safe only when individuals felt capable of exercising hygienic control. Thus, safety is not a static property of a location, but a performative outcome contingent on the patient’s agency. The feeling of being safe is deeply rooted in the patient’s ability to exert environmental control through disciplined routine [29].

Home as a negotiated boundary space

The home emerged as a space where treatment and everyday life were co-located yet deliberately separated. Participants organized their domestic environment to maintain self-defined safety, exercising spatial control within their own territory rather than simply complying with medical instructions [30]. This control is supported by South Korea’s residential standards. As shown in our results, small household sizes and multi-room layouts enable patients to shield their daily routines by maintaining physical distance between medical procedures and domestic life.

These findings suggest that home safety is maintained through social interactions and the physical organization of medical supplies [31]. By designating specific rooms for dialysis and systematically arranging medical equipment, patients limited the intrusion of medical life into their private space [32, 33]. The home is not transformed into a hospital but is adapted to accommodate treatment while protecting its domestic character. Thus, the home remains a site of self-managed safety [34], where patients actively manage environmental variables to preserve the integrity of their domestic space.

Spaces outside the home: the paradox of flexibility

Spaces beyond the home were often characterized as unstable and difficult to manage. Participants evaluated travel destinations primarily through their perceived suitability for hygienic control and privacy. Unlike hospitals or homes, these spaces appeared to lack established safeguards and required deliberate reconstruction to become temporarily safe for dialysis [35–37].

While PD is promoted for the freedom to choose treatment locations, our findings reveal a “paradox of spatial liberation”: the autonomy gained from leaving the hospital is contingent upon the patient’s ability to maintain constant environmental control, which often leads to self-imposed spatial constriction in unfamiliar settings. In many cases, travel was reduced and spontaneous activities were avoided due to the need for rigorous pre-screening. Freedom, therefore, generated a form of vigilance that could progressively narrow the geography of everyday life. While previous studies have documented travel burdens in PD [35, 38], these findings suggest that the freedom of movement is conditional, as the spatial responsibility transforms mobility into a process of ongoing risk assessment.

These observations indicate that PD-related flexibility may be contingent rather than absolute. Spatial freedom often operates under conditions of self-surveillance, where each unfamiliar environment becomes a site of risk calculation. This suggests a structural tension: while PD decentralizes treatment from the hospital, it simultaneously redistributes the burden of environmental stabilization to the patient.

Self-managed safety through spatial practice

Taken together, these findings suggest that self-managed safety in PD is fundamentally a spatial practice. Participants were observed to continually assess whether environments permit hygienic control, separation, and controlled procedural practice. These spatial judgments appeared to shape mobility, social participation, and emotional security [39]. In this sense, infection control may be interpreted not merely as a technical protocol, but as an ongoing form of “spatial labor” where the patient actively negotiates their environment to fulfill their internalized responsibility.

By foregrounding this labor, the focus shifts from dialysis as a medical technology to PD as an environmental practice embedded in the domestic and social fabric of life [38, 40, 41]. This study extends research on infection control—which has largely focused on technical competence [36, 37, 42]—by conceptualizing environmental management as a proactive safety mechanism. These results highlight that maintaining a “safe space” is an essential, yet often invisible, prerequisite for successful treatment.

Spatial capacity as a critical factor in shared decision-making

These findings have practical implications for modality counseling and pre-dialysis education. If spatial management serves as an informal clinical prerequisite, then a patient’s “spatial capacity”—defined as the functional ability to exercise control over the environment to preserve the freedom of daily life and fulfill medical responsibilities—should be evaluated as a formal clinical variable during modality selection. Counseling for PD should move beyond medical eligibility to include a structured evaluation of: (1) the feasibility of a dedicated dialysis area to minimize contamination, (2) the ability to regulate household movement (e.g., managing shared spaces or pets), (3) storage capacity (approx. 30–50 cartons), and (4) anticipated mobility patterns.

To operationalize this, the use of a “spatial readiness checklist” is suggested during shared decision-making. Clinicians can provide visual aids or specific dimensions to help patients assess “environmental fit” before committing to PD. Integrating these spatial contingencies allows for a more realistic alignment between modality choice and the patient’s lived environment. Rather than framing PD solely as a “flexible” option, the environmental responsibilities and the necessity of hygienic control should be explicitly discussed. Positioning “environmental fit” as a component of shared decision-making ensures that spatial capacity is recognized as a significant dimension of the patient experience [4, 38]. Consequently, potential barriers to adherence can be identified early, enabling the development of tailored spatial-management strategies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected at a single tertiary hospital, where PD management is highly centralized. Consequently, the participants’ perceptions of safety and space may have been inherently conditioned by the specific training protocols and safety indications provided by the institution [43]. While the study setting serves a diverse population, the participants were mostly from urban or suburban areas, with little representation from remote rural regions. Second, selection bias may exist as this study focuses exclusively on the perspectives of PD patients, omitting the insights of HD patients, the discontinued PD patients, family members, and medical professionals. Finally, the transferability of these findings may be limited by South Korea’s unique social welfare and demographic context. In Korea, PD patients are classified as having a disability, receiving extensive government support such as subsidized housing. Furthermore, the low average household size and the prevalence of elderly patients living alone or as a couple significantly mitigate domestic spatial conflicts. These specific conditions may alleviate the spatial burden more effectively than in other global contexts, so these results should be interpreted with caution when applied to different welfare systems or larger household structures. Future multicenter research incorporating diverse geographical backgrounds and caregiver insights is needed.

Conclusion

This study highlights that space is not a neutral backdrop to treatment, but a central dimension of the PD experience. Understanding how patients perceive and manage their environment is essential for meaningful modality discussions, realistic education, and patient-centered communication. Without addressing these spatial dynamics, PD risks being presented as flexible in principle while remaining constraining in practice.
