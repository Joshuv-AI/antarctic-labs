Microbial profile and antimicrobial resistance patterns in clinical specimens from a secondary-level hospital in Accra, Ghana: a five-year retrospective study
Authors: Wendy Larbi, Leslie Larry Afutu, Emmanuel Adu Sarpong, Abdul-Samad Alhassan, Nicholas Donkor, Alberta Serwah Anning, George Ghartey-Kwansah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a significant global health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where surveillance data remain limited. Understanding local microbial patterns and resistance trends is critical for guiding empirical therapy and implementing effective antimicrobial stewardship policies. This study aimed to characterize the microbial profile and antimicrobial resistance patterns of pathogens from clinical specimens at the Cocoa Clinic Laboratory, Ghana, from 2019 to 2023.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 6,395 clinical specimens collected between January 2019 and December 2023 from a secondary-level hospital in Accra, Ghana. Samples, including midstream urine, high vaginal swabs (HVS), and blood, were processed for bacterial and fungal isolation using standard microbiological techniques. Descriptive and trend analyses were conducted on specimen types, pathogen prevalence, and resistance patterns. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed, and data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, SPSS version 27, and GraphPad Prism 8.ResultsA total of 6,395 clinical specimens were analyzed, of which 22.2% (95% CI: 19.6–24.9) yielded positive microbial growth. Urine samples constituted the majority of positive cultures (50.6%), followed by HVS (30.3%), with other specimen types accounting for 19.1%. Gram-negative organisms predominated (62.7%), with Escherichia coli emerging as the leading bacterial isolate across most years and specimen types. Notably, a shift in pathogen distribution was observed in 2023, when Candida species became the most frequently isolated organism, suggesting a possible change in pathogen distribution (p < 0.001). Females exhibited a higher culture positivity than males (61% vs. 39%, p < 0.0001), and adults aged 18–64 years accounted for the majority of isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed high effectiveness of amikacin, ciprofloxacin, and gentamicin (> 89% susceptibility), whereas cefuroxime, co-trimoxazole, and ampicillin/sulbactam showed high resistance rates (> 68%).ConclusionOverall, temporal trends indicated an increase in infection rates from 2021 to 2023, contrasting with lower detection during the early COVID-19 period. This highlights an urgent need for continuous AMR surveillance, strict antibiotic stewardship, and the development of targeted infection control strategies. These findings provide valuable baseline data to inform clinicians, policymakers, and public health authorities in optimizing treatment guidelines and combating AMR in Ghana.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Antibiotics, Microbial profile, Multidrug resistance, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Candida spp

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), the ability of microorganisms to withstand the effects of drugs designed to eliminate them, has become a critical global health threat. Although resistance can occur naturally through genetic mutations or horizontal gene transfer, its emergence has been significantly accelerated by the widespread misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in human medicine, veterinary care, agriculture, and aquaculture [1, 2]. The increasing prevalence of multidrug‑resistant (MDR), extensively drug‑resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) organisms has resulted in treatment failures, prolonged hospital stays, and elevated mortality rates worldwide [3]. Moreover, the emergence of resistance among fungal pathogens, such as Candida species, presents an additional challenge to global health [4, 5].

The World Health Organization [6] identifies AMR as one of the top ten global public‑health threats, projecting that if unaddressed, infections caused by resistant pathogens could result in millions of annual deaths and economic losses exceeding US$2.9 trillion by 2050 [2, 7]. In low‑ and middle‑income countries, weak surveillance systems, poor regulatory control over antimicrobial sales, and limited diagnostic capacity have worsened the problem [8, 9].

Across Africa, unregulated antibiotic access and self‑medication are key drivers of AMR. Vialle‑Valentin found that in several African communities, more than one‑third of individuals with acute illness used antibiotics, and a significant proportion obtained antibiotics without prescription [10]. Subsequent studies have confirmed widespread resistance among bacterial pathogens in both community and hospital settings across the continent [5, 9]. In Ghana, similar trends are evident, and recent systematic reviews highlight a high burden of resistance alongside still‑limited national surveillance data [11, 12]. This persistent knowledge gap not only signals a growing public health threat but also establishes a critical research imperative in Ghana. Despite increasing reports of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), reliable long-term, facility-level surveillance data remain limited, particularly within secondary-level healthcare facilities where resistance patterns directly influence patient management and empirical treatment decisions. Most AMR studies in Ghana have focused on tertiary hospitals or specific pathogens, resulting in limited longitudinal local resistance data and the underrepresentation of secondary-level hospitals in AMR surveillance and research.

Although the Ghana National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2017–2021) and subsequent frameworks emphasize surveillance strengthening, antimicrobial stewardship, and rational antibiotic use, implementation challenges persist. Limited microbiological diagnostic capacity, inadequate routine surveillance systems, weak stewardship enforcement, insufficient local antibiogram data, and continued reliance on empirical prescribing continue to undermine effective AMR control efforts.

There is therefore an urgent need for comprehensive, locally generated evidence to guide empirical treatment protocols, inform policy, and monitor evolving resistance dynamics within specific institutional and regional contexts. This study investigates a five-year trend in microbial profiles and antimicrobial resistance patterns among clinically significant bacterial isolates obtained from Cocoa Clinic, a secondary-level healthcare facility in the Greater Accra Region. Findings from this study are expected to strengthen national AMR surveillance efforts, support evidence-based antimicrobial stewardship interventions, and contribute to more rational antimicrobial use and improved patient management in Ghana.

Materials and methods

Study area and design

This retrospective longitudinal surveillance study was conducted at the Cocoa Clinic laboratory, located at Kaneshie, Greater Accra, Ghana. The study analyzed routine bacterial isolates recovered from various clinical specimens submitted to the microbiology laboratory between January 2019 and December 2023. Each isolate was tested against a panel of antibiotics to determine its susceptibility profile.

Data extraction

A total of 6395 laboratory records containing clinical culture and antimicrobial susceptibility results were retrieved from the microbiology unit’s database for the study period. A census sampling was employed, whereby all available records meeting the study eligibility criteria were included in the analysis. Specimens comprised of urine, HVS, wound swabs, blood, and other body fluids. Records with incomplete information or evidence of contamination were excluded. The use of convenience sampling was justified by the retrospective nature of the study and the availability of archived laboratory data.

Processing and identification of isolates

All specimen processing and identification procedures adhered to the laboratory’s standard operating procedures (SOPs), which align with internationally accepted guidelines [13]. Samples were cultured on routine microbiological media and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Plates showing no growth after 24 h were re-incubated for up to 48 h before being classified as negative. The identification of isolates was based on colonial morphology, Gram staining, and a series of standard biochemical tests. Gram-positive bacteria were identified through hemolytic pattern observation on blood agar, catalase and coagulase reactions, DNase production, and bile esculin hydrolysis. Optochin and bacitracin sensitivity tests were used for further differentiation, where applicable. Gram-negative bacteria were characterized using oxidase testing, triple sugar iron (TSI) reactions, motility, indole ornithine (MIO) medium, citrate utilization, lysine iron agar (LIA), and urease activity [14]. Quality control for identification procedures was ensured using standard reference strains Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was carried out using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, specifically the CLSI M100 Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, 34th Edition 2024 [15]. Briefly, bacterial suspensions were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity and evenly inoculated on agar plates. Antibiotic disks were placed aseptically on the inoculated surface, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Zone diameters of inhibition were measured in millimeters, and results were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to CLSI criteria.

Data analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel, cleaned, and analyzed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data as frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables such as age groups, gender, and infection type were tested using the chi-square test. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated where appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Specimen distribution and culture positivity

A total of 6395 clinical specimens were processed between 2019 and 2023, of which 22.2% (95% CI: 19.6–24.9) yielded positive microbial growth. Among these, urine samples accounted for 50.6% (95% CI: 47.2–54.0) of all positive cultures, followed by high vaginal swabs (HVS) at 30.3% (95% CI: 27.1–33.6). Other sample types, including wound swabs, sputum, and blood, collectively represented the remaining 19.1% (Table 1). Gram-negative isolates predominated (approximately 62.7%, 95% CI: 59.8–65.6), which is consistent with prior studies highlighting their dominance in nosocomial and community-acquired infections [16]. Gram-positive bacteria and fungi constituted smaller proportions, but notable variation occurred across years, as observed in Fig. 1.

Yearly trends in microbial infection

From 2021 to 2023, there has been a steady increase in microbial infection, as observed in Fig. 2. Also, from 2019 to 2022, E. coli remained the leading isolate, representing 37.6% (95% CI: 34.3–41.0) of all positive cultures in those years. However, in 2023, a noticeable increase was observed: Candida species, a fungal pathogen, became the most frequently recovered organism (Fig. 3). The increase in Candida isolation from 12.03% in 2022 to 15.0% in 2023 was statistically significant (χ² for trend = 21.5, df = 4, p < 0.001), suggesting a possible alteration in disease presentation. E. coli maintained its role as the predominant bacterial pathogen across most specimen types, particularly urine and HVS, while Klebsiella spp., Enterococcus spp., and Staphylococcus spp. were also detected but at lower frequencies (range: 7.9–14.4, 95% CI: 6.4–16.6). Occasional co-infections such as Klebsiella and Candida, and Enterococcus and Candida, were identified in less than 2% of positive samples. These were mostly recovered from urine and HVS specimens, and though infrequent, they may represent complex polymicrobial infections warranting further investigation (Table 2).

Demographic distribution

Analysis by sex revealed a higher culture positivity among females (61%, 95% CI: 57.0-64.8) compared to males (39%, 95% CI: 35.2–43.0) (Fig. 4). The association between gender and infection type was statistically significant (χ² = 151.24, df = 1, p < 0.001). This pattern is likely related to anatomical and physiological factors that predispose females to urinary and reproductive tract infections [17]. Age distribution, in Table 3, showed that the 18−64-year group contributed the highest number of isolates (approximately 71.4%, 95% CI: 69.1% − 73.6%), followed by the ≥ 65-year group. The lowest frequency occurred among children aged 0–12 years (8.3%, 95% CI: 6.9% − 9.7%). The association between age and infection type was significant (χ² = 377.90, df = 2, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Generally, there has been a steady increment of microbial infection from the year 2021 to 2023, which is in contrast with the decrease in infection from previous years (2019–2020) of the study duration (Fig. 2). This may be partly explained by the COVID-19 pandemic, which had a significant effect on the lifestyle of individuals, leading to low hospital records [18]. The dominance of Gram-negative bacteria observed here agrees with earlier studies identifying these organisms as major causes of both community-acquired and hospital-associated infections in Africa [16, 19]. Their intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms likely contribute to their persistence in hospital environments. The predominance of E. coli as the leading isolate, particularly from urine samples, reinforces its established role as the principal uropathogen worldwide [20]. Although the current study lacked antifungal susceptibility data, the observed increase in Candida spp. isolation in 2023 underscores the growing importance of fungal surveillance alongside antibacterial resistance monitoring [21–23].

Gender-based differences in culture positivity reflect findings from prior research [24] and emphasize the need for sex-specific preventive strategies, particularly in female-dominated outpatient settings. The concentration of infections among adults aged 18–64 years likely reflects greater healthcare utilization and occupational exposures within this group [25]. The antibiotic susceptibility data reveal both strengths and concerns. The high activity of amikacin and ciprofloxacin suggests that aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones demonstrated high in vitro susceptibility options for many Gram-negative infections in this facility. However, the pronounced resistance to β-lactams and co-trimoxazole highlights the continuing threat of empiric therapy failure [26]. The significant variability between this study’s findings and those from Egypt or Kuwait [27, 28] may stem from differences in prescribing practices, infection control policies, and population characteristics.

Despite these observations, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study was retrospective and limited to one hospital, which may restrict generalizability. Species identification was based on conventional biochemical methods rather than molecular assays, and antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed. Additionally, incomplete clinical data prevented correlation of laboratory findings with patient outcomes. Nevertheless, the statistically significant associations between infection patterns and demographic factors, combined with high resistance rates to several commonly used antibiotics, signal a pressing need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and continuous laboratory surveillance [23].

We recommend that future studies incorporate molecular characterization of resistance genes, broader geographic sampling, and antifungal resistance profiling to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the evolving resistance landscape.
