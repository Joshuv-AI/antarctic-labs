A Dynamical Approach to Schur’s Theorem
Sonia L’Innocente, Francesco G. Russo, Ilaria Svampa

Abstract
. A classical result of Schur of 1904 shows that an infinite (discrete) group E E with finite central quotient E / Z ⁡ ( E ) E/Z(E) should have finite derived subgroup [ E , E ] [E,E] . Schur’s Theorem has many important consequences, which have been extensively investigated in the literature. Here we focus on topological Hausdorff groups, which are not necessarily discrete groups, and show a dynamical version of Schur’s Theorem via the notion of topological entropy of Adler, Konheim and McAndrew. Their perspective follows some original intuitions of Kolmogov and Sinai from the area of the dynamical systems. Firstly, we investigate the topological entropy of continuous endomorphisms of maximal almost periodic groups whose closed derived subgroup is compact. The properties of these groups were known to Takahashi in 1952 and among them we find the 𝖹 \mathsf{Z} -groups of Grosser and Moskowitz. Secondly, we give a new dynamical interpretation of the Schur’s Theorem, showing that a 𝖹 \mathsf{Z} -group G G with continuous endomorphisms of finite topological entropy should have closed derived subgroup [ G , G ] ¯ \overline{[G,G]} with continuous endomorphisms of finite topological entropy. Finally, we illustrate a series of constructions and examples, which allow us to justify our interpretation of Schur’s Theorem as generalization of the original version. Keywords and Phrases : Locally compact group; Schur’s Theorem; Z-group; Takahashi group. Mathematics Subject Classification 2020 : 22A05, 22A25, 22D45, 37B40, 54C70.

Introduction and statement of the main results

Following
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
, a topological group

Discrete groups are examples of topological groups, where we consider the discrete topology; among them, we find finite groups. In particular, finite groups can be regarded as compact groups with the discrete topology, see
[

Let
G
be a group. Then,

(1.1)

Z(G)=\{g\in G\mid[g,x]=1\ \ \forall x\in G\}

denotes the

, while the

is defined by

(1.2)

[G,G]=\langle[g,x]=g^{-1}x^{-1}gx\ \ \mid\ \ x,g\in G\rangle.

The original results of Schur
[
28

are foundational in many aspects of pure mathematics.

Theorem 1.1

(Schur’s Theorem, 1904)
.

Assume that
G
is an infinite (discrete) group. Then the following implication is true:

(1.3)

G/Z(G)\ \ \mbox{is finite}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ [G,G]\ \mbox{is finite}\

There is a recent line of investigation which is devoted to classify locally compact groups via the finiteness of the topological entropy of their continuous automorphisms: this will offer a new perspective for Theorem
1.1
. Let’s see how: finite abelian groups and locally compact abelian groups of finite rank (according to
[
12
]
) may be classified by structural decompositions, but it is also possible to measure their topological entropy, since this turns out to be small enough.

We will work with a notion of entropy which appears in works of Kolmogorov
[
19
]
and Sinai
[
29
]
, who introduced the metric entropy in the ergodic theory. Drawing inspiration from their approach, the topological entropy was adapted by Adler, Konheim, and McAndrew
[
2
]
to continuous self-maps on compact topological spaces. This had subsequent generalizations, most notably by Bowen
[
3
]
and Hood
[
17
]
. Then the topological entropy of continuous endomorphisms has been explored as a dynamical approach to the study of the structure of locally compact groups in
[
9
,
22
,
27
]
, and its definition relies on that of Haar measure on the group
[
8
]

Definition 1.2

(See
[
2
,
3
,
17
]
, Topological Entropy for Locally Compact Groups)
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group,
\mu
the Haar measure on

,
\mathcal{CT}(G)
the collection of all compact neighbourhoods of the identity of

and
\mathrm{End}(G)
the ring of continuous endomorphisms of

. If

,
V\in\mathcal{CT}(G),\varphi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
and

\varphi^{-n}(V)=\underbrace{\varphi^{-1}\left(\varphi^{-1}\dots(\varphi^{-1}(V))\dots\right)}_{n-\mbox{times}}
, we define the

-th
\varphi
-cotrajectory of

as the set

(1.4)

C_{n}(\varphi,V)=V\cap\varphi^{-1}(V)\cap\ldots\cap\varphi^{-n+1}(V)

which belongs to
\mathcal{CT}(G).

The
topological entropy of
\varphi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
with respect to
V\in\mathcal{CT}(G)
is defined by

(1.5)

\mathsf{H}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi,V)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log\mu(C_{n}(\varphi,V))}{n},

and the
topological entropy of
\varphi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
is defined by

(1.6)

\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi)=\sup\left\{\mathsf{H}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi,V)\ \mid\ V\in\mathcal{CT}(G)\right\}.

The
topological entropy of
G
is defined by

(1.7)

\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{top}}(G)=\{\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi)\ \mid\ \varphi\in\mathrm{End}(G)\}.

We say that

is
invariant
under
\varphi
if
\varphi(V)\subseteq V
, i.e. if
\varphi^{-1}(V)\supseteq V
, and that

is

if it is invariant under all continuous endomorphisms

in
\mathrm{End}(G)
. Then,

captures how far

is from being invariant under
\varphi
at any
(n-1)
-th iteration of
\varphi^{-1}
. The less invariant

is under
\varphi
, the larger
-\log\mu(C_{n}(\varphi,V))
is, and vice versa. As in many other contexts, the notions of topological entropy quantifies chaos: here

reflects the degree to which
\varphi
moves elements of
G
and its subsets

. The topological entropy of

is the spectrum of values of the topological entropy of all the continuous endomorphisms on
G
.

We investigate the cardinality of (
1.7
), as done in
[
22
,
27
,
31
]
. Note that
\mathrm{Aut}(G)\subseteq\mathrm{End}(G)
, where

\{\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi)\ \mid\ \varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(G)\}\subseteq\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{top}}(G),

and several useful results on topological entropy are formulated for automorphisms rather than for arbitrary endomorphisms.
Following
[
2
]
we may introduce

\mathfrak{E}_{0}=\{G\ \mbox{locally compact group}\ \mid\ \mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{top}}(G)=\{0\}\},

\mathfrak{E}_{<\infty}=\{G\ \mbox{locally compact group}\ \mid\ \mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{top}}(G)\subseteq[0,+\infty[\}

and there are results which describe when a locally compact group belongs to

Theorem 1.3

Let

(i)

If

is compact and

(ii)

\overline{[G,G]}
is compact and
\overline{[G,G]}\in\mathfrak{E}_{0}

2.
Preliminary results of representation theory of locally compact groups

From
[
14
, Chapter 1]

has

(briefly,

\mathsf{SIN}
-

-group, but the class of

Definition 2.1

(See

, p.64, (1))

A topological group

if for every neighborhood

It is convenient to look at Definition

(2.1)

\mathcal{N}(G)=\{N\ \mbox{is a closed normal subgroup of}\ G\mid G/N\ \mbox{is a Lie group}\}

and noting that

is a pro-Lie group if and only if

is locally compact and
\lim\mathcal{N}(G)=1
. As usual the convergence of a filter at a point
\lim\mathcal{N}(G)=1
means that for every neighborhood
U_{0}
of the identity
1
there is an
N\in\mathcal{N}(G)
such that

. In particular, this is equivalent to say that a locally
compact group
G
is a pro-Lie group if
G=\underset{N\in\mathcal{N}(G)}{\lim}G/N
is the projective limit of Lie groups

with a descending family

of closed compact normal subgroups of

satisfying
\underset{N\in\mathcal{N}(G)}{\bigcap}N=1
.

A classical result which structurally describes locally compact groups is the following:

Lemma 2.2

(See
[
13
]
, Gelfand-Raikov Theorem, pp.13–14)

Let
G
be a locally compact group. The set of all (complex unitary) irreducible representations of

separates the points of
G
. That is, for every

, there exists a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a unitary irreducible representation
\rho\colon G\rightarrow\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\rho(g)\neq\rho(h)
.

Another classical result which describes compact groups is due to their
regular representation
in the invertibles
\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(G,\mathbb{K}))
of the Hilbert space

of square-integrable functions
from

to
\mathbb{K}
, where

is either the field of real numbers

or that of complex numbers

, see
[
14
, Example 2.12]
. This implies what is known as Fundamental Theorem on Unitary Modules
[

, Theorem 2.22]
and consequently a relevant result of structure for compact groups:

Proposition 2.3

(See
[
14
]
, Corollaries 2.27 and 2.29)

If

is a compact group, then the finite dimensional simple modules separates the points. In particular, every compact group

is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of a product of orthogonal matrices

and of a product
\prod_{j\in J}\mathrm{U}(n_{j})

4.
The first main result and its proof

Historically, the first generalizations of Theorem
1.1
have been found, looking at classes of discrete infinite groups larger than those with finite central quotient:
for instance, “
Chernikov
”
[
26
, Chapter 5, 5.4.23]
can replace the word “finite” in (
1.3
), see
[
7
, §2]
.

A second level of generalization of Theorem
1.1
has been found, when (
1.1
) is replaced by larger sets containing
Z(G)
. Firstly,
Z(G)
is not only a subset of
G
but a characteristic subgroup of
G
, hence we shall check that we choose sets containing
Z(G)
which preserve the structure of group and allow us to pass through quotients by
G
. Secondly,
Z(G)
contains (in its definition) the so-called
commutator word

[g,x]=g^{-1}x^{-1}gx
and this is a very special product of elements of
G
, since it allows us to consider consequently
[G,G]
, which is the subgroup generated by all the commutator words (see
[
1
]
and
[
26
, §2.2, §2.3]
and the so-called
Hall’s Problem
for words on groups).

A third level of generalization of Theorem
1.1
has been found when we look at an asymptotic behaviour of the series of the centres in a group. In fact it is well known that we may introduce the notion of
nilpotent group

G
via the
upper central series

Z_{i}(G)
for
i=0,1,2,\ldots
putting
1=Z_{0}(G)
,
Z_{1}(G)=Z(G)
,
Z_{2}(G)=\{g\in G\mid[[g,x],y]=1\ \ \forall x,y\in G\}
, … and so on. In this situation
G
is called nilpotent of class
i
, if there is some
i
such that
Z_{i}(G)=G
, but is called
hypercentral
if
Z_{\infty}(G)=G
. Dually we may consider the
lower central series

\gamma_{i}(G)
of
G
, introducing
G=\gamma_{1}(G)
,
[G,G]=\gamma_{2}(G)
,
[[G,G],G]=\gamma_{3}(G)
and so on. It turns out that
G
is nilpotent of class
i
if and only if
\gamma_{i+1}(G)=1
(resp.
G
is hypercentral if and only if
\gamma_{\infty}(G)=1
), see
[
26
, Chapter 5]
. At this point, one could ask whether there is still something of interesting to observe when
Z(G)
is replaced by
Z_{\infty}(G)
in (
1.3
)
[
7
, Section 3]
. Also the works of Podoski and others
[
24
]
follow a similar logic via the so-called
rank restrictions
.

Just to give an idea of the wide area which we are trying to describe, Niroomand
[
23
]
and other authors studied conditions in which it is even possible to reverse the implication (
1.3
), that is, the problem of the
Converse of Schur’s Theorem
(essentially this is possible when we deal with finitely generated discrete groups). Finally, we list a fourth generalization of Theorem
1.1
, being impossible to summarize decades of scientific production on this line of research. In fact Schur studied another delicate concept: the second cohomology group
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
of a discrete group
G
with complex coefficients, known as
Schur multiplier
of
G
in
[
18
]
. In cohomological group theory
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
is related to the central extensions of
G
, but also to
[G,G]
by the Hopf’s Formula, see
[
18
,
26
]
. Roughly speaking, it is possible to prove Theorem
1.1
, arguing that
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
is small enough and this implies that
[G,G]
is also small. In all these variations on the theme of Theorem
1.1
there is no real mention of topology, since the groups are discrete.

The situation changes drastically when we begin with an infinite topological group which is nondiscrete. Concerning compact groups and locally compact groups, Hofmann and Leptin
[
15
,
20
,
21
]
studied the Schur multiplier and explored its deep connections with representation theory and homological algebra. The notion of dimension of a topological group in Remark
3.7
plays a fundamental role, since totally disconnected locally compact groups have properties which differ substantially from connected locally compact groups. For instance, the Schur multiplier of a connected compact abelian group turns out to be
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{T})
, where
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
denotes the torus group. Therefore the generalization of Theorem
1.1
is subtle in topological group theory.

It is appropriate to briefly discuss how to topologize
G/Z(G)
in Theorem
1.3
. Let
G
be a locally compact group,
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
the group of all topological automorphisms of
G
, and
H
a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
; put on
H
a topology
\tau
. It is known that the action of
H
on
G
is continuous if and only if
\tau
is finer than the compact open topology, but in general the compact open topology does not provide a group topology on
H
. So, we may consider the coarsest group topology finer than the compact open topology for which the things work properly. We may endow
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
with the so-called
Birkhoff topology
, that is, the group topology induced by a local basis at the identity formed by the sets

(4.1)

\mathcal{S}(C,U_{0})=\{\varphi\in H\mid\ \varphi(g)\in gU_{0},\ \varphi^{-1}(g)\in gU_{0}\ \ \ \forall g\in C\},

where
C
is a compact subset and
U_{0}
a neighbourhood of the identity of
G
. Details can be found in
[
10
]
. In other words, if the compact open topology makes
H
a topological group, then the two topologies coincide, but generally this is not the case. Note also that for a connected Lie group
G
the Birkhoff topology coincides with the compact open topology, and so
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
turns out to be a Lie group. This is quite far from the families of locally compact groups in Definition
3.5
, while very close to those in Proposition
3.10
.

According to the terminology of van Est
[
10
]
, a locally compact group
G
is a
CA
-
group
if
\mathrm{Inn}(G)
is closed in
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
. If
G
is a connected Lie group, it can be checked that this is almost always the case, in fact
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
is a Lie group, and
\mathrm{Inn}(G)
is closed in
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
if and only if
\mathrm{Inn}(G)
is locally compact.
It should be also mentioned that for a locally compact group
G

(4.2)

x\in G\longmapsto\psi_{x}\in\mathrm{Inn}(G),\ \mbox{where}\ \psi_{x}:g\in G\mapsto xgx^{-1}\in G,

is a continuous natural homomorphism. Indeed, since the kernel of this mapping is
Z(G)
, one may only check that the induced group isomorphism
G/Z(G)\to\mathrm{Inn}(G)
is continuous. Clearly, the action
\alpha:(G/Z(G),\tau_{q})\times G\to G
defined by
\alpha(xZ(G),y)=xyx^{-1}
is continuous, where
\tau_{q}
is the quotient topology on
G/Z(G)
.
Since the Birkhoff topology
\tau_{B}
is the coarsest group topology making the natural action of
\mathrm{Inn}(G)
on
G
continuous, we get that
\tau_{B}\subseteq\tau_{q}
, which shows that the mapping is continuous. These properties of topological nature were studied also by van Est
[
10
]
.

Example 4.1
.

The following topological groups are all CA-groups:
Compact groups; connected Lie group of dimension
\leq 4
; connected nilpotent Lie groups;
semisimple connected Lie groups.
Moreover, it is useful to note that

(4.3)

\mbox{a connected Lie group}\ G\ \mbox{is CA-group}\Longleftrightarrow\ \ \mathrm{Inn}(G)\simeq G/Z(G).

Remark 4.2
.

If we have a locally compact group
G
such that the condition

is satisfied, then Theorem

can be interpreted in the following way as well:

(4.4)

\mbox{if}\ \mathrm{Inn}(G)\simeq G/Z(G)\ \mbox{is small enough}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \mbox{then so is}\ \overline{[G,G]}.

An evidence is offered by the aforementioned CA-groups of van Est, but later on we will see also some examples of Heisenberg groups of the type
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
with

locally compact

-adic ring, in order to support the interpretation of (

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

(i) Let
G
be a locally compact
\mathsf{Z}
-group such that

. From Lemma

(iv) we find that

is a

-group, and in particular
\overline{[G,G]}
is a compact group. Now the question is to check that the compact group
\overline{[G,G]}
belongs to
\mathfrak{E}_{<\infty}
. By Lemma
2.6
, we have that
G\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}\times H
for some

and for some closed (hence locally compact) subgroup

containing an open compact subgroup
K
. Moreover
\overline{[G,G]}\simeq\overline{[H,H]}
, so now we have that
\overline{[H,H]}
is a compact group in a locally compact group

.

Case (a)
Assume that

. We apply Lemma
3.15
(i) to the group

with
N=\overline{[H,H]}
. Note that
\overline{[H,H]}
is indeed a closed fully invariant subgroup of

\overline{[H,H]}

\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\left.\varphi\right\rvert_{\overline{[H,H]}})\leq\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi)<\infty
for any
\varphi\in\mathrm{End}(H)
. But this is true for every continuous endomorphism of
\overline{[H,H]}
(not only for every continuous endomorphism of

), hence this is sufficient for
\overline{[H,H]}=\overline{[G,G]}\in\mathfrak{E}_{\infty}
and Case (a) is complete.

Case (b)
Assume that
H\not\in\mathfrak{E}_{<\infty}
. If
H
is abelian, then
\overline{[H,H]}=\overline{[G,G]}=1
and the result follows easily. Therefore we must suppose that
H
is a nonabelian nondiscrete group. We must note from Lemma

that in this situation

G=HC_{G}(H),\ \mbox{where}\ C_{G}(H)=\{g\in G\mid gh=hg\ \ \forall h\in H\}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times Z(H)=Z(G)

is the centralizer of

in
G
. Since
H
is normal in
G
, we have that

is normal in

\overline{[H,H]}=\overline{[HZ(G),HZ(G)]}=\overline{[HZ(G)/Z(G),HZ(G)/Z(G)]}=\overline{[G/Z(G),G/Z(G)]}

can be seen as a fully invariant closed subgroup

. Now we apply Lemma

(i) with

\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\left.\varphi\right\rvert_{\overline{[H,H]}})\leq\mathsf{h}_{\mathsf{top}}(\varphi)<\infty
for every continuous endomorphism of

, hence
\overline{[H,H]}\in\mathfrak{E}_{\infty}

(ii) The same argument of (i) above can be repeated, replacing

with

.

where
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),

\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n}),

\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)\in R^{n}
,
\mathbf{b}^{\top}
is the transpose of
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{0}^{\top}
the transpose of
\mathbf{0}
and
c\in R
. We topologize the set

(5.2)

\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)=\{M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},c)\mid a_{i},b_{j},c\in R;\ i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\}

with the induced topology, which is obtained by the product topology of
(n+2)^{2}
copies of
R
(i.e.: the product topology of
R^{(n+2)^{2}}
).

Remark 5.2
.

The set
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
has the structure of nilpotent group. The product of two matrices in
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
is given by

(5.3)

M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},c)\ M(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},w)=M(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{v},\,c+w+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{v}^{\top}),

where
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{v}^{\top}=a_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}v_{n}
according to the ring operations and this sum originates from a bilinear form over
R
. In particular, one can note from the
(1,n+2)
entry
c+w+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{v}^{\top}
that the Heisenberg group is nonabelian. Indeed, the centre of the Heisenberg group is

(5.4)

Z\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right)=\{M(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},c),\,c\in R\}\simeq R.

The inverse of a matrix in
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
is given by

(5.5)

M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},c)^{-1}=M(-\mathbf{a},-\mathbf{b},-c+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{\top}).

The commutator between two matrices in
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
is

(5.6)

\left[M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},c),M(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},w)\right]=M(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{a}\mathbf{v}^{\top}-\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{b}),

therefore the derived subgroup is

(5.7)

\left[\mathbb{H}_{n}(R),\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right]=Z\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right),

and this turns out to be always a closed subgroup in
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
. Thus, the abelianized group of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
(i.e: the largest abelian quotient of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
) is given by

(5.8)

\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)/\left[\mathbb{H}_{n}(R),\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right]\simeq R^{2n}.

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
is nilpotent of nilpotency class
2
, because
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)/Z\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right)
is abelian by (
5.7
). For a prime
p
, the
p
-th power of a matrix in a Heisenberg group is

(5.9)

M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},c)^{p}=M\left(p\mathbf{a},p\mathbf{b},pc+\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}k\Big)\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\right),

as can be readily checked by induction.
If
R
is topologically finitely generated, then a set of topological generators of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
is given by

(5.10)

\left\{M(r\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{0},0),\ M(\mathbf{0},r\mathbf{e}_{k},0)\mid r\ \mbox{is a topological generator of }R\right\}_{k=1,\ldots,n},

where
\mathbf{e}_{k}=(0,\dots 0,1,0,\dots,0)
is the sequence of all zeros except from a
1
in the
k
-th position.

Now,
E=\{M(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{0},0),\,\mathbf{a}\in R^{n}\}\simeq R^{n}
and
F=\{M(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{b},0),\,\mathbf{b}\in R^{n}\}\simeq R^{n}
are abelian subgroups of
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)
with trivial intersection, and we also find that
H_{1}=Z\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right)\times E\simeq R^{n+1}
and
H_{2}=Z\left(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right)\times F\simeq R^{n+1}
are maximal abelian subgroups such that
\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\simeq H_{1}\rtimes F\simeq H_{2}\rtimes E.

Note also from (
5.6
) that

(5.11)

\overline{\left[\mathbb{H}_{n}(R),\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)\right]}=\overline{\left[E,F\right]}=Z(\mathbb{H}_{n}(R)).

Remark 5.5
.

For

, for every

be a locally compact
p
-adic ring with unity as per Corollary

. Consider the direct product
R=\prod_{k=1}^{N}R_{k}
, which is a new locally compact
p
-adic ring with respect to the product topology. Consider the general linear group
\mathrm{Gl}(n,R)
of
n\times n
matrices over

, which is a locally compact group with the induced topology by
R^{n^{2}}
, see
[
14
, Corollary 1.5]
. Then consider also
\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{Gl}(n,R_{k})
, which is another locally compact group (again with respect to the product topology). In this situation the following map is an isomorphism of locally compact groups:

(5.12)

\phi\colon\mathrm{Gl}\left(n,\ \prod_{k=1}^{N}R_{k}\right)\rightarrow\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{Gl}(n,R_{k}),\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}(A_{ij,k})_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\mapsto\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}.

Indeed for every
A,B\in\mathrm{Gl}\left(n,\ \prod_{k=1}^{N}R_{k}\right)
, we have

(5.13)

\phi(A,B)=\phi\left(\begin{pmatrix}(A_{ij,k})_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}(B_{ij,k})_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)\phi\left(\begin{pmatrix}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{n}(A_{i\ell,k})_{k}(B_{\ell j,k})_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)

=\phi\left(\begin{pmatrix}\left(\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{n}A_{i\ell,k}B_{\ell j,k}\right)_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{n}A_{i\ell,k}B_{\ell j,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}

=\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}B_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}=\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}\left(\begin{pmatrix}B_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}=\phi(A)\phi(B).

Also,
\phi
is invertible, as
\phi^{-1}\colon\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{ij,k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}\right)_{k}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}(A_{ij,k})_{k}\end{pmatrix}_{ij}

. Finally,

and

are continuous with respect to the usual topologies considered on

The intersection of all open subgroups plays a fundamental role in profinite group theory, see
[
12
,
25
,
26
]
. The following notion is well known for profinite groups (see
[
25
]
), but less for periodic locally compact groups.

Definition 5.6
.

If
G
is a periodic locally compact
p
-group of finite
p
-rank, an element
g\in G
is a
nongenerator
of
G
if
G=\overline{\langle X,g\rangle}
implies
G=\overline{\langle X\rangle}
for each generating set
X\subseteq G
. The
Frattini subgroup

\mathrm{Frat}(G)
of
G
is the intersection of all maximal open subgroups of
G
.

A nongenerator of a periodic locally compact group is an element which can be removed from a set of generators, since it does not belong to the minimal number of generators which are necessary to generate topologically the group. From
[
25
, Lemma 2.8.1]
, a profinite group
G
has always
\mathrm{Frat}(G)
which coincides with the set of all nongenerators of
G
. But there are also other classes of groups with a similar characterization of their Frattini group:

Lemma 5.7
.

If
G
is a periodic locally compact
p
-group of finite
p
-rank, then
\mathrm{Frat}(G)
coincides with the set
S
of all nongenerators of
G
.

Proof.

First of all one can see that periodic locally compact
p
-groups of finite
p
-rank have maximal open subgroups, see
[
12
]
. Now if
g\in S
and
H
is maximal open in
G
but
g\not\in H
, then
G=\overline{\langle H,g\rangle}
and
G\neq H
. This is a contradiction with
g
nongenerator. Thus there is no such maximal subgroup
H
, hence
g\in\mathrm{Frat}(G)
. We conclude the first inclusion
S\subseteq\mathrm{Frat}(G)
. Conversely, if
g\in\mathrm{Frat}(G)
and
g\not\in S
, then there would be some subset
X
of
G
such that
G=\overline{\langle X,g\rangle}
and
G\neq\overline{\langle X\rangle}
. On the other hand,
\overline{\langle X,g\rangle}=\overline{\langle\overline{\langle X\rangle},g\rangle}
and we may consider
\overline{\langle X\rangle}
as the intersection of all open subgroups of
G
containing
\overline{\langle X\rangle}
. Therefore there is some open subgroup
H
of
G
which is maximal with respect to the property of containing
\overline{\langle X\rangle}
but not
g
. If
K
is another open subgroup of
G
containing
H
, then
K\supseteq\langle X,g\rangle
and so
K=G
, but
g\not\in H
would imply
g\not\in\mathrm{Frat}(G)
, which is a contradiction. Therefore
g\in S
as claimed.
∎
