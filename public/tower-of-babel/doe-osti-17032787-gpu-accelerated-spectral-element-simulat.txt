 Summary

The objective of the present study is to validate the high-order continuous Galerkin, spectrally stabi-
lized level-set method of Saini et al. [1], as implemented in the GPU-accelerated NekRS framework,
for turbulent two-phase flows involving sustained interface deformation, breakup, fragmentation,
and coalescence. Previous validation of the method focused primarily on canonical two-phase prob-
lems in quiescent or weakly turbulent environments, while its performance under fully developed
turbulence and repeated interface topology changes had not been established. Unlike conventional
interface-resolved approaches based predominantly on finite-volume or discontinuous-Galerkin dis-
cretizations, the present formulation enables two-phase flow simulation using a high-order contin-
uous Galerkin spectral element method on modern GPU-based computing systems.

 A statistically stationary single-phase homogeneous isotropic turbulent precursor is first gen-
erated using constant-TKE forcing before an initially spherical dispersed phase is introduced into
the developed flow. The two-phase system is evolved under carrier-phase-specific forcing until the
interfacial and turbulence statistics reach a statistically stationary state, after which the forcing is
removed to examine turbulence decay and interface coalescence.

 The forcing controller maintains a carrier-phase TKE close to unity and a mean Taylor-
microscale Reynolds number of approximately 86.5, in close agreement with the target value of
Reλ,t = 87. The method robustly captures extensive interface deformation and fragmentation
while maintaining the relative dispersed phase volume error below 1% throughout the long-time
simulations. The mixture TKE spectrum retains the expected energy-containing and inertial-range
behavior, while the phase-specific spectra show good agreement with the trends reported in avail-
able reference data. Following removal of the forcing, the method captures the coupled decay of
turbulence, reduction of surface energy, and progressive coalescence of the dispersed phase. These
results demonstrate that the continuous Galerkin two-phase formulation in NekRS can accurately
and efficiently represent turbulent multiphase flows with complex interface topology. The imple-
mentation of this formulation in the hardware-agnostic, GPU-capable NekRS framework [2] enables
large-scale two-phase simulations on leadership-class supercomputing systems available at national
laboratories. Thus, previously intractable interface-resolved turbulent multiphase problems are
brought within practical computational reach.

1 Introduction

The computation of multi-phase flows poses several numerical challenges, including, but not limited
to the persistence of Gibbs oscillations [4] in the vicinity of material interface, where sharp property
transitions preclude the solution using a high-order basis. Multi-phase flow computations have
been historically limited to low-order, finite volume [5] or finite element [6, 7, 8], methods or
discontinuous Galerkin methods [9, 10] where the explicit control over fluxes and upwinding allows
for the positivity and monotinicity of the solution to be imposed. However, the recent foundational
method of Saini et al [1] enables the computation of incompressible two-phase flows using continuous
Galerkin high-order spectral element method. The initial implementation of their approach was
made available in the NEAMS code Nek5000 [11, 12]. However, recently the method has also been
implemented in the hardware agnostic, GPU capable, counterpart of the code NekRS [2], which
now enables the unprecedented computation of two-phase flows on leading supercomputing systems
available at national laboratories. Thus, the solution of hitherto intractable large-scale multi-phase
problems is brought under achievable purview.

 Saini et al [1] validated their numerical method using an array of two-phase problems involving
high density / viscosity ratios including the canonical Rayleigh-Taylor instability problem, free-
surface dam break problem and bubble-rising problems in quiescent carrier fluid undergoing complex
deformation. However, the performance of the method in complex turbulent environment, with the
interface undergoing breakup, fragmentation and coalescence was not yet explored. The purpose of
this report is to bridge that validation gap and demonstrate the application of the high-fidelity two-
phase implementation in NekRS on the quintessential homogeneous isotropic two-phase turbulence
problem.

 The present study considers homogeneous isotropic turbulence in a triply periodic cubic domain.
A single-phase turbulent precursor is first generated and evolved under constant-TKE forcing until a
statistically stationary state is established. An initially spherical dispersed phase is then introduced
into this developed turbulent field and subjected to carrier-phase-specific forcing. The resulting
configuration provides a demanding validation problem in which the interface undergoes sustained
deformation, breakup, fragmentation, and coalescence while interacting with a broad range of
turbulent scales.

 The primary validation case considered in this report corresponds to a unity density and viscosity
ratio at a target Taylor-microscale Reynolds number of Reλ,t = 87 and Weber number W e = 12.5.
The results are compared, where possible, with the forced two-phase HIT calculations of Jain et al. [3].
The analysis considers the evolution of turbulent kinetic energy, viscous dissipation, Taylor-microscale
Reynolds number, interfacial area, surface energy, and dispersed-phase volume conservation. Mixture
and phase-specific energy spectra are also examined to evaluate the distribution of turbulent kinetic
energy across the resolved scales. Finally, the forcing is removed from the statistically stationary
two-phase state to investigate the decay of turbulence and the associated coalescence and relaxation
of the dispersed-phase interface.

 The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the numerical
formulation employed for interface transport, stabilization, surface tension, and variable-property
incompressible flow. Section 3 describes the two-phase HIT configuration, forcing procedure,

nondimensional parameters, and initialization of the turbulent precursor. Section 4 presents the
single-phase precursor statistics, the statistically stationary forced two-phase results, comparisons
with available reference data, and the subsequent turbulence-decay calculations.

2.4 Pressure Correction: Splitting Approach

The standard approach for solving the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the Nek5000 code
is the projection or the pressure correction method [19], introduced in the spectral element context
by Tomboulides et al. [20], assuming constant properties. For spatially varying properties, however,
a variable density coefficient appears in the pressure Poisson equation. For two-phase flows with
sharp property transitions, the variable coefficient dramatically increases the number of iterations
required by multigrid solvers [21] for convergence, which is the standard class of Poisson solvers
in Nek5000/NekRS. There are several methods available in literature to overcome this limitation,
with the common notion of splitting the variable coefficient pressure gradient term into constant
and variable components [22, 23, 24]. The resulting variable term is then treated explicitly in the
Poisson equation, while the constant term is retained as the coefficient of pressure gradient. In the
NekRS implementation, we adopt the coefficient splitting approach by Dodd et al. [24] applied to
the spectral element method framework and integrated with existing time integration schemes as
described in Tomboulides et al. [20].

3 Problem Description: Two-Phase HIT

The validation study presented in this report follows the two-phase homogeneous isotropic turbulence
(HIT) configuration of Jain and Elnahhas [3]. Two-phase HIT provides a canonical configuration for
studying the fundamental physics governing the deformation, breakup, and coalescence of bubbles
and droplets in a turbulent flow.

 The simulations are performed in the triply periodic domain Ω = [−π, π]3 using 363 uniform
spectral elements of polynomial degree 7. This discretization provides approximately 2523 unique
grid intervals and is therefore comparable in resolution to the 2563 finite-volume mesh employed by
Jain and Elnahhas [3]. Single-phase simulation is first performed, allowing the turbulence in the
domain to develop to a quasi-steady state under the effect of constant TKE forcing. Subsequently, a
dispersed phase sphere of diameter π is introduced in the fully developed instantaneous single-phase
field. The two-phase flow is then allowed to develop to quasi-steady state before collecting the
statistics for validation. As shown by Jain et al [3], while the choice of two-phase initialization
may alter the dynamics of interface in the initial few eddy turnover times, the behavior for all
initialization methods eventually produces consistent quasi-steady conditions. Informed by this
finding, the simplest approach of initializing a sphere is adopted in this study without adjusting the
velocity in the domain to compensate for introduction of another phase or capillary forces.

canonical turbulence signature of an approximate κ−5/3 region in the inertial range, 5 < κ < 25 and

4.1.2 Two-phase Results

We analyze two forcing regions of two-phase HIT. First, commencing from the single-phase solution,
the dispersed phase sphere is initialized and then allowed to develop under the action of constant
carrier phase TKE forcing, as described in Section 3. The development phase is marked by a
rapid increase in surface energy, fueled by the TKE of the mixture. The surface energy eventually
asymptotes, characterizing a phase with a statistically stationary balance of external forcing, mixture
TKE, surface energy and dissipation. The volume-averaged quantities are reported for the surface

 17

energy growth region and turbulence statistics are reported for the asymptotic region.

 Subsequently, the decay of turbulence is studied by switching off external forcing from a
statistically stationary state. The evolution of relevant volume averaged quantities are reported for
this region.

4.1.2.1 Constant TKE forcing region

 Figure 4.3 shows the evolution of the interface of the initially spherical dispersed phase after
it is introduced into the fully developed single-phase turbulent velocity field. The corresponding
increases in surface energy and interfacial area are reported in Figure 4.4. As discussed in Section 3.1,
the Weber number, W e = 12.5, is selected such that the initial surface energy is congruent with the
value reported by Jain et al. [3], as is evident in Figure 4.4.

 The initial growth trend predicted by NekRS is consistent with that reported by Jain et
al. [3]. The time required for the surface energy to approach a statistically stationary range is
also comparable, with both results reaching this regime at approximately t/te ≈ 10. The present
simulation, however, predicts a lower asymptotic surface energy. For t/te > 10, the mean surface
energy is se ≈ 0.077 compared with an approximate value of 0.093 extracted from the results of
Jain et al. [3]. The corresponding mean interfacial area is approximately 238.5, representing an
increase by a factor of approximately 7.68 relative to the initial area.

 The lower asymptotic surface energy is most likely associated with the difference between the
effective surface-tension coefficient employed here and that implied by the Weber number reported
by Jain et al. As discussed in Section 3.1, direct use of their reported integral-scale Weber number
does not reproduce their reported initial surface energy under the present nondimensionalization.
The value W e = 12.5 was therefore selected by matching the initial surface energy rather than
by directly adopting their nominal Weber number. Although this procedure provides a consistent
initial condition, the two simulations may not have identical effective capillary strengths throughout
their evolution. Since interfacial deformation and breakup can depend nonlinearly on the Weber
number, even a moderate difference in effective surface tension can produce a noticeable difference
in the statistically stationary interfacial area and surface energy.

 The evolution of the relative dispersed-phase volume error is shown in Figure 4.5. The magnitude
of the relative error remains below approximately 0.7% over the entire simulation interval considered,
confirming excellent long-time global conservation of the dispersed-phase volume. Furthermore, this
volume error is substantially smaller than the approximately 17% difference between the asymptotic
surface energies predicted by NekRS and Jain et al. The discrepancy in surface energy is therefore
unlikely to be explained by the global volume drift and is primarily attributable to differences in
Weber number specification, as mentioned earlier.

 The evolution of the mixture and phase-specific TKE, Taylor-microscale Reynolds number, and
volume-averaged dissipation is shown in Figure 4.6. In the statistically stationary interval t/te > 10,
the carrier-phase TKE has the mean value kc ≈ 0.999 indicating that the forcing controller maintains
the prescribed carrier-phase TKE of unity. The corresponding mean carrier-phase Taylor-microscale
Reynolds number is Reλ,c ≈ 86.5 which is close to the target value Reλ,t = 87. Both quantities

 18

therefore confirm that the carrier turbulence remains close to the intended statistically stationary
state after introduction of the dispersed phase.

 The dispersed-phase TKE undergoes an initial decline as the initially spherical interface adjusts
to the surrounding turbulence and kinetic energy is exchanged with the interface. It subsequently
recovers and approaches a statistically stationary mean value of kd ≈ 0.890. The corresponding mean
mixture TKE is kmix ≈ 0.992 The sum of mixture TKE and surface energy remains statistically
stationary, with kmix + se ≈ 1.069, indicating that the increase in interfacial energy does not produce
a continuing drift in the total stored kinetic and surface energies once the statistically stationary
regime is reached.

 The phase-averaged dissipation in the dispersed phase is higher than that in the carrier phase.
For t/te > 10, the corresponding mean values are

                                     εd ≈ 0.671,        εc ≈ 0.464.                               (97)

Thus, the dispersed-phase dissipation is approximately 45% greater than the carrier-phase dissipation.
This behavior is consistent with the observations of Jain et al. [3]. Because the dispersed phase is
bounded by the interface, it cannot readily accommodate the largest energy-containing eddies. Its
motion consequently contains a relatively greater contribution from smaller-scale velocity gradients,
resulting in greater viscous dissipation relative to its kinetic energy.

 The mean mixture dissipation predicted by NekRS is εmix ≈ 0.477, whereas Jain et al. report
a value of approximately 0.437. The NekRS result is therefore approximately 9.2% higher. The
mixture dissipation exhibits low-frequency fluctuations about its mean, as expected for a finite
periodic realization of forced turbulence, but no persistent long-time drift is observed. The remaining
difference may arise from differences in the effective Reynolds number, forcing implementation,
numerical dissipation, spatial resolution, and interfacial regularization employed in the two numerical
methods. In contrast, the discrepancy in surface energy is more directly associated with differences
in effective capillary strength and the Weber-number convention discussed in Section 3.1.

 Figure 4.7 shows the comparison of mixture TKE spectra for the single and two-phase simulations
and the comparison of phase-specific TKE spectra, also compared with trend extracted from Jain et
al [3]. Note that the two-phase data to generate these spectra are obtained from the statistically
stationary state, specifically data collected from t/te > 10.259. The single and two-phase mixture
spectra exhibit excellent agreement throughout the energy-containing and inertial ranges, indicating
that the presence of the dispersed phase does not significantly alter the large-scale turbulent
motions or the inertial transfer of energy. Noticeable differences are primarily confined to the
higher-wavenumber dissipation range. This behavior is consistent with interfacial deformation acting
predominantly on the smaller turbulent scales, where part of the kinetic energy is transferred to
and stored as surface energy instead of being dissipated.

 The carrier and dispersed-phase spectra also show good agreement with the trends reported
by Jain et al. [3], both in their overall magnitudes and their variation with wavenumber. In
particular, the simulations reproduce the similar phase-specific energy levels at low wavenumbers
and the increasing separation between the carrier and dispersed spectra at higher wavenumbers.
The comparatively lower dispersed-phase energy at these scales is consistent with the restriction of

 19

turbulent motions by the finite size of the dispersed structures. Overall, the agreement indicates
that the present formulation captures the phase-wise distribution of turbulent kinetic energy across
the resolved range of scales.

4.1.2.2 Decay Region: Forcing TKE Off

 The evolution of volume averaged quantities is analyzed by turning off the constant TKE
forcing term from a statistically stationary state at t/te ≈ 10.259. Figure 4.8 shows the evolution of
interface in the decay time region. In the absence of externally forced TKE, the highly fragmented
interfaces start to coalesce causing a rapid initial reduction in surface energy. The evolution of
mixture and individual phase TKE is shown in Figure 4.9. The TKE decay trend curves overlap
indicating that the TKE is distributed consistently in both phase during the decay phase. On very
long time-integration, t/te → 50 the dispersed phase reduces into a few fragmented closed volumes
with the corresponding surface energy seen to asymptote towards its original value of se ≈ 0.01.
It is conjectured that further extending the time integration will result in the original spherical
shape being recovered given that the disparate dispersed volumes drift in close enough proximity for
capillary forces to take over and cause further coalescence. The phase specific Reλ and dissipation
plots are shown in Figure 4.10. Since the initial eddies are smaller in the dispersed phase, restricted
by the interface, the dissipation in dispersed phase remains higher upto t < te ≈ 20. Finally, the
relative volume error for entire growth and decay region is shown in Figure 4.11. The maximum
reported error through the long-time integration of decay period is ∼ 0.5%.

4.2 Single-phase Studies: Reλ = 60 and 138

The report also documents higher-resolution single-phase HIT studies intended as precursors to
future two-phase simulations at larger Reynolds numbers. Two simulations were performed in the
same triply periodic geometry using 483 and 723 spectral elements with polynomial order N = 8.
The instantaneous Q-criterion isosurfaces are shown in Figure 4.12, with the structures colored by
the streamwise velocity. The 723 element case contains a substantially denser population of fine-scale
vortical structures, consistent with its larger Reynolds number and wider range of dynamically
active turbulent scales.

 The temporal evolution of the integral-scale and Taylor-microscale Reynolds numbers is shown
in Figure 4.13. The simulations produce mean Taylor-microscale Reynolds numbers of approximately
65 and 155 for the 483 and 723 element cases, respectively. Although the instantaneous Reynolds
numbers exhibit the low-frequency fluctuations expected in forced HIT within a finite periodic domain,
they fluctuate about stable long-time mean values without a persistent drift. These simulations
demonstrate the ability of NekRS to sustain more highly resolved single-phase turbulence on GPU-
based systems and provide developed precursor fields for future two-phase studies at larger Reynolds
numbers.

5 Conclusions and Future Work

This work demonstrates the simulation of forced and decaying two-phase homogeneous isotropic tur-
bulence using the high-order continuous Galerkin spectral element formulation in NekRS performed
on GPU-based computing system. The method robustly captures sustained interface deformation,
breakup, fragmentation, and coalescence while maintaining the relative volume error below 0.7%
over long time integration. The carrier-phase forcing maintains the prescribed TKE and target
Taylor-microscale Reynolds number, while the mixture and phase-specific energy spectra show good
agreement with the expected turbulence behavior and the trends reported by Jain et al. [3]. The
results demonstrate that the CG two-phase formulation can accurately represent both large-scale
turbulent dynamics and complex interface evolution, characteristic of two-phase HIT, although
some quantitative differences in surface energy remain due to uncertainty in the Weber number
normalization used in the reference simulations.

 Future work should focus on further validation over a broader range of density ratios, viscosity
ratios, Reynolds numbers, and Weber numbers, including air-water-like conditions. Systematic
spatial and temporal convergence studies, together with comparisons of energy budgets, droplet-size
distributions, and phase-conditioned turbulence statistics, are required to establish the accuracy
limits of the method for high density ratio turbulent multiphase flows.
