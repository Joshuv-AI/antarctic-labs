Globally Simple Heffter Arrays H ⁡ ( n , k ) H(n;k) with k ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) k\equiv 1\pmod{4} Thanks: This research was funded by TÜBİTAK, Grant/Award Number: 124F360
Erik Pelttari, Selda Kücükçifçi, E. Şule Yazıcı

Abstract
Heffter arrays are combinatorial structures used to construct orthogonal cyclic cycle decompositions and biembeddings of complete graphs onto surfaces. A Heffter array H ⁡ ( m , n , h , k ) H(m,n;h,k) is an m × n m\times n partially filled array with distinct nonzero entries from ℤ 2 ​ n ​ k + 1 \mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1} such that each row contains h h filled cells, each column contains k k filled cells, the elements in the filled cells form a half-set of ℤ 2 ​ n ​ k + 1 \mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1} , and every row and column sums to zero modulo 2 ​ n ​ k + 1 2nk+1 . If these row and column sums equal zero over the integers, the structure is called an integer Heffter array. Furthermore, such an array is called globally simple if the partial sums of the entries in each row and column, evaluated in their natural order, are distinct modulo 2 ​ n ​ k + 1 2nk+1 . When m = n m=n and h = k h=k , the array is square and denoted by H ⁡ ( n , k ) H(n;k) . While the existence of globally simple square Heffter arrays has been established for several congruence classes, the cases where k ≡ 1 , 2 ( mod 4 ) k\equiv 1,2\pmod{4} for k > 10 k>10 have remained an open problem [ 17 ] . In this work, we address this gap in the literature by explicitly constructing globally simple integer Heffter arrays H ⁡ ( n , k ) H(n;k) for the previously open cases where k ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) k\equiv 1\pmod{4} and n ≡ 0 , 3 ( mod 4 ) n\equiv 0,3\pmod{4} . Consequently, these constructions guarantee the existence of orthogonal cyclic k k -cycle decompositions of the complete graph K 2 ​ n ​ k + 1 K_{2nk+1} for these parameters. Keywords and MSC codes: Heffter arrays, globally simple, compatible orderings, cycle decompositions. 05B30, 05C51

Introduction

1.1
Overview and Definitions

Heffter arrays were first introduced by Archdeacon
[
1
]
in 2015 as a combinatorial tool to construct orthogonal cyclic cycle decompositions and biembeddings of complete graphs onto surfaces. As their name suggests, these structures can be viewed as a two-dimensional generalization of Heffter’s first difference problem, originally posed in 1896
[
19
]
. While the classical difference problem seeks to partition the set
\{1,2,\dots,3m\}
into triples that sum to zero modulo
6m+1
, Heffter arrays extend this concept to a matrix format where the sequence of entries in every row and column must sum to zero modulo
2nk+1
.

Definition 1.1
.

A
Heffter array

H(m,n;h,k)
is an
m\times n
matrix with entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
, where
mh=nk
, such that:

(i)

Each row contains exactly
h
filled cells and each column contains exactly
k
filled cells.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}\setminus\{0\}
, exactly one of
x
or
-x
appears in the array.

(iii)

The sum of the elements in every row and every column is
0\pmod{2nk+1}
.

When the array is square (i.e.,
m=n
and thus
h=k
), it is denoted simply as
H(n;k)
.

Definition 1.2
.

If there are no empty cells, the array is called a
tight Heffter array
and is denoted
H(m,n)
.

Definition 1.3
.

If the elements in every row and column sum to zero over the integers
\mathbb{Z}
(rather than merely modulo
2nk+1
), the array is called an
integer Heffter array
.

Definition 1.4
.

The
support
of a Heffter array is the set of the absolute values of its non-empty entries. By condition (ii) of
1.1
, it follows that the support is precisely the set
\{1,2,\dots,nk\}
.

While Heffter arrays are interesting combinatorial objects in their own right, their utility lies in their applications. Specifically, these arrays, with several additional properties, provide a recipe for decomposing complete graphs and embedding them onto surfaces. The next set of definitions covers these graph-theoretic and topological concepts.

Definition 1.5
.

A cycle decomposition of a graph
G
is a set of cycles, all of the same length, whose union contains every edge of
G
exactly once. Two such decompositions,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, are
orthogonal
if any cycle from
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and any cycle from
\mathcal{C}_{2}
intersect in at most one edge
[
6
]
.

Definition 1.6
.

An ordering of the entries in a row or column is called
simple
if the partial sums of its entries are all distinct modulo
2nk+1
. If such orderings exist for all rows and columns, the Heffter array is said to be simple. Given an arbitrary Heffter array, it can be difficult to determine if a simple ordering actually exists on its rows and columns. Because of this, arrays that are simple as constructed are of particular interest. A Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
is defined to be
globally simple
if all of its rows and columns are simple under their natural left-to-right and top-to-bottom orderings
[
10
]
. If a Heffter array is simple, its rows produce a cyclic decomposition of the complete graph
K_{2nk+1}
into
h
-cycles, the columns produce a cyclic decomposition of
K_{2nk+1}
into
k
-cycles, and these decompositions are orthogonal to each other.

As an illustration, the Heffter array in Example
1.1
is not globally simple. If we evaluate the partial sums of its first row, we obtain the sequence
-1,4,6,-1,-10,
and
0
. Because the partial sum
-1
appears twice, the natural ordering of this row fails the condition for global simplicity.

Definition 1.7
.

The cyclic ordering of the entries in a row or column of a partially filled array (such as a Heffter array) can be viewed as a permutation on the set of entries in filled cells of the array. Let
\omega_{r}
denote the permutation formed by the composition of the row cycles, and let
\omega_{c}
denote the corresponding permutation formed by composition of the column cycles. If the composition
\omega_{r}\circ\omega_{c}
is a single cycle of length equal to the total number of filled cells in the array, we say the row and column orderings,
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
respectively, are
compatible
.

Definition 1.8
.

A cellular embedding of a graph
G
on a surface is called a
biembedding
if its faces are
2
-colorable. In a biembedding, every edge of
G
is on exactly one face of each color. If the faces of the biembedding are simple cycles, the biembedding is said to be simple.

In our context, given a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
, we would like to determine if we can produce orthogonal cycle decompositions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of the complete graph
K_{2nk+1}
such that the cycles in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
have length
h
and the cycles in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
have length
k
. In addition, we would like to determine when we can construct a biembedding of
K_{2nk+1}
such that the cycles in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
correspond to the faces in one color class and the cycles in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
correspond to the faces in the second color class.

This connection between the structure of Heffter arrays and biembeddings was established by Archdeacon:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
1
]

Given a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
with compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
on the rows and
\omega_{c}
on the columns, there exists an orientable embedding of
K_{2nk+1}
such that every edge is on a face of size
h
and a face of size
k
. Moreover, if
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
are both simple, then all faces are simple cycles.

If a Heffter array possesses compatible orderings on the rows and columns but fails to be simple, we still obtain a biembedding on an orientable surface; however, the rows or columns (whichever does not have a simple ordering) will produce circuits instead of cycles, resulting in faces that are not simple. Conversely, if the array is simple but lacks a compatible ordering, its rows and columns still produce orthogonal cyclic decompositions of the complete graph. Topologically, however, the lack of a single full-length permutation cycle causes the structure to pinch at one or more of the vertices, resulting in an embedding on a pseudosurface rather than an orientable surface
[
1
]
.

1.2
Previous results and open cases

The study of Heffter arrays naturally begins with the question of their existence. Square Heffter arrays trivially require
3\leq k\leq n
, and for square integer Heffter arrays, we additionally require
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
. Through the combined efforts of several authors, the existence problem for these arrays was completely solved:

Theorem 1.2
.

[
2
,
7
,
18
]

A square Heffter array
H(n;k)
exists if and only if
3\leq k\leq n
, and a square integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
exists if and only if
3\leq k\leq n
and
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
.

With existence established, the focus shifted toward finding Heffter arrays that also possess the
globally simple
property, as these arrays are guaranteed to yield orthogonal cyclic decompositions of complete graphs. The baseline existence of globally simple arrays for smaller values of
k
was established by Costa, Morini, Pasotti, and Pellegrini:

Theorem 1.3
.

[
10
]

For
3\leq k\leq 10
, a globally simple Heffter array
H(n;k)
exists if and only if
k\leq n
and
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
.

For broader classes where
k\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
, Burrage, Cavenagh, Donovan, and Yazıcı constructed globally simple Heffter arrays under specific conditions:

Theorem 1.4
.

[
5
]

There exist globally simple Heffter arrays
H(n;k)
for
3\leq k\leq n
in the following cases:

1.

k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,

2.

n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, and

3.

n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, provided
n\gg k
.

However, the existence of globally simple Heffter arrays for the cases where
k\equiv 1,2\pmod{4}
and
k>10
has remained an open problem.

Regarding compatible orderings, which imply biembeddings on orientable surfaces, the following theorem states the necessary conditions on a general Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
.

Theorem 1.5
.

[
8
,
16
,
17
]

If there exist compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
for a Heffter array
H(m,n;h,k)
, then either:

1.

m,n,h
and
k
are all odd;

2.

m
is odd,
n
is even and
h
is even; or

3.

m
is even,
n
is odd and
k
is even.

For square integer Heffter arrays, this implies that
nk
is odd. This requirement leaves two primary cases for odd
n
and
k
. In their work on biembeddings of cycle systems, Cavenagh, Donovan, and Yazıcı proved the existence of compatible orderings for
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
with sporadic exceptions
[
8
]
. Their work is a generalization of theorems presented by Costa, Dalai, and Pasotti in “A Tour Problem on a Toroidal Board” which were developed around the same time
[
11
]
.

In the case where
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, several propositions exist when the entries of
H(n;k)
are entirely contained in
k
consecutive diagonals. These are known as
cyclically
k
-diagonal
arrays.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
11
]

Let
3\leq k<200
be an integer and let
H(n;k)
be cyclically
k
-diagonal. Then there exist two compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
if and only if
n
and
k
are both odd.

Proposition 1.2
.

[
10
,
11
]

Let
3\leq k
be an odd integer and let
H(n;k)
be cyclically
k
-diagonal. If
\gcd(n,k-1)=1
, then there exist two compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
.

Proposition 1.3
.

[
11
]

Let
3\leq k
and let
H(n;k)
be cyclically
k
-diagonal of size
n\geq(k-2)(k-1)
. Then there exist two compatible orderings
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{c}
if and only if
n
and
k
are both odd.

The constructions presented in this paper are not cyclically
k
-diagonal. Instead, they have two sets of consecutive diagonals with two sets of empty diagonals, both of which have width
\frac{n-k}{2}
. Since this meets the conditions of the following additional proposition from Costa et al., we know that for an infinite set of
n
and
k
values, the constructed Heffter arrays have compatible orderings.

Proposition 1.4
.

[
11
]

Let
3\leq k<n
be odd integers. Let the entries of
H(n;k)
be entirely contained in
k
diagonals with two sets of empty diagonals, both of which have width
\frac{n-k}{2}
. If
\gcd(n,k-2)=1
, then there exist compatible orderings of the rows and columns of
H(n;k)
.

1.3
Main Results

Since
1.3
gives results for globally simple
H(n;k)
with
3\leq k\leq 10
and
nk\equiv 0,3\pmod{4}
, in this paper, we address the open problems regarding globally simple Heffter arrays for
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
k\geq 13
by stating and proving the following results:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
,
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
n>k\geq 13
, then there exists an integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
that is globally simple.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
n>k\geq 13
, then there exists an integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
that is globally simple.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In
section

2
, we describe the construction of the array
H(n;k)
for
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. In
section

3
, we rigorously prove that this construction yields a globally simple array by demonstrating that the row and column partial sums are distinct modulo
2nk+1
. Finally, in
section

4
and
section

5
, we outline the proof for the
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
globally simple case. Additional calculations can be found in
appendix

A
.

2
H(n;k)
construction for
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}

We construct integer Heffter arrays
H(n;k)
for all
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
n>k\geq 13
. The construction proceeds by initializing a base
H(n;5)

[

, and augmenting with sets of 4 diagonals until

2.1
Diagonal Definition

We define a diagonal of length
\ell
, denoted by
\text{diag}(r,c,v,\delta,\delta_{v},\ell)
, as the set of
\ell
filled cells starting at cell
(r,c)
with value
v
. Subsequent cells are determined by incrementing the row and column indices by
\delta
and the value by
\delta_{v}
. Formally, for
j\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
, the diagonal contains the entry
v+j\delta_{v}
in cell
(r+j\delta,c+j\delta)
. Unless otherwise stated, all row and column indices are taken modulo
n
on the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

H(n;5)
when
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}

Let

and

The following specific entries are set directly:

\displaystyle H[1,1]=n-1,

\displaystyle H[1,2]=5n-2,

2.3
Augmentation with
k-5
New Diagonals

To increase the number of filled cells in each row and column from 5 to
k
, we add diagonals in groups of 4. Let
t=(k-5)/4
. For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,t\}
, we define the following four diagonals:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{1+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(1+2i,1,(n+4ni+1),1,2,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(2+2i,1,(-3n-4ni-1),1,-2,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{h+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(1+2i,h+1,(-n-4ni-2),1,-2,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(2+2i,h+1,(3n+4ni+2),1,2,n)

The support of the added diagonals partitions the range
\{5n+1,\dots,nk\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{1+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{n+4ni+1,n+4ni+3,\dots,3n+4ni-1\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{2+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{3n+4ni+1,3n+4ni+3,\dots,5n+4ni-1\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{h+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{n+4ni+2,n+4ni+4,\dots,3n+4ni\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{3n+4ni+2,3n+4ni+4,\dots,5n+4ni\}

Combined with the base support
\{1,\dots,5n\}
from the
H(n;5)
, the constructed
H(n;k)
has the required support
\{1,2,\dots,nk\}
.

2.4
Row and Column Sums

The rows and columns of the base
H(n;5)
sum to zero. We verify the added diagonals do as well. Consider the contribution of the
i
-th set of diagonals to any fixed row
r
. The row
r
intersects
\mathbf{D_{1+2i}}
at some column
c
with value
v
, and intersects
\mathbf{D_{h+2i}}
at column
c^{\prime}
with value
v^{\prime}
. By the diagonal definitions:

v+v^{\prime}=(n+4ni+1+2(r-1-2i\pmod{n}))+(-n-4ni-2-2(r-1-2i\pmod{n}))=-1.

Similarly, the values from
\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}
sum to
+1
. Thus, the total contribution to row
r
is
-1+1=0
. For any fixed column
c
, the values from
\mathbf{D_{1+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}
sum to
-2n
, while the values from
\mathbf{D_{h+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}
sum to
+2n
. Thus, the total contribution to column
c
is
-2n+2n=0
.

Since the support is correct and every row and column sum is zero over the integers, this construction produces a valid integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
.

Remark
.

According to
1.6
, a row or column is simple if its partial sums are distinct and non-zero modulo
2nk+1
when evaluated in their natural order. However, we have flexibility in how we calculate these sums. First, starting the summation at a different cell merely applies a constant shift to the sequence. Second, computing the sums in the reverse direction (such as right-to-left instead of left-to-right) simply negates the resulting values. Since both shifting by a constant and multiplying by
-1
are bijective operations over
\mathbb{Z}_{2nk+1}
, neither operation affects the distinctness of the elements in the sequence. Therefore, to significantly simplify our calculations in this paper, we choose specific, varying starting cells for each row and column, and we evaluate the rows from right to left. Proving that these modified partial sums are distinct is strictly equivalent to proving global simplicity.

We construct globally simple Heffter arrays

for all

with

. The construction proceeds by initializing a base

is reached.

4.1
The Base Array
H(n;5)
when

Let
h=n/2
and
q=n/4
. The base
H(n;5)
is constructed using the following diagonals and ad hoc entries.

Diagonal Assignments

\displaystyle\text{A: diag}(h+1,h+2,(h-2),1,-1,h-3)

\displaystyle\text{H: diag}(h+2,h+2,-(5n-q-4),2,1,q-1)

\displaystyle\text{M: diag}(2,h+2,(3n-4),1,-2,h-2)

4.2
Augmentation with
k-5
New Diagonals

To increase the number of filled cells in each row and column from 5 to
k
, we add diagonals in groups of 4. Let
t=(k-5)/4
. For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,t\}
, we define the following four diagonals:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(2+2i,1,(-n-4ni-1),1,-4,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{3+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(3+2i,1,(n+4ni+3),1,4,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(2+2i,h+3,(n+4ni+2),1,4,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D_{h+2+2i}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(3+2i,h+3,(-n-4ni-4),1,-4,n)

The support of the added diagonals partition the range
\{5n+1,\dots,nk\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{2+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{5n+1,5n+5,\dots,5n+4nt-3\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{3+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{5n+3,5n+7,\dots,5n+4nt-1\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{5n+2,5n+6,\dots,5n+4nt-2\}

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mathbf{D_{h+2+2i}})

\displaystyle=\{5n+4,5n+8,\dots,5n+4nt\}

Combined with the base support
\{1,\dots,5n\}
from the
H(n;5)
, the constructed
H(n;k)
has the required support
\{1,2,\dots,nk\}
.

4.3
Row and Column Sums

The rows and columns of the base
H(n;5)
sum to zero. We verify the added diagonals do as well. Consider the contribution of the
i
-th set of diagonals to any fixed row
r
. The row
r
intersects
\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}
at some column
c
with value
v
, and intersects
\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}
at column
c^{\prime}
with value
v^{\prime}
. By the diagonal definitions:

v+v^{\prime}=(-n-4ni-1-4(r-2-2i\pmod{n}))+(n+4ni+2+4(r-2-2i\pmod{n}))=1.

Similarly, the values from
\mathbf{D_{3+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{h+2+2i}}
sum to
-1
. Thus, the total contribution to row
r
is
1-1=0
. For any fixed column
c
, the values from
\mathbf{D_{2+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{3+2i}}
sum to
2
, while the values from
\mathbf{D_{h+1+2i}}
and
\mathbf{D_{h+2+2i}}
sum to
-2
. Thus, the total contribution to column
c
is
2-2=0
.

Since the support is correct and every row and column sum is zero, this construction produces a valid integer Heffter array
H(n;k)
.

6
Concluding Remarks and Open Problems

In this paper, we addressed a significant gap in the literature by explicitly constructing globally simple square Heffter arrays
H(n;k)
for the previously open cases where
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. By leveraging a base
H(n;5)
array and systematically augmenting it with sets of four diagonals, we proved that the resulting row and column partial sums are distinct modulo
2nk+1
. Building upon these results, we identify four primary avenues for future research.

The first avenue directly extends our existence results to the two remaining open cases for globally simple square Heffter arrays. Specifically, our next objective is to adapt this framework to establish the existence of globally simple Heffter arrays when
n\equiv 0,2\pmod{4}
and
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.

A second direction for future work concerns the biembeddings of the arrays constructed here. While this paper resolves the globally simple property for the
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
arrays, the question of their biembeddings remains partially open. For the specific sub-cases where
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
with
\gcd(n,k-2)>1
, compatible orderings may still exist; successfully constructing them remains an engaging open problem.

Third, researchers could shift focus from square to rectangular Heffter arrays. It is interesting to note that there are currently relatively few constructions that are both simple and compatible in the rectangular case, and the known results primarily concern arrays with no empty cells. For instance, while there are simple and compatible constructions for all
H(3,n)
, results for
H(5,n)
are currently restricted to
n\leq 100

[
16
]
. Expanding this catalog represents a substantial open challenge.

Finally, a fourth broad avenue encompasses variants and generalizations of Heffter arrays. These include weak Heffter arrays,
\lambda
-fold Heffter arrays, relative Heffter arrays, non-zero sum Heffter arrays, Archdeacon arrays, and Heffter spaces
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
12
,
13
,
9
,
14
,
15
,
20
]
. Exploring connections to decompositions and embeddings within these generalized structures provides a highly fruitful area of inquiry.
