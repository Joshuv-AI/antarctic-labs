Deep feature extraction and explanation consistency analysis for fine-grained aircraft type classification in remote sensing imagery
Authors: Jiachen Liu, Lei Dong, Zijing Sun, Bo He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fine-grained aircraft type recognition in remote sensing imagery is of great value for military intelligence analysis and battlefield situation awareness. However, the limited transparency of deep learning decision-making processes restricts its application in high-stakes decision-making scenarios. To address this issue, this paper proposes an explainable aircraft type classification framework, termed EC-AT, which integrates transfer learning, explanation consistency analysis, and trustworthiness evaluation. Specifically, eight representative aircraft categories are selected from the MAR20 dataset to evaluate multiple deep neural network models under two settings: transfer-learning fine-tuning and random initialization. By leveraging pretrained model parameters and fine-tuning the classification layers, the proposed framework effectively improves feature representation capability and convergence efficiency under limited and class-imbalanced data conditions. To further interpret model decisions, three representative explainable artificial intelligence methods, namely Grad-CAM, LIME, and RISE, are introduced to analyze discriminative image regions from complementary perspectives, including class-discriminative activation, local perturbation-based approximation, and randomized input sampling. In addition, an IoMin-based spatial overlap metric is designed to quantitatively assess the consistency among regions generated by different explanation methods. Beyond explanation consistency analysis, the proposed framework is further evaluated using the Multisource AI Scorecard Table (MAST), which provides a structured assessment of model trustworthiness from multiple dimensions, including accuracy, consistency, logical argumentation, and visualization. Experimental results show that the transfer-learning-based models achieve excellent classification performance, with classification accuracy reaching up to 99.9%. The explainability analysis demonstrates that the proposed framework can identify key aircraft-related regions while revealing potential background interference and decision biases. Furthermore, the explanation consistency analysis and MAST-based evaluation jointly provide objective evidence for explanation results and the overall trustworthiness of the framework. Overall, the proposed EC-AT framework significantly improves the classification performance and explainability of fine-grained aircraft recognition in remote sensing imagery, offering a useful reference for trustworthy intelligent remote sensing analysis.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of modern warfare transitioning from mechanization toward informationization and intelligentization, the capability to collect, process, and analyze battlefield intelligence has become a central determinant of operational success1. As key platforms for achieving air superiority and strategic projection, aircraft—when rapidly and accurately identified in remote sensing imagery—can reveal force dispositions and combat intentions and thereby provide a foundation for tactical adjustments and threat early warning2,3. For example, Ukraine’s “Spider Web” initiative leveraged remote sensing imagery to determine the deployment locations and patterns of Russian strategic bombers such as the Tu-95MS and Tu-22M3, thereby substantially contributing to the success of subsequent operations. Consequently, the rapid and precise identification of aircraft types and states from massive, multi-source remote sensing data has emerged as both a research focus and an application frontier in contemporary military intelligence analysis4–6.

Conventional remote sensing image analysis methods primarily rely on low- and mid-level visual cues—such as local shape, texture, edges, and statistical encodings7—and achieve acceptable performance only in scenarios with simple backgrounds and low noise. Their feature-design processes are often laborious, and their generalization capability is limited, rendering them inadequate for military applications that demand both high accuracy and rapid responsiveness. In recent years, deep learning approaches, represented by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have achieved transformative progress, fundamentally reshaping the field of computer vision8. CNN-based object detection frameworks extract deep image representations via a feature-extraction backbone and then localize and classify targets through regression and classification heads based on these features and their spatial arrangements. A wide range of CNN architectures have demonstrated strong feature-extraction capabilities9 and have served as backbones for single-stage detectors (e.g., YOLO10 and SSD11) and two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN12 and Mask R-CNN13), which are extensively used for aircraft detection and classification in remote sensing imagery14,15. For instance, Ghazouali et al.16 benchmarked seven mainstream object detection algorithms for aircraft detection in remote sensing images and found YOLOv5 to offer outstanding accuracy and robustness across diverse imaging conditions. CHEN et al.17 designed a novel feature-extraction module, C2f_DEF, for YOLOv8n and optimized its loss function, significantly improving detection accuracy in dense and complex scenes while preserving lightweight and real-time performance. ZHANG et al.18 proposed a Faster R-CNN–based method for detecting small aircraft in remote sensing images, in which K-means was used to optimize anchor boxes and the backbone was refined, yielding an mAP improvement of roughly 3% over existing approaches without sacrificing inference speed.

Beyond CNNs, Transformer-based architectures have rapidly advanced as an alternative deep-learning paradigm19. Motivated by the Transformer’s success in natural-language processing, the Vision Transformer (ViT) introduced self-attention into image feature processing, outperforming many CNNs on several benchmark image-classification tasks. A stream of hybrid architectures has since emerged that combines the locality and efficiency of convolution with the global-dependency modeling of self-attention, enabling multi-scale and multi-view aggregation of local semantic features and further extending the capabilities of intelligent remote-sensing image analysis20.

Despite these advances, both CNNs and ViT suffer from complex internal architectures and opaque decision-making processes, making their internal reasoning difficult to explain. The “black-box” nature of deep learning therefore poses a significant barrier to deployment in critical domains such as military operations, environmental monitoring, and healthcare21. To address this, researchers have begun embedding explainability into aircraft recognition and classification. For example, CHEN et al.22 proposed an interpretable recognition model that incorporates aircraft-part parsing priors, decomposing an aircraft into five key components (e.g., nose, wings, fuselage) and employing a knowledge-driven attention module to concentrate on the most discriminative parts; this approach both improves recognition accuracy and provides rationales for decisions. GUO et al.23 developed an interpretable Swin Transformer–based classification framework that uses dynamic attention fusion, adaptive image-pair interaction, and a binary neural-tree module to mitigate small inter-class differences and large intra-class variations while constructing explicit feature pathways that enhance the transparency of fine-grained aircraft classification decisions.

Meanwhile, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has also attracted significant attention from military institutions and international organizations. For example, the U.S. Department of Defense’s Guidebook for Developmental Test and Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Systems24, the International Organization for Standardization’s Information Technology—Artificial Intelligence—Guidance on Risk Management25, and the SAE International standard under development, Development and Assurance Guidelines for Aeronautical Systems and Equipment Implemented with Machine Learning26, all emphasize the necessity of conducting explainability analysis during the development of intelligent systems and recommend a variety of explainable methods.

At present, a large number of explainability methods have emerged in the field of explainability. According to the principle by which explanations are generated, these methods can be broadly divided into two categories, namely model-specific explainability methods and model-agnostic explainability methods. Model-specific explainability methods regard deep learning models as white-box systems and generate explanations of the decision-making behavior by exploiting internal architectural information, neuron parameters, or other hyperparameters of the model. Common examples include LRP27, CAM28, and DeepLIFT29. In contrast, model-agnostic explainability methods do not depend on any specific model structure. They treat the target model as a black box and explain its decision behavior by establishing certain correlations between inputs and outputs, without using any internal model information. Such methods usually provide post hoc explanations and can be used as tools to detect and improve anomalous behaviors or incorrect predictions. Representative methods include, LIME30, RISE31, and SHAP32.

Related work

Deep learning for fine-grained classification in remote sensing images

Deep learning-based remote sensing image classification has become increasingly mature in recent years33–37. In contrast, fine-grained classification focuses specifically on dividing objects into more detailed subcategories. It is typically performed on pre-cropped images containing only the target object, such as an aircraft, without involving any object detection process38,39. Compared with general classification tasks, which aim to distinguish among broad categories such as aircraft, ships, and vehicles, fine-grained classification seeks to discriminate between visually similar subcategories within the same superordinate class, such as different aircraft models. This makes the task substantially more challenging, since inter-class differences are often extremely subtle, while intra-class variations can be considerable.

In recent years, research on fine-grained classification in remote sensing imagery has increasingly shifted toward deep learning methods, particularly CNNs and ViTs, which have shown strong capability in extracting discriminative features. ZHANG et al.40 proposed a ViT-based model that improves fine-grained classification performance through attention patch combination, critical region filtering, and complementary token integration. WANG et al.41 introduced a hybrid CNN-ViT architecture, P2FEViT, which enhances fine-grained remote sensing image classification by integrating local and global feature representations.

For SAR imagery, Xu and LANG42 proposed a fine-grained classification framework for SAR ship images. To address the low signal-to-noise ratio and complex background of SAR imagery, they introduced a metric learning method combined with distribution shift regularization, which explicitly improves inter-class separability. Built upon manifold preservation and pairwise constraints, their method further incorporates distribution shift regularization to enhance the robustness of feature learning. Nevertheless, due to the inherent difficulty of feature extraction in SAR images, the classification accuracy of this model in fine-grained scenarios remains limited.

By comparison, optical imagery, with its higher spatial resolution and clearer visual characteristics, has been more widely studied for fine-grained classification. HAN et al. 43 proposed an Efficient Information Reuse Network (EIRNet), which combines feature fusion and attention mechanisms. Through a bidirectional fusion strategy, the network maximizes the utilization of multi-level information while reducing redundancy, thereby strengthening the extraction of detailed features and improving fine-grained ship classification accuracy. CHEN et al.44 proposed a Push-Pull Network (PPNet) for fine-grained ship classification. This network adopts an asynchronous contrastive learning strategy, in which pushing apart is performed before pulling together, so as to avoid the conflict caused by conducting both processes simultaneously. Their method achieved state-of-the-art performance on two public optical remote sensing ship image datasets, demonstrating its effectiveness for fine-grained ship classification.

In the domain of fine-grained aircraft recognition, CHEN et al.45 proposed APPEAR, a knowledge-driven deep learning framework based on a part parsing prior. This method employs a CNN-based segmentation and classification network to explicitly model the rigid structure of aircraft through pixel-level part parsing. Such a fine-grained prior provides reliable part localization for describing aircraft structure and imposes spatial constraints among different parts, thereby effectively reducing the search space for model optimization and enabling the identification of subtle inter-class differences. In addition, NIE et al.46 proposed a classifier-adaptive earth mover’s distance method, Adap-EMD, for few-shot fine-grained aircraft recognition. Adap-EMD consists of an Efficient Block Attention Mechanism (EBAM) and an Adaptive Feature Measurement Filter (AFMF). EBAM effectively integrates channel-wise and spatial correlations to capture global features with stronger pixel-level relevance and contextual information, while the non-parametric AFMF emphasizes key information from the adapted feature map to achieve more accurate similarity measurement.

Model-agnostic explainability methods for deep learning

In recent years, a considerable body of research has attempted to incorporate explainability into deep learning models47,48, thereby enabling methods that can provide not only prediction results but also corresponding reasons or evidence. At present, according to the different ways of computing saliency maps that highlight the decision regions of a model, explainable deep learning methods are generally divided into model-specific and model-agnostic approaches. Model-specific explainability methods require access to the internal architecture of the model and generate explanations by exploiting the corresponding information flow. In contrast, model-agnostic explainability methods do not require access to the model internals; they only rely on the input-output interface of the model and derive explanations by transforming inputs and mapping them to outputs. Since this paper focuses more on the common technical issues of explanation consistency, we mainly consider model-agnostic explainability methods.

The Locally Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) algorithm proposed by RIBEIRO et al.30 is a pioneering work in model-agnostic explainability. The core idea of LIME is to fit a simple and interpretable surrogate model within the local neighborhood of the instance to be explained. Specifically, LIME generates local neighborhood samples through random perturbations, obtains predictions from the target model on these samples, and then trains a surrogate model based on the prediction results to explain the behavior of the original model. Grad-CAM, proposed by SELVARAJU et al.37, is also a representative model-agnostic explainability method. This method backpropagates the class score to the convolutional layer to compute the gradient corresponding to each pixel in the feature maps, and then uses global average pooling (GAP) to convert the gradients into channel-wise weights. These weights are then used instead of the fully connected layer weights in Class Activation Mapping (CAM) to weight the feature maps, thereby generating a saliency map that highlights the regions attended by the model during decision-making. PETSIUK et al.50 proposed Randomized Input Sampling for Explanation (RISE). This method uses a Monte Carlo strategy to sample the instance to be explained. It generates random small-region masks and combines them into large masks, which are multiplied element-wise with the image to produce perturbed samples. The target model is then used to make predictions on these perturbed samples. Finally, the prediction probabilities of the perturbed samples are linearly weighted to obtain a heatmap that reflects the contribution of pixel-level features to the final prediction. The main advantage of RISE is that it can produce heatmaps with higher resolution without requiring access to model gradients or intermediate features, making it applicable to any black-box image model.

In the study of explanation fairness, LUNDBERG et al.32 proposed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm based on game theory. SHAP quantifies the contribution of each input feature to a specific model decision by computing its Shapley value, taking into account the game-theoretic interactions among features. SHAP unifies multiple explainability methods and further develops variants for different scenarios: KernelSHAP is constructed based on LIME and game theory; LinearSHAP provides efficient computation for linear models; and DeepSHAP combines DeepLIFT with Shapley values for deep learning models. The advantage of SHAP lies in its theoretically grounded fairness in explanation, and it can be applied to different models and data types. However, its computational complexity grows exponentially with the number of features, and its reliance on the assumption of feature independence may introduce bias in the explanation results.

Explainable classification framework for aircraft type

The proposed Explainable Classification of Aircraft Types (EC-AT) framework is designed to support fine-grained aircraft type recognition in remote-sensing imagery while enhancing the explainability and trustworthiness of model predictions. The framework consists of three tightly connected stages: model construction, comparative explanation analysis, and trustworthiness evaluation. It operates in an offline manner and does not require any modification to the network architecture or learned parameters, thereby preserving the original predictive behavior of the classification models.

A schematic overview of the EC-AT framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, the model construction stage establishes multiple aircraft classification models under both transfer-learning and random-initialization settings, enabling a systematic comparison of classification performance across different backbone architectures. Second, the comparative explanation analysis stage applies multiple explainability methods, such as Grad-CAM, LIME, and RISE, to generate class-specific saliency maps. These visual explanations highlight the image regions most influential to the model’s decision and reveal whether the network focuses on aircraft-relevant structural cues, such as wings, fuselage, and tail configuration. Importantly, this stage also incorporates explanation consistency verification, where the spatial agreement among different explanation methods is quantified using the proposed IoMin-based metric. By combining visual comparison with quantitative overlap analysis, the framework can assess not only where the model attends, but also whether such attention is stable and semantically meaningful. Third, the trustworthiness evaluation stage adopts the Multisource AI Scorecard Table (MAST) to provide a structured assessment of the framework from the perspective of uncertainty, logical, consistency and accuracy.Fig. 1Overall architecture of the EC-AT framework.

Fine-grained aircraft type classification based on transfer learning

Deep learning models

Fine-grained aircraft type classification in remote-sensing imagery requires the model to capture both global airframe configuration and subtle structural differences among aircraft categories, while also maintaining computational efficiency and architectural diversity for subsequent visualization and explainability analysis. To this end, the EC-AT framework adopts four representative backbone networks, namely MobileViTV250, EfficientViT51, MobileNetV352, and DenseNet12153, to build the classification models. These backbones respectively represent lightweight CNN-Transformer hybrids, efficient vision Transformers, mobile-oriented lightweight CNNs, and densely connected CNNs, enabling a systematic comparison of different feature modeling mechanisms for the target task.

MobileViTV2 is a lightweight hybrid architecture that combines the local feature extraction capability of convolutional layers with the global dependency modeling ability of Transformer blocks. It first uses convolutions to capture low-level features such as edges, contours, and local textures while preserving spatial structure, and then applies lightweight Transformer modules to model long-range interactions across different image regions. For fine-grained aircraft classification in remote-sensing imagery, this joint modeling of local structural details and global configuration is beneficial for recognizing discriminative cues such as wing shape, fuselage aspect ratio, and mission-specific components. Therefore, MobileViTV2 provides a favorable trade-off between classification accuracy and computational efficiency.

EfficientViT is designed for efficient visual recognition, with particular emphasis on reducing the computational burden of global context modeling. By adopting a hardware-friendly efficient attention mechanism and combining it with lightweight convolutional operations for local feature extraction, EfficientViT is able to capture both global semantic information and local spatial patterns with improved efficiency. For aircraft targets cropped from remote-sensing images, this architecture is well suited to modeling overall aircraft contours as well as spatial relationships among key components, making it a strong candidate for evaluating the effectiveness of efficient Transformer-based representations in fine-grained classification.

MobileNetV3 is a lightweight convolutional network optimized for mobile and edge deployment through hardware-aware neural architecture design. It introduces lightweight Squeeze-and-Excitation modules into inverted residual blocks and employs the computationally efficient hard-swish activation function, thereby enhancing channel-wise feature selection while maintaining low computational cost. In the fine-grained aircraft classification task, MobileNetV3 can extract discriminative structural representations with a relatively small model size, making it suitable as an efficient CNN baseline and valuable for subsequent comparative analysis of model explainability.

DenseNet121 adopts a dense connectivity pattern in which each layer receives the feature maps of all preceding layers as input. This design strengthens feature propagation, encourages feature reuse, and alleviates the vanishing-gradient problem. Compared with conventional feed-forward CNNs, DenseNet121 is better able to preserve shallow visual details while integrating high-level semantic information, which is particularly important for distinguishing subtle structural differences among aircraft types. In this study, such differences include wing sweep configurations, tail arrangements, radar dome structures, and fuselage proportions. Therefore, DenseNet121 serves as a strong convolutional baseline for validating the effectiveness of multi-level feature reuse in fine-grained aircraft type classification.

Transfer-learning training strategy

To cope with the scarcity typical of aircraft remote-sensing datasets, this study employs a transfer-learning strategy that leverages knowledge from large-scale image domains54. Concretely, ImageNet is used as the source domain. The selected backbones are initialized with weights pretrained on ImageNet, enabling the models to start with generic low-level to mid-level feature representations rather than random initialization.

To adapt these generic representations to the target fine-grained aircraft-classification task, the original classification heads of all backbones are removed. Instead, we design and append a unified custom classification module to ensure consistency across different architectures. Specifically, the output features from the backbone first pass through a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer, followed by a sequence of Dropout (rate=0.3), Batch Normalization, a fully connected Dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation, another Dropout (rate=0.2), and a final Batch Normalization layer. The ultimate output is generated by a Dense layer with a Softmax activation function corresponding to the 8 aircraft categories.

Contrary to step-wise unfreezing methods, we adopt a direct end-to-end global fine-tuning strategy. During the training process, all layers within the backbones are set as trainable. This full-network adaptation allows the models to maximally map the generic feature space to the highly specialized remote-sensing domain, ensuring that the network learns subtle structural cues unique to distinct aircraft types from the very beginning. LIME algorithm

Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) is a post-hoc local explainability algorithm that provides an effective way to understand individual predictions of complex models. Specifically, for a prediction instance x to be provided with explainability, LIME first generates perturbed samples in an explainability representation space. Then, it uses the target model f to predict these perturbed samples. By combining the prediction results with the similarity weights between the samples and the original instance, LIME learns an explainability model g that is locally faithful to the original target model. The framework of the LIME algorithm is detailed in Algorithm 1. For an instance requiring explainability, a set of perturbed samples is generated in the explainability representation space (e.g., binary vectors of super-pixels for images). Perturbation involves randomly retaining a subset of features while setting the rest to default values. These samples are converted back to the original input representation and fed into the target model f to obtain labels. The similarity weight for each perturbed sample is then calculated as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi_{x} (z) = \exp ( - D(x,z)^{2} /\sigma^{2} )$$\end{document}

Where D(x,z) is a distance metric and σ is the kernel width. Finally, a sparse linear model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(z^{\prime}) = w_{g} \cdot z^{\prime}$$\end{document} is learned by minimizing the locally weighted square loss:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}(f,g,\pi_{x} ) = \sum\nolimits_{{z,z^{\prime} \in {\mathcal{Z}}}} {\pi_{x} (z)} (f(z) - g(z^{\prime}))^{2}$$\end{document}

Furthermore, to simplify the calculation of Ω (g) in Equation (6), LIME employs Lasso regularization to select features, and then trains the linear model by learning weights through the least squares method. Finally, the features and their corresponding weights that have a significant impact on the prediction are extracted from the learned explainability model, serving as the explainability for the target model’s prediction. In aircraft type fine-grained classification tasks, super-pixels with larger weights are extracted to provide explainability.

RISE algorithm

Randomized Input Sampling for Explainability (RISE) is a perturbation-based black-box explainability method. As illustrated in Fig. 3, RISE generates an explainability result for a single image by combining randomly sampled masks (MASK). The algorithm begins by generating a large number of masks through Monte Carlo sampling. To ensure smoothness and reduce computational costs, low-resolution masks are first generated and then upsampled to the original image size. The Hadamard product of these masks M and the original image I produces perturbed images, which are fed into the black-box model f to obtain prediction probabilities. The final map is produced by summing the masks weighted by these probabilities.Fig. 3Workflow of the RISE algorithm.

Workflow of the RISE algorithm.

As a stochastic approximate inference method, RISE uses sampling to approximate complex integrals or expectations. Let x represent a specific condition and v represent a random variable. By sampling from a distribution p(v|x), a set of independent and identically distributed samples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{ v_{1} ,v_{2} , \ldots ,v_{N} \}$$\end{document} can be generated, where N is the total number of random samples. The expression for the expected value of a function h(v) is given in Equation (9):9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{v|x} [h(v)] = \int {p(v|x)h(v)dv} \cong \frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{N} {h(v_{i} )}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i} (i = 1,2, \ldots ,N)$$\end{document} denotes the i-th sample obtained after random sampling. In the RISE algorithm, for the aircraft type of the perturbed image, the prediction probability can be regarded as the importance of the region retained by the mask. The importance of the final generated explainability region can be viewed as the expected value obtained from all masks, as shown in Equation (10):10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} S_{I,h} (\lambda ) = E_{M} [h(I \circ M)|M(\lambda ) = 1] \\ = \frac{1}{P[M(\lambda ) = 1]}\sum\nolimits_{m} {h(I \circ m) \cdot m(\lambda ) \cdot P[M = m]} \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

Since each mask m follows a 0−1 distribution, the equation simplifies to:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{I,h} (\lambda ) = \frac{1}{E[M(\lambda )]}\sum\nolimits_{m} {h(I \circ m) \cdot m(\lambda ) \cdot P[M = m]}$$\end{document}

This equation demonstrates that the heatmap can be obtained by the weighted summation of masks obtained from random sampling, based on the prediction probabilities of the perturbed images.

Explanation consistency validation metric

At present, evaluation methods for explainable artificial intelligence can generally be classified into two aircraft types: qualitative evaluation and quantitative evaluation 55. Qualitative evaluation relies on experts’ cognitive perceptions and subjective judgments. Although it can capture the interactivity and intuitive comprehensibility of explanations, it is susceptible to cognitive bias and requires substantial human effort. In contrast, quantitative metrics seek to automatically assess the rationality and robustness of explanations from mathematical and statistical perspectives; however, they still face two major challenges: the absence of unified evaluation standards and the lack of ground-truth explanations56. To address these limitations, the EC-AT framework proposes a dedicated validation metric termed Intersection over Minimum Area (IoMin) to measure the spatial-overlap consistency of explanation results. Specifically, based on feature-importance maps generated by explainability methods are first extracted using adaptive thresholding and morphological operations. When only two explainability methods are involved, IoMin is computed as the ratio of the intersection area to the smaller area of the two masks:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{IoMin}}(A,B) = \frac{{{|}Mask_{A} \cap Mask_{B} |}}{{\min (|Mask_{A} |,|Mask_{B} |)}}$$\end{document}

The value of IoMin ranges from 0 to 1. This metric possesses several ideal mathematical properties for consistency validation, including symmetry (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{IoMin}}(A,B) = {\mathrm{IoMin}}(B,A)$$\end{document}), monotonicity (the value increases as the intersection area expands), and scale invariance (the ratio is independent of absolute image resolution). A critical consideration is the choice of the “minimum area” as the denominator. While the minimum area can be sensitive to small noisy artifacts, this is mitigated in our framework through adaptive thresholding and morphological denoising, ensuring that only statistically significant salient regions are compared. Compared to the conventional Intersection over Union (IoU), which heavily penalizes differences in explanation granularity, IoMin is better suited for capturing "subset consensus."

When δ explainability methods are available, the overall explanation consistency is obtained by averaging the IoMin values over all pairs of explainability methods:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{{\mathrm{IoMin}}}} = \frac{{p_{i} }}{{\left( { \, \begin{array}{*{20}c} \delta \\ 2 \\ \end{array} \, } \right)}} \, \sum\limits_{1 \le a \le b \le \delta } {{\mathrm{IoMin}}(a,b)} = \frac{{{2}p_{i} }}{\delta (\delta - 1)} \, \sum\limits_{1 \le a \le b \le \delta } {{\mathrm{IoMin}}(a,b)}$$\end{document}

Here,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the posterior probability estimate, i.e., confidence, that the deep learning model predicts a sample to belong to aircraft type k, which is typically produced by the Softmax function. For a classification task with K classes, where j serves as the index for summation over all categories, the Softmax probability of the k-th aircraft type is calculated as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i} = {\mathrm{Softmax(}}\beta_{k} {) = }\frac{{e^{{\beta_{k} }} }}{{\sum {_{j = 1}^{K} e^{{\beta_{k} }} } }}$$\end{document}

After Softmax normalization, neural networks usually adopt the cross-entropy loss function as the optimization objective:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}} = - \sum {_{k = 1}^{K} } y_{k} \log (p_{k} )$$\end{document}

This optimization objective is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation, encouraging the model probability distribution to closely approximate the true distribution, thereby ensuring the probabilistic rationality of the IoMin metric57.

It is crucial to clarify the distinction between IoMin and existing XAI metrics, such as LSE58 and SIDU59. Existing metrics like SIDU are primarily designed to evaluate the faithfulness or localization accuracy of a single explainability method by comparing it against the model’s output variations or ground-truth bounding boxes. In contrast, they are not intended to quantify the consistency among multiple XAI algorithms. Instead, IoMin fills a unique gap by evaluating the spatial agreement across different explainability methods in the absence of absolute ground truth.

In EC-AT framework, we define a consistency threshold T to determine thetrustworthiness of the explanations. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{{\mathrm{IoMin}}}} > T$$\end{document}, the salient regions identified by different methods achieve high spatial overlap on important structures (e.g., wings, fuselage), thereby enhancing the trust of intelligence analysts in the model’s decision. Conversely, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{{\mathrm{IoMin}}}} \le T$$\end{document}, the regions of interest are considered spatially dispersed, suggesting that the network’s discriminative features may be unstable or that the training data may contain noise and bias. The threshold T serves as a tunable parameter that reflects the required level of consensus for a given application.

Comparative analysis of explanation regions in fine-grained aircraft type classification

In this section, we conduct a comparative analysis of the explanation heatmaps generated by the proposed EC-AT framework and use them as an important basis for assessing whether the model has truly learned semantically meaningful aircraft-type features. In the fine-grained aircraft type classification task using remote-sensing images, explainable discriminative evidence should be highly related to aircraft-type characteristics, such as wing configuration, fuselage proportion, tail geometry, engine position, and radome appearance. We argue that a trustworthy fine-grained aircraft type classification model should not only achieve high classification accuracy but also consistently focus on regions highly related to aircraft characteristics across different explainability methods.

The C2 class shown in Fig. 10 is a typical transport aircraft sample with relatively clear fuselage and wing structures. For M1~M4 and M7, Grad-CAM usually concentrates on the middle fuselage and wing-root regions, LIME tends to select superpixels corresponding to the aircraft contour and main body, and RISE assigns higher importance to the central fuselage and adjacent wing regions. Although the three methods operate in different ways, they generally converge on similar semantic parts. This indicates that these models have captured robust and structurally meaningful discriminative features for the C2 class.

By comparison, the randomly initialized models M5, M6, and M8 show much larger Grad-CAM activation regions and even noticeable spatial shifts. LIME produces fragmented or partially irrelevant superpixels, while RISE tends to form dispersed high-response distributions that are difficult to concentrate on the aircraft body. This inconsistency suggests that, without pretrained priors, the feature combinations relied upon by the models during prediction are not sufficiently stable. Among these models, M7 performs relatively better, and its explanation regions are closer to those of the transfer-learning-based models, which is consistent with its higher classification performance.

The F2 class shown in Fig. 11 exhibits even more pronounced problems. Similar to the observations in Figs. 5 and 6, the RISE explanations for F2 are highly diffuse and fail to provide informative localization across all eight models. For M1~M4 and M7, Grad-CAM and LIME tend to localize the main structural parts, such as the fuselage and wings. This indicates that these models are able to integrate multiple structural cues through Grad-CAM and LIME, thereby forming more consistent discriminative evidence. However, for M5, M6, and M8, the explanation heatmaps become significantly less stable and less explainable. Grad-CAM sometimes activates overly large regions, while LIME may include background areas or incomplete aircraft fragments. This problem is especially prominent in the fine-grained recognition of fighter aircraft, where discriminative differences are often subtle and spatially localized. It is also worth noting that high confidence scores do not necessarily correspond to high-quality explanations, as some models with near-saturated prediction confidence still produce diffuse or unstable heatmaps.

From the above comparisons, two conclusions can be drawn. First, explanation behavior is strongly influenced by the training strategy. Transfer learning generally produces explanation heatmaps that are more compact, more semantically meaningful, and more consistent across methods. Second, even transfer-learning-based models may still produce relatively weak explanation results for some difficult classes, indicating that classification performance alone is insufficient to guarantee explanation trustworthiness. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce a quantitative metric to measure the consistency among different explainability methods. Although visual inspection can roughly judge whether explanations are reasonable, it cannot provide a reproducible and comparable standard for spatial consistency. In the next section, we therefore introduce the IoMin metric to quantify the degree of overlap among explanation regions generated by different methods.

Trustworthiness evaluation of intelligent systems based on MAST

A trustworthy intelligent system should not only achieve high accuracy, but also exhibit transparency, explainability, and the ability to provide evidence supporting its analytic outputs. Based on this understanding, this study introduces the Multisource AI Scorecard Table (MAST) to conduct a structured and comparable trustworthiness evaluation of the proposed EC-AT framework from a more general and standardized perspective.

ICD 203, also known as the Intelligence Analysis Standards, is an important directive used by the U.S. Intelligence Community to regulate the quality and workflow of intelligence analysis products. Originally designed to evaluate human intelligence analysts, ICD 203 provides a standardized framework covering multiple dimensions of analytic tradecraft, including sourcing, argumentation, accuracy, consistency, and visualization. With the growing adoption of artificial intelligence in intelligence analysis, AI systems are increasingly taking over tasks once performed by human analysts. This raises an important question: can the evaluation logic of ICD 203 also be extended to AI systems in order to assess the trustworthiness and transparency of their outputs?

To address this issue, the Multisource AI Scorecard Table (MAST) proposed in the literature argues that the nine ICD 203 standards are also applicable to AI-based intelligence analysis systems61. By mapping each standard to a corresponding scoring dimension, MAST provides a standardized and quantifiable evaluation framework that enables systematic assessment of the strengths and weaknesses of AI systems and offers guidance for future improvement. For AI systems, the nine standards are defined as follows:Sourcing: How well can the system identify the sources and methods on which its results are based?Uncertainty: How well can the system indicate and explain the uncertainty associated with its derived results?Distinguishing: How well can the system distinguish derived results from underlying data?Analysis of Alternatives: How well can the system identify and assess plausible alternative results?Customer Relevance: How well can the system provide useful information and insight to users?Logical Argumentation: How well can the system help users understand how its results are derived?Consistency: How well can the system help users understand how its derived results are consistent with, or differ from, previous analyses of the same or similar topics?Accuracy: How well can the system make the most accurate judgments possible based on the available information?Visualization: How well can the system incorporate visual information to clarify the analytic message and enhance the presentation of data and analysis?

In MAST, each standard is evaluated on a four-point scale ranging from 0 to 3, where 0 denotes poor performance with little or no compliance, 1 indicates partial compliance with substantial deficiencies, 2 represents good performance with room for improvement, and 3 signifies excellent performance with full compliance. The maximum possible total score is 2762. This scoring scheme not only captures performance across individual standards but also provides an overall assessment of the system’s trustworthiness and transparency.

The scoring criteria for the EC-AT framework were established with reference to the evaluation procedures reported in61 for the aforementioned three systems. The MAST scoring results demonstrate that the proposed EC-AT framework achieves a high level of trustworthiness, with an average expert score of approximately 18 points. Compared to the reference systems, EC-AT significantly outperforms the MIDAT system (10 points) and reaches a level comparable to the operational ACT system (19 points).

The primary strengths of the EC-AT framework lie in Accuracy (Standard 8) and Visualization (Standard 9). By leveraging transfer learning and advanced explainability methods, the system generates intuitive explanation heatmaps that clarify the evidential basis for its classifications. Moreover, the introduction of the IoMin metric significantly enhances its performance in Consistency (Standard 7) and Logical Argumentation (Standard 6), providing a quantitative mathematical foundation to verify the spatial consistency of explanations across different models. However, it should be noted that this consistency is primarily confined to "between-method" and "between-model" agreement; it does not yet fully address the continuous temporal stability of the EC-AT framework over extended periods.

Conversely, the evaluation identifies notable weaknesses, particularly in Analysis of Alternatives (Standard 4) and Customer Relevance (Standard 5). While experts acknowledged that the framework is highly task-oriented and effective for fine-grained aircraft type classification, they also pointed out a lack of integrated Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) capabilities. The system currently lacks mechanisms for analysts to interactively explore alternative hypotheses or customize outputs according to specific operational requirements. To enhance its practical utility, future iterations should incorporate Top-N candidate results and robust HCI modules to better support collaborative intelligence analysis in complex decision-making environments.

Conclusions and future work

This paper proposes an explainable aircraft type classification framework, namely EC-AT. Based on the MAR20 dataset, eight representative aircraft classes were selected for experimentation, and multiple deep learning models were systematically evaluated under two settings: transfer-learning-based fine-tuning and random initialization. The experimental results show that, under conditions of limited samples and class imbalance, transfer learning can significantly improve model convergence speed and classification accuracy, whereas models trained from scratch with random initialization usually exhibit slower convergence and weaker discriminative capability.

In addition to classification performance, Grad-CAM, LIME, and RISE were used to further investigate the explainability of model decisions. The explanation maps generated by transfer-learning-based models are generally more compact, more concentrated on the main aircraft body, and more semantically consistent; in contrast, the explanation maps produced by randomly initialized models are more dispersed and more likely to be influenced by background regions. However, some samples indicate that a higher classification accuracy does not necessarily correspond to high-quality explanations; in certain difficult classes, the model may still produce dispersed response regions, focus on background information, or capture only partial structural fragments. To address this issue, an IoMin-based metric was introduced to quantify the spatial overlap among regions generated by different explainability methods. The experimental results show that this metric can effectively characterize the consistency of explanation results and provide a more objective basis for explainability evaluation.

This paper also evaluated the proposed EC-AT framework using the Multisource AI Scorecard Table (MAST). The evaluation results show that, thanks to the introduction of a quantitative explanation verification mechanism, EC-AT performs well in terms of accuracy, visualization, consistency, and logical reasoning. However, the framework still has certain limitations in user relevance and alternative analysis, indicating that its current design is more suitable for task-specific classification scenarios rather than interactive decision-support scenarios.

Future work will focus on improving the model’s robustness to background noise and reducing its dependence on contextual cues. Possible research directions include mask-based learning methods, background suppression mechanisms, and the integration of multiple explainability methods to further enhance model explainability. In addition, the framework can be extended from fine-grained classification to object detection tasks, thereby broadening its application in remote sensing image analysis. Further investigations integrating different object detection algorithms may help improve human-computer interaction capabilities and strengthen the performance of MAST-related dimensions, especially user relevance and alternative analysis.
