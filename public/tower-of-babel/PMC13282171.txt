Functional network contributions to longitudinal tau spread in Posterior Cortical Atrophy
Authors: Yuta Katsumi, Ryan Eckbo, Scott M McGinnis, Jorge Sepulcre, Gil D Rabinovici, Renaud La Joie, Bradford C Dickerson, Deepti Putcha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we investigated longitudinal tau spreading in 23 amyloid-positive individuals with early-stage posterior cortical atrophy (PCA), a clinical syndrome typically characterized by progressive visual cognitive deficits emerging largely from underlying Alzheimer’s disease-related tau deposition in posterior cortical areas. Each PCA participant underwent structural MRI and 18F-Flortaucipir PET at baseline and follow-up (mean interval between baseline and follow-up PET = 1.17 ± 0.29 years). Using directional regression analysis (i.e., a regression-based model testing temporal directionality in tau spread), we quantified how tau epicenters (top 10% regions by baseline tau) predicted longitudinal tau accumulation. Seed-based analysis revealed evidence supporting the role of posterior cortical epicenters in the visual and dorsal attention networks (DAN), showing directional spreading of tau to anterior DAN nodes and the default mode network (DMN), with the largest effects in anterior DAN regions. Complementary graph theory analysis identified the visual network and posterior DMN as hubs of tau spread. These findings collectively suggest that longitudinal tau spread in PCA follows hierarchical progression from primary epicenters within the visual network and DAN to secondary epicenters, including the posterior DMN, possibly mediated by dynamic connectivity reorganization as primary epicenters become saturated with tau pathology.Subject terms: Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Posterior cortical atrophy (PCA) is a clinical syndrome that typically presents in the mid-50s–mid-60s, with a heterogeneous symptom constellation including visuospatial and visuoperceptual deficits1,2. In most cases (~94%), PCA syndrome arises from Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-related pathologic changes1 and has been referred to as the visual variant of AD3. PCA due to AD is most typically characterized by the prominent co-localization of AD-related accumulation of neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau and neurodegeneration in the occipital and posterior temporo-parietal cortical areas2,4–11. Current evidence highlights the accumulation of tau pathology as a major driver of subsequent neurodegeneration and cognitive decline across clinical phenotypes of AD, including PCA7,9,12–15. Thus, a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the longitudinal spreading of tau in PCA has important implications for more accurate characterization of disease progression, clinical prognostication, and outcomes monitoring in patients treated with disease-modifying therapies.

Mounting evidence from human imaging and animal studies supports the hypothesis that pathological tau proteins spread through neuronal network connections in AD, possibly from syndrome-specific “epicenters”16–26. Cortical regions with stronger intrinsic functional connectivity with tau epicenters tend to have higher levels of tau themselves17,23,27 and also exhibit faster rates of longitudinal tau accumulation17,19. In PCA, tau epicenters are localized within the visual network (e.g., lateral occipital cortex) and the posterior nodes of the dorsal attention network (DAN) (e.g., superior parietal lobule, anterior MT+)13,17,23,24,28. Using cross-sectional data, we previously demonstrated that the ratio of tau positron emission tomography (PET) signal between the anterior and posterior nodes of the DAN was associated with the strength of intrinsic functional connectivity between these nodes as well as the severity of visuospatial cognitive deficits in PCA13. This suggests that, as the disease progresses, tau may spread from the posterior to the anterior nodes of the DAN and that this mechanism is facilitated by the strength of functional connectivity within this network.

This hypothesis is supported by a few longitudinal tau PET studies in PCA identifying relatively faster rates of tau accumulation in the frontal and anterior temporal cortices, which are spatially distinct from posterior cortical tau epicenters17,29,30. Convergent with these observations, another cross-sectional study modeling disease progression based on a large number of tau PET scans also identified a subtype of AD characterized by early tau deposition in the occipital and posterior temporo-parietal cortices with gradual anterior progression31. In addition to the visual network and DAN, recent work also highlights the shared vulnerability of the default mode network (DMN) across the AD syndromic spectrum, where tau accumulation and functional connectivity disruption are first observed in the posterior nodes, including the posteromedial cortex and inferior parietal lobule32–35. Taken together, these findings based on cross-sectional and longitudinal data point to posterior-to-anterior propagation of tau pathology in PCA. However, it remains unclear which specific networks and regions within those networks drive this mechanism.

In the present study, we addressed this question by characterizing the spatial pattern of longitudinal tau spread in a sample of patients with PCA at early symptomatic stages of illness. We employed a directional regression approach to quantify the contribution of tau epicenters defined at baseline to predicting the magnitude of longitudinal tau accumulation across the cerebral cortex. Additionally, we performed a data-driven analysis of tau propagation based on graph theory to identify cortical hub regions where tau at baseline explained tau in many other cortical regions at follow-up. These analyses enabled clarification of the role of specific networks and their nodes in tau spreading in PCA syndrome, extending prior longitudinal work that examined more general associations between interregional functional connectivity and tau covariance17. Building upon prior work pointing to the anterior progression of tau pathology in PCA, we hypothesized that PCA participants would show faster rates of tau accumulation in anterior cortical regions that are spatially distinct from the topography of brain regions with elevated tau at baseline. Furthermore, we hypothesized that baseline tau in these PCA syndrome-specific tau epicenters within the visual network and DAN would predict the magnitude of longitudinal tau accumulation in other cortical regions within the same networks, as well as in other networks that have shared vulnerability across the AD syndromic spectrum. We specifically hypothesized the involvement of the DMN, in which cortical hubs showing extensive connectivity with the rest of the brain may facilitate pathological tau spreading in AD36,37.

Results

Sample characteristics

The present study included 23 individuals who fulfilled diagnostic criteria for PCA2,38,39 at baseline and had longitudinal tau PET data. Nineteen participants were recruited from the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Frontotemporal Disorders Unit PCA program40,41, and the remaining four participants were recruited from the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Memory and Aging Center; the same diagnostic criteria were applied at both sites by site-specific neurologists. All PCA participants underwent neuroimaging sessions that included brain structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and tau PET scans using 18F-Flortaucipir (FTP) at baseline and follow-up (mean time interval from baseline to follow-up = 1.17 ± 0.29 years). At baseline, all PCA participants also underwent amyloid PET imaging using either 11C-Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB) or 18F-Florbetaben (FBB) and were classified as amyloid positive based on a centiloid (CL) threshold of 2542,43. In addition, we included a sample of amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired (CU) control participants, which was used as a reference for quantifying the magnitude of elevated FTP uptake in PCA participants at baseline. Table 1 summarizes demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics of individuals with PCA and CU participants. The majority of PCA participants (65%) were classified as having very mild/mild cognitive impairment (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] global score 0.5) at baseline. At follow-up, 7 of 15 patients (~47%) with a baseline CDR score of 0.5 had progressed to mild dementia (CDR 1), and 2 of 8 patients (25%) who had mild dementia at baseline had progressed to moderate dementia (CDR 2).

Spatial topography of tau epicenters in PCA

For each participant, we processed baseline MRI and FTP PET scans to calculate standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) maps. Using individual baseline FTP SUVR maps as inputs, we constructed whole-cortex vertex-wise multivariable linear regression models to identify areas of the cerebral cortex where PCA participants showed elevated tau burden compared with CU participants. Replicating prior work, we found that individuals with PCA exhibited prominently elevated FTP SUVR in the occipital, ventral and lateral temporal, lateral and medial parietal, and lateral frontal cortical areas bilaterally (Supplementary Fig. 1). Tau epicenters—defined as the top 10% vertices showing the largest effect sizes of group differences in FTP SUVR—were selectively localized to posterior cortical regions including the lateral occipital, ventrolateral temporal, and posterior superior parietal cortices (Fig. 1). Regarding membership within functional networks, ~52% of tau epicenter vertices fell within the boundaries of the visual network while ~36% were within the boundaries of the DAN. The remaining vertices were spatially adjacent to these networks and did not singularly represent other networks. The relative proportion of tau epicenter vertices was statistically greater in the right (12.67%) than the left (9.2%) hemisphere (permutation test p < 0.0001), although this effect was small in magnitude (Cohen’s h = 0.11). We found no evidence of hemispheric asymmetry in epicenter tau burden at the group level: Mean asymmetry index = −1.61 ± 13.88; t = −0.55, p ≤ 0.59. We found no cortical regions where CU participants showed elevated FTP SUVR relative to PCA participants.

Tau epicenters in mild PCA fall primarily within the visual and dorsal attention networks.

Eighty-eight percent of tau epicenter vertices were identified within the visual or dorsal attention networks.

Spatial topography of longitudinal tau accumulation in PCA

To estimate the rate of longitudinal tau accumulation in PCA participants, we constructed vertex-wise linear mixed-effects models. In stark contrast to the topography of elevated FTP SUVR at baseline, this sample of PCA participants showed longitudinal increases in FTP SUVR predominantly in bilateral anterior cortical regions, including medial and lateral frontal, insular, and anterior lateral and ventral temporal areas, with additional involvement of the posteromedial and medial occipital cortices. These regions partially overlapped with anterior nodes of the DAN and DMN (Fig. 2). The relative proportion of suprathreshold vertices showing longitudinal increases in FTP SUVR was comparable across hemispheres (left: 49.65% vs. right: 49.44%; p ≤ 0.24). Subregional analysis within each network identified stronger effects in the anterior DAN and the DMN; no longitudinal increase in FTP SUVR was observed in the visual network or the posterior DAN (Table 2). We found no cortical regions where PCA participants showed a decrease in FTP SUVR over time.

Epicenter-driven longitudinal spread of tau in PCA

We conducted a seed-based directional regression analysis to estimate the spatiotemporal trajectory of longitudinal tau spread from the epicenters illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, we used AIC model comparison to identify cortical regions showing evidence of tau propagation from epicenters (“targets” of tau propagation). For each vertex, we compared forward models (baseline epicenter tau predicting follow-up tau) against backward models (follow-up epicenter tau predicting baseline tau) (Fig. 3A; see Supplementary Fig. 2 for beta coefficient maps from forward and backward models). Vertices where the forward model showed superior fit (lower AIC) and was statistically significant were considered as targets of tau propagation from the epicenters over time. This analysis revealed evidence of tau spread from the epicenters to regions within the DAN (frontal eye fields, inferior frontal junction, superior parietal lobule, and anterior intraparietal sulcus), visual network (lateral occipital cortex), and DMN (posteromedial cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and lateral temporal cortex) (Fig. 3B and Table 3). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests suggested evidence of hemispheric asymmetry in epicenter-driven tau spread, with notable left-lateralization in the inferior parietal lobule (D = 0.91), inferior frontal junction (D = 0.80), lateral temporal cortex (D = 0.74), precuneus/posterior cingulate (D = 0.66), and frontal eye fields (D = 0.56), and to a lesser extent posterior temporo-parietal areas in the DAN (D = 0.33) and the visual network (D = 0.28) (all ps < 0.0001).

A series of sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings. Specifically, the pattern of epicenter-driven tau spread was very similar to the original results when changing the size of epicenter seeds by ±5% of all cortical vertices (Supplementary Fig. 3), when modeling baseline amyloid burden as a possible moderator of longitudinal tau accumulation and spreading trajectories (Supplementary Fig. 4), when controlling for MRI-derived cortical thickness (Supplementary Fig. 5), and when controlling for the tau asymmetry index derived from baseline epicenter tau (Supplementary Fig. 6). In addition, we conducted a separate regression analysis by defining seeds as tau epicenter vertices falling within the boundaries of the visual network or DAN. The spatial topographies of longitudinal tau spread were generally similar between the visual- and DAN-masked epicenters, although we found that baseline tau in the DAN-masked epicenters uniquely predicted follow-up tau in the key DAN nodes, including the left frontal eye fields, inferior frontal junction, and anterior intraparietal sulcus (Supplementary Fig. 7). The mean FTP SUVR at baseline was highly similar between the visual- and DAN-masked epicenters across participants (r = 0.94, p < 0.000001).

Cortical hubs of longitudinal tau spread in PCA

We extended our seed-based regression approach by examining vertex-wise tau spreading patterns across the entire cortical surface. To summarize the contribution of each vertex, we computed vertex-wise weighted out-degree from a graph representing directional relationships in tau spread (Fig. 4A). These weighted out-degree hubs, therefore, represented the localization of cortical regions where baseline tau predicted follow-up tau in many other regions. Consistent with our initial seed-based approach, we found that weighted out-degree hubs were localized mostly to posterior cortical regions (Fig. 4B). However, only ~26% of weighted out-degree hubs spatially overlapped with the tau epicenters from our seed-based analysis, with the remaining hubs localized to regions including more medial portions of the occipital cortex along with some of the posterior DMN regions where tau had spread from the initial epicenters over time (e.g., posteromedial cortex, inferior parietal lobule; see Fig. 3B). In terms of whole networks, ~56% of weighted out-degree hubs fell within the boundaries of the visual network, followed by ~27% within the DMN, while only ~6% were found within the DAN. Vertex-wise correlation analysis revealed at the group level that cortical regions showing larger Cohen’s d values (reflecting higher FTP SUVR in PCA than CU participants) at baseline tended to have higher weighted out-degree (r = 0.71, p ≤ 0.02; corrected for spatial autocorrelation via a spin test). Finally, we repeated all analyses based on non-partial volume corrected FTP PET data and found overall similar results, suggesting that partial volume correction did not fundamentally drive the observed pattern of longitudinal tau spread in PCA (Supplementary Figs. 8–11).

Discussion

Individuals with PCA typically present with predominant impairment in visuospatial processing and other posterior cortical functions as the earliest signs of illness, arising from dysfunction of the visual cortices1,2,41. Despite this relatively focal initial pattern of symptoms, non-visuospatial cognitive impairment usually emerges fairly soon as the illness progresses44, involving cortical regions outside of the visual network13,45,46. Clearer characterization of the mechanisms underlying AD-related tau spreading in PCA, therefore, has the potential to better predict the rate of disease progression and inform the design of clinical trials in this atypical AD population by refining tau PET-based endpoints. In this study, we examined longitudinal FTP PET scans acquired from a sample of individuals with PCA in early clinical stages (CDR 0.5 or 1.0 at baseline). Consistent with our hypotheses, we found evidence that tau pathology spreads from posterior syndrome-specific tau epicenters within the visual network and DAN to more anterior nodes of the DAN as well as the DMN. A data-driven analysis based on graph theory additionally provided evidence supporting the role of the posterior DMN as a hub of tau spread over time in this syndrome. Taken together, these findings implicate multiple cortical networks in tau spreading over time in PCA, where syndromically vulnerable networks (visual network and DAN) may initiate early propagation, while secondary networks such as the DMN may contribute to the acceleration of tau spread due to extensive connectivity with the rest of the cerebral cortex.

Consistent with previous reports, the current sample of PCA participants exhibited elevated tau burden at baseline in a distributed network of cortical regions, with the epicenters localized to posterior cortical regions corresponding to the visual network and posterior components of the DAN5,7,13,15,23,30. In the frontal cortex, we found baseline tau aggregation localized selectively to the frontal eye fields extending into the inferior frontal junction, although to a smaller magnitude compared with the posterior epicenters. The frontal eye fields are key anterior nodes of the DAN47, and the inferior frontal junction shows functional coupling with the major DAN nodes at rest and during tasks48–50. In a previous cross-sectional study, we demonstrated that the magnitude of tau burden in the anterior DAN (adjusted for the level of tau in the posterior DAN) was associated with the severity of visuospatial cognitive impairment in PCA13. Collectively, these findings underscore the involvement of the visual network and DAN as syndromically vulnerable functional networks in the early clinical stages of PCA.

We also found that this sample of PCA participants showed longitudinal increases in tau accumulation predominantly in more anterior cortical regions, including the anterior DAN (bilateral frontal eye fields and inferior frontal junction) and DMN (lateral and medial prefrontal cortex, anterior and ventral lateral temporal cortex) as well as the frontal cortical regions that are part of the frontoparietal, salience, and cingulo-opercular networks50–52. These results are consistent with available longitudinal tau PET imaging evidence identifying faster rates of longitudinal tau accumulation in the frontal and anterior temporal regions in PCA compared with healthy controls17,29,30. Topographically similar patterns of longitudinal tau accumulation, particularly in the lateral frontal cortex, have also been reported in individuals presenting with heterogenous clinical syndromes of AD29,53. This evidence suggests that greater frontal accumulation of tau may partially reflect a shared feature of disease convergence across the phenotypic spectrum of AD, and could represent a biomarker outcome across syndromes of AD—particularly those with younger age-of-onset.

Our seed-based directional regression approach revealed the trajectory of epicenter-driven tau spread in PCA, characterized by the involvement of multiple cortical association networks. Specifically, we found evidence consistent with the pattern of tau spread from the visual network and posterior DAN epicenters to the anterior DAN (frontal eye fields and inferior frontal junction) as well as nodes of the posterior DAN that are spatially distinct from the epicenters (anterior intraparietal sulcus and more dorsal aspects of the superior parietal lobule). This finding provides support to the hypothesis based on our prior cross-sectional work that, in PCA, tau pathology spreads from posterior cortical regions with maximal tau deposition (including the posterior DAN) to the anterior DAN, possibly facilitated by the strength of intra-DAN connectivity13. Although we did not examine conventional measures of network connectivity in this study (e.g., based on functional MRI data), inter-subject tau covariance has been shown to strongly mirror the organization of functional connectivity in the brain24,54. Taken together, these observations highlight the utility of tau PET-based identification of longitudinal tau spreading pathways in PCA, extending our initial work in preclinical AD22.

Furthermore, our regression-based findings suggest that tau may also spread from the visual network and DAN epicenters to posterior nodes of the DMN (posteromedial cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and caudal lateral temporal cortex). The posterior DMN shows shared vulnerability across the syndromic spectrum of AD, characterized by abnormal functional connectivity and tau accumulation in early clinical stages32–35. Individuals with PCA exhibit stronger intrinsic functional connectivity between the visual network and DMN than healthy controls34, which may underlie accelerated tau spreading from the epicenters to the posterior DMN in this syndrome.

Our seed-based regression results were robust to various methodological and biological parameters, some of which warrant additional discussion. Partial volume correction did not fundamentally influence the topography of baseline tau burden, longitudinal tau accumulation, epicenter-based tau spread, or weighted out-degree of tau spread. While previous studies identified the role of amyloid in accelerating cortical spread of tau in AD55–58, we found no evidence that baseline amyloid burden influences the rate of longitudinal tau accumulation or the topography of epicenter-based tau spreading in the current sample of PCA participants. These studies primarily emphasize the interaction of amyloid and tau early in the disease course, while the topographies of amyloid and tau accumulation do not overlap extensively. We previously showed that, in early symptomatic stages of PCA due to AD (CDR 0.5 or 1.0), cortical amyloid and tau accumulation are both diffuse, with a substantial degree of spatial overlap in the occipital and posterior temporo-parietal regions32,59. Therefore, it is possible that epicenter-driven tau spread in these clinical stages of PCA occurs largely independently from amyloid-mediated mechanisms.

We additionally confirmed that controlling for cortical thickness as a proxy for gray matter atrophy has a minimal effect on the topography of longitudinal tau accumulation and epicenter-driven tau spreading. In some regions, the magnitude of these effects was slightly attenuated once cortical thickness was included in the models. However, this is not surprising given that tau accumulation is known to precede and drive downstream neurodegeneration14. Our data suggest that controlling for the magnitude of cortical atrophy in the analysis of longitudinal tau accumulation and spread may introduce overadjustment bias, which, in our view, should be avoided. Nonetheless, the overall consistency of the spatial pattern supports the robustness of our findings.

Our seed-based directional regression results were characterized by a slightly left-lateralized pattern of tau spread from the epicenters. Hemispheric asymmetry in FTP uptake among individuals with PCA has been previously noted15 and prior research also described anatomical subtypes of PCA characterized by spatially distinct patterns of neurodegeneration (e.g., right- vs. left-lateralization)6,10. In the present study, we found that the localization of tau epicenters at baseline was slightly right-lateralized, although the magnitude of epicenter tau burden was not statistically different across the two hemispheres. Importantly, however, statistically controlling for the degree of hemispheric asymmetry in baseline epicenter tau burden yielded similar results relative to simpler models without this additional covariate, leaving our findings unlikely to be driven by this factor. Future work may consider contributions of other biological features to further clarify this asymmetry in epicenter-based tau propagation, including interhemispheric structural and functional connectivity as well as amyloid asymmetry60.

Seed-based regression results were generally similar when the epicenter seeds were defined uniquely within the boundaries of the visual network and (posterior) DAN, with some notable differences. Specifically, we found evidence consistent with a pattern of tau spreading uniquely from the DAN-masked epicenters to more anterior regions of this network, including the left frontal eye fields, inferior frontal junction, and anterior intraparietal sulcus. This suggests that these posterior cortical epicenters within the two networks have both similar and dissociable contributions to tau spread, the latter of which may be driven by selective intra-network connectivity13.

Our data-driven analysis based on vertex-wise weighted out-degree provided support to the epicenters’ role in driving longitudinal tau spread in PCA, particularly with respect to the lateral occipital regions, part of the visual network. Intriguingly, the posterior DAN regions were minimally involved as out-degree hubs of tau spread. Examination of unthresholded weighted out-degree maps revealed relatively higher values (>50th percentiles), although they were not identified as statistically significant in our permutation test. One possibility is that, in PCA, tau spreads from the posterior DAN nodes via more selective, intra-network connectivity, whereas the visual network may serve as the source of tau for multiple networks via increased inter-network connectivity. This hypothesis is supported by recent work demonstrating that PCA is associated with increased intrinsic functional connectivity between the visual network and DMN34, but not between the DAN and DMN61. In addition, this analysis revealed the involvement of other regions as hubs of cortical tau spread, including the posteromedial cortex and the inferior parietal lobule part of the posterior DMN, to which tau spread from the epicenters over time. These results support the hypothesis that the initial seeding of tau leads to secondary tau seeding events that cascade throughout the cerebral cortex25. As such, tau epicenters may be inherently dynamic, and their locations likely evolve over the course of the disease23. It is possible that the window of PCA syndrome examined in the current study, reflecting a typical stage where individuals may present for clinical evaluation and care, represents a transitional period during which a shift in the involvement of primary and secondary tau epicenters was observed.

Although we observed a strong relationship between vertex-wise weighted out-degree and FTP SUVR at baseline, this does not necessarily mean that the hubness merely reflects high baseline tau burden. This relationship is expected as spreading sources require elevated tau pathology and forms the biological foundation of our seed-based analysis testing epicenter-driven tau spread. Critically, however, this correlation does not automatically indicate confounding because our directional regression framework involving AIC-based model selection inherently controls for cross-sectional tau covariance. Regions with high baseline tau would demonstrate high weighted out-degree only if (1) baseline tau predicted follow-up tau in other regions (forward model) and (2) this prediction significantly exceeded the reverse temporal relationship (backward model). Regions showing mere cross-sectional correlation would produce similar forward and backward fits (ΔAIC ≈ 0), contributing minimally to weighted out-degree after thresholding.

Based on the present findings, we propose a network “saturation-invasion” model of tau propagation in PCA (Fig. 5). In the earliest clinical stages of the PCA syndrome, tau likely originates in the occipital cortical regions corresponding to the visual network and the adjacent regions of the posterior DAN (e.g., superior parietal lobule, anterior MT+)13. Transsynaptic propagation of tau then occurs from these primary epicenters to other cortical regions through preferential, intra-network functional connections, which may be accelerated by neuronal hyperactivity and hyperconnectivity triggered by amyloid deposition55,62. Once the initial epicenters within the visual network and the posterior DAN become saturated with tau, this may lead to tau-induced intra-network functional hypoconnectivity33,63 as well as increased inter-network connectivity between the primary tau epicenters and other cortical regions that are connectivity hubs in the brain, potentially as a compensatory mechanism in an attempt to offload processing burden34,64,65. These regions may become secondary tau epicenters that further accelerate the spreading of tau throughout the cerebral cortex. Our finding identifying the posterior DMN as a presumptive secondary tau epicenter dovetails with evidence regarding these regions’ extensive connectivity with other connectivity hubs36,66,67, as well as evidence showing increased connectivity between the visual network and DMN in PCA34. Our hypothesis is also consistent with prior work showing that amyloid-dependent inter-network hyperconnectivity at baseline predicts greater longitudinal tau spread in preclinical AD55,68 and a recent framework for connectivity-based propagation of pathological proteins in neurodegenerative disease62. Taken together, the current findings extend the literature by suggesting a plausible spatiotemporal trajectory of tau pathology propagation in PCA through the differential contributions of multiple functional networks operating in sequential phases.

A network “saturation-invasion” model of tau propagation in PCA.

This schematic provides a simplified overview of a possible mechanism of longitudinal tau spread in PCA, which we have developed based on the present findings. A In PCA, tau pathology likely first develops in posterior cortical regions functionally corresponding to the visual network and dorsal attention network (DAN). B, C Tau may spread from these posterior cortical epicenters via preferential connections that exist within each network (intra-network connectivity; yellow arrows), starting with local connections and then followed by long-range connections (e.g., the intraparietal sulcus to the frontal eye fields within the DAN). D Once the epicenters are saturated with tau (darkest red), this may lead to weaker intra-network connectivity coupled with stronger inter-network connectivity (light blue arrows). This increased inter-network connectivity may subserve tau spread from the epicenters to major nodes of other networks showing hub properties, including the posteromedial and posterolateral cortices within the default mode network (DMN). E Once tau reaches one of these major hubs in the DMN, intra-network connectivity may again promote selective tau spread within the network. We focus here on the DMN as an illustrative example. However, there may exist parallel pathways of tau spread involving other association networks that contain cortical hub regions, such as the frontoparietal, salience, and cingulo-opercular networks36,67,87. The window of PCA syndrome examined in this study may represent a transitional period (e.g., between C and D) characterized by a shift in the involvement of primary and secondary tau epicenters.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of some limitations of the study. First, although PCA is a relatively rare syndrome, the modest sample size remains an important issue to address in future studies to replicate and expand our results. Our model emphasizes dynamic reconfiguration of brain network connectivity over the disease course and its influence on the trajectory of longitudinal tau spread in PCA. To formally test this hypothesis, future work needs to study samples of PCA participants across a wider symptomatic spectrum. Our study, based on two small yet independent cohorts of PCA participants, represents an important step toward future collaborative efforts involving multiple research centers across the world1. However, we were also limited in our ability to estimate participant-specific patterns of tau spread due to the analytical constraints. Larger, more diverse samples of individuals with PCA would afford additional analytical opportunities, including the examination of the role of demographic factors (e.g., sex, education) in moderating network vulnerability underlying tau spread69,70, longitudinal tau spreading trajectories by neuroanatomical subtypes of PCA2,6,8,10, and the relationship between imaging biomarkers of longitudinal tau spread and clinical impairment. It is possible, for example, that greater longitudinal tau spread from the posterior to anterior DAN, or from the primary to secondary epicenters, would be associated with progressively faster clinical decline across individual participants. Global clinical assessments like the CDR scores are well established and widely used as a clinical outcome measure in therapeutic and observational studies. However, such measures do not sufficiently capture the visuospatial impairment that is typically and prominently observed in early stages of PCA. Ongoing efforts include refining and validating a specific clinical instrument to fill this need (the visual impairment rating [VIR] scale7), which we hope will function as an adjunct to complement the traditional assessment tools and be sensitive to early cognitive dysfunction in PCA. Such tools, when ready for use by other clinicians and researchers, would pave the way for improving the accuracy of imaging-based clinical prognostication specifically tailored to individuals with PCA. Second, the lack of longitudinal FTP PET data in CU participants limited our ability to isolate syndrome-specific patterns of longitudinal tau accumulation and spread in PCA. Future studies should address this issue by explicitly modeling the interaction between time and groups29,30,53. Third, we estimated tau-spreading pathways solely based on FTP PET data. Future work might consider incorporating established measures of brain connectivity based on functional and diffusion MRI for more comprehensively characterizing longitudinal tau spread in PCA. Finally, while we focused in this study on identifying weighted out-degree hubs (i.e., sources) of longitudinal tau spread, future studies might also consider characterizing in-degree hubs (i.e., targets) in PCA. Such regions may represent “convergence zones” where multiple different tau-spreading pathways intersect. Clarification of in-degree hubs would provide additional mechanistic insight into our hypothesized model of multi-phase tau propagation in PCA.

In sum, our findings suggest that longitudinal tau spread in PCA may follow a hierarchical progression from primary epicenters within the visual network and DAN to secondary epicenters, including the posterior DMN, possibly mediated by dynamic shifts from intra-network to inter-network connectivity patterns. This mechanistic framework not only provides a comprehensive account of connectivity-driven tau propagation in PCA but also generates testable hypotheses for future longitudinal studies examining whether interventions targeting inter-network hyperconnectivity during critical clinical transitional periods could potentially modulate the rate and extent of pathological tau propagation across cortical regions. Investigations of other syndromic phenotypes of AD are additionally warranted to further clarify dissociable contributions of syndromically vulnerable networks and other networks that exhibit shared vulnerability across phenotypes (e.g., DMN).

Methods

Participants

All participants received a comprehensive clinical evaluation comprising a structured history obtained from both patient and informant to inform clinician scoring on the CDR scale71, and also underwent comprehensive neurological and psychiatric examinations and neuropsychological assessment. We additionally included a group of 24 amyloid-negative CU control participants32, all of whom had normal brain structure based on MRI and low cerebral amyloid based on quantitative analysis of PiB PET data (CL < 25). No longitudinal FTP PET data were available from CU participants. Individuals were excluded from our patient and control groups if they had a primary psychiatric or other neurologic disorder, including major cerebrovascular infarct or stroke, seizure, brain tumor, hydrocephalus, multiple sclerosis, HIV-associated cognitive impairment, or acute encephalopathy. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards for human research at the MGH (IRB protocol numbers: 2016P001421 and 2018P000859), UCSF (IRB protocol number: 10-00619), and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (IRB protocol number: 073H026) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their surrogates.

Neuroimaging data acquisition and preprocessing

Structural MRI data were acquired from each participant on either a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma Fit scanner using a T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid acquisition sequence (MPRAGE) (repetition time [TR] = 2300 ms, echo time [TE] = 2.98 ms, flip angle = 9°, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view [FOV] = 240 × 256 mm2) or on a 3 Tesla Siemens Tim Trio using a multi-echo MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2530 ms, TEs = 1.64/3.5/5.36/7.22 ms, flip angle = 7°, slice thickness = 1 mm, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2). Each participant’s (ME)MPRAGE data were processed via the longitudinal stream72 implemented in FreeSurfer v7.1.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). Specifically, MRI data from each time point were first cross-sectionally processed using FreeSurfer’s recon-all routine, after which an unbiased, within-subject template space and image were created using robust, inverse consistent registration73,74. Several processing steps, such as skull stripping, Talairach transforms, atlas registration, as well as spherical surface maps and parcellations, were then initialized with common information from the within-subject template72.

PET data were acquired using a Siemens ECAT HR + scanner or a GE Discovery MI scanner (MGH) and a Siemens Biograph PET/CT scanner at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (UCSF). For all participants, tau PET images were acquired from 80 to 100 min after the bolus injection of ~10.0 mCi of FTP (4 × 5 min frames). For MGH participants, amyloid PET data were acquired either from 90 to 110 min after the injection of ~8 mCi of FBB (4 × 5 min frames) or based on a 60 min dynamic acquisition after the injection of 8.5–10.5 mCi of PiB (69 frames; 12 × 15 s followed by 57 × 60 s). For UCSF participants, amyloid PET data were based on a 90 min dynamic acquisition after the injection of ~15 mCi of PiB (35 frames; 4 × 15, 8 × 30, 9 × 60, 2 × 180, 10 × 300 s, followed by 2 × 600 s). All PET data were reconstructed and attenuation corrected; each frame was evaluated to verify adequate count statistics, and interframe head motion was corrected. Individual FBB and FTP PET frames were aligned to the first via FSL FLIRT75 and were averaged. For PiB PET data, all frames were first aligned to the frame at 10 min post-injection, then the frames corresponding to 40–60 min (MGH) or 50–70 min (UCSF) were averaged.

Further processing of FTP PET data was performed via the PETSurfer tools76,77. Each participant’s FTP PET data were first rigidly co-registered to their anatomical volume from the same time point (aligned with the within-subject template), and the accuracy of cross-modal spatial registration was confirmed by visual inspection. FTP PET data were then corrected for partial volume effects. Specifically, based on each participant’s high-resolution tissue segmentation derived by the Desikan–Killiany atlas78, the symmetric geometric transfer matrix method was used to correct for spill-in and spill-out effects between adjacent brain tissue types, with a point spread function of 6 mm. Using partial volume-corrected data, we derived the FTP SUVR image per participant with the inferior cerebellar gray matter as a reference region. Finally, FTP SUVR maps were resampled to fsaverage space (163,842 vertices per hemisphere) and smoothed geodesically with an FWHM of 8 mm.

Prior work demonstrated high test-retest consistency of regional FTP SUVR measures in a mixed sample of AD participants and healthy controls, with intraclass correlations being ~0.99 across cortical regions of interest79. To address potential effects of participant cohort and PET scanner (MGH-Siemens-ECAT, MGH-GE-Discovery, UCSF-Siemens-Biograph), we performed harmonization of vertex-wise FTP SUVR data using ComBat, a technique that estimates site- and/or scanner-specific effects on features of interest using empirical Bayes, which are corrected via linear regression80. We modeled age and sex of each participant as covariates to preserve the variance potentially attributable to these factors. Each participant’s amyloid PET data were processed using published guidelines to quantify global cortical amyloid burden in CL units81,82.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 and MATLAB version R2023a (MathWorks, Inc., www.mathworks.com). Using individual baseline FTP SUVR maps as inputs, we constructed whole-cortex vertex-wise multivariable linear regression models to identify areas of the cerebral cortex where PCA participants showed elevated tau burden compared with CU participants. These models included baseline age and sex as covariates of no interest. Statistical significance was assessed with a vertex-wise intensity threshold at q < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) across both hemispheres. At each vertex, we calculated Cohen’s d as the difference in the estimated means of FTP PET SUVR between PCA and CU participants divided by the pooled standard deviation. Following prior work, we identified tau epicenters as cortical regions exhibiting the highest level of tau accumulation in PCA participants at baseline19,23,55,83. Specifically, tau epicenters in this study were defined as the top 10% vertices with the largest Cohen’s d effect sizes of differences in FTP SUVR between PCA and CU participants.

To examine the degree of hemispheric asymmetry in the magnitude of epicenter tau burden at baseline, we calculated the asymmetry index following prior work15. For each PCA participant, we first computed the mean FTP SUVR across the tau epicenter vertices separately for each hemisphere. The asymmetry index (%) was then calculated as 200 × (R−L)/(R + L), with more negative values indicating greater baseline epicenter tau burden in the left > right hemisphere and more positive values indicating greater baseline epicenter tau in the right > left hemisphere.

Next, to estimate the rate of longitudinal tau accumulation in PCA participants, we constructed vertex-wise linear mixed-effects models using the lmer function from the lme4 package (version 1.1.35.5)84. These models were fit by maximizing the restricted likelihood and initialized with time since baseline (in years), baseline age, and sex as fixed predictors with random intercepts and random slopes for time for each participant, with the outcome variable being FTP SUVR at each vertex. Our preliminary analysis found no evidence of statistically significant improvement in model fit due to the inclusion of random slopes. As such, all mixed-effects model results reported here are based on random intercept-only models. Statistical significance of the estimated rate of FTP SUVR change over time was assessed with a vertex-wise threshold of q < 0.05, FDR-corrected, across both hemispheres.

The linear mixed-effects framework enables identification of cortical regions where tau accumulation on average accelerates over time at the group level. This approach, however, provides no information about potential pathways along which tau propagates. To address this issue, we conducted a seed-based directional regression analysis that allows inference of temporal directionality in longitudinal tau spread across all individuals with PCA22. We first defined our seed regions as the tau epicenters, which we identified as exhibiting the largest effect sizes of group differences in FTP uptake between PCA and CU participants at baseline, as described above. We then constructed vertex-wise multivariable regression models to predict FTP SUVR at follow-up at each cortical vertex based on mean baseline FTP SUVR in tau epicenters, with baseline age, sex, and time since baseline as covariates. We refer to these models as forward models, as vertex-wise regression coefficients for epicenter FTP SUVR represent the extent to which epicenter tau at baseline predicts tau in other regions at follow-up. We additionally constructed regression models to retrospectively predict FTP SUVR at baseline at each cortical vertex based on mean follow-up FTP SUVR in tau epicenters, with baseline age, sex, and time since baseline as covariates. These models are referred to as backward models, as regression coefficients for epicenter FTP SUVR represent the extent to which epicenter tau at follow-up “predicts” tau in other regions at baseline (thus testing the strength of temporal associations in the reverse direction). Multicollinearity among predictor variables was minimal for both forward (mean = 1.44 ± 0.31) and backward (mean = 1.36 ± 0.19) models85. At each vertex, we then calculated the difference in regression coefficients for epicenter tau (Δb = bforward–bbackward), with more positive values indicating stronger directional preference for tau spreading from epicenters over time. To assess the statistical significance of vertex-wise Δb, we calculated Akaike information criterion (AIC) values at each vertex from its forward and backward models, where ΔAIC (AICforward–AICbackward) < −2 was considered evidence for significant improvement in model fit favoring the forward model86. We additionally thresholded the Δb map by only retaining the vertices where baseline epicenter tau significantly predicted follow-up tau at q < 0.05, FDR-corrected across both hemispheres. This ensured that evidence of epicenter-driven tau spread was evaluated in terms of temporal directionality as well as reliability of the forward association. A series of Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were conducted to examine the degree of hemispheric asymmetry in epicenter-driven tau spread.

Complementing a seed-based approach to the directional inference of tau spreading, we performed a data-driven analysis based on graph theory to identify cortical regions from which tau propagates to other regions over time. For this analysis, we first downsampled each PCA participant’s FTP SUVR maps to fsaverage5 space (10,242 vertices per hemisphere) for computational efficiency. Similarly to the seed-based directional regression analysis described above, we constructed vertex-wise regression models to predict follow-up FTP SUVR based on baseline FTP SUVR and vice versa, between every unique vertex-pair resulting from 20,484 cortical vertices (excluding ~1800 vertices corresponding to the medial wall). This procedure resulted in two vertex-wise directed graphs of the same size: (1) regression coefficients for tau representing forward models (Bforward) and (2) regression coefficients for vertex-wise tau representing backward models (Bbackward). We then performed edge-wise subtraction (Bforward–Bbackward) to generate a graph representing vertex-wise differences in regression coefficients for tau, which was thresholded to retain only positive values and by ΔAIC < −2 to ensure directional influences over tau propagation in all suprathreshold vertices. Finally, we computed the weighted out-degree of each vertex, defined as the sum of all edge weights in each row/vertex in this graph.

We used a permutation test to demonstrate that each vertex’s weighted directional influence was higher than expected given its number of outgoing connections. Specifically, for each of 5000 permutations, a random directed graph was generated based on the delta B matrix (Bforward−Bbackward) using a configuration model approach that preserved the observed binary degree sequences. Sources (rows) and targets (columns) were randomly rematched such that each vertex retained its number of edges, while the specific pattern of which vertices connected to which was randomized. Self-loops and duplicate edges were removed to ensure valid simple directed graphs. Edge weights were then randomly permuted across all edges and assigned to the rewired topology. Vertex-wise weighted out-degree was computed in each null network as the sum of all edge weights in each row, yielding a null distribution of 5000 weighted out-degree values per vertex. Observed weighted out-degree was compared against this null distribution at q < 0.05, FDR-corrected. Vertices exhibiting high weighted out-degree represent cortical hub regions where FTP SUVR at baseline explained that of many other regions at follow-up. All vertex-wise regression analyses included age, sex, and time since baseline as covariates.

To characterize the observed tau effects in terms of large-scale functional networks across these analyses, we utilized an established cortical parcellation with seven spatially non-overlapping networks50. In addition to the functional networks commonly exhibiting phenotypic vulnerability in PCA (visual network and DAN), we examined the involvement of the DMN, given the shared vulnerability of this network across clinical phenotypes of AD, including PCA32–35. Complementing vertex-wise statistical maps, we also report results summarized at the level of major nodes within each network. These nodes were defined by Yeo et al.50 via a connected component analysis.
