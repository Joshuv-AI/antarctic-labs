Clinical outcomes and costs associated with viral lower respiratory tract disease hospitalisations
Authors: Ekaterina Maslova, Malin Fagerås, Trung Tran, Benjamin Emmanuel, Mina Khezrian, Neil Branscombe, Susan Johnson, Gopal Dalal, Hashmath Ulla Syed, Nadir Yehya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn updated comprehensive assessment of the burden of hospitalised viral lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) is especially salient after the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, we aimed to comprehensively describe clinical characteristics and outcomes of US adults hospitalised with viral LRTD between 2015 and 2023.MethodsIn this retrospective analysis, adults hospitalised between 1 January 2015 and 31 March 2023 were identified using Optum’s deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database. Index date was the first International Classification of Diseases, 9th/10th Revision (ICD-9/10) code for viral LRTD hospitalisation. Outcomes included intensive care unit (ICU) admission, non-invasive ventilation (NIV), invasive mechanical ventilation/extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (IMV/ECMO), costs, 30-day mortality and 30-day readmissions. Outcomes were stratified by viral aetiology (ICD-9/10), clinical severity and time period (2015–2019; 2020–2021; 2022–2023). Time-to-event data were assessed using Kaplan-Meier estimates.ResultsOf 387 449 patients hospitalised for viral LRTD, 38.1% were admitted to ICU within 30 days of index (84.6% of these were admitted on index date); 8.4% and 8.7%, respectively, received NIV and IMV/ECMO within 30 days of index and 30-day mortality was 15.1%. Median (IQR) length of stay and mean (SD) hospitalisation costs were 7 (4–15) days and $42 752 ($44 841), respectively. Admission to ICU, mortality and costs were substantial across all aetiologies but peaked from 2020 to 2021; readmissions and costs remained high even into 2022–2023.ConclusionsSevere viral LRTD incurred substantial burden for US healthcare systems across all viral aetiologies, even in non-pandemic years, with frequent ICU admission and ventilation use, and with considerable associated costs. Readmission rates and costs remain high even after the peak of COVID-19.Keywords: Respiratory Infection, Viral infection, COVID-19

Introduction

Respiratory viruses, including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), parainfluenza, human metapneumovirus (hMPV), rhinovirus, coronavirus and adenovirus can lead to lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD).1,3 LRTD is associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly among older adults and those with underlying comorbidities.1 4 Severe viral LRTD is characterised by hypoxaemia requiring hospitalisation and oxygen supplementation therapy.5 6 According to 2019 estimates, LRTDs were associated with substantial global health burden and mortality.7

The emergence of COVID-19 also had a considerable impact on the global disease burden of viral LRTD. However, in the peri- and postpandemic clinical landscape, the overall burden of hospitalised LRTD is not well characterised. Furthermore, previous analyses of clinical characteristics and outcomes in severe viral LRTD often focus on specific viruses and subpopulations28,14 and do not fully capture the disease burden incurred across the broad range of known viral pathogens. Additionally, existing global reports often present viral and bacterial infection data collectively,11,15 giving an incomplete picture of the burden-associated predominantly by viruses. This is an important gap, as most viruses lack targeted therapies, and an improved understanding of their clinical and economic burden is requisite to identifying high-risk groups that may benefit from targeted therapies. SARS-CoV-2, influenza and RSV are among the most commonly monitored aetiologic causes of viral LRTD.11,15 However, the degree to which the COVID-19 pandemic changed the burden of illness of other viral LRTDs remains unclear.

With the ongoing development of novel therapies aimed at mitigating disease severity in patients with viral LRTD, evidence on the unmet need and disease burden in this patient population is urgently required. In this study, we aimed to provide an update on the epidemiology of adults hospitalised with viral LRTD by examining clinical characteristics, outcomes and economic burden in a real-world US setting. In order to provide a comprehensive picture of the viral LRTD burden prior to COVID-19, we examined patients with an index hospitalisation as early as 2015; in an effort to provide robust and current estimates, we also examined time periods early (2020–2021) and later (2022–2023) in the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a retrospective cohort study of US patients hospitalised with a viral LRTD from 1 January 2015 to 31 March 2023 using Optum’s deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (Optum CDM). Optum CDM is derived from a database of administrative health claims for over 65 million enrollees of large commercial and Medicare Advantage health plans. Eligible patients were at least 18 years of age on the date of the earliest viral LRTD-related hospitalisation (defined as index date) during the study period, had continuous insurance enrolment for at least 12 months before index (baseline period) and had at least 60 days of available follow-up enrolment. Viral LRTD hospitalisations were identified using virus-specific International Classification of Diseases, 9th/10th Revision (ICD-9/10) codes (online supplemental table S1) for the following aetiologies: coronavirus (including all coronavirus strains), influenza, RSV, hMPV, parainfluenza, rhinovirus, adenovirus, multiviral (consisting primarily of coronavirus and another viral aetiology) and other viral (includes enterovirus, varicella, measles, rubella and unknown viral aetiologies). Patients were followed from index date until the earliest of death, the date of last available data or the end of study observation period (31 May 2023). The primary analysis assessed outcomes 30 days after index date. A sensitivity analysis assessed outcomes 60 days post-index date.

Study variables

Demographic characteristics were captured at index admission. Clinical characteristics were determined by claims prior to the index date. Comorbidity burden was measured by using Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Prespecified comorbidities were assessed over variable lookback periods, which could extend prebaseline. Immunocompromised status was established using predefined criteria (online supplemental table S2).

Clinical outcomes including intensive care unit (ICU) admission, use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV), invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), mortality and readmissions were identified using revenue codes, Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System, Current Procedural Terminology, Fourth Edition codes, ICD-10 codes and Systemized Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms codes (online supplemental table S3–S6). Healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) measures included length of index hospitalisation (including both inpatient and skilled nursing facility stays—capped at 99th percentile (67 days)) and proportion of patients with all-cause and viral-LRTD-related readmission. Hospitalisation costs during the index hospitalisation were calculated (also capped at 67 days) based on costs reported by Optum CDM and were adjusted to 2023 US$ using the annual medical care component of the Consumer Price Index.

Outcomes were assessed in all eligible patients (overall cohort), in patients who progressed to IMV/ECMO during index hospitalisation (IMV/ECMO subgroup), and in patients who did not survive for 60 days post-index (non-survivor subgroup) (figure 1). As IMV/ECMO is indicated for patients with life-threatening respiratory conditions, these subgroups provided insight into the impact of disease severity. Analyses in the overall cohort were further stratified by viral aetiology (coronavirus, influenza, RSV, hMPV, parainfluenza, rhinovirus, adenovirus, multiviral and other) and by time period (1 January 2015–31 December 2019; 1 January 2020–31 December 2021 and 1 January 2022–31 May 2023, providing additional detailed information on the study population and clinical outcomes and burdens before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, respectively).

Attrition of study populations. ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; IMV, invasive mechanical ventilation; LRTD, lower respiratory tract disease.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (mean (SD)/(SE) or median (IQR)) and categorical variables (n (%)) were reported descriptively. Viral aetiologies and time periods were compared using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and χ2 tests with p values <0.05 denoting statistical significance. Time-to-event data were calculated using Kaplan-Meier estimates.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design and conduct of this research. All data in Optum’s deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database are Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996) compliant and deidentified to adhere with all relevant US regulations and privacy laws. As such, this study analysed only deidentified data, which are a priori exempt from institutional review board approval according to the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects ‘Common Rule’ (1991, revised 2018).

The mean age was 73 (SD 13.5) years (78.7% were aged ≥65 years), 65.6% were white, 53.1% were female and 21.0% were immunocompromised. Overall mean CCI at baseline was 3 (SD 3.0), and the mean number of pre-existing distinct comorbidities per patient was 10.0 (SD 5.0). Hypertension was the most frequently reported comorbidity (85.5%), followed by hyperlipidaemia (81.6%).

The number of patients with a viral LRTD hospitalisation was 49 203 in 2015–2019, 208 207 in 2020–2021 and 130 039 in 2022–2023. Influenza was the viral aetiology ICD-9/10 code most frequently associated with LRTD hospitalisation from 2015 to 2019 (71.2%) (online supplemental figure S1). Non-SARS-CoV-2 viruses account for a substantial minority (12.8%) of all LRTD-related aetiologies in 2022–2023, particularly influenza (7.5% of all hospitalisations) and RSV (1.9%).

ICU, NIV, IMV/ECMO and mortality within 30 days of index

In the 30 days after index hospitalisation, 38.1% of patients were admitted to the ICU (84.6% of these were admitted on index date), 8.4% of patients received NIV (77.2% on index date) and 8.7% received IMV/ECMO (86.8% on index date) (figure 2). Within the 30 days following index, 15.1% of patients died; 2.1% of these died on index date. Frequencies of ICU admission, NIV use, IMV/ECMO use and 30-day mortality were higher in the IMV/ECMO and non-survivor subgroups; mean time-to-event durations were also consistently shorter in these subgroups (figure 2).

The proportion of patients admitted to the ICU within 30 days of index ranged from 28.3% (rhinovirus) to 49.2% (adenovirus); the proportion of patients receiving NIV ranged from 5.9% (rhinovirus) to 12.9% (hMPV) and for IMV/ECMO, the proportion ranged from 1.1% (rhinovirus) to 14.7% (adenovirus). The proportion of patients who died within 30 days of index was higher for coronavirus (17.2%) than other aetiologies (p<0.05 for all) but was substantial across all aetiologies, ranging from 2.7% (rhinovirus) to 9.5% (adenovirus). Outcomes were also assessed across three time periods; proportion of patients admitted to the ICU, receiving NIV and receiving IMV/ECMO within 30 days of index were highest during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021) (table 2 and online supplemental table S7). Mortality within 30 days of index was also significantly higher in the 2020–2021 and 2022–2023 time periods compared with 2015–2019 (p<0.001 for both).

Results from the 60-day sensitivity analysis were comparable to the 30-day primary analysis (online supplemental figure S2); 19.0% of patients in the overall cohort died within 60 days of index, with a mean time to death of 19.7 (SD 13.9) days. The median length of hospital stay was 7 (IQR 4–15) days in the overall cohort and was longer in the IMV/ECMO (17 (10–28) days) and non-survivor (11 (6–19) days) subgroups. When stratified by viral aetiology, length of hospital stay was longer in patients with coronavirus (median (IQR): 8 (5–17) days; p<0.001 for all except adenovirus) and adenovirus (7 (5–14) days; p<0.05 for all except coronavirus and multiviral). Length of stay was also longer during 2020–2021 (median (IQR): 7 (4–14) days) and 2022–2023 (8 (5–17) days) compared with the prepandemic period (2015–2019: 5 (4–9) days; p<0.001 for both). When assessing all-cause readmission within 30 days of index date (excluding patients who died during their index hospital stay), 6.1% of patients in the overall cohort, 2.0% in the IMV/ECMO cohort and 4.7% in the non-survivor cohort experienced a readmission. The proportion of patients with any readmission within 30 days of index was broadly similar across viral aetiologies (7.7%–9.7%) but significantly lower for coronavirus compared with all other viral aetiologies except adenovirus (5.4%; p<0.05 for all except adenovirus). The proportion of patients with any readmission within 30 days of index was 9.3% during 2015–2019 and was significantly lower during 2022–2023 (8.0%) and 2020–2021 (4.2%) (p<0.001 vs 2015–2019) (table 3 and online supplemental table S7). For the 60-day sensitivity analysis, all-cause readmission rates were higher, but trends were comparable with the 30-day primary analysis (online supplemental table S9).

The mean hospitalisation cost per patient was $42 752 (SD $44 841) in the overall cohort (table 3 and online supplemental table S7); costs incurred by the IMV/ECMO and non-survivor subgroups were higher ($101 820 ($53 988) and $56 074 ($56 824), respectively). Mean index hospitalisation cost per patient was substantially higher for coronavirus ($47 383) compared with other viral aetiologies (ranging $15 935–$43 754; p<0.001 for all vs coronavirus). Mean index hospitalisation costs per patient increased from $23 097 during 2015–2019 to $48 382 during 2020–2021 (p<0.001). Costs subsequently decreased during 2022–2023 ($41 174) but remained higher than pre-COVID-19 pandemic levels (p<0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated the substantial clinical burden in US adults hospitalised with viral LRTD between 2015 and 2023, with poor clinical outcomes and considerable HCRU and associated costs. To our knowledge, this study is the first to provide a comprehensive evaluation of short-term clinical outcomes, HCRU and HCRU-related cost associated with viral LRTD hospitalisations among US adults across multiple viral aetiologies and timeframes, including during the COVID-19 pandemic.

As anticipated, hospitalisations for viral LRTD were greatly elevated during the COVID-19 pandemic peak years (2020–2021), compared with 2015–2019, and fell in 2022–2023. This postpeak pandemic fall in viral LRTD hospitalisation in 2022–2023 may be partly attributable to the decline in prevalence of non-SARS-CoV-2 viruses, possibly due to measures implemented in response to COVID-19.15 16 Following the pandemic peak years, SARS-CoV-2 remained the most frequently reported aetiology, associated with over 85% of LRTD hospitalisations. Compared with other aetiologies, coronavirus accounted for a significantly higher mortality within 30 days of admission, higher costs and longer hospital stays; it therefore seems reasonable to infer that coronavirus continues to incur the greatest healthcare burden of any viral aetiology during the postpandemic period. While SARS-CoV-2 prevalence remained high, the widespread testing and increased public and clinical awareness of the importance of testing promoted earlier in the pandemic were also likely to have endured into 2022–2023. A Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surveillance report showed comparable trends, with hospitalisation rates in adults being higher for coronavirus (7.5–239.5 per 100 000 population) than for influenza or RSV (0.4–67.0 and 0.3–20.0 per 100 000 population, respectively) in the postpeak pandemic period.17 Notably, however, more than 1 in 8 LRTD hospitalisations in the post-peak pandemic period were related to non-SARS-CoV-2 viral pathogens. It should be noted that readmission frequency appeared to be lower in patients hospitalised for LRTD with SARS-CoV-2 compared with other viral aetiologies. However, as mortality was higher in these patients, this may suggest that mortality was a competing event, with many of the most vulnerable patients who may otherwise have experienced readmission from a different virus dying during their index hospitalisation. We also note that age of admission for hospitalised LRTD did not differ across the time periods, suggesting that differences in case mix are not sufficient to explain the higher mortality during the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Almost 40% of patients in the overall cohort were admitted to the ICU within 30 days (ranging from 28.3% for rhinovirus to 49.2% for adenovirus), with most (84.6%) admitted on their index date. Although this is likely to indicate that patients hospitalised for viral LRTD were often critically ill at index hospitalisation, emergency department boarding could also be an influencing factor. If a patient’s condition worsened while awaiting inpatient accommodation, they may be transferred directly to the ICU.18,20 Notably, approximately 1 in 10 patients had a record of NIV or IMV/ECMO use and nearly 1 in 5 patients hospitalised with viral LRTD did not survive 60 days postindex date. We also noted some key characteristics associated with increased rates of hospitalisation; the geographical south of the US appeared to be over-represented, accounting for almost 45% of patients hospitalised for viral LRTD. This may reflect known healthcare inequities in southern regions.21 Black/African–American and Hispanic/Latino minorities also appeared to account for higher proportions of hospitalised patients than one may expect given the demographic characteristics of the USA as a whole. These two observations are likely to be connected, given that the US south contains higher proportions of Black/African–American and Hispanic/Latino residents than other US regions, and these minorities have been shown to have poorer healthcare outcomes.22 23 A subgroup of patients with fungal/bacterial coinfection at index were also identified; just under 17% of all patients hospitalised for LRTD had a record of fungal/bacterial coinfection, which is in line with previous observations in patients hospitalised with SARS-CoV-2.24 As anticipated, they experienced a higher frequency of clinical outcome events (ICU, NIV and IMV/ECMO use and mortality) than was seen with any individual viral aetiology; a recent retrospective single-centre study similarly reported higher disease severity and mortality in patients presenting at the emergency department with LRTD.25

Across all viral aetiologies, the proportion of patients admitted to the ICU within 30 days was consistently high; receipt of NIV or IMV/ECMO during hospitalisation was also broadly consistent across most aetiologies. Because severity and progression of viral LRTD is driven largely by a dysregulated immune response regardless of the underlying viral aetiology, one may hypothesise that the patient symptom experience and burden are broadly similar across aetiologies, and timely intervention is likely to be a more crucial clinical factor than viral identification. Reports directly comparing ICU admission, NIV use and IMV/ECMO use in viral LRTD across different viral aetiologies are limited. A study of US adults hospitalised with RSV or influenza reported median (range) length of hospitalisation stays of 4.5 (2–17) days and 4 (1–18) days for RSV and influenza, respectively, consistent with the values reported in our analysis; RSV patients more frequently required oxygen supplementation, relative to influenza patients.26 Our study further adds to the existing literature by providing an updated and comprehensive assessment of the burden of severe viral LRTD requiring hospitalisation across multiple viral aetiologies. While the proportion of patients who were admitted to ICU or received NIV or IMV/ECMO use increased slightly during the peak pandemic years, they generally returned to prepandemic levels in 2022–2023 despite coronavirus remaining the predominant aetiology. This may be due to improved treatments for SARS-CoV-2 (including vaccinations) that mitigated disease severity or alternatively may be due to reduced disease severity of subsequent viral variants. Mortality within 30 days was highest among patients with a coronavirus infection but remained substantial across all other viral aetiologies. Of note, a substantial number of patients with a coronavirus infection died before receiving IMV/ECMO. Mortality slightly decreased after the peak pandemic years but remained nearly two times as high as prepandemic level. Epidemics associated with seasonal SARS-CoV-2 species that reappeared once protective measures were lifted, combined with the resurgence of non-SARS-CoV-2 viruses,15 may partially explain this finding. Reports on mortality trends in the USA following the peak COVID-19 era remain scarce. One study in England in the years following the peak COVID-19 pandemic reported continued excess mortality among middle-aged and young adults, citing cardiovascular diseases as one of the primary causes.27 In the USA, the top two leading causes of death in 2022 among hospitalised elderly patients were heart diseases and malignant neoplasms; COVID-19, chronic lower respiratory diseases and influenza/pneumonia ranked third, sixth and seventh, respectively.28

Patients in the IMV/ECMO and in the non-survivor subgroups had higher average index hospitalisation costs compared with the overall cohort, consistent with the previous studies.29 30 Coronavirus was associated with the highest average costs. Importantly, the costs incurred by hospitalisations due to non-SARS-CoV-2 LRTD were also substantial. As expected, duration of hospitalisation and associated costs increased with disease severity and were highest during the peak years of the COVID-19 pandemic. While average costs per patient for LRTD hospitalisations decreased during 2022–2023, they remained higher than the costs incurred in the pre-COVID-19 period, consistent with other reports.31 32 As reference, the 2023 Milliman Medical Index report projected an annual healthcare cost for an average insured person in 2023 in the USA to be $7221 (increasing from $6472 in 2021), with inpatient services expected to account for 17% of the overall cost.33 The findings reported here more precisely quantify the short-term burden of severe viral LRTD on patients and healthcare systems and provide a better characterisation of patients hospitalised across viral aetiologies.

Our study is strengthened by use of a large claims database, which provided detailed information relating to HCRU and outcomes across a representative cohort. Assessing data over a long time period allowed the analysis of temporal trends, including the impacts of the peak period of COVID-19 and its immediate aftermath. Stratifying data by severity and across multiple viral aetiologies permitted identification of populations with higher burden relative to others. With nearly 90% of individuals in this analysis over age 55 years, our findings highlight the elderly population at increased risk for severe hospitalised viral LRTD.2,47 12

This study has some limitations. Optum CDM is based on insurance claims data, which does not capture the uninsured and underinsured patient population, those with lower insurance coverage, or those on Medicaid. Consequently, the reported clinical outcomes and HCRU trends based on this dataset may be a conservative estimate of the overall healthcare burden of severe viral LRTD healthcare since they do not include the uninsured or underinsured population who potentially incur greater burden and experience worse outcomes. Additionally, patients hospitalised for LRTD represent a heterogeneous group and we acknowledge that the presence of certain risk factors such as concomitant chronic conditions or immunocompromised status may predispose patients to worse outcomes and higher clinical and economic burden.36 Notably, there is some suggestion that immunocompromised status and some chronic conditions may have been more prevalent in patients hospitalised for LRTD in the postpandemic era, compared with 2015–2019. We were unable to stratify our findings by immunocompromised status or comorbidities, but the proportion of patients with immunocompromised status was broadly in line with previous observations,37 and did not appreciably differ between viral aetiologies or time periods. Moreover, claim-based data lack clinical details (such as laboratory values or physician notes) captured in electronic medical records, and future studies (which could potentially incorporate multivariable analyses assessing clinical risk factors) should incorporate these data. However, we used ICU admission and use of respiratory supports as surrogates for disease severity. Notably, details on testing procedures received by patients were not available, and consequently a selection bias seems feasible whereby patients with more severe disease were more likely to have received multipathogen viral testing. Although most hospitalisations in the overall cohort were due to coronavirus, one in eight were due to other viral aetiologies. A decline in the circulation of non-SARS-CoV-2 infectious agents beginning in March 2020, and a subsequent decrease in respiratory infections caused by other viruses like influenza or RSV, has been reported elsewhere.15 This may be due to reduced transmission of non-coronavirus infections as a consequence of public health measures introduced to mitigate the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 or may reflect reduced non-SARS-CoV-2 testing after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, caution must be taken when interpreting the study results for different viral aetiologies during the periods between 2020 and 2023, including direct comparison of viral aetiologies. Additional limitations are misclassified or missing data inherent to administrative claims studies, including incorrect coding particularly during the peak pandemic period.

In conclusion, severe viral LRTD represents a significant healthcare burden in the USA, consistent across viral aetiologies, with high mortality and significant rates of readmission. Viral LRTDs are a substantial driver of costs and HCRU. It remains important to understand the determinants of disease progression within the acute setting. Improved treatments remain crucial to reduce the burden on patients, caregivers and healthcare systems.
