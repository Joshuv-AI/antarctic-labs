1. Executive Summary

Over the course of the award, the Dartmouth team has made foundational contributions to quantum
simulation, optimization, and the crucial interface between theory and practice, directly addressing the
goals of the DOE ASCR FAR-QC program. Our work has resulted in significant advances in
resource-efficient quantum algorithms, a deeper understanding of fundamental computational limits, and
the development of tangible software tools for the scientific community.

In Quantum Simulation Thrust, we pioneered novel fermion-to-qubit encodings that dramatically reduce
qubit requirements, culminating in a unifying framework for custom fermion-to-qubit transforms and an
invited technical review for Nature Reviews Physics. We also investigated the fundamental topological
properties of quadratic bosonic systems, leading to the first theoretical prediction of a quantized
transverse response—a bosonic analog of the integer quantum Hall effect. Furthermore, we resolved a
key open question in measurement-based quantum computing by using a fermion-based analysis to
prove that circle graph states are not a universal resource.

In the Optimization Thrust, we have developed Quiqbox.jl, an open-source Julia package for basis set
optimization, providing an essential tool to adapt quantum chemistry for fixed-size NISQ devices. This
work was complemented by complexity-theoretic results that clarified the fundamental intractability of
electronic structure and the limitations of using quantum resources for problems like Hartree-Fock.

The project produced over 20 scientific products and, most importantly, supported the training and
successful Ph.D. completion of two graduate students, who are now key contributors to the quantum
information science (QIS) workforce.

2. Project Accomplishments by Objective

Our research efforts under the FAR-QC program were highly productive, yielding new algorithms,
theoretical insights, and practical tools. The accomplishments are detailed below, organized by the
primary research thrusts of the Dartmouth team.

2.1. Objective: Advance Quantum Simulation Algorithms

Our work in quantum simulation focused on two key areas: developing resource-efficient methods for
fermionic systems and exploring novel topological phenomena in bosonic systems.

 ● Pioneering Resource-Efficient Fermionic Encodings: A central theme of our work was to reduce
 the substantial qubit overhead required for fermionic simulation. We began by generalizing the
 Bravyi-Kitaev Superfast (BKSF) encoding, creating a scheme that could simultaneously simulate
 fermionic physics and protect against single-qubit errors (Setia et al., 2019). Our analysis
 revealed that its performance is optimal for localized single-particle states, common in lattice
 models (Chien et al., 2019). Building on this, we developed a comprehensive, customizable
 framework for designing spin-to-fermion transforms, allowing for a direct trade-off between qubit
 count and circuit depth (Chien et al., 2020). Further innovations included a "compact encoding"
 technique for fixed-particle-number Fock spaces (Harrison et al., 2022) and a novel "Sierpinski
 Triangle" transform (Harrison et al., 2024). This entire body of work, representing a major

 2

 contribution of the grant, was summarized in an invited Nature Reviews Physics article (currently
 in press), solidifying our leadership in this domain.
 ● Uncovering Novel Topology in Bosonic Systems: Led by co-PI Viola, we conducted a deep
 investigation into the physics of quadratic bosonic Hamiltonians (QBHs). Our analysis established
 fundamental no-go theorems proving the absence of certain topological features in gapped
 bosonic systems, in stark contrast to their fermionic counterparts (Xu et al., 2020). In parallel, we
 showed that dynamically stable, number non-conserving QBHs can always be mapped to a
 simpler, number-conserving dual system, a result with significant implications for analog
 simulation (Flynn et al., 2020). This research culminated in the theoretical prediction of a
 quantized transverse response in a 2D bosonic system—a bosonic analog of the quantum Hall
 effect. This phenomenon is tied to a non-trivial bosonic topological invariant and represents a
 realizable transport signature of non-Hermitian dynamics in photonic or magnonic systems.
 ● Defining Fundamental Limits of Simulation: We clarified the boundaries of quantum advantage by
 investigating computational complexity. In collaboration with USC, we showed that for the
 Hartree-Fock problem, the core difficulty lies in the optimization landscape, limiting both classical
 and quantum solvers (Gulania & Whitfield, 2021). We also sharpened the understanding of
 electronic structure complexity by proving its intractability even for fixed basis sets (O'Gorman et
 al., 2022). More recently, through a collaboration with UT Austin and Sandia, we used a mapping
 between Gaussian fermionic states and circle graph states to resolve an open question, proving
 that these states are not universal for measurement-based quantum computation (Harrison et al.,
 2025).

2.2. Objective: Develop Quantum-Inspired Optimization Methods

Our optimization efforts focused on creating practical tools for near-term devices and applying novel
computational paradigms.

 ● Developing a Practical Tool for Basis Set Optimization: To address a key challenge for NISQ-era
 quantum chemistry, we developed Quiqbox.jl, an open-source Julia package for basis set
 generation and optimization (Wang & Whitfield, 2023). Unlike conventional methods where basis
 sets can be expanded for a given problem, quantum algorithms must operate within the fixed
 qubit count of the hardware. Quiqbox.jl provides the necessary flexibility to tailor basis sets to
 the device, a critical step for practical quantum advantage. This tool was presented at a joint
 ARQC/FAR-QC meeting and is being considered for integration into the collaboration's code
 stack.
 ● Applying Machine Learning to Physical Systems: We explored the intersection of machine
 learning and quantum chemistry by designing a method to learn the Kohn-Sham potential directly
 from the dynamics of time-dependent systems. This demonstrates a promising, data-driven
 approach to a foundational problem in density functional theory (Yang & Whitfield, 2023).

2.3. Objective: Bridge the Theory-Practice Interface

A cross-cutting goal was to translate our theoretical advances into practical strategies for NISQ
computers.

 ● Designing Concrete Resource Reduction and Mitigation Strategies: Early in the project, we
 demonstrated how to leverage molecular point group symmetries to "taper" or remove qubits in
 VQE algorithms without affecting the result, providing a concrete, low-overhead resource-saving
 technique (Setia et al., 2020). Later, we conducted a systematic study of the effects of noise on

 subspaces and provided crucial insights for selecting encodings for real hardware (Chien et al.,

3. Project Impact

The research conducted under this FAR-QC award has generated significant and lasting impacts on the
principal discipline of quantum information science, on adjacent scientific fields, on the development of a
national quantum workforce, and on the research and educational infrastructure at Dartmouth College.

3.1. Impact on the Principal Discipline of Quantum Information Science (QIS)

 ● Establishing a New Paradigm for Fermionic Simulation: Our most significant contribution was the
 development of a comprehensive framework for fermion-to-spin mappings. This work moved
 beyond standard methods, providing a customizable approach that connects simulation with
 quantum error correction. The impact is evidenced by its adoption by other theoretical and
 experimental research groups and has been synthesized in a forthcoming invited review article
 for Nature Reviews Physics.
 ● Opening New Directions in Bosonic Topology: Our theoretical investigation into quadratic bosonic
 systems has opened a new research direction, culminating in the first-time prediction of a
 quantized transverse response in a non-interacting bosonic system. This provides an
 experimentally realizable target for validating non-Hermitian dynamics.
 ● Providing Rigorous Assessments of Quantum Advantage: Our research has served to both seed
 optimism and provide necessary rigor regarding the application of quantum computers, helping to
 delineate which problems are fundamentally hard for both classical and quantum computers and
 guiding the community toward more promising applications for near-term devices.

3.2. Impact on Other Scientific Disciplines

 ● Computational Chemistry and Materials Science: The open-source Quiqbox.jl package
 provides a tangible tool for the chemistry community, enabling flexible basis set design that is
 essential for adapting algorithms to the fixed-size constraints of today's quantum hardware.
 ● Theoretical Computer Science & Experimental Physics: Our work on QMA-completeness
 contributes to fundamental complexity theory, while our predictions for bosonic systems provide
 direct targets for experimental validation in photonics and magnonics.
 ● STEM Education: We have promoted a "probability-first" pedagogical approach to quantum
 mechanics through a published book chapter and new course materials, aimed at improving
 conceptual understanding for the next generation of scientists.

3.3. Impact on Human Resource Development

 ● Advanced Graduate Training: The grant directly supported the successful completion of two Ph.D.
 dissertations (Dr. Riley W. Chien and Dr. Vincent P. Flynn), with a third student (Brent Harrison)
 nearing completion. Connections made through the FAR-QC project were instrumental in placing
 other PhD students into industrial internships.
