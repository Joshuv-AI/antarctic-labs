Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica essencial oil enhances proliferation and telomerase activity in senescent mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Nahid Askari, Amirhesan Yahyapour, Morvarid Bayat, Setayesh Sadat Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging impairs stem cell function, diminishing tissue regeneration. Dental pulp-derived mesenchymal stem cells (DPSCs) hold significant regenerative potential but are susceptible to senescence. Natural compounds like essential oils (EOs) offer low-toxicity strategies to enhance stem cell function. This study evaluated the in vitro senescence-modulating effects at the cellular level effects of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica green hull essential oil (PaEO) on senescent DPSCs.Methods and resultsPaEO was extracted via hydrodistillation and characterized by GC-MS, identifying 22 major components (e.g., limonene, linalool, thymol, α/β-pinene). DPSCs were isolated from healthy third molars, cultured, and treated with varying PaEO concentrations. MTT assay and population doubling time (PDT) assessed viability and proliferation. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining quantified senescent cells. Real-time PCR measured telomere-related genes (TRF1, RAP1). PaEO treatment significantly enhanced DPSC viability and proliferation dose-dependently. The highest concentration (1000 µg/mL) increased viability by 18% and reduced senescent cells (SA-β-gal positive) by 19.9%. Furthermore, PaEO upregulated the expression of TRF1 and RAP1 genes, indicating improved telomere maintenance and genomic stability.ConclusionPistacia atlantica subsp. mutica essential oil exhibits potent anti-senescence effects on DPSCs. It enhances cell viability and proliferation, reduces senescence markers, and modulates telomere-related gene expression. These findings suggest that PaEO exerts senescence-modulating effects at the cellular level in DPSCs. Further in vivo and clinical studies are warranted to validate these promising in vitro results.Keywords: Anti-aging, Pistacia atlantica subsp. Mutica, Essential oil, DPSCs, GC-MS

Background

Aging is a complex and inevitable biological process characterized by a progressive decline in physiological function and increased susceptibility to disease. This process, resulting from the accumulation of molecular and cellular damage over time, leads to impaired homeostasis and a heightened risk of developing age-related diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and diabetes. Understanding the mechanisms of aging and finding ways to slow its pace or ameliorate its adverse outcomes has become a major challenge in global health [1].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have garnered widespread attention in regenerative medicine and anti-aging studies due to their high self-renewal capacity, multi-lineage differentiation potential into various immunomodulatory properties, and cell types such as bone, fat, and cartilage. By secreting trophic factors and signaling molecules, these cells can repair the cellular microenvironment and enhance tissue function [2]. DPSCs, a type of MSC, are readily obtainable from extracted teeth and have shown significant potential in treating various conditions, including degenerative and age-related diseases [3]. However, like other cells, DPSCs are also subject to the aging process, which can negatively impact their proliferative capacity, differentiation potential, and ultimately, their therapeutic potential [4]. Therefore, finding strategies to preserve or improve the function of DPSCs in the face of aging-related stress is of paramount importance. In this study, aging-related changes in DPSCs were evaluated through senescence-associated cellular features rather than through induced, replicative, or donor chronological aging models.

Medicinal plants have long been utilized in traditional medicine as a rich source of bioactive compounds for treating a wide range of diseases. These compounds, including polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids, possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anti-cancer properties [5]. Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica, also known as wild pistachio, is a species in the Anacardiaceae family, endemic to arid and semi-arid regions, including Iran [6]. Traditionally, this plant has been used to treat ailments such as gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory conditions, and inflammation. Scientific studies have also confirmed the pharmacological properties of various parts of this plant, including its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities [7].

Essential oils are complex and volatile mixtures of chemical compounds extracted from various parts of plants. Owing to their broad spectrum of biological activities, including potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties, these oils have gained increasing attention in the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries [8]. The pistachio hull, an agricultural byproduct that is typically discarded, can be a valuable source for extracting bioactive compounds with high therapeutic potential [9].

The TRF1 protein binds to telomeres and plays a role in maintaining their stability and regulating their length. Increased expression of the TRF1 gene can indicate greater activity in telomere maintenance and genomic stability, whereas its downregulation may lead to telomeric instability and the induction of apoptosis [10]. Meanwhile, RAP1 is a member of the shelterin complex, which is involved in telomere structure and function. Upregulation of RAP1 can be effective in the negative regulation of telomere length and protection of chromosome ends, while its reduction may be associated with telomere elongation or telomeric dysfunction [11]. As a housekeeping gene, ACTB is involved in many vital cellular processes, and its expression is relatively stable; for this reason, it is used as an internal control for the normalization of target gene expression [12].

Given the potential antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica and the key role of oxidative stress and inflammation in the process of cellular senescence, this study was designed to investigate the anti-aging effects of the essential oil from its green hull on DPSCs. The evaluation will focus on the impact of this essential oil on key markers of cellular aging, including cell proliferation and viability, telomerase activity, the expression of genes related to senescence and oxidative stress, and the differentiation capacity of DPSCs under induced senescence conditions. This research could offer novel insights into the therapeutic potential of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica for anti-aging interventions, particularly concerning the preservation and rejuvenation of stem cells.

Methodology

Preparation and extraction of wild pistachio hull essential oil

The green hulls of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica (accession number KF 1136) were collected from Rabar County, Kerman province, Iran, between late July and late October 2023. Collection was conducted on public land with formal authorization from the Natural Resources Department of Kerman Province, the governing body responsible for such areas, and carried out in accordance with the “Law on Protection and Exploitation of Forests and Rangelands” approved by the National Consultative Assembly. The plant material was formally authenticated by botanists at the Rafsanjan Pistachio Research Center, who confirmed its identity as Pistacia atlantica, commonly known as the Baneh or wild pistachio tree. Additionally, a voucher specimen (accession number KF 1136) has been deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Kerman University of Medical Sciences. Following collection, the hulls were spread on a substrate in the shade and air-dried at room temperature, after which the dried portions were stored in a dark place until further use.

The essential oil was extracted from the pistachio hulls using a Clevenger-type apparatus. To this end, 150 g of dried pistachio hulls were mixed with 2000 mL of distilled water. The contents of the flasks were boiled for 4 h to perform the extraction. After collection, the extracted essential oil was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual moisture and stored in dark glass vials at -20 °C.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis

The GC-MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890 A system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m length × 0.25 mm internal diameter × 0.25 μm film thickness). The system was coupled to an Agilent 5977B MSD quadrupole mass detector and a computer furnished with the Wiley 7n library. The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C for 5 min, then ramped to 250 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min, and finally held at 250 °C for 10 min. The injector temperature was 250 °C, and the injection volume was 0.1 µL with a 1:50 split ratio. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The ionization potential was 70 eV, the ionization current was 150 µA, and the mass range scanned was 35–465 m/z. Compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with the data available in the Wiley 7n library and by their Retention Index (RI). The RI for each compound was calculated using a homologous series of n-alkanes (C8–C40). The relative amount of each constituent was determined by calculating the peak area percentage from the chromatogram without using a calibration factor.

Cell culture and maintenance of DPSCs

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Graduate University of Advanced Technology, Kerman, Iran, under the ethical approval number “03.94323” at KGUT. Following ethical guidelines, dental pulp was extracted from healthy third molars of young adult donors who provided informed consent. The collected teeth were disinfected with povidone-iodine and then cracked to remove the pulp tissue for further analysis.

The pulp was minced into small fragments and subsequently digested with a collagenase/dispase enzyme solution. The extracted cells were cultured in Alpha-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and antibiotics (typically 1% penicillin-streptomycin). The cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every two days, and cell growth was monitored daily. Colonies typically formed within 7 to 10 days. Upon reaching 80–90% confluency, the cells were passaged and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen using a freezing medium containing DMSO [13].

MTT and PDT assay

Cell viability and the cytotoxicity of the P. atlantica subsp. mutica essential oil on DPSCs were assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells per well. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were treated with various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 µg/mL) of the essential oil for an additional 24 h.

Following the treatment period, the medium was discarded, and 100 µL of fresh culture medium along with 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS; ATR-MED, Iran) were added to each well. The plates were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator (BINDER, Germany), allowing for the formation of purple formazan crystals. Subsequently, the MTT solution was removed, and 100 µL of DMSO (Merck) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plates were gently agitated on a shaker to ensure complete homogenization of the contents. The optical density (OD) was measured at wavelengths of 570 nm and 630 nm using a microplate reader (Iranian Nano Mahan), and the percentage of cell viability was calculated relative to the control group. Finally, to evaluate the effect of the essential oil on the proliferation rate of DPSCs, their PDT was estimated from the results according to the following formula: Population Doubling Time = CT / log (N1 / N0) × 3.31 Where CT is the duration of the experiment (h), N0 is the initial number of cells, and N1 is the final number of cells. In this formula, the constant 3.31 represents 1/log10(2), which converts the logarithmic increase in cell number into the number of cell doublings.

Gene expression analysis by real-time PCR

The Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) reaction was performed using the (Takara, Japan) kit and a (Thermocycler Model) thermocycler system in 96-well plates. Cells were treated with various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 µg/mL) of the pistachio essential oil for 24 h. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate. The untreated control group consisted of cells cultured in medium alone. The final reaction volume for RT-qPCR was 20 µL, comprising 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (Pars Tous, Iran), 1 µL of cDNA, 1 µL of the forward and reverse primer mix (final concentration of 0.5 µM for each primer), and 8 µL of nuclease-free water. Specific primers for the target genes, TRF1 and RAP1, were used, with the housekeeping gene ACTB serving as the internal control for normalization. Primer sequences were designed using AlleleID software, verified with GeneRunner software, and then custom-synthesized by Pishgam (Iran). The primer specifications are listed in Table 1. The data obtained from the instrument were analyzed using the ΔΔCt (delta-delta Ct) method.

SA-β-gal staining

To perform this assay, DPSCs were treated with various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000 µg/mL) of the pistachio essential oil for 24 h at 37 °C. Following incubation and a washing step, the cells were fixed with 2% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature (25 °C). For staining, the cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C (in a non-CO₂ incubator) with a freshly prepared SA-β-gal staining solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). This solution contained 1 mg/mL of X-Gal in a pH 6.0 buffer, which included 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide, and 2 mM MgCl2. Senescent cells were subsequently identified as blue-stained cells under a light microscope [14].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v.9). Ordinary two-way ANOVA was used to compare groups and to evaluate the effects of treatment and experimental conditions, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. The significance level (p-value) used for all tests was set at 0.05.

Real-time PCR results

Gene expression analysis revealed that the treatment of DPSCs with Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica essential oil resulted in a concentration-dependent upregulation of both TRF1 and RAP1 genes. This increased expression was observed at all tested concentrations compared to the untreated control group. The upregulation of TRF1 was slightly more pronounced than that of RAP1. Collectively, these findings indicate the potential of the essential oil to enhance mechanisms related to telomere stability and counteract cellular senescence in mesenchymal stem cells (Fig. 4).

SA-β-gal staining

The anti-senescence effects of the pistachio essential oil on DPSCs, assessed by SA-β-gal staining, revealed a significant, concentration-dependent decrease in the percentage of β-gal-positive (β-gal+) cells compared to the control group. In the control group, 32.5% of the cells were β-gal-positive. Treatment with the 50 µg/mL concentration of the essential oil reduced the percentage of senescent cells to 28.7%, representing an 11.7% decrease relative to the control. This effect was markedly enhanced with increasing concentrations; at 100, 200, 500, and 1000 µg/mL, the percentage of senescent cells dropped to 24.3%, 19.8%, 15.2%, and 12.6%, respectively. The most substantial reduction was observed at the 1000 µg/mL concentration, resulting in a 19.9% decrease in β-gal+ cells compared to the control group (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In recent decades, extensive efforts have been made to enhance the therapeutic potential of DPSCs. However, conventional methods, such as the use of chemical compounds or genetic manipulations, are often associated with challenges including adverse immune responses, the risk of tumorigenicity, and cellular damage [20]. In this context, natural compounds have emerged as promising alternatives for stimulating proliferation and inhibiting senescence in MSCs.

The present study demonstrated that the essential oil of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica not only enhances the viability of DPSCs but also effectively inhibits the cellular senescence process. These findings were corroborated by a significant reduction in the PDT, a decreased percentage of senescent cells, and an increased expression of genes related to senescence inhibition, all of which occurred concomitantly with the rising concentration of the essential oil.

Cellular senescence is a complex, multifactorial process regulated at the molecular level, partly through mechanisms involving the Shelterin complex and telomere stability [21]. A well-established challenge in regenerative medicine is the diminished proliferative capacity of stem cells associated with increasing donor age [22, 23]. The findings of this study suggest that the essential oil from pistachio hulls can act as an anti-senescence agent, potentially mitigating this limitation.

This effect is likely linked to the potent antioxidant properties of the plant, particularly its green hull, which is known to be a rich source of phenolic compounds [24]. Given that oxidative stress and the production of free radicals play a crucial role in cellular senescence, the observed effects of the Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica essential oil can be partly attributed to its capacity to neutralize these damaging agents [25].

Plant-derived compounds exhibit significant senescence-modulating potential through diverse biological mechanisms. Many plant extracts and phytochemicals counter cellular senescence by reducing oxidative stress; they prevent damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids through the direct neutralization of free radicals and by upregulating endogenous antioxidant systems, such as activating the Nrf2 pathway [26, 27].These compounds also modulate inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines and factors associated with the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype [28].

Critically, certain plant-based compounds activate longevity pathways (e.g., AMPK/SIRT1), enhance proteostasis and autophagy to clear damaged cellular components, promote mitochondrial biogenesis, and in some cases, preserve telomere integrity or telomerase activity in stem cells [29]. Collectively, these multi-faceted actions delay age-related functional decline at both cellular and organismal levels.

Previous studies have also highlighted the positive role of plant extracts in enhancing the proliferation and maintaining the potential of pluripotent stem cells, and have even noted their anti-cancer properties [30]. Our findings align with this body of research, suggesting that the essential oil of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica can influence DPSCs via multiple pathways, although their precise elucidation warrants further investigation. The use of natural compounds such as this essential oil holds the potential to provide methods with lower toxicity and higher efficacy for combating cellular senescence in MSCs. This is of particular importance given the existing challenges in the commercialization and clinical application of medicinal plants in cell therapy. These challenges include the variability in chemical composition depending on the plant’s source and the extraction method used, as well as their impact on key senescence-regulating pathways [20].

As the GC-MS analysis revealed, the pistachio hull essential oil contains a diverse composition of natural chemical compounds. The presence of these constituents can explain the significant senescence-modulating effects observed in the present study on DPSCs, including improved cell viability and proliferation, a reduction in senescence markers, and favorable changes in the expression of related genes.

The presence of several compounds with known biological properties in the sample can account for these senescence-modulating effects. For example, Limonene, one of the major monoterpenes identified in this essential oil, has demonstrated potent antioxidant properties in previous studies. It has been reported that limonene can protect cells against oxidative stress and prevent the premature senescence it induces [31]. Furthermore, Linalool, another identified compound, is known for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential. Since chronic inflammation and oxidative stress are key drivers of the cellular aging process, Linalool’s ability to modulate these pathways may have contributed to the protective and anti-aging effects observed in DPSCs [32]. The presence of the phenolic compound Thymol in the essential oil is also notable. Phenolic compounds are widely studied for their antioxidant and cytoprotective activities. Thymol, in particular, has been shown to prevent DNA damage and improve cellular responses to stress [33]. Other identified monoterpenes, such as alpha-Pinene and beta-Pinene, also have reported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [34]. These compounds could potentially contribute to the overall protective effects of the essential oil on DPSCs. The chemical profile of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica green hull essential oil revealed in this study, characterized by dominant monoterpenes such as α-pinene, limonene, and linalool, aligns with the established phytochemical signature of this species. This consistency is exemplified by the work of Rezaie et al. (2015), which identified a similar volatile composition in Bene hull oil and, critically, correlated this profile with significant in vitro antioxidant and antibacterial potency [15]. The presence of these specific bioactive compounds provides a compelling phytochemical rationale for our observed biological effects. Monoterpenes like α-pinene and limonene, along with phenolic components such as thymol, are increasingly recognized in the literature not only for their direct radical-scavenging capacity but also for their ability to modulate key cellular pathways involved in stress response and genomic maintenance [35, 36]. Importantly, these pathways, particularly those regulated by sirtuins and involved in oxidative stress response, are central to the establishment and maintenance of the cellular senescence program [35]. The broader pharmacological context of Pistacia species further supports the bioactivity of such compounds [37]. Consequently, it is hypothesized that the anti-senescence effects of PaEO on DPSCs, manifested as enhanced proliferative capacity, reduced senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity, and upregulation of telomere-regulatory genes (TRF1, RAP1), are likely mediated by the synergistic action of its constituent monoterpenes and phenols. These compounds may collectively attenuate oxidative stress and promote genomic stability, thereby mitigating a primary driver of stem cell functional decline.

Therefore, although determining the precise contribution of each of these compounds to the observed senescence-modulating effects requires further studies with purified compounds, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the positive effects observed on DPSCs including an improved proliferative profile, reduced senescence markers, and modulated gene expression result from the combined and possibly synergistic action of this suite of phytochemicals. The phytochemical profile of the pistachio hull essential oil highlights its potential as a natural agent for countering cellular senescence, particularly in the context of preserving stem cell function. Despite the promising results, the present study had its limitations. Our focus was confined to only two key genes in the aging pathway, whereas aging is a complex network of molecular interactions. Furthermore, the in vitro nature of this study, although essential for preliminary investigations, cannot fully simulate the complex conditions and systemic responses of a living organism.

Therefore, future research should conduct a more comprehensive investigation of the molecular mechanisms involved in the anti-aging effects of the essential oil, including assessing the expression profiles of genes and proteins related to senescence and oxidative stress signaling pathways. Moreover, evaluating the effects of this essential oil in animal models of aging or age-related diseases will be a crucial step toward understanding its therapeutic potential under in vivo conditions. It should be noted that the present findings reflect modulation of senescence-associated features in DPSCs and do not represent systemic or organismal anti-aging effects.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that the essential oil of Pistacia atlantica subsp. mutica hull, a natural compound with a history of safe oral consumption in traditional medicine, possesses significant potential to counteract the senescence and enhance the survival of DPSCs. It appears that these positive effects are mediated through the regulation of genes associated with telomere stability in these cells. Given the growing public interest in medicinal plants, these properties make the aforementioned essential oil a promising candidate for the development of novel and more effective oral therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating the aging process in mesenchymal stem cells and improving their applications in regenerative medicine. However, for the definitive confirmation of these findings and a more comprehensive assessment of its therapeutic potential, further studies involving animal models and, ultimately, rigorous clinical trials are essential.
