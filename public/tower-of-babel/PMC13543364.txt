Nurses’ communication network position and perceptions of teamwork during evidence-based practice implementation
Authors: Élise N. Arsenault Knudsen, Dominique Jamison, Erin Jonaitis, Hyeonmi Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTeamwork and communication are essential to ensuring the provision of high-quality, safe, evidence-based nursing care. Open and effective communication among members of a team is recognized to facilitate implementation of evidence-based practices (EBPs) and communication is an essential element of teamwork; however, how these factors relate to one another during implementation is unknown. This study was designed to fill this gap by concurrently exploring nurses’ perceptions of teamwork and communication within the context of the implementation of EBPs. The purpose of this study was to test the relationships between the positionality of nurses within communication networks for practice change and their perceptions of teamwork.MethodsThis exploratory, descriptive, cross-sectional survey study collected perceptions of teamwork, using the Nursing Teamwork Survey, and communication network data, using a sociometric survey. Teamwork scores, centrality and coreness of participants across two intensive care units (ICUs) were calculated. Data were analyzed across the whole network and subnetworks. Relationships between centrality measures and teamwork scores were analyzed using univariate regression models; coreness and teamwork perceptions were compared using t-tests. The assortativity coefficient was calculated to determine homophily. Data were collected between January to March 2019.ResultsAcross the two ICUs, 145 nurses reported overall high perceptions of teamwork (mean = 4.04). Few significant relationships were seen between communication centrality metrics or coreness and teamwork subscale measures within the whole network nor subnetworks. The assortativity coefficients suggest that those who are closely connected in these communication networks do not have similar perceptions of teamwork. The hypothesis that members central to the communication network would hold higher perceptions of teamwork was not supported in this analysis.ConclusionsAlthough teamwork and communication are each established factors known to influence the implementation process of EBPs, this study did not reveal a clear relationship between nurses’ positionality in communication networks and perceptions of teamwork during implementation. This finding was surprising; however, possible explanations for these findings offer insight into how to approach future research to understand team boundaries, team composition, and determining appropriate and meaningful measurements of teamwork during the implementation process.

Introduction

The delivery of high-quality, safe, evidence-based healthcare is contingent upon numerous factors – one prominent influencing factor is teamwork. High-functioning healthcare teams and higher perceptions of teamwork are pivotal to improving patient outcomes, such as reduced morbidity and mortality, and are associated with increased adherence to evidence-based care, improved patient safety, and reduced cost [1, 2]. In nursing, greater teamwork is correlated with improved nurse sensitive quality indicators [3], improved patient-centered care [4], and reduced missed care [5]. Nurses also report reduced acute and chronic fatigue [6, 7], less burnout [8], and higher job satisfaction [9] when perceptions of teamwork are higher. Teamwork refers to two or more people working collectively towards accomplishing a shared goal using behaviors such as communication, coordination, and collaboration [1, 10]. Communication is a vital element of teamwork and is closely related to team performance [11]. Teamwork and communication are essential to ensuring high-quality, safe patient care as they collectively prevent errors and mitigate preventable patient harm [5, 10].

Another mechanism to improve patient outcomes, decrease patient harm, and reduce health care costs is by using evidence-based practices (EBPs) [12, 13]. Unfortunately, barriers at the individual and organizational levels prevent clinicians from consistently using EBPs [14]. As a result, patients may experience preventable harms, such as health care–associated infections, falls, and pressure injuries, which can lead to prolonged hospital stays and other negative sequelae [15]. Implementation is required to overcome barriers and to facilitate clinicians’ adoption of EBPs into their routine practice and improve patient outcomes. The implementation process is complex and multi-faceted; ongoing optimization is needed to ease changes in practice and close the “know-do” gap. The growing field of implementation science has identified numerous determinants and contextual factors that influence implementation success. Among them, teamwork and communication have been identified as two contextual determinants that impact implementation within established implementation models and frameworks, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [16] and Theoretical Domains Framework [17], and synthesized knowledge [14, 18]. However, there is little empirical evidence that explores the role of teams and teamwork within implementation science [18–20], nor the associated communication.

Teamwork and communication are related, yet separate, pervasive contextual factors that exist within individual nursing units and transcend across healthcare organizations. In general, it is acknowledged that open and effective communication among members of a team facilitates implementation [18] and that communication is an essential element of teamwork [10]. During the implementation of practice changes, communication networks can shape how practice-change information spreads and how social influence occurs, while a unit’s teamwork climate may shape whether nurses can coordinate and adapt their work to enact new practices [14]. However, how these factors relate to one another during implementation is unknown and it remains unclear how nurses who are most central in practice-change communication networks also perceive the unit’s teamwork. This study was designed to explore teamwork and communication concurrently in nursing within the context of the implementation of EBPs to explore this gap in knowledge.

Communication is an established and vital element within the implementation process. Patterns or channels of communication among individuals and groups form social networks [21]. Understanding these communication channels among members of a team (i.e., network) is essential, as they shape the innovation-decision process, which results in the decision to adopt or reject a new practice [22]. Social network analysis (SNA) is a methodological tool that measures communication interactions and informs our understanding of how these relational ties may impact individual and group behaviors [21, 23]. Communication within social networks reflects individual determinants, such as knowledge, social norms, decision-making [17], as well as contextual determinants of implementation, such as relational connections and communication within and across the inner setting [16]. Understanding nurses’ patterns of communication about practice changes across whole networks and subnetworks can inform communication-related strategies to improve uptake of EBPs [24].

Although SNA has been underutilized to explore communication interactions among healthcare professionals [25] and remains limited in nursing [26, 27], emerging evidence suggests its utility in enhancing improvement activities and improving patient safety [28]. Mapping communication networks helps identify central members who communicate frequently about practice changes and may act as champions; because of their network position, these members are also likely to influence team processes and unit culture, which in turn impact the success of implementation [14, 24, 28]. Communication is a team-based process that occurs during the implementation process [18]; however, empirical evidence is lacking to describe how the prominence of members in a communication network relates to nurses’ perceptions of teamwork.

This study recognizes that nurses are instrumental in providing high quality evidence-based care to improve patient outcomes and that the implementation process is riddled with challenges that can inhibit it. Furthermore, this study is grounded in the established knowledge that both teamwork and communication are central to the implementation process and to facilitating the adoption of EBPs [14, 18], but how they relate to each other is unclear. In this study, we treat the inpatient unit as a bounded social network and explore whether nurses who occupy more central positions in communication networks also report stronger perceptions of unit teamwork. The communication networks in this study are specific to hospital acquired condition (HAC)-related practice changes, which is representative of ongoing implementation efforts [29] of EBPs that can be used to prevent HACs, such as falls, central-line associated bloodstream infections, and pressure injuries. Exploring the synergistic relationship between nurses’ perceptions of teamwork with communication patterns occurring during the implementation process is a first step to informing how the context for implementation or implementation strategies could be modified to promote successful implementation of EBPs.

Purpose

This study sought to answer the question, during the implementation of practice changes, how are teamwork perceptions and communication patterns about practice change related? Specifically, this study tested the relationships between the positionality of members within the communication networks for HAC-related practice changes and their perceptions of teamwork. It was hypothesized that those who were most central to the communication network would also hold the highest perceptions of teamwork.

Methods

Design

This exploratory, descriptive, cross-sectional survey study includes a primary data analysis of perceptions of teamwork and a secondary data analysis of communication network data. All data were collected simultaneously between January and March 2019. These data remain relevant as the challenges of implementation persist [13, 29].

Study setting and sample

Registered Nurses were recruited from two inpatient nursing units within one Magnet® designated Academic Medical Center in the Midwest. In an attempt to detect variation across units, two similar units were purposively sampled, comparable based on the similar number of nurses and inpatient beds. Both units selected were intensive care units (ICUs) and are referred to as Unit A and Unit B. All eligible nurses were invited to participate in the study during unit meetings, via email, and through study fliers posted on the unit.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Every Registered Nurse working as core staff on the two units was eligible to participate in this study, regardless of duration as a nurse, years of employment, or the shifts or number of hours worked. In addition to direct care nurses, the units’ nurse leaders (i.e., charge nurses, nurse manager and Clinical Nurse Specialist) were eligible to participate in the study. These criteria were intentionally broad to achieve as close to a 100% response rate as possible. Nurses not employed by the hospital (i.e., traveler or agency nurse) or did not work consistently on one of the two units (i.e., float nurses) were not eligible to participate.

Data collection

After receiving IRB approval and endorsement from each of the ICU’s unit managers, recruitment began. With support from the unit managers, the study team invited nurses to complete the survey in person during their work shift, either on the unit or in the break room. Each participant received a printed study information sheet and their completion of the survey implied consent. The surveys were completed via pen-and-paper and returned to the study team upon completion.

The Nursing Teamwork Survey (NTS) [30] was used, with permission, to collect nurses’ perceptions of overall teamwork on their unit. The NTS, a 33-item questionnaire, measures overall teamwork across five elements: mutual trust, team orientation, backup behaviors, shared mental model, and team leadership [30]. Participants indicated how often each statement applies to the team of nurses they work with using a 5-point Likert scale from rarely (1) to always (5). Higher teamwork scores represent higher perceptions of teamwork. The NTS conceptualized teamwork as a unit-level measure [30], but it has also been measured and analyzed as an individual attribute [6, 9]. The NTS was chosen because it is a well-known tool for measuring teamwork among nurses and its association with nursing practice, such as missed nursing care [5], has been tested. Furthermore, some items align with change-related processes; however, to our knowledge its use during the implementation process is novel.

The validity, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency of the NTS is documented [30]. The internal consistency of the data collected in this sample was measured on each unit. On Unit A, the overall scale had a Cronbach’s α of 0.79 and on Unit B, 0.75. Values of Cronbach’s α for each of the subscales ranged from 0.73 to 0.86 (Table 1), comparable to those recorded in other studies, which have ranged from 0.74 to 0.957 [3, 9, 30].

Demographic data

In alignment with the method, SNA, a sociometric survey was developed for this study to collect communication frequency among each of the unit nurses (core staff and unit leaders) about HAC-related practice changes. For each unit, each nurse’s name was listed on a roster within the survey to aid memory recall; names were converted to participant identification numbers during data entry to ensure anonymity [24]. The frequency of communication was rated using a 4-point scale, from not at all (1) to a lot (4). Additional information about the data collection is detailed in the primary publication by Arsenault Knudsen et al. [24].

In addition to the NTS and sociometric survey data, demographic data were collected to describe the characteristics of the nursing sample. Items included their nursing role on the unit, shift and weekend worked, years of experience, hours worked per week, percentage of time spent in direct patient care, age, and degrees obtained.

Data analysis

All survey data were entered into Microsoft Excel, creating spreadsheets for each unit’s teamwork data, communication networks, subnetwork data, and demographic item responses. The accuracy of the data entry was independently verified by a study team member. Each participant’s mean overall teamwork score was computed as an unweighted average of all 33 NTS items. Nine items were reverse coded to ensure that the desirable end of the scale had a higher value. Additionally, the mean subscale scores were calculated for each of the five subscales.

The communication network data were analyzed in UCINet [31], a social network analysis software. This secondary analysis of communication data focused on individual’s network measures, rather than the whole network and subnetwork measures previously reported [24]. Specifically, centrality measures were calculated for each participant within each unit. Centrality is used in SNA to describe the key members within a social network. Four network centrality measures were calculated (1) indegree centrality, which represents the sum of all nominations received by each participant; (2) outdegree centrality, which is the sum of all nominations sent from each participant to other members in the network; (3) betweenness centrality, determines the degree in which a participant is connected to those on the shortest path connecting to all the members in the network; and (4) eigenvector centrality, measuring participants’ influence in the network by determining which participants are the most connected [21]. Respectively, these measures indicate who is most prominent in the network based on who received and sent communication most frequently, who may be “gatekeepers” of communication, and who is the most connected to those with the most connections in the network. In addition to centrality, UCINet’s core/periphery analysis was used to classify each participant’s coreness, meaning their location in the core or peripheral portion of the communication network.

In addition to the analysis conducted of all the members within the network, members’ centrality measures and coreness metrics were further analyzed at the subnetwork level; these subnetworks were identified in the primary analysis based on weekend and shifts worked [24]. Any nurse who reported working rotating shifts was included in both the day and night shift subnetworks.

Relationships between centrality measures and teamwork scores were analyzed using univariate regression models, run in R 4.5.1 [32]. This analysis was conducted with the whole network and each subnetwork across overall and subscale teamwork scores. In subsequent analyses, teamwork perceptions between core and periphery members of Units A and B were compared with independent-samples t-tests. When Levene’s test indicated unequal variances between the two groups, Welch’s t-tests were used. Again, this analysis was conducted using both the overall and subscale teamwork scores across each unit and subnetwork. Finally, to further explore the relationship between perceptions of teamwork and frequency of communication, we looked for evidence of homophily in communication networks, assessing whether nurses who most frequently communicate with one another share similar perceptions of teamwork. The assortativity statistic was computed using the R package igraph [33, 34].

Finally, in alignment with social network analysis, sociograms were constructed to visually represent both communication networks and participants’ teamwork perceptions. The sociograms were constructed in UCINet’s program NetDraw. Due to the high density of connections noted using full network data, the data were pruned to include only those connections that represented “a lot” of communication, which is consistent with the pruning done in the primary analysis [24].

Results

Across the two units, a total of 145 nurses participated in this study. Unit A had 96 eligible participants, of whom 84 participated (88% response rate). Unit B had 64 eligible nurses and 61 participated (95% response rate). The sample included mostly nurse clinicians (n = 116, 80%) who worked 12 h shifts (n = 138, 95%) in direct patient care (n = 110, 76%), were bachelor’s prepared (n = 123, 85%) and identified as female (n = 118, 81%). Participants’ years of experience as a nurse and working on their unit varied. The shifts and weekends worked across the sample were evenly distributed across day and night shifts and each of the three weekends. See full details in Table 1.

Nursing teamwork scores

Overall teamwork scores ranged from 2.67 to 4.96 (mean = 4.04). The scores per unit for each subscale are included in Table 2. The highest subscale scores were seen in shared mental model and team leadership across both units.

Centrality and teamwork

Few significant relationships were seen between communication centrality metrics and the six teamwork subscale measures. For Unit A, higher indegree centrality was associated with a modest decrease in subscale scores for team orientation, mutual trust, and the overall average, contrary to our hypothesis. In addition, eigenvector centrality was associated with lower scores in the mutual trust subscale. Within Unit B, no relationship was observed between communication centrality and any teamwork scores (all p > 0.05). Refer to Table 3 for full metrics.

Shift worked, subnetwork centrality, and teamwork

When centrality measures were computed within subnetworks, results were similar to those reported for the full network. Of those participants on Unit A who worked day shift, a negative relationship was observed between indegree centrality, the team orientation and mutual trust subscale scores, and overall teamwork scores, as well as between eigenvector centrality and the mutual trust subscale. In addition, new negative relationships were seen between indegree centrality and the backup subscale scores, and between eigenvector centrality and the overall teamwork score. For Unit B participants who worked day shift, modest positive relationships between outdegree centrality, the mutual trust and backup subscale scores, and overall teamwork scores, as well as between eigenvector centrality and the overall teamwork score were seen. The pattern among participants working night shift on Unit A was relatively similar to those on day shift; among Unit B night shift participants, no significant relationship was observed (see Table 4).

Discussion

This exploratory study aimed to test the relationship between nurses’ positionality within HAC-related practice change communication networks and their perceptions of teamwork. Within each unit’s network and their subnetworks, nurses’ positions were determined using centrality measures and categorization as core or peripheral members of the network. Across all members of each unit’s network, teamwork perceptions were relatively high; the highest rankings were noted in shared mental model and team leadership subscales and the lowest in team orientation. The high perceptions of teamwork are consistent with other studies reporting on nurse teamwork scores [3, 6, 9]. The most central roles to these networks were hypothesized to hold higher perceptions of teamwork; however, this was not supported in the analyses. Rather, only a modest inverse relationship was noted between centrality and teamwork perceptions on one unit (Unit A) and no relationship was observed on the other unit (Unit B). Similarly, further analysis among teamwork subscale scores, subnetworks, coreness, and homophily identified few significant relationships.

The premise of this study was that teamwork and communication both influence the implementation of EBPs. This study sought to fill a knowledge gap and inform our understanding about the relationship between these interacting contextual factors by measuring them simultaneously and testing the relationship. In summary, few relationships were found. The relationships, or lack thereof, identified in this study were unexpected, yet they are interesting and should be explored further. Therefore, potential explanations of these findings and ideas for practical implications will be discussed.

Possible explanations for findings

Boundary mismatch

The NTS measures the teamwork climate of a unit, which is informed by the perceptions of all the nurses who work on a single unit [30]. In this study, we defined the team to include all the nurses working on an inpatient unit [30], conceptualizing them as a pre-existing team [19] working together toward a common goal of providing patient care and reducing the risk of HACs. Yet it is possible that the boundary of the nursing team caring for patients on a unit doesn’t match the nursing team working together to change their practice and achieve reductions in HACs.

Among ICU teams, it is well established that communication is essential to multiple team tasks, including providing clinical care; however, there is inconsistency with whom communication is occurring due to frequent rotation of interdisciplinary team members [35, 36]. Nurses are vital members of the ICU team, focusing on providing care for one to two critically ill patients at a time, each of whom are also cared for by a distinct interdisciplinary team [35]. Therefore, while the NTS captured nurses’ perceptions of teamwork among nurses on their unit, reflecting the work done to maintain day-to-day clinical care, it may not inherently align with the team boundaries focused on implementing practice changes or more specifically working together to mitigate risk of HACs. In this study, centrality reflects prominence in HAC-related practice-change communication, which may function more as information dissemination or seeking clarification about a practice than as reciprocal teamwork. Therefore, centrality in this specific network may not correspond to broader perceptions of unit teamwork measured by the NTS.

Furthermore, change-related communication noted in the networks may reflect information sharing about the change, rather than the collaborative discussions for collectively identifying a solution or overcoming challenges. The latter may more often occur with the network of interdisciplinary healthcare team members that are bound by a patient rather than a unit. Finally, communication can occur in multiple forms (e.g. in-person, via e-mail, or messaging applications) and with varied members of an organization; these details were not captured in this study. However, they are worthy of future exploration because these interactions may influence teamwork processes and performance [28].

Temporal mismatch

Teams, by definition, are temporal in nature; they evolve and shift over time [11]. Similarly, the implementation process is complex and is conceptualized in phases (i.e [37, 38],. Each phase of the implementation process has specific goals. Similarly, teams may be working toward varied or multi-purposed goals [11]. Communication influences every step in the implementation process to arrive at decisions to adopt or reject a new practice [22] and during the implementation process communication can serve multiple functions [39]. Therefore, it is plausible that the cross-sectional design of this study may not be adequate to capture the temporality and dynamic nature of these processes. Data for this study were collected in a relatively short period of time (January to March); however, perceptions and communication patterns could change day-to-day. Similarly, the HAC-related practice changes were indicated as areas of constant improvement; therefore, a distinct phase of the implementation process was not the focus of data collection.

Coordination burden

In these data sets, the most central members of the communication network held a leadership role, such as charge nurse, nurse manager, or clinical nurse specialist. Leaders play a vital role in implementation, both through nurturing a context and culture that supports implementation and by maintaining structural resources to facilitate implementation [13]. Due to the nature of their leadership roles, these nurses are likely to experience heavier workloads which could contribute to higher levels of chronic fatigue, despite generally high perceptions of teamwork [6].

In this study, we did not directly measure workload, interruptions, or perceived burden; however, possible explanations of our findings are hypothesis-generating. These insights could be tested in future studies to explore these interconnected variables. Frequent communication between unit nurses and nurses with high centrality may compound their higher workload and represent a burden resultant of interrupted work to attend to answering questions, communicating, and championing the practice change [28]. The effort required to champion a change may be invisible to peers but could explain some of the inverse relationships between positionality and perceptions of teamwork measured, such as an understanding of each other’s roles and responsibilities (shared mental model) and the leadership structure (team leadership). Furthermore, while efficiency of communication was not measured in this study, ineffective communication can be a signal of poor team performance among overburdened teams, which often is a result of high workloads [28], and it hinders implementation [18]. These combined factors could create a self-fulfilling cycle. When implementation efforts are not successful, that often results in poor outcomes and tends to increase communication efforts [39], which could increase the burden on the central members to communicate more frequently about practice change and contribute to perceptions of lack of shared mental model and team leadership. Therefore, in these networks, it is plausible that the high frequency of communication is indicative of inefficient communication among network members and influencing perceptions of teamwork; future research is needed to test this further.

Measurement malalignment

A multitude of tools have been developed to measure the core dimensions of teamwork [1]. The NTS was chosen for this study to match the context of nurses bounded by a hospital unit. The NTS is a valid measure of unit teamwork, grounded in teamwork theory, and across five subscales it measures perceptions of mutual trust; working together to maximize strengths; providing backup or assistance; a shared understanding of team member’s roles and responsibilities; and the team’s leadership structure and support [6, 30]. Some survey items align with change-related processes, such as team members’ engagement in making improvements, readily sharing information, and communicating clearly (mutual trust). Additionally, items inquire about perceptions of how team members react when an improvement need is identified (team orientation) and the need for working together “to do a quality job” (backup). Even though some items touch on improvement behaviors, the overall instrument may not isolate teamwork specific to implementation tasks; therefore, other measures may need to be considered, such as team effectiveness [40] or relational coordination [41]. However, determining the exact measure may be difficult because teamwork has been defined and measured inconsistently in implementation science [18].

Practical implications

Based on the findings from this study and the potential explanation for these findings, we offer practical implications for advancing knowledge for the field of implementation science, specifically related to hospital nurses and the implementation process to promote a practice change and EBP adoption. Further research should examine or aim to understand team boundaries, team composition, and determine appropriate measurement of teamwork.

First, the boundaries of teams for practice change should be explored further for hospital nurses. To reiterate, the generally accepted definition of teamwork refers to two or more people collectively working towards a shared goal through behaviors such as communication, coordination, and collaboration [1, 10]. Kalisch and colleagues [30] identified the natural boundary of nursing teams to be demarcated by inpatient units. However, the findings of this study suggest that the members comprising a team for nursing-related practice changes may not neatly align with the nurses working on a single unit and communicating about that practice change. Therefore, this is a ripe area for future research. Understanding how nurses characterize the boundaries of the team while working on implementing practice change is a practical next step toward advancing our knowledge in this area. Recently, a narrative review found that many implementation projects were led by teams with members that spanned organizational levels, suggesting team boundaries do extend beyond a unit [42]; however, more research is needed to define the boundaries.

Second, an exploration of who nurses identify as members of the teams they work within for implementation, including nurses and interdisciplinary colleagues, is an important next step. Considering how little is known about the role of teamwork in implementation, framing an exploration of team composition with an archetype of implementation teams [19] could be a useful reference. Furthermore, a pragmatic understanding of the intersections of teams is needed to establish how teams work together with other teams to achieve similar goals. For example, in practice, there are teams charged with leading changes in practice and teams who are “being told” to enact the change [43]; the integration and distinction of these teams should be explored. Furthermore, the composition of teams may vary depending on the implementation phase, the change itself, or the inpatient setting (i.e., general care unit, ICU). Similarly, communication frequency across the phases of implementation likely changes [39]. Examining the boundaries and composition of these nested, multi-leveled, and perhaps intersecting teams, will lead to a more accurate conceptualization of what teams contribute to the implementation process, setting the stage for further studies of their teamwork and correlated communication.

Finally, the measurement of teamwork may need to be altered when examining teamwork during implementation. Teamwork itself has been identified as a contextual factor that influences implementation [14]. Furthermore, the formal and informal networks of team members in that context also influence the adoption of new practices [16]. However, teamwork may not be the best construct to measure the way members work together. For example, because implementation of EBPs is intended to improve outcomes, team effectiveness may be a more meaningful measure, capturing the team’s performance, functions, and viability as scaffolded competencies applicable to teams focused on improving patient safety and outcomes [40]. Another way to consider measuring team interactions during practice change is by measuring their relational coordination. Relational coordination theory describes the dynamic way in which work is coordinated through communication, shared goals and knowledge, across organizational structures to achieve performance outcomes [41]. Relational coordination has been associated with improved patient outcomes [41] and among nurses, higher relational coordination was associated with lower rates of burnout and increased job satisfaction and work engagement [44]. In implementation science, the relational nature of many implementation strategies is recognized, such as facilitation and identification of champions [45]. In combination with SNA, which can identify facilitators and champions, measuring relational coordination could be useful for further exploration of these relational aspects of practice change.

The findings of this study do not offer conclusive relationships about how perceptions of teamwork relate to nurses’ communication patterns during implementation of HAC-related practice changes; however, it does reinforce the need for ongoing exploration of these variables. The implications for continued study of teamwork and communication are not purely academic; rather, there is a logical argument to establishing high levels of teamwork via evidence-based interventions [10, 40] to create a nurturing context to facilitate implementation and practice changes. Positive perceptions of teamwork among clinical and non-clinical staff were recently found to be related to improved communication about changes and higher levels of change readiness, an antecedent to successful implementation [46]. Enhanced teamwork and team effectiveness will likely improve implementation; however, additional research is needed to identify how individual nurses’ position within the network can be leveraged to improve communication and coordination efforts required for successful implementation.

Limitations

Teamwork and communication during the implementation process and practice changes are complex, multifaceted, intimately intertwined, and not straightforward [2]; therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted cautiously, and limitations named. First, the sample for this study was from two ICUs within one hospital. Additional exploration of these factors should be expanded to larger samples across more units in future studies to determine how larger samples may inform the relationships of positionality and teamwork perceptions. Next, as described, implementation and teamwork are dynamic and temporal variables; in this cross-sectional study the data and related findings are limited to the moment of time they were collected. Since these data were collected in 2019, hospitals and nurses have responded to a global pandemic and, inevitability, have continued to change practices to improve patient care; yet, these findings remain relevant as the challenges with implementation of EBPs persist and the need to better understand teamwork during implementation is reinforced [18]. Due to the nature of survey studies, this analysis is limited by self-reported data, which is subject to human fallibility of recall and emotions at the time of completing the survey. Finally, this study was exploratory in nature; therefore, the null results should be interpreted recognizing the limitations of the study design. Future studies could consider alternative measures and methods to further examine the relationships between communication and teamwork during the implementation process.

Conclusion

Although teamwork and communication are both established factors that can contribute to the implementation process of EBPs, and communication is a key attribute that comprises teamwork, this study did not reveal a clear relationship between nurses’ positionality in communication networks and perceptions of teamwork. This null finding was surprising; however, exploration of possible explanations for this finding lends new insight into the boundaries and composition of nursing teams, as well as measurement tools. More research is needed to examine communication and teamwork as co-occurring pervasive contextual factors that influence implementation, and the insights from this study should be considered in future study designs. Finally, in practice settings, ongoing efforts to facilitate and enhance teamwork and effective communication during implementation are needed to reap the numerous positive outcomes they collectively have on improving nursing and patient outcomes.
