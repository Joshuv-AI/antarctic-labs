Hybrid additive manufacturing-based assembly of sacrificial isomalt scaffolds and gas exchange membranes for microfluidic artificial lung development
Authors: Anand Sojan, Gerhard Fusch, Shahad Amin, Niels Rochow, Christoph Fusch, Ponnambalam Ravi Selvaganapathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preterm neonates are susceptible to respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) due to their underdeveloped lungs and require mechanical ventilation. Microfluidic blood oxygenators (MBOs) are a suitable alternative due to their small priming volume and their potential to reduce iatrogenic effects of ventilation. Current MBOs use lithographic fabrication that limits their manufacturability due to scalability and integration issues. Additive-manufacturing has been recently explored to overcome fabrication challenges, but has its own limitations in realizing scalable thin-membrane devices with high degree of channel patency. This work presents a new hybrid additive-manufacturing strategy for artificial lungs, by combining extrusion printing of sacrificial isomalt scaffolds with spin-coating of thin polydimethylsiloxane membranes. It eliminates the use of synthetic polymers or toxic solvents, yielding complete channel patency and biocompatibility. Using this scalable process, oxygenators with 11 alternating blood/gas layers separated by thin (121 µm) membranes were manufactured, achieving complete channel patency and a 0% rejection rate. In vitro evaluation showed reduced form-factor while achieving a high oxygen-transfer efficiency of 184 mL O2 min−1 m−2. Furthermore, the performance metrics of the devices were evaluated against the clinical requirement, substantiating their ability to support a 1 kg neonate with RDS, achieving 1.73 mL O2 min−1 oxygen uptake at 30 mL min−1 blood flow rate, with 8.6 mL priming volume and offering pumpless operation.

Introduction

Infants born before 37 completed weeks of gestational age, termed as premature or preterm neonates1 are susceptible to respiratory issues and increased mortality rates. In this population, extreme prematurity (born before 28 weeks’ gestational age) remains the leading cause of infant morbidity and mortality2,3. Their mortality risk is around 38.4%, compared to 0.2% for full-term infants4. In these neonates, the lungs are structurally and functionally immature, limiting effective oxygen exchange. While inadequate surfactant production is a major contributor to this impairment, the underlying structural immaturity of the alveoli and pulmonary vasculature often prevents efficient gas exchange even if surfactant is present. Positive-pressure ventilation, often used to support breathing in preterm neonates with respiratory distress syndromes (RDS), is invasive and has inadvertent iatrogenic consequences5. This intervention has been linked to several adverse effects, such as reduction in surfactant production and increased resistance in the lung’s blood vessels, making it more difficult for oxygen to reach the bloodstream. Additionally, this may trigger both localized lung inflammation and widespread inflammatory responses throughout the body5. Over time, these physiological stresses contribute to the development of serious complications such as intraventricular hemorrhage6, sepsis, and retinopathy of prematurity7. In cases where mechanical ventilation proves to be inadequate, Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) has been utilized. These systems remove blood from the body, oxygenate it extracorporeally, and return it back to the circulatory stream. Although they significantly reduce mortality, it comes with certain drawbacks. Notably, the current ECMO devices have a large filling volume, requiring priming with donor blood, and both veno-venous and veno-arterial ECMO systems rely on support from external pumps that can trigger thrombosis. Its execution involves an invasive surgical procedure to connect the device to central blood vessels, and necessitates systemic anticoagulation, posing a heightened risk of hemorrhage8. These circuits make use of hollow-fiber membrane (HFM) based oxygenators for gas exchange. Risk of hemolysis9 and thrombosis10 associated with the flow of blood inside the lumen11 has led to the configuration where the blood flows on the shell side (around the fibers), while the sweep gas flows in the lumen. This configuration is associated with an increase in priming volume of the oxygenator, necessitating transfusion with donor blood. An external pump is required to overcome the pressure drop across the oxygenator, further impacting its compactness or portability. Therefore, an ideal oxygenation apparatus suitable for preterm and term neonates should possess a small priming volume (<10 mL), able to oxygenate ~30 mL min−1 of blood, the ability to function without a pump, and utilize the arterio-venous pressure for perfusion12. The drawbacks associated with both the configurations of the HFM-based oxygenators make them not suitable for use with such a population.

The advancements in microfluidic device design and production technology have led to the emergence of microfluidic-based blood oxygenators (MBOs) that can be adjusted to specific requirements13–16. Compared to HFM-based oxygenators, these scaled-down devices offer enhanced gas exchange efficiency at reduced priming blood volume and minimized contact area, making them suitable for neonatal applications. They have a much better form factor, and most of the microfluidic channel networks mimic the physiological microflow paths17,18. The potential of these miniaturized lung assistive devices is currently limited by their dependence on conventional 2D patterning techniques. The freedom to scale up is constrained to a single plane and is further limited by the wafer size available for lithography, commercially limited to 12″. Hence, there is a need to connect multiple units of these individual MBOs, thereby increasing the form factor. This assembly or connection necessitates the use of additional connectors, which are prone to clotting and further add to the blood priming volume19. The success of these individual MBOs further depends on the leak-free bonding of patterned layer to the bottom layer. Often the manufacturing yield of these devices is low.

The advancements in the available materials and process capabilities of additive manufacturing (AM) have enabled its use in the development of 3D microfluidic-based lung assist devices. Fabrication of microfluidic modules with embedded hollow microchannels and integrated gas exchange membranes are possible20, which was previously limited by the resolution and material capabilities of 3D printing. Direct printing of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)21 and use of sacrificial molds to cast PDMS to form gas exchange modules22 have been reported. But these techniques were limited by their characteristic dimensions, and the fabricated membrane thicknesses were far higher than the physiologically relevant diffusion distances. Furthermore, Hesselmann et al.22 reported high rejection rates for the manufactured modules, and the use of acetone to dissolve away the sacrificial mold is incompatible with clinical manufacturing standards, posing a risk of residual solvent leaching, which has not been addressed. Fleck et al.20 reported the development of a miniaturized prototype device, built on a custom photocurable resin derived from the work of Bhattacharjee et al.23. Although they were able to incorporate the highest density of microchannels, only 72% of blood channels and 59% of gas channels were patent due to resin entrapment in the microchannels. This is an inherent limitation of resin 3D printing of hollow microchannels or intricate features, as resin viscosity plays a crucial role in their patency24. Thus, there is still a need to investigate alternate fabrication strategies for manufacturing 3D microfluidic oxygenators, incorporating patent hollow channels and thin gas exchange membranes. Ideally, the fabrication approach should not involve use of toxic chemicals or solvents, neither in the functional material nor in the post processing steps.

Adhering to these and to overcome the shortcomings of the already investigated methods, we combined extrusion printing of sacrificial isomalt (biosafe and dissolvable in water) channel layers with spin-coated structural PDMS membranes to manufacture multi-layer microfluidic lung assist devices with alternating blood and gas channel layers. This layered assembly equips each blood-flowing layer with double-sided gas transfer, delivering enhanced gas exchange efficiency while imparting overall compactness to the device (Fig. 1). When carbohydrate glass blends are melted at temperatures exceeding 90 °C, they undergo reduction in their viscosities, allowing smooth extrusion through nozzles. These prints solidify into the desired structure once they are cooled to their glass transition temperatures25. We extruded isomalt using a pneumatic extrusion printer, to form the sacrificial microchannels, and conformal PDMS layers were added by spin-coating. The isomalt templates were etched away in a hot water bath by sonication, leaving behind hollow microchannel networks which were further flushed with hot water to achieve complete channel patency. Testing was performed in two stages- first on a batch of single oxygenator modules (SOMs) comprising of single blood layer sandwiched between two gas layers, and once performance metrics were identified with these modules, we proceeded to the fabrication and testing of multi-layer oxygenator modules (MOMs). We first tested all the devices for inter-layer leakage and complete channel patency. Sacrificial templating with isomalt

Figure 2a illustrates how the feed rate affects the width of the printed lines and the corners, with inset images of the resulting channel cross-sections. This relationship between the feed rate, extrusion pressure, and deposited isomalt filament width is further quantified in Fig. 2b, where a Channel Spread Index (CSI) is defined, which compares the measured channel width with the nominal channel width (Ø 400 µm). The total print time is inversely proportional to the feed rate, with higher feed rates resulting in faster prints. However, across all the applied pressure cases, the deposited filament width decreased with increased feed rates, which is expected due to the necking induced by the drawdown of the extruded filament. As the feed rate increases proportionally than the material extrusion rate, the filament gets stretched, resulting in deposited filaments even narrower than the diameter of the nozzle (CSI < 0). At the lowest feed rate of 100 mm min−1, the mean filament widths ranged from 408 µm at 100 kPa (CSI = 0.02) to 733 µm at 500 kPa (CSI = 0.83). Irrespective of the extrusion pressures, increasing the feed rates beyond 600 mm min−1 induced necking, resulting in widths less than nozzle diameter. At high feed rates and lower extrusion pressure, the deposition mode shifts from continuous to discrete droplets, whereas lower feed rates, while maintaining continuous deposition, result in longer print times. Furthermore, this influence of the feed rate on the printed filament width can be leveraged to print hierarchical multi-scale flow networks, where a thicker channel bifurcates into thinner channels in multiple stages. Figure 2c depicts how the different feed rates affect the gap between the channels, with thicker lines “spreading” into the nominal gap. A nominal gap of 400 µm was fixed between the channels, and a Gap Filling Index (GFI) was defined, which quantifies how much the printed lines fill the nominal gap. A feed rate of 100 mm min−1 results in an overlap of channels (GFI = 1), and as the feed rate increases, the GFI reduces until when the printed lines are thinner, resulting in GFI < 0. A GFI of 0.5 implies the adjacent channels have spread into the gap by 50%. To maximize the single-layer channel density, the gap between the channels needs to be reduced while providing enough structural support between layers. To fabricate devices based on this requirement, an extrusion pressure of 400 kPa and a feed rate of 300 mm min−1 were chosen, which would print a channel width of 503 ± 13 µm, while resulting in a GFI of 0.4 ± 0.1.

Fabrication of single oxygenator modules

SOMs were fabricated to test the manufacturability of the proposed approach and evaluated them for their layer-wise performance. These consisted of a single blood flowing layer sandwiched by two gas flowing layers, with each blood layer having 76 and each gas layer having 63 microchannels, respectively. The blood channels were 60 mm in length, while the gas channels were 70 mm, and these channels in alternate layers were oriented orthogonally, like the configuration adopted by Fleck et al.20. The SOM layout and the final fabricated device are shown in Fig. 3a, b. Channels were perfused with colored water to detect inter-layer leakages before in vitro testing, and Fig. 3c illustrates the same. The PDMS gas exchange membrane had a mean thickness of 172 µm. Both the blood and gas microchannels were around 481 ± 14 µm wide with a height of 121 ± 5 µm (Fig. 3d). For the SOMs, triangular hollow elements that connect to all the microchannels in the layer were incorporated to aid with the blood distribution. Burst pressure provides a fundamental measure of the structural integrity of the oxygenator and defines the upper limit of its operating range. To determine this, the devices were tested until failure, depicted by the onset of either inter-layer rupture or delamination. The central gas exchange modules of the tested devices (n = 5) were able to withstand 158 mmHg without evidence of delamination, corresponding to a safety factor of approximately 2.5. Increasing the pressure beyond this led to the leakage at the interface between the commercial silicone tubings and oxygenator modules (Fig. S6), while the central channel networks fabricated using the described technique remained structurally intact.

Mathematical performance modeling of SOM

Layer-wise performance was modeled based on as-fabricated channel dimensions and membrane thickness. The current configuration derives its architectural advantage from a dual-sided gas exchange and 100% channel patency advantage resulting from hybrid fabrication. The total resistance to diffusion was calculated as the parallel combination of resistances from top and bottom membranes, yielding a value of 5.54 × 106 s mmHg cm−1. A mathematical model previously described by Potkay26 was used to calculate the rated flow of single blood layer. Theoretically, a single blood-layer having an array of 76 microchannels can sustain a flow of 7 mL min−1, producing ~25% increase in oxygen saturation, with a corresponding pressure drop of ~ 50 mmHg across the array.

Performance of SOMs using porcine whole blood

The performance of SOMs was tested using porcine whole blood at flow rates ranging from 1 to 10 mL min−1, while oxygen gas was constantly perfused through the adjacent sweep gas layers at a flow rate of 16 ± 3 mL min−1.

The pressure drops across various devices were monitored to identify the flow rates they could support within the operating pressure drop range (20–60 mmHg) that is required for successful pumpless operation. As expected, roughly linear trend was observed between the pressure drop across the devices and the tested blood flows. Figure 4a depicts the blood test pressure drop results for the tested flow rates. The single blood layered devices could operate within the working range even for the higher blood flow rates tested, with 10 mL min−1 operating at the borderline pressure (mean of 67 mmHg). The gas exchange capacity was evaluated at these operating flow rates. The increments in oxygen partial pressure (pO2) and blood oxygen saturation (SaO2) were evaluated across the inlet and outlet of the device. Three-way valves in the circuit, connected on either side of the oxygenator, aided with the sample collection, and samples were immediately run using the point-of-care blood gas analyzer. The values were averaged across four different devices at each flow rate, otherwise specified on the plots. Figure 4b depicts the increase in oxygen saturation achieved by the oxygenators at various flow rates, with the mean initial oxygen saturation being 63.6 ± 1.50% (SaO2 at inlet). The results showed that using just a single blood layer, the oxygen saturation level of blood can be increased by 36% when the flow is within 5 mL min−1. When the flow is increased, it leads to smaller residence time for blood in the devices, giving the red blood cells a lower chance at adsorption of oxygen. This inverse correlation can be seen in Fig. 4b where the increment in oxygen saturation achieved gradually drops with the increase in blood flow rates. The SOMs were able to attain an oxygen saturation of 91% at a blood flow rate of 6 mL min−1 while functioning well within the optimal pressure drop range (pressure drop of 43 mmHg). An increased flow rate of 8 mL min−1 pushed the devices to the upper limit of the desired pressure range, yet were still capable of raising the oxygen saturation to 83%.

In theory, SOMs were predicted to increase the oxygen saturation by 25% at 7 mL min−1 flow rate. In vitro testing revealed that the SOMs could not meet the targeted increment of 25% at 7 mL min−1 but were able to deliver 27% increase in oxygen saturation at 6 mL min−1 flow rate.

Fabricating multi-layered modules in the scope of this work is to support preterm neonates with ~1 kg body weight, and hence the oxygenator needs to support a total flow of 30 mL min−1 in the extracorporeal circuit27. Theoretically, if each layer can sustain 7 mL min−1 flow, splitting the total flow will take more than 4 layers. Furthermore, based on the experimental results, 6 mL min−1 is the realistic flow that the layers can sustain to achieve at least 25% increase. Assuming uniform flow distribution to the multiple layers, non-limiting diffusion, and dimensional uniformity, the total oxygen transfer scales linearly with the number of layers in the module. Therefore, based on both theoretical and experimental results, 5 layers were configured for prototype multi-layered modules to experimentally demonstrate their oxygenation capability. The pressure drop should not be significantly impacted due to parallel stacking. As each layer has a volume of 0.27 mL, additional 4 layers increase the priming volume of to 1.35 mL. Given the 10 mL upper limit of the blood volume available in the extracorporeal circuit28, a configuration with 5 layers still leaves enough scope for distribution manifolds or further addition of layers as required.

Figure 6d illustrates the resulting oxygen saturation at the outlet of the devices versus the blood flow, when it is passed through the devices at various flow rates. The baseline oxygen saturation of the blood prior to entering the devices was 59.6 ± 0.5%. The devices were able to output the highest improvement in oxygen saturation by 41%, at a starting flow rate of 5 mL min−1. At 10 mL min−1, the outlet saturation was 98.3%, corresponding to an increase of 39%. As the flow was further increased, the magnitude of blood oxygenation declined: 32% at 20 mL min−1 (SaO2 = 91.8%), 27% at 30 mL min−1 (SaO2 = 86.3%), and 25% at 40 mL min−1 (SaO2 = 85%). The subsequent decline in achieved oxygen saturation at increased flows can again be attributed to the shorter residence time for the blood in the central gas exchange module (1.28 mL volume). While higher flows increase the volumetric throughput of the device, they limit the period of blood contact with the gas exchange membranes, reducing the extent to which each unit volume of blood is oxygenated. Nevertheless, devices were able to achieve substantial enhancement in oxygen saturation, with the highest tested flow rate (40 mL min−1) yielding a 25% increase. Most importantly, at the target nominal flow rate of 30 mL min−1, corresponding to supporting a 1 kg neonate with RDS, the devices achieved an increase of 27%, while remaining within operational limits.

Furthermore, as the blood was able to carry higher quantities of oxygen at increased flow rates, the measured oxygen uptake of the devices increased monotonically with the increasing flow rates, as depicted in Fig. 7a. At the lowest tested flow rate of 5 mL min−1, the mean oxygen uptake was 0.4 mL O2 min−1. Increasing the blood flow to 30 mL min−1 quadrupled the mean oxygen uptake to 1.6 mL min−1, while the highest tested flow of 40 mL min−1 achieved an oxygen uptake of 2 mL O2 min−1. This is attributed to the combined effect of increased volumetric throughput and convective transport at higher flow rates. The higher flow improves the oxygen uptake opportunity for the passing blood, and the blood near the membrane gets replaced faster, maintaining the oxygen concentration gradient across the membrane. The fact that the oxygen uptake increased with the increased flow rates implies that in the tested flow range, oxygen transfer was not yet limited by the diffusion across the membranes, and the controlling factor was the amount of blood being perfused through the device. Similar trend was observed with CO2 release, as shown in Fig. 7b. The lowest tested flow rate of 5 ml min−1 achieved a CO2 release of 0.6 ml min−1. As the flow rate was increased from 5 to 40 ml min−1, the CO2 release was 4.19 mL min−1. Discussion

Microfluidic oxygenators provide enhanced gas exchange efficiency at reduced priming volumes, making them suitable for neonatal applications. But the manufacturability is limited by its dependence on 2D patterning and poor bonding techniques, imposing constraints on the scalability and form factor of the devices. Recent efforts to employ additive manufacturing techniques to fabricate microfluidic lung assist devices have demonstrated considerable potential. However, none have simultaneously overcome the challenges of gas transfer efficiency, priming volume, or characteristic dimensions; improvements in one aspect are typically offset by shortcomings in another. The work presented here reports the first all-PDMS-based microfluidic multi-layered artificial lung assist prototype with enhanced gas exchange efficiency, reduced priming volume, and diffusion-relevant characteristic dimensions, suited to support preterm neonates with RDS.

Our manufacturing approach is based on sacrificial isomalt templates that can be readily dissolved away from the PDMS matrix using hot water. This strategy is particularly advantageous for clinical use devices as it eliminates the reliance on non-biosafe sacrificial synthetic polymers and etching solvents22, thereby avoiding issues of leaching and minimizing the need for post-processing. This extrusion-based step was employed to form clinically relevant channel dimensions, and bench-top spin-coating helped to form the thin gas-permeable PDMS membranes on top. Six devices were manufactured, all of which achieved complete channel patency, highlighting the advantage of this strategy over resin-based approaches that rely on nascent photocurable resins that are unestablished and compromise channel patency due to their high viscosities20. The chosen channel dimensions for this prototype configuration were purely based on manufacturability and equipment limitations. Channel height was constrained to ~120 µm, as the utilized XYZ-positioner lacked an automated bed-levelling system, and instead manual levelling was performed. Use of an extrusion stage with an integrated non-contact probe offers the possibility to achieve finer layer heights in the future. The width of the channels was chosen based on the need to maximize the area available for blood contact, while maintaining good structural integrity between the layers.

The central gas exchange module had a priming volume of only 1.28 mL, and even with the current prototype end distributors, total priming volume of the devices were 13.4% less than the neonatal blood (assuming 1 kg neonate) available for extracorporeal circulation. The current distributors were designed for ease of fabrication and proof-of-concept demonstration. Design iterations to minimize their volume would focus on mimicking the hierarchical vasculature, where the inlet would branch into daughter channels of smaller dimensions in multiple steps, to finally connect with the central gas exchange module (Fig. S10). Murray’s law provides a first-order design principle for gentle flow distribution in microfluidic branching networks, while minimizing hydraulic resistance and blood damage29. Although the goal is minimal volume and uniform path lengths, optimization is further dictated by manufacturability. These hollow, intricate branching networks need to be resolved by the process choice (assuming 3D printing as the manufacturing method). Hence, the design optimization would need to additionally consider design for manufacturability, which is out of scope of this current work and would be considered for the next device iterations.

Hollow fiber oxygenators remain the clinical standard for extracorporeal support. But their application in preterm neonatal settings is constrained by inherent system-level limitations. Commercially available HFM-based oxygenators have high device priming volumes that are comparable to or higher than the total blood volume of a preterm neonate (see Supplementary Information) and still require integration with a roller pump, risking non-physiological flow conditions and shear-induced blood trauma. Additionally, these demand flow tuning or bypassing to cater to preterm neonatal requirements, leading to inefficient operation. By using microfluidic blood networks, the compact oxygenator demonstrated in this work is specifically configured to address these limitations by minimizing the volume and operating within the arterio-venous pressure difference for pumpless perfusion.

The operational and gas exchange performance of the devices was evaluated with porcine whole blood, with the devices achieving high gas transfer efficiencies (O2 exchange efficiency of 184 mL min−1 m−2 and a CO2 exchange efficiency of 379 mL min−1 m−2) while showcasing the potential for pumpless operation. In vitro testing of the 5-layer modules validated the linear scaling logic for layering, derived from the single-layer performance data. When normalized to a hemoglobin concentration of 14.8 g dL−1 to match the experimental blood used for 5-layer configuration, at a flow rate of 6 mL min−1 the single-layer modules achieved an oxygen uptake of ~0.32 mL min−1. Based on this, linear scaling predicts an oxygen uptake of 1.63 mL min−1 for the 5-layered modules. Experimental results (Fig. 7) closely matched this projection, with the 5-layer configuration demonstrating 1.6 mL min−1 O2 uptake at 30 mL min−1 flow rate (~2% deviation). A comparison between the O2 uptake values predicted by linearly scaling (5x) the single-layer results with the experimental results from in vitro testing of MOMs is presented in Fig. S11 for further reference.

A typical neonate has a heart rate of ~150 beats min−1 and a stroke volume of 2 mL kg−1 body weight30. With only 10% of the cardiac output safely directed to the device to avoid hemodynamic compromise28, the resulting extracorporeal flow is 30 mL min−1 kg−1. This defines the flow rate the devices should sustain and oxygenate, to establish a meaningful performance for clinical support of preterm neonates with ~1 kg body weight27. Based on an average hemoglobin concentration of 16 g dL−1 and O2 binding capacity of 1.34 mL O2 per gram Hb31, the 30 mL min−1 kg−1 flow corresponds to a total O2 carrying capacity of ~6.4 mL O2 min−1 kg−1 (from 0% to 100% saturation). In neonates suffering from RDS, the incoming venous blood is at ~70% saturation, and according to FDA guidance document for cardiopulmonary bypass oxygenators, the target outlet saturation is 95%32. Therefore, the full arterialization only requires 25% increase in O2 saturation, corresponding to a requirement of ~1.6 mL O2 min−1 kg−1 of O2 uptake. Our current prototype devices were able to achieve a mean O2 uptake of 1.6 mL O2 min−1 at 30 mL min−1 when tested using porcine blood with a hemoglobin concentration of 14.8 g dL−1. If the hemoglobin concentration is corrected to 16 g dL−1 for human blood, the O2 uptake would be 1.73 mL O2 min−1, which meets the nominal oxygen transfer requirement targeted in this study. As this was a proof-of-concept study, only nominal performance targets were set. To ensure clinical viability, future devices need to withstand the inherent variability of the hematological environment. The current configuration can accommodate these tolerances or support higher targets by addition of layers with as-fabricated dimensions, or with optimized dimensions (Fig. S9).

Among the previously published works, Lachaux et al.33 have reported the highest ever gas transfer rates for both oxygen and carbon dioxide. They hypothesized that their device architecture has gas exchange happening not only vertically across the membranes, but also laterally through the thin walls. Thus, they state that their devices’ effective gas exchange area is underestimated, which would explain the very high area-specific gas transfer efficiency33. Furthermore, the design, which is constrained by conventional planar manufacturing, significant changes in blood flow direction as it enters from the distribution manifold into the individual oxygenator units, as well as has large stagnation zones that can lead to shear-induced thrombosis in practical use. Isenberg et al.34 reported an oxygen transfer rate of 192 mL O2 min−1 m−2 and the highest tested flow rate of 750 mL min−1. However, their devices had a large form factor with priming volume of 116 mL, which in this case, is 10 times the clinically feasible device volume for a preterm neonate. In our work, the hybrid fabrication technique offers compactness to the devices with its multi-layered architecture, maintaining the priming volume within 10 mL and achieving an oxygen transfer rate of 184 mL O2 min−1 m−2 and a carbon dioxide transfer rate of 379 mL CO2 min−1 m−2. With future optimizations in device design, this work positions our fabrication technique to be best suited for the manufacture of compact oxygenators for neonatal applications. In addition, our flow path (Fig. 5) for blood is straight with no abrupt changes in direction and considerably lower dead zones, which make it benign to effects of shear-induced thrombosis. Although the characteristic channel dimensions in our devices were comparable to other reported devices, the mean membrane thickness in this study was 172 µm, which is approximately 2–4 times thicker than those in the previous studies. Membrane thickness is an important design parameter as it contributes to the overall diffusive resistance of the blood oxygenator. In this prototype study, this coating thickness was deliberately chosen to ensure reliable and conformal coverage across all the layers and devices, as thinner coatings proved susceptible to the inconsistencies induced by variations in hotplate levelling during curing. In our present study with whole blood, oxygen uptake increased monotonically with blood flow rate in the tested range (Fig. 7), without any indication of mass transfer plateau. This suggests that under the current tested operating conditions, the devices did not reach a membrane resistance-dominated regime, instead blood-side transport, including convective renewal, plasma diffusion, and oxygen uptake by red blood cells in the whole blood, likely contributed substantially to the observed behavior. Considering our as-fabricated dimensions and double-sided gas exchange, the total resistance to diffusion in this configuration is 5.54 × 106 s mmHg cm−1, with the blood side resistance contributing to ~ 81% of the overall mass transfer limitation. This is also evident from the analysis provided in Fig. S9. Reducing the membrane thickness by 75% only leads to ~18% improvement in the oxygen uptake, while reducing the channel height (keeping the same aspect ratio of channel, W/H = 4) by 33% results in ~37% increase in the oxygen uptake.

Furthermore, most of the reported works in literature feature idealized geometries and do not fully account for the inactive regions within the device. For instance, Thompson et. al. reported O2 exchange efficiency of 153 mL O2 min−1 m−2; however, because only 46% of blood contacting surface actively contributed to gas exchange35, the efficiency of the entire device substantially drops to 66 mL O2 min−1 m−2. When the active participation of membrane area is accounted for, it reflects the incomplete utilization of available area for gas exchange. This highlights the importance of effective utilization of total gas exchange in determining the device performance. In contrast, our technique ensures that all the channels are patent and the entire designed membrane area actively participates in gas exchange. There is evidently no discrepancy between intrinsic and device-level performance.

Fleck et al.20 had reported similar configuration with alternating blood and gas layers, with thinner gas-permeable membranes, achieving oxygen transfer rate of 78 mL O2 min−1 m−2 and the highest reported carbon dioxide transfer rate of 668 mL CO2 min−1 m−2. But their membrane material (resin) needs further validation studies20 and the stereolithography-based fabrication was hindered by channel patency challenges. Although sacrificial molding was previously reported by Hesselmann et al.22, their use of ABS sacrificial material and acetone for etching requires extensive post-processing to address solvent residue, leaching, and swelling of the functional silicone36. Possibility of residual solvent or sacrificial material in the device can be a potential manufacturing issue that can affect the approval of device with regulatory authorities. The approach introduced in this work does not rely on synthetic polymers nor etching solvents, and with future process optimization, could establish a potential additive manufacturing pathway for fabricating microfluidic artificial lungs. This technique could be optimized by integrating a film applicator with a doctoral blade directly on the levelled print platform (with temperature control), thereby eliminating the need to transfer the substrate to the spin-coater and hotplates. Device design can be further optimized in the future to reduce the resistance to diffusion and accommodate higher flow rate per layer (Fig. S9). Additionally, the impact of the gas side flow rate was not systematically investigated in this current study. Future experiments will investigate the role of the gas side geometry and flow on the overall gas exchange performance.

As the primary objective of this study was to demonstrate the potential of hybrid fabrication technique for multi-layered oxygenators, the in vitro experiments presented here do not fully establish the comprehensive performance metrics necessary to support clinical translation of the oxygenators. To ensure future clinical viability of the devices, subsequent iterations should prioritize hemocompatibility and implement clot-mitigation strategies. While surface modifications were beyond the scope of this proof-of-concept study, their incorporation and extended in vitro evaluation are essential to address thrombogenicity challenges.

Conclusion

This work focused on the development of prototype microfluidic artificial lungs, capable of supporting preterm neonates with RDS. The proposed strategy does not depend on the use of synthetic polymer scaffolds nor toxic solvents for medical device manufacture. We were able to achieve complete channel patency and 0% rejection rate for the manufactured devices. Although the current prototypes were integrated with gas-permeable PDMS membranes as thin as 121 µm, further development of the process itself can help achieve thinner membranes. The in vitro evaluation using porcine blood demonstrated high gas exchange performance for the prototypes. Corrected for human blood, the prototypes were able to demonstrate an oxygen uptake of 1.7 mL O2 min−1 at a clinically relevant blood flow of 30 mL min−1, underscoring the criteria required for supporting a 1 kg neonate. The total priming volume was ~8.6 mL, which can be further reduced by redesigning the distributors that took up ~85% of the blood volume. Extended hemocompatibility of the devices is critical for clinical translation. Future iterations will leverage on our group’s previous success with dual-modification strategy for PDMS membranes to mitigate thrombus formation37. The presented hybrid fabrication strategy can be further tailored to other microfluidic devices, like the development of a multi-layered dialysis and oxygenator configuration, with the integration of a selective membrane with molecular weight cut-off for solute removal and protein retention, along with the PDMS membranes for gas exchange.

Materials and methods

Gas exchange module configuration

The gas exchange modules consisted of alternating blood-flowing and gas-flowing layers, with each blood layer having 76 and each gas layer having 63 microchannels, respectively. Channels in alternate layers were oriented orthogonally. Both the blood and gas microchannels were designed to be ~500 µm wide with a height of 120 µm, based on the layer height. The dimensions were selected based on the chosen nozzle diameter for extrusion and the capabilities of the printer used. Both single modules and multi-layer modules were completely fabricated out of PDMS. For all the single modules, triangular distribution and collection units that connect to all the microchannels in the layer were added to aid with the testing. In case of the multi-layer LADs, distributors and collectors were designed based on the optimized flow inlet designs from our previous studies19,38. The configurations for the single and multi-layer modules are illustrated in Fig. 1. The selected configuration was optimal for developing prototype units. Custom g-code scriptings for the parallel channel configurations were performed in Notepad++ (Don Ho) and validated using CAMotics (Cauldron Development LLC).

Gas exchange module fabrication

All functional gas exchange membranes were made by spin-coating PDMS using a SCS6800 spin-coater (Specialty Coating Systems Inc., USA). Sylgard184 base and curing agent (Dow Corning, USA) were mixed in 10:1 ratio and degassed to eliminate air bubbles. The prepared PDMS was kept at room temperature and used only after 2.5 h to increase its viscosity by curing. This was performed to achieve conformal coating over the printed structures and to control edge run-off. Preliminary curing trials of PDMS with embedded isomalt microstructures at 40 °C < T < 57.85 °C (glass transition temperature of isomalt39) resulted in localized deformations. Hence, all isomalt embedded layers were cured at 40 °C for 5–7 h on levelled hotplates. Furthermore, each preceding PDMS surface was plasma-treated for 180 s, using a hand-held plasma gun (Plasma Wand, Plasma Etch Inc., USA) to introduce wettability before spin-coating the subsequent layer.

Sacrificial microchannel templates were printed with isomalt–a sugar alcohol widely used in the food industry for its low-calorie content and non-carcinogenicity40. In contrast to other carbohydrate glass melts that typically crystallize above their glass transition temperature, isomalt tends to crystallize through dihydrate formation. By removing the moisture and erasing thermal history, it can be maintained in a glassy stable state, making it suitable for prolonged extrusion processes41. Isomalt granules were sourced from local vendor (Lorann, USA) and loaded into stainless steel 10 cc barrel, where it was preheated to 200˚C for 20 min and then cooled to room temperature. All extrusion printing steps were performed using a Ø 400 μm metal nozzle (Allevi, 3D Systems, USA), while maintaining the barrel at 110 °C using an external heating jacket (Syringe heater, Braintree Scientific Inc., USA). Extrusion was executed using a pneumatic control system synchronized with the XYZ positioner stage based on the UNI-PRINT 3D printer (“The Cool Tool” GmbH), using the machine interface developed by “The Cool Tool”42.

Initially, a thick layer of PDMS was spin-coated (300 rpm for 15 s) on to a Ø 150 mm silicon wafer (University Wafers) and cured. The first gas layer was printed with a nozzle-bed clearance of ~120 µm (channel height), and once the surface was plasma treated, another layer of PDMS was spun on top at 685 rpm for 15 s to completely engulf the isomalt microchannels but forming a thin flat membrane on top. After curing, the blood layer was printed on top, and the same coating procedure was followed. This sequential combination of printing, plasma treatment, and coating were repeated until the required number of layers were printed. As the customized printhead did not have a dedicated layer height fine-tuning, all the isomalt layers were printed by adjusting the nozzle-bed clearance manually using a standard sheet of known thickness ~120 µm. In case of single modules, 3 layers (G-B-G) were printed, while for the multi-layered modules, it was 11 layers (6G, 5B). The final layer of PDMS coating was as thick as the initial base layer.

For single oxygenator modules—inlet holes were punched at the vertices of the distributors (Ø 3.18 mm), silicone tubes of same inner diameter were attached, and a PDMS layer was bonded to base, in case of through holes. Perpendicular inlet configurations are used in most single-layered microfluidic devices, owing to the ease of fabrication. These were then sonicated in water at 60 °C using a commercial ultrasonic cleaner (DK Sonic, China) for 24 h to etch away the isomalt structures. After sonication, the modules were dried overnight in an oven at 70 °C to remove the water.

For multi-layer modules – The center functional units were punched out using a cutter (68 mm × 58 mm) and sonicated similarly to etch the isomalt. These were dried at 70 °C in an oven overnight. Inlet distributor and outlet collector molds were 3D printed with PLA using Prusa MK4 (layer height 0.1 mm), and PDMS cast from them were wet bonded to the lateral sides (two blood sides and one gas side) of the modules, using Sylgard 186 (10:1).

Leakage, patency, and burst pressure testing

Dyed water was perfused through the blood side and gas side separately to confirm channel patency. Furthermore, the flushed water exiting the blood and gas sides was monitored for color change/mixing, indicating inter-layer leakages. Subsequent flushing with DI water and drying were performed to ready the devices for further testing.

An experimental setup shown in Fig. S5 was used to validate the bond strength of the sandwich membrane layers and identify the maximum pressure the blood channels could withstand before rupture or delamination. It consisted of a digital peristaltic pump (Masterflex Ismatec Reglo digital, Avantor, USA), which served as the pressure source, connected to the device through a pressure transducer (TruWave Transducer, Edwards Lifesciences LLC, Irvine, CA, USA). The pressure readings were monitored using a connected monitor (SpaceLabs 90369 Patient Monitor, SpaceLabs Medical, Inc.). The gas sides were constantly perfused with oxygen (O2, ALPHAGAZ 1, Air Liquide, Canada) at a flow rate of 16 ml min−1, to reproduce the in vitro testing condition on the gas side. The blood layer was first perfused with 0.9% saline, and the 3-way connector at the outlet was shut to close the circuit. The pressure was built up using the pump while it was monitored, till the device being tested either ruptured or leaked.

In vitro gas exchange testing with porcine whole blood

Gas exchange capabilities of single modules and multi-layer modules were evaluated using porcine whole blood (1 L, Sodium EDTA anticoagulant, Cedarlanelabs, Burlington, ON, Canada; Cat. No. 7204909-1LTR). Prior to testing, the blood was heparinized at a final concentration of 3 units mL−1. To simulate the partially saturated arterial blood conditions of the natural lung, the blood was preconditioned using a commercial hollow fiber membrane oxygenator (PDMSXA-1.0, PermSelect®, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). A controlled gas mixture containing 5% CO2 and 95% N2 (v/v) was supplied through the lumen side at a rate of ~20 L h−1, while the blood was circulated in the shell side at a flow rate of 30–50 mL min−1. This was performed to reach a target oxygen saturation of ~60%, and once this SaO2 was attained, the blood was collected in a sealed container and stored at 4 °C overnight, to allow equilibration before use.

Imaging and sample preparation

Optical microscopy was carried out using an EVOS XL imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to image channel geometry and cross-sections. Higher resolution cross-sectional features were obtained using a scanning electron microscope (TESCAN VEGA, Czech Republic), operated at a voltage of 5 kV. Samples were sputter-coated with a thin ~15 nm gold layer to prevent charging. All images were analyzed, and dimensions were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA). Nikon M2 225 KV X-ray computed tomography (XCT) was used for the non-destructive 3D visualization of microfluidic artificial lungs. The scan was performed using a voltage of 150 kV, a power of 27 W. 2500 projections with 2 frames per projection were acquired over a 360° rotation with an exposure time of 345 ms per projection. X-ray imaging and volumetric reconstruction were performed using the Nikon InspectX control system and CT Pro 3D 6.9 Reconstructor software, incorporating center shift and standard beam hardening corrections to enhance image quality and segmentation accuracy. Finally, the reconstructed 3D volume was analyzed using Dragonfly (Dragonfly v 2024.1; Object Research System, Inc.) software for volume rendering, 3D visualization, segmentation, and quantification.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as Mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for comparison across multiple flow rates. In cases of limited or missing replicates, a mixed-effects analysis model was employed using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. A p value of <0.05 was defined as the threshold for significance (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad PRISM.
