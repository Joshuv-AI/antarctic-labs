Delivery of cardiac rehabilitation through hybrid models and alternative settings by country income classification and decade: Comparisons from the International Council of Cardiovascular Prevention and Rehabilitation’s 2025 Global Audit Update

Gabriela L M Ghisi, Rachael P Carson, Karam Turk Adawi, Rongjing Ding, Monserrat Cruz Rivero, Claudia V Anchique, Dinah L van Schalkwijk, Jonathan Gallagher

ABSTRACT
Objective: Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is an outpatient model of secondary prevention that improves patient outcomes, yet use remains limited. Hybrid/alternative CR addresses these challenges, but global availability, structure, and volumes have not been characterized since the pandemic. Methods: The International Council of Cardiovascular Prevention and Rehabilitation’s (ICCPR) 2025 Audit Update is a cross-sectional survey of phase II CR programs worldwide. After member Associations supported identification of countries with CR, program data were collected via REDCap between May-September/2025. Data were compared by country income classification, and with prior ICCPR Audits where possible. Results: Of 6,905 CR programs identified globally, 1,505 initiated a survey (median program response rate = 62.3% per country) from 90/113 (79.6%) countries with CR. Globally, 286 (23.2%) programs in 40 (35.4%) countries reported reimbursement for hybrid/alternative models, varying significantly by country income class (p < 0.001); this was higher than 2016 (14.5%), but not significantly different from the pandemic (21.0%). 285 (31.1%) programs reported offering a hybrid/alternative CR model, in 57 (50.4% with CR) countries (higher in lower-middle income countries, then upper-middle income countries [UMICs]). This was not higher than previous Audits. Programs were staffed by exercise professionals, nurses and physicians (more in UMICs). Dose was a median of 10 sessions (one/week over 8 weeks), lower than centre-based, but consistent across income classes and over time. Patients were allocated to model based on clinical criteria, choice (69.5%), but also cost (30.2%; higher in middle-income countries and than 2016). Hybrid/alternative models served a median of 50 (P25-P75 = 10–100) patients/program annually, lower than centre-based (median = 135; p < 0.001); volumes were higher in UMICs. 160 (45.8%) programs perceived insufficient capacity to meet demand, most commonly due to staffing and funding. Conclusions: Hybrid/alternative CR availability has not increased since 2016, and decreased since the pandemic. Reimbursement drives availability, but remains limited and inadequate, frequently shifting costs to patients, constraining services and limiting equitable scale-up.

Introduction The increasing incidence and reduced case fatality of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) have resulted in a growing number of people globally living with CVD as a chronic condition [ 1 ]. Consequently, there is an increasing need for secondary prevention to optimize both quantity and quality of life among people living with CVD. The disease, health system, and economic burden of CVD is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries, which account for approximately 80% of cardiovascular deaths globally [ 2 ]. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a multi-phase continuum of care spanning inpatient, early outpatient, and longer-term community-based secondary prevention services. Phase II represents the early outpatient stage of CR and provides a structured secondary prevention program, delivered over approximately two months on average globally [ 3 ], to support comprehensive risk-factor management and return to life roles [ 4 ]. Participation is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality, hospital readmissions, and improve quality of life [ 5 ]. Traditionally, CR has been delivered in clinical settings, most commonly within a hospital. Globally, approximately 80% of programs are hospital-based [ 6 ]. Despite its proven benefits, CR remains markedly under-utilized [ 7 ]. Common barriers to participation include distance from CR centres, transportation challenges, and competing work or time commitments [ 8 , 9 ] – barriers that disproportionately affect patients in rural regions, those of lower socioeconomic status, and working-age populations. To address these barriers, home-based models have increasingly been implemented. Home-based CR refers to a structured program in which core CR components are delivered outside of a traditional centre, with patients monitored and supported remotely by healthcare professionals [ 10 , 11 ]. Hybrid models combining centre- and home-based care have also become more commonly offered. These models may be delivered with or without digital technologies, such as synchronous supervised group exercise classes via video streaming. In addition, some programs offer services in community settings, which too can mitigate barriers related to distance and transportation costs. These models were designed chiefly to mitigate patient-level CR barriers to access [ 8 , 14 ]. Evidence shows that hybrid and/or alternative models of CR are safe [ 15 ], achieve clinical outcomes comparable to traditional centre-based models [ 12 ], and may be lower cost than traditional centre-based models [ 12 , 13 ]. The International Council of Cardiovascular Prevention and Rehabilitation (ICCPR) conducted a first-ever Global Audit of CR delivery in 2016 [ 16 , 17 ]. It included assessment of hybrid and/or alternative CR models. Programs in 51 countries with CR (45.9%; or 25.1% of all countries globally) offered any such models, with 31% of programs globally indicating their availability [ 18 ]. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, adoption of technology-enabled CR delivery increased substantially to reduce infectious risk. Indeed, during ICCPR’s COVID-19 Rapid Audit, approximately 40% of programs reported delivering hybrid and/or alternative models, primarily through low-technology approaches. However, the extent to which these models are currently implemented worldwide remains unknown, particularly given their historically limited reimbursement [ 18 , 19 ]. Therefore, this 2025 ICCPR Audit Update sub-study focused on the implementation of hybrid/alternative models globally. The objectives were to describe globally phase II CR hybrid/alternative model: (1) reimbursement, (2) availability, (3) nature / implementation of model services (i.e., structure, staffing, communication); (4) dose; (5) patient model allocation criteria; as well as (6) annual patient volumes and capacity. Findings were examined by: (a) country income classification, and where possible, compared with (b) center-based models and/or (c) the prior ICCPR Audits [ 16 , 17 ]. Methods Design & procedure This Global Audit Update was cross-sectional and observational in design. The protocol was approved by ICCPR Executive and publicly posted prior to study initiation ( https://globalcardiacrehab.com/2025/26-Audit-Update ). Ethical approval was obtained from York University (Toronto, Canada; e2025-057). Procedures are depicted elsewhere (“Turk-Adawi K, Ghisi GLM, Arar N, et al. [Unpublished]”). Audit findings regarding CR capacity and coverage (“Turk-Adawi K, Ghisi GLM, Arar N, et al. [Unpublished]”); referrals and access [ 6 ]; staffing, indications and dose [ 3 ]; as well as core components [ 20 ] are reported elsewhere (results by region are forthcoming [ 21 ]). The World Health Organization’s list of countries [ 22 ] was used as a basis for the Audit (with United Kingdom countries counted separately herein; N = 199). To determine which countries had existing CR programs, those reported to have any program availability in previous Audits (111 of 203 countries) [ 16 , 17 ] were first considered. CR and related Societies (including ICCPR members), or other national leaders identified through peer-reviewed publications, ICCPR’s network including umbrella Associations (e.g., International Society of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation), or organizational websites verified any national phase II CR service provision, and if affirmative the number of programs meeting Audit inclusion / exclusion criteria. In countries without CR, regional leads confirmed its current absence, or a local contact was made if disconfirmed. Identified leaders in countries with ≤9 CR programs were asked to share the contact email for each program in return for free ICCPR CR Foundations Certification (CRFC; value $125USD) [ 23 ]. Identified leaders in countries with ≥10 CR programs were asked to ascertain and share the program email addresses or distribute the survey invitation letter and link with their Society program email distribution list. Those who contributed were invited to collaborate to ensure accurate and fulsome dissemination of the results. Societies were offered a personalized national comparative results summary. An email invitation to complete the online survey was sent to each CR program email via REDCap (SurveyMonkey in China), or by the Society leader to their distribution lists. The email requested that the “lead clinician” of each program complete the survey, and that program staff communicate to ensure only one survey be completed per program. Audit participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey initiation. Respondents were not asked to identify themselves or their programs to reduce the potential for socially desirable responding. Participants were offered free ICCPR CRFC online training [ 23 ], or to identify a member of their team to receive this free access. Data were collected between May 1 st and September 1 st , 2025. Up to two reminders were issued to non-responders at a two-week interval. ICCPR also distributed the survey invitation through their four social media channels, as well as their program and Council member email distribution lists. Audit leads and ICCPR Executive Board members also disseminated the survey invitation during CR-related conferences held in the data collection period. National leads were provided with their response percentage after one month, and where <40%, alternative strategies to enhance response were promoted, including providing a list of emails that failed delivery to identify an alternate, personal contact via phone or text, and distribution through Society social media channels or newsletters. Respondents with technical issues were provided the survey in PDF to complete, and responses were then entered by the Audit team to REDCap. Participants The study population comprised all phase II CR programs identified worldwide. The study sample included all programs from the population that were successfully identified and contacted to participate in the Audit. Note that in the initial Audit only a random subsample of programs was contacted in countries with ≥250 programs. The inclusion criterion was programs offering post-discharge services delivering at least: (1) an initial patient assessment; (2) structured aerobic exercise training, whether supervised or unsupervised (+/- technology); and (3) at least one additional core component [ 4 ] aimed at controlling cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., patient education, psychosocial or dietary counselling, tobacco cessation). Program staff were invited to contact the study email address where they were unsure about meeting these criteria, and some did. Programs that operated solely and temporarily as part of a research study were excluded. Study materials made it explicit to potential participants that phase I or maintenance programs were not to be reported. Measures The survey instrument was based on the 2016 Global CR Audit which comprised items harmonized across available national CR program surveys as described elsewhere [ 24 ], and some adaptations from the COVID-19 Rapid Audit survey [ 19 ]. The survey was updated for currency, brevity and clarity. The survey was publicly posted on the ICCPR website for three weeks to obtain input from the global CR community. It was revised accordingly. The full survey is available on ICCPR’s website ( https://globalcardiacrehab.com/2025/26-Audit-Update ). Similar to the previous Audits, response options included numbers (constrained to plausible maximum and minimums to reduce human errors), closed-ended options as well as open-ended text (e.g., for “other” responses). Branching logic was applied to minimize respondent burden where applicable. Initial items characterized respondent and program characteristics to describe the sample (e.g., year program opened). Country of program was coded into income classifications based on the World Bank [ 25 ]. Where available, comparable responses from the 2016 Audit [ 17 , 18 , 26 ] were extracted for comparison purposes, to enable inferential testing of decade change. There was a section on centre-based models, as well as hybrid and alternative models / settings still meeting inclusion criteria. In centre-based CR, exercise sessions are primarily supervised by clinical staff and education is offered in-person [ 17 ]; patients are often directed to exercise unsupervised at home or in their community between sessions [ 4 ], and to review patient education materials independently [ 27 ]. In home-based models, exercise can be supervised using technology or unsupervised [ 10 ]. Education can be delivered individually with a patient by a member of the clinical team through technology (mobile phone, video call) or in a group [ 28 ]. As in traditional centre-based models, patients may also be encouraged to review educational materials independently [ 28 ]. Herein, hybrid CR is defined as subsuming what programs label ‘home-based’ CR, given delivery almost always requires at least one on-site visit to establish safety, but also for rapport [ 10 , 29 ]. Alternative CR models are defined as phase II CR delivered in community-based settings (maintenance programs excluded), or “other” settings (programs directed to specify open-ended for coding). Survey items relevant to this sub-study included first whether alternative models were reimbursed (yes/no). With regard to availability, survey items queried whether hybrid and/or alternative CR models were offered (yes/no), and if yes, which types (i.e., home, community, and/or hybrid; if latter, program respondents were asked to specify details open-ended) and start year. Communication modes and their frequency of use were queried. Perceived barriers to communication with patients in these models (yes/no from a list of options based on the qualitative literature [ 30 ]) were also assessed. Program respondents were also asked what materials patients are provided to use to support them off-site (check all that apply). Programs were also asked to select all that apply from allocation criteria options, as well as acceptance of patient level of risk. With regards to dose, programs were to report the number of times patients are asked to come on site; total sessions (i.e., product of total prescribed weeks x frequency / week but not including session length) was also computed separately for center-based and hybrid/alternative delivery models. With regards to volumes and capacity, items assessed the proportion of programs’ patients served in hybrid/alternative models annually (percentage, %), and perception of insufficient alternative model capacity. Finally, programs answered an open-ended item regarding strategies to increase hybrid/alternative CR model capacity. Data analysis Data were analyzed using SPSS version 31 [ 31 ]. All initiated surveys were included in the analysis. Country information was missing for 18 (1.2%) initiated surveys; country response proportion was computed based on available responses. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and medians with 25th–75th percentile ranges (P25-P75) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, were calculated using all available data, accounting for branching logic. Descriptive analyses regarding criteria to allocate patients to a hybrid/alternative model were complemented by a heat map illustrating the frequencies by country income class, created using Microsoft Excel. Open-ended responses were coded using content analysis [ 32 ]. Non-parametric tests were used where data were not normally distributed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 throughout; however, where multiple comparisons were performed such as when comparing by income class, a more conservative threshold of p < 0.01 was applied. Where applicable, effect sizes for chi-square tests were quantified using Cramér’s V, and interpreted using conventional thresholds: 0.10 small, 0.30 moderate, and 0.50 large effects. Inferential testing for differences by Audit assessment point (initial [ 18 ], COVID-19 [ 19 ] and current) were undertaken where appropriate, however caution is warranted in over-interpretation as these are repeated cross-sectional comparisons, with the COVID-19 sample skewed by pervasive program closures. Note it was not considered appropriate to test patient volume or capacity changes from the initial Audit given hybrid/alternative models were investigated more fulsomely in the Audit Update given changes since the pandemic. Clustering of program-level data within countries was addressed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM), specifying country as a random effect. Continuous outcomes (dose) were modeled assuming Poisson or negative binomial distributions, depending on dispersion. Residual distributions were examined to confirm model assumptions. Where significant, post-hoc pairwise Bonferroni comparisons were performed by income classes. Where a larger model was not warranted / exploratory purposes, as applicable chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used as applicable. Global availability and structure of hybrid/alternative models Globally, 371/1258 (29.5%) programs offered some form of hybrid/alternative model ( Fig 1 and Table 3 by country), with 269/371 (72.5%) of those home-based, 151 (40.7%) offering hybrid, and 141 (38.0%) community-based services. Content analysis of open-ended responses revealed substantial heterogeneity in program descriptions of their hybrid delivery models. Most programs described requiring an initial in-person assessment prior to transition to home-based delivery (64%). Step-down models – whereby patients completed an initial period of centre-based CR followed by supervised home-based rehabilitation – were the next most-commonly reported (56%). Nearly half of programs (49%) incorporated on-going supervision of home-based exercise through telephone or tele-health follow-up. Technology-enabled delivery – including videoconferencing platforms, Apps, and remote physiological monitoring – was reported by 41% of programs. Next, 30% of programs described flexible or individualized hybrid pathways tailored uniquely to meet patient needs. Integration with community-based settings (e.g., gyms, local physiotherapy services) was reported by 23% of programs. A smaller proportion (19%) reported minimally-supervised or low-intensity hybrid models, typically involving limited in-person contact with remote follow-up. Finally, few programs reported ‘other alternative’ phase II delivery models open-ended. These included digitally-integrated treatment pathways (33.3%), prescription-based or de-centralized specialist delivery models without clinic attendance (33.3%), and CR delivered through primary care or payer-linked management plans, such as health fund or general practitioner-led telephone programs (33.3%). In their hybrid/alternative model, globally, 67/333 (20.1%) of programs reported not having any prescribed on-site visits for patients (i.e., fully home-based); other programs offering this model request patients come on-site a median of 2 times/program (P25-P75 = 1–4). Median on-site visit requirements were significantly higher in UMICs (median = 3; P25-P75: 1–4; Kruskal–Wallis H(3)=25.90, p < 0.001). Relatedly, 160/344 (46.5%) programs ‘always’ and 115 (33.4%) ‘mostly’ (i.e., exceptions made on case-by-case basis) request that patients come on-site for a post-program assessment. The requirement for patients to attend an on-site post-program assessment differed significantly by country income classification (χ²(8)=28.29, p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.20). Specifically, programs in UMICs were more likely to report consistently requiring on-site post-program assessments, whereas programs in HICs more frequently reported flexibility, including partial or fully remote assessments. Among programs requesting on-site post-program assessments, they estimated a median of 70.0% (P25-P75 = 40–90) of patients oblige; this too differed significantly by country income classification (Kruskal–Wallis H(2) = 23.01, p < 0.001), with higher attendance reported by programs in HICs (median = 90.0%) compared with those in UMICs (median = 70.0%) and LMICs (median = 65.0%). Of the programs offering any hybrid/alternative CR models, 165/363 (45.5%) programs reported these models as reimbursable by government or insurance companies in their region (χ²(6)=25.07, p < 0.001, vs 54.5% programs not offering these models). At least some programs (not necessarily all given healthcare policy often varies by region within countries) in 48 countries offering hybrid/alternative models reported these services as not reimbursed, Countries spanned all World Bank income groups [ 25 ], with the largest numbers of programs reporting non-reimbursement located in China, India, Australia, United Kingdom, Canada, Brazil, and Mexico. Availability of hybrid/alternative CR delivery models also differed significantly by country income class (χ²(4)=12.31, p = 0.015), although the magnitude of association was small (Cramér’s V = 0.099; Table 3 ). Post-hoc pairwise χ² tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.01) indicated that availability was significantly higher in UMICs compared with HICs (p = 0.006). Differences between LMICs and HICs (p = 0.05) and between LMICs and UMICs (p = 0.80) did not reach the corrected significance threshold. In the 2016 Initial Audit [ 18 ], 285 (31.1%) programs reported offering hybrid and/or alternative models of CR, a proportion that did not differ from that reported in the 2025 Global Audit (χ²(1)=0.65, p = 0.42). Among programs offering alternative models, 82 (30.5%) reported these models as reimbursable in 2016 [ 18 ]. As reported in the Rapid COVID-19 Audit [ 19 ], 150 (18.5%) programs reported offering hybrid and/or alternative CR models immediately before the COVID-19 pandemic was declared, with 172 (21.2%) programs offering such models during the pandemic. This was significantly lower than the proportion reported in the 2025 Global Audit (χ²(1)=17.10, p < 0.001), which could reflect that most programs were at least temporarily closed in 2020 [ 19 ]. Among programs offering alternative models, 131 (41.5%) reported these models as reimbursable during the pandemic. In this 2025 Audit Update, programs globally reported that these hybrid/alternative models were initiated early in the pandemic (median year = 2020; P25-75: 2018–2023). By income class (Kruskal–Wallis H(3)=10.05, p = 0.018), the median year of model initiation was significantly more recent in UMICs and LICs (median = 2021). In programs reporting physicians directly interacting with patients in hybrid/alternative models, the most common specialty was general cardiology (n = 31), and cardiologists with subspecialty training in preventive cardiology or CR specifically (n = 10) were also reported. Next were physical medicine and rehabilitation (physiatrists; n = 13), sports medicine (n = 3) and primary care physicians (n = 2). Other non-physician provider types interacting with patients in these models were: dietitians/nutritionists (n = 14), psychologists (n = 7), and occupational therapists (n = 4). Less commonly reported were social workers, technicians, and therapy/healthcare assistants (each n = 2). Single mentions were: other allied health provider (unspecified), care navigator, pharmacist, and one program specified no direct staff-patient interaction. Model allocation criteria, annual patient volumes and capacity Globally, programs most commonly reported offering hybrid/alternative models on the basis of: patient choice, distance to centre, transportation barriers, risk stratification, patient indication, time or work constraints during CR centre hours, and cost. Discussion This global Audit Update provides the most current and comprehensive examination to date of hybrid and alternative CR models worldwide. As evidence has accumulated suggesting comparable efficacy of home-based and alternative CR models relative to traditional centre-based delivery [ 12 ], there have been increasing calls to expand their availability as a strategy to address the persistent under-capacity of CR globally [ 34 , 35 ]. Leveraging ICCPR’s network, this Audit is unique in its global scope, high country- and program-level response proportions, and its ability to characterize contemporary practice following the rapid expansion of alternative delivery models during the COVID-19 pandemic. Overall, approximately one-quarter of programs reported reimbursement for hybrid/alternative models, available in roughly one-third of countries with CR globally. Notably, despite increased evidence supporting the effectiveness of hybrid and alternative CR models and their widespread uptake during the COVID-19 pandemic [ 19 , 36 ], their overall availability does not appear to have substantially increased compared with the initial 2016 ICCPR Audit [ 18 ]. This suggests that pandemic-related expansion was largely temporary and not translated into sustained integration of alternative models within routine CR delivery. Encouragingly, programs delivering these models reported multidisciplinary patient care and provision of education, exercise tracking and resistance training materials to patients to support their secondary prevention. However, compared with centre-based models, in hybrid/alternative programs, patients were generally prescribed a lower dose of CR, and in one-fifth of programs patients received fewer core components. While patient choice and clinical factors appropriately guided CR model allocation, patient cost emerged as an increasingly prominent deterrent. UMICs appeared to be leading implementation efforts, yet substantial barriers related to staffing, financing, and digital access persist. Reimbursement and availability of hybrid and alternative models Reimbursement for hybrid and alternative CR models remains limited. Fewer than one-quarter of programs reported that these models were reimbursed, stemming from only approximately one-third of countries where CR exists. While given the nature of the design no causal conclusions can be drawn, a clear gradient was observed by country income classification, as is observed for universal health coverage more broadly [ 37 ], with reimbursement negligible in LICs and LMICs. Even where reimbursement was reported through government or insurance mechanisms, patients appeared to continue to bear a substantial proportion of delivery costs, with reported averages of approximately one-third of total program costs, although this was notably lower than the out-of-pocket burden observed where programs were not reimbursed. Reimbursement was more frequently reported where these models were available, underscoring that reimbursement, while necessary, is insufficient on its own to eliminate financial barriers to participation (but again, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Among programs reporting reimbursement, community-based models and asynchronous exercise or education sessions were most commonly covered, particularly in HICs, whereas one-to-one or group education sessions were more frequently reimbursed in MICs. Notably, reimbursement for hybrid and alternative models was reported to be higher during the pandemic [ 19 ] and, in some cases, higher in the initial ICCPR audit than at present [ 18 ], suggesting a retrenchment of financial support as emergency measures expired. Availability of hybrid and alternative CR models varied in parallel with reimbursement patterns. Approximately 30% of programs reported offering such models, most commonly home-based, although over one-third of these were delivered in community settings. Programs with reimbursement were substantially more likely to offer hybrid or alternative models. While given the cross-sectional design, this association cannot establish whether reimbursement increases availability, it does suggest policy advocacy might be a potential lever to expand funding as a mechanism to increase availability. Interestingly, availability was reported as more common in LMICs than in UMICs, despite lower reimbursement, suggesting that some programs may be innovating out of necessity. Availability rates were similar to that reported in 2016 [ 18 ], but appeared lower during the COVID period [ 19 ], potentially reflecting widespread program closures at that time. Importantly, the proportion of programs offering hybrid or alternative models was similar to that reported in 2016 [ 18 ] and, in some settings, lower than during the pandemic period [ 19 ], although a higher proportion of a program’s patients were allocated to these models than in 2016 [ 18 ]. While caution in interpretation is warranted given the repeated cross-sectional design, this suggests that gains in availability achieved during COVID-19 were not maintained; this potentially reflects a withdrawal of temporary reimbursement mechanisms and emergency policy flexibilities, not necessarily a lack of feasibility or acceptability of these models. Nature, implementation, and dose of hybrid and alternative models In terms of program structure and implementation, approximately one-fifth of programs reported that the overall content of hybrid or alternative models was equivalent to centre-based CR, and a similar proportion indicated that these models enabled greater individual tailoring to patient needs. However, concerns regarding comprehensiveness were also evident: around 20% of programs reported less comprehensive assessments and consequently fewer risk factors addressed, particularly in UMICs, and one-fifth reported delivery of fewer core CR components, although this was less common in HICs. The dose of CR delivered through hybrid and alternative models was consistently lower than that of centre-based programs, with a median of 10 sessions, typically delivered once weekly over approximately eight weeks. This dose was observed across country income classifications and remained stable relative to prior ICCPR Audits [ 18 , 19 ]. While 20% of programs reported no on-site visits, those incorporating in-person contact delivered a median of two visits per patient. Post-program assessments were most-commonly conducted on-site, reported by approximately 80% of programs, with higher rates in UMICs; however, programs perceived patient greater attendance at on-site assessments in HICs, suggesting ongoing access barriers elsewhere. Patients participating in hybrid and alternative models were exposed to multidisciplinary teams, most frequently including exercise professionals, nurses, and physicians. Physician involvement was reported more often in UMICs, potentially reflecting differences in workforce availability, remuneration structures, or scope of practice. Communication modalities revealed a bimodal pattern: programs tended to rely either on high-technology solutions (e.g., Apps, web platforms, email) or low-technology approaches. Email communication was less common in MICs, while text messaging was more frequently used and landline telephone use was less prevalent in LMICs. Compared with 2016 [ 18 ], texting was more commonly used, reflecting broader global shifts in communication practices. Model allocation criteria Allocation of patients to hybrid or alternative CR models was primarily patient-driven, with approximately 70% of programs citing patient choice as a key criterion, reflecting patient-centredness [ 38 ]. Importantly, clinical considerations also featured prominently: patient risk level and clinical indication were each reported by roughly 60% of programs, and were more frequently cited in UMICs. This suggests that as hybrid and alternative models expand in these settings, implementation is increasingly aligned with contemporary evidence and clinical guidance. Compared with the 2016 Audit [ 18 ], clinical factors were more heavily weighted, representing a positive shift toward evidence-informed practice. Distance and transportation barriers were also commonly cited allocation criteria, consistent with patient-reported determinants of model selection in prior research [ 14 ]. These considerations were particularly salient in lower-income settings, where patient resource constraints are greater. Conversely, cost was identified as a factor influencing allocation in approximately 30% of programs, representing an increase from the initial Audit [ 19 ]. This finding is concerning; financial barriers continue to deter participation even where alternative models are available. Acceptance of high-risk patients into hybrid or alternative models was reported by approximately 20% of programs, and remained consistent across income groups and over time [ 16 , 17 ], although acceptance of moderate-risk patients appeared lower than in 2016, a finding that may warrant further investigation. Patient volumes and barriers to scale-up Despite their potential to address CR under-capacity [ 39 ], hybrid and alternative models currently serve a modest proportion of patients. Where available, approximately one-fifth of a program’s annual CR patients were enrolled in these models, with lower proportions observed in lower-income settings. Programs reported serving a median of 50 patients annually through hybrid or alternative delivery, indicating limited scale. Volumes and capacity were higher in UMICs and were reported to be greater during the COVID period [ 19 ], likely reflecting both necessity-driven adoption and temporary policy supports [ 40 ]. Approximately half of programs reported barriers to expanding hybrid or alternative CR delivery, a proportion similar to that reported in 2016 [ 18 ] but lower than during the pandemic [ 19 ]. The most-commonly cited barriers were insufficient staffing, inadequate financial support, and limited digital assets, with these challenges reported more frequently in low-income countries and UMICs. These findings highlight persistent structural constraints that limit the scalability of alternative CR models, even where feasibility and acceptability have been demonstrated. Implications for practice, policy, and research From a practice perspective, findings suggest that while hybrid and alternative CR models are being delivered by a multidisciplinary team and with patient-centred allocation, attention is needed to ensure adequate dose and comprehensive coverage of core CR components. Programs may benefit from guidance on minimum standards for content and dose that preserve effectiveness while maintaining flexibility, such as is offered in the models module of ICCPR’s CR Foundations Certification online training for multidisciplinary professionals [ 23 ]. Policy implications are substantial. Reimbursement was strongly associated with availability, yet remains limited and, in some cases, lower than during the pandemic. Policy-makers should consider sustained funding mechanisms for hybrid and other alternative CR models as one potential strategy to expand secondary prevention services and reduce inequities in access. The ICCPR and affiliated professional Societies are well positioned to advocate for harmonized policy approaches and to support member countries in integrating alternative models into health systems [ 41 , 42 ]. The lack of sustained growth in hybrid and alternative CR availability represents a missed opportunity. The pandemic demonstrated that rapid scale-up is feasible when policy, reimbursement, and organizational supports align. Failure to maintain these gains risks perpetuating global CR under-capacity despite the availability of effective delivery models. Consideration of dose should be foremost in implementation planning. Research priorities include evaluating strategies to increase program scale without compromising quality, understanding patient outcomes associated with lower-dose delivery, and identifying cost-effective implementation approaches across diverse settings. Further work is also needed to address digital inequities and to develop low-tech solutions that maintain fidelity to evidence-based CR. Limitations This Audit has several limitations. Chiefly, non-response bias is possible . Although the recruitment approach was designed to achieve broad international representation, responding programs may differ systematically from non-responding programs in characteristics relevant to this sub-study, including program resources, reimbursement, delivery models, and use of digital technologies. If programs with greater resources or more established services were more likely to respond, the availability of hybrid/alternative models, reimbursement, and digital technology use may be over-estimated. Conversely, programs with fewer resources or limited capacity may have been less likely to respond, particularly in lower-resource settings. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as describing responding programs rather than providing definitive estimates of global hybrid/alternative CR availability or reimbursement. Relatedly, while global in scope, representativeness may be limited, particularly in LICs, where programs were under-represented. In addition, within-country heterogeneity may not be fully captured; in large countries especially, CR penetration and practices may vary substantially across regions. Therefore, data reported by participating programs may not fully reflect national-level availability or service delivery. Data were self-reported and as such may be subject to social desirability bias, with additional potential for variability due to health system, cultural, and language differences across regions. Comparisons over time are constrained by the repeated cross-sectional design, particularly related to program closures during the COVID period. With regard to analyses, there are considerations related to country-level clustering and statistical inference. Programs were nested within countries, and country-level factors such as reimbursement mechanisms, healthcare systems, and regulatory frameworks may have influenced program characteristics. The number of responding programs also varied substantially across countries, meaning that countries with larger numbers of programs contributed more observations to program-level analyses. For analyses where appropriate, country was included as a random effect in GLMM to account for clustering; however, not all descriptive analyses or comparisons were amenable to multi-level modeling. Consequently, some observations may not have been statistically independent, and estimates from program-level analyses should be interpreted with consideration of potential country-level clustering and unequal country representation. Finally, multiple inferential comparisons increase the risk of type I error; findings should be interpreted cautiously. Conclusions In this global Audit Update, hybrid and alternative CR models were available in approximately one-third of programs and reimbursed in only one-quarter, despite growing evidence supporting their effectiveness and their potential to address global CR under-capacity. While models were generally delivered with multidisciplinary involvement and patient-centred allocation, they were characterized by lower dose and, in some cases, reduced comprehensiveness compared with centre-based CR. Financial, staffing, and digital barriers appear to constrain scale-up, with notable disparities by country income classification. These findings highlight the potential need for sustained reimbursement, implementation support, and policy alignment to realize the full potential of hybrid and alternative CR models in advancing equitable global cardiovascular care.
