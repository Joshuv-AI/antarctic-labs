Traumatic Failure of a Left Internal Mammary Artery Graft Following Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting: A Case Report
Authors: Daniel Philipson, Dustin Lee, Celso Diaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic injury to coronary artery bypass grafts is an uncommon but clinically significant cause of recurrent myocardial ischemia after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Internal mammary artery grafts are generally associated with excellent long-term patency; however, blunt chest trauma may result in graft distortion, anastomotic injury, or occlusion. We report a case of a 78-year-old man with hypertension who presented with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and was found to have severe three-vessel coronary artery disease. He underwent uncomplicated three-vessel CABG consisting of left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to left anterior descending artery (LAD), right internal mammary artery (RIMA) to right posterior descending artery (RPDA), and saphenous vein graft (SVG) to obtuse marginal artery (OM). Three months later, he sustained blunt chest trauma after falling from the roof of a shed and subsequently developed exertional angina. Stress myocardial perfusion imaging demonstrated apical ischemia in the LAD territory. Coronary angiography revealed severe stenosis of the distal LIMA-LAD anastomosis and occlusion of the SVG-OM graft. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) confirmed traumatic distortion of the distal LIMA graft. The patient underwent successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the LIMA-LAD graft with overlapping drug-eluting stents (DES) measuring 2.5×38 mm and 2.5×18 mm, as well as PCI of the native circumflex artery with DES implantation. At follow-up, his anginal symptoms had completely resolved. This case highlights blunt chest trauma as a rare mechanism of graft failure following CABG and underscores the role of multimodality imaging and percutaneous revascularization in management.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting remains a cornerstone therapy for patients with complex multivessel coronary artery disease, particularly among those with diabetes mellitus or left ventricular dysfunction [1]. The left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery (LAD) is considered the gold-standard conduit because of its superior long-term patency and association with improved survival outcomes compared with venous grafts [1,2]. Despite these advantages, graft failure may occur due to thrombosis, technical complications, intimal hyperplasia, progressive atherosclerosis, or competitive flow [3-7].

Traumatic injury to bypass grafts after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is exceedingly rare [8,9]. Blunt chest trauma may compromise graft integrity through stretching, compression, dissection, or disruption of distal anastomoses. The anterior course of the internal mammary arteries along the chest wall may predispose these conduits to injury following thoracic trauma [8]. Because symptoms may initially be attributed to musculoskeletal injury, diagnosis may be delayed. We present a case of traumatic distal left internal mammary artery (LIMA) graft stenosis resulting in recurrent ischemia shortly after CABG.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old man with a medical history significant for hypertension presented with substernal chest pain radiating to the left arm. Electrocardiography demonstrated non-specific ST-segment abnormalities, and serial high-sensitivity troponin measurements were elevated, consistent with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without significant valvular disease. Coronary angiography revealed severe three-vessel coronary artery disease involving the LAD, left circumflex, and right coronary artery territories (Figures 1A-1C).

The patient underwent uncomplicated three-vessel CABG with LIMA-to-LAD, right internal mammary artery (RIMA)-to-right posterior descending artery (RPDA), and saphenous vein graft (SVG)-to-obtuse marginal artery (OM) grafting. His post-operative course was uneventful, and he was discharged home on guideline-directed medical therapy including aspirin, clopidogrel, beta-blocker therapy, and high-intensity statin therapy.

Approximately three months following surgery, the patient fell while working on the roof of a shed at his home and sustained blunt trauma to the anterior chest wall. Although he did not initially seek medical evaluation, he subsequently developed progressive exertional chest discomfort consistent with stable angina. Given recurrent symptoms, stress testing with single-photon emission computed tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (SPECT MPI) was performed, which demonstrated reversible ischemia involving the apical LAD territory (Figure 2).

Repeat coronary angiography revealed complete occlusion of the mid LAD, severe focal stenosis at the distal anastomosis of the LIMA-LAD graft, as well as complete occlusion of the SVG-OM graft (Figure 3). The RIMA-RPDA graft remained widely patent. Coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrated focal narrowing and distortion of the distal LIMA graft near the anastomotic site, findings consistent with traumatic graft injury (Figure 4).

Given ongoing symptoms, objective evidence of ischemia, and now complete occlusion of the native LAD, the patient underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the LIMA-LAD graft using overlapping 2.5×38 mm and 2.5×18 mm drug-eluting stents (DES) with an excellent angiographic result and restoration of Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) 3 flow. PCI of the native circumflex artery was also successfully performed with DES implantation due to SVG occlusion. The patient tolerated the procedures without complications. At follow-up evaluation, he reported complete resolution of exertional angina and improved functional capacity, with no recurrent symptoms.

Discussion

Early graft failure after CABG is most commonly related to technical issues, thrombosis, or intimal hyperplasia, whereas late graft failure is generally attributable to progressive atherosclerosis [3-6]. Trauma-related graft compromise is rare but represents an important and likely underrecognized mechanism of recurrent ischemia following CABG [8,9].

The LIMA graft is characterized by excellent long-term durability and resistance to atherosclerosis due to favorable endothelial properties and nitric oxide production [1,2]. However, because the conduit courses along the anterior thoracic wall before entering the mediastinum, blunt chest trauma may produce mechanical injury through stretching, compression, torsion, or disruption of the distal anastomosis [8,9]. In the present case, the temporal relationship between blunt thoracic trauma and subsequent anginal symptoms strongly supports trauma as the precipitating factor for graft failure.

Several mechanisms of traumatic graft failure have been proposed, including intimal disruption, intramural hematoma formation, dissection, external compression, graft kinking, and anastomotic distortion [8,9]. The distal LIMA anastomosis may be particularly susceptible to injury due to tethering forces at the coronary insertion site during sudden deceleration or blunt impact.

Recognition of traumatic graft injury requires a high index of suspicion, particularly in patients with recent CABG presenting with recurrent angina after thoracic trauma. Functional imaging such as single-photon emission computed tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (SPECT MPI) may identify ischemic territories, while invasive coronary angiography remains the gold standard for defining graft patency and lesion severity. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) provides complementary anatomic detail and may better characterize graft distortion, external compression, or traumatic structural abnormalities not fully appreciated angiographically.

Management strategies depend on lesion anatomy, symptom burden, ischemic territory, and surgical risk. Redo sternotomy and repeat CABG are associated with substantially increased morbidity and mortality in elderly patients. Consequently, PCI has emerged as an effective and less invasive alternative for selected graft lesions [7]. Interventions involving internal mammary artery grafts can be technically challenging because of vessel tortuosity and risk of spasm or dissection [7]. Nevertheless, advances in guide catheter support, wire technology, and DES platforms have improved procedural success rates and long-term outcomes [10].

This case also illustrates the vulnerability of recently implanted grafts during the post-operative healing period. Patients recovering from CABG should be counseled regarding activity restrictions and the importance of prompt medical evaluation following significant chest trauma.

Conclusions

Traumatic injury to bypass grafts is a rare but important cause of recurrent ischemia after CABG. Internal mammary artery grafts, despite excellent long-term patency, may be susceptible to blunt thoracic trauma, particularly in the early post-operative period. Multimodality imaging, including stress testing, coronary angiography, and CCTA, can facilitate diagnosis. PCI represents a safe and effective treatment strategy in appropriately selected patients and may provide excellent symptomatic relief while avoiding the risks associated with redo surgery.
