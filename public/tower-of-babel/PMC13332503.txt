ECG challenge: ST-segment elevation after pulsed-field ablation
Authors: Thai Duy Vo, Ngoc Dung Kieu, Chin-Yu Lin
Published: 2026

Clinical vignette

An 85-year-old woman with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, prior hemicolectomy for ascending colon adenocarcinoma, and end-stage kidney disease on maintenance haemodialysis underwent pulsed-field ablation for symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, with concomitant left atrial appendage occlusion. Pulmonary vein isolation and occluder deployment were completed without immediate complication under fluoroscopic and electroanatomic guidance (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2). One hour later, she developed acute dyspnoea and severe hypertension (blood pressure 210/130 mmHg). She reported no typical chest pain; however, her renal failure, advanced age, and recent left atrial instrumentation made silent peri-procedural myocardial infarction an important concern. A 12-lead ECG was obtained (Figure 1). It showed an intermittent junctional rhythm at 51 b.p.m., apparent ST-segment elevation in V1–V4, and broad, hyperacute T waves in II, III, and aVF. No single coronary territory explained all the abnormalities, as the limb and precordial leads were involved in a non-contiguous pattern. The differential diagnosis included acute coronary occlusion, coronary air embolism, pulsed-field ablation-related coronary vasospasm, a hypertensive repolarisation abnormality, hyperkalaemia, and technical artefact.

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram obtained 1 h after pulsed-field ablation and left atrial appendage occlusion, showing an intermittent junctional rhythm, apparent ST-segment elevation in V1–V4, and broad, hyperacute T waves in the inferior leads.

Correct answer: C

Explanation: Although the tracing appeared alarming, the pattern was anatomically discordant: the limb and precordial leads were distorted without a coherent single-vessel distribution. Arterial pulse-tapping artefact arises when an electrode overlies, or is mechanically coupled to, a pulsatile artery or vascular access; cardiac-cycle-synchronous motion can produce apparent ST-segment elevation and pseudo-hyperacute T waves. Here, complete normalization after electrode repositioning strongly favoured this technical mimic.1

Correct answer: B

Explanation: Post-procedural ST-segment elevation should be regarded as potentially ischaemic until promptly reassessed. In a stable patient, however, an immediate ECG audit is low-risk and high-yield: verify lead identity, calibration, and electrode contact, then repeat the tracing after moving the electrodes away from pulsatile sources. In this case, the repeat ECG after blood pressure control and electrode repositioning normalized, avoiding unnecessary invasive escalation.1,2
