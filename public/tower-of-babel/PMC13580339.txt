N. Paul Rosman, M.D. (1934–2026)
Authors: Alcy Torres, Laurie Douglass, Phillip L Pearl, Karl Kuban
Published: 2026

N. Paul Rosman, MD, CM, legendary child neurologist and consummate leader devoted to the neurological care of children, died in Boston, MA, on March 18, 2026, at age 91. Dr. Rosman founded and led the child neurology division at Boston University's Boston City Hospital and carried the title of Professor Emeritus of Pediatrics and Neurology at the Boston University Chobanian & Avedisian School of Medicine at the time of his death.

Dr. Rosman helped shape the modern field of child neurology and influenced generations of child neurologists worldwide. For more than six decades, he was admired widely for his clinical insight, intellectual rigor, and deep compassion for his patients and their families.

Born in Ottawa, Canada, in 1934, Dr. Rosman graduated magna cum laude from McGill University, where he earned both a BSc and his medical degree. He then completed training in New York and returned to McGill for further residency. In 1963, he moved to Boston, where he spent the remainder of his career and played a key role in advancing clinical care, education, and research in pediatric neurology. In 1969, he became the first chief of child neurology at Boston City Hospital, where he remained for 17 years [1]. He then became chief of the division of pediatric neurology at the Floating Hospital for Children at Tufts–New England Medical Center, where he co‐founded and directed the Center for Children with Special Needs, advancing multidisciplinary, family‐centered care for children with complex neurodevelopmental conditions. Upon his retirement from the Floating, more than 70 of his former trainees, colleagues, and friends from at least 15 states and five foreign countries gathered at a festschrift to celebrate his lifetime of patient care, teaching, mentoring, and research. He remained clinically active at Boston Medical Center for many more years, returning in 2004, when he generously helped his colleague, friend, and newly appointed chief of child neurology, Karl Kuban, rebuild a board‐approved child neurology residency program at Boston Medical Center/Boston University.

Internationally recognized for his scholarship and leadership, Dr. Rosman authored more than 200 articles and book chapters, received more than 30 research grants, and made foundational contributions to the understanding of febrile seizures and neurodevelopmental disorders. He co‐wrote a classic text, Pediatrics, Neurology, and Psychiatry—Common Ground, which melded his interest in the overlap between neurology and behavior. He continued to be academically productive to the end of his life; his final paper, “Intubation in Children Presenting with Seizures to a Pediatric Emergency Department in a Safety‐Net Hospital,” was published posthumously in Frontiers in Pediatrics [ 3 ].

Dr. Rosman was a diplomate of the American Board of Pediatrics and a diplomate of the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology (Neurology with Special Competence in Child Neurology, as well as Subspecialty Certification in Neurodevelopmental Disabilities).

Dr. Rosman served in many capacities in professional organizations, including president of the Child Neurology Society (1978–1979), director of the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology (1983–1990), and a member of the executive board of the International Child Neurology Association (1986–2002). He was honored by his peers with the prestigious Hower Award from the Child Neurology Society in 1998 for outstanding contributions to child neurology, and he received numerous teaching honors at Tufts and Boston University, including the Metcalf Teaching Award given to a single individual campus‐wide across all undergraduate and graduate programs at Boston University. His other honors included an honorary Doctor of Science in 2023 from his alma mater, McGill University, which he said was one of the best days of his life.

Dr. Rosman was a loyal and generous supporter of McGill University, establishing the Dr. N. Paul Rosman McGill Medical Student Bursary to help medical students pursue research opportunities under the supervision of a McGill faculty member. Medical students often sent him their research papers for review, and he developed friendships with several of them. He organized many of his medical school class reunions at McGill. He also loved the Osler Library at McGill.

Dr. Rosman's clinical care was exemplary, and his interview and examination techniques with his young patients were creative. His imaginative engagement with them might include anything from discussing World Wrestling Federation fighters to a favorite movie or TV show (two of his were Friends and Dancing with the Stars!).

As an educator and inspirational model, Dr. Rosman's influence has been remarkable. He helped shape the careers of thousands of medical students, directly mentored and trained more than 100 pediatric neurologists, and inspired generations of clinicians and scientists to carry forward his traditions of dedicated patient care, teaching, and research. Many of these trainees now lead their own divisions and programs in pediatric neurology [2]. A reflection of his commitment to education is the routine inclusion as co‐authors of medical students, pediatric and neurology residents, child neurology trainees, and fellows, as well as junior staff. Dr. Rosman's efforts in editing his collaborators' drafts of papers were notoriously reflected in the broad splatter of micrographic red pencil edits across each page, which prompted visions of a Jackson Pollock work.

Despite the demands Dr. Rosman placed on himself and others, he was equally known for his warmth, humor, and storytelling. He retained a personable demeanor even after long days of work, and his ability to connect with people, whether with colleagues, trainees, or children, remained a defining feature of his personality throughout his life.

At a celebratory event for Dr. Rosman's retirement from Boston University, the then chief of pediatric neurology, Karl Kuban, shared a few action pearls he had gleaned from his time working with Dr. Rosman, including:

1.Recognize the importance in having faith and belief in the potential of trainees and dreamers regardless of their medical school pedigree. The most important factor for success was the hunger to learn and the willingness to expend effort.

2.Emphasize the value of the examination and history as keys to excellence in clinical care even in this era of sophisticated testing and imaging and in the face of constraints imposed by using standard business models to manage medicine.

3.Know that having a small program does not mean educational goals, clinical exposures, or experiences need to be diminished or compromised. It means being creative, using undiscovered resources, and widely sharing history and examination of instructive patients by having them participate live in educational forums.

4.Recognize that smaller programs sometimes have hidden advantages, which may include greater flexibility and individualized support. At times, this can translate into more time for finding innovation opportunities, participating in professional organizations, developing educational repertoires, winning grants, publishing, pursuing leadership development opportunities, and enjoying work‐life balance.

5.Teach by modeling what it means to be an exceptional teacher‐leader‐human. This includes being personable and showing interest in the life and stories of patients and others whose paths you cross, nurturing camaraderie, bringing humor and zest into the educational theater, and recognizing that at times being human means blurring the boundaries between colleague, friend, mentor, teacher, and student.

The support of Dr. Rosman's family was fundamental to his achievements. He was deeply devoted to his wife, Syrille, who was his biggest supporter for over 65 years, as well as his three children (Michael, Adam, and Elizabeth) and six grandchildren.

Being able to say, “I trained with Paul Rosman,” immediately conveys a special, life‐changing experience shared by those who were privileged to train under, work with, or associate with him. Dr. Rosman achieved the rarefied status of a quadruple threat, adding to the classic triad of expertise in clinical care, teaching, and research the humanistic attributes that made him the mentor and friend he was to so many people (Figures 1 and 2).

Above all, we will remember Dr. Rosman for his humanity, uncompromising standards, devotion to his trainees, unwavering commitment to children and their families, and deeply felt friendship. Dr. Rosman helped nurture the field of child neurology through its infancy into the vibrant mature field that exists today. He influenced the field through his hundreds of meticulously written publications and with his modeling of what it means to be an excellent child neurologist that continues to be passed down the chain from educator to trainee to educator to trainee.
