Enhancing prediction accuracy for Parkinson’s disease using advanced machine learning models
Authors: Pradeepta Kumar Sarangi, Rajnish Srivastava, Monica Dutta, Ashwin Dobariya, Subhanshu Goyal, Sunil Lavadiya, Samah Alshathri, Walid El-Shafai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Globally, Parkinson’s disease (PD), a neurological disorder, has a negative influence on the health of older adults. PD has no validated means of treatment, and thus, affects both social (i.e., mental) and physical health among individuals who experience PD. Therefore, it is essential to develop new methods for detecting PD to benefit the health of everyone involved. There is an opportunity to improve PD detection and accuracy by leveraging structured data sources and employing machine learning (ML). Ultimately, the goal of this research work is to provide an overall descriptive ML approach to diagnose PD utilizing acoustical data from the UCI Parkinson’s disease dataset and comparing XGBoost, random forest (RF), Support vector machine (SVM), and K-nearest neighbours (KNN), four of the most common algorithms for classifying data, in their ability to classify PD. The research project was conducted in four phases: (1) establishing a baseline model; (2) Stratifying a 10-Fold cross-validation; (3) Balancing the classes with synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE); and (4) Reducing dimensions with principal component analysis (PCA). XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy (97.22%) among all models for classifying PD across all four phases of the research project, and also the highest Matthews correlation coefficient among all algorithms tested. This proves that XGBoost is better suited to model non-linear relationships. The results of the experiment showed that using SMOTE significantly improved the performance of the ML classification algorithm, especially that of the KNN algorithm. PCA significantly reduced the dataset’s dimensionality without sacrificing discriminative information. The outcomes demonstrated that the ML classification algorithms exhibited apparent performance differences, which diminished after applying SMOTE and PCA. Hence, it can be concluded that all machine learning classification algorithms had statistically similar classifier performance after the use of SMOTE and PCA. The results show that machine learning classification algorithms, particularly XGBoost and KNN, are effective at detecting PD early. The integration of SHAP-based explainability demonstrates that the proposed framework is not only accurate but also interpretable, thereby bridging the gap between predicted and clinically obtained results in PD diagnosis.

Introduction

Predictive modelling is used to identify early stages of disease in a variety of environments. By examining information such as past patient use history, genetics, and/or lifestyle, predictive modelling enables providers to estimate a patient’s risk of developing disease1. This will allow healthcare providers to use preventive measures to reduce patient risk and improve patient outcomes. Predictive modelling for identifying disease at its earliest stage is also heavily reliant on ML algorithms’ ability to sift through large volumes of complex healthcare data, identify correlations and patterns, and produce accurate predictions2. By using machine learning algorithms to analyse large datasets of healthcare data, healthcare professionals may be able to identify early warning signs of various diseases and provide timely interventions. The accuracy of predictive modelling is expected to increase as machine learning algorithms are applied and refined over time, with models optimised and retrained using datasets3.

Parkinson’s disease (PD) occurs from dead or malfunctioning midbrain neurons. The primary cause of this condition is a disorder that destroys neurons that produce dopamine in the substantia nigra (SN) of the human brain (Fig. 1). The presence of PD causes various motor control disturbances, including bradykinesia, tremor, muscle stiffness, postural instability, and difficulty walking, all of which result from a significant drop in dopamine levels (Fig. 2)4. Dopamine is a messenger that carries signals from the brain’s SN to another area called the corpus striatum, which helps control smooth muscle movement and body movement5. In motor control, dopamine helps the nigrostriatal pathway function more effectively by enhancing communication between the SN and the striatum. This pathway ensures a smooth start to voluntary actions by modulating motor circuit activity. Dementia and depression are among the cognitive and behavioural problems that may manifest as the disease progresses6. However, these non-motor symptoms may vary from one patient to another and are not easily measurable. They are also not specific. PD cannot be diagnosed based on non-motor symptoms alone. However, non-motor symptoms may be used to support diagnosis. Medical observations and signs are used to diagnose PD. The traditional methods used to diagnose PD may be subjective because they rely on observing subtle movements that are difficult to classify. Non-motor symptoms that individuals may experience may be minor and result from other diseases7. As a result, these indications are frequently disregarded, which makes it challenging to diagnose PD upfront. To improve diagnosis and assessment, machine learning methods have been used to classify patients with comparable clinical signs, such as movement abnormalities, into PD and other Parkinsonian conditions, or into PD and healthy controls8. Healthcare is using machine learning more. Machine learning lets a logical program learn and understand meaning from data semi-automatically9. Serum, CSF (cerebrospinal fluid), neurological imaging, speech, handwriting motifs, motion, cardiac scintigraphy, and OCT (optical coherence tomography) are used to diagnose PD using ML models10. ML can also diagnose PD using MRI and SPECT data. Early and accurate PD detection allows effective interventions and treatments to slow disease progression11. Machine learning can analyse large, complex datasets, making it a better method for early-disease detection12. The utilisation of both clinical and biomedical data for early PD detection using specific ML tools is currently demanded by neuroscientists13. Using a comprehensive methodology, we strive to present a robust framework for early PD detection14,15. We use the XGBoost algorithm, a gradient-boosting technique, to show machine learning’s potential in healthcare. Model accuracy, precision, and recall are critical evaluation metrics that reveal the ability and efficacy of ML to detect PD16. Machine learning can be utilised to identify pertinent characteristics that are not currently used for PD detection but can be leveraged and associated with prominent preclinical and atypical PD pathophysiology17,18. The present study not only utilised various ML models for early PD detection but also conducted a comparative analysis of their outcomes to identify the best model using principal component analysis (PCA). These translational approaches aim to incorporate ML applications into future clinical practice19.

In this study, we investigate how ML models can be used to identify PD. Our objectives include knowing the accuracy of different ML models in predicting early PD symptoms and developing methodologies to expedite PD identification. The primary contribution of this study will be to provide (a) a thorough comparison of each of the four different ML models in terms of their ability to predict early symptoms of PD, which will ultimately lead to an accurate identification of PD; (b) an assessment of the results generated by each ML model; and (c) an evaluation of each ML model’s ability to predict PD with respect to accuracy, confusion matrix, recall, sensitivity, F1-score, specificity, and precision.

Research gap, motivation and objectives

Several important issues and research gaps remain despite substantial research on the application of machine learning (ML) to Parkinson’s disease (PD) detection. The first motivation for conducting this research is that several important issues and research gaps in applying ML to detect PD remain unexplored. Prior research used basic classifiers based on limited feature sets and small sample sizes; therefore, while they may produce accurate disease predictions, they are not very robust or generalizable. In addition, many studies failed to report key performance metrics important in medicine, such as specificity and F1-score. Lastly, most previous studies evaluated only an algorithm, rather than comparing multiple algorithms within the same study. Another missing aspect of the previous research was adequate feature analysis. These weaknesses motivate this research.

Methodology

The proposed approach uses a structured machine learning workflow to produce valid calculations and prevent data leakage. The dataset was preprocessed with both feature scaling and encoding before applying the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to balance the class distribution. SMOTE was applied to the training (T) dataset of each fold of all K (n = 10) folds with a 1:1 ratio, while not used for the test dataset. A stratified 10-fold cross-validation protocol was employed, with K = n=10 and the original dataset randomly split into K = 10 folds. SMOTE was used to create a balanced dataset for training each fold’s training (T) dataset, while using the original test dataset to assess performance. The use of a 10-fold (n = 10) cross-validation protocol is based on prior research indicating it provides an optimal balance of bias and variance. Performance estimates based on a 10-fold cross-validation protocol have been shown to reduce variability in model performance estimates compared to smaller K, e.g., 5-K, which ultimately results in increased stability and reliability of performance estimates for testing and training datasets. Therefore, the use of a 10-K cross-validation protocol will provide an optimal balance between cost and the quality of model performance evaluation, while also allowing sufficient time for testing datasets. To reduce dimensionality and eliminate redundant features, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied, retaining approximately 97% of the variance across nine components. Four machine learning models—XGBoost, RF, SVM, and KNN—were trained using optimised hyperparameters obtained via Grid Search. Performance of each model was calculated using various metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, MCC, and ROC-AUC, and further validated using statistical tests such as the Friedman test and Nemenyi post-hoc analysis to ensure reliable and unbiased comparison. Finally, SHAP-based explainability is used to quantify each feature’s contribution to model predictions.

Dataset and attributes

The performance and classification of PD were evaluated with the publicly available UCI Parkinson’s dataset. The original dataset comprises 195 biomedical voice recordings from 31 unique people, including both PD patients and healthy individuals. Each person provided multiple voice recordings, resulting in repeated measurements within individuals rather than fully independent samples.

In this study, 180 instances with 24 attributes were utilised after preprocessing, with 15 samples removed during data cleaning. The dataset includes a variety of acoustic features derived from sustained phonations, such as frequency measures (e.g., MDVP: Fo, MDVP: Fhi, MDVP: Flo), amplitude perturbation measures (e.g., shimmer-related features), and nonlinear dynamic features (e.g., RPDE, D2, PPE), which are known to be relevant indicators of vocal impairment associated with Parkinson’s disease.

For model evaluation, an 80:20 train–test split was initially applied for baseline experiments. Additionally, stratified 10-fold cross-validation was used in subsequent experiments to ensure balanced class distribution across folds and to improve the robustness of performance estimation. It must be noted that, since the dataset contains many recordings per subject, the samples are not entirely independent. In the current study, data partitioning was performed at the recording level, meaning that recordings from the same person could appear in both the training and test sets. While this approach is commonly adopted in existing literature, it may introduce potential data leakage due to intra-subject correlation. To ensure transparency, this limitation is acknowledged, and future work will incorporate subject-wise (patient-level) cross-validation, in which all recordings from a given individual are confined to the train or the test sets. This strategy would provide a more realistic evaluation of model generalisation in clinical settings. Types of ML models used

The four ML models used in this experiment are XGBoost, KNN, SVM, and RF.

XGBoost model

The ML algorithm XGBoost is well-known for its exceptional precision and effectiveness. It is a collective model that combines different predictors into a single strong model. Using a gradient-boosting framework, XGBoost iteratively trains models by adding new trees that minimise the residuals from earlier iterations43. This continues until the accuracy plateaus or the model reaches a predetermined number of iterations. Because it can handle missing values, outliers, and imbalanced classes, XGBoost is particularly useful for large datasets. Regression, classification, and ranking tasks are widely used across industries such as online advertising, healthcare, and finance. Figure 5 describes the functionality of the XGBoost model.

The classification of new data points is subsequently performed using this hyperplane. SVMs are renowned for their excellent precision, effectiveness, and ability to handle large, complex datasets44,45. Confusion matrix

The use of various classifiers is the foundation for PD detection. Measurement criteria differentiate between them, such as classification accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, F-score, etc. A formula for calculating these measurement criteria can be used to choose the most appropriate classifier for the research. We must pay close attention to the confusion matrix before defining these criteria. A contingency table evaluates how well a learning model performs in classification problems by measuring how closely its predictions match the true labels. The confusion matrix is typically used to assess a classifier’s performance. We consider a classifier’s performance utilising the measurement criteria from this matrix. Several authors focused on accuracy levels to evaluate overall performance. It shows the percentage of all reference sites that have been accurately mapped and evaluates performance regardless of class size54,55. Whenever a classification has a value of 100%, it is considered ideal, as all reference sites have been categorised. Usually, the overall accuracy is given as a percentage. Previous research typically used only one or two classification approaches; this work uses four machine-learning classifiers to assist doctors in diagnosing. To compare their ability to classify correctly, we performed a comparative study using the same dataset. The XGBoost algorithm is an ensemble-based gradient-boosting method with excellent predictive accuracy, is very computationally efficient, and can be used effectively to model multivariate linear relationships when complex nonlinear relationships are present in the population being classified. The use of regularization techniques with the XGBoost machine prevents individual classifications from being biased by outliers and provides good performance in handling missing data56. One helpful technique for capturing the local structure and features of a dataset is the KNN (K-nearest neighbours) classification method, which uses a nonparametric approach to assign labels according to the majority vote of the k nearest neighbours. When classifying large datasets, however, the KNN approach often requires substantial computational resources57,59. To enhance the accuracy and generalizability of the classification model, RF training trains numerous DTs using randomly selected feature subsets. Each decision tree then applies decision rules to split features into two output classes. With excellent precision, recall, and F1 Scores, the SVM model demonstrated the most balanced performance, earning it the designation as a stable and durable model. While both Random Forest and XGBoost showed impressive performance, XGBoost was more consistent, and Random Forest produced flawless recall scores. In the PCA-SMOTE evaluation, there was relatively slight variation among the classifiers, with KNN showing somewhat better discriminative performance, despite XGBoost’s superior performance in the baseline and cross-validation studies.

Explainable AI using SHAP

To enhance the interpretability and transparency of the proposed ML framework, SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) was employed. SHAP provides a unified approach to quantify each feature’s contribution to model predictions by assigning Shapley values based on cooperative game theory. This enables both global and local interpretation of model behaviour, which is crucial for clinical decision support systems.

Figure 25 presents the SHAP summary plot, showing the total effect of features on predictions across all samples. Features are ranked by importance, with MDVP: Fhi (Hz), PPE, and spread1 identified as the most influential predictors of Parkinson’s disease. In the plot, positive SHAP values indicate contributions toward the Parkinson’s class, while negative values correspond to the healthy class. The colour gradient represents feature magnitude, with red showing higher values and blue showing lower values. The analysis reveals that higher values of PPE, D2, and spread1 consistently push predictions toward Parkinson’s disease, confirming the importance of nonlinear dynamic speech features.Conversely, frequency-based features such as MDVP: Fhi(Hz) and MDVP: Fo(Hz) exhibit mixed contributions, indicating complex relationships with the target variable. The widespread use of SHAP values further highlights the presence of nonlinear interactions, justifying the effectiveness of ensemble models such as XGBoost in capturing such patterns.

Limitations

ML models also have certain limitations, despite the promise for early PD detection. The most crucial issue is the dataset’s lack of demographic diversity, which stems from its modest size. The second drawback is that PD features are clinically diverse and depend on more than simply voice, unlike voice-derived acoustic features, which rely on a single modality. Finally, no DL nor hybrid ensemble techniques were used in the investigation; instead, traditional ML models were utilised.

Conclusion

Before it is too late, we need to figure out how to diagnose Parkinson’s disease. With prompt assistance, victims’ lives would be better. To assess how well four popular classifiers, XGBoost, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, and K-Nearest Neighbours, perform on a Parkinson’s disease speech dataset, this research primarily aims to develop an extensive machine learning framework. The framework used multiple evaluation methods at different stages, including baseline modelling, stratified 10-fold cross-validation performance metrics, class balancing via SMOTE, and dimensionality reduction via PCA. Results from quickly evaluated baseline and cross-validation methods highlighted XGBoost as the most predictive model, achieving the highest predictive performance scores in modelling the data’s nonlinear relationships. Generally, Random Forest performed similarly to other methods, but it achieved a high recall, making it a better choice for practical clinical applications when false negatives must be avoided. After applying SMOTE, performance improved significantly for the SVM and KNN models, reinforcing the necessity of class balancing in imbalanced medical datasets. In addition, PCA reduced feature dimensionality while preserving most of the discriminative information in the original high-dimensional feature space, resulting in more consistent predictive performance across models.

Statistical validation using the Friedman and Nemenyi tests showed statistically significant differences among models in the baseline condition; however, these differences disappeared when PCA and SMOTE were applied, so all classifiers had statistically equivalent performance. This indicates that pre-processing and validation methods are critical in assuring an unbiased evaluation of models. This research confirms that, under optimal conditions, machine learning (especially XGBoost and KNN) is a valuable tool for identifying early signs of PD. Even with limited data, the proposed framework can support clinical decision-making. Future research must aim to create multimodal clinical datasets, develop larger, more varied datasets, apply advanced deep learning and explainable AI techniques, and ultimately improve diagnostic accuracy and the real-world applicability of diagnoses. Automated hyperparameter tuning was used to optimise model performance by systematically searching for the optimal hyperparameter combination. Grid Search with stratified 10-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate multiple configurations for every classifier. The optimal hyperparameters were chosen based on the highest mean cross-validation accuracy. This approach ensures reproducibility, reduces manual bias, and improves generalisation performance. All experiments were implemented in Python 3.10 using scikit-learn (v1.4) and XGBoost (v2.0). Random seeds were fixed to 42 for data partitioning, stratified cross-validation, and model initialisation. Experiments were executed on a standard desktop environment, and all parameter configurations and evaluation metrics are reported to support reproducibility.

Future direction

The future research can be focused on further exploring the datasets with more clinically relevant and diverse parameters to include varied subjects, both typical and atypical PD, early and late-stage PD, to enhance its generalizability. A multimodal approach integrating symptomatic features and biomarkers can improve predictive performance and model efficacy; furthermore, it enhances the validity of ML, particularly in terms of performance.

Furthermore, advancing ML methods through DL and hybrid architecture modules can improve model accuracy and interpretability. The need for external validation using independent datasets or prospective clinical studies remains to be explored in the present study. To overcome dataset-specific biases, it is essential to assess the dataset’s real-world applicability. Furthermore, integrating deep neural networks, hybrid ensemble architectures, and explainable AI will advance machine learning techniques, potentially improving diagnostic accuracy and interpretability. Incorporating longitudinal data for progression monitoring rather than static classification will enhance the predictability of ML models in real-time applications. The development of a web application may also be helpful for the effective use of machine learning models. Clinician collaboration could support the development of ML models for patient-centric, non-invasive, remote, and real-time PD screening in telemedicine environments.
