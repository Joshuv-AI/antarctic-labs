Examining the Roles of Retrieval Practice and Post-Retrieval Feedback in Learning Among Autistic Adults
Authors: Brianna E. Cairney, Eileen Haebig, Heather D. Lucas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Retrieval practice enhances learning and is most effective when feedback is provided. Despite robust evidence that retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback benefit numerous clinical populations, these techniques have not been studied in autism. In two experiments, we measured retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback effects on associative memory in autistic and non-autistic adults residing in the United States. Participants attempted to memorize semantically unrelated word pairs, which were then reviewed during a practice phase consisting of retrieval practice with and without feedback. In Experiment 1, word pairs were presented to participants (40 autistic; 40 non-autistic) auditorily. In Experiment 2, stimuli were presented visually and restudy trials were added to the practice phase. Additionally, feedback for retrieval practice trials was manipulated between-subjects (80 participants per group) and a four-alternative recognition test was used in the practice phase. In both experiments, the testing phase consisted of cued recall. Both experiments yielded benefits of feedback following initial retrieval practice across groups. In addition, Experiment 2 demonstrated that both groups benefited from retrieval practice compared to restudy, even without feedback. Our results suggest that the well-documented effects of retrieval practice, particularly in concert with post-retrieval feedback, extend to support learning among autistic as well as non-autistic individuals.Lay AbstractEvery autistic person has their own unique sensory, perceptual, and social experiences that impact learning in different ways. Although many autistic learners have average or above-average IQ, individuals with a diagnosis of autism have an increased likelihood of co-occurring learning disabilities and episodic memory difficulties. These memory-related challenges make it especially important to identify strategies that can support learning in autistic populations. Retrieval practice may be one such strategy, which involves testing oneself on previously learned information. Notably, retrieval practice supports learning among many clinical populations who experience memory and learning difficulties, yet it has not been investigated among autistic learners. This study measured the effects of retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback among autistic and non-autistic adults (40 per group in Experiment 1; 80 per group in Experiment 2) residing in the United States. The task was divided into three sections. First, participants completed a “learning” block where they attempted to memorize unrelated word pairings (e.g., typing flower). Second was a “practice” block, in which they reviewed the recently learned word pairs. Practice trials consisted of retrieval practice with feedback, retrieval practice without feedback, and restudy. In restudy trials, participants reviewed the full word pair (e.g., typing flower) and selected the second word (e.g., flower) among a list of three similar words (e.g., blossom, bud, bloom). In retrieval practice trials, participants were given the first word (e.g., typing) and attempted to remember its corresponding word (e.g., flower). In feedback trials, after participants made their response, they were presented with the full word pair (e.g., typing flower) regardless of their accuracy. In the third and final “testing” block, participants’ memory for word pairs was tested once more. This allowed us to compare the effect of retrieval practice with feedback, retrieval practice without feedback, and restudying on final test performance. In addition, we measured the generalizability of the testing effect in different learning contexts (listening to word pairs in Experiment 1; reading word pairs in Experiment 2) and retrieval practice formats (cued recall in Experiment 1; multiple choice in Experiment 2). In both experiments, the benefits of feedback following retrieval practice were evident, and retrieval practice was a more effective learning strategy than restudying word pairs. These outcomes were present in autistic and non-autistic learners alike. This study is the first to demonstrate that retrieval practice with feedback promotes learning in autistic adults. It is well-documented that autistic individuals face suboptimal educational and vocational outcomes compared to their non-autistic peers; thus, strategies that support learning are important to study. These findings contribute to a large body of existing research affirming retrieval practice as a flexible learning strategy that is most effective when accompanied by feedback and may have the potential to improve outcomes for autistic learners.

Introduction

During learning, retention improves when the learner is tested on the information (i.e., retrieval practice, also referred to as the testing effect; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006a, 2006b) Retrieval practice is a powerful technique that can support learning across the lifespan, provides long-term benefits (Ma et al., 2020; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006a), and is robust across learning contents and test formats (Carpenter et al., 2022). Its benefits have been shown to extend to classroom environments (Hopkins et al., 2016; McDaniel et al., 2011; Roediger et al., 2011), facilitate the generalization and transfer of knowledge (Johnson & Mayer, 2009; Siler & Benjamin, 2020), and improve workplace information retention (Okano et al., 2018). While effective in its own right, retrieval practice is optimized when feedback is provided (Butler et al., 2008; Ma et al., 2020; Pashler et al., 2005), particularly when test difficulty increases (Kang et al., 2007). Post-retrieval feedback is widely regarded as an “error correction mechanism” (Butler et al., 2008) that amplifies the effects of retrieval practice.

The purpose of this study is to examine how retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback influence associative memory in autistic adult learners, for whom benefits have not been directly established. Autistic traits and symptom intensity differ from person to person, and each member of the autistic community has their own unique set of sensory, perceptual, and social experiences that impact learning in different ways. For example, a significant proportion of autistic individuals experience co-occurring learning disabilities such as dyslexia or language disorder (Klefbeck, 2023). In a 2020 report, 33% of autistic children met criteria for intellectual disability (Maenner, 2023; Maenner et al., 2020), while nearly half of autistic children included in the report had average or above-average IQ. Autistic individuals must navigate social and sensory environments that present unique obstacles, and autistic individuals who do not have learning disabilities can face less optimal educational and vocational outcomes compared to their non-autistic peers (Solomon, 2020; Wei et al., 2018). It is therefore crucial to identify flexible and robust support tools for autistic learners.

Given their successful track record across a number of populations, retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback are strong candidates for such tools. Retrieval practice has been shown to enhance word learning in non-autistic children with various learning disorders (Wilschut et al., 2025) and with diverse language abilities (Haebig et al., 2019; Leonard et al., 2020; Moreira et al., 2019). It is also an effective memory strategy for individuals with memory impairments, such as Alzheimer’s disease (Oren et al., 2014) and traumatic brain injury (Coyne et al., 2015). Importantly, autism is also associated with episodic memory difficulties (Desaunay et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2024; Griffin et al., 2022), and atypical memory processing may be mechanistically linked to core features of autism (Chen et al., 2024). These findings further underscore the need to consider memory processes and abilities when developing supports for autistic people.

While no studies have directly examined retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback in autistic learners, studies that examine word learning in autism provide an analogous comparison. Increased exposure (i.e., repeated study) to novel words promotes long-term retention for autistic and non-autistic learners alike (Arunachalam et al., 2024; Hayakawa et al., 2019; Joseph et al., 2019; Rothwell et al., 2024). Hartley and colleagues (2019) found that autistic and non-autistic children performed similarly on a task that required them to associate novel words with unfamiliar objects, both during initial learning and on a retention test 5 min later. In a second experiment, it was shown that autistic children’s performance on this task benefited from social feedback to a greater extent than non-autistic children, providing impetus for examining how post-retrieval feedback impacts learning among autistic adults.

Previous studies investigating how autistic learners process and respond to feedback have yielded mixed results. For example, Broadbent and Stokes (2013) found that autistic adolescents and adults completing an abstract reasoning task benefited from response accuracy feedback, but only when feedback reinforced a correct response. When feedback followed an incorrect response (termed “negative feedback”), autistic learners tended to perseverate on errors rather than correct them, whereas non-autistic individuals were unaffected by feedback type. The authors speculated that negative feedback created additional task demands for autistic learners, thereby increasing cognitive load. Indeed, some forms of feedback have been shown to impede task performance in non-autistic participants (Baer et al., 2022), and working memory capacity may limit the extent to which feedback improves task performance (Wardlow & Heyman, 2016). It is therefore possible that post-retrieval feedback may be less beneficial for autistic learners, who are more likely to experience reduced working memory (Wang et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2005). Such findings highlight the nuanced effects of feedback for autistic learners and the opportunity to clarify whether post-retrieval feedback will help or hinder subsequent memory.

Across two experiments, we investigated the effects of retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback on recall performance for autistic and non-autistic adults completing a word-pair associative learning task. Both experiments were comprised of three phases: learning, practice, and testing. In the learning phase, participants studied semantically unrelated verb-noun word pairs (e.g., “driving walnut”). In the practice phase, participants engaged in retrieval practice for the word pairs and post-retrieval feedback was included as an experimental manipulation. For retrieval practice trials in which feedback was provided, participants were reminded of the correct word pairing after making their responses, regardless of accuracy. To match participants’ additional exposure to some word pairs via feedback, restudy trials were incorporated into Experiment 2. The testing phase consisted of a final cued recall test for all word pairs. To examine the generalizability of the results, Experiments 1 and 2 used different stimulus modalities (auditory versus textual presentation, respectively) and retrieval practice methods (cued recall versus recognition).

Given the favorable results of retrieval practice reported across a wide range of clinical and non-clinical populations alike, we expected that final recall performance would improve following retrieval practice compared to restudy trials for both groups. We anticipated that post-retrieval feedback would further bolster recall performance for non-autistic learners, consistent with previous work (Abel & Bäuml, 2020; Butler et al., 2008; Kliegl et al., 2018). However, it was less clear how feedback would affect the performance of autistic learners. While post-retrieval feedback may reinforce learning and boost performance in both groups, it is possible that this boost may be offset in the autistic group due to increases in cognitive load imposed by post-retrieval feedback.

Experiment 1

Methods

Participants

Prior to data collection, we conducted a power analysis for a repeated measures within-subjects ANOVA with a power of 0.8 and ηp2 = 0.1, which yielded a sample size of 74 total participants. Thus, we aimed to include 40 participants per group. As described in the Analytic Strategy section, the final analyses ultimately employed a Bayesian multilevel modeling approach rather than the originally planned ANOVA framework. We shifted to this approach because it allows evidence for both the null and alternative effects to be quantified while accounting for across-participant variability.

The task was completed by 55 autistic (Aut) and 57 non-autistic (NonAut) adults, and the final sample consisted of 40 participants per group. Exclusionary decisions and demographics are detailed in Table 1. Participants resided in the United States and were recruited using the online recruitment platform, Prolific (https://prolific.co), which allows experimenters to prescreen for representative samples based on participant profile information, including self-reported autism diagnosis status. After enrolling in the study on Prolific and providing consent, participants were redirected to Qualtrics (www.qualtrics.com), where they confirmed their self-reported diagnosis status (either non-autistic or having received a formal diagnosis from a clinician 1 ) and other demographic information. To promote compliance and data quality, participants affirmed an honesty statement indicating that they would refrain from cheating or using external memory aids (Supplemental Materials Figure S1) and that their compensation would be based on effort rather than accuracy. They were then directed to OpenLab (https://open-lab.online; Shevchenko, 2022) to complete the experiment. Participants were compensated US$7.50. Participants completed the experiment at their own pace; median completion time was 23 min.

Stimuli

The stimuli consisted of 27 auditorily presented unrelated cue-target word pairs (e.g., “typing flower”). Audio was recorded in a soundproof booth using a Marantz Professional MPM-2000 large diaphragm condenser microphone and Marantz Professional solid-state recorder (PMD661 MK II). Recordings were cropped and edited using Praat (Boersma, 2001) to normalize the decibel amplitude across sound files and remove noise. Two clips were created per stimulus pair, one containing the entire pair (“typing flower”), and another containing only the cue word (“typing”). Cues ranged from 695 to 1,364 ms in length (M = 929, SD = 159) and targets from 593 to 1,149 ms (M = 845, SD = 168). One second of silence was added after every word for both pair and cue-only clips. Thus, cue-only clips lasted 1,695–2,364 ms (M = 1,929, SD = 159) and full word pair trials were 3,390–4,308 ms (M = 3,773, SD = 237) long.

Procedure

The 27 word pairs were split between two blocks such that one contained 14 trials and the other had 13, and the order of word pairs within blocks was randomized for each person. Each block contained three phases: learning, practice (T1), and testing (T2). Feedback was provided during the practice phase in one of the two blocks. The assignment of stimuli to condition (feedback; no feedback) and block order was counterbalanced across participants, resulting in four different versions of the task (see Supplemental Materials, Table S1). Sample trials from each phase are shown in Figure 1. The experimental paradigm was built and hosted on lab.js (Henninger et al., 2024).

During each trial, the audio clip of the word pair began playing 500 ms after the appearance of the actor. Four seconds after audio onset, the image disappeared and participants were prompted to provide a judgment of learning (JOL; included in both experiments for purposes unrelated to this report) for the word pair they had just heard. The learning phase concluded with an untimed distractor math task consisting of nine simple arithmetic problems.

The practice phase (T1) immediately followed the distractor task and was comprised of a cued recall test meant to prompt retrieval practice. During the practice phase, participants listened to audio clips of cues from the prior learning phase (e.g., “typing”) and attempted to remember the corresponding target for each one (e.g., “flower”). As with the learning trials, the still image was presented for 500 ms prior to the onset of each audio cue and remained on the screen for a total of 4,500 ms. Once the still image disappeared, participants were prompted to type the appropriate target word and provide a corresponding confidence rating. Confidence ratings were included in both experiments for purposes unrelated to this report. Typed responses and ratings were required for each trial in order to proceed. In the feedback block, upon submitting their response, participants heard the complete word pair again (e.g., “typing flower”) while viewing the still image superimposed with the word “feedback”. The audio of the word pair was presented 500 ms into each trial, which ended after 4,500 ms. During the no-feedback block, participants viewed the still image superimposed with the words “no feedback” in silence for 2,500 ms.

Following the practice phase and another distractor math task, the testing phase (T2) commenced, which consisted of a final cued recall test for the word pairs. The still image, audio of each cue, and stimulus presentation timing were the same as in the practice phase, and participants were again prompted to type the associated target and make a confidence rating. There was no time limit for recall or confidence responses, and feedback was never provided during the testing phase. At the end of the experiment, participants were thanked for their time and redirected back to Prolific.

Participatory Methods

The research team consisted of autistic, otherwise neurodivergent, and neurotypical individuals. Autistic research team members contributed to both studies in respect to the study designs, stimuli, analyses, and data interpretation. Although a fully community-based participatory design was not used, the current study had active and substantive contributions from autistic adults.

Analytic Strategy

Our primary aim was to measure whether autistic participants benefited from post-retrieval feedback similarly to non-autistic participants. We also wanted to determine whether relations among these factors depended on the accuracy of participants’ responses during cued recall (i.e., the practice phase), herein referred to as T1 Accuracy. Our analytic approach had two components. First, we included feedback Condition, Group, and T1 Accuracy as fixed effects in Bayesian logistic mixed-effects models (GLMMs), which allowed us to quantify evidence for both the null and alternative effects while accounting for across-participant variability. We fit these models using the brms R package (v2.23.0; Bürkner, 2021) in the R programming environment. T2 Accuracy (0 = incorrect, 1 = correct) was modeled with a Bernoulli likelihood and logit link. We included Condition (feedback vs. no-feedback), Group (autistic vs non-autistic), and T1 Accuracy as fixed effects, as well as all interactions. Condition and Group were sum-coded. The model included random intercepts for subjects and correlated random slopes for Condition and T1 Accuracy. We used weakly informative priors for fixed effects intercepts (m = 0, sd = 1), random-intercept standard deviation (m = 0, sd = 2.5), and model intercept (m = 0, sd = 1.5). The models were fit using four chains, with 4000 iterations per chain, which included 1000 iterations for sampler calibration.

Second, we computed Bayes factors (BFs) by comparing nested Bayesian GLMMs that differed by a single fixed effect, with all other fixed and random effects held constant. The primary test examined the Group × Condition interaction (i.e., whether feedback effects differed between autistic and non-autistic participants). We also tested the main effect of Group, the Group × T1 Accuracy interaction, and the Group × Condition × T1 Accuracy interaction. For clarity, we report BF values as BF01 (or 1/BF10), which quantifies evidence for the simpler model. Following common conventions (Wagenmakers et al., 2018), BFs between 1 and 3 were interpreted as anecdotal evidence, 3 to 10 as moderate, and >10 as strong evidence.

JOLs and confidence ratings were not analyzed for this report, but descriptive statistics are available in the Supplemental Materials (Table S2).

Finally, as described in Table 1, the autistic and non-autistic groups differed in gender composition, with a higher proportion of male and non-binary participants in the autistic group. Discussion

Our results cohere with prior research by demonstrating that the provision of feedback during retrieval practice (i.e., T1 practice phase) effectively functioned as an error correction mechanism, enhancing final test recall (i.e., T2 testing phase) specifically for trials in which T1 recall attempts were inaccurate. Notably, autism status was not associated with a reduced tendency to integrate feedback, as evidenced both by the overlap with zero of the posterior credible interval for the Group × Condition interaction term and a BF01 providing moderate evidence in favor of a model without this term. These findings replicate previous feedback and retrieval practice research in non-autistic adults (e.g., Abel & Bäuml, 2020; Pashler et al., 2005). Moreover, these findings extend prior work by demonstrating that feedback following retrieval practice enhances learning through an error correction mechanism in autistic adults, although evidence from the three-way interaction was equivocal as to whether the selectivity of this enhancement for erroneous trials differed in magnitude between groups.

One limitation of Experiment 1 was the unbalanced exposure to word pairs across conditions. During the T1 practice phase, participants listened to word pairs a second time in the feedback block, whereas no additional exposure occurred in the no-feedback block. Accordingly, the boost in T2 performance following feedback for initially incorrect responses may have resulted from additional exposure rather than feedback per se. To address this possibility, we conducted a second experiment in which we restructured the practice phase to also include “restudy” trials, wherein the cue and corresponding target were presented together without a corresponding retrieval attempt (see Zhang et al., 2019, for a similar approach). This modification allowed T2 Accuracy for restudied word pairs to be compared against retrieval practice trials to account for effects of re-exposure. Experiment 2 also differed from Experiment 1 in the modality of the stimuli (visually rather than auditorily presented word pairs) and the format of the retrieval practice (associative recognition rather than cued recall) to examine the generalizability of the results.

Experiment 2

Methods

Participants

Participants residing in the United States were recruited via Prolific and redirected to Qualtrics to give informed consent, affirm their autism diagnosis status (either non-autistic or formally diagnosed), and agree to an Honesty Statement before completing the experiment. Participant sample size followed Experiment 1 (n = 40 per group); however, Experiment 2 was structured to include a between-subjects manipulation (described below) and required twice as many participants (n = 80 per group). Thus, Experiment 2 was completed by 95 adults who self-reported having a diagnosis of autism (Aut) and 100 adults who reported that they have never received an autism diagnosis (NonAut). Eighty participants in each group met the inclusionary criteria (see Table 1 for details and demographics). Participants were compensated US$11.25. Participants completed the experiment at their own pace; median completion time was 57 min.

Procedure

Experiment 2 consisted of a single learning, practice (T1), and testing (T2) phase. Unlike Experiment 1, in which all practice trials included retrieval practice, half of the practice trials in Experiment 2 were restudy trials and the other half were retrieval practice trials. In addition, feedback was manipulated between-subjects, with half of the participants receiving feedback on each T1 retrieval practice trial and the other half never receiving feedback. Experimental procedure and sample trials for Experiment 2.

Note. Experiment 2 consisted of a learning phase (A), a practice phase (B1 or B2), and a testing phase with cued recall (C). Stimuli were presented, and retrieval practice took the form of a four-alternative forced-choice test. The practice phase (T1) consisted of restudy trials in addition to retrieval practice trials. Half of the participants received feedback during T1 (feedback; B1) and the other half did not (no-feedback; B2).

During the learning phase, participants viewed and attempted to memorize 80 semantically unrelated word pairs, following the pattern in Experiment 1 (e.g., “clapping valley”). The word pairs were presented in text on the center of the screen for 4 s in a randomized order. After a word pair disappeared, participants provided a JOL on a 0–100 slider scale and progressed to the next trial. After the learning phase, participants were given the same brief distractor math task used in Experiment 1.

The practice phase (T1) used a four-alternative forced choice test in which each participant restudied half of the word pairs and engaged in retrieval practice for the remaining half. During T1, retrieval practice and restudy trials were presented in a different random order for each participant. During restudy practice trials, a previously-studied cue-target pair appeared on the screen for participants to review (“CLAPPING – VALLEY”). After 4 s, the correct target (“VALLEY”) and three semantically related lures (“CANYON”, “LOWLAND”, “RAVINE”) appeared (see Supplemental Table S3 for stimulus list). During retrieval practice trials, only the previously-studied cue appeared on the screen with a question mark in place of the target word (“CLAPPING – ?”) before the correct target and three lures appeared 4 s later. Regardless of trial type, participants were instructed to choose the correct second word from the list of four options. The target and lures remained on the screen until a response was selected and the advance arrow was pressed. After every response, participants used a 1–100 slider scale to indicate their confidence in remembering the word pair.

For retrieval practice trials, half of the participants (i.e., 40 Aut; 40 NonAut) did not receive feedback and immediately proceeded to the next trial after submitting their responses. For participants assigned to the feedback condition (40 Aut; 40 NonAut), the word pair appeared on the screen again for 4 s (“The correct answer is CLAPPING – VALLEY”) before the next trial started. Exposure time to word pairs was matched between the restudy condition and the retrieval practice with feedback condition. The assignment of word pairs to condition and practice trial type was counterbalanced across participants (see Supplemental Table S4 for counterbalancing details).

Another distractor math task was given before participants commenced to the testing phase (T2) where they completed cued recall for all 80 word pairs. Similar to Experiment 1, the test phase was structured so that each cue appeared on the screen and participants were asked to type the corresponding target in a textbox. At the end of the experiment, participants filled out a short demographics survey, were thanked for their time, and redirected back to Prolific.

Analytic Strategy

The goals of this experiment were twofold. The first was to leverage the restudy condition to examine whether the gains shown in Experiment 1 for the feedback condition could be attributed to repeated exposure or a bolstering effect of feedback. To test this question, we analyzed final cued recall accuracy (i.e., T2 Accuracy) using a Bayesian GLMM with Trial Type (restudy, practice), Group (autistic, non-autistic), and Condition (feedback, no feedback) as predictors. The models also included a random intercept for participant and a random slope for Trial Type.

The second goal was to replicate the feedback-based improvement shown for both autistic and non-autistic participants in Experiment 1 using a different stimulus modality and testing design. To accomplish this, we used the same set of analyses as in Experiment 1, which included a Bayesian GLMM to test the effects of Condition, Group, and practice phase accuracy (i.e., T1 Accuracy), and all interactions on final T2 cued recall performance overall, as well as a participant-level random intercept and a random slope for T1 Accuracy. We used the same priors and model settings as in Experiment 1. Finally, as in Experiment 1, we augmented the Bayesian GLMM with Bayes Factor tests of each term involving Group.

JOLs and confidence ratings were not analyzed for this report, but descriptive statistics are available in the Supplemental Materials (Table S5).

Results

Model convergence for the Bayesian GLMM model that included Trial Type was acceptable (all Rhat = 1.00). We report all estimates, estimated errors, and 95% credible intervals in Table 4. Posterior credible intervals indicated a credible main effect of Trial Type, such that T2 recall was higher following retrieval practice than restudy during the T1 practice phase. Credible intervals also indicated a Trial Type × Condition interaction, suggesting that the magnitude of the retrieval practice benefit differed between the feedback and no-feedback conditions. In contrast, the posterior credible interval for the main effect of Group overlapped with zero, as did the intervals for all remaining interactions, indicating no credible evidence for those effects.

To better understand the source of the Trial Type × Condition interaction, we conducted Bayesian follow-up comparisons within the feedback and no-feedback groups separately. For each subset, we compared two nested models that differed only in the inclusion of the population-level effect of Trial Type (H₀: fixed Trial Type effect constrained to zero; H₁: fixed Trial Type effect estimated), with no additional fixed effects and an identical random effects structure.

Within the feedback Condition, there was overwhelming evidence for a Trial Type effect (BF₁₀ = 9.08 × 10¹⁰), indicating higher T2 recall following retrieval practice that included feedback than restudy. Within the no-feedback Condition, there was also strong evidence for a Trial Type effect (BF₁₀ = 19.45), again favoring retrieval practice over restudy. Thus, retrieval practice improved subsequent recall in both conditions, but this benefit was magnified when feedback was provided, consistent with the observed interaction. Importantly, this pattern provides assurance that the increased recall for feedback test trials observed in Experiment 1 was not due to additional exposure to word pairs alone. Because restudy practice trials were included primarily to control for this exposure, we limited subsequent analyses to retrieval practice trials.

Discussion

Experiment 2 built on the results of Experiment 1 by including restudy trials during the practice phase. Cued recall was higher for word pairs that were tested at T1 relative to those that were restudied, and the provision of feedback following incorrect T1 responses amplified this improvement. The larger T2 Accuracy for the items within retrieval practice without feedback trials was particularly striking given that the alternative (restudy) trial types provided twice as much exposure to the word pairs. This pattern suggests that the improvements we observed in Experiment 1 were due to the facilitative effects of feedback rather than additional stimulus exposure, and aligns with previous work demonstrating that retrieval practice leads to better learning and retention than repeat exposure (i.e., restudy) even without feedback. Most importantly, we extended these effects for the first time to an autistic adult population. T1 retrieval practice benefited both autistic and non-autistic participants, and Bayesian analyses suggest that both groups received comparable boosts from post-retrieval feedback. These results join those of Experiment 1 to suggest that, at least in contexts similar to the current study, autistic individuals benefit from retrieval practice and feedback during learning.

General Discussion

Retrieval practice—the act of testing newly acquired information—reliably strengthens memory (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006a). Post-retrieval feedback can further enhance these benefits (Kang et al., 2007) by functioning as an error correction mechanism (Butler et al., 2008). Here, we contribute additional support to the robust body of extant literature positioning retrieval practice as an effective learning strategy and, for the first time, extend this finding to autistic adults.

Given that retrieval practice supports learning among individuals with memory impairment and language disorders, we expected to observe a similar effect among autistic learners. Indeed, autistic participants were more successful at committing novel word pairs to memory when they engaged in retrieval practice compared to simply restudying (Experiment 2). Furthermore, across both experiments, autistic and non-autistic participants benefited from feedback—particularly for initially incorrect responses—to a similar extent. These findings have positive implications for autistic learners, who face an increased likelihood of learning disorders (Ibrahim, 2020) and episodic memory impairment (for meta-analyses, see Desaunay et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2024; Griffin et al., 2022). That said, there is likely no single educational approach or instructional model equally beneficial to all autistic learners (Klefbeck, 2023). Thus, a priority for future research should be to identify in which contexts (e.g., different domains, modalities, and environments) these strategies are most effective. For example, memory decrements in autistic relative to non-autistic people tend to be larger for free recall relative to cued recall or recognition (Desaunay et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2024; Griffin et al., 2022). Thus, it is possible that retrieval practice and/or feedback may be less beneficial if implemented using less a structured format such as free recall. In addition, future work should investigate retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback in autistic learners with higher support needs relative to the current sample.

In this study, post-retrieval feedback benefits were present in both groups. We can contrast these findings with those of Broadbent and Stokes (2013), in which performance on an abstract reasoning task in autistic adults benefited from feedback that reinforced correct responses, but not from corrective feedback following incorrect responses. Two key differences between the present study and that of Broadbent and Stokes (2013) may explain this discrepancy. First, participants in Broadbent and Stokes (2013) completed the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task (Grant & Berg, 1948) which is designed to measure cognitive flexibility and executive function rather than associative memory. The task used in our study may be more closely aligned with past studies that report beneficial effects of reward (Demurie et al., 2011), positive reinforcement, and high-intensity feedback (Eckes et al., 2023) on learning among autistic children. A second, related difference is that Broadbent and Stokes (2013) provided feedback via a binary accuracy indication (i.e., “correct” or “incorrect”). By contrast, feedback in the present study was more informative because it involved presenting the correct target after each retrieval attempt. Future research could directly test whether the informativeness of the feedback impacts the success with which it can be integrated, particularly in autistic learners. In addition, research that includes a measure of working memory capacity—a facet of cognition that may influence when feedback is helpful versus harmful (Kercood et al., 2014)—may provide a more nuanced understanding of post-retrieval feedback effectiveness.

The similarities in performance between autistic and non-autistic participants reported here should be interpreted with some caution, given that the current study was administered online and relied on self-reports of having received a formal autism diagnosis. We used Prolific’s screening tools to set specific selection criteria to recruit participants based on independently collected demographic data. Prolific participants are naïve as to why a study is made available to them and we did not make mention of autism in our study description. In addition, we asked all participants to affirm their autism diagnostic status and compared these responses against Prolific profiles to ensure consistency. These procedures follow established methodological recommendations for minimizing the likelihood that participants inaccurately report eligibility information to access studies (Chandler & Paolacci, 2017). Nonetheless, because formal clinical verification was not possible, replication in samples with formally validated diagnoses would further strengthen confidence in group comparisons.

In addition, because the experiments were completed online without experimenter supervision, the possibility of undisclosed cheating or use of external memory aids represents an added limitation. To reduce this risk, we implemented several safeguards including affirmation of an honesty statement and screened responses for evidence of inattention or noncompliance. Although it is expected that these procedures reduce the likelihood of dishonest responding, replication in a supervised laboratory setting is also warranted.

Conclusion

This is the first study to our knowledge that extends the broad benefits of retrieval practice and post-retrieval feedback to an autistic cohort. Associative memory performance in autistic participants, as well as the beneficial effects of retrieval practice and feedback on learning, were on par with the effects in non-autistic participants, shedding important light on learning abilities in autism.
