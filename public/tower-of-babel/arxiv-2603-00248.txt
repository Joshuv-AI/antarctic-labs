Targeted Local Projections
Aleksei Nemtyrev, Otilia Boldea

Abstract
Local projection (LP) and structural vector autoregression (SVAR) are commonly employed to estimate dynamic causal effects of macroeconomic policies at multiple horizons. With enough lags as controls, LP estimators have little bias but their variance can increase with the horizon due to accumulating additional shocks. Because they typically employ fewer lags or suffer from local misspecification, SVAR estimators typically incur higher bias, but their variance decreases with the horizon due to exponentiation. We propose to target the LP estimators towards their SVAR counterparts - constructed with fewer lags than LP at each horizon - to reduce their variance at the cost of incurring some bias. The resulting targeted LP estimator is a linear combination of the LP and SVAR estimators. We propose choosing this linear combination optimally to minimize the mean-squared error of the new estimator. Our simulations show that, under a locally misspecified SVAR model, targeting substantially reduces the LP variance at longer horizons while maintaining near-nominal coverage in small samples when a double bootstrap is employed. Keywords: Targeted Local Projections, Local Projections, Smooth Local Projections, SVAR, Bootstrap, Mean Symmetric Double Bootstrap JEL Codes: C12, C14, C32

Introduction

Identifying and estimating dynamic causal effects of structural shocks on macroeconomic outcomes is a fundamental goal in macroeconomics. To study these dynamic causal effects, researchers use a variety of tools, including taking to the data theoretical models such as dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models, and other models such as structural vector autoregressions (SVARs) and local projections (LPs).

In this paper, we are interested in pointwise estimation instead. In that case, it is possible to reduce the variance of the LP estimates at the cost of a small bias. The reason why this may be useful for applied work is that the original LP estimators exhibit variances that increase with the horizon, as shown in

1

However, smoothing nearby horizons does not guarantee an exponential decay similar to that one would expect if the true data generating process was a stationary SVAR of finite or infinite order.

In contrast,

To obtain their Bayesian LP (BLP) estimator, they employ a quasi-Bayesian LP methodology where the prior mean of the coefficient is equal to a VAR estimator on a pre-sample, while the prior variance is based on a Minnesota prior, with a shrinkage parameter that maximizes the posterior marginal density of the data. However, due to small samples in macroeconomics, pre-sample VAR estimates are rarely reliable. Additionally,

However, their focus is reduced form estimation and on multi-step forecasts rather than structural parameters. For point estimation of target structural parameters, it is unclear that minimizing prediction error would be desirable.

In this paper, we introduce a novel frequentist shrinkage estimator -
Targeted Local Projections (TLP)
. After projecting out controls, this estimator shrinks the scalar LP structural impulse responses of interest
at each horizon
toward their counterpart IRFs estimated from a SVAR model in the same sample. Since both LP and SVAR estimate the same theoretical IRF -
22

The resulting estimator at each horizon is a linear combination of the LP and the SVAR estimators, with weights depending on the difference in bias and variance across the two estimators. We propose choosing the shrinkage parameter optimally at each horizon, to minimize the asymptotic mean-squared error of the new estimator. Assuming that the SVAR is locally misspecified as in
25
,
11

21

While, with local misspecification, the implied weights on the LP and SVAR reflect the bias-variance trade-off between the two estimators, with no misspecification, our estimator reduces to SVAR, as desired.

Compared to smooth local projections
(
1
;
23
)
, TLP differs in both its regularization target and its treatment of horizons. SLP penalizes differences across adjacent horizons, shrinking the IRFs toward each other, without a data-driven target. TLP instead shrinks toward the SVAR IRFs, which exhibit the exponential decay consistent with stationary autoregressive dynamics. SLP also applies a single smoothing parameter across all horizons and creates distortions at boundary horizons; TLP allows shrinkage to vary by horizon and does not impose cross-horizon smoothness. Compared to Bayesian local projections
(
7
)
, TLP uses the full sample for both LP and SVAR estimation, whereas BLP relies on a pre-sample VAR as the prior. Additionally, BLP assumes correct VAR specification and our simulations show that under misspecification, it exhibits severe coverage distortions. Finally, TLP is a linear combination of LP and SVAR with transparent, data-driven weights, while BLP is not a linear combination of the two, making the latter harder to interpret.

In the case of local misspecification, the SVAR estimates will exhibit asymptotic bias, distorting coverage of TLP. To remove these distortions, we rely on double bootstrap, which was shown to restore coverage in the presence of asymptotic bias by
3
. Therefore, for inference, we propose a Mean Symmetric Double Bootstrap (MSDB) procedure that
provides near-nominal coverage for LP, SVAR, SLP, and TLP even when the SVAR is
misspecified, as shown in our simulations. The procedure uses a nested bootstrap structure: the first level
generates bootstrap samples via moving-block resampling of SVAR residuals, while the
second level provides variance estimates needed for studentization, which are necessary
because the delta method performs poorly for SVAR and TLP impulse responses in finite samples. Intuitively, the second layer of bootstrap uses the first layer as the true data generating process, so it features the same asymptotic bias as the first layer, which was obtained by bootstrapping a misspecified model. This asymptotic bias then cancels out in constructing the variances from the second layer of bootstrap.
Two innovations are central to achieving near-nominal coverage. First, we center the
bootstrap t-statistics around the bootstrap mean rather than the SVAR-implied impulse
response to remove the bias arising from bootstrapping a misspecified model. Second, we construct symmetric confidence intervals

(
10
)
, which help to correct the finite sample asymmetry in the TLP distributions
arising due to asymptotic bias. In simulations featuring
recursively identified SVARs under local misspecification, we demonstrate that
TLP substantially reduces variance at longer horizons while maintaining near-nominal
coverage when the MSDB bootstrap is employed.

Bias - variance trade-off.

The asymptotic properties of LP and VAR suggest a natural trade-off:

(i)

Bias.
LP is consistent under local misspecification, while
VAR exhibits an asymptotic bias of order
T^{-\zeta}
. The bias arises because
VAR omits the MA component present in the true DGP.

(ii)

LP Variance.
The LP variance can increase with the horizon due to
the accumulated shocks in
u^{h}_{i,t+h}
. The LP estimator we consider can include more lags than the true data generating process; in that case, the lag augmentation kills the autocovariances in the moment conditions under an additional forward martingale assumption as shown in
21
. Under this assumption and using the fact that the errors in the LP are a moving average of order
h
as shown in
21
, it can be shown that the LP variance increases with the horizon.

(iii)

VAR Variance.

Under no misspecification and with i.i.d. normal structural shocks, the VAR estimator is the maximum likelihood estimator and is therefore more efficient than LP. The local misspecification just shifts the distribution by a constant equal the asymptotic bias, therefore the same conclusion holds under local misspecification. Under conditional heteroskedasticity, it is not known how the VAR and LP variances compare, but it is likely that VAR still has lower variance at higher horizons, as this variance eventually declines under
stationarity because
A^{h}\to 0
as
h\to\infty
, while this is not the case for LP.

These observations motivate targeting LP towards the VAR: the variance of LP can be reduced by shrinking
toward VAR, accepting a small amount of bias in exchange for lower variance.

2.3
Targeted Local Projections

We propose a targeted local projections (TLP) estimator that shrinks LP impulse
responses toward their VAR counterparts at each horizon. This subsection defines
the estimator, derives its closed-form solution, and discusses its relationship
to existing methods.

Estimator.

The TLP estimator
\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}
minimizes a penalized LP
objective function at each horizon

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\beta_{h})=\big(Y_{h}-X\beta_{h}\big)^{2}+\lambda_{h}\big(\beta_{h}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}\big)^{2},

(10)

where
\lambda_{h}>0
controls the degree of shrinkage and

\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}
is the impulse response obtained from a VAR.
We denote this estimator as TLP
(p,q)
, where
p
refers to the number of lags
in the local projection and
q
to the number of lags in the target VAR.
Solving for
\beta_{h}
yields:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}=(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}(X^{\prime}Y_{h}+\lambda_{h}\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}),

(11)

where
X^{\prime}X
is a scalar after projecting out controls. This expression can be
rewritten as a linear combination of LP and VAR estimators:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}

\displaystyle=(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}X^{\prime}Y_{h}+(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}\lambda_{h}\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}

\displaystyle=(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}X^{\prime}X\,\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}+\big(1-(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}X^{\prime}X\big)\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}

\displaystyle=v(\lambda_{h})\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}+(1-v(\lambda_{h}))\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR},

(12)

where
v(\lambda_{h})=(X^{\prime}X+\lambda_{h})^{-1}X^{\prime}X
is a scalar weight and is by construction in interval
(0,1)
. The TLP
impulse response is therefore a linear combination of LP and VAR, with weights
determined by the second moment of the data
X^{\prime}X
and the shrinkage parameter

\lambda_{h}
. Note that when
\lambda_{h}\rightarrow 0
,
the weight
v(\lambda_{h})\rightarrow 1
and TLP reduces to LP, while when
\lambda_{h}\to\infty
,
the weight
v(\lambda_{h})\to 0
and TLP reduces to VAR.

Horizon-specific weights.

Unlike model averaging approaches that apply uniform weights across horizons

(
21
)
, TLP allows the shrinkage parameter
\lambda_{h}
to vary
with the horizon. This flexibility is desirable because the relative performance
of LP and VAR differs across horizons. At short horizons, LP estimates have low
bias and are therefore more informative. At higher horizons, LP variance increases
due to accumulated shocks in the regression error, making it preferable to place
greater weight on VAR, whose impulse responses exhibit exponential decay and
lower variance under stationarity.

2.4
Comparison to Other Estimators

2.4.1
Bayesian Local Projections

In the case of no additional controls, the mean of the TLP estimator is equivalent
to the mean of the normal posterior distribution of the Bayesian local projections
(BLP) developed by
7
. BLP constructs a pseudo-likelihood
using Bayesian techniques, treating a pre-sample VAR estimate as a prior. Despite
this connection, three distinctions are worth noting:

(i)

BLP assumes the VAR is correctly specified.

(ii)

Although BLP and TLP share the same posterior mean when controls
are projected out, BLP does not project out controls, so the BLP impulse
responses are not necessarily linear combinations of LP and VAR impulse responses.

(iii)

BLP relies on a pre-sample VAR, discarding in-sample information.
TLP uses the full sample available for both LP and VAR estimation.

2.4.2
Smooth Local Projections

Targeted local projections and smooth local projections (SLP)

Estimator.

Stack

\displaystyle=I_{h^{max}}\otimes X,\qquad\tilde{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{0}\\

H

Define the second-order difference matrix:

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2&1&0&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}^{\text{SLP}}=(\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}+\tilde{\lambda}P)^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{Y}.

In the limit

Conversely, when

(i)

Cross-validation.

(ii)

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{\lambda}}\hat{R}(\tilde{\lambda})=T\|\hat{\beta}^{\text{SLP}}-\hat{\beta}^{LP}\|^{2}_{W}+2\operatorname{tr}\{W\psi(\tilde{\lambda})\hat{\Sigma}\},

Key differences to TLP.

Three features distinguish TLP from SLP:

(i)

Regularization target.
The shrinkage penalty in SLP is not data-driven. In contrast, TLP shrinks toward the
VAR-implied impulse responses, which exhibit exponential decay consistent
with stationary autoregressive dynamics.

(ii)

Horizon-specific regularization.
SLP applies a single
smoothing parameter
\tilde{\lambda}
uniformly across all horizons. TLP
allows the shrinkage parameter
\lambda_{h}
to vary with the horizon,
reflecting the different bias - variance trade-offs at short and long horizons.
As the variance of LP can increase with the horizon, while the variance of VAR decreases with the horizon and the asymptotic bias of VAR remains the same across horizons, horizon-specific weights are desirable.

(iii)

Boundary behavior.
SLP smooths adjacent horizons toward
each other, which creates distortions at boundary horizons. TLP does not
impose smoothness across horizons and therefore avoids this boundary problem.

3
Selecting the Shrinkage Parameter

The TLP estimator depends on the shrinkage parameter
\lambda_{h}
, which governs
the weight placed on LP versus VAR at each horizon. A key advantage of TLP is
that the optimal weight has a closed-form solution and can be computed directly
from the data. This section derives this solution by minimizing mean squared error,
yielding a criterion similar in form to the unbiased risk estimator of

23
for SLP and related to the focused information criterion
of
4
.

Objective function.

We select the shrinkage parameter at each horizon by minimizing a consistent
estimator of the asymptotic mean squared error:

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda_{h}}\,R_{h}(\lambda_{h})=\argmin\,T\,\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}-\beta^{*}_{h}\big)^{2}\big],

(18)

where
\beta^{*}_{h}
is the true impulse response. The risk decomposes into squared
bias and variance:

\displaystyle R_{h}(\lambda_{h})=\underbrace{T\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}(\lambda_{h})-\beta^{*}_{h}]\big)^{2}}_{\text{bias}^{2}(\lambda_{h})}+\underbrace{T\,\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}(\lambda_{h})-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\text{TLP}}(\lambda_{h})]\big)^{2}\big]}_{Var(\lambda_{h})}.

(19)

Assume we have consistent estimators
\hat{\Sigma}^{\mu}_{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\Sigma^{\mu}_{h},\text{ for }\mu\in\{LP,VAR,COV\}
. Consistent estimators for LP can be obtained by standard HAC estimators, however we use the bootstrap. Consistent estimators for the rest of the quantities are discussed in the next subsection.

Theorem 3
.

Consistent risk criterion
. Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, for
\zeta=1/2
, the feasible risk criterion:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{h}(\lambda_{h})

\displaystyle=T(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\Big(v(\lambda_{h})^{2}\hat{\Sigma}^{LP}_{h}+(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}\hat{\Sigma}^{VAR}_{h}+2v(\lambda_{h})(1-v(\lambda_{h}))\hat{\Sigma}^{COV}_{h}\Big)

(20)

is consistent:
\hat{R}_{h}(\lambda_{h}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}R_{h}(\lambda_{h})
.

Theorem
3
derives a consistent risk criterion under local misspecification or order
T^{-1/2}
, the only one under which a meaningful trade-off bias-variance is possible. This criterion has the same structure as the unbiased risk estimator (URE) for smooth local projections in
equation (
17
): both take the form bias
2
+ 2
\times
variance. The
difference lies in what each method treats as bias: TLP measures the discrepancy
between LP and VAR, while SLP penalizes second differences across adjacent horizons. The criterion (
20
) is quadratic in
v(\lambda_{h})
, so minimization
yields a unique closed-form solution.

Let the optimal shrinkage parameter be the one that minimizes the feasible risk criterion:

\hat{\lambda}_{h}=\mbox{ arg}\min_{\lambda_{h}\in(0,\infty)}\hat{R}_{h}(\lambda_{h})).

We then obtain the following closed-form expression for the TLP weights:

Theorem 4
.

Optimal weight
. Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, for
\zeta=1/2
,

\displaystyle v(\hat{\lambda}_{h})=\frac{T(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}+2(\hat{\Sigma}^{VAR}_{h}-\hat{\Sigma}^{COV}_{h})}{T(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}+2(\hat{\Sigma}^{LP}_{h}+\hat{\Sigma}^{VAR}_{h}-2\hat{\Sigma}^{COV}_{h})}.

(21)

If, furthermore, the LP and VAR have the same number of lags, and the structural shocks are i.i.d., then

\displaystyle v(\hat{\lambda}_{h})=\frac{T(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}}{T(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}+2(\hat{\Sigma}^{LP}_{h}-\hat{\Sigma}^{VAR}_{h})}.

(22)

The result (
22
) follows from Corollary A.2 of
21
, which shows that with same number of lags and i.i.d. structural shocks,
\Sigma^{VAR}_{h}=\Sigma^{COV}_{h}
.

The expression for the weights in (
22
) can be interpreted in a very simple way. The numerator,

T(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}
, measures the squared
discrepancy between estimators, which under local misspecification is a consistent estimator of the VAR asymptotic bias squared. The denominator adds twice the variance difference

\hat{\Sigma}^{LP}_{h}-\hat{\Sigma}^{VAR}_{h}
, reflecting the efficiency gain from
using VAR. It is worth noting:

(i)

If
1/4<\zeta<1/2
, the bias term dominates the variance terms, the weight
v(\hat{\lambda}_{h})
approaches one, and TLP places all weight on LP, avoiding the biased VAR.

(ii)

If
\zeta>1/2
, the VAR has no asymptotic bias, and TLP reduces to VAR asymptotically, but only if
\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}=\Sigma_{h}^{COV}
. In such a case, there is no evidence of misspecification, so we use the more efficient estimator. However, when this is not the case, for example when the errors are not i.i.d. or LP uses more lags than the VAR, then some weight is still placed on LP.

(iii)

If
0<\zeta\leq 1/4
, a case not covered by our assumptions, both LPs and VAR have asymptotic bias, of a larger order than the variance terms, but the asymptotic bias of VAR is larger than that of LP, and TLP places all weight on LP.

Comparison to uniform model averaging.

An alternative to minimizing risk is to choose weights based on theoretical
misspecification bounds.
21
propose selecting the weight
based on
M=\lVert\alpha(L)\rVert
, the norm of the misspecification
polynomial, which applies to all horizons uniformly. Their Corollary 4.2 states
that the optimal weight
\omega
between LP and VAR should be:

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{M^{2}}{M^{2}+1}.

(23)

Comparing equations (
22
) and (
23
), both
express the weight as a ratio with bias squared in the numerator and denominator, and the difference in the case of i.i.d. errors and same lag length is that as the difference between LP and VAR variances increase with the horizon, TLP places more weight on VAR.

Since the weight in (
21
) converges to a constant, unlike for other model averaging estimators, the asymptotic distribution of TLP is normal and available in closed form. To see this, let

v=\frac{aBias_{h}^{2}+2(\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}-\Sigma_{h}^{COV})}{aBias_{h}^{2}+2(\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}-2\Sigma_{h}^{COV})}.

(24)

Theorem 5
.

Asymptotic distribution of TLP
. Under Assumptions
1
-
2
and
\zeta=1/2
,

v(\hat{\lambda})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}v
, and

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{TLP}-\beta^{*})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}((1-v)\text{aBias}_{h},\Sigma_{h}^{TLP}),

(25)

where

\Sigma_{h}^{TLP}=v^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+(1-v)^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}+2v(1-v)\Sigma_{h}^{COV}.

(26)

If
\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}=\Sigma_{h}^{COV}
, then:

\Sigma_{h}^{TLP}=v^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+(1-v^{2})\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}.

(27)

Since the weights are in the unit interval, Theorem
5
shows that the asymptotic bias of TLP is smaller in absolute value than that of the VAR, and that the variance of TLP is smaller than that of LP when
\Sigma_{h}^{LP}>\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}
, which as discussed, is true with i.i.d. structural shocks, and is likely to be the case in general at higher horizons due to the exponentiation in the VAR.

4
Variance estimation

Constructing confidence intervals for impulse response functions requires reliable
variance estimates. For LP, standard variance estimators are available. For VAR
impulse responses, however, it is known that the delta method
performs poorly in finite samples. TLP inherits these challenges since it combines
LP with VAR estimates.

One simplification for TLP is available when LP and VAR use identical lag structures:
the covariance between LP and SVAR estimators equals the variance of the VAR
estimator
(
21
)
. However, this restricts both estimators to the
same lag length. Our approach allows for different lag structures across
estimators. Since we do not impose this restriction, and furthermore, we would still need an estimate of the VAR variance, we employ the bootstrap.

4.1
Mean Symmetric Double Bootstrap (MSDB)

We propose a Mean Symmetric Double Bootstrap (MSDB) procedure for
inference on impulse response functions. This bootstrap is applied to LP, VAR, SLP,
and TLP estimators, providing near-nominal coverage for VAR and TLP as long as the misspecification in the VAR is sufficiently small.

To ensure that the asymptotic bias does not distort the VAR coverage,
21
employ bias-aware critical values leading to confidence intervals that are larger than for the LP. Instead, we employ the double bootstrap which also adjusts the critical values but the confidence intervals are not as large as the ones for LP. Recently,
3
have demonstrated that in the presence of asymptotic bias, one can use either pre-pivoting, or double bootstrap to restore coverage. In their paper, they use a nested bootstrap structure to generate bias-corrected p-values, while we focus on studentized
test statistics to construct confidence intervals. The studentization is important to achieve asymptotic refinements for data generating processes where the misspecification is not too large.
Our procedure also shares similarities with the VAR-based resampling scheme in

19
and
21
, but differs in several aspects:

(i)

We use the a recursive residual moving block
bootstrap
(
2
)
, while
21
use a recursive residual wild bootstrap. Both are valid in the set-up of our and their simulations, however, as shown in
2
, the wild bootstrap is not valid in more general cases where the mean and the variance estimators in the VAR are correlated.

(ii)

We employ a double bootstrap rather than a single
bootstrap. A single bootstrap cannot provide variance estimates needed for
studentization, since there is no reliable analytical formula for estimating the variance
of VAR impulse responses (the delta method severely undercovers in small
samples). TLP inherits this problem. The double bootstrap provides consistent
variance estimates for standardization; intuitively, the second layer of bootstrap is generated with the true DGP being the first bootstrap, so it has no bias relative to that DGP, rendering consistent estimates of the VAR variances and the covariance between LP and VAR.

(iii)

When constructing t-statistics, we subtract the bootstrap mean
rather than the VAR-implied impulse response (the pseudo-truth under their
resampling scheme). This mean-centering removes the bias from the bootstrap
t-distribution that would otherwise arise under VAR misspecification. When
the data are generated by a correctly specified VAR, the bootstrap mean and
the VAR-implied response should coincide, so the two approaches are equivalent
in that case.

(iv)

We use symmetrized confidence intervals
(
10
)
. Asymmetric intervals work well for LP, as there is no significant skewness of the implied t-statistics, but they do not account for the finite sample coverage distortions arising from local misspecification.

Resampling scheme.

2
showed that if Assumptions
1
-
2
hold, and the model is correctly specified, the recursive moving block bootstrap is valid under conditional heteroskedasticity of unknown form for the VAR estimates. This bootstrap is the one we employ and it proceeds in the following steps:

1.

Estimate the reduced-form VAR and obtain the residuals
\hat{u}_{t}
.

2.

Divide the residual series into overlapping blocks of length

\ell=T^{1/3}
, following
8
.

3.

Draw blocks with replacement until the series

\{u_{t}^{(b)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
reaches length
T
.

4.

Reconstruct the bootstrap sample recursively

\displaystyle y_{t}^{(b)}=\hat{A}y_{t-1}^{(b)}+u_{t}^{(b)},

(28)

using the estimated VAR coefficients and the resampled residuals.

5.

For each bootstrap replication, estimate impulse responses using both
VAR and LP.

Double bootstrap procedure.

The double bootstrap proceeds as follows:

1.

Treat the original sample as
b_{1}=1
and generate
B_{1}-1
bootstrap
samples using the moving-block procedure described above. For each replication

b_{1}=1,\dots,B_{1}
, estimate impulse responses
\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{\mu}

at each horizon
h
for
\mu\in\{LP,VAR,SLP\}
.

2.

For each first-level sample, apply the same moving-block resampling
procedure to generate
B_{2}
second-level bootstrap replications, now treating
the first-level sample as the data. This yields

\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1},b_{2}}^{\mu}
for
b_{2}=1,\dots,B_{2}
.

3.

Compute the variance from second-level replications:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\mu}_{h,b_{1}}=\text{VAR}_{b_{2}}\left(\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1},b_{2}}^{\mu}\right).

(29)

4.

Compute the covariance between LP and VAR from second-level replications:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{COV}_{h,b_{1}}=\text{Cov}_{b_{2}}\left(\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1},b_{2}}^{LP},\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1},b_{2}}^{VAR}\right).

(30)

For LP and VAR, these quantities are sufficient to construct the t-statistics.
TLP is constructed from LP and VAR using the weights described below. SLP
requires additional steps to obtain variance estimates.

Method-specific details.

TLP.
To compute the weights
v(\hat{\lambda}_{h})
, we need consistent
estimates of
\hat{\Sigma}_{h}^{LP}
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{h}^{VAR}
, and

\hat{\Sigma}_{h}^{COV}
. We obtain these by averaging the double-bootstrap estimates across
first-level bootstrap samples:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{h}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{1}}\sum^{B_{1}}_{b_{1}=1}\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{\mu},\quad\mu\in\{LP,VAR\},

(31)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{h}^{COV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{1}}\sum^{B_{1}}_{b_{1}=1}\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{COV}.

(32)

These are used to compute the weights
v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})
using
equation (
21
). We
re-estimate the bias components of the weights of TLP in each first level bootstrap replication. The bootstrap replications of TLP are then

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{TLP}(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})=v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{LP}+(1-v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}}))\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{VAR},

(33)

and the variance of each first-level replication is

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{TLP}(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})

\displaystyle=v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})^{2}\,\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{LP}+(1-v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}}))^{2}\,\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{VAR}

\displaystyle\quad+2\,v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}})(1-v(\hat{\lambda}_{h,b_{1}}))\,\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{COV}.

(34)

SLP.
In principle, it is possible to also estimate the SLP variance using the
second bootstrap layer. However, to reduce computational burden, we instead calculate the variance only in the first-level loop, using the
LP variance estimates from the second-level bootstrap:

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{b_{1}}^{SLP}=(\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}+\tilde{\lambda}P)^{-1}(\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X})\,\hat{\Omega}_{b_{1}}^{LP}\,(\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X})(\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}+\tilde{\lambda}P)^{-1},

(35)

where
\hat{\Omega}_{b_{1}}^{LP}=\text{diag}(\hat{\Sigma}_{0,b_{1}}^{LP},\dots,\hat{\Sigma}_{h^{max},b_{1}}^{LP})
stacks the LP variance estimates across all horizons
H
,
and
\tilde{\lambda}
is fixed at its sample estimate. This approach avoids the

B_{2}
second-level iterations for SLP while still using the double-bootstrap
variance estimates for LP. The result is an
H\times H
covariance matrix; the
diagonal element
[\hat{\Sigma}_{b_{1}}^{SLP}]_{hh}
gives the variance at
horizon
h
.

Constructing t-statistics.

For all estimators
\mu\in\{LP,VAR,SLP,TLP\}
, we construct t-statistics by
centering around the bootstrap mean:

\displaystyle t^{\mu}_{h,b_{1}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{\mu}-\bar{\beta}_{h}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{h,b_{1}}^{\mu}}},\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{\beta}_{h}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{B_{1}}\sum_{b_{1}=1}^{B_{1}}\hat{\beta}_{h,b_{1}}^{\mu}.

(36)

Subtracting the bootstrap mean rather than the VAR-implied impulse response
removes the bias introduced by VAR-based resampling under misspecification.

Symmetric confidence intervals.

The VAR bias under misspecification shifts the location of its confidence interval and
leads to asymmetric t-statistic distributions. TLP inherits some of this asymmetry by
construction. Additionally, the exponentiation in VAR exacerbates the issues. Symmetric confidence intervals
(
10
)
address
this problem and exhibit faster convergence rates than their asymmetric
counterparts.

For any estimator
\mu\in\{LP,VAR,SLP,TLP\}
, we rank the bootstrap
t-statistics by absolute value and take the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of

|t^{\mu}_{h,b_{1}}|
as the critical value
\hat{t}^{\mu}_{h,1-\alpha}
. The confidence
interval is:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\mu}-\hat{t}^{\mu}_{h,1-\alpha}\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{h,1}^{\mu}},\quad\hat{\beta}_{h}^{\mu}+\hat{t}^{\mu}_{h,1-\alpha}\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{h,1}^{\mu}}\right),

(37)

where
\hat{\Sigma}_{h,1}^{\mu}
is the variance estimate from the original
sample.

Summary.

The three components of our procedure address distinct sources of
distortion. Studentization corrects for scale differences across estimators and
horizons. Mean-centering corrects for location shifts induced by VAR-based
resampling under misspecification. Symmetric intervals guard against the skewness
in t-distributions that arises when the estimator exhibits asymptotic bias. Together, these elements yield confidence intervals with near-nominal coverage for TLP and VAR,
as long as the misspecification in the VAR is not too large.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces targeted local projections, a frequentist shrinkage estimator
that averages across LP and VAR impulse responses with data-driven, horizon-specific weights.
By shrinking LP toward VAR at each horizon, TLP reduces variance at longer horizons
where LP estimates become noisy, while the optimal weights ensure that the estimator
places more weight on the asymptotically unbiased LP when the discrepancy between estimators is
large. The shrinkage parameter has a closed-form solution, making implementation
straightforward.

For inference, we propose the Mean Symmetric Double Bootstrap, which improves
coverage relative to the standard single bootstrap procedures, delivering near-nominal coverage for LP, VAR, SLP, and TLP. Monte Carlo
simulations confirm that TLP combined with MSDB achieves substantial variance
reduction while maintaining near-nominal coverage.

Appendix A
The Importance of Projecting Out Controls in Regularized Local Projections

All estimators discussed in this paper—
\hat{\beta}^{LP}_{h}
,
\hat{\beta}^{TLP}_{h}
,

\hat{\beta}^{SLP}_{h}
, and
\hat{\beta}^{VAR}_{h}
—are scalars. This is achieved by
projecting out control variables
W
prior to estimation. Without this step,
regularization becomes problematic for two reasons.

First, if we apply uniform regularization to all parameters including controls, substantial further derivations are required to identify the target for the control coefficients.

Second, if we regularize only the parameter of interest while setting zero targets
for controls, the objective function for TLP becomes

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\theta)=\|\check{Y}_{h}-\theta_{h}\check{X}\|^{2}+\tilde{\lambda}\|\theta_{h}-\hat{\theta}_{h}^{VAR}\|^{2}_{\Lambda}

(42)

where
\check{X}=[Y_{2}\;W]
,
\check{Y}_{h}=Y_{1,h}
, and

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{h}^{VAR}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}_{(pk+2)\times 1},\quad\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}_{(pk+2)\times(pk+2)}.

(43)

The solution is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\text{TLP}}_{h}={\phi}(\tilde{\lambda})\hat{\theta}_{h}^{LP}+(I_{pk+2}-{\phi}(\tilde{\lambda}))\hat{\theta}_{h}^{VAR},

(44)

where
{\phi}(\tilde{\lambda})=(\check{X}^{\prime}\check{X}+\tilde{\lambda}\Lambda)^{-1}(\check{X}^{\prime}\check{X})
.
The matrix
{\phi}(\tilde{\lambda})
is not diagonal, so any
\tilde{\lambda}\neq 0
introduces
bias into the control coefficients.

29
shows that projecting out controls preserves both the
parameter of interest and its variance, up to a scaling factor, in ridge regression (but not in generalized ridge regression).
This approach eliminates the targeting ambiguity, prevents cross-contamination
between the parameter of interest and controls. For this reason, projecting out controls is
essential for both TLP and SLP estimators.

Proof of Theorem 3.

Risk decomposition.

For TLP at horizon
h
, the risk function is

\displaystyle R_{h}(\lambda_{h})

\displaystyle=T\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\hat{\beta}^{TLP}_{h}(\lambda_{h})-\beta_{h}^{*}\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{T\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{TLP}_{h}(\lambda_{h})]-\beta_{h}^{*}\big)^{2}}_{\text{bias}^{2}(\lambda_{h})}+\underbrace{T\,\mbox{Var}\!\left(\hat{\beta}^{TLP}_{h}(\lambda_{h})\right)}_{\text{variance}(\lambda_{h})}.

(45)

Asymptotic expansions.

We begin with the LP estimator:

\displaystyle T\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{LP}_{h}]\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=T\left(\beta^{*}_{h}+\mathbb{E}\!\big[(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}\epsilon_{h}\big]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=T(\beta^{*}_{h})^{2}+T\,\text{tr}\!\left\{\mathbb{E}\!\big[(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}X^{\prime}\epsilon_{h}\epsilon_{h}^{\prime}X(X^{\prime}X)^{-1}\big]\right\}+o(1)

\displaystyle=T(\beta^{*}_{h})^{2}+\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+o(1),

(46)

where
\Sigma_{h}^{LP}
denotes the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}
.

Analogously, for the VAR estimator and the covariance term:

\displaystyle T(\hat{\beta}^{VAR}_{h})^{2}

\displaystyle=T\big(\beta_{h}^{*}+T^{-1/2}C_{h}\big)^{2}+\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}+o_{p}(1),

(47)

\displaystyle T\,\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}

\displaystyle=T\big(\beta_{h}^{*}+T^{-1/2}C_{h}\big)\beta_{h}^{*}+\Sigma_{h}^{COV}+o(1),

(48)

where
C_{h}=\textup{aBias}_{h}
denotes the local asymptotic bias of the VAR estimator under
misspecification, and
\Sigma_{h}^{COV}
denotes the asymptotic covariance between
the LP and VAR estimators.

Bias of TLP .

Under the local asymptotic framework, its squared bias satisfies

\displaystyle T\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{TLP}_{h}]-\beta_{h}^{*}\big)^{2}=(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}C_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+v(\lambda_{h})^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}+2v(\lambda_{h})(1-v(\lambda_{h}))\Sigma_{h}^{COV}+o(1).

(49)

The first term captures the squared asymptotic bias inherited from the VAR component,
while the remaining terms arise from the variance components in the asymptotic
expansion of the estimators.

Variance of TLP.

The asymptotic variance of the shrinking estimator is

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{TLP})=v(\lambda_{h})^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}

\displaystyle+2v(\lambda_{h})(1-v(\lambda_{h}))\Sigma_{h}^{COV}+o(1).

(50)

Final empirical risk criterion.

Replacing population objects with consistent estimators yields the empirical
variance component.
A consistent estimator of
C_{h}
is

\hat{C}_{h}=T^{1/2}\big(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR}\big).

Combining the estimated bias and variance terms, the empirical risk criterion is

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{h}(\lambda_{h})

\displaystyle=T(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}(\hat{\beta}_{h}^{LP}-\hat{\beta}_{h}^{VAR})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\Big(v(\lambda_{h})^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{LP}+(1-v(\lambda_{h}))^{2}\Sigma_{h}^{VAR}+2v(\lambda_{h})(1-v(\lambda_{h}))\Sigma_{h}^{COV}\Big).

(51)

This criterion trades off the discrepancy between LP and VAR impulse response
estimates, interpreted as the estimated bias, against their sampling variability,
explicitly accounting for their covariance.
\square
