Executive Summary
 The Natural Convection Shutdown Heat Removal Test Facility (NSTF) at Argonne National Laboratory
has continued to generate empirical validation data on the performance of water-based reactor cavity
cooling system (RCCS) for seven years. This data is actively being used to support the development of
passive decay heat removal systems for advanced reactors. Distinguishing this facility are 1) the large, ½
scale of the facility and 2) its governance under an NQA-1 qualified program for producing data of the
highest pedigree for advanced reactor designers and regulators. In addition to the experimental activities
discussed in this report, a computational modeling program continues to support the experimental program
and further accuracy and understanding of the computational models. Together, the experimental and
computational work create a mutually beneficial relationship integral to the overall program objective of
advancing the understanding of the RCCS technology.

 This report details experimental activities performed during the seventh year of water-based operation.
This year’s work continues the work begun in the previous year, performing parametric and accident-
scenario testing with the new, lower tank inlet configuration and comparing those results with analogous
tests performed with the previous mid-tank inlet. Nine tests were performed during FY25, totaling 237
hours of heated operation and 12.6 MWh of electrical heating for the year. Eight of the nine tests were
classified as Accepted per NQA-1, and one was classified as Failed. Discussions presented in this work
cover thermal hydraulic performance, instability analysis, and two-phase flow analysis.

 This year saw the continuation of the power parametric series begun in FY24, resulting in four unique
power levels corresponding to full-scale decay heat powers of 1.4 MWt – 2.4 MWt. Higher powers were
resulted in increased voiding in the upper chimney, which in turn resulted in quicker development of two-
phase instabilities. When like input powers are compared, higher mass flow rates and steam generation rates
are associated with the mid-tank inlet as compared to the lower tank inlet. A pressure parametric series of
two tests was also conducted, comparing baseline conditions (3 psig) to elevated pressure (10.5 psig).
Minimal differences in thermal hydraulic and heat removal performance were observed as a result of the
pressure change, so zero further tests were conducted in this series. A header inlet throttling test was
conducted to examine the parametric effects of increasing loss factor in the single phase region from loss
factors of K=0.048 – 56. 3. Two phase oscillations initiated as Type-I density wave oscillations (DWOs),
damped with increasing throttling, progressed into Type-II DWOs before fully suppressing, and finally
transitioned to geysering instabilities at extreme throttling. Oscillation damping was found to occur at a
lower loss factor for the mid-tank inlet as compared with the lower tank inlet. An accident scenario test
examined the progression from steady-state, active, normal operating conditions into a passive mode
following a prototypic decay heat removal curve. The system successfully demonstrated satisfactory heat
removal through 76 hrs of accident conditions, corresponding to 560 hrs for the full-scale design.

 Two unique types of test were also conducted in FY25, examining the effects of adding substances to
the water to alter the thermophysical and thermochemical properties of the coolant and comparing them to
a baseline test with deionized water at 20% inventory fill. Xanthan gum was added in two different
concentrations, 0.5% and 1.0% by weight, to examine the effects of increasing the liquid viscosity. The
lower concentration temporarily resulted in significantly decreased average flow and increased intensity
flow excursions from chimney flashing before the flow excursions damped out and stabilized into two phase
flow. At the higher viscosity condition, flow remained stagnant, resulting in geysering in the heated risers
for a period before stabilizing into slow two-phase flow. Ultimately, however, in both cases two-phase flow
resumed and successfully performed in removing the heat from the system. The second unique test
examined the effects of a solute to increase the heat capacity of the system. Sodium chloride was added to
create a 10.3% salinity solution, lower the heat capacity of the water by 11.8%, and increase the solution
density by 8%. Minor changes in the thermal hydraulic performance of the system were observed due to
these combined effects, including extended two-phase oscillations and decreased steam generation rate
when compared to the baseline. However, the ultimate impact on heat removal performance was minimal.

1. Introduction

 The Natural convection Shutdown heat removal Test Facility (NSTF) at Argonne National Laboratory
(Argonne), Figure 1, is a large-scale test facility for studying thermal hydraulic behaviors of passive decay
heat removal (DHR) safety systems in advanced nuclear reactors. Since 2005, the NSTF program has
supported the DOE vision to provide US stakeholders with high quality performance data from a reactor
cavity cooling system (RCCS) concept. The NSTF was initially designed as an air-based RCCS, which was
designed, constructed, and operated from 2010 through 2016 [1], [2]. Following this, the facility was
converted into a water-based RCCS, modeled after the Framatome (formerly AREVA) 625 MWt SC-HTGR
RCCS [3] – [6]. In addition to support of industry, the NSTF design as a boiling water thermosiphon
positions it to advance fundamental understanding of phenomena such as natural circulation boiling, two-
phase flow, and flow instabilities. The water-based NSTF began operation in 2018 and has continued
operating through this seventh year of experimental testing. To date, data generated by the air-based and
water-based NSTF facilities have been formally shared with 13 different organizations, illustrating the
value the NSTF program provides to industry.

 This report will focus on the experimental testing campaigns performed during the period of calendar
years 2024-2025. Tests performed this year can be broadly classified into two categories. The first is
extending the testing begun in FY24 with the lower tank inlet configuration that repeated tests performed
with the mid-tank inlet configuration. These tests can be compared to examine potential design trade-offs
about the RCCS chimney and inventory tank. Additionally, a suite of new tests were performed to examine
the system response to physical and chemical modifications of the water coolant in the RCCS. This report
first prevents an overview of the NSTF program and facility, which remains largely unchanged from the
previous year. The test plan and methodology of individual tests follow. The bulk of the report details the
FY25 NSTF testing results and draws comparisons between analogous tests performed with the mid-tank
inlet. Two-phase stability and upper chimney analyses are presented near the end of the report. Finally, this
concludes with a summary of the year’s activity and a look-ahead to next year’s objectives.

 1.1. Program Objectives
 The NSTF program’s support of the DOE and US vendors in advancing the RCCS technology for
advanced reactors is twofold. First, it provides vendors with high-quality data suitable for code validation
for licensing applications and design optimization of advanced conceptual designs. Second, it aims to
advance the understanding of the natural phenomena inherent in advanced safety concepts. By coupling
large-scale, integral-level observations with local high-resolution measurements, the NSTF aims to create
a shift from uncertainty to predictability of successful decay heat removal solutions.

 These programmatic objectives are supported by two principles of rigor and flexibility. Rigor and quality
have been an integral part of NSTF since the design stage. The scaling methodology of NSTF [7] carefully
preserves key thermal hydraulic behaviors of the original design, allowing extrapolation of testing results
to real-world conditions. The data is produced within a controlled, traceable, NQA-1 compliant program,
as discussed below. Comparison of experimental and computational results of integrated system behaviors
is a vital element of validating codes for industry applications and has been an active component of the
NSTF program since its inception. To-date modeling efforts of the water-based NSTF can be found in
previous reports [8] – [14]. Flexibility is necessary in ensuring the NSTF program can readily adapt to meet
the needs of industry and maintain long-term relevance. Many of the tests performed have also been driven
by input and feedback from industry and government stakeholders, highlighting the essential role of
designed-in flexibility. Tests such the Framatome accident scenario illustrate the collaborative nature of
this program towards meeting the needs of industry.

 1.2. Quality Assurance Program Plan
 To ensure the highest level of integrity in the data, the research and development activities of the NSTF
experimental program are under the control of an NQA-1 compliant quality assurance program plan
(QAPP). This plan is based on the requirements set forth in the American Society of Mechanical Engineers
(ASME) NQA-1-2008 (with 1a 2009 addenda) Quality Assurance (QA) requirements for nuclear facility
applications [15] and takes on a graded approach. These guidelines have been established to provide
reliable, reproducible, and timely data on the effects of the reactor cavity cooling system on the heat removal
from the reactor vessel wall. As part of the NSTF QAPP, the program undergoes regular auditing, including
both internal and external audits, of the programmatic and operational elements. One internal audit was
conducted this year, led by Roberta Riel. The results of this audit included zero findings, one observation,
and one recommendation, both related to administrative procurement elements.

 1.3. Previous Water Testing Accomplishments
 Since the 2018 start of test operations with the water-based NSTF facility, 85 tests have been conducted
on NSTF over a total of 1,629 hours of active test operation. The first year focused on initial testing,
qualification, and single-phase testing. The second year established baseline characterizations of two-phase
boiling operation, focusing on repeatability and early parametric variations. Year three expanded these
parametric variations, examining initial inventory level, tank gas pressure, heater power to the cavity, and
loss coefficient testing. Upgrades during year three significantly expanded operational space, including a
condensate refill system for maintaining steady-state conditions over extended operational periods and an
accelerated drain for exploring late-term boil-off scenarios. Year Four began with a six-month maintenance
period, including major repairs, cleaning, and installation of new hardware. Experiments in Year Four and
Year Five focused on off-normal and accident scenarios, including throttling and loss coefficient parametric
tests, expanded power parametric tests, an simulated extended accident scenario, and separate effects
explorations of static boiling and geysering phenomena that may occur due to flow stagnation. A facility
reconfiguration was executed early in Year Six to alter the height of the upper chimney such that it
discharged into a lower tank inlet (as opposed to the previous mid-tank inlet). Testing in Year Six focused
on establishing a baseline with this new tank inlet and repeating parametric testing of the loop for
comparison between inlet heights. Details of these test can be found in previous reports and papers [16] –
[21][20], and summarized below in Table 1.

2. Facility Design
 The water NSTF reflects a ½ axial scale and 12.5° sector slice of the primary design features of a full
scale RCCS concept for the Framatome 625 MWt SC-HTGR. In its current configuration, the test facility
stands at a total height of 18.1 m (59.2 ft). Between the primary water inventory tank and the loop piping,
the facility has a capacity of 4540 liters (1200 gal). The as-built NSTF is shown in Figure 2, and details of
the facility and design can be found in previous reports [6], [16] - [21]. In addition to the primary loop, a
secondary loop between the inventory tank and the heat exchanger enables cooling of both single-phase
and two-phase inventory. Steam generated during two-phase operation may be condensed and collected in
a set of two condensate reservoir tanks or directly vented to atmosphere.

 2.1. Overview of Facility Configuration
 While detailed in the previous FY24 report [21], it is worth summarizing the upper chimney and tank
inlet reconfiguration again here as it significantly informs the testing performed in this cycle and analyses
of the system response. The two configurations are compared in Figure 3. Testing prior to FY24 was
performed using the mid-tank inlet configuration, while testing from FY24 onwards used the lower-tank
inlet configuration. The lower tank inlet configuration includes a full bore ball vale in the upper chimney
for eventual throttling testing near or within the two-phase region. The trade-off examined here is the
increased amount of inventory above the tank inlet, increasing the available inventory for boil off in an
accident scenario, against the increased opportunity for short-circuiting the tank inlet and discharge coolant
by siting these pipes closer in height, which could negatively impact on the flow rate and heat removal of
the facility.

 2.2. Instrumentation
 The water-based NSTF is instrumented with a suite of sensors for measuring integral and local thermal
hydraulic behaviors as well as system safety parameters during both single-phase and two-phase modes of
operation. The instrumentation was selected and installed as to be minimally intrusive and exhibit low
uncertainty. High priority system measurements address the loop mass and thermal balance, including flow
rate, differential pressure (DP), and temperature measurements. Table 2 summarizes all of the employed
sensors on the water-based NSTF. A facility diagram of instrumentation locations on the primary loop and
heat exchanger is provided in Figure 4. A total of 568 instrument, control, and indicator channels are
recorded as part of the standard NSTF data acquisition suite. Complete details of the instrumentation can
be found in a previous report [6].

 As in previous years, two types of instruments, shown in Figure 6, are installed to quantify two-phase
flow parameters such as void fraction and bubble velocity. The first was a bulk, volume-averaged method
using a gamma densitometer installed in the upper chimney section. The densitometer uses a Cs-137 source
on one side of the pipe and a detector on the opposite side (180° around the pipe) to measure the attenuation
of the gamma signal. The horizontal radiation path from source to detector is perpendicular to the vertical
chimney pipe and is collimated to emit a beam in a 13° cone. Unlike the twin-tipped optical probes, this
measurement is an average measurement of the bulk pipe volume. A two-point calibration is performed for
the empty (void fraction α = 1.0) and filled (α = 0.0) pipe. The measured intensity is converted into an
average density of the measured volume of the pipe using a logarithmic relation between the measured and
calibration intensities and programmed physical constants for the water and gas phases. Based on operating
experience and literature, we conservatively apply a derived error of ≤ 8% across our void fraction data,
although errors of ≤ 5% are expected in slower regimes [21], [22]. Finally, a correction for the change in
density as a function of temperature is applied to reach the final reported temperature-corrected void
fraction.

 The second instrument type are twin-tipped optical probes, which are local point measurements that
operate by sensing the change in refraction index of the medium around the probe tips. The optical probes
directly measure local void fraction, but quantities such as bubble velocity, bubble diameter, and phase
velocities can be derived using the known tip geometries. Depending on the local fluid state around the
probe tips, optical light transmitted from an amplifier to the probe is either refracted away from the probe
(liquid) or reflected back along the probe and fiber to the receiver to generate a series of 0’s or 1’s
representing transitions to the liquid or to the gas state, respectively. Data from either tip may be integrated
over the acquisition window to derive void fraction as a function of time. Using the known distance between
tips and the timing data, the velocity of a given bubble front and the size of the bubble may be determined.
The sampling rate for this system is 10 kHz, and the acquisition window is 10 seconds. A convergence
analysis performed on data taken during NSTF operation finds the measured void fraction converges within
1.0% (highest bubble count) to 3.0% (lower bubble count) of the final value within 7.0 seconds. Their
positions along the chimney relative to the tank inlet are shown in Figure 8. Optical probes were installed
such that the tips were positioned radially along the chimney centerline. Further details of the densitometer
and optical probe operation can be found in previous reports [19], [20].

 3.3. Reduced Heat Capacity Methodology
 One new test performed this year was the introduction of a contaminant, such as a salt or other solute,
to reduce the heat capacity of the water and affect its ability to remove heat from the system. Seawater has
also been examined as a potential alternative coolant to fresh water or to replenish boiled-off inventory in

accident scenarios, as at Fukushima-Daiichi [23], [24]. To this end, a test was executed in NSTF using
sodium chloride (NaCl) salt to alter the thermophysical properties of the deionized (DI) water coolant. All
concentration percentages are given as concentrations by weight (abbreviated as “concentration”). Figure
9 - Figure 11 illustrate how various thermophysical properties, specific heat capacity, density, and latent
heat of vaporization, respectively, change with increasing concentration of salt in seawater (of which NaCl

kJ/kg-K as concentration increases from 0.0% to 16%. The density at 25 °C increases slightly from 997 to

concentration range. Each of these properties can have implications for the heat removal performance of a
water-based RCCS, particularly as the system approaches two-phase conditions. The saturation limit for
NaCl in water at room temperature is 26.3 %, bounding any such testing with NaCl.

 4.1. Power Parametric
 The objective of this parametric study is to investigate the effects of both the heat load and the tank inlet
elevation on the RCCS system performance through the scaled water-based NSTF. Power parametric series
were performed with the mid and lower tank inlets, respectively. Each power parametric series consists of
four cases corresponding to prototypic decay heat loads of 1.4, 1.75, 2.1, and 2.4 MWt (34.4, 43.0, 51.6,
and 58.8 kWt scaled). To exclude potential effects by the tank inventory level, a consistent starting tank

procedure was followed for all the tests. Starting at room temperature, a power ramp to the targeted power
level was first applied. Both the facility and the water inventory were gradually heated up, eventually
leading to boiling/flashing in the water tank and subsequently the upper chimney. Following the initiation
of boiling/flashing inside the water tank, the two-phase transient accompanied by natural boil-off was
continued for over 4 hours, followed by the power ramp down to conclude the test. A complete summary
of the performed test cases for the present study is provided in Table 8.

 Initiation of boiling inside the primary water tank was first detected as the tank gas space pressure started
to increase accompanied by the decrease of the tank inventory level, as shown in Figure 12. In the water-
based NSTF, a horizontal thermocouple (TC) rake with multiple junctions has been installed at the
centerline for both the lower and mid tank inlets, which is also capable of detecting local boiling inside the
tank. Whenever a TC junction detects local boiling, the measured fluid temperature will experience a
sudden drop due to the heat absorption by the phase change. For the present power parametric series, the
second TC junction (TC672) that is located ~3” away from the tank inlet pipe exit has been detecting the
local boiling inside the tank. As seen from Figure 13, for the highest two power cases, TC672 almost
immediately detects the local boiling upon boiling incipience inside the tank. However, as the thermal
power input reduces, the local boiling is not detected by TC672 until a later time of ~0.23 and 1.20-hr for
the 43.0 and 34.4 kWt cases, respectively. The observed delays are apparently caused by the reduced
thermal power input. As the hot water stream exits the tank inlet pipe (extending 6 inch into the tank), it
starts to boil due to the sudden expansion pressure drop and thus the reduced local saturation temperature.
With the reduced power, the momentum of the hot stream exiting the tank inlet pipe is also reduced and
thus could not reach the location of TC672 initially when boiling initiates inside the water tank. As the test
progressed and the total flow continued to develop, the hot two-phase stream eventually accumulated
sufficient momentum to reach TC672, leading to the detection of local boiling. Another finding from Figure
13 is that the local liquid temperature reaches a higher value when local boiling is detected by TC672 for
the two reduced power cases. This is because there is already buildup of system pressure by the time TC672
detects local boiling in the two lower power cases, as shown in Figure 12, thus resulting in higher local
saturation temperatures.

and form losses as a negative value. Therefore, the DP4 value renders a direct indication of the total driving
force for the two-phase flow (excluding the minor contribution from the temperature difference between
the hot and cold legs). Therefore, the measured DP4 also follows a smooth linear trend similar to that of
the loop flow, as shown in Figure 14.

 The same power parametric testing was previously performed with the mid tank inlet, prior to changing
the system configuration to the lower tank inlet. Most of the observations and conclusions previously
obtained from the power parametric testing with the lower tank inlet are still applicable and will not be
repeated. The discussion in this section will focus on the effects of the tank inlet elevation on the system
performance as the thermal power input varies. As discussed in the previous study [21], the tank inlet
elevation can affect both the boiling front progression and the system performance at the two-phase quasi-
steady state.

tank inlet configuration, as seen from Figure 12. Lastly, the measured void fraction at the upper chimney

 Comparing the two tank inlet configurations, the test data for the mid tank inlet are, overall, more
scattered and don’t closely follow a well-fit linear trend as those for the lower tank inlet. One major reason
for that is the power parametric cases for the mid tank inlet were performed over a wide range of test
numbers and time span, as shown in Table 8. Over the 36-month span, multiple modifications were made
to the test facility, including installation of a full port throttling valve in the steam vent line, improvement
of the building air conditioning, and most notably those made during a 6-month maintenance period.
Despite the efforts to minimize the changes of the system characteristics during those modifications, it is
still challenging to keep consistency in the test conditions for the power parametric cases performed over
such a long span, not to mention there will always be aging effects on the test facility (e.g., the change of
the cooling panel surface emissivity due to rusting). In contrast, the power parametric cases for the lower
tank inlet were completed in four consecutive tests over a much shorter span of 3 months, for which the
conditions were relatively more consistent (except for the ambience temperatures in the case of 43.0 kWt).
As already demonstrated in the previous discussion, some of the system operation parameters can be
susceptible to variations in the test boundary conditions.

 From Figure 14 - Figure 18, the tank inlet elevation is seen to have major impacts on most of the system
operation parameters. Most of these impacts can be attributed to the difference in the hydrostatic head in
the chimney section, induced by the different tank inlet elevations. With the same thermal power input, the
generated vapor quality in the upper chimney section should be similar. However, due to the lower
hydrostatic head and thus smaller suppression of vapor in the upper chimney section associated with the
mid tank inlet, both the measured void fraction and the flow driving force (DP4) are higher, as shown in

ARL-ART-310                                           36

Figure 14. Accordingly, the average loop flow is also higher with the mid tank inlet configuration. For the
liquid in the cold leg of the NSTF, it remains subcooled throughout the test. Therefore, the cold leg liquid
temperatures will continue to rise during the test and only be dependent on the total heat addition. This
explains the similar liquid temperatures (differences comparable to the temperature measurement
uncertainty of 0.3°C) at both the riser inlet and tank outlet for the two tank inlet configurations, as shown
in Figure 16. With comparable riser inlet temperature, the higher system flow for the mid tank inlet will
therefore result in lower riser outlet temperature when the thermal power input into the test section is the
same. At the tank inlet (or equivalently the chimney outlet), the two-phase mixture temperature is mainly
determined by the local liquid saturation temperature. Naturally, the mid tank inlet configuration with
smaller hydrostatic head in the upper chimney will lead to lower tank inlet temperature as seen from Figure
16.

 For the steam/condensate generation rate, the lower tank inlet configuration yields slightly but
consistently smaller values, as shown in Figure 15. As explained earlier, the generated steam due to flashing
in the upper chimney will have to travel through the subcooled bulk tank inventory before exiting the tank.
During this process, some of the steam will be condensed inside the tank. Despite the similar vapor quality
at the chimney exit with the same thermal power input, the generated steam will have to travel a longer path
through the subcooled tank inventory for the lower tank inlet configuration, thus leading to more
condensation inside the tank. This has resulted in the lower steam/condensate generation rate as seen in
Figure 15. Understandably, the tank gas space pressure for the lower tank inlet is also lower, since it is
caused by the back pressure of the net steam flow exiting the tank.

 Lastly, the effects of the tank inlet elevation on the structure temperatures are discussed. For the cooling
panel, the tube wall temperature is more affected by the liquid temperature due to the dominance of the
convective heat transfer inside the tubes compared to the radiative heat transfer on the outer surface of the
tubes. As discussed above, the lower tank inlet configure would lead to higher riser liquid average
temperature, and thus higher cooling panel tube temperature as shown in Figure 18. For the heated plate,
although the lower tank inlet configuration yields hotter heat sink (riser liquid), the corresponding plate
temperature is observed to be lower as shown in Figure 17. This is likely caused by the rusting of the cooling
panel surface (as observed during the 6-month maintenance period) that leads to increased surface
emissivity and thus smaller radiative heat transfer resistance between the heated plate and the cooling panel
for the case of lower tank inlet. The cooling panel fin temperature, which is affected by both the riser liquid
temperature and the heated plate temperature, exhibits similar results for the two tank inlet configurations,
as shown in Figure 18. For the cold wall, the mid tank inlet configuration yields higher temperatures despite
the corresponding cooling panel temperatures being lower. This may be partially due to the influences of

 4.2. Framatome Accident Scenario Testing
 For most of the NSTF testing, including the power parametric series, a constant power has been applied.
With this constant power, the transient behaviors corresponding to a varying decay heat load that is expected
in the prototypic reactor design can not be captured. In addition, these performed NSTF testing started from
a stagnant condition at room temperatures, which is again not representative of the true operation state of
the prototypic reactor prior to initiation of accident events. In regard of this, an ‘Accident Scenario’ test
case was developed within the experimental NSTF program. This ‘Accident Scenario’ test case is defined
as the facility transitioning from a ‘normal’ single-phase steady-state operation with active cooling to an
extended ‘accident’ two-phase transient operation with continuous inventory boil-off. This specific test case
centers on incorporating the power-time history of thermal load on a water-based RCCS during an accident
scenario (as shown in Figure 20) and has been conducted in the test of DataQuality074 with the previous
mid tank inlet configuration. Details about the method, results, and analysis for this test can be found in the
report [19]. In FY25, this same ‘Accident Scenario’ testing was repeated in DataQuality099 with the current
lower tank inlet configuration.

 Void fraction data from the densitometer, located 380 mm below the tank inlet centerline, is shown in
Figure 81. Comparisons between the baseline and salted conditions are more complex due to the limitations
of the instrumentation. The densitometer outputs a void fraction signal resulting from an internal calculation
that is based on the attenuation of a gamma beam through a medium and predefined calibration points
representing 0.0% and 100% void fraction. The signal itself is limited to the calibration range of the
instrument. In typical testing scenarios the only changes in physical properties are changes in density with
fluid temperature. The decrease in density causes the raw void fraction to appear to increase with
temperature, even in single-phase flow. A simple correction based on the actual (high temperature) and
reference (calibration temperature) densities is sufficient to correct for this change. Such as temperature-
corrected void fraction is plotted as the baseline curve in Figure 81.

 However, the addition of NaCl to the fluid has a more complex interaction with the densitometer, as
now both the density and the gamma attenuation cross-section have been altered. The simple temperature
correction is no longer sufficient to account for changes to the gamma beam attenuation. Furthermore, the
output 4-20 mA signal from the densitometer is limited by the reference density (997 kg/m3). Because the
density of the 10.3% NaCl solution exceeds the reference density, any single-phase or two-phase mixture
with an aggregate density exceeding the reference density will be equally reported as having a void fraction
of 0.0%. For these reasons, we have elected to present the raw void data in Figure 81 as representative of
the chimney void fraction. Comparisons accounting changes solely to the density suggest an estimated error
of ~±5% (void fraction) between the raw data and the actual value for void fractions reported > 0.0%.

 The discrepancies between the baseline and the 10.3% NaCl void fractions mirror those of the flow rate.

 One indicator of the presence of NaCl in the inventory was electrical conductivity sampling. The water
used in NSTF comes out of the filtration system with a base conductivity of < 1 μS/cm. Sodium hypochlorite
was then added to the inventory to raise the conductivity to ~30 μS/cm for the purposes of both maintaining
cleanliness and to exceed the minimum conductivity needed for the electromagnetic flowmeters. In contrast,
the addition of sodium chloride to a concentration 10.3% sharply increases the inventory conductivity
to > 200 mS/cm (a four orders of magnitude increase). The addition of even a fraction of this quantity of
NaCl would be readily apparent in any periodic sampling or live monitoring of the conductivity.

 Minimal studies on salt water or seawater as a coolant in natural circulation are available for making
meaningful comparisons with this work. Franken et al. [23] compared 3.5% seawater, made using
commercial sweater salts, with tap and deionized water in a small-scale test loop (0.6 m heated section plu
0.6 m chimney, 25 mm inner diameter) at powers up to 2.7 kW. They observed much finer bubbly flows in
the flow as compared to the tap or DI water. Both salt deposition on the heater surface impacting bubble
formation and the relative viscosity of sea water and the presence of salt ions (particularly Mg2+ and SO42-)
within the bulk flow inhibiting bubble coalescence were cited as explanations of this two-phase phenomena.
The DP across the riser was smaller for seawater than for tap or DI water, attributed to the larger two-phase
friction factor for the larger bubbles in DI water as compared to the finer bubbles in seawater. Li, et al. [24]
performed testing in a similar small-scale (1 m high heated section plus 1 m high chimney, 20 mm inner
diameter) loop heated with 2.2 kW. As with this NSTF work, the two-phase phenomena observed in both
their deionized water and 3.5% salt concentration experiments were similar—in this case, primarily
geysering-like events of comparably very low intensity (~0.02 kg/s), featuring a stagnant incubation period,
flow excursion, and restoration period. Li, et al. observed slightly more intense flow excursions in deionized
water than salted water, as opposed to the slightly stronger oscillations in the 10.3% salt water on NSTF.
The authors observed a finer “dense foam” of bubbles dominating two-phase structure under salted
conditions as compared to the cap and slug bubbles found in their DI water, consistent with the visual
observations of Franken, et al.

 Both studies examined the differences in riser pressure drops between the salinized and deionized water.
However, care must be taken when attempting to interpret DP data from the 10.3% NSTF test. The impulse

lines between the loop and the instruments were bled with deionized water prior to filling with salt water
to minimize the potential for long-term damage to the instruments resulting from salt deposits. This results
in a density discrepancy between the loop and impulse tubing and skews the DP reading for the 10.3% NaCl
data. To attempt to quantitatively compare the 10.3% NaCl solution data to the baseline base, the raw DP
measurements are be corrected first for the density ratio between solution. After these hydrostatic losses
are accounted for, we expect there to be zero additional frictional losses at quiescent conditions (i.e. at the
start of the test). As such, we then shift the density-corrected 10.3% NaCl curve along the y-axis such that

Any one oscillation initiates due to flashing and sudden pressure changes in the upper chimney, propagates
through the tank, downcomer, and ultimately ends in the lower-to-mid chimney. The oscillations observed
in the test section (Figure 82a) and lower chimney (Figure 82b) are on the tail end of their journey through

saturation point due to the high hydrostatic head of the above inventory. In all three of these legs, too, the
average DP of the 10.3% NaCl coolant consistently drops at a faster rate than the baseline. Because this is
single phase flow, this cannot be attributed to a difference in flow regime or bubbling, as observed in
literature [23], [24]. However, the higher liquid temperatures in the chimney with lower average flow rate
of the 10.3% NaCl liquid may be responsible for the frictional pressure drop decreasing faster.

5. Stability Analysis
 Due to the lower operation pressure, the water-based RCCS can be subject to flow instabilities through
one or more instability mechanisms. Utilizing the test data from the water-based NSTF to enhance the
understanding of the instability mechanism will provide valuable insights for the real design and operation
of the prototypic RCCS. Entering FY25, efforts on the stability analysis of the NSTF data were continued
with focus on the header inlet throttling tests (DataQuality071 and 104), as well as the void fraction analysis
utilizing the RBI twin-tipped probe data for the tests where they were deployed.

 5.1. Single-phase Throttling at Inlet Header
 5.1.1. Instability Mechanism
 A detailed stability analysis for the header inlet throttling test with mid tank inlet configuration
DataQuality071 was previously performed [20]. Specifically, a transition prosses of stabilization ->
destabilization -> stabilization with the increase of the header inlet throttling was found. The system
experienced Type-I DWO’s during the initial stabilization process, and temporarily entered the Type-II
DWO’s regime when the throttling K factor increased to 159.4. One method used there to identify the
specific instability mechanisms is to examine the relationship between the flow oscillation period and the
system transit time defined as:

where 𝑉𝑉 and 𝑄𝑄̇𝐿𝐿 denote the volume of a specific section and total volumetric flow rate of liquid respectively.
Shown in Figure 84 is such a plot for the previous DataQuality071. As seen, the test results form two distinct
groups, suggesting the existence of two types of DWO’s. The group of the Transient and Stages 0 – 2

 In this test the upper optical probe was deliberately placed as close to the densitometer centerline as
possible to facilitate simultaneous measurements with both instruments of the same location and compare
their measurements. Minor differences emerge during boiling incipience and the transition from tank
boiling to chimney flashing (t=12 – 13 hr), as the optical probe is able to detect small, localized bubble

development of two-phase oscillations, very close agreement is observed between both instruments,
suggesting near-unform two-phase behavior across the radius of the pipe. As the throttling increases through
K=66 the optical probe void fraction measurement increasingly diverges from the densitometer, indicating
a divergence in the centerline two-phase character, likely a peaked void fraction, as compared to the rest of
the pipe. Non-zero void fractions, indicating a boiling front at or below the probe height, are not also
consistently observed until K=66, indicating the boiling front in between flow excursions remains within
370 mm from the tank inlet centerline. Meanwhile, very little flashing activity is observed on the lower
optical prove, 690 mm below the tank inlet centerline. Occasional non-zero void fractions are observed,
evolving into consistent flashing at the lower probe begins at K=8.6 and increasing in intensity at K=23.

 A curious transient is observed upon throttling to K=66 at t=18.44 hrs. In addition to the sudden jump
in flow rates (which eventually damps out), the void fraction traces on all three instruments are nearly
identical. This is consistent with earlier discussion (Section 4.4, Figure 48 and Figure 49), where
temperature and pressure measurements suggest a sudden, temporary migration of the boiling front from
the upper to mid-chimney in response to the valve actuation. A two-phase excursion originating in the mid-
chimney will rapidly propagate through the mid and upper chimney, such that the three void fraction
instruments in the upper chimney will see near-identical flow patterns. As this transient resolves and the

ANL-ART-310                                              106

boiling front gradually migrates back up the chimney (t=18.77), the discrepancy between the instruments
re-emerges.

 Following the collapse of the two-phase oscillations to steady-state boiling at K=91, the lower probe
void fraction continues to increase with increasing throttling, closing the gap between the upper and lower
probes, and indicating the boiling front descending further beneath the lower probe. During the pre-
geysering transitional stage of K=423 loop instrumentation again indicates the origin of two-phase
instabilities as low as the middle chimney (Figure 49) and the lower probe void fraction again merges with
the densitometer, though with minor discrepancies between the two optical probes. During geysering at
K=563, which originates within the risers, all three instruments are nearly identical, reflecting the velocity
and consistency of the geysering front as it propagates up the loop.

 As in previous reports [19] – [21], data from the two-phase probe can be processed to glean information
about bubble velocity and potential flow regime behavior within the upper chimney. The phase maps
presented here are rooted in drift-flux correlations by Mishima and Ishii [31] for small (<100 mm diameter)
vertical pipe, modified for large pipe by Schlegel, et al. [32] based on work by Kataoka and Ishii [33] and
Hibiki and Ishii [34]. The large pipe corrections is necessary because true slug flow cannot be sustained in
such large diameter pipe. Rather, local recirculation phenomena results in a “cap bubble” structure. Optical
probe data in this work is plotted against these flow maps to quantitatively understand the likely two-phase
flow patterns. There is an understood limitation of the accuracy of two-sensor probe measurements within
the cap-bubble or churn regimes where the measured interfacial velocity may not be equivalent to the true
gas phase velocity. Vapor motion within cap bubbly flow or chaotic droplets, bubbles, and ligaments in
churn flow complicate the accuracy of the twin-tipped optical probe measurements. Because of the opaque
stainless steel pipe, optical methods for defining flow regimes are unavailable. In lieu of these limitations
and no further instrumentation, we must assume the gas phase velocity is accurately represented by the
measured interfacial velocity and accept an increased uncertainty in the mapping of these results.
Nevertheless, they still serve as a useful illustration of the evolution of two-phase behavior within the
chimney.

 Flow data from the upper optical probe, 370 mm below the tank inlet centerline, are plotted in Figure
91. Here, and elsewhere in this report, select stages are omitted for clarity. Zero and low-throttling stages
(K=0.048 – 23) feature an incomplete loop of plotted data that starts in low, bubbly flow, increases in flow
rate and gas space velocity towards cap bubbly flow, and returns to low bubbly flow as the flow oscillation
slows. The unplotted data points (the missing portion of the loop) represent zero void fraction points, during
the valleys of oscillations, while the boiling front resides above the probe. The majority of data points, even
during flow excursions, reside within the bubbly regime. This regime is defined in the drift-flux correlations
as void fraction α < 30% [31], [32]. As throttling increases, a larger fraction of the data during the excursion
peak falls in the cap bubbly regime. When the flow oscillations begin to collapse, K=66, they collapse in
this phase space initially to a region firmly within the cap-bubbly regime. As throttling continues to
increases this collapsed data migrates towards the cap bubbly-churn transition line. This collapsed data is
not inconsistent with measurements of cap bubbly flow. Oscillations corresponding to alternating portions
of higher-void and liquid flow are not observed as they are expected to occur on a time scale much faster
than the 10 second acquisition window for the optical probe data. Finally, during pre-geysering
destabilization and geysering, K=423 and K=563, respectively, the mapped data points greatly expand out
from this collapsed point, occupying space in bubbly, cap bubbly, or churn regimes depending on where it
falls in the flow excursion cycle. Flow regime is most sparse during geysering due to stagnant or slow
single-phase flow between geysers.

 Figure 92 plots flow regime data from the lower optical probe, 690 mm below the tank inlet centerline.
The trends are similar to those of the upper probe (Figure 91), but shifted left (towards the bubbly regime)
on the map as a result of the lower elevation, increased hydrostatic head, and increased saturation
temperature at the lower probe. Flashing is observed occasionally during peaks of early throttling stages,
but is otherwise typically single-phase flow. As the flow oscillations collapse to steady-state boiling at
K=66 – 272 the plotted flow regime remains in the bubbly regime through K=127, begins to transition to
cap-bubbly at K=183, and resides firmly within the cap-bubbly regime at K=272. Again, these data are
consistent with the definition of the bubbly-cap bubbly transition line at α = 30% and Figure 90, which
indicates an average void fraction of ~33% at K=183. Data in the pre-geysering and geysering conditions
is focused around the cap bubbly regime and expands outwards to both the bubbly and churn regimes as
the flow excursion cycle progresses. Again, this follows the general trend of slower bubble velocities and
lower void fractions expected at lower chimney elevations.

 These measurements are the first set of optical probe data measured with the lower tank inlet
configuration. Observations are similar to those observed for the mid-tank inlet configuration [20], [30].
However, two-phase data at these same inventory, power conditions, and probe height within the chimney
for the mid-tank inlet configuration are not available, precluding a 1:1 comparison. The incomplete loops
within flow regime space observed at low throttling at 370 mm below tank inlet centerline for this work
(due to zero void fraction measurements) consistently represent interpolations between the fully-completed

                                                      109                                         ARL-ART-310

loops seen at higher elevations (150 mm below centerline) and the largely incomplete loops of the lower
inlet probe (690 mm below centerline). This makes sense for the intermediate probe height of -370 mm
used in this test, reflecting the gradual migration of the boiling front lower in the chimney with increased
throttling. Likewise, optical probe data at -150 mm also showed a tendency to collapse (post-oscillation)
just on the churn side of the cap-bubbly-churn transition line, while the probe oscillations at -370 mm in
this work collapsed in the cap bubbly regime and only migrated towards the transition line to churn flow at
much higher throttling. The churn flow regime is associated with higher void fraction and higher gas
velocity, both of which would be expected characteristics of two-phase flow at higher chimney elevation.

 One additional takeaway from this work is that the bulk measurement of the densitometer is nearly
equivalent to the local centerline optical probe void fraction measurement while flow is in the bubbly
regime. Relocating the upper optical probe higher in the chimney would expand void fraction profiling as
a function of height while the densitometer remains a reliable middle data point. As the gas speed
accelerates under cap bubbly or churn conditions, the local centerline two-phase state departs from the bulk-
averaged two-phase state and the densitometer becomes a less reliable proxy for the optical probe. However,
at the extreme high-void geysering transients that initiate well below the chimney, the near-completely
voided state has fully evolved low enough in the chimney such that there is no observable change as a
function of height.

 Here, the densitometer data has undergone a temperature correction; however, no correction was made
for salinity and altered density. One advantage of the optical probes in the salt solution is that no correction
for salinity is necessary to process the void fraction data. The digital signal returned to the probe amplifier
is simply a function of the liquid or air state of the local fluid and its ability to reflect or refract the optical
signal; water chemistry does not impact this measurement. As discussed in the earlier section, the optical
probe installed near the densitometer centerline and densitometer are highly consistent in bubbly flow of
deionized water. Here, we can use the optical probe as a proxy for the densitometer to assess the accuracy
of the densitometer void fraction measurement in the 10.3% NaCl water. The optical probe observes non-
zero void fraction for the entire cycle by t=8.47 hrs, indicating the descent of the boiling front beneath the
probe. The densitometer signal, with only a temperature correction that assumes deionized water, displays
an astonishing agreement with the optical probe signal for the majority of the oscillation cycle. Minimal
discrepancy of <3% between the two instruments is observed near the peaks, less than the estimated baseline
error of the instrument. This may be because at higher void fractions a lower volume of salinized liquid is
present, so discrepancies due to increased attenuation through the 10.3% NaCl liquid is less significant. As

discussed earlier, the densitometer loses accuracy for low void fractions in the 10.3% NaCl water, given
that the solution density exceeds the calibration density, causing low, non-zero void fractions to be reported

0% or offset by some inconsistent value ranging from 2 – 16% by the densitometer. Secondary oscillations

 The lower optical probe at 690 mm below the tank inlet centerline shows the strongest void fraction
oscillation at the start of the test, peaking at up to 46%. However, these fall off at a much faster rate than
those at the higher elevation, suppressing almost entirely by t=10.2 hrs. In contrast with the inlet header
throttling test, this suggests the flashing front migrates upwards over the duration of the run as the flow
oscillations damp. This lower probe displays no response to the secondary oscillation modes observed by
the mass flow rate and upper void fraction instrumentation, suggesting an origin of this phenomenon above
the probe height. This is the first observation of this phenomenon with the twin-tipped optical probes on
NSTF. While many previous tests have examined the damping of flow oscillations due to external factors
such as valve throttling, this is the first test in which flow oscillations were allowed to damp naturally, and
the first suggestion of what is happening to the flashing front inside the upper chimney under these
conditions.

 Two-phase flow regime maps for the upper and lower optical probes are shown in Figure 94 and Figure
95, respectively. The data was divided into four arbitrary stages to aid in understanding, corresponding to
initial boiling incipience (t=6.93 – 8.35 hrs), smooth oscillations (8.35 – 9.23 hrs), initial emergence of
secondary oscillation modes or perturbations (t=9.23 – 10.23 hrs) and run conclusion (10.23 – 11.12 hrs).
Flow oscillations during the smooth oscillations outline a closed loop in the flow regime phase space, as
has been previously observed here and in previous NSTF works [30]. This loop initiates in the low bubbly
regime and only crosses into the cap bubbly regime and barely into the churn regime at the apex of the
oscillations. The addition of the secondary oscillations modes (perturbations) have a minor effect of shifting
the centroid of this loop on the phase map to the right, towards the cap bubbly regime. The apex of the
oscillations remains mostly unchanged, just shifted slightly left within the cap bubbly regime with the
gradually decreasing intensity of the primary oscillations. However, the increased bubble and gas phase
velocities from these secondary modes result seem to fill in the phase space previously outlined by the
smooth oscillations, still well within the bubbly regime. The flow regime map for the lower probe, by
comparison, has much less activity due to the flashing front descending to the lower probe only during the
apex of oscillations. These points that are registered by the probe manifest as a straight line, confined within
the bubbly regime, that contracts horizontally in place as the oscillation intensity damps. The sparse points
measured in the “Conclusion” stage fall off of this line, but still remain in the low bubbly flow regime.

 As noted earlier, previous works examining two-phase, natural circulation flow in seawater observed a
distinction in the visual flow patterns between seawater and deionized water, albeit under different
conditions than those of NSTF. The presence of the salt inhibited the ability of bubbles to coalesce and
resulted in finer bubbly flows as opposed to slug flow in the deionized water [23], [24]. In this work, two
phase flow also remained confined to the bubbly flow regime except under high-intensity flow at the
oscillation peaks. While this observation about bubbly flow is consistent with the literature, we cannot be
sure at this time whether this is due to the presence of salt. Limited comparisons may be made between
these data and the baseline conditions, as the optical probes were not installed on the baseline run. Data
from 20% fill height at either tank inlet configuration is likewise not available. nor is any data from runs
where oscillations were allowed to damp naturally, without external influences such as valve actuation, that
would enable comparisons between the secondary oscillations modes of the 10.3% NaCl coolant and natural
damping behavior. When compared to the inlet header throttling tests (70% initial fill, as opposed to 20%
initial fill for the 10.3% NaCl test), the only other test performed at the same lower tank inlet configuration,
both the superficial liquid and gas phase velocities and are similar. The 10.3% NaCl peak displays a sharp
curvature near the bubbly-cap bubbly transition line, as opposed to the more convex shape of the phase map
loop observed in previous NSTF tests. However, without comparisons at these same power, inventory level,
and tank inlet configurations, it is difficult to attribute this solely to salt inhibition of cap bubble formation.
The salt inhibition of slug bubbles was also in small-diameter pipe where true slug flow may occur. Salt
may not have the same inhibiting effect on cap bubble formation due to the additional localized recirculation
flows that already prevent true slug formation.

 Another behavior that was observed in the literature was the spontaneous emergence of slug bubbles
penetrating and rupturing the dense bubbly structure, driving a flow excursion akin to geysering [24]. While
the observed flow was otherwise stagnant in the case of Li, et al. [24], NSTF flow is constant circulating.
However, this posed a question about whether the secondary oscillation modes observed in the 10.3% NaCl
test on NSTF were the result of a spontaneous emergence of a cap bubble or similar coalescence. The flow
regime map in Figure 94 provides some additional insights to this question. All secondary oscillations
manifest as data points within the primary “loop” in phase space outlined by the primary oscillations, rather
than spontaneous emergences further in the cap bubbly regime on the flow map. The rightwards shift of
data points within the phase map (i.e. higher superficial gas velocity) during primary oscillation valleys
while maintaining nearly-consistent peak locations—within the cap bubbly regime—also points to some
change in the two-phase character due to the secondary modes. A related question is whether the 10 second
acquisition window of any one of these points is sufficiently long that the higher gas velocity and limited
presence of the secondary mode that cap bubble emergence would be detected over the average

ANL-ART-310                                            114

measurement. From examination of Figure 73c and d and Figure 93c and d, individual secondary modes
persist for ~20 seconds—enough to fully occupy one or two optical probe acquisition windows. The
amplitudes reach as high as 50% by the conclusion of the test—exceeding the transition line definition for
cap bubbly flow [32]. It is possible that as the flow oscillations damp and the valley mass flow rate
increases, individual cap bubbles are able to emerge more frequently and overcome the inhibitions due to
the presence of salt. They may be emerging at low enough frequency that over a 10 second acquisition
window the majority of observed flow is still bubbly, but at a high enough frequency or intensity to drive
up the average superficial gas velocity and void fraction. Without visual observations and without optical
probe measurements at the 20% fill baseline conditions, however, it is difficult to definitively answer this
question.

6. Summary and Path Forward
 It is anticipated that the next year (calendar years 2025 – 2026) will be the final period for the
experimental water-based NSTF testing program under the current DOE-NE sponsor. Testing will be
limited to a select number of test cases with the water-based facility, including remaining gaps in any series
conducted in previous years, off-normal scenarios including partially and fully blocked riser channels and
blockages of the chimney piping, etc. It is expected that this testing will be completed by mid-year, at which
point the program will have conducted all planned experimental testing objectives and personnel will begin
the process of placing the test facility into cold-standby. This work will include fully draining of the coolant
inventory, documentation of material and component condition, cataloging of related instrumentation and
subassemblies, etc., and will support the ability to answer future inquiries on as-tested conditions, and also
facilitate ease of restart should a new sponsor or purpose be identified.

 Efforts will also include data reduction of remaining data sets, data archival of all collected data for long
term storage, technical documentation, and publication of reports. A final project technical report will be
published documenting the entirety of the water-based program testing series, along with one or more
journal article(s) submitted to relevant journals. Finally, is anticipated that the project team will release a
white paper documenting the overall program observations including mechanics of operating the test
facility, and experience of best recommendations for a viable full-scale implementation. Data archival
activities will comprise final packaging of all collected water-based data sets, and transmittal to a centrally
hosted database that will be controlled and maintained by the Idaho National Laboratory.
