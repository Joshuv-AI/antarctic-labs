The Physical Limit of Neural Hypoxia Detection in the Black Sea from Satellite Observations Thanks: This work was co-supervised by Marilaure Grégoire and Gilles Louppe.


Abstract
Coastal hypoxia ( O 2 < 63 O_{2}<63 [ m ​ m ​ o ​ l / m 3 mmol/m^{3} ]) threatens ocean health worldwide. Bottom oxygen consumed by respiration cannot be renewed, making monitoring essential to protect vulnerable marine ecosystems and reduce biodiversity loss. Despite the growing availability of Black Sea satellite observations, no operational system currently exploits them to directly infer the oxygen state in real time. This can be framed as a Bayesian inverse problem relating surface observations to the complete Black Sea states. Here, we solve it using a deep generative neural network trained on numerical model outputs, providing a tractable approximation of the true posterior distribution of sea states. We find that accurate state estimation is limited to the mixing layer, because its homogeneity makes surface conditions representative of subsurface states. During summer, we detect 38% of all hypoxic events shelf-wide with a precision of 47%. Improving results will likely require longer assimilation windows or additional observations.

Plain Language Summary

In coastal waters, excessive fertilizer use causes eutrophication, producing organic matter whose decomposition consumes oxygen. When ventilation cannot replenish this loss, oxygen drops below a critical threshold, creating hypoxia that threatens marine ecosystems. Despite the growing availability of satellite observations, no operational system currently exploits them to directly infer oxygen levels in real time. We address this gap by solving the Bayesian inverse problem that estimates the complete Black Sea state, including oxygen, from satellite surface observations alone. This is challenging since multiple states may produce the same observations. Traditional methods estimate only the most likely state, overlooking rare hypoxic events. Here, we use a deep generative model trained on numerical model outputs to recover the full range of plausible states, from which oxygen is inferred alongside the other observed variables. We find that surface observations enable accurate state estimation within the mixing layer year-round, because homogeneity within this layer makes surface conditions representative of subsurface states. During summer, we detect 38% of all hypoxic events shelf-wide, with 47% of our detections correctly identifying hypoxia. Improving accuracy across depths will likely require longer assimilation windows or additional observations (e.g., float measurements).

Introduction

Hypoxia (
O_{2}<63
[
mmol/m^{3}
]) is a worldwide phenomenon affecting the bottom of many coastal ocean areas
26
;
5
;
21
. It has detrimental effects on benthic life, particularly crustaceans and bivalves, and more generally on low-mobility organisms that cannot escape. Hence, accurate and timely estimates of oxygen levels are key to supporting informed management decisions
6
. The standard approach for estimating oxygen levels and detecting hypoxia relies on numerical ocean models, which simulate the full dynamics of the ocean. In the framework of the Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service (CMEMS)
30
, 10-day forecasts and multidecadal reanalysis are routinely produced for the ocean physics and biogeochemistry, and, in particular, for oxygen. Yet these physics-based models are complex to run and not easily amendable by non-experts.

In operational conditions, satellites continuously observe the sea surface and measure temperature, salinity, chlorophyll, and sea surface height at ever-increasing temporal, spatial, and spectral resolutions
30
. However, these observations remain confined to the sea surface and do not directly measure oxygen levels. No operational system currently exploits them to infer oxygen without first running a numerical simulation and then using the observations to correct it. The real-time availability of these observations thus raises the question of whether surface data alone could enable reliable state estimation or hypoxia detection without requiring a numerical simulation.

The possibility of estimating the current sea state, and particularly inferring oxygen levels, from the present-day surface observations is supported by physical and biogeochemical processes
6
. Bottom hypoxia typically occurs when oxygen consumed by benthic respiration exceeds physical replenishment. This happens under poor ventilation and intense organic matter degradation, most commonly in summer when: (
1
) the water column is stratified, (
2
) bottom temperatures peak, and (
3
) sediments receive organic matter exported after the main surface bloom. This summer decoupling between surface and bottom waters challenges bottom condition retrieval from surface observations. Yet, winter mixing couples surface (temperature, chlorophyll) to the bottom oxygen levels, which condition summer hypoxia. Overall, we argue that the inverse problem linking current surface observations to the subsurface state is indeed physically supported.

Motivated by the remarkable effectiveness of deep generative neural networks in solving inverse problems across various tasks
26
;
14
;
32
;
3
;
33
;
20
, we address ours through a two-stage approach. First, we train a diffusion model on a numerical simulation of sea physics and biogeochemistry evolution to learn daily dynamics. Then, we use the diffusion model to estimate the actual sea state by constraining the generation to surface observations. Due to its stochastic nature, the diffusion model enables generation of multiple state estimates. Combined, these plausible solutions quantify estimation uncertainty and define the posterior distribution of the inverse problem. Concurrently,
29
used the same approach to reconstruct subsurface velocity and buoyancy fields from sea surface height in an idealized double-gyre configuration. Our work extends theirs to a realistic sea and to biogeochemical variables.

The paper is organized as follows. We first validate our diffusion prior through experiments that include analyzing seasonal means, power spectra, and reproducibility of spatial structures. We then solve the inverse problem and assess state estimation accuracy, identify its depth limitations, and establish baseline hypoxia detection results from surface observations alone.

Background

An inverse problem can be posed as a Bayesian inference problem
25
;
2
, where the solution is the posterior distribution
p(x\mid y)\propto p(y\mid x)\,\,p(x)
. Here,
p(x)
is the prior distribution over plausible sea states
x
, and
p(y\mid x)
is the likelihood describing how observations
y
relate to a given state
x
. To solve the inverse problem, we start by learning a diffusion model of the prior
p(x)
. Following
28
, samples
x\sim p(x)
are progressively perturbed through a diffusion process expressed as a stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\operatorname{d}\!{x(t)}=f(t)\,x(t)\operatorname{d}\!{t}+g(t)\operatorname{d}\!{w(t)}\,,

(1)

where
f(t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the drift coefficient,
g(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the diffusion coefficient,
w(t)
denotes a standard Wiener process, and
x(t)
is the perturbed sample at time
t\in[0,1]
. Since the SDE is linear in
x(t)
, the perturbation kernel from
x
to
x(t)
is Gaussian and takes the form
p(x(t)\mid x)=\mathcal{N}(x(t)\mid\alpha(t)\,x,\Sigma(t))
, where
\alpha(t)
and
\Sigma(t)=\sigma(t)^{2}\,\mathbb{I}
are derived analytically from
f(t)
and
g(t)
. The forward SDE (
1
) admits a family of reverse SDEs
1
,

\operatorname{d}\!{x(t)}=\left[f(t)\,x(t)-\frac{1+\eta^{2}}{2}\,g(t)^{2}\,\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(x(t))\right]\operatorname{d}\!{t}+\eta\,g(t)\operatorname{d}\!{w(t)}\,,

(2)

where
\eta\geq 0
controls stochasticity. Thus, we can draw noise samples from
x(1)\sim p(x(1))\approx\mathcal{N}(\alpha(1),\Sigma(1))
and gradually denoise them by simulating Eq. (
2
) from
t=1
to
0
to obtain
x(0)\sim p(x(0))\approx p(x)
using an appropriate discretization scheme
18
. In practice, the score function
\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(x(t))
is unknown, but it is approximated by a neural network trained via denoising score matching
31
. Finally, to generate from
p(x\mid y)
, we decompose the posterior score as:

\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(x(t)\mid y)=\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(x(t))+\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(y\mid x(t))\,,

(3)

and plug it into the reverse SDE in Eq. (
2
). The perturbed likelihood score
\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(y\mid x(t))
in Eq. (
3
) can be approximated under moderate assumptions on the observation process
p(y\mid x)

8
;
24
;
9
.

3
Materials and Method

3.1
Training Dataset

To train the diffusion model, we need a large amount of data from which the spatio-temporal dynamics can be learned, but this cannot be provided by real observations
27
. Instead, we use a multidecadal three-dimensional gridded synthetic dataset delivered by an ocean modeling system that couples the physical model NEMO
19
and the biogeochemical model BAMHBI
16
. This modeling system is state of the art and provides operational prediction of the Black Sea within the CMEMS. The dataset consists of a trajectory
\mathbf{x}=\{x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\}
, where
n
is the number of simulated days between 1980 and 2023. Each state
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times X\times Y\times Z}
, with
N
the number of variables and
X
,
Y
, and
Z
the numbers of mesh points along latitude, longitude, and depth, respectively, consists of daily-averaged fields. In this work, we keep only chlorophyll, salinity, temperature, sea surface height, and oxygen as the emulated variables. These fields are simulated on a numerical domain covering the northwestern Black Sea, with a horizontal resolution of
0.025^{\circ}
and 32 vertical levels. In our configuration, this is a grid of size
128\times 256\times 32
. To ease training, we reshape
x^{i}
by merging the channel (i.e., variable) dimension
N
and depth dimension
Z
into a single axis. Thus, our state
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times X\times Y}
, where
C=N\cdot Z
, becomes a stack of two-dimensional grids. In addition, we define two context variables that provide temporal and spatial information. The temporal context
d
represents the day of the year, normalized by
366
to the interval
[0,1]
. The spatial context
m
encodes the position of each grid cell through three channels containing normalized indices in
[0,1]
for longitude, latitude, and depth.

Including the context variables
d
and
m
, the score network input will have a total of
C=133
channels:
129
for state variables (
4\times 32+1
),
1
for temporal context, and
3
for spatial mesh coordinates. The dataset is split into training (
1998
to
2017
), validation (
2018
to
2020
), and test (
2021
to
2022
). Finally, the training dataset defines the prior
p(x\mid d)
, which the diffusion model learns to approximate.

3.2
Score Neural Network

We train a neural network
N_{\theta}(x^{i}(t),\sigma(t))
with parameters
\theta
, to approximate the prior score function
\nabla_{x^{i}(t)}\log p(x^{i}(t)\mid d)
. Before passing through the network, the context variables
d
and
m
are projected and concatenated to the noised state
x^{i}(t)
. For notational convenience, we continue referring to this extended representation as
x^{i}(t)
. Following
18
, the network estimates the denoised posterior mean
\mathbb{E}[x^{i}\mid x^{i}(t)]
by minimizing

\mathbb{E}_{x^{i}\sim p(x)}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma(t)^{2}\mathbb{I})}\,\,\lambda(\sigma(t))\,\lVert N_{\theta}(x^{i}+\epsilon,\sigma(t))-x^{i}\rVert^{2}_{2},

(4)

where
\lambda(\sigma(t))=1+1/\sigma(t)^{2}
(more details in SI Sec.
3
). Using Tweedie’s first-order formula
12
, we have
\nabla_{x^{i}(t)}\log p_{\theta}(x^{i}(t)\mid d)\approx(N_{\theta}(x^{i}(t),\sigma(t))-x^{i}(t))\,/\,\sigma(t)^{2}
, which allows us to sample from our prior approximation
p_{\theta}(x\mid d)
. As the score network
N_{\theta}
, we use a U-Net
23
with diffusion time conditioning
17
.

3.3
Idealized Observation Model

To compute the perturbed likelihood score
\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(y\mid x(t))
in Eq.
3
, we first need to define the observation process
p(y\mid x)
. We define an idealized observation process that captures the essential characteristics of satellite measurements. We approximate it as a linear Gaussian forward model, which we define as

p(y\mid x)\approx\mathcal{N}(A\,x+\mu_{S},\,\Sigma_{S}),

(5)

where
A
is a linear transformation operator that downsamples surface state variables, and
\mu_{S}
and
\Sigma_{S}=\sigma_{S}^{2}\,\mathbb{I}
are the bias and covariance matrix modeling the satellite observational error. The Gaussian form is standard in data assimilation
13
;
7
. It is physically motivated by the aggregation of independent instrument errors
10
, and yields an analytically tractable score
\nabla_{x(t)}\log p(y\mid x(t))
required by our posterior sampling framework. Furthermore, we assume a diagonal
\Sigma_{S}
, as the full error covariance is rarely known in practice or hard to estimate
4
. This assumption is conservative: spatially uncorrelated errors carry less information and thus provide a weaker observational constraint. Finally, the diagonal entries of
\Sigma_{S}
are calibrated to match satellite instrument noise levels derived from sensor documentation (see SI Sec.
5
, Table S
3
). We do not use spatial masks to partially hide the surface due to the difficulty of generating patterns that match actual satellite coverage
11
.

3.4
Evaluation Protocol

Experiments fall into two categories: (1) we validate our diffusion prior and (2) we analyze posterior samples. For (1), we draw samples
x\sim p_{\theta}(x\mid d)
and assess how well they reproduce the state dynamics of the training dataset. For (2), we first generate idealized observations
y
from each state
x
in the test dataset using our idealized observation model (Eq.
5
). Afterwards, we use these observations to draw samples
x\sim p_{\theta}(x\mid y,d)
and create an ensemble of
128
state estimates
x
. Each member is generated independently by solving Eq.
2
discretized in
256
timesteps, taking
1.25
[min] on a single A100 (
40
[GB]) GPU. Since all
128
members are independent, they can be generated simultaneously, one per GPU, so the full ensemble is produced in
1.25
[min] total. Important metrics used in the experiments are defined below.

3.4.1
Metrics and Experiments

Wasserstein distance
The 1-Wasserstein distance
22
quantifies the dissimilarity between two uni-dimensional probability distributions by measuring the minimum work needed to transform one into the other. It returns a non-negative scalar and the smaller the value, the more similar the two distributions. For each variable and depth, we estimate it from spatial samples of size
n=X\times Y
as

W(P,Q)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p_{(i)}-q_{(i)}|,

(6)

where
p_{(i)}
and
q_{(i)}
denote the
i
-th smallest values of the samples drawn from
P
and
Q
, respectively, with both samples sorted in ascending order. We compute three distances per variable, referred to as
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
, and
W_{3}
: (
W_{1}
) measures the irreducible variability between two finite approximations of
p(x\mid d)
, serving as our target distance; (
W_{2}
) measures how well our learned prior
p_{\theta}(x\mid d)
approximates the reference prior
p(x\mid d)
; (
W_{3}
) measures the distance between the daily prior
p(x\mid d)
and the yearly marginal distribution
p(x)
. A well-calibrated prior should satisfy
W_{2}\approx W_{1}
, indicating successful approximation within irreducible sampling variability.

Spread-Skill Ratio
The spread-skill ratio (SSR)
15
combines skill (RMSE of the ensemble mean) and spread (square root of ensemble variance)

\text{SSR}=\sqrt{\frac{M+1}{M}}\,\,\frac{\text{Spread}}{\text{Skill}},

(7)

where
M
is the ensemble size. A well-calibrated ensemble has an SSR close to
1
, meaning its uncertainty appropriately reflects its error. An SSR below
1
indicates over-confidence (too narrow relative to error), while an SSR above
1
indicates under-confidence (too dispersed relative to error).

Classification Metrics
We evaluate our hypoxia detection performance using three standard metrics computed from the confusion matrix (true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN))

\text{Balanced Accuracy}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}}+\frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN}+\text{FP}}\right),

(8)

\text{Precision}=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}},

(9)

\text{Recall}=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}}.

(10)

All three metrics range from
0
to
1
, with higher values indicating better results. The precision measures the fraction of predicted hypoxic voxels that are truly hypoxic, the recall measures the fraction of true hypoxic events correctly detected, and the balanced accuracy measures overall skill at distinguishing hypoxic from non-hypoxic regions.

Hypoxia density
For a grid voxel
(i,j)
, the hypoxia density measures the percentage of daily states over a given period for which hypoxia was detected at any depth level:

\text{Hypoxia Density}_{i,j}=\frac{100}{N_{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{N_{T}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\min_{k}\,x_{i,j,k}^{t}<\theta\right],

(11)

where
N_{T}
is the number of days in the period,
t
indexes daily states,
k
indexes depth levels,
\theta
is the hypoxia threshold, and
\mathbf{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function.

Results

4.1
Prior Approximation Validation

4.1.1
Seasonal and Spatial Patterns

The emulated seasonal dynamics (Fig.
1
) reproduce the seasonal variation of shelf dynamics influenced by the Danube. The prior captures the summer extension of the Danube’s plume, which spreads across the entire shelf as river discharge peaks. In summer, low-salinity waters occupy the shelf and productive coastal waters extend offshore. The temperature seasonal cycle, lowest in winter-spring and highest in summer-fall, is particularly well represented in the Danube plume. Oxygen dynamics are linked to temperature through solubility effects and to river discharge through photosynthesis and respiration, especially at depth. The emulated oxygen field matches the reference in terms of spatial patterns and intensity. In all fields, the influence of river discharges from the Danube’s Dniester and Dniester is well represented, and sub-mesoscale structures are present at the frontal interface.

Visual inspection (examples in Figs. S

4.3.2
Improved Surface Observations

We find that high-resolution observations
y^{*}
improve all classification metrics globally, which demonstrates that observation quality directly affects the vertical oxygen distribution and that a genuine margin of improvement exists. However, the spatial precision maps obtained (Fig.

This means that better surface observations refine our oxygen estimates where hypoxia was already detected, but detections remain concentrated in high-density regions. Sparse, spatially isolated hypoxic events are located far from these regions and go undetected regardless of observation quality. Therefore, we argue that improving surface observations alone is insufficient to resolve the detection gap for rare hypoxic events, suggesting that additional observation sources beyond surface remote sensing are needed to fully capture the spatial diversity and structure of hypoxia.

5
Conclusion

We trained a deep generative neural network on a reanalysis dataset to solve the Bayesian inverse problem relating satellite surface observations (temperature, salinity, chlorophyll, and sea surface height) to the three-dimensional physical-biogeochemical states of the northwestern Black Sea. This includes oxygen, which is inferred solely from these surface-observed variables without any direct oxygen observation. This approach captures the full posterior distribution, estimates uncertainty, and can leverage any combination of observations at native resolution without retraining the neural network.

We start by validating our diffusion prior, confirming that
p_{\theta}(x\mid d)
accurately approximates
p(x\mid d)
through a Wasserstein distance analysis, power spectral comparisons of emulated variables, and visual inspection of seasonal dynamics. We then conduct two sets of experiments using idealized satellite observations. The first reveals that below the mixing layer, surface observations provide no predictive skill, a direct consequence of the physical decoupling between the surface and the sea interior. The second provides a quantitative assessment of hypoxia detection over the full water column. This physical limit is reflected in the detection metrics: averaged over the full water column during summer, we achieve only
38.1\%
recall and
46.9\%
precision. The spatial precision maps analysis further shows that higher-resolution surface observations improve detection but cannot overcome this information barrier, suggesting the need for additional observation sources beyond satellite surface fields.

Finally, we acknowledge that the test period (2021 to 2022) lies only four years beyond the end of training (2017), during which Black Sea oxygen dynamics may not have changed substantially. Evaluating on temporally more distant data would better assess whether detection performance remains consistent as oxygen conditions evolve. Future work will refine the observation model to account for spatial correlations, extend the framework to the full Black Sea, and integrate additional observation sources such as Argo and BGC-Argo floats to better capture the spatial diversity of hypoxia.
