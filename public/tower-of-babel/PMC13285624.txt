Assessing the burden of diabetes mellitus in dengue patients: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingjing Lin, Assunta Navarra, Serena Vita, Alessandra D'Abramo, Francesca Faraglia, Maria Virginia Tomassi, Patrizia De Marco, Emanuele Nicastri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is a rapidly expanding mosquito-borne viral disease and a growing global public health challenge. Simultaneously, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) continues to rise worldwide. DM, often referred to as a silent pandemic, is characterized by chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired immune responses, mechanisms that may worsen dengue outcomes.ObjectivesGiven this context, our study aimed to systematically synthesize evidence on the association between DM and severe dengue outcomes, especially severe dengue and dengue-related mortality.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, and Embase were searched, alongside manual screening of reference lists, for English-language observational studies published between 1987 and 2025 reporting dengue outcomes in patients with and without DM. Severe dengue definitions based on both WHO 1997 and WHO 2009 classifications were considered. Screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using random-effects models.ResultsA total of 27 eligible studies involving 734,123 participants met the inclusion criteria. Compared with non-diabetic patients, those with DM had significantly higher odds of severe dengue (OR: 2.66, 95% CI: 1.90-3.73) and of dengue-related mortality (OR: 3.95, 95% CI: 2.98-5.23).ConclusionsDM is independently associated with severe dengue and dengue-related mortality. These findings underscore the need for early identification and close monitoring of diabetic patients in dengue-endemic settings, and for integrated management strategies addressing both infectious and non-communicable diseases. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and evaluate targeted interventions in this vulnerable population.Keywords: Dengue, Dengue virus (DENV) infection, Diabetes mellitus (DM), Severe dengue (SD), Dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF), Dengue shock syndrome (DSS), Neglected tropical diseases, Mosquito-borne diseases, Public health, Systematic review

Introduction

Dengue, transmitted primarily by Aedes mosquito vectors, is one of the most important mosquito-borne viral infections worldwide, with a rapidly increasing global burden and more than half of the world's population at risk [1,2]. In 2024, more than 14 million illnesses and 10,000 dengue-related deaths were reported, marking a historic high and significantly worsening the disease's burden worldwide [3] (see Fig. 1). It is noteworthy that dengue is no longer a disease confined primarily to tropical and subtropical regions but is increasingly emerging in mainland Europe, where several autochthonous cases have recently been reported in France, Italy, and Spain [4,5]. This expanding geographic distribution reflects the combined effects of climate change, urbanization, and increased population mobility, raising growing concerns about the spread of vector-borne diseases in previously non-endemic regions [6].

Prevalence of non-communicable disease (diabetes) and communicable disease (dengue) worldwide in 2024 [3,11].

The WHO 2009 classification categorized dengue into dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, and severe dengue, replacing the former entities of dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS) used under the 1997 framework [7,8]. Severe dengue is characterized by severe plasma leakage, severe hemorrhage, severe organ dysfunction, and increased mortality risk [8]. The 2018 refinement proposed by Tomashek and colleagues [9] further emphasizes the central role of severe plasma leakage in the pathophysiology of severe disease. Despite improvements in supportive care, dengue continues to represent a major public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where healthcare resources may be limited [10].

At the same time, the global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) has risen dramatically, with approximately 589 million adults affected in 2024 [11] (see Fig. 1). DM is characterized by chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired immune responses, and metabolic dysregulation, mechanisms that may worsen dengue outcomes by promoting vascular permeability, exaggerated inflammation, thrombocytopenia, and multi-organ dysfunction [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Dengue virus infection itself induces significant metabolic reprogramming of host cells, which may further dysregulate immune responses in individuals with pre-existing metabolic disease such as DM [16]. Although observational studies suggest that DM is associated with increased risk of severe dengue and dengue-related mortality [[17], [18], [19]] the available evidence remains heterogeneous due to differences in study design, patient populations, dengue severity definitions, and methods of DM ascertainment. Clarifying this association is clinically relevant, as diabetic patients may require earlier recognition, closer monitoring, and tailored management during dengue infection.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize the available evidence on the association between pre-existing DM (exposure) and severe dengue or dengue-related mortality (outcomes) in patients with laboratory-confirmed dengue infection (population), compared with non-diabetic dengue patients (comparator), across observational studies (study design). By examining the interplay between these two conditions, this review seeks to inform clinical triage strategies, identify early prognostic factors, and support preventive efforts in regions where both diseases represent a significant public health threat.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [20]. The review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO and no formal review protocol was prepared or published prior to study conduct. However, the review methodology, including eligibility criteria, search strategy, data extraction, and statistical analyses, was predefined and applied consistently throughout.

Search strategy and article identification

Two authors (JL & SV) independently searched PubMed, Scopus, and Embase to identify relevant articles published from January 1, 1987 until January 31, 2025. Manual searches of the reference lists of the pertinent systematic reviews previously published on this subject were also performed to identify any additional relevant articles.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree terms (for Embase), and free-text keywords related to dengue and diabetes, using Boolean operators (AND, OR).

The full search strategies for all three databases are provided in Supplementary File S1. Rayyan, a centralized platform, was used for title/abstract screening (https://www.rayyan.ai/). Any conflicts were resolved by discussion and consensus.

Eligibility and study selection

Using PICO framework, the research question guiding the study selection was: “Is the presence of diabetes mellitus associated with an increased risk of severe dengue or dengue-related mortality compared to non-diabetic dengue patients, and what is the magnitude of this association across observational studies?”. Eligible studies included original observational studies (retrospective or prospective cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies) reporting dengue outcomes in patients with and without DM. Only peer-reviewed full-text articles published in English from January 1, 1987 until January 31, 2025 were considered eligible. Grey literature was not systematically searched. Studies published only in abstract form, correction articles, review articles and meta-analyses, preprints, case reports, editorials, clinical trial protocols, conference proceedings, non-observational studies and studies that involved non-human experiments and no-full text available were excluded from further assessment in the evaluation.

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by two authors (JL & SV) using a pre-defined, standardized Microsoft Excel 365 spreadsheet. For each study included, the following information was extracted: first author, publication year, country, study period, study design, sample size, age and sex distribution of participants, severe dengue classification (WHO 1997 or WHO 2009 criteria), contemporaneous national DM prevalence estimates from IDF (closest available year to the study period), study-level DM prevalence, and reported effect estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer (AD) when necessary.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [21], which evaluates the study quality across three domains: selection (maximum 4 stars), comparability (maximum 2 stars), and outcome (maximum 3 stars), for a total maximum score of 9. Studies were categorized as good (≥8), fair (5-7), or poor quality (<5). Quality assessment was performed independently by two investigators (JL & SV), and discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus.

Main outcome

The primary outcomes were: (i) severe dengue, defined according to the classification system used in each included study (WHO 1997 or WHO 2009); and (ii) dengue-related mortality.

Synthesis methods

Studies were grouped according to the predefined outcomes of interest, severe dengue and dengue-related mortality. Studies were eligible for quantitative synthesis if they reported effect estimates (odds ratios (OR), relative risks (RR), adjusted odds ratios (aOR) conditional odds ratios (cOR), or sufficient raw data to derive an effect estimate) for the association between DM and the outcome of interest. Studies providing data for both outcomes contributed to both syntheses independently. Studies lacking sufficient quantitative data were included only in the qualitative synthesis.

Study characteristics and extracted data were summarized in structured tables, and individual as well as pooled effect estimates were visually presented using forest plots. Funnel plots and Galbraith plots were used to explore small-study effects and potential outliers visually.

The data were analyzed by comparing two groups with binary outcomes and the effect estimate was the ORs derived from raw data. To ensure a symmetric distribution and stabilize variance, ORs and their corresponding standard errors were converted into natural log-transformed values (log OR) prior to pooling. All statistical syntheses and inverse-variance weightings were performed on this additive log scale. Final pooled estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were back-transformed to be presented as standard ORs for clinical interpretation, in accordance with recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [22]. The random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method was used to estimate the pooled effect sizes. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and homogeneity by the Chi‐square Q test [23]. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata software (Stata Corp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Statistical analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates.

Cumulative meta-analysis ordered by study size was performed to assess the stability of pooled estimates as evidence accumulated.

Leave-one-out analysis. Each study was sequentially removed from the pooled analysis to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect size [24].

Bias assessment

Potential publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Begg's and Egger's tests for small-study effects, applied separately for each outcome [25,26].

Meta-regression and subgroup analyses

Potential heterogeneity across studies was examined using univariable meta-regressions and subgroup analyses. We considered a range of study-level and country-level variables. Study-level factors included age criteria (e.g., inclusion or exclusion of individuals under 14 years), the proportion of female participants, study design, year of publication, and geographic. For the severe dengue outcome, studies were stratified according to the classification system used, WHO 1997 (which priorities severe haemmorrhage) versus WHO 2009 (which places severe plasma leakage as central to pathophysiology). This analysis explored whether the definition of severe dengue used modifies the association with DM, and whether DM may differentially affect haemorrhagic versus plasma leakage components of severe dengue pathophysiology.

At the country-level, we assessed health and socioeconomic indicators, including income level, percentage of health expenditure in relation to gross domestic product and DM mortality rate (only for dengue-related mortality outcome). Moreover, studies were stratified according to whether their reported study-level DM prevalence fell below or above the contemporaneous national DM prevalence estimate.

Results

Studies selection

A total of 1452 records from different databases were identified and imported for screening. After removal of 543 duplicates, 909 records were screened by titles and abstracts, resulting in 89 articles assessed for full-text eligibility. In parallel, an additional eight studies were identified through manual screening of reference lists from previously published systematic reviews and were also assessed for eligibility. Following full-text assessment, 27 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in our systematic review. We adhered to reporting and guidance based on the PRISMA (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of the literature search and selection included in the systematic review.

Studies characteristics and quality

Twenty-seven articles meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis. The general characteristics of the selected studies are shown in Table 1. All included studies were observational, encompassing retrospective cohorts (13/27), case-control studies (9/27), prospective (2/27) and cross-sectional analyses (3/27). Studies were conducted across diverse geographic regions, including Asia (two studies in Malaysia; three in India; four each in Singapore and Pakistan; nine in Taiwan) [14,[28], [29], [30],[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39],[41], [42], [43], [44],[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]], Latin America (one in Brazil; two in Mexico; one in Mexico, Brazil, and Colombia) [27,31,40,45], and the Indian Ocean territories (one study in Réunion Island) [15]. The studies involved a total of 734,123 subjects ranging from 50 to 678,836 participants, of whom 4925 had DM. Around 44.5% of the studies included were conducted during the last decade. Dengue severity was classified according to the WHO 1997 criteria (DHF or DSS) [7] or WHO 2009 criteria (SD) [8]. Ten studies adopted the WHO 1997 classification, eight used WHO 2009 classification, six used both, and three did not report the criteria used. In most included studies, dengue infection was confirmed by the presence of dengue virus RNA by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen test and/or IgM, IgG antibodies. In this review, the term diabetes mellitus (DM) refers to all types of DM, as most included studies did not specify the type. DM diagnosis was based on information recorded in medical records or on self-reported diagnoses. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Overall, 41% of the studies achieved a score of 8, while most scored between 6 and 7. Among studies with fair-quality ratings, the most common limitation was inadequate reporting of control selection (Supplementary Table S2).

Studies objectives

DM as an independent risk factor for severe dengue

Overall, 22 studies were included in the meta-analysis evaluating the effect of DM on severe dengue (Supplemental Table S3A). The pooled random-effects analysis demonstrated a statistically significant, with an overall OR of 2.66 (95% CI: 1.90-3.73; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of pooled analysis of studies evaluating the association between DM and severe dengue (SD).

Assessment of heterogeneity, robustness, and bias. Given the significant heterogeneity (I2 = 91.1%), we conducted extensive analyses to probe its sources and test the robustness of the primary finding. Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed asymmetry, suggesting the potential for publication bias or small-study effects (Supplemental Figure S1A, contained in S4 File), supported by a significant Egger's regression test (p = 0.002; Begg's test p = 0.071). A Galbraith plot further identified specific outlier studies contributing to the heterogeneity (Supplemental Figure S1B). Cumulative meta-analysis ordered by increasing study size illustrated that while smaller studies reported widely variable and often inflated effects, the point estimate stabilized and converged towards the final pooled estimate as larger, more precise studies were incorporated into the model. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the overall result. The recalculated pooled OR remained statistically significant throughout, ranging from 2.40 (95% CI: 1.75-3.29) to 2.83 (95% CI: 1.97-4.06) upon omission of any single study (Supplemental Figure S2A-S2.B, S4 File).

Meta-regression and subgroup analyses. Univariable random-effects meta-regression was performed to identify potential sources of the heterogeneity (Table 2). We found that geographic area was a significant modifier of the effect size (p = 0.013), with studies conducted in Asian countries reported a significantly stronger association (OR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.16-3.52) than those from Latin America. Study design also significantly influenced the estimated effect; case-control and cross-sectional analyses yielded significantly lower effect estimates than retrospective cohort studies (OR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.20-0.79 and OR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17-0.71, respectively). No significant differences were observed with respect to other study-level characteristics, including age restrictions, mean age, or proportion of female participants, nor to national-level characteristics such as income level, diabetes mortality, nor to publication year. Consistent findings were observed in subgroup analyses (Supplemental Figs. S3–S5, S4 File). The severe dengue classification system did not significantly modify the association between DM and severe dengue. However, studies using WHO 2009 showed a non-significant trend toward higher effect estimates compared with studies using WHO 1997 (OR: 1.77, 95% CI: 0.83-3.76; p = 0.141), whereas studies incorporating both classifications showed intermediate estimates (Supplemental Figs. S6 and S4 File). Moreover, exploratory comparisons between contemporaneous national DM prevalence estimates and study-level DM prevalence suggested possible heterogeneity in DM ascertainment across studies. Several Latin American cohorts reported markedly lower DM prevalence than expected from national estimates, whereas some Asian hospital-based studies reported substantially higher DM prevalence than national estimates, suggesting possible enrichment of diabetic patients through selection bias. Subgroup analysis according to the ratio between study and national DM prevalence showed variability in effect estimates across underrepresented, representative, and overrepresented groups, although the association between DM and severe dengue remained consistently positive (Supplemental Figs. S7 and S4 File). Despite accounting for these variables, considerable residual heterogeneity persisted within subgroups, indicating that other unmeasured factors, such as differences in clinical practice, dengue serotypes, or DM management, likely contribute to the observed variability in effect estimates.

DM as a risk factor for mortality in dengue patients

Fifteen studies were included in the meta-analysis evaluating the effect of DM on mortality in dengue patients (Supplemental Table S3B). The pooled random-effects model demonstrated a statistically significant association, with an overall OR of 3.95 (95% CI: 2.98-5.23; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). A moderate level of heterogeneity was observed among the study results (I2 = 44.64%; p = 0.03) [23], suggesting a greater consistency of effect across studies for mortality than for severe dengue.

Forest plot of pooled analysis of studies evaluating the association between DM and mortality in dengue patients.

Assessment of robustness and bias. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplemental Figure S8A, contained in S5 File) showed mild asymmetry, less pronounced than in the severe dengue analysis. A Galbraith plot identified the study by Karunakaran et al. (2014) [46] as a potential outlier contributing most to the observed heterogeneity (Supplemental Figure S8B, S5 File). The robustness of this finding was confirmed through sensitivity analyses. Cumulative meta-analysis ordered by increasing study size showed that the effect estimate stabilized early and remained consistently significant as larger studies were added to the model (Supplemental Figure S9A, S5 File). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed stability of the pooled OR, ranging from 3.71 to 4.28 upon omission of any single study (Supplemental Figure S9B, S5 File). In contrast to the analysis for severe dengue, no significant publication bias was detected, as indicated by a non-significant Egger's test (p = 0.295) and Begg's test (p = 1.000).

Meta-regression and subgroup analyses. Univariable meta-regression and subgroup analyses were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity (Table 3).

Geographic region served as a significant effect-measure modifier. The association was significantly stronger in Latin America studies (OR: 5.24, 95% CI: 3.71-7.40) than in Asia studies (OR: 2.92, 95% CI: 2.11-4.04; subgroup difference p = 0.02) (Supplemental Figs. S10 and S5 File). This geographic disparity was confirmed by the meta-regression (p = 0.010) (Table 3). Furthermore, the mortality burden related to DM in the country of the study also emerged as a significant modifier of the effect size. Studies conducted in countries with higher diabetes-related mortality demonstrated stronger associations between DM and dengue mortality than studies from lower-mortality settings (Supplemental Fig. S11, Table 3), suggesting that broader population-level diabetes burden and healthcare context may influence dengue outcomes. The WHO severe dengue classification system did not significantly modify mortality estimates, although studies using WHO 1997criteria tended to report slightly higher effect sizes than those using WHO 2009 definitions (Supplemental Figs. S12 and S5 File). Similarly, subgroup analysis based on comparison between study-level and national DM prevalence showed some variability in mortality estimates across underrepresented, representative, and overrepresented groups, but the association between DM and dengue-related mortality remained consistently elevated (Supplemental Figs. S13 and S5 File). Despite accounting for these variables, a moderate level of residual heterogeneity remained, indicating that other unmeasured factors also contribute to the observed outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that pre-existing DM is significantly associated with both severe dengue and dengue-related mortality. Compared with non-diabetic dengue patients, individuals with DM had higher odds of severe dengue (OR: 2.66, 95% CI: 1.90-3.73) and dengue-related mortality (OR: 3.95, 95% CI: 2.98-5.23). These findings are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that DM is associated with worse dengue outcomes, including severe dengue and mortality [17,39,52]. The biological plausibility of this association is supported by several interconnected mechanisms. Importantly, the risk appears to be influenced not only by the presence of DM itself but also by the degree of glycemic control. Among diabetic patients with confirmed dengue, suboptimal glycaemic control (HbA1c > 7%) has been associated with a higher risk of severe dengue compared with well-controlled DM (HbA1c < 7%) [32,53]. DM is characterized by chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired immune responses, and metabolic dysregulation [12,13], all of which may amplify the pathophysiological processes underlying severe dengue. In particular, endothelial dysfunction may predispose to severe plasma leakage, while dysregulated immune activation may contribute to excessive inflammatory responses, thrombocytopenia, organ dysfunction, and shock [54,55]. Our findings extend beyond clinical severity to critical outcomes. Consistent with previous literature [15,35,50], diabetic individuals were more likely to experience severe dengue, require ICU admission, and suffer in-hospital death. Differences in dengue severity definitions may also partly explain variability across studies. The 2009 WHO classification places greater emphasis on severe plasma leakage and organ dysfunction, whereas the 1997 classification focused more heavily on hemorrhagic manifestations [7,8]. Given the vascular and endothelial abnormalities associated with DM, this distinction may be clinically relevant. Although our meta-regression did not identify a statistically significant difference, studies using the WHO 2009 classification tended to report higher effect estimates. Interpretation of these findings requires consideration of the limited comparability across included studies. Substantial heterogeneity was observed, particularly for the severe dengue outcome, likely reflecting important differences in study design, patient selection, healthcare settings, and case definitions. In addition, diabetes ascertainment was inconsistent, ranging from documented medical history to self-report or unspecified chart review, introducing potential misclassification. Some studies reported markedly lower DM prevalence than expected from contemporaneous national estimates [28,40], suggesting possible underdiagnosis or underreporting. Conversely, some hospital-based cohorts which reported substantially higher DM prevalence than national estimates enriched for more severe disease through selection bias [32,33,35]. Together, these factors likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting pooled estimates. A notable finding was the regional divergence between severe dengue and mortality outcomes: while the association between DM and severe dengue appeared stronger in Asian cohorts, mortality risk remained elevated in both Asia and Latin America and was numerically higher in Latin America. This may reflect differences in healthcare system capacity, access to critical care, case management practices, circulating dengue serotypes, or baseline DM management, with important implications for regional resource allocation.

Beyond disease pathophysiology, therapeutic and preventive considerations also deserve attention. Limited evidence suggests that metformin may reduce the risk of severe dengue in diabetic patients [56]. Beyond glycemic control, metformin has demonstrated anti-inflammatory, endothelial-protective, and potential antiviral effects through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which may impair dengue viral replication in experimental models [57,58]. However, clinical evidence remains inconclusive, as the MeDO trial did not demonstrate clear virological or clinical benefit [59].

The implications of these findings may also extend to dengue prevention strategies. Because DM appears to identify a higher-risk population, dengue vaccination may represent a promising preventive approach. However, important uncertainties remain, particularly in dengue-naïve individuals, where vaccine-induced antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) has historically raised safety concerns. Specifically, CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia®) has been associated with an increased risk of severe dengue in seronegative recipients, likely due to immune enhancement mechanisms resembling natural secondary infection [60]. Although no comparable ADE signal has been conclusively demonstrated for TAK-003 (QDENGA®), efficacy has varied by serotype and longer-term safety data remain under evaluation [61,62]. To date, no studies have specifically assessed vaccine safety or efficacy in diabetic populations, representing an important evidence gap.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that diabetic patients presenting with suspected dengue should be considered a higher-risk population requiring early diagnosis, closer monitoring, and careful hemodynamic and metabolic management, particularly older individuals with multiple comorbidities [63].

This study has several limitations. First, all included studies were observational, limiting causal inference and increasing susceptibility to residual confounding. Second, substantial heterogeneity was present, particularly for the severe dengue outcome, despite subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Third, diabetes ascertainment was inconsistent across studies and often based on medical records or self-reported diagnoses, while information on glycemic control and diabetes severity was rarely available. Fourth, some included studies were conducted in selected hospital-based populations, potentially limiting generalizability to broader dengue populations. Finally, a formal assessment of certainty in the body of evidence (e.g., using GRADE) was not performed, limiting the ability to quantify overall confidence in the pooled estimates.

Overall, our findings support DM as an important risk factor for adverse dengue outcomes and highlight the need for improved risk stratification, targeted management strategies, and further research focused on preventive interventions in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that pre-existing diabetes mellitus is associated with a significantly increased risk of both severe dengue and dengue-related mortality. These findings support the recognition of DM as an important clinical risk factor in dengue assessment and triage, particularly in endemic settings with a high burden of metabolic disease. Given the observational nature and heterogeneity of the available evidence, further prospective studies are needed to better define the role of glycemic control, antidiabetic therapies, and preventive strategies, including vaccination, in this high-risk population.
