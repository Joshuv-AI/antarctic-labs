The triglyceride to HDL‑C ratio explains part of the association between fatty liver and incident type 2 diabetes: a statistical mediation analysis of the NAGALA cohort
Authors: Bin Zhang, Xusheng Chen, Zhaolong Zhan, Yuanguo Li, Xiaosong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFatty liver is associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). However, the mediating role of the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio (THR) in this association remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of fatty liver on diabetes risk and explore the mediating effect and predictive performance of THR.MethodsThis secondary analysis included 15,453 non-diabetic participants from the NAGALA cohort. Fatty liver was diagnosed using ultrasonography, and T2DM was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 7 mmol/L, hemoglobin A1c level ≥ 6.5%, or self‑report. THR was calculated as triglycerides (mmol/L) divided by HDL-C (mmol/L). Cox regression, mediation analysis, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 5.4 years, 373 (2.4%) participants developed diabetes. Fatty liver was associated with a significantly increased risk of diabetes [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 2.88; 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.22–3.73; P < 0.001]. The association was more pronounced in women and younger individuals (< 45 years). Fatty liver was also significantly associated with a higher THR (adjusted β 0.35; P < 0.001). Compared with the lowest THR quartile (< 0.30), the highest quartile (> 0.90) showed an adjusted HR of 2.08 (95% CI 1.27–3.40) for diabetes. THR mediated 11.63% (95% CI 6.80–19.58%) of the fatty liver–T2DM association, with significant mediation in men but not in women. THR yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.75 (95% CI 0.72–0.77) for predicting diabetes, with a sensitivity of 69.71% and specificity of 68.58% at an optimal cutoff value of 0.75.ConclusionsFatty liver is significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes, and THR statistically explains part of this association, particularly in men. THR demonstrates moderate predictive value and may serve as a useful biomarker for risk stratification in patients with diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05021-y.Keywords: Fatty liver, Triglyceride to HDL-C ratio, Type 2 diabetes, Mediation analysis, Predictive performance

Introduction

Fatty liver, characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation, has emerged as a highly prevalent condition worldwide and is closely associated with the rising epidemic of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1, 2]. Accumulating evidence from prospective cohort studies indicates that fatty liver is not only a consequence of insulin resistance but also an independent risk factor for the development of T2DM, with affected individuals exhibiting a two-to three-fold higher risk than those without fatty liver [3, 4]. Despite this well-established association, the underlying mechanisms linking fatty liver to incident diabetes remain incompletely understood, and identifying modifiable mediators is crucial for improving risk stratification and intervention strategies.

The triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio (THR) has gained attention as a simple, cost-effective marker of insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia [5, 6]. Elevated THR reflects a lipid profile characterized by hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL-C, which are commonly observed in individuals with fatty liver and are closely linked to ectopic fat deposition, systemic inflammation, and impaired glucose metabolism [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that THR is associated with the risk of T2DM [9, 10]; however, whether THR statistically explains part of the association between fatty liver and diabetes and to what extent has not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, the predictive performance of THR for incident diabetes in the context of fatty liver disease remains underexplored.

Sex differences in the pathophysiology of both fatty liver and T2DM are well recognized, with men typically showing a higher prevalence of fatty liver and a more adverse metabolic profile than premenopausal women [11, 12]. Whether the mediating role of THR differs by sex has not been addressed in previous studies, representing an important gap in understanding the sex-specific mechanisms underlying the fatty liver–diabetes relationship. Therefore, using a large, well-characterized longitudinal cohort, this study aimed to (1) evaluate the independent association between fatty liver and incident T2DM, (2) quantify the mediating effect of THR on this association, overall and by sex, and (3) assess the predictive performance of THR for diabetes risk.

Methods

Data source and participants

The data used for this secondary analysis were obtained from a public database (Dryad) provided by Okamura et al. (https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.8q0p192), which allows users to freely download the data [13]. In accordance with Dryad’s terms of service and the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial (CC BY-NC 4.0) license, these data may be used for secondary studies without infringing on the authors’ rights. As a follow-up study based on nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in the Gifu Area Longitudinal Analysis (NAGALA) database, the original study identified participants from the medical examination program at Murakami Memorial Hospital (2004–2015) and followed them for the development of T2DM [13]. The original study included 15,464 participants after applying the following exclusion criteria: medication usage at enrollment (n = 2,321), missing data (n = 863), excessive ethanol consumption (> 60 g/day for men or > 40 g/day for women; n = 739), known liver disease (n = 416), T2DM at enrollment (n = 323), and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 6.1 mmol/L (n = 808). In the present secondary analysis, we excluded 11 participants with missing baseline HDL-C data, resulting in a final sample of 15,453 participants.

Variables and outcomes

The NAGALA database systematically collects medical records and lifestyle information using a standardized approach, as detailed in previous studies [13, 14]. It integrates multidimensional baseline clinical measurements, including liver function markers [alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)], lipid profile parameters [total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and glucose parameters [FPG, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)], along with anthropometric measurements such as blood pressure (BP), body weight, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference. Standardized lifestyle assessments through questionnaires covered alcohol consumption (classified as none/minimal: <40 g/week; light: 40–140 g/week; moderate: 140–280 g/week; heavy: >280 g/week), smoking status (never smoker, past smoker, current smoker), and exercise habits [15]. The database includes baseline fatty liver diagnosis, longitudinal follow-up duration, and incident T2DM events as key outcome variables. In this study, the diagnosis of fatty liver at baseline was established through abdominal ultrasonography conducted by trained technicians and was regarded as the exposure, whereas T2DM was defined as FPG ≥ 7 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or self-report and served as the outcome [16, 17]. THR was treated as a mediator and calculated as triglycerides (mmol/L) divided by HDL-C (mmol/L). No missing values were observed for any of the variables in the present study.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and EmpowerStats (version 5.2; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA). Following normality testing (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), descriptive statistics were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. For group comparisons between individuals with and without fatty liver disease at baseline, as well as between those with and without incident diabetes at the end of follow-up, appropriate statistical tests were applied: the independent samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for skewed continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The independent impact of fatty liver on diabetes risk was assessed using Cox proportional hazard models. The proportional hazards assumption for this model was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; the test for fatty liver yielded χ² = 0.4324 (P = 0.511), indicating no violations. Both unadjusted and fully adjusted models were applied, with the latter adjusting for age, sex, BMI, waist circumference, BP, exercise habits, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and laboratory findings (ALT, AST, GGT, HDL-C, total cholesterol, and triglycerides). The selection of confounding variables for adjustment was based on the previous literature and clinical knowledge [18–24]. Subgroup analyses were performed using stratified Cox proportional hazards regression models stratified by sex, age, BMI, waist circumference, BP, and HbA1c. Continuous variables were categorized based on clinical cutoffs: age (< 45 vs. ≥45 years), BMI (< 24, 24–28, and ≥ 28 kg/m²), BP (systolic < 140 and diastolic < 90 mmHg vs. systolic ≥ 140 or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg), and HbA1c (< 5.7% vs. ≥5.7%) [17, 25]. All covariates from the fully adjusted models were included, except for the stratification variables. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to illustrate the cumulative incidence of diabetes across the THR quartiles (Q) during follow-up, with comparisons made using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both higher versus lowest THR quartiles and per 1-SD increment in the THR. For the THR models, the proportional hazards assumption was also tested; the test for THR yielded χ² = 1.2834 (P = 0.257), confirming that the assumption was held. Additionally, we applied a generalized additive model to examine the non-linear association between THR and diabetes risk. Linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between fatty liver and triglycerides, HDL-C, and THR, with or without adjustment for relevant covariates (the same variables in the fully adjusted models except triglycerides and HDL-C). We conducted a mediation analysis using the R mediation package (counterfactual framework, linear regression for THR, probit regression for diabetes, and 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples). This analysis quantified the proportional contribution of THR to the association between fatty liver and diabetes risk, adjusting for the same covariates as in the linear regression analysis. Finally, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the predictive performance of THR and other laboratory parameters for diabetes risk, as quantified by the area under the curve (AUC).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 15,453 non-diabetic participants were included in the analysis, of whom 2,737 (17.7%) had fatty liver and 12,716 (82.3%) did not. The baseline characteristics stratified by the presence or absence of fatty liver disease are summarized in Table 1. Participants with fatty liver were more likely to be male than those without fatty liver (82.27% vs. 48.53%, P < 0.001). They also had significantly higher median values for weight (71.40 vs. 57.20 kg), waist circumference (85.50 vs. 74.00 cm), BMI (25.08 vs. 21.21 kg/m²), systolic BP (122.50 vs. 111.50 mmHg), and diastolic BP (77.50 vs. 69.50 mmHg) (all P < 0.001). With regard to lifestyle factors, the proportion of participants who engaged in regular exercise was lower in the fatty liver group than in the non-fatty liver group (14.65% vs. 18.13%, P < 0.001). The fatty liver group also had a higher proportion of past or current smokers (55.28% vs. 38.64%, P < 0.001). Although the alcohol consumption patterns differed significantly between the two groups (P = 0.031), the overall difference was modest. The laboratory findings revealed marked differences between the two groups. Participants with fatty liver had higher median levels of ALT (27.00 vs. 15.00 U/L), AST (21.00 vs. 17.00 U/L), GGT (23.00 vs. 14.00 IU/L), total cholesterol (5.43 vs. 4.99 mmol/L), triglycerides (1.25 vs. 0.67 mmol/L), FPG (5.38 vs. 5.11 mmol/L), HbA1c (5.30% vs. 5.10%), and THR (1.08 vs. 0.44), whereas median HDL‑C levels were lower (1.15 vs. 1.48 mmol/L). All differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001 for each comparison). Among all participants, those who later developed T2DM showed significantly higher levels of triglycerides (median: 1.21 vs. 0.72 mmol/L, P < 0.001) and THR (median: 1.09 vs. 0.50, P < 0.001) and significantly lower HDL-C levels (median: 1.13 vs. 1.42 mmol/L, P < 0.001; Table S1, Fig. 1).

Stratified analyses of the association between fatty liver and diabetes risk

Over a median follow-up period of 5.4 years (IQR 2.7–9.4), 373 (2.4%) participants developed T2DM. The association between fatty liver and the risk of developing T2DM was further examined across subgroups stratified by sex, age, BMI, BP, and HbA1c (Table 2). HRs with 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, with adjustments for potential confounders, except for the stratification variable itself. In the overall cohort, fatty liver was associated with a significantly increased risk of diabetes after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR: 2.88; 95% CI: 2.22–3.73; P < 0.001). This association was more pronounced in women (adjusted HR: 4.15; 95% CI: 2.37–7.25; P < 0.001) than in men (adjusted HR: 2.51; 95% CI: 1.87–3.36; P < 0.001). When stratified by age, the association remained significant in both younger (< 45 years) and older (≥ 45 years) participants, with adjusted HRs of 3.46 (95% CI: 2.21–5.41; P < 0.001) and 2.69 (95% CI: 1.92–3.76; P < 0.001), respectively. In the analyses stratified by BMI, fatty liver was associated with an increased risk of diabetes across all BMI categories, with adjusted HRs of 2.74 (95% CI: 1.84–4.07; P < 0.001) for BMI < 24.0 kg/m², 2.91 (95% CI: 1.90–4.46; P < 0.001) for BMI 24.0–28.0 kg/m², and 2.52 (95% CI: 1.15–5.54; P = 0.022) for BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m². Stratification by BP status showed that fatty liver was significantly associated with T2DM risk among participants with normal BP (systolic < 140 mmHg and diastolic < 90 mmHg; adjusted HR, 2.85; 95% CI, 2.16–3.77; P < 0.001). Similarly, among those with elevated blood pressure (systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg), the association was also significant (adjusted HR: 2.70; 95% CI: 1.28–5.70; P = 0.009). Finally, when stratified by baseline HbA1c levels, fatty liver was associated with a significantly increased risk of T2DM in both the HbA1c < 5.7% (adjusted HR: 2.52; 95% CI: 1.82–3.49; P < 0.001) and HbA1c ≥ 5.7% groups (adjusted HR: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.23–3.03; P = 0.004).

Relationships of fatty liver with triglycerides, HDL-C, and THR

Table 3 presents the regression coefficients for the relationship between fatty liver and lipid parameters, including triglycerides, HDL-C, and THR, using linear regression models. In the unadjusted analysis, the presence of fatty liver was significantly associated with higher triglyceride levels (β: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.64–0.69; P < 0.001), lower HDL-C levels (β: -0.33; 95% CI: -0.35 to -0.31; P < 0.001), and higher THR (β: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.73–0.79; P < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, BP, lifestyle factors, and relevant laboratory parameters, the associations remained statistically significant but were attenuated: the β coefficients for fatty liver were 0.30 (95% CI: 0.27–0.32; P < 0.001) for triglycerides, -0.11 (95% CI: -0.12 to -0.09; P = 0.005) for HDL-C, and 0.35 (95% CI: 0.32–0.39; P < 0.001) for THR.

Relationship between THR and diabetes risk

The relationship between THR and the risk of developing T2DM was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with THR analyzed as quartiles and per SD increase (Table 4). The number of diabetes cases increased progressively across the THR quartiles, from 22 (0.57%) in Q1 (< 0.30) to 47 (1.22%) in Q2 (0.30–0.50), 82 (2.12%) in Q3 (0.51–0.90), and 222 (5.74%) in Q4 (> 0.90), with a significant trend (P < 0.001). As depicted in Fig. 2, the Kaplan-Meier curve demonstrated that the cumulative risk of incident diabetes varied significantly across the THR quartiles, showing a gradual upward trend and reaching a maximum in Q4 (log rank P < 0.001). When Q4 was compared with Q1, participants in Q4 had a significantly higher risk of developing T2DM. In the unadjusted analysis, the HR was 7.99 (95% CI: 5.15–12.38; P for trend < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment for potential confounders, the association remained statistically significant, with an adjusted HR of 2.08 (95% CI: 1.27–3.40; P for trend < 0.001). When THR was analyzed as a continuous variable, each 1‑SD (0.75) increase was associated with a significantly increased risk of diabetes in both the unadjusted (HR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.29–1.37) and adjusted models (HR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.14–1.28). In a sensitivity analysis using quartiles of natural log-transformed THR, the HRs for each quartile were identical to those from the raw THR quartiles (Table S2), confirming the robustness of our findings. The per‑SD (0.79) HR for log THR increased from 1.21 to 1.49 after adjustment, reflecting the stabilization of variance and a multiplicative interpretation on the log scale. After controlling for age, sex, BMI, waist circumference, BP, exercise habits, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and laboratory findings (ALT, AST, GGT, total cholesterol, FPG, and HbA1c), THR showed a significant nonlinear relationship with diabetes risk, characterized by a progressively ascending slope as THR increased (P < 0.001, Fig. 3).

Mediation analyses

Mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the extent to which THR mediated the association between fatty liver and diabetes risk (Table 5). In the overall population (n = 15,453), the total effect of fatty liver on T2DM risk was 0.018 (95% CI: 0.012–0.025; P < 0.001), with a significant mediated effect through THR of 0.002 (95% CI: 0.001–0.003; P < 0.001), corresponding to a mediated proportion of 11.63% (95% CI: 6.80–19.58%; P < 0.001). When stratified by sex, the mediated effect was significant in males (n = 8,419; mediated effect: 0.003, 95% CI: 0.001–0.004; mediated proportion: 12.99%, 95% CI: 6.90–24.66%; P < 0.001), whereas in females (n = 7,034), the mediated effect did not reach statistical significance (mediated effect: 0.001, 95% CI: -0.001 to 0.002; mediated proportion: 5.19%, 95% CI: -3.14% to 19.10%; P = 0.172).

Discussion

Main findings

In this large population-based cohort of 15,453 non-diabetic individuals, fatty liver diagnosed by ultrasonography was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of incident T2DM (adjusted HR = 2.88). This association was more pronounced in women and younger adults (< 45 years). Notably, THR statistically explained part of the fatty liver–T2DM relationship, accounting for 11.63% of the total effect. However, significant mediation was observed only in men and not in women. Furthermore, THR showed a significant non-linear positive association with T2DM risk. The predictive performance was moderate (AUC = 0.75). These findings suggest that THR not only statistically explains part of the fatty liver–diabetes association but also holds potential as a practical biomarker for risk stratification in patients with diabetes.

Interpretation

Fatty liver was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of incident T2DM (adjusted HR 2.88). This finding is consistent with the findings of previous large-scale cohort studies. For example, the Framingham Heart Study reported a directionally consistent association with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.98 for diabetes in individuals with fatty liver after adjustment for traditional risk factors [7]. The comparable effect sizes between the two studies indicate that the association between fatty liver and diabetes risk is robust and generalizable to the general population. The study populations differed: Framingham was community-based with predominantly White participants, whereas NAGALA was a Japanese cohort representing an East Asian population. The diagnostic methods also varied (computed tomography vs. ultrasonography); however, the findings remained consistent. This association may be primarily driven by insulin resistance. Insulin resistance is a central pathophysiological feature of both fatty liver and T2DM, promoting hepatic glucose production and impairing peripheral glucose uptake [26]. In addition, lipotoxicity resulting from excessive hepatic triglyceride accumulation leads to pancreatic β cell dysfunction and systemic insulin resistance [27]. More broadly, a constellation of interrelated mechanisms contributes to this association [28–31]. These include hepatic insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, altered hepatokine secretion, and ectopic fat deposition in insulin-sensitive tissues. Collectively, these systemic metabolic derangements induced by fatty liver, such as dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, and increased release of proinflammatory cytokines, further promote the development of diabetes [32].

The partial mediation of THR in linking fatty liver to T2DM suggests a complex interplay between hepatic lipid dysregulation, sex-specific metabolic pathways, and additional pathophysiological mechanisms. Notably, mediation was significant in men but not in women. This sex difference may reflect distinct biological mechanisms. However, the lower statistical power in women (87 incident diabetes cases vs. 286 in men) should also be considered. First, the THR reflects hepatic lipid overflow, a hallmark of fatty liver. This drives ectopic fat deposition in insulin-sensitive tissues, such as the skeletal muscle and pancreas [33]. Elevated hepatic triglyceride levels promote very low-density lipoprotein secretion, raising plasma triglyceride levels. Impaired reverse cholesterol transport or hepatocyte dysfunction lowers HDL-C [34]. This dysregulation, captured by THR, directly contributes to lipotoxicity and insulin resistance, which are proximal causes of T2DM [27]. However, other pathways beyond THR (e.g., inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction) probably contribute to diabetes risk independently of THR [35–38]. Second, sex differences in the hormonal milieu may play a role. In men, androgenic effects favor visceral fat accumulation, which exacerbates lipid dysregulation and elevates the THR [34]. In women, estrogen-mediated protection enhances HDL-C metabolism, reduces triglyceride levels, and buffers THR’s impact of THR [39]. Additionally, women exhibit greater metabolic flexibility, including preferential subcutaneous fat storage and augmented fatty acid oxidation [40]. These adaptations could redirect fatty liver pathophysiology toward non-lipid pathways (e.g., adipokine dysregulation or gut microbiome alterations). This may diminish THR’s explanatory power relative to men [41]. Third, genetic and epigenetic modifiers may influence the mediation effect. Sex‑specific polymorphisms in genes involved in lipid metabolism or insulin sensitivity could differentially modulate THR’s role between sexes [42]. Furthermore, lifestyle factors that are more prevalent in men (e.g., higher alcohol intake) may synergize with THR to amplify the risk of diabetes [43]. Fourth, the absence of significant mediation in women raises the possibility that other unmeasured mediators or confounders could contribute more prominently to the fatty liver–T2DM link in women [44, 45]. Examples include inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin‑6, tumor necrosis factor-α) and hormonal contraceptive use. Finally, the lack of significant mediation in women may be partially explained by the lower statistical power. Women had only 87 incident diabetes cases (1.24%) compared with 286 (3.40%) in men. Future studies with larger female cohorts or higher event rates are needed to determine whether the sex difference is truly biological or partly a power issue. In addition, studies incorporating longitudinal THR assessments and dynamic lipid profiling could further clarify these temporal and mechanistic relationships.

In this secondary analysis, we further examined the discriminative ability of the THR for incident diabetes. When used alone, THR had a low positive predictive value (PPV = 5.2%). This suggests limited utility as a standalone screening or diagnostic tool in a low‑incidence population (2.4% over 5.4 years). Nevertheless, the high negative predictive value (NPV = 98.9%) indicates that a low THR level effectively ruled out diabetes. This could be clinically useful for identifying individuals at a very low risk and avoiding unnecessary follow‑up testing. Furthermore, the addition of THR to FPG significantly improved the AUC from 0.805 to 0.825 (P < 0.0001). This finding indicates an incremental predictive value beyond that of FPG alone. Therefore, although THR alone is not sufficient for risk prediction in absolute terms, it may serve as a complementary marker in combination with other clinical variables, particularly for ruling out disease.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths. These include a large sample size, a median follow‑up of 5.4 years, and comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders. In addition, multiple analytical approaches were applied, such as Cox regression, mediation analysis, generalized additive models, and ROC curve analysis. Together, these methods provided a multidimensional assessment of the role of THR in the fatty liver–T2DM association. However, this study has several limitations. First, as a secondary analysis of existing data, this study is subject to the limitations of the original cohort. These include potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors (e.g., dietary habits, genetic predisposition, hepatokines, or other lipid indices). Specifically, participants who were on baseline antidiabetic or lipid-lowering medications, had excessive alcohol intake, had known liver disease, or had missing data were excluded. While these exclusions were necessary to minimize confounding factors and ensure a healthy reference group, they may have reduced the generalizability of our findings to broader clinical or community populations. Second, mortality data during follow-up were not available in the original dataset. Therefore, we could not account for the competing risk of death before the diagnosis of T2DM. Given the relatively young and healthy nature of the cohort (non‑diabetic at baseline, median follow‑up 5.4 years), any bias from competing events is likely minimal; however, this remains a limitation. Third, fatty liver was diagnosed using abdominal ultrasonography rather than liver biopsy, which may have introduced misclassification. Although ultrasonography is widely accepted in large-scale epidemiological studies, it cannot reliably detect mild steatosis, and its accuracy is operator-dependent. Consequently, some degree of non-differential misclassification is possible, which would probably bias the association. THR was derived only from baseline measurements, and repeated assessments during follow-up were not available. This precludes the analysis of time-varying mediation effects and may underrepresent the mediating role of THR during the follow-up period. Fourth, although mediation analysis assumes a temporal ordering of fatty liver, THR, and diabetes based on biological plausibility, the observational design cannot fully establish causality. Standard mediation assumptions also require no unmeasured confounding of the exposure mediator, mediator outcome, and exposure outcome relationships, as well as no measurement error in the mediator. While we adjusted for a wide range of covariates, residual confounding and single‑timepoint measurements of THR may still bias the indirect effect estimate. Therefore, the reported mediation effect should be interpreted as an associative finding and not as evidence of a causal pathway. Fifth, it is also acknowledged that the risk of type I error is increased by multiple subgroup analyses (by sex, age, BMI, blood pressure, and HbA1c). Consequently, subgroup results should be regarded as exploratory, and confirmation by independent studies is required. Finally, the generalizability of the findings is limited to Japanese adults who underwent health checkups. Whether these results extend to other ethnic groups, clinical populations, or community-based settings requires further investigation in diverse cohorts.

Conclusion

In summary, fatty liver shows a significant positive association with incident diabetes, and THR accounts for a portion of this association statistically, particularly in males. Moreover, THR demonstrates a nonlinear positive relationship with T2DM risk and exhibits moderate predictive ability. These findings suggest that THR may serve as a practical and accessible biomarker for T2DM risk stratification in individuals with fatty liver. Future prospective studies with repeated measurements and diverse populations are warranted to further clarify the mediating pathways and to validate the clinical utility of THR.
