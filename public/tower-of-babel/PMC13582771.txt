Impact of reduced 18F-MK6240 PET/MR acquisition duration on image quality and tau pathology assessment in patients with cognitive impairment
Authors: Kai Liao, Jia Li, Fangyang Jiao, Zhu Zhang, Lu Xu, Zhengjie Wang, Wenbo Li, Hua Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background¹⁸F-MK6240 tau positron emission tomography (PET) is critical in Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring. However, the standard 20-minute static acquisition poses challenges for cognitively impaired patients with limited tolerance for prolonged scans. This study evaluates the feasibility of reducing scan time while maintaining diagnostic accuracy through image quality and quantitative comparisons.Methods69 memory clinic patients (27 males and 42 females) who underwent 18F-MK6240 PET examinations were retrospectively analyzed. All 20-minutes scans were acquired 90 min post-injection of ~ 3.7 MBq/kg ¹⁸F-MK6240 and reconstructed mainly into 5-, 10-, 15-, and 20-minute datasets. Images were rated on a 5-point scale for overall quality, noise, and diagnostic confidence. Tau status (positive/negative) was the visually defined, and standardized uptake value ratios (SUVr) of Braak regions were calculated using cerebellar gray matter as reference. Agreement across durations was assessed via Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsAmong the 69 patients, 50 were classified as tau-positive and 19 as tau-negative. Diagnostic efficacy remained consistent across acquisition times compared to the 20-minute reference. The slight reduction in image quality for the 5-minute images relative to the other groups, but none of the images across any of the four durations were rated as poor (score < 3). For 16 predefined regions used for visual assessment of regional tau involvement, both readers showed near-perfect agreement in scoring the extent of involvement (0%, 1–25%, 26–75%, or > 75%) across the four scan durations, as evidenced by Kendall’s W coefficients ranging from 0.984 to 1.000 (all p < 0.001). Negative scans showed lower SUVr spread over Braak stages of four different time duration. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated high agreement in Braak-stage SUVr values between the 20-minute images and the shortened acquisitions with mean differences close to zero across all comparisons.ConclusionShortening ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET acquisition to 5 min on a modern high-sensitivity PET/MR system results in a small, clinically acceptable decline in image quality. It does not compromise the reliability of visual reads or the stability of semi-quantitative measures compared to the standard 20-minute scan. These findings support the feasibility of 5-minute protocols, which offer practical benefits like reduced motion artifacts and improved patient throughput, as an operationally efficient alternative in clinical tau imaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40658-026-00914-z.

Introduction

Tau, the most abundant microtubule-associated protein encoded by the microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT) gene on chromosome 17, binds to tubulin scaffolds and plays an essential role in stabilizing microtubules. Under pathological conditions, mutations in the coding regions of tau or disruptions in tau mRNA splicing, coupled with aberrant post-translational modifications and cellular stressors, concurrently promote tau aggregation and impair pathological tau clearance [1]. These alterations result in the assembly of tau monomers into cross-β-sheet amyloid filaments-a pathological hallmark of tauopathies, a group of neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), Huntington’s disease (HD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [1–3]. Accurately identifying and targeting tau deposits in vivo holds significant value for diagnosis and therapeutic research.

A major research effort to develop selective tau tracers for positron emission tomography (PET) imaging has culminated in a significant breakthrough. These tracers, some now available for clinical evaluation, represent a major advance in research on tauopathies [4]. Among them, the pyridoindole derivative 18F-flortaucipir, which gained FDA approval for clinical use in May 2020 as the first tracer of its kind, has demonstrated utility in diagnosing Alzheimer’s disease [5]. However, its application in non-AD tauopathies is limited due to low affinity for non-AD tau fibrils. Consequently, numerous second-generation candidates are under investigation, with 18F-MK6240 emerging as a particularly promising probe owing to its superior imaging characteristics with Ki = 0.36, Bmax = 7–93.4 nM, and no off-target binding to MAO-A, MAO-B [6, 7]. Using cerebellar gray matter as the reference region, the standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr) can be calculated as a simplified quantitative metric from PET data. This ratio serves as a valuable parameter for distinguishing tauopathies and contributes to a biological diagnosis when combined with clinical assessment and other biomarkers (such as amyloid-PET and CSF analysis), thus enhancing diagnostic certainty [6, 8, 9]. Additionally, 18F-MK6240 enables visualization of the topographic spread of tau pathology throughout the brain to help determine the extent of tau burden, correlating with disease severity and progression rate. This capability allows the Braak staging system, historically defined through post-mortem neuropathology, to be approximated in living individuals. Braak’s hierarchical staging system categorizes progression based on tau aggregation in distinct anatomical regions: transentorhinal cortex (Stage I), entorhinal cortex and hippocampus (Stage II), inferior temporal cortex (Stage III), association cortices (Stages IV–V), and primary sensory cortices (Stage VI) [10–12].

18F-MK6240 PET scanning typically requires only a 20-minute static acquisition performed 90–110 min post-injection [13], but this prolonged scan duration may pose challenges for patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), dementia, or advanced age due to physical discomfort and psychological distress. Head motion artifacts associated with prolonged scans may impede clinical workflows and limit the broader adoption of tau PET in the growing population of dementia and elderly patients. Conversely, reducing scan duration may compromise the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), potentially affecting diagnostic accuracy. Therefore, optimizing scan duration while preserving sufficient image quality for clinical diagnosis remains a pressing challenge in PET imaging. Continuous advancements in modern PET detector technology have paved the way for shorter imaging protocols. Specially, the implement of highly sensitive silicon photomultiplier (SiPM) detectors combined with time-of-flight (TOF) capabilities provides a substantial gain in photon sensitivity and SNR [14]. These high-sensitivity configurations, achievable in both modern PET/CT and PET/MR platforms, offer the potential to reduce radiotracer acquisition time significantly without compromising image quality. In this retrospective study, we investigated the effect of an accelerated 18F-MK6240 acquisition protocol utilizing a contemporary integrated PET/MR system (GE SIGNA) equipped with SiPM detectors. By evaluating the impact on image quality and diagnostic performance, these findings aim to provide clinically relevant insights for improving patient compliance and operational efficiency in tauopathy imaging.

Materials and methods

Study participants

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET/MRI scanning at the PET/CT-MRI Center within the Department of Nuclear Medicine, The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, between September 1, 2024, and March 30, 2025. All participants underwent a comprehensive assessment that included clinical history review, neuropsychological examination, cognitive evaluations using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) [15] administered by qualified physicians, and blood biochemical tests. These data were retrospectively collected from the Hospital Information System of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Patients were excluded if they had severe structural brain lesions (e.g., major strokes or tumors), severe systemic or psychiatric diseases that could interfere with cognition or imaging interpretation, or contraindications to PET or MRI. The final cohort comprised 69 individuals (mean age 72.1 ± 8.4 years; 57.3% female), and the age range of the participants was from 55 to 89 years. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.: [2025-498-01]). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants had been informed that their anonymized medical data may be used for scientific research. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to undergoing ¹⁸F-MK6240 brain PET/MRI scanning.

Image data acquisition

All PET scans were acquired using an integrated time-of-flight 3T PET/MR scanner (SIGNA, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). All subjects received an intravenous bolus injection of 3.7 MBq/kg ¹⁸F-MK6240 into the antecubital vein. PET/MR scanning commenced 90 min post-injection, with each session lasting 20 min. Simultaneously with PET, a 3D T1-weighted BRAVO MR sequence was acquired using an 8-channel high-resolution head array coil (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA). Acquisition parameters were as follows: sagittal plane; TE: 3.2 ms; TR: 8.5 ms; TI: 450 ms; flip angle: 12°; receiver bandwidth: 31.25 kHz; NEX: 1; isotropic voxel size: 1 × 1 × 1 mm.

Image processing

The PET data were acquired in four sequential 5-minute frames. From these frames, four distinct PET images were reconstructed, each representing progressively longer integration times: (1) a 5-minute image using only the first frame; (2) a 10-minute image combining the first two frames; (3) a 15-minute image combining the first three frames; and (4) a 20-minute image using all four frames. All reconstructions utilized a 256 × 256 matrix and an ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm (4 iterations, 28 subsets). The reconstruction process incorporated corrections for scatter, random coincidences, deadtime, radioactive decay, and attenuation (based on zero-echo time (ZTE) MR data), along with point spread function (PSF) modelling. Finally, images underwent isotropic Gaussian post-smoothing with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 4.5 mm.

Subjective visual assessment

Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians, both specializing in neuroimaging and blinded to all clinical and diagnostic details, independently reviewed all PET images presented in random order. To prevent readers from directly comparing different durations of the same subject, which could bias the assessment, images from the different scan durations were presented to the readers independently and in a fully randomized order. To evaluate intra-reader consistency, a randomly selected subset (10%) of the images—stratified to ensure an equal distribution across the four scan durations—was re-assessed by each physician. Subjective image quality was evaluated on a 5-point scale across three defined dimensions: overall quality, noise level, and diagnostic confidence. The specific evaluation standards were predefined as follows: 5 (Excellent): Superior gray/white matter contrast and structural sharpness, minimal to negligible noise, and unequivocal diagnostic confidence; 4 (Good): Clear anatomical delineation with mild visual noise that does not interfere with scan interpretation, alongside high diagnostic confidence; 3 (Adequate): Acceptable contrast with moderate graininess; regional boundaries are less sharp but sufficient for a reliable clinical diagnosis; 2 (Poor): Suboptimal contrast and substantial noise severely obscuring cortical details, leading to an equivocal or low-confidence interpretation; 1 (Uninterpretable): Massive noise or degradation rendering anatomical structures unrecognizable and clinical diagnosis impossible. Additionally, the visual assessment for tau status (positive/negative) was fundamentally performed based on the standardized operational algorithm proposed by Seibyl et al. [16]. The diagnostic results derived from the standard 20-minute acquisition served as the reference standard for each participant. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of visual diagnoses were calculated separately for each physician. In cases where the visual assessments of two physicians disagreed, a senior nuclear medicine physician resolved the discrepancy.

Image semi-quantitative analysis

For quantitative analysis, all imaging data were processed using PMOD Neuro Tool (PNEURO v4.1, PMOD Inc., Zurich, Switzerland). Subject-specific T1-weighted MR images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), followed by spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 152 standard space. The Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas [17] was used to precisely delineate the anatomical ROIs corresponding to the consecutive topographic spread of tau pathology. Consistent with previous PET studies [18, 19], the composite regions for each PET Braak-like stage were defined as follows: Braak I included the transentorhinal cortex; Braak II encompassed the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus; Braak III comprised the amygdala, parahippocampus gyrus, fusiform gyrus, and lingual gyrus; Braak IV consisted of the insula, inferior temporal, lateral temporal, posterior cingulate, and inferior parietal cortices; Braak V involved the orbitofrontal, superior temporal, inferior frontal, cuneus, anterior cingulate, supramarginal gyrus, lateral occipital, precuneus, superior parietal, superior frontal, and rostro medial frontal regions; and Braak VI was represented by the paracentral, postcentral, precentral, and pericalcarine cortices. Standardized uptake values (SUV) were then extracted from these anatomically defined regions. Regional standardized uptake value ratios (SUVr) values were calculated for the predefined Braak-stage composite regions, including Braak I–II, Braak III–IV, and Braak V–VI, by normalizing the mean radioactivity concentration in each target region to that of cerebellar gray matter reference region.

Statistical analysis

Subjective image quality scores were summarized as medians per acquisition group. Inter-group differences in score distributions were assessed using the Friedman test, with a statistical significance threshold set at p < 0.05. The consistency of visual ratings for tau involvement extent (0%, 1–25%, 26–75%, or > 75%) across the four scan durations was assessed using the Kendall’s W coefficient of concordance. For quantitative analyses, demographic, clinical characteristics and SUVr (5, 10, 15, and 20 min) were compared between tau-positive and tau-negative cohorts using independent t-tests (for continuous variables, assuming homogeneity of variance) or χ² tests (for categorical variables). Pairwise paired t-tests were performed because the 5-, 10-, 15-, and 20-minute images were reconstructed from the same PET acquisition in the same participants. Inter-protocol SUVr agreement was quantified using Bland-Altman limits of agreement (95% CI; significance: p < 0.05). DeLong’s test was used for comparing the differences between the two ROC curves. All statistical analysis and the creation of statistical graphs were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), and MedCalc 23.2.6 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 69 participants included, 50 were visually diagnosed as tau positive (AD pattern) based on the 20-minute PET scan. All participants had a clinical diagnosis of either AD or MCI. Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics and radiotracer injection levels for both the tau-positive and tau-negative groups. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, weight, body mass index (BMI), folic acid, and Vitamin B12, suggesting these factors likely do not substantially influence tau status detection via PET. In contrast, participants with positive tau scans showed significantly worse cognitive function. Specifically, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was lower in the tau-positive group (10.22 ± 6.78) than in the tau-negative group (17.95 ± 6.89; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score (1.67 ± 0.75 vs. 0.97 ± 0.47, p < 0.001) were higher in the tau-positive group, while the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) score was 35.08 ± 13.67 in the tau-positive group versus 33.47 ± 11.73 in the tau-negative group (p = 0.631) (Table 1).

Two specialists independently evaluated the subjective image quality for all different scan durations. The assessment results from each physician are summarized in Table 2. The frequency distribution of subjective image quality scores across five scan durations are summarized in Table S1. Despite the slight reduction in image quality for the 5-minute images relative to the other long time duration groups, none of the images across any of the four durations were rated as poor (score < 3), suggesting that even the shortest scans provided acceptable diagnostic quality. However, for the shortest 2-minute scans, although Physician 2 assigned a single score of 4, the predominant ratings for the 2-minute scans were low (1–3). Due to the unacceptably low subjective image quality scores from both physicians, the 2-minute scan duration will not proceed to further evaluation. Objective quantitative analysis

To objectively and quantitatively discriminate tau status, SUVr of the Braak stages were compared between the tau-positive and tau-negative groups across all scan durations. Individual read data (Fig. 2) demonstrated clustering of negative scans showed lower SUVrs spread over a wider range of four different time duration. As shown in Table S3, at each acquisition duration from 5 to 20 min, Braak-stage SUVr values were significantly higher in the visually defined tau-positive group than in the tau-negative group. To directly compare SUVr values across acquisition durations, pairwise paired t-tests were additionally performed for Braak-stage regions. In the total cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed between any pair of acquisition durations for Braak I–II, Braak III–IV, or Braak V–VI SUVr values (Table S4). Bland-Altman plots (Fig. 3) demonstrated high concordance among PET measurements across all four scan durations. It demonstrates excellent agreement between the standard 20-minute scan and shorter acquisitions, with progressively wider limits of agreement as scan time decreases. For the 20-minute versus 15-minute comparison, the 95% limits of agreement are consistently the narrowest across all analyses, indicating near-perfect interchangeability.

ROC curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on all Braak tau region was conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of SUVr measurements across the four scan durations (5, 10, 15, and 20 min), as shown in Fig. 4. The analysis yielded consistently high area under the curve (AUC) values for all durations (0.982, 0.982, 0.982, and 0.984, respectively), indicating strong discriminative power in distinguishing tau-positive cases. Notably, no meaningful reduction in diagnostic sensitivity was observed with shorter acquisition times. Moreover, the optimal diagnostic thresholds for cortical gray matter SUVr values exhibited a gradual upward trend with increasing scan duration: 1.090 at 5 min post-injection, increasing to 1.098 at 10 min, 1.100 at 15 min, and 1.094 at 20-minute (Table S5).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that reducing ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET acquisition time to as short as 5 min maintained diagnostic and quantitative integrity comparable to the standard 20-minute protocol. This conclusion is robustly supported by multiple lines of evidence: visual assessment yielded perfect diagnostic agreement (100% accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity across 5-, 10-, and 15-minute scans, as shown in Table S2); semi-quantitative SUVr measurements in Braak stage-specific regions showed no significant variation across durations (all p > 0.05, Table S3); and visual ratings of regional tau involvement extent exhibited near-perfect consistency (Kendall’s W ≥ 0.984, Table 3). Although subjective image quality scores for the 5-minute protocol were slightly but significantly lower than those for longer acquisitions (p < 0.05, Table 2), they remained within the clinically acceptable range (median score ≥ 4), confirming that the slight increase in image noise did not compromise diagnostic interpretability or quantitative outcomes.

Analysis of clinical characteristics revealed no statistically significant differences in age or sex between the tau-positive and tau-negative groups. Nevertheless, age may still act as a contributing factor in the pathogenesis or progression of cognitive decline, giving its known association with neurodegenerative processes. Similarly, tau-positive participants exhibited significantly worse performance on multiple cognitive assessments compared to the tau-negative group. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET imaging effectively captures the spatiotemporal dynamics of tau deposition, which correlates strongly with cognitive decline [20–22]. As demonstrated in prior studies, the clinical and research utility of 18F-MK-6240 PET extends to early detection of subclinical tau pathology, differentiation among neurodegenerative disease subtypes, and prediction of disease progression in cognitively impaired populations [18–20, 23]. These applications are rooted in the pivotal role of tau pathology in the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment. Hyperphosphorylated tau species destabilize microtubules, impair axonal transport, and ultimately lead to synaptic dysfunction. This pathological cascade culminates in progressive synaptic loss and neuronal degeneration, providing a direct neurobiological basis for cognitive decline. Consequently, the extent of tau burden shows a strong clinicopathological correlation with the severity of cognitive impairment and serves as a validated biomarker for Braak staging and prognostic assessment.

In clinical practice, the standard 20-minute ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET scan is challenging for elderly patients with cognitive impairment, who may struggle to remain still due to disorientation, anxiety, or discomfort. This motion can compromise image quality. Several studies have systematically investigated scan time reduction strategies for β-amyloid PET imaging. Cumulative evidence indicates that the acquisition duration for standard 20-minute scans can be significantly reduced to 5 min or less, while maintaining comparable diagnostic accuracy, as verified through quantitative measures such as SUVr and ROC curve analyses [24–26]. However, there is currently a lack of evidence specifically supporting scan time reduction in tau PET imaging.

This study systematically evaluated reduced acquisition protocols for ¹⁸F-MK6240 tau PET imaging using the SIGNA PET/MR system. The hybrid system integrates GE Healthcare’s SiPM detectors with 25-mm lutetium-yttrium oxyorthosilicate (LYSO) scintillation crystals, providing high photon sensitivity (≥ 22 kcps/MBq) and sub-2 mm FWHM spatial resolution through time-of-flight capabilities. A comprehensive assessment of image quality was conducted across four acquisition durations (5, 10, 15, and 20 min), combining both visual inspection and quantitative evaluation. Independent visual assessments by two nuclear medicine physicians identified a statistically significant reduction in image score for the 2-minute acquisitions (p < 0.05), likely due to reduced photon statistics and resultant degradation in SNR, particularly in low-uptake reference regions. As our subjective image quality assessment (Table 2 and S1) conclusively showed, the 2-minute PET images received consistently and unacceptably low-quality scores from both readers. This objective assessment confirmed that the image noise at this duration is too high for reliable interpretation. Therefore, we concur that further quantitative or diagnostic analysis of the 2-minute data is not justified, as it does not represent a clinically useful protocol. Notably, despite the reduced scores, no 5-minute scan was rated as poor quality (i.e., score < 3 on a 5-point scale), indicating that the observed decline in image quality remained within clinically acceptable thresholds for diagnostic interpretation. Since obtaining 3D MRI sequences for brain structure assessment and registration requires at least 5 min, so we set the shortest time point for quantitative comparison to 5 min.

The study further confirmed that shortening the scan duration did not compromise the accuracy of ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET in differentiating tau-positive from tau-negative cases. These findings collectively support the feasibility of significantly reducing scan time without undermining diagnostic confidence in the assessment of tau pathology. The diagnostic robustness of quantitative PET metrics plays a critical role in enhancing clinical decision-making. Our analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences in SUVr between tau-positive and tau-negative groups across all scan durations by all different Braak stages (Fig. 2; Table S3). In addition, direct paired comparisons across acquisition durations showed no statistically significant differences in Braak-stage SUVr values in the total cohort or in exploratory tau-positive and tau-negative subgroup analyses (Table S4). Bland-Altman analysis showed excellent agreement between SUVr values obtained from the 20-minute reference scans and the shortened protocols in all Braak stages, with mean differences close to zero across all comparisons. These narrow limits of agreement collectively confirm that scan duration has minimal effect on quantitative SUVr outcomes. However, as visualized in Fig. 3, the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) progressively widened as the scan duration decreased. The broader LoA for the 5-minute scan is an expected consequence of reduced photon counts and the consequent increase in image noise. This higher measurement variability at the shortest acquisition time means that, while the group average remains accurate, the SUVr value for an individual subject is subject to greater random uncertainty when derived from a 5-minute scan compared to a longer one. Critically, this increased variability did not translate to a loss of diagnostic utility. The quantitative thresholds (Table S5) and diagnostic performance (Fig. 4, ROC analysis) remained stable. Therefore, while the 5-minute protocol exhibits greater quantitative measurement variance, this variance lies within a range that does not compromise its clinical validity for distinguishing tau-positive from tau-negative cases. The primary advantage of the 5-minute scan—significantly improved patient tolerance and workflow efficiency—is thus achieved without sacrificing reliable diagnostic or quantitative assessment. However, a 5-minute acquisition may be sufficient for cross-sectional clinical tau-status assessment on a high-sensitivity PET/MR system, it may be less suitable for longitudinal follow-up or therapeutic monitoring, where small changes in tau burden need to be detected. In such settings, the variability introduced by a very short acquisition may combine with normal test–retest variability and reduce sensitivity to subtle interval changes. Therefore, longer acquisitions may remain preferable for longitudinal quantitative applications.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the moderate sample size (n = 69) and single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to wider clinical populations. In addition, the retrospective cohort may have included a relatively high proportion of visually clear tau-positive and tau-negative cases, which could have contributed to the strong group separation and the high agreement across acquisition durations. The absence of early-stage or borderline cases with inter-reader disagreement limits our ability to determine the minimum acquisition duration required for more challenging clinical scenarios. Future prospective studies should include uncertain tau status, low-burden tau cases, and cases with discordant reader interpretations to better evaluate the robustness and limitations of shortened ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET protocols. Second, shortened acquisition protocols may reduce spatial resolution and image clarity, potentially increasing inter-reader variability, especially among less experienced clinicians. This limitation is further compounded by the lack of histopathological confirmation. While the 20-minute PET acquisition served as the reference standard for tau positivity, its alignment with postmortem Braak staging has yet to be validated. Third, the reduced photon counts in ultra-short scans lead to lower signal-to-noise ratios, which may increase the risk of false-positive or false-negative interpretations by either mimicking pathological uptake or masking subtle tau accumulation. Furthermore, the frequency with which patient motion renders a scan non-evaluable in a clinical population was not quantified in this retrospective study and warrants prospective investigation. To address these limitations, future research should: (1) expand recruitment through multicenter studies with at least 200 participants across a spectrum of cognitive profiles; (2) establish standardized reader training protocols incorporating datasets with varying SNR levels; (3) A systematic investigation comparing different reconstruction schemes (e.g., varying subsets while holding iterations constant) specifically for 5-minute and potentially shorter ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET scans is needed.

Conclusion

Reducing ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET acquisition time to 5 min on a modern PET/MR system equipped with silicon photomultiplier detectors and time-of-flight technology results in a measurable but clinically acceptable decline in image quality. This reduction does not compromise the reliability of visual assessment or the stability of quantitative measurements compared to the standard 20-minute protocol. Our findings demonstrate that 5-minute acquisitions preserve the essential imaging information required for tau pathology evaluation. The adoption of shorten time protocols offers practical advantages, including reduced susceptibility to patient motion artifacts, increased scanner throughput, and improved accessibility for claustrophobic or cognitively impaired individuals. Therefore, a 5-minute ¹⁸F-MK6240 PET scan presents a feasible and operationally efficient alternative to the conventional 20-minute protocol in clinical tau imaging, provided it is performed on scanners with sufficiently high sensitivity.
