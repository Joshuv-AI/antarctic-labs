Pancreaticoduodenectomy for ampullary adenocarcinoma performed in a conflict-zone field hospital during an active armed conflict: a case report
Authors: Khaled Alshawwa, Amjad Abu-Alqumboz, Abed Dawood, Matar Mohammed, Nafez Qirrem, Mais Dahdooh, Nasser Radwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Ampullary adenocarcinoma is a rare periampullary malignancy for which pancreaticoduodenectomy (the Whipple procedure) represents the only potentially curative surgical intervention. This complex operation is universally performed in high-volume tertiary hospitals with dedicated hepatopancreatobiliary surgical teams and a full intensive care infrastructure. We report the first documented case of a Whipple procedure performed in a field hospital in an active conflict zone.Case presentation:A 58-year-old male presented with a three-month history of painless obstructive jaundice to a conflict-zone field hospital in Gaza during the armed conflict. CT imaging demonstrated a dilated common bile duct (2 cm) with ampullary-level tapering and moderate intrahepatic biliary dilatation. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography confirmed a tumorous ampulla; biliary stenting and biopsy were performed, with histopathology confirming well-differentiated ampullary adenocarcinoma. Given the complete absence of tertiary referral options due to the destruction of hospital infrastructure, a pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed under field-hospital conditions (admission 20 July 2025, discharge 28 July 2025). Final histopathology confirmed well-differentiated ampullary adenocarcinoma, pT1a N0, with the tumor limited to the sphincter of Oddi, all margins negative (R0), and 0/14 lymph nodes involved. At discharge on postoperative day 8, the patient was clinically well, with a clean wound, a soft abdomen, and acceptable laboratory values. Four-month follow-up CT demonstrated a clear operative bed with no local recurrence, no regional lymphadenopathy, and no distant metastases.Conclusion:This case demonstrates that a technically demanding, curative-intent Whipple procedure can be successfully performed in an active conflict-zone field hospital, achieving oncological outcomes equivalent to high-volume centers. It represents a notable advance in humanitarian surgery and underscores the importance of surgical expertise in austere environments.

Introduction

Ampullary carcinoma is a malignancy arising from the epithelium of the ampulla of Vater – the anatomical confluence of the common bile duct, the main pancreatic duct, and the duodenal mucosa at the papilla. Although rare, accounting for approximately 0.2% of all gastrointestinal malignancies and roughly 6% of periampullary tumors[1], it carries a significantly more favorable prognosis than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, particularly when diagnosed at an early stage. Five-year survival for pT1 N0 disease following curative resection ranges from 70% to 90%, making timely surgical management critical[2,3].

The standard surgical treatment for resectable ampullary carcinoma is pancreaticoduodenectomy, universally referred to as the Whipple procedure. First described in 1935 by Whipple, Parsons, and Mullins[4], this operation involves en-bloc resection of the pancreatic head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, regional lymph nodes, and gallbladder, followed by complex reconstruction requiring three anastomoses: pancreaticojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and gastrojejunostomy. Pylorus preservation, as a modification of the classical Whipple technique, was introduced by Traverso and Longmire in 1978 and has since been widely adopted[5]. The procedure is among the most technically demanding abdominal operations performed today, with operative times routinely ranging from 4 to 6 hours and significant risks of life-threatening postoperative complications, including pancreatic fistula, delayed gastric emptying, post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage, biliary leak, and sepsis[6].

The volume-outcome relationship in pancreatoduodenectomy is one of the most robustly established associations in the surgical literature. Birkmeyer and colleagues demonstrated perioperative mortality rates of less than 3% at high-volume centers compared with 15–20% at low-volume institutions[7]. These findings formed the evidentiary basis for the international centralization of pancreatic surgery to specialized hepato-pancreato-biliary (HPB) units equipped with dedicated surgical teams, anesthesiology, intensive care units, interventional radiology, and comprehensive blood banking[7,8].

The Gaza Strip has experienced sustained armed conflict since October 2023, resulting in the near-total destruction and incapacitation of formal healthcare infrastructure, including tertiary hospital services[9]. Under these conditions, field hospitals – temporary surgical facilities established by local and international humanitarian organizations – represent the sole available surgical resource for the civilian population[10]. Field hospitals provide emergency and general surgical services under extreme resource constraints. Semi-elective major visceral surgery, particularly of HPB complexity, has not previously been reported in such an environment[11–13].

We report what we believe to be the first documented case of a pancreaticoduodenectomy performed in an active conflict-zone field hospital, achieving curative-intent resection for early-stage ampullary adenocarcinoma with excellent oncological and perioperative outcomes, confirmed by 4-month postoperative follow-up imaging.

Case presentation

Patient demographics and presentation

A 58-year-old male patient (date of birth: 7 January 1967) with no significant prior medical or surgical comorbidities presented with a 3-month history of progressive, painless obstructive jaundice. The patient had no history of abdominal surgery, hepatic disease, or malignancy. He was admitted on 20 July 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for clinical management and academic publication of this case. All personally identifying information has been de-identified in accordance with applicable ethical guidelines.

Preoperative imaging

Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen with contrast was performed at another facility and demonstrated the following findings: the common bile duct (CBD) was markedly dilated to 2 cm in diameter, with smooth ampullary-level tapering and faint periampullary soft-tissue enhancement consistent with an obstructive ampullary lesion. Moderate intrahepatic biliary dilatation was present bilaterally. The liver parenchyma showed no focal lesions. The main pancreatic duct was dilated to 4.5 mm without a discrete pancreatic mass. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and preoperative biopsy

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed at another capable facility. Endoscopic examination revealed a prominently bulging, tumorous-appearing ampulla of Vater with significant CBD dilation (2 cm) and intrahepatic biliary involvement. A 7-French plastic biliary stent was placed for preoperative biliary decompression[14]. Targeted biopsies were obtained from the ampullary mass; three small pieces of soft tissue measuring 0.4 × 0.3 cm were submitted in formalin.

Histopathological analysis of the ERCP biopsy specimens demonstrated fragments of duodenal and/or ampullary mucosa with underlying involvement of dysplastic glandular tissue, consistent with well-differentiated adenocarcinoma.

Surgical decision-making in the context of active conflict

Following a confirmed histopathological diagnosis of resectable ampullary adenocarcinoma, the multidisciplinary surgical team reviewed the case. Given the complete absence of any tertiary referral pathway due to the ongoing armed conflict and systematic destruction of regional hospital infrastructure in Gaza[9], and acknowledging that non-operative management would inevitably result in disease progression and death, the decision was made – with the patient’s fully informed consent – to proceed with curative-intent pancreaticoduodenectomy within the field hospital setting[11,15].

Surgical procedure

Open pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed in a containerized operating room (OR) under general anesthesia administered by an in-field anesthesiologist[5]. Figure 2 shows the OR setting during the procedure. The resection phase included en-bloc removal of the pancreatic head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, gallbladder, and regional lymphadenectomy. Reconstruction was performed via standard pancreaticojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and gastrojejunostomy. Two surgical drains were placed: right-sided and left-sided intraperitoneal drains for postoperative monitoring of pancreatic fistula and biliary leak[6]. Total operative time was 240 minutes. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was 150–200 mL; no blood transfusion was required. The pancreatic parenchyma had a firm texture with a dilated main pancreatic duct measuring 5–6 mm; a 3 Fr × 5 cm pancreatic duct stent was placed during pancreaticojejunostomy reconstruction. Intraoperative fluid management was conducted within the limitations of field hospital resources. The patient was recovered in the field hospital’s dedicated containerized Intensive Care Unit (ICU). Postoperative course and discharge

The patient was transferred from the ICU after a 5-day stay and discharged to a tent-based ward within the same hospital. On postoperative day 8, clinical and laboratory assessments demonstrated a satisfactory recovery. The patient was afebrile with no vomiting. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, lax abdomen with a clean surgical wound. Laboratory values at discharge were: white blood cell count 6.3 × 103/μL, hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL, platelet count 312 × 103/μL, fasting blood glucose 82 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 18 mg/dL, creatinine 46 μmol/L. Drain outputs were examined for amylase levels on day 5 and were within normal limits, with no biochemical or clinical evidence of pancreatic fistula or biliary leak[6]. The patient had stable vital signs and was discharged on day 8, under the ongoing constraints of the conflict setting.

Four-month follow-up CT

A follow-up CT of the abdomen was performed approximately four months postoperatively (November 2025). The radiological findings were as follows: a clear operative bed with no local recurrence and no local or regional lymph node enlargement; a pancreatic stent was seen in situ within the pancreatic duct; prominent intrahepatic biliary radicles with pneumobilia were noted, consistent with expected post-Whipple reconstruction anatomy[2]; no abdominal or pelvic free fluid was identified; a small subpleural septation was noted in the right middle lobe, considered an incidental non-oncological finding. These findings confirm disease-free status at 4 months post-resection (Figs. 4 and 5). Discussion

This case report describes the first documented successful pancreaticoduodenectomy performed in a field hospital in an active conflict zone, resulting in curative-intent resection of early-stage ampullary adenocarcinoma (pT1a N0, R0, 14-node harvest), with an uneventful 8-day postoperative course and no evidence of disease on 4-month CT follow-up. The oncological result achieved – the gold-standard benchmark for this indication – was comparable to outcomes reported from high-volume HPB centers[2,7,8], a result accomplished under conditions of extreme resource deprivation imposed by armed conflict.

Ampullary adenocarcinoma: prognosis and the imperative for resection

Ampullary carcinoma constitutes a clinically distinct entity within periampullary malignancies, with a markedly more favorable prognosis than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma due to its early biliary obstructive symptoms that prompt diagnosis before distant dissemination[1]. The AJCC 8th Edition classifies pT1a disease as a tumor confined to the sphincter of Oddi, the earliest T category[16]. Published series consistently report 5-year survival rates of 70–90% for pT1 N0 ampullary carcinoma following R0 resection[2,3], compared to 10–20% for stage-equivalent pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma[17]. In this patient, all favorable prognostic determinants were achieved: pT1a staging, R0 resection, well-differentiated histology, and a lymph node yield of 14, with none involved – exceeding the minimum 12-node harvest recommended for accurate staging[18]. Without surgical resection, this patient’s prognosis would have been determined by disease progression in a setting with no chemotherapy or radiation therapy infrastructure. The decision to operate was therefore not only justifiable but ethically imperative.

The Whipple procedure in a field hospital: challenges overcome

The pancreaticoduodenectomy is among the most demanding abdominal operations, traditionally requiring high-volume HPB surgical teams, dedicated anesthesiology with invasive hemodynamic monitoring, intensive care capability, interventional radiology for the management of hemorrhagic complications, and robust blood banking[6–8]. The Birkmeyer volume–outcome data established mortality gradients of 3–16% between high- and low-volume centers[7,8]. The field hospital environment lacks most of these standard requisites. That curative R0 resection was achieved, with a clean wound and acceptable laboratory values at discharge on day 8, without documented fistula, hemorrhage, or sepsis, reflects the skill of the operating team. It must be acknowledged, however, that this outcome should not obscure the substantial risks inherent to performing pancreaticoduodenectomy in an austere setting. The complete absence of interventional radiology means that post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), a life-threatening complication occurring in 3–8% of cases at high-volume centers, could not have been managed by angioembolisation if it had occurred. Similarly, clinically significant postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF, Grade B/C) – with an incidence of 10–20% – would have posed a major challenge given the limited capacity for CT-guided drainage, total parenteral nutrition, and prolonged intensive care. The absence of these complications in this case was fortunate and should not be interpreted as evidence that such risks are acceptable as routine practice in resource-limited environments.

The performance of cholecystectomy alongside the pancreaticoduodenectomy – the standard of care at all centers – further underscores the surgical team’s adherence to oncological principles even under austere conditions[2]. The minimal abdominal drain outputs at discharge, without biochemical evidence of pancreatic fistula, represent an excellent early postoperative result by internationally accepted criteria[6]. The 4-month follow-up CT, demonstrating a clear operative bed and no recurrence, provides the first medium-term outcome data for a pancreaticoduodenectomy performed in a conflict-zone setting.

The use of a containerized OR and containerized ICU – infrastructure components increasingly deployed in prolonged-conflict humanitarian responses – was integral to the feasibility of this case. The 5-day ICU stay following a major oncological resection demonstrates meaningful post-operative monitoring capacity within the field hospital model, representing a significant departure from the traditional field hospital paradigm of damage-control-only surgery[10,12].

The field hospital: a model for humanitarian HPB surgery

Field hospitals in conflict and disaster settings have traditionally focused on hemorrhage control, wound debridement, fracture management, and damage control laparotomy[10,12,13]. The present case extends the documented scope of field hospital surgery into major semi-elective oncological resection. The field hospital represents a model of sustained humanitarian surgical capacity in one of the most resource-depleted conflict environments in modern history[11,15,19].

It is worth noting that the preoperative diagnostic workup – CT imaging, ERCP with biliary stenting, and biopsy with histopathological analysis – was accomplished with difficulty across different facilities despite the conflict environment. The ERCP and the surgical specimen examination were performed at a regional hospital’s pathology department, located within the Gaza Strip and operational at the time of the case, and the preoperative and four-month follow-up CT scans were performed at a regional hospital, also within the Gaza Strip and operating in a limited capacity – both representing functioning, if severely compromised, components of the remaining, fragmented healthcare system. The collaboration between these entities in maintaining a standard of oncological care for this patient is itself noteworthy and underscores the collective resilience of Palestinian healthcare workers under occupation and conflict[9].

Limitations

This report carries several inherent limitations. Long-term oncological follow-up (5-year survival data) is not yet available and will require ongoing effort, given the operational context. The postoperative monitoring environment in a field hospital does not replicate the safety net of a tertiary ICU, and the management of potential complications such as pancreatic fistula or post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage would have been severely limited had they occurred. The follow-up period of 4 months, while encouraging, is limited relative to the oncological surveillance recommended for ampullary adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, systematic assessment of endocrine and exocrine pancreatic function – including HbA1c measurement and evaluation for pancreatic exocrine insufficiency – could not be performed during follow-up, as the patient was displaced due to the ongoing conflict; this limitation was unavoidable under these exceptional circumstances[6]. Finally, as a single case report, no generalizable conclusions about the routine feasibility of pancreaticoduodenectomy in field settings can be drawn.

Conclusion

We report the first documented pancreaticoduodenectomy performed at a field hospital in an active conflict zone in Gaza – resulting in curative-intent R0 resection of early-stage ampullary adenocarcinoma (pT1a N0, 0.5 cm, well-differentiated, 0/14 lymph nodes). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 8 in clinically stable condition, with no evidence of disease on 4-month follow-up CT. This case demonstrates that surgical expertise, not institutional infrastructure alone, is the primary determinant of operative outcomes[7,8] and expands the documented scope of surgery achievable in humanitarian settings[11,15,19]. This case reinforces the principle that every patient – regardless of the circumstances of conflict – deserves the highest standard of care that skilled hands can provide.
