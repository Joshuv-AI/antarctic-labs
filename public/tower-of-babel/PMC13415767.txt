Buried-interface stabilization for efficient and bright blue perovskite LEDs
Authors: Xin-Mei Hu, Yang Shen, Shi-Chi Feng, Long-Xue Cao, Zhen-Huang Su, Yu-Hang Zhang, Bing-Feng Wang, Ying-Ying Li, Hao Ren, Xing-Yu Gao, Song-Shan Zeng, Jian-Xin Tang, Yan-Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal halide perovskites are highly promising luminescent materials; however, the development of high-performance blue perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) remains constrained by buried interface degradation. Here, we construct a three-dimensional cross-linked network of pentaerythritol tetraacrylate on the hole transport layer (HTL) to achieve efficient and bright blue PeLEDs. This dense cross-linked network serves as a physical barrier, shielding the HTL from polar solvent erosion and preserving its structural integrity. The abundant carbonyl groups in the network form strong coordination interactions with the perovskite lattice, effectively passivating interfacial defects and enhancing interfacial stability. Furthermore, an interfacial dipole formed at the HTL/network interface modulates the energy level alignment, facilitating efficient hole injection. The resulting PeLEDs across blue spectral regions deliver outstanding external quantum efficiencies of 28.0% at 488 nm, 21.3% at 476 nm, and 15.4% at 466 nm, with maximum luminance values of 26,240, 10,750, and 2444 cd m−2, respectively.Subject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Metal halide perovskites have emerged as promising luminescent materials for next-generation display technologies owing to their exceptional color purity, wide color gamut, high photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs), and cost-effective solution processability1–8. Achieving efficient and bright perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) is critical for advanced full-color displays, especially in the fields of near-eye displays (e.g., augmented reality, virtual reality, mixed reality). Although recent research breakthroughs have enabled green and red PeLEDs to simultaneously reach external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) exceeding 30% and high luminance above 10,000 cd m−2, the comprehensive performance of blue PeLEDs remains far less advanced9–17.

The performance limitations of blue PeLEDs stem not only from the inherent constraints of wide-bandgap perovskite materials, but more critically from the intrinsic vulnerability of the buried interface within the device18–20. Serving as both the physical pathway for charge injection and substrate for grain growth, the stability of buried interface directly governs the interfacial charge dynamics and perovskite crystallization. As a widely used HTL underneath perovskites, poly(9-vinylcarbazole) (PVK) demonstrates favorable hole transport properties and is extensively utilized in current blue device architectures. However, the linear molecular architecture of this polymer HTL features an intrinsic structural vulnerability, making it susceptible to swelling and dissolution when directly exposed to those aprotic polar solvents employed in perovskite precursor solution. This structural instability subsequently leads to poor film coverage, severe pinhole formation, and misaligned energy level, consequently causing increased leakage current, imbalanced carrier injection, and high-density trap states, which constitute fundamental limitations to electroluminescence (EL) performance of blue PeLEDs.

Previous studies have attempted to mitigate such issues through interfacial engineering approaches. For instance, introducing a [2-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl]phosphonic acid (2-PACz) self-assembled monolayer at the inorganic nickel oxide (NiOx)/PVK heterointerface can strengthen interfacial adhesion through tridentate coordination bonds with NiOx and effectively passivates surface trap states of NiOx, contributing to improved EL performance of blue PeLEDs21. However, these prevailing interfacial engineering strategies focus on static optimization of energy-level alignment and interfacial bonding, yet remain ineffective in suppressing dynamic solvent-induced erosion of PVK layers during perovskite film deposition. Penetration and swelling of polar solvents within linear polymer matrices trigger interfacial morphological collapse and concomitant electrical degradation, representing a fundamental bottleneck in current device architectures. Accordingly, the development of integrative interfacial strategies that concurrently preserve physical integrity, enable effective chemical passivation, and afford precise energy-level control remains a central priority for advancing high-performance blue PeLEDs.

In this work, we propose that an interfacial engineering approach utilizing a three-dimensional (3D) cross-linked network derived from pentaerythritol tetraacrylate (PETA) enables efficient and bright blue perovskite LEDs (Fig. 1). This thermally cross-linked 3D network offers distinct interfacial modulation advantages over conventional linear polymers. Benefitting from its exceptional solvent resistance, the cross-linked network forms a robust physical barrier that effectively shields the underlying PVK from erosion during perovskite solution processing. Moreover, abundant carbonyl groups within the cross-linked network form stable coordination interactions with the perovskite lattice, effectively passivating interfacial defects and thereby enhancing heterojunction stability. Meanwhile, charge transfer between PVK and polymerized PETA (PPETA) generates an interfacial dipole that increases the work function of the PVK layer and thus enhances hole injection. Implementing this strategy yields highly efficient blue emission, with the resulting PeLEDs achieving EQEs of 28.0% at 488 nm, 21.3% at 476 nm, and 15.4% at 466 nm, together with maximum luminance values of 26,240, 10,750, and 2444 cd m−2, respectively, representing state-of-the-art performance among reported blue PeLEDs.

Results

Protective mechanism of the PPETA cross-linked network

Mixed-halide (Br/Cl) quasi-two-dimensional (quasi-2D) perovskites were employed as the blue-emissive layer in this work. Before the solution processing of perovskite films, solubility tests reveal that PVK exhibits high solubility in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Supplementary Fig. 1), implying that PVK film is susceptible to dissolution during the deposition of the perovskite layer onto PVK. Such interfacial damage induces non-radiative recombination and causes local current leakage in devices, ultimately degrading the electroluminescence (EL) performance of blue PeLEDs.

To achieve uniform and compact perovskite films, we introduced a solution-processable and thermally in situ cross-linkable monomer, PETA, as an interfacial protection layer between PVK and the perovskite layer to reconstruct the buried interface. Supplementary Fig. 2 displays the electrostatic potential (ESP) configuration of the PETA molecule. To verify the successful cross-linking of PETA monomer, we conducted Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurement. As shown in Fig. 2a, after thermal annealing at 140 °C, the characteristic stretching vibration peak of the carbon-carbon double bond υ(C=C) at 1635 cm−1 exhibited significantly diminished intensity. Simultaneously, the characteristic peaks associated with the vinyl group (−CH₂=CH), including υ(=C-H) at 3107 cm−1, υ(=CH2) at 3037 cm−1, and γ(=CH2) at 907 cm−1, disappeared completely after thermal annealing. These spectral changes confirm that PETA monomer undergoes effective cross-linking polymerization via its acrylate groups, resulting in the formation of a 3D cross-linked network in the PPETA protection layer (Supplementary Fig. 3). Figure 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4 further illustrate the solubility transition of PETA in isopropanol (IPA) and DMSO before and after thermal cross-linking, showing a clear change from complete dissolution to an insoluble gel state. This evolution visually confirms the occurrence of the crosslinking reaction and the resulting solvent resistance, thereby establishing the foundation for its interfacial protection capability.

To evaluate the protective effect of the PPETA interlayer on PVK, we performed atomic force microscopy (AFM) to compare the surface morphology of PVK films with and without PPETA modification before and after DMSO rinsing. As depicted in Fig. 2c, d, the pristine PVK film exhibits severe surface erosion and increased roughness after DMSO solvent rinsing, accompanied by dramatic morphological change. In contrast, the PPETA-based PVK film maintains its structural integrity with only a slight increase in surface roughness after the same treatment (Fig. 2e, f). This significant improvement in surface uniformity plays a critical role in boosting device performance by mitigating interfacial defects and promoting uniform charge transport. These results highlight that the PPETA interlayer serves as an effective physical barrier to protect the underlying organic HTL from polar solvent erosion and preserve the electronic structure integrity of the buried interface during perovskite deposition. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterizations show corroborative results (Supplementary Fig. 5), further confirming the protective role of the cross-linked PPETA network. To gain more insights into the interfacial chemical composition evolution, we carried out X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The residual PVK content was semi-quantitatively evaluated by monitoring the intensity ratio between the N 1s peak from PVK to the In 3d core-level peak from indium tin oxide (ITO), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6. After DMSO rinsing, the N/In atomic ratio in the pristine sample dramatically decreases from 16.78% to 0.29%, while the PPETA-based sample exhibits modest decrease from 15.35% to 4.77% (Fig. 2g). These results clearly demonstrate that the PPETA interlayer effectively inhibits the dissolution of PVK by the perovskite precursor solution. Furthermore, the introduction of PPETA leads to a slight attenuation in the N 1s signal intensity originating from the PVK underlayer, which can be ascribed to the formation of a uniform and compact overlayer. This observation is further corroborated by Raman spectroscopy analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the characteristic Raman peak intensity of the pristine sample exhibits significant decrease after solvent rinsing. In contrast, the PPETA-based sample maintains nearly identical spectral features under identical treatment condition. These collective findings confirm the exceptional solvent resistance enabled by the in situ cross-linked PPETA protection layer.

Through density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we quantitatively evaluated the interfacial interaction energies among PVK, PPETA and perovskite components. The optimized adsorption configurations (Supplementary Fig. 8) reveal that PPETA possesses significantly higher adsorption energy with perovskite surface compared to PVK (Fig. 2h). DFT calculations verify that the PPETA interlayer facilitates the formation of a stable buried interface through enhanced intermolecular interactions with the perovskite structure. These computational insights establish a theoretical framework that cross-linked PPETA network functions as a robust interfacial bridge to tightly connect perovskite and HTL layers.

Electronic properties at the HTL/perovskite heterointerface

Based upon the successful construction of the PPETA crosslinked protection layer, we systematically further investigated its regulation effect in the interfacial band alignment and carrier dynamics at the HTL/perovskite heterointerface. Differential charge density analysis visualized the charge redistribution at the HTL/perovskite interface (Fig. 3a, c), with pronounced electron accumulation (yellow region) localized around PPETA molecules and corresponding electron depletion (blue region) observed on the adjacent perovskite surface. Figure 3b, d illustrates the planar-averaged differential charge density profiles along the direction perpendicular for the PVK/perovskite and PPETA/perovskite interfaces. Comparison to the PVK/perovskite interface, the PPETA/perovskite system exhibits a significantly larger net charge transfer, as evidenced by the greater magnitude and distinct profile of its integral curve. This indicates a stronger interfacial electronic interaction between PPETA and the perovskite lattice, which primarily originates from the coordination bonds formed between the multiple C=O groups of PPETA and undercoordinated Pb atoms (Fig. 3e, f). The enhanced interfacial electronic interaction strengthens the physical contact between perovskite layers and HTL, thereby promoting and stabilizing the hole injection under bias. In addition, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements demonstrate that PPETA treatment substantially increases the work function (WF) of PVK from 4.37 to 4.78 eV, with a downward shift in the Fermi level (EF) (Fig. 3g). Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) characterization provides a clearer view of the surface potential distribution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, a lower surface potential is observed at the PPETA-modified surface, indicating an increase in its surface work function, which is consistent with the Fermi level downshift measured by UPS. This work function increase is mainly attributed to the interfacial dipole formed between PVK and PPETA. When PPETA is deposited onto PVK, partial electron transfer occurs from the electron-rich carbazole units of PVK to the electron-withdrawing ester groups of PPETA, generating an interfacial dipole that shifts the vacuum level upward at the PVK surface, thereby optimizing the energy level alignment for efficient hole injection22–24. To further investigate the impact of band structure modulation on carrier dynamics, capacitance-voltage (C-V) characterization was conducted. Due to the dielectric property of perovskite, PeLED is considered as a capacitor25,26. C-V measurements reflect carrier transport behaviors under varying applied voltages, including injection, accumulation, and recombination processes. At the beginning, the number of carriers within the device is negligible, so the capacitance of the device can be thought of as its geometric capacitance, which is a constant. As the applied voltage slowly increases, a sharp capacitance increase in PeLED device indicates carrier injection from the charge transport layer (CTL) into the emissive layer, while the subsequent rapid decrease signifies the onset of efficient recombination within the device. As shown in Fig. 3h, PPETA-based device exhibits larger accumulation region capacitance and steeper depletion region edges compared to pristine devices. Notably, the peak capacitance of PPETA-based device appears at lower bias voltages, implying more efficient hole injection and thus more balanced carrier recombination. To further evaluate the effect of PPETA on hole injection, we fabricated hole-only devices with the structure ITO/PVK/(PPETA)/4,4’-N,N’-dicarbazole-biphenyl (CBP)/molybdenum oxide (MoO3)/silver (Ag). The PPETA-based hole-only device exhibits higher current density under the same bias (Fig. 3i), providing direct evidence that the PPETA layer facilitates hole injection at the interface18. Besides, the PPETA-based PeLED yields a much smaller semicircle radius in the Nyquist plot, which further validates the enhanced hole injection and transport with the introduction of the cross-linked PPETA network (Supplementary Fig. 10)27,28.

Regulation effect of cross-linked PPETA network on perovskite films

After clarifying the interfacial carrier regulation capability of the PPETA layer, we further studied the intrinsic properties of perovskite films deposited on pure PVK and PVK/PPETA HTLs. We systematically analyzed the influence of the PPETA layer on optoelectronic and crystalline characteristics of the perovskite films. Based on theoretical calculations, we systematically investigated the interfacial interactions between PPETA and perovskite crystal surfaces. Density-of-states (DOS) analysis of the PPETA/perovskite interface model shows a significant reduction in the density of states near the Fermi level within critical energy regions for PPETA-based perovskite, confirming effective suppression of interfacial defect states (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 11)29. Further projected density-of-states (PDOS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12) reveals that this suppression effect primarily originates from electronic interaction between specific functional groups of PPETA and surface atoms of the perovskite lattice. To gain deeper insights into the atomic-scale interaction mechanism, we computed the electron localization function (ELF) of PPETA on the perovskite surface (Fig. 4b, c). Pronounced electron localization is observed between O and Pb atoms, confirming the strong coupling effect between C=O groups and perovskite surface. This leads to the formation of coordination bonds with distinct covalent characteristics, effectively passivating the undercoordinated Pb atoms on the perovskite surface. By this way, the generation of deep-level defect states near the Fermi level is substantially suppressed. To experimentally study the nature of interfacial chemical interactions, XPS analysis reveals a negative binding energy shift for Pb 4f orbitals (Fig. 4d), with corresponding shifts observed for Cl 2p and Br 3d core level peaks, confirming chemical interactions between PPETA and the perovskite lattice (Supplementary Fig. 13). This interfacial interaction originates from electron donation by C=O of PPETA to undercoordinated Pb2+, which modulates local charge distribution and enhances electrostatic stabilization between Pb2+ and halide anions (Br−/Cl−)30–32. This coordination mechanism is further verified through Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 4e). The downward shift of the stretching vibration of the C=O at ~1728 cm−1 after mixing with PbBr2 is ascribed to the strong electron-withdrawing effect of Pb2+, causing the electron cloud of the C=O to relocate toward the oxygen atom. Consequently, the decrease in electron cloud density in the middle of the C=O results in a reduction of the force constant and a shift to a lower wavenumber33.

The observed decrease in Urbach energy from 78.6 to 62.9 meV reflects effective suppression of band-edge disorder in PPETA-based perovskite films (Fig. 4f). Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) analysis was employed to examine the charge recombination dynamics in perovskite films (Fig. 4g). The PL decay traces were fitted by the tri-exponential model and lifetime parameters were displayed in Supplementary Table 1, indicating that the average carrier lifetime in perovskite films is prolonged from 35.77 to 48.09 ns with PPETA. This notable prolongation implies the effective suppression of nonradiative recombination channels, which can be attributed to the diminished defect density near the Fermi level. Given this improvement, space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements were then performed on hole-only devices with an architecture of ITO/PVK/(PPETA)/perovskites/CBP/MoO3/Ag to quantitatively estimate the trap densities. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 14, the trap filling voltage (VTFL) of the PPETA-based perovskite film decreased from 0.37 to 0.28 V, with a corresponding reduction in trap density from 5.90 × 1017 to 4.46 × 1017 cm−3. This reduction originates primarily from passivation of interfacial defects at the buried PVK/perovskite interface. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 15, the PPETA-based device exhibits a faster EL rise under electrical pulse, reflecting faster and more efficient radiative recombination enabled by reduced defect density and enhanced carrier injection35. PL mapping serves as a robust diagnostic approach for assessing the spatial emissive uniformity of perovskite thin films. Through high-resolution confocal Raman fluorescence microscopy, the perovskite film prepared on pristine PVK substrate exhibits pronounced PL nonuniformity, accompanied by numerous localized quenching centers within a 20 µm × 20 µm scanning area (Fig. 4h). This behavior suggests distinct defect-induced exciton trapping and spatially uneven carrier recombination pathways36,37. By contrast, the perovskite film deposited on PVK/PPETA substrate shows substantially enhanced PL homogeneity and diminished density of quenching centers (Fig. 4i), reflecting a more homogeneous emissive landscape and mitigated defect-mediated carrier dissipation38. To investigate whether the PPETA buried interface affects the phase distribution of quasi-2D perovskite, we performed transient absorption (TA) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 16). The results reveal that the PPETA buried interface leads to a modest suppression of low-n phases with a slight increase in high-n emitting phases, indicating that the influence of PPETA on perovskite phase distribution is minor. The time traces of n = 3 and n = 4 phases were extracted by multiple-exponential fitting to investigate the charge dynamics (Supplementary Table 2), which demonstrate the slightly accelerated energy transfer from low-n to high-n phases in the PPETA-modified perovskite film. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) characterization was conducted to examine the crystalline properties of perovskite films as deposited on different substrates (Supplementary Fig. 17). Compared to the pristine film, the PPETA-based perovskite film exhibits brighter diffraction rings, and azimuthal integration reveals a modest increase in the (100) and (110) plane peak intensities as well as a slight reduction in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of these diffraction peaks. This variation indicates slightly improved crystallinity of the perovskite film by employing PPETA network. AFM images demonstrate that the cross-linked PPETA network leads to a slight reduction in the surface roughness of perovskite films from 6.92 to 6.06 nm (Supplementary Fig. 18), which can be attributed to the improved wettability of perovskite precursor solution on the PVK/PPETA substrate (Supplementary Fig. 19). The improved wettability is attributed to the polar carbonyl and ester groups in the PPETA layer, which increase the surface polarity and surface energy compared to pristine PVK39.

Device structure and performance of blue PeLEDs

Encouraged by the above advantages of PPETA cross-linked network, we fabricated a series of blue PeLEDs with an ITO/PVK/PPETA/quasi-2D perovskites/[1,1’-Biphenyl]−3-carbonitrile, 3’,3”‘,3”“‘-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)tris (CN-T2T)/lithium fluoride (LiF)/Al configuration (Fig. 5a). In this device architecture, PVK and CN-T2T function as the hole and electron transport layers, respectively. The energy-level alignment of the PeLEDs was determined using UPS combined with bandgap estimation from Tauc plots (Supplementary Figs. 20, 21). The device energy-level alignment is depicted in Fig. 5b. Device characteristics are strongly correlated with PETA concentration when constructing the cross-linked interlayer, establishing it as a governing factor in performance optimization (Supplementary Fig. 22). At a PETA concentration of 0.5 mg mL−1, the peak EQE increases from 18.1% to 22.93% at 488 nm, while the maximum luminance is improved from 6908 to 18,960 cd m−2. Compared with the pristine device, the PPETA-based devices exhibit higher EQE values across the entire luminance range. However, excessive PETA (at 2.0 mg mL−1) leads to degraded luminance and EQE due to increased series resistance, accompanied by a marginal rise in turn-on voltage. The EL spectra of devices with different PETA concentrations show nearly identical peak positions and FWHM, indicating that the PETA modification does not affect the emission color purity of perovskite films. Statistical analysis shows that the PPETA-based sky-blue PeLEDs achieve a higher average peak EQE of 26.12 % (versus 15.51% for the pristine) with a smaller relative standard deviation of 14.1% (versus 58.6% for the pristine), confirming the excellent reproducibility and effectiveness of PPETA interlayer (Supplementary Fig. 23). Building on these results, we increased the chloride ratio in the precursor solution to shift the emission from sky-blue to pure-blue and deep-blue spectral regions, achieving EL peaks at 488, 476, and 466 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 24). The corresponding Commission Internationale de l’Élcairage (CIE) chromaticity coordinates are (0.072, 0.277), (0.106, 0.131), and (0.128, 0.069), as illustrated in Fig. 5c. With the optimized PETA concentration of 1 mg mL−1, the champion sky-blue PeLEDs achieve a low turn-on voltage of 2.6 V, a peak EQE of 28.0% at 142.3 cd m−2, and a maximum luminance of 26,240 cd m−2 at 7.0 V (Fig. 5d, e). The angular dependence of EL emission follows a Lambertian distribution (Supplementary Fig. 25). The pure-blue and deep-blue counterparts at 476 and 466 nm yield high EQEs of 21.3% and 15.4%, with maximum luminance values of 10,750 and 2444 cd m−2, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). These results represent state-of-the-art performance in terms of both efficiency and luminance among reported blue PeLEDs (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 4). Besides, compared with the pristine device that shows observable emission redshift with increasing bias, the PPETA-based device exhibits superior spectral stability with negligible EL variations under different operating voltages (Supplementary Fig. 26). The operational stability of these blue PeLEDs was further evaluated at an initial brightness of 100 cd m−2. The PPETA-based sky-blue PeLED shows an extended half-lifetime (T50) of 122.4 min compared to 7.8 min of pristine device (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 27). Under identical testing conditions, the optimized pure-blue and deep-blue PeLEDs at 476 nm and 466 nm achieve T50 of 57.6 min and 25.2 min, respectively. To visually assess the operational stability enhancement by using PPETA, we performed EL imaging on large-scale (400 mm2) devices under various bias (Fig. 5h, i). Apparently, the PPETA-based device maintains higher brightness of pure-blue emission across driven voltages from 4 to 8 V. By contrast, the pristine device shows obvious local quenching and brightness nonuniformity at 8 V, whereas the PPETA-based device preserves uniform EL emission without observable color shift or brightness degradation. The PPETA-based large-area pure-blue device (400 mm2) exhibits enhanced performance with a maximum luminance of 8206 cd m−2 and a peak EQE of 17.27%, along with good emission uniformity across the active area (Supplementary Fig. 28). These findings collectively demonstrate that the robust PPETA cross-linked interlayer enhances interfacial stability and suppresses nonradiative recombination under high electric fields, providing a key strategy for developing efficient and high-brightness PeLEDs.

Discussion

We demonstrate that a robust cross-linked network derived from PETA enables the simultaneous enhancement of efficiency and luminance in blue PeLEDs. Acting as a buried interfacial layer, this polymer network combines excellent solvent resistance with C=O functionalities, allowing it to function both as an effective physical barrier protecting the underlying PVK layer and as a chemical modifier of the perovskite interface. Consequently, the buried grain-growth and charge-transport interface is effectively shielded from solvent-induced degradation. Strong coordination interactions between the C=O groups and the perovskite lattice efficiently passivate interfacial trap states and markedly enhance interfacial stability, resulting in a more robust heterojunction. Moreover, charge transfer between PVK and PPETA forms an interfacial dipole, which raises the work function of the PVK layer and thus promotes hole injection. Leveraging these combined advantages, the resulting PeLEDs exhibit efficient and ultra-bright sky-blue, pure-blue, and deep-blue emission, achieving EQEs of 28.0%, 21.3%, and 15.4% and maximum luminance values of 26,240, 10,750, and 2444 cd m−2, respectively. Our work highlights the critical role of buried-interface stabilization for high-performance blue PeLEDs and provides valuable insights to guide further development of perovskite-based display technology.

Methods

Materials

Cesium bromide (CsBr), lead bromide (PbBr2), lead chloride (PbCl2), and formamidinium bromide (FABr) were bought from Advanced Election Technology Co., Ltd. PETA were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. Poly(9-vinylcarbazole) (PVK), lithium fluoride (LiF), lithium bromide (LiBr), and molybdenum oxide (MoO3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) were bought from J&K Scientific. Organic spacer p-fluorophenylethylammonium bromide (p-F-PEABr) was purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd. [1,1’-Biphenyl]−3-carbonitrile, 3’,3”‘,3”“‘-(1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triyl)tris (CN-T2T) and 4,4’-N,N’-dicarbazole-biphenyl (CBP) was purchased from Nichem. All materials were directly used without further purification.

Perovskite precursor solutions preparation

For blue perovskite precursor solution preparation, CsBr, PbBr2, PbCl2, p-F-PEABr, and LiBr were dissolved in DMSO with a molar ratio of 1:0.28:0.44:0.36:0.1 and a CsBr concentration of 0.12 M was maintained. Ammonium benzenesulfonate and lithium benzoate were incorporated into the perovskite precursor for defect passivation and crystallization optimization. For PPETA-based blue perovskites, various concentrations of PPETA were used as an interfacial blocking layer. For perovskites with emission spectra ranging from sky-blue to deep-blue, the molar ratios of PbCl2/PbBr2 were 1.6:1, 3.8:1 and 5.2:1, respectively. All precursor solutions were stirred in a nitrogen filled glovebox at room temperature overnight before use. The solution was stirred for 2 h before use. PVK was dissolved in chlorobenzene at a concentration of 6 mg ml−1. PETA was dissolved in isopropyl alcohol.

Device fabrication

ITO-patterned glass substrates were sequentially cleaned with detergent solution, deionized water, ethanol, and isopropanol for 15 min each by sonication and then dried at 80 °C for 6 h in an oven. Clean ITO substrates were treated with ultraviolet-ozone treatment for 30 min before use. Devices were fabricated with a multilayer structure of ITO/PVK/PPETA/perovskite/CNT2T/LiF/Al. The PVK solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm polytetrafluoroethylene membrane filter before use. The solution was spin coated onto ITO substrates at 6000 rpm for 30 s and baked at 140 °C for 15 min. PPETA thin layer was deposited by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealing at 140 °C for 15 min. All perovskite films were subsequently deposited onto PVK/PPETA-coated substrates by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 60 s and then baked at 70 °C for 7 min in an N2 glovebox. Finally, the substrates were transferred into a high-vacuum thermal deposition chamber (vacuum pressure <4 × 10−4 Pa). The CNT2T (70 nm), LiF (1 nm), and Al (100 nm) films were successively deposited through thermal evaporation with shadow masks. The device had an active area of 10 mm2 as determined by the overlap between ITO and Al electrodes. Hole-only devices with and without the PPETA interlayer were fabricated following the same procedure described above, with the only variation being the inclusion of the PPETA layer. All devices were completed by thermal evaporation of CBP (30 nm), MoO3 (6 nm) and Ag (100 nm) at a vacuum pressure of <4 × 10−4 Pa.

Device characterizations

The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics were measured with a computer-controlled Keithley 2400 source meter. EL spectra were recorded using a photonic multichannel analyzer PMA-12 (Hamamatsu C10027-01). The EQE of the devices was calculated by measuring the light intensity with an integrating sphere within a spectral range of 300–980 nm. The devices were placed inside the integrating sphere and driven by a Keithley 2400 source meter with a step voltage of 0.2 V. All measurements were performed on encapsulated devices in ambient air at room temperature. The operational lifetime of the encapsulated devices was tested through a 64 channel ZJLS-4 type LED life aging testing system in the constant current mode. The angular light distribution was measured by fixing devices onto a computer-controlled rotating stage. All the characterizations of the perovskite LEDs were carried out in ambient air with device encapsulated. C-V measurements were performed using a Keithley 4200 semiconductor characterization system. The AC frequency (f) was 50 kHz. and the scanning range of DC bias was 0–8 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of the devices at an applied bias of 3.5 V was carried out by an Agilent 4294 A electrochemical workstation using an AC amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range from 1 to 10 MHz. The transient EL (TREL) decay curves of PeLEDs were measured under an electrical excitation with the pulse (duty cycle of 20%) generated by a pulse generator (Keysight 81150A).

Film characterizations

AFM measurements were conducted on a Veeco Multimode V AFM instrument. The field-emission SEM images were captured by the Hitachi Regulus SU8230. The UV-vis absorption spectra were collected with a UV/vis/near-IR spectrometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 950). All perovskite films in absorption measurements were deposited on the glass substrates. The steady-state PL spectra were obtained with a Fluoromax 4 fluorescence spectrometer (Horiba Jobin Yvon) under ambient environment. Time-resolved fluorescence decay measurements were conducted with a Quantaurus-Tau fluorescence lifetime spectrometer (C11367-32, Hamamatsu Photonics) at room temperature with a pulsed laser of 373 nm (pulse width of 100 ps and repetition rate of 5 KHz). The PL mapping of perovskite films was characterized by a confocal PL imaging system (WITEC Alpha300 R) with a 405 nm CW laser (WITEC-405) excitation source. The minimum area for analysis was 20 × 20 μm2. Resonance Raman spectroscopy data for PVK film and PETA-modified PVK film before and after DMSO rinsing were acquired using a 532 nm Raman pump, from a continuous-wave diode laser. The FTIR spectra were acquired on an absorption infrared spectrophotometer (VERTEX 70, Bruker). The spectra were recorded at 2 cm−1 resolution in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. For FTIR measurements, a small amount of pure PETA liquid was measured before and after thermal annealing at 140 °C for 15 min. XPS profiles were measured using a Kratos AXIS Ultra-DLD ultra-high vacuum surface analysis system with a monochromatic Al Kα source (hν = 1486.6 eV, where h is the Planck constant and ν is the frequency of the X-ray photons) with a total instrument energy resolution of 500 meV. The hydrocarbon C 1 s line is formed from amorphous carbon at 284.8 eV for reference energy. UPS measurements were performed in an ultra-high vacuum SPECS photoelectron spectroscopy system using a He I radiation source (21.22 eV), with an applied bias of −3 V and a total energy resolution of 80 meV. During data acquisition, the analysis chamber was maintained at ≈3 × 10−10 mbar. The perovskite films on the ITO substrate were in direct electrical contact with the Au reference sample. This electrical connection ensures that the Fermi level of the perovskite film aligns with that of the Au reference, which is calibrated to 0 eV. The work function (Φ) was calculated using the following equation: hυ − Φ = EFermi − Ecutoff, where EFermi and Ecutoff are the values of the Fermi level position and the secondary electron cutoff edge position in the UPS spectrum, respectively. KPFM characterizations were conducted using a Bruker Icon Dimension atomic force microscope in Kelvin probe force mode. The surface potential of pristine PVK and PVK/PPETA films deposited on ITO substrates was measured under ambient conditions. During the measurement, the conductive substrate of the sample was connected to the sample stage, and an AC bias was applied to the tip while the sample was grounded. The AC bias generated an electric force on the microcantilever, which is proportional to the potential difference between the conductive AFM tip and the sample, allowing for the determination of the local surface potential. TA measurements were performed using a Helios pump-probe system (Ultrafast Systems LLC) coupled with a femtosecond laser system (Coherent, 35 fs, 1 kHz, 800 nm). Probe pulses (320–650 nm) were generated by focusing a portion of the 800 nm laser into a 1 mm CaF2 crystal, while 365 nm pump pulses were produced by an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS 800-fs). GIWAXS measurements were carried out at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The samples were prepared on ITO substrates using the same preparation conditions as for the devices. The data were obtained through an area charge-coupled device detector with 1475 × 1679 pixels at beamline BL03HB. The X-ray wavelength was 1.2398 Å, and the incidence angle was 0.2°. The signal was detected by the Pilatus3s 2 m at an exposure time of 100 s at a distance of ≈287 mm away from the sample. 2D X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using FIT2D software and displayed in the scattering vector q coordinates.

Density functional theory calculations

Molecular calculations were performed on the basis the Gaussian 09 software package at the B3LYP/6-311+G(2d, p) level with DFT-D3. All the calculations are performed in the framework of the density functional theory with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method to describe interactions between ion cores and valence electrons, as implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)40,41. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation was selected for the exchange-correlation potential42,43. The kinetic cut-off energy for plane-wave is set to 500 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10−5 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn–Sham equation. The Brillouin zone integration is performed using a 1 × 1 × 1 Γ-centred Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid44. All the structures are fully relaxed until the Hellmann–Feynman forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.03 eV/Å. Van der Waals interactions were accounted for using the Grimme D3-BJ correction45. The bottom three layers were fixed, while the remaining atoms were fully relaxed. A vacuum layer of at least 20 Å was introduced along the z-direction to prevent periodic interactions. The enthalpy change of the desired reaction was derived by subtracting the total energy of the products and reactants. The adsorption energies (Eads) were calculated as Eads = Ead/slab − Ead − Eslab, where Ead/slab, Ead, and Eslab are the total energies of the optimized adsorbate/perovskite supercell, the adsorbate, and the perovskite supercell, respectively. The 3D isosurfaces and 1D planar averaged results of charge density differences (CDD) between the perovskite supercell and the adsorbate were obtained by subtracting the electron densities of the isolated perovskite supercell and adsorbate from that of the combined system. The charge density difference visualizations were rendered using the VESTA program46.
