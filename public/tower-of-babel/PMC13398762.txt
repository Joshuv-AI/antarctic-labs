Clinical Study of Ultrasound‐Guided Modified Percutaneous Endoscopic Interlaminar Discectomy Combined With Medical Chitosan in Lumbar 4–5 Disc Herniation Treatment
Authors: Liying Cao, Jun Zhang, Mingjing Jiang, Binzhen Lin, Nan Zhong, Xiaofeng Yin, Yiyun Guan, Xiaochun Huang, Kaiming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePercutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) is a preferred minimally invasive surgery for lumbar disc herniation (LDH); however, postoperative recurrence, nerve root damage, and hyperesthesia continue to present key clinical challenges. Chitosan represents a novel preventive approach against postoperative complications. This study aimed to assess the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided percutaneous endoscopic interlaminar discectomy (PEID) plus medical chitosan in treating lumbar 4–5 disc herniation (L4‐5 LDH).MethodsTotally, 200 L4‐5 LDH patients were prospectively recruited and randomly divided into the control group (ultrasound‐guided PEID) and observation group (ultrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan). Low back and leg pain and lumbar dysfunction were assessed preoperatively and postoperatively using Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA). Efficacy and postoperative complications were evaluated according to the modified MacNab criteria. Logistic regression analyses identified the risk factors. Mann–Whitney U test was used for inter‐group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for timepoint comparisons.ResultsThe two groups showed significant differences in pre‐treatment Pfirrmann classification and operation time. As time progressed, patients exhibited reduced VAS and ODI scores and increased JOA scores postoperatively. Significant differences were observed in VAS, JOA, and ODI scores in the observation group at 2 weeks postoperatively. The observation group demonstrated superior treatment efficacy and a lower complication incidence than the control group. Age, disease course, and postoperative bleeding were independent risk factors affecting clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided PEID plus chitosan.ConclusionUltrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan reduces postoperative pain and perioperative complications, demonstrating superior clinical efficacy.Keywords: clinical efficacy, interlaminar approach, lumbar 4–5, lumbar disc herniation, medical chitosan, percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy, ultrasound guidance

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is prevalent among youth, with a higher prevalence in men [1]. This condition arises from lumbar disc degeneration and nucleus pulposus protrusion from the defective annulus fibrosus compressing the spinal nerve root, and is characterized by pain in legs and low back [2]. Clinically, nucleus pulposus protrusions of LDH occur predominantly in the lower lumbar segments, particularly in the L4–L5 and L5–S1 segments, due to the weight‐bearing nature of these segments [3, 4, 5]. Given the impact of LDH on patients' quality of life, it is imperative to explore effective treatment strategies.

Percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) is a safe and effective surgical technique that is commonly utilized for the management of LDH and its associated complications [6]. There are two main surgical techniques commonly employed for PELD, namely percutaneous endoscopic interlaminar discectomy (PEID) and percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy (PETD) [7]. The transforaminal approach is suitable for cases with lateral or unilateral nerve root compression and accesses via the foramen [8]. This approach may involve longer operation time, more fluoroscope frequencies, and demands higher surgical skill, particularly for foraminal or far‐lateral LDH [9, 10]. In PEID, instruments are introduced using a posterior approach through the interlaminar window to access the herniated discs, and the herniated discs can be removed under direct visualization [11]. PEID has demonstrated favorable clinical efficacy in managing L5‐S1 LDH and it offers benefits such as rapid puncture, reduced radiation exposure, and shorter duration [12]. Moreover, the interlaminar fully endoscopic spine surgery demonstrates superior therapeutic efficacy compared to the transforaminal approach in treating lumbar 4–5 disc herniation (L4‐5 LDH) [13]. The use of a V‐shaped blade tip needle‐style scalpel has been shown to shorten operative time for patients undergoing ultrasound‐guided percutaneous A1 pulley release [14]. Furthermore, patients undergoing ultrasound‐guided procedures experience better postoperative pain relief [15]. These findings suggest that ultrasound guidance enhances the accuracy and safety of percutaneous spinal procedures. However, patients undergoing PEID remain susceptible to postoperative complications, such as nerve root injury, postoperative recurrence, burning nerve root pain, and nerve root‐related hyperesthesia [16]. Consequently, it is essential to identify pharmacological agents to alleviate postoperative pain and complications in PEID patients.

Currently, the delivery of anti‐inflammatory biomaterials to the defect site following discectomy presents a highly promising therapeutic strategy for modulating the inflammatory microenvironment [17, 18]. Chitosan, the deacetylated derivative of chitin, which is the second most abundant biopolymer, has emerged as a promising nature‐derived functional material. It exhibits versatile antimicrobial, analgesic, antifungal, and antitumor activities, demonstrates reliable biocompatibility, and can be degraded and absorbed in vivo [19]. Chitosan reduces scar tissue formation, inflammation, and epidural fibrosis after laminectomy [20]. Moreover, chitosan possesses lubricating properties with physicochemical characteristics akin to those of articular glycosaminoglycans. Its film‐forming capability establishes a biological barrier between tissues prone to adhesion postoperatively, thereby reducing friction among nerve roots, dura mater, and adjacent tissues to minimize injury risk [21]. Chitosan exhibits notable antibacterial properties and possesses the ability to modulate its own inflammatory response, thereby alleviating nerve root pain associated with LDH induced by inflammatory stimuli [22]. However, there is a paucity of clinical research investigating the efficacy of medical chitosan combined with ultrasound‐guided modified PEID in patients with LDH. Based on this context, this study aimed to analyze the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID plus medical chitosan for the treatment of L4‐5 LDH. Specifically, (i) this study was intended to compare the clinical outcomes of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID with and without medical chitosan, thereby identifying the advantages of the combined regimen in alleviating pain and improving lumbar function; (ii) we sought to assess the preventive effect of medical chitosan on postoperative complications following ultrasound‐guided modified PEID, with a focus on the incidence of complications between patients in the two groups, thereby exploring its value in reducing the risk of such adverse events; (iii) this article further aimed to investigate the safety of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID plus medical chitosan for L4–L5 LDH by monitoring postoperative changes in vital signs, laboratory abnormalities, and the rate of adverse reactions, thus providing evidence to support the broader clinical adoption of this therapeutic approach.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Longyan (No. LYEY‐KY‐2020‐001) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or their guardians signed an informed consent.

Sample Size Estimation

Assuming equal case numbers between the experimental and control groups (N1 = N2), a one‐tailed test was selected in this study. With α = 0.05 and 1 − β = 0.90, sample size estimation was performed using GPowerNT software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), yielding a minimum sample size of 88 cases. Ultimately, 242 cases were prospectively enrolled.

Study Subjects

A cohort of 242 patients diagnosed with L4‐5 LDH and admitted to The Second Hospital of Longyan between March 2020 and January 2023 were prospectively selected. Then, 203 patients were screened based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among them, 12 patients were excluded for incomplete medical data, 10 cases for significant lumbar spinal stenosis, 4 cases for complication of spinal tumors, infections, or spondylolisthesis or instability, 6 cases for far‐lateral LDH, 7 cases for a history of lumbar surgery, and 3 cases for refusal to participate in the study. Finally, 200 L4‐5 LDH patients were included in the study and were randomly divided into the control group (ultrasound‐guided PEID, n = 100) and the observation group (chitosan plus ultrasound‐guided PEID, n = 100) in a 1:1 ratio using the random number table method. Patients' general information, imaging examination results and follow‐up information were recorded. All procedures in this study adhered to randomization and blinding principles: Researcher 1 first assigned numbers 1–200 to all patients, followed by grouping using a random number table method. Subsequent procedures, including surgery, postoperative assessment, follow‐up, and data analysis, were performed by different researchers. All personnel involved in the study were blinded to each other's assignments to prevent human interference in the randomization process.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with LDH according to the Expert Consensus and Clinical Pathway of Diagnosis and Treatment of LDH (2013 version); (2) patients who exhibited symptoms and signs indicative of L4‐5 herniation, in alignment with the imaging findings; (3) patients whose symptoms had not improved significantly or had worsened after conservative treatment; (4) patients and their families were duly informed and provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria comprised the following: (1) unavailability of complete medical records; (2) evident lumbar spinal stenosis; (3) complication of spinal tumor, spinal infection, lumbar spondylolisthesis or instability; (4) far‐lateral LDH; (5) a history of lumbar surgery; (6) refusal to participate in the study.

Surgical methods: (1) the control group: Ultrasound‐guided localization: An ultrasound pain specialist assisted the surgeon to perform ultrasound‐guided PEID. A preoperative ultrasound examination was conducted to determine the positions of the lumbar segments, midline, and facet joints. On ultrasound images, the sacrum appeared as a continuous hyperechoic arc, which served as a landmark for identifying lumbar segments. The midline was identified by continuous spinous processes and was visualized as a series of upward‐protruding hyperechoic lines with posterior acoustic shadows in the longitudinal plane. On the sagittal plane, the facet joints appeared as hyperechoic arcs beneath hypoechoic bands with inferior acoustic shadows. Next, the positions of the lumbar segment, midline, and facet joints were marked on the skin. Under ultrasound guidance, the puncture needle was inserted into the predetermined position (the lower edge of the superior lamina, the upper edge of the inferior lamina, or the medial edge of the articular process). After anteroposterior and lateral confirmation, a 0.7 cm skin incision was created and gradually dilated under the guidance of the ultrasound, and a working sleeve and endoscope were inserted. The operation began with continuous irrigation using saline to maintain a clear surgical field. Compressive nucleus pulposus and fragmented disc tissues were removed by laminar Rongeur. After that, adherent and proliferated nerve roots and ligaments were carefully dissected to decompress the nerve roots. Fluoroscopic homeostasis was achieved using a bipolar electrocautery knife. Once the operation was completed, the working sleeve was gradually withdrawn. After confirming that the wound was not contaminated, the wound skin was percutaneously sutured and covered with a sterile dressing. (2) The observation group: Following nerve root decompression, the endoscope was withdrawn. Subsequently, 2 mL of medical chitosan (2 mL/vial, Haohai Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) utilized for intra‐articular injection [23] was injected through the working sleeve near the nerve root and articular process using a sterile syringe. The endoscope was then reinserted into the working sleeve to push the chitosan to the area around the nerve root, and the injection maintained for 1 min. Hemostasis was confirmed before withdrawing the spinal endoscope and working sleeve. Other procedures mirrored those followed in the control group.

Pfirrmann classification was assessed via lumbar magnetic resonance imaging, and patients were classified into grade I to IV (from mild to severe) based on the severity of lumbar disc degeneration.

Postoperative follow‐up indicators: Patients underwent regular re‐examinations at spinal surgery outpatient clinic in our hospital, or provided postoperative discharge data via mobile phone, telephone, or other means. (1) Pain intensity: The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was adopted to assess lower back pain and radicular pain in the lower limbs preoperatively and at 2 weeks, 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. Scores ranged from 0 to 10, with lower scores indicating less pain. (2) Lumbar function: lumbar spine function of patients preoperatively and at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively was evaluated using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, which included symptoms, signs, and lumbar function improvement, with a total score of 29 points. The score was directly proportional to the lumbar function. (3) Functional disability: The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) was utilized to assess the lumbar functional disability of patients, including 10 questions (e.g., lifting objects). Each question was scored out of 5 points, with a total score of 50 points. The actual score was expressed as actual total score/50 × 100%. The score was directly proportional to functional disability. (4) Efficacy evaluation criteria and grouping: At 1 year postoperatively, treatment efficacy was evaluated according to the MacNab criteria. Excellent: resolution of clinical symptoms (low back pain, lower limb pain, numbness) and a return to the normal daily life and occupational activities; Good: mild symptoms and slight limitations in physical activities, and no adverse effect on patients' ability to perform basic work and daily living; Fair: relieved symptoms and restricted activities, and disrupted work and daily activities; Poor: no difference was observed before and after treatment, and symptoms might be exacerbated. Patients evaluated as excellent, good, or fair according to the MacNab criteria were assigned into the effective group, whereas those rated as poor were arranged to the ineffective group. The formula for calculating the effective rate was as follows: Effective rate = [(excellent cases + good cases + fair cases)/total number of cases] × 100% [24].

Statistical Analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normal distribution tests were performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Measurement data that conformed to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with the t‐test applied for inter‐group comparisons. Non‐normally distributed measurement data were presented as median (minimum, maximum). Inter‐group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons across different time points within each group were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical data were expressed as the number of cases and percentages, and inter‐group comparisons were made using the Chi‐square test. The logistic regression analysis was employed to identify influencing factors for the treatment efficacy of LDH. In this analysis, Pfirrmann grade II was assigned a value of 1, Pfirrmann grade III was assigned a value of 2, and Pfirrmann grade IV was assigned a value of 3. The differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Comparisons of VAS Score, JOA Score, and ODI Value

The preoperative baseline data analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in VAS score, JOA score, or ODI value between the two groups (all p > 0.05), indicating that the two groups were comparable.

Dynamic follow‐up outcomes demonstrated that as postoperative recovery time increased, patients' clinical symptoms exhibited progressive improvement. Compared to preoperative baseline data, both groups displayed substantial reductions in VAS score and ODI value and an elevation in the JOA score at 2 weeks, and the final follow‐up postoperatively (all p < 0.05). Importantly, the inter‐group analysis revealed that patients in the observation group presented more pronounced improvements as early as 2 weeks postoperatively: statistically significant differences were found between two groups in VAS score, JOA score, and ODI value (all p < 0.05) (Table 2). These findings suggested that the application of ultrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan accelerated the improvement of early postoperative clinical symptoms in patients, demonstrating a short‐term therapeutic advantage.

Evaluation of Clinical Treatment Efficacy Using Modified MacNab Criteria

All patients were followed up for 1 year postoperatively, with 90 patients in the control group and 93 patients in the observation group ultimately included as study subjects. Clinical efficacy was assessed using the modified MacNab criteria. A total of 74 patients in the control group achieved effective outcomes, yielding an effective rate of 82.22%; in the observation group, 87 patients demonstrated effective outcomes, yielding an effective rate of 93.55%. Furthermore, the observation group manifested superior efficacy compared to the control group (p < 0.05, Table 3).

The Occurrence of Surgical Complications

A systematic observation of perioperative complications in two groups of patients was conducted to assess surgical safety. It was found that the overall incidence rate of perioperative complications in the observation group was 8.00% (two cases of dural injury, four cases of intraoperative bleeding, two cases of persistent radicular symptoms such as low back pain and numbness in the lower limbs at 2 weeks postoperatively), which was lower than that in the control group (19.00%; three cases of dural injury, seven cases of intraoperative bleeding, five cases of persistent radicular symptoms such as low back pain and numbness in the lower limbs at 2 weeks postoperatively, four cases of postoperative recurrence) (p = 0.037, Table 4). In the control group, one case involved direct instrument injury during ligamentum flavum removal, and another case involved scraping of the dural sac during nucleus pulposus grasping. In the observation group, two cases involved direct instrument injury during ligamentum flavum removal, and another case involved scraping of the dural sac during nucleus pulposus grasping. Upon identification of dural injury, chitosan injection was immediately paused to address the breach. Repair was performed, and chitosan use was resumed after the dural tear was fully closed and the risk of cerebrospinal fluid leakage was mitigated. Direct contact with the repair site should be avoided, and postoperative anti‐infection measures and cerebrospinal fluid leakage monitoring should be intensified. All patients with complications achieved symptom resolution following standard conservative treatment, and no severe complications, such as infection or deep vein thrombosis, occurred during follow‐up.

Analysis of Influencing Factors for the Clinical Efficacy of Ultrasound‐Guided PEID Plus Chitosan

The above results indicated that the observation group (n = 93) demonstrated superior clinical efficacy and a lower incidence of surgical complications relative to the control group. Patients in the observation group evaluated as excellent, good, or fair according to the MacNab criteria were assigned into the effective group (n = 87, 93.55%) and those rated as poor were arranged to the ineffective group (n = 6, 6.45%) to analyze the risk factors influencing the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided PEID combined with chitosan. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, with therapeutic efficacy acting as the dependent variable (0 = effective, 1 = ineffective) and indicators with p < 0.05 in the univariate logistic regression analysis (age, disease course, Pfirrmann classification, intraoperative bleeding) as independent variables. With regard to the results, older age (p = 0.039, OR = 1.344), longer disease course (p = 0.033, OR = 1.298), and greater intraoperative bleeding (p = 0.040, OR = 1.370) were independent risk factors affecting the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided PEID plus chitosan (Table 5).

Discussion

Main Findings

This study evaluated the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID plus medical chitosan for treating L4‐5 LDH. The findings demonstrated that the combination therapy improved clinical outcomes, as evidenced by greater reductions in VAS scores, ODI values, and JOA scores, alongside a higher overall effective rate compared to PEID alone. Furthermore, this combination therapy was associated with a lower incidence of perioperative complications. Older age, longer disease course, and greater intraoperative bleeding were identified as independent risk factors affecting its clinical efficacy.

Rationale for Ultrasound‐Guided Modified PEID Combined With Medical Chitosan in L4‐5 LDH

The management of LDH commonly involves a conservative approach, which often leads to the spontaneous resorption of the herniated disc [25]. PELD has gained popularity among spine surgeons for managing LDH, as it can achieve similar outcomes as traditional open surgery while offering the advantage of being minimally invasive [26]. Although PELD has demonstrated efficacy in the majority of cases involving LDH, it is important to note its limitations, such as the occurrence of intraoperative fluoroscopy, potential complications, and the associated risks of spinal cord and nerve damage [27]. The interlaminar approach accesses the spinal canal through the interlaminar space to remove nucleus pulposus tissues, which eliminates the need for disc puncture, reduces the frequencies of X‐ray exposures, provides a larger operative space, facilitates more extensive nucleus pulposus removal, and results in less trauma and faster postoperative recovery [28, 29]. Ultrasound‐guided lumbar surgery enables precise localization, reduces the risk of maloperation, shortens operation time, and allows clear identification of the joint cavity while real‐time monitoring of drug injection, ensuring procedural accuracy [30, 31]. Furthermore, chitosan is a naturally derived polysaccharide with distinguished biocompatibility, biodegradation, and antimicrobial properties [32]. Chitosan and its derivatives demonstrate anti‐inflammatory properties primarily by modulating the expression of inflammatory cytokines and related signaling pathways [33]. The interlaminar approach has been found to be superior to the transforaminal approach for treating L4/5 LDH [13], and patients receiving the former experience less postoperative low back pain than the latter [34]. While the transforaminal approach allows targeted delivery to the affected nerve root, it may be associated with higher risks of complications like nerve or vascular injury [35]. Based on the provided context, this research examined the clinical effectiveness of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID plus medical chitosan for treating L4‐5 LDH.

Short‐Term Efficacy of Ultrasound‐Guided PEID Plus Medical Chitosan

Our study revealed that ultrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan was effective in improving early clinical symptoms. VAS score and ODI demonstrate reductions at 1‐day, 3‐month, and the final follow‐up post patients' receipt of PEID, suggesting that all patients experience notable pain alleviation and functionality enhancement following the surgical intervention [36]. Additionally, the mean VAS scores for back and leg pain, as well as the ODI, demonstrate significant improvement in patients with L5–S1 LDH undergoing PELD [37]. Dexamethasone exhibits potent anti‐inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects [38], which have been shown to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting and alleviate postoperative pain [39], yet it has only reported the safety of a low dose of dexamethasone (5–10 mg). Epidural injections of dexamethasone and vitamin B12 alongside PEID improve JOA value and ODI scores 7 days post‐surgery, but other time points do not show significant changes [40]. Moreover, the long‐term use of dexamethasone carries numerous side effects, such as adrenal insufficiency, increased infection risk, and osteoporosis [41]. Furthermore, postoperative blood glucose or pressure elevation and metabolic disorder risks are concerns in diabetic or hypertensive patients [42, 43], which may limit the clinical application of dexamethasone in patients with underlying diseases. Chitosan has been demonstrated to reduce scar tissue formation, inflammation, and epidural fibrosis after laminectomy [20]. Importantly, our study revealed significant improvements in VAS score, ODI value, and JOA score as the postoperative recovery period lengthens. At 2 weeks postoperatively, the observation group (ultrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan) showed more significant clinical improvement compared to the preoperative level alone, showing its superior short‐term efficacy. However, the differences were no longer significant at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. This may be attributed to chitosan being readily metabolized, converted, and absorbed in various tissues and organs in vivo [44].

Clinical Efficacy, Risk Factors, and Clinical Application of Ultrasound‐Guided PEID Plus Medical Chitosan

In this analysis, there were evident differences between the PEID plus chitosan and PEID groups in terms of clinical efficacy. The former group exhibited a more pronounced effective rate compared to the latter group. As for surgical complications, a significantly different overall incidence was observed between ultrasound‐guided PEID plus medical chitosan and ultrasound‐guided PEID for L4‐5 LDH treatment, with the current study data showing that the former group displayed lower perioperative complications than the latter group. Chitosan promotes wound healing, including hemostasis, inflammation, cell migration, proliferation, tissue repair, and regeneration, by stimulating the release of anti‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors [45]. It has also been used for dural repair [46]. However, chitosan is poorly soluble in water with low adsorptivity, which acts as a weak local hemostatic agent, and may have suboptimal hemostatic effects [47]. A recent study has also reported the benefit of chitosan for the management of refractory intraoperative bleeding in the surgical treatment of orbital vascular malformations [48]. Furthermore, the degree of acetylation of chitosan affects the mechanical properties and degradation time of conduits, thereby influencing the regeneration process [49]. Consequently, the protocol of pausing chitosan injection and prioritizing dural repair before cautious reintroduction carries potential additional risks: if the dural defect is not completely sealed or if monitoring is insufficient, repair failure due to cerebrospinal fluid pressure could lead to headaches, infection, or even meningitis; pausing chitosan might lead to epidural adhesions due to hematoma organization or fibrous tissue proliferation, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative nerve root adhesion and recurrence. Next, we performed a logistic regression analysis and found that older age, longer disease course, and greater intraoperative bleeding were independent risk factors affecting the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided PEID plus chitosan. Advanced age is often associated with poor tissue repair, reduced lumbar muscle strength, and higher incidence of comorbidities, delaying functional recovery and attenuating therapeutic benefits [50, 51]. A prior study revealed that older age might be an independent risk factors for symptom recurrence following PELD [52]. It has been documented that PEID is associated with shortened operation time, decreased hospital stay, and reduced intraoperative bleeding [53]. A prolonged disease course is likely associated with more severe nerve root compression, chronic inflammatory responses, and irreversible neural damage, which may limit complete symptom relief and increase the risk of postoperative symptom recurrence [54]. Furthermore, excessive intraoperative bleeding is also linked to the recurrence of symptoms after the treatment of lumbar disc herniation [55]. Thus, the control of these risk factors is crucial to preserve the biological and clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided PEID plus chitosan.

Limitations and Prospects

There are several limitations to consider. First, the long‐term efficacy of chitosan might gradually diminish due to the continuous degradation of its active components. Therefore, in the future, we will explore the combination of medical chitosan with other long‐term treatment strategies to maintain its sustained efficacy. Second, a direct comparative analysis on the postoperative efficacy of dexamethasone and chitosan (alone or in combination) may be a future research direction, contributing to better interpretation of the postoperative effects of chitosan on patients with L4‐5 LDH. Third, chitosan, as an exogenous material, could potentially serve as a nidus for microbial colonization, increasing infection risk. In the future, we will further investigate the correlation between the interlaminar approach and nerve root injury risk and compare the safety profiles of different surgical approaches owing to the lack of relevant findings. Fourth, this study lacks an objective quantitative assessment of the potential risks and anti‐inflammatory effects of chitosan. Future research will incorporate inflammatory biomarkers (such as C‐reactive protein and IL‐6) and imaging indicators (such as the volume of local inflammatory exudation measured by ultrasound) to more comprehensively validate the anti‐inflammatory and long‐term efficacy of chitosan in ultrasound‐guided modified PEID. Moreover, this study was a single‐center investigation with a relatively small sample size. Although existing studies have indicated that PEID or PETD are effective and safe surgical methods for treating calcified LDH [56, 57], caution should be exercised when treating LDH with severe calcification (Grade IV) [58]. The present study did not exclude LDH patients with severe calcification. In future research, we intend to grade the degree of calcification and further investigate the differential clinical efficacy of PEID combined with medical chitosan in various calcification types. Additionally, the one‐year follow‐up period, during which no cases of recurrence or fibrosis were observed, may be insufficient to rule out delayed complications (such as fibrosis or chronic inflammatory sequelae). In the future, we will conduct multicenter prospective studies with a larger sample size and prolonged follow‐up to enhance the reliability of the findings.

Conclusion

To sum up, our study emphasized the clinical efficacy of ultrasound‐guided modified PEID combined with medical chitosan for treating L4‐5 LDH. This approach significantly reduces postoperative pain and surgical complications. Therefore, it represents a safe and effective method that merits consideration for clinical use and further investigation.
