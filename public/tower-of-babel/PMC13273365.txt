Efficacy and Safety of Bloodletting Therapy in Patients with Knee Osteoarthritis: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Shuai Dong, Lan-Xin Wang, Qing-Yu Yu, Duan-Yu Mou, Dong-Sheng Qiu, Guo-Ping Ye, Xi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disease that severely undermines the quality of life in middle-aged and elderly people. Pain is the predominant clinical symptom of KOA. Existing pharmacological treatments often cause side effects including gastrointestinal damage and cardiovascular complications. Common non-pharmacological interventions like exercise therapy also fail to achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes with limited curative effect. As a classic external therapy in traditional Chinese medicine, bloodletting therapy (BLT) has gradually demonstrated promising value in the clinical management of KOA. Despite its growing application, there still lacks systematic evidence-based evaluation on the overall efficacy and safety of BLT for KOA treatment, which restricts its standardized clinical promotion and popularization.PurposeThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of BLT in the treatment of KOA and provide the best current evidence for clinical practice.Patients and MethodsThis study followed the PRISMA guidelines (2020) and systematically retrieved randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from three Chinese databases, two English databases, and one trial registry as of July 10, 2025. The inclusion criteria included adult patients with KOA, BLT as the intervention measure (either alone or in combination), and RCTs comparing it with Western medicine, Western medicine plus rehabilitation, acupuncture, etc. The primary outcome was pain intensity (VAS/WOMAC); the secondary outcomes included inefficient rate (inefficient rate= Ineffective/total number of participants×100%), response rate (response rate = (clinical control+ apparent effect) / total number of participants×100%), quality of life (SF-36) and adverse reactions. The risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane ROB 2.0 tool, and a Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4.1. The quality of evidence was assessed using the GRADE tool.ResultsA total of fifteen RCTs involving 1108 patients were included. The types of BLT included picking BLT, wet cupping, and Zhuang medicine BLT. The most commonly used BLT acupoint is the Ashi point. The comparison types included BLT vs. Drugs (n=4), BLT plus drugs vs. Drugs (n=1), BLT plus drugs and rehabilitation vs. Drugs plus rehabilitation (n=1), BLT plus drugs vs. Drugs plus rehabilitation (n=1), and BLT plus acupuncture vs. acupuncture (n=8). Of all included trials, one was assessed as “low risk of bias”, one was rated as “high risk of bias” and thirteen were assessed as “some concern”. The results showed that BLT improved the response rate by 23% compared with drugs (sodium hyaluronate and ibuprofen tablets) alone. BLT plus rehabilitation reduced the pain intensity by 13.31 scores in the WOMAC and increased the response rate by 80% compared with drugs (diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets) plus rehabilitation. BLT plus acupuncture alone reduced the inefficient rate by 74%, improved the response rate by 44%, and improved the quality of life score (SF-36 scale) by 50.40 scores, and the subgroup analysis showed that when there were fewer bloodletting locations, the better the pain relief effect. BLT plus acupuncture may increase the risk of adverse effects compared with acupuncture alone. The quality of the evidence was generally low or very low, mainly due to methodological limitations and heterogeneity.ConclusionCurrent very low-quality evidence suggests that BLT alone or combined with acupuncture, drugs and rehabilitation, and drugs may have certain advantages in alleviating KOA pain, reducing inefficient rate, and increasing response rate. Compared with acupuncture, BLT plus acupuncture may increase the risk of adverse reactions. However, due to the low methodological quality of the included studies, the conclusion should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with higher quality and larger sample sizes are needed to further verify its efficacy and safety.Keywords: bloodletting, osteoarthritis, knee, efficacy, safety

Introduction

Knee Osteoarthritis (KOA) is the most common joint degenerative disease worldwide, accounting for 79% of all osteoarthritis sites, and is the leading cause of physical disability in middle-aged and elderly people. Globally, an estimated 595 million people had osteoarthritis in 2020, with knee being the most commonly affected joint; cases of knee osteoarthritis are projected to increase by nearly 75% by 2050.1 Its core pathological characteristics include progressive articular cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone hyperplasia and synovial inflammation. Clinically, these changes manifest as knee pain, swelling, stiffness and limited range of motion; in severe cases, joint deformity occurs, markedly impairing patients’ activities of daily living and overall quality of life.2

Evidence from national joint registries and population epidemiological statistics indicates that the clinical and economic burden of KOA is projected to rise substantially in the coming decades.3 Moldovan et al predicted that the demand for primary knee arthroplasty will increase by nearly 80% by 2060, with the most remarkable growth among patients aged 80 years and above, underscoring an urgent need for safe and effective non-surgical treatment options.3

Pain is the most prominent symptom in patients with KOA, typically worsening after activity and improving with rest. In the advanced stage, persistent resting pain or even nocturnal pain may occur.4 The pathogenesis is complex and involves multiple factors such as abnormal mechanical load, soft tissue degradation mediated by inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β, TNF-α),5 and increased nerve sensitivity.6 Commonly used drugs include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular injections of glucocorticoids or hyaluronic acid. Although NSAIDs can relieve pain temporarily, long-term use may cause adverse reactions such as gastrointestinal damage and cardiovascular risks;7 the efficacy of intra-articular injections varies among individuals, and the long-term safety is not yet clear.8

Among non-pharmacological treatments, exercise therapy and physical therapy (such as hydrotherapy) can improve joint function, but some patients find it difficult to persist due to severe pain. Hydrotherapy only provides short-term improvement in function.9 Importantly, despite strong recommendations for non-pharmacological therapies in international guidelines, their implementation in clinical practice remains suboptimal, and evidence for many traditional techniques such as bloodletting therapy is still lacking.9 Surgical treatment (such as joint replacement) is suitable for patients in the terminal stage, but it involves significant trauma, high costs, and a long postoperative rehabilitation period.10 A meta-analysis involving 894 patients with pain showed that compared with drugs, BLT is better in relieving pain and safety.11 BLT is one of the classic external treatments. It releases a small amount of blood by puncturing specific acupoints or local blood stasis, promoting blood circulation, eliminating blood stasis, and unblocking meridians.12 Around 1940, Western doctors also used BLT to treat pneumonia.13

Today, some clinicians believe that BLT (for example, pricking BLT, Zhuang medicine BLT) works by regulating local circulation, removing inflammatory factors, and inhibiting pain signals.14 BLT alleviates KOA by clearing intra-articular blood, iron deposits and inflammatory mediators. It blocks iron overload–induced oxidative stress and chondrocyte ferroptosis, reduces synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation, restores synovial fluid function, and delays disease progression.15 Clinical studies have reported the potential therapeutic effect of BLT in relieving pain in patients with KOA.16,17 Two systematic reviews on similar topics have been published. However, their inclusion criteria and outcomes were inconsistent with this study. One18 was published in 2019, and the included trials were published before 2017 without harmonizing efficacy criteria. In another review19 focused on warm acupuncture, in which only two included trials evaluated the effects of BLT on KOA. Compared with those reviews, the present study has several distinctive features: (1) it includes trials published up to July 2025; (2) it standardizes definitions of response rate (proportion of patients achieving clinical control or marked improvement) and inefficient rate (proportion of patients with no improvement or insignificant improvement); (3) it applies GRADE; and (4) it provides separate analyses for different control comparisons.

Materials and Methods

This study (PROSPERO registration CRD420251044020) focused on RCTs of bloodletting therapy for patients with KOA. We organized this study in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.20 The PRISMA 2020 checklist was provided in Supplementary File 1.

Eligibility Criteria

This meta-analysis included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the efficacy and safety of BLT for patients with KOA, without restrictions on language or publication status. Included studies had to meet the following criteria: (1) Participants were adult patients diagnosed with KOA based on traditional Chinese medicine criteria for knee bi-syndrome, Chinese diagnostic standards (Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Osteoarthritis (2018)21), or international criteria (American College of Rheumatology criteria, ACR).22 (2) The intervention was BLT, an acupuncture technique that involves pricking the surface of the body to release a small amount of blood in order to remove stasis, including all forms of BLT, such as pricking BLT, Zhuang medicine BLT.23 Co-interventions with Chinese herbal medicine or other non-pharmacological therapies (for example, auricular acupoint pressing) were permitted, provided the control group received the same concurrent co-interventions. Trials with differing co-interventions between the intervention and control groups were excluded from this study. (3) Control interventions included other therapies, conventional medication (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, chondroprotective agents, sodium hyaluronate injection), placebo, or no treatment. Notably, the treatment duration of included trials ranged from 9 days to 8 weeks, which is relatively short. Given that KOA is a chronic progressive disease, meaningful evaluation of long-term efficacy and safety generally requires follow-up of at least 6 months after treatment completion. Therefore, the current evidence cannot support conclusions regarding the long-term efficacy or safety of BLT for KOA. This limitation is further discussed in the Discussion section.

Data Sources and Search

Computerized searches were conducted in the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), Wanfang Database, and Embase from their inception up to July 10, 2025. Clinical trial registries was also searched, including the International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number Registry (ISRCTN), ClinicalTrials.gov, the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR), the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP), and the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR).

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two researchers (QYY, YZZ) independently screened the literature and extracted data according to the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (LXW). Information on study design, participants, interventions and controls, follow-up data, outcome data, and analysis data was extracted.

Outcome Measures

Pain is the core symptom of KOA, and therapeutic effect was crucial for clinical decision-making. Therefore, pain intensity was selected as the primary outcome.

Pain intensity, was assessed using any type of pain intensity rating scale, primarily including: (1) Visual Analogue Scale (VAS);24 (2) Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC).25

Secondary outcome measures included inefficient rate (inefficient rate= Ineffective/total number of participants×100%), and response rate (response rate = (clinical control + apparent effect) / total number of participants×100%) and quality of life scores (such as QoL, SF-36) and the incidence of adverse reactions.

The definitions and calculation formulas for “inefficient rate” and “response rate” adopted in this meta-analysis were derived from two published Chinese clinical efficacy evaluation guidelines for traditional Chinese medicine.26,27 Such evaluation criteria have been widely used clinically in Chinese orthopedics and traumatology for a long time; however, they have not been validated against internationally recognized assessment instruments (eg, VAS, WOMAC, SF-36), nor aligned with the core outcome set for arthritis recommended by the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET) initiative.

Inefficient rate and response rate, ten studies28–37 reported inefficient rate, and nine studies28,30–37 reported response rate, using explicit evaluation criteria/tools. These evaluation criteria/tools (for example, Guiding Principles for Clinical Research of New Chinese Medicines,26 Criteria of Diagnosis and Therapeutic Effects of Internal Diseases in Traditional Chinese Medicine27) were summarized and categorized into two types, named “Criterion 1” and “Criterion 2” in this meta-analysis.

Criterion1: Evaluate the effect for diseases, clinical control: pain and other symptoms disappeared, joint activity was normal, score (VAS, WOMAC) reduction ≥ 95%, X-ray showed normal. Apparent effect: Pain and other symptoms disappeared, joint movement was not restricted, score (VAS, WOMAC) reduction≥70%, <95%, and X-ray showed significant improvement. Ineffective: Pain and other symptoms did not improve significantly with joint activity, score (VAS, WOMAC) reduction <30%, and X-ray showed no change.

Criterion2: Evaluate the effect on the primary symptoms or signs, clinical control: disappearance or almost disappearance of major symptoms or signs. Apparent effect: Significant improvement in major symptoms or signs. Ineffective: no significant improvement in major symptoms or signs.

For both types of evaluation criteria, the response rate and inefficient rate were calculated using the following formulas:

Inefficient rate = Ineffective/total number of participants×100%;

Response rate = (clinical control + apparent effect) / total number of participants×100%;

Risk of Bias Assessment

Two researchers (QYY, YZZ) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB 2.0). Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (LXW). The ROB tool covers five domains: randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result.

The evaluation results of each domain were determined as one of the following options: (a) low risk of bias, (b) high risk of bias, or (c) some concerns.

Overall, the methodological quality of the included trials was suboptimal. Most trials lacked adequate randomization methods and allocation concealment, and blinding was generally infeasible due to the nature of BLT, resulting in a high or unclear risk of bias in these domains, including performance bias induced by the lack of blinding, which is reasonable given the characteristics of BLT.

Data Synthesis Methods

Two researchers (QYY, YZZ) independently extracted data from the included studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (LXW). Information on study design, participants, interventions and controls, follow-up data, outcome data, and analysis data was extracted.

Substantial clinical heterogeneity was observed across the included trials due to marked variations in bloodletting methods, acupoint selection, bleeding volume, and treatment frequency, which represented a major source of heterogeneity in this study.

All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.4.1 software. We summarized dichotomous outcomes using risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and continuous outcomes using mean differences (MD) with 95% CI. When different studies used different scales, standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% CI were used. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. If the I2 value was less than 25%, a fixed-effect model (FEM) was used to pool data. If the I2 value was between 25% and 75%, the source of heterogeneity was estimated through subgroup analysis based on bloodletting site, method, frequency, volume, and number of sites. If significant statistical heterogeneity existed (that is I2 greater than 75%) and could not be explained or addressed through subgroup analysis, data were not pooled. If data permitted, sensitivity analyses were conducted to exclude outlier studies. We also performed sensitivity analyses to explore the impact of trial quality on the effect estimates. Furthermore, if more than 10 trials were included in a meta-analysis, funnel plots were used to explore the possibility of publication bias.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation)38 approach. The online Guideline Development Tool (GDT) was used to generate a Summary of Findings (SOF) table (GRADE Working Group, 2020). Evidence for BLT regarding the primary outcome (pain intensity) across all relevant comparisons was presented in the SOF table, and the quality of the evidence body for primary outcome was rated. The GRADE system evaluated the certainty of evidence across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, classifying the quality of evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low to objectively reflect the reliability of the study conclusions.

Characteristics of the BLT of the Included Studies

This study incorporated BLT from both Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and Zhuang Medicine. TCM BLT was categorized into two types: pricking BLT and wet cupping therapy. Picking BLT refers to the use of bloodletting instruments (such as three-edged needles, plum-blossom needles) to prick specific acupoints and the superficial veins around them, with the bleeding volume controlled by finger squeezing. Wet cupping therapy involves applying a cupping jar over the bleeding site to promote increased blood loss, generally retaining the cup for 5–10 minutes. Details of the BLT introduction are shown in Table 1. The two most frequently used sites for pricking BLT were veins such as the popliteal vein28,33,36 (3 trials) and the BL4036(1 trial). The five most frequently used sites for wet cupping therapy were Ashi points29,32,34,45,48,49 (6 trials), EX-LE4, ST35, GB34, and ST3432,34,36,48 (4 trials). The Zhuang Medicine BLT included in the studies was needle-scraping therapy, a characteristic external treatment popular in the Guangxi Zhuang region, which uses an acupuncture needle to scrape and break a small amount of subcutaneous fibers, then squeezes out a small amount of blood, randomly selecting 5 scraping points from the obviously painful areas of the knee joint.47

In summary, the top three bloodletting points were: the Ashi acupoint (7 trials),29,32,34,36,45,48,49 superficial collaterals around the knee joint, the popliteal vein and other veins,28,33,36,49 and the BL4032,36,47–49 (5 trials). Details of the BLT were showed in Table 2.

Characteristics of the Control Treatment of the Included Studies

The control types in the included studies consisted of 5 categories, respectively: BLT vs. Drugs, BLT plus drugs vs. Drugs, BLT plus drugs and rehabilitation vs. Drugs plus rehabilitation, BLT plus rehabilitation vs. Drugs plus rehabilitation, and BLT plus acupuncture vs. acupuncture. The Western medications included in the studies were: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (Ibuprofen Sustained-Release Capsules,49 Celecoxib,36 and Diclofenac Sodium Sustained-Release Tablets29,32,48) for pain relief and inflammation reduction; a cartilage protective agent (Glucosamine Sulfate45) to provide raw materials for cartilage synthesis and alleviate symptoms; and Viscosupplementation Therapy (Sodium Hyaluronate injection35). The acupuncture techniques included fire needling, warm needling, abdominal acupuncture, and filiform needling, among others. These techniques involved puncturing specific acupoints to depths ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 cun (approximately 1.67 to 5.00 cm) using appropriate needling instruments (for example, filiform needles, fire needles). After achieving the deqi sensation, the needles were retained for 30 minutes, during which lifting and thrusting manipulations could be performed. For fire needling, the needle tip must be heated until red-hot before insertion. For warm needling, a moxa roll must be wrapped around the needle handle and ignited during the retention period. The total treatment duration ranged from 9 days to 8 weeks. 5 studies29,32,45,46,49 had treatment durations of 14 days or less; 7 studies28,31,33–35,37,48 had treatment durations between 14 and 35 days; and 3 studies30,36,47 had treatment durations of 45 to 56 days.

Characteristics of Outcome Measures of the Included Studies

15 trials reported changes in pain intensity. Among these, 6 trials30,31,35–37,46 reported the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and 11 trials28–33,36,45,47–49 reported the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC). 10 studies28–37 reported the inefficient rate, while 9 studies28,30–37 reported the response rate. 2 studies37,47 reported quality of life, and 1 study30 reported the incidence of adverse reactions.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the ROB 2.0 tool. The primary outcome in this study included pain intensity, response rate, and inefficient rate; we did not perform separate assessments for each individual outcome.

Eight trials29–33,36,47,48 used a random number table to generate the random sequence, one trial35 used hospital admission numbers for randomization, two trials34,46 used the order of hospital visits for randomization, and the remaining three trials28,45,49 did not specify the method for generating the random sequence. Only one trial37 reported using sealed, opaque envelopes for allocation concealment. All trials were judged to be at low risk of bias in the domains of “Missing outcome data” and “Measurement of the outcome”, as the outcome data were largely complete and unlikely to influence the results, and the outcome measurement was appropriate (n=15, 100%). All trials were judged to have “Some concerns” regarding risk of bias in the domain of “Deviations from intended interventions” (effect of adhering to intervention). This is mainly because blinding of participants and personnel was generally infeasible due to the nature of bloodletting therapy, which inevitably introduces performance bias. Most trials (n=13, 86.67%) raised “Some concerns” regarding risk of bias in allocation concealment. 1 trial (6.67%)49 was judged to be at high risk of bias due to selective reporting, 1 trial (6.67%)31 was judged to be at low risk of bias for selective reporting, and 13 trials (86.67%) raised “Some concerns” regarding bias in selective reporting. Consequently, one included trial was rated as having a high overall risk of bias, while the other trials were all assessed as having an overall risk of bias indicating “Some concerns” (Figure 2).

Estimate Effects

Due to the insufficient number of included trials in one meta-analysis, most of the subgroup analysis could not be conducted. For the results of subgroup analyses that had been conducted, we only reported those that could explain the sources of heterogeneity.

BLT vs Drugs

Pain Intensity (VAS, WOMAC)

Four studies35,37,48,49 assessed pain intensity using different scales.

Two studies35,37 reported that BLT was superior to drugs (sodium hyaluronate injection,35 and ibuprofen tablets37) in alleviating pain. The meta-analysis of two trials evaluating via the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) showed a significant reduction in pain for the BLT group (MD = −1.09cm, 95% CI −1.58 to −0.60cm, 195 participants, I2 = 0%, P < 0.0001) (Figure 3).

Pain intensity (VAS) in BLT vs Drugs.

Regarding the reduction in WOMAC scores, a significant difference was observed between BLT and drugs controls. One trial48 found that BLT provided significantly better pain relief compared to diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets (MD = −14.99 scores, 95% CI −15.68 to −14.30 scores, 92 participants, P < 0.00001).

Inefficient Rate

There was a significant difference in the results between BLT and the drug control (sodium hyaluronate injection,35 ibuprofen37). One trial35 found that BLT was more effective in reducing the inefficient rate compared to sodium hyaluronate injection (RR = 0.30, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.98, 60 participants, P = 0.05). In contrast, the other trial37 showed no significant difference in effect between BLT and ibuprofen (RR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.40 to 2.11, 135 participants, P = 0.83).

Response Rate

Two trials35,37 showed that BLT yielded a 32% higher response rate compared to drugs alone (sodium hyaluronate35 and ibuprofen37) (RR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.63, 195 participants, 2 trials, I2 = 0%, P = 0.01) (Figure 4).

Respond rate in BLT vs Drugs.

Quality of Life

One study37 indicated that BLT demonstrated superior efficacy in improving general health compared to ibuprofen, as measured by the General Health (GH) subscale of the SF-36 (MD = 9.77 scores, 95% CI 4.54 to 15.00 scores, 135 participants, 1 trial, P = 0.0003).

BLT Plus Drugs vs Drugs

Pain Intensity (WOMAC)

One trial45 reported this outcome, which assessed pain intensity using the WOMAC scale. The meta-analysis result demonstrated that BLT plus glucosamine sulfate capsules resulted in greater pain reduction compared to glucosamine sulfate capsules alone (MD = −8.54 scores, 95% CI −10.15 to −6.93 scores, 96 participants, 1 trial, P < 0.00001) (Figure 5).

Pain intensity (WOMAC) in BLT plus drugs vs Drugs.

BLT Plus Drugs and Rehabilitation vs Drugs and Rehabilitation

Pain Intensity (WOMAC)

One included trial29 assessed this outcome using the WOMAC scale. The meta-analysis result indicated that the combination of BLT with diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets, omeprazole, quadriceps functional training, and health education proved superior efficacy in pain relief (MD = −8.60 scores, 95% CI −10.26 to −6.94 scores, 93 participants, 1 trial, P < 0.00001).

Inefficient Rate

One study29 found no difference in this outcome between groups (RR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.26 to 1.38, 93 participants, 1 trial, P = 0.23).

BLT Plus Rehabilitation vs Drugs and Rehabilitation

Pain Intensity (WOMAC)

One trial32 reported this outcome using the WOMAC scale for pain assessment. The meta-analysis demonstrated significantly superior pain relief with BLT plus rehabilitation compared to diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets plus rehabilitation (MD = −13.31 scores, 95% CI −14.20 to −12.42 scores, 60 participants, 1 trial, P < 0.00001) (Figure 6).

Pain intensity (WOMAC) in BLT plus rehabilitation vs Drugs and rehabilitation.

Inefficient Rate

One study32 reported the inefficient rate. The meta-analysis showed no statistically significant difference in reducing inefficient between BLT plus rehabilitation and rehabilitation plus diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets (RR = 0.14, 95% CI 0.02 to 1.09, 60 participants, 1 trial, P = 0.06).

Response Rate

One study32 reported the response rate. The meta-analysis results showed that a significantly higher response rate for BLT plus rehabilitation compared to diclofenac sodium sustained-release tablets plus rehabilitation (RR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.62, 60 participants, 1 trial, P = 0.002), indicating an 80% relative improvement in response rate (Figure 7).

Respond rate in BLT plus rehabilitation vs Drugs and rehabilitation.

BLT Plus Acupuncture vs Acupuncture

Pain Intensity (VAS, WOMAC)

Seven trials28,30,31,33,36,46,47 evaluated pain intensity using different scales. All trials consistently demonstrated that BLT plus acupuncture delivered superior pain relief compared to acupuncture alone. Four trials30,31,36,46 evidenced a significant reduction in VAS scores (MD = −1.10 cm, 95% CI −1.35 to −0.84 cm, 220 participants, I2 = 64%, P < 0.00001) and six trials28,30,31,33,36,47 reported this outcome using the WOMAC scale (MD = −5.57 scores, 95% CI −6.97 to −4.17 scores, 377 participants, I2 = 90%, P < 0.00001).

A sensitivity analysis was performed on the WOMAC outcome by sequentially removing each study. The exclusion of the Xu 2024 trial36 altered the result (MD = −4.03 scores, 95% CI −5.53 to −2.54 scores, 337 participants, 5 trials, I2 = 75%, P = 0.002) (Figure 8). Due to this substantial unexplained heterogeneity, we present the findings descriptively rather than pooling the data. Individual studies showed that WOMAC pain scores were lower in the BLT plus acupuncture group than in the acupuncture-only group, but the magnitude of the effect varied widely across trials.

Pain intensity (WOMAC) in BLT plus acupuncture vs Acupuncture.

Subgroup analysis of trials using VAS scores, stratified by the number of bloodletting sites, revealed a stronger pain reduction effect when a single site was used (MD = −1.41cm, 95% CI −1.75 to −1.06cm, 100 participants, 2 trials, I2 = 15%, P < 0.00001) compared to three sites (MD = −0.72cm, 95% CI −1.10 to −0.34cm, 120 participants, 2 trials, I2 = 0%, P = 0.0002) (Figure 9). This suggests that using fewer bloodletting sites may be associated with better therapeutic outcomes. However, this subgroup analysis was conducted post hoc and should be explicitly regarded as an exploratory analysis rather than a confirmatory conclusion.

Pain intensity (VAS) in BLT plus acupuncture vs Acupuncture.

Inefficient Rate

Six studies28,30,31,33,34,36 reported the inefficient rate. The meta-analysis verified a significantly lower inefficient rate in the BLT plus acupuncture group compared to the acupuncture alone group (RR = 0.26, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.50, 367 participants, 6 trials, I2 = 0%, P < 0.0001) (Figure 10).

Inefficient rate in BLT plus acupuncture vs Acupuncture.

Response Rate

A meta-analysis of six trials28,30,31,33,34,36 confirmed that BLT plus acupuncture yielded a significantly higher response rate compared to acupuncture alone (RR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.74, 367 participants, 6 trials, I2 = 0%, P < 0.0001) (Figure 11).

Respond rate in BLT plus acupuncture vs Acupuncture.

Quality of Life

One study47 reported that the combination of BLT and acupuncture was associated with greater improvement in quality of life compared to acupuncture alone, as measured by the SF-36 scale (MD = 50.40 scores, 95% CI 17.58 to 83.22 scores, 90 participants, 1 trial, P = 0.003) (Figure 12).

Quality of life in BLT plus acupuncture vs Acupuncture.

Adverse Events

One study30 monitored adverse events, assessing a predefined list of 12 potential adverse reactions (including gastralgia, hematochezia, vomiting, peptic ulcer, subcutaneous or intra-articular ecchymosis, aggravated pain after intra-articular injection, nerve injury with impaired joint mobility, fainting during acupuncture treatment, wound infection with suppuration, allergic reactions, aggravated joint pain after acupuncture, and localized pain exacerbation after bloodletting; 2 points were assigned to each item. The safety score comparison indicated a potentially increased risk of adverse events associated with BLT plus acupuncture compared to the control group (Table 3). In summary, only one of the 15 included trials systematically monitored adverse events, and no other studies reported safety outcomes. The insufficient safety data across the included studies preclude any definitive conclusion regarding the safety profile of BLT for KOA. More high-quality studies with rigorous adverse event monitoring are urgently needed to evaluate the potential risks associated with BLT.

Discussion

Summary of Major Findings

A total of 15 RCT studies were included. Very low-quality evidence indicated that BLT increased the response rate by 32% compared to drugs (sodium hyaluronate, ibuprofen tablets) alone. BLT plus rehabilitation reduced WOMAC scores by 13.31 scores and increased the response rate by 80% compared to drugs (sodium diclofenac sustained-release tablets) plus rehabilitation. BLT plus acupuncture reduced the inefficient rate by 74% and increased the response rate by 44% compared to acupuncture alone. It also improved quality of life scores (SF-36 scale) by 50.40 scores. Subgroup analysis indicated that fewer bloodletting sites and lower VAS scores correlated with better pain relief. Notably, this subgroup analysis was conducted as a post-hoc exploratory analysis rather than a pre-specified hypothesis-driven comparison. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and primarily serve hypothesis-generating purposes, rather than being regarded as definitive clinical conclusions, which need further verification by large-scale and high-quality prospective RCTs. Compared to acupuncture alone, BLT plus acupuncture may increase the risk of adverse reactions.

Advantages and Limitations

Compared with previously published systematic reviews on BLT for KOA, the present review provides several updated contributions. Unlike earlier relevant reviews, we included more recently published RCTs, performed comprehensive comparisons of BLT monotherapy and combined interventions (with medication, rehabilitation and acupuncture), and adopted updated methodological tools including PRISMA 2020, ROB 2.0 and GRADE for evidence evaluation.

This study included clinical trials of BLT for KOA, employing the latest methodological quality assessment tools and evidence grading methods. The main outcome measure of this system evaluation was pain intensity and the indicators are more clearly defined and easier to understand. This study strictly adhered to established implementation standards for reviews, aimed to provide the best available evidence for clinical practice.

However, due to the widespread risk of bias in the original trials, the evidence level of this study was low or very low, which affected the reliability of the results. Additionally, due to language limitations, literature published in other countries commonly using BLT for pain management (such as South Korea and Japan) was not included in this study. Most of the included studies originated from China, and the results may be influenced by the context effect.41 Furthermore, the evidence is limited by the small sample sizes and low number of trials per comparison, as well as the exclusive conduct of all studies in China, which restricts geographic generalizability. Importantly, a systematic search of international databases did not identify any eligible randomized controlled trials of pure bloodletting therapy for knee osteoarthritis originating outside China. This geographical gap limits the global generalizability of our findings and underscores the need for international research on this traditional therapy. Moreover, substantial heterogeneity across the included trials was observed in terms of intervention protocols (for example, bloodletting techniques, acupoint selection, bleeding volume, treatment frequency) and outcome measurements (for example, VAS, WOMAC, and composite Chinese efficacy criteria for response/inefficient rates). These methodological and clinical differences may reduce the credibility of the pooled estimates, and therefore the findings of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution. Where possible, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the impact of these sources of heterogeneity. Given the methodological limitations and the low to very low certainty of the evidence, all conclusions drawn from this study should be interpreted with caution.

Hypothesis-Generating Observations for Future Research

Despite the very low certainty of evidence, this study provides preliminary exploratory findings that may inform the design of future research on bloodletting therapy (BLT) for knee osteoarthritis. These findings are not intended as clinical recommendations but as hypothesis-study generating clues for subsequent trials. Three forms of BLT were included: pricking BLT, wet cupping, and Zhuang medicine BLT. Ashi points were the most frequently used sites. Most studies adopted BLT every other day with a blood volume of 3–10 mL per session. For pain control, BLT combined with other interventions (eg, rehabilitation, acupuncture) appeared to yield favorable outcomes in the included trials. Compared with drugs plus rehabilitation, BLT plus rehabilitation was associated with a higher response rate. Compared with acupuncture alone, BLT combined with acupuncture yielded superior therapeutic effects, and fewer BLT sites were related to stronger pain reduction. Notably, BLT plus acupuncture may be associated with a higher risk of adverse events than acupuncture monotherapy. These findings need to be verified in well-designed, large-scale randomized controlled trials prior to clinical application.

Revelations for Future Research

The primary reason for the low quality of existing evidence is the poor methodological quality of included studies. Future trials evaluating the efficacy of BLT for KOA should consider strategies to minimize bias and adhere to reporting guidelines (for example, the CONSORT statement).50 Study protocols should be pre-registered. Researchers should rigorously implement random allocation generation and concealment. Although blinding participants is challenging due to the specificity of BLT, alternative study designs (for example, dynamic randomized controlled trials or partially randomized patient preference trials)51 may be employed to minimize the influence of patient preferences.

The studies included in this meta-analysis exhibited significant deficiencies in reporting quality. Some reports lacked standardization: one study altered the internationally standardized VAS scale from 0–10 to 0–100,37 while two studies did not fully report the bloodletting acupoints.33,48 None of the included studies reported information on the practitioners. It is advisable to improve standardized training for practitioners and corresponding preparations. And the study classified bloodletting into three types, but the needle instruments used for the same type of bloodletting were inconsistent. Therefore, it is recommended to standardize the needle instruments.

Given the limitations and potential value of existing evidence revealed by this study, future research should systematically consider the impact of key operational parameters of BLT (for example, acupoint’s selection, blood volume, frequency of bloodletting, and the number of bloodletting points). In particular, the differences in efficacy of different bloodletting techniques (pricking BLT, wet cupping, and Zhuang medicine BLT) should be compared.

Furthermore, the inflammatory factor-mediated cartilage degradation process in the pathophysiology of KOA remains incompletely elucidated.5 Basic research or molecular biology techniques could be employed to explore the therapeutic targets of BLT. Given our finding that BLT combination therapies may yield superior outcomes, we recommend conducting cost-effectiveness analyses to compare the economic investment and clinical benefits of different combination regimens.

Furthermore, future studies should actively adopt internationally accepted core outcome sets (eg, VAS, WOMAC, OMERACT) to replace non-standardized composite outcomes such as “response rate” and “inefficient rate”, thereby enhancing the global comparability of evidence.

Conclusion

Limited or single-study evidence suggests very low-certainty evidence that bloodletting therapy may have potential benefits across several comparisons.

Regarding safety, only one trial reported adverse events, and there is no clear evidence to establish the safety of BLT.

Among the included studies, the most frequently used BLT protocol was wet cupping at Ashi points, with a blood volume of 3–10 mL per session, performed every other day for 2–4 weeks. This regimen was associated with better pain relief in several comparisons, but the evidence remains very low certainty.

The non-standard composite outcomes (“response rate” and “inefficient rate”) are derived from Chinese guidelines and need alignment with international core sets (eg, VAS, WOMAC, OMERACT).

International, high-quality RCTs with standardized protocols, validated outcome instruments, and rigorous safety monitoring are urgently needed.
