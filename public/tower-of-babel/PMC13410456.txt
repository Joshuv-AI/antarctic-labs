Nonlocal Metaspire: A Scalable Elastic Material Platform With Decoupled Mechanical Modes
Authors: Seung Han Kim, Myung Hwan Bae, Ye Jeong Shin, Joo Hwan Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNonlocal metamaterials, which are metamaterials that interact with non‐neighboring unit cells, have recently attracted growing attention as a new paradigm for wave control owing to their extraordinary band structures. However, existing nonlocal metamaterials remain limited in scalability, making it difficult to implement multiple nonlocal couplings and higher‐dimensional arrays. Elastic nonlocal metamaterials have an additional limitation of mode coupling, which hinders clear investigations of unique nonlocal wave phenomena in individual elastic wave modes. This study addresses these limitations by proposing a new material platform for elastic nonlocal metamaterials, leveraging the “Metaspire” architecture with sequential rotation. Subsequently, the detailed wave motions around the maxon and roton are investigated, and the role of nonsymmorphic symmetry in flexural band behavior is further examined. In addition, actual fabrication and experimental validation are conducted to further support the proposed approach. The results quantitatively demonstrate the high scalability of the proposed method and its suppression of mode coupling. The proposed material platform provides a basis for the development of multifunctional wave platforms, thereby enabling various new‐wave systems and devices.Keywords: beyond‐nearest‐neighbor coupling, elastic waves, mode decoupling, nonlocal metamaterials, programmable dispersion, roton‐like dispersions, sequential rotation

Introduction

Through artificial subwavelength structures [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], metamaterials have enabled dynamic responses that are unattainable by intrinsic material properties, such as bandgap formation [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], backward propagation [13, 14, 15, 16], and flat bands [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Recently, growing interest in the unique physics of nonlocality has led to extensive exploration of nonlocal behavior in various wave‐based systems, including elastic and acoustic metamaterials. Nonlocality is a wave system in which the response at a point depends on the interactions with neighboring regions (local interactions) and distant regions (nonlocal interactions). Owing to the nonlocal interactions, nonlocal metamaterials are known to exhibit unique wave phenomena similar to rotons or maxons in quantum physics. Accordingly, various theoretical studies have been conducted on nonlocal metamaterials. Analytical bandgap prediction via strain‐gradient modeling [22] and tunable roton‐like dispersion via parametric excitation [23] have been explored. Banerjee et al. reported that nonlocal coupling can increase the effective damping and broaden the attenuation bands [24], whereas Edge et al. demonstrated zero‐group‐velocity (ZGV) tuning in discrete Euler–Bernoulli lattices [25]. Lawrie et al. proposed a nondiffracting resonant angular filter using a nonlocal periodic acoustic metagrating, thereby enabling angle‐selective transmission in the k‐space [26]. Nonlinear extensions have further revealed amplitude‐dependent roton‐like dispersions [27] and the use of tailored nonlinearity to reshape band structures [28]. Inverse‐design approaches have also enabled customized dispersion control in one‐ [29] and two‐dimensional systems [30], including complex dispersions with attenuation [31]. In addition, numerous attempts have been made to realize nonlocal metamaterials [32] using various methods, such as delocalized zero modes [33, 34, 35, 36], structural chirality [32, 37, 38], mode hybridization (or mode coupling) [37, 38, 39, 40], as well as to realize dual‐wavevector characteristics [41].

Nonlocal metamaterials realized via beyond‐nearest‐neighbor (BNN) coupling are especially considered here. Among the various approaches for implementing nonlocal metamaterials, BNN coupling provides local and nonlocal interactions via direct geometric connections between neighboring and distant unit cells. This enables direct and tunable control over the nonlocal dispersive characteristics through geometric parameters, offering intuitive control at the design level. Based on BNN coupling, roton‐like dispersion characteristics have been demonstrated in nonlocal metamaterials incorporating third‐neighbor coupling between resonant elastic units in both the elastic and airborne acoustic regimes, which have also been experimentally validated [42, 43]. Building on this foundation, structurally embedded BNN couplings have enabled tunable flexural wave dispersion and backward‐wave propagation in reconfigurable Meccano‐based elastic architectures [44]. This concept has also been extended to one‐dimensional [45], two‐dimensional [46], and three‐dimensional [47] acoustic systems. Zhu et al. confirmed multiple roton‐like dispersive features and multidirectional propagation in two‐dimensional acoustic platforms through experimental observations [48]. In addition to these advances, nonlocal interactions have been explored through alternative structural implementations. Chen et al. introduced cable‐network systems that realize reconfigurable nonlocal dispersion through BNN coupling [49]. Micropolar metabeams with feed‐forward control have also been developed to realize tunable roton‐like and nonreciprocal responses [50], and related piezo‐circuit metabeams have enabled amplitude‐dependent nonreciprocity and topological wave control [51]. In a different structural route, rotor‐in‐rotor structures achieve wave splitting through internal geometric nonlocality [52]. Additional efforts have demonstrated controlled phonon transmission using nonlocal slabs [53] and conduction effects in metawires [54].

As explained above, the main barrier to realizing nonlocal metamaterials via BNN coupling is designing nonlocal connections without any geometric overlap. Excluding approaches based on active control, most previous approaches have largely relied on frontier research [42] to achieve BNN coupling via a side‐to‐side geometric connection. For instance, to realize third‐neighbor coupling, as shown in Figure 1a, a previous approach introduced the side‐to‐side connection to avoid any geometric overlap, as shown in Figure 1b. However, extending this approach to systems with multiple BNN coupling is extremely challenging. For example, when incorporating second‐, third‐, and even higher‐order neighbor couplings simultaneously, realizing these BNN couplings without any geometric overlap becomes highly challenging with the side‐to‐side connection. This scalability problem becomes more severe in elastic cases involving various elastic wave modes. As shown in Figure 1b, the previous approach requires asymmetric structures to avoid geometric overlapping. However, such structures inherently provide coupling between various elastic wave modes. This mode coupling issue obscures the nonlocal dispersive characteristics (e.g., roton‐like dispersion), and ultimately limits the advancement of nonlocal metamaterials. Such limits become more severe for the longitudinal or torsional wave modes, where various interactions occur with the band‐folded flexural wave dispersion curves when mode coupling exists. Although several approaches, such as attaching mirror‐symmetric counterparts, have been proposed to suppress mode coupling in single‐order nonlocal systems [34], these methods are impractical for multiple‐order configurations, as discussed earlier. Furthermore, these two structural challenges, i.e., limited scalability and mode coupling, hinder the extension of such configurations to two‐dimensional or three‐dimensional elastic nonlocal metamaterials. As such, a coherent and rigorous framework for BNN‐based nonlocal metamaterials remains unexplored.

(a) Schematic of a periodic mass‐spring chain with local and nonlocal connections; (b) previously and (c) newly proposed metamaterials to realize the chain shown in (a).

In this study, we introduce a new way to achieve elastic nonlocal metamaterials that resolves the issues related to scalability and mode coupling in longitudinal or torsional wave modes. The proposed nonlocal metamaterial is constructed using the Metaspire structure, whose mass units possess rotational sequencing, as shown in Figure 1c. The case in which a longitudinal wave propagates through the metamaterials is considered to explain the main rotational sequencing approach, as shown in Figure 1b,c. In the case with no rotational sequencing as in Figure 1b, a side‐to‐side connection is essential to achieve the nonlocal connection while avoiding any overlap with the neighboring mass blocks. However, in the proposed metamaterial, each mass unit is sequentially rotated so that BNN coupling can always be introduced using only face‐to‐face connections. In addition, such a rotation does not affect the local connections because the system remains equivalent to the monoatomic mass‐spring chain, despite the sequential rotation. Consequently, the side‐to‐side connection can be replaced with the face‐to‐face connection, and the scalability and mode coupling issues can be overcome. By adjusting the angle for sequential rotation and introducing a proper shape for the mass unit, multiple nonlocal connections are possible without side‐to‐side connections. This design supports scalability toward complex systems, such as multiple nonlocal configurations or two‐ and three‐dimensional extensions, while enabling a mode‐decoupled nonlocal system through a single and simple rotational symmetry that eliminates structural asymmetry.

The proposed approach is clarified by presenting a generalized and systematic material platform for the nonlocal Metaspire, incorporating BNN couplings. This platform enables programmable and versatile nonlocal systems, in which only multiple‐order interactions and higher‐dimensional extensions are solely achieved by tuning the rotation angles of each mass unit. Subsequently, the proposed platform is validated through numerical and experimental evaluations of several cases. In particular, we demonstrate how the Metaspire structure can be harnessed to construct elastic nonlocal metamaterials in which mode coupling is suppressed. In addition, the mechanism for the generation of a roton–maxon structure at distinct Bloch wavenumbers owing to BNN coupling is demonstrated, as well as how the presence of nonsymmorphic symmetry further introduces unique band characteristics in the dispersion relations. Such unique symmetry leads to geometrically structured unit cells exhibiting interesting behaviors that cannot be fully captured by conventional discrete mass‐spring models. These effects are discussed in detail.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we present a generalized design strategy that enables the realization of diverse nonlocal metamaterial configurations, which have proven difficult in previous studies. Subsequently, we explore the scalability of the proposed framework using theoretical and numerical analyses. In addition, we demonstrate that the proposed architecture exhibits mode‐decoupled behavior and conclude Section 2 with additional investigations into the roton–maxon phenomena, as well as several features in flexural waves with a nonsymmorphic symmetry. In Section 3, we provide an experimental validation of the proposed elastic nonlocal metamaterial. Finally, Section 4 concludes the study by summarizing the main contributions and potential future directions.

Theoretical and Numerical Analyses

Design Strategy of the Metaspire

This study overcomes the scalability and mode coupling issues by introducing a design framework that is solely based on face‐to‐face connections. The main idea is to introduce rotational symmetry to each mass. The rotational symmetry is determined by two angles: i) the periodic sequence angle (θp ) and ii) the orientation angle (θs ) of the mass unit, as shown in Figure 2. First, we begin with θp , which defines the relative rotation angle with respect to the neighboring mass units. By rotating θp , the structure can secure a geometric space to place a straight BNN connection through each mass unit. Accordingly, BNN coupling is achieved solely through face‐to‐face connections, avoiding side‐to‐side layouts and, in turn, removing any issues with mode coupling due to geometric asymmetry. Next, θs , which is the orientation angle that determines the shape of the mass unit, is introduced to ensure the scalability of the metamaterial. As can be seen in Figure 2, the mass unit comprises multiple branches, each aligned along the corresponding orientation angle θs,j with respect to the neighboring branch. The number of branches is the same as the number of BNN couplings, and θs,j are properly defined so that each branch provides the corresponding BNN couplings.

Numerical Validation for the Scalability and Mode‐Coupling Issues

In the previous section, the design strategy for achieving a nonlocal metamaterial without a side‐to‐side connection was outlined. Here, the numerical support for the scalability and mode coupling issues is presented. To this end, three nonlocal metamaterials are designed, as shown in Figure 3a–c: (a) third‐order coupling only (1‐3), (b) second‐ and third‐order couplings (1‐2‐3), and (c) second‐, third‐, and fourth‐order couplings (1‐2‐3‐4). The wave dispersion curves of the metamaterials are investigated to check for scalability and mode coupling issues. We numerically obtain the dispersion relations using the commercial finite element software COMSOL Multiphysics. The geometric parameters of the three designed nonlocal metamaterials in Figure 3 are as follows (note that the parameters are expressed with a constant a = 0.035 m): b = 1.2a, c = 0.6a, d = 0.3a for (a) and (b), and d = 0.2a for (c). The lengths of the NN and BNN connections are given by Li = Nia – c (i = 1, 2, 3, 4). The radii assigned to the connections are R 1 = 0.015a, R 2 = 0.02a, R 3 = 0.03a, and R 4 = 0.03a. The connection radii were chosen to satisfy the geometric feasibility condition to avoid any overlap between the BNN connection and the neighboring mass unit branches. This condition is governed by the branch thickness d, the radial position of the BNN coupling, and the sequence rotation angle θp (for more details, see Note S1). The base material of each mass unit was aluminum with a density of 2700 kgm−3, Young's modulus of 70 GPa, and Poisson's ratio of 0.33. For numerical clarity, the NN and BNN connections were assigned the same Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio but negligible density to suppress the complex local resonances in the band structure. This choice was only made for clarity. The key conclusions remained unchanged if the same density as that of the mass unit was used (see Note S2).

Nonlocal metamaterials with (a) third‐order coupling, (b) second and third order couplings and (c) second‐, third‐, and fourth‐order couplings. (d–f) Band structures calculated for the metamaterials shown in a–c, respectively, together with representative longitudinal and torsional mode shapes at the marked points A and B.

Before presenting the results of the numerical analyses, it is important to note that the geometric and physical unit cells differ owing to their nonsymmorphic nature. According to the definition of θp , the geometric unit cell comprises Ntot mass units, as shown in Figure 3a–c. However, considering the physics of the longitudinal and torsional motions, no physical difference exists between the mass units inside the geometric unit cell. In other words, the geometric unit cells shown in Figure 3a–c are physically supercells with Ntot identical masses and springs. Thus, although the geometric unit cell is considered in the simulation with periodicity A, the longitudinal and torsional motions retain the physical periodicity a. Therefore, for the longitudinal and torsional modes, the numerical band structure contains various supercell‐folded bands associated with the larger geometric periodicity A. These folded bands are valid eigenmodes of the supercell representation. For a comparison with the analytical dispersion based on the physical period a, we first interpreted the numerical supercell bands by unfolding them. The corresponding longitudinal and torsional bands were then identified using spatial Fourier analysis, as described in Note S3.

Figure 3d–f plots the analytically and numerically calculated band structures for the three nonlocal metamaterials in Figure 3a–c. The blue and red solid lines denote the numerical results for the longitudinal and torsional modes, respectively, whereas the dashed lines represent the corresponding analytical predictions. Note that the actual numerical calculation contains various supercell‐folded bands, as depicted in the gray curves. For clarity, only the longitudinal and torsional bands corresponding to the physical period a are emphasized for comparison with the analytical dispersion. As shown in Figure 3d–f, all numerical results closely follow the analytically predicted curves. Among them, the (1–3) nonlocal metamaterial in Figure 3a is a well‐known metamaterial that exhibits the roton‐like dispersion curve. As shown in Figure 3d, it exhibits a single maximum (maxon) and a single minimum (roton) for both the longitudinal and torsional wave modes. For the (1‐2‐3) and (1‐2‐3‐4) nonlocal metamaterials, the additional second‐ and fourth‐order nonlocal couplings modify the wave propagation characteristics, resulting in unique dispersion relations. As shown in Figure 3e, the (1‐2‐3) nonlocal metamaterial case retains an overall roton‐like profile; however, the wavenumbers of the maximum and minimum are shifted, and the slope of the dispersion curve after the minimum becomes less steep. In the case of the (1‐2‐3‐4) nonlocal metamaterial shown in Figure 3f, the wavenumbers of the maximum and minimum are also shifted, and each branch contains two maximum and two minimum. This is because cumulative nonlocal interactions generate multiple rotons and maxons across the Brillouin zone. As shown in Figure 3, the nonlocal couplings are clearly achieved with the designed metamaterials, indicating that the proposed design can handle multiple nonlocal couplings, validating its scalability.

In addition, we examined the mode coupling issue of the designed nonlocal metamaterials. We validated whether the proposed design method resolves the mode coupling issue for the longitudinal and torsional modes by conducting an investigation based on the dispersion curve and based on the dynamic actuation. For comparison, we considered two baseline layouts: i) a side‐to‐side connection and ii) a face‐to‐face connection, both incorporating third‐neighbor coupling, as in the (1–3) case in Figure 3a. The geometric parameters of the two configurations were the same as those in Figure 3a, except for the length of the BNN connections in the side‐to‐side case. For the side‐to‐side connection, the same periodicity was maintained; however, the BNN connection lengthened as the inclination angle increased. In this study, the inclination angle was set to 16.7°. Note that this comparison is mainly intended to examine whether mode coupling takes place, and we have designed two metamaterials to have the same mass‐unit geometry, periodic arrangement, target BNN order, and BNN radius.

First, we analyzed the dispersion relations of the metamaterials using the unit cells shown in Figure 4a,b, and calculated the wave dispersion curve via COMSOL Multiphysics. The corresponding dispersion curves are plotted for the side‐to‐side (Figure 4a) and face‐to‐face connections (Figure 4b). Note that the metamaterials exhibit four wave modes—longitudinal, torsional, and two types of flexural modes (a vertical flexural (z‐flexural) mode and a lateral flexural (y‐flexural) mode). As shown in Figure 4a, the dispersion curves of the longitudinal and torsional branches are split and coupled with the flexural waves. Thus, determining which mode corresponds to each branch is very complicated. In Figure 4a, the longitudinal mode (blue solid line) is coupled with the vertical flexural mode (green dashed line), and the torsional mode (red solid line) is coupled with the lateral flexural mode (purple dashed line). This indicates that the inclined geometry, owing to the side‐to‐side connection, induces coupling between the various wave modes. Although the inclined geometry also changes the effective stiffness, such changes do not affect the existence of the mode coupling. This can be verified by adjusting the stiffness of the side‐to‐side and/or face‐to‐face connections; while the stiffness change could alter the dispersion curves, the mode‐coupling features such as the existence of the intersections between different wave‐mode branches are not altered. The mode coupling is mainly governed by the geometric symmetry, as well known from classical theory [55] and previous researches [56, 57]. Consequently, the distorted dispersion curves make it difficult to accurately assess the influence of nonlocality. By contrast, in the proposed metamaterial, the dispersion curves of the longitudinal and torsional modes are not split, forming clear nonlocal dispersion curves. As shown in Figure 4b and its enlarged view, the longitudinal and torsional branches are completely separated from the branches of the flexural waves, enabling an accurate realization of the targeted roton‐like dispersion. In addition, the longitudinal and torsional branches show clear intersections with the flexural branches, indicating that the wave modes are decoupled. The enlarged view of the face‐to‐face dispersion further shows that the colored flexural modes also cross each other without any band splitting. Consequently, the longitudinal, torsional, and flexural modes remain distinguishable in the proposed structure, thereby realizing an elastic metamaterial that is clearly mode‐decoupled over the analyzed band structure.

Using the modal energy fractions, the mode coupling effect can be quantized as follows: If the actuation is longitudinal (or torsional), then only the longitudinal (or torsional) modal energy fraction exists, whereas the other fractions are approximately zero when no mode coupling occurs. However, if a mode coupling effect is observed, other modal energy fractions would become significant, and the mode coupling effect can be quantized by the modal energy fraction.

Figure 5b–e show the results of the vibration analysis. For clarity, the dominant components over the entire frequency range are plotted as solid lines, whereas the extremely small contributions are shown as dashed lines. The previous side‐to‐side connection exhibits a significantly large modal energy fraction for both the longitudinal and z‐directional flexural motions for longitudinal actuation (Figure 5b), suggesting that the mode coupling is significant. By contrast, in the proposed face‐to‐face connection (Figure 5c), only the modal energy fraction for the longitudinal motion dominates, strongly indicating that the mode coupling is almost negligible. The same is true for torsional actuation: the modal energy fraction of the torsional motion dominates for the proposed face‐to‐face connection (Figure 5e), whereas both the torsional and y‐directional flexural motions are significant for the previous side‐to‐side connection (Figure 5d). Together, these results confirm that the longitudinal and torsional elastic modes in the proposed metamaterial are mutually decoupled from other wave modes.

Moreover, the proposed design mechanism is readily extendable to two‐ and three‐dimensional elastic nonlocal metamaterials, as shown in Figure 6. In a direct analogy with the one‐dimensional configuration, rotationally symmetric arrangements are implemented independently along the x‐, y‐, and z‐directions. As a representative example, in Figure 6a, the basic mass unit has a spherical shape, and the connection branches are properly designed with xy‐, yz‐, and zx‐planes, enabling BNN connections along each axis. In particular, the xy‐plane boundary hosts the z‐direction connection branch (pink), the yz‐plane boundary hosts the x‐direction connection branch (blue), and the zx‐plane boundary hosts the y‐direction connection branch (green). Different cross‐sectional patterns are assigned to the x‐, y‐, and z‐oriented branches and rotated accordingly to realize face‐to‐face connections along all three directions without geometric overlap. Further discussion of the geometric arrangement constraints for multidimensional Metaspire architectures is provided in Note S1. Because the cross‐sectional patterns assigned to the x‐, y‐, and z‐directions differ, the three directions are not mutually symmetric. Consequently, the geometric unit cell expands to a 3 × 3 × 3 supercell in a three‐dimensional array, whereas the two‐dimensional case uses the geometric unit cell with a 3 × 3 supercell size, as shown in Figure 6a. Nevertheless, for both higher‐dimensional arrays in the longitudinal mode of interest, the underlying physical unit cell remains periodic with a in three directions (a = ax = ay = az ) in both the two‐ and three‐dimensional arrays, as in the one‐dimensional case. For the COMSOL numerical validations in the three‐dimensional arrays, Floquet–Bloch periodic boundary conditions are applied along the propagation direction, whereas continuity conditions are imposed on the remaining directions. Consequently, the numerical results in Figure 6b show identical roton‐like dispersions along Γ–X (blue solid line), Γ–Y (green solid line), and Γ–Z (pink solid line) independently, and longitudinal branches emerge without the mode coupling issue. These results confirm that the proposed scalable design strategy enables the straightforward extension of directional nonlocal coupling to two‐ and three‐dimensional configurations while preserving mode decoupling.

Further Investigation

Roton and Maxon Behaviors

As previously shown, the proposed metamaterial exhibits suppressed mode coupling for longitudinal and torsional waves. As such, their dispersion curves and corresponding mode shapes are extremely clear, thus providing better insights into the wave motion around the roton and maxon (the minimum and maximum points inside the first Brillouin zone (FBZ), as shown in Figure 7). Herein, several further investigations on the dynamic motion around the roton and maxon are presented.

Before investigating the wave motion, the theoretical background for the roton and maxon is briefly introduced. To this end, we considered the simplest longitudinal mode of the (1–3) configuration as a representative example. This allows a systematic examination of how nonlocality governs the emergence and evolution of the roton and maxon features and the associated wave phenomena. K 1 ​and K 3 represent the stiffnesses of the NN (a) and BNN (3a) connections, respectively. Figure 7a shows the typical dispersion curve with a plot of the local (K 1) and nonlocal (K 3) terms. While K 1 term shows a monotonically increasing pattern (black dashed line) inside the irreducible Brillouin zone, K 3 term has a repeatedly increasing–decreasing nonmonotonic pattern (pink dashed line). Accordingly, when K 3 is sufficiently large, the influence of the nonlocal interaction becomes sufficiently strong, and a local minimum (roton) and maximum (maxon) naturally emerge in the dispersion curve, as shown in Figure 7a.

Based on the results shown in Figure 7, the wave motions at the maxon and roton are investigated. First, the wave motion at the maxon is plotted in Figure 7b. The nth and (n+3) th mass units exhibit almost out‐of‐phase motion. From Equation (13), it can be clearly seen that the K 3 term has a maximum value when the nth and (n+3) th (or (n−3) th ) mass units have an out‐of‐phase motion, suggesting why the local maximum frequency (maxon) is formed with the wave motion in Figure 7b. Similarly, the wave motion at the roton, plotted in Figure 7c, shows that the nth and (n+3) th mass units have almost the same motion so that the overall metamaterial acts as a triad rigid cluster. Again, the in‐phase motion provides zero deformation at K 3 and minimizes the K 3 term in Equation (13), resulting in a local minimum frequency (roton). Therefore, through these behaviors, the nonlocal connection K 3 governs the formation of rotons and maxons, leading to anomalous roton‐like dispersion shaping.

Notably, the above explanation mainly focuses on the effect of the nonlocal connection K 3 on the rotons and maxons. As in Equation (13), the roton and maxon are determined by both K 1 and K 3. Therefore, the maxon does not form exactly when un = −un +3, i.e., k = π /3a, and the roton does not form exactly when un = un +3, i.e., k = 2π /3a. The locations of the roton and maxon on the dispersion curve vary with the ratio of K 3 to K 1. Furthermore, the maxon and roton do not appear when K 3 ≤ K 1/3, indicating that the nonlocal interaction is not sufficiently strong to produce the roton‐like dispersion [29, 42].

Issues With the Flexural Waves and Nonsymmorphic Symmetry

In previous investigations, the longitudinal and torsional waves were the main focus. However, in this study, several interesting points regarding the flexural waves in the proposed nonlocal metamaterial are briefly discussed. As explained, for longitudinal and torsional waves, sequential rotation changes the geometric appearance of each mass unit but does not change the relevant mass or inertia. Therefore, the physical and geometric unit cells are different: the physical unit cell remains periodic with a, whereas the geometric unit cell has length A(= Ntota). However, for the flexural waves, sequential rotation significantly alters the bending inertia of each mass unit. Thus, the physical and geometric unit cells are the same for the flexural wave, and the metamaterial is considered to be a multi‐atomic periodic chain (with supercell periodicity Ntota). Figure 8 shows the band structure of the flexural waves of the proposed metamaterial, as shown in Figure 3a. In this case, the structure is equivalent to a tri‐atomic periodic chain because the three mass units in the unit cell possess distinct bending inertias. In addition, owing to the unique structure of the sequential rotation, the resulting flexural modes have a complicated motion in which each mass unit exhibits translational motion along the vertical (or horizontal) line and the circular path in the yz‐plane. Thus, as in the previous nonlocal metamaterial shown in Figure 4a, two flexural waves are observed in the proposed metamaterial; however, they are not clearly distinguishable into the y‐ and z‐flexural motions. Nevertheless, the dispersion curves shown in Figure 8 show that the flexural waves exhibit wave dispersion characteristics similar to those of the tri‐atomic periodic chain, rather than typical nonlocal metamaterials as shown in Figure 7.

Band structure for the flexural waves of the nonlocal metamaterial shown in Figure 3a.

Interestingly, the wave dispersion curve in Figure 8 suggests that one flexural mode has a typical Bragg gap at k = π / A, whereas the other mode exhibits the band sticking that there is no bandgap. This is owing to the different flexural mode shapes. Representative mode shapes corresponding to the colored flexural bands in Figure 8 are provided in Note S4 to clarify these modal characteristics. At k = π / A, the translational motion along the vertical (or horizontal) line and the translational motion along the circular path are totally decoupled. The wave modes depicted as the green (B) and yellow (D) branches in Figure 8 exhibit purely translational motion along the vertical (or horizontal) line, whereas the wave modes of the pink (A) and blue (C) branches exhibit purely translational motion along the circular path in the yz‐plane. Considering the geometry of the metamaterial, it can be inferred that if each mass unit exhibits translational motion along a circular path in the yz‐plane, the dynamic motions (including the inertia effect) of all mass units become identical. In other words, at k = π / A for the A and C branches in Figure 8, the physical unit cell changes so that the physical periodicity is a and the geometric periodicity is A. Thus, k = π / A is no longer the band edge, which results in band sticking as in Figure 8. Conversely, for the B and D branches, the physical and geometric unit cells remain the same so that the Bragg gap forms at k = π / A.

Notably, the aforementioned band sticking originates from the geometry of the metamaterial and not from the connections that provide the BNN couplings. Such phenomena can also be observed when the metamaterial has only centered connections (which provide NN coupling for the longitudinal and torsional waves). The main physics behind band sticking is nonsymmorphic symmetry. Nonsymmorphic symmetry indicates that the configuration remains unchanged under rotation and partial translation. For instance, the metamaterial in Figure 3a remains unchanged when the overall structure is rotated at θp and moves along the x‐direction with a (A /3), so the metamaterial has nonsymmorphic symmetry. With the structure having a nonsymmorphic symmetry, band degeneracy may occur at the band edge, resulting in band sticking [58].

As explained above, the proposed design strategy creates additional interesting issues with respect to flexural waves. However, because we primarily focus on nonlocal metamaterials, this study only reports on the issues and relationships with nonsymmorphic symmetry.

Experimental Validation

Finally, the actual nonlocal metamaterial is designed based on the proposed framework, and experimental validations are performed. To clarify the scope of the experimental validation, two complementary experiments were conducted. First, the dispersion relations of the Metaspire were experimentally extracted to verify the physical realization of the targeted roton‐like longitudinal and torsional branches. Second, additional dynamic‐actuation measurements were performed at a representative mass unit by measuring all wave modes with given excitation to clarify the mode coupling behavior. To this end, the (1–3) configuration is considered a representative example, in which roton‐like dispersions clearly form and can be experimentally extracted for both the longitudinal and torsional modes. Figure 9a shows the fabricated sample used for experimental validation. The sample metamaterial, comprising 39 mass units, was manufactured using stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing with an ABS‐like photopolymer resin whose material properties are as follows: density of 1140 kgm−3, Young's modulus of 2.6 GPa, and Poisson's ratio of 0.36. The overall design of the unit cell is the same as that in Figure 3a, but with the following parameters: b = 1.2a, c = 0.6a, d = 0.3a, L 1 = a – c, L 3 = 3a – c, R 1 = 0.03a, R 3 = 0.06a, with lattice constant a = 0.035 m. For the actuation, the cylindrically symmetric excitation fixture in the blue dashed circle of Figure 9a was integrated. In the excitation fixture, two types of holes are used to actuate the longitudinal or torsional waves by the shaker. The center hole is used to actuate the longitudinal wave, whereas one of the holes in the wing‐like part is used to actuate the torsional wave. The clamping fixture in the purple dashed circle in Figure 9a is attached to the opposite end of the metamaterial sample. This fixture structure prevents the static deformation of the sample.

(a) Fabricated nonlocal metamaterial and experimental settings for the (b) excitation and (c) measurement of longitudinal or torsional waves. Experimentally measured dispersion curves for (d) longitudinal and (e) torsional actuation, and local response measurements under (f) longitudinal and (g) torsional actuation.

For the first validation experiment, Figure 9a–c shows the experimental setup used to extract the dispersion relations of the fabricated Metaspire. The excitation part of the sample was connected to a vibration exciter (shaker) (type 4809, Brüel & Kjær). Because a single shaker is used, almost purely longitudinal actuation (green box in Figure 9b) is possible, whereas torsional actuation (red box in Figure 9b) generates both torsional and z‐directional flexural waves. The issues related to torsional actuation are discussed later. For the signal input, we used Gaussian‐windowed sinusoidal bursts whose center frequencies are swept from 50 to 3250 Hz at every 200 Hz interval for longitudinal actuation and from 50 to 200 Hz at every 50 Hz interval for torsional actuation. For the measurement, the dynamic responses of each mass unit were measured using a laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV, OFV‐505, Polytec). The measurement points were designated by attaching small reflective films to each mass unit. Here, the measurement points for each excitation case are different: the face of each mass unit is used to measure the longitudinal wave (green solid box in Figure 9c), whereas the tip of each mass unit is focused on the torsional wave (red solid box in Figure 9c). Note that when measuring a longitudinal wave, the LDV should be obliquely aligned at a small angle, which may affect the measured data. Thus, we maintained the same angle for all mass units so that such an effect would not be significant during data processing. After the measurement, the wave dispersion curves were extracted for each actuation case using the well‐known two‐dimensional fast Fourier transform (2D‐FFT) in both the time and space domains. Boundary effects near the excitation fixture and the end of the sample were reduced by excluding the mass units close to both fixtures. For longitudinal actuation, the responses from the fourth to the 38th mass units were used in the 2D‐FFT analysis. However, for torsional actuation, because the signal was much weaker than the longitudinal case, the effective analysis region was limited from the fourth to the 21st mass units, where the torsional response remained sufficiently measurable. The resulting k‐ω amplitude matrix was then normalized for each frequency band by its maximum value, so that the dispersion plots highlight the relative distribution of energy in wavenumber k.

For the second validation experiment, additional dynamic‐actuation measurements were performed using the same fabricated sample and excitation setup. In this experiment, single‐frequency harmonic excitation was applied. Instead of measuring a single wave mode, we measured the longitudinal, torsional, and flexural response components for each excitation at the 20th mass unit. The measurement was done by adjusting the direction of the laser vibrometer. Specifically, the longitudinal wave component was measured by aligning the laser vibrometer along the axial direction (green dotted box in Figure 9c), and the torsional wave component was evaluated by aligning the laser vibrometer along the circumferential direction and measuring two points to extract the pure rotation of the mass unit (red dotted box in Figure 9c). Here, although there are two flexural wave modes, their motions are mixed in the proposed Metaspire rather than being purely separated into independent transverse directions. Therefore, the radial motion of the mass unit was measured to simultaneously capture the flexural wave components. For each excitation frequency, the measured response was normalized by the simultaneously measured power‐amplifier reference, and those normalized amplitudes of each wave mode were compared under each actuation condition. If the mode coupling exists, other wave modes would appear in the measured responses. However, if the other modes are significantly smaller than the actuated wave mode, the mode purity can be experimentally supported. In the experiment, the frequency range is carefully chosen so that all wave modes are well actuated and measured, and none of the wave modes belong to the numerically predicted bandgap.

Figure 9d,e presents the experimentally measured dispersion relations for the longitudinal and torsional excitations. For better comparison, the numerical dispersion curves calculated for the same configuration with the same material properties are shown as white solid lines in Figure 9d,e. Because the torsional case involves the actuation of both torsional and z‐directional flexural waves, as mentioned previously, their dispersion curves (overlaid with gray solid lines) are plotted for the torsional case. The torsional spectrum appears less sharp than the longitudinal spectrum, mainly due to the weaker torsional signal and the resulting shorter effective analysis spatial range, which reduces the k‐space resolution. Nevertheless, both the longitudinal and torsional modes clearly exhibit a typical roton‐like dispersion, closely following the numerical predictions. The flexural modes agree with the numerical results, and the measured spectrum follows the gray prediction lines. In addition, Figure 9f,g show the local response measurements at the 20th mass unit. As can be seen in Figure 9f,g, when the longitudinal wave is actuated, the measured longitudinal wave mode is significant, whereas other wave modes are almost negligible. Similarly, when the torsional and flexural waves are actuated (note that these two wave modes are actuated simultaneously due to the actuation setup), the measured longitudinal wave is relatively small compared with the other wave modes. This supports that the longitudinal wave is indeed decoupled from the torsional and flexural waves.

Perhaps one may wonder that the mode decoupling between the torsional and flexural waves could also be supported with the experimental results. In fact, this can be also seen from the k‐ω maps in Figure 9e. If mode coupling exists between the torsional and flexural waves, band splitting would be expected where the numerically predicted dispersion curves intersect. However, as shown in Figure 9e, no such band splitting is observed at the intersection points. This indicates that the experimentally measured dispersion branches also intersect each other as in the numerical results, validating that there is no mode coupling between the two wave modes.

Together, the clear roton‐like dispersion relations and the local response measurements support the mode‐decoupled behavior of the proposed Metaspire. The three experimentally observed dispersion branches remain well separated throughout the spectrum and are unaffected by other higher‐order bands. The local measurements also show that the target responses are dominant under the corresponding actuation conditions. These results demonstrate that the proposed material platform enables the physical realization of a nonlocal metamaterial with suppressed mode coupling.

Conclusions

In this study, we proposed a novel design paradigm for nonlocal elastic metamaterials using a Metaspire structure. Most existing nonlocal metamaterial designs typically relied on side‐to‐side connection strategies, which inherently had scalability issues that resulted in severe geometric constraints when extended to more complex nonlocal systems. Moreover, their asymmetric connection patterns inevitably induced mode coupling in the elastic regime, thereby limiting the achievable dispersion characteristics. By contrast, the present material platform introduces face‐to‐face connections using a rotational sequencing mechanism that inherently incorporates nonsymmorphic symmetry, thereby resolving both the scalability and mode coupling issues. We demonstrated that complex nonlocal systems can be easily designed by adjusting only the periodic sequence and orientation angles of each mass unit. Crucially, the resulting Metaspire architectures clearly exhibited mode‐decoupled behavior across longitudinal and torsional elastic waves from one‐dimensional implementations to two‐ and three‐dimensional arrays. The proposed platform was validated through extensive numerical analyses and experiments, which confirmed its reliability. Through additional numerical investigations, we identified the roton and maxon behaviors induced by the BNN couplings and clarified how wave motion occurs in each case. Moreover, we revealed distinctive flexural wave phenomena, including band sticking at the band edge, arising from nonsymmorphic symmetry.

Using this platform, we identified application opportunities that had been difficult to access in previous nonlocal metamaterial designs. First, a broad range of nonlocal metamaterials can now be realized using a structurally simple and reconfigurable route. In turn, these capabilities allow the dispersion curves to be flexibly tuned while enriching the attainable band characteristics, such as rotons and maxons. Second, in the elastic regime where complex architectures are typically indispensable, the proposed structures exhibit independent modal behaviors. This distinct advantage allows the application of elastic nonlocal metamaterials across diverse elastic wave‐based systems without modal constraints. These mode‐decoupled characteristics, reinforced by the scalability of the platform to accommodate complex nonlocal couplings, provide a basis for the development of multifunctional wave platforms, thereby enabling various new‐wave systems and devices. Third, the embedded nonsymmorphic symmetry in Metaspire architectures naturally produces symmetry‐enforced band sticking at the band edge. This observation highlights the role of nonsymmorphic symmetry in shaping the flexural band structure of the proposed Metaspire and may provide a useful basis for future investigations of topological wave phenomena in nonlocal elastic systems.
