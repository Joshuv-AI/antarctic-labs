Intermediate clinical endpoints as surrogates for overall survival after salvage prostatectomy
Authors: Navid Roessler, Zuzanna Nowicka, Marcin Miszczyk, Giorgio Calleris, Alessandro Dematteis, Malte W Vetterlein, Simone Albisinni, Henk G Van Der Poel, Paul Cathcart, Giovanni E Cacciamani, Inderbir S Gill, Raj A Persad, Steven Joniau, Rafael Sanchez‐Salas, Joseph A Smith, Robert Jeffrey Karnes, Mohamed E Ahmed, Pawel Rajwa, Derya Tilki, Shahrokh F Shariat, Paolo Gontero, Giancarlo Marra, the Young Academic Urologists Prostate Cancer Working Party
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate biochemical recurrence‐free survival (BRFS) and metastasis‐free survival (MFS) as potential intermediate clinical endpoints (ICEs) for overall survival (OS) in patients undergoing salvage radical prostatectomy (sRP).Patients and MethodsEvaluable patients were selected from a retrospective dataset, resulting in a cohort of 879 patients with recurrent, non‐metastatic, hormone‐sensitive prostate cancer treated with sRP at 13 centres. ICE surrogacy was evaluated using a two‐stage approach: (i) at the individual‐patient level by fitting Clayton copula models and estimating Kendall's τ (τ >0.7 indicating a strong association) and (ii) at the centre level, by fitting weighted linear regression of 5‐year OS on 3‐year BRFS and MFS.ResultsThe two‐step analysis included 759 patients for BRFS (366 events) and 476 for MFS (137 events), with median follow‐up of 37 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 36–42 months) and 34 months (95% CI 31–37 months), respectively. At the individual‐patient level, MFS showed a strong association with OS (Kendall's τ 0.85, 95% CI 0.82–0.88), which was not observed for BRFS (Kendall's τ 0.63, 95% CI 0.56–0.69). At the centre level, neither 3‐year BRFS (R 2 = 0.15; slope = 0.34, P = 0.2) nor 3‐year MFS (R 2 = 0.21; slope = 0.26, P = 0.14) predicted 5‐year OS with sufficient explanatory power. Limitations include the fact that centre‐level analysis was based on single‐arm associations rather than on treatment‐effect surrogacy across arms, and retrospective data collection.ConclusionsThe BRFS should not be used as a surrogate endpoint in the sRP setting. MFS requires further validation in future prospective studies to confirm its association with OS. Patient‐reported outcomes, such as quality‐of‐life and treatment‐related toxicity, should be considered in parallel with OS.Keywords: surrogate endpoints, intermediate clinical endpoint, biochemical recurrence‐free survival, metastasis‐free survival, salvage radical prostatectomy

Introduction

Biochemical recurrence (BCR) represents a pivotal challenge in the management of prostate cancer (PCa), occurring in up to 50% of patients following primary therapy [1]. Given the prolonged natural history and low disease‐specific mortality of localised PCa [2], BCR‐free survival (BRFS) has been used as a surrogate endpoint to expedite trials assessing efficacy of PCa treatments [1, 3]. Several studies showed prognostic value of BCR, demonstrating its association with an increased risk of distant metastases, PCa‐specific mortality (PCSM), and overall mortality [4], and PSA response consistently predicts outcomes of systemic therapies [5]. However, recent analyses based on large datasets from randomised controlled trials failed to confirm BRFS as an intermediate clinical endpoint (ICE) for overall survival (OS) in localised disease setting [3].

Building on the work of the International Intermediate Clinical Endpoints in Cancer of the Prostate (ICECaP) Working Group [6], which defined and validated key ICE for localised PCa, recent meta‐analyses have established metastasis‐free survival (MFS) as a more robust surrogate endpoint for OS, demonstrating high correlations across multiple trials, particularly in radiotherapy‐based settings [7, 8]. MFS better reflects the long‐term prognosis of patients with localised or biochemically recurrent PCa, highlighting the limitations of relying solely on BCR for assessing treatment outcomes [7]. However, MFS events on conventional imaging are comparatively sparse and generally occur several years later than BCR, limiting efficiency of MFS as an ICE in expediting clinical trial results.

Patients with BCR after primary therapy represent a particularly high‐risk population with an increased risk of PCSM [9], and recent comparative data demonstrate that both salvage focal therapy and salvage radical prostatectomy (sRP) achieve comparable cancer‐specific survival, while differing substantially in perioperative morbidity [10]. Validated ICEs are therefore of particular importance in this setting: patient numbers are comparatively limited, restricting trial accrual, and the largely non‐commercial nature of this research field means that resources are often insufficient to sustain the long follow‐up required to record PCa mortality, especially given that modern effective treatments can delay death even in the metastatic setting [11].

In this study, we evaluated the utility of BRFS and MFS as surrogate endpoints for OS in patients undergoing sRP following BCR after primary treatment, using a multi‐institutional retrospective database. The aim of the study was to inform design of future trials testing sRP in the management of recurrent PCa management following primary non‐surgical therapies.

Patients and Methods

We conducted a retrospective multi‐institutional study, collecting data from patients who underwent sRP for local PCa recurrence following non‐surgical primary treatment at 13 referral institutions. Eligible patients had recurrent, non‐metastatic, hormone‐sensitive PCa and were clinically node‐negative (cN0) and free from metastasis (cM0) on conventional imaging. Patients with clinically node‐positive (cN+), metastasis‐positive (cM+), or castration‐resistant PCa after primary treatment were excluded from this analysis. Internal Review Board approval was obtained in accordance with each institution's policy. Data quality was independently verified by two authors (N.R., M.M.), with original centres contacted for clarification in cases of uncertainty or missing information. The outcomes of the complete cohort have been previously reported [12]. In accordance with established ICE surrogacy methodology [13, 14, 15], we evaluated whether BRFS or MFS could serve as surrogate endpoints for OS in the sRP setting by requiring (i) strong associations at both the individual‐patient and (ii) the individual‐centre levels.

Definition of Endpoints

Endpoints were defined as follows: BCR was defined as a postoperative PSA rise on two consecutive measurements or the initiation of salvage therapy due to rising PSA. BRFS was measured from the date of sRP to the first documented BCR, death from any cause, or last follow‐up, with censoring at the last follow‐up for patients without events (BCR or death). MFS was defined as the time from sRP to the first confirmed systemic progression (excluding local recurrence or nodal metastasis only) on conventional imaging or death from any cause, with censoring at last follow‐up for patients without events (systemic progression or death). Deaths recorded later than 6 months after the end of clinical follow‐up were not used as events to reduce potential immortal‐time‐bias arising from the use of census data to verify survival status, and patients were censored at end of clinical observation instead. OS was measured from the date of sRP to death from any cause or last follow‐up, with censoring at the last follow‐up for patients without events.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in using the ‘survival’ and ‘dplyr’ packages for data processing and survival analysis, with visualisation performed using ‘ggplot2’. Follow‐up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. OS, BRFS, and MFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with 95% CIs, including only patients with available data for each endpoint. The weighted regressions employed base R lm() functionality with weights specified by sample sizes. Individual patient‐level joint modelling was done using the ‘Weib.reg.Clayton’ function from the’ Copula.surv’ R package (version 2.0), specifying intercept‐only marginal Weibull models for each endpoint.

Individual Patient Level Correlation between Endpoints

At the individual patient level, we evaluated the association between BRFS or MFS and OS using several different copulas (Clayton, Frank, and Gumbel). We specified the marginal distributions for each endpoint and constructed bivariate copula models to estimate the dependence parameter, summarised by the correlation coefficient (Kendall's τ); a Kendall's τ >0.7 was considered evidence of a strong individual‐level association [8]. For both BRFS and MFS, we used Clayton copula because it provided the best fit according to the Akaike Information Criterion. The analysis accounted for right‐censoring through pseudo‐likelihood. Both the copula parameter (α) and corresponding Kendall's τ were estimated, with bootstrapped 95% CI for τ.

Centre‐Level Association between Survival Endpoints: Analysis by Centre and Weighted Linear Regression

For each centre, summary statistics were computed separately. Centres were filtered, and only patients with available survival times or event status were included in each survival endpoint analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were fit to estimate median survival times and survival probabilities at 36 and 60 months. The number of patients included, event counts, censored counts, median survival time, and survival probabilities at pre‐defined evaluation times are summarised in File S1.

To explore the relationships between survival metrics across centres, weighted linear regression models were fit using the lm() function, with weights equal to the patient counts (n) per centre with available endpoint data. The OS outcomes at 5 years was modelled as dependent variable and regressed on the 3‐year BRFS and MFS survival probabilities, respectively.

Patient Demographics and Baseline Clinical Characteristics

The median (interquartile range [IQR]) age at sRP was 67 (62–71) years. At initial diagnosis, the median (IQR) PSA level was 7 (4.9–11) ng/mL (n = 680), and Gleason scores (n = 746) were 7 in 319 (43%) and 8–10 in 86 (12%). Most patients underwent external beam radiotherapy as primary treatment (56% [n = 488]), followed by brachytherapy (20% [n = 171]), high‐intensity focused ultrasound (8.6% [n = 76]), and irreversible electroporation/laser‐induced thermotherapy (4.1% [n = 36]), with 121 patients (14%) receiving neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), 44 (5%) adjuvant ADT, and 14 (1.6%) both.

The median (IQR) time from primary therapy to BCR prior to sRP was 52 (24–89) months (n = 364). Before sRP, the median (IQR) PSA level was 4.6 (2.6–7.6) ng/mL (n = 859), with a PSA doubling time of ≤9 months in 142 patients (51% [n = 277]). At sRP, 141 patients (25% [n = 563]) received perioperative ADT. Gleason scores at sRP (n = 833) were ≤6 in 55 patients (6.6%), 7 in 480 (58%), and 8–10 in 298 (36%), and surgical margins were positive in 247 patients (29% [n = 866]). Extended lymphadenectomy was performed in in 240 patients (54% [n = 443]). After sRP, most patients had a pathological T stage of (pT)2a–c (46% [n = 403]) or pT3a–b (52% [n = 453]).

Follow‐up Outcomes

The median follow‐up for BRFS was 37 months (95% CI 36–42 months, n = 759), with BCR after sRP occurring in 363 patients (44% [n = 833]). The median follow‐up for MFS was 34 months (95% CI 31–37 months, n = 476), with systemic progression observed in 76 patients (14% [n = 532]); of these, 61 patients developed distant metastases, while 15 patients had lymph node metastases. The median follow‐up for OS was 37 months (95% CI 36–39 months, n = 703), with death from any cause occurring in 83 patients (10% [n = 865]), of which 24 (29% of deaths) were cancer‐specific.

Kaplan–Meier estimated median survival times were 48 months (95% CI 42–63 months, n = 759) for BRFS and not reached for MFS (n = 476) and OS (n = 703). Survival analysis revealed 3‐year survival probabilities of 56% (95% CI 53–60%, n = 238 at risk) for BRFS and 82% (95% CI 78–86%, n = 192 at risk) for MFS, and a 5‐year OS probability of 87% (95% CI 84–91%, n = 185 at risk; File S2; Table 2).

Individual Patient Level Correlation Between Endpoints

Among 832 patients with available data for both endpoints (Table 2), 366 BRFS events and 83 OS events occurred. Kendall's τ was 0.63 (95% CI 0.56–0.69), representing a moderate individual‐level association between the two survival endpoints. For the analysis of association between MFS and OS, data of 617 patients were available with 137 MFS events and 83 OS event occurring during follow‐up. Kendall's τ was 0.85 (95% CI 0.82–0.88), indicating a strong positive association between the endpoints at the individual patient data level.

Centre‐Level Association between Survival Endpoints

A weighted linear regression was performed to investigate the relationship between the 3‐year BRFS and 5‐year OS across centres (Fig. 1; Table 2). The regression coefficient (slope) for BRFS at 3 years was 0.34 (standard error [SE] 0.25, P = 0.2), indicating that longer BRFS was not significantly associated OS. The model's explanatory power was poor (R 2 = 0.15), reflecting limited correlation between these endpoints at the centre level. Similarly, on the centre level (Fig. 2; Table 2), 3‐year MFS was not predictive of 5‐year OS (slope 0.26, SE 0.16, P = 0.14), and the model explained little of the variation in 5‐year OS (R 2 = 0.21).

Supplementary Analysis: BCR vs Metastatic Progression

In a supplementary analysis, Kaplan–Meier estimates were used to assess OS following BCR or metastatic progression (File S3), to examine the relationship between these events and survival while minimising overlap between surrogate endpoints and death. Only patients who experienced BCR (n = 363) or distant metastases (n = 61) were included. At 2 years, the probability of survival was higher for patients after BCR (84%, 95% CI 79–89%; n = 143 at risk) compared with patients after metastatic progression (69%, 95% CI 56–84%; n = 19 at risk).

Discussion

In this multi‐institutional cohort, we evaluated whether BRFS or MFS could function as reliable ICEs for OS in patients undergoing sRP after non‐surgical primary therapy. At the individual‐patient level, BRFS showed only a moderate association with OS (Kendall's τ= 0.63), and this association did not translate into meaningful correlation across centres, where BRFS failed to predict 5‐year OS with acceptable power. In contrast, MFS demonstrated a strong individual‐level association with OS (Kendall's τ = 0.85). However, similarly to BRFS, MFS did not show sufficient predictive value at the centre level, indicating that neither endpoint meets criteria for a validated ICE within the salvage setting. While based on established ICE frameworks, our findings remain exploratory, as formal surrogate validation requires treatment‐effect surrogacy in comparative study designs.

The ICECaP Working Group established MFS as a surrogate endpoint for OS in patients treated with primary treatment for localised disease [7, 8]. However, this relationship requires careful qualification in the sRP setting. MFS is a composite endpoint that includes both the occurrence of distant metastases, closely associated with PCSM, and death from any cause [7, 8, 16]. In localised PCa, PCSM accounts for only approximately one quarter of patients undergoing primary therapy [2, 17, 18, 19]. As a result, the dominant events contributing to MFS are often deaths unrelated to PCa, which inherently increases the observed correlation between MFS and OS. Previous analyses confirmed a robust trial‐level correlation between MFS and OS, whereas other endpoints, including biochemical failure and local recurrence, showed poor correlation with OS [7]. These findings emphasise that MFS remains a more promising ICE, but its predictive value is highly context‐dependent, especially considering limited utility of MFS as an ICE in the sRP setting.

Patients undergoing sRP are selected for adequate performance status and limited comorbidity burden, reflecting the inherent surgical selection bias; this population therefore appears broadly comparable in age and fitness to other contemporary sRP cohorts [12]. While prior local therapy with or without short‐term ADT represents the typical treatment history in this setting, non‐PCa‐related deaths may nevertheless increasingly occur before metastatic progression, thereby diminishing the biological linkage between metastasis development and PCSM through therapy‐independent competing risks. Undetected micro‐metastases remain a major challenge in the salvage setting, as conventional imaging may underestimate disease burden. In our cohort, prostate‐specific membrane antigen‐positron emission tomography (PSMA‐PET) was not available during the study period, which likely resulted in under‐detection of metastatic disease and potential misclassification of MFS events. Incorporating PSMA‐PET in future studies could improve detection, refine risk stratification, and guide individualised treatment planning [20, 21]. Earlier identification of disease may also weaken the association between MFS events and PCSM by detecting patients in a potentially curable oligometastatic state and enabling personalised metastasis‐directed therapies [22].

In contrast to MFS, BRFS has consistently demonstrated only weak surrogacy, and our findings reinforce this limitation in the salvage setting. BCR represents an early and frequent event that is only modestly associated with PCSM, while non‐PCa‐related deaths constitute the predominant cause of mortality in most contemporary cohorts [9]. As a result, treatment effects on BRFS (or BCR‐related endpoints) show limited ability to reflect downstream effects on survival [7]. In the context of sRP, where competing comorbidities and heterogeneous progression patterns further diminish the prognostic relevance of PSA‐based endpoints, BRFS lacks the biological and clinical coherence required of a surrogate for OS. Accordingly, BRFS cannot be recommended as a reliable endpoint. In the sRP setting, future studies may benefit from incorporating patient‐reported outcomes, treatment‐related toxicity, and measures of treatment intensification alongside traditional survival endpoints, reflecting the multifactorial nature of clinical benefit in a pre‐treated population where competing risks increasingly influence OS.

The results of this study should be interpreted considering several limitations. The retrospective, multi‐institutional design introduces potential selection bias as well as substantial centre‐level heterogeneity in surgical techniques, pathological assessment, postoperative management, and follow‐up schedules, all of which may have influenced time‐to‐event endpoints. The relatively high proportion of Gleason ≤6 disease at initial diagnosis likely reflects historical biopsy practices, including limited sextant sampling, which may have underestimated tumour grade. The short median follow‐up and limited number of OS events reflect immaturity of survival data in this cohort. Given the long natural history of PCa, particularly in the salvage setting, this may have limited the precision and stability of survival estimates and reduced the ability to detect robust associations between BRFS, MFS, and OS. The heterogeneity of primary treatment modalities preceding sRP represents an additional limitation, as stratified analyses by primary treatment type were not feasible due to limited event counts within individual subgroups. Incomplete availability of key variables across centres precluded robust risk stratification and their association with survival outcomes. In addition, the absence of comparative treatment arms and incomplete information on post‐recurrence systemic therapies preclude formal surrogate endpoint validation within the ICECaP framework and restrict interpretation of these findings to exploratory, single‐arm prognostic associations rather than treatment‐effect surrogacy. Assessment of metastatic progression relied exclusively on conventional imaging modalities throughout the study period, without incorporation of PSMA‐PET imaging, which likely resulted in under‐detection of metastatic disease and potential misclassification of MFS events. MFS could only be evaluated in a subset of patients due to incomplete imaging or follow‐up data required to reliably document metastatic progression. This reduced the analytical sample size and may have introduced selection bias if patients with missing data differed systematically from those included in the MFS analysis. Finally, incomplete information on patient comorbidities, competing causes of death, and ADT exposure before or after sRP limited adjustment for their potential confounding effects on ICEs and OS.

Conclusion

Conducting adequately powered studies in the salvage setting remains inherently challenging due to limited patient numbers and the long follow‐up required to observe survival events, as reflected in the rarity of sRP in contemporary practice. BRFS should not be used as a surrogate endpoint in sRP setting. While MFS showed some correlation at the patient level in our analysis, it cannot be considered a fully validated endpoint in this setting. MFS therefore requires further validation to assess its relationship with OS. Patient‐reported outcomes, such as quality‐of‐life and treatment‐related toxicity, should be considered in parallel with OS.
