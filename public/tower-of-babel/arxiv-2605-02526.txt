Set-Based Training of Neural Barrier Certificates for Safety Verification of Dynamical Systems
Miriam Kranzlmüller, Lukas Koller, Tobias Ladner, Matthias Althoff

Abstract
Barrier certificates are scalar functions over the state space of dynamical systems that separate all unsafe states from all reachable states. The existence of a barrier certificate formally verifies the safety of the dynamical system. Recent approaches synthesize barrier certificates by iteratively training a neural network. In each iteration, the candidate is formally verified—if successful, the barrier certificate is found. Instead, we propose a set-based training approach that tightly integrates verification into training via a set-based loss function that soundly encodes all barrier certificate properties. A loss of zero formally proves the validity of the barrier certificate, collapsing the iterative training and verification into a single training procedure. Our experiments demonstrate that our set-based training approach scales well with the system dimension and naturally handles complex nonlinear dynamics.

•

A novel approach to synthesize barrier certificates in a single set-based training procedure, eliminating the training-verification cycle.

•

A sound set-based loss that encodes the properties of a barrier certificate.

•

An iterative refinement procedure to dynamically enclose the zero-level set and a sound enclosure of the Lie derivative required for property P III.

•

An extensive evaluation on diverse benchmarks with linear, polynomial, and nonpolynomial dynamics. Moreover, we include ablation studies to justify our design choices.

II-B

Dynamic Systems and Barrier Certificates

We consider continuous-time dynamical systems that are modeled as ordinary differential equations and operate within a continuous state space
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
38
, Sec. 2]
:

\dot{x}(t)=f(x),

(1)

where
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuous vector field.
We want to formally verify the safety of such a dynamical system by
providing a guarantee that, starting from any initial state in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{I}}
, the system never enters a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.
To prove this, we require the Lie derivative.

Definition 1

(Lie Derivative,
[
54
, Def. 2.2]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector field. The Lie derivative of a continuously differentiable function
B_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
w.r.t.
f
,
\mathcal{L}_{f}B_{\theta}(x)\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\mathcal{L}_{f}B_{\theta}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial B_{\theta}}{\partial x_{(i)}}(x)\cdot f_{(i)}(x)\right)\text{.}

A barrier certificate is a scalar function over the state space, which separates unsafe states and reachable states along its zero-level set.
The following theorem characterizes barrier certificates to prove the safety of continuous-time dynamical systems.

Theorem 1

(Barrier Certificates,
[
54
, Thm. 2.3]
)
.

The system in (
1
) is safe, i.e., there is no trajectory starting in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{I}}
that reaches any state in
\mathcal{X}_{U}
,
if there exists a differentiable function
B\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}\colon

\displaystyle B(x)>0\text{,}

(P I)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{I}}\colon

\displaystyle B(x)\leq 0\text{,}

(P II)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}\colon

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f}B(x)<0\text{,}

(P III)

where
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid B(x)=0\}
is the zero-level set.

\displaystyle h_{0}

1
Set-Based Forward Propagation

To compute the set-based loss function required for our approach, we conservatively propagate zonotopes through all layers of the neural network.
The image of a linear layer is computed by applying an affine map (
2
).
The image of nonlinear layers must be enclosed, as zonotopes are not closed under nonlinear functions.

Proposition 1

(Set-Based Forward Prop.,
[
31
, Sec. 2.3]
)
.

Given a neural network
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
and an input set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
, an enclosure
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
of the output set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}=\Phi(\mathcal{X})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, is computed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}W_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k-1}+b_{k}&\text{if $k$-th layer is linear,}\\
\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\phi_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}\text{,}

where
k\in[\kappa]
and
\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\phi_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\supseteq\phi_{k}(\mathcal{H}_{k-1})

k

We write
\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\Phi,\mathcal{X}\right)=\mathcal{Y}
to denote the set propagation through the entire neural network.

Set-Based Training

Standard loss functions cannot provide safety guarantees, because they evaluate the neural network at finitely many output points.
Therefore, we use set-based training, which generalizes standard neural network training by integrating the set propagation into the training process
[
27
]

Using the set propagation during training, we can define set-based loss functions, i.e., loss functions defined for entire output sets.
Intuitively, the goal of set-based neural network training is to minimize a set-based loss function
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathscr{K}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}})\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\to\mathbb{R}

\min_{\theta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{X},t)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\mathcal{Y}=\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\Phi_{\theta},\mathcal{X}\right)\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y},t)\text{,}

(5)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a dataset containing input sets
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
and targets
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}

III-A

Sound Set-Based Loss Function

In this subsection, we define a set-based loss function that can verify the validity of a barrier certificate.
For each P I – P III, we define a set-based loss function that can verify the corresponding property.

P I requires all unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}
to be mapped to positive values by
B_{\theta}
.
We enclose the image of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}
using
proposition

1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}}=\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(B_{\theta},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}\right)\supseteq B_{\theta}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}})\text{,}

(6)

and the loss function penalizes the lower bound
\underline{y_{\mathrm{U}}}
of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}}
, i.e., for
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}}\subseteq\left[\underline{y_{\mathrm{U}}},\overline{y_{\mathrm{U}}}\right]
and some small
\epsilon>0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}})\coloneqq\max(0,-\underline{y_{\mathrm{U}}}+\epsilon)\text{.}

(7)

Intuitively, if
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}})=0
, then
-\underline{y_{\mathrm{U}}}+\epsilon\leq 0\implies\underline{y_{\mathrm{U}}}>0
and hence P I is satisfied.
We require a small
\epsilon>0
to ensure a strict inequality.

P II requires all initial states to be mapped to non-positive values.
Hence, we define the set-based loss analogously to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}
, i.e., for
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}}=\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(B_{\theta},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{I}}\right)\supseteq B_{\theta}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{I}})
with bounds
\left[\underline{y_{\mathrm{I}}},\overline{y_{\mathrm{I}}}\right]\supseteq\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}}
, the loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}})\coloneqq\max(0,\overline{y_{\mathrm{I}}})\text{.}

(8)

Again, if
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}})=0
, then
\overline{y_{\mathrm{I}}}\leq 0
and hence P II is satisfied.

P III requires the Lie derivative of all zero-level points to be negative. The zero-level set
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}
is not known given
\mathcal{X}
and
B_{\theta}
, and changes after each training loop as the parameters
\theta
are updated.
Thus, we compute a zero-level set enclosure
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{Z}}
from
\mathcal{X}
in each training iteration; the details are deferred to
section

III-B
.

On a high-level, we enclose the zero-level set using a union of zonotopes:

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\lambda}{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}\supseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}\text{,}

(9)

which we write as follows for convenience:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{Z}}\coloneqq\{{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,1},\ldots,{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,\lambda}\}.

(10)

For each
{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{Z}}
, we enclose the Lie derivative:

{\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}=\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{f}B_{\theta},{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{L}_{f}B_{\theta}({\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i})\text{.}

(11)

Analogously, we collect these enclosures into a set:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathrm{Z}}\coloneqq\{{\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,1},\ldots,{\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,\lambda}\}.

(12)

Finally, we penalize the upper bound of each set, i.e., for
{\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}=\left[\underline{y_{\mathrm{Z,i}}},\overline{y_{\mathrm{Z,i}}}\right]
and some small
\epsilon>0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{III}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathrm{Z}})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda}\max(0,\overline{y_{\mathrm{Z,i}}}+\epsilon)\text{.}

(13)

Hence, if
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{III}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathrm{Z}})=0
, then for all
i\in[\lambda]
:
\overline{y_{\mathrm{Z,i}}}+\epsilon\leq 0
, i.e.,
\overline{y_{\mathrm{Z,i}}}\leq-\epsilon<0
, and hence P III is satisfied.
We require
\epsilon>0
to enforce the strict inequality required by P III.

Ultimately, the total set-based loss function sums the losses for three properties:

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}},\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathrm{Z}})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{U}})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}(\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{I}})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{III}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathrm{Z}})\text{.}

(14)

The set-based loss function

encodes all three characteristic properties of a barrier certificate (

Enclosure of the Lie Derivative

For the set-based loss of P III, we enclose the Lie derivative for each

The Lie derivative (

Proposition 3

The results show that no architecture is the optimal choice across all benchmarks.

Summary

Overall, the ablation study highlights a fundamental trade-off.
While the verification with the simple N1 network is fast and reliable for the linear benchmark and has a moderate success rate for the polynomial benchmark, it fails for the nonpolynomial benchmark.
In contrast, deeper architectures N2 and N3 provide the expressive power required for polynomial and nonpolynomial systems, at the cost of increased computational effort.
Moreover, the choice of enclosure parameters becomes increasingly important as the complexity of the network and, especially, the system dynamics grows.

V
Related Work

The related work can be broadly categorized into three main research directions: traditional methods for generating barrier certificates through optimization, approaches using neural networks for the synthesis, and certified training techniques that integrate formal guarantees into the learning process.

V-
1
Barrier Certificate Generation

The generation of barrier certificates can be formulated as an optimization problem
[
42
]
. There exist multiple approaches to simplify and generalize the synthesis of barrier certificates, like exponential conditions
[
29
]
, compositional conditions
[
47
]
, Darboux polynomials
[
50
]
, and the combination of two functions to represent one barrier certificate
[
14
]
. Other techniques employ interval analysis
[
11
]
or simulation data
[
25
]
to generate candidate barrier certificates. All of these approaches are unsound as they are based on iterative and numerical computation
[
38
]
and cannot guarantee safety in general. Therefore, to guarantee the safety of the system, the generated candidate barrier certificates have to be formally verified in a subsequent step, e.g., using satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers
[
25
]
.
PRoTECT
[
49
]
enables simultaneous verification of multiple candidate barrier certificates using parallelization.
These ideas have also been extended to stochastic systems
[
39
]
and to control barrier functions (CBFs)
[
7
,
48
]
, which are used for real-time safe control synthesis rather than offline safety verification.

V-
2
Synthesis Using Neural Networks

Neural networks are suitable for barrier certificate synthesis because, by the universal approximation theorem
[
13
,
23
,
32
]
, they can represent any continuous function, in particular valid barrier certificates.
The general pipeline for synthesizing neural barrier certificates
[
54
]
follows two phases: first, a neural network is trained to satisfy the barrier certificate properties on samples of the state space, second, to ensure the validity of the barrier certificate across the entire state space, formal verification is required.

An instance of this pipeline is the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS)
[
38
]
, which uses an iterative cycle between a learner and a verifier:
The learner updates the neural network to satisfy the barrier conditions, while the verifier (e.g., an SMT solver) either certifies the candidate or returns counterexamples that guide the next training iteration.
FOSSIL
[
1
,
17
]
implements this approach as a software tool.
There are different variants of CEGIS that mainly use different verifiers, e.g., cylindrical algebraic decomposition solvers
[
16
]
that can produce multiple counterexamples, or sum-of-squares programming (SynNBC
[
53
]
and SynHBC
[
52
]
).
The cycle can be augmented with a distillation phase between training and verification, which reduces the complexity of the neural network to simplify the subsequent verification task
[
35
]
.
SEEV
[
51
]
takes a geometric approach by exploiting the piecewise-linear activation regions of ReLU neural networks.

A closely related line of work targets neural Lyapunov functions rather than barrier certificates, incorporating the required properties into the training loss
[
12
,
15
]
.
A comprehensive survey of learning-based certificate synthesis is provided in
[
15
]
.

V-
3
Certified Training via Set-Based Propagation

Formal verification of neural network properties using abstract interpretation
[
19
,
46
]
and SMT solvers
[
26
]
has motivated the idea of incorporating set-based computing directly into training
[
36
,
22
,
28
]
, which mainly focuses on input-output robustness of classifiers; instead, we propagate sets to dynamically verify neural network properties during training, thereby eliminating the training-verification cycle entirely.

VI
Conclusions

We propose a novel set-based training approach to synthesize neural barrier certificates.
Prior work follows an iterative training-verification cycle: train a candidate barrier certificate on samples of the state space, verify its validity with an external solver, and repeat until success.
We replace this costly cycle with a single set-based training step, where verification is integrated directly into the training loss function.
Our sound set-based loss encodes all three barrier certificate properties via conservative set propagation over zonotopes; when the loss reaches zero, the barrier certificate is valid by construction and no separate verification step is needed.
All operations reduce to efficient matrix multiplications, yielding polynomial complexity (
section

-A
) that lets our set-based training scale to high-dimensional systems.
Moreover, the approach is largely agnostic to the type of system,
such that it can also naturally handle complex nonlinear dynamics.
Our experiments demonstrate that our approach outperforms prior works on most benchmarks, particularly on high-dimensional and nonpolynomial systems.
Thus, our set-based synthesis of barrier certificates can efficiently generate barrier certificates without requiring subsequent verification.

) is linear in the number of zero-level sets
\lambda
and in
\tau_{f}
, and quadratic in the layer width, and in particular in the state dimension

.

Proposition 4

(Time Complexity of an Iteration in
algorithm

1
)
.

An iteration in
algorithm

1
has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\,n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa+\lambda\,\tau_{f})
w.r.t.
n_{\text{max}}
,
q
, the number of layers
\kappa
, the number of zero-level sets
\lambda
, and
\tau_{f}
.

Proof.

Computing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}
each require

(i)

a set-based forward propagation (
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa)
) and

(ii)

computing the interval bounds of the output zonotope (
\mathcal{O}(q)
).

Hence, computing loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}
takes a total time of
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa)
.

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{III}}
is computed for each zero-level set, which requires

(i)

enclosing the set of gradients (
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa)
),

(ii)

enclosing the Lie derivative (
\mathcal{O}(q\,n+\tau_{f})
), and

(iii)

enclosing the zonotope product (
\mathcal{O}(q\,n^{2})
).

Hence, in total, computing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{III}}
takes time
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\,(n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa+q\,n+\tau_{f}+q\,n^{2}))=\mathcal{O}(\lambda\,n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa+\lambda\tau_{f})
; since
n\leq n_{\text{max}}
and
n\leq q
, the terms
q\,n^{2}
and
q\,n
are absorbed into
n_{\text{max}}^{2}q\kappa
.

Moreover, aggregating the losses and updating the parameters using backpropagation takes time
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa)

[
27
, Sec. 4.4]
.

Thus, in total, an iteration in
algorithm

1
takes time
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\,n_{\text{max}}^{2}\,q\,\kappa+\lambda\tau_{f})
.
∎

We prove the gradient enclosure by induction on
k=\kappa,\dots,1
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}={\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{Z}}}_{,i}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}
be the enclosures of output sets of the hidden layers (
proposition

1
).
If
k=\kappa
, all gradients are
\nabla_{h_{\kappa}}B_{\theta}(x)\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{def:setbasedForwardProp}}}{=}\nabla_{h_{\kappa}}h_{\kappa}=\mathbf{1}
, hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}=\left<1,\mathbf{0}\right>_{Z\,}\supseteq\{\nabla_{h_{\kappa}}h_{\kappa}\mid h_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}\}\text{.}

(17)

For
k<\kappa
, we assume

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}\supseteq\{\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}\text{,}

(18)

and we show
\mathcal{G}_{k-1}\supseteq\{\nabla_{h_{k-1}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\}
.
We split cases on the type of the
k
-th layer and simplify the terms.

Case (i).

The
k
-th layer is linear. Hence, we have
[
10
, Sec. 5.3]

\displaystyle\nabla_{h_{k-1}}B_{\theta}(x)=W_{k}^{\top}\,\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\text{.}

(19)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=W_{k}^{\top}\,\mathcal{G}_{k}\overset{\text{\eqref{eq:proof:grad_enc:assms}}}{\supseteq}W_{k}^{\top}\,\{\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\{W_{k}^{\top}\,\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

\displaystyle{=}\{\nabla_{h_{k-1}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\}\text{.}

Case (ii).

The
k
-th layer is nonlinear. We enclose the derivative of the activation function, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\phi_{k}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\supseteq\{\nabla_{h_{k-1}}\phi_{k}^{\prime}(h_{k-1})\mid h_{k-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\}\text{.}

(20)

Moreover, we have
[
10
, Sec. 5.3]
for each
i\in[n_{k}]

\displaystyle\nabla_{h_{k-1(i)}}B_{\theta}(x)=\phi_{k}^{\prime}(h_{k-1(i)})\,\nabla_{h_{k(i)}}B_{\theta}(x)\text{.}

(21)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\phi_{k}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\cdot\mathcal{G}_{k}

\displaystyle{\supseteq}\operatorname{{\mathtt{enclose}}}\left(\phi_{k}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\right)\cdot\{\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

\displaystyle{\supseteq}\{\phi_{k}^{\prime}(h_{k-1(i)})\,\nabla_{h_{k}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

\displaystyle{=}\{\nabla_{h_{k-1}}B_{\theta}(x)\mid h_{k-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{k-1}\}\text{.}
