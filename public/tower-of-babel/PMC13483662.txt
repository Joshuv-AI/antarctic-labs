Age-dependent associations of high-intensity statin therapy with 3-year clinical outcomes after acute myocardial infarction: longitudinal LDL-C profiles in a nationwide registry
Authors: Youngjoon Kwon, Namkyun Kim, Do-Hoon Kim, Byungwon Kim, Jong Sung Park, Yoon Jung Park, Bo Eun Park, Hong Nyun Kim, Jang Hoon Lee, Dong Heon Yang, Hun Sik Park, Yongkeun Cho, Kyung-Kuk Hwang, Seung-Jae Joo, Seok Kyu Oh, Myung Ho Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-intensity statin therapy is an important strategy for secondary prevention after acute myocardial infarction (AMI), but its clinical benefit in very elderly patients remains uncertain. We aimed to evaluate whether the association of high-intensity statins with clinical outcomes differs by age in older AMI patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsUsing the Korean Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–National Institutes of Health (KAMIR-NIH), we compared outcomes of high-intensity versus non–high-intensity statins among 4,147 patients aged ≥ 65 years treated with PCI and discharged on statins, stratified by age (65–74 years, n = 2,316; ≥75 years, n = 1,831), in overall and propensity score–matched cohorts (matched on age, sex, body mass index, Killip class, comorbidities, left ventricular ejection fraction, estimated glomerular filtration rate, baseline LDL-C, discharge medications, and procedural characteristics). The primary endpoint was 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, or any coronary revascularization); the death/MI composite was a key secondary outcome. Post-discharge statin persistence, LDL-C target attainment, and longitudinal LDL-C profiles were additionally examined.ResultsHigh-intensity statins were prescribed at discharge in 748 (32.3%) patients aged 65–74 years and 520 (28.4%) aged ≥ 75 years. In the 65–74-year group, high-intensity statins were associated with lower risks of MACE (overall cohort: 13.6% vs. 18.9%, adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.87, p < 0.01; matched: 13.7% vs. 18.1%, HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.92, p = 0.01) and the death/MI composite (overall: 7.9% vs. 11.3%, adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.51–0.94, p = 0.01; matched: 8.0% vs. 10.9%, HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–0.99, p = 0.04). In patients aged ≥ 75 years, neither composite outcome showed a statistically evident association with statin intensity. Post-discharge high-intensity statin persistence was comparable by age. Measured-only LDL-C < 70 mg/dL attainment was similar between age groups within each statin-intensity stratum, but when missing values were treated as non-attainment, lower attainment emerged in patients aged ≥ 75 years compared with those aged 65–74 years within each statin-intensity stratum, paralleling lower follow-up measurement rates. LDL-C separation and reductions in variability were also less distinct in this age group.ConclusionsDischarge high-intensity statin therapy was associated with lower risks of the principal composite outcomes in patients aged 65–74 years but not in those aged ≥ 75 years. In very elderly patients, this attenuation may reflect residual geriatric confounding, incomplete LDL-C ascertainment, and uncertainty in sustained treatment exposure rather than absence of benefit, underscoring the need for prospective evaluation of target-oriented strategies in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06087-y.

Introduction

High-intensity statin therapy remains a cornerstone of secondary prevention after acute myocardial infarction (AMI), supported by randomized trial evidence showing reductions in recurrent ischemic events and cardiovascular mortality [1, 2]. Key studies such as the PROVE-IT-TIMI 22 trial confirmed the superiority of intensive statin therapy over moderate-intensity regimens [2]. Accordingly, current European and American guidelines recommend intensive low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) lowering in very high-risk patients, centered on high-intensity statin therapy and, when needed, stepwise addition of non-statin agents such as ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, or inclisiran to achieve guideline-recommended LDL-C goals [3–5].

Despite these recommendations, the clinical effectiveness of high-intensity statin therapy in older adults—particularly those aged ≥ 65 years—remains debated, and real-world prescribing often deviates from guideline-directed intensity because of concerns about tolerability, comorbidity burden, and polypharmacy. Moreover, elderly patients have been underrepresented in pivotal randomized trials and exhibit substantial heterogeneity in baseline risk and treatment response, which may translate into age-dependent differences in both statin selection and outcomes. Therefore, using a large-scale nationwide registry in Korea, we evaluated the association between discharge high-intensity statin therapy and long-term clinical outcomes in elderly patients with AMI undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention. We also sought to examine whether discharge statin intensity translated into sustained documented LDL-C control during follow-up, and whether this relationship differed between patients aged 65–74 years and those aged ≥ 75 years.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective analysis of data from the Korean Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry – National Institutes of Health (KAMIR-NIH) registry, including patients diagnosed with AMI across 20 tertiary cardiovascular centers in South Korea between November 2011 and December 2015. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 65 years, underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with second-generation drug-eluting stents (DES), and received statin therapy at discharge. Patients who received thrombolysis prior to PCI or were treated with plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA), drug-eluting balloon (DEB), bare-metal stents (BMS), or first-generation DES were excluded; cohort eligibility was based solely on these baseline clinical and procedural criteria and was not contingent on the availability of follow-up LDL-C measurements. The study cohort was divided into two age subgroups (65–74 years and ≥ 75 years) and stratified by statin intensity at discharge (Fig. 1).

Procedures and treatment

AMI was defined according to international criteria [3, 6]. PCI procedures and adjunctive medical therapies adhered to contemporary standards of care. All patients received dual antiplatelet therapy and evidence-based pharmacotherapy, including beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and statins. High-intensity statin therapy was defined as atorvastatin ≥ 40 mg or rosuvastatin ≥ 20 mg, consistent with contemporary guideline-based intensity categories [7]. In the present cohort, the non–high-intensity comparator was predominantly composed of moderate-intensity regimens (1,540/1,568 (98.2%) in patients aged 65–74 years and 1,287/1,311 (98.2%) in those aged ≥ 75 years).

Study endpoints and lipid parameters

Time-to-event follow-up was measured from the date of the index coronary angiography. The primary endpoint was 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as all-cause death, recurrent MI, or any coronary revascularization. The death/MI composite, defined as all-cause death or recurrent MI, was evaluated as a key secondary composite outcome. All-cause death, cardiac death, recurrent MI, and any coronary revascularization were additionally analyzed as individual clinical outcomes. All outcomes were compared according to discharge statin intensity within each age subgroup.

Secondary lipid-related measures included persistence of high-intensity statin therapy, visit-specific LDL-C target attainment, follow-up LDL-C measurement availability, and visit-to-visit LDL-C variability. Persistence was defined as the proportion of patients discharged on high-intensity statins who remained on high-intensity therapy at prespecified follow-up visits through 36 months.

For visit-specific target attainment, the primary LDL-C target was defined as LDL-C < 70 mg/dL at 12, 24, and 36 months, reflecting the treatment era of the registry; LDL-C < 55 mg/dL was examined in sensitivity analyses. Target attainment was primarily evaluated among patients with available LDL-C measurements at each visit (measured-only analysis). Because follow-up LDL-C testing was incomplete, we also performed sensitivity analyses in which missing LDL-C values among all patients eligible for assessment at each follow-up visit were treated as non-attainment and separately examined follow-up LDL-C measurement availability according to age group and discharge statin intensity. Although LDL-C assessments were aligned with the 12-, 24-, and 36-month follow-up visits, lipid testing reflected routine clinical care rather than a study-mandated laboratory schedule; therefore, the exact number and timing of available measurements could vary slightly across patients.

Longitudinal LDL-C trajectories and visit-to-visit LDL-C variability were analyzed in patients with serial LDL-C data available through follow-up. Visit-to-visit LDL-C variability was assessed among patients with at least two LDL-C measurements available during follow-up. LDL-C variability was summarized using the standard deviation and coefficient of variation of serial LDL-C values; the coefficient of variation was defined as the standard deviation divided by the mean LDL-C level.

Data acquisition and statistical analysis

Clinical, laboratory, angiographic, and procedural data were prospectively collected using standardized electronic case report forms and stored in a centralized anonymized database. Baseline comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia, were based on documented medical history recorded in the registry at enrollment. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyungpook National University Hospital (IRB No: KNUH-2022-01-011), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], according to their distribution, and were compared between groups using independent 2-sample t-tests with Welch’s correction or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using logistic regression including prespecified clinical and procedural covariates (age, sex, BMI, Killip class, comorbidities, LVEF, eGFR, baseline LDL-C, discharge medications, and procedural characteristics) [8]. Nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.2 SD caliper was applied [9]. Associations with outcomes were estimated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and stratified Cox models in the matched cohort with robust (Huber–White) variance estimators; proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Kaplan–Meier methods and log-rank tests were used for time-to-event comparisons. LDL-C variability metrics were compared using generalized linear models after log transformation and reported as geometric mean ratios (GMRs) with 95% CIs. For cause-specific outcomes, the cause-specific Cox model was used in the main analyses, and a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was applied as a sensitivity analysis to account for competing risks. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1, and a 2-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

High-intensity statins were prescribed at discharge in 32% (n = 748) of patients aged 65–74 years and 28% (n = 520) of those aged ≥ 75 years. In both age strata, patients receiving high-intensity statins exhibited characteristics consistent with more aggressive treatment and/or higher baseline risk. After propensity score matching, covariates were well balanced between treatment groups (standardized mean differences generally < 0.1) [10].

Clinical outcomes

Among patients aged 65–74 years, high-intensity statins were associated with lower 3-year incidences of both MACE (13.6% vs. 18.9%, p = 0.002; matched cohort, 13.7% vs. 18.1%, p = 0.024) and the death/MI composite (7.9% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.011; matched cohort, 8.0% vs. 10.9%, p = 0.038). In Cox models, these associations remained consistent (MACE: adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.87, p < 0.01; matched HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.92, p = 0.01; death/MI composite: adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.51–0.94, p = 0.01; matched HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–0.99, p = 0.04). Sensitivity analyses using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models for cause-specific outcomes yielded estimates concordant with the cause-specific Cox results in both age groups (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, neither composite outcome showed a statistically evident association with statin intensity in patients aged ≥ 75 years, with concordant findings in the Kaplan–Meier analyses (Tables 3 and 4; Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

Individual clinical outcomes showed directionally consistent patterns in patients aged 65–74 years, whereas no statistically evident associations were observed in those aged ≥ 75 years (Tables 3 and 4; Supplementary Fig. 2). When age was modeled as a continuous variable, the association between high-intensity statin therapy and MACE progressively attenuated with advancing age (p for interaction per 5-year increase = 0.041 univariable, 0.039 multivariable-adjusted; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Exploratory lipid parameters during follow-up

Among patients discharged on high-intensity statins, persistence through 36 months was broadly similar between age groups (Fig. 5A and B). Visit-specific LDL-C measurement availability is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 3; across the study cohort, the median number of LDL-C measurements per patient was 2.0 (IQR 1.0–3.0; mean 1.97 ± 0.94; range 1–4). In measured-only analyses using LDL-C < 70 mg/dL as the primary target, attainment was similar between age groups within each statin-intensity stratum at 12 months, but in the high-intensity stratum the difference widened progressively, reaching borderline statistical significance at 36 months (p = 0.054; Fig. 5C). When missing values were treated as non-attainment, lower attainment in patients aged ≥ 75 years compared with those aged 65–74 years was observed within each statin-intensity stratum, paralleling lower follow-up measurement availability (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Sensitivity analyses using LDL-C < 55 mg/dL yielded broadly similar patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Longitudinal LDL-C trajectories and variability analyses were restricted to patients with serial measurements and should be interpreted as descriptive secondary analyses. In this subset, LDL-C separation between statin-intensity groups was less distinct in patients aged ≥ 75 years (Supplementary Fig. 6), and reductions in visit-to-visit LDL-C variability with high-intensity therapy were smaller and not statistically significant in this age group (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this nationwide registry of older patients with AMI undergoing PCI, discharge high-intensity statin therapy was associated with lower 3-year risks of the principal composite outcomes, MACE and the death/MI composite, in patients aged 65–74 years, whereas no statistically evident association was observed in patients aged ≥ 75 years. However, the absence of statistical significance in the very elderly should not be interpreted as absence of benefit. Given the limited precision of subgroup estimates, residual confounding, and the potential influence of unmeasured geriatric factors, modest benefit in patients aged ≥ 75 years cannot be excluded. This uncertainty is clinically relevant given the growing burden of AMI in older populations and the increasing complexity of care in older survivors [11–13]. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior evidence in elderly populations. In PROSPER, a dedicated randomized trial in patients aged 70–82 years, pravastatin reduced coronary events but its effect on all-cause mortality and stroke was less consistent [14]. In a Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ Collaboration meta-analysis, the relative benefit of statin therapy per unit reduction in LDL-C tended to be attenuated in patients aged > 75 years compared with younger participants [15].

Because cumulative LDL-C exposure is a major determinant of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk [16, 17], older patients with AMI likely carry substantial lifetime LDL-C burden. Contemporary guidelines therefore recommend maximally tolerated lipid-lowering therapy with stringent LDL-C targets in very high-risk patients [3, 6]. In this context, our findings do not suggest that intensive lipid lowering is unimportant in advanced age; rather, they indicate that, in routine practice, discharge statin intensity alone may be an imperfect marker of sustained documented lipid control in very elderly patients.

This distinction is important in an observational study in which treatment was not randomly allocated. In older adults, prescribing decisions are influenced not only by measured cardiovascular risk but also by frailty, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, functional status, and clinician concern regarding tolerability. In addition, older age itself has previously been reported as a determinant of reduced high-intensity statin prescription after PCI, supporting the likelihood that age-related treatment selection contributed to the prescribing patterns observed in our registry [18]. These same factors are also closely related to prognosis. Although multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching were performed, selective prescribing of high-intensity statins to healthier or less frail patients cannot be excluded. In this context, “healthier” refers primarily to overall general health and frailty rather than to cardiovascular risk; within similar levels of general health, high-intensity statins may still be preferentially prescribed to patients with higher cardiovascular risk profiles. These two selection dimensions may operate concurrently, which could partly account for the directionally mixed patterns observed in propensity score–matched patients aged ≥ 75 years (Supplementary Fig. 2), in whom point estimates for cardiac death, recurrent MI, and revascularization were directionally higher under high-intensity therapy while all-cause death was directionally lower, although none of these differences reached statistical significance. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as associations rather than direct evidence of treatment effectiveness.

Among patients discharged on high-intensity statins, persistence through 36 months was broadly similar between age groups, suggesting that differential persistence alone is unlikely to explain the weaker associations in the very elderly. However, in the high-intensity stratum, LDL-C target attainment diverged progressively between age groups at later follow-up visits, and this divergence became more pronounced when missing LDL-C values were treated as non-attainment, paralleling lower follow-up measurement availability in older patients. These findings suggest that the weaker associations in the very elderly may in part reflect a real-world gap between discharge treatment intensity and sustained documented LDL-C control, although age-related differences in pharmacologic response and other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Even when discharge prescriptions were maintained, actual medication adherence and dose titration may have been less consistent in very elderly patients, although these dimensions are not directly captured by registry-based prescription data. The achieved level of LDL-C control may thus be more informative than discharge statin intensity alone in this population.

Longitudinal LDL-C trajectories and variability analyses, restricted to a serial-measurement subset vulnerable to survivorship bias, showed less distinct LDL-C separation and smaller variability reductions in patients aged ≥ 75 years; these findings should be regarded as exploratory.

Closer follow-up LDL-C monitoring and assessment of adherence barriers could be considered in this group, particularly to help distinguish incomplete ascertainment from true suboptimal lipid control. The attenuated association in patients aged ≥ 75 years does not imply that low-intensity regimens are preferable; moderate-intensity statins remain a reasonable choice in this population when high-intensity therapy is not used. Although current guidelines support stepwise intensification with ezetimibe and, when needed, PCSK9 inhibitors or inclisiran when LDL-C goals are not achieved [3, 6, 19–22], these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and support prospective evaluation of target-oriented lipid-lowering strategies integrated with frailty assessment and treatment feasibility in very elderly AMI survivors.

This study has several limitations. First, as a non-randomized observational analysis, it remains susceptible to residual confounding and confounding by indication despite multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching. In particular, the registry did not capture dedicated geriatric assessments such as frailty, cognitive impairment, or functional status, all of which may influence both treatment selection and prognosis. Second, the primary composite endpoint included any coronary revascularization, which may in part reflect staged procedures or practice patterns rather than spontaneous ischemic events alone. Third, follow-up LDL-C ascertainment was incomplete and differed by age group, especially at later time points, making estimates of documented lipid control vulnerable to informative missingness. Missingness was likely related to worse functional status, recurrent hospitalization, or earlier death. As a result, documented attainment probably lies between the measured-only estimates, which may favor returning survivors, and the missing-as-non-attainment estimates, which may overstate non-attainment. In addition, LDL-C measurements were obtained as part of routine clinical care across participating centers rather than through a study-specific centralized laboratory protocol, which may have introduced some inter-center measurement heterogeneity. Moreover, longitudinal LDL-C trajectories and variability analyses were restricted to a selected serial-measurement subset and should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Fourth, statin exposure was defined at discharge, and time-varying changes in statin intensity or use of non-statin therapy were not incorporated into the outcome models; similarly, persistence analyses were descriptive and were not modeled as time-varying exposures linked to clinical outcomes. De-escalation or discontinuation of high-intensity therapy and intensification or substitution within the non–high-intensity comparator may both occur during long-term follow-up, particularly in older patients. The resulting estimates may therefore attenuate or accentuate the apparent association of high-intensity statins with outcomes. Fifth, the number of very elderly patients receiving high-intensity statins was relatively small, limiting precision to detect modest differences in that subgroup. In addition, no formal adjustment was made for multiple comparisons across subgroups and endpoints, which may inflate the risk of type I error for individual secondary outcomes; the principal composite endpoints were prespecified as the main analyses. Lastly, the registry enrollment period (2011–2015) preceded the broader adoption of ezetimibe combination therapy, the introduction of PCSK9 inhibitors and inclisiran, and the more stringent LDL-C goals recommended in subsequent guidelines, so that the present associations should be interpreted within that practice context.

Conclusion

In this nationwide registry of older patients with AMI undergoing PCI, discharge high-intensity statin therapy was associated with lower 3-year risks of the principal composite outcomes, MACE and the death/MI composite, in patients aged 65–74 years, whereas no statistically evident association was observed in those aged ≥ 75 years. In very elderly patients, this observed attenuation should not be interpreted as absence of benefit; residual geriatric confounding, incomplete follow-up LDL-C ascertainment, and uncertainty in sustained treatment exposure may all have contributed to this finding. Clinical decision-making in this population should continue to involve individualized weighing of potential benefits, tolerability, frailty, and patient preferences, alongside prospective evaluation of target-oriented lipid-lowering strategies.
