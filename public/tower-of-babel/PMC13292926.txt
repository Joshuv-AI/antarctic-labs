Long-stranded XNA-cssDNA hybrids for robust data storage
Authors: Xinyu Sun, Yufeng Pei, Pan Tan, Tianyuan Bian, Ruoyu Wang, Yan Wang, Jiacheng Xie, Liang Hong, Jie Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA is a promising medium for data storage due to its high density and low energy cost. Long-stranded DNA with minimal indexing improves storage density but suffers from poor stability. To address this, we introduce a long-stranded xeno nucleic acid–circular single-stranded DNA (XNA-cssDNA) hybrid strategy to enhance storage robustness. We evaluated multiple XNAs under extreme conditions and identified 2′-fluoro-arabinonucleic acid (FANA) as the optimal storage material. To overcome XNA synthesis length and speed limitations, we used a temperature-guided language model to evolve a FANA polymerase Tgomut, which is ~4.4-fold faster than TgoD4K and enables synthesis of strands exceeding 7500 nucleotides. Data-encoded cssDNA were produced using M13 bacteriophage. The resulting FANA-cssDNA hybrids show strong resistance to chemical and enzymatic degradation. Using a dual-strategy framework, we achieved reliable data writing and reading with 100% data recovery, even after DNA degradation, and successfully visualized encoded digital data and an enhanced green fluorescent protein gene in mammalian cells.

INTRODUCTION

With the exponential growth of digital data, the predicted global data volume is projected to reach 175 zettabytes by 2025 (1). Existing silicon-based storage devices are struggling to keep pace with these escalating capacity demands. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel storage media. DNA has emerged as a promising candidate due to its ultrahigh theoretical storage density (up to 1.7 × 1019 bytes/g) and programmability (2–4). Since the first experimental demonstration in 1996, DNA data storage has advanced substantially, using various encoding strategies to archive digital data (5–7). Despite these achievements, widespread implementation of DNA data storage faces two major obstacles: environmental instability and limited strand length. First, natural DNA is fragile; although stable under ideal conditions [dry, without deoxyribonuclease (DNase) and chemicals attacking], it degrades rapidly within minutes when exposed to nucleases, acids, or harsh chemical environments (8–12). Second, current chemical synthesis limits DNA strands to short oligonucleotides [<200 nucleotides (nt)] (13). Storing data on such short strands necessitates substantial redundant indexing sequences for file reconstruction, which drastically compromises the effective storage density (14). Therefore, developing long-stranded nucleic acids that are inherently resistant to biological and chemical degradation is critical for next-generation storage.

Chemically modified xeno nucleic acids (XNAs) have been proposed as a solution to the stability issue (15). Because of their unnatural backbones or sugar rings, XNAs exhibit superior resistance to enzymatic and chemical degradation compared to natural DNA. However, existing XNA storage methods are predominantly restricted to short oligos (15, 16). This restriction exists because natural polymerases tolerate XNA modifications poorly, making the enzymatic synthesis of long-stranded XNA challenging. Many efforts were developed on XNA polymerase evolution (17, 18); however, the evolution of mutant polymerases capable of synthesizing long XNA is a complex, labor-intensive process, which has hindered the development of long-read XNA storage systems. Moreover, considering the scale-up commercial application, a high-yield, high-purity long single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) template is also required for producing substantial XNA strands. Although various methods have been developed, such as enzymatic processing by lambda-exonuclease (19), microbead separation from double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) (20), asymmetric or single-primer polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (21, 22) and methanol-responsive polymer PCR (23), rolling circle amplification (24), and copolymerization and electrophoresis methods (25, 26), these methods capable of producing more than 100 μg of data-encoded, nonrepetitive long ssDNA per batch remain challenging.

To overcome these synthesis bottlenecks, we present a robust long-stranded XNA-DNA hybrid-based data storage platform using 2′-fluoro-arabinonucleic acid (FANA). Moving beyond traditional screening methods, we used a deep learning–assisted strategy to efficiently predict and engineer a mutant polymerase, Tgomut, capable of synthesizing long FANA strands. To produce long ssDNA templates, we used M13 bacteriophages for producing high-yield (submilligram level per batch) data–encoded circular single-stranded DNA (cssDNA). The Tgomut extended FANA-cssDNA hybrid enables the generation of storage media that integrate the high stability of XNA with long nucleic strands featuring minimal index redundancy. We successfully demonstrated the reliability of this FANA-cssDNA hybrid-based platform for data storage and retrieval in both in vitro harsh environments and in vivo systems, highlighting its potential as a durable alternative to traditional DNA storage.

RESULTS

Stability of various XNAs

To obtain long-stranded XNA, specific polymerase is required. First, we screened the polymerases recognizing several representative XNAs, including 2′-deoxyadenosine 5′-triphosphate to 2′-deoxy-2,6-diaminopurine-5′-triphosphate (dZTP) (dZ-DNA) for base modification; FANA and threose nucleic acid (TNA) for sugar modifications; and phosphorothioate DNA (PS-DNA) for backbone modification (Fig. 1A). To verify the specificity of candidate polymerases on various XNAs, we designed a 68-nt DNA template strand as the template and 18-nt DNA with Cy5 fluorophore as the primer (table S1), with different modified nucleoside triphosphates (xNTPs) as substrates (fig. S1). The relative activities of these polymerases are depicted in Fig. 1B. Based on these results, we synthesized a series of short strands incorporating these XNAs (Fig. 1, C and D; sequence is given in table S1). Subsequently, the stability of these modified oligonucleotides was evaluated under various conditions including hydrochloric acid, heating, formamide, serum, restriction endonucleases (Pdm I and Nde I) and exonuclease III (Exo III) (Fig. 1E and fig. S2). Degradation products from these assays were quantified using grayscale densitometry of both GelRed staining conditions and Cy5 fluorophore labels. Because the residual single-stranded XNA is technically useful and relevant to XNA data storage, the final quantitative analysis result was the summation of both XNA-DNA hybrids and ssDNA or single-stranded XNAs. The analysis revealed distinct stability profiles for each modification compared to natural DNA (Fig. 1, F to H, and fig. S3). dZ-DNA modification showed only restriction endonucleases resistance compared to DNA, indicating that base modification can help DNA to escape degradation by restriction endonucleases (27). PS-DNA exhibits enhanced biostability, due to modifications in the phosphate backbone that reduce its recognition by nucleases (28). Notably, restriction endonuclease Pdm I can still effectively cleave PS-DNA, indicating that PS-DNA does not confer effective resistance against all nucleases. Chemical stability of PS-DNA is comparable to or even lower than that of unmodified DNA. This may be attributed to the increased susceptibility of the sulfur-substituted backbone to oxidative damage by reactive oxygen species, ultimately leading to PS-DNA degradation (29, 30). As shown in fig. S3D, FANA shows no resistance to Exo III, whereas TNA effectively evades the cleavage activity of Exo III and the band corresponding to the residual single-stranded TNA (ssTNA) following Exo III digestion is marked by a yellow arrow, which is consistent with previous reports (31). A small amount of TNA is cleaved by the restriction of endonuclease Nde I, indicating that despite its notable advantage in resisting nuclease degradation, TNA is not effective against all nucleases. Furthermore, the marked enhancement in the chemical stability of both FANA and TNA is also consistent with prior reports (32, 33).

Ideal medium for long-stranded XNA data storage

While our screening identified FANA and TNA as having excellent chemical and biological stability (Fig. 1, F and G), their suitability for robust data storage required further evaluation. A practical data storage medium must also have high mechanical stability to withstand physical shocks and microscopic mechanical forces. To compare the mechanical properties of FANA-DNA and TNA-DNA hybrids, we assessed mechanical stability using a single-molecule magnetic tweezers assay. A single-molecule hairpin unfolding experimental setup was established (Fig. 2A and fig. S4). The hairpin was synthesized containing a 20–base pair (bp) stem, which is a pure DNA duplex or hybrids (FANA-DNA or TNA-DNA) linked by six T-base loops (table S2). Then, the hairpin was sandwiched between two ~500-bp dsDNA handles and tethered between a magnetic bead and the surface of a functionalized coverslip (see Materials and Methods for details). A single tether was stretched by controlling the distance between the magnets and the bead. To measure the unfolding force of the pure DNA hairpin or hybridized hairpin, force-ramp experiments with 1 pN/s constant loading rate were performed. Figure 2B shows the time trace of the bead height as the force increases. A jump signal can be observed in the trace indicating a hairpin unfolding event. By repeating the stretching cycles and recording the unfolding forces, the distribution of unfolding forces can be obtained (Fig. 2C). The average unfolding force of pure DNA hairpin is ~12 pN. Compared with the pure DNA hairpin, the unfolding force of FANA-DNA hairpin increased significantly, reaching ~15 pN. However, TNA-DNA hairpin displayed the lowest unfolding force, which is ~10 pN, suggesting that TNA-DNA hybridization is less stable compared with DNA-DNA and FANA-DNA. The results of comparing different hairpin unfolding forces (FANA-DNA > DNA-DNA > TNA-DNA) indicate FANA hybridizing to DNA may increase the mechanical stability of the duplex, while TNA decreases the mechanical stability of duplex. To further examine the contribution of FANA on self-folding of a single strand, we constructed a full FANA (FANA-FANA) hairpin. This FANA-FANA hairpin displayed a larger unfolding force reaching ~19 pN, suggesting that FANA-FANA self-folding displays superior mechanical stability (fig. S5). By analyzing the unfolding stepsizes from the stretching traces, all the hairpins were observed fully folded, indicating that all the hairpins underwent two state transition (from fully folded state to unfolded state); thus, unfolding free energy can be calculated (fig. S6).

We next measured the folding and unfolding kinetics of the pure DNA hairpin and two hybridized hairpins using constant force stretching at equilibrium state (fig. S7A). By holding the hairpin under different constant forces, the unfolding and refolding time traces can be recorded. They were then fitted by hidden Markov chain model to determine the dwell time of folded and unfolded states (fig. S7B). After fitting with the first-order kinetic function, the folding rate and unfolding rate under the constant forces were estimated (fig. S7C). Subsequently, to acquire the transition kinetics in the native state, we fit the transition rates under different forces to Bell’s model: kforce=k0eFΔx∗kBT, where the kforce is the measured transition rate under applied forces, k0 is the transition rate at zero force (native state), and Δx∗ is the distance between initial state and intermediate state. From the fitting, the folding and unfolding rate at zero force can be obtained (fig. S7, D and E). The results show that among these three hairpins, TNA-DNA hairpin has the lowest folding rate (5.08 × 108 s−1) and fastest unfolding rate (2.04 × 10−10 s−1), while FANA-DNA hairpin has the lowest unfolding rate (2.60 × 10−13 s−1). These results indicate that compared with FANA, TNA tends to dissociate from DNA templates. In contrast, FANA exhibits stronger binding affinity and forms more stable duplexes with DNA. Considering long-term data storage, FANA is more suitable for maintaining the integrity of data during long-term storage.

Furthermore, the ability to synthesize long strands is also a critical prerequisite for storing large amounts of data. We evaluated the long-strand synthesis ability using a long ssDNA template and DNA primer M13 Ori-R with Cy5 fluorophore (Fig. 2D and table S1). The FANA strand was efficiently synthesized to ~2000 nt using TgoD4K polymerase in just 20 min (Fig. 2E), whereas TNA synthesis with KOD10–92 polymerase was severely limited, showing minimal product even after 72 hours (Fig. 2F). This poor processivity for TNA is likely due to structural strain from its shorter backbone, which prevents effective long-range pairing with the DNA template. Based on the superior performance in both mechanical stability and enzymatic synthesis, FANA-DNA was chosen as the ideal medium for our data storage platform.

Directed evolution of FANA polymerase based on deep learning

To increase the extension rate of FANA polymerase, a sequence-based Pro-PRIME deep learning model was adopted for the evolution of polymerases with higher stability and activity (Fig. 3A). A comprehensive approach involving three rounds of deep learning prediction and experimental evaluation was conducted. Initially, a series of single-site mutants were generated using the Pro-PRIME model based on the previously obtained TgoD4K (18, 34). We then focused on the 27 mutants with the highest scores. The polymerase mutants were expressed in Escherichia coli and purified by Ni-NTA resin (fig. S8). The extension rates of FANA synthesis of these mutants on FANA were evaluated at 55°C using the polymerase kinetic profiling (PKPro) method established earlier (35). The rate of FANA polymerase was determined from the slope of the time-dependent FANA extension curve through linear fitting and normalization (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, the evaluation results of these single-site mutants were input to the next round of deep learning models for prediction, generating a series of multisite mutants, with the top 30 mutants being selected based on scores. Experimental evaluations were then performed on these mutants, and the results from the first two rounds of experiments were incorporated into the third round of prediction. Among the obtained set of mutants, the top 30 mutants with the highest score were selected for further evaluation. After three rounds of screening, we successfully identified Tgomut [TgoD4K: Ile693→Trp(I693W), Tyr481→Gly(Y481G), Ile471→Glu(I471E)] exhibiting an extension rate ~4.4-fold faster than the existing TgoD4K polymerase from the 87 mutants (Fig. 3B and tables S3 and S4). To evaluate polymerase processivity, we measured the DNA synthesis rate of Tgoexo− and the FANA synthesis rates of Tgoexo−, TgoD4K, and Tgomut (Fig. 3C and fig. S9). The results demonstrated that Tgomut exhibited improved processivity in FANA synthesis compared to TgoD4K, although it remained lower than the DNA synthesis processivity of Tgoexo−. We then used 2323-, 4856-, and 7560-nt cssDNA as the templates of the polymerase extension assay. Figure 3D demonstrates that the Tgomut polymerase entirely transcribed the complementary FANA strands. As the FANA-cssDNA band migrated slightly faster than the DNA-cssDNA band, we sought to verify the full extension of the FANA strand. The 2323-bp product was subjected to heat denaturation to separate the double strands (fig. S10). Both the Cy5 fluorescence signal and the GelRed-stained band comigrated with the cssDNA control, confirming that the FANA strand was fully extended. The observed migration differences are governed by multiple factors, including solvent accessibility and molecular conformation, rather than molecular weight alone. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have reported that circular and linear single-stranded RNA and DNA display gel-dependent shifts in electrophoretic mobility as a function of length and conformation (36, 37). These results indicate that Tgomut is a high-processive FANA polymerase facilitating long-stranded FANA synthesis.

The mechanical, chemical and biological stability of FANA-cssDNA hybrids

Although we had already observed the enhanced stability of short FANA-DNA hybrids compared to their canonical DNA counterparts, it is unclear whether this stability would remain in long-stranded constructs. Therefore, we proceeded to compare the stability of long-stranded FANA–DNA hybrids (F-D) against long dsDNA (D-D). We first measured the shear forces of double helical F-D and D-D nucleic acids to compare the mechanical stability using magnetic tweezer (Fig. 4A). To conduct the shear force measurement of the double helix, the double helical nucleic acid was labeled with thiol and biotin at the 5′-terminal of each strand. We constructed double helical F-D and D-D with ~1.8 kilo–base pair (kbp) length. D-D double helix was produced by PCR amplification with labeled primers. However, F-D hybrids cannot be simply obtained by PCR. Thus, we first synthesized linear ssDNA by a single-primer PCR method and then extended the opposite FANA strand using Tgomut polymerase with ssDNA as a template (fig. S11 and table S5). The double-helical strands were subsequently tethered between the surface and magnetic beads as mentioned above. A similar force-ramp experiment was also conducted with 1 pN/s loading rate from 1 to ~80 pN. Figure 4B shows the force-bead height curve of stretching F-D hybrid and D-D double helix. The curve of the D-D double helix from 1 to ~60 pN displays a typical dsDNA stretching curve with an entropic stretching region and an elastic region (38). As the forces reached ~65 pN, the dsDNA underwent a sudden increase in the extension with an ~0.7-fold length of dsDNA, which is known as “overstretching transition.” The overstretching transition signal is caused by multiple possible reasons, such as the peeling off of ssDNA, bubble formation, and B- to S-form transition (39, 40). Nevertheless, the F-D hybrid showed a delay in the overstretching transition. To quantify this delay phenomenon, the onset forces and end forces of the overstretching transition were analyzed (Fig. 4C). The onset forces of the D-D double helix and F-D hybrid display similar distributions. The mean onset forces of D-D and F-D are ~62 and ~61 pN, respectively. However, the end force of the F-D hybrid is much higher than the D-D double helix (~71 pN versus ~66 pN), suggesting that F-D hybrid is more stable than the D-D double helix under the shear force.

Next, we conducted a comparative analysis of chemical stability between long-stranded FANA–cssDNA hybrids and circular double-stranded DNA (cdsDNA). To obtain a high-yield ssDNA template, we determined to produce cssDNA by M13 bacteriophage (fig. S12). The FANA-cssDNA hybrid and cdsDNA were synthesized by Tgomut polymerase and Phanta polymerase, respectively. We first performed an antinucleophile assay and evaluated time-dependent degradation by agarose gel electrophoresis. The nucleic acids were incubated at 95°C in the presence of formamide from minutes to hours and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis with both GelRed staining and Cy5 fluorophore labels. The coimaging and quantitative analysis of both GelRed staining conditions and Cy5 fluorophore labels showed that cdsDNA was fully degraded within 30 min, whereas the FANA strand remained stable and persisted for up to 12 hours (Fig. 4D, fig. S13A, and table S6). We also performed a comparative analysis of the stability of nucleic acid strands in extreme chemical environmental conditions in HCl solution with pH 1. After incubating in HCl at 37°C, cdsDNA experienced rapid degradation and complete degradation within 10 min, while observable bands of FANA-cssDNA group persisted for up to 2 hours (Fig. 4E and fig. S13B). Last, to test the resistance of FANA-cssDNA hybrid to biological attack, we measured the stability of nucleic acid strands in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The FANA-cssDNA hybrid exhibited remarkable stability for over 24 hours, whereas the cdsDNA strand underwent degradation within ~6 hours (Fig. 4F and fig. S13C).

After chemical induced degradation, the degraded products appeared as bands that comigrated with the ssDNA. Therefore, these bands were tentatively identified as single-stranded FANA (ssFANA). We supposed that the antiformamide effect is attributed to the hydrophobic effect of fluorine atoms, which prevent intermediates from water molecules during the formamide degradation pathway (fig. S14A) (41). In addition, the presence of the fluorine atom at the 2′ position in FANA induces an electron-pulling effect, impeding electron transfer to imine ions and thereby inhibiting acid hydrolysis (fig. S14B) (32). After incubation in serum, the digestion products formed a diffuse smear, which we attribute to partially degraded DNA fragments remaining annealed to the complementary FANA strand. The structural deviations of FANA from canonical DNA likely render it a poor substrate for serum nucleases, thus conferring its high stability (Fig. 4H). Together, these results indicate that even in long strands, FANA also showed higher chemical and biological stability of the hybrid than that of DNA.

Sequence-based data storage using long-stranded FANA–cssDNA

Encouraged by the higher mechanical, chemical, and biological stabilities of FANA-DNA hybrids, we next performed sequence-based data storage using the FANA-cssDNA hybrid system. The schematic diagram illustrating the FANA-protected DNA data storage is depicted in Fig. 5A. To evaluate the performance of data storage systems, we encoded an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)–coding gene sequence data [occupied 702-nt coding sequence (CDS) of EGFP and 508-nt cytomegalovirus (CMV) enhancer and promoter sequence] followed by a string data “The quick brown fox jumps over 13 lazy dogs.” (brief in “fox,” occupied 344 nt) into the plasmid containing pScaf phagemid (fig. S15 and tables S7 and S8). Through cotransfection with the helper plasmid, the cssDNA was extracted from the M13 phage (42, 43). Compared with other strategies for the synthesis of ssDNA, this method has very short fixed sequence regions and can synthesize cssDNA with high purity and yield (~300 μg per batch). Subsequently, the Tgomut polymerase was used for the extension of the cssDNA to produce FANA-cssDNA hybrid. When the integrity of this hybrid structure is preserved and not compromised by environmental contamination, the enclosed cssDNA can be used as template for data reading. Conventional PCR can be performed using DNA polymerases to amplify the nucleic acid strand harboring CDS of EGFP and CMV enhancer and promoter sequence. Subsequently, Sanger sequencing can be used to determine the sequence, enabling the decoding and recovery of the storage data (Fig. 5B). If the FANA-cssDNA hybrid for data storage becomes contaminated, leading to degradation of the enclosed cssDNA, strategy involving the utilization of the remaining FANA is implemented to recover the stored data. The synthesis of 89-bp FANA-DNA transcripts confirms the capability of Bst polymerase large fragment (LF) to catalyze the reverse transcription of FANA into DNA (fig. S16). Then Bst LF polymerase was used to perform reverse transcription of the FANA strand harboring the string data into DNA, which is subsequently subjected to conventional PCR amplification (fig. S17). Because of the utilization of long-stranded nucleic acids for data storage, rather than the previous use of short-stranded nucleic acids, Sanger sequencing can be used for sequencing purposes, which expands our methods for data reading. The sequencing results showed that both methods can recover the data entirely. The data from samples exposed to adverse environmental conditions were also recovered. The hybrid can protect data from formamide or acid with nearly 100% fidelity, whereas the cssDNA lost all the data. To sum up, FANA can protect the data stored in DNA through multiple manners.

To validate the data storage ability of the FANA-cssDNA system in vivo, we conducted the transfection experiment of FANA-cssDNA (containing EGFP sequence and “fox” data) on mammalian Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell line. The construction of FANA-cssDNA hybrid was similar to the above. To increase the resistance of nucleic acids against nuclease, we labeled the primer at the 5′-terminal by a phosphate group for ligation of the nick point by T4 ligase. After ligation, a closed FANA-cssDNA double strand was obtained. Next, we transfected 0.5 pmol FANA-cssDNA into the MDCK cell line and incubated for 24 hours, following the detection of EGFP expression. We found that FANA-cssDNA strand with artificial nucleic acids incorporation could also reach a considerable EGFP expression level (40%) compared with DNA-cssDNA duplex (~81%) and pure cssDNA (~57%) (Fig. 5C). To recover the data, we extracted DNA from cells and sequenced “fox” fragments and EGFP fragments. As a result, both of the two sequence-coded data can be fully recovered (Fig. 5D). This result suggests that FANA-cssDNA hybrid can also be used in in vivo data storage. Although FANA is classified into XNAs, mammalian cells can also recognize FANA-DNA hybrid and produce proteins from expressible data in vivo, leading to the stored data being directly visible. Previous study suggests that FANA-A incorporation in DNA template may not interfere with the transcription of mRNA while other FANA bases decrease or inhibit mRNA production (44). To determine whether FANA strands directly participate in EGFP mRNA production, we examined EGFP expression using both a cell-free protein expression (CFE) system and a T7 RNA polymerase–catalyzed in vitro transcription (IVT) system driven by a T7 promoter (fig. S18). We constructed a cssDNA containing the T7 promoter and the EGFP CDS as the nontemplate strand, such that the complementary DNA or FANA strand could potentially serve as the transcription template. The DNA-cssDNA duplex exhibited robust EGFP expression and efficient mRNA production in both the CFE and IVT systems. In contrast, only weak EGFP expression was detected in the CFE system when the FANA-cssDNA hybrid was used. Moreover, no detectable mRNA was produced in the IVT system in the presence of the FANA-cssDNA hybrid (fig. S18). These results indicate that the observed EGFP expression is unlikely to arise from direct transcription using the FANA strand as a template. Helicase in mammalian cells might recognize the FANA-cssDNA hybrid and unwind the helix of FANA-cssDNA, leading to cssDNA regenerating a complementary strand for gene expression.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated a robust molecular data storage strategy based on long FANA-cssDNA hybrids. We first assessed several XNAs modified at the phosphate backbone, sugar, and base moieties as potential media for stable and durable data storage. Among them, FANA and TNA exhibited superior resistance to degradation. However, TNA showed weaker hybridization affinity and a faster dissociation rate from DNA compared to FANA. Besides, long TNA strands could not be fully synthesized by polymerase extension. Thus, FANA was identified as the optimal candidate for long-stranded data storage. To enable rapid and efficient synthesis, we used deep learning–assisted directed evolution of FANA polymerases, generating a variant Tgomut that extended FANA strands ~4.4-fold faster than the existing TgoD4K polymerase. Using this polymerase, FANA strands over 7500 nt were successfully synthesized. We further applied this system to develop both in vitro and in vivo data storage schemes. Compared to linear ssDNA, cssDNA extracted from M13 phage has higher yield and stability, which is resistant to exonucleases. The long FANA-cssDNA hybrid encoded two data layers—an expressible EGFP gene and a text sequence—and exhibited higher stability, supporting reliable data storage and retrieval. Last, we also demonstrate that the FANA-cssDNA hybrid can also be recognized by mammalian cells and enable in vivo EGFP expression, allowing direct visualization of data recovery.

F atom modifications on sugar C2′ can alter the biophysical properties of nucleic acids (45). It is supposed that F substitution at the C2′ position of the nucleic acid sugar may introduce steric constraints that restrict free rotation of the glycosidic bond, thereby preorganizing the conformation of sugars, leading to a favorable enthalpy of hybridization and ultimately enhancing hybridization stability (45). In data storage using XNA-DNA hybrid, enhanced hybridization stability is conducive to the protection of duplex from dissociation, while insufficient stability risks spontaneous strand loss during temperature-governed PCR, duplex denaturation, capture, or strand-displacement process. Besides, higher hybridization or mechanical stability may resist the effect of microscopic mechanical forces (such as ultrasound) in storage medium for maintaining their structural integrity during data preservation. However, excessive stability may hinder polymerase-mediated data reading and processing. For example, the unfolding force of FANA-FANA self-folded hairpin reaches ~19 pN, which is closer to the stall force (~25 pN) of RNA polymerase (46), making in vivo data visualization challenging. Hence, FANA-DNA hybrids that exhibit moderate stability enhancement may enable broader applicability in data storage applications.

While oligonucleotide-based data storage has achieved substantial progress, we contend that long-strand–based data storage represents a more advanced paradigm. A primary limitation of oligonucleotide-based methods is the reduction in net data density caused by the substantial indexing and redundant sequences required to index countless short fragments (47–49). For instance, 3633 bits of image data can be encoded into 17 long DNA strands (~1000 bp), with only one index sequence (~16 bases) at each strand (47). In addition, a critical challenge for long-stranded data storage is the inherent instability of natural DNA. Its greater number of phosphodiester bonds provides more potential sites for stochastic cleavage, leading to fragmentation during long-term storage. Our work has addressed these challenges by storing data in long and continuous strands, FANA-DNA hybrid, which not only increased the data density but also provided a strong stability against several degraded factors. (Figs. 1D and 4, D to G). Furthermore, long-stranded storage fundamentally simplifies the data retrieval process. Unlike oligonucleotide-based systems, which necessitate next-generation sequencing (NGS) for readout, our long-stranded platform allows for data recovery through Sanger sequencing. This method has an intrinsic fault tolerance; because Sanger sequencing analyzes a population of molecules, the signal from rare mutations is effectively masked by the overwhelming signal from the correct, consensus sequence, drastically reducing the dependency on complex error-correction codes.

A central bottleneck for realizing long-stranded XNA storage is the enzymatic synthesis, which is hampered by the lack of efficient and processive polymerases. Conventional directed evolution platforms, such as the compartmentalized self-tagging (18) method, compartmentalized self-replication (18) method, and the droplet-based optical polymerase sorting (17) method, have been developed. However, the extension rates of the polymerases evolved using these methods remain substantially lower than their corresponding wild-type polymerases. Moreover, these approaches necessitate specialized experimental skills and incur considerable labor costs. To overcome this, we adopted a fundamentally different strategy, using the deep learning algorithm Pro-PRIME to guide the evolution of our FANA polymerase. This deep learning–guided approach dramatically enhances screening efficiency and predictive power. As a result, we successfully engineered Tgomut, which exhibited an ~4.4-fold faster extension rate than its parent TgoD4K, making it the fastest FANA polymerase reported to date (Fig. 3, B and C). This substantial performance gained was achieved with a minimal mutational load of only three amino acid mutations. The high processivity of Tgomut enabled the complete extension of templates exceeding 7500 nt, which is a critical breakthrough for practical long-stranded data storage. This result highlights the transformative potential of deep learning in protein engineering, allowing for the rapid development of highly active enzymes that would be difficult to obtain through traditional screening methods.

Besides, considering scale-up for commercial use, high-yield and high-purity ssDNA is required as a template for synthesis of XNA. In our study, we used M13 bacteriophage for producing data-encoded long-stranded cssDNA. One batch containing 1 liter of bacterial culture produced >300 μg of cssDNA with more than 2000-nt length in our laboratory. Once the cssDNA encodes gene sequences, cssDNA can be recognized by polymerase and initiate protein expression in mammalian cells and cell-free systems (43, 50). cssDNA uniquely lacks a complementary strand, enabling programmable binding of exogenous sequences and synthesis of XNAs, thereby facilitating the construction of tunable gene circuits (42, 43, 50) and robust data storage systems. Relying on the compatibility of cssDNA in vivo, FANA-cssDNA hybrid encoding EGFP can be expressed, although FANA strand cannot directly transcript mRNA.

Another key feature of our storage system is its versatile and robust data retrieval mechanism. Data can be recovered from the intact FANA-cssDNA hybrid or independently from either the cssDNA or the ssFANA strand. Even when the DNA strand is completely degraded, the data stored in the FANA strand can be fully recovered by reverse transcription with Bst LF polymerase, followed by standard PCR and sequencing. This strategy may further offer opportunities for molecular steganography and encrypted data storage (51). A current consideration for all XNA-based platforms, however, is the challenge of direct sequencing. Standard sequencing polymerases used in Sanger and NGS technologies do not efficiently recognize most XNAs, including FANA. This necessitates the reverse transcription step, adding another layer to the retrieval workflow. Thus, the development of direct XNA sequencing method is a critical next frontier. A recent study demonstrated that the nanopore method can sequence the FANA incorporated nucleic acid strand, displaying promise for direct XNA sequencing (52). However, the nanopore method of the FANA sequencing is limited by its ability to accurately detect oligonucleotide of only a few dozen bases. Besides, reverse transcriptase with high processivity is necessary for XNA sequencing. Although various polymerases have been evolved for XNA reverse transcription, most require thermophilic conditions (high temperatures), rendering them incompatible with standard nanopore sequencing platforms (53). Furthermore, indirect sequencing via the generated cDNA inevitably introduces errors inherent to the reverse transcription process. Therefore, there is a critical need to develop reverse transcriptases specifically optimized for sequencing applications. Beyond sequence-based storage, the exceptional stability of FANA makes it an ideal material for XNA nanotechnology. Long ssFANA–containing origami (54) could serve as a promising platform for storing structural data, representing another exciting avenue for XNA-based data carriers.

In summary, our study demonstrates a robust long-stranded FANA–DNA hybrid–based data storage platform. Our findings confirm that the introduction of long-stranded XNA-DNA hybrids offers a solution to mitigate the risks associated with DNA storage systems, preventing data loss due to nucleic acid degradation under open environmental conditions. Long XNA-DNA hybrids have potential for improving data storage density and facilitating data retrieval and recovery. We believe that long-stranded XNA presents a remarkable opportunity for XNA as carriers for data storage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Triphosphates of DNA (dNTPs) were obtained from NEB (New England Biolabs, USA). Triphosphates of TNA (tNTPs) were provided by H. Yu (Nanjing University, China). Triphosphates of FANA (fNTPs) were obtained from Metkinen Chemistry (Kuusisto, Finland). dZTP was obtained from Stong (Hefei Stong Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Triphosphates of PS-DNA (dNTP-αS) were obtained from Stong (Hefei Stong Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Plasmid of pGDR11-Tgoexo− was provided by Y. Wang (Hangzhou Institute of Medical Sciences, China). Plasmid of pET-KOD was obtained from Hedgehog (Hedgehog Science and Technology Ltd., China) and cloned into vector pGDR11. Other variants containing Tgoexo−, TgoD4K, KODwt, KODRSGA, KOD10–92, and all mutants for screening were generated by site-directed mutagenesis. ExTaq polymerase was obtained from Takara (China). Bst LF polymerase was obtained from NEB (New England Biolabs, USA). Phanta polymerase was obtained from Vazyme (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Oligonucleotides were obtained from Sangon (Shanghai, China) or Tsingke (Nanjing, China) unless stated otherwise.

Substrate scope of the polymerase panel

Template 1 and primer 1 were preannealed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer. Reactions were performed using template 1 (2 μM), primer 1 (2 μM), and a mixture of xNTPs (0.25 mM each) and incubated at 55°C (Bst LF, Tgoexo−, TgoD4K, KODwt, KODRSGA, and KOD10–92; 2 μM each) or 72°C (Taq and Phanta; 2 μM each) for 1 hour. The samples were separated on a 10% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and visualized with both Cy5 label and GelRed poststain. Polymerases were able to transcribe the template and incorporate the modified NTP resulting in full length extension. Polymerases with no activity toward the modified NTP resulted in truncated products. Control reactions were performed in the absence of polymerase to determine the rate of background misincorporation of noncomplementary bases.

Acid resistance assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were added to 4 μl of acidic buffer (130 mM HCl and 50 mM KCl in ddH2O) and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. The products were adjusted to neutral by 1 μl of alkaline buffer (900 mM NaHCO3 and 100 mM Na2CO3). The samples were incubated with 8 M urea at 95°C for 30 min and analyzed on a 12% urea PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

Thermal stability assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were incubated in nuclease-free water at 95°C for 1 hour. The samples were analyzed on a 12% native PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

Formamide resistance assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were incubated in 80% (v/v) formamide at 95°C for 2 hours. The samples were analyzed on a 12% native PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

FBS resistance assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were incubated with 10% FBS in a total volume of 10 μl. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours and analyzed on a 12% native PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

Restriction endonuclease (Nde I and Pdm I) resistance assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were incubated with 1 U of Nde I or Pdm I (NEB) in a total volume of 10 μl using 1× CutSmart Buffer (NEB), respectively. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 12 hours and analyzed on a 12% native PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

Exo III resistance assay for short strands

A total of 250 ng of XNA-DNA duplexes were treated with 4 mU/μl of Exo III (NEB) in a total volume of 10 μl using 1× Exo III Buffer (Takara). The reaction mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 4 hours and analyzed on a 12% native PAGE to visualize the extent of degradation.

Construction of mutant plasmids

The plasmids for single-site mutants were obtained using a circular mutagenesis approach. Using the pGDR11-TgoD4K plasmid as the template, oligonucleotides containing the desired mutation sites were used as primers (table S3). The reaction was performed with Phanta polymerase (Vazyme, China) following the program outlined below: 95°C for 30 s, (95°C for 10 s, 55°C for 5 s, and 72°C for 70 s) × 18, and 72°C for 1 min. The resulting PCR products were digested by Dpn I at 37°C for 30 min and then transformed into E. coli BL21 cells for plasmid amplification and sequencing. The plasmid for multiple-site mutants was obtained using overlap extension PCR. The generation of protein mutants containing the desired target mutation sites was performed using a two-step PCR strategy. First, PCR reactions were conducted with primers containing the specific mutation sites to obtain multiple insert fragments harboring the mutations of interest. Subsequently, these insert fragments were mixed together, and a second round of PCR was carried out using the outermost primers to generate the complete protein mutant sequence with the desired target mutation site. Last, the amplified sequence was ligated to a linearized pGDR11 vector through Exnase II (Vazyme, C112-01), and the resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 cells for amplification and sequencing.

Synthesis of cssDNA for data storage by pScaf phagemid

pScaf-EGFP(+) plasmid (43) and DNA fragment with data were linearized by PCR amplification, respectively. The two fragments were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified by the gel extraction purification kit (Sangon). These fragments were then assembled through Gibson assembly (ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit). The plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing in Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. A total of 400 ng of pScaf-EGFP(+) plasmid was then cotransfected with 400 ng of helper plasmid (pSB4423) into XL1-Blue competent cell. After incubation at 37°C for 24 hours on solid medium, the colonies were harvested by washing with 2× YT medium. The resulting suspension was inoculated into 600 ml of 2× YT medium and cultured for an additional 18 to 20 hours at 30°C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.4. The supernatant was then collected via centrifugation, and phages were precipitated by adding polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 800 and NaCl to final concentrations of 4% (w/v) and 0.5 M, respectively. Following 2 hours of mixing, the phage pellet was recovered by centrifugation, and the cssDNA was extracted by QIAGEN kit (EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit) with high purity.

Enzymatic synthesis of long-stranded XNA

For FANA, reaction was performed using 200 nM cssDNA (2003 nt), 500 nM primer M13_ori-R, 1 μmol TgoD4K polymerase, and 150 μmol fNTP in 1× ThermoPol buffer with the following program: 95°C for 3 min and 55°C for 30 min. For TNA, 200 nM cssDNA (2003 nt) and 500 nM primer M13_ori-R were preannealed in 1× ThermoPol buffer, and then, 1 μmol KOD10–92 polymerase and 150 μmol tNTP were added and incubated at 55°C for 72 hours. To compensate for the loss of enzymatic activity, fresh polymerase was added every 12 hours.

Polymerase expression and purification

Polymerases were expressed and purified as previously reported (55). Briefly, TgoD4K and its mutants’ genes were cloned into the pGDR11 vector and transformed into E. coli BL21 cells. The cultures were grown in 50 ml of LB medium containing ampicillin (100 μg/ml) at 37°C with shaking at 240 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.6. Then, the cultures were induced by adding isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (0.5 mM) and incubated at 16°C with shaking at 240 rpm for 20 hours. The cells were collected by centrifugation, and the pellet was lysed by sonication in buffer (10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM KCl, and 10% glycerol). The lysate was centrifuged for 30 min at 16,200g at 4°C, and the clarified supernatant was heated for 1 hour at 80°C and then immediately cooled for 30 min on ice. The lysate was clarified again by centrifugation for 30 min at 4°C and 16,200g. Polyethyleneimine (0.5%, v/v) was added to precipitate the nucleic acids, and then, the lysate was centrifuged for 30 min at 16,200g at 4°C. Ammonium sulfate (60%, w/v) was added to precipitate the polymerase; after incubating for 1 hour at 4°C, it was centrifuged for 30 min at 16,200g at 4°C. Protein pellets were suspended in 4°C precooled buffer (10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, and 10% glycerol). The supernatant was loaded onto Ni-NTA resin. All proteins eluted at 200 mM imidazole were dialyzed in 4°C buffer (10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, and 10% glycerol). The purity of fractions obtained was verified by SDS-PAGE and stored at −80°C.

Measurement of synthesis rates of polymerase mutations

To measure the synthesis rates of the polymerase, kinetic measurements were performed as previously reported (35). Each measurement (10 μl) contained 1 μM of 30-mer template, 100 μM of each nucleotide triphosphate, 1× ThermoPol buffer, 2× LC Green Plus fluorescent dye, and 20 nM polymerase. Reactions were denatured for 3 min at 95°C and extended for 30 min at 55°C, with fluorescence intensity recorded at 6-s intervals. Fluorescence data for each polymerase were normalized and converted to nucleotides per polymerase. The synthesis rate was determined by linear fitting of nucleotides per polymerase over reaction time. The reported values are the average of three independent replicates.

Syntheses and purification of nucleic acids used in storage

Phanta polymerase and Tgomut were used to make cdsDNA and FANA-cssDNA, respectively. For cdsDNA, the extensive reaction was performed using 200 nM cssDNA, 500 nM primer, 0.2 U Phanta polymerase, and 150 mmol dNTP in 1× Phanta Flash buffer with the following program: 95°C for 3 min and 55°C for 15 min. For FANA-cssDNA, the reaction was performed using 200 nM cssDNA, 500 nM primer, 1 μmol Tgomut polymerase, and 150 μmol fNTP in 1× ThermoPol buffer with the following program: 95°C for 3 min and 55°C for 15 min. Target products were purified by QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit.

Formamide resistance assay for long strands

A total of 100 ng of nucleic acids (FANA-cssDNA hybrid/cdsDNA) were added to 8 μl of formamide and incubated at 95°C for 15 min to 12 hours with different intervals. The products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel. ImageJ was used for the qualification of undegraded nucleic acids.

Acid resistance assay for long strands

To determine acid resistance of nucleic acids, 100 ng of nucleic acids (FANA-cssDNA hybrid/cdsDNA/cssDNA) were added to 4 μl of acidic buffer (130 mM HCl and 50 mM KCl in ddH2O) and incubated at 37°C for 12 min to 8 hours with different intervals. Then, the products were adjusted to neutral by 1 μl of alkaline buffer (900 mM NaHCO3 and 100 mM Na2CO3). The reaction was incubated with 8 M urea at 95°C for 30 min and analyzed by 1% agarose gel.

FBS resistance assay for long strands

A total of 100 ng of nucleic acids (FANA-cssDNA hybrid/cdsDNA) were digested with 10% FBS in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) for 1 to 24 hours with different intervals. The products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel.

Construction of nucleic acid strands used in single-molecule assay

In this study, we constructed two types of nucleic acid strands: hairpin for unzipping force measurement and double helical strand for shear force measurement. The hairpin tethers were constructed as in previous publications (56, 57). Briefly, the hairpin sequences were first annealed with two flank sequences and then ligated with two Bst XI endonuclease (Takara, China) digested and 5′-terminal labeled (thiol and biotin, respectively) dsDNA handles (~500 bp) (fig. S4). Because of the FANA-DNA hybrid and FANA-FANA full hairpin being hardly obtained by solid-phase synthesis, we used Tgoexo− polymerase to extend the FANA fragment at 55°C temperature for 1 hour. Similarly, TNA-DNA hybrid hairpin was also obtained by KODRSGA polymerase extension. For shear force measurement, the 1.8-kbp double-helical DNA was amplified by PCR with two 5′-terminal labeled primers, while the FANA-DNA hybrid required a more intricate process. The ssDNA should be linearly amplified by single-primer PCR with a thiol-labeled primer initially, and then using the ssDNA as a template, the FANA strand could be extended by Tgomut polymerase with a biotin-labeled primer at 60°C temperature for 1 hour (fig. S11). All the double-stranded PCR products were purified by a PCR purification kit (Sangon, China), and the single-primer PCR products, ligated products, or extended products were purified by a gel extraction kit (Sangon, China).

Single-molecule measurements using magnetic tweezers

The single-molecular measurements were performed using homemade high-throughput magnetic tweezers. The processes of single-molecular experiments followed well-established protocols in previous studies (56, 57). To conduct a single-molecular assay, a 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane–modified coverslip was used for building the single-molecule flow chamber. The well-constructed nucleic acid tethers are covalently bound to the surface of the coverslip by sulfo-SMCC (Aladdin Biotech Co., Ltd., China) coupling. Before experiment, the streptavidin-coated Dynabeads M270 magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were flowed into the chamber and incubated for 10 min for binding the biotin-labeled nucleic acid tethers, and then, the free beads were flowed out by PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The forces applied to the beads were determined by the distance between the magnets and the beads (58). The extension of the nucleic acid strands was determined by the diffraction pattern. The movement of the bead was recorded by a 410-Hz charge-coupled device camera. All the single-molecule experiments were performed at room temperature and conducted in a PBS buffer environment. Data were analyzed by homemade MATLAB code (MathWorks).

Reverse transcription of FANA oligonucleotides

To isolate ssFANA, template 2 was modified with biotin-dUTP using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently extended using primer 1 and fNTPs as above. The extension products were purified using streptavidin agarose beads to yield Cy5-labeled ssFANA. For reverse transcription, 5 pmol of the purified ssFANA was annealed with 5 pmol of the reverse transcription primer in 1× ThermoPol Reaction Buffer (NEB). The reaction was initiated by the addition of dNTPs and 2 U of Bst LF polymerase (NEB), followed by incubation at 55°C for 3 hours. Last, the reaction products were analyzed via 12% native PAGE.

Recovering data from FANA-cssDNA hybrid

In the intact state of cssDNA, conventional PCR amplification experiments were used for data retrieval and storage. The reaction was performed using 30 ng of FANA-cssDNA hybrid, 500 nM primer, and 1× PrimeSTAR Max Premix with the following program: 95°C for 3 min, (95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 2 min) × 25, and 72°C for 5 min. FANA was used as a template for data recovery when cssDNA underwent degradation due to contamination in an open environment. The reaction was performed using 10 ng of FANA, 200 nM primer, 100 μM dNTPs, 5 mM MgCl2, and 4 U Bst LF polymerase in 1× ThermoPol buffer. FANA and primer were preannealed before addition of Bst LF polymerase. The samples were incubated at 50°C for 18 hours, and Bst LF polymerase was replenished per 6 hours. Then, 20 ng of proteinase K was used to digest the Bst LF polymerase for 60 min at 37°C and heated to be inactive for 15 min at 95°C. cDNA was subsequently amplified by conventional PCR as mentioned above.

Cell culture, transfection, fluorescence detection, and flow cytometry assays

MDCK cell line was provided by J. Wang (State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity, China). The cells were cultured in the medium containing high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, catalog no. 11995040), 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, catalog no. 15140122) at a temperature of 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The transfection processes were the same as reported in the publication (43). Initially, the cells were seeded in a 48-well plate for 12 hours of incubation. cssDNA, DNA-cssDNA, and FANA-cssDNA hybrid (0.5 pmol for each well) were then transfected by Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11668019). Both nucleic acid strands were loaded by 1 μl of Lipofectamine 2000 as the commercial protocol suggested. The results of transfection were visualized by an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus CKX53) after 24 hours of transfections. To quantify the EGFP-positive cell ratio, the cells were collected in PBS and measured by flow cytometry (MoniSight flow cytometer, Gaugene, Beijing). Data were processed by FlowJo V10 software.

CFE system and IVT system

Experiments using the CFE system were performed as described in our previous publication (50). Briefly, the ProteinFactory fast reaction mixture (Kangma-Healthcode, Shanghai), containing the necessary protein expression components, was reconstituted in 10 ml of water. For EGFP expression measurements, 20 μl of DNA-cssDNA or FANA-cssDNA hybrids at varying concentrations were added to 40 μl of the ProteinFactory reaction mixture. The resulting 60-μl reaction was transferred to a 96-well plate. EGFP fluorescence was monitored using a multifunctional microplate reader (Tecan SparkControl) with continuous shaking at 150 rpm and incubation at 28°C for 5 hours.

As instructed, DNA-cssDNA duplex or FANA-cssDNA hybrids were mixed with A/G/C/UTP, polymerase, and 10 × buffer, followed by 3-hour reaction at 37°C. After reaction, 2 μl of DNase I was incorporated for digesting the template for 1 hour. The mRNA production of each sample was measured and visualized by gel electrophoresis.

Training and inference protocol of PRIME

Our model uses exclusively mutated or wild-type protein sequences as input, without incorporating Protein Data Bank structures. The workflow commenced with Round 1, wherein PRIME was trained on 28 sequence-activity data points (including D4K) from (34). This trained model is used to predict activity scores for all multisite mutants derived from Round 1 single-site variants, with the top 30 scoring mutants selected for experimental validation. We then experimentally determined the activity of these top mutants. These previously uncharacterized mutants were then incorporated into the training set to retrain an enhanced model designated PRIME-Round 2. During Round 3, PRIME-Round 2 generated fitness predictions across all multisite mutants, leading to experimental characterization of another 30 top-scoring variants. It should be noted that the training data solely comprised point-mutated sequences, resulting in limited sequence diversity.

The training and inference protocol for Rounds 2 and 3 used mean square error as the loss function to minimize discrepancies between predicted and true activity values. During this training, the learning rate is 1 × 10−4, and the batch size is 16. The training epochs are dynamically decided. We begin by randomly splitting the dataset into five folds. In each iteration, we use four folds for training and the remaining one for validation. We track the number of epochs needed for the validation set across these iterations, resulting in five epoch counts. The final number of training epochs for the entire dataset is determined by averaging these five epoch numbers. In the final training phase, we do not use a validation set or test set; instead, we train on all of the mutated data using the previously determined number of epochs. After training, the model can be used to predict the fitness of unseen mutated sequences.
