A study of the impacts sky view factor on urban environments and pedestrians’ comforts
Authors: Hui Yin, Kairui Gao, Yuantao Chen, Quanzhang Mo, Feng Zhu, Jianhua Lai, Fengjiao Yang, Yang Zhang, Huan Li, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban environmental quality (EnQ) encompassing thermal comfort and air quality is a critical determinant of urban livability. Global warming and urban heat island have sharpened their negative effects. The sky view factor (SVF), a key metric of urban geometry, is known to influence EnQ, but its integrated and context-dependent effects across different urban settings remain inadequately quantified. This study presents a comprehensive field measurement investigation in Chengdu, China, a humid subtropical megacity, employing a comparative approach with synchronized microclimate monitoring and extensive questionnaire surveys (N = 1692) across contrasting street and park settings. findings in this study reveal a fundamental context-dependency in both environmental mechanisms and human perception. Pedestrians exhibited high thermal sensitivity, with neutral physiologically equivalent temperatures (PET) of 24.2 °C in streets and a significantly lower 19.2 °C in parks. In contrast, sensitivity to air pollution was weaker, with a discomfort threshold for PM2.5 identified at 46 µg/m³. Crucially, the dominant factors shaping environmental quality diverged between settings: in streets, thermal comforts were primarily governed by surface albedo, whereas in parks, it was predominantly controlled by SVF. A striking paradox was observed for air quality; contrary to conventional understanding, multivariate analysis controlling for traffic variables showed that higher SVF was associated with increased PM2.5 concentrations in street canyons, yet it facilitated pollutant dispersion and improved air quality in the park. These findings underscore the complex and often countervailing interplay between urban form and environmental performance. The study provides a robust, evidence-based foundation for moving beyond one-size-fits-all solutions, advocating for context-sensitive urban design strategies that are precisely calibrated to the distinct environmental logic of streets and green spaces to enhance pedestrian comfort and public health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56461-1.

Introduction

The accelerating pace of global urbanization has placed the environmental quality (EnQ) of cities at the forefront of contemporary challenges1. They had direct severe implications for human health, energy sustainability, and social well-being. Among the most pressing issues are the synergistic threats of urban overheating2 and atmospheric pollution3. Extreme heat, often intensified by urban heat island (UHI) and global warming anomalies effect, compromises outdoor thermal comfort4, escalates the demand for energy-intensive cooling5, and contributes to a vicious cycle of elevated carbon emissions6 that further degrades air quality7. This intertwined environmental stress manifests in severe societal consequences, including increased risks of cardiorespiratory mortality, psychological stress8, and diminished public productivity9.

The urban microclimate is not a monolithic entity but is intricately sculpted by the city’s physical form and land use10,11. Key morphological parameters, such as the sky view factor (SVF)—a geometric measure of sky visibility ranging from 0 (fully obstructed) to 1 (completely open)—fundamentally modulate the local environment by controlling the access to solar radiation and the efficacy of natural ventilation12–14. The thermal impacts of SVF are paradigmatic of its dual nature: a low SVF provides shading that can mitigate heat stress15, whereas a high SVF typically leads to greater solar heat gain, elevating the physiologically equivalent temperature (PET)16. Concurrently, the role of SVF in air quality is equally critical yet complex; restricted ventilation in low-SVF settings can trap locally emitted pollutants17, while open geometries may, in theory, facilitate their dispersion18. This duality creates a persistent conundrum for urban planners and designers: an intervention that alleviates heat stress (e.g., by increasing shading) might inadvertently worsen air quality, and vice versa.

Furthermore, the efficacy of these physical factors is highly context-dependent, varying significantly between densely built streets, which are often dominated by traffic emissions, and vegetated parks, which may act as pollution sinks19,20. While the individual effects of SVF on temperature or pollution have been studied in isolation21, a critical gap remains in quantitatively understanding its integrated and context-specific impact on the multi-faceted EnQ and, most importantly, on the resulting human perception and comfort22,23. Most prior research has either focused on a single environmental parameter24 or has decoupled objective physical measurements from subjective human feedback25 defining as outdoor thermal comfort (OTC). For instance, while studies have established PET-SVF relationships in generic urban canyons26, the comparative influence of SVF versus other factors like albedo across contrasting land uses (streets vs. parks) is poorly quantified and rarely compared within the same city under identical meteorological conditions27,28. Similarly, the discrepancy between objectively measured pollution levels and the public’s often-delayed or attenuated perception of air quality risk represents a crucial knowledge gap for public health communication and the design of self-protective urban spaces23,29,30.

To address these interconnected gaps, this study conducts a rigorous comparative field investigation in Chengdu, a major Chinese megacity characterized by a humid subtropical (Cfa) climate—a zone experiencing increasing climate stress and representing a vast, rapidly urbanizing region31,32. This study employed a novel framework that concurrently examines street and park environments, integrating high-resolution microclimate and air pollution monitoring33 with a large-scale structured questionnaire34. The work is designed to achieve three primary objectives:

Methodology

Study area and site selection

Chengdu (30°05′ – 31°26′ N, 102°54′ – 104°53′ E), a pivotal megacity in western China (Fig. 1), serves as the context for this study31,35. With a population exceeding 21 million, it is experiencing intense urban environmental pressures36. Situated in a humid subtropical climate zone (Cfa under the Köppen-Geiger classification), Chengdu is characterized by hot, humid summers and generally mild winters37, leading to pronounced annual thermal stress38. Decades of rapid urban development have intensified environmental challenges, including the urban heat island effect and significant air pollution39, thereby directly impacting the city’s livability40. The city’s multi-ring road structure defines its urban form, with our study focused on the metropolitan core within the 3rd Ring Road to ensure high pedestrian activity and environmental variability.

This research employed a comparative approach, selecting two contrasting land-use types for investigation: high-density urban streets and a central urban park. They belong to Qingyang District (one of the city central populated administration district of Chengdu).

Street (S) Zone: Sampling was conducted along Shudu Avenue, a main city thoroughfare known for high traffic density and commercial activity, representing a classic high-density built-up environment with significant vehicular emissions41.Park (P) Zone: The People’s Park, located approximately 800 m from Tianfu Square (the city center), was selected as a representative urban green space. It is vital for daily recreation and tourism, featuring a mix of open lawns, and densely vegetated areas, thus offering a stark contrast to the street environment42.

Calculation of SVF

The SVF is a dimensionless parameter ranging from 0 to 1, representing the fraction of the overlying hemisphere occupied by the sky visible from a given point on the ground45. It is a critical indicator of urban geometry, influencing both solar access and radiative cooling potential46.

In this study, SVF was calculated from upward-facing hemispherical (fisheye) photographs taken at the precise location of each sampling point at a height of 1.5 m, corresponding to the pedestrian perspective. The photographs were captured using a digital camera equipped with a 180° fisheye lens under uniform overcast sky conditions to ensure consistent contrast between the sky and obstructions (e.g., buildings, trees)47.

The SVF was computed using the specialized software WinSCANOPY (2017 version), which is widely used in urban climate and forestry studies. The software analyzes the fisheye image by classifying pixels as either “sky” or “obstruction” based on a user-defined threshold. The SVF is then calculated as the ratio of sky pixels to the total number of pixels within the circular hemisphere image24.

Environmental quality parameters

The study focused on two critical and interlinked aspects of EnQ49: the thermal environment and air quality.

Thermal environment/comfort indices: The PET the most widely used index in OTC studies50. It was selected for comprehensive indexing by this study as its well adaptation various conditions51. PET integrates the effects of air temperature (Ta), air velocity (Va), relative humidity (RH), and mean radiant temperature (MRT) – the latter being a crucial parameter representing the uniform temperature of an imaginary enclosure in which the radiant heat transfer from the human body equals the radiant heat transfer in the actual non-uniform environment52. PET was then calculated using the RayMan Pro model (Version 3.1, Fig. 6), a widely recognized software for human biometeorological assessments. The model integrates the measured MRT, Ta, RH, Va, and incorporates standardized values for personal parameters: a clothing insulation (clo), a metabolic rate (met), a body weight, and a height. They were all from individual factors collected at survey sites. This approach ensures comparability across individuals and sites, as the collected individual clothing and metabolic data (Table 3) showed limited variation and were highly correlated with the standardized scenario for our summer measurement period. The use of RayMan allows for a consistent and reproducible calculation of PET based on the fundamental heat balance of the human body54,55

Air quality: Particulate matter concentrations, specifically PM2.5 and PM10, were adopted as the key air quality indicators due to their significant health impacts and prevalence in urban environments, largely originating from vehicle exhaust and other combustion processes57. As these pollutants often share common sources and exhibit strong collinearity, this study initially assessed their relationship. A significant positive linear correlation was observed between PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations across all samples (R² > 0.9, p < 0.001, Fig. 7), confirming that PM2.5 is a robust proxy for particulate pollution levels in this study context58. Given this strong collinearity and the greater health relevance of finer particles, the density of PM2.5 (PM2.5D) was selected as the primary air quality variable for all subsequent regression analyses to avoid model redundancy, while PM10 data were excluded from further modeling.

Factors affecting EnQ

A comprehensive set of independent variables were measured or calculated to explain the variations in EnQ parameters (PET and PM2.5D):

Physical factors:

Albedo: Measured in situ for dominant ground surface materials at each sample point using a portable albedometer, representing the reflectivity of surfaces.

Distance from the main road (DFtR): The perpendicular distance from each sampling point to the central line of Shudu Avenue was geolocated using Gaode Maps, serving as a proxy for exposure to traffic-related pollution.

Anthropogenic factors:

Vehicle flow: The number of vehicles (including cars, buses, and trucks) passing a designated line near the sampling point per unit time was manually recorded during measurement periods.

Pedestrian density: The number of observable people within a 360° horizontal range of the sampling point was recorded as a proxy for human-generated emissions and activity.

Meteorological covariates:

Official hourly data for Ta, RH, and Va were from the Chengdu People’s Park National Basic Meteorological Station. This station is located approximately 300 m away from the center of the measurement zones of this study (Fig. 2). These data were incorporated into the models to account for background synoptic conditions that influence both thermal comfort and pollutant dispersion59.

Field measurement and survey protocol

The field campaign was conducted during the summer of 2024 (from July 22 to August 6), exclusively on clear-sky days to ensure data comparability and minimise the confounding effects of precipitation and cloud cover60. The daily measurement period spanned from 08:00 to 20:00 with data recorded every 1 min, capturing a full diurnal cycle of environmental conditions. All participants completed the questionnaire voluntarily and anonymously. There was no illegal harm to the participating subjects. It was also confirmed that informed consent has been obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians. The whole questionnaire was ethically approved by Southwest University of Science and Technology. Meanwhile, all methods via in this study were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of this journal. Data collection at each point followed a synchronized protocol over a 30-minute period61.

Instrumental monitoring: Two sets of instruments were deployed simultaneously: a Testo 480 thermal comfort meter and a BR-SMART 128 S particulate matter sensor. All sensors were positioned at a standard height of 1.5 m above ground, representing the pedestrian breathing zone and center of gravity33. The instruments recorded data at 1-minute intervals. Prior to the field campaign, all sensors and instruments were strictly calibrated according to the manufacturers’ guidelines to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the collected data. All devices were tested in standard conditions to compare their readings for error avoidance. Results

Contextual variations in subjective perceptions

The analysis of 1692 valid questionnaires revealed a profound context-dependence in how pedestrians perceive their thermal and air quality environments. The relationship between TSV and PET was robust across all sites, yet the derived comfort benchmarks varied markedly. Linear regression models demonstrated significant positive correlations between TSV and PET in the combined dataset (R² = 0.51, p < 0.001), the street (S Zone, R² = 0.66, p < 0.001), and the park (P Zone, R² = 0.47, p < 0.001), as detailed in Fig. 8. The derived neutral PET (NPET) was 21.16 °C overall, with a marked spatial divergence: 24.18 °C in streets and 19.16 °C in the park (Fig. 8). Furthermore, the slopes of the regression lines differed, suggesting that pedestrians in streets exhibited a slightly lower rate of change in TSV per unit change in PET (0.038 °C⁻¹) compared to those in the park (0.045 °C⁻¹), though both were highly sensitive.

In stark contrast, pedestrian sensitivity to air quality was substantially lower and more uniform. The correlation between AQSV and PM2.5D was weak across all contexts (R² < 0.3), as visualized in Fig. 9. Despite this weak correlation, the model allowed for the estimation of the PM2.5 discomfort threshold (ASV = 0). The neutral PM2.5D was 47.50 µg/m³ overall, with site-specific values of 46.29 µg/m³ in streets and 48.22 µg/m³ in the park. The neutral PM2.5D values (46.29 µg/m³ in streets, 48.22 µg/m³ in park) exceeded the Chinese national standard of 35 µg/m³ for good air quality.

Divergent drivers of the thermal environment

To understand the contextual drivers of thermal stress, separate multiple linear regression models were developed for PET in streets and parks (Table 4). The models included SVF, albedo, and HTa as predictors, with HTa included to account for background thermal conditions. The models revealed a clear dichotomy in the dominant controlling factors between the two land-use types.

In urban streets (S Zone), the model was highly effective (R² = 0.796, Sig < 0.001):

Contrasting mechanisms governing the air quality

The factors influencing PM2.5 concentrations were equally context-dependent and revealed striking, countervailing relationships, as detailed in the MLR models presented in Table 5.

The model for streets (S Zone) was significant (R² = 0.446, p < 0.001):

Further, this study deconstructed how physical design factors directly influence comfort under varying levels of thermal stress (Table 7). The results reveal a complex, non-stationary relationship. For instance, in streets during moderate heat (PET 26–29 °C), comfort decreased with proximity to the road and higher SVF, but increased with higher albedo. As heat stress intensified (e.g., PET 38–41 °C), the negative impact of high SVF became dramatically more pronounced. In the park, the influence of SVF was often reversed, correlating with greater comfort, especially under lower PET conditions. This nuanced analysis confirms that the comfort benefit of a specific urban design feature is not absolute; it is contingent upon both the macro land-use type and the prevailing microclimatic conditions, demanding an adaptive approach to urban design.

Discussion

This study provides a nuanced understanding of how urban form, specifically the SVF, differentially orchestrates environmental quality and pedestrian comfort across distinct land-use contexts48. The integrated methodology, combining high-resolution environmental monitoring with a large-scale perceptual survey, moves beyond siloed approaches to reveal the complex and often countervailing mechanisms at play66,71. The central finding is that the role of SVF is not monolithic but is fundamentally reinterpreted by the prevailing context—specifically, the dominant emission sources and the presence of vegetation72,73.

Thermal perception and the contextual neutral temperature

This study’s results confirm that pedestrians are highly sensitive to thermal conditions, but their benchmark for comfort—the neutral PET—is psychologically and physiologically adapted to the immediate surroundings74. The significantly lower NPET in the park (19.2 °C) compared to streets (24.2 °C) underscores a phenomenon of contextual thermal adaptation. While the street value aligns with findings from similar high-density settings in Chengdu, the park value reveals a different reality75. This study posited that this discrepancy arises from physiological acclimatization and psychological conditioning, where individuals associate green spaces with respite and thus have higher expectations for coolness76, consistent with findings on contextual thermal adaptation77. This has a critical design implication: a thermal condition deemed “acceptable” in a street canyon may be perceived as “uncomfortably warm” within a park, demanding distinct thermal management strategies for each space78.

The dichotomy of SVF and albedo in thermal regulation

A pivotal contribution of this work is the clear dissociation of the dominant thermal regulating factors between streets and parks, a nuance seldom quantified in previous literature79. In park environments, SVF emerges as the principal governor of PET. This is a direct manifestation of the shading effect of tree canopies80, which physically intercepts solar radiation before it can heat surfaces and emit longwave radiation81. The strong positive correlation between SVF and PET in the park quantifies the powerful cooling service provided by dense vegetation, which operates through both radiation blocking and transpirative cooling82.

Conversely, in the hardscape-dominated streets, the thermal environment was more significantly governed by surface albedo. The high heat capacity and thermal conductivity of materials like asphalt and concrete make them potent reservoirs of heat83. Here, a high albedo directly boosts the reflection of solar radiation, thereby reducing the amount of heat absorbed and subsequently re-radiated. The modest role of SVF in streets suggests that the geometric shading from buildings is less effective than the material properties of the surfaces themselves in mitigating heat at the pedestrian level84. This finding critically informs mitigation strategies: in streets, investing in high-albedo “cool” pavements may yield greater thermal comfort returns than complex architectural manipulations to alter SVF, a point that merits emphasis in urban policy85.

The paradox of SVF in air quality management

Perhaps the most striking finding of this study is the opposing role of SVF in air quality regulation. The conventional wisdom that higher SVF universally improves ventilation and dispersion is challenged by our data86. In street canyons, we observed appositive correlation between SVF and PM2.5. This paradox may be explained by two non-exclusive mechanisms. Initially, in a complex urban setting, points with higher SVF might be coincidentally located near major traffic intersections or experience wind conditions that lead to the accumulation, rather than dispersion, of vehicle-emitted pollutants87. Furthermore, lower-SVF (deeper) street canyons might sometimes inhibit the vertical mixing that brings ground-level pollutants to the breathing zone, a phenomenon noted in previous modeling studies88. This suggests that simplistic prescriptions for “opening up” streets for ventilation can be counterproductive if not coupled with rigorous analysis of local wind flow and pollution sources30.

In the park, however, the relationship reverts to the expected negative correlation. Freed from the intense, direct emission sources of the streets, the park’s air quality is more influenced by the background pollution transported from its surroundings. Here, a higher SVF effectively facilitates the dilution and removal of this advected pollution, affirming the value of open spaces for ventilation within green infrastructure89. This stark contrast underscores that SVF’s impact on air quality cannot be assessed in isolation. Effects of SVF conflicted between the park and the street. This expressed the different atmospheric performances of SVF under various contexts.

The integrated view of pedestrian comfort

The final layer of this analysis synthesizes how these environmental and physical factors converge to shape the human experience (Table 7). The GCV models reveal that comfort is multi-faceted. In streets, the overwhelming sensory input is thermal, rendering air quality a secondary concern90. This “thermal dominance” in high-stress environments has been observed elsewhere and likely reflects the immediate and palpable nature of heat stress compared to the often-invisible threat of air pollution33.

In parks, where thermal stress is often mitigated by vegetation, pedestrians’ attention broadens, and air quality emerges as a co-equal determinant of discomfort. This shift in sensitivity is crucial for holistic urban design; it implies that improving air quality in recreational green spaces is as vital as providing thermal comfort91.

Furthermore, the comforting effect of physical factors like SVF and albedo varies with the background thermal stress level (Table 7) is a significant advancement92. It demonstrates that pedestrian perception is not a linear function of a single variable but a dynamic, state-dependent response. For instance, the desire for shaded (low-SVF) spaces intensifies during periods of extreme heat, particularly in streets93. This demands an adaptive design philosophy where the optimization of environmental parameters is tailored to the specific climate and land use94. This observation aligns with the findings of Zhang, et al. 95 who have noted the irregular correlations between subjective responses and physical environments.

Limitations and future research

While this study provides valuable insights into the context-dependent roles of urban morphology, several limitations should be acknowledged as they define fruitful avenues for future inquiry.

First, the study was conducted exclusively during the summer season. This focus was strategic for capturing peak heat stress conditions, but it necessarily limits the generalizability of our findings. The interplay between SVF, albedo, and thermal comfort could be fundamentally different in winter. For instance, the solar heat gain associated with high SVF, which is a detriment in summer, might become a valuable asset for thermal comfort in winter. Similarly, the dynamics of PM2.5 pollution in Chengdu are highly seasonal. A future investigation encompassing a full annual cycle is essential to develop a comprehensive, seasonally-aware model for urban design84.

Further, this study did not account for other sensory dimensions such as acoustic and visual comfort, which interact with thermal and air quality perception101. The perception of an environment is multisensory; traffic noise in a street or the aesthetic quality of a park view could potentially confound or modulate the subjective votes for thermal and air quality comfort. Future research adopting a truly multi-sensory framework would provide a more holistic understanding of human comfort in urban spaces102.

Third, the proxies used for anthropogenic emissions—manual counts of vehicle and pedestrian flow—though practical for a field study, introduce a degree of approximation. The use of more continuous and precise monitoring technologies, such as automatic traffic counters and advanced crowd-sensing techniques, could refine the quantification of emission sources and strengthen the predictive power of air quality models103.

Subjective responses toward contextual meteorology varied. Yet rarely regularly variations were witnessed. The SVF showed different effects on PM2.5 in park and street. This is an unaddressed issue needing further explorations. They should be explained by future further studies.

Finally, this research was grounded in the specific context of a humid subtropical, high-density Chinese city. The transferability of our quantitative models (e.g., the exact coefficients of the regression equations) to cities with different climatic zones, cultural backgrounds, or urban densities requires validation. We encourage future studies to apply our comparative framework in diverse geographical and cultural settings to build a more universal theory of context-sensitive urban design.

Conclusion

This study has moved beyond a monolithic understanding of the SVF by systematically dissecting its contextual and contrasting impacts on urban environmental quality (EnQ) and pedestrian comfort. Through an integrated methodology of synchronized field monitoring and extensive surveys in the streets and parks of Chengdu, we demonstrate that the efficacy of urban design parameters is not absolute but is fundamentally reinterpreted by the dominant land use, pollution sources, and human expectations. The core of our contribution lies in quantifying these critical dichotomies.

findings of the study have establish that the thermal environment is governed by different physical principles in different settings: surface albedo is the dominant cooling lever in streets, whereas SVF (through vegetative shading) plays the primary role in parks. This demands a land-use-specific approach to heat mitigation.

Furthermore, the human dimension of this study reveals that pedestrian perception is equally nuanced. The public’s thermal comfort benchmark is context-dependent, being significantly lower in parks than in streets, while their sensitivity to air pollution is lagging behind regulatory standards. Ultimately, pedestrian comfort is shaped by a dynamic interplay of environmental stressors and physical settings, where the desirability of a design feature like shade can intensify with rising heat stress.

The implications for sustainable urban development are clear and actionable. We propose a differentiated design strategy:

Future work should expand these findings to different climatic seasons and incorporate additional sensory modalities to further refine this holistic model.
