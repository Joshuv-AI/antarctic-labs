A lightweight graph-enhanced deep learning framework for explainable cucumber leaf disease diagnosis
Authors: Raiyan Gani, Maherun Nessa Isty, Yusuf Salehin, Mohammad Rifat Ahmmad Rashid, Raihan Ul Islam, Ahmed Wasif Reza, Shamim H Ripon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and efficient identification of cucumber leaf diseases is a critical step in preventing losses and facilitating timely intervention in agricultural activi-ties. However, most state-of-the-art plant disease recognition models, including those employing deep learning, often fail to identify spatial dependencies among symptomatic leaf feature regions, require high computational resources, and lack robustness in their predictions. To overcome these challenges, this paper pro-poses MobileGraph, a graph-aided deep learning model that jointly reasons local texture patterns and spatial dependencies among CNN-derived cucumber leaf feature regions using MobileNetV3 as a lightweight feature extractor. Experi-ments on a publicly available cucumber leaf disease dataset containing 5 classes and 4,000 images show that the proposed model achieves 99.75% accuracy, 99.75% macro F1-score, and 99.69% MCC, outperforming several state-of-the-art models including ResNet-152, EfficientNet-B7, DenseNet-201, ConvNeXt, and VGG16, while having a significantly lower computational cost of 0.465 GFLOPs. Explainability results from Grad-CAM and LIME indicate that the model is focused on biologically important regions of plant lesions. Furthermore, a proto-type mobile application illustrates the feasibility of real-time cucumber disease diagnosis for practical agricultural monitoring. These results indicate that Mobi-leGraph provides an efficient and interpretable solution for intelligent crop health surveillance.Keywords: Plant disease diagnosis, Cucumber leaf disease, Deep learning, Graph convolutional networks (GCN), Lightweight model, Explainable AI (XAI), Mobile and edge computing, Precision agricultureSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Cucumber is one of the most widely grown vegetable species worldwide and plays an imperative role in food security and agriculture. Cucumber growth, however, remains under constant threats from leaf spot diseases such as anthracnose, downy mildew, bac-terial wilt, and Gummy Stem Blight, which have the potential to spread very fast and result in heavy production loss if not identified and monitored on a regular basis1. It is therefore imperative for farmers to be aware of the disease at an early stage and to undertake necessary measures to reduce unnecessary pesticide use and promote healthy growing practices. Manual diagnosis, which requires knowledge and expertise, remains fundamental for detecting disease occurrence in most farming areas2.

A relatively new area involving deep learning techniques in image analysis for plant disease diagnosis also shows promise. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have proven successful at learning highly discriminative image features of disease lesions, such as color, shape, and texture, directly from images3. As pointed out in a recent survey on this area, despite the popularity of CNNs and transformer models, there still remain methods that perform poorly, particularly at identifying disease lesions that are very subtle, very similar among various diseases, and muddled with background noise common in field images3,4.

To tackle these challenges, recent efforts have focused on developing more efficient architectures for cucumber and general crop diseases, ranging from light-detection models to advanced feature aggregation. To be specific, Liu et al. introduced an effi-cient YOLO model for augmenting cucumber disease recognition in more complex field conditions5. Recent research has broadened the scope of plant disease diagno-sis by investigating deep learning-based recognition, intelligent agricultural analytics, and privacy-aware distributed learning frameworks6–10. Collectively, these stud-ies show that effective agricultural AI systems must address not only classification accuracy, but also deployment constraints, heterogeneous data, and privacy preser-vation. For example, a homomorphic-encryption-based federated learning framework was proposed for privacy-aware plant disease detection8, while another work com-bined federated learning, few-shot learning, and ensemble voting to classify cauliflower leaf diseases under non-IID data distributions10. In contrast, the present study emphasizes lightweight graph-enhanced spatial reasoning for efficient and explainable cucumber leaf disease diagnosis. Moreover, some works have used graph reasoning models with convolutional learning, such as GAT-GCN, and have shown that incorpo-rating relationships among disease regions in plants can improve classification accuracy across various species11. All these works have identified two prominent goals in agricultural AI/ML research.

Despite advances achieved within this research area, there remains a considerable research gap, as very few research papers have successfully tackled all three prob-lems: modeling spatial dependencies among leaf feature regions, high computational efficiency, and interpretability within a single method for cucumber leaf disease. Also, despite their emerging popularity in agrimaging research, graph neural networks have yet to be widely adopted for cucumber disease diagnosis4,5,11.

To address these challenges, we propose a solution using our graph-enhanced deep learning method for cucumber leaf disease diagnosis, which we refer to as MobileGraph. Our solution uses convolutional feature extraction and graph convolution reasoning. It addresses challenges in leaf disease diagnosis and aims to improve class separation among similar diseases. Moreover, we have ensured that our solution is efficient and can perform real-time processing on mobile platforms. Our solution will include an explainable AI method capable of locating biologically meaningful regions. The main contributions of this work are:

Graph-enhanced spatial representation: MobileGraph integrates MobileNetV3 feature extraction with graph convolutional reasoning to model spatial dependencies among feature-map regions of cucumber leaves, rather than relying only on global CNN representations.

Related works

Deep learning for plant disease classification

A systematic review of deep learning methods for classifying plant disease conducted by Pacal et al. (2024) highlighted the widespread adoption of benchmark datasets such as PlantVillage and field images, with CNNs and transfer learning often achieving accuracy above 95%12. Furthermore, challenges were identified regarding mobile diagnosis and performance under field conditions. Zhao et al. (2025) surveyed more general methods based on CNNs and transformers for leaf disease classification and noted the high accuracy achieved on benchmark datasets, but they also highlighted problems associated with imbalanced datasets and a lack of explainable reasoning13. The applicability of Deep Learning in the agricultural context of Bangladesh and a CNN-based system for assisting crop diagnosis were explored and proposed by Chowdhury et al. (2025)14, achieving more than 95% accuracy in lab condi-tions but struggling with applicability for region-specific disease diagnosis and data availability. To improve generalization capabilities, Krishna et al. (2025) introduced a multi-Dataset Deep Learning approach15, achieving above 96% accuracy with better robustness but still experiencing accuracy drops in noisy and real-world condi-tions, underscoring the need for better Interpretability. Practical applicability within implementation tools was given prime importance by Tembhurne et al. (2023), who developed a CNN-based mobile app to assist disease diagnosis16, achieving about 95% accuracy but remaining vulnerable to image noise and varying imaging angles during implementation. A Depthwise CNN incorporating Squeeze-and-Excitation and Residual Learning concepts was proposed by Ashurov et al. (2025)17, achieving above 96% accuracy and improving computational speed, yet remaining vulnerable in Real-Scenario Conditions.

Lightweight and efficient architectures for agricultural AI

To enable remote observation and monitoring of crop growth, RTR-Lite-MobileNetV2, with an accuracy above 95% and efficient on-device computation, but with lim-ited robustness to varying illumination conditions, was proposed by Duhan et al. in 202518. Abid et al. in 2024 worked on a crop recognition system with YOLOv8 as the detector for Bangladeshi crops19 and reached an average accuracy ranging from 94–96%, but it depends on the level of obscuration and presence of background com-plexity. Liu et al. in 2025 introduced an agriculture field-based system for cucumber leaf disease classification using transfer learning, achieving accuracy above 95% on real-leaf images, but it relies on several preprocessing steps5.

Sahin et al. introduced CucuNetCNNs in 2025 as an ensemble of deep CNNs, achieving more than 97% accuracy, surpassing single-model methods but at higher computational cost and lower mobility20. Bilal et al. included fuzzy logic reasoning for CNN architectures in 2025, which better tackled uncertain spots on cucum-ber leaves with 96–97% accuracy but with higher computation costs and poorer performance under varying light, as cited limitations21.

Graph neural networks and structural reasoning in plant imaging

A dual-branch convolutional graph attention network was proposed and implemented by Raman and Jayaraman in 2025 for rice leaf disease diagnosis, achieving accuracy above 96% but at a high computational cost22. Bossoun and Ying introduced graph neural networks for early disease diagnosis in 2024, but their deployability was limited due to the complexity of implementing graph representations, with accuracy ranging from 94–96%23.

Bera et al. proposed PND-Net24 that uses spatial dependencies for nutrient deficiencies and disease classification with more than 95% accuracy but depended on accurate segmentation and had constraints for mobile computing. Maruthai et al. com-bined CNN and GNN modules25 for coffee pest and disease detection, achieving more than 96% accuracy, but at the cost of high computational requirements and seg-mentation. Bajpai et al. experimented with spectral-graph-directed classifications26 with lesser accuracies ranging from 93–95%, but computation constraints hindered applicability.

Explainable AI (XAI) in automated plant diagnosis

Rashid et al. (2025) coupled ensembles of CNNs with explainable AI (XAI) tech-niques27 and achieved an accuracy above 96% while improving user trust through Grad-CAM visualizations. However, interpretability was limited to saliency maps and came at the cost of reduced prediction performance. Alhammad et al. (2025) designed an XAI booster for potato disease classification with accuracy above 95%28; however, accuracy degraded with field noise and showed limited interspecies generalizability.

Kondaveeti and Simhadri (2025) analyzed explainable architectures for inter-pretable rice disease diagnosis with accuracy values ranging from 95% to 97%29. However, applicability and generalizability for other crop species are limited. Kaur et al. (2025) incorporated optimization heuristics with convolutional neural networks and implemented XAI, achieving accuracy above 94% with CNNs30. Nonetheless, applicability in realistic, demanding environments remains a bottleneck. Al-Falluji and Albahar (2025) put forth an interpretable multi-model strategy for rice leaf detection, which aligns with green AI and achieved an accuracy value of 95% with limitations related to applicability and explainability31.

Methodology

A brief overview of the methodological pipeline (Fig. 1) followed in the research work includes preprocessing, feature extraction, structural reasoning, and model evaluation. To begin with, cucumber leaf images from five classes were normalized for uniform distribution and size, and then stratified into test, validation, and training sets. These images were then passed through a pretrained MobileNetV3-Large feature extractor, which produced a feature representation, on which a graph structure was imposed. This enabled learning correlations among CNN-derived spatial feature regions with graph convolution. The resultant feature representation at an abstract level was then used to classify different classes using a prediction head. To enable interpretation and visualization regarding model predictions, Grad-CAM and LIME were implemented.

Workflow diagram.

Based on performance analysis, system assessment on various multi-class metrics was conducted and implemented on a real-time platform.

Dataset description

To conduct experiments on cucumber disease diagnosis, a publicly available dataset titled Cucumber Disease Recognition Dataset, available on the Mendeley Data repos-itory32, was used. The dataset contains images of cucumber leaves belonging to five categories: Anthracnose, Bacterial Wilt, Downy Mildew, Fresh Leaf, and Gummy Stem Blight. Each category contains 800 images, totaling 4,000 samples.

The images vary in cucumber leaf visual characteristics, including disease severity, lesion shape, texture, lighting conditions, and background scenes. Such images simulate real-world agricultural imaging conditions and are helpful in designing effective disease classification models. Baseline models

To provide a rigorous, justifiable comparison, four common and highly successful con-volutional neural network (CNN) architectures were selected as benchmarks. Each of these models captures a distinct trade-off among depth, representation capacity, and efficiency, making each essential for understanding MobileGraph’s contribution.

MobileNet-V2 is designed for a low-resource setting through the application of inverted residual blocks and depthwise-separable convolutions. In this manner, it significantly decreases computational complexity and the number of model parame-ters required without undermining feature learning capacity. Due to its lightweight architecture, MobileNet-V2 is competitive for real-time execution on mobile plat-forms in agricultural applications, though it may not be as effective for learning complex disease presentations due to its limited capacity.

MobileGraph architecture

The proposed MobileGraph model (Fig. 4) is formulated to combine, within a single framework, the strengths of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for spatial feature representation and graph convolution for relational reasoning over leaf structures. The model has three key elements: (i) convolutional feature representation based on

MobileGraph Architecture.

MobileNetV3, (ii) creation of a learnable graph representation to model long-range dependencies within leaf structures, and (iii) graph-aware classification to support global semantic reasoning. This end-to-end model remains compact and can run on relatively low-powered hardware, making it suitable for agricultural applications where computational resources must be carefully allocated.

MobileNetV3 feature extraction backbone

The backbone of the network, a MobileNetV3, was selected as it provides an effective balance of representational power and computational cost. This is especially true com-pared with earlier models, such as MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNet, which also provide lightweight alternatives. The addition of neural architecture search-optimized blocks, squeeze-and-excite attention, and improved activation functions provides a boost to the network’s representational power. These characteristics make it especially well-suited for agricultural image analysis tasks, where the texture of lesions and their colors must be effectively captured. The small feature map produced by MobileNetV3 is also effective as an input representation for the graph convolutional reasoning mod-ule, allowing it to learn relationships among receptive-field feature regions without high computational cost. This is especially useful for the MobileGraph framework, allowing it to provide a high level of predictive performance.

Training pipeline and hyperparameters

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and conducted using a MobileNetV3 backbone coupled with a learnable graph convolutional network (GCN) head. The overall training pipeline consisted of four stages: data preprocessing, dataset splitting, mini-batch loading, and model optimization. The model demonstrated fast conver-gence during training. This was because the model used a pre-trained MobileNetV3 backbone and an evenly distributed dataset.

Data preprocessing

The raw cucumber leaf images were first resized to a fixed spatial resolution of 224×224 pixels to match the backbone network’s input size. Each image was then converted to a PyTorch tensor and channel-wise normalized to the range [−1,1] using a fixed mean and standard deviation of (0.5,0.5,0.5) for all three RGB channels:

The values (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) were used as the fixed mean and standard deviation to scale pixel intensities to the range [−1, 1], a commonly adopted normalization strategy that ensures stable training across images captured under varying conditions.

Dataset organization and splitting

The data was organized using the ImageFolder utility, with each disease category in a separate folder, which serves as the label for that class. The categories are: Anthracnose, Bacterial Wilt, Downy Mildew, Fresh Leaf, and Gummy Stem Blight.

Stratified random division with 70%, 20%, and 10% for the training, validation, and testing sets maintained balanced classes within each subset. This division resulted in 2,800 samples for the training subset and another 800 and 400 for the validation and testing subsets, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5. This ensured control over data leakage while allowing a fair evaluation of performance.

Mini-batch data loading

For each subset, PyTorch DataLoader objects were used to construct mini-batches of images and labels. A batch size of 32 was used throughout all experiments. The train-ing loader was shuffled at every epoch to reduce the risk of the model overfitting to data ordering, while the validation and test loaders were kept deterministic (no shuffling) to ensure stable evaluation. Model optimization

The classification model architecture consisted of a MobileNetV3 backbone, followed by two dense layers incorporating a graph convolutional neural network (GCN) layer operating on a 7 × 7 spatial support, and a fully connected classification layer that outputs logits for five classes. The model was trained using a cross-entropy loss func-tion, a commonly used loss function for multiclass classification. Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, applied uni-formly across all model parameters, including both convolutional and graph-based layers. Model parameters were updated iteratively using mini-batches sampled from the training dataset, while model performance was evaluated on a validation dataset at the end of each training iteration. The model weights corresponding to the best validation performance were saved and used for analysis during testing.

Evaluation metrics

To assess the classifiers’ performance in the multiclass cucumber disease recognition problem, several metrics were used. Accuracy is measured by the proportion of correct predictions. This provided insight into the classifiers’ performance.

To prevent misleading inferences due to variation in class-level performance, pre-cision, recall, and F1-score values were reported for all classes to assess false-positive and false-negative patterns, as well as the harmonious balance between them. Since the classes in the dataset are evenly distributed, the macro F1-score was particularly helpful and did not favor any class.

A confusion matrix has been created to visualize misclassification rates at the class level and to analyze overlapping symptoms for each disease. Moreover, the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) has been added. This is an appropriate metric for multiclass classification when the number of instances per category is equal. Musica et al. have used MCC to calculate the accuracy of machine learning models.

Explainable AI (XAI)

To enhance transparency and provide assurance of trustworthy deployment in agri-cultural decision-making, two different techniques of explainability methods were combined within the framework of MobileGraph, which were Grad-CAM and LIME. The Grad-CAM algorithm achieves this by back-propagating class-specific gradi-ents through the final convolutional layers to derive saliency heat maps of areas most relevant to a prediction. This allows for a visually correct confirmation that biologically significant structures, such as chlorosis, necrosis, or vein abnormalities, are selectively highlighted rather than unnecessary background information.

Feature perturbation-based interpretation is provided by local surrogate models that generate approximations of MobileGraph’s output near each image input. The positive or negative contributions of specific textural and color regions to a given decision are determined using superpixel masking in each image.

Statistical evaluation

To ensure reliable and generalizable performance reporting, several statistical tech-niques were applied to the MobileGraph model evaluation.

Confidence intervals for accuracy

A Wilson binomial confidence interval was calculated to quantify the uncertainty around the observed test accuracy, providing a probabilistic estimate of how accuracy may vary on unseen data.

Bootstrap for performance metrics

Nonparametric bootstrap resampling (≈ 1000 iterations) was performed to obtain confidence intervals for key metrics, including Macro-F1, MCC, and per-class Preci-sion/Recall/F1, without relying on distributional assumptions.

Class stability analysis

A stratified bootstrap was additionally used to estimate variability separately for each disease class, helping identify categories with more fluctuating prediction behavior.

Real time application

To demonstrate the deployment potential of the proposed MobileGraph model, a real-time application was created using the Gradio library. The real-time application allows the user to input an image of a cucumber leaf, which then displays the disease pre-diction result with a bar chart representing the confidence level. In addition, the user is provided with the heatmap results from the Grad-CAM algorithm, highlighting the regions in the leaf image used in the decision-making process for disease diagnosis.

Discussion

Effectiveness of graph-enhanced disease representation

This work demonstrates the effectiveness of combining convolutional feature extrac-tion with graph relational reasoning for automated cucumber leaf disease diagnosis. Although standard CNN architectures can capture global texture and color infor-mation, they may struggle with local and irregular lesion structures. The proposed MobileGraph framework addresses this issue by modeling relationships among CNN-derived feature regions through graph-based reasoning, which improves feature representation and reduces ambiguity among visually similar disease classes.

Computational efficiency and explainability

MobileGraph outperformed conventional models such as ResNet-152 and EfficientNet-B7 while requiring substantially lower computational resources. Its lightweight design, low parameter count, and fast inference speed make it suitable for mobile and edge-based agricultural applications. In addition, the Grad-CAM and LIME visualizations confirm that the model focuses on biologically relevant symptom regions rather than irrelevant background areas, increasing the interpretability and trustworthiness of the prediction.

Practical relevance for precision agriculture

The significance of this contribution extends beyond image classification. Early and accurate detection of cucumber leaf diseases can support timely intervention, reduce unnecessary pesticide use, and help minimize crop loss. The smartphone-based proto-type further demonstrates the practical potential of MobileGraph for assisting farmers and agricultural workers through real-time disease prediction and visual explanation.

Additional-dataset generalization and domain shift

The cross-dataset accuracy decrease from 99.75% to 91.25% indicates a domain shift rather than model failure, likely caused by differences in illumination, background, leaf orientation, disease severity, class distribution, and original image resolution. Con-vNeXt achieved the same accuracy on the independent Kaggle dataset because its larger convolutional capacity can capture broader visual variations; however, Mobile-Graph still produced slightly better precision, recall, F1-score, and MCC with much lower computational cost, making it more suitable for mobile deployment. Future work will reduce this gap through more diverse field images, stronger augmentation, multi-source training, domain adaptation, and larger cross-location validation.

Relevance to drone-assisted monitoring

Although the present work focuses on leaf-level image classification, the MobileGraph framework is also relevant to drone-assisted crop disease monitoring. UAV-based systems can capture large-scale field observations, where disease symptoms often appear as spatially related regions. The graph reasoning component of MobileGraph may therefore be useful for modeling such spatial dependencies in future large-scale agricultural monitoring systems.

Limitations and future work

Despite its strong performance, this study has several limitations. The dataset may not fully represent extreme real-world field conditions involving severe illumination variation, occlusion, mixed infections, and different crop growth stages. Some visu-ally similar disease classes, such as Anthracnose and Gummy Stem Blight, may also remain challenging when symptoms overlap or appear at early stages. Future work will extend the framework using more diverse field datasets, adaptive graph construction, multi-modal sensing, hyperspectral imaging, meteorological data, and larger real-world deployment studies.

A further limitation is that MobileGraph does not explicitly segment lesion instances before graph construction. The graph is built over CNN feature-map regions; therefore, the learned adjacency should be interpreted as feature-level spatial depen-dency rather than direct biological lesion-to-lesion connectivity. Future work will include adjacency visualization and lesion-aware graph construction using segmenta-tion or attention-guided node selection to better validate long-range disease-region interactions.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel graph-regularized deep learning framework, Mobile-Graph, to improve the reliability of cucumber leaf disease diagnosis. The proposed MobileGraph model leverages convolutional representation learning and structural reasoning in graph form using graph convolutional networks to obtain state-of-the-art performance with high efficiency. The experimental evaluation demonstrates that MobileGraph outperforms well-known convolutional networks in both efficacy and effi-ciency, which verifies the significance of relational feature learning for symptom-level discrimination in plant disease diagnosis. Apart from the high efficacy of the proposed model in plant disease diagnosis, its efficiency and real-time learning capabilities are also significant indicators of its potential for use in agricultural fields. Explainabil-ity analysis also shows that MobileGraph focuses more on meaningful biological-level symptom patterns, increasing its potential for use in agricultural fields for disease intervention and crop preservation.

The future work includes two practical extensions of the proposed framework. First, a more comprehensive assessment of the proposed model’s robustness using additional field data collected across various agricultural environments is necessary to test how well it performs under varying lighting conditions, background clutter, and disease development stages. A cross-domain testing strategy is also required to evaluate how well the model generalizes to other data sources. Second, extending the framework to detect multiple crops affected by various plant diseases is necessary to enable a unified architecture that can detect a wide range of plant diseases. This extension of the framework is also expected to be tested through cross-dataset testing and field testing.
