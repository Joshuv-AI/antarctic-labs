Domain-adapted modeling of wheat protein content across grain and flour morphological scales
Authors: Shaohua Zhang, Mengdie Wang, Xinghui Qi, Changjun Dai, Jichun Tian, Wei Feng, Chenyang Wang, Tiancai Guo, Li He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and efficient estimation of grain protein content (GPC) plays a crucial role in wheat quality grading and processing quality assessment. Due to the differences in physical structure and optical scattering characteristics between grain and flour, models developed under a single morphology suffer from limited cross-morphology generalization performance, which makes it difficult to balance the integrated detection requirements for rapid quality evaluation and traceability. This study constructed a cross-morphology transfer learning framework to achieve bidirectional and high-precision collaborative prediction of GPC between grain and flour, thereby effectively overcoming the sample-morphology dependency of the models. This research investigated GPC prediction using hyperspectral data collected from both grain and flour samples. Original reflectance (OR) spectra and wavelet features (WF) were extracted to characterize spectral information. To evaluate feature relevance, Pearson correlation analysis, two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2D-COS), and variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis were applied. To address spectral discrepancies between grain and flour, a cross-morphology transfer-learning framework based on a wavelet-feature-enhanced Wasserstein distance adversarial neural network (WF-WDANN) was developed. Results showed that WF exhibited stronger correlations with GPC compared with OR features. For the proposed WDANN model, the highest prediction accuracies reached R2 = 0.97 and NRMSE = 0.21% for grain, and R2 = 0.99 and NRMSE = 0.19% for flour. In cross-morphology transfer scenarios, the WDANN model achieved an average independent validation performance of R2 = 0.97, RMSE = 0.35%, and NRMSE = 0.17% for grain-to-flour transfer, and R2 = 0.96, RMSE = 0.32%, and NRMSE = 0.15% for flour-to-grain transfer. These results demonstrate that integrating wavelet-based feature representation with adversarial transfer learning significantly improves GPC prediction across different sample morphologies, providing a robust and morphology-agnostic approach for rapid quality assessment in wheat processing chains.Keywords: Grain protein content, Hyperspectral, Wavelet features, Deep transfer networks, Wasserstein distance adversarial neural network

Introduction

Wheat, as one of the world's most important staple food crops, plays a vital role in ensuring food security, supporting the agricultural economy, and maintaining consumer health. Grain protein content (GPC) is a critical indicator that not only determines the nutritional quality of wheat but also significantly affects flour processing performance (Dizlek and Awika, 2023). Due to morphological instabilities of the grain (e.g., expansion, contraction, or rupture), GPC is highly susceptible to environmental factors (Longmire et al., 2023). Although flour exhibits a relatively stable morphology, the milling process alters the original GPC distribution within the grain, and the efficiency of GPC estimation lags behind. Therefore, separate calibration models for GPC are typically developed for grain and flour, leading to repeated model construction, increased calibration costs, and limited data sharing across the wheat production and processing chain. Establishing a bidirectional transfer-learning framework that can bridge the spectral gap between grain and flour would help improve the efficiency of flour quality assessment, enable grain quality traceability, and allow the reuse of historical flour quality data for grain quality evaluation. This is of considerable scientific significance and practical value for integrated quality monitoring throughout the entire wheat production and processing chain.

Recent advances in optical instrumentation and analytical methodologies have opened new avenues for food quality evaluation and identification. Near-infrared hyperspectral technologies, widely used for quality monitoring at different scales such as grain and flour, often suffer from inconsistencies in spectral bands across regions (Duarte et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2024). For instance, Zheng et al. (2024) successfully estimated GPC in rice grain using an NIR-SWIR hyperspectral sensor covering 980–2500 nm. Similarly, Hu et al. (2024) compared VIS-NIR (400–1000 nm) and NIR (900–1700 nm) hyperspectral sensors for matcha quality classification, finding that VIS-NIR data yielded superior classification accuracy. Hyperspectral technologies spanning the VIS-NIR-SWIR spectral range thus provide a more comprehensive assessment framework, enabling the capture of GPC-related information across both grain and flour forms at multiple scales.

Reflectance signals carry essential spectral information for monitoring GPC in crops. However, raw reflectance spectra are often susceptible to noise and external interference, which limits their capacity to extract informative features from complex biological samples (Chu et al., 2023). The Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) has emerged as a robust signal processing technique that offers notable advantages over unprocessed reflectance data. Particularly under conditions of large reflectance fluctuations, CWT can effectively suppress noise while preserving critical spectral information, thus improving model stability and predictive accuracy (Chen et al., 2023). Moreover, CWT facilitates the detection of spectral feature variations at different scales, supporting more accurate GPC predictions across different physical morphologies of grain and flour (Zhang et al., 2024b). However, regarding the transfer of GPC estimation between grain and flour morphologies, the effectiveness of CWT-based data has not yet been evaluated, and its potential positive impact on GPC estimation remains to be assessed.

Supervised learning is increasingly applied in food quality analysis, particularly for high-dimensional spectral data (Kwon et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023). For example, Zheng et al. (2024) used deep generative models and CWT to extract GPC-sensitive features in Japonica rice, achieving the highest accuracy at WF1743 (R2 = 0.58). Similarly, Zhang et al. (2024c) applied CWT-based RF models for wheat GPC prediction, with best performance in flour at WF2170 (R2 = 0.51). Due to the complexity and high dimensionality of spectral data, advanced methods like two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2D-COS) are essential for improving detection accuracy. 2D-COS enhances spectral analysis by revealing molecular-level changes through two-dimensional transformations (Fudge et al., 2013; Li et al., 2021). It has been effectively applied to identify sensitive wavelengths in Liupao tea and to analyze chlorophyll variations in vegetation (Zhang et al., 2024a). In this study, 2D-COS combined with CWT was used to improve GPC-related feature identification in wheat, reduce noise, and enhance accuracy in complex matrices.

Wheat grain and flour exhibit significant structural and physicochemical differences that influence their spectral characteristics. In this context, transfer learning has emerged as a promising approach for improving model generalizability in plant phenotyping tasks (Shen et al., 2022; Skobalski et al., 2024). By leveraging knowledge from related domains or tasks, transfer learning can improve model performance under data scarcity and task variability, reduce training costs, and enhance learning efficiency. Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA), a branch of transfer learning, addresses domain shift by aligning the feature distributions of source and target domains without requiring labeled target data (Wang et al., 2022). UDA techniques are typically categorized into transformation-based, discrepancy-based, and adversarial-based approaches (Kouw and Loog, 2019; Wang and Deng, 2018). For instance, Wan et al. (2022) proposed a method that combines Transfer Component Analysis with Support Vector Regression to achieve cross-species transfer in the estimation of leaf nitrogen content. Among adversarial approaches, Domain Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) are among the most widely used UDA models (Joshi et al., 2024). Priyatikanto et al. (2023) demonstrated that DANN significantly enhanced the transferability of maize yield prediction models. In such frameworks, a domain classifier is trained adversarial against the feature extractor, enabling the model to learn domain-invariant representations until alignment is achieved (Ma et al., 2024a, Ma et al., 2024b). Consequently, exploring effective strategies for transferring GPC monitoring models between grain and flour morphologies is critical for improving prediction accuracy and robustness. The challenge of cross-morphological transfer caused by scattering, particle size, and moisture heterogeneity remains underexplored, highlighting the need for transfer learning strategies to improve the robustness and generalizability of GPC estimation.

In this study, we innovatively conducted the first investigation on cross-domain model transfer for GPC prediction between wheat grain and flour morphologies. Specifically: (1) Trends in OR and WF under different GPC datasets were analyzed; (2) GPC-sensitive spectral regions were identified using one-dimensional and two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy, and combined with variable importance in projection VIP scores for feature selection; (3) GPC estimation models for both grain and flour were constructed using PLSR and RFR, and their direct transfer performance across morphologies was evaluated; (4) Three transfer learning algorithms—TCA-PLSR, TCA-PSO-RFR, and WDANN—were employed to assess model universality, and the effectiveness of feature domain alignment was validated using t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding visualization.

Materials and methods

Test samples and GPC determination

In this study, wheat grain and flour samples were collected from three major wheat-producing provinces in China—Henan, Shandong, and Heilongjiang—representing central, eastern, and northeastern agroecological zones, respectively. A total of 347 grain samples were collected in 2022, including 115 from Henan, 115 from Shandong, and 117 from Heilongjiang. The corresponding flour samples totaled 320 in 2022, comprising 105 from Henan, 105 from Shandong, and 110 from Heilongjiang. In 2023, a total of 120 grain samples and 120 flour samples from Heilongjiang, Henan, and Shandong were collected and employed for the independent validation of the model (Table 1). GPC was determined using an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer. Briefly, 0.2 g of each sample was accurately weighed and digested at 420 °C for 120 min. Hyperspectral data acquisition and spectra extraction

Spectral measurements of all samples were conducted in a darkroom environment using a FieldSpec® 4 hyperspectral spectrometer (Analytical Spectral Devices Inc., USA), equipped with a 50-W tungsten halogen lamp serving as the illumination source. Specifically, spectra within the 350–1000 nm region were acquired at 1.4 nm intervals with a spectral resolution of 3.0 nm, while those within the 1000–2500 nm region were sampled at 1.1 nm intervals with a spectral resolution of 6.0 nm. During measurement, the sensor probe was positioned vertically downward, with a 25° field-of-view (FOV) and maintained at a vertical distance of approximately 30 cm from the sample surface, resulting in a FOV diameter of 13.2 cm. Both grain and flour samples were placed in circular containers with a diameter of 15 cm, and the sample layer thickness was uniformly maintained at 5 cm to ensure consistency in optical path conditions. All hyperspectral measurements were conducted in a closed dark chamber to eliminate interference from ambient and natural light. Radiometric calibration was performed using a standard reference panel with a reflectance of 1, and the measured raw spectra were converted into reflectance values accordingly to ensure data comparability. For each sample, five spectral scans were acquired and two replicate measurements were performed. The average of the resulting ten spectra was calculated and used as the OR value for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2a). The hyperspectral data preprocessing procedure, including spectral cleaning, noise reduction, and outlier removal steps, to ensure data quality and consistency of model inputs. In spectral measurements, the FOV was calculated from the height of the optical fiber (h) and the spectrometer field of view (θ) in °.

Continuous wavelet transform (CWT)

In the CWT framework, a mother wavelet serves as the fundamental generating function, which is scaled and translated to construct a family of wavelets with different time–frequency localization properties. In this study, four commonly used mother wavelet functions—Daubechies, Morlet, Gaussian4, and Haar—were systematically evaluated. A sensitivity analysis based on the top 5% feature importance (defined by correlation strength with GPC) was conducted to compare their representational capability (Fig. 1). The Gaussian4 wavelet demonstrated the best overall performance and was therefore selected for subsequent analysis (Zheng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). After selecting the optimal mother wavelet, the continuous wavelet transform was applied to the original reflectance spectra by convolving the spectral signal with scaled and shifted versions of the mother wavelet across multiple scales. In this study, the scale parameter was set to 8 scales to capture multiresolution spectral information ranging from coarse to fine frequency components. The resulting wavelet coefficients form a time–frequency representation of the original spectra, effectively decomposing the reflectance signal into localized spectral–frequency patterns.

Two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2D-COS)

In the present study, 2DCOS was employed to examine spectral reflectance changes under varying levels of GPC. The core analytical focus was on characterizing incremental or decremental changes in reflectance at specific wavelengths and observing peak or trough displacements across the spectrum as GPC increased. These changes provide insights into the sensitive wavelength regions associated with protein accumulation. To enhance the sensitivity and accuracy of 2DCOS, a CWT-enhanced two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (CWT-2DCOS) method was proposed. Specifically, the spectral dataset was arranged according to increasing GPC levels (low → medium → high) to construct the perturbation variable sequence. Prior to analysis, the CWT spectra were mean-centered to eliminate baseline effects and emphasize dynamic spectral variations. The 2D-COS synchronous and asynchronous correlation maps were then calculated using the classical Noda method, which enables the identification of correlated and sequential spectral responses across wavelength variables. This integrated approach leverages the multi-scale feature extraction capability of CWT to amplify relevant spectral information before 2DCOS computation.

Cluster analysis methods

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) are two widely adopted dimensionality reduction techniques, frequently employed in high-dimensional data analysis and visualization (Zhang et al., 2024b). PCA is a linear transformation method that reduces data dimensionality by projecting the original data onto a set of orthogonal principal components that retain the maximum variance. In contrast, t-SNE is a non-linear dimensionality reduction algorithm specifically designed for the visualization of high-dimensional datasets. In this study, both PCA and t-SNE were applied to reduce the dimensionality of the hyperspectral data, transforming it into two- or three-dimensional representations.

Machine learning Model construction for GPC

Partial least squares regression

PLSR is a widely used multivariate regression technique designed to handle datasets with highly collinear and high-dimensional independent variables (Maimaitijiang et al., 2020). In the present study, PLSR was employed to model the relationship between spectral input variables and GPC. The optimal number of latent variables was selected from a candidate range of 1 to 10. To assess the contribution of each predictor to the model, the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores were calculated. VIP scores quantify the influence of each independent variable based on its contribution to the latent variable space and predictive power (Farres et al., 2015). Variables with VIP values less than 1 were considered to have a minor contribution and were excluded to reduce model redundancy. Conversely, variables with VIP scores equal to or greater than 1 were retained and used to build the final GPC estimation models for both grain and flour samples.

Particle Swarm Optimization-Random Forest Regression

The PSO-RFR hybrid model integrates the PSO algorithm with RFR to enhance predictive performance. PSO is a population-based heuristic optimization technique inspired by the social behavior of bird flocks, which iteratively explores the multidimensional parameter space by updating particle positions and velocities based on individual and collective experience (Zhang et al., 2023a). RFR, an ensemble learning method, improves prediction accuracy and robustness by aggregating multiple decision trees, thereby reducing overfitting and improving generalization capability. In this study, PSO was employed to fine-tune the critical hyperparameters of the RFR model, specifically the number of trees (n_estimators, ranging from 200 to 800) and the maximum number of features considered at each split (max_features, ranging from 0.3 to 0.9). The optimization process used a population size of 30 particles with a maximum of 60 iterations. The inertia weight decreased linearly from 0.9 to 0.4, while the acceleration coefficients were set to c1 = c2 = 1.49. To improve search efficiency, velocity was constrained to 20% of the parameter range, and boundary violations were corrected using a reflection strategy. Early stopping was triggered if no improvement was observed over 10 consecutive iterations. The optimization objective was to minimize the RMSE based on 5-fold cross-validation, and iterations continued until convergence according to the defined swarm stability criterion.

Analysis of model universality for the GPC dataset

Morphology gap refers to the spectral discrepancies induced by physical and structural differences between grain and flour samples, which lead to distinct reflectance characteristics and domain distribution shifts in hyperspectral data, thereby reducing the universality and generalization capability of prediction models.

Correlation Alignment

Correlation Alignment (CORAL) is a domain adaptation method that reduces the distribution discrepancy between source and target domains by aligning their second-order statistics. Specifically, CORAL minimizes the Frobenius norm distance between the covariance matrices of source and target feature representations, thereby achieving domain alignment in a linear transformation manner. In this study, the regularization weight λ was set to 1 to balance the alignment loss and model stability.

Transfer Component Analysis

TCA achieves domain adaptation by projecting data from both the source and target domains into a shared latent feature space. Specifically, TCA integrates PCA with the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) criterion to maximize the similarity between source and target distributions while minimizing their divergence (Wan et al., 2022). In this study, a Gaussian (RBF) kernel was employed to capture nonlinear relationships and enhance the alignment of complex spectral distributions between grain and flour samples. The reduced dimensionality of the latent subspace was set to 20 based on cross-validation performance, ensuring a balance between information preservation and model generalization capability. In this study, TCA was combined with PLSR and PSO-RFR models to evaluate their transferability and improve the accuracy of GPC estimation across different wheat morphologies, namely grain and flour.

Domain adversarial networks combined with Wasserstein distance (WDANN)

To resolve the transfer problem of the model in different GPC levels of wheat grain and flour states, the present study introduces the concept of adversarial learning by using DANN, which reduces the distributional difference between the source and target domains using iterative adversarial feedback from both the feature extractor and the domain discriminator (Joshi., 2024). The feature extractor (Gf) is implemented as a three-layer fully connected neural network with 256, 128, and 64 neurons, respectively. Each hidden layer employs a ReLU activation function followed by batch normalization, and outputs a 64-dimensional latent feature representation. The regression predictor (Gy) consists of three fully connected layers with 32, 16, and 1 neurons, respectively. ReLU activation functions are applied to the hidden layers, while the output layer uses a linear activation to predict the GPC. The domain discriminator (Gd) is implemented as a three-layer fully connected neural network with 64, 32, and 16 neurons, followed by a sigmoid output layer for binary domain classification. To satisfy the 1-Lipschitz constraint required by the Wasserstein distance, the proposed WDANN adopts the gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) strategy. Specifically, a gradient penalty term is incorporated into the domain discriminator loss, where the gradient norm is computed on interpolated feature representations between the source and target domains. This strategy enforces the 1-Lipschitz constraint during adversarial training and improves optimization stability (Fig. 2b). The loss function of the task is only a qualitative evaluation of the features of different domains, and it does not specifically represent the degree of distributional differences. Therefore, the Wasserstein distance is introduced to optimize the loss function of the domain discriminator, and the original task is replaced using network nonlinear approximation to fit the Wasserstein distance, which achieves the quantification of distributional differences (Liu et al., 2024). Once trained, the model required only 0.26 ms per sample during inference.

Dataset partitioning and model accuracy evaluation

According to the Chinese wheat product quality standard T/TFHT C001-2019, GPC is classified into three categories, weak-gluten varieties: low content level (LL, ≤11.5%), medium-gluten varieties: medium content level (ML, 11.5% < ML < 14%), and strong-gluten varieties: high content level (HL, ≥14%). For grain GPC, samples with low, medium, and high protein contents were categorized into Dataset #1 (62), Dataset #2 (112), and Dataset #3 (173), respectively, in 2022. Similarly, for flour GPC, samples were categorized into Dataset #4 (55), Dataset #5 (112), and Dataset #6 (153), respectively, in 2022. In this study, the 2022 grain and flour datasets were mutually used as training and validation sets in a cross-morphology manner, where grain-based models were trained and validated on flour datasets and vice versa to enable bidirectional transfer learning. The separately developed PLSR and PSO-RFR models for grain and flour were subjected to 5-fold cross-validation with the 2022 dataset. The 2023 grain and flour datasets were independently collected and strictly used as external test sets to evaluate model generalization performance under unseen conditions. The flour and grain samples collected in 2023 were therefore not involved in model training or parameter tuning, ensuring an unbiased independent validation of model robustness. Model transfer was conducted bidirectionally, whereby models developed on grain datasets were applied to flour datasets, and conversely, models constructed on flour datasets were transferred to grain datasets. Model performance was evaluated using three metrics: coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE). Generally, models exhibiting superior predictive ability showed higher R2 values and lower RMSE and NRMSE values (Fig. 2c).

Results and analysis

Spectral variation of different GPC datasets

As shown in Fig. 3(a–e), GPC variations in wheat grain and flour differed across protein content levels. Grain OR mainly ranged from 0 to 0.7, with reflectance under ML and HL conditions generally lower (0–0.6) than under LL (Fig. 3b–d). Notable spectral peaks occurred near 370, 875, 945, 1110, 1300, 1660, 1860, 2020, and 2230 nm, with a weaker peak at 2410 nm. The first three principal components of grain OR spectra explained over 97% of the variance. PC1 was consistently positive, especially in the NIR and SWIR regions, while PC2 showed mixed coefficients across VIS–SWIR. Under LL and ML, PC patterns were similar, but HL samples showed higher PC1 loadings in the VIS region.

Spectral transfer feature selection with VIP score

VIP analysis was used to identify key spectral bands for GPC estimation in both grain and flour morphologies (Fig. 7). In grain datasets (Dataset#1–3), important OR bands were mainly located at 350–360 nm and 970–1200 nm, with a few in the SWIR (2460–2475 nm); the highest OR-VIP value was 1.67 at 1140 nm. In flour datasets (Dataset#4–6), key OR bands appeared at 350–420 nm, 1000–1100 nm, and 1960–2230 nm, with a maximum OR-VIP of 3.71 at 351 nm. For WF spectra, important bands in grain were observed at 350–380 nm, 980–1200 nm, 1850–1940 nm, and 2480–2500 nm, with the highest VIP (3.63) at 2493 nm. In flour, WF-sensitive bands were mainly at 350–450 nm, 990–1015 nm, 1680–1700 nm, 1980–1990 nm, and 2480–2500 nm, with a peak VIP of 6.68 at 2493 nm. Overall, based on all grain and flour datasets, a total of 24 OR spectral bands with VIP ≥1 were selected, while 101 WF features satisfied the VIP ≥1 criterion. Further sensitivity analysis confirmed that all selected features exhibited statistically significant correlations with GPC, indicating their robustness and relevance for subsequent modeling.

GPC estimation accuracy and direct transfer accuracy

The GPC estimation models were built using VIP-selected features from grain and flour (Table 2). The separately developed PLSR and PSO-RFR models for grain and flour were subjected to 5-fold cross-validation with the 2022 dataset. For grain, the best performance was achieved by PSO-RFR with WF (R2 = 0.83, RMSE = 1.15%, NRMSE = 0.56%), representing a 33.87% increase in R2 and a 33.59% decrease in RMSE compared to PLSR with OR. For flour, PSO-RFR with WF also performed best (R2 = 0.95, RMSE = 0.48%, NRMSE = 0.23%), while PLSR with WF achieved comparably high accuracy (R2 = 0.93). Discussion

Comparison of GPC-sensitive spectral features between grain and flour

With ongoing advancements in hyperspectral sensor technology, both the dimensionality and the information richness of acquired spectral data have markedly increased. This is particularly evident when employing VIS-NIR-SWIR spectra for grain protein content (GPC) estimation, which captures more nuanced and detailed spectral features. In this study, GPC-sensitive spectral bands in grain morphology predominantly spanned 350–360 nm, 970–1200 nm (NIR), and 1850–1940 nm (SWIR), whereas in flour morphology, key sensitive regions were concentrated around 350–450 nm, 1000–1200 nm, 1960–2010 nm, 2080–2120 nm, and 2190–2230 nm. These findings align broadly with previous work by Zhang et al. (2023b), who identified GPC-sensitive bands in grain at 350–450 nm, 900–1600 nm, 1300–1500 nm, and 1901–2100 nm, and in flour at 330–430 nm, 550–600 nm, 1300–1400 nm, and 1990–2050 nm. The substantial overlap in sensitive spectral regions between grain and flour morphologies indicates a conserved spectral response likely driven by absorption features of nitrogen-containing compounds such as proteins and amino acids, which share characteristic absorption profiles. However, differences in the VIS-NIR-SWIR sensitivity patterns between grain and flour reflect morphological and structural changes: proteins in grain remain in their native form, shielded by the seed coat, whereas milling alters protein distribution and flour fineness, significantly affecting light scattering, absorption, and reflectance properties (Zhang et al., 2023a; Zheng et al., 2024).

In this study, cross-peaks were observed between 1350 and 2500 nm and the VIS region across both flour and grain morphologies. Notably, CWT-2DCOS exhibited stronger correlation peaks near 1000 nm and 1800 nm within the NIR and SWIR bands, further validating these regions as sensitive for GPC detection and highlighting the synergistic interplay of spectral signals associated with protein content variations. This phenomenon can be attributed to the molecular absorption and vibrational characteristics of proteins, whose fundamental building blocks—amino acids—are primarily composed of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. Hyperspectral reflectance predominantly arises from the combined absorption effects of C–H, O–H, and N–H bonds. Specifically, the band near 1000 nm corresponds to third overtone vibrations of C–H and N–H groups; the 1200–1500 nm region relates to C–H third overtones and O–H stretching vibrations; while the 1800–2000 nm range aligns with N–H vibrational frequencies (Lian et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2012). Complementing these findings, VIP-based feature selection underscored the critical importance of these spectral bands for GPC estimation. The integration of multi-dimensional spectral information thus enhances the accuracy and real-time capability of protein quality monitoring, providing a robust scientific foundation for precision quality assessment (Lin et al., 2014).

Influence of sample morphology and ML algorithms on the GPC estimation models

Sample morphology plays a crucial role in the accuracy of GPC monitoring, and assessing GPC across different morphologies enables comprehensive evaluation of wheat quality. In this study, Models based on flour morphology achieved higher accuracy (R2 = 0.89) compared to those based on whole grain (R2 = 0.71). This improvement is attributed to the fine particle size and homogenous compositional distribution of flour, which generate more stable and consistent spectral signals during acquisition, facilitating more effective model fitting (He et al., 2023). By contrast, whole grains exhibit larger particle sizes, rougher surfaces, and heterogeneous internal GPC distribution, rendering their spectral signals more susceptible to scattering and interference, thereby reducing monitoring accuracy (Shi et al., 2024). From a mechanistic perspective, flour, with its smaller and more uniform particles, minimizes Mie scattering effects and provides shorter, more stable optical paths, while also exposing protein and starch molecules more evenly to incident light, resulting in stronger and more reproducible absorption features. In contrast, intact wheat grains, due to their coarse structure, high surface roughness, and heterogeneous water distribution, induce stronger multiple scattering, baseline drift, and variability in water-related absorption bands (e.g., around 970, 1450, and 1940 nm), which distort the true chemical signals and complicate model calibration. These mechanistic differences explain the consistently higher predictive performance observed in flour compared to grain morphology, aligning with previous reports (Zhang et al., 2024c; Zheng et al., 2024). Despite the lower accuracy, GPC monitoring in grain morphology offers the advantage of reflecting the native protein state, avoiding losses incurred during processing, and enabling timely assessment at harvest. This capability provides a vital foundation for wheat quality evaluation, classification, and storage management. Nonetheless, further refinement of GPC estimation in grain morphology remains a priority.

In this study, the GPC estimation accuracy of the PSO-optimized random forest regression (PSO-RFR; R2 = 0.83) outperformed that of partial least squares regression (PLSR; R2 = 0.78) across both grain and flour morphologies. This difference arises because, in complex and high-dimensional spectral datasets, RFR effectively captures nonlinear relationships between spectral features and GPC. The PSO algorithm further enhances RFR by dynamically tuning hyperparameters during training, leading to improved model fitting (Zhang et al., 2023a). By contrast, PLSR, a linear regression method, reduces dataset dimensionality via principal component analysis and fits primarily linear correlations, limiting its ability to model complex nonlinear patterns (Maimaitijiang et al., 2020). While machine learning models exhibit strong fitting capabilities, their direct transfer performance between grain and flour morphologies remains poor—PSO-RFR and PLSR showed low transfer accuracies (R2 = 0.19 and 0.15, respectively), highlighting the challenges posed by morphological inconsistencies. Consequently, developing tailored transfer learning approaches is critical for enhancing GPC estimation in grain morphology.

Evaluation of transfer learning approaches for cross-domain GPC prediction

CWT, as a powerful signal processing tool, enhances model transfer accuracy and robustness by optimizing feature extraction quality (Li et al., 2022). In this study, the GPC estimation model combining PSO-RFR with WF outperformed all others. This improvement arises because WF reveals more pronounced peaks during sensitive band screening and exhibits stronger correlations at key wavelengths compared to OR. Moreover, the VIP values derived from WF exceed those of OR, with a greater proportion of features selected from WF. By effectively extracting high-frequency spectral information, CWT captures subtle differences in GPC-related spectral signals across grain and flour morphologies. Notably, two distinctive peaks at 1245 nm and 1390 nm emerged exclusively in WF spectra, highlighting WF's superior ability to resolve complex spectral variations linked to GPC that remain obscure in OR data. Additionally, WF offers intrinsic advantages in denoising and localized feature extraction, enabling multi-scale decomposition of spectral signals and improving sensitivity to GPC-induced spectral changes (Lin et al., 2021). Zheng et al. (2024) similarly demonstrated that CWT-extracted WF at multiple scales facilitates precise GPC monitoring in grain morphology. Taken together, these findings underscore WF's critical advantage in enhancing sensitivity and accuracy for GPC estimation.

To improve the estimation accuracy of GPC across different morphological forms, it is crucial to construct a transfer model for GPC estimation between grain morphology and flour morphology. Previous studies have mainly focused on GPC prediction within a single sample morphology. For example, Zheng et al. (2024) employed PLSR combined with WF to estimate GPC in rice grains, achieving an R2 of 0.58. Zhang et al. (2023b) developed an SVM model for grain GPC prediction (R2 = 0.789) and a BPNN model for flour GPC prediction (R2 = 0.789). These approaches were established and validated only for individual sample morphologies and therefore cannot be directly applied across different sample forms. By contrast, this study developed cross-morphology transfer models based on TCA-PLSR, TCA-RFR, and WDANN. Among them, the proposed WDANN achieved the best performance, reaching an R2 of 0.95 for GPC prediction while effectively transferring knowledge between grain and flour spectra. TCA is a domain adaptation method that reduces the distribution discrepancy between the source and target domains by projecting them into a common latent subspace through kernel mapping (Pan et al., 2024). However, TCA relies on a fixed transformation and lacks the dynamic feature learning and adversarial optimization capability required to model the complex nonlinear spectral variations caused by differences in sample morphology. Therefore, its ability to address severe morphology-induced domain shifts is limited (Pan et al., 2024). In contrast, the proposed WDANN adopts an adversarial domain adaptation strategy, in which the feature extractor, regression predictor, and domain discriminator are jointly optimized. By simultaneously utilizing labeled source-domain samples and unlabeled target-domain samples, the model learns domain-invariant feature representations while preserving GPC-related information, thereby reducing the dependence on labeled target-domain data and improving the generalization capability across different morphologies. Furthermore, compared with the conventional DANN, WDANN introduces the Wasserstein distance into the domain discriminator. Rather than treating domain adaptation as a binary domain classification problem, the Wasserstein distance directly quantifies the discrepancy between the source and target feature distributions. This provides a smoother optimization objective and more informative gradients during adversarial training, resulting in more stable optimization and more accurate alignment of spectral feature distributions (Liu et al., 2024). In this study, the WDANN transfer model effectively mitigated overestimation and underestimation issues observed in the LL and HL datasets, narrowing the feature distribution gap between grain and flour spectra. t-SNE visualization further confirmed that WDANN adversarial training substantially reduced discrepancies in feature distributions across morphologies. Consistent with these findings, Cao et al. (2025) reported that integrating Wasserstein distance with generative adversarial networks markedly improved firmness estimation in pear samples. The transfer learning strategy combining WF, VIP-based feature selection, and WDANN significantly enhanced GPC monitoring accuracy while enabling a shared model between grain and flour morphologies. More importantly, the proposed framework enables a shared prediction model across different sample morphologies, eliminating the need to develop separate calibration models for grain and flour samples, thereby providing greater flexibility and practical value for rapid GPC monitoring.

Application prospects and challenges

With rapid advancements in sensor technology and the ongoing refinement of deep learning architectures, the use of high-dimensional spectral data for GPC estimation is becoming increasingly prevalent. From a practical perspective, the two transfer directions address complementary needs across the wheat production and processing chain. Grain-to-flour transfer enables calibration models developed from readily available grain samples to be directly applied to flour quality evaluation, thereby reducing the need for repeated model development and extensive flour sample labeling during wheat processing. This provides an efficient solution for rapid flour quality assessment, particularly in milling enterprises where timely monitoring of flour protein content is required (Yang et al., 2025). Conversely, flour-to-grain transfer offers unique value for quality traceability and resource utilization. By inferring the protein characteristics of the original grain from flour spectra, the proposed framework can support retrospective quality assessment of raw materials, facilitate the identification of potential batch mixing or quality inconsistencies, and strengthen quality traceability throughout the supply chain (Cibecchini et al., 2020). In addition, when historical flour quality records are available but corresponding grain measurements are unavailable, the transfer model provides a practical means of reconstructing grain GPC information, thereby improving the utilization of existing spectral databases and supporting long-term analyses of cultivar performance and regional quality variation. More importantly, the proposed bidirectional transfer-learning framework establishes a quantitative bridge between grain and flour protein information, enabling information sharing across different stages of the wheat value chain. Compared with developing independent calibration models for each sample morphology, the proposed approach substantially reduces calibration costs, improves model reusability, and facilitates the construction of an integrated quality prediction system spanning grain production, storage, and food processing.

Nonetheless, constructing robust GPC monitoring models remains costly and data-intensive, particularly across diverse morphological and environmental conditions where sample distribution shifts pose significant challenges for direct application of deep learning models. Recent progress in transfer learning—especially approaches leveraging adversarial training and domain adaptation—holds promise for enabling efficient and accurate GPC prediction in complex, large-scale field environments (Cao et al., 2025; Longmire et al., 2023). Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations. The dataset was collected from three wheat-producing provinces over two consecutive years, and further validation using more diverse cultivars, environments, and growing seasons is still required. In addition, the proposed framework was evaluated only for GPC prediction between grain and flour, and its applicability to other quality traits and cereal crops remains to be investigated. Future studies will focus on expanding the dataset and improving the generalization capability of the transfer model under more diverse production conditions. At the same time, the relatively high cost of hyperspectral instruments, together with inherent limitations in portability and acquisition speed, may hinder their widespread application in field or industrial scenarios. In addition, the deployment of deep learning–based transfer models requires substantial computational resources, which poses further challenges regarding compatibility with embedded or portable devices. Therefore, future research should focus on instrument miniaturization, algorithmic simplification, and hardware optimization to enable real-time, low-cost, and scalable applications. Artificial intelligence micro-models represent a powerful computational tool with broad potential in GPC monitoring (Liu et al., 2025).

Conclusion

To enhance GPC monitoring accuracy across wheat sample morphologies and to develop a universal estimation model, this study integrated Pearson correlation analysis, 2DCOS, and VIP evaluation with WDANN. Overall, the combination of PSO-RFR with WF achieved robust performance, but when paired with WDANN for transfer learning, model generalizability and predictive accuracy were markedly enhanced. Specifically, the WDANN model achieved the highest GPC estimation accuracy for grain with R2 = 0.97 and NRMSE = 0.21%, and for flour with R2 = 0.99 and NRMSE = 0.19%. Transfer learning based on WF-WDANN further enabled reliable bidirectional model transfer across grain and flour morphologies t-SNE visualization confirmed the effectiveness of WDANN in aligning cross-domain feature distributions. These findings demonstrate that integrating advanced spectral feature extraction with domain-adaptive deep learning enables accurate, morphology-agnostic GPC prediction, paving the way for scalable, efficient grain quality monitoring.
