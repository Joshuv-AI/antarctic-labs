Improving Latent Generalization Using Test-time Compute
Arslan Chaudhry (arslanch@google.com), Sridhar Thiagarajan (sthiagarajan@google.com), Andrew Lampinen (andrewlampinen@gmail.com)

Abstract
Language Models (LMs) exhibit two distinct mechanisms for knowledge acquisition: in-weights learning (i.e., encoding information within the model weights) and in-context learning (ICL). Although these two modes offer complementary strengths, in-weights learning frequently struggles to facilitate deductive reasoning over the internalized knowledge. We characterize this limitation as a deficit in latent generalization , of which the reversal curse is one example. Conversely, in-context learning demonstrates highly robust latent generalization capabilities. To improve latent generalization from in-weights knowledge, prior approaches rely on train-time data augmentation, yet these techniques are task-specific, scale poorly, and fail to generalize to out-of-distribution knowledge. To overcome these shortcomings, this work studies how models can be taught to use test-time compute, or ’thinking’, specifically to improve latent generalization. We use Reinforcement Learning (RL) from correctness feedback to train models to produce long chains-of-thought (CoTs) to improve latent generalization. Our experiments show that this thinking approach not only resolves many instances of latent generalization failures on in-distribution knowledge but also, unlike augmentation baselines, generalizes to new knowledge for which no RL was performed. Nevertheless, on pure reversal tasks, we find that thinking does not unlock direct knowledge inversion, but the generate-and-verify ability of thinking models enables them to get well above chance performance. The brittleness of factual self-verification means thinking models still remain well below the performance of in-context learning for this task. Overall, our results establish test-time thinking as a flexible and promising direction for improving the latent generalization of LMs.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable generalization abilities across many domains especially when the task at hand is being presented in-context. The in-context generalization of LLMs is remarkably consistent even when they are queried for knowledge that is logically implied but not directly stated in the information present in the context. Contrary to in-context learning (ICL), however, when models are finetuned on new tasks, where they are expected to generalize from their in-weights knowledge, the generalization can be surprisingly brittle -- particularly for information that is logically deducible but not directly stated in the training set. We find that a model which is encouraged to do self-verification in its thinking trace shows non-trivial performance on pure reversals in comparison to models which do not use thinking. However, we find that models are often brittle at factual self-verification, and hence their performance remains well below the ICL baseline. Nevertheless, we view the utilization of test-time reasoning traces as a highly promising paradigm for advancing latent generalization in large language models.

2
Method

To facilitate latent generalization, our method aims to enhance the model’s capacity to effectively elicit its in-weights knowledge and surface relevant information in-context within its intermediate reasoning traces. We achieve this in the following three stages:

2.1
Knowledge Acquisition

To study the latent generalization under a controlled setup, we first add new knowledge in a pre-trained LLM by finetuning it on a dataset that is not leaked during the pre-training stage. During this supervised finetuning (SFT) phase, we also jointly train on auxiliary datasets to preserve the model’s instruction-following and pre-existing reasoning capabilities. However, we note that this stage is only necessary when the target knowledge required for effective generalization is not already encoded within the model’s parameters.

2.2
Thinking Bootstrapping

Once the new knowledge is incorporated in the LLM (
LLM_{s}
) and before starting the reinforcement learning phase, we induce desired reasoning behaviors in
LLM_{s}
to improve latent generalization. Contrary to standard reasoning traces that are primarily optimized for logical deduction, our thinking behaviors facilitate the appropriate composition of factual knowledge required to resolve queries testing latent generalization. For this, we first construct a prompt (detailed in Appendix
B.5
) that explicitly defines the desired properties of a reasoning trace for effective latent generalization: focus-driven recall, self-probing, generate-and-verify etc. We use this prompt on an off-the-shelf teacher LLM
(
13
)
(separate from
LLM_{s}
) to curate a dataset with golden reasoning traces on a small subset of examples designed to evaluate latent generalization (e.g., queries requiring the reversal of knowledge acquired in the preceding phase). To reduce hallucinations in these reasoning traces, we condition the teacher LLM on the ground-truth answer for each query and discard any completions that fail to arrive at the correct final answer. Once this initial dataset is constructed, we further fine-tune the
LLM_{s}
via SFT on this dataset. This procedure is analogous to the Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) framework by
29
, with the distinction that we utilize a more capable teacher model to generate the bootstrapping traces rather than the model being trained (
LLM_{s}
). For fair comparison, we use the same teacher model for the augmentation baseline as well.

2.3
Reinforcement Learning (RL)

Using the resulting checkpoint from the thinking bootstrapping as the initial policy, we perform RL on a combined dataset comprising prompts that necessitate latent generalization, alongside prompts that directly evaluate knowledge present in the training distribution. The policy is updated using a variant of the REINFORCE algorithm
(
27
)
.

To test out-of-distribution generalization, we keep a disjoint dataset that is strictly excluded from both the bootstrapping and RL training mixtures, and is exposed to the model only during the knowledge acquisition phase.

3
Experiments

3.1
Model & Datasets

Our objective is to investigate the effect of increased test-time compute on latent generalization. Therefore, we start off with a pre-trained model that has already undergone standard reasoning fine-tuning (i.e., it is capable of generating extended Chain-of-Thought (CoT) traces for specific prompts already). Specifically, we utilize Gemini 2.5 Flash
(
13
)
as the base model for all baselines and subsequently perform fine-tuning on controlled datasets to evaluate latent generalization.

For our controlled experimental setup, we utilize two datasets, ensuring strict exclusion from the model’s pre-training corpus to prevent data contamination:

Semantic Structure Benchmark
(
15
)

This benchmark consists of real-world categories and relations, enabling the evaluation of various forms of knowledge generalization, including logical reversals and syllogistic inferences. For instance, the model might be exposed to the premise “dogs are a type of mammal” during training, and subsequently queried on the reversal “mammals include dogs” at test time. Alternatively, if exposed to an additional premise, “mammals are warm-blooded,” the model could be evaluated on the syllogistic inference “dogs are warm-blooded,” which requires composing the two independent facts acquired during training. For the out-of-distribution structure, we keep similar depth and branching factor as the in-distribution structure, but modify several categories to have different properties, for example a capability instead of an attribute. More details on how we use this dataset for our experiments are given in
Section

B.1
.

This dataset enables us to evaluate latent generalization under the following conditions:

•

Syllogisms
: Requires the logical composition of two distinct facts present in the training documents to deduce the correct answer.

•

Reversals
: Requires the logical inversion of a trained fact. We evaluate two specific sub-types:
(a) non-strict reversals
, where the model can deduce the answer via an alternative multi-hop reasoning path, and
(b) strict reversals
, where no alternative reasoning path exists aside from the direct inversion of the premise.

•

Category holdouts
: Evaluates generalization when the training documents contain only a single fact about a specific category (e.g., explicitly specifying its parent category), while all other valid inferences are held out for testing. We further partition these holdouts into:
(a) category as subject
, where the held-out category acts as the subject in the test proposition, and
(b) category as target
, where the held-out category acts as the object or target.

Reversal Curse Dataset
(
5
)

This dataset comprises fictional celebrity names paired with their corresponding descriptions. During training, the dataset presents specific celebrity names followed by their descriptions (i.e., name-to-description). At test time, the model is provided with a description and queried to generate the corresponding celebrity name (i.e., description-to-name). During the knowledge acquisition phase, we introduce a data augmentation step requiring the model to unconditionally output a randomly sampled name from the dataset. This ensures the model acquires a non-zero probability of generating these fictional names, as it must observe them as target tokens during training. This dataset parallels the strict reversal condition of the semantic structure benchmark, as no alternative reasoning path exists to deduce the correct answer other than the direct reversals. 3.2
Baselines

We evaluate our approach against the following baselines:

1.

Fine-tuning (SFT):
Standard supervised fine-tuning conducted exclusively during the knowledge acquisition phase.

2.

Fine-tuning + RL:
The initial knowledge acquisition phase followed by RL, but
without generating intermediate reasoning tokens
. This baseline isolates the performance gains attributable specifically to the reasoning process, ruling out improvements stemming solely from RL optimization on the factual prompts, which is known to independently enhance factual accuracy
(
24
)
.

3.

Augmentation:
Following
15
, we apply data augmentation by providing the entire dataset in-context and subsequently training on this augmented dataset during the knowledge acquisition phase.

4.

Augmentation + RL:
The aforementioned augmentation strategy followed by the RL phase, again omitting the generation of intermediate reasoning tokens.

5.

In-Context Learning (ICL):
The model is evaluated with the entire training dataset provided in the context window. Large language models typically exhibit strong latent generalization under this ICL paradigm.

6.

Reasoning (Ours):
Initial SFT during the knowledge acquisition phase, followed by reasoning bootstrapping and RL, adhering to the methodology detailed in Section
2
.

During all fine-tuning procedures, we jointly train on the base model’s (Gemini 2.5 Flash) original instruction-tuning and reasoning datasets. This co-training ensures that our empirical results are not confounded by the catastrophic forgetting of pre-existing instruction-following and reasoning capabilities. Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
3\times 10^{-4}
, a batch size of
16
, and a maximum sequence length of
8192
tokens. For all baselines, we employ early stopping based on the validation loss.

3.3
Evaluation & Metrics

For evaluation, we employ a closed-book Question Answering (QA) framework, sampling generations from the model at a temperature of
T=1.0
, rather than computing the log-likelihood of ground-truth completions. We ensure un-leaked evaluation prompts during the Reinforcement Learning (RL) phase, and take additional care to prevent leakage via thought traces, which we discuss in
B.3
. For all baselines, we compute best@1 and best@
N
metrics, corresponding to a single attempt and
N
attempts, respectively, and use a bootstrapped estimator over all completions to reduce variance.

For the
Semantic Structure Benchmark
, the model is prompted to generate a comprehensive list of all valid completions for a given test query. The ground truth comprises a set
\mathcal{A}_{all}
representing all valid completions—some of which may have been explicitly stated in the training documents or RL prompts—and a subset
\mathcal{A}_{heldout}\subset\mathcal{A}_{all}
representing completions strictly held out from the training documents to evaluate true latent generalization (see Figure
4
in the appendix). Because the model’s output constitutes a set of strings, we evaluate performance using Precision, Recall, and F1 scores. Specifically, we report:

•

Overall Precision, Recall, and F1
: Computed with respect to the complete set of valid answers,
\mathcal{A}_{all}
.

•

Train Recall
: The recall evaluated strictly on the valid answers exposed during training, defined by the set difference
\mathcal{A}_{all}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{heldout}
.

•

Held-out Recall
: The recall evaluated exclusively on the held-out subset
\mathcal{A}_{heldout}
, serving as a direct measure of latent generalization.

•

Pass@N
: The rate at which the model’s predicted set exactly matches
\mathcal{A}_{all}
within
N
independent attempts. This is a rigorous exact-match metric that yields a score of zero if the generated set contains any omissions or false inclusions.

•

Primary Reference Inclusion
: A binary indicator of whether the specific completion targeting the held-out condition (e.g., a specific reversal or syllogism) is present in the model’s output. This provides an additional proxy for true latent generalization.

Note that these metrics are inherently correlated; for instance, a model achieving a higher Overall F1 score is proportionally more likely to achieve a higher Pass@
N
. However, it is possible for a model to obtain reasonable F1 score without necessarily predicting the completion which tests the latent-learning behavior, so it is useful to look at all these metrics independently. For the
Reversal Curse Dataset
, each query possesses a single unique correct completion; therefore, we exclusively report exact string-match accuracy.

3.4.1
Semantic Structure Benchmark

Prior research, such as

Out-of-Distribution Latent Generalization

We now evaluate out-of-distribution (OOD) latent generalization. In this setting, we measure performance on holdouts from a distinct dataset that was only available to the model during the initial knowledge acquisition phase; crucially, no train-time data augmentation or RL fine-tuning (with or without intermediate reasoning) was applied to this secondary dataset. We previously hypothesized that while train-time data augmentations are intrinsically tied to the specific training distribution and fail to generalize to novel datasets, training a model to utilize test-time compute to reason over its internal parametric knowledge achieves a task-agnostic latent generalization.

Figure

2(b)
presents the results for this OOD evaluation (only all F1 is reported here, other metrics can be found in
Figures

8
and
9
where similar results hold). The empirical findings demonstrate that, in this regime, the data augmentation baseline yields no measurable improvement over standard supervised fine-tuning. The small improvement of the augmentation baseline on the strict reversals seen in the in-distribution case is completely gone once the potential leakage from the augmentation process is stopped by using a disjoint dataset. In stark contrast, our reasoning-based approach maintains OOD latent generalization performance comparable to its in-distribution results. On strict reversals, the performance of thinking, although better than augmentation, significantly trails ICL. This indicates that although intermediate reasoning enables LLMs to better elicit and generalize over their internalized parametric knowledge, this capability is fundamentally constrained when a query cannot be resolved by logically chaining multiple training facts. In other words, LLMs employing intermediate reasoning still struggle to execute direct, zero-hop logical reversals. We investigate reversal performance of thinking models in more detail on the Reversal Curse dataset (

Overall, the out-of-distribution structure results confirms that leveraging test-time compute to dynamically elicit and reason over parametric knowledge is a more robust and flexible mechanism than relying on static train-time data augmentations, thereby validating the central hypothesis of this work.

This dataset is analogous to the strict reversal condition in the Semantic Structure Benchmark: the training documents contain no intermediate reasoning chains that the model can exploit to deduce the correct answer. Instead, the model must directly invert the learned factual association. The corresponding results are illustrated in Figure

As shown in the
Figure

3
, standard SFT fails to generalize, and scaling the number of parallel attempts (up to
16
) yields no performance improvement. On the other hand, the model trained to generate intermediate reasoning traces exhibits measurable improvement, yet its performance remains inferior to that of ICL. An example of a successful reasoning trace generated by our model is provided in Appendix
B.4
. The trace reveals that the specifically encouraged generate-and-verify behavior in the golden traces helps the model as it proposes candidate entities acquired during the knowledge acquisition phase and subsequently self-verifies these candidates using its internalized parametric knowledge regarding each entity. Notably, this self-verification step does not require logical inversion, because the forward name-to-description mapping is explicitly encoded in the training data. If the retrieved description aligns with the target description provided in the query, the model terminates the reasoning process and outputs the verified entity.

However, this heuristic is not entirely reliable, as LLMs frequently exhibit brittle factual self-verification capabilities. Furthermore, the efficacy of this strategy is strictly bottlenecked by the requirement that the model possesses a non-zero probability of initially generating the correct entity as a hypothesis that undergoes verification. While scaling test-time compute via multiple attempts predictably increases the overall success rate, the pass@1 accuracy remains low.
Ultimately, these findings indicate that executing strict reversals derived purely from parametric knowledge remains a persistent challenge, even when models are augmented with intermediate reasoning capabilities.
ICL continues to be the most effective paradigm for resolving such direct logical inversions.

Related Work

Generalization from fine-tuning and latent learning:

Several works look at some of the surprising ways in which models generalize from their dataset
(
4
;
20
)
, or fail to generalize
(
5
;
2
;
3
)
study the generalization abilities of fine-tuning in contrast to in-context learning. Borrowing terminology from
(
6
;
25
)
, the problem of latent learning is discussed in
(
16
)
, as one in which the model learns information that is not relevant to the task at hand, but that might be useful for a different future task. We operate in a similar train-test setup as some of these works studying generalization, but specifically focus on the ability of test-time compute to improve the latent learning ability of these models. We focus on the comparison of this test-time thinking strategy to other established methods like train-time augmentation.

Data augmentation to improve generalization:

Data augmentation is a long-established solution to improving the generalization of models after fine-tuning. For specific instances of the latent learning problem such as the reversal curse, augmentation strategies, such as adding inverse inference to the training data
(
14
)
have been proposed in prior works. Knowledge augmentation is also extensively studied in
(
3
)
, shedding light on the role of augmentation in both pre-training and fine-tuning phases and its impact on generalization. More general augmentation mechanisms were proposed in
(
15
)
, where model’s in-context inferences on a subset of the training data are added to the training dataset. They show that this improves performance at least on the knowledge structure where this augmentation is performed. Another variant proposed in
(
22
)
, leveraged similar in-context learning ability along with additional question-answers to improve model performance. A more comprehensive review on the topic of data augmentations to improve model performance is provided by
(
7
;
11
)
. We compare our method to a similarly flavored augmentation to
(
15
)
in our work.

Thinking to improve reasoning:

The use of CoT thinking to greatly improve performance on reasoning tasks has been studied in several prior works
(
17
;
21
;
10
)
. We refer the reader to
(
8
)
for a more comprehensive overview of this area. This line of research typically focuses on mathematical reasoning, science, and programming, and does not specifically look at how thinking can be leveraged to improve the use of factual knowledge stored in a model’s weights.

Reinforcement learning for factuality:

Several works study how reinforcement learning in the post-training phase can greatly improve the factuality of language models in the absence of test-time compute
(
24
;
18
)
. In our work, we leverage these findings by ensuring that both the train-time augmentation and fine-tuning baselines undergo RL. On the other hand, for methods that explicitly use test-time compute (thinking)
2
study the utility of chain-of-thought in the ability of a model to access and manipulate its parametric knowledge, finding that chain-of-thought is critical for utilizing knowledge even if the knowledge is encoded perfectly. For the specific case of the reversal curse (termed inverse knowledge search), they find that language models cannot perform this task even with chain-of-thought. In our work, we find that when sufficient support exists in the data, or if the model can efficiently leverage self-verification, the task of reversal might be tractable.
(
23
)
propose a method where the model is encouraged to recite relevant passages from its pre-training data before producing an answer, acting as a form of chain-of-thought for factual knowledge.
9
focus on improving long-form factuality using reasoning, where the model can make a tradeoff between specificity and recall in various claims of a response. Closely related to our work is concurrent work by
19
, where they study how chain-of-thought can help factuality on single-hop questions such as those found in SimpleQA
(
26
)
. They find that models can be trained to utilize chain-of-thought to improve behaviors on factual benchmarks. Another closely related concurrent work by
12
studies the effect of reasoning on single-hop factual queries. They find that encouraging thinking can greatly improve pass@k without any training, and identify two key drivers behind the mechanism: thinking about related topics (factual priming), and the ability of the model to leverage additional thinking-token compute independent of the actual thinking content.

Our work distinguishes itself from these in that we care about factuality not only with respect to the direct knowledge the model is exposed to during training, but also facts which are latent and not directly stated in the data. We control the facts the model sees during training using our synthetic datasets, and test on facts which are latent in the dataset and not explicitly stated. This enables us to make fine-grained conclusions about the generalization of various methods on difficult subsets, such as strict reversals. We also compare the efficacy of test-time compute (thinking) vs. train-time compute (augmentation) on these subsets.

5
Discussion & Conclusion

Recent advances in test-time scaling, often implemented via intermediate reasoning or “thinking” traces, have enhanced the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). We investigate whether models can leverage this additional test-time compute to improve latent generalization whereby they can resolve deductive reasoning challenges, such as syllogisms and logical reversals, when generating responses exclusively from parametric knowledge. Through controlled experiments, we demonstrate that LLMs can indeed utilize test-time compute to enhance latent generalization, proving to be a more flexible and domain-agnostic strategy than relying on static train-time data augmentations.

Despite this success, strictly pure logical reversals remain elusive, even for models augmented with intermediate reasoning and self-verification. We hypothesize this limitation stems from the rigid key-value associative memory structures that models acquire as a consequence of the transformer architecture and the autoregressive next-token prediction objective. The reversal curse and related failures of latent generalization highlight fundamental deficiencies in the contemporary LLM paradigm, encompassing both the underlying architecture and the optimization objective. While scaling compute, whether during training (e.g., via data augmentation) or at inference time (e.g., via reasoning traces), may address some of these limitations, we believe exploring stronger inductive biases, novel architectures, alternative loss functions, or a combination thereof in parallel could provide a more efficient path to obtaining better models.

B.2
Reversal Curse Dataset
(
5
)

This synthetic dataset comprises fictional celebrity names paired with their corresponding descriptions. During training, the dataset presents specific celebrity names followed by their descriptions (i.e., name-to-description). At test time, the model is provided with a description and queried to generate the corresponding celebrity name (i.e., description-to-name). During the knowledge acquisition phase, we introduce a data augmentation step requiring the model to unconditionally output a randomly sampled name from the dataset. This ensures the model acquires a non-zero probability of generating these fictional names, as it must observe them as target tokens during training. This experimental design parallels the strict reversal condition of the semantic structure benchmark, as no alternative reasoning path exists to deduce the correct answer other than directly inverting the premise observed in the training documents. Consistent with our processing of the semantic structure benchmark, we format the dataset for instruction tuning by prepending task-specific instructions to the documents. We partition the dataset based on the celebrity names, strictly ensuring that the target names required for the test queries do not appear as ground-truth outputs within either the thinking bootstrapping or RL training sets. The composition of the dataset across the various training and evaluation phases is summarized below:

•

Knowledge Acquisition Data
: This split contains the following components, each augmented with paraphrased variants to encourage generalization:

–

30 examples containing both name-to-description and description-to-name pairs.

–

60 examples containing only name-to-description pairs (i.e., the corresponding description-to-name pairs are withheld).

–

30 examples containing only description-to-name pairs (i.e., the corresponding name-to-description pairs are withheld).

–

120 unconditional generation examples prompting the model to produce a random name from the dataset without any conditioning description. For each instance, the target is uniformly sampled from the 120 available names.

•

Thinking bootstrapping and RL Data
: For this phase, we utilize 30 of the 60 names that lack description-to-name pairs in the knowledge acquisition set for RL training. A small subset of these (fewer than 5) is allocated to generate the dataset for reasoning bootstrapping. Additionally, the RL training mixture includes prompts for which the description-to-name pair was already provided during knowledge acquisition; this serves to regularize the policy, teaching the model to directly retrieve explicitly stated knowledge with minimal intermediate reasoning.

•

Test Data
: The test set comprises the description-to-name task for the remaining 30 names that were strictly held out from the description-to-name format across all preceding phases. We construct multiple paraphrased versions of each test query to mitigate prompt sensitivity and report the average performance across these variants.

B.3
Leakage prevention in thinking models

During reinforcement learning training of models which use chain-of-thought, it is possible that held-out knowledge which the model is tested on is present in the thinking trace and reinforced by a positive reward. In order to prevent this behavior, we use a separate prompt prefix for the in-distribution and out-of-distribution structures in the Semantic Structure dataset. This results in the model purely considering entities in one of the structures when it is questioned about an entity from that structure. This ensures that the chain-of-thought does not contain entities from the held-out structure during training, ensuring that performance on the out-of-distribution structure is purely due to improved latent learning ability, and not leakage of knowledge in the reinforced chain-of-thoughts. Although we did not do this procedure for the reversal curse dataset, we find that the Pass@1 score of the thinking model corresponds very closely to the strict reversal condition on the out-of-distribution semantic structure benchmark. Hence we believe leaked knowledge in thought traces did not play a role in improved performance.
