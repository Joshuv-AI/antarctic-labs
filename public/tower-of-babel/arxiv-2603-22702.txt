Testing Properties of Edge Distributions
Yumou Fei

Abstract
We initiate the study of distribution testing for probability distributions over the edges of a graph, motivated by the closely related question of “edge-distribution-free” graph property testing. The main results of this paper are nearly-tight bounds on testing bipartiteness, triangle-freeness and square-freeness of edge distributions, whose sample complexities are shown to scale as Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) , n 4 / 3 ± o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{4/3\pm o(1)} and n 9 / 8 ± o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{9/8\pm o(1)} , respectively. The technical core of our paper lies in the proof of the upper bound for testing square-freeness, wherein we develop new techniques based on certain birthday-paradox-type lemmas that may be of independent interest. We will discuss how our techniques fit into the general framework of distribution-free property testing. We will also discuss how our results are conceptually connected with Turán problems and subgraph removal lemmas in extremal combinatorics.

A distribution over such a domain can be viewed as a edge-weighted graph on

1.3
Related Work

Possibly due to the close connection with PAC learning, many studies on distribution-free property testing focused on functions on the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
(e.g.
[

A few papers

studied a model called “vertex-distribution-free” graph property testing. In that model, the unknown distribution is over the vertices of a graph, and (more critically,) distances between graphs are measured with respect to the vertex distribution: suppose

are two edge sets, then the distance between

and

\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E_{1}\triangle E_{2}}\mu(\{u\})\cdot\mu(\{v\}),\quad\text{where }E_{1}\triangle E_{2}\text{ is the symmetric difference between }E_{1}\text{ and }E_{2}.

This roughly corresponds to taking
\Lambda=\binom{[n]}{2}
in Definition
1.3
and restricting the unknown distribution
\mu
over
\binom{[n]}{2}
to be a “product distribution” (see e.g.
[
20
, Section 10.1.3]
). It turns out that such product distributions behave not too differently from the uniform distribution in many important aspects. In particular, subgraph removal lemmas (that are well-known in the uniform distribution setting; see e.g.
[
30

) still hold
[
22
]
, implying that any subgraph-freeness property can be tested in constant queries
4
4

4

\varepsilon
but not on the number of vertices of the graph.
in the vertex-distribution-free model.

In contrast, the questions considered in the present work correspond to taking
\Lambda=\binom{[n]}{2}
in Definition
1.3
but not imposing any restriction on the unknown distribution

In this model, (especially since distances between graphs are no longer measured with respect to a product distribution) subgraph removal lemmas no longer make sense, and many basic properties such as triangle-freeness cannot be tested in constant queries, as already observed in
[

attempts to answer.
5

5

Another type of distribution-free models that has been considered for graphs features unknown distributions over

1.4
Further Motivation for Edge-Distribution-Free Testing

As mentioned in Section
1.3
, it has been more popular to study the case
\Lambda=\{0,1\}^{n}
in Definition
1.3
than the case
\Lambda=\binom{[n]}{2}
. However, sometimes questions about the latter domain naturally arise when studying questions about the former. In particular, in the paper
[
8

on distribution-free testing of decision lists (a class of Boolean functions on the hypercube), it turns out that the “hardest” case for a decision list tester is (roughly speaking) when the unknown distribution over

is actually supported on the “weight-2 slice”

\big\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\,\big|\,\text{the Hamming weight of }x\text{ is }2\big\},

which is clearly equivalent to the domain
\binom{[n]}{2}
. The class of functions
\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that are decision lists, when restricted to the weight-2 slice, becomes a class of functions
\binom{[n]}{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
or equivalently a class of graphs known as

. In order to show that the property of being a decision lists on
\{0,1\}^{n}
can be tested in
O(n^{11/12})
queries, the authors of
[
8
]
had to (roughly speaking) first show the following:

Theorem 1.9

(Implicit in
[
8
]
)
.

The property of being a threshold graph on
n
vertices can be tested in the edge-distribution-free model (with queries; see Remark
1
) using at most
O(n^{2/3})
queries (samples and queries combined; see Remark
1
).

It seems likely that in order to obtain an query-optimal distribution-free decision list tester, one has to first optimize the query complexity in Theorem
1.9

\Omega(\sqrt{n})
for testing decision lists and (implicitly) for testing threshold graphs was shown in

Technical Overview

In this section, we provide an overview of our proof techniques.

2.1
General Framework

We start by describing a general framework for distribution-free property testing.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty family of Boolean valued functions on a finite domain

is an
f

but no proper subset of

is an
f
-violation of
\mathcal{H}
. The collection of minimal
f
-violations of
\mathcal{H}
is the edge set of a hypergraph on the vertex set

that we call the

of

against
\mathcal{H}
.

The notion of violation hypergraph was formally introduced in
[

2.2
Applying the Framework to Graph Problems

The framework in Section
2.1
is especially suitable for analyzing subgraph-freeness properties. It is easy to see that for any graph
H
and any
f:\binom{[n]}{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, the minimal
f
-violations of
\mathcal{G}^{H\textup{-free}}_{n}
(defined in Theorem
1.8
) are the copies of
H
in the edge set
f^{-1}(1)
. Therefore, for any graph
H
with
t
edges, any violation hypergraph against
H
-freeness is
t
-uniform. We may thus apply Lemma
2.3
and immediately get:

Theorem 2.5
.

For any simple graph
H
with
t\geqslant 1
edges, we have

\mathsf{sam}\big(\mathcal{G}^{H\textup{-free}}_{n},\varepsilon\big)\leqslant O(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\binom{n}{2}^{(t-1)/t}=O(n^{2(t-1)/t}/\varepsilon).

The upper bound part of (
1.2
) follows as a special case of Theorem
2.5
, by taking
H
to be a triangle.

However, if we apply Theorem
2.5
to the square-freeness property, we can only get the upper bound
\mathsf{sam}\big(\mathcal{G}^{\textup{squ}}_{n},\varepsilon\big)\leqslant O(n^{3/2}/\varepsilon)
, failing to reach the optimal bound
n^{9/8\pm o(1)}
as stated in (
1.3
). In order to obtain the desired upper bound
O(n^{9/8}/\varepsilon)
, we have to develop new techniques based on Lemma
2.3
:

1.

We first open up the proof of Theorem
2.3
(due to
[
8
]
) as a white box. The main idea of the proof is to use linear programming duality to turn the
universally-quantified
condition about vertex cover into an
existence
of “fractional matching” in the violation hypergraph (Lemma
5.2
). In the context of testing square-freeness, the edges of the violation hypergraph are copies of squares in the input graph, so the “fractional matching” would translate to a family of “weighted squares” whose convex combination is dominated by the edge distribution (Definition
5.5
).

2.

The fractional matching effectively allows us to “embed” a classical-birthday-paradox structure into the edge distribution. One can use Carathéodory’s theorem to limit the number of squares in the fractional matching, i.e. the number of “birthday slots,” to at most
O(n^{2})
. In
O(n^{3/2})
samples, with high probability there are four people sharing a birthday, i.e. four edges forming a square. This is how Theorem
2.3
is proved in
[
8
]
(and works perfectly well for testing triangle-freeness), but falls short of the optimal bound for testing square-freeness by a polynomial factor.

3.

The main new idea is to
delay
the application of Carathéodory’s theorem, and to try to milk the “fractional matching” for more. It turns out that there are
two
different sources of squares that we could hope to reveal by samples. On the one hand, there is the family of weighted squares “planted” into the edge distribution by the fractional matching, which has been our only source of squares. On the other hand, if these squares are planted in a sufficiently “dilute” manner, we argue that a huge number of
unintended
squares will inevitably be created during the process, and these unintended ones will be our second source of squares.

4.

In this subsection we summarize general notational conventions used throughout this paper.

Sets.

For two subsets

Probability.

For a finite domain

\mu

\Lambda

and

Sampling.

Given a finite domain
\Lambda

is a random element

of an extended domain
\Lambda\cup\{\textup{{nil}}\}

\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y=x\right]=p_{x}\text{ for any }x\in\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y=\textup{{nil}}\right]=1-\sum_{x\in\Lambda}p_{x}.

The special symbol

will always be used as an “outside” placeholder element in such contexts. Samples from sub-probability mass functions are similarly defined.

Empirical vectors.

Given a sequence of elements
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\in\Lambda
, we define the

of this sequence to be vector

where the coordinate

equals the number of indices
i\in[n]
such that
y_{i}=x
, for each element
x\in\Lambda
. The
empirical indicator vector
of this sequence is the vector

defined by

.

3.2
Stochastic Domination

Sampling processes (as formally introduced in Section

) are of central importance in this paper. In order to meaningfully compare different sampling processes, we make the following definition of stochastic domination. Recall from Section

that the output of a sampling process is a random element of the space
\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
for some index set
\Lambda
, so it suffices to “compare” distributions over
\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite set and let
\mu,\nu
be probability distributions over
\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
. For parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in(0,1]
, we say
\nu
is
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

by
\mu
, written as

\nu\leqslant_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\mu,

if there exists a coupling distribution
\rho
over
\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}\times\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
such that the following conditions hold:

(1)

\mathbb{P}_{(w,z)\sim\rho}\left[w\succeq z\right]\geqslant\lambda_{1}
.
10
10

10

For vectors
w,z\in\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
, we write

if
w_{x}\geqslant z_{x}
for all
x\in\Lambda
.

(2)

For any subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\Lambda}
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{(w,z)\sim\rho}\left[w\in S\right]=\mu(S)
.

(3)

For any subset

, we have
\lambda_{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{(w,z)\sim\rho}\left[z\in S\right]\leqslant\nu(S)
.

When

Our definition of stochastic domination has the following basic property.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite set, and let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in(0,1]
be constants. Suppose

are probability distributions over

-dominated by
\mu

Proof.

It suffices to notice the union bound inequality

\underset{(x,y)\sim\rho}{\mathbb{P}}\left[x\in S\right]\leqslant\underset{(x,y)\sim\rho}{\mathbb{P}}\left[y\in S\right]+\underset{(x,y)\sim\rho}{\mathbb{P}}\left[x\not\succeq y\right],

and then replace the three terms in the inequality by the desired quantities, using the three conditions in Definition
3.1
.
∎

Let

In this subsection, we prove the lower bound part of Theorem

Lemma 4.3

Let

\Gamma:=\bigl\{\{u,v\}:u\in V_{i},\ v\in V_{j},\ 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k\bigr\}.

Fix a proper
(k-1)

denote its color classes. For each

Now fix a color

(4.2)

Define

, respectively, have total variation distance less than
1/3
if
m<n/3
. Both
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}
can be alternatively generated by first sampling edges
\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\dots,\{u_{m},v_{m}\}
uniformly at random from the edge set

E_{n,k}=\left\{\bigl\{(a,i),(b,j)\bigr\}\,\middle|\,a,b\in[n],\;\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}\right\},

and then letting

e_{i}=\big\{(u_{i},t_{i}),(v_{i},s_{i})\big\}\text{ for some suitably chosen }s_{i},t_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}

for all
i\in[m]
. Note that the first step (choosing
u_{i}
’s and
v_{i}

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}
, while the second step may be implemented differently for the two. Furthermore, if the collection
\bigl\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\dots,\{u_{m},v_{m}\}\bigr\}
sampled in the first step does not contain a cycle or repeated edges, the second step is also identical for
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}

\big\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\dots,\{u_{m},v_{m}\}\big\}

\sum_{r=2}^{+\infty}(kn)^{r}\cdot\frac{m^{r}}{\binom{k}{2}^{r}n^{2r}}\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{r},

we have
\|\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}-\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{+\infty}\left(m/n\right)^{r}<1/3
if
m<n/3
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

For any
k\geqslant 3
and any fixed simple graph
H
with
(k-1)
vertices and at least one edge, we have
\mathsf{sam}\big(\mathcal{G}^{H\textup{-hom}}_{2kn},k^{-2}\big)\geqslant n/3
.

Proof.

For any

\mu

(considered as an edge set over

(because any graph homomorphic to

5
Upper Bound for Square-Freeness

In this section, we prove our main result

, following the ideas outlined in Section

. In Section

5.1
Birthday Paradox Lemmas

5.1.1
Birthday Paradox in Grids

Suppose there is a probability distribution over the cells in a grid with
n
rows and
r
columns. The classical birthday paradox states that in
O(\sqrt{n})
samples from the distribution, with high probability there are two samples falling in the same row. It turns out that for our applications, it is important to additionally ask that the two samples fall in
different cells
of the same row. However, such an event is no longer guaranteed to happen with high probability if the distribution over cells is arbitrary (for example, consider the case the distribution is supported on a single column). The following lemma identifies a condition (on the distribution) under which this event must happen with high probability in
O(\sqrt{n})
samples.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be constants, and let
r,n
be positive integers. Suppose
p\in[0,1]^{n\times r}
is a sub-probability vector such that

\sum_{a=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{r}p_{ab}-\max_{b\in[r]}p_{ab}\right)\geqslant\varepsilon.

Then, in

m=64\left\lceil\varepsilon^{-1}\log(2/\delta)\sqrt{n}\right\rceil

independent samples drawn from
p
, the probability is at least
1-\delta
that there exist two sampled pairs
(a,b)
and
(a,c)
with the same first coordinate
a\in[n]
but different second coordinates
b,c\in[r]
.

Proof.

We define a sub-probability vector
q\in[0,1]^{n}
by letting

q_{a}=\sum_{b=1}^{r}p_{ab}-\max_{b\in[r]}p_{ab}

for each
a\in[n]
. Let
A=\{a\in[n]\mid q_{a}\geqslant\varepsilon/(2n)\}
. Since
\sum_{a=1}^{n}q_{a}\geqslant\varepsilon
and

\sum_{a\in[n]\setminus A}q_{a}<n\cdot\varepsilon/(2n)=\varepsilon/2,

it follows that
\sum_{a\in A}q_{a}\geqslant\varepsilon/2
.

Processing first half of samples.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be a sequence of
m
independent samples drawn from
p
. For integers
k=1,2,\dots,m/2
, we iteratively define (random) subsets
S_{k}\subseteq[n]
and
Q_{k}\subseteq[n]\times[r]
using the following procedure.

1.

Initialize
S_{0}=\emptyset
.

2.

Repeat the following for
k=1,2,\dots,m/2
:

(i)

If
X_{k}=\textup{{nil}}
or the first coordinate of
X
is not in
A\setminus S_{k-1}
, let
S_{k}=S_{k-1}
and
Q_{k}=\emptyset
.

(ii)

If
X_{k}=(a,b)
for some
a\in A\setminus S_{k-1}
and
b\in[r]
, let
S_{k}=S_{k-1}\cup\{a\}
and
Q_{k}=\{(a,c)\mid c\in[r]\setminus\{b\}\}
.

We then define random variables
Z_{k}
by

Z_{k}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\sum_{a\in S_{k-1}}q_{a}\geqslant\varepsilon/4,\\
q_{a^{*}},&\text{if }\sum_{a\in S_{k-1}}q_{a}<\varepsilon/4\text{ and }S_{k}\setminus S_{k-1}\text{ is a singleton set }\{a^{*}\},\\
0,&\text{if }\sum_{a\in S_{k-1}}q_{a}<\varepsilon/4\text{ and }S_{k}\setminus S_{k-1}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

for all positive integers
k\leqslant m/2
. It is easy to see that for any such
k
, we have
12
12

12

To see this, notice that if
\sum_{a\in S_{k-1}}q_{a}\geqslant\varepsilon/4
, then
Z_{k}=1
with conditional probability 1. Otherwise, the (conditional) probability that the first coordinate of
X_{k}
lies in
A\setminus S_{k-1}
is at least
\left(\sum_{a\in A}q_{a}\right)-\left(\sum_{a\in S_{k-1}}q_{a}\right)\geqslant\varepsilon/4
.

\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{k}\geqslant\varepsilon/(2n)\,\middle|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1}\right]\geqslant\varepsilon/4.

Therefore, the random variable
2\varepsilon^{-1}n\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{m/2}Z_{k}
stochastically dominates the sum of
m/2
independent Bernoulli random variables with mean
\varepsilon/4
. By the multiplicative Chernoff bound, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[2\varepsilon^{-1}n\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{m/2}Z_{k}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon m}{16}\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon m}{8}\right)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}.

Since
\sum_{a\in S_{m/2}}q_{a}\geqslant\min\left\{\varepsilon/4,\sum_{k=1}^{m/2}Z_{k}\right\}
by construction, it therefore follows that

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{a\in S_{m/2}}q_{a}<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{32n},\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}\right]\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}.

(5.1)

Processing second half of samples.

Now consider the set
Q=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m/2}Q_{k}\subseteq[n]\times[r]
. If any of the second half of samples
X_{m/2+1},\dots,X_{m}
falls in
Q
, by definition there exist a pair
(a,b)\in\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m/2}\}
and a pair
(a,c)\in\{X_{m/2+1},\dots,X_{m}\}
with the same first coordinate
a\in[n]
but different second coordinates
b,c\in[r]
. So it suffices to show that with probability at least
1-\delta
, at least one of
X_{m/2+1},\dots,X_{m}
falls in
Q
. Since

\sum_{(a,b)\in Q}p_{ab}\geqslant\sum_{a\in S_{m/2}}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{r}p_{ab}-\max_{b\in[r]}p_{ab}\right)=\sum_{a\in S_{m/2}}q_{a},

we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\left[Q\cap\{X_{m/2+1},\dots,X_{m}\}=\emptyset\,\middle|\,\sum_{a\in S_{m/2}}q_{a}\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{32n},\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{32n},\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}\right)^{m/2}\leqslant\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m^{2}}{64n},\frac{\varepsilon m}{8}\right\}\right)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}.

(5.2)

The desired conclusion then follows by combining (
5.1
) and (
5.2
).
∎

5.1.2
Vertex Cover and Fractional Matching

As discussed in Section
2.2
, the duality between vertex covers and fractional matchings is the key idea behind Lemma
2.3
. The duality argument is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant, and let
G=(V,E)
be a
k
-uniform hypergraph. Suppose
p\in[0,1]^{V}
is a sub-probability vector such that for any vertex cover
C
of
G
we have
\sum_{v\in C}p_{v}\geqslant\varepsilon
. Then there exists a sub-probability vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{e})_{e\in E}\in[0,1]^{E}
such that the following holds:

(1)

The indicator vectors
1_{e}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
,
13
13

13

The indicator vector
1_{e}
is defined by
1_{e}(v)=1
if
v\in e
and
1_{e}(v)=0
if
v\not\in e
, for vertices
v\in V
.
for
e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)
, are linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{V}
.

(2)

We have the coordinate-wise vector inequality
\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_{e}\cdot 1_{e}\preceq p
.

(3)

The sum
\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_{e}
lies in the range
[\varepsilon/k,1/k]
.

Proof.

Consider the following linear program over the variables
\lambda_{e}
, for
e\in E
:

maximize

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_{e}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\lambda_{e}\cdot 1_{e}

\displaystyle\preceq p

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{e}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,\quad\text{for all }e\in E.

(5.4)

Let
\lambda^{*}=(\lambda^{*}_{e})_{e\in E}
be an optimal solution to the linear program with minimum possible support size. Consider the edge set

C:=\left\{v\in V\,\middle|\,\sum_{e\in E}\lambda^{*}_{e}\cdot 1_{e}(v)=p_{v}\right\}.

By the optimality of
\lambda^{*}
, it is easy to see that
C
is a vertex cover of
G
, and hence

\varepsilon\leqslant\sum_{v\in C}p_{v}=\sum_{v\in C}\sum_{e\in E}\lambda^{*}_{e}\cdot 1_{e}(v)\leqslant k\sum_{e\in E}\lambda^{*}_{e}.

Note that the constraint (
5.3
) implies

\sum_{e\in E}\lambda^{*}_{e}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in E}\lambda^{*}_{e}\cdot 1_{e}(v)\leqslant\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}p_{v}\leqslant\frac{1}{k},

and hence the vector
\lambda^{*}
satisfies the requirement (3) of the lemma. Combined with (
5.4
), this also implies
\lambda^{*}
is a sub-probability vector. The requirement (2) is obviously satisfied by
\lambda^{*}
due to (
5.3
). It now remains to show that
\lambda^{*}
satisfies requirement (1) of the lemma.

Suppose the indicator vectors
1_{e}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
, for
e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})
, are not linearly independent. Then there exists a not-all-zero coefficient vector
c=(c_{e})_{e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})}
such that

\sum_{e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})}c_{e}\cdot 1_{e}=0.

(5.5)

In particular, we have

\sum_{e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})}c_{e}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})}c_{e}\cdot 1_{e}(v)=0.

(5.6)

Therefore, there exists some
e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})
such that
c_{e}<0
. Define a positive number
d
to be

d:=\min_{e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*}),\;c_{e}<0}\left(-\frac{\lambda^{*}_{e}}{c_{e}}\right).

It is easy to see that
\lambda^{*}+d\cdot c
is another optimal solution to the linear program introduced at the beginning of the proof (the feasibility is due to (
5.5
) and the definition of
d
, while the optimality is due to (
5.6
)). Furthermore, by the definition of
d
, the support size of
\lambda^{*}+d\cdot c
is smaller than the support size of
\lambda^{*}
by at least 1. This contradicts the definition of
\lambda^{*}
. Therefore, the indicator vectors
1_{e}
, for
e\in\mathrm{supp}(\lambda^{*})
, must be linearly independent.
∎

We note that a weaker version of the above lemma, where the linear independence condition in the first item is replaced with
|\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)|\leqslant|V|
, serves as a crucial step in the proof of Lemma
2.3
by
[

]
. As discussed in Section
2.2
, for applications in testing square-freeness, we need to extract this step from
[

, Proof of Lemma 2.2]
and strengthen it slightly (from
|\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)|\leqslant|V|
to linear independence).

5.1.3
Birthday Paradox in Hypergraphs

Another component of
[
8
]
’s proof of Lemma
2.3
is a “classical birthday paradox” on grids (somewhat similar to Lemma
5.1
). For our applications, we also need to slightly strengthen this component (
[
8
, Lemma 6.5]
), as stated in the next lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k,n
be positive integers. Suppose
q\in[0,1]^{n+1}
is a sub-probability vector such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}q_{i}=1/k
. Define a probability vector
\widetilde{q}\in[0,1]^{(n+1)\times k}
such that
\widetilde{q}_{ij}=q_{i}
for each
(i,j)\in[n+1]\times[k]
. For any integer
m
with

m\geqslant 18k\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{k}\right)^{-1/k},

in
m
independent samples from
\widetilde{q}
, with probability at least
4/5
there exists
i\in[n]
such that the pairs
(i,j)
, for
j\in[k]
, are all sampled.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{N}^{(n+1)\times k}
be the empirical count vector of the
m
samples. Let
y\in\mathbb{N}^{(n+1)\times k}
be a random vector such that the coordinates
y_{ij}
, for
(i,j)\in[n+1]\times[k]
, are mutually independent, and each coordinate
y_{ij}
follows the Poisson distribution with mean
mq_{i}
. A basic property of Poisson distribution is that for any nonnegative integer
t
, the distribution of
y
conditioned on the event
\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{ij}=t\right\}
is exactly the distribution of the empirical count vector of
t
independent samples from
\widetilde{q}
. Furthermore, since
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{ij}
follows the Poisson distribution with mean

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}mq_{i}=m,

it is easy to see (for example by the Berry Esseen theorem) that

\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{ij}\leqslant m\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

It is then straightforward to deduce that the distribution of
y
is
(1,\frac{1}{4})
-dominated by the distribution of
x
, by constructing a coupling as per Definition
3.1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{(n+1)\times k}
be the downward-closed subset defined by

S=\left\{z\in\mathbb{N}^{(n+1)\times k}\,\middle|\,\forall i\in[n],\,\exists j\in[k]\text{ such that }z_{ij}=0\right\}.

By independence between the coordinates of
y
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y\in S\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left[\big.\exists j\in[k]\text{ such that }y_{ij}=0\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\big(1-\exp(-mq_{i})\big)^{k}\right).

If there exists
i\in[n]
such that
mq_{i}\geqslant 3k
, then

\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y\in S\right]\leqslant 1-\big(1-\exp(-3k)\big)^{k}\leqslant k\exp(-3k)\leqslant\frac{1}{20}.

If
mq_{i}\leqslant 3k
for all
i\in[n]
, then
\exp(-mq_{i})\leqslant 1-mq_{i}/(6k)
for all
i
and we have

\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y\in S\right]\leqslant\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left(\frac{mq_{i}}{6k}\right)^{k}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{mq_{i}}{6k}\right)^{k}\right)\leqslant\exp(-3^{k})\leqslant\frac{1}{20}.

Therefore, in either case we have
\mathbb{P}\left[y\in S\right]\leqslant 1/20
. It then follows from Proposition
3.2
and the conclusion of the last paragraph that

\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[x\in S\right]\leqslant 4\cdot\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[y\in S\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{5},

as desired.
∎

5.2.1
From Edges to Squares to Edges

Consider the
4

\binom{[n]}{2}

\mathsf{Square}(n)

\varepsilon
-far from square-free, the condition in Lemma

\varepsilon
-square-witness

.

We now claim that if

is a sub-probability vector whose indicator vectors
(1_{\zeta})_{\zeta\in\mathrm{supp}(q)}
are linearly independent in

, then there exists a sub-probability vector
p^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
that is a descendant of

.

By Definition
5.6
, it suffices to construct an injective map

\varphi:\mathrm{supp}(q)\rightarrow\binom{[n]}{2}

such that for every square
\zeta\in\mathrm{supp}(q)
the edge
\varphi(\zeta)
belongs to
\zeta
.

To this end, consider the bipartite incidence relation between
\mathrm{supp}(q)
and
\binom{[n]}{2}

\zeta\in\mathrm{supp}(q)
is adjacent to an edge

whenever

. By Hall’s matching theorem, it suffices to show that for every subset

E(Z):=\bigcup_{\zeta\in Z}\zeta

denote the set of edges covered by

Suppose for contradiction that

\mathrm{span}\big((1_{\zeta})_{\zeta\in Z}\big)\subseteq\mathrm{span}\big((1_{\{e\}})_{e\in E(Z)}\big).

Hence

\dim\mathrm{span}\big((1_{\zeta})_{\zeta\in Z}\big)\leqslant\dim\mathrm{span}\big((1_{\{e\}})_{e\in E(Z)}\big)\leqslant|E(Z)|\leqslant|Z|-1,

which contradicts the assumed linear independence of the vectors

(1_{\zeta})_{\zeta\in\mathrm{supp}(q)}

As stated in Lemma

Let

5.2.2
The Diluteness Notion

The notion of “diluteness” hinges upon the following three natural definitions. First of all, a distribution over
\binom{[n]}{2}
naturally induces a distribution over the vertex set
[n]
:

Definition 5.9
.

Let
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be a sub-probability vector. We define a sub-probability mass function
\deg_{p}:[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
by letting

\deg_{p}(a)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a\}}p_{ab}\quad\text{for all }a\in[n].

The function
\deg_{p}(\cdot)
is a sub-probability mass function because of the identity

\sum_{a\in[n]}\deg_{p}(a)=\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p_{ab}.

A distribution over
\binom{[n]}{2}
also induces a distribution over the collection
\mathsf{Wedge}(n)
(see Section
3.1
), since the wedges
(\{a,c\},b)
corresponds to
length-2 walks
(from
a
to
b
to
c
) on
[n]
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be a sub-probability vector, and let
B\subseteq[n]
be a subset of vertices. We define a sub-probability mass function
\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]:\mathsf{Wedge}(n)\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows. For all wedges
\big(\{a,c\},b\big)\in\mathsf{Wedge}(n)
, we let

\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]\big(\{a,c\},b\big):=\begin{cases}p_{ab}p_{bc}/(2\deg_{p}(b)),&\text{if }b\in B,\\
0,&\text{if }b\not\in B,\end{cases}

with the convention that expressions of the form
0/0
are taken to be
0
. When
B=[n]
, we abbreviate
\mathsf{Walk}[p]:=\mathsf{Walk}[p,[n]]

Note that for any sub-probability vector
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
we have

\sum_{(\{a,c\},b)\in\mathsf{Wedge}(n)}\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]\big(\{a,c\},b\big)\leqslant\sum_{b\in B}\frac{1}{2\deg_{p}(b)}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{a\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}p_{ab}\right)^{2}=\sum_{b\in B}\deg_{p}(b)\leqslant 1.

(5.8)

By taking the marginal on the start and end vertices of the distribution on length-2 walks, we naturally obtain a distribution over “hops.”

Definition 5.11
.

Let
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be a sub-probability vector, and let
B\subseteq[n]
be a subset of vertices. We define two functions
\mathsf{Hop}[p,B],\mathsf{HopD}[p,B]:\binom{[n]}{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows. For all
\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, we let

\displaystyle\mathsf{Hop}[p,B](a,c)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]\big(\{a,c\},b\big),

\displaystyle\mathsf{HopD}[p,B](a,c)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Hop}[p,B](a,c)-\max_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]\big(\{a,c\},b\big).

(5.9)

When
B=[n]
, we abbreviate
\mathsf{Hop}[p]:=\mathsf{Hop}[p,[n]]
and
\mathsf{HopD}[p]:=\mathsf{HopD}[p,[n]]
.

We are now ready to define the notion of diluteness: given a sub-probability vector
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
that is far from square-free, we classify it as “dilute” if for some
descendant

p^{\prime}
of some
\varepsilon
-square-witness

q
of
p
, the sub-probability mass function
\mathsf{HopD}[p^{\prime}]
has sufficiently large total mass. Note that the following lemma will be applied to the descendant
p^{\prime}
instead of the original vector
p
.

Lemma 5.12

(Dilute case lemma)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant. Suppose
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
is a sub-probability vector such that

\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p](a,c)\geqslant\varepsilon.

(5.10)

Then, in
m=10^{3}\left\lceil\varepsilon^{-1}n\log(12n)\right\rceil
independent samples drawn from
p
, the probability is at least
2/3
that there exist four sampled pairs
\{a,b\}
,
\{b,c\}
,
\{c,d\}
and
\{d,a\}
forming a square.

Lemma
5.12
will be proved in Section
5.2.3
.

Intuitively speaking, if the total mass of
\mathsf{HopD}[p^{\prime}]
is too small, then either the first inequality of (
5.8
) is very loose, or the subtraction of the maximum in (
5.9
) loses too much weight. In both cases, we can argue that
p^{\prime}
is “concentrated” in a suitable sense. This intuition will be formalized in Section
5.2.4
, where we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.13

(Concentrated case lemma)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant. Suppose
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
is a sub-probability vector such that

\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p_{ab}-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p](a,c)\geqslant\varepsilon.

(5.11)

Then we have

\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p_{ab}^{4}\geqslant\frac{2\varepsilon^{4}}{n^{9/2}}.

Before proving Lemmas
5.12
and
5.13
, we first derive from these lemmas the desired upper bound for testing square-freeness:

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant and let
n
be a positive integer. Suppose
\mu
is a distribution on
\binom{[n]}{2}
that is
\varepsilon
-far from square-free. Then in
O(n^{9/8}/\varepsilon)
independent samples from
\mu
, with probability at least
2/3
there exist four sampled pairs
\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,d\}
and
\{d,a\}
forming a square.

Proof assuming Lemmas
5.12
and
5.13
.

We first apply Lemma
5.7
to obtain sub-probability vectors
q\in[0,1]^{\mathsf{Square}(n)}
and
p^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
such that
q

\varepsilon
-square-witness of
p
and

\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p^{\prime}_{ab}=\sum_{\zeta\in\mathsf{Square}(n)}q_{\zeta}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Therefore, we have either

\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p^{\prime}](a,c)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{8}

(5.12)

or

\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p^{\prime}_{ab}-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p^{\prime}](a,c)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{8}.

.

Corollary 5.15
.

We have
\mathsf{sam}\big(\mathcal{G}^{\textup{squ}}_{n},\varepsilon\big)\leqslant O(n^{9/8}/\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

\varepsilon

5.2.3
The Dilute Case

The main idea of the proof of Lemma
5.12
is to apply Lemma
5.1
, the birthday paradox lemma on grids. The grid on which we will apply the birthday paradox is
\binom{[n]}{2}\times[n]
, which contains
\mathsf{Wedge}(n)
as a subset (see (
3.1
)). In order apply Lemma
5.1
, we have to be able to sample
cells
of the grid, which in our setting corresponds to sampling from the sub-probability mass function
\mathsf{Walk}[p]
given sampling access to the sub-probability vector
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
. A natural way to sample wedges given edge samples is the following:

Definition 5.16
.

Let
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be a sub-probability vector. For any integer
m\geqslant 1
, we define
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}(p,m)
to be the output distribution of the following sampling process:

1.

Independently sample
m
edges from the distribution
p
, and let
x\in\mathbb{N}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
be the empirical count vector of the
m
samples.

2.

For each wedge
(\{a,c\},b)\in\mathsf{Wedge}(n)
, we let
w_{ac,b}=x_{ab}x_{bc}
.

3.

Output the vector
w\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathsf{Wedge}(n)}
.

The next lemma shows that if the degree of every vertex is not too small, the sampling process defined above is sufficient for simulating access to
\mathsf{Walk}[p]
(as far as stochastic domination is concerned).

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be constants. Suppose

is a subset of vertices and

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Walk}[p,B],m)\leqslant_{(1-\delta,1)}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}(p,5m).

Proof.

We divide the proof into 4 steps.

Step 1: preparation.

Let

(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots,(a_{m},b_{m}),(b_{m+1},c_{m+1}),(b_{m+2},c_{m+2}),\dots,(b_{5m},c_{5m})

(5.14)

p
.
14

i

(b_{i},c_{i})=(\textup{{nil}},\textup{{nil}})

(5.16)

Step 2: construction of coupling.

We now describe a procedure that generates

The next lemma is a standard argument showing that vertices with too small degrees can be safely ignored when choosing the middle vertex of a length-2 walk.

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a constant. Suppose
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
is a sub-probability vector and
B
is the set of vertices
b\in[n]
such that
\deg_{p}(b)\geqslant\varepsilon/(2n)
. Then we have

\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p](a,c)-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p,B](a,c)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof.

By Definition
5.10
, the function
\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]
is no larger than the function
\mathsf{Walk}[p]
on any input, so for any
\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
we have

\max_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p,B]\big(\{a,c\},b\big)\leqslant\max_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p]\big(\{a,c\},b\big).

By Definition
5.11
, it follows that

\mathsf{HopD}[p](a,c)-\mathsf{HopD}[p,B](a,c)\leqslant\mathsf{Hop}[p](a,c)-\mathsf{Hop}[p,B](a,c).

(5.17)

Expanding and rearranging using Definitions
5.10
and
5.11
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{Hop}[p](a,c)-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{Hop}[p,B](a,c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus B}\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{b\}}{2}}\mathsf{Walk}[p]\big(\{a,c\},b\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus B}\sum_{a,c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}\frac{p_{ab}p_{bc}}{2\deg_{p}(b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus B}2\deg_{p}(b)

\displaystyle\leqslant(n-|B|)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2n}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Combining this with (
5.17
) immediately yields the conclusion.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Walk}[p,B],m/5)\leqslant_{(1-\delta/2,1)}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}(p,m).

Since the set

S=\left\{w\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathsf{Wedge}(n)}\,\middle|\,\text{there are no distinct }a,b,c,d\in[n]\text{ s.t. }w_{ac,b},w_{ac,d}\geqslant 1\right\}

is a downward-closed subset of
\mathbb{N}^{\mathsf{Wedge}(n)}
, it follows from Proposition
3.2
that

\underset{w\sim\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}(p,m)}{\mathbb{P}}\left[w\in S\right]\leqslant\underset{w\sim\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(\mathsf{Walk}[p,B],m/5)}{\mathbb{P}}\left[w\in S\right]+\frac{\delta}{2}.

By the conclusion of the last paragraph, the first summand on the right-hand side is at most
\delta/2
. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}(p,m)}\left[w\in S\right]\leqslant\delta
. In other words, with probability at least
1-\delta
, there exist distinct vertices
a,b,c,d\in[n]
such that all four pairs
\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,d\},\{d,a\}
appear in a batch of
m
independent samples from
p
, as desired.
∎

5.2.4
The Concentrated Case

To prove the concentrated case lemma, we need the following result from spectral graph theory.

Proposition 5.19
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
S
be a symmetric subset of
[n]\times[n]
(i.e. for any
(i,j)\in S
we have
(j,i)\in S
as well). For any real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, we have

\sqrt{|S|}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\geqslant\sum_{(i,j)\in S}x_{i}x_{j}.

Proof.

Consider the symmetric matrix
M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
defined by

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }(i,j)\in S,\\
0,&\text{if }(i,j)\not\in S,\end{cases}\qquad\text{for all }(i,j)\in[n]\times[n].

We know that
M
has
n
real eigenvalues, and we order them decreasingly as
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}
. The matrix
\lambda_{1}I_{n}-M
is positive semi-definite, where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. In particular, we have

0\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{i}(\lambda_{1}I_{n}-M)_{ij}x_{j}=\lambda_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-\sum_{(i,j)\in S}x_{i}x_{j}.

The conclusion thus follows from the fact that
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{|S|}
. To see this, note that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\mathrm{trace}(M^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}M_{ji}=|S|,

which clearly implies
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{|S|}
.
∎

We have now arrived at the crux of the proof — showing that (
5.11
) implies a nontrivial lower bound on the
\ell_{4}
-norm of the vector
p
.

Proof of Lemma
5.13
.

For each pair
\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, we let
h\big(\{a,c\}\big)
be an arbitrary vertex in the set of maximizers

\argmax_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p]\big(\{a,c\},b\big).

For each
b\in[n]
, we define a set
S_{b}\subseteq[n]\times[n]
by

S_{b}:=\big\{(a,c)\,\big|\,a,c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}\text{ such that }a\neq c\text{ and }h\big(\{a,c\}\big)=b\big\}\cup\big\{(a,a)\,\big|\,a\in[n]\setminus\{b\}\big\}.

Note that

\sum_{b=1}^{n}|S_{b}|=\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}2+\sum_{b=1}^{n}(n-1)=2n(n-1).

(5.18)

For each
b\in[n]
, we define

\beta_{b}:=\sum_{a,c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}p_{ab}p_{cb}=(2\deg_{p}(b))^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{b}:=\sum_{(a,c)\in S_{b}}p_{ab}p_{cb}.

Now by Definitions
5.10
and
5.11
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{HopD}[p](a,c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{Hop}[p](a,c)-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\max_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\mathsf{Walk}[p]\big(\{a,c\},b\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus\{a,c\}}\frac{p_{ab}p_{cb}}{2\deg_{p}(b)}-\sum_{\{a,c\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathsf{Walk}[p]\big(\{a,c\},h\big(\{a,c\}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{a,c\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}\frac{p_{ab}p_{cb}}{2\deg_{p}(b)}-\sum_{(a,c)\in S_{b}}\frac{p_{ab}p_{cb}}{2\deg_{p}(b)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\frac{\beta_{b}-\gamma_{b}}{\sqrt{\beta_{b}}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\frac{\beta_{b}-\gamma_{b}}{\sqrt{\beta_{b}}+\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{\beta_{b}}-\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\left(2\deg_{p}(b)-\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\right)=\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}.

Therefore, the assumption (
5.11
) implies
\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\geqslant 2\varepsilon
.
On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\{a,b\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}}p_{ab}^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sum_{a\in[n]\setminus\{b\}}p_{ab}^{4}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in[n],\;S_{b}\neq\emptyset}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S_{b}|}}\sum_{(a,c)\in S_{b}}p_{ab}^{2}p_{cb}^{2}\right)

(using Proposition
5.19
)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in[n],\;S_{b}\neq\emptyset}\left(\frac{1}{|S_{b}|^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{(a,c)\in S_{b}}p_{ab}p_{cb}\right)^{2}\right)

(by Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\right)^{4}}{\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sqrt{|S_{b}|}\right)^{3}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\right)^{4}}{\left(n\sum_{b=1}^{n}|S_{b}|\right)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{8n^{9/2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}\right)^{4}\geqslant\frac{2\varepsilon^{4}}{n^{9/2}},

∎

6
Upper Bound for Tree-Freeness

The goal of this section is to prove the upper bounds for testing tree-freeness and testing cliques, as stated in Theorems
1.8
and
1.7
, respectively. Although they are seemingly unrelated results, the proofs of these two upper bounds are quite similar, and in particular they rely on the same type of birthday-paradox argument. In Sections
6.1
and
6.2
, we develop the birthday-paradox-type lemmas underlying the proofs. The two sample complexity upper bounds will then be proved in Section
6.3
.

6.1
More Birthday Paradox

In one formulation of the classical birthday paradox, two batches of samples are drawn from the same probability distribution, and the goal is to show that, with high probability, there exists a common sample appearing in both batches. In this subsection, we take this perspective a step further: we show that the set of common samples of the two batches can, in a rough sense, be viewed as a single batch of samples drawn from the same distribution.

The “effective size” of this derived batch depends on the sizes of the two original batches. The classical birthday paradox can then be interpreted as establishing that this effective size is at least
1
, and hence that the intersection of the two batches is likely to be non-empty.

The notion of taking the “intersection” of two batches of samples can be formalized as follows.

Definition 6.1
.

Suppose
w^{(1)},w^{(2)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
are empirical indicator vectors (see Section
3.1
). We let
\mathcal{P}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
be the entry-wise product vector
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
defined by
w_{b}=w^{(1)}_{b}w^{(2)}_{b}
for all
b\in[n]
. If
\mu
and
\nu
are probability distributions over
\mathbb{N}^{n}
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\mu,\nu)
be the distribution of
\mathcal{P}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
where
w^{(1)}\sim\mu
and
w^{(2)}\sim\nu
are independent random vectors.

We will also need the following “matrix-vector multiplication” version of Definition
6.1
.

Definition 6.2
.

Suppose
w^{(1)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
w^{(2)}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
are empirical indicator vectors. We let
\mathcal{J}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
be the vector
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
defined by

w_{b}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }w^{(1)}_{a}=1\text{ and }w^{(2)}_{ab}=1\text{ for some }a\in[n],\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
\mu
and
\nu
are probability distributions over
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
\mathbb{N}^{n\times n}
, respectively, then we let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}(\mu,\nu)
be the distribution of
\mathcal{J}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
where
w^{(1)}\sim\mu
and
w^{(2)}\sim\nu
are independent random vectors.

To prepare for the main lemma of this subsection, we make the following two standard definitions. The first allows us to take “marginals” of sub-probability mass functions:

Definition 6.3
.

Let
f:[n]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a sub-probability mass function. Define sub-probability mass functions
\pi_{1}f,\pi_{2}f:[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
by letting

\pi_{1}f(a)=\sum_{b=1}^{n}f(a,b)\qquad\text{for all }a\in[n],\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{2}f(b)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}f(a,b)\qquad\text{for all }b\in[n].

The next definition is motivated by the standard trick of “ignoring elements with too small weights,” which have already been used in Section
5
(see, for example, Lemma
5.18
) and will continue to come into play frequently in this section.

Definition 6.4
.

For any finite domain
\Lambda
and two sub-probability mass functions
f,g:\Lambda\rightarrow[0,1]
, we say that
g
is an
\varepsilon
-pruning of
f
if
g(x)\leqslant f(x)
for all
x\in\Lambda
and
\sum_{x\in\Lambda}(f(x)-g(x))\leqslant\varepsilon
.

We are now ready to state the main lemma of this subsection.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\beta,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
C>0
be constants such that
\beta+\gamma\leqslant 1
and
\gamma\delta\varepsilon\cdot C\geqslant 16
. For sufficiently large positive integers
n
and

m_{1}=\left\lceil Cn^{1-\beta}\right\rceil,\qquad m_{2}=\left\lceil Cn^{1-\gamma}\right\rceil,\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{3}=\left\lceil Cn^{1-\beta-\gamma}\right\rceil,

we have (recall the notion of stochastic domination in Definition
3.1
):

(1)

Any sub-probability mass function
f:[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
has an
\varepsilon
-pruning
g
such that

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(g,m_{3})\leqslant_{(1-\delta,1)}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(g,m_{1}),\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(g,m_{2})\big).

(6.1)

(2)

Any sub-probability mass function
f:[n]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
has an
\varepsilon
-pruning
g
such that

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\pi_{2}g,m_{3})\leqslant_{(1-\delta,1)}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\pi_{1}g,m_{1}),\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(g,m_{2})\big).

For an element
a\in[n]
with very small weight
f(a)
under a sub-probability mass function
f:[n]\to[0,1]
, the probability that
a
appears in the intersection of two independent batches of samples is roughly proportional to
f(a)^{2}
, whereas the probability that it appears in a single batch is proportional to
f(a)
. Thus, for small values of
f(a)
, the former is significantly smaller than the latter. Consequently, a key difficulty in establishing the stochastic domination in (
6.1
) is handling elements with small weight.

In particular, if there exist elements with extremely small weight — namely those
a\in[n]
with
0<f(a)\lesssim 1/(Cn)
— then it is impossible for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(f,m_{1}),\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(f,m_{2}))
to dominate
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(f,m_{3})
. This necessitates an
\varepsilon
-pruning step to exclude such elements. For elements with moderately small weights, for instance those
a\in[n]
with
f(a)\approx 1/n
, the next lemma provides a useful bound on their appearance in a single batch of samples.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\gamma,\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and
C\geqslant 1
be constants. Fur sufficiently large positive integers
n
, the following statement holds. Suppose
f:[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
is a sub-probability mass function such that for all
a\in[n]
, either
f(a)=0
or
f(a)\geqslant\varepsilon/n
. Then for
m=\left\lceil Cn^{1-\gamma}\right\rceil
, we have

\underset{w\sim\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}(f,m)}{\mathbb{P}}\left[w_{a}\leqslant\frac{2n}{\gamma\varepsilon}\cdot f(a)\textup{ for all }a\in[n]\right]\geqslant 1-\delta.

Proof.

For each
a\in[n]
such that
f(a)\neq 0
, the coordinate
w_{a}
is the sum of
m
Bernoulli random variables with mean
f(a)
. By Chernoff bound, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[w_{a}\geqslant t\right]\leqslant\left(\frac{4\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[w_{a}\right]}{t}\right)^{t}\qquad\text{for any }t\geqslant\mathbb{E}\left[w_{a}\right].

(6.2)

Now let
t_{a}=2\gamma^{-1}\varepsilon^{-1}n\cdot f(a)
. Since
f(a)\geqslant\varepsilon/n
, we have
t_{a}\geqslant 2\gamma^{-1}
. Furthermore, we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[w_{a}\right]}{t_{a}}=\frac{m\cdot f(a)}{2\gamma^{-1}\varepsilon^{-1}n\cdot f(a)}\leqslant\frac{C\gamma\varepsilon}{n^{\gamma}}.

Plugging into (

), it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left[w_{a}\geqslant t_{a}\right]\leqslant\left(\frac{4C\gamma\varepsilon}{n^{\gamma}}\right)^{2\gamma^{-1}}=\frac{(4C\gamma\varepsilon)^{2\gamma^{-1}}}{n^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{n},

where we used the condition that
n
is sufficiently large in the last transition. Now, taking a union bound over all

such that

yields the conclusion.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma

.

6.2
Induction on the Number of Edges

Our main idea for proving the sample complexity upper bound in Theorem
1.8
is to induct on the number of edges in the tree. However, we cannot directly use the statement of Theorem
1.8
as an induction hypothesis. Instead, we will formulate a “tree version” of the birthday-paradox-type statement in Lemma

that is specifically designed to be provable by induction.

For the convenience of the induction argument, we view the edges of a tree as directed edges that converge to a designated root vertex.

Definition 6.7
.

Let
V
be a finite set. Given a finite set

of
(|V|-1)
ordered pairs

.

(2)

For every

such that

.

(3)

Every vertex has a path to

v_{0}\in V
, there is an integer

such that
v_{\ell}=v^{*}
and
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in T
for all

To formulate a “tree version” of Definition

7.1
Triangle-Freeness Constructions

As discussed in Section
2.2.1
, the lower bound for testing triangle-freeness is proved by combining the Rusza-Szemerédi construction (Proposition
2.7
) with a standard technique that lifts lower bounds for one-sided-error tester to two-sided-error tester.

The technique is reminiscent of that used in Section
4.2
.
Given an edge set
E\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
, we consider the two-fold blow-up of the graph
([n],E)
,
in which each vertex
a\in[n]
is replaced by a pair of copies.
For any two such pairs corresponding to vertices
a,b\in[n]
with
\{a,b\}\in E
,
the blow-up graph contains all four possible edges between the two pairs.

The key idea is to retain exactly two of these four edges for each
\{a,b\}\in E
.
The structure of the resulting graph can then vary in an interesting way,
depending on how the two edges are selected in each case.
We formalize this operation in the following definition.

Definition 7.1
.

For any edge set
E\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
and any vector
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E}
, we define an edge set

R_{y}(E)=\Bigl\{\bigl\{(a,t),(b,y_{ab}+t)\bigr\}\,\Big|\,\{a,b\}\in E\text{ and }t\in\mathbb{F}_{2}\Bigr\}\subseteq\binom{[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}}{2}.

over the vertex set
[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Note that the vector
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E}
specifies for each
\{a,b\}\in E
how two of the four edges between the
a
-copies
(a,0),(a,1)
and the
b
-copies
(b,0),(b,1)
are selected. The main observation is that if every edge in
E
is contained in exactly one triangle, then we can easily make
R_{y}(E)
either triangle-free or far-from triangle-free, by picking suitable vectors
y
for each case.

Definition 7.2
.

Suppose
E\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
is an edge set such that every edge in
E

is contained in exactly one triangle. We define two collections of vectors
Y^{\mathrm{yes}}_{\triangle}(E)
and
Y^{\mathrm{no}}_{\triangle}(E)
by

\displaystyle Y^{\mathrm{yes}}_{\triangle}(E)

\displaystyle=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E}\,\Big|\,y_{ab}+y_{bc}+y_{ca}=1\text{ for all triangles }\{\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,a\}\}\subseteq E\Big\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle Y^{\mathrm{no}}_{\triangle}(E)

\displaystyle=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E}\,\Big|\,y_{ab}+y_{bc}+y_{ca}=0\text{ for all triangles }\{\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,a\}\}\subseteq E\Big\},

Proposition 7.3
.

Suppose
E\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
is an edge set such that every edge in
E

is contained in exactly one triangle. We have

(1)

For any
y\in Y^{\mathrm{yes}}_{\triangle}(E)
, the edge set
R_{y}(E)
is triangle-free.

(2)

For any
y\in Y^{\mathrm{no}}_{\triangle}(E)
, the edge set
R_{y}(E)
is the edge-disjoint union of
2|E|/3
triangles. Consequently, we have
|R_{y}(E)\setminus E^{\prime}|\geqslant|R_{y}(E)|/3
for any triangle-free edge set
E^{\prime}\subseteq\binom{[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}}{2}
.

Proof.

The second statement is obvious. For the first statement, it suffices to note that for any
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E}
, any triangle in
R_{y}(E)
must “projects” to a triangle in
E
under the canonical projection map from the vertex set
[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
to the vertex set
[n]
.
∎

We next show that when

is randomized in either

or

, it is impossible to distinguish the two cases apart if one is only given

.

Lemma 7.4
.

Fix an edge set
E\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
such that every edge in
E

is contained in exactly one triangle. Suppose there is a randomized map

\mathcal{A}:\binom{[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}}{2}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}

that satisfies the following.

(1)

For a uniformly random

and independent edge samples

, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})=1\right]\geqslant 2/3
.

(2)

For a uniformly random
y\in Y^{\mathrm{no}}_{\triangle}(E)
and independent edge samples
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\in R_{y}(E)
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})=0\right]\geqslant 2/3
.

Then we must have

.

Proof.

In the two assumptions on
\mathcal{A}
stated in the lemma, the input
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
to
\mathcal{A}
follow two different distributions. It suffices to show that these two distributions over
\binom{[n]\times\mathbb{F}_{2}}{2}^{m}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}
, respectively, have total variation distance less than
1/3
if
m<|E|^{2/3}
. Both
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}
can be alternatively generated by first sampling edges

for all

. Note that the first step (choosing

, while the second step may be implemented differently for the two. Furthermore, if the collection

and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{no}}
. Since

contains a triangle with probability at most (by union bound)

\{(i-1)r+1,\dots,ir\}
. In other words, the edge sequence
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
is well-behaved if no
t
edges come from the same copy of
H^{(j)}
.

Analysis.

For any edge
e\in T
, there are exactly
2^{t-2}
copies of
e
in both
H^{(0)}
and
H^{(1)}
. Thus both
H^{(0)}
and
H^{(1)}
have
2^{t-2}t
edges. The main observation is that, for any edge
e\in T
, if we remove all copies of
e
from
H^{(0)}
and
H^{(1)}
, the two graphs become isomorphic. From this observation, it is easy to see that the distributions
\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{1,m}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{1,m}
are identical if
m\leqslant t-1
. Consequently, for any positive integers
n
and
m
, a random
well-behaved
sample from
\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{n,m}
is indistinguishable from a random well-behaved sample from
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{n,m}
. For each
j\in\{0,1\}
, a random sample
(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})\sim\mathcal{D}^{(j)}_{n,m}
is well-behaved with probability at least (using union bound)

1-n\cdot\frac{m^{t}}{n^{t}}>\frac{2}{3}\qquad\text{if }m<\frac{1}{3}n^{(t-1)/t}.

Therefore, we have

\left\|\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{n,m}-\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{n,m}\right\|<\frac{1}{3}\qquad\text{if }m<\frac{1}{3}n^{(t-1)/t}.

(7.5)

On the other hand, since
H^{(1)}
is
H
-free, any graph
G
in the support of the distribution
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(1)}
is
H
-free. Since
H^{(0)}
contains a copy of
H
, for any graph
G
in the support of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(0)}
, at least
n
edges must be removed from
G
to make it
H
-free; in other words, the uniform distribution over the edge set of
G
is
\varepsilon
-far from
H
-free, where
\varepsilon=2^{-(t-2)}t^{-1}
. Therefore, any sample-based distribution-free tester for
\mathcal{G}^{H\textup{-free}}_{rn}
with proximity parameter
\varepsilon=2^{-(t-2)}t^{-1}
and sample complexity
m
must distinguish
\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{n,m}
from
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{n,m}
with probability at least
2/3
. By (
7.5
), this requires

. We thus conclude that

\mathsf{sam}\Big(\mathcal{G}^{H\textup{-free}}_{rn},2^{-(t-2)}t^{-1}\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{3}n^{(t-1)/t}.\qed
