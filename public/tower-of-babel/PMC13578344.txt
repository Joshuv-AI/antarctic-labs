A novel laboratory methodology to evaluate the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio or particle lidar ratio of aerosols
Authors: Alain Miffre, Clément Pacoret-Berenger, Adrien P Genoud, Patrick Rairoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile atmospheric particles are ubiquitous in nature and contribute to the Earth’s radiative budget, their backscattering and extinction remain insufficiently quantified due to their complexity in size, shape and chemical composition. In this paper, a novel laboratory methodology is proposed to evaluate the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio or lidar ratio of aerosols. This methodology, called LR-lab, is based on a robust light backscatter experiment at 180.0° angle, enhanced with a cavity ring down experiment to evaluate the particle extinction. No assumption is required on the particle size, shape, nor complex refractive index. Our methodology is validated on homogeneous spherical water droplets, which follow the analytical Mie theory, for different water droplets sizes and number concentrations. The particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio of mineral dust, a heterogeneous mixture of non-spherical particles, is then evaluated at 532 nm wavelength in laboratory. The dust lidar ratio is found to be equal to (47 ± 2) sr while the dust depolarization ratio is equal to (33.6 ± 0.4) %. By proposing a combined laboratory evaluation of the particle lidar and depolarization ratios, we believe our methodology can meet the growing need for lidar-based aerosol typing and for validation of light scattering numerical models on complex-shaped particles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61997-3.Keywords: Extinction-to-backscatter ratio, Lidar ratio, Backscattering, Extinction, Mineral dustSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

As underscored in the latest IPCC report1, atmospheric particles contribute to the Earth’s radiative budget by scattering and absorbing electromagnetic radiations, which reduce the amount of energy, a physical process called extinction2,3. Among all scattering directions, the backward direction is of prime importance as it governs cooling albedo effects, while absorption leads to a warming of the Earth’s climate. Hence, the particles extinction-to-backscatter ratio3, i.e., the ratio of the particle extinction and backscatter coefficients, relates to the particles’ ability to warm or cool the Earth’s climate. Quantifying this ratio is then essential to account for the role of aerosols on global warming1,3. This task is however challenging since aerosols vary in time and space over the globe through a complex vertical layering, which has to be revealed. In this context, lidar remote sensing instruments, based on light backscattering and extinction, are coveted and represent a major source of global data on atmospheric particles optical properties. Moreover, aerosols present a wide range of sizes and a high degree of complexity in shape, often irregular sometimes with sharp edges, as for mineral dust, which prevents from analytical solutions to the Maxwell’s equations, except for simplified geometries such as homogeneous spheres, for which the analytical Mie theory can be applied2,3, but are seldom encountered in nature.

In the literature, the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio has been addressed in field from ground-based4–9 and satellite-based10–13 lidar remote sensing instruments. Light backscattering and extinction have then been described in a review paper with a lidar perspective14. Retrieving the particle backscatter and extinction coefficient from a single lidar measurement is however an under-constrained problem. The particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio or lidar ratio has hence been introduced in lidar studies by Klett, Fernald and Sasano15–17 to overcome this constrain. Indeed, the solution introduced by the mathematician Klett requires prior knowledge of the particle lidar ratio. This robust formalism has since been broadly adopted in the analysis of elastic lidar observations4–13. The HSRL and Raman lidar methods18,19 enable independent measurement of the particle backscatter and extinction, hence allowing to retrieve the particle lidar ratio in field. Also, a precise knowledge of the particle lidar ratio is key for satellite missions, such as CALIPSO or EarthCARE, assuming an aerosol-type dependent lidar ratio. Moreover, prior knowledge of the lidar ratio for a given particle type, such as mineral dust, is required to apply lidar partitioning algorithms20–24, which allow revealing the complex vertical particle layering associated with specific particle types. To retrieve the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio, an alternate approach is to take benefit from light scattering and extinction numerical simulations, which are now becoming more and more accurate, with major advances25–33. Their ability to reproduce the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio of complex-shaped particles, such as mineral dust, however remains to be assessed as the morphology of these atmospheric particles is difficult to represent mathematically25–33. In this context, laboratory experiments on complex-shaped particles are looked for to provide quantitative evaluation of the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio, while accounting for the particle complexity in terms of size, shape, and complex refractive index (and thus particle type).

In laboratory, aerosols light extinction has been addressed by digital holography34, photo-acoustics35 or by using a ring down optical cavity36–45. Complementary use of polar nephelometry has allowed for simultaneous measurements of particle extinction and scattering46–52, without however addressing the specific backward scattering direction, as being truncated for the purpose of measuring light extinction46–50. However, for precise evaluation of the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio in laboratory, addressing the 180.0° backward scattering direction is mandatory. Indeed, for complex-shaped particles such as mineral dust, no light scattering model2,3 can predict the deviation of the scattering matrix elements from their value at backscattering angle (θ = π) even at near backscattering angles (θ < π)53–56. The 180.0° backward scattering direction is indeed particularly sensitive to the particle inhomogeneity and non-sphericity57,58. Moreover, as published by our group59, addressing aerosol light backscattering at 180.0° angle in laboratory is difficult as it requires overcoming two challenges, that of the backward geometry at 180.0°, which has to be set without blocking the incident light with the detector, and that of the weakness of the particle backscattered light intensity, which intrinsically relates to the particle light backscatter efficiency. This is particularly the case for non-spherical particles, for which particle backscattering is weaker57. Also, to deal with aerosols (airborne particles), particles should be probed in laboratory ambient air, as in lidar applications, rather than deposited on a substrate. We overcame these difficulties by measuring the particle depolarization ratio of various aerosols in laboratory at 180.0°58–63, without however evaluating their particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio. This latter evaluation is even more demanding. To figure out this challenge, for 1 μm water droplets over a 1 cm path length, the Mie theory gives an extinction efficiency 2,3 of around 4, so single-pass Beer–Lambert transmission changes stay below 0.3 %, even at 105 particles.cm− 3, making single-path extinction measurements challenging. Longer paths or higher concentrations could improve sensitivity, but long paths are limited by sample inhomogeneity, while higher concentrations may cause multiple scattering, thus biasing the evaluation of the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio. Conversely, lowering the particle number concentration may render the particle backscatter measurement inaccessible as the backscatter efficiency 2,3 may already be low (only about 0.4 in our example), especially for non-spherical particles for which backscatter is weaker57. Therefore, setting the aerosols sample path length and the particle concentration is one of the challenges to be addressed. Moreover, the temporal and spatial fluctuations encountered in the aerosol generation have to be minimized to allow an accurate evaluation of the particle extinction and backscatter at 180.0°, by keeping the particle size distribution stable over the whole duration of the acquisition, as the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio is size dependent.

In this paper, we report a novel laboratory methodology allowing to accurately evaluate the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio or particle lidar ratio of aerosols in laboratory. This methodology, hereafter called LR-lab for Lidar Ratio-laboratory methodology, is based on a robust laboratory light backscatter experiment at 180.0° angle, also measuring the particle depolarization ratio, enhanced with a cavity ring down experiment to evaluate the particle extinction. To our knowledge, no published attempt exists to evaluate the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio of aerosols in laboratory. The following will present the performances of the LR-lab methodology and propose the first laboratory evaluation of the lidar ratio of mineral dust, a heterogeneous mixture of non-spherical particles.

Validation of the LR-lab methodology

The validation of the LR-lab methodology consists in evaluating the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio of homogeneous spherical particles then comparing it with the analytical Mie theory2. Spherical water droplets were then used as targeted particles, thus following the approach published in37 for cavity ring-down experiments, with corresponding particle backscattering and extinction acquisitions given in supplementary information S4, 5 and data available statement. Within experimental error bars (see Discussion section), the laboratory-retrieved water droplets lidar ratio accurately agrees with the Mie-predicted water droplets lidar ratio. Moreover, this validation experiment has been carried out for several water droplets size distributions and number concentrations, thus proving the ability and the precision of our LR-lab methodology to evaluate such a particle lidar ratio.

Discussion

This section discusses on the advantages, limitations and underlying assumptions behind the LR-lab methodology. The laboratory-retrieved particle lidar ratios are compared with field-retrieved and numerically-retrieved particle lidar ratios.

Advantages and limitations of the LR-lab methodology

The LR-lab methodology presents several distinct advantages. Firstly, following Eq. (1), the accuracy on primarily depends on the precision in the experimental evaluation of the backscatter ratio Rβ = βp /βref and in the corresponding extinction ratio Rα = αp /αref. Hence, in LR-lab, the ratio of the optical cavity length to the sample length does not need to be accurately known as Rα is independent on , in contrary to αp and αref taken separately. Determining the latter would require precise knowledge of , with potential errors documented in the literature on cavity-ring-down experiments41,42. Likewise, the scattering volume does not need to be quantified as long as it is identical for the reference and the targeted particles. This is a strength of our novel methodology. Secondly, the particle extinction-to-backscatter ratio is determined without demanding prior knowledge of any particle concentration. While αp, αref, βp and βref depend on the particle number concentration, Rα, Rβ, and can be determined without requiring any particle concentration measurement. Systematic errors inherent to particle counters40–42 are thus avoided, which brings accuracy on . Thirdly, the LR-lab methodology does not either require assumptions on the particle size, shape, nor complex refractive index of the considered particles. The particle size is however measured over the eight minutes duration of the acquisition as a control measurement, as particle extinction-to-backscatter ratios are size dependent. Fourthly, the fluctuations in the aerosol generation, which inevitably occur during the duration of the acquisition, are accounted for in the LR-lab methodology by considering the resulting fluctuations in the ring down time rates as ultimate evaluations of 1/τp – 1/τ0 (see Methodology section). Nevertheless, the accuracy on depends on the accuracy achieved in the numerical evaluation of and to apply the Mie theory, the reference particles must be homogeneous perfect spheres, of known size and complex refractive index. A key advantage of our methodology is that the particle sphericity can be checked over the whole duration of the backscatter experiment (see Methodology section). Monodisperse particles were left out as reference particles since, as we experienced, the particle depolarization ratio of micron-sized silica spheres was found to differ from the expected zero-value for perfect spheres, thus highlighting discrepancies with Mie theory. In the literature, such discrepancies are attributed to the formation of dimers or/and higher aggregates during the particle aerosolization, as reported in cavity ring-down40 and in light backscattering experiments62. The main limitation in the evaluation of then stems from the precision achieved in evaluating the size distribution of reference particles with the particle sizer.

Backscattering and extinction underlying assumptions

Our methodology assumes that particle backscattering and extinction occur under the single-scattering approximation64. This approximation is actually valid and reliable in our experimental set-up, firstly due to the very small field of view of 0.2° (3.5 milliradians) of the backscatter experiments. Moreover, in case of multiple scattering, light depolarization is no longer an intrinsic single-particle property but becomes a bulk property depending on the particle density and hence on the particle concentration65,66. By contrapositive, the absence of any dependence of the PDR on the particle concentration in Tables 1, 2 and 3 implies that multiple scattering is negligible for all particles considered. Also, the observed variations of βref of βdust with the particle number concentration, to be seen in Fig. 3a, remain linear, as expected under the single-scattering approximation. Furthermore, similar linear relations are observed in Fig. 3b between the changes in ring down time rates and the particle number concentration for both reference and considered dust particles. Hence, while the change in ring down times rates from 1/τref – 1/τ0 to 1/τdust – 1/τ0 is key in the LR-lab methodology (see Results section), the assumption of linearity of the cavity ring down experiment is valid for the reference particles on the one hand, and for the considered dust particles on the other hand. It confirms key assumptions used in the cavity ring down experiment : the absence of significant multiple scattering, the linearity of losses, and the negligible recoupling into the fundamental cavity transverse mode37,40,42 (the fraction of the light intensity that can be forward scattered and re-trapped by the optical cavity, evaluated with Mie theory, was found negligible for both reference and considered dust particles). If the single scattering approximation was not fulfilled, the linearity of with the particle concentration observed in Fig. 3 would not be established down to the origin point corresponding to the absence of particles. To provide a more quantitative criterion, we followed the conclusions from Mishchenko et al. (2007)64: for the single-scattering approximation to be valid, the particles ensemble must be sufficiently dilute (packing density < < 1) and sufficiently sparse (k1⟨d⟩≫1, where k1 is the wavevector in the surrounding medium and d is the average inter-particle distance). In our experiment, the packing densities are indeed low (7.4 × 10− 6 for reference particles, 7.4 × 10− 6 for dust), calculated from corresponding size distributions (see Fig. 5 and S.I.2b), by considering a 106 cm− 3 as a high estimate. The mean distance between neighbour particles was calculated from67 to get k1⟨d⟩ = 1 × 108 > > 1 for reference particles, k1⟨d⟩ = 5 × 107 > > 1 for dust.

Comparison of LR-lab retrieved lidar ratios with lidar field experiments

The laboratory-retrieved value of can be compared with field lidar measurements of dust lidar ratios at 532 nm wavelength4–13. In field, retrieved dust lidar ratios range from 30 to depending on the dust chemical composition and on the dust particle size4–10,12,13. The typical dust lidar ratio is equal to for most dust regions, except the western Sahara4. For Arizona dust, field measurements are more seldom but a value of was reported68,69 for the Arizona dust extinction-to-backscatter ratio at sizes comparable to ours (see Fig. S.I.−2), which agrees with our laboratory measurement within experimental error bars. Moreover, our combined evaluation of the dust lidar ratio and of the dust particle depolarization ratioin laboratory can be compared with lidar-based aerosol typing8–12. To our knowledge, it is the first time that such aerosol typing is verified in laboratory.

Comparison of LR-lab retrieved lidar ratios with Mie and T-matrix numerical simulations

The laboratory-retrieved value of can be likewise compared with light backscattering and extinction numerical models25–33 used to mimic the laboratory-retrieved dust lidar ratio. Application of the Mie theory to the modal parameters given in Fig. SI-2b led to a Mie-predicted dust lidar ratio of 14.3 sr at 180.0° lidar backscattering angle, using a for the refractive index of Arizona dust, from Di Biaggio et al70. The observed discrepancy with the laboratory-retrieved value of 47 sr may be questioned. To account for the uncertainty in the dust refractive index and in the bi-modal dust size distribution, a sensitivity study was performed over a nine-dimensional parameter space (6 for the double-mode size distribution, 2 for the dust refractive index, 1 for the dust dynamic shape factor), explored through a Sobol-based global sensitivity analysis. The Mie-predicted dust lidar ratio was then found equal to (14.4 ± 4.6) sr, (90 % confidency interval: [7.3–21.5] sr), driven almost exclusively by the real part of the refractive index. Likewise, integration over the experimental angular range (180.0 did not modify the retrieved value of the dust lidar ratio as the scattering phase function varied by no more than 0.05 % in between 179.8° and 180.0°. Therefore, the observed discrepancy with Mie theory can be safely attributed to the limitations of Mie theory: dust particles are irregular and heterogeneous and therefore their lidar ratio deviate from the homogeneous spherical-particle assumption. Further computations, such as T-matrix computations, are then highly desirable, though beyond the scope of this paper. To the best of our knowledge, no such numerical simulation exists on Arizona dust. However, numerically simulated dust lidar ratios at 532 nm wavelength lie in the same range as our laboratory finding, in agreement with our laboratory methodology. Moreover, the achieved accuracy achieved on the laboratory-retrieved value of the dust lidar ratio may be used to discuss on the applicability of such numerical models25–33. Such a comparison however requires intensive laboratory work and numerical simulations, which are far beyond the scope of this paper. The measured dust size distribution is however given in supplementary information S.I.−5 to initiate such a cooperation.

Outlooks of the LR-lab methodology

By proposing a combined evaluation of the particle lidar ratio and of the particle depolarization ratio in laboratory, we believe our methodology can meet the growing need for lidar-based aerosol typing8–12 and for validation of numerical models25–33 on complex-shaped particles. Outlooks of this work are then numerous, ranging from applying LR-lab to other particle sizes and complex refractive indices to EarthCARE satellite inversions at 355 nm wavelength10,12. Still, such promising future directions highlight the value of the proposed LR-lab methodology.

Light backscatter by particles

Laboratory set-up for particle light backscatter at 180.0°

Our backscatter experimental set-up is schemed in Fig. 1. Light backscatter is observed with a ns-pulsed laser emitting at 532 nm wavelength as in most lidar applications. The laser source is a diode-pumped frequency doubled Nd: YAG laser source, with 7 ns pulse duration, as published in71.” The laser divergence is low (< 1 milliradian) and ns-laser pulses are emitted at a 1 kHz pulse repetition rate. The backscatter geometry is set by precisely aligning a well-specified polarizing beam splitter cube ( PBC, on the optical pathway from the laser source to the particle scattering medium with a precision of 1 mm out of 10 m, to cover the lidar exact backscattering angle with accuracy: . The experiment is carried out in laboratory ambient air to avoid using chamber walls and windows, with inherent AR-coatings, that may provoke a strong backscattering signal or/and modify the polarization state of the backscattered radiation, which is to be accurately analysed. Likewise, to deal with aerosol particles, the particles are not deposited on a substrate but are probed in their surrounding medium, the laboratory ambient air. As schemed in Fig. 1, the s-component of the backscattered electromagnetic radiation is detected after retroreflection on the PBC. Systematic errors affecting the particle backscattered light intensity were minimized58. For instance, polarization cross-talks were minimized by inserting a secondary PBC (not represented in Fig. 1) on the return path after the retroreflecting PBC. To gain in accuracy, the particle backscattered light intensity is recorded over a complete rotation of a quarter-wave plate ( QWP), labeled with the ψ-rotation angle introduced in Fig. 1(ψ is the angle between the fast axis of the QWP plate and the horizontal scattering plane).

Light extinction by particles

Laboratory experimental set-up for particle extinction using cavity ring down methodology

Our cavity ring down experiment is schemed in Fig. 1. For accurate evaluate of the particle lidar ratio, the same ns-pulsed laser is used as for the backscatter experiment, using polarizing beam-splitter cube () and half-wave plate () to spread the laser power between the backscatter and extinction parts of the experimental setup. The optical cavity was placed along the x-axis, perpendicularly to the backscatter z-axis, to avoid obstruction of backscattering measurements. The optical cavity was composed of two highly reflective plane-concave mirrors and (Layertec, at nm, radius of curvature of 6 m), placed at a L=75 cm distance. To maximize coupling into the cavity transverse mode, a mode-matching optics composed of lenses and has been added to our experimental set-up. TEM₀,₀ is the fundamental transverse electromagnetic mode of an optical resonator, characterized by a Gaussian intensity profile with no transverse nodes. It is the lowest-loss, highest-quality resonant mode of the cavity. The two lenses allowed adjusting the laser waist and longitudinal position to coincide with the waist and location of the optical cavity mode. Two lenses were used for precise mode matching to provide two independent degrees of freedom, allowing simultaneous adjustment of the beam waist size and its position to match the cavity fundamental transverse mode TEM0,0. Their focal lengths (f1 = 50 mm, f2 = 100 mm) were adjusted using Gaussian beam optics, as detailed in reference literature, for example Svelto’s book, Chap. 472. To enable accurate statistical determination of the particle extinction, the particles were injected in the optical cavity through a continuous laminar flow and a buffer volume was used to stabilize the particle distribution. Finally, the light intensity transmitted through the cavity end mirror was recorded with a photomultiplier ( PM).

Equation (5) assumes a linear relationship between the change in the ring down time rates 1/τp – 1/τ0 and the particle extinction coefficient αp, which is proportional to the particle number concentration. The validity of this assumption is discussed in the Discussion section, alongside with the possibility that a fraction of forward-scattered light recouple into the mode of the cavity and that scattered photons take multiple paths to partially recouple to this mode37,40. Under these assumptions, the change in the ring down time rates can be evaluated by fitting the exponential decay of the light intensity . Each individual exponential decay was acquired by first recording the baseline before the arrival of the laser pulse, then the exponential transient of the intensity until complete decay, using a sufficiently high sampling rate45, before adjusting the recorded data points with a functional form exp(-t/τp). To accurately retrieve the inverse 1/τp of the ring-down time, we followed the methodology published in36–44 by taking the natural logarithm of the base-line corrected signals, which allowed verifying the linearity obtained in Ln-scale and the absence of systematic structure in the residue plot, thus proving the single exponential nature of the ring-down decay time, in agreement with the use of a mode matching optics. As in41, the error in such fits remained small compared with the standard deviation of successive extinction measurements. Therefore, the statistical distribution of retrieved 1/τp-values has been plotted in the form of a histogram. Following the central limit theorem, such histogram could be fitted with a Gaussian law, indicating that the observed variations were statistical, thus providing a reliable evaluation of 1/τp through the mean and standard deviation of the Gaussian histogram. However, over the eight minutes duration of the acquisition, fluctuations occurred from one extinction measurement to another, resulting in variations in the corresponding histograms. These fluctuations were attributed to fluctuations in the particle concentration. Fluctuations in the laser power density and in the laser mode stability were indeed negligible compared with the imperfect stability of the aerosol generator. To account for these fluctuations, the combined Gaussian distribution of 1/τp was considered, using the mean value and standard deviation of the combined histogram as ultimate evaluation of 1/τp. The same methodology was applied to the reference particles to likewise evaluate the change in ring-down times rates 1/τref – 1/τ0. In all these extinction experiments, the ratio was maintained constant, by operating at constant detector gain settings, while ensuring a constant overlapping volume of the aerosol particle sample with the intracavity laser beam, by injecting the particles into the optical cavity through a fixed nozzle, located at a fixed position.

Reference lidar ratio

To accurately determine the reference lidar ratio αref/βref, an analytical solution of the Maxwell’s equations is valuable3, as it provides an exact evaluation of light backscattering and extinction. Among all analytical solutions, the Mie theory2,3 is the most widely used, with available numerical code3, but however requires rigorous homogeneous spheres. Reference particles should then be aerosolized without any solvent, to avoid forming a layered sphere, whose optical properties differ from those expected for a homogeneous particle of uniform refractive index42. Consequently, spheres usually available within a liquid solvent, such as polystyrene latex spheres, cannot be used as reference particles. Likewise, particle size standards, such as silica size standards, though monodisperse, should be also left out, as explained in the Discussion section. Therefore, to apply the Mie theory for evaluating , reference particles have to be homogeneous, perfect spheres, of known particle size and complex refractive index. The size distribution of the reference particles then needs to be accurately known, which requires a precise particle sizer.

Implementation of the LR-lab methodology

This section details the implementation of the LR-lab methodology, by presenting examples of recorded backscattering and extinction acquisition curves for both reference and mineral dust, used as an application case study of deviation to the Mie theory.

Reference particles and mineral dust particles

As reference particles, we chose DEHS-particles (di-ethyl-hexyl-sebacate, C26H50O), as DEHS is non-volatile liquid, known to form perfectly spherical droplets, which has been widely used in the literature in nephelometry73,74 and cavity ring-down experiments75. A pure DEHS liquid solution was hence nebulized to produce spherical DEHS-droplets with no layered sphere, using a commercial atomizer (TSI 3079). For the mineral dust sample, a natural mineral dust (Arizona Test Dust, ATD, Powder Technology Inc.) was considered as a proxy for mineral dust, as being composed of a heterogeneous mixture of complex-shaped particles. The dust particles were aerosolized with a solid aerosol generator (SAG 410), supplied with dried compressed air, then dispersed into a buffer volume to stabilize the particle size distribution before entering the extinction-to-backscatter experiment.
