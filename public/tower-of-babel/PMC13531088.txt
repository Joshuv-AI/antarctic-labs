Unexpected Intraoperative Diagnosis of Acute Type A Aortic Dissection in an Octogenarian
Authors: Abdul Rasheed Bahar, Yasemin Bahar, Frank Alexander Baciewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a high-mortality surgical emergency, particularly in octogenarians, and may be overlooked with overlapping cardiac symptoms.Case SummaryAn 86-year-old woman with severe aortic stenosis and multivessel coronary artery disease presented with chest pain. Three months earlier, she underwent evaluation for transcatheter aortic valve replacement, but no intervention was pursued. Because of persistent chest pain, surgical aortic valve replacement and coronary bypass were planned. Intraoperatively, the ascending aorta appeared abnormal, and transesophageal echocardiography confirmed ATAAD. As the dissection originated in the distal ascending aorta, a focal bovine patch repair was performed without ascending aortic replacement. She recovered and remains alive 3 years later.DiscussionThis case underscores the importance of thorough diagnostic investigation and timely imaging in persistent chest pain and an individualized operative approach when unexpected ATAAD is encountered.Take-Home MessageLimited repair may be appropriate in carefully selected high-risk elderly patients.

History of Presentation

An 86-year-old woman with a history of multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and severe aortic stenosis (AS), without prior cardiac surgery, was admitted with persistent, unremitting chest pain despite 24 hours of medical therapy with nitroglycerin, intravenous heparin, and β-blockade. She denied associated dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, focal neurologic symptoms, or altered mental status. On initial presentation, she was hemodynamically stable and normotensive. Physical examination revealed mild distress secondary to chest discomfort but no pulse deficits, blood pressure discrepancies, signs of end-organ hypoperfusion, or focal neurologic abnormalities. Past Medical History

Coronary angiography performed 4 months before presentation demonstrated significant stenosis of the left anterior descending and right coronary arteries. Transthoracic echocardiography, performed both previously and during the current admission, demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of approximately 35%, severe AS with a calculated valve area of <0.6 cm2, a mean gradient of 45 mm Hg, and a calcified ascending aorta. A computed tomography (CT) study performed 3 months earlier using a transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) protocol demonstrated a heavily calcified ascending aorta. In the context of advanced age, reduced LVEF, significant CAD, and overall clinical status, she had not been offered TAVR or percutaneous coronary intervention after multidisciplinary assessment.

Differential Diagnosis

During the index hospitalization, dedicated CT angiography for acute aortic syndrome was not performed, as the patient's persistent chest pain was attributed to her known severe AS and multivessel CAD. After clinical evaluation, her symptoms were considered most consistent with progressive AS and ischemic heart disease.

Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation was unremarkable, with normal cardiac troponin levels, serum creatinine, and hemoglobin. Chest radiography did not demonstrate mediastinal widening, pulmonary congestion, or other acute abnormalities. An electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with nonspecific St-T wave abnormalities and no acute ischemic changes.

Management

In this context, she had not been deemed a candidate for transcatheter or percutaneous intervention. After shared decision-making with the patient and her family, the plan was to proceed with surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). The patient was subsequently taken to the operating room for definitive surgical management. After sternotomy and mobilization of the left internal mammary artery, the pericardium was opened. The ascending aorta then appeared edematous and dusky with an abnormal surface that was immediately concerning for an acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) (Figure 1). Given the intraoperative diagnosis, the transverse arch was cannulated to establish cardiopulmonary bypass. During systemic cooling, SAVR and a left internal mammary artery-to-left anterior descending artery bypass graft were completed. After achieving target hypothermia, a brief period of circulatory arrest was instituted. Inspection revealed a dissection flap along the undersurface of the transverse arch, which was secured to the native aortic wall using a bovine pericardial patch. Given her age and overall comorbidities, replacing the entire ascending aorta would have substantially increased operative time and risk; therefore, the ascending aorta was reapproximated (Figure 2). Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty. Her postoperative course included a 21-day stay in the intensive care unit and was complicated by pleural effusion and respiratory issues. She remains alive 3 years after surgery.

Discussion

ATAAD remains a surgical emergency associated with substantial mortality, particularly in octogenarians, in whom operative risk, frailty, and comorbid burden complicate decision-making.1,2 Registry-based analyses report operative mortality rates in octogenarians of approximately 35%, exceeding those observed in younger populations.3 In addition to elevated surgical risk, diagnosis may be challenging, as presenting symptoms often overlap with other causes of acute chest pain and classical features are not consistently present. Contemporary data further underscore this complexity: octogenarians undergoing ATAAD repair experience nearly double the short-term mortality of younger patients, with reported 5-year survival of approximately 54%.4,5 Management therefore remains controversial; advanced age is frequently associated with multiple comorbidities, increased frailty, and higher operative risk, which may lead to a preference for conservative approaches,6 underscoring the importance of individualized decision-making that incorporates physiological reserve, comorbid conditions, patient preferences, and surgical expertise rather than age alone. This case highlights the unexpected intraoperative diagnosis of ATAAD in an octogenarian undergoing planned valve and coronary surgery, and demonstrates that a deliberately limited repair strategy can result in durable long-term survival.

In elderly patients, ATAAD often lacks the classic abrupt, tearing pain and may present without pulse deficits or hemodynamic instability. This muted presentation can delay recognition, particularly when coexisting cardiac disease offers a more apparent explanation for symptoms.7 In this patient, competing cardiac diagnoses, severe AS and ischemic heart disease, naturally framed the initial clinical reasoning and lowered suspicion for acute aortic pathology. The chest pain was described as a heavy, dull pressure sensation rather than classic abrupt, tearing pain, and there were no pulse deficits, overt malperfusion, hemodynamic instability, severe hypertension, neurologic abnormalities, or ischemic biomarker elevation, further lowering suspicion for dissection. In addition, there was no dyspnea or other findings strongly suggestive of pulmonary embolism. Consequently, advanced aortic imaging was not prioritized preoperatively, and the diagnosis became apparent only on direct surgical inspection and intraoperative echocardiographic confirmation. This case reinforces the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with persistent acute chest pain and considering targeted preoperative evaluation for major life-threatening causes, including acute coronary syndrome, acute aortic syndrome, and pulmonary embolism, when clinically appropriate. In retrospect, additional evaluation such as D-dimer testing and dedicated CT angiography may also have facilitated earlier recognition of acute aortic pathology. This sequence illustrates how cognitive anchoring to known pathology can influence diagnostic pathways in elderly patients with complex cardiovascular disease. During the index admission, given our patient's persistent symptoms, known multivessel CAD, severe AS, reduced LVEF, advanced age, heavy ascending aortic calcification, and overall functional status, a combined surgical approach with SAVR and CABG, rather than TAVR and percutaneous coronary intervention, was considered the most definitive treatment option after multidisciplinary reassessment. In addition, severe aortic calcification raised concern for potential procedural risk with TAVR, including aortic root injury or embolization. After shared decision-making with the patient and family, the decision was made to proceed with SAVR and CABG.

Although preoperative CT imaging is generally recommended before aortic valve replacement and in patients with suspected aortic dissection, repeat imaging was not obtained in this case because the patient was normotensive and had undergone a TAVR-protocol CT scan 3 months earlier. Standard surgical management of ATAAD involves emergency replacement of the proximal ascending aorta to prevent rupture and cardiac tamponade. In selected cases, extension to hemiarch or total arch replacement may be considered; however, these procedures are technically more complex and time-consuming, often requiring longer cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest times. The patient's clinical condition, including advanced age and physiological reserve, therefore plays a central role in determining the appropriate extent of repair. Consequently, the optimal surgical strategy in octogenarians remains debated, and age is frequently incorporated into operative decision-making.8 These considerations become particularly relevant when the diagnosis is made unexpectedly in the operating room. In our case, the intraoperative recognition of dissection required an immediate reassessment of the surgical plan. Although formal ascending aortic replacement represents the standard approach, the patient's advanced age, medical comorbidities, and heavily calcified aorta made a more extensive reconstruction less appealing. Extending cardiopulmonary bypass and circulatory arrest in this setting would have added physiologic stress without clear incremental benefit. A focused repair was therefore performed to eliminate the dissection entry tear.

Patient selection substantially influences reported outcomes, as many octogenarians are never offered surgery due to frailty, extensive comorbidity, or severe neurologic presentation that makes meaningful recovery unlikely. In one center's recent experience, most “turned-down” ATAAD referrals were >80 years old, with frailty and severe neurologic deficits among the most common reasons for nonoperative management.9 In our experience, the outcome in this patient reflects thoughtful intraoperative judgment rather than an attempt to overcome age alone. Despite being 86 years old with significant comorbid disease, she recovered without major complications and remains alive 3 years later. This case suggests that the central question in elderly patients with ATAAD may not simply be whether to operate, but how much to repair. When anatomy is favorable and physiologic reserve permits, limiting operative complexity while addressing the immediate life-threatening pathology may provide a reasonable balance between durability and tolerance. Advanced age should prompt careful deliberation, but it should not serve as an automatic contraindication to intervention.

Conclusions

This case illustrates an unexpected intraoperative diagnosis of ATAAD in an 86-year-old woman whose chest pain was initially attributed to severe AS and CAD. A limited repair strategy was selected based on her anatomy and overall risk profile, resulting in an uneventful recovery with 3-year survival. This experience reinforces the importance of clinically guided, up-to-date diagnostic imaging in symptomatic patients before surgery, along with the need to individualize the operative strategy.
