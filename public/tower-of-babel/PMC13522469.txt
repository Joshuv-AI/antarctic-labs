Plasma derived ceramic-metal hybrid PEO coatings with embedded bismuth phases for multifunctional magnesium surfaces
Authors: Renuga Devi K, Sarathchandran KG, Sreekanth Dondapati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngineering hybrid surface architectures that couple corrosion resistance with biological functionality is critical for extending the performance envelope of biodegradable magnesium-based systems. In this study, a plasma-derived ceramic-metal hybrid coating was engineered on a multicomponent magnesium alloy using plasma electrolytic oxidation (PEO) with controlled incorporation of bismuth species. Sodium bismuthate (1 and 5 g/L) was introduced into a phosphate-alkaline electrolyte, enabling plasma-assisted in situ formation of a multiphase composite coating comprising a MgO/Mg3(PO4)2 ceramic matrix embedded with metallic Bi and Bi2O3 phases. X-ray diffraction confirmed the evolution of phase assemblage with increasing bismuth content, while SEM-EDS analyses revealed a refined hybrid microstructure characterized by uniformly distributed Bi-containing domains and partial sealing of discharge channels. This composite architecture resulted in enhanced surface hydrophilicity and modified electrolyte transport pathways. As a consequence, the Bi-rich hybrid coating exhibited a substantial reduction in corrosion rate from 1.52 mm/year for the Bi-free PEO coating to 0.56 mm/year, accompanied by moderated alkalization during 28 days of immersion in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium. Beyond electrochemical stabilization, the hybrid coating demonstrated multifunctional surface responses. Extract-based in vitro studies using MG63 osteoblast-like cells showed improved cytocompatibility, reduced apoptotic fraction, and enhanced osteogenic activity, as evidenced by increased alkaline phosphatase expression and extracellular matrix mineralization relative to the Bi-free coating. In parallel, the incorporation of Bi-containing phases imparted effective antibacterial performance, with the Bi-rich coating achieving approximately 80% inhibition against Escherichia coli and 85% inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus. Overall, this work demonstrates that plasma-enabled ceramic-metal hybridization via bismuth incorporation transforms conventional PEO layers into multifunctional composite coatings, where controlled degradation kinetics, electrochemical stability, and biological performance are intrinsically linked to the hybrid microstructural design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54747-y.Keywords: Magnesium alloy, Plasma electrolytic oxidation, Bismuth, Corrosion resistance, Osteogenic activity, Antibacterial performanceSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Biodegradable metallic implants that safely resorb after supporting tissue repair are an active area of research for orthopaedic applications because they can eliminate the need for secondary removal surgeries and reduce long-term complications associated with permanent devices. Magnesium (Mg) and its alloys are particularly attractive for such applications due to their low density, elastic modulus close to cortical bone, and the physiological role of magnesium ions in bone metabolism1,2. However, the rapid corrosion of Mg in chloride-containing physiological media leading to premature loss of mechanical integrity, hydrogen evolution and local alkalization remains the principal barrier to clinical translation3. These degradation phenomena can compromise implant function and impair tissue integration, motivating the development of alloying and surface-engineering strategies to control Mg resorption.

Among surface treatments, plasma electrolytic oxidation is widely used to generate relatively thick, adherent, ceramic-like oxide layers on light metals including Mg4,5. The PEO process involves high-voltage micro-discharges that locally melt and rapidly quench the surface, producing mixed oxide, phosphate and/or silicate matrices whose composition and porosity depend strongly on the electrolyte chemistry and processing parameters. For biodegradable Mg systems, PEO coatings can retard initial corrosion, provide a bio-active topography and serve as reservoirs for functional additives; consequently, incorporation of secondary phases into PEO layers has been explored as a route to combine barrier protection with biological functionality6.

A growing body of work has shown that particle or ion additions to PEO electrolytes such as ZrO27, CeO28, TiO29 or bioactive species10 can modify discharge behavior, reduce effective pore connectivity, and improve long-term dissolution by acting as inert fillers or by forming more cohesive corrosion products11. These effects depend on the additive chemistry, concentration and spatial distribution within the coating; when optimally incorporated, such additives can partially seal discharge channels and reduce electrolyte transport to the substrate, thereby lowering corrosion rates during immersion. However, not all particulate additions produce the same outcome effects on wettability, adhesion, and biological response vary with phase chemistry and morphology12. Therefore, mechanistic linkage between additive identity, coating microstructure and multifunctional performance requires careful, case-by-case evaluation.

Bismuth (Bi)13 and bismuth oxide (Bi2O3)14 have attracted attention in biomedical materials research for several reasons. Bismuth compounds have an established clinical history in non-implant formulations and several recent studies report favourable cytocompatibility of certain Bi(III) species and Bi-containing ceramics15. In addition, Bi2O3 exhibits intrinsic antibacterial activity across multiple bacterial strains and has been used to impart radiopacity in composite biomaterials. Incorporation of Bi has been associated with enhanced osteoblast responses and reduced bacterial viability, although the effects depend on Bi oxidation state, concentration and local release kinetics16. These reported properties make Bi a promising candidate for multifunctional PEO additives that aim to concurrently moderate corrosion and enhance bioactivity.

Despite these attractive attributes, the plasma-assisted incorporation of bismuth into PEO coatings on magnesium substrates has been comparatively little explored. Most prior PEO studies that examined Bi containing coatings have focused on inert substrates like titanium or on bulk Mg-Bi alloys rather than on Bi-functionalized PEO coatings on biodegradable Mg alloys17,18 where coating-substrate interactions, plasma chemistry and degradation coupling are distinct and critical. Furthermore, the multifunctional requirements for orthopaedic coatings simultaneous control of early-stage corrosion kinetics, moderation of alkalization during immersion, promotion of osteogenic cell responses, and antibacterial protection necessitate a systematic assessment of how Bi precursor concentration affects phase assemblage, coating microstructure, electrochemical barrier properties and biological outcomes when processed by PEO.

In this study, plasma electrolytic oxidation is employed to fabricate bismuth-modified coatings on a multicomponent magnesium alloy intended for orthopedic use, using a phosphate-KOH electrolyte with controlled additions of sodium bismuthate. The work systematically investigates the influence of bismuth incorporation on the phase assemblage and microstructure of the PEO layers, their electrochemical corrosion behavior in simulated physiological conditions, and their long-term biodegradation in cell culture medium. Furthermore, the cytocompatibility, apoptosis, osteogenic mineralization and alkaline phosphatase activity of MG63 osteoblast-like cells exposed to coating extracts are assessed, together with the antibacterial performance against representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

The multicomponent magnesium alloy MAGMA-7 (Mg-0.2Zn-0.2Ce-0.2Sr-0.15Ca-0.1Ag-0.1Mn-0.1Sn, wt%) served as the substrate material in this study. The alloy fabrication route, along with its microstructural and mechanical characteristics, has been reported previously and is used here as the foundational process19. In brief, high-purity Mg (99.93%), Zn (99.97%), Sn (99.85%) and Ag (99.8%) procured from MatRICS - The Technological Solutions, Tamil Nadu, India, along with master alloys of Mg-15Mn, Mg-30Ce, Mg-20Sr and Mg-10Ca, were melted at 750 °C in a resistance-heated furnace under a protective mixture of 2% SF6 and 98% Ar. After homogenization for 90 min and removal of floating impurities, the melt was cast into a steel mold of 200 × 250 × 30 mm3. Test specimens measuring 10 × 10 × 3 mm3 were extracted from the cast block by wire-cutting and were sequentially ground up to 2000-grit SiC paper. The polished specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), rinsed in distilled water (Millipore -Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and dried in warm air prior to surface treatment.

PEO coating fabrication

Plasma electrolytic oxidation was performed using a pulsed DC power source with the magnesium specimens connected as the anode and a stainless-steel vessel serving as the counter electrode. The electrolyte was prepared by adding 5 g/L Na3PO4·12H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 2 g/L KOH (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to 1 L of deionized water, into which sodium bismuthate (NaBiO3) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was introduced at concentrations of 0, 1–5 g/L. Coatings were produced under constant-current operation at a current density of 0.25 A/cm2, with a duty cycle of 50%, at a frequency of 50 kHz processed at a final voltage of 550 V and treated for 10 min. Continuous agitation was employed to maintain uniform dispersion of electrolyte constituents, and a cooling system ensured that the bath temperature did not exceed 30 °C. After the PEO process, the coated specimens were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and dried using a hot gun. The resulting coatings were designated with specimen codes as given in Table 1.

Phase analysis and surface morphology

Surface features and chemical distribution within the coatings were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; EVO/18, Zeiss, Germany) equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Both surface and cross-sectional morphologies were analyzed, with cross-sectional specimens prepared by sectioning and mounting the coated specimens. FESEM imaging was carried out at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. EDS analysis and elemental mapping were performed at 20 kV. Elemental maps were acquired for 15 min per area, and at least three different regions per specimen were analyzed to ensure reproducibility. Surface porosity and average pore size were quantified from three independent SEM micrographs using ImageJ open-source software (National Institutes of Health, USA) by threshold-based image segmentation of representative surface regions. The values are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Phase composition was assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, X’pert Pro, PANalytical, The Netherlands; Cu Kα radiation, 45 kV, 30 mA), scanned over 10–90° (2θ) at 1°/min with a 0.02° step size. Surface roughness measurements were carried out using a 3D optical profilometer (Bruker Contour GT-K, USA). The surface roughness parameters (Ra) and depth profiles were obtained over a scan area of 0.8 mm×0.8 mm from three different regions for each specimen.

Wettability and coating adhesion

The wettability of the PMA, PBI1 and PBI5 was determined using the sessile drop method. A 5 µL droplet of distilled water was placed on each surface, and the static contact angle was measured at room temperature using a goniometer (DSA100, KRUSS, Germany). Adhesion strength was assessed with a BGD500 digital pull-off adhesion tester (Biuged Precise Instruments, Guangzhou, China). Before testing, the coated surfaces were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), while the metal dollies were prepared by gentle polishing with fine sandpaper followed by solvent cleaning. A high-strength epoxy adhesive was applied to each dolly, which was then pressed onto the coating and allowed to cure completely. After curing, the dolly was connected to the tester, and a steadily increasing tensile load was applied until failure occurred. The maximum force recorded in MPa was taken as the adhesion strength.

Electrochemical corrosion behavior

Electrochemical behavior was evaluated using a potentiostat (Gamry, Interface 1010 T, U SA) in simulated body fluid (SBF) medium which was prepared in-house using Kokubo’s20 protocol and using a conventional three-electrode configuration. The coated specimen was used as the working electrode with an exposed area of 0.5 cm2, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) acted as the reference, and a platinum mesh as the counter electrode. Open-circuit potential (OCP) was recorded for 1 h before polarization. Potentiodynamic scans were then performed by polarizing the specimen at ± 0.3 V with respect to OCP with a scanning rate of 0.5 mV/s. Corrosion parameters, including corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (icorr) were determined by Tafel extrapolation.

Cytocompatibility evaluation

Cytocompatibility evaluation was performed in accordance with ISO 10993-522 guidelines. MG63 osteoblast-like cells (National Centre for Cell Science, Pune) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2). Sterile coated specimens (10 × 10 × 3 mm3) were immersed in 1 mL of DMEM and incubated to generate extracts containing released ionic species. These extracts were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes and diluted with fresh medium to produce 1:1, 1:2 and 1:4 concentrations for biological assays. Cells were seeded at ~ 40% confluence into 24-well plates and allowed to attach for 24 h before exposure to the diluted extracts. Media were refreshed every two days. At days 1, 4 and 7, metabolic viability was quantified by the MTT assay. Osteogenic differentiation and mineralization

For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assessment, cells were lysed after culture in α-MEM under osteogenic conditions. Protein content was quantified by Lowry’s method. Antibacterial activity

Antibacterial performance against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus (Microbial Type Culture Collection and Gene Bank, IMTECH, Chandigarh) was evaluated following adjustment of bacterial suspensions to 0.5 McFarland standard. Specimens were sterilized under UV irradiation, placed in nutrient broth containing bacterial cultures, and incubated at 37 °C. Optical density was recorded at 410 nm (E. coli) and 650 nm (S. aureus) after 24 and 48 h. Fluorescence staining with AO/EtBr was used to visualize bacterial viability at the specimen surfaces.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using three independent specimens (n = 3), and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC). Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test before applying one-way ANOVA, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Phase analysis

The X-ray diffraction patterns of the PEO coatings formed in phosphate-alkaline electrolytes with and without sodium bismuthate additions are presented in Fig. 1. For the Bi-free coating (PMA), the diffraction pattern is dominated by reflections corresponding to MgO (JCPDS No. 01–078-0430) and Mg3(PO4)2 (JCPDS No. 96–900-1028), which are typical phase constituents of PEO coatings produced in phosphate-based electrolytes. In addition, weak reflections from the Mg substrate (JCPDS No. 00–004-0770) are detected, indicating partial X-ray penetration through the porous oxide layer.

XRD patterns of specimens PMA, PBI1 and PBI5.

With the introduction of sodium bismuthate into the electrolyte, a clear evolution in phase constitution is observed. For the coating produced with 1 g/L NaBiO3 (PBI1), the characteristic reflections of Mg3(PO4)2 remain prominent, indicating that phosphate incorporation into the growing oxide layer remains active at lower Bi precursor concentrations. Simultaneously, additional diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic bismuth (Bi, JCPDS No. 96–712-3353) emerge, confirming the successful incorporation of Bi species into the PEO coating during plasma processing.

A markedly different phase assemblage is observed for the coating produced with 5 g/L NaBiO3 (PBI5). In this case, the intensity of Mg3(PO4)2 reflections is significantly reduced, while pronounced peaks corresponding to both metallic Bi and bismuth oxide (Bi2O3, JCPDS No. 96–901-2329) are detected. The diminished presence of magnesium phosphate phases at higher Bi precursor concentration suggests that the local plasma-electrolyte interactions and discharge chemistry are modified, favoring the incorporation and retention of Bi-containing phases over phosphate species.

The coexistence of metallic Bi and Bi2O3 within the PEO coating can be rationalized based on the inherently heterogeneous thermal and electrochemical environment associated with the PEO process. Sodium bismuthate introduces high-valent bismuth species into the electrolyte, which are transported toward the substrate under the applied electric field. During micro-discharge events, localized regions of extremely high temperature and transient reducing conditions are generated within discharge channels, enabling partial reduction of bismuth species and the formation of crystalline metallic Bi. Upon rapid cooling and exposure to oxygen-rich regions surrounding the discharge sites, a fraction of the incorporated bismuth undergoes oxidation, resulting in the formation of Bi2O3. The simultaneous presence of Bi and Bi2O3 therefore reflects the spatial variation in redox conditions across the PEO coating, where strongly reducing discharge cores and comparatively oxidizing peripheral regions coexist. The XRD results demonstrate that increasing sodium bismuthate concentration systematically alters the phase composition of PEO coatings on magnesium, transitioning from a predominantly magnesium oxide/phosphate matrix to a Bi-enriched composite structure. These phase-level changes provide a foundation for understanding the subsequent microstructural evolution and functional performance discussed in later sections.

Surface morphology

The surface morphologies of the PEO coatings formed with and without sodium bismuthate are presented in Fig. 2. The PMA coating (Fig. 2a) exhibits a relatively uniform surface characterized by shallow discharge pores and rounded microcavities, with an average pore diameter of approximately 5.2 ± 0.1 μm and a surface porosity of 3.2 ± 0.1%. Such morphological features are typical of phosphate-based PEO coatings formed under stable discharge conditions, where localized dielectric breakdown events generate discrete discharge channels that rapidly solidify into a ceramic matrix. The absence of large resolidified globules or secondary deposits indicates a comparatively uniform discharge regime in the absence of heavy-metal precursor species.

SEM micrographs at higher magnification of PEO-coated specimens: (a) PMA, (b) PBI1, and (c) PBI5. SEM images with corresponding EDS elemental maps of Mg, O, P, and Bi for (d) PBI1, (e) PBI5 and EDS spectrum and chemical composition of (f) PBI1 and (g) PBI5.

The incorporation of bismuth through sodium bismuthate addition results in pronounced changes in coating morphology. For the PBI1 coating (Fig. 2b, d), the surface displays a substantially higher density of discharge craters, accompanied by an increase in average pore size to approximately 7.3 ± 0.2 μm and a corresponding increase in surface porosity to 16.5 ± 0.3%. The pore edges appear irregular and locally thickened, suggesting intensified melting and resolidification within discharge channels. Numerous bright particulate features are observed across the surface, particularly along pore rims and crater boundaries. EDS elemental mapping confirms that these particulates are enriched in bismuth, while the concurrent detection of oxygen within these regions, together with the XRD results, indicates the presence of both metallic Bi and Bi2O3 within the coating.

At higher sodium bismuthate concentration, the PBI5 coating (Fig. 2c, e) exhibits a further evolution in surface morphology. EDS mapping reveals a more intense and uniformly distributed Bi signal across the surface compared to PBI1, reflecting a higher density and broader dispersion of Bi-containing features. This spatial distribution is consistent with the stronger Bi and Bi2O3 reflections observed in the XRD patterns of PBI5. It is noted that Na and K species originating from the electrolyte were not detected in the EDS spectra or elemental maps, indicating negligible incorporation into the PEO coatings. Also, the EDS spectrum presented in Fig. 2f, g is displayed up to ~ 10.5 keV for clarity, as no significant peaks were observed at higher energies. The coating surface appears more consolidated, with interconnected pore walls, smoother crater boundaries, and a more continuous ceramic-metal network. Although the average pore diameter remains comparable to that of PBI1 (approximately 7.5 ± 0.2 μm), the overall surface porosity decreases to 7.3 ± 0.2%, indicating that a larger fraction of discharge channels undergoes partial filling or sealing during coating growth, as further confirmed by cross-sectional SEM analysis (Fig. 3).

Cross-sectional SEM images and 3D surface roughness profiles of (a) PMA, (b) PBI1, and (c) PBI5.

From a microstructural perspective, the evolution from PMA to PBI1 and subsequently to PBI5 highlights the role of Bi-containing species in modifying discharge-driven coating growth. The accumulation of Bi-rich features along pore boundaries and within discharge channels alters the connectivity and continuity of surface porosity, resulting in a more compact morphology at higher precursor concentration. These morphological and cross-sectional characteristics collectively influence electrolyte transport pathways and interfacial exposure, thereby governing the corrosion behavior discussed in subsequent sections.

Cross-sectional SEM analysis (Fig. 3) provides further insight into the structural evolution of the coatings with bismuth incorporation. The PMA coating exhibits a relatively thin and uniform layer with an average thickness of ~ 13.6 ± 0.4 μm. In contrast, the incorporation of bismuth significantly increases the coating thickness to ~ 30.3 ± 0.6 μm for PBI1 and ~ 31.9 μm for PBI5. This increase in thickness can be attributed to enhanced plasma discharge activity and increased material deposition in the presence of bismuth species. Surface roughness measurements reveal a similar trend, with the average roughness (Ra) increasing from 13.11 ± 0.4 μm for PMA to 16.56 μm for PBI1 and 19.35 μm for PBI5. The increase in roughness is consistent with the formation of nodular and resolidified features associated with intensified discharge events and the incorporation of Bi-containing phases. Notably, despite the higher thickness and roughness, the PBI5 coating exhibits a comparatively denser cross-sectional morphology with reduced through-thickness porosity, consistent with partial filling or sealing of discharge channels. This observation supports the formation of a more compact barrier layer, which is expected to limit electrolyte penetration and enhance corrosion resistance.

Wettability and surface free energy

The wettability behavior of the PEO coatings was evaluated using static water contact angle measurements, and the corresponding surface free energy values were estimated using the Neumann equation of state (Fig. 4). Wettability provides insight into the interaction of coated surfaces with aqueous environments and physiological fluids, which can influence interfacial hydration, protein adsorption, and early-stage surface-liquid interactions.

The PMA coating exhibited a contact angle of 68.5°, corresponding to a surface energy of 42.6 mJ m− 2, indicating moderately hydrophobic behavior. This response is consistent with its relatively low surface porosity (3.2 ± 0.1%) and a surface chemistry dominated by MgO and Mg3(PO4)2, which typically exhibit limited surface polarity. The presence of predominantly closed and shallow pores, as observed in SEM, can restrict liquid penetration and limit spreading of the water droplet. With the incorporation of bismuth, the PBI1 coating displayed a reduced contact angle of 64.1° and an increased surface energy of 45.3 mJ/m2, indicating enhanced wettability. This change corresponds to the more open microstructure (surface porosity of 16.5 ± 0.3%) and the appearance of Bi-rich particulates at pore rims. The presence of metallic and oxide Bi phases likely introduces chemically heterogeneous regions and higher-energy surface sites, which can facilitate improved solid-liquid interactions.

The most pronounced hydrophilic behavior was observed for the PBI5 coating, which exhibited a contact angle of 40.1° and the highest surface energy (59.3 mJ/m2). The increased γs is consistent with the dense and more uniformly distributed Bi-containing domains observed in SEM-EDS and the coexistence of Bi and Bi2O3 phases identified by XRD. In addition, partial filling and sealing of discharge channels in PBI5 produce a more continuous solid-liquid interface, allowing for rapid wetting and spreading of the water droplet. The adhesion strength of the PEO coatings was evaluated using pull-off testing. All coatings exhibited high interfacial strength, sustaining stresses of approximately 40 MPa irrespective of electrolyte composition. In all cases, failure occurred at the resin-coating interface rather than at the coating-substrate interface, indicating that the adhesive strength of the epoxy was lower than the intrinsic bonding strength of the PEO layer to the magnesium substrate. This behavior reflects the conversion-type growth mechanism inherent to plasma electrolytic oxidation, in which the ceramic coating forms through partial oxidation and incorporation of the substrate material, resulting in strong mechanical interlocking and chemical continuity at the coating-substrate interface. The consistently high adhesion observed across all specimens indicates that bismuth incorporation does not compromise the interfacial integrity of the PEO coatings.

The PMA coating exhibits the most negative corrosion potential (Ecorr = − 0.423 ± 0.008 V) and the highest corrosion current density (icorr = 22.5 ± 1.300 × 10⁻⁷ A/cm2), indicating limited barrier effectiveness against electrochemical attack. Although SEM analysis revealed a relatively low surface porosity (3.2 ± 0.1%) and smaller discharge pores (average diameter ≈ 5.2 ± 0.1 μm), the coating primarily consists of MgO and Mg3(PO4)2 phases without secondary pore-filling constituents. As a result, interconnected discharge channels remain available for electrolyte penetration, facilitating localized anodic dissolution of the magnesium substrate.

Incorporation of bismuth at a lower precursor concentration (PBI1) result in a notable improvement in corrosion behavior, with a positive shift in Ecorr to −0.329 ± 0.006 V and a reduction in icorr to 8.06 ± 0.700 × 10− 7A/cm2. Despite the higher surface porosity (16.5 ± 0.3%) and larger pore size (≈ 7.3 ± 0.2 μm) observed in SEM, the presence of Bi-rich deposits along pore boundaries introduces additional resistance to electrolyte transport. XRD and EDS analyses confirmed that these deposits consist of a combination of metallic Bi and Bi2O3 phases. The incorporation of chemically stable Bi2O3 and metallic Bi domains modifies the electrochemical response by reducing the effective active area exposed to the electrolyte and increasing interfacial impedance, as reflected by the increased polarization resistance (Rp = 1.5 ± 0.08 × 105 Ω). The higher cathodic Tafel slope (βc = 0.164 ± 0.010 V/dec) further suggests a change in cathodic reaction characteristics in the presence of Bi-containing phases.

The most pronounced improvement is observed for the PBI5 coating, where Ecorr shifts further in the noble direction to −0.169 ± 0.005 V and icorr decreases to 1.17 ± 0.090 × 10− 7 A/cm2, corresponding to nearly one order of magnitude reduction compared to PMA. The polarization curve of PBI5 exhibits an extended passive-like region, indicative of a more stable electrochemical response. SEM observations revealed a more compact and interconnected surface morphology for PBI5, with partial sealing of discharge channels and a more uniform distribution of Bi-containing phases, which is consistent with the higher polarization resistance (Rp = 3.7 ± 0.150 × 105 Ω). This behavior is further supported by the increased coating thickness and reduced through-thickness porosity observed in cross-sectional analysis, which limit electrolyte penetration.

Across the investigated coatings, the progressive reduction in corrosion current density with increasing bismuth content reflects the combined influence of phase composition and surface morphology. The formation of a multiphase ceramic-metal composite incorporating MgO, Mg3(PO4)2, metallic Bi, and Bi2O3 modifies electrolyte access pathways and electrochemical activity at the coating and electrolyte interface, leading to a more stable corrosion response under simulated physiological conditions.

Biodegradation and alkalization

The long-term degradation behavior of the PEO coatings was evaluated through immersion in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and the corresponding corrosion rates and pH evolution profiles are presented in Fig. 6. Distinct differences in dissolution behavior are observed among the coatings as a function of bismuth incorporation.

(a) Corrosion rates of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings after 28 days of immersion in DMEM. The dashed line represents an indicative degradation threshold reported in literature for Mg-based systems under in vitro conditions22. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3), and letters (a-c) indicate statistically significant differences (p < 0.05); (b) corresponding pH evolution profiles illustrating alkalization behavior during long-term immersion.

The PMA coating exhibits the highest degradation rate, reaching 1.52 mm/year after 28 days of immersion. This behavior reflects the limited long-term barrier effectiveness of the MgO-Mg3(PO4)2 matrix in the absence of secondary pore-filling phases. As observed in SEM, the presence of open and interconnected discharge channels facilitates sustained electrolyte ingress, resulting in continuous magnesium dissolution during immersion. This trend is consistent with the higher corrosion current density measured electrochemically.

In contrast, the Bi-containing coatings show a progressive reduction in degradation rate, with PBI1 exhibiting a moderate decrease (1.41 mm/year) and PBI5 demonstrating the lowest dissolution rate (0.56 mm/year). This reduction correlates with the microstructural evolution identified earlier, where Bi-rich phases partially fill discharge channels and reduce the connectivity of electrolyte transport pathways. XRD confirmed that bismuth is present in the coating as both metallic Bi and Bi2O3. These phases contribute differently to the degradation response. Metallic Bi can occupy and partially block discharge channels, thereby reducing direct electrolyte access to the underlying magnesium substrate. In parallel, Bi2O3, owing to its chemical stability and low aqueous solubility, forms inert regions within the coating that limit ion transport and slow dissolution during prolonged immersion. The combined presence of these Bi-containing phases therefore contributes to reduced coating permeability and more regulated degradation behavior, as reflected in the lower corrosion rates observed for PBI1 and particularly PBI5.

The pH evolution during immersion mirrors the observed degradation trends. All coatings exhibit an initial rise in pH associated with magnesium dissolution and the formation of Mg(OH)2. However, the extent and rate of alkalization vary substantially. The PMA coating shows a rapid and sustained increase in pH, reaching approximately 9.9 by Day 28, indicative of accelerated Mg2+ release and limited buffering by the coating layer. Such pronounced alkalization reflects insufficient control of interfacial reactions under prolonged exposure.

The Bi-modified coatings display more moderated pH profiles. PBI1 exhibits a reduced rate of alkalization compared to PMA, consistent with partial obstruction of dissolution pathways by Bi-containing deposits. The most controlled pH evolution is observed for PBI5, where the pH increases gradually and stabilizes at a lower value (~ 9.1 at Day 28). This behaviour is consistent with the presence of a denser coating morphology and a higher fraction of chemically inert Bi2O3 phases, which reduce the effective dissolution area and slow the release of Mg2+ ions. In addition, the incorporation of stable oxide phases is known to promote the formation of more cohesive and less permeable corrosion product layers during immersion, thereby limiting further dissolution under physiological ionic conditions.

Cytocompatibility behavior

The cytocompatibility of the PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings was evaluated by culturing MG63 osteoblast-like cells in their respective extracts at dilution ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:4 over 1, 4, and 7 days (Fig. 7). For all specimens and extract concentrations, cell viability remained above the ISO 10993-523 cytotoxicity threshold of 70%, confirming the non-cytotoxic nature of the coatings. However, a clear and progressive improvement in cell response was observed with increasing bismuth incorporation.

Cell viability of MG63 cells cultured with extracts of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings at three dilution ratios (1:1, 1:2, and 1:4) over Days 1, 4, and 7. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 compared to the control group at the corresponding dilution and time point. Bars without asterisks represent non-significance. The dashed line at 70% represents the cytotoxicity threshold according to ISO 10993-5.

At Day 1, the PMA extracts showed a noticeable reduction in cell viability, particularly at the 1:1 dilution, where values decreased to approximately 80%. In comparison, PBI1 and PBI5 extracts maintained higher viability, ranging from ~ 75–90% depending on dilution. With prolonged incubation, the differences became more pronounced. By Day 4, PBI5 exhibited substantially higher viability values (~ 90–100%) across all dilutions compared with PMA and PBI1. This trend further intensified by Day 7, where PBI5 consistently achieved viability values exceeding 100% relative to the control, indicating enhanced cell proliferation rather than simple cytoprotection. In contrast, PMA extracts continued to show the lowest viability across all time points and dilution ratios.

The observed trends in cell viability correlate with the degradation and alkalization behavior discussed earlier. PMA extracts are associated with higher Mg2+ release and more pronounced pH elevation, which can impose ionic and alkaline stress on osteoblasts. In contrast, Bi-containing coatings, particularly PBI5, exhibit reduced degradation rates and moderated alkalization, resulting in extract environments that more closely approximate physiological conditions and are therefore more conducive to sustained cell growth.

The optical microscopy images (Fig. 8) further support the quantitative viability results. At Day 1, cells cultured in PMA and PBI1 extracts exhibited normal attachment but limited spreading, with a higher fraction of rounded or partially spread cells. In contrast, cells exposed to PBI5 extracts displayed more elongated and spindle-shaped morphologies, comparable to the control group, indicating improved cytoskeletal organization even at early time points. By Day 4, PBI5-treated cells showed increased cell density, pronounced elongation, and emerging intercellular networks, whereas PBI1 showed moderate spreading and PMA exhibited comparatively sparse cell distribution. By Day 7, cultures treated with PBI5 extracts formed dense and well-interconnected cell layers, closely resembling the control, while PMA-treated cells displayed the lowest confluence, consistent with the reduced viability values.

Optical microscopy images of MG63 cells cultured in 1:2 diluted extracts of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings compared with the control at Days 1, 4, and 7.

The improved cytocompatibility observed for PBI5 can be rationalized based on its controlled extract chemistry arising from the presence of both metallic Bi and Bi2O3 within the coating. These phases reduce excessive Mg dissolution and limit abrupt pH elevation, thereby preserving a biochemical environment that supports osteoblast attachment and proliferation. Although extract-based assays do not directly probe surface-mediated cell interactions, the enhanced hydrophilicity associated with Bi incorporation may further influence ion-protein interactions during immersion, indirectly contributing to the improved cellular response observed in the extracts.

Cell adhesion and apoptosis analysis

The AO/EtBr fluorescence assay was employed to evaluate cell adhesion and apoptotic response of MG63 osteoblast-like cells cultured directly on the coated surfaces. While extract-based assays provide insight into degradation-mediated cytocompatibility, direct-contact evaluation enables a more realistic assessment of how surface chemistry, morphology, wettability, and localized degradation influence cell behavior at the material interface.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 9a) indicates that all coatings support a high proportion of viable cells (> 80%), confirming the absence of acute cytotoxic effects under direct-contact conditions. Nevertheless, clear differences in apoptotic response are observed among the specimens. The PMA coating exhibits the highest apoptotic fraction (~ 16%), whereas PBI1 shows a reduced value (~ 14%). The lowest apoptotic response is observed for PBI5, with apoptotic cells accounting for approximately ~ 10% of the total population. These quantitative trends are consistent with the qualitative fluorescence observations presented in Fig. 9b.

AO/EtBr fluorescence analysis of MG63 cells cultured directly on coated surfaces. (a) Quantitative assessment of live and apoptotic cells, presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3); different letters above the live cell bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). (b) Representative fluorescence micrographs illustrating cell adhesion, morphology, and apoptotic response for Control, PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 groups, where live cells appear green and apoptotic cells appear orange/red.

On the PMA surface, a noticeable number of yellow-orange nuclei indicative of early apoptosis, along with occasional red-stained cells corresponding to late apoptotic or necrotic states, are observed. In contrast, cells cultured on PBI1 display predominantly green fluorescence with fewer apoptotic nuclei and more elongated morphologies, suggesting improved cell adhesion and reduced cellular stress. The PBI5 surface supports the formation of dense, well-organized cell layers composed almost exclusively of green-fluorescent cells, closely resembling the healthy control, with minimal evidence of apoptotic staining.

The observed differences in apoptotic response can be discussed in relation to the combined effects of degradation behavior, local alkalization, surface wettability, and microstructural features established in the preceding sections. The PMA coating, which exhibits the highest corrosion rate and most pronounced pH increase during immersion, is expected to generate a more aggressive local ionic environment at the cell-material interface, which can compromise membrane integrity and increase cellular stress. Additionally, the relatively higher contact angle and lower surface energy of PMA may limit initial surface conditioning by adsorbed proteins, thereby weakening cell-surface interactions.

Incorporation of bismuth in PBI1 modifies both the surface chemistry and microstructure, leading to improved wettability and partial obstruction of dissolution pathways. These changes are associated with a reduced apoptotic fraction and more homogeneous cell morphology compared to PMA. The most favorable response is observed on PBI5, which combines the lowest corrosion rate, the most controlled alkalization behavior, and the highest surface hydrophilicity. The dense and uniform distribution of Bi and Bi2O3 phases results in a more stable interfacial environment with reduced ionic fluctuations, supporting sustained cell adhesion and minimizing apoptosis under direct-contact conditions.

Osteogenic differentiation

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was quantified to assess early-stage osteogenic differentiation of MG63 osteoblast-like cells cultured with extracts of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings (Fig. 10a). As expected for an early osteogenic marker, ALP activity increased progressively from Day 1 to Day 7 for all experimental groups. However, a clear dependence on coating composition was observed, with PBI5 consistently exhibiting the highest ALP activity at all time points, followed by PBI1, while PMA showed the lowest values.

(a) Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and (b) Alizarin Red S (ARS) quantification of MG63 cells cultured in extracts of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings at different time points. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Different letters indicate statistically significant differences between groups at the final time point (p < 0.05), demonstrating enhanced early osteogenic differentiation and mineralization with increasing Bi incorporation.

On Day 1, ALP activity for PBI5 was already higher than that of PMA and PBI1, indicating an earlier onset of osteogenic response. This difference became more pronounced at later time points. By Day 7, ALP activity for PBI5 approached ~ 0.95 U L− 1, compared to ~ 0.92 U L− 1 for PBI1 and ~ 0.90 U L− 1 for PMA, demonstrating a sustained enhancement in early osteogenic differentiation with increasing bismuth incorporation.

The observed increase in ALP activity with Bi-containing coatings can be interpreted in relation to the physicochemical characteristics established in earlier sections. Extracts derived from PMA are associated with higher degradation rates and more pronounced alkalization, conditions that can disrupt cellular homeostasis and suppress differentiation-related enzyme expression. In contrast, the reduced corrosion rate and moderated pH evolution observed for Bi-modified coatings, particularly PBI5, result in a more stable ionic environment that is conducive to early osteogenic activity.

Surface-related effects may also contribute indirectly. PBI5 exhibits substantially higher surface hydrophilicity and surface free energy compared to PMA, characteristics that are known to favor the adsorption of serum proteins involved in osteogenic regulation. Although extract-based assays do not directly probe surface-mediated cell-material interactions, the improved wettability of Bi-containing coatings may influence the composition and conformation of proteins present in the extract medium, thereby supporting enhanced ALP expression.

PBI1 demonstrates intermediate ALP activity, consistent with its moderate improvements in surface hydrophilicity, partial Bi/Bi2O3 incorporation, and less controlled degradation behavior relative to PBI5. Conversely, the comparatively lower ALP activity observed for PMA reflects its higher dissolution tendency and less favorable extract chemistry.

Osteogenic mineralization

The mineralization potential of MG63 osteoblast-like cells cultured with extracts of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings was assessed using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining (Figs. 10b and 11). ARS selectively binds to calcium-rich extracellular matrix deposits and is therefore widely used as an indicator of late-stage osteogenic differentiation. Both quantitative ARS measurements and qualitative staining images reveal a strong dependence of mineralization behavior on the extent of Bi incorporation within the coatings.

Representative Alizarin Red S-stained micrographs of MG63 cells cultured in PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 extract media at 7, 14, and 28 days. The red staining indicates calcium-rich mineralized nodules.

At all measured time points, PBI5 exhibited the highest ARS values, followed by PBI1, while PMA consistently showed the lowest mineral deposition. Quantitative ARS concentration for PBI5 increased markedly with culture time, reaching significantly higher values than both the control and other coated specimens by Day 7 and continuing to increase through Days 14 and 28. The corresponding ARS-stained micrographs corroborate this trend. As early as Day 7, cultures treated with PBI5 extracts display numerous, well-defined mineralized regions. By Days 14 and 28, these deposits evolve into extensive and intensely stained nodules distributed uniformly across the culture surface. In comparison, PBI1 shows moderate but clearly enhanced mineral formation, while PMA exhibits sparse, weakly stained deposits with delayed nodule development.

The enhanced mineralization observed for Bi-containing coatings can be discussed in relation to the physicochemical characteristics established in earlier sections. PBI5 exhibits the lowest degradation rate and the reduced alkalization behavior during immersion, resulting in extract environments that better preserve calcium-phosphate balance during long-term culture. Excessive Mg2+ release and strong alkalization, as observed for PMA, are known to interfere with mineral deposition by disrupting extracellular ionic equilibrium and inhibiting calcium phosphate nucleation. In contrast, the more stable ionic conditions associated with Bi-modified coatings support sustained extracellular matrix maturation and mineral accumulation.

Surface-related effects may also contribute indirectly. The substantially higher surface hydrophilicity and surface free energy of PBI5, compared to PMA and PBI1, are known to influence protein adsorption and organization during the initial conditioning of the extract medium. Although ARS assays do not directly probe surface-mediated cell-material interactions, such differences in extract conditioning can affect the availability and conformation of matrix-associated proteins that serve as nucleation sites for mineral deposition.

The mineralization trends observed here are consistent with the ALP activity results discussed previously. PBI5 exhibited higher ALP activity at earlier time points, indicating enhanced early-stage osteogenic activity, which is reflected at later stages by increased extracellular matrix mineralization. PBI1 showed intermediate behavior in both ALP activity and ARS staining, whereas PMA demonstrated the weakest response across both early and late differentiation markers.

Antibacterial studies

The antibacterial performance of PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings was evaluated against Escherichia coli (Gram-negative) and Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) using quantitative inhibition assays and AO/EtBr fluorescence imaging (Figs. 12 and 13). A clear enhancement in antibacterial efficacy was observed with increasing bismuth incorporation.

Percentage inhibition of E. coli and S. aureus following exposure to extracts from PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 coatings. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Different letters indicate statistically significant differences between groups within each bacterial strain (p < 0.05), demonstrating enhanced antibacterial activity with increasing Bi incorporation.

AO/EtBr fluorescence images showing live (green) and dead (red-orange) bacteria after exposure to PMA, PBI1, and PBI5 extracts.

Quantitative inhibition results (Fig. 12) show that PMA exhibits moderate antibacterial activity, achieving approximately ~ 66% inhibition against E. coli and ~ 72% against S. aureus. This response is consistent with the alkaline environment and elevated Mg2+ concentration generated during degradation of Mg-based coatings, which can impair bacterial growth but typically do not result in complete bactericidal effects. Incorporation of Bi leads to a measurable increase in antibacterial performance. PBI1 achieves inhibition levels of ~ 70% for E. coli and ~ 75% for S. aureus, while PBI5 exhibits the highest antibacterial activity, reaching ~ 80% and ~ 85%, respectively. The progressive increase in inhibition confirms a concentration-dependent antibacterial contribution associated with Bi incorporation.

The AO/EtBr fluorescence images (Fig. 13) qualitatively support the quantitative inhibition results. Control groups display dense green fluorescence, characteristic of viable bacterial populations. Bacteria exposed to PMA extracts show mixed green and orange fluorescence, indicating partial membrane compromise and reduced viability. In contrast, PBI1-treated specimens exhibit a higher fraction of orange/red fluorescence, reflecting increased membrane damage and cell death. The most pronounced bactericidal effect is observed for PBI5, where bacteria appear predominantly red/orange with markedly reduced surface coverage, consistent with extensive loss of membrane integrity.

The enhanced antibacterial response of Bi-containing coatings can be discussed in relation to their compositional and physicochemical characteristics established earlier. XRD and SEM-EDS analyses confirmed the presence and uniform distribution of metallic Bi and Bi2O3 phases within the PEO matrix, particularly for PBI5. Bismuth-based materials are widely reported to exhibit antibacterial activity through interactions with bacterial cell envelopes and membrane-associated processes, leading to compromised membrane integrity and reduced viability. The higher inhibition observed against S. aureus relative to E. coli is consistent with differences in bacterial cell wall structure, where the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria may be more susceptible to disruption by metal-based antibacterial agents.

In addition to chemical composition, surface-related factors may contribute indirectly. PBI5 exhibits the highest surface hydrophilicity and surface energy among the coatings, which can influence bacterial adhesion and the local interaction between antibacterial species and bacterial cells. Furthermore, the reduced corrosion rate and moderate alkalization behavior of Bi-modified coatings provide a more stable interfacial environment that supports sustained antibacterial activity without excessive Mg-driven effects.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that incorporating sodium bismuthate into the electrolyte during plasma electrolytic oxidation enables the formation of a Bi/Bi2O3-containing composite coating that significantly improves the functional performance of biodegradable magnesium alloys. The incorporation of Bi leads to the development of a compact and partially pore-sealed ceramic layer with uniformly distributed Bi-containing phases, while maintaining strong coating-substrate adhesion (~ 40 MPa).

The Bi-rich coating formed at higher precursor concentration (PBI5) exhibits markedly improved degradation behavior, with a corrosion rate reduced to 0.56 mm/year compared to 1.52 mm/year for the Bi-free coating. This improvement is accompanied by moderated alkalization during long-term immersion, with PBI5 stabilizing at a lower terminal pH (~ 9.1) after 28 days. Such control over degradation and interfacial chemistry is critical for maintaining a biologically compatible environment during early implant service.

Bi incorporation also enhances in vitro biological responses. PBI5 extracts support high MG63 cell viability (> 120%) with reduced apoptotic fraction (~ 12%), alongside improved early osteogenic activity, as indicated by elevated ALP expression. These early responses are reflected at later stages by substantially increased extracellular matrix mineralization, with ARS deposition higher than that of the Bi-free coating by Day 28. In parallel, Bi-modified coatings demonstrate enhanced antibacterial performance, achieving inhibition levels of approximately 80% against E. coli and 85% against S. aureus.

Overall, the integration of Bi into PEO coatings provides a multifunctional surface modification strategy that combines controlled degradation, improved cytocompatibility and osteogenic response, and effective antibacterial activity, highlighting its potential for application in biodegradable orthopedic implant systems.
