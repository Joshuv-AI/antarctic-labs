1.
Introduction

In 1926, Nevanlinna
[
20
]
established the value distribution theory for meromorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
by proving the second main theorem for meromorphic functions, with the targets being sets of values in
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}
. Subsequently, H. Cartan
[
8
]
extended Nevanlinna theory to holomorphic mappings into projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, with the targets being families of hyperplanes in general position. In 1985, by introducing the notion of Nochka weights for families of hyperplanes in subgeneral position, E.I. Nochka
[
21
]
generalized Cartan’s result to the case of hyperplanes in subgeneral position.

In recent years, Nevanlinna theory has been further developed for the case of meromorphic mappings where the targets are families of hypersurfaces. Most of the methods used to establish the second main theorem in Nevanlinna theory rely on complex analysis, complex geometry, and potential theory. This analytic approach, however, still is restricted to mappings defined on complex affine spaces.

In 1986, T. K. Carne
[
9
]
initiated the study of the relationship between Brownian motion and Nevanlinna theory. Later on, A. Atsuji, in a series of publications
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, applied Brownian motion and stochastic calculus to the study of Nevanlinna theory for holomorphic mappings on general Kähler manifolds. More recently, X. Dong and his collaborators
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
have carried out significant research on using stochastic calculus and Brownian motion to establish the second main theorem for mappings defined on spaces more general than complex affine spaces. Although their results have not yet reached the strength of those in the case of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, they nevertheless provide hope for a method that could generalize all known results in Nevanlinna theory for mappings defined on
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
to the setting of general Kähler manifolds.

Inspired by the work of X. Dong and his collaborators
[
13
]
, together with the notion of distributive constant of an arbitrary family of hypersurfaces introduced by the second author
[
25
]
, in this paper we will establish a new second main theorem based on Brownian motion and stochastic calculus for holomorphic curves from
{\mathbb{C}}
into projective subvarieties, with arbitrary families of hypersurfaces where the counting functions are truncated by a level sufficiently small to allow the application of the uniqueness problem for meromorphic mappings. To present the results of this paper, we first recall the main concepts of Nevanlinna theory and some of the recent developments in the field.

Let
f:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
be a holomorphic map with reduced representation
\tilde{f}=(f_{0},\cdots,f_{n}),
where
f_{0},\cdots,f_{n}
are holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
without common zeros. The characteristic function
T_{f}(r)
is defined by

T_{f}(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\|\tilde{f}(re^{i\theta})\|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi},

where
\|\tilde{f}\|=\sqrt{|f_{0}|^{2}+\cdots+|f_{n}|^{2}}
. This definition is independent, up to an additive constant, of the choice of the reduced representation
\tilde{f}
of
f.

Let
Q\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in variables
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
. The proximity function of
f
with respect to
Q
is defined by

m_{f}(r,Q)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\frac{\|\tilde{f}(re^{i\theta})\|^{d}\|Q\|}{|Q(\tilde{f})(re^{i\theta})|}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi},

where
\|Q\|
is the sum of the absolute values of all coefficients of
Q
. This definition is also independent, up to an additive constant, of the choice of the reduced representation of
f
.

Denote by
\nu_{Q(\tilde{f})}
the divisor of the holomorphic function
Q(\tilde{f})
on
{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
\nu_{Q(\tilde{f})}(z)
is the zero multiplicity of
Q(\tilde{f})
at
z
. For a positive integer
M
(
M
maybe
+\infty
), we define
n^{[M]}_{f}(r,Q)=\sum_{|z|<r}\min\{M,\nu_{Q(\tilde{f})}(z)\}
. The counting function of
f
with respect to
Q
is defined by

N^{[M]}_{f}(r,Q)=\int_{1}^{r}\frac{n^{[M]}_{f}(t,Q)-n^{[M]}_{f}(0,Q)}{t}dt+n^{[M]}_{f}(0,Q)\log r.

By the Jensen’s formula, we have

N_{f}(r,Q)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|Q(\tilde{f})(re^{i\theta})|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}+O(1).

Let
D\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a hypersurface of degree
d
, defined by a homogeneous polynomial
Q
of degree
d
. Define

m_{f}(r,D)=m_{f}(r,Q)\text{ and }N^{[M]}_{f}(r,D)=N^{[M]}_{f}(r,Q).

Thus, the first main theorem in Nevanlinna theory can be written as

dT_{f}(r)=m_{f}(r,D)+N_{f}(r,D)+O(1).

Let
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a projective subvariety of dimension
k
,
and let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}
(
q\geq k+1
) be hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})

such that
V\not\subset D_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq q
.
The hypersurfaces
D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}
are said to be in
N
-subgeneral position with respect to
V\ (k\leq N\leq q-1)
if

D_{j_{0}}\cap\cdots\cap D_{j_{N}}\cap V=\varnothing

for every
1\leq j_{0}<\cdots<j_{N}\leq q.
If
N=k
then we say that
D_{1},\cdots,D_{q}
are in general position with respect to
V
.

In 2004, M. Ru
[
28
]
established a second main theorem for algebraically non-degenerate holomorphic maps from
\mathbb{C}
into
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
with respect to families of hypersurfaces in general position. Later on, in 2009, Ru
[
29
]
generalized his result by considering the case of algebraically nondegenerate holomorphic maps into a projective subvariety
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
, as follows.

Theorem A
(see
[
29
, The main result]
)
Let
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a smooth projective subvariety of dimension
k\geq 1
,
and let
D_{1},\dots,D_{q}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be hypersurfaces in general position with respect to
V
. Let
f
be an algebraically nondegenerate holomorphic map from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
,

\bigl\|\ (q-n-1-\epsilon)T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\deg D_{i}}N_{f}(r,D_{i}).

Here, the notation
\|P
means that the assertion
P
holds for all
r\in(0,\infty)
outside a subset of finite linear measure.

Theorem A had been generalized and extended by many authors. We refer the reader to several important works
[
5
,
11
,
12
,
18
,
24
,
32
,
31
]
concerning the establishment of second main theorems in Nevanlinna theory. Recently, in
[
25
]
the author introduced the notion of the distributive constant of a family of hypersurfaces as follows.

Definition B
(see
[
25
, Definition 1.1]
).
Let
V
be a projective subvariety of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
of dimension
k
, and let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}
be
q
hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. The distributive constant of the family
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}\}
with respect to
V
is defined by

\Delta:=\underset{\varnothing\neq\Gamma\subset\{1,\ldots,q\}}{\max}\dfrac{\sharp\Gamma}{k-\dim\left(\bigcap_{j\in\Gamma}D_{j}\right)\cap V}.

Here, we note that
\dim\varnothing=-\infty
.

Using the notion of distributive constant, in
[
25
]
the second author proved the following second main theorem.

Theorem C
(see
[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
)
Let
V
be a smooth projective subvariety of dimension
k\geq 1
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Let
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}\}
be a family of hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
with distributive constant
\Delta
with respect to
V
and
\deg D_{i}=d_{i}\ (1\leq i\leq q)
. Let
f
be an algebraically nondegenerate holomorphic map from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
. Then, for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

\bigl\|\ \left(q-\Delta(k+1)-\epsilon\right)T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{d_{i}}N^{[M_{0}]}_{f}(r,D_{i}),

where
M_{0}=\left[d^{k^{2}+k}\deg(V)^{n+1}e^{k}\Delta^{k}(2k+4)^{k}(k+1)^{k}(q!)^{k}\epsilon^{-k}\right]
and
d
is the least common multiple of
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}
, i.e.,
d=lcm(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}).

However, in all the above mentioned second main theorems, the truncation levels for counting functions are disregarded or are very large. As a consequence, their effectiveness in applications to related problems, such as the unicity problem for holomorphic curves, is substantially constrained. Actually, the earliest second main theorem for holomorphic maps from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and a family of hypersurfaces with truncated counting functions (with a small enough truncation level) was given by D. P. An, S. D. Quang and D. D. Thai
[
6
]
in 2013. Later on, in
[
23
]
, Quang and An generalized that result by proving a second main theorem for a holomorphic curve from
{\mathbb{C}}
into a projective subvariety
V
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and a family of hypersurfaces in
N
-subgeneral position with respect to
V
. In order to state the result of Quang and An, we recall the following.

Let
V
be as above. We denote by
I(V)
the ideal of homogeneous polynomials in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
defining
V
and by
H_{d}
the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of all homogeneous polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},...,x_{n}]
of degree
d.
Define

I_{d}(V):=\dfrac{H_{d}}{I(V)\cap H_{d}}\text{ and }H_{V}(d):=\dim I_{d}(V).

Then
H_{V}(d)
is called the Hilbert function of
V
. Each element of
I_{d}(V)
which is an equivalence class of an element
Q\in H_{d},
will be denoted by
[Q]
,

For a holomorphic map
f
from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
, we say that
f
is degenerate over
I_{d}(V)
if there is
[Q]\in I_{d}(V)\setminus\{0\}
such that
Q(\tilde{f})\equiv 0
for a reduced representation
\tilde{f}
of
f
. Otherwise, we say that
f
is nondegenerate over
I_{d}(V)
. The result of Quang and An is stated as follows.

Theorem D
(see
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
).
Let
V
be a complex projective subvariety of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
of dimension
k\ (k\leq n)
. Let
\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{q}
be hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
in
N
-subgeneral position with respect to
V
with
\deg D_{j}=d_{j}\ (1\leq j\leq q)
. Let
d=lcm(d_{1},...,d_{q})
. Let
f
be a holomorphic map from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
which is nondegenerate over
I_{d}(V)
. Then, we have

\biggl\|\ \left(q-\dfrac{(2N-k+1)H_{V}(d)}{k+1}\right)T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\dfrac{1}{d_{j}}N^{[H_{V}(d)-1]}_{f}(r,D_{j})+o(T_{f}(r)).

Our aim in this paper is to establish a second main theorem for a holomorphic map from
{\mathbb{C}}
into a projective subvariety
V
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
with respect to an arbitrary family of hypersurfaces and its distributive constant. Our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V
be a projective subvariety of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}\}
be a family of
q
hypersurfaces of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
which has distributive constant
\Delta
with respect to
V
. Let
M=H_{V}(d)-1
and
d=lcm(\deg D_{1},\ldots,\deg D_{q})
. Let
f
be a holomorphic map from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
which is nondegenerate over
I_{d}(V)
. Then, for any positive
\epsilon
and
\delta
, we have

\|\,(q-\Delta(M+1+\epsilon))T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{d}N^{[M]}_{f}(r,D_{j})+\Delta\delta\log r+O(1).

Remark. Let
N=\ell k,q=\ell p\ (p\geq k+1)
and
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}\}
be a family of hypersurfaces with
D_{tp+s}\equiv D_{s}
for every
1\leq t\leq\ell-1,1\leq s\leq p
and
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{p}\}
being a family of hypersurfaces in general position w.r.t.
V
. In this case
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}\}
is in
(\ell k+1)
-subgeneral position w.r.t to
V
and has distributive constant
\Delta
w.r.t to
V
equal to
\ell
. Obviously that
\Delta=\ell\leq\frac{(2\ell k-k+1)}{k+1}
. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
is new and maybe better than Theorem D in some case.

As an application of Theorem
1.1
, the second aim of this paper is to give a uniqueness theorem for holomorphic maps of
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
sharing a few hypersurfaces without counting multiplicity. Our uniqueness theorem is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V
be a projective subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{q}
be a family of
q
hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the distributive constant
\Delta
with respect to
V
. Let
d=lcm(degD_{1},...,degD_{q})
. Let
f
and
g
be holomorphic maps from
{\mathbb{C}}
into
V
which are nondegenerate over
I_{d}(V)
. Assume that
f=g\text{ on }\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}(f^{-1}(D_{i})\cup g^{-1}(D_{i})).

(a)

If
q>\Delta\left(\frac{2k(H_{V}(d)-1)}{d}+H_{V}(d)\right),
then
f=g.

(b)

Suppose further that
f^{-1}(D_{i}\cap D_{j})=\varnothing
for every
1\leq i<j\leq q.
If
q>\frac{2(H_{V}(d)-1)}{d}+\Delta H_{V}(d),
then
f=g.

2.
Probability theory

We review certain notions and auxiliary results in stochastic calculus due to
[
13
]
.

2.1 Expectation of random variables

A probability space is a triple (
\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathrm{P}
), where
\Omega
is a non-empty set,
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
\Omega
and
P
is a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. A real (resp. complex) random variable
X
is a measurable function
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(resp.
\mathbb{C}
). The expectation of
X
is defined as

\mathrm{E}[X]:=\int_{\Omega}X(w)d\mathrm{P}(w).

Lemma 2.1

(see
[
7
, Proposition 1.3]
)
.

If
g
is convex and
X
and
g(X)
are integrable, where
X
is a random variable, then

g(\mathrm{E}[X])\leq\mathrm{E}[g(X)].

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
be a sub
\sigma
-algebra. The conditional expectation, denoted by
\mathrm{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]
, is an random variable
Y
such that
Y
is
\mathcal{G}
-measurable and
\int_{A}Yd\mathrm{P}=\int_{A}Xd\mathrm{P}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{E}[Y;A]=\mathrm{E}[X;A]
, for any
A\in\mathcal{G}
.

2.2 Stochastic process and stopping times

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. A
filtration

\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is a family of sub-
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{F}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, indexed by
I
(typically
I=\mathbb{R}^{+}
or
I=\mathbb{N}
), such that
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}
whenever
s<t
. In this paper, we always assume that the filtration is
right-continuous
, meaning

\mathcal{F}_{t+}:=\bigcap_{s>t}\mathcal{F}_{s}=\mathcal{F}_{t},\quad\forall\,t\in I.

A
stochastic process
is a family of random variables
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in I}
. The process is called
discrete-time
if
I=\mathbb{N}
, and
continuous-time
if
I=\mathbb{R}^{+}
and, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the sample path

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\ (\text{or }\in{\mathbb{C}})

is continuous.

The process is said to be
adapted
to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in I}
if
X_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for each
t\in I
.
In this paper, we only consider continuous processes. A nonnegative random variable
T
is called a
stopping time
with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
if

\{T\geq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},\quad\forall\,t\in I.

For a process
\{X_{t}\}
and a stopping time
T
, we define a random variable

X_{T}(\omega):=X_{T(\omega)}(\omega),\quad\omega\in\{T<\infty\}.

2.3 Martingales and Brownian motion

A martingale is a stochastic process
\{M_{t}\}_{t\in I}
that is adapted to a given filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
and satisfies

\mathbb{E}[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]=M_{s},\quad\forall\,t\geq s,\;t,s\in I.

Definition 2.2

(Brownian motion)
.

A Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a stochastic process
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies the following properties

(i)

For any
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
, the random variables
B_{t_{1}},B_{t_{2}}-B_{t_{1}}
,
\ldots,B_{t_{n}}-B_{t_{n-1}}
are independent.

(ii)

For any
t>s\geq 0
, the random variables
B_{t}-B_{s}
satisfies the normal distribution
N(0,t-s)
, i.e.,
\mathrm{P}\left\{\left(B_{t}-B_{s}\right)\in dV(x)\right\}=p(t-s,x)dV(x)
, where
p(t,x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2t}\right)
and
dV
is an infinitesimal volume element for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(iii)

B_{t}(w)
is continuous in
t
for a.s.
w\in\Omega
.

As a stochastic process, the Brownian motion is a martingale. According to
[
7
]
, the Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{C}}
always exists.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{C}}
started at
0
. For
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
, denote by
\mathrm{P}^{x}
and
E_{x}
the probability and expectation associated to the Brownian motion
\{(X_{t}+x)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Let
g_{r}(0,z)
be the Green function of the disc
D_{r}:=\{z|\ |z|<r\}
with the Dirichlet boundary condition. By
[
19
, Subsection 7.4]
, we have

(1)

\displaystyle g_{r}(0,z)dV(z)=\frac{1}{2}E_{0}\left(\text{times }X_{t}\text{ spends in }dV(z)\text{ before }\tau_{r}\right)

for an infinitesimal area
dV(z)
about
z\in D_{r}
, where
\tau_{r}=\inf\left\{t>0\mid X_{t}\notin D_{r}\right\}
is the stopping time. Thus, for any bounded continuous function
\psi
on
D_{r}
, we have the co-area formula as follows

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}\psi\left(X_{t}\right)dt\right]=\int_{D_{r}}g_{r}(0,y)\psi(y)dV(y).

Lemma 2.3

(see
[
13
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

Let
u
be a function on
\mathbb{C}
with mild singularities. Assume that
x
is not a singular point of
\log|u|
. Then, for any stopping time
T
such that

\left.\mathrm{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(\Delta\log|u|)\left(X_{s}\right)\right)ds\right]<\infty

we have

\mathrm{E}_{x}\left[\log|u|\left(X_{T}\right)\right]-\log|u|(x)+N_{x}(T,\log|u|)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T}(\Delta\log|u|)\left(X_{s}\right)ds\right],

where

N_{x}(T,v):=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\mathrm{P}^{x}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}v^{+}\left(X_{s}\right)>\lambda\right)-\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\mathrm{P}^{x}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}v^{-}\left(X_{s}\right)>\lambda\right)

with
v^{+}=\max\{0,v\}
and
v^{-}=-\min\{0,v\}
. In particular, if
T=\tau_{r}
and
x=0
, then

\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[\log|u|\left(X_{\tau_{r}}\right)\right]-\log|u|(0)+N_{u}(r,\infty)-N_{u}(r,0)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}(\Delta\log|u|)\left(X_{s}\right)ds\right].

Lemma 2.4

(see
[
13
, Lemma 3.5]
)
.

Let
u
be a non-negative, locally integrable function on
D
. Assume
u
is bounded on a neighborhood of the origin. Let
\tau_{r}:=\inf\left\{t>0,\left|X_{t}\right|\geq r\right\}
be the stopping time. Then, for any
\delta>0
,

\|\,\log\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[u\left(X_{\tau_{r}}\right)\right]\leqslant(1+\delta)^{2}\log\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}u\left(X_{s}\right)ds\right]+\delta\log r.

3.
The theory for holomorphic curves

We will follow the methods presented in
[
13
]
and
[
26
]
to establish the sum into product inequality (see Theorem
3.5
) and then prove a second main theorem for holomorphic curves and hypersurfaces concerning the Wronskian of the curves (see Theorem
3.7
).

3.1 Associated curves

Let
f:{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a linearly nondegenerate holomorphic map with a reduced representation
\tilde{f}=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
.
Consider the holomorphic maps
\tilde{F}_{k}
defined by

\tilde{F}_{k}=\tilde{f}\wedge\tilde{f}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{f}^{(k)}:\mathbb{C}\to\bigwedge^{k+1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}.

Since
f
is linearly nondegenerate,
\tilde{F}_{k}\neq 0
for
0\leq k\leq n
.
We define the
k
-th associated map by

F_{k}=P(\tilde{F}_{k}):\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{k+1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})=\mathbb{P}^{N}(\mathbb{C}),

where
N=\binom{n+1}{k+1}-1
and
P
is the natural projection.

The
k
-th characteristic function is defined by

T_{F_{k}}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dt}{t}\int_{|z|<t}F_{k}^{*}\omega,

where
\omega
is the Fubini-Study form on
\mathbb{P}^{N}(\mathbb{C})
, and

T_{f}(r)=T_{F_{0}}(r).

Lemma 3.1

3.3 Second main theorem concerning the Wronskian

We recall the following formulas in Nevanlinna theory. For a homogeneous polynomial
Q
in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the proximity function of
f
with respect to
Q
is given by

m_{f}(r,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\varphi_{0}(Q)(re^{i\theta})\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

The height function of
F
is defined by

T_{F_{k}}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dt}{t}\int_{|z|<t}F_{k}^{*}\omega=\int_{|z|<r}\log\frac{r}{|z|}F_{k}^{*}\omega.

Let
X_{t}
be a Brownian motion on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Let
\tau_{r}:=\inf\{t>0,\,|X_{t}|\geq r\}
be the stopping time. From the inequality (
1
) with
D_{r}=\{z\mid|z|<r\}
, we have

m_{f}(r,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{0}[\log\varphi_{0}(Q)(X_{\tau_{r}})],

and from the inequality (
2
) with

, we have

T_{F_{k}}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{r}}e_{k}(f)(X_{s})\,ds\right],

where

and

.

(Second main theorem with counting function of Wronskian)

f

{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\frac{M(M+1)}{4}\mathbb{E}_{0}[\log{h}^{*}(X_{\tau_{r}})]

2.3

a_{0}\geq a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n-1}\geq 1
, we have

a_{0}^{t_{1}-t_{0}}a_{1}^{t_{2}-t_{1}}\cdots a_{n-1}^{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}\leq(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1})^{\Delta}.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{q}
be the homogeneous polynomials defining
D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}
respectively. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\deg Q_{j}=d
for all
1\leq j\leq q
. Using the notation in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, we have

(4)

\displaystyle\|\,(q-\Delta(M+1+\epsilon))T_{f}(r)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{d}N_{f}(r,Q_{j})-\frac{\Delta}{d}N_{W}(r,0)+\Delta\delta\log r+O(1),

where
M=H_{V}(d)-1.
Let
\tilde{f}=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
be a reduced representation of
f
.We now establish the following inequality for the counting functions of divisors:

\sum_{i=1}^{q}N_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)-\Delta N_{W}(r)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[M]}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(r).

Indeed, let
z
be a zero of some
Q_{i}(\tilde{f})
. We may assume that

\nu^{0}_{Q_{1}(\tilde{f})}(z)\geq\nu^{0}_{Q_{2}(\tilde{f})}(z)\geq\ldots\geq\nu^{0}_{Q_{\ell}(\tilde{f})}(z)>0=\nu^{0}_{Q_{\ell+1}(\tilde{f})}(z)=\cdots=\nu^{0}_{Q_{q}(\tilde{f})}(z).

Note that if
q>\Delta(M+1)
then
\frac{\ell}{k}\leq\Delta<\frac{q}{M+1},
i.e.,
\ell<\frac{kq}{M+1}\leq q-1.
Then, there are indices
i_{0},\ldots,i_{p},i_{p+1}
such that
1=i_{0}<i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq\ell<i_{p+1}
and

\mathrm{rank}\{Q_{i};1\leq i\leq t\}=s\ \forall\ i_{s}\leq t\leq i_{s+1}-1,\,0\leq s\leq p.

Since
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{q}
has distribution constant
\Delta
, we have

\Delta\geq\frac{i_{s}-1}{k-\dim(\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq i_{s}-1}D_{i_{j}}\cap V)}\geq\frac{i_{s}-i_{0}}{\mathrm{rank}\{Q_{i};1\leq i\leq i_{s}-1\}}=\frac{i_{s}}{s}\,\forall 1\leq s\leq p+1.

We set
t_{i}=\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(z)
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. Then, by Lemma
4.1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{p}(i_{s+1}-i_{s})\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i_{s}}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta(\sum_{s=0}^{p}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i_{s}}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}).

We take
M-p
homogeneous polynomials
P_{p+1},\ldots,P_{M}
of degree
d
in variables
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
such that
([Q_{i_{0}}],\ldots,[Q_{i_{p}}],[P_{p+1}],\ldots,[P_{M}])
is a basis of
I_{d}(V)
. Then, by usual argument in Nevanlinna theory, we have

\displaystyle\nu^{0}_{W}(z)

\displaystyle=\nu^{0}_{W(Q_{i_{0}}(\tilde{f}),\ldots,Q_{i_{p}}(\tilde{f}),P_{p+1}(\tilde{f}),\ldots,P_{M}(\tilde{f}))}(z)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s=0}^{p}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i_{s}}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}+\sum_{s=p+1}^{M}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{P_{s}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s=0}^{p}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i_{s}}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(f)}(z)-\Delta\nu^{0}_{W}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(f)}(z)-\Delta\sum_{s=0}^{p}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i_{s}}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(f)}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{q}\max\{0,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(z)-M\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\min\{M,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(z)\}.

This yields that

\sum_{i=1}^{q}N_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)-\Delta N_{W}(r)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[M]}_{Q_{i}(\tilde{f})}(r).

Combining this inequality and (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\,(q-\Delta(M+1+\epsilon))T_{f}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{d}N^{[M]}_{f}(r,Q_{j})+\Delta\delta\log r+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\deg D_{j}}N^{[M]}_{f}(r,D_{j})+\Delta\delta\log r+O(1).

Hence, the theorem is proved.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Assume that
\tilde{f}=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
and
\tilde{g}=(g_{0},\ldots,g_{n})
are reduced representations of
f
and
g,
respectively. Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{q}
be the homogeneous polynomials defining
D_{1},\ldots,D_{q}
respectively. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\deg Q_{j}=d
for all
1\leq j\leq q
.

Suppose that
f\neq g
. Then there exist two indices
s,t
with
0\leq s<t\leq n
such that
H:=f_{s}g_{t}-f_{t}g_{s}\not\equiv 0.

By the definition of the characteristic function and by the Jensen formula, we have

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}N_{H}(r)&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|f_{s}(re^{i\theta})g_{t}(re^{i\theta})-f_{t}(re^{i\theta})g_{s}(re^{i\theta})|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\|f(re^{i\theta})\|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\|g(re^{i\theta})\|\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\\
&=T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r).\end{split}

(a) By the assumption of the theorem, we have
H=0
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}f^{-1}(D_{i})\cup g^{-1}(D_{i})
.
If
z
is a zero of exactly
\ell
functions
Q(\tilde{f})
then
\Delta\geq\frac{\ell}{k}
, i.e.,
\ell\leq\Delta k.

Therefore,

\nu^{0}_{H}(z)\geq\Delta k\sum_{i=1}^{q}\min\{1,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(f)}\}.

It follows that

(6)

\displaystyle N_{H}(r)\geq\Delta k\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r).

Combining (
5
) and (
6
), we have

T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)\geq\Delta k\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r).

Similarly, we have

T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)\geq\Delta k\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r).

Summing both sides of the above inequalities, we get

\displaystyle 2(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r))

\displaystyle\geq\Delta k\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r)\right).

This yields that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\Delta k}(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\dfrac{1}{H_{V}(d)-1}N^{[H_{V}(d)-1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{q}\dfrac{1}{H_{V}(d)-1}N^{[H_{V}(d)-1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{d}{H_{V}(d)-1}(q-\Delta(H_{V}(d)+\epsilon))(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r))+o(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)).

Letting
r\longrightarrow+\infty
and then letting
\epsilon\longrightarrow 0
, we get

2\Delta k\geq\dfrac{d}{H_{V}(d)-1}(q-\Delta H_{V}(d)),

\text{i.e., }q\leq\Delta\left(\frac{2k(H_{V}(d)-1)}{d}+H_{V}(d)\right).

This is a contradiction.
Hence
f=g
.

(b) Since
H=0
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}f^{-1}(D_{i})\cup g^{-1}(D_{i})
, we have

\nu^{0}_{H}(z)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\min\{1,\nu^{0}_{Q_{i}(f)}\}.

It follows that

N_{H}(r)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r).

Combining (
5
) and this inequality, we obtain

T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)\,\text{ and }\,T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r).

Summing-up both sides of the above two inequalities and applying Theorem
1.1
we have

\displaystyle 2(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{q}N^{[1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{q}\dfrac{1}{H_{V}(d)-1}N^{[H_{V}(d)-1]}_{Q_{i}(f)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{q}\dfrac{1}{H_{V}(d)-1}N^{[H_{V}(d)-1]}_{Q_{i}(g)}(r)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{d}{H_{V}(d)-1}(q-\Delta(H_{V}(d)+\epsilon))(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r))+o(T_{f}(r)+T_{g}(r)).

Letting
r\longrightarrow+\infty
and then letting
\epsilon\longrightarrow 0
, we get

2\geq\dfrac{d}{H_{V}(d)-1}(q-\Delta H_{V}(d)),

\text{i.e., }q\leq\frac{2(H_{V}(d)-1)}{d}+\Delta H_{V}(d).

This is a contradiction.
Hence
f=g
.
∎
