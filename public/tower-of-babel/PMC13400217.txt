Early surgical excision of elbow heterotopic ossification after prolonged intensive care unit hospitalization: excellent functional recovery without recurrence
Authors: Mahmut Kaynak, Muhammed Yusuf Afacan, Alican Barış, Süha Ahmet Aktaş
Published: 2026

Introduction

Myositis ossificans (MO) is a benign, non-neoplastic condition characterized by the heterotopic formation of mature lamellar bone within soft tissues, predominantly skeletal muscle.1,13 While its exact pathophysiology remains complex, it is widely attributed to the inappropriate differentiation of pluripotent mesenchymal progenitor cells into osteoblasts, often triggered by local trauma, burns, or central nervous system injuries.8 In addition, prolonged immobilization, particularly in patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalization, has been recognized as an important contributing factor in the development of heterotopic ossification (HO).6 The elbow joint is one of the most frequently affected sites due to its high susceptibility to postinjury stiffness and HO, which can lead to a significant reduction in the functional range of motion (ROM) and overall quality of life.11,14

Although conservative management is the first-line treatment in the early stages, surgical intervention becomes mandatory when the ectopic bone acts as a mechanical block, preventing a functional arc of motion.3 Historically, surgeons advocated delaying excision until the lesion reached full radiographic maturity, typically after 12 to 24 months. However, recent evidence suggests that early surgical intervention can prevent secondary complications such as muscular atrophy and soft tissue contractures without increasing the risk of recurrence.6,9

This report aims to describe the clinical course and surgical management of elbow HO arising after prolonged ICU hospitalization following subarachnoid hemorrhage, with particular emphasis on the rationale for early excision in the setting of a defined mechanical block. By presenting the imaging characteristics, operative strategy, and 1-year post-operative outcome, we aim to underscore that carefully selected patients may achieve excellent functional recovery with early surgical intervention combined with structured rehabilitation, with no recurrence observed during follow-up.

Case report

A 53-year-old female patient presented to our orthopedic clinic with complaints of progressive stiffness and pain in her right elbow. The patient had a history of a traffic accident resulting in subarachnoid hemorrhage, which required prolonged hospitalization in the ICU. Following discharge from the ICU, she gradually developed pain and restricted motion in the right elbow over the subsequent months. Physical examination revealed a significant restriction in the elbow's ROM, measured from 30° of extension to 90° of flexion. A firm, palpable mass was noted on the posterior aspect of the elbow. Neurologic examination was intact, with no evidence of ulnar or median neuropathy. Radiographic evaluation, including plain radiographs (Fig. 1, A and B) and computed tomography (CT; Fig. 1, C and D), demonstrated a well-defined, well-circumscribed HO mass measuring approximately 3 × 3 cm located in the posterior capsular/olecranon fossa region, consistent with the diagnosis of HO. Based on the CT localization and intraoperative findings, the lesion most likely arose from the posterior capsular/periarticular soft tissues in the olecranon fossa region, rather than from the collateral ligament complex. Pre-operative clinical photographs demonstrated limited flexion and extension due to a mechanical block (Fig. 2, A and B).

Surgical intervention was planned after unsuccessful conservative management, including supervised rehabilitation and ROM exercises, because a persistent mechanical block prevented functional recovery. Surgical excision was performed at six months following the initial injury, before the traditionally recommended 12–24-month delay. The decision for early excision was based on adequate lesion demarcation on CT, defined by a well-circumscribed ossified margin, clear separation from adjacent osseous structures, and identifiable surgical planes, together with persistent functional limitation caused by a mechanical block. Under general anesthesia, the heterotopic mass was completely excised through a combined medial and lateral approach, with careful identification and protection of the ulnar nerve. Because of the extensive medial exposure and concern for post-operative scarring or tethering, anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve was performed (Fig. 3). Following excision, an intraoperative ROM of 0–140° was achieved. Post-operative radiographs demonstrated complete excision of the HO mass and restoration of joint congruity (Fig. 4, A and B). Post-operatively, the patient received a 4-week course of indomethacin (25 mg, 3 times daily) for HO prophylaxis and was enrolled in a structured physical therapy program starting on the second post-operative day. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient was pain-free, with a stable elbow and a maintained ROM of 0–140° (Fig. 2, C and D). No radiographic recurrence was observed on follow-up radiographs.

Discussion

MO is a localized form of HO characterized by ectopic mature lamellar bone formation within soft tissues, most commonly following trauma, but it may also develop after central nervous system injury and prolonged immobilization, particularly in patients requiring ICU hospitalization.1,13,14 In the present report, the lesion is discussed within the broader spectrum of HO, while retaining the diagnosis of MO based on its localized clinical and radiographic features. Based on the CT localization and intraoperative findings, the lesion most likely arose from the posterior capsular/periarticular soft tissues in the olecranon fossa region, rather than from the collateral ligament complex.

The elbow's susceptibility is attributed to its complex periarticular anatomy and high tendency toward post-traumatic or immobilization-related stiffness, which may result in substantial functional limitation when heterotopic bone creates a mechanical block.11,14 The pathophysiology of MO is believed to involve aberrant osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells triggered by local inflammation, tissue hypoxia, and bone morphogenetic protein signaling following soft-tissue or neurologic injury.8 HO around the elbow has been reported in a wide range of prevalence rates, largely influenced by injury severity, associated neurologic factors, and duration of immobilization.11,14 Anatomic and imaging-based studies have demonstrated that heterotopic bone formation most commonly involves the anterior capsule, collateral ligaments, or posterior capsule, a distribution consistent with the posterior capsular/olecranon fossa localization observed in the present case.14

Beyond focal capsular lesions, HO around the elbow may also involve the collateral ligament complex, producing a more advanced form of stiffness in which excision alone may be insufficient to restore function while preserving stability. In such cases, resection of the ossified ligamentous structures combined with collateral ligament reconstruction has been shown to yield marked gains in motion, near-excellent functional scores, and absence of clinically meaningful instability during follow-up. This observation further supports the principle that the anatomic extent of ectopic bone involvement should guide operative strategy. Whereas our patient had a localized posterior capsular/olecranon fossa mass causing a mechanical block and could therefore be treated successfully with isolated excision, more extensive HO involving stabilizing ligamentous structures may require simultaneous reconstructive procedures to achieve comparable restoration of motion without compromising elbow stability.5 Traditionally, surgical excision of HO was delayed until radiographic maturation, typically 12 to 24 months after injury, to reduce the risk of recurrence.6 In this context, radiographic maturation and adequate lesion demarcation should be regarded as related but distinct concepts. Radiographic maturation traditionally refers to a later stage of HO, characterized by stable lesion size, mature cortical and trabecular organization, and presumed biological quiescence. In contrast, adequate lesion demarcation refers to an earlier surgical-readiness criterion, in which cross-sectional imaging demonstrates a clearly circumscribed ossified mass, distinguishable borders from adjacent bone and soft tissues, and identifiable surgical planes that permit safe excision. Therefore, in the present case, surgery was not delayed until complete radiographic maturation in the traditional sense; rather, early excision was performed once CT demonstrated sufficient demarcation of the lesion and the patient continued to have a clinically relevant mechanical block despite structured rehabilitation. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that prolonged delay may lead to progressive soft-tissue contracture, muscular atrophy, and worsening functional outcomes without providing a clear protective effect against recurrence.6,9

Comparative studies evaluating early versus delayed excision have demonstrated superior improvements in ROM and functional scores following early intervention, with no significant difference in recurrence or complication rates.3,9 In the present case, surgical excision was performed at six months, representing early intervention relative to the traditionally recommended waiting interval. The favorable clinical course supports previous observations that progressive loss of motion in such patients is often driven more by mechanical restriction and periarticular contracture than by continued biologically significant expansion of the lesion. This observation supports timely surgical intervention once a functional arc of motion cannot be achieved with conservative management.

Long-term follow-up studies have consistently shown that surgical excision combined with structured rehabilitation results in durable improvements in elbow motion and pain relief.9 Severe untreated cases may progress to complete elbow ankylosis, a rare but debilitating condition associated with loss of hand positioning ability and compensatory overuse of adjacent joints.12 Although ankylosis was not present in our patient, the presence of a well-demarcated posterior capsular/olecranon fossa mass causing a clear mechanical block justified operative management to prevent further functional deterioration. Reported outcomes of operative treatment for even advanced cases of elbow HO have been favorable when meticulous excision and post-operative rehabilitation protocols are applied.9

Neurologic complications, particularly ulnar neuropathy, represent an important consideration due to the close relationship between heterotopic bone and neurovascular structures around the elbow.10 While no neurologic deficit was identified in our case, HO has been recognized as a potential space-occupying cause of ulnar nerve compression, emphasizing the need for careful perioperative evaluation.10 In our patient, anterior transposition was performed prophylactically because the planned medial dissection and local post-operative scarring were considered potential risk factors for delayed ulnar nerve irritation.

Post-operative prophylaxis remains controversial, as randomized controlled trials have failed to demonstrate a clear benefit of indomethacin in reducing HO incidence following elbow surgery.2 Nevertheless, indomethacin was used in the present case as part of an individualized post-operative strategy, given the patient's high-risk clinical context, including neurologic injury, prolonged immobilization, and established periarticular HO. Similarly, meta-analyses comparing nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and radiation therapy have reported no significant differences in recurrence rates or final ROM, supporting individualized prophylactic strategies.4 Early mobilization and adherence to a structured rehabilitation program remain critical determinants of successful outcomes following excision.7 This case supports current evidence suggesting that early surgical excision of MO causing a mechanical block can restore functional elbow motion without increasing recurrence risk when combined with appropriate rehabilitation.3,6,9

Conclusion

HO of the elbow following prolonged immobilization and neurologic injury can result in significant functional impairment when a posterior capsular/periarticular ossified mass creates a mechanical block to motion. This case illustrates that, in appropriately selected patients, early surgical excision combined with structured rehabilitation may restore functional elbow motion effectively, with no clinical or radiographic recurrence observed at 1-year follow-up. Timely recognition of mechanical restriction and appropriate surgical intervention are essential, particularly in patients with a history of prolonged ICU hospitalization.
