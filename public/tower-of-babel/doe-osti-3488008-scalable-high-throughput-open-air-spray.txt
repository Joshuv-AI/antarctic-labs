3. Executive Summary:
This report presents a comprehensive analysis of a novel plasma-based in-line manufacturing process for
large-area, LLZO-separator-based, solid-state lithium-ion batteries, demonstrating both technical
feasibility and economic advantages over conventional vacuum deposition methods. The technical
validation shows that spray-deposition with plasma curing achieves comparable electrode and separator
quality to vacuum techniques while enabling continuous processing of components and industrially
relevant film areas. Critical material interfaces maintain low porosity and high ionic conductivities, which
confirm the process's ability to overcome the primary limitation of conventional methods - the trade-off
between deposition quality and economically-viable production scale.

Material characterization revealed that the plasma-cured LLZO separators exhibit exceptional density and
interfacial stability, with no delamination observed after thermal cycling. The in-line process particularly
excels in cathode manufacturing, where it eliminates the need for post-deposition sintering while
retaining capacity. These technical achievements directly enable the economic benefits demonstrated in
subsequent analysis, as the elimination of vacuum chambers and batch processing steps reduces both
capital expenditures, energy consumption, and overall production throughout, which all coalesce to
increase the manufacturing costs per kWh of outputted capacity.

The technoeconomic analysis transitions from these technical foundations to establish a rigorous cost
model, beginning with the evolution from a discount-factor based analysis to eventually sourcing bulk-
volume price quotes directly from industrial scale vendors. The accuracy of the latter approach over the
former, especially for precursors which are not currently demanded in such high volumes across the
industry, and the volatility of such costs as a function of time in the broader lithium-ion battery industry
is also discussed

The cost- modelling effort then focuses on overall manufacturing costs, which highlights the drastically
reduced equipment costs and much greater $/kWh efficiency of the proposed manufacturing method
over established, conventional routes. It is also importantly noted that actual market adoption of such
technology would inevitable have reflecting effects on the very industrial-scale prices used for outputting
these cost values and drawing conclusions, but would benefit from a future impacted cost analysis to help
deconvolute potential inaccuracies on account of the very real lack of scale present in the existing solid-
state-battery industry.

5. Project Objectives:
The goal of our project is to develop a fully scalable, solid-state battery (SSB) using our spray-plasma
platform, based on proven fabrication technologies. The battery will demonstrate improved
manufacturing speed/cost relative to standard SSB, and superior energy density/safety relative to
standard lithium-ion batteries. We intend to investigate various innovations to the standard process,
including plasma curing, delayed ignition combustion oxidation (DICO) , 3-d patterned solid-state
electrolyte (SSE) surfaces, polymer interlayers and spraying both a ceramic separator and a structured
electrode.

The objective for the first Budget Period is to develop a structured electrode and a ceramic separator via
our unique processes that can demonstrate charging and provide high ionic conductivity (respectively).
We will then validate both components individually in standard form factor cells. The objective for the
second Budget Period is to integrate the structured electrode, ceramic separator, and other relevant
components (current collectors, bonding layer) into a single solid-state cell and validate our processes via
full cell cycling, and establish the fundamental basis for the technoeconomic validation of this higher-
throughput process. The objective for the third Budget Period is to fully verify the stability and
reproducibility of this cell, and demonstrate its far superior $/kWh efficiency over conventional
approaches with a full-scope manufacturing and materials cost model.

6. Project Results and Discussion:

6.1 Open-Air, Spray Plasma Processing of Solid-State Separator and Cathode

6.1.1 Processing of LLZO Solid-State Electrolyte layer

Figure 1. Schematic illustration of the open-air plasma curing process for thin-film LLZO solid-state
electrolyte fabrication.

Fig. 1 illustrates the overall processing strategy developed in BP1 for the fabrication of thin-film LLZO solid-
state electrolytes using an open-air plasma curing approach. In this process, LLZO precursor solutions are
deposited directly onto the current collector or the cathode through either spray coating or blade coating,
followed by rapid plasma-assisted curing. The plasma system was specifically designed to provide uniform
energy delivery across large areas, with a shrouded plasma head positioned at a controlled distance above
the substrate. Unlike conventional vacuum-based deposition or high-temperature sintering routes, which
are often not compatible with the contemporary roll-to-roll manufacturing line, this method enables
direct integration of the electrolyte layer within battery architectures.

                                                Page 10 of 34

                                                                                 Award # DE-EE0010227

Figure 2. Characterization of the open-air plasma environment used for LLZO thin-film processing. (a)
Representative open-air mass spectrometry spectra collected under a forming-gas shroud, revealing the
presence of reactive ionic and radical species generated during plasma operation. (b) Schematic
illustration of the plasma–liquid interaction during the plasma process and generated plasma species.

A plasma environment was analyzed using open-air mass spectrometry, and the results are summarized
in Fig. 2. We confirmed that the plasma can generate a combination of heat, ions and radicals, and UV
photons that enable the rapid processing of thin-film LLZO film. Reactive radical ions generated through
interactions between N2 plasma species and humidity-controlled air in the dry box are expected to
accelerate metal oxide formation. These reactive ions perform a dual role in film formation: the localized
bombardment of plasma-derived reactive ions delivers kinetic energy, assisting the film densification,
while oxygen-containing radicals react to reconstruct metal oxide bonds.

Next, key process parameters - nozzle height, nozzle speed, and duty cycle - were systematically studied
to establish optimized processing parameters. Variations in temperature, plasma power, and plasma
current were examined in conjunction with tuning the key parameters, as shown in Fig. 3. These findings
are critical for establishing plasma processing as a viable manufacturing tool rather than a laboratory-
scale post-treatment technique.

Figure 3. Optimizing plasma processing parameters for LLZO film curing. Variation of (a) temperature, (b)
plasma power, and (c) plasma current with varying nozzle height, scan rate, and duty cycle.

After optimizing the plasma conditions, an amorphous LLZO (aLLZO or LLZO) film was fabricated through
the following procedure. First, the sol–gel solution was formulated to achieve a target stoichiometry of
Li14Zr3La2Ox, with lithium tert-butoxide serving as both the lithium source and the base upon hydrolysis.
Lithium tert-butoxide was first dissolved in anhydrous 2-methoxyethanol (2-ME), followed by the
sequential addition of lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate and zirconium propoxide to obtain a 3 M total metal
concentration. All precursor preparation was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the solution

                                             Page 11 of 34

                                                                                  Award # DE-EE0010227

was aged at 60 °C for 72 h to ensure complete complexation and homogeneity. Then, the solution was
deposited onto a Pt-coated Si wafer through blade coating using a 10 µm gap blade. Immediately after
blade coating, the film was plasma-cured under low-humidity (<2% RH) open air.

Figure 4. Microstructural characterization of plasma-cured LLZO thin films. (a) A plane view SEM image
taken at secondary electron (SE) mode. (b) A cross-sectional SEM image of LLZO/Pt/Si stack taken at
backscattering electron (BSE) mode. The obtained images show conformal and defect-free LLZO thin films.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were conducted to figure out film morphology,
conformality, and defect distribution. Fig. 4a shows a plane view and Fig. 4b demonstrates a cross-
sectional image of plasma-cure LLZO film, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4, the LLZO film did not
demonstrate significant voids or cracks, suggesting the uniformity of the LLZO film. The cross-sectional
SEM image (Fig. 3b) was obtained in the backscattering electron (BSE) mode of which the contrast is
strongly correlated to the atomic number (Z). The uniform contrast throughout the LLZO film indicates
the absence of secondary phases (e.g., lanthanum zirconium oxide (LZO), lithium carbonate (Li2CO3)) or
significant compositional inhomogeneities within the LLZO layer. Furthermore, no voids or delamination
were noticed at the LLZO/substrate interface, and this proves strong adhesion and mechanical integrity
of the film. Since even a small crack or pinhole can lead to short-circuit formation during the cell cycles,
the mechanical integrity of the LLZO electrolyte layer is critical to thin-film solid-state battery
performance.

6.1.2 Solution Combustion Synthesis (SCS) of LLZO Solid-State Electrolyte Layer

Figure 5. A scheme of solution combustion synthesis (SCS) pathway for low-temperature ceramic thin film
formation.

                                              Page 12 of 34

                                                                                 Award # DE-EE0010227

Parallel to the plasma-curing pathway, we have developed another route for LLZO film fabrication: the
solution combustion synthesis (SCS) pathway. Fig. 5 illustrates the SCS reaction pathway and its potential
applications in energy storage devices such as solid-state batteries and supercapacitors. A solution that
consists of metal cations, an oxidant, and a complexing fuel was invented. Acetylaceton (AcAc) was chosen
as the complexing fuel given that AcAc forms stable complexes with Li, La, and Zr. By carefully adjusting
the fuel-to-oxidizer ratio, the SCS process enables rapid combustion-driven oxide formation at relatively
low temperature (<400 °C) in a few minutes.

Figure 6. Microscopic analyses of SCS-derived non-lithiated oxide films. Optical microscopy (OM) images
of (a) ZrOx and (b) LaOx films respectively. (c) Atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of LaZrOx film.

Figure 7. Microscopic analyses of SCS-derived LLZO film. (a) A plane view of SEM image and (b) AFM image
of LLZO film.

To evaluate the conformality of SCS-derived oxide thin-films, we performed microscopic analyses. First,
before LLZO formation, non-lithiated oxides (ZrOx, LaOx, LaZrOx) with simpler stoichiometry were
fabricated and studied as model systems. Fig. 6a,b present optical microscopy (OM) images of ZrOx and
LaOx, respectively. Fig. 6c displays an atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of LaZrOx (LZO). As shown,
SCS-derived films showed a nearly defect-free smooth surface. It is worth noting that LZO film
demonstrated nm-scale roughness, supporting that SCS enables thin-film with excellent uniformity
despite its rapid combustion process. Following that, LLZO films were fabricated via SCS and investigated
using SEM and AFM as shown in Fig. 7. A patchy contrast is observed in the SEM image (Fig. 7a), which
may originate from surface Li2CO3 formation induced by brief air exposure during sample transfer.

However, the film did not show a significant crack or void. Similar to LZO film, LLZO film features smooth
morphology with nm-scale roughness.

6.1.3 Ionic conductivity measurements of Plasma-cured and SCS-derived LLZO Solid-State Electrolytes

Figure 8. A graphical comparison between two different electrochemical measurement setups. (a) A
through-plane configuration consisting of a vertical metal-LLZO-metal stack. (b) an in-plane configuration
that is made of interdigitated electrodes covered with a LLZO thin film.

Next, we performed electrochemical analysis of LLZO thin films to measure ionic conductivity, a key factor
for a cell cycle. In general, two different cell configurations exist for electrochemical measurements: a
through-plane method and an in-plane method. First, the through-plane method applies potential or
external current to a vertical stack of metal-electrolyte (testing material)-metal; Pt-LLZO-Pt for instance.
Since this configuration mimics practical battery architecture, this approach can provide more intuitive
insights. Also, with a proper setup (e.g., pouch cell or coin cell), one can apply external pressure during
the test, which makes the result more relevant to real conditions. However, this approach requires a stack
of multiple thin layers, so it is inherently sensitive to defects during the fabrication process. On the other
side, the in-plane configuration consists of two layers; the electrolyte (testing material) is deposited on
the interdigitated electrodes. Typically, each side of the interdigitated electrodes has multiple metallic
fingers with a fixed distance, and an in-plane electric field is applied. Though this configuration does not
fully replicate a real battery environment, it can provide accurate extraction of intrinsic ionic transport
properties due to its defect-tolerant nature. Fig. 8 compares two different configurations schematically.
In this project, we mainly employed the in-plane method during initial conductivity measurements and
utilized the through-plane approach during full cell test.

                                               Page 14 of 34

                                                                                 Award # DE-EE0010227

Figure 9. Ionic conductivity characterization of plasma-cured LLZO thin films measured using in-plane
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). (a) Arrhenius plot of ionic conductivity as a function of
inverse temperature, showing thermally activated Li+ transport with a fitted activation energy of ~0.91
eV. (b) Corresponding Nyquist plots collected at temperatures ranging from 30 to 100 °C, illustrating the
progressive decrease in impedance with increasing temperature.

Figure 10. Ionic conductivity characterization of SCS-derived LLZO thin films measured using in-plane
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). (a) Arrhenius plot of ionic conductivity as a function of
inverse temperature, showing thermally activated Li+ transport with a fitted activation energy of ~1.2 eV.
(b) Corresponding Nyquist plots collected at temperatures ranging from 30 to 95 °C, demonstrating the
similar trend to plasma-cured LLZO film.

Ionic conductivity of LLZO thin film was evaluated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).
EIS measurements were performed using the in-plane setup (Fig. 8b) at different temperatures using a
hot plate, and the exact temperature was recorded using a thermocouple (TC). Fig. 9 summarizes the EIS
result obtained from plasma-cured LLZO film. The obtained conductivities across temperature are
displayed in Fig. 9a, and corresponding Nyquist plots are shown in Fig 9b. At room temperature, plasma-
cured LLZO demonstrated ionic conductivity of 2 ·10-6 S cm-1, which is higher than the milestone goal,

                                             Page 15 of 34

                                                                                     Award # DE-EE0010227

10-8 S cm-1, by two orders. In addition, due to high activation energy of ~0.91 eV, ionic conductivity
significantly increases with increasing temperature, suggesting a promising usage for high-temperature
applications. Same tasks were conducted to SCS-dervied LLZO film, and the result is highlighted in Fig 10.
In the case of SCS LLZO film, ionic conductivity of 2 ·10-7 S cm-1 and activation energy of ~1.2 eV were
observed. It is worth noting that these values were obtained without any doping. Many previous works
on garnet-type electrolytes suggest that introducing a dopant such as Ta, Nb, and Al can enhance ionic
conductivities. [1-4] We firmly believe that higher conductivities of LLZO could be achieved with the
doping strategy, as future studies.

6.1.4 Development of Plasma-Assisted Rapid Cathode Processing and Structured Cathode Integrated with
LLZO Solid-State Electrolyte

Figure 11. Schematic illustration of a continuous roll-to-roll processing strategy for integrated cathode
and solid-state electrolyte fabrication using open-air deposition and plasma-assisted curing.

In contemporary battery manufacturing, cathode processing represents one of the most critical steps for
two primary reasons. First, the cathode largely determines the achievable cell capacity and energy
density; therefore, both cathode composition and microstructural architecture exert a direct influence on
overall electrochemical performance. [5, 6] Second, conventional cathode fabrication relies on slurry
casting using N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as the solvent, which necessitates a time- and energy-
intensive solvent drying step and imposes significant manufacturing bottlenecks. [7, 8] Consequently, the
development of cathode processing routes that are inherently compatible with solid-state electrolyte
fabrication is essential for scalable solid-state battery manufacturing. Fig. 11 illustrates a continuous roll-
to-roll processing strategy for fabricating and integrating structured cathode and solid electrolyte layers
using open-air deposition combined with plasma-assisted curing. In the first stage, a composite cathode
layer composed of active material particles, conductive additives, and polymeric binders is deposited via
slurry casting and subsequently cured under controlled conditions to establish a percolated electronic and
ionic transport network. To accelerate solvent removal, plasma treatment can be applied during this
stage; the role of plasma-assisted drying and curing will be discussed in detail later.

                                                Page 16 of 34

                                                                                   Award # DE-EE0010227

Following cathode formation, a polymeric or sol-gel derived electrolyte precursor layer is deposited
directly on top of the cathode. Please note that introducing polymer or liquid electrolyte, as catholyte,

believed to dry the solvent within a minute, enabled by high peak temperature as well as nonthermal

migration issue can be suppressed owing to the limited reaction time.

delivers activated molecules and ions, which helps the sample reach the equilibrium immediately. Overall,
we revealed that rapid cathode drying is possible using an open-air plasma process.

A few patterns stand out from even this initial estimation, some more obvious than others. Immediately
noticeable are the savings derived from an anode-free chemistry, given the costliness of battery grade-
graphite. Another clear pattern is the consistently lower cost of solid-state separator materials
compared to the solvents and lithium salts necessary for liquid electrolytes, notably on a $/kWh basis.
The variation of costs for the same cell component across cathode chemistries, both for liquid and solid-
state chemistries, is another notable pattern, and has a more nuanced explanation which also elucidates
the inner workings of the technoeconomic model built for the project.

The model works by fixing a yearly production output for a hypothetical battery manufacturing plant (10
GWh of battery capacity per year for all the data present here unless explicitly stated otherwise), then
calculating the quantity of materials necessary to achieve this scale depending on the varying technical
parameters of each cell. These quantities are then passed through the most up-to-date costs available
and adjusted to account for economies of scale (another nuanced point which will be elaborated on in a
subsequent discussion), resulting in the final $/kWh value.

Most important of said varying technical parameters for the present discussion is the energy density of
each considered cathode chemistry, measured in mAh/g. Given this varying energy density, the number
of cells that a given hypothetical plant would need to produce to achieve the 10GWh/year target also
varies. LFP, for example, has the lowest energy density of the chemistries considered, so a greater net
total number of cells is necessary to reach the target. Thus the cathode cost is clearly the lowest, but
because more LFP cells are necessary to achieve the target yearly capacity, more anode, separator, and
current collecting materials are needed by extension, which inflates their cost on a $/kWh basis as they
are all normalized by the same 10GWh.
