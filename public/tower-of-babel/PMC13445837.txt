Introduction

Dislocations of the tibiotalar joint without concomitant fractures, termed “pure dislocations,” are uncommon injuries, accounting for approximately 0.065% of ankle injuries and 0.46% of ankle dislocations [1, 2]. The inherent stability of the ankle mortise, derived from bony congruence and robust ligamentous constraints, typically necessitates high-energy trauma to produce dislocation, often resulting in associated malleolar or talar fractures [3]. Posterior dislocations, in particular, are rare and frequently linked to mechanisms combining plantarflexion and axial loading, such as motor vehicle accidents or falls from height. However, isolated cases involving low-energy sports-related activities have been documented, underscoring the role of predisposing factors like ligamentous laxity or malleolar hypoplasia [2, 4].

The management of pure posterior tibiotalar dislocation prioritizes immediate reduction to mitigate risks of neurovascular injury, avascular necrosis, or post-traumatic arthritis [1, 2, 5]. While existing literature predominantly focuses on high-energy trauma in adults, reports involving adolescents or low-energy mechanisms remain limited [1]. This case report describes a pure posteromedial tibiotalar dislocation without fracture in a 17-year-old student, sustained during recreational basketball. It aims to elucidate the clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and therapeutic strategy for this rare injury, while contextualizing findings within current evidence to guide optimal care in young, active populations.

Case report

Patient information and clinical findings

A previously healthy 17-year-old male recreational basketball player presented to the emergency department following an acute injury to his right ankle (Fig. 1A and B). The mechanism involved a forced inversion and plantarflexion while landing from a jump during a game, resulting in immediate pain, deformity, and an inability to bear weight. Upon presentation, physical examination revealed a significant deformity with the foot positioned posteriorly relative to the tibia, with visible skin dimpling, talus prominence, and loss of the normal ankle contour, indicative of posterior tibiotalar dislocation. The skin was intact without laceration. Distal neurovascular status was a primary concern; however, capillary refill was within normal limits (< 3 s), and motor function and sensation to light touch were intact in the dorsal and plantar web spaces. The dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses were palpable. Light-touch sensation over the dorsal foot, plantar foot, and first web space was intact, and active toe flexion and extension were preserved.

Timeline, diagnostic assessment, and therapeutic intervention

Given the concerning clinical presentation, an urgent closed reduction was performed in the emergency department under local anesthesia combined with intravenous analgesia. Local infiltration anesthesia was achieved using 10 mL of lidocaine hydrochloride injection containing 0.2 g lidocaine, and intravenous analgesia was provided with one 2-mL ampoule of tramadol hydrochloride injection containing 100 mg tramadol. Oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory status were monitored during the procedure. The knee was flexed to relax the gastrocnemius-soleus complex, and longitudinal traction was applied with manual correction of the deformity. An audible clunk was appreciated, signifying a successful reduction. Immediate post-reduction neurovascular examination remained normal. A post-reduction computed tomography (CT) and computed tomography angiography (CTA) was obtained to thoroughly evaluate the peri-articular structures and vascular integrity due to the high-energy nature of the injury mechanism. The CT confirmed an anatomic reduction of the tibiotalar joint, again identified no fractures (Fig. 3A, B and C), and the CTA revealed no evidence of injury to the anterior tibial or posterior tibial arteries (Fig. 3D and E).

The patient was admitted for observation, pain management, and limb elevation. The initial therapeutic intervention consisted of immobilization in a well-padded posterior splint with strict non-weight-bearing instructions were given, with crutches used for assistance and weight-bearing completely restricted (Fig. 3F). The inpatient pharmacologic regimen included intravenous analgesics, prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin for thromboembolism prevention. Ice therapy and strict elevation were emphasized to control edema.

On hospital day 3, due to increased swelling, the initial splint was exchanged for a new well-moulded below-knee splint to maintain comfort and stability. Figure 3G and H are the clinical photographs of the patient’s ankle joint obtained on the third hospital day, captured between removal of the initial management cast and application of the secondary cast.

The patient was discharged on hospital day 7 after significant reduction in edema was achieved, continuing with non-weight-bearing status in the splint.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was followed closely in the outpatient clinic. At the two-week post-injury visit, the splint was removed. Clinical and radiographic examination revealed no signs of instability. The immobilization was transitioned to a controlled ankle motion (CAM) walker boot for an additional four weeks, during which time gentle active range-of-motion exercises out of the boot were initiated, while weight-bearing remained prohibited.

At the six-week post-discharge visit, progressive weight-bearing was initiated. The patient was permitted to bear weight as tolerated in the CAM boot, using crutches for assistance, and was referred to a structured physical therapy program. The therapy protocol focused on restoring full range of motion, strengthening the peroneal and stabilizing musculature, and improving proprioception.

By eight weeks post-injury, the patient had weaned off crutches and was ambulating exclusively in the CAM boot. At the twelve-week mark, the boot was discontinued, and the patient advanced to unassisted gait. Formal physical therapy continued for a total of four months, focusing on advanced strengthening, balance retraining, and a gradual return-to-sport protocol.

Functional outcomes were serially assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot Score. The score demonstrated significant improvement throughout the recovery period, as detailed in Table 1. At the one-year final follow-up, the patient reported no pain, no subjective feelings of instability, and had successfully returned to recreational basketball without limitations. Physical examination revealed a well-aligned ankle with symmetrical range of motion compared to the contralateral side and no ligamentous laxity on stress testing. Final radiographs showed a congruent tibiotalar joint with no signs of post-traumatic arthrosis or avascular necrosis (Fig. 4A and B).

Discussion and literature review

Pure posterior tibiotalar dislocation without associated fractures is an exceedingly rare injury, accounting for approximately 0.065% of ankle injuries and 0.46% of ankle dislocations [1, 3, 6]. This case report describes a successful outcome in a 17-year-old male recreational basketball player who sustained a closed posterior tibiotalar dislocation without fracture following a forced inversion and plantarflexion mechanism. The management involved immediate closed reduction, immobilization, and structured rehabilitation, resulting in excellent functional recovery at one-year follow-up. This discussion contextualizes our findings within the existing literature, highlighting the mechanisms, treatment strategies, outcomes, and prognostic factors associated with this injury.

Mechanism and pathophysiology

The ankle joint is inherently stable due to its bony architecture and robust ligamentous support, particularly the medial and lateral collateral complexes [3]. Pure posterior tibiotalar dislocation typically require high-energy trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents or falls from height, but can occasionally occur with lower-energy mechanisms during sports activities, as in this case [7–9]. The mechanism of posterior tibiotalar dislocation often involves a combination of plantarflexion and axial loading, forcing the talus posteriorly out of the mortise [1, 3, 10]. In our patient, the injury occurred during a basketball jump-landing sequence, with forced inversion and plantarflexion, consistent with the mechanisms described in prior reports [2, 7, 8]. The mechanism of injury in this case was a combination of forced inversion and plantarflexion, which resulted in a pure posterior tibiotalar dislocation. While the patient did not have any known predisposing factors such as ligamentous laxity, malleolar hypoplasia or previous ankle sprains the extreme force vector applied during the jump-landing sequence may have been sufficient to overcome the inherent stability of the ankle joint [3]. It is also possible that a subtle, unrecognized anatomical variant contributed to the occurrence of this injury, although such factors were not identified in this case.

Treatment considerations

Immediate closed reduction under sedation or anesthesia is paramount to mitigate neurovascular complications, such as anterior tibial artery injury or peroneal nerve damage [3, 10–11]. In this case, reduction was performed urgently in the emergency department, with post-reduction CT and CTA confirming anatomical alignment and vascular integrity. This aligns with recommendations from multiple studies emphasizing the need for prompt reduction and advanced imaging to rule out occult fractures or vascular injuries [3, 11]. Although MRI may be useful for evaluating ligamentous, chondral, or osteochondral injuries, it was not obtained in this case because the patient had stable reduction, no persistent mechanical symptoms, and excellent clinical recovery. Similarly, electromyography was not performed because repeated neurological examinations revealed no sensory or motor deficit.

Immobilization duration varies in the literature, but 6–8 weeks of non-weight-bearing casting or splinting is commonly advocated [2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12–15]. Our patient was immobilized in a splint for 2 weeks, followed by a CAM boot for 4 weeks, with progressive weight-bearing and physical therapy. This approach yielded excellent functional outcomes, as measured by the AOFAS score, which improved from 0 pre-reduction to 83 at six weeks. The structured rehabilitation program focused on range of motion, peroneal strengthening, and proprioception, which is critical for preventing chronic instability or re-injury [3, 6, 13].

Limitations

This study is limited by its single-case design, which precludes generalizability. However, the detailed follow-up and comprehensive imaging add strength. Future multi-center studies could further elucidate optimal management protocols.

Conclusion

Pure posteromedial tibiotalar dislocation is a rare injury that requires immediate reduction and structured rehabilitation. Our case demonstrates that with prompt treatment, excellent functional outcomes can be achieved, allowing return to high-level sports. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for vascular injury and neurological deficit, and combine advanced imaging with serial clinical examinations to guide management. The literature supports non-operative treatment with immobilization as the cornerstone of therapy, with surgical intervention reserved for irreducible dislocations or open injuries.
