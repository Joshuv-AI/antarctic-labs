Introduction

Hydatid disease, also known as cystic echinococcosis, is a zoonotic parasitic infection caused mainly by Echinococcus granulosus. Humans become accidental intermediate hosts through ingestion of parasite eggs shed in the feces of infected canids. After ingestion, embryos penetrate the intestinal mucosa and spread via the bloodstream to various organs, where they develop into hydatid cysts[1]. The liver is the most frequently affected organ, accounting for approximately 60–70% of cases, followed by the lungs (20–25%). Other organs, such as the spleen, bones, brain, muscles, and peritoneum, may also be involved, although much less commonly[2].

Primary involvement of the abdominal wall is extremely rare, representing a small fraction of all hydatid disease localizations. Subcutaneous or parietal hydatid cysts account for roughly 0.6–2.6% of cases[3]. Because of its rarity, this localization can mimic other cystic or soft tissue masses such as lipomas, abscesses, hematomas, or neoplasms, making preoperative diagnosis difficult. In this report, we describe a case of a hydatid cyst arising in the abdominal wall, detected on CT imaging, and review the relevant literature regarding its epidemiology, diagnosis, and management. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[5].

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a progressively enlarging mass in the right flank that had evolved over the previous 2 months and had recently become painful. The patient reported gradual swelling associated with mild discomfort but denied systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, or weight loss. On physical examination, a large mass was palpated in the right flank. The lesion was well-circumscribed, soft in consistency, and mobile relative to the superficial plane. It was mildly tender on palpation without overlying inflammatory skin changes. The patient was hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Examination of other systems revealed no abnormalities. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 12.3 × 10⁹/L and a markedly elevated C-reactive protein level of 120.5 mg/L. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a well-defined oval mass located in the right flank, measuring 79 × 144 × 137 mm, with a thickened wall (Fig. 1). Notably, the lesion lacked typical radiological features suggestive of a parasitic origin, such as daughter cysts, internal septations, calcifications, or the characteristic multiloculated appearance usually seen in hydatid disease. The thickened wall and homogeneous content instead raised suspicion for a soft-tissue abscess or cystic tumor. No intra-abdominal extension or fistulous tract was identified. Considering that the patient lived in an endemic area for hydatid disease, serological testing for echinococcosis was performed. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (NovaLisa Echinococcus IgG) was positive with a value of 21.20 NTU, while serological testing for amebiasis was negative. The patient subsequently underwent complete surgical excision of the lesion with meticulous precautions to prevent cyst rupture and dissemination. The operative field was irrigated with 20% hypertonic saline solution as a scolicidal agent. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst by demonstrating the characteristic laminated membrane and germinal layer. Postoperatively, the patient received albendazole therapy for 6 months to reduce the risk of recurrence. The postoperative course was uneventful, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up.

Discussion

Primary hydatid cysts of the abdominal wall are exceedingly rare, accounting for less than 2–3% of all cystic echinococcosis cases[6,7]. This rarity makes each documented case valuable for understanding the variable presentations and optimal management strategies. In reviewing the literature, most reported cases involve adult patients presenting with a slowly enlarging, painless mass, often without systemic symptoms[3,4]. The clinical presentation is subtle and nonspecific, which frequently leads to initial misdiagnosis as benign soft tissue tumors, abscesses, or hematomas[8,9]. The pathogenesis of primary parietal hydatid cysts remains a topic of debate. Hematogenous dissemination bypassing the hepatic and pulmonary capillary filters is considered the most plausible mechanism[2]. In rare situations, lymphatic spread or direct extension from intra-abdominal structures has been suggested[10,11]. Unlike secondary implantation following cyst rupture, primary abdominal wall cysts occur without prior surgery or known intra-abdominal involvement, as observed in most reported cases, including ours[12].

Imaging is critical for preoperative evaluation. Ultrasonography (US) can identify cystic lesions and daughter cysts, but CT provides superior delineation of the lesion’s size, wall characteristics, internal septations, and its relation to musculature[13]. CT imaging has been instrumental in multiple published reports; for example, Moshref et al[3] described a well-defined cystic lesion with septations that strongly suggested hydatid disease before surgical excision. Similarly, Kofina et al[4] highlighted a case where CT imaging differentiated a parietal hydatid cyst from a soft tissue tumor, guiding safe surgical management. MRI has also been used selectively, especially to characterize complex lesions or atypical localizations[14].

Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment. The objective is the complete removal of the cyst without rupture, which prevents recurrence and secondary dissemination[15]. Various scolicidal agents, such as hypertonic saline or povidone-iodine, are applied intraoperatively to minimize the risk of parasite spread[16]. In previously published cases, including those reported by Gülen et al[12] and Godazandeh et al[2], complete excision combined with careful handling was associated with favorable outcomes and no recurrence during follow-up. Adjuvant antihelmintic drugs, such as mebendazole (50 mg/kg daily) or albendazole (10 mg/kg daily), are frequently recommended for 4–6 months to sterilize cyst contents and reduce recurrence risk, particularly for larger or multiple cysts[15,17]. Several authors have reported that a combination of surgery and albendazole therapy leads to an excellent prognosis, as evidenced by the absence of recurrence in follow-up ranging from 6 months to 2 years[2–4,11].

Comparison with other reported cases reveals consistent patterns: patients typically present with an isolated, slow-growing mass; imaging findings often show well-circumscribed cysts with daughter cysts; and complete surgical excision with antiparasitic therapy yields favorable outcomes[3,17]. However, some reports emphasize diagnostic challenges in non-endemic regions, where hydatid disease is not initially suspected, resulting in delayed or unexpected intraoperative diagnosis[8,9,13].

Overall, the present case supports existing evidence that primary abdominal wall hydatid cysts, although rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic soft tissue masses in endemic regions. Early recognition using imaging, careful surgical management, and adjuvant antiparasitic therapy are essential to prevent recurrence and complications. The accumulation of such case reports contributes to improved awareness, diagnosis, and management strategies for this unusual presentation.

Conclusion

Hydatid cyst of the abdominal wall is an exceptionally rare manifestation of cystic echinococcosis. Because the clinical presentation may mimic other soft-tissue masses, diagnosis can be challenging. Imaging modalities, particularly CT, are essential for preoperative evaluation. Surgical excision combined with antiparasitic therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment. In endemic areas, hydatid disease should always be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic masses of the abdominal wall.
