Identification of characteristic genes of pressure ulcers based on angiogenesis-related genes and construction of miRNA, transcription factor, and molecular drug regulatory networks
Authors: Na Wang, Haijing Xiao, Liping Wu, Dongmei Bao, Yawen Ma, Qianqian Wang, Qing Xu, Xiaoli Xu, Xi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to analyze transcriptomic changes in pressure ulcer (PU) samples, identify angiogenesis-related differentially expressed genes (AR-DEGs), and explore their potential as diagnostic and therapeutic targets for PU, with a particular focus on the role of CDKN1A. Transcriptomic data from dataset GSE137897, which included 381 PU samples and 391 healthy controls, were utilized to investigate these changes. Differential expression analysis was performed to identify AR-DEGs, which were subsequently analyzed for enrichment in regulatory and signaling pathways. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was carried out to highlight key hub AR-DEGs. In addition, correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between these genes. Regulatory networks involving microRNAs (miRNAs), transcription factors (TFs), and targeted drugs were constructed using miRNet and DGIdb. A rat PU model was established to validate the expression of CDKN1A in vivo and to investigate the effects of CDKN1A knockout on PU healing. Through differential expression analysis, 128 AR-DEGs were identified and found to be enriched in various regulatory and signaling pathways. PPI network analysis identified seven hub AR-DEGs (JUN, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, HDAC1, CDKN1A, CCL2), which exhibited strong diagnostic potential for PU. Correlation analysis revealed that CDKN1A was negatively correlated with CCND1, CCL2, and FOS, while HIF1A was positively correlated with CDKN1A and HDAC1. Validation experiments in a rat PU model confirmed that CDKN1A was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in wound tissues. Furthermore, silencing CDKN1A in dermal fibroblasts enhanced cell viability, migration (wound healing), and collagen secretion in vitro. In vivo, CDKN1A knockout significantly accelerated wound healing in PU rats, as evidenced by improved wound closure, reduced inflammation, and increased collagen deposition. This study demonstrates that CDKN1A plays a pivotal role in modulating inflammation and collagen synthesis in PUs. The findings suggest that CDKN1A holds promise as a therapeutic target for PU treatment, providing novel insights into the management of this condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-026-00866-0.

Introduction

Pressure ulcers (PUs) are prevalent and serious skin and soft tissue injuries, primarily affecting bedridden, elderly, or paralyzed individuals. The primary cause is prolonged immobility, which results in sustained pressure on specific body areas, impairing blood flow. Infected PU wounds represent a significant clinical challenge, as they are notoriously difficult to heal. These wounds not only severely diminish patients’ quality of life but also extend hospital stays and increase healthcare costs [1]. The pathophysiology of PUs involves ischemia-reperfusion injury, chronic inflammation, and dysfunctional extracellular matrix remodeling [2]. Wound healing in PUs is a multifaceted process that encompasses hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling phases. Of these, the proliferation phase is critical, characterized by the proliferation of repair cells, angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, and matrix synthesis, leading to granulation tissue formation. Angiogenesis is particularly essential, as blood vessels supply vital nutrients, facilitate waste exchange, and support cell adhesion, migration, and survival [3, 4].

Current clinical treatments for PUs include conventional wound care practices, such as dressing changes and debridement, along with more advanced interventions like skin grafts in severe cases. The mechanisms underlying PU wound healing, particularly the role of angiogenesis, are central to these treatments. However, despite advancements in understanding PU pathophysiology, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal. The complexity of biological systems and incomplete knowledge of intercomponent interactions hinder a thorough understanding of disease onset and progression [5]. While multi-drug regimens offer some clinical benefits for managing complex diseases [6], high-throughput approaches, such as genome-wide association studies (GWAS), have identified numerous candidate genes associated with PU, though their precise roles remain unclear [7]. In molecular therapy, microRNAs (miRNAs)—short non-coding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length—regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. Aberrant miRNA expression has been implicated in various complex diseases, including those involving tissue injury and repair. Given the limitations of single-target drugs for treating multifactorial diseases, miRNAs offer the potential for multi-target therapies, as they can regulate multiple genes and entire gene networks. Thus, validating therapeutic targets within the broader disease context is critical for effective PU treatment.

Advancements in high-throughput molecular biology techniques have facilitated the generation of multi-level biological multi-omics data [8]. High-throughput sequencing, for instance, can yield extensive genetic information. Bioinformatics analysis, encompassing the study of biological networks such as protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, gene regulatory networks (GRNs), miRNA-gene targeting networks, and metabolic networks, plays a pivotal role in examining disease signal transduction and regulatory processes. It enables a systemic understanding of disease mechanisms, the identification of potential therapeutic targets and diagnostic biomarkers, and supports the discovery of system-based drug models [9, 10]. Databases like the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), which archives high-throughput sequencing data [11], the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), which assigns functional significance to genes and genomes at molecular and higher levels [12, 13], and Gene Ontology (GO), which provides a standardized semantic framework for describing gene and protein functions across species [14, 15], are invaluable resources for such analyses. Consequently, leveraging bioinformatics to identify targeted drugs and molecules for PUs holds substantial clinical value.

In this study, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between patients with PU and healthy controls (HCs) were screened from the GEO database. These DEGs were intersected with angiogenesis-related genes (ARGs) from GeneCards, yielding angiogenesis-related DEGs (AR-DEGs). GO/KEGG enrichment analysis, PPI network construction, and Cytoscape visualization were employed to pinpoint angiogenic signature genes in PUs. Furthermore, regulatory network analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships between AR-DEGs, miRNAs, transcription factors (TFs), and targeted drugs, aiming to guide PU drug screening. Additionally, the study focused on the role of CDKN1A in PU pathology. A rat PU model was established, and molecular biology and histopathology techniques were used to analyze the dynamic expression of CDKN1A in PU tissues. The impact of CDKN1A on wound healing was assessed using CDKN1A knockout animals, and gene expression profiles along with pathological changes were integrated to explore its potential regulatory role in PU healing. This study provides novel insights into targeted PU therapies and drug screening, contributing to improved treatment strategies for PUs.

Materials and methods

Data source of pressure ulcer samples

The PU sample data were retrieved from the high-throughput sequencing dataset GSE137897, available on the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), and gene probes were mapped to their corresponding gene names using platform annotation information. The dataset GSE137897 included single-cell RNA sequencing data from nine donors (three groups: PU wounds, healthy skin, and acute wounds), comprising a total of 1,170 cells. For differential analysis, 381 cells from PU wounds and 391 cells from HCs were aggregated as pseudo-replicates, following standard normalization protocols for scRNA-seq data. In addition, human PU samples were collected from consenting patients at General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. The samples included full-thickness skin tissues from the necrotic center and the adjacent inflammatory margin (approximately 1 cm from the wound edge) of chronic PUs (Stage III/IV). Control samples were obtained from healthy skin sites during elective surgeries. All samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection and stored at -80°C until analysis. The use of these samples was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Identification of differentially expressed genes in pressure ulcers

After downloading the dataset from GEO, single-cell expression matrices were aggregated into pseudo-bulk counts by averaging gene expression across cells from each donor. The original gene expression matrix of GSE137897 was corrected and normalized using the “limma” package to identify DEGs between PU and HC samples. A |logFC| (fold change) > 1 and corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. DEGs and their expression levels were extracted, and the results were visualized through heatmaps and volcano plots using the R packages pheatmap and ggplot2.

Identification of angiogenesis-related differentially expressed genes in pressure ulcers

ARGs were sourced from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org), and AR-DEGs in PU were identified by intersecting these with the DEGs. The chromosomal locations of these genes were visualized.

GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of AR-DEGs

GO enrichment analysis (including BP, CC, and MF) was performed using the R packages clusterProfiler, enrichment, ggplot2, and GOplot to explore the common biological functions of AR-DEGs. KEGG enrichment analysis was then employed to identify key pathways implicated in PU molecular mechanisms. A corrected p-value (q-value) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

The regulatory interactions of AR-DEG proteins in PU biological systems were systematically analyzed using the STRING database (http://www.string-db.org/). The PPI network was screened for highly expressed genes using the cytoHubba plug-in in Cytoscape software. The top ten highly expressed genes, defined as high AR-DEGs (HAR-DEGs), were identified based on the Degree algorithm. The PPI network and HAR-DEG sub-network were visualized.

ROC curve analysis and correlation analysis of HAR-DEGs

To assess the relative expression of HAR-DEGs in different samples, gene expression matrices were processed using the R packages limma and ggpubr. Differential expression of HAR-DEGs between the PU and HC groups was compared and visualized through box plots. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted using the R packages glmnet and pROC to calculate the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the ROC curve for HAR-DEGs. HAR-DEGs were considered characterized genes of PU if AUC > 0.7. Additionally, the expression correlation between the characterized genes was tested using the Spearman correlation test (absolute correlation coefficient > 0.3 and p-value < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant), and the results were visualized through correlation heatmaps and scatter plots using the R packages ggplot2, ggpubr, ggExtra, corrplot, and circlize.

Prediction of miRNAs and transcription factors (TFs) binding to characterized genes

The miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) provides a comprehensive map of miRNA and TF interactions with target genes, integrating data from 14 distinct miRNA databases (TarBase, miRTarBase, miRecords, miRanda, miR2Disease, HMDD, PhenomiR, SM2miR, PharmacomiR, EpimiR, starBase, TransmiR, AdmiRE, and TAM 2.0) for functional annotation. First, the list of characterized genes was input into the search box, selecting “Homo sapiens” as the organism, “Official gene symbol” as the ID type, “Skin” as the tissue type, and miRNA or TF as the regulatory elements. Upon submission, the interaction table of miRNAs and TFs with the characterized genes was downloaded. Finally, Cytoscape software was used to visualize their interactions and regulatory relationships.

Interaction analysis between characterized genes and targeted drugs

The Drug-Gene Interaction database (DGIdb) (https://www.dgidb.org/) offers genetic data concerning both established and potential drugs. This study entered the characterized PU genes into the search box to retrieve interaction data between these genes and targeted drugs, and visualized these interactions using Cytoscape software.

Grouping of experimental animals

Healthy adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 220–250 g) were selected as experimental subjects. All animals were acclimatized to a standard SPF-grade laboratory environment for one week before the experimental design, which was performed using a completely randomized grouping method. Two independent grouping systems were established. The first subgroup (basic model validation group) included: a control group (n = 6), which received routine feeding management with no PU modeling treatment, and a PU group (n = 6), where the modified ischemia-reperfusion injury method was employed to establish a standard PU model. The second grouping system (gene function research group) included: a WT (PU) group (n = 6), where wild-type SD rats were used to establish the standard PU model, and a CDKN1A−/− (PU) group (n = 6), where CDKN1A knockout rats, generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technology, were used to establish the PU model.

All animals were housed in a controlled environment with a temperature of 22 ± 1℃, humidity at 50 ± 5%, and free access to food and water, maintaining a 12-hour light/dark cycle. To control experimental variables, all PU modeling procedures were conducted by designated personnel at the same time each day. The subgroup design not only validated the PU model but also specifically investigated the role of CDKN1A in PU pathogenesis. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University and adhered to the “3R” principles of animal experimentation.

Rat pressure ulcer (PU) modeling

In this study, a modified ischemia-reperfusion injury method was employed to establish a rat PU model. The procedure was as follows: (1) Preoperative Preparation: The rats were fasted and deprived of water for 12 h prior to the experiment. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg), and once the anesthesia took effect, the rats were positioned prone on a surgical plate. Hair was shaved from both sides of the dorsal spine (T10-L2 level) using an electric shaver, covering an area of approximately 3 × 4 cm2. The skin in the operative area was disinfected alternately with iodine vapor and 75% ethanol, applied three times (2). PU Modeling: A special magnetic pressure device (1.5 cm diameter, adjusted to apply a pressure of 100 mmHg) (BioPuls™, Harvard Apparatus, USA) was placed vertically on the left side of the rat’s back. A digital pressure sensor (SITRANS P200/P300, Siemens, Germany) was used to monitor contact pressure in real time for precise pressure control. The ischemic injury model was induced by continuous compression for 2 hours, during which the rats’ vital signs were carefully monitored. After compression, the device was gently removed, restoring local blood perfusion and resulting in typical ischemia-reperfusion injury (3). Control Group: Rats in the control group were anesthetized, and the skin was prepared and sterilized under the same conditions, but no mechanical compression was applied. All operations were performed under aseptic conditions, and the rats were housed individually post-surgery. The wound was monitored daily, and antibiotic ointment was applied to prevent infection.

This model reliably simulates the pathophysiological processes of clinical PUs by precisely controlling compression time and pressure parameters. Pre-experimentation demonstrated that this method could induce typical pathological changes of PUs within 7 days, including epidermal necrosis, dermal inflammatory infiltration, and collagen degradation.

RT-qPCR validation of characterized gene expression

On the 7th day post-modeling, whole skin tissues were aseptically collected from both the center and 1 cm peripheral regions of the wounds in the control and PU groups. Additionally, human PU tissue samples (from the center and periphery) and healthy control skin samples collected clinically were processed similarly. All tissues were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen for quick-freezing and then stored at -80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the tissues using the TRIzol reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were verified using Nanodrop (A260/A280 ratio 1.8-2.0) (ND-2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One microgram of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis via reverse transcription with the PrimeScript RT kit (RR036A, Takara Bio, Japan). cDNA was quantified using SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (4309155, Applied Biosystems, USA) in the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (A28140, Applied Biosystems, USA) to measure the expression of seven characteristic genes, including CDKN1A, with GAPDH as the internal reference. Primer sequences (Table S1) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and all primers were verified for specificity using NCBI Primer-BLAST. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot validation of characterized protein expression

PU and normal control tissues were collected and stored at -80 °C after liquid nitrogen snap-freezing. Tissues were homogenized using RIPA lysis buffer (containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors) on ice, and the resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay and adjusted to 2 µg/µL. The samples were denatured by adding 5 × SDS sample buffer and heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Electrophoresis was performed using a 12% SDS-PAGE gel (20 µg per well), with the running conditions set to 80 V for the concentrate gel and 120 V for the separator gel. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer method (300 mA, 90 min). After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h. Primary antibodies, CDKN1A (rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000) (#ab109199, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), FOS (rabbit polyclonal, 1:2000) (#ab190289, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), JUN (rabbit monoclonal, 1:2000) (#ab40766, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), HDAC1 (rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000) (#ab280198, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), HIF1A (rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000) (#ab179483, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CCL2 (rabbit polyclonal, 1:2000) (#ab7202, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CCND1 (rabbit monoclonal, 1:20000) (#ab134175, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Collagen I (rabbit recombinant multiclonal, 1:1000) (#ab316222, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Collagen III (rabbit polyclona, 1:2000) (#ab7778, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and internal references GAPDH (rabbit monoclonal, 1:10000) (#ab181602, Abcam, Cambridge, UK)) or β-actin (rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000) (#ab8227, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1:5000, SA00001-1, Proteintech, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. ChemiDoc imaging was performed after ECL development, and the Image Lab software was used to analyze CDKN1A expression, normalized to the gray scale ratio of the internal reference.

Cell culture and transfection

Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) with catalog number PCS-201-010. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (catalog number 11995065, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (catalog number 10099141, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For CDKN1A knockdown experiments, fibroblasts were transfected with either a specific siRNA targeting CDKN1A (si-CDKN1A) or a si-negative control (si-NC), The si-CDKN1A sequence was synthesized by Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA (catalog number sc-817). The si-NC (catalog number sc-37007, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) was used as a control. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (catalog number L3000001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells at approximately 70-80% confluence were incubated with the siRNA-Lipofectamine 3000 complexes for 6 hours, after which the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting analysis of CDKN1A protein expression 48 h post-transfection, as described in section 2.12.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was quantitatively assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay at 48 hours post-transfection, following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, transfected human dermal fibroblasts were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and cultured under standard conditions. After the incubation period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were further incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the si-negative control (si-NC) group, with all experiments performed in triplicate.

Scratch wound healing assay

Cell migration capability was evaluated using a standardized scratch wound healing assay. After transfection, human dermal fibroblasts were cultured to form a confluent monolayer in 6-well plates. A uniform, straight scratch was created across the monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. The cells were then gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline to remove detached cells and incubated with fresh serum-free medium. Images of the scratch were captured at 0 hours (immediately after scratching) and 48 hours post-scratch using an inverted phase-contrast microscope at 100× magnification. The wound closure area was quantified by analyzing the images with ImageJ software (Version 1.53, National Institutes of Health, USA). To assess extracellular matrix secretion, the expression levels of Collagen I and Collagen III were detected by western blot in cell lysates collected 48 hours post-transfection, as described in detail in section 2.12.

Wound observation and assessment

Wound dynamics in WT and CDKN1A−/− PU rats were monitored on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 after PU model establishment. Standardized digital photography was used to document the wound progression: the rats were positioned on a shooting platform, and a digital camera (DS126831, Canon Inc., Japan) with a scale was used to capture images from a fixed distance (30 cm) and under controlled lighting (Kaiser RB-5007 LED Light Box, Kaiser Fototechnik, #RB-5007, Germany). The images were analyzed using ImageJ software (Version 1.53, National Institutes of Health, USA), and the pixel area of the wound was manually outlined and converted to actual area (mm2) based on the scale. The wound healing rate was calculated as: (wound area at 24 h after modeling - wound area at observation)/wound area at 24 h after modeling × 100%. Wound inflammation and necrosis were scored as follows: 0 (no abnormality), 1 (mild erythema), 2 (marked erythema with minor exudation), and 3 (severe erythema with purulent exudation or necrotic crusting). All assessments were performed independently by two blinded researchers.

Histopathological analysis

On the seventh day post-modeling, wound tissue was collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (J19943.K2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 24 h, and then embedded in paraffin. Serial sections, 4 μm thick, were prepared using a rotary slicer (14047235611, Leica Biosystems, Germany). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed using standard protocols: hematoxylin staining for 5 min, differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, and eosin staining for 2 min. Infiltration of inflammatory cells, epidermal regeneration, and granulation tissue formation were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan). Epidermal thickness (μm) was measured using the microscope's calibrated software in five random fields per sample. For Masson’s trichrome staining, collagen fibers were visualized in blue, and myofibrils in red. The arrangement of collagen fibers was analyzed using a polarized light microscope (Nikon LV100POL, Japan). Collagen fiber area percentage was quantified using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 image analysis system (Media Cybernetics, USA), by selecting five randomly chosen fields at 400 × magnification. Measurements were repeated three times for each sample, and the average value was calculated. All sections were evaluated independently by two pathologists in a double-blind manner.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software. The independent samples t-test was used for pairwise comparisons, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons across multiple groups. Levene’s test was used to assess equality of variances. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All tests were two-sided, with a significance level of α = 0.05. Welch’s correction was applied for data that were normally distributed but lacked homogeneity of variance, while nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test) were used for non-normally distributed data.

Results

Identification of AR-DEGs in PUs

The dataset GSE137897, which contained 381 PU samples and 391 HC samples, was first curated, resulting in an expression matrix comprising 23,876 genes (Table S2). Differential gene expression analysis identified a total of 1,976 DEGs between the PU and HC groups, including 1,620 upregulated genes and 356 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A). A heatmap was generated to visualize the top 50 most significantly expressed upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1B). Additionally, 6,136 ARGs were collected from the GeneCards database, and 840 ARGs were retained based on a relevance score > 2.00. These DEGs were then intersected with the ARGs, yielding 128 AR-DEGs (Fig. 2A). Figure 2B and C show the chromosomal distribution of these AR-DEGs across 23 human chromosomes.

Functions and pathway enrichment of 128 AR-DEGs in PUs

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to explore the potential roles of the 128 AR-DEGs. The results of GO enrichment analysis indicated that the BP terms were mainly related to epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of angiogenesis, amoeboid cell migration, and wound healing. CC terms were associated with collagen-containing extracellular matrix, adhesion plaques, cell-substrate junctions, outer plasma membrane, and endoplasmic reticulum lumen. MF terms included growth factor binding, glycosaminoglycan binding, sulfur compound binding, growth factor receptor binding, and structural components of the extracellular matrix (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the AR-DEGs were primarily involved in molecular pathways such as the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, adhesion plaques, MAPK signaling pathway, miRNAs and proteoglycans in cancer, human papillomavirus infection, lipids and atherosclerosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and Ras signaling pathway (Fig. 3B).

PPI network on 94 AR-DEGs

A PPI network was constructed by introducing 94 AR-DEGs (73 upregulated and 21 downregulated) after removing genes without interactions. The connectivity of each AR-DEG in the network was analyzed using the Degree algorithm, leading to the identification of the top 10 AR-DEGs with the highest connectivity, termed HAR-DEGs. Cytoscape software was used to visualize the AR-DEGs and HAR-DEGs in the interaction network, as shown in Fig. 4A and B, with the 10 HAR-DEGs highlighted: STAT3, JUN, MYC, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, RELA, HDAC1, CDKN1A, and CCL2.

Validation and correlation of HAR-DEGs in PUs

Box plots were generated to visualize the differential expression of the 10 HAR-DEGs between the PU and HC groups. CDKN1A, FOS, HDAC1, HIF1A, JUN, MYC, RELA, and STAT3 were significantly upregulated in the PU group, while CCL2 and CCND1 were significantly downregulated in the PU group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5). The AUC values for JUN, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, HDAC1, CDKN1A, and CCL2, shown in Fig. 6A, were all greater than 0.700, indicating high diagnostic accuracy for PU. Furthermore, Fig. 6B and C show the expression correlation between the characterized genes. CDKN1A expression exhibited a strong negative correlation with CCND1, CCL2, and FOS (correlation coefficient < -0.3 and p-value < 0.05). HIF1A expression showed a strong positive correlation with CDKN1A and HDAC1 (correlation coefficient > 0.3 and p-value < 0.05), as well as with CCL2 and CCND1 (correlation coefficient > 0.3 and p-value < 0.05).

Regulatory network between characterized genes and targeted drugs

Using the DGIdb database, an extended table of interactions between the characterized genes and their targeted drugs was obtained (Table S5). A drug-target regulatory network was then visualized using Cytoscape, based on interactions with scores greater than 0.5. In this network, characteristic genes are represented by red ellipse nodes, while molecular drugs are depicted by green triangle nodes. The connecting lines indicate interactions between the nodes. The drug regulatory network includes 7 characteristic genes, 78 molecular drugs, and 78 interaction pairs, as shown in Fig. 7C.

CDKN1A expression was significantly up-regulated in PU: Validation from human data to rat models

Consistent with the bioinformatics predictions, the expression of the seven identified hub genes (FOS, JUN, CDKN1A, HDAC1, HIF1A, CCL2, CCND1) was validated across different experimental contexts, confirming their relevance to PU pathology. Figure 8A,B presents validation data primarily derived from human clinical samples, illustrating the differential expression patterns between PU and control groups. It demonstrates that the mRNA and protein expression levels of FOS, JUN, CDKN1A, and HDAC1 were significantly upregulated in the PU context, while CCND1 was downregulated. Notably, CDKN1A exhibited the most pronounced upregulation at the transcriptional level. Figure 8C, D provides focused experimental validation specifically from the established rat PU model, offering detailed molecular evidence. The results consistently show significant upregulation of CDKN1A at both mRNA and protein levels in PU tissues compared to controls. Quantitative densitometric analysis revealed that protein expression mirrored the transcriptional upregulation, the magnitude of increase was greater than that of mRNA levels, suggesting the involvement of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. These consistent findings at both the transcriptional and translational levels strongly suggest that CDKN1A plays a critical role in PU pathology. Its upregulation likely reflects involvement in key biological processes, including cell cycle regulation and tissue repair during PU development.

Silencing CDKN1A promotes fibroblast function in vitro

To explore the functional role of CDKN1A in wound healing, CDKN1A expression was knocked down in human dermal fibroblasts using siRNA (Fig. 9A). The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that silencing CDKN1A significantly enhanced cell viability compared to the si-NC group (Fig. 9B) (P < 0.05). The scratch wound healing assay further showed that CDKN1A knockdown markedly accelerated cell migration, resulting in a larger wound closure area after 48 h (Fig. 9C). Western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of Collagen I and Collagen III were significantly increased in the si-CDKN1A group (Fig. 9D) (P < 0.001), indicating enhanced collagen synthesis and secretion.

CDKN1A deficiency inhibits wound formation and promotes healing in a rat PU model

To assess the in vivo effect of CDKN1A, wound healing was compared between WT and CDKN1A−/− rats subjected to PU modeling. Genetic ablation of CDKN1A significantly accelerated PU healing in rat models (Fig. 10). Longitudinal wound measurements showed enhanced healing kinetics in CDKN1A−/− animals (Fig. 10A). Starting from day 3 post-modeling, knockout rats exhibited significantly smaller wound areas (P < 0.05), with the difference increasing over time. By day 9, CDKN1A−/− wounds displayed a 40% greater healing rate compared to WT controls, accompanied by more prominent wound edge epithelialization. Histopathological analysis revealed clear distinctions between the groups: WT rats exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and immature granulation tissue formation (Fig. 10B, C), while CDKN1A−/− specimens showed markedly reduced inflammatory responses. Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated improved tissue remodeling in knockout rats, with CDKN1A−/− wounds showing more abundant collagen deposition (68 ± 7% vs. 42 ± 5% in WT, P < 0.001), although the fibers remained loosely organized compared to the sparse, disordered collagen observed in WT controls. Notably, CDKN1A deficiency was associated with a sustained reduction in inflammatory scores throughout the observation period (1.2 ± 0.4 vs. 2.8 ± 0.6 in WT, P < 0.01). Western blot analysis confirmed successful CDKN1A knockout in the CDKN1A−/− rats under both normal and PU conditions (Fig. 10D). Collectively, these findings highlight that CDKN1A deletion influences critical aspects of wound healing, including inflammation modulation, collagen synthesis, and tissue remodeling.

Discussion

PUs are common chronic wounds with a high mortality rate, yet their molecular pathology remains unclear, hindering the development of effective treatments [16]. Recent advances in bioinformatics and sequencing technologies have uncovered cellular diversity and intercellular communication, making them key areas of research in understanding disease mechanisms [17]. The present study utilized bioinformatics approaches to identify DEGs from high-throughput sequencing transcriptome profiles, aiming to uncover key molecular regulatory mechanisms and provide new insights for clinical treatment of PUs.

In this study, 1,976 DEGs were identified from the transcriptome data of 381 patients with PU and 391 HCs in the GEO dataset GSE137897. By intersecting these with 6,136 ARGs from the GeneCards database, 128 potential AR-DEGs were screened. GO enrichment analysis revealed that BP terms primarily involved epithelial cell proliferation and regulation, angiogenesis, amoeboid cell migration, and wound healing. CC terms were related to collagen-containing extracellular matrix, adhesion plaques, cell-substrate junctions, the outer plasma membrane, and the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum. MF terms included growth factor binding, glycosaminoglycan binding, sulfur compound binding, growth factor receptor binding, and structural components of the extracellular matrix. KEGG pathway analysis showed that these AR-DEGs were primarily involved in pathways such as the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, adhesion plaques, MAPK signaling pathway, miRNAs and proteoglycans in cancer, human papillomavirus infection, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and Ras signaling pathway. Apoptosis [18], inflammation [19], and neoangiogenesis play critical roles in the development of PU [20]. The enriched pathways identified in this study are all associated with these processes, supporting the validity of the findings.

The enrichment of AR-DEGs in pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and HIF-1 signaling corroborates the well-established role of these pathways in angiogenesis and wound repair. Our contribution is the systematic identification of these pathways within the specific context of PU using angiogenesis-focused gene sets, and the subsequent prioritization of hub genes from this analysis for functional study. This study incorporated 94 AR-DEGs into a PPI network. Using the Degree algorithm, the top 10 most highly connected hub AR-DEGs (HAR-DEGs) were identified: STAT3, JUN, MYC, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, RELA, HDAC1, CDKN1A, and CCL2. These findings suggest that PU formation may be associated with dysregulation of ARGs. Among these, STAT3 plays a central role by binding to response elements in the cytosol of target gene promoters. This binding activates extensive transcription of genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, growth factors, and cytokines. Notably, STAT3 enhances the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a key angiogenic factor, and contributes to immunosuppression, such as through IL-6 regulation [21]. JUN, an integral component of the Activator Protein-1 (AP-1) TF complex, is implicated in various cellular processes. Its involvement in cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion [22] makes it relevant to PU and wound healing. Moreover, JUN plays a critical role in regulating VEGF expression. As VEGF is a key pro-angiogenic factor in angiogenesis [23], JUN’s modulation of VEGF highlights its importance in tissue angiogenesis, a key process in pressure ulcer healing [24]. In addition to JUN, several other transcription factors among the identified HAR-DEGs have also been implicated in angiogenesis. For instance, MYC has been reported to participate in angiogenic processes [25], while HIF1A is well established as a key regulator of hypoxia-driven angiogenesis [26]. CCND1 has likewise been associated with angiogenic activity [27]. Moreover, FOS [28], RELA [29], HDAC1 [30], CDKN1A [31], and CCL2 [32] have all been linked to the regulation of angiogenesis in previous studies. The enrichment of these genes among the top-connected nodes in our PPI network further supports a potential association between dysregulated angiogenesis-related gene expression and the development of pressure sores.

The differential expression of the 10 HAR-DEGs was visualized using box plots, revealing that JUN, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, HDAC1, CDKN1A, and CCL2 were significantly relevant, while STAT3 and MYC were excluded. Cell cycle protein-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 A (CDKN1A), a key marker of cellular senescence, is closely linked to the inflammatory response in diseases and plays a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, senescence, and angiogenesis [33]. Cyclin D1, a pivotal cell cycle protein, is often amplified and overexpressed in various tissues, playing a central role in cell proliferation and apoptosis [34]. CCL2, or monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1), belongs to the CC chemokine family and is secreted by monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and platelets. Platelet-derived growth factor is a major inducer of CCL2 expression. CCL2 has been widely implicated in angiogenesis [35]. The present study found that CDKN1A expression strongly negatively correlated with CCND1, CCL2, and FOS (correlation coefficient < -0.3, p-value < 0.05). Additionally, HIF1A showed a strong positive correlation with CDKN1A and HDAC1 (correlation coefficient > 0.3, p-value < 0.05), while a negative correlation was seen with CCL2 and CCND1 (correlation coefficient < -0.3, p-value < 0.05).

This study constructed a signature gene-miRNA regulatory network encompassing seven ARGs (JUN, HIF1A, CCND1, FOS, HDAC1, CDKN1A, and CCL2) with their corresponding miRNAs and TFs. This network comprised seven signature genes, 38 miRNAs, and 221 interaction pairs. Similarly, the signature gene-TF regulatory network included 7 signature genes, 203 TFs, and 359 interactions. In the miRNA-regulatory network, hsa-miR-124 was identified as targeting all seven signature genes. miR-124 is a species-specific miRNA, and the downregulation of miR-124-3p has been observed during retinal vascular endothelial cell angiogenesis in vitro [36]. In the signature gene-TF regulatory network, the TF EGR1 was found to target all seven signature genes. Early growth response 1 (EGR1), a key small molecule involved in nuclear transcription, plays a critical role in processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, senescence, and apoptosis [37]. Previous studies have linked EGR1 to cardiovascular, digestive, neurological, and tumor diseases [38]. Notably, Yang et al. demonstrated that miR-124 targeting EGR1 in an isoflurane-induced neurological dysfunction model in neonatal rats impacted disease progression [39, 40]. This study integrated the signature gene-miRNA and signature gene-TF regulatory networks to hypothesize that PU formation may be driven by miR-124 targeting EGR1, which in turn modulates angiogenesis and facilitates PU development.

In this study, the DGIdb database was utilized to construct a drug-gene interaction regulatory network, which was visualized using Cytoscape. This network comprised 7 signature genes, 78 molecular drugs, and 78 interaction pairs, with tumor angiogenesis inhibitors (TAs) being most closely associated with the signature genes. Tumor angiogenesis inhibitors are a class of drugs that prevent tumor formation and metastasis by disrupting or inhibiting angiogenesis. Their mechanisms of action include: targeting vascular endothelial cells to affect their proliferation and apoptosis; regulating the endothelial cell microenvironment to inhibit tumor angiogenesis; and exerting combined effects. Based on the characteristic gene-miRNA-TF regulatory network, tumor angiogenesis inhibitors may block the expression of pro-angiogenic factors, enhance the expression of inhibitory factors at the gene level, and disrupt vascular endothelial cell receptor signaling pathways.

The drug-gene interaction network identified numerous compounds, including several approved tumor angiogenesis inhibitors, that theoretically target the PU hub genes (Fig. 7C and Table S5). This list provides a conceptual starting point for investigating drug repurposing strategies for PU. However, it is crucial to recognize that most of these drugs are not approved for wound care, and their systemic toxicity profiles may be unsuitable for chronic wound management. The network presented here is a prediction-based resource; rigorous preclinical validation in wound models, safety assessments, and prioritization based on wound-specific pharmacology are essential steps before any clinical translation can be considered. The regulatory networks involving miRNAs, TFs, and potential drugs were constructed based on database predictions. These networks represent putative interactions and offer a valuable resource for hypothesizing regulatory mechanisms and identifying candidate molecules for further investigation. However, the specific interactions within these networks, such as the proposed miR-124-EGR1-CDKN1A axis, remain speculative and require dedicated experimental validation in future studies to confirm their biological relevance in PU.

Interestingly, the pathological microenvironment of chronic non-healing PUs shares conceptual similarities with the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). Similar to tumors, chronic wounds such as PUs are characterized by persistent inflammation, dysregulated immune cell infiltration, and abnormal angiogenesis, which collectively impede tissue repair [41–43]. In the TME, immune checkpoint interactions and pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways often suppress effective immune responses and promote a dysfunctional vasculature that supports tumor growth. Likewise, in PUs, prolonged inflammatory signaling and altered interactions between immune cells (e.g., macrophages), endothelial cells, and fibroblasts may impair angiogenesis and hinder tissue remodeling. The hub gene CDKN1A (p21), identified in this study, plays context-dependent roles in both cell cycle arrest and modulation of inflammatory responses, key processes in cancer biology and wound healing. Drawing parallels between these processes may offer novel therapeutic insights; for example, strategies aimed at “re-educating” the wound immune microenvironment or normalizing angiogenic signaling—concepts derived from immunotherapy and vascular normalization in oncology—could offer promising approaches for PU treatment.

In this study, CDKN1A was significantly upregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in the PU model, consistent with previous research on CDKN1A’s role in tissue injury. As a critical regulator of the cell cycle, increased CDKN1A expression likely contributes to the inhibition of local cell proliferation and delays the tissue repair process [44]. Notably, the protein level of CDKN1A in this study was lower than its mRNA level, a discrepancy that may be attributed to several factors: (1) the hypoxic microenvironment in PUs, which could reduce protein translation efficiency via pathways like HIF-1α; (2) specific miRNAs (e.g., the miR-17-92 family) that may post-transcriptionally regulate CDKN1A mRNA; and (3) the activation of proteasomal degradation mechanisms. These hypotheses warrant further investigation in future studies.

The pro-healing effect of CDKN1A gene deletion is particularly noteworthy, as supported by both in vitro and in vivo gain-of-function and loss-of-function data. In fibroblasts, a key cell type in wound repair, silencing CDKN1A led to enhanced cell proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis. Histopathological analysis revealed that the inflammatory response in wounds of CDKN1A−/− rats was significantly reduced, aligning with the well-established pro-inflammatory role of CDKN1A [45]. It has been demonstrated that CDKN1A promotes the release of inflammatory factors through the NF-κB pathway, and this process was attenuated upon gene knockdown. More importantly, Masson staining revealed that CDKN1A deletion significantly increased collagen deposition and improved collagen fiber alignment. This effect is likely due to two mechanisms: first, CDKN1A deletion relieved the inhibition on fibroblast proliferation, leading to a higher number of collagen-producing cells; second, it may have altered the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, which regulates the balance between collagen synthesis and degradation.

The functional validation of CDKN1A focused on its impact on dermal fibroblast viability, migration, and collagen secretion, as well as on overall wound closure, inflammation, and collagen deposition in vivo. These parameters are intimately linked to the formation of granulation tissue, which is a prerequisite for successful angiogenesis. While our findings demonstrate that CDKN1A inhibition creates a pro-healing environment, they do not provide direct evidence for its effect on endothelial cell function or new blood vessel formation. The acceleration of wound healing in CDKN1A-/- rats may result from a combination of improved fibroblast function, modulated inflammation, and potentially indirect effects on angiogenesis. Future studies should include direct assessments of angiogenesis, such as endothelial cell tube formation assays or immunohistochemical analysis of CD31/VEGF in wound tissues, to elucidate CDKN1A’s specific role in vascular regeneration during PU healing.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study have important translational value. Current clinical treatments for PUs primarily focus on symptomatic management, with a lack of targeted therapies. Given that CDKN1A is a pharmacologically modifiable target, its inhibitors could provide a promising direction for developing novel pro-healing drugs. Notably, our study indicates that CDKN1A deficiency both reduces inflammation and enhances tissue remodeling, a dual action that is particularly beneficial in the context of chronic wound healing. However, considering CDKN1A’s critical role in tumor suppression, future research must investigate safe regulatory windows that promote healing without triggering tumorigenesis.

Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations. First, the animal model used does not fully replicate the complex pathological processes of human PUs. Second, the specific downstream pathways regulated by CDKN1A remain unidentified, and long-term safety assessments have not been conducted. Future research should focus on several key areas: using single-cell sequencing to investigate the roles of CDKN1A in different cell types; developing targeted delivery systems for local regulation of CDKN1A expression; and conducting multi-center clinical studies to validate the expression pattern of CDKN1A in human PUs.

Conclusion

This study identified AR-DEGs in PUs by integrating GEO and GeneCards data, followed by GO/KEGG and PPI network analysis. A regulatory network linking AR-DEGs with miRNAs, TFs, and drugs was constructed, providing insights for drug screening in PU treatment. Notably, CDKN1A emerged as a key regulator in PU healing, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target. These findings lay a foundation for developing more precise and effective treatments for PUs.
