ABSTRACT
IntroductionBiomarkers have been identified to predict, diagnose and prognosticate acute kidney injury (AKI) but existing studies are heterogenous and contradictory.ObjectiveTo compare diagnostic performance of AKI biomarkers, evaluate the quality of AKI biomarker studies and to develop standards for reporting studies of diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) of AKI biomarkers.MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted to identify studies focusing on the diagnostic performance of AKI biomarkers published before February 2025. Retrieved DTA studies were assessed for methodological quality and completeness using the QUADAS-2 and Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) 2015 checklists. An international 17 member expert panel was convened to agree consensus standards for AKI biomarker studies (STARDaki) via a modified Delphi process.Results122 DTA studies for AKI biomarkers were identified, but 15 were insufficiently reported. Of the remaining 107 studies, only 19 reported on diagnosis of AKI within 48 h of sampling. Of these studies, only 16 were considered high-quality based on the QUADAS-2 criteria. The compliance level with the STARD checklist was too low to permit meta-analysis. The expert panel agreed criteria for patient selection, reference standards, and reporting of test–retest reliability to supplement the STARD guidance for AKI biomarker studies.ConclusionMost studies examining AKI biomarker performance fail to conform to the STARD standards for reporting, leading to poor diagnostic accuracy estimates and reduced clinical applicability and generalizability. An expert panel proposed STARDaki criteria to advance the development and clinical use of AKI biomarkers (www.stardaki.icu).Visual abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00134-026-08549-5.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious clinical problem with a prevalence of 5–12% in hospitalized patients and 50% or more in the intensive care unit (ICU) [1–3]. AKI is associated with a high mortality rate, especially in ICU patients and in those with severe AKI where it can exceed 50% [1]. Patients surviving an AKI episode may face progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD) and an increased risk of cardiovascular events [4, 5].

AKI is defined by a rise in serum creatinine and/or decrease in urine output based on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [6]. However, these markers reflect kidney dysfunction rather than structural injury and have inherent limitations, including a substantial detection lag and susceptibility to non-renal confounders such as muscle mass, fluid balance, liver disease, common medications, and renal replacement therapy [7]. This may lead to delays in diagnosis and missed opportunities for prevention and management [8].

Various novel biomarkers have been identified to detect early kidney injury before functional decline occurs, including neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) and the product of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP-2) and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), which have received regulatory approval in Europe and the United States [9–12]. However, existing biomarker studies are heterogeneous with variable results, and the clinical utility of these tools remains incompletely defined.

The Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) were developed to improve the transparency and comparability of diagnostic accuracy research [13]. The 2015 update refined the checklist to 25 items emphasizing bias assessment and applicability [14]. Several domain-specific STARD extensions have since been published, including STARDdem for dementia [15], STARD-BLCM (2017) for Bayesian Latent Class Models [16], and STRADAS-paraTB for paratuberculosis [17]. These extensions retain STARD's foundational principles while addressing methodological nuances unique to specialized technologies or clinical settings.

A critical methodological characteristic of AKI biomarker research merits explicit discussion. In most diseases, the diagnostic reference standard is based on pathological or anatomical evidence (e.g., tissue biopsy, angiography). In AKI, however, renal biopsy is rarely feasible, and the current KDIGO standard relies on functional surrogates rather than direct tissue evidence. When novel biomarkers detect tubular stress or injury before creatinine rises, this is often termed 'prediction,' but it more accurately reflects detection of injury before the functional standard can detect it. This ambiguity is not a design flaw, but a consequence of a reference standard reliant on delayed functional signals. STARD remains appropriate for evaluating an index test against the KDIGO standard[14].

This study aims to address the critical gaps in the reporting and methodological standardization of AKI biomarker studies. The objectives were to review existing AKI biomarker studies and a) to evaluate the reporting quality (based on the STARD criteria) and the methodological quality according to the QUADAS-2 (Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies version 2) checklist [18]; b) to identify sources of heterogeneity; c) to summarize the diagnostic or prognostic performance of candidate biomarkers to inform their clinical utility; and d) to develop the STARD extension for AKI biomarkers (STARDaki). The overall design was informed by the development of STARDdem[15], which employed a systematic review and Delphi framework. The present work is therefore best characterized as a consensus statement that proposes a domain-specific extension of STARD for AKI biomarker studies.

Methods

Systematic reviews

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement and pre-registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024590896) [19]. The search for pertinent studies was systematically conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. The search time frame was set from the inception of each database until February 1, 2025. The complete search strategy is presented in Appendix 1.

Literature screening and information extraction

Any studies reporting diagnostic results for AKI biomarkers were considered. However, studies with timeframes from sampling to diagnosis of AKI lasting longer than 48 h or lack of information on baseline kidney function, exact timeframe, assay tools were excluded from meta-analysis or descriptive analysis. This 48-h window was chosen based on the physiological rationale that serum creatinine elevation is delayed following kidney injury, often by 24–36 h, and that as glomerular filtration rate declines, the half-life of creatinine increases, further delaying detection of AKI to up to 48 h. Consequently, biomarkers detected within this window may capture tubular injury before a creatinine rise is established. The STARD 2015 checklist was used to assess the reporting quality of included trials, and the QUADAS-2 instrument was used to evaluate the quality of methodology [14, 18]. QUADAS-2 was used to assess risk of bias(ROB) across four domains (patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing); detailed criteria are provided in Appendix 2. The literature screening and quality assessment were undertaken independently by two authors (HY&GPM); a third expert (ZP) was consulted in case of any disagreement.

Evidence synthesis

The PROSPERO protocol pre-specified meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy measures for biomarkers with at least five published reports. However, upon review of the included studies, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity became apparent, including differences in patient populations, biomarker assays, timing of measurement, diagnostic thresholds, and reference standard implementation, that would violate the assumptions of hierarchical summary of ROC models. Formal quantitative synthesis was therefore not performed, and results, including sensitivity and specificity, are descriptively presented as ranges across studies, stratified by biomarker type, clinical context (cardiac surgery-associated AKI, sepsis-associated AKI), and baseline creatinine level.

Extension development

Expert panel formation

Experts were identified based on their academic impact in AKI, as quantified by citation metrics compiled by Expertscape.com. The list was refined to ensure adequate representation of experts in biomarkers and clinical biostatistics, and to achieve diversity in terms of sex, career stage, specialty, and geographic region of practice. The final 17-member panel included experts from 10 countries across Europe, North America, and Asia. Disciplinary representation spanned critical care medicine, nephrology, anesthesiology, pediatric intensive care, and clinical biostatistics. Two panel members were women. Career stages ranged from early-career investigators to senior professors.

Modified delphi survey

The panel followed the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method, a structured consensus approach that integrates iterative Delphi surveys with moderated panel discussions to refine recommendations [20]. After an initial version of the STARDaki checklist was drafted, it was refined through a modified Delphi process, including an open-ended survey followed by multiple rounds of closed Likert scale-based 1–5 scoring and collection of feedback. Participants and feedback were anonymized by unique identifiers; the process was managed independently from the data synthesis. The Delphi process concluded when all of the following criteria were met: 1) the mean consensus score for all checklist items exceeded 4 points (on a five-point Likert scale), 2) the Agreement Ratio (AR) reached 80%, and 3) the expert panel voted to finalize the process [21]. Additionally, the dispersion of scores (Standard Deviation, SD) was calculated. The formulas for the mean consensus score, AR, and SD are listed in Appendix 1 Table S8.

Finalization and release of the STARDaki extension

Following the conclusion of the Delphi process, we compiled the results into a provisional extension for confirmation by the expert panel. After the collaborative refinement, the expert panel conducted a final vote to approve and formally release the extension.

Results

Systematic review

Literature search and study characteristics

The literature search resulted in 13,075 articles. After exclusion of 4563 duplicates, an additional 8372 articles were excluded through title and abstract screening. Full texts of 122 papers were screened. Of these 122, five did not provide data on AKI occurrence, five did not report extractable diagnostic data, nine did not provide complete descriptions of biomarker measurements and outcome follow-up, and one was a letter without complete evidence available for ROB evaluation (Appendix 2). This yielded 107 articles, of which only 28 (27%) reported AKI within 48 h after biomarker sampling (literature screening process presented in Fig. 1). Of these 28 studies, 19 reported sufficient methodological detail—including baseline kidney function, AKI definition and staging, sampling and event timing, and assay methodology—to permit meaningful descriptive evaluation. Of the 19 included studies, 11 achieved the highest QUADAS-2 rating (score of 7). Quality reporting

All 107 included studies underwent STARD compliance and QUADAS-2 quality assessments. Evaluation of compliance with STARD 2015 criteria demonstrated heterogeneous reporting patterns. Core items such as structured abstracts and study objectives achieved near-perfect adherence (97–100%). However, there were gaps in critical methodological details: only 5% of articles documented whether clinical information and index test results were available to the investigators assessing the reference standard (item 13b), 17% reported sample size justification (item 18), 4% clarified how indeterminate results were handled (item 15), and definitions of, and rationale for, test cutoffs or result categories of the index test (items 12a/b) were incompletely reported by 34–74% of studies. Overall, on average, each report only properly reported 54.5% (14.7%-76.5%) of the STARD items. For urinary [TIMP-2] × [IGFBP7], sensitivity ranged from 23 to 100% and specificity from 39 to 96% across individual studies (Table 1). The 23% lower bound originated from a study applying the 0.3 (ng/mL)2/1000 cutoff to detect stage 2–3 AKI over 48 h in a mixed ICU population, a context differing substantially from the biomarker's FDA-approved indication (prediction of moderate-to-severe AKI within 12 h)[22]. When restricted to any-stage AKI within shorter sampling windows, sensitivity exceeded 70%. For serum NGAL, sensitivity ranged from 45 to 93% and specificity from 69 to 100%, reflecting the diversity of clinical settings (cardiac surgery, sepsis, general ICU) and AKI definitions (KDIGO, AKIN, RIFLE, AKIN plus RIFLE) across the contributing studies.

Of the 19 studies, 11 achieved the highest QUADAS-2 rating (score of 7); only 10 articles met the 70% threshold for STARD compliance, and none achieved full compliance. In the quality-restricted subset (7 studies at QUADAS-2 score 7), urinary [TIMP-2] × [IGFBP7] performance was consistent with the full dataset. Taken together, the wide performance ranges observed across studies are not shortcomings of individual biomarkers but a predictable consequence of applying fixed cutoffs across heterogeneous populations, using different AKI definitions, and applying variable sampling windows. This observation reinforced the rationale for standardized reporting as operationalized in STARDaki.

STARDaki expert panel and evaluation of current AKI biomarker studies

The expert panel identified critical methodological limitations in existing AKI biomarker research. First, temporal ambiguity was widespread: the majority of studies failed to define the relationship between biomarker sampling, AKI risk exposure periods, and timelines of diagnostic confirmation, undermining diagnostic accuracy estimates. Second, clinical granularity was often insufficient: most studies omitted AKI severity stratification while about 70% lacked baseline kidney function data, compromising both the credibility of endpoint classification as well as risk-adjusted analyses. Third, non-standardized outcome adjudication prevailed: few studies documented clinical endpoint verification by independent committees.

Technical shortcomings further limited reliability: many studies utilized research-grade assays without clinical-grade validation, and the discordance between functional markers (creatinine) and injury markers (e.g., urinary [TIMP-2] × [IGFBP7]) complicated validation. The Panel endorsed [TIMP-2]x[IGFBP7] and NGAL as the biomarkers with most robust evidence for early AKI detection and urged prospective validation in biomarker-naïve cohorts.

Modified delphi survey

The 17 experts panel evaluated the 17 STARDaki checklist items in a modified online survey. Sixteen experts participated in round 1. The average score of all items was 4.66(4.44–4.94), with a standard deviation of 0.54(0.24, 0.87). The average AR was 95.40% (81.30%-100%) (Fig. 3a). Although the criteria for "consensus" were met in the first round, we revised the text and checklist based on the collected comments and conducted a second round of scoring. Finalization and release

The expert panel approved the final STARDaki checklist (Table 2), which supplements the STARD 2015 items with AKI-specific requirements (www.stardaki.icu). Key additions include mandatory reporting of baseline kidney function, CKD status, and AKI etiology in eligibility criteria; documentation of assay platform and validation parameters; explicit specification of sampling time windows relative to clinical triggers of AKI; stratification of diagnostic accuracy by AKI severity; and reporting of fluid status, nephrotoxic exposures, and methods for handling missing data. Studies should also discuss context-specific clinical utility and distinguish adverse events and etiological subgroup comparisons. Discussion

Our analysis underscores a critical gap in AKI biomarker research. Only a few of the evaluated DTA studies adhere to established reporting standards or methodological assessment criteria. This widespread non-compliance exacerbates methodological heterogeneity and undermines the validity of pooled evidence [23, 24]. Importantly for clinical practice, it also limits the ability of clinicians to critically appraise biomarker studies for bedside decision-making. Specifically, without transparent reporting of sampling windows, assay characteristics, and baseline kidney function, the clinical utility of even well-conducted studies remains uncertain. STARDaki directly addresses this gap by standardizing the reporting elements that clinicians need to judge whether a biomarker's performance is applicable to their patient population and clinical setting. As the Cochrane Handbook for DTA reviews notes, the reporting quality of included trials may dramatically alter conclusions [25].

A notable exception is urinary [TIMP-2]x[IGFBP7] [26, 27]. Early validation studies demonstrated its favorable diagnostic efficacy for predicting moderate-to-severe AKI within 12 h [11, 22], leading to FDA approval in 2014 [27]. Its performance in clinical translation is supported by rigorous methodological transparency, including standardized sampling protocols, explicit temporal alignment with AKI triggers, and clinical-grade assay validation. As the only FDA-approved AKI biomarker panel for adults, urinary [TIMP-2]x[IGFBP7] illustrates how high-quality reporting can facilitate the translation of bench discoveries into bedside tools [28]. FDA also approved urinary NGAL for pediatric use [29], though the available evidence was insufficient to support a recommendation in the present review.

The STARDaki checklist codifies three principles to address systemic limitations. First, precise biomarker sampling windows should be mandated to prevent validity erosion from delayed measurements (temporal sensitivity). Second, baseline kidney function and fluid status reporting should be enforced to isolate biomarker-disease relationships (confounder control). Third, harmonizing with KDIGO/ADQI frameworks should occur while guarding against circularity in novel biomarker validation (standard compatibility).

Current AKI criteria provide an imperfect window into actual kidney health, involving arbitrary cutoffs in a continuum of injury/dysfunction. The STARDaki recommendations are intended for assessing biomarkers against the current KDIGO standard and do not provide benchmarks for "acceptable performance". For instance, the performance of TIMP-2 and IGFBP7 may be near the biological maximum achievable given the current AKI standard. The STARDaki framework evaluates biomarker performance against the current KDIGO reference standard. Because biomarkers often detect tubular injury that precedes functional decline, studies in this field inherently operate at the interface between early diagnosis and prediction. While STARDaki remains appropriate for studies using KDIGO criteria as the reference standard, studies focused primarily on risk prediction model development should follow the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guidelines [30]. We have explicitly delineated these boundaries to guide authors in selecting the appropriate reporting framework.

Standards may evolve. Emerging AKI definitions that integrate functional and injury markers [12] may eventually shift the reference standard against which biomarkers are evaluated. However, biomarker selection remains contentious, hindered by inconsistent reporting and insufficient high-quality evidence. Here, STARDaki provides a critical pathway: by standardizing biomarker performance reporting, it enables robust cross-study comparisons to identify optimal biomarker panels for next-generation diagnostic criteria. If novel biomarkers are incorporated into future AKI definitions, they will immediately attain perfect performance as part of the gold standard. Such evaluations are beyond the scope of these extensions, but the standardized reporting facilitated by STARDaki will minimize bias in future efforts to redefine AKI and incorporate biomarkers.

STARDaki provides specialized reporting extensions for studies evaluating the DTA of biomarkers for diagnosing AKI early. Its scope extends to assessing biomarker performance for evaluating AKI progression or prognostic outcomes. However, its methodological boundaries must be clarified: studies focused on prediction models, even if they employ DTA-related metrics like sensitivity, specificity or AUC, should follow the TRIPOD extensions, not STARDaki [31, 32]. This is particularly relevant to the combination of biomarkers, an area with extensive literature and diverse analytical tools. The combination of biomarkers, whether in isolation or with clinical data for risk prediction modeling, is beyond the scope of this document and requires a TRIPOD-like framework. While both STARDaki and TRIPOD emphasize transparency and reporting completeness, their core objectives lead to fundamental structural differences: DTA studies focus on validating agreement between an index test and a reference standard (e.g., KDIGO criteria), whereas prediction model studies concentrate on model development, internal/external validation, and clinical utility analysis. Given AKI-specific complexities, such as reference standard heterogeneity and dynamic time-dependent variables, future development of a TRIPOD-AKI extension may be warranted. Furthermore, formal discussions on these results with broader public representatives were not held. Future updates to this extension and the development of similar standards may benefit from incorporating broader stakeholder engagement.

Conclusions

By adopting STARDaki, future biomarker studies can replicate the rigor of landmark trials, improve cross-study comparability, and accelerate the translation of emerging biomarkers into practice (www.stardaki.icu). STARDaki-compliant reporting would also enable reliable subgroup analyses and clarify contexts where biomarkers outperform creatinine-based diagnostics. Ultimately, this framework empowers the AKI research community to navigate biomarker validation with methodological precision, fostering discoveries that are both scientifically robust and clinically transformative.
