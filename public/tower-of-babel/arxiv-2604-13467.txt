Abstract

1
Introduction

Let
{\cal A}
be a finite alphabet and let
\Omega={\cal A}^{{\mathds{N}}}
be the one–sided shift
space, equipped with the
\sigma
–field
{\cal F}
generated by the cylinder sets

[x_{1}^{N}]:=\{y\in\Omega:y_{1}^{N}=x_{1}^{N}\}
, where
x_{1}^{N}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
for

x=(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in\Omega
.
Throughout,
{\mathds{P}}
denotes a shift–invariant probability measure on

(\Omega,{\cal F})
, and
T:\Omega\to\Omega
,
(Tx)_{k}=x_{k+1}
, the left shift.

The Shannon–McMillan–Breiman (SMB) theorem is a cornerstone of information theory
and ergodic theory.
It asserts that there exists a shift–invariant function
h_{\mathds{P}}\in{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
(the
entropy rate function) such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])=h_{\mathds{P}}(x),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}),

and whose expectation
{\mathds{E}}_{\mathds{P}}(h_{\mathds{P}})=h({\mathds{P}})
is the entropy rate of
{\mathds{P}}

(see (
2.2
) in Section
2.1
).
If
{\mathds{P}}
is ergodic, then
h_{\mathds{P}}=h({\mathds{P}})
holds
{\mathds{P}}
–a.s. (see, e.g.,

[
McM53
,
Bre57
,
Bre60
,
Shi96
]
).

In many applications, however, one does not work directly with full blocks of the form

x_{1}^{N}
, but rather with variable–length
parsings
of these blocks.
In full generality, a parsing of
x_{1}^{N}
is a decomposition

\displaystyle x_{1}^{N}=w_{1}^{(N)}(x)\cdots w_{c_{N}(x)}^{(N)}(x)

(1.1)

into contiguous blocks, whose lengths may vary arbitrarily and may depend on the
underlying sequence
x
.
Such parsings arise naturally in parsing–based
entropy and cross–entropy estimation schemes, including those inspired by the methodology of
Lempel–Ziv
[
LZ77
,
LZ78
,
MZ93
]
. Parsings also appear in
ergodic–theoretic constructions, where discrete–time trajectories are
partitioned into contiguous blocks according to stopping times or successive
return times; see, for example,
[
Shi96
]
. Finally, block decompositions of
discrete–time samples are a standard tool in large–deviation and concentration
arguments, where long trajectories are split into contiguous blocks and boundary
effects are controlled under additional dependence assumptions; see, e.g.,

[
Rio00
]
.

Given a parsing as in (
1.1
), we consider the quantity

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}-\log{\mathds{P}}\bigl([w_{i}^{(N)}(x)]\bigr),

(1.2)

and ask under which conditions on the parsing and the underlying measure
{\mathds{P}}
the quantity in (
1.2
)
converges
{\mathds{P}}
–almost surely and in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
to the entropy rate function
h_{\mathds{P}}
.

A central difficulty in answering this question is that the quantity (
1.2
)
replaces the probability of the entire block
x_{1}^{N}
by a product of probabilities
of smaller blocks, thereby suppressing all dependence between successive
segments. The SMB theorem, by contrast, concerns the single global quantity

-\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])
, in which correlations are fully retained. The parsing–based
sum (
1.2
) therefore treats the blocks as if they were
probabilistically independent.

Moreover, the block boundaries may depend arbitrarily on the observed sample
and on
N
, and may be constructed by any (possibly random)
data–dependent procedure. In particular, the parsing need not arise from
stopping times, nor satisfy any structural compatibility with the left shift.
The blocks could, for instance, be selected so as to minimize or otherwise
bias conditional probabilities across block boundaries.

In the main theorem below (Theorem
1.1
), we show that this
convergence question nevertheless admits an affirmative answer at a very high
level of generality. Specifically, the convergence holds whenever the number
of parsing blocks
c_{N}
grows sublinearly with the observation length
N

and
{\mathds{P}}
is shift–invariant.

The argument does not rely on
large–deviation refinements of the SMB theorem, nor on Gibbsian or explicit
decoupling assumptions controlling conditional probabilities between
successive blocks, as in
[
BGPR24
,
BGPR26
]
. Instead, it is based on the
martingale decomposition underlying Breiman’s original proof of the SMB
theorem
[
Bre57
,
Bre60
]
, and brings out a structural
feature of Breiman’s decomposition—its robustness under arbitrary
sublinear block decompositions—which does not appear to have been
explicitly isolated in this generality.

At this level of generality on the parsing procedure and the underlying measure

{\mathds{P}}
, the sublinear growth condition (
1.3
) below is essentially sharp: if the block
count is allowed to grow linearly, there exist parsings and ergodic measures
{\mathds{P}}

for which the quantity in (
1.2
) fails to converge. This is discussed in
Section
2.6
.

Main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\mathds{P}}
be a shift–invariant probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
, and

\{w_{i}^{(N)}\}_{i=1}^{c_{N}}
any parsing of the form (
1.1
) satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{c_{N}(x)}{N}=0,\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.}

(1.3)

Then, with
h_{\mathds{P}}
the entropy rate function appearing in the SMB theorem,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}-\log{\mathds{P}}\bigl([w_{i}^{(N)}(x)]\bigr)=h_{\mathds{P}}(x),\qquad\text{holds }{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}).

In particular,
{\mathds{E}}_{\mathds{P}}(h_{\mathds{P}})=h({\mathds{P}})
.

Theorem
1.1
admits the following structural reformulation as
an approximate factorization of cylinder probabilities under mere
shift–invariance.

Corollary 1.2

(Approximate Factorization)
.

Let
{\mathds{P}}
be a shift–invariant probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
,
and let
x_{1}^{N}=w_{1}^{(N)}\cdots w_{c_{N}}^{(N)}
be any parsing of the form
(
1.1
) satisfying the condition (
1.3
).
Then there exists a sequence of measurable functions

r_{N}:\Omega\to{\mathds{R}}
satisfying
\frac{r_{N}(x)}{N}\to 0
,
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s and in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
, such that

{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])=\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{c_{N}}{\mathds{P}}([w_{i}^{(N)}])\Bigg)\exp\{r_{N}(x)\}.

(1.4)

Remark 1.3
.

If one relaxes the assumption on the parsing to

\frac{c_{N}(x)}{N}\xrightarrow{{\mathds{P}}}0
,
then the
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
convergence in Theorem
1.1
still holds.

Remark 1.4

(Robustness under subextensive perturbations of the parsing)
.

A robustness feature of our argument, explored in
Section
3.1
, is that the stability
Theorem
1.1
is insensitive to subextensive perturbations of the
parsing blocks.
More precisely, for every
N
, we can replace each
w_{i}^{(N)}
either by

•

a
sub–block

\underline{w}_{i}^{(N)}\subseteq w_{i}^{(N)}
, or

•

a
super–block

w_{i}^{(N)}\subseteq\overline{w}_{i}^{(N)}
satisfying
a non–degeneracy condition, see Section
3.1
,

and denote in both cases the resulting block by
\widetilde{w}_{i}^{(N)}
.
Assuming that the total modification is subextensive, namely

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}|\widetilde{w}_{i}^{(N)}(x)|=N+o(N),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.},

(1.5)

the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
still holds.

Notation and Proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1
Notation

Let the space
\Omega\coloneqq\{(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathds{N}}}:x_{k}\in{\cal A}\text{ for all }k\in{\mathds{N}}\}
be
equipped with the
\sigma
–field
{\cal F}
generated by cylinder sets of the form

[x_{1}^{n}]:=\{y\in\Omega:y_{1}^{n}=x_{1}^{n}\}
, where

x_{1}^{n}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
for the sequence
x=(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in\Omega
.
The
shift
map
T:\Omega\to\Omega
, defined by
(Tx)_{k}\coloneqq x_{k+1}
,
is a measurable surjection.
We let
{\mathds{P}}
denote a shift–invariant (i.e.
T
–invariant) probability measure
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
.
For such a measure, we denote by
{\mathds{P}}_{n}
its
n
–th marginal, that is, the
probability measure on
{\cal A}^{n}
defined by

{\mathds{P}}_{n}(x_{1}^{n}):={\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])
. For two positive sequences of real numbers
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we write

a_{n}\sim b_{n},

whenever

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=1.

We define

\supp{\mathds{P}}_{n}:=\{a\in{\cal A}^{n}:{\mathds{P}}_{n}(a)>0\}

and

\supp{\mathds{P}}:=\{x\in\Omega:x_{1}^{n}\in\supp{\mathds{P}}_{n}\text{ for all }n\in{\mathds{N}}\}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathds{N}}}\supp{\mathds{P}}_{n}.

For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
H({\mathds{P}}_{n})
the Shannon entropy of the
n
–th marginal,
defined by

\displaystyle H({\mathds{P}}_{n}):=-\sum_{a\in{\cal A}^{n}}{\mathds{P}}_{n}(a)\log{\mathds{P}}_{n}(a)\in[0,\log|{\cal A}|].

(2.1)

Finally, the entropy rate of
{\mathds{P}}
is defined as the limit

\displaystyle h({\mathds{P}}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H({\mathds{P}}_{n}),

(2.2)

which exists in
[0,\log|{\cal A}|]
by sub-additivity.

2.2
A Martingale Decomposition

As stated in the introduction, the proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a
martingale naturally associated to a shift–invariant probability measure

{\mathds{P}}
on
(\Omega,{\cal F},({\cal F}_{n})_{n\geq 1})
, where
{\cal F}_{n}
denotes the
\sigma
–field
generated by the cylinder sets
\{[x_{1}^{n}]:x_{1}^{n}\in{\cal A}^{n}\}
.
We recall here the basic properties of this martingale and the resulting decomposition of

-\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])
.
The following result is standard, and its proof is postponed to the
appendix (see Section
A.1
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathds{P}}
be a shift-invariant probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
.
Define
(Z_{n}(x))_{n\geq 1}
for
x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}
by

\displaystyle Z_{n}(x):=\frac{{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}])}{{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])},\qquad n\geq 2,

(2.3)

and set
Z_{1}(x):={\mathds{P}}([x_{1}])^{-1}
. Then:

(i)

(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a

\big(({\cal F}_{n})_{n\geq 1},{\mathds{P}}\big)
-martingale with values in
[1,\infty)
.
In particular, we have the
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s. convergence
Z_{n}\to Z
, thus also
\log Z_{n}\to\log Z
.

(ii)

If
Z_{\max}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}Z_{n}
, then

{\mathds{P}}(Z_{\max}>t)\leq\frac{|{\cal A}|}{t},\qquad{\mathds{P}}(\log Z_{\max}>t)\leq|{\cal A}|\;\mathrm{e}^{-t},\qquad\forall t>0.

Consequently,
\log Z_{\max}\in{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
and
\log Z_{n}\to\log Z
also holds in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
.

(iii)

With the notation of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have

{\mathds{E}}(\log Z_{n})=H({\mathds{P}}_{n})-H({\mathds{P}}_{n-1}),\quad{\mathds{E}}(\log Z)=h({\mathds{P}}).

For any
x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}
, we observe the identities

\displaystyle-\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log Z_{n-k}(T^{k}x)=I_{n}(x)+J_{n}(x),

(2.4)

where

I_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log Z(T^{k}x),\qquad J_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{n-k}(T^{k}x),\quad\varphi_{r}:=\log Z_{r}-\log Z.

The 1957 Breiman proof of the SMB amounts essentially to proving that

n^{-1}J_{n}\to 0
, both
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s. and in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})

(see
[
Bre57
,
Bre60
]
).

We also define, for fixed
M\in{\mathds{N}}
, the truncated and residual quantities

I_{n,M}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log Z_{M}(T^{k}x),\qquad J_{n,M}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{n-k,M}(T^{k}x),\quad\varphi_{r,M}:=\log Z_{r}-\log Z_{M}.

Then

-\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])=I_{n,M}(x)+J_{n,M}(x),

(2.5)

and the original decomposition (
2.4
) is recovered by letting
M\to\infty
.
This truncation ensures that all relevant quantities depend only on finitely many
coordinates, and will simplify the proof that follows.

2.3
Parsings

Definition 2.2

(Parsing)
.

A
parsing
of
x_{1}^{N}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
is an array of random variables

\big\{(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\big\}_{N\geq 1},

where
c_{N}:\Omega\to{\mathds{N}}_{\leq N}
and

w_{i}^{(N)}:\Omega\to\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}{\cal A}^{k}
,
such that for every
N\in{\mathds{N}}
and every
x\in\Omega
,

x_{1}^{N}=w_{1}^{(N)}(x)\,w_{2}^{(N)}(x)\cdots w_{c_{N}(x)}^{(N)}(x).

We write

\ell_{i,N}:=|w_{i}^{(N)}|,\qquad L_{i,N}:=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell_{j,N},\qquad L_{0,N}:=0.

2.4
Three Auxiliary Lemmas

Auxiliary notation.

Let
(w_{i}^{(N)})_{i=1}^{c_{N}}
be a parsing of
x_{1}^{N}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
.
For
(m,M)\in{\mathds{N}}^{2}
and
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, define the random indices

\displaystyle r_{i,k,N}:=L_{i-1,N}+\ell_{i,N}-1-k,

(2.6)

and introduce the random variables

\displaystyle Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x):=\log Z_{k+1}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)+\log Z_{M}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle Y^{(N)}_{0,k,M}(x):=\log Z_{k+1}(T^{N-k}x)+\log Z_{M}(T^{N-k}x).

(2.8)

We define

\displaystyle J_{n,M}(w_{i}^{(N)}(x)):=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{i,N}-1}\varphi_{n-k,M}(T^{k+L_{i-1,N}}x),

(2.9)

and suppress the
N
and
x
dependence when the context is clear.
Finally, we define

\displaystyle\psi_{m,M}:=\sup_{k\geq m}\big|\log Z_{k}-\log Z_{M}\big|,\qquad(m,M)\in{\mathds{N}}^{2}.

(2.10)

Remark 2.3
.

The non-negative sequence
(\psi_{m,M})_{m,M\geq 1}
is uniformly bounded by

2\log Z_{\max}\in{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
(see Lemma
2.1
) and is therefore uniformly integrable.

Lemma 2.4

(Birkhoff sum–parsing decomposition)
.

Let
\{(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a parsing.
Then, for every
m\geq M
and every
N\geq 1
, we have the decomposition

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\big|J_{\ell_{i},M}(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))\big|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m,M}(T^{k}x)+\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)

(2.11)

in terms of the “Birkhoff sum”

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m,M}(T^{k}x)

and the
“parsing-dependent” term

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\varphi_{r,M}=\log Z_{r}-\log Z_{M}
. From definition (
2.9
),

J_{\ell_{i},M}(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{i}-1}\varphi_{\ell_{i}-k,M}(T^{L_{i-1}+k}x),

and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\big|J_{\ell_{i},M}(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))\big|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{i}-1}\big|\log Z_{\ell_{i}-k}(T^{L_{i-1}+k}x)-\log Z_{M}(T^{L_{i-1}+k}x)\big|.

Reversing the index inside each block yields

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell_{i}-1}\big|\log Z_{k+1}(T^{L_{i-1}+\ell_{i}-1-k}x)-\log Z_{M}(T^{L_{i-1}+\ell_{i}-1-k}x)\big|.

Fix
m\geq M
. Splitting the inner sum at
m
gives, in the notation of (
2.6
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=m+1}^{\ell_{i}-1}\big|\log Z_{k+1}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)-\log Z_{M}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)\big|

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big|\log Z_{k+1}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)-\log Z_{M}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)\big|,

By Definition
2.2
and (
2.10
), the first term is bounded by

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=m+1}^{\ell_{i}-1}\psi_{m,M}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m,M}(T^{k}x),

where the inequality holds even when
\ell_{i}<m
, since
\psi_{m,M}\geq 0
.
For the second term, using
|\log Z_{k+1}-\log Z_{M}|\leq\log Z_{k+1}+\log Z_{M}
, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big|\log Z_{k+1}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)-\log Z_{M}(T^{r_{i,k,N}}x)\big|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x).

Combining the two bounds yields

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\big|J_{\ell_{i},M}(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))\big|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m,M}(T^{k}x)+\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)

and proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.5

(Negligibility of the parsing-dependent term in (
2.11
))
.

Let
\{(w_{i}^{(N)}(x))_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a parsing with
c_{N}=o(N)
,
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s.
Then, for every fixed
m\geq M
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)=o(N),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s. and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}).

(2.12)

In particular, the parsing-dependent term in (
2.11
) of Lemma
2.4

is negligible at scale
N
.

Proof.

Fix integers
m\geq M
.
Since
Z_{k+1}
and
Z_{M}
depend on finitely many coordinates and
{\cal A}
is finite,
we have

\sup_{x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}}\log Z_{k+1}(x)<\infty,\qquad\sup_{x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}}\log Z_{M}(x)<\infty.

It follows that

\Delta_{m,M}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}\\
0\leq k\leq m\end{subarray}}\Big(\log Z_{k+1}(x)+\log Z_{M}(x)\Big)<\infty,

and
Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)\leq\Delta_{m,M}
for every
x\in\supp{\mathds{P}}
,
N\in{\mathds{N}}
,
1\leq i\leq c_{N}(x)
, and
0\leq k\leq m
.
Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)\leq(m+1)\,\Delta_{m,M}\,c_{N}(x).

Since
c_{N}=o(N)

{\mathds{P}}
-a.s., we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)=o(N),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.},

establishing the
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s. claim in (
2.12
). To deal with the
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
claim, we note that by non-negativity and uniform boundedness, the sequence

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)\Bigg)_{N\geq 1}

(2.13)

is uniformly integrable. Since it converges to zero
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s., the convergence also holds in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Lemma
2.5
is the only point in the argument where the sublinearity
assumption (
1.3
) is used. Because the sequence (
2.13
) is
uniformly integrable, convergence to zero in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
already follows from the
weaker condition

\frac{c_{N}}{N}\xrightarrow{{\mathds{P}}}0.

This condition ensures that, for every fixed
m\geq M
, the sequence (
2.13
)
converges to zero in probability as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.7

(Negligibility of the Birkhoff sum in (
2.11
))
.

There exist sequences of natural numbers
(\widetilde{M}_{j})_{j\geq 0}
and non-negative functions
(\widehat{\psi}_{\infty,\widetilde{M}_{j}})_{j\geq 1}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\psi_{m,\widetilde{M}_{j}}(T^{k}x)=\widehat{\psi}_{\infty,\widetilde{M}_{j}},\qquad\lim_{j\to\infty}\widehat{\psi}_{\infty,\widetilde{M}_{j}}=0,\qquad\text{hold }{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.}

Moreover,

3.1
Robustness Under Subextensive Perturbations

We noted in Remark
1.4
that the argument underlying
Theorem
1.1
is robust under subextensive perturbations of the
parsing blocks. We now make this statement precise.

For each
N\geq 1
, let

\bigl\{w_{i}^{(N)}(x)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}

denote the blocks of the parsing of

, and define the corresponding block

\ell_{i,N}(x):=|w_{i}^{(N)}(x)|,\qquad L_{i-1,N}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\ell_{j,N}(x).

We consider two types of perturbations of the blocks

\bigl\{w_{i}^{(N)}(x)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}

Sub–block perturbations.

The block

\underline{w}_{i}^{(N)}\subseteq w_{i}^{(N)}.

Super–block perturbations.

The block
w_{i}^{(N)}
is replaced by a super–block

\overline{w}_{i}^{(N)}\supseteq w_{i}^{(N)}.

Writing
\overline{\ell}_{i,N}:=|\overline{w}_{i}^{(N)}|
and denoting by

\overline{L}_{i-1,N}
the starting index of the super-block
\overline{w}_{i}^{(N)}
, we assume that
the interval

\bigl[\overline{L}_{i-1,N},\,\overline{L}_{i-1,N}+\overline{\ell}_{i,N}\bigr)

has non--empty intersection only with the neighboring original blocks
1
1

1

The argument also extends to the case where the intervals

[\overline{L}_{i-1,N},\,\overline{L}_{i-1,N}+\overline{\ell}_{i,N})

are allowed to intersect a uniformly bounded number of intervals of the form

[L_{i,N},\,L_{i,N}+\ell_{i+1,N})
.
,
namely

\bigl[L_{i-2,N},\,L_{i-2,N}+\ell_{i-1,N}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl[L_{i,N},\,L_{i,N}+\ell_{i+1,N}\bigr).

For each

, the choice of perturbation scheme is allowed to vary from block to

\{\widetilde{w}_{i}^{(N)}(x)\}_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}

the resulting modified blocks, we assume that the total modification is

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}|\widetilde{w}_{i}^{(N)}(x)|=N+o(N),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.}

Under these perturbations, the conclusion of Theorem

Proof.

3.2
Coarse–graining

In this section, we draw a parallel between Theorem
1.1
, the
robustness results of Section
3.1
, and the notion of

coarse–graining
familiar from statistical mechanics.

From an information–theoretic viewpoint, a length–
N
cylinder

[x_{1}^{N}]
may be regarded as a microscopic configuration, whose information
content is measured by
-\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])
. The Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem
asserts that, for
{\mathds{P}}
–almost every realization,

-\frac{1}{N}\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])\longrightarrow h({\mathds{P}}),

so that the specific information content of a typical microscopic configuration
converges to a deterministic limit
h({\mathds{P}})
whenever
{\mathds{P}}
is ergodic.

Coarse–graining, in a broad sense, consists of replacing a detailed microscopic
description by a collection of aggregated observables at a “mesoscopic” scale defined on larger
“blocks,” and studying the corresponding normalized quantities. In our
setting, a parsing
x_{1}^{N}=w_{1}^{(N)}\cdots w_{c_{N}}^{(N)}
with
c_{N}=o(N)
induces
such a coarse description, where the relevant observables are the block information contents

-\log{\mathds{P}}([w_{i}^{(N)}])
.

Theorem
1.1
shows that, despite this coarser description, the
normalized sum of blockwise information contents

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}}-\log{\mathds{P}}([w_{i}^{(N)}])

has the same almost–sure limit as the microscopic quantity

-\frac{1}{N}\log{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{N}])
. By contrast, Section
2.6
shows that
allowing
c_{N}
to grow faster than sublinearly may destroy the existence of this
limit. Finally, the robustness results of Section
3.1
reinforce
this coarse–graining analogy, by showing that subextensive modifications of the
parsing do not affect the asymptotic specific information content.

3.3
Extension to Non-finite Alphabets

In this section we briefly indicate how Theorem
1.1

can potentially be extended to the setting of Barron’s generalized
Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem for densities
[
Bar85
]
.

The principal technical ingredient in the proof of
Theorem
1.1
is Lemma
2.1
,
which relies on the finiteness of the alphabet
{\cal A}
.
Barron showed in his seminal work
[
Bar85
]
that an analogue of
Lemma
2.1
continues to hold in the setting of densities,
where conditional probabilities are replaced by Radon–Nikodym
derivatives with respect to a reference Markov measure.
Thus, the martingale component of the argument admits a natural
extension beyond finite alphabets.

Apart from Lemma
2.1
, finiteness of
{\cal A}

enters the proof through Lemma
2.5
, where it is used to
control the boundary error terms

\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M},

arising from the arbitrary parsing.
More precisely, finiteness of
{\cal A}
ensures that these boundary
contributions are negligible, in the sense that for every fixed
m\geq M
,

\sum_{i=1}^{c_{N}(x)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}Y^{(N)}_{i,k,M}(x)=o(N),\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}).

In the finite–alphabet setting, this ultimately follows from a
uniform boundedness property of the local error terms, which
prevents the random boundary effects induced by the parsing from
accumulating at linear scale.

The finiteness assumption may, however, be bypassed by appealing
directly to an ergodic–theoretic argument. The following consequence
of the ergodic theorem provides the required control; its proof is
given in Appendix
A.2
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
T:\Omega\to\Omega
be a measure–preserving transformation
of the probability space
(\Omega,{\cal F},{\mathds{P}})
, and let

g\in{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
. Let
A_{N}:\Omega\to 2^{\{1,\dots,N\}}

be a sequence of set–valued functions satisfying

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A_{N}(x)|}{N}=0,\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.}

Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A_{N}(x)}g(T^{k}x)=0,\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}).

Proposition
3.2
shows that sublinear collections of indices
cannot contribute at linear scale to ergodic averages of
integrable functions. Consequently, once the martingale
decomposition is available in the density setting,
there appears to be no essential obstruction to extending
Theorem
1.1
by replacing the information contents

A.1
Proof of Lemma
2.1

Proof.

Observe that, given the first bound in (ii), the second is trivial, and then the rest of the claim is a simple application of the representation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathds{P}}(X)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathds{P}}(X>t)\;dt,

valid for any measurable
X\geq 0
, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem. Also, the
{\mathds{P}}
-a.s. existence of the limit
Z
is guaranteed by Doob’s Martingale Convergence Theorem, because
Z_{n}^{-}=0
for every
n
and so
\sup_{n\geq 2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathds{P}}(Z_{n}^{-})=0<\infty
.

To show that
Z_{n}
is a martingale, it suffices to prove

\frac{1}{{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathds{P}}}(Z_{n+1}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1}^{n}]})=Z_{n}(x_{1}^{n}),

for any
x_{1}^{n}\in\supp{\mathds{P}}_{n}
. To this end, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathds{P}}}(Z_{n+1}\mathds{1}_{[x_{1}^{n}]})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\cal A},\;{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])>0}Z_{n+1}(x_{1}^{n}a){\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\cal A},\;{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])>0}\frac{{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}a])}{{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])}{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\cal A},\;{\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}a])>0}{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}a])

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in{\cal A}}{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}a])

\displaystyle={\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}])={\mathds{P}}([x_{1}^{n}])Z_{n}(x_{1}^{n}),

which proves (i.). To prove (iii), we adapt the argument of Ionescu–Tulcea
[
Ion61
]

to the present setting.
Fix
t>0
and consider the set
A:=\{Z_{\text{max}}>t\}
and the disjoint sets

A_{k}:=\{Z_{1}\leq t,\dots,Z_{k-1}\leq t,Z_{k}>t\}\in{\cal F}_{k}
with
\bigcup_{k\geq 1}A_{k}=A
.
Applying Markov’s inequality to
{\mathds{P}}(A_{k})
gives

{\mathds{P}}(A)=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{P}}(A_{k})\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{A_{k}}Z_{k}\;\text{d}{\mathds{P}}\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{x_{1}^{k}\in A_{k}}{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{k}]).

Now consider the unique probability measure
\nu
on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
with marginals
\nu_{n}
defined by

\nu_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{|{\cal A}|}{\mathds{P}}([x_{2}^{n}]),\qquad n\in{\mathds{N}}

(this is a standard application of the Daniell–Kolmogorov Consistency Theorem).
The above estimate becomes

{\mathds{P}}(A)\leq\frac{|{\cal A}|}{t}\sum_{k\geq 2}\nu_{k}(A_{k})=\frac{|{\cal A}|}{t}\nu(A)\leq\frac{|{\cal A}|}{t}.

Here we have used the fact that the sets
A_{k}\in{\cal F}_{k}
,
k\in{\mathds{N}}
are disjoint, and that
\nu
is a probability measure on
(\Omega,{\cal F})
. This proves (ii). The first part of (iii) is a simple computation. The second part follows from an application of (ii), the Cesàro Lemma, and the fact that for any shift-invariant
{\mathds{P}}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{H({\mathds{P}}_{n})}{n}=h({\mathds{P}}).

∎

A.2
Proof of Proposition
3.2

Proof.

Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and decompose

g=g_{M}+h_{M},\qquad g_{M}:=g\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|g|\leq M\}},\quad h_{M}:=g\,\mathbf{1}_{\{|g|>M\}}.

(A.1)

Since
|g_{M}|\leq M
, we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A_{N}(x)}|g_{M}(T^{k}x)|\leq M\,\frac{|A_{N}(x)|}{N}.

(A.2)

By assumption,

\frac{|A_{N}(x)|}{N}\to 0,\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.,}

(A.3)

and hence the term in (
A.2
) converges to
0

{\mathds{P}}
-a.s. and in
{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
.

For the tail part,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A_{N}(x)}|h_{M}(T^{k}x)|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|h_{M}(T^{k}x)|.

(A.4)

By the ergodic theorem,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|h_{M}(T^{k}x)|\longrightarrow{\mathds{E}}\big[|h_{M}|\mid\mathcal{T}\big],\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}),

(A.5)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the
T
-invariant
\sigma
-field.

Since
|h_{M}|\leq g\in{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}})
and
h_{M}\to 0

{\mathds{P}}
-a.s., we have

\lim_{M\to\infty}{\mathds{E}}\big[|h_{M}|\mid\mathcal{T}\big]=0,\qquad{\mathds{P}}\text{-a.s.\ and in }{\mathds{L}}^{1}({\mathds{P}}).

(A.6)

Finally,

\frac{1}{N}\Big|\sum_{k\in A_{N}(x)}g(T^{k}x)\Big|\leq M\,\frac{|A_{N}(x)|}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{M}(T^{k}x).

Letting
N\to\infty
and using (
A.3
) and (
A.5
),
then letting
M\to\infty
and applying (
A.6
), yields the result.
∎
