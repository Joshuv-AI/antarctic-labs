Spatial Power Estimation via Riemannian Covariance Matching
Or Cohen 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, Alon Amar 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, Ronen Talmon 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}

Abstract
We propose a new method for spatial power spectrum estimation in array processing that leverages the Riemannian geometry of Hermitian positive definite (HPD) matrices. We show that conventional approaches minimize variants of the Euclidean distance between the sample covariance matrix and a model covariance matrix, without considering the fact that covariance matrices lie on the Riemannian manifold of HPD matrices. By exploiting this manifold, we present a Riemannian-aware covariance matching algorithm, termed SERCOM, using the Jensen–Bregman LogDet (JBLD) divergence, which, unlike other Riemannian distances, can be evaluated efficiently without eigen-decomposition. We theoretically compare the JBLD divergence to other Euclidean- and Riemannian-based distances, demonstrating robustness to spectral distortions. Experimental results demonstrate that SERCOM consistently outperforms existing methods in direction-of-arrival (DOA) and power estimation, particularly in challenging scenarios with low SNR, limited number of snapshots, and correlated sources.

INTRODUCTION

Spatial power estimation is a fundamental problem in array signal processing, with a long history across statistical, subspace, and more recently deep learning–based methods
[
38
,
19
,
20
,
30
,
43
,
22
,
28
,
24
]
. It is closely connected to direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation, since DOAs typically appear as dominant peaks in the estimated spectrum. Classical beamformers such as the delay-and-sum (Bartlett’s beamformer) and the minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR)
[
7
,
32
]
rely on a single-source model, while subspace methods such as multiple signal classification (MUSIC)
[
33
,
23
,
45
]
estimate DOAs directly without power estimation.

Recently, deep learning–based approaches have also been proposed for DOA and spatial spectrum estimation, showing strong empirical performance in specific operating regimes, including low-SNR scenarios, when trained on representative datasets
[
43
,
22
,
28
,
24
]
. However, they typically require supervised training with labeled data and may need retraining or adaptation when the array geometry, signal model, or operating conditions differ between the training and deployment stages, which limits their applicability in settings with limited training data or varying sensing conditions.

Among model-based methods, greedy sparse recovery approaches are often computationally attractive when the number of sources is small
[
10
]
. However, on fine angular grids the steering dictionary can become highly coherent, which may reduce robustness for closely spaced or correlated sources. Moreover, the greedy selection process is susceptible to noise and small sample sizes, which can cause the algorithm to converge to incorrect directions. Accordingly, many
sparse formulations, including regularization-based methods
[
10
,
46
]
and sparse iterative covariance-based estimation (SPICE) algorithms
[
36
,
37
,
40
]
, adopt a multi-source model, jointly estimating the entire spatial spectrum.

Many of these approaches can be recast using the covariance matching framework, where the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is matched to a model covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}
conveying the expected spatial power spectrum at predefined directions
[
27
,
21
,
44
]
.
This covariance matching approach formulates the estimation of the spatial power as the minimization of a dissimilarity measure between
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
and
\mathbf{R}
, often augmented by regularization terms.
Specifically, in most widely used formulations this dissimilarity reduces to the Euclidean distance between covariance matrices or to a weighted variant of this distance
[
35
,
36
]
.

For example, a common dissimilarity criterion in covariance matching is the asymptotic minimum variance (AMV)
[
27
,
21
,
1
]
, which can be interpreted as a generalized least squares (GLS) fit applied to the vectorized form of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}
, treating it as a vector in Euclidean space. Under suitable statistical conditions, minimizing this criterion is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation of the power spectrum, which in turn coincides with the minimum variance unbiased estimator. Two additional examples are the SPICE algorithm
[
36
,
37
]
, which was introduced as a convex approximation of AMV with an efficient iterative solver, and the iterative sparse asymptotic minimum variance (SAMV) algorithm
[
1
]
, which was proposed for the direct minimization of AMV.

Research gap.
Although effective in some scenarios, these popular covariance matching formulations treat covariance matrices as vectors in Euclidean space, thereby ignoring their intrinsic geometry as elements of the Riemannian manifold of Hermitian positive definite (HPD) matrices
[
6
]
.

Specifically, Euclidean-based methods often experience substantially degraded performance when dealing with ill-conditioned sample covariance matrices or in low-snapshot regimes. By contrast, the Riemannian framework offers a more robust alternative by accounting for the curved geometry of covariance matrices, which inherently regularizes the estimation.

The HPD manifold admits several Riemannian distances that capture this geometry, including the affine-invariant Riemannian metric (AIRM)
[
29
]
, which is perhaps the most widely studied, as well as other alternatives developed in the literature. This viewpoint has been successfully exploited in fields such as computer vision, neuroscience, and medical data analysis, consistently outperforming Euclidean approaches
[
11
,
17
,
13
]
.
Following this success, in this paper, we propose a Riemannian geometry–aware covariance matching approach for spatial power spectrum estimation.

Related Work.

Recent works have explored leveraging the Riemannian geometry of covariance matrices for DOA and array processing. One line of research focuses on geometry-aware averaging of covariances, e.g., using the Riemannian (Karcher) mean to suppress interferences while preserving shared spectral components, which can be integrated into classical beamformers or DOA estimators
[
5
]
.
Another approach formulates DOA estimation directly via Riemannian distances. Early methods compute pseudo-spectra using the AIRM between the sample covariance and a unit-power single-source model, sometimes extended to include source scaling
[
14
,
15
]
. Related strategies construct per-snapshot HPD covariances, aggregate them with the Riemannian mean, and perform 1D search against single-source models
[
9
,
8
]
. These methods highlight robustness benefits from geometry-aware matching but remain restricted to single-source models and per-direction searches.
In
[
42
]
, a
gridless
method for sparse linear arrays was proposed, employing covariance matching with the Bures–Wasserstein distance. Instead of directly estimating the power spectrum, it was combined with root-MUSIC for DOA estimation. Recently,
[
31
]
introduced a covariance-matching beamformer based on the Log-Euclidean (LE) metric
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
under the single-source covariance model, which allowed a closed-form expression of the beamformer with respect to a steering vector and improved performance compared to classical, Euclidean-based methods.

Our Contributions.

We propose
SERCOM
, Spatial power Estimation via Riemannian COvariance Matching, a new method that formulates a Riemannian covariance matching task for
estimating
the power spectrum across multiple directions on a predefined grid, from which the DOAs are subsequently identified as the dominant peaks.
Unlike previous HPD geometry-aware approaches, we introduce a multi-source model and apply a scalable optimization by minimizing the
Jensen–Bregman LogDet (JBLD)
divergence
[
12
,
34
]
between
\mathbf{R}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
. This allows for an efficient Riemannian distance-based estimation that maintains computational tractability even as the number of sensors increases.

We establish an analytical connection between SPICE, AMV, AIRM, and JBLD criteria, clarifying their similarities and differences in the context of spatial power estimation. The proposed SERCOM method with the JBLD criterion outperforms the SAMV and SPICE algorithms while reducing computational complexity compared to the AIRM, yet preserving key aspects of the intrinsic affine-invariant geometry of covariance matrices. Furthermore, unlike approaches restricted to single-source or unit-power models
[
14
,
15
,
9
,
31
]
, our formulation is general and incorporates a model of the full power spectrum, offering a geometry-aware alternative to traditional Euclidean-based techniques. Experimental results further show that SERCOM is a computationally practical framework for spatial power estimation, yielding improved source power and direction estimates compared to existing methods in challenging scenarios such as low SNR conditions and correlated sources.

BACKGROUND: RIEMANNIAN MANIFOLD OF HPD MATRICES

Complex
n\times n
signal-plus-noise covariance matrices lie in the space of HPD matrices, denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{n}
:

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\left\{\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\mid\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{H},\mathbf{R}\succ 0\right\}.

(1)

A widely used Riemannian metric in this space is the AIRM
[
29
]
, which admits a Riemannian manifold structure with well-defined geometry
[
6
]
.
This Riemannian geometry admits the logarithmic mapping
\text{Log}_{\mathbf{R}}:\mathcal{P}_{n}\to T_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{P}_{n}
that maps any
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
into the tangent space of
\mathbf{R}
:

\text{Log}_{\mathbf{R}}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\mathbf{R}^{1/2}\text{log}\big(\mathbf{R}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{R}^{-1/2}\big)\mathbf{R}^{1/2}.

(2)

At any
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the AIRM defines the following inner product in the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{P}_{n}
:

\langle\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\rangle_{\mathbf{R}}=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\Big),\quad\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in T_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{P}_{n}.

(3)

This inner product induces a norm of the tangent vectors. In turn, this yields the following Riemannian distance between any two HPD matrices
\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
:

\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2})=\big\|\text{log}(\mathbf{R}_{1}^{-1/2}\mathbf{R}_{2}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{-1/2})\big\|_{F}^{2},

(4)

which is the squared length of the shortest geodesic connecting
\mathbf{R}_{1}
and
\mathbf{R}_{2}
on the manifold.

Since computing the Riemannian distance induced by the AIRM (
4
) requires matrix square roots and matrix logarithms via
eigen-decomposition,
which are computationally intensive operations
, several approximations have been proposed to offer more efficient alternatives while approximately preserving the underlying geometry.
One widely used approximation is the LE distance
[
3
]
:

\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\text{LE}}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2})=\|\text{log}(\mathbf{R}_{1})-\text{log}(\mathbf{R}_{2})\|^{2}_{F},

(5)

where the matrix logarithm maps covariance matrices into a Euclidean space, allowing the standard Euclidean distance to be applied.
However, the LE metric requires the computation of matrix logarithms, which incurs significant computational overhead and may consequently hinder the efficiency of downstream tasks.

Another geometry-aware alternative to the AIRM is the JBLD
[
12
]
. It is obtained by the symmetrized Bergman divergence
[
4
]
[
26
]
, and defined by

\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2})=\text{log}\left|\frac{\mathbf{R}_{1}+\mathbf{R}_{2}}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{2}\text{log}|\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{2}|.

(6)

It was proposed in
[
12
]
for fast nearest-neighbor retrieval in a
dataset of covariance matrices
and was shown to outperform LE and other geometry-aware distances in applications involving covariance similarity measures. In
[
34
]
, it was shown that the square root of the divergence
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}
is a distance, with tight links to the AIRM and its desirable properties.
Moreover, unlike AIRM and LE, JBLD avoids the computationally intensive matrix logarithm for distance and gradient evaluation, and can be efficiently and stably computed
via
Cholesky factorization
[
12
]
.

PROBLEM FORMULATION AND EXISTING METHODS

Consider an
M
-sensor array with arbitrary but known configuration, receiving
K
far-field, narrowband sources at a common carrier frequency, impinging from directions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{K}
with respective powers
\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{K}^{2}
. Given
N
snapshots collected by the array, the goal is to accurately estimate the directions and powers of the sources, while the number of sources
K
is unknown. The snapshot of the received signals
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
at all sensors is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathbf{a}_{\phi_{k}}s_{k}(t)+\mathbf{n}(t)}

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathbf{s}(t)+\mathbf{n}(t),\;\;t=1,...,N

where
\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=[\mathbf{a}_{\phi_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{\phi_{K}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times K}
is the steering matrix, with each column being a steering vector
\mathbf{a}_{\phi_{k}}
, a known function of
\phi_{k}
given by the carrier frequency and the array configuration. The vector
\mathbf{s}(t)=[s_{1}(t),\ldots,s_{K}(t)]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
is the concatenation of all incident signals, where
s_{k}(t)
is the complex signal emitted by the
k
-th source with zero mean and power
\sigma_{k}^{2}
. The additive noise
\mathbf{n}(t)\sim\mathcal{CN}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I})
has a known power
\sigma_{n}^{2}
. It is assumed that
\mathbf{s}(t)
and
\mathbf{n}(t)
are independent and that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(t)\mathbf{s}(\tilde{t})^{H}]=\delta_{t,\tilde{t}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},...,\sigma_{K}^{2})
, where
\delta_{t,\tilde{t}}
is 1 only if
t=\tilde{t}
and 0 otherwise.

A common line of direction and power estimation methods, termed
on-grid methods
[
38
]
, defines an angular grid with
D
examined directions,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{D}]^{T}
and estimates the power at each grid point. This can be formulated as a covariance matching problem
[
27
,
36
]
, where the population covariance
\mathbf{R}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}(t)\mathbf{y}(t)^{H}]
is parameterized by the spatial power spectrum and matched to the sample covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}(t)\mathbf{y}(t)^{H}
, under the standing assumption that
N\geq M
, so that
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is HPD.

Specifically, the on-grid
multi-source
covariance model incorporates all steering vectors of the angular grid:

\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\text{diag}(\mathbf{p})\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{H}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I},

(8)

where
\mathbf{p}
is the spatial power spectrum over the grid, denoted simply as
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})
hereafter. Then, the following global optimization problem is solved to jointly estimate all grid powers:

\widehat{\mathbf{p}}=\underset{\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{\text{argmin}}}\;\mathcal{D}^{2}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}),\widehat{\mathbf{R}}),\;\;\;\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+},

(9)

for a chosen distance measure
\mathcal{D}
.
Methods that solve (
9
) are agnostic to the sensor arrangement: it enters only through the covariance model in (
8
), which can be defined for ULA/UCA/UPA or any other known array configuration.

The linear relation between the covariance matrix model
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})
and the grid power vector
\mathbf{p}
led to the derivation of the AMV criterion
[
27
]
,
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}),\widehat{\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r})^{H}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|_{F}^{2},

(10)

where
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r}}\triangleq\mathbf{R}^{H}\otimes\mathbf{R}
,
\mathbf{r}\triangleq\vec{(\mathbf{R})}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{r}}\triangleq\vec{(\widehat{\mathbf{R}})}
. This criterion depends non-linearly on the power vector
\mathbf{p}
, and it is not convex. Importantly, it led to the following criterion used in the well-known SPICE algorithm
[
36
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}),\widehat{\mathbf{R}})=\|\mathbf{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(11)

This is a convex objective
[
36
]
, which can be efficiently optimized using an iterative process. In
[
1
]
, another iterative algorithm, called SAMV, was suggested, directly minimizing the AMV criterion while maintaining a sparse solution.

In the context of this paper, we emphasize that both SAMV and SPICE minimize a weighted
Euclidean distance
between covariance matrices. However, they do not take into account the geometry of covariance matrices as points on the Riemannian manifold of HPD matrices.

PROPOSED ALGORITHM

Following the on-grid covariance matching approach with a multi-source model (
9
), we propose to estimate the source power spectrum by minimizing the Jensen–Bregman LogDet divergence (
6
) between the model and sample covariance matrices:

\hat{\mathbf{p}}=\underset{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}}{\arg\text{min}}\;\;\text{log}\left|\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{R}}+\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{2}\text{log}|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})|,

(12)

where the constant term
-\tfrac{1}{2}\text{log}|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}|
is omitted since it does not depend on
\mathbf{p}
.
This covariance matching criterion considers the HPD Riemannian geometry of the covariance matrices, in contrast to the “Euclidean” AMV, and SPICE criteria (
3
)-(
11
).
Moreover, unlike the AIRM distance (
4
), the JBLD-based formulation in (
12
) remains valid even when
N<M
, since
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
does not need to be a full rank matrix.

We will show that it leads to superior performance while maintaining similar or better computational demand.

The optimization of the proposed estimator (
12
), termed SERCOM, is outlined in Algorithm
1
.
For initialization, we set
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}
using the classical delay-and-sum beamformer:

\mathbf{p}^{(0)}_{d}=\mathbf{a}_{\theta_{d}}^{H}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{a}_{\theta_{d}},\quad d=1,\ldots,D.

(13)

The power vector
\mathbf{p}
is then updated iteratively in a gradient-based manner, where the gradient at each iteration is given by

\nabla_{p_{d}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}^{2}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}),\widehat{\mathbf{R}})=\mathbf{a}_{\theta_{d}}^{H}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}^{2}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}),\widehat{\mathbf{R}})\right)\mathbf{a}_{\theta_{d}}

(14)

where

\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})=(\mathbf{R}+\widehat{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}^{-1}

(15)

The
update of the
vector
\mathbf{p}
is carried out using the
Adam
optimizer
[
18
]
, which combines momentum
[
25
]
and adaptive learning rates
[
16
]
. The momentum stabilizes updates for convergence in direction of consistent descent, while the adaptive learning rates
use
the squared gradients for each component of
\mathbf{p}
to ensure that coordinates with large variability are dampened. This coordinate-wise normalization is well-suited for sparse spectrum estimation, where only a small subset of entries in
\mathbf{p}
are expected to be nonzero, corresponding to active sources, and different coordinates require different scaling. After each Adam update, the power vector is projected onto the non-negative orthant by enforcing
p_{d}=\text{max}(0,p_{d})
, consistent with its interpretation as a power spectrum. Together, momentum, adaptive normalization, and non-negativity projection yield a stable optimization trajectory that favors accurate and sparse recovery of the sources. The iterations continue until the relative change in
\mathbf{p}
between consecutive iterations falls below a predefined threshold,
\epsilon_{p}
, ensuring convergence to a stable estimate.

While second-order strategies are natural candidates for log-determinant objectives, our preliminary experiments with MM-style and Newton-type updates were substantially slower per iteration and did not yield improved solutions. For this reason, we focus on the Adam-based implementation in the reported results.

The objective in (
12
) is generally non-convex in the vector
\mathbf{p}
. Consequently, SERCOM is not guaranteed to converge to the global optimum, and may in principle converge to suboptimal stationary points. In practice, we observed stable convergence with the Adam updates and low sensitivity to initialization across several reasonable choices, including delay-and-sum, MVDR, and a Log-Euclidean–based initialization
[
31
]
.

In this section, we theoretically examine the AMV, SPICE, AIRM, and JBLD as criteria for matching a model covariance
1
1

1

In this section, we write
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})
simply as
\mathbf{R}
for brevity.

\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})
to the sample covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
, obtained from
N
snapshots, and establish the conditions under which the criteria coincide and under which they diverge.

We begin by demonstrating that these criteria share a common mathematical structure, even though they arise from different perspectives (Euclidean and Riemannian).
To this end, we define the HPD matrix
2
2

2

For this theoretical analysis section, we assume
N\geq M
such that
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is surely HPD

\mathbf{Q}\triangleq\mathbf{R}^{-1/2}\,\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\,\mathbf{R}^{-1/2}\;\in\;\mathcal{P}_{M}.

(16)

Then, the AIRM distance
\|\text{log}(\mathbf{Q})\|_{F}^{2}
(
4
) reduces, after linearizing the matrix logarithm near the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
,

\text{log}(\mathbf{Q})\approx\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{R}^{-1/2}(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R})\mathbf{R}^{-1/2},

to the AMV criterion (
3

Thus, AMV may be interpreted as a first-order approximation of AIRM around
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
(i.e., when
\mathbf{R}=\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
). From a manifold viewpoint, the AIRM distance measures the Riemannian norm (
3
) of the logarithmic map
\mathrm{Log}_{\mathbf{R}}(\widehat{\mathbf{R}})
(
2
), namely, the mapping of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
to the tangent space at
\mathbf{R}
, which is often viewed as the Riemannian counterpart of Euclidean subtraction. The AMV replaces this Riemannian operation simply by the Euclidean subtraction
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{R}
.

Given the known proximity of JBLD to AIRM
[
12
,
34
]
, and the close relationship between SPICE and AMV, it is natural to expect that when
\mathbf{R}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
are close, all four criteria exhibit similar behavior.

For formal analysis, we express the closeness of
\mathbf{R}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
in terms of two main factors. First, the
model mismatch
, i.e., how well
\mathbf{R}
represents the population covariance
\boldsymbol{\bar{\mathbf{R}}}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}
that generates the data
\{\mathbf{y}(t)\}_{t=1}^{N}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{CN}(0,\bar{\mathbf{R}})
. This model mismatch often depends on factors such as array calibration imperfections and correlated or off-grid sources. Second, the
sampling error
, i.e., the deviation of the sample covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
from the population covariance
\boldsymbol{\bar{\mathbf{R}}}
, due to the finite number of snapshots
N
.
Using these two factors, the proximity
between

\mathbf{R}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is bounded by:

\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\|\boldsymbol{\bar{\mathbf{R}}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\;+\;\|(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}-\boldsymbol{\bar{\mathbf{R}}})\mathbf{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\;\triangleq\;\varepsilon,

(17)

where
\varepsilon
consists of two parts: the first term represents the model mismatch,
and the second term represents the sampling error. Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the spectral norm,
i.e., the largest singular value.

5.1

Define the AIRM-ball of radius

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\bar{\mathbf{R}},\rho)=\left\{\,\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\bar{\mathbf{R}},\mathbf{R})<\rho\right\},

(18)

and the condition number
\kappa(\bar{\mathbf{R}})=\|\bar{\mathbf{R}}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bar{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\|_{2}
.

The following result shows that with sufficiently small model mismatch and a large enough number of samples, all four criteria become asymptotically close in a neighborhood of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
. In particular, their differences vanish at different orders of
\varepsilon
defined in (
32
), all relative to the SPICE criterion, which serves as a convenient baseline.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic equivalence of criteria)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
be the sample covariance from
N
snapshots.
Given a target
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, confidence
1-\delta
, and radius

\rho\in(0,\text{log}(1+\varepsilon))
, if

N\;\geq\;N_{0}(\varepsilon,\delta,\rho):=\frac{\kappa(\bar{\mathbf{R}})^{2}\,e^{2\rho}}{\big(\varepsilon-(e^{\rho}-1)\big)^{2}}\,\Big(M+\text{log}(2/\delta)\Big),

(19)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every

\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\bar{\mathbf{R}},\rho)
we have

\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,

(20)

and

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{AMV}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot C_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{3},

(21)

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot C_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{4},

\displaystyle\big|8\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot C_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{4},

where
C_{\mathrm{AMV}},C_{\mathrm{AIRM}},C_{\mathrm{JBLD}}
are functions
of
\varepsilon

\varepsilon\to 0
.

In particular, as
N\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, all criteria become equivalent in a neighborhood of the shared minimizer
\mathbf{R}=\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
9
.
Theorem
1
states that in the absence of model mismatch, i.e., when
\mathbf{R}
can approach
\boldsymbol{\bar{\mathbf{R}}}

\mathbf{R}=\widehat{\mathbf{R}}

5.2
Criteria Robustness

All examined criteria can be expressed as a sum of
M
scalar functions of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
(
16
),
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
:

\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}^{2}(\mathbf{R},\widehat{\mathbf{R}})\;=\;\sum_{m=1}^{M}\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda_{m}),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\lambda-1)^{2},

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sqrt{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\Big)^{2},

(23)

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(\text{log}\lambda)^{2},

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\text{log}\!\Big(\tfrac{1+\lambda}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\Big).

See derivation in Appendix
8
. Figure
1
illustrates these functions
\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda)
.

Figure 1
:
The
\psi(\lambda)

\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}
is scaled to ease visualization.

Expressing the different criteria in this unified form allows us to examine their robustness.
By the definition of
\mathbf{Q}
(
16
), when
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
and
\mathbf{R}
are close,
\mathbf{Q}
reduces to the identity matrix and its eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
equal 1. However, in practice, abrupt noise, limited snapshots, or other distortions of
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
can give rise to “outlier” eigenvalues in
\mathbf{Q}
, deviating significantly from
1
. While AMV penalizes deviations from
\lambda=1
quadratically (see Figure
1
), SPICE grows roughly linearly for large
\lambda
. In contrast, AIRM and JBLD increase logarithmically, and are therefore expected to be less sensitive when an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
becomes substantially larger than 1. The following theorem formalizes this observation.

Theorem 2

(Criteria robustness to eigenvalue deviations)
.

Suppose that for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
all but one of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
remain close to
1
, i.e.
|\lambda_{m}-1|\leq\varepsilon
for all
m\neq r
, while a single eigenvalue can be expressed as
\lambda_{r}=1+\Delta
, with
\Delta
dominant in logarithmic scale:

\text{log}(1+\Delta)\ \geq\ \text{max}\{\text{log}(1+\varepsilon),\,-\text{log}(1-\varepsilon)\}.

Define the relative contribution of
\lambda_{r}
under criterion
\bullet
as

s_{r}^{(\bullet)}=\frac{\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda_{r})}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda_{m})},

where the different
\psi_{\bullet}
are defined in (
23
). Then

s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AMV})}\;\geq\;s_{r}^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}\;\geq\;s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}\;\geq\;s_{r}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}.

The proof is provided in Appendix
10
.
Theorem
2
states that when a single eigenvalue deviates strongly from
1
, AMV assigns
the highest relative weight to the outlier, followed by SPICE, AIRM, and
JBLD in decreasing sensitivity.
In spatial power estimation and DOA, large eigenvalue deviations may arise from spurious interference or multi-path propagation. Thus, Theorem
2
implies that in such cases the AIRM- and JBLD-based methods may help mitigate such effects, yielding more robust covariance matching compared to the AMV and SPICE criteria.

CONCLUSION

We introduced SERCOM, a novel spatial power estimation method based on Riemannian covariance matching, applied to DOA estimation. We established a connection between widely used Euclidean criteria and HPD geometry-aware distances, which motivated the use of the computationally efficient JBLD divergence as a criterion for covariance matching on the manifold.
The proposed method achieves improved direction and power estimation relative to SPICE and SAMV, particularly at low SNR, with limited snapshots, and for correlated sources. While the RMSE performance is comparable across the different Riemannian metrics tested, our JBLD-based approach demonstrates a slight advantage for correlated sources and a significant reduction in runtime
.
These results suggest that SERCOM should be considered a robust candidate for evaluation in practical, real-world settings where signal conditions are non-ideal.

Future work will explore extending the proposed framework to
gridless
DOA estimation, aiming to combine the robustness of Riemannian metrics with the resolution benefits of continuous-domain formulations.

\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(x)=\text{log}^{2}(1+x)=x^{2}-x^{3}+r^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}_{3}(x),

(39)

with the remainder defined for some
\xi\in(0,x)
by:

r^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}_{3}(x)=x^{4}\cdot\frac{11-6\text{log}(1+\xi)}{12(1+\xi)^{4}},

(40)

hence:

|r^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}_{3}(x)|\leq\varepsilon^{4}\cdot\frac{11+6|\text{log}(1-\varepsilon)|}{12(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}.

(41)

To align the leading quadratic term with the other criteria, we develop the 3-rd order Taylor expansion around zero of
8\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(x)
. It is expressed by:

\displaystyle 8\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(x)

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle 8\text{log}(1+\tfrac{x}{2})-4\text{log}(1+x)

(42)

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle x^{2}-x^{3}+r_{3}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}(x)

with the remainder defined for some
\xi\in(0,x)
by:

r_{3}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}(x)=x^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{(\xi+1)^{4}}-\frac{1}{(\xi+2)^{4}}\right)

(43)

hence:

|r_{3}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}(x)|\leq\varepsilon^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{(2-\varepsilon)^{4}}\right)

(44)

We now derive the per-eigenvalue bounds and then sum over
m
. Each of
\big\{\mathrm{AMV},\mathrm{AIRM},\mathrm{JBLD}\big\}
will be compared to
\mathrm{SPICE}
criterion.

(i) AMV vs. SPICE:

Compute

\displaystyle|\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(x)-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(x)|=|\frac{x^{3}}{x+1}|\leq\varepsilon^{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}.

Define
C_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}
, which converges to a finite constant when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Now, bounding criteria absolute difference using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{AMV}}^{2}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}\big|\

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m}{|\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\lambda_{m})-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})|}

(45)

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle M\cdot C_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon^{3}

(ii) AIRM vs. SPICE:

Compute

\displaystyle|\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(x)-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(x)|

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle|r^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}_{3}(x)-\frac{x^{4}}{x+1}|

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{11+6|\text{log}(1-\varepsilon)|}{12(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)

Define
C_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\varepsilon)=\left(\frac{11+6|\text{log}(1-\varepsilon)|}{12(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)
, which converges to a finite constant when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Now, bounding criteria absolute difference using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{AIRM}}^{2}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}\big|\

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m}{|\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{m})-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})|}

(46)

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle M\cdot C_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon^{4}

(iii) JBLD vs. SPICE:

Compute

\displaystyle|8\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(x)-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(x)|

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle|r^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}_{3}(x)-\frac{x^{4}}{x+1}|

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{(2-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)

Define
C_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\varepsilon)=\left(\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{(2-\varepsilon)^{4}}+\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)
, which converges to a finite constant when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Now, bounding criteria absolute difference using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\big|8\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{JBLD}}^{2}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SPICE}}^{2}\big|\

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m}{|\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda_{m})-\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})|}

(47)

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle M\cdot C_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\varepsilon)\cdot\varepsilon^{4}

Combining the bounds (i)–(iii) yields the stated inequalities, which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem 2 (Criteria robustness to eigenvalue deviations)

To exploit monotonicity, we work in the log–eigenvalue coordinates:

u_{m}\triangleq\tfrac{1}{2}\text{log}\lambda_{m}

so that
\lambda_{m}=e^{2u_{m}}
.
The scalar penalties in (
23
) can be written as functions of
u
:

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\lambda)=(e^{2u}-1)^{2},\;

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda)=4\sinh^{2}u,

(48)

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda)=4u^{2},\;

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda)=\text{log}(\cosh u).

Denote
U_{\text{max}}\triangleq\tfrac{1}{2}\text{max}\{\text{log}(1+\varepsilon),-\text{log}(1-\varepsilon)\}
. Notice that by theorem assumptions, for
m\neq r
:

|u_{m}|\leq U_{\text{max}}\leq u_{r}.

Now, we derive a useful equivalence that will aid the proof.

Set for each criterion:

S^{(\bullet)}\!\triangleq\!\sum_{m\neq r}\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda_{m}).

(49)

Observe that
\psi_{\bullet}(\lambda)\geq 0
for all criteria, which can be verified algebraically and is illustrated in Figure
1
. Consequently,
S^{(\bullet)}\geq 0
holds for all criteria.

For any two criteria
A
and
B
,

s_{r}^{(A)}\leq s_{r}^{(B)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\psi_{A}(\lambda_{r})}{S^{(A)}}\leq\frac{\psi_{B}(\lambda_{r})}{S^{(B)}}.

Equivalently,

s_{r}^{(A)}\leq s_{r}^{(B)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{S^{(B)}}{S^{(A)}}\leq\frac{\psi_{B}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{A}(\lambda_{r})}.

(50)

with the convention that if
S^{(A)}=S^{(B)}=0
then
s_{r}^{(A)}=s_{r}^{(B)}=1
and the inequality holds with equality.

Next, we show twp monotonicity lemmas on the log–scale.

Observe the following two lemmas.

(L1)

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
, define the ratio:

\displaystyle\alpha(u)\triangleq\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda)}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda)}=\Big(\frac{\sinh u}{u}\Big)^{\!2},\;\text{with $\alpha(0)\triangleq 1$.}

Then
\alpha(u)
is strictly increasing in
|u|
.

Proof.

For
u>0
, define
f(u)=\sinh u/u
. Then,

f^{\prime}(u)=\frac{u\cosh u-\sinh u}{u^{2}}
.
The numerator
g(u)=u\cosh u-\sinh u
satisfies
g^{\prime}(u)=u\sinh u>0
and
g(0)=0
,
so
g(u)>0
for
u>0
. Hence
f^{\prime}(u)>0
and
\alpha(u)=f(u)^{2}
increases on
u>0
. Notice that
\alpha(u)
is an even function, hence
\alpha(u)
increases in
|u|
.

(L2)

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
, define the ratio:

\beta(u)\triangleq\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda)}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda)}=\frac{\text{log}(\cosh u)}{4u^{2}},\;\text{with $\beta(0)\triangleq 1/8$.}

Then
\beta(u)
is strictly decreasing in
|u|
.

Proof.

For
u>0
,

\beta^{\prime}(u)=\frac{u\tanh u-2\text{log}(\cosh u)}{4u^{3}}
.
Define the numerator
h(u)\triangleq u\tanh u-2\text{log}(\cosh u)
. We got
h^{\prime}(u)=u\text{sech}^{2}u-\tanh u
and
h^{\prime\prime}(u)=-2u\cdot\text{sech}^{2}u\cdot\tanh u
.
For
u>0
,
h^{\prime\prime}(u)
is negative, and
h^{\prime}(0)=0
, it follows that
h^{\prime}(u)<0
. Hence, for
u>0
,
h(u)
is strictly decreasing with
h(0)=0
, so
h(u)<0
and thus
\beta^{\prime}(u)<0
. Notice that
\beta(u)
is an even function, hence
\beta
decreases in
|u|
.

We are now ready to prove the following inequalities

(i)
s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AMV})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}
:

Notice that

\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\lambda_{m})=(\lambda_{m}-1)^{2}=\lambda_{m}(\sqrt{\lambda}_{m}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}_{m}})^{2}=\lambda_{m}\,\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m}),

for all
m
. Hence

\frac{S^{(\mathrm{AMV})}}{S^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}}=\frac{\sum_{m\neq r}\lambda_{m}\,\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})}{\sum_{m\neq r}\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})}\leq\text{max}_{m\neq r}\lambda_{m}\leq\lambda_{r},

since,
\text{max}_{m\neq r}\lambda_{m}\leq 1+\varepsilon\leq 1+\Delta=\lambda_{r}
.

Therefore,

\frac{S^{(\mathrm{AMV})}}{S^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}}\leq\lambda_{r}=\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{AMV}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{r})},

and by (
50
) (with
B=\mathrm{AMV}
,
A=\mathrm{SPICE}
)
we conclude
s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AMV})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}
.

(ii)
s_{r}^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}
:

For each
m\neq r
, by (L1), evenness of
\alpha(u)
and
|u_{m}|\leq U_{\text{max}}\leq u_{r}
:

\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{m})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{m})}=\alpha(u_{m})=\alpha(|u_{m}|)\leq\alpha(u_{r})=\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}.

Multiplying by
\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{m})\geq 0
and summing over
m\neq r
yields

S^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}\leq\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}\,S^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}.

Equivalently (for non-degenerate case where
S^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}>0
:

\dfrac{S^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}}{S^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}}\leq\dfrac{\psi_{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}.

Applying (
50
) (with
B=\mathrm{SPICE}
,
A=\mathrm{AIRM}
) gives

s_{r}^{(\mathrm{SPICE})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}
.

(iii)
s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}
:

For each
m\neq r
, by (L2), evenness of
\beta(u)
and
|u_{m}|\leq U_{\text{max}}\leq u_{r}
:

\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda_{m})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{m})}=\beta(u_{m})=\beta(|u_{m}|)\geq\beta(u_{r})=\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}.

Multiplying by
\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{m})\geq 0
and summing over
m\neq r
gives

S^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}\geq\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}\,S^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}.

Equivalently,

\dfrac{S^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}}{S^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}}\geq\dfrac{\psi_{\mathrm{JBLD}}(\lambda_{r})}{\psi_{\mathrm{AIRM}}(\lambda_{r})}.

Applying (
50
) (with
B=\mathrm{AIRM}
,
A=\mathrm{JBLD}
) yields

s_{r}^{(\mathrm{AIRM})}\geq s_{r}^{(\mathrm{JBLD})}
.

Combining (i),(ii) and (iii) concludes the proof.
∎
