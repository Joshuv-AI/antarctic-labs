Perfect Parallelization in Mini-Batch SGD with Classical Momentum Acceleration
Sachin Garg, Michał Dereziński

Abstract
Accelerating stochastic gradient methods with classical momentum schemes, such as Polyak’s heavy ball, has proven highly successful in training large-scale machine learning models, particularly when combined with the hardware acceleration of large mini-batch computations. Yet, the effect of classical momentum on stochastic mini-batch optimization has been poorly understood theoretically, with prior works requiring strong noise assumptions and extremely large mini-batches. In this work, we develop a general theory of stochastic momentum acceleration for optimizing over quadratics in the interpolation regime, a popular abstraction for studying deep learning dynamics which also includes classical methods such as randomized Kaczmarz and coordinate descent. Our framework encompasses both heavy ball and Nesterov-style momentum, allows for arbitrary mini-batch sizes, and makes minimal assumptions on the stochastic noise. In particular, we show that acceleration from classical momentum is directly proportional to the gradient mini-batch size (up to a natural saturation point), thereby enabling perfect parallelization of mini-batch computations. Our theory also provides a simple choice for the momentum parameter, which is shown to be effective empirically.

Introduction

Consider the following optimization task which naturally arises in training machine learning models:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ F(\mathbf{w}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[f(\mathbf{w};\xi)\big],

(1)

where
\mathbf{w}
represents the model parameters and
\xi\sim\mathcal{D}
represents the stochastic noise. Problem (
1
) provides a powerful abstraction for designing and analyzing algorithms for model training, particularly in the context of deep learning and large language models
[
31
,
24
,
14
,
54
]
.

When given oracle access to the full gradient
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})=\nabla F(\mathbf{w})
of the function
F
, problem (
1
) is very well understood. Here, the simplest strategy is to iteratively follow the direction of steepest descent,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\alpha\,\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}_{t}).

(2)

When
F
is smooth and strongly convex, this Gradient Descent algorithm (GD) attains linear convergence, i.e., it reaches an
\epsilon
-approximate solution after
O(\kappa_{F}\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations, where
\kappa_{F}
is an appropriate condition number of the deterministic function
F
. In 1964, Polyak
[
44
]
showed that the convergence rate of GD can be improved by introducing momentum into its descent trajectory:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\alpha\,\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}_{t})+\beta\,(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t-1}).

(3)

With an appropriate choice of step size
\alpha
and momentum parameter
\beta
, this Heavy Ball Momentum algorithm (HBM) reaches an
\epsilon
-approximate solution after
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{F}}\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations. In 1983, Nesterov
[
40
]
proposed a refinement of this scheme, known as Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG), which essentially incorporates one additional gradient step to both
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\mathbf{w}_{t-1}
in the momentum.

For large-scale problems, instead of the full gradient we often only have access to stochastic gradients
\nabla f(\mathbf{w},\xi)
. By aggregating
m
such gradients into the vector
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f(\mathbf{w};\xi_{i})
, the GD update (
2
) turns into the popular mini-batch Stochastic Gradient Descent algorithm (SGD,
[
47
]
), which has become a workhorse of modern machine learning. The success of SGD in deep learning has been partly attributed to the fact that it is particularly effective in the
interpolation regime

[
4
,
38
,
3
]
, i.e., when all of the functions
f(\cdot,\xi)
can be simultaneously minimized (the model fits perfectly to the training data). In this setting, for smooth and strongly convex objectives, SGD attains linear convergence with
O(\kappa_{f}\log(1/\epsilon))
iteration complexity, where
\kappa_{f}
is a corresponding condition number of the stochastic function
f
. The use of large mini-batch size
m
allows us to leverage parallel computing architectures while reducing the variance of the stochastic gradient
[
34
]
, however its effect on the iteration complexity of vanilla SGD is limited
[
7
]
and not fully understood in practice
[
18
]
.

A natural strategy for improving the performance of mini-batch SGD is to incorporate the classical momentum update (
3
) of Polyak, or its Nesterov counterpart. This approach has proven highly successful empirically, particularly when paired with large mini-batches
[
50
,
24
,
48
,
16
]
, and it inspired state-of-the-art training algorithms such as Adam
[
30
]
as well as many other more elaborate acceleration schemes for SGD
[

,

,
28
]
. However, this has led to a fundamental theory-practice gap: While there is significant theoretical progress on accelerating SGD through carefully designed schemes

,

,
26
,

,
35
,

]
, the SGD algorithms used in practice still largely rely on the simple and classical momentum based on HBM, whose remarkable effectiveness remains poorly understood. Initial efforts in that direction were done by Loizou and Richtarik
[
37
]
, who showed linear convergence of SGD with HBM, but without attaining any acceleration. Bollapragada, Chen and Ward
[
5

showed that HBM-accelerated mini-batch SGD can exhibit faster convergence on quadratic tasks, with related and follow-up works by
[

,

,

1.1
Our contributions

In this work, we address the above theory-practice gap by developing a general framework for analyzing the convergence of momentum-based stochastic processes, which encompasses mini-batch SGD with both HBM and NAG acceleration for stochastic quadratic minimization in the interpolation regime. Our analysis leads to a strikingly simple conclusion about the role of classical momentum in large-scale stochastic optimization:

Classical momentum enables perfect parallelization for mini-batch SGD

in the interpolation regime.

Main result.
Concretely, our main technical result (Theorem
1
) shows that properly tuned SGD using mini-batch size
m
and either HBM or NAG momentum attains
O\big((\kappa_{f}/m+\sqrt{\kappa_{f}})\log(\kappa_{f}/\epsilon)\big)
iteration complexity in the interpolation regime. Note that for
m=1
(no mini-batching) this result essentially recovers the previously mentioned linear convergence for SGD, but as we increase
m
, the iteration complexity improves proportionally to
1/m
until it saturates at an accelerated
\sqrt{\kappa_{f}}
rate. Thus, up to the point of saturation, the overall work (number of stochastic gradients computed) remains constant, allowing us to fully exploit the embarrassingly parallel mini-batch computations. Such perfect parallelization does not in general hold for mini-batch SGD without acceleration, even over quadratics in the interpolation regime. Thus, our analysis provides an explanation for the success of momentum acceleration in allowing deep learning optimizers such as Adam to benefit from large mini-batches and highly parallelizable GPU architectures.

Applications.
Our analysis framework can also be directly applied to accelerate several popular SGD algorithms for solving large-scale regression tasks, such as randomized Kaczmarz
[
49
]
and coordinate descent
[
20
]
. In particular, we use our theory to develop a simple momentum-accelerated block coordinate descent algorithm, and
we evaluate it on several kernel ridge regression tasks. Our numerical results confirm the perfect parallelization phenomenon, and they also show that a simple choice of
\beta=1-1/m
, as motivated by our theory, already leads to meaningful acceleration. We also compare classical momentum with an optimally tuned state-of-the-art acceleration scheme for block coordinate descent
[
11
]
, showing that, surprisingly, in certain large mini-batch regimes classical momentum yields substantially better performance.

1.2
Overview of our techniques

Limitations of prior approaches.

The convergence behavior of a momentum-accelerated stochastic method follows a dynamical system that is characterized by a product of non-symmetric random matrices. Existing matrix concentration results can be used to bound the spectral norm of a product of random matrices in terms of the product of the spectral norms of the expected values of the matrices
[
25
]
. Unfortunately, this strategy proves vacuous for momentum acceleration, as the product of norms diverges to infinity even when the norm of the product converges to zero, because of the lack of symmetry.
To address this,
[
5
]
diagonalize the matrices so that they can use matrix product concentration effectively. However, passing from the original matrices to the diagonalized form incurs a penalty factor that appears both in the convergence bound and in the required mini-batch size, and can blow up independently of the condition number of the problem. In fact, the penalty can even be infinite, as the underlying matrices may not be diagonalizable. To get around this,
[
41
]
diagonalize the matrices to a Jordan form, however this still does not avoid a potentially large penalty factor in both the convergence bound and the mini-batch size.

Our approach.

In order to get around these issues, we abandon both the diagonalization step and matrix product concentration. Instead, we use the Schur decomposition together with a matrix recursion to bound the original matrix product directly. Our analysis consists of two key components, following a bias-variance decomposition of the expected error.

1. Bias:
Convergence of expected iterates via Schur decomposition
.
For the expected iterates, it suffices to analyze the product of certain
deterministic
non-symmetric matrices. Here, we start by rearranging the matrices into a block-diagonal form, and carefully analyze both their real and complex eigenvalues in order to bound the spectral radius. Next, we turn this into a spectral norm bound for the matrix product. In this step, instead of using diagonalization as done in prior work, we rely on the Schur decomposition
[
43
]
, thereby avoiding condition number factors in our bound.

2. Variance:
Convergence of expected error via matrix recursion
.
In order to control the random iterates, we must bound the stochastic error incurred at each iteration of the process. Instead of relying on black-box matrix concentration results, we do this by deriving a recursion for the expectation of the product of the random matrices, which relates its spectral norm to all monomials of the individual block matrices from the block-diagonal representations derived in the bias analysis. Unfolding this recursion proves quite technical as it requires separately handling real and complex eigenvalues of the diagonal blocks.

Our analysis is not specific to one algorithm, but rather provides a general framework for effectively characterizing the convergence behavior of stochastic momentum methods. We illustrate this by stating our results for a class of stochastic processes which includes both HBM and NAG as special cases, and can be instantiated to provide convergence guarantees for a variety of SGD-type algorithms.

In particular, even in the setting of quadratic objectives, a comprehensive explanation of when and why these classical momentum methods yield acceleration is still lacking.

A substantial body of work has analyzed the behavior of mini-batch SGD with HBM and NAG

,

, attributing observed empirical gains primarily to variance reduction induced by large mini-batches. Despite these negative results, more recent works have revisited this question under restrictive settings, including large mini-batches, carefully tuned step sizes and momentum parameters, and specific assumptions on the data and noise

,
51

. For instance,

3
Stochastic momentum acceleration framework

Notation.

We represent vectors by lowercase boldface letters and matrices by uppercase boldface letters. For symmetric matrices, we use Loewner ordering and say
\mathbf{A}\preceq\mathbf{B}
if
\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}
is a positive semidefinite (psd) matrix.
\|\mathbf{A}\|
denotes spectral norm,
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}
denotes Frobenius norm of matrix
\mathbf{A}
. For a psd matrix
\mathbf{A}
,
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{A}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}
denotes the Mahalanobis norm of
\mathbf{x}
. We let
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
denote the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\mathbf{A}
. We use
\tilde{O}
,
\tilde{\Omega}
notation to hide logarithmic factors.

Stochastic contraction process.

We start by defining a class of stochastic dynamical systems called stochastic contraction processes
[
9
]
. This framework can be instantiated to represent the dynamics of many stochastic algorithms, as described below, and thus serves as a useful abstraction for our theory.

Definition 1

For a random psd matrix
\Pib
such that
\mathbf{0}\preceq\Pib\preceq\mathbf{I}
and scalar
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we let
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Delta};\alpha):=(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\Pib)\mathbf{\Delta}
define a stochastic contraction with average rate
\bar{\Pib}=\mathbb{E}[\Pib]
. We call
\Pib
the stochastic rate matrix of
\mathcal{A}
. Given a sequence of independent
\mathcal{A}_{t}
with the same
\bar{\Pib}
and
\alpha
, we call
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
a stochastic contraction process.

To see how this definition corresponds to an SGD algorithm, consider the minimization problem (
1
) over
F(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\mathbf{w};\xi)]
, where for every
\xi
, function
f(\mathbf{w};\xi)
is a convex quadratic with respect to
\mathbf{w}
that is minimized at some
\mathbf{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As long as function
f(\cdot;\xi)
is
L
-smooth, i.e.,
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w},\xi)-\nabla f(\mathbf{v},\xi)\|\leq L\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}\|
, then the evolution of the error vector
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}
resulting from the SGD update (
2
) with
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};\xi_{t})
follows a stochastic contraction process:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t};\alpha),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta};\alpha)=\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\alpha}{L}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{w}_{t};\xi_{t})\Big)\mathbf{\Delta}.

(4)

The stochastic rate matrix defining the stochastic contraction process
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is
\Pib_{t}=\frac{1}{L}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{w}_{t};\xi_{t})
, and its average rate is
\bar{\Pib}=\frac{1}{L}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
. Note that the Hessian is the same everywhere since both
f(\cdot;\xi_{t})
and
F
are assumed to be quadratic.

Stochastic momentum process.

We next show how to introduce momentum into this process by incorporating information from two preceding steps. We define these dynamics in sufficient generality to capture both HBM and NAG momentum as special cases.

Definition 2

Given a stochastic contraction process

(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
, initial vectors
\mathbf{\Delta}_{-1},\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}
, and parameters
\beta,\omega\geq 0
, define the corresponding stochastic momentum process recursively as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t};\alpha)+\beta\Big[\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t};\omega)-\mathcal{A}_{t-1}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1};\omega)\Big].

(5)

Note that if we set the momentum parameter
\beta
to zero, then we recover the usual SGD dynamics of a stochastic contraction process. On the other hand, if we set
\beta>0
but
\omega=0
, then the momentum term simplifies to
\beta[\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}-\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}]
, which precisely corresponds to Polyak’s heavy-ball momentum (HBM). On the other hand, by choosing
\omega=\alpha
we introduce one more SGD step to the momentum term, thereby recovering Nesterov’s momentum (NAG). For example, we can use this framework to model HBM acceleration for the SGD process
\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
from (
4
) by letting

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};\xi_{t})+\beta(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t-1}).

It is easy to verify that such a sequence
\{\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a stochastic momentum process associated with
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
, momentum parameter
\beta
, and
\omega=0
. A similar derivation follows immediately for Nesterov’s momentum by setting
\omega=\alpha
.

Example 1: Randomized Kaczmarz.

As a concrete example of an algorithm that falls directly under our framework, we consider the randomized Kaczmarz (RK) algorithm
[
49
]
, along with its block versions
[
39
]
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
define a linear regression problem with the loss function
F(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
. Also, let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over subsets
S\subseteq\{1,...,n\}
, and consider the following update:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\ =\argmin_{\mathbf{w}:\,\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{y}_{S_{t}}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\|^{2}\ =\ \mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{S_{t}}),\qquad S_{t}\sim\mathcal{D},

(6)

where
\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}
denotes the submatrix consisting of rows indexed by
S_{t}
. In particular, when
S\sim\mathcal{D}
generates single-sample sets such that
\Pr[S\!=\!\{i\}]\propto\|\mathbf{A}_{i,:}\|^{2}
, then this recovers classical randomized Kaczmarz. When there exists
\mathbf{w}^{*}
such that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}^{*}=\mathbf{y}
(i.e., in the interpolation regime), the update (
6
) can be cast as a stochastic contraction process
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},1)
by letting
\Pib_{t}=\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}
. In particular, for randomized Kaczmarz, a simple calculation shows that this process has an average rate
\bar{\Pib}=\mathbb{E}[\Pib_{t}]=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}/\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}
. Similar or better rates have been derived for block versions of Kaczmarz
[
13
,
11
]
, and other extensions such as sketch-and-project
[
12
,
46
,
45
]
. By applying momentum via Definition
2
to
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},1)
, we can precisely recover convergence dynamics of the error vector of the accelerated Kaczmarz algorithm considered by
[
5
]
.

Example 2: Coordinate Descent.

Another application of our framework comes in the context of solving positive semidefinite linear systems, such as in kernel ridge regression. For a positive definite matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the (block) coordinate descent (CD,
[
33
]
) update:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{S_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{K}_{S_{t},S_{t}})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{y})_{S_{t}},\qquad S_{t}\sim\mathcal{D},

(7)

which only updates the coordinates of
\mathbf{w}_{t}
indexed by
S_{t}
. Letting
\mathbf{w}^{*}
denote the solution of the linear system
\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{y}
, we can cast (
7
) as a stochastic contraction process
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},1)
by choosing
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}=\mathbf{K}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*})
and
\Pib_{t}=\mathbf{K}_{S_{t}}^{1/2}(\mathbf{K}_{S_{t},S_{t}})^{\dagger}\mathbf{K}_{S_{t}}^{1/2}
. Here, the canonical distribution
\mathcal{D}
for single-sample coordinate descent is
\Pr[S\!=\!\{i\}]\propto K_{i,i}
, which yields average rate
\bar{\Pib}=\mathbf{K}/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K})
.

Convergence rate of a stochastic contraction process.

The above examples are of base algorithms, i.e., without momentum acceleration. Before we analyze their accelerated variants through the stochastic momentum framework, we establish their base convergence rate via our general formalism.

Proposition 1

Given a stochastic contraction process
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
with average rate
\bar{\Pib}
, and an initial vector
\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\in\mathrm{range}(\bar{\Pib})
, consider the sequence
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t};\alpha)
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}\leq\Big(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}\Big)^{t}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\|^{2},\qquad\text{where}\quad\kappa=\alpha/\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\bar{\Pib}).

We will refer to
\kappa
as the stochastic condition number associated with the process
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
. For instance, by plugging in our concrete examples of (single-sample) randomized Kaczmarz and coordinate descent
[
49
]
, we recover the classical convergence guarantees for these methods, with stochastic condition numbers
\kappa_{\mathrm{RK}}=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}\|\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\|^{2}
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{CD}}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K})\|\mathbf{K}^{\dagger}\|
.

Effect of mini-batching.

The stochastic contraction framework is naturally amenable to mini-batching of the updates via simple averaging of the contractions. It is thus natural to ask how mini-batching affects the convergence dynamics.

Definition 3

Given a stochastic contraction
\mathcal{A}
with average rate
\bar{\Pib}
, let
\mathcal{A}^{[m]}
denote the average of
m
independent copies of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{[m]}(\mathbf{\Delta};\alpha):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{A}^{(i)}(\mathbf{\Delta};\alpha),\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{A}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{A}.

(8)

For example, if
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
is a stochastic contraction process for the SGD algorithm given by (
4
), then
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}^{[m]}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
corresponds to mini-batch SGD where
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=\frac{1}{mL}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t},\xi_{t}^{(i)})
. Note that mini-batching is different from choosing a larger block set
S_{t}
in randomized Kaczmarz or coordinate descent, since those schemes do more than just averaging the samples (such as inverting the matrices
\mathbf{A}_{S_{t}}
and
\mathbf{K}_{S_{t},S_{t}}
). In fact, combining blocking and mini-batching in RK and CD is a very natural strategy for making these methods scalable to parallel architectures, as discussed later.

Proposition 2

If
\mathcal{A}
is a stochastic contraction with average rate
\bar{\Pib}
, then
\mathcal{A}^{[m]}
is also a stochastic contraction with the same average rate
\bar{\Pib}
. Moreover, if
\Pib^{[m]}
is the stochastic rate matrix associated with
\mathcal{A}^{[m]}
, then it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\Pib^{[m]}-\bar{\Pib}\big)^{2}\Big]\preceq\frac{1}{m}\cdot\bar{\Pib}(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\Pib}).

(9)

Proposition
2
, together with Proposition
1
, illustrates why mini-batching of SGD does not in general lead to faster convergence rates, since the average rate matrix
\bar{\Pib}
(and thus also the associated stochastic condition number) remains the same regardless of
m
. However, mini-batching does reduce the variance of the stochastic noise matrix, as shown in (
9
), which will be crucial for the stochastic momentum dynamics. We defer the proofs of Propositions
1
and
2
to Appendix
D
.

4
Main result

We are now ready to state our main result, which characterizes the convergence dynamics of a stochastic momentum process (Definition
2
) corresponding to stochastic contractions with mini-batching (Definitions
1
and
3
). Crucially, our result makes no assumptions on the mini-batch size
m
or the stochastic noise, and applies to both heavy-ball and Nesterov momentum.

Theorem 1

Consider a stochastic contraction process

(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
with
\alpha\in[0,1]
, average rate
\bar{\Pib}
, and condition number
\kappa=\alpha/\lambda_{\min}^{+}(\bar{\Pib})
. For
m\geq 1
,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\in\mathrm{range}(\bar{\Pib})
, let
\mathbf{\Delta}_{-1}=\mathbf{0}
,
\{\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a stochastic momentum process for
(\{\mathcal{A}_{t}^{[m]}\}_{t\geq 0},\alpha)
with
\omega\in[0,\alpha]
and momentum parameter

\displaystyle\beta=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\quad m\leq c_{1},\\
1-\frac{c_{1}}{2m}&\text{if }\quad m\in[c_{1},c_{2}\sqrt{\kappa}],\\
1-\frac{c_{1}}{2c_{2}\sqrt{\kappa}}&\text{if }\quad m\geq c_{2}\sqrt{\kappa},\end{cases}

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are absolute constants. Then, there are absolute constants
C,c>0
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}\leq Ct^{3}\bigg(1-c\min\Big\{\frac{m}{\kappa},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\Big\}\bigg)^{t}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\|^{2}\qquad\text{for any }t\geq 1.

Remark 1

In particular, we get
\mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\|^{2}
after
t=O((\kappa/m+\sqrt{\kappa})\log(\kappa/\epsilon))
iterations, compared to
t=O(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
without momentum. Thus, for
m\in[1,\Theta(\sqrt{\kappa})]
, momentum enables perfect parallelization up to a logarithmic factor (which is caused by the
t^{3}
term in the bound).

Remark 2

In the regime
m\in[1,\Theta(\sqrt{\kappa})]
, our result yields a simple problem-independent momentum parameter
\beta=1-\Theta(1/m)
. Numerical experiments confirm that this choice works well in practice.

4.1
Proof sketch

First, without loss of generality we can assume that
\alpha=1
(otherwise, we can simply rescale
\bar{\Pib}
appropriately). Also, note that the regime of
1\leq m\leq c_{1}
follows immediately from Proposition
1
. Next, let
\{\Pib_{t}^{[m]}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the stochastic noise matrices associated with
\{\mathcal{A}_{t}^{[m]}\}_{t\geq 0}
. From Proposition
2
, these matrices satisfy the variance bound (
9
). In fact, this is the only property we need out of the mini-batching, so to simplify the notation, we will drop the superscript and consider a sequence
\{\Pib_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of matrices such that
\mathbb{E}\,(\Pib_{t}-\bar{\Pib})^{2}\preceq\frac{1}{m}\bar{\Pib}(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\Pib})
. With these conventions, after simple calculations the stochastic momentum process (
5
) can be expressed as the following recursion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}=\big((1+\beta)\mathbf{I}-(1+\beta\omega)\Pib_{t}\big)\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}-\beta\big(\mathbf{I}-\omega\Pib_{t-1}\big)\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}.

(10)

For notational inconvenience, we restrict ourselves to NAG acceleration in this proof sketch (i.e.
\omega=\alpha=1
). The detailed proof in the appendix covers HBM and NAG momentum simultaneously.
Let
\bar{\Pib}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
be the eigendecomposition of
\bar{\Pib}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{d})
and
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}
. Let
\mathrm{rank}(\bar{\Pib})=r\leq d
, so that
\lambda_{i}=0
for
i>r
. Following prior works
[
26
,
5
,
41
]
, we study the dynamics of stochastic process
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
in (
10
) via the following two-step matrix transition rule:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Pib_{t}\mathbf{V})&-\beta(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Pib_{t-1}\mathbf{V})\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}}_{:=\mathbf{Y}_{t}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{{t-1}}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-2}\end{bmatrix}

(11)

We break down the proof of Theorem
1
into two main components, starting with the convergence of the expected iterates (bias), and then analyzing the effect of the stochastic noise (variance).

4.1.1
Bias: Convergence of
\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|

The convergence behavior of the expected iterates is characterized by a simpler transition rule:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{T}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{where }\ \mathbf{T}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_{t}]=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda})&-\beta(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

The first thing that comes to mind is to use
\big\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|\leq\|\mathbf{T}\|^{t}
, but, unfortunately, for non-zero
\beta
the spectral norm of
\mathbf{T}
is greater than
1
, making this naive idea ineffective. However, the spectral radius of
\mathbf{T}
is less than
1
and this paves the way ahead, suggesting a two step approach:

Step 1:
Bound the spectral radius of
\mathbf{T}^{t}
, denoted as
\rho(\mathbf{T}^{t})
;

Step 2:
Using structural properties of
\mathbf{T}
, bound
\big\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|
in terms of
\rho(\mathbf{T}^{t})
.

Step 1.

By carefully permuting rows and columns of
\mathbf{T}
, we can transform it into a block-diagonal matrix with
2
by
2
blocks on the diagonal. In particular, the following lemma holds:

Lemma 1
(Block-diagonalization)

There exists a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
such that,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\diag(\mathbf{T}_{1},\mathbf{T}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{T}_{d}),\quad\text{where }\ \mathbf{T}_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)(1-\lambda_{i})&-\beta(1-\lambda_{i})\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Due to Lemma
1
, taking maximum over the spectral radius of each
\mathbf{T}_{i}
will upper bound
\rho(\mathbf{T})
. Interestingly, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
depend on interaction between
\lambda_{i}
and
\beta
, and can be either real or complex depending upon whether
\lambda_{i}\leq\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
. Let
\gamma_{i}
denote the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
with the larger magnitude, so that
|\gamma_{i}|
equals the spectral radius of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
. We prove the following result:

Lemma 2
(Spectral radius of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
)

Let
\beta=1-\frac{1}{\phi}
with
\phi\geq 2
. Then,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i}|^{2}\leq\begin{cases}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{\phi}\big)(1-\lambda_{i}),&\text{if }\ \lambda_{i}\geq\tfrac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}\;\text{ (i.e., when $\gamma_{i}$ is complex)},\\[4.0pt]
\big(1-\tfrac{\phi\lambda_{i}}{2}\big)^{2}(1-\lambda_{i})^{2},&\text{otherwise}\;\text{ (i.e., when $\gamma_{i}$ is real).}\end{cases}

It is easy to see that the maximum is attained at
i=r
, thus yielding an upper bound on
\rho(\mathbf{T})
.

Step 2.

To go from
\rho(\mathbf{T}^{t})
to
\big\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|
, previous work
[
5
]
assumes that
\mathbf{T}
is diagonalizable, i.e., there exists an invertible matrix
\mathbf{C}
such that
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}
is a diagonal matrix. This assumption implies,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|\leq\big\|\mathbf{C}\|\cdot\big\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\|\cdot\rho(\mathbf{T}^{t}).

Unfortunately,
\mathbf{T}
need not be diagonalizable. In fact, if for any
i
,
\lambda_{i}=\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
, then
\mathbf{T}
is not diagonalizable. More problematically, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\|=\Omega\Big(\frac{\phi}{\delta}\Big),\quad\text{where \ $\delta=\min_{i}\Big\{\big|\lambda_{i}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}\big|\Big\}$}.

To avoid this blow up, we abandon the diagonalization approach and rely on a fundamental matrix decomposition result in linear algebra, known as Schur’s decomposition.

Lemma 3
(Schur’s decomposition
[
43
]
)

For every
i
, there exists a unitary matrix
\mathbf{U}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{H}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{i1}&x_{i}\\
0&\gamma_{i2}\end{bmatrix},

where
\gamma_{i1}
and
\gamma_{i2}
are the two eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
,
1\leq|x_{i}|\leq 3
and
\mathbf{U}_{i}^{H}
denotes the hermitian of
\mathbf{U}_{i}
.

Using Schur’s decomposition it readily follows that for any
i
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\|=\|\mathbf{U}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\mathbf{U}_{i}\|\leq O(t)\cdot|\gamma_{i}|^{t-1}.

Using the above in conjunction with Lemma
2
we show that:

Theorem 2
(Convergence of
\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|
)

For
\rho=\big(1-\frac{\phi}{2\kappa}\big)(1-\frac{1}{\kappa})
, the transition rule (
11
) satisfies,

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}\leq O(t^{2})\cdot\rho^{t-2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\|^{2}.

4.1.2
Variance: Convergence of
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}

Note that (
11
) implies,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}=\Big(\underbrace{\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}_{t-j}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}}\Big)\cdot\mathbf{P}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

To establish convergence of
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}
, it suffices to bound
\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\|
. Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}
is a product of non-symmetric random matrices, upper bounding
\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\big\|
is challenging. Prior work
[
5
]
uses matrix product concentration
[
25
]
and require careful tuning of the momentum parameter to keep
\mathbf{T}
diagonalizable while controlling
\|\mathbf{C}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\|
. This leads to mini-batch requirements scaling as
\|\mathbf{C}\|^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}\|^{2}
, which can become arbitrarily large near non-diagonalizability, and introduces additional condition number dependence in
m
. Consequently, their guarantees degrade rapidly unless the mini-batch size is sufficiently large. These limitations stem from the reliance on product concentration results and diagonalization. In contrast, we avoid both and directly bound
\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\|
.

Recursive approach to bound
\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\|
.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]
denote the matrix mean and
\mathbf{M}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]-\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}
denote the matrix variance of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}
. Moreover, let
\bar{\mathbf{Y}}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]
and
\mathbf{R}_{t}:=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_{t}])\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
. Then, for any
p_{t-1}\geq\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}]\big\|
, we have the following matrix recursion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{t}\preceq p_{t-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}_{t-1}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}.

(13)

Note that,
\bar{\mathbf{Y}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}
are block-diagonal due to Lemma
1
. If one can show that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]
is upper bounded by block-diagonal matrices, then via the matrix recursion, so is
\mathbf{M}_{t}
. We show this in the following lemma.

Lemma 4

Let
\mathbf{P}
be the permutation matrix from Lemma
1
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]

\displaystyle\preceq 4\cdot\diag(\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{W}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{W}_{n}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbf{W}_{i}=\diag\Big(\frac{\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}{m},\frac{\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}{m}\Big).

Using Lemma
4
and the repeated use of (
13
), it follows that both
\mathbf{M}_{t}
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]
are also upper bounded by block-diagonal matrices. By taking a maximum over those blocks, we prove the following recursive expression for
\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\big\|
.

Lemma 5

For any
t\geq 1
, if there exist
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{t-1}
such that
\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}]\big\|\leq p_{k}
for
0\leq k\leq t-1
, then there exists
p_{t}
such that for
q_{i}=4\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})/m
,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]\big\|\leq p_{t}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\big\|+q_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}{p_{j}\cdot\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-1-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-1-j}\big\|}\Big).

Unfolding the recursion.

The next step is to unfold the recursive expression for
p_{t}
. By coupling with a simpler recursion, we show that, defining
\rho_{t}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\big(|\gamma_{i}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\big)
,

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t}^{t}+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}{\rho_{t}^{j}\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-j}\big\|},

(14)

for a carefully defined function
\ell_{i}(t)
, see (
25
). We can now use the bias analysis (Lemmas
2
and
3
) to obtain tight bounds for the terms
\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-j}\big\|
. The final challenging task is to bound
\rho_{t}
.

Upper bounding
\rho_{t}
.

We provide a fine-grained analysis of
\rho_{t}
by carefully analyzing
|\gamma_{i}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)
for every single
i\leq r
. Recall that, depending upon the interaction between
\lambda_{i}
and
\beta
, the eigenvalue
\gamma_{i}
can be real or complex (Lemma
2
).

Case 1:
\lambda_{i}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
is sufficiently positive.

This is the easy case where
\gamma_{i}
is complex, and we get

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)<1-\frac{1}{32\phi},\qquad\text{for}\ \phi\leq\frac{m}{C}.

(15)

Case 2:
\lambda_{i}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
is close to zero.

In this case, we enter the regime of near non-diagonalizability of
\mathbf{T}
. A crude analysis here would lead to a blow up factor in
m
. However, we are able to avoid this by exploiting the specific form of
\ell_{i}(t)
and combine it with
\lambda_{i}\approx\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
to show that (
15
) still holds.

Case 3:
\lambda_{i}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
is sufficiently negative.

Previous works such as
[
5
,
41
,
32
]
select large values for the momentum parameter
\beta
and for the mini-batch size
m
, because they enforce the condition that
\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
. Our crucial insight is that, for smaller values of
\beta
, the quantity
\lambda_{i}-\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(1+\beta)^{2}}
can be negative and sufficiently bounded away from
0
for some
i
. In fact, this is the case that determines the convergence rate for
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\|^{2}
and allows us to handle SGD with classical momentum even under small mini-batches (see details in Appendix
B
). In particular, in this case we show that for
\phi\leq\frac{m}{C}
,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\leq 1-\frac{\phi\lambda_{i}}{4}.

(16)

Combining (
15
), (
16
) and maximizing over
i
, bounds
\rho_{t}
. Substituting in (
14
) and bounding
\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-j}\big\|
bounds
p_{t}
. Noting the relation between
m
and
\phi
finishes the proof of Theorem
1
.

(\beta=1-1/m)

. Furthermore, as shown in Fig.

6
Conclusions

We develop a theoretical framework for quantifying the effect of classical momentum schemes like heavy ball on the convergence of mini-batch SGD. Our results demonstrate that momentum enables perfect parallelization of mini-batch computations, which is confirmed by numerical experiments.

Appendix A
Convergence analysis for expected iterates

We start by recalling the matrix transition rule (
11
),

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)\mathbf{I}-(1+\beta\omega)\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Pib_{t}\mathbf{V}&-\beta\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\omega\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Pib_{t-1}\mathbf{V})\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}}_{=\mathbf{Y}_{t}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t-1}\end{bmatrix}

where
\omega\in[0,1]
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{-1}=0
. As
\text{range}(\Pib_{t})\subset\text{range}(\bar{\Pib})
, we note that if
\mathbf{\Delta}_{0},\mathbf{\Delta}_{1}\in\text{range}(\bar{\Pib})
, then for all
t
,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\in\text{range}(\bar{\Pib})
. In this section, we analyze the update rule (
11
) in expectation.
Let
\mathbf{T}
denote
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}_{t}
. We have,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)\mathbf{I}-(1+\beta\omega)\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\Pib}\mathbf{V}&-\beta\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\omega\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\Pib}\mathbf{V})\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

It is useful to transform
\mathbf{T}
into a block-diagonal form. The following lemma guarantees that a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
exists such that
\mathbf{T}
can be transformed into a block-diagonal matrix.

Lemma 6
(Block-diagonalization via permutation)

There exists a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
such that,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\diag(\mathbf{T}_{1},\mathbf{T}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{T}_{n}),

where
\mathbf{T}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)-(1+\beta\omega)\lambda_{i}&-\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\\
\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\\
\cdots\\
\mathbf{v}_{d}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\\
\mathbf{v}_{d}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\end{bmatrix}.

The resulting block-diagonal structure helps us throughout the analysis. As
(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}^{t}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
and
\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}^{t}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\|=\|\mathbf{T}^{t}\|
, we need to upper bound
\|(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}\|
. Due to the block-diagonal form of
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, we have
\|(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}\|=\max_{i}\|\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\|
. For any fixed
i
, let
\gamma_{i1},\gamma_{i2}\in\mathbb{C}
denote the two eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
. We always take
\gamma_{i1}
to be the eigenvalue with larger magnitude. In the next lemma, we provide a tight upper bound on the magnitude of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
.

Lemma 7
(Bounding spectral radius of transition matrix)

Let
\omega\in[0,1]
and
\beta=1-\frac{1}{\phi}
for some
\phi\geq 2
. Then, the following holds:

If
\mathbf{T}_{i}
has complex eigenvalues then,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i2}|^{2}=|\gamma_{i1}|^{2}=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\phi}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i}).

If
\mathbf{T}_{i}
has real and equal eigenvalues then,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i2}|^{2}=|\gamma_{i1}|^{2}=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\phi}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i}).

If
\mathbf{T}_{i}
has real and distinct eigenvalues then,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i2}|^{2}<|\gamma_{i1}|^{2}<\Big(1-\frac{\phi\lambda_{i}}{2}\Big)^{2}(1-\omega\lambda_{i})^{2}.

Proof

We have,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+\beta)-(1+\beta\omega)\lambda_{i}&-\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}

It is easy to verify that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
, i.e.,
\gamma_{i1}
and
\gamma_{i2}
are determined by the following expression.

\displaystyle(\gamma_{i1},\gamma_{i2})=\frac{1}{2}\Big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\pm\sqrt{\big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\big)^{2}-4\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})}\Big).

The expression inside the square root can be negative, zero, or positive. So we consider three cases:

1.

\big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\big)^{2}-4\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})<0
: In this case, both
\gamma_{i1}
and
\gamma_{i2}
are complex and have the same magnitude. We get,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{i2}|^{2}=|\gamma_{i1}|^{2}=\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\phi}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i}).

(17)

2.

\big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\big)^{2}-4\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})=0
. In this case,
\gamma_{i1}^{2}=\gamma_{i2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\Big)^{2}=\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma_{i2}^{2}=\gamma_{i1}^{2}=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\phi}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i}).

(18)

3.

\big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\big)^{2}-4\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})>0
. The larger eigenvalue is given as,

\displaystyle\gamma_{i1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\pm\sqrt{\big((1-\lambda_{i})+\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})\big)^{2}-4\beta(1-\omega\lambda_{i})}\Big).

(19)

Let
u_{i}=\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{i1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+\sqrt{\big(u_{i}+\beta\big)^{2}-\frac{4\beta}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+\sqrt{u_{i}^{2}+\beta^{2}+\frac{2\beta(1-\lambda_{i})}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}-\frac{4\beta}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+\sqrt{u_{i}^{2}+\beta^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1+\lambda_{i})}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+\sqrt{u_{i}^{2}+\beta^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\lambda_{i})}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}-\frac{4\beta\lambda_{i}}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+\sqrt{(u_{i}-\beta)^{2}-\frac{4\beta\lambda_{i}}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

Let
x=u_{i}-\beta
,
y=\sqrt{\frac{4\beta\lambda_{i}}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}}
, then we have
x=\frac{yr}{2}
, where
r=\frac{2x}{y}>2
. Now, if
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
x>y>0
and
x\leq\frac{yr}{2}
for some
r>2
, then
\sqrt{x^{2}-y^{2}}\leq x-\frac{y}{r}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\gamma_{i1}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{i}+\beta+u_{i}-\beta-\frac{y}{r}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\Big(u_{i}-\frac{y^{2}}{4x}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{y^{2}}{4(1-\beta)}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i}).

The last inequality holds as
u_{i}\leq 1
and
x\leq 1-\beta
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{i1}<\Big(1-\frac{\phi\beta\lambda_{i}}{1-\omega\lambda_{i}}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

Now for
\beta\geq 0.5
, we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{i1}<\Big(1-\frac{\phi\lambda_{i}}{2}\Big)(1-\omega\lambda_{i})

(20)

Combining the relations (
17
), (
18
) and (
20
) we finish the proof.

Lemma 9

For every

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}]-\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t-1}\big)\bar{\mathbf{Y}}+\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}]\mathbf{R}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]-\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}}_{\mathbf{M}_{t}}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]\mathbf{R}_{t}\big]+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}]-\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t-1}\big)\bar{\mathbf{Y}}.

By induction hypothesis
\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1}]\big\|\leq p_{t-1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{t}

\displaystyle\preceq p_{t-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}_{t-1}\bar{\mathbf{Y}},

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}]

\displaystyle\preceq\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{t}+p_{t-1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}_{t-1}\bar{\mathbf{Y}},

(24)

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{R}_{t}]=\mathbf{M}_{1}

10

\mathbf{M}_{1}

\mathbf{D}_{k}

\mathbf{D}_{k-1}

23

\mathbf{M}_{k}\preceq\mathbf{D}_{k}

\mathbf{M}_{k-1}\preceq\mathbf{D}_{k-1}

\forall k,\ \mathbf{M}_{k}\preceq\mathbf{D}_{k}
, and
\mathbf{D}_{k}^{\prime}s

\mathbf{D}_{k}=p_{k-1}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{1}+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}_{k-1}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}

\mathbf{D}_{t}

\mathbf{T}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{t,i}

\displaystyle=p_{t-1}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{1,i}+\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(p_{t-2}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{1,i}+\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}_{t-2,i}\mathbf{T}_{i})\mathbf{T}_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{t-1}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{1,i}+p_{t-2}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}_{1,i}\mathbf{T}_{i}+(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{2}\mathbf{D}_{t-2,i}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}{p_{j}\cdot(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-1-j}\mathbf{D}_{1,i}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-1-j}},\text{or}

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{t-1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t-2}{p_{j}\cdot(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-2-j}\mathbf{D}_{1,i}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-2-j}}.

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\big\|

Using triangle inequality and norm submultiplicativity, we get,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\big\|\leq p_{t}=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\big\|+\|\mathbf{D}_{1,i}\|\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}{p_{j}\cdot\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-1-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-1-j}}\big\|\Big).

The proof follows by substituting for

10

in Lemma

Lemma 12

For any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{k}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{k}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq 11\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{2k}\cdot h_{i}^{2}(k).

where
h_{i}(k)=\min\big\{\frac{k}{|\gamma_{i1}|},\frac{2}{|\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2}|}\big\}
if
\gamma_{i1}\neq\gamma_{i2}

if
\gamma_{i1}=\gamma_{i2}

Proof

We use Lemma
9
to upper bound
\|\mathbf{T}_{i}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}
. Note that,

\displaystyle\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{k}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{k}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{T}_{i}^{k}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(|\gamma_{i1}|^{2k}+|x_{i}|^{2}|g_{i}(k)|^{2}+|\gamma_{i2}|^{2k}\Big),

where
g_{i}(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\gamma_{i1}^{j}\gamma_{i2}^{k-1-j}}
. We have,
|g_{i}(k)|=|\gamma_{i1}|^{k}\cdot\min\big\{\frac{k}{|\gamma_{i1}|},\frac{|1-(\gamma_{i2}/\gamma_{i1})^{k}|}{|\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2}|}\big\}\leq|\gamma_{i1}|^{k}\cdot h_{i}(k)
. Note that
h_{i}(k)
is always larger than
1
. Moreover, as

|x_{i}|\leq 3
, we get,

\displaystyle\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{k}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{k}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq 11\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{2k}\cdot h_{i}^{2}(k).

Now suppose
t>0
and our aim is to upper bound
p_{t+1}
. In the following analysis, we construct an upper bound for
p_{t+1}
, by defining
f_{t+1}
, which itself has a recursive definition, but a simpler one compared to
p_{t+1}
. We will use Lemma
11
and Lemma
12
to build this recursion. For convenience, consider the following notations:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(t):=\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t}\big\|,\ \ q_{i}:=\frac{4\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}{m},\ \ \ell_{i}(t):=\max_{0\leq k\leq t}\Big\{11\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{k}\cdot h_{i}^{2}(k)\Big\},

(25)

where
h_{i}(k)
is defined in Lemma
12
.
We have
p_{0}=1
, and

\displaystyle p_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big\{\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t+1}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t+1}\big\|+q_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{t}{p_{j}\cdot\big\|(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{t-j}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{t-j}\big\|}\Big\}

\displaystyle<\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big\{\alpha_{i}(t+1)+q_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{t}{p_{j}\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{t-j}\cdot\ell_{i}(t-j)}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big\{\alpha_{i}(t+1)+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{t}{p_{j}\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{t-j}}\Big\}.

Informed by the above relations, we construct upper bounds for
p_{k}
for
0\leq k\leq t+1
, denoted by
f_{k}
and defined as follows:

\displaystyle f_{0}=1,

\displaystyle f_{k+1}=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\alpha_{i}(k+1)+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k}{f_{j}\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{k-j}}\Big)\ \ \text{for $0\leq k\leq t$.}

Note that
p_{k+1}\leq f_{k+1}
for all
0\leq k\leq t
. For any fixed
k
such that

,

\displaystyle f_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\alpha_{i}(k+1)+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k}{f_{j}\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{k-j}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\alpha_{i}(k+1)+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\cdot f_{k}+|\gamma_{i1}|\cdot\underbrace{q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{f_{j}\cdot|\gamma_{i1}|^{k-1-j}}}_{*}\Big)

Now note, irrespective of the index
i

*
is always upper bounded by

. Therefore,

\displaystyle f_{k+1}

\displaystyle<\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\Big(\alpha_{i}(k+1)+\big(|\gamma_{i1}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\big)\cdot f_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\big(|\gamma_{i1}|+q_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(t)\big)\cdot f_{k}+\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(k+1)

\displaystyle\leq\rho\cdot f_{k}+\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(k+1).

where we let

\displaystyle f_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\cdot f_{t}+\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2}\cdot f_{t-1}+\rho\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(t)+\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{t+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{t}{\rho^{j}\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\alpha_{i}(t+1-j)}.

(26)

We need to bound

. In the following lemma, we provide a bound on the term

for any

Lemma 13

\displaystyle|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(4\beta(1-\omega\lambda)-\big((1-\lambda)+\beta(1-\omega\lambda)\big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle|\gamma_{1}|+q\cdot\ell(t)<\Big(1-\frac{1}{2\phi}\Big)+\frac{44\lambda}{m}\cdot\min\Big\{2(k^{*})^{2},\frac{16}{4\beta\delta-(1+\beta\delta)^{2}(1-\lambda)}\Big\}\cdot|\gamma_{1}|^{k^{*}}

(28)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4\beta\delta}{(1+\beta\delta)^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\beta\delta)^{2}}

(30)

(b)

2.

Case 2:

Finally using (

Under certain incoherence assumptions on the data matrix

, the condition number of

can be bounded as

Proof of Proposition
1

As
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{A}_{t}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{t};\alpha)=(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\Pib_{t})\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\Pib_{t})^{2}]\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\Pib_{t})]\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\qquad\text{(since $\mathbf{0}\preceq\Pib_{t}\preceq\mathbf{I}$ and $0<\alpha\leq 1$)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\alpha\bar{\Pib})\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}.

As
\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\in\mathrm{range}(\bar{\Pib})
, all
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\in\mathrm{range}(\bar{\Pib})
with probability
1
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq\Big(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}\Big)\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{0}\|^{2},

where
\kappa=\alpha/\lambda^{+}_{\min}(\bar{\Pib})
.

Proof of Proposition
2

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\Pib^{[m]}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\Pib^{(i)}},

where
\Pib^{(i)}
is the stochastic rate matrix associated with
\mathcal{A}^{(i)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[(\Pib^{[m]}-\bar{\Pib})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=(\Pib^{[m]})^{2}+\bar{\Pib}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\Pib^{[m]}]\cdot\bar{\Pib}-\bar{\Pib}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Pib^{[m]}]

\displaystyle=\big(\Pib^{[m]}\big)^{2}-\bar{\Pib}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\Pib^{(i)}\big)^{2}\big]}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{m}{\mathbb{E}\big[\Pib^{(i)}\Pib^{(j)}\big]}-\bar{\Pib}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\Pib^{(1)}\big)^{2}\big]-\frac{1}{m}\cdot\bar{\Pib}^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{m}\cdot\bar{\Pib}(\mathbf{I}-\bar{\Pib}).
