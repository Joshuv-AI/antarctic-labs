Excessive postpartum body condition score loss coincides with skeletal muscle proteolysis in dairy cows: molecular and biochemical evidence
Authors: Saman Chalaki, Essa Dirandeh, Zarbakht Ansari-Pirsaraei, Barry Bradford, William W Thatcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transition period (− 21 to + 21 days relative to calving) in dairy cows is characterized by profound metabolic adaptations that often result in negative energy balance (NEB) and mobilization of body reserves. This study evaluated the association of postpartum body condition score (BCS) loss on the expression of skeletal muscle genes involved in protein metabolism and related biomarkers in blood. Two hundred high-yielding multiparous Holstein cows (≥ 30 kg milk/day) without postpartum disorders were grouped according to BCS loss between calving and + 42 days in milk (DIM): 0.00, − 0.25, − 0.50, and − 0.75 units. Muscle biopsies (n = 40) were collected on days 21 and 42 postpartum for quantification of mRNA abundance of ubiquitin–proteasome pathway genes (UBE2G1, UBE2G2, UBA1, MuRF1, Atrogin-1) and mTOR signaling genes (mTOR, EIF3K, 4EBP1). Cows losing − 0.50 or − 0.75 BCS units exhibited significantly higher expression of UBE2G1, UBE2G2, UBA1, MuRF1, and Atrogin-1, and lower expression of 4EBP1, compared with cows with no or mild BCS loss (P < 0.05). mTOR and EIF3K expression were unaffected. Cows with greater postpartum BCS loss (0.50 and 0.75 units) had significantly higher (P < 0.05) plasma concentrations of creatinine and 3-methylhistidine compared with cows that lost 0 to 0.25 BCS units. These increases were proportional to the extent of BCS loss, with the highest concentrations observed in the 0.75-unit group. These findings indicate that moderate to severe BCS loss is associated with activation of proteolytic pathways and suppression of key regulators of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle, underscoring the importance of maintaining optimal BCS during the transition period to preserve muscle integrity and metabolic health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58740-3.Keywords: Body condition score, Dairy cow, Transition period, Skeletal muscle, Gene expression, Protein metabolismSubject terms: Biochemistry, Molecular biology, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

The transition period, defined as the interval from approximately 3 weeks prepartum to 3 weeks postpartum, represents the most critical stage in the productive life of a dairy cow. During this time, cows experience dramatic physiological adaptations to support the onset of lactation, which often leads to negative energy balance (NEB) due to insufficient dry matter intake (DMI) relative to energy demands1,2.

To cope with NEB, cows mobilize body reserves in the form of adipose tissue and skeletal muscle. While lipid mobilization has been extensively studied, there is increasing recognition that muscle protein catabolism plays a crucial role in providing gluconeogenic substrates and amino acids for milk protein synthesis, thereby sustaining milk production during periods of energy deficit3–5.

Body condition score (BCS) is widely used as a non-invasive indicator of energy balance in dairy cows. Excessive postpartum BCS loss (> 0.5 units within 30–40 d postpartum) has been linked with decreased fertility, reduced milk yield, impaired immune function, and increased risk of metabolic disorders6–8. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the relationship between postpartum BCS loss and skeletal muscle metabolism remain poorly defined.

The dynamic balance between protein synthesis and protein degradation regulates skeletal muscle protein turnover. The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway acts as a central regulator of protein synthesis, whereas the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), involving E3 ubiquitin ligases such as muscle RING finger-1 (MuRF1) and Atrophy F-box (Atrogin-1), mediates protein degradation9,10. Studies in dairy cattle have demonstrated activation of UPS genes during NEB, but few have investigated how the magnitude of postpartum BCS loss modulates both anabolic and catabolic gene networks simultaneously4,11,12.

Recent omics-based studies have suggested that cows with high postpartum tissue mobilization exhibit transcriptomic signatures of oxidative stress, immune activation, and altered amino acid metabolism13–15. Despite these advances, there is limited knowledge on whether prolonged postpartum BCS loss is associated with sustained suppression of protein synthesis pathways alongside activation of proteolysis in skeletal muscle.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the effects of postpartum BCS loss on skeletal muscle gene expression related to protein synthesis (mTOR, EIF3K, 4EBP1) and protein degradation (UBA1, UBE2G1, UBE2G2, MuRF1, Atrogin-1) in dairy cows during early lactation. We hypothesized that greater postpartum BCS loss would be associated with upregulation of proteolytic genes and downregulation of protein synthesis genes, along with reduced dry matter intake (DMI) and milk yield.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics and welfare

All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sari Agricultural Sciences and Natural Resources University (SANRU), Sari, Iran. Procedures conformed to national ethical guidance issued by the Iranian Ministry of Science, Research and Technology (Project No. 1403-12-17). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations for the care and use of animals in research. The study was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Written informed consent was obtained from the owner/manager of the Mahdasht Dairy Farm (Sari, northern Iran) prior to the commencement of the study, authorizing the use of animals and farm facilities for research purposes.

Animals and dietary treatments

The study was conducted at Dashte-Naz Dairy Farm, the largest raw milk production unit in northern Iran, located in Sari, Mazandaran Province. Laboratory analyses were performed at the Central Laboratory of the University of Agricultural Sciences and Natural Resources of Sari. Cows were housed in free-stall barns, milked three times daily, and managed using Modiran herd software. They received a total mixed ration (TMR) formulated to meet NRC (2001) nutrient requirements for Holstein cows (651 kg BW, 35–45 kg milk/day), including forages and protein supplements. Based on calving date, reproductive history, and initial BCS, two hundred multiparous Holstein cows (third parity) producing more than 30 kg milk/day were selected. Cows with dystocia, mastitis, lameness, or uterine infections were excluded. All cows were enrolled within a 10-day calving window to minimize environmental variation among animals. Animals were assigned to four groups according to BCS loss between calving and 42 DIM: 0.00, − 0.25, − 0.50, and − 0.75 units.

Body condition scoring and body weight monitoring

Body condition score loss was calculated from calving to + 42 DIM because this period captures the most intense phase of negative energy balance and body tissue mobilization in high-yielding dairy cows. The majority of BCS loss typically occurs within the first 4–8 weeks postpartum, and changes during this period are strongly associated with metabolic health, production performance, and subsequent reproductive performance.

Milk recording and sampling

Milk yield was recorded weekly. Weekly samples were analyzed for fat, protein, and lactose content according to AOAC (1990) guidelines.

Blood collection

Blood samples (n = 200) were collected from the coccygeal vein at 0, + 7, + 14, + 21, and + 42 days postpartum at 0600 h, prior to feeding, into 10-mL heparinized vacuum tubes. Samples were kept on ice immediately after collection and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma. Aliquots of plasma were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

3-methylhistidine (HPLC–MS/MS)

Plasma 3-methylhistidine (3-MH) was quantified according to Houweling et al. 2012. Briefly, plasma proteins were precipitated with perchloric acid and centrifuged, and the supernatant was analyzed by HPLC–MS/MS using a C18 reversed-phase column (150 × 2.1 mm, 3 µm particle size). Separation was achieved with a water–acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% formic acid. Detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) of the protonated molecular ion at m/z 170 and its characteristic fragment ion at m/z 124.

Creatinine (HPLC–UV)

Plasma creatinine was determined using the rapid RP-HPLC method described by16. Proteins were precipitated with acetonitrile, centrifuged, and 20 µL of supernatant were injected for analysis. Separation was carried out on a C18 reversed-phase column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm particle size) under isocratic conditions with a sodium acetate buffer (pH = 5.0) containing 1% methanol at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. Detection was performed by UV absorbance at 235 nm, with a total runtime of 4 min.

Muscle tissue biopsies

Muscle tissue (n = 40) was collected at + 21 and + 42 DIM. Biopsies were collected from the longissimus dorsi muscle located between the 12th and the 13th rib. After clipping and disinfecting the area, local anesthesia (5 mL lidocaine, 2%) was administered subcutaneously and intramuscularly. Using a Bergström needle (5 mm diameter), approximately 200 mg of muscle tissue were obtained per cow at each time point at same place. Samples were immediately rinsed in sterile saline, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis of mRNA abundance

Total RNA was isolated from 20 mg of muscle using the Parstous Total RNA Extraction Kit (A101231) per the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and only samples with RIN > 8.0 were used. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA with the SuperScript™ IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher, 18091050) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 (Roche) in accordance with MIQE guidelines using SYBR Green master mix (YTA, YT2551) and gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table) targeting mRNA levels of UPS-related genes (UBE2G1, UBE2G2, UBA1, MuRF1, Atrogin-1) and mTOR pathway genes (mTOR, EIF3K, 4EBP1). Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Melt-curve analysis confirmed amplicon specificity. Relative transcript abundance was calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCT method, normalized to LRP10, GAPDH, and EMD (Emerin).

Statistical analysis

Relative abundance values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), with the Control group at 21 DIM serving as the calibrator. Fold changes were log2-transformed before statistical analysis to improve normality. All performance and mRNA abundance data were analyzed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A mixed model (PROC MIXED) was used, with BCS loss group, time (21 vs. 42 DIM), and their interaction as fixed effects, and cow within group as a random effect. Repeated measures were accounted for using an autoregressive covariance structure. Least squares means were compared using Tukey’s adjustment. Significance was declared at P < 0.05, and tendencies at 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 0.10.

Results

Dry matter intake, milk production and composition

DMI was reduced in cows with greater BCS loss (P < 0.05), with the lowest intake observed in the 0.75-unit group (Table 1). Milk yield was also lower in the 0.50- and 0.75-unit groups compared with control and 0.25-unit cows (P < 0.05). Losses of 0.50 or 0.75 units were associated with lesser milk fat percentage and yield (P < 0.05), with no association with protein or lactose content.

Plasma concentrations of creatinine and 3-methylhistidine

Blood metabolite analysis provided additional evidence of enhanced protein catabolism. Plasma concentrations of creatinine and 3-methylhistidine increased progressively with the degree of BCS loss (Table 2). Cows with 0.50 and 0.75 unit loss had significantly higher concentrations of both metabolites compared with the control and 0.25 groups (P < 0.05).

Expression of genes related to muscle metabolism

EIF3K and MTOR expression did not differ significantly among groups (P > 0.05). UBA1, UBE2G1, UBE2G2, MuRF1, and Atrogin-1 were significantly upregulated in cows losing ≥ 0.50 BCS units (P < 0.05), while 4EBP1 was downregulated. These results indicate enhanced proteolytic activity and suppressed protein synthesis in cows experiencing moderate to severe BCS loss (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Skeletal muscle metabolic regulation during postpartum BCS loss

The present study provides new insights into how postpartum body condition score (BCS) loss coincides with skeletal muscle metabolic regulation in transition dairy cows. We demonstrated that increasing BCS loss was associated with coordinated upregulation of genes within the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS; UBA1, UBE2G1, UBE2G2, MuRF1, Atrogin-1) and downregulation of genes involved in protein synthesis pathways (4EBP1). These molecular shifts became more pronounced at 42 DIM compared with 21 DIM, suggesting that prolonged NEB leads to sustained catabolic signaling in skeletal muscle. Importantly, these transcriptional changes were accompanied by measurable reductions (10%) in DMI and milk yield, emphasizing the biological and economic consequences of excessive BCS mobilization.

Skeletal muscle proteolysis and UPS activation

The UPS plays a central role in regulating skeletal muscle protein degradation. Our data are consistent with previous reports demonstrating increased expression of E3 ligases such as MuRF1 and Atrogin-1 during catabolic states9,10. In dairy cows, increased expression of these ligases has been observed in the context of NEB and postpartum metabolic stress4,11,17. The graded response observed here, whereby greater BCS loss was associated with progressively higher transcript abundance of UPS genes, supports the idea that proteolytic activity in muscle is proportional to the degree of energy deficit. This is consistent with recent findings that excessive mobilization of muscle protein occurs in cows with severe NEB and is closely linked to impaired immune function and delayed reproductive recovery18,19.

The non-linear expression patterns we observed suggest that proteolytic activation is not simply a threshold phenomenon but a progressive adaptation that intensifies as energy demands remain unmet. Similar non-linear responses have been reported in cattle undergoing feed restriction20,21 and in dairy cows experiencing accelerated lipid mobilization22. Together, these results indicate that the UPS system is a sensitive biomarker of muscle catabolism during early lactation and may serve as a useful target for nutritional or pharmacological interventions aimed at preserving lean tissue.

Suppression of protein synthesis signalling

In contrast to the activation of UPS genes, the downregulation of 4EBP1 observed in this study highlights the suppression of protein synthesis during postpartum BCS loss. The 4EBP1 pathway is a nutrient- and insulin-sensitive regulator of protein anabolism4. Reduced 4EBP1 activity has been documented in cows under NEB23 reflecting diminished amino acid availability and impaired insulin signaling. The sustained suppression at + 42 DIM compared with + 21 DIM suggests that prolonged catabolism exacerbates the decline in protein synthesis potential. Recent metabolomic and transcriptomic studies confirm that extended NEB alters amino acid flux and reduces anabolic signaling in skeletal muscle24,25. Our findings therefore reinforce the notion that severe BCS loss coincides with net protein degradation, supporting gluconeogenesis and milk production at the expense of muscle reserves.

Performance and production implications

Although tissue catabolism can provide substrate for milk component synthesis, extreme versions of tissue mobilization do not necessarily correspond with greater milk yield in early lactation dairy cattle. Our findings demonstrated reduced DMI and milk yield in cows with greater BCS loss. This may be a reflection of cows with poor DMI relying more dramatically on adipose and muscle tissue catabolism to meet nutrient demands, but nevertheless being unable to sustain high milk yield. Similar findings were reported by26, who showed that cows with excessive postpartum BCS loss produced less milk and were more prone to metabolic disorders. More recent large-scale studies confirm that postpartum BCS dynamics are stronger predictors of performance than absolute BCS at calving6,27.

Although it is not possible to evaluate in this study, it is possible that signaling associated with muscle catabolism will contribute to impaired appetite and milk yield in some cows. The reduction in DMI observed in the most catabolic cows may reflect impaired metabolic signaling, including altered insulin sensitivity and leptin regulation28,29. This reduced feed intake exacerbates NEB and perpetuates the cycle of muscle mobilization. Managing cows to minimize excessive BCS loss therefore represents not only a metabolic but also a behavioral and nutritional challenge.

Plasma concentrations of creatinine and 3-methylhistidine

Creatinine, a by-product of creatine phosphate degradation, reflects muscle mass and turnover, while 3-methylhistidine is a well-established biomarker of myofibrillar protein breakdown30,31. Their increase in high-loss cows indicates enhanced proteolytic activity in vivo, complementing the gene expression findings.

Similar increases in circulating 3-methylhistidine have been reported in cows experiencing severe NEB during early lactation and in ruminants subjected to nutrient restriction20,21,32, also emphasized that protein catabolism contributes significantly to the energy supply of transition cows, especially when adipose mobilization alone cannot meet metabolic demands3,5. Our data extend these findings by linking BCS loss magnitude with parallel molecular and biochemical indices of muscle degradation.

Emerging transcriptomic and metabolomic studies provide important context for interpreting our targeted gene expression results

Emerging omics-based research provides further context for interpreting our findings. Consistent with our observations, Moran et al.13 demonstrated that transcriptomic profiling of liver and skeletal muscle in Holstein cows revealed coordinated alterations in nutrient-partitioning pathways, accompanied by molecular signatures of oxidative stress and heightened inflammatory activity. Together, these findings highlighting the considerable metabolic pressure imposed during early lactation13. Similarly, studies have demonstrated that cows with greater BCS loss exhibited transcriptomic profiles consistent with systemic inflammation and muscle wasting33,34. Our results align with these observations by providing specific molecular evidence at the level of UPS and mTOR gene expression.

More recent high-throughput studies have emphasized the interplay between muscle and liver during NEB13,14. For example, Kuhla et al.3 reported that upregulation of muscle proteolysis genes coincided with activation of hepatic gluconeogenic pathways, reinforcing the role of skeletal muscle as a critical amino acid donor during energy deficit3. Thus, excessive BCS loss may represent a systemic reprogramming of nutrient flux that sacrifices lean tissue to support liver-driven glucose production for lactation.

Conclusion

Excessive postpartum body condition score loss is associated with activation of skeletal muscle ubiquitin–proteasome signaling, reduced translational regulation, and increased markers of myofibrillar protein degradation, indicating enhanced muscle catabolism during early lactation. These findings demonstrate a mechanistic association between substantial body reserve mobilization and impaired muscle protein metabolism, underscoring the importance of limiting excessive body condition loss to support metabolic stability in high-producing dairy cows. A limitation of this study is that classical indicators of adipose tissue mobilization (NEFA) and ketogenesis (BHBA) were not measured. Although these parameters are strongly associated with overall negative energy balance, the present work specifically targeted muscle protein turnover. Future studies integrating both adipose and muscle mobilization markers would provide a more holistic view of tissue partitioning during the transition period.
