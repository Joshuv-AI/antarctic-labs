Saturation and isomorphism of abstract harmonic spaces
Haoming Wang

Abstract
. This paper models the theory of abstract harmonic spaces in the syntax of the continuous first-order logic of Banach lattices. It addresses a topological question asking when a one-to-one harmonic map onto smooth manifolds M n M^{n} is a diffeomorphism. We give M n M^{n} ( n ≤ 2 n\leq 2 ) a characterization by U U -rank and elementary saturation for large cardinals. Polar sets are characterized by several equivalent conditions from the omitting type theorem. Consequently, harmonic measures on the ideal boundary in Martin representation are bijectively mapped to Keisler measures supported on non-principal types. Further problems concerning o-minimality and non-local potentials are finally discussed.

Introduction

Axiomatic potential theory, initiated by M. Brelot
8
, provides a magnificent method for studying harmonic functions on locally compact spaces. At its heart lie the structural interplays between harmonic sheaves, excessive functions, and the balayage (sweeping) operators. This theory culminates in the Martin representation

on the minimal boundary
\partial U

21
. The subsequent development of flux theory by Walsh
24

14
and Doob
13
. Nevertheless, these classical treatments of balayage and flux operators in potential theory remain tethered to heavy measure-theoretic machinery, most notably the capacity introduced by Choquet
10

Parallel to these analytic achievements, continuous model theory has emerged as a comprehensive language for metric structures, developed by Chang and Keisler
9
, Ben Yaacov, Usvyatsov, Pedersen
4
;
5

3

are replaced by the unit interval
[0,1]
. To date, applications of continuous model theory mainly focus on two principal directions. The first concerns the classification of groups and fields in analytic and algebraic settings, including topological groups
12
, difference fields
19

25

2
;
18
;
17

While continuous model theory has proved effective in non-commutative algebra and related areas, the study of abstract harmonic analysis itself, despite providing canonical examples of metric structures, has remained largely unexplored. This includes fundamental aspects of stability and dimension theory, such as omitting types
16
, quantifier elimination
6
, and large cardinals
7

The present paper establishes an interpretation of the theory of abstract harmonic spaces within the syntax of continuous first-order logic. Our main point is that harmonic axioms for sheaves, regular bases, and the Harnack principle can be encoded by a many-sorted metric structure in continuous logic. Martin compactification is interpreted as the closure of complete types, where harmonic measures correspond to Keisler measures on the ideal boundary formed by non-principal types. In this section, we present the axiomatic potential theory of superharmonic functions and establish its reformulation as a metric structure of Banach lattices in continuous first-order logic.

The base space is a locally compact (not compact yet), locally connected, Hausdorff space

3.2.
Green function and Martin boundary

In classical harmonic spaces, such as harmonic functions on Euclidean space or the unit disk in complex plane, there exists a symmetric Green function
G(x,y)
such that for any fixed point
y
in the base space
X
(locally compact and not compact, locally connected, and Hausdorff as before), the function

is a positive harmonic function on

K(x,y)=\frac{G(x,y)}{G(x_{0},y)}\quad(y\neq x_{0}),\quad K(x_{0},y)=1.

For each fixed

is also a positive harmonic function on
X\setminus\{y\}
.

For a fixed base point

induces a pseudo‑metric on

f_{y}(\cdot)=K(\cdot,y),{y\in X}
separates points of

f_{\alpha}(\cdot),\alpha\in A
separates points we mean that

\text{ for each $x\neq x^{\prime}$, there exists $\alpha\in A$ such that }f_{\alpha}(x)\neq f_{\alpha}(x^{\prime}).

For example, in a compact Hausdorff space, continuous functions separate points.

The Martin compactification

is the completion of

Minimal positive harmonic functions are exactly those of the form

with

In the continuous first‑order language

4.1.
Polar sets and omitting types

A set

such that

is bounded on
X\setminus A
. For compacta

{\rm Cap}(K)=\inf\left\{\int_{X}ud\mu:u\in S^{+}(X),u\geq 1\text{ on }K\right\},

where

{\rm Cap}_{*}

can be defined via compacta from inside and open sets from outside similarly.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the continuous first‑order language of abstract harmonic spaces and let

\kappa

For a definable set

Here

is the sort of bounded positive superharmonic functions (after the bounded transform

Let

(Characterizations of polar sets)
.

Let

(1)

(2)

\operatorname{Cap}(A)=0

(3)

The partial type

is omitted in the standard model
\mathfrak{M}_{0}

\kappa
-saturated elementary extension
\mathfrak{M}\succ\mathfrak{M}_{0}

(4)

(5)

\overline{A}\in\mathrm{NS}_{\kappa}
in

Proof.

(3)
\Leftrightarrow
(4) We claim the two types are logically equivalent, that is, they have the same realizations in any model. If

\Rightarrow
(3). Assume

4.2.
Fine topology and the
d
-metric

In classical potential theory, the fine topology on a harmonic space
X
is the coarsest topology making all positive superharmonic functions continuous.
It is strictly finer than the original topology and plays a fundamental role in the study of boundary behaviour, thinness, and the Dirichlet problem.

In the continuous first-order logic, the type space
S_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{M})
carries a natural metric
d_{\varphi}
induced by the Martin kernel

d_{\varphi}(p,q)=\sup_{x\in X_{0}}|K(x,p)-K(x,q)|,

where
X_{0}
is any countable dense subset of
X
. The restriction of
d_{\varphi}
to the embedded image of
X
(via
x\mapsto\operatorname{tp}_{\varphi}(x/\emptyset)
) defines a metric
d
on
X
, which we call the
d
-metric
. Explicitly,

d(x,y)=\sup_{z\in X_{0}}|K(z,x)-K(z,y)|.

It is well known that the
d
-metric topology on
X
coincides with the fine topology. The following theorem makes this precise in our logical setting.

Theorem 4.4

(Fine topology as the
d
-metric topology)
.

The fine topology on
X
is identical to the topology induced by the
d
-metric. Moreover, the embedding
\iota:X\to S_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{M})
,
\iota(x)=\operatorname{tp}_{\varphi}(x/\emptyset)
, is a homeomorphism onto its image when
X
is equipped with the fine topology and
S_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{M})
with the logic topology.

Proof.

By the minimal principle for harmonic functions, for any
x\in X
and any net
(x_{\alpha})
in
X
, one has
x_{\alpha}\to x
in the fine topology if and only if
h(x_{\alpha})\to h(x)
for every positive harmonic function
h
. Since the Martin kernels
\{K(\cdot,y):y\in X\}
form a separating family for the cone of positive harmonic functions (after taking linear combinations and limits), this convergence is equivalent to
K(z,x_{\alpha})\to K(z,x)
for every
z\in X_{0}
. By the definition of the
d
-metric, the latter condition means exactly
d(x_{\alpha},x)\to 0
. Thus the fine topology coincides with the
d
-metric topology.

For the second statement, recall that the logic topology on
S_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{M})
is also induced by the metric
d_{\varphi}
. Since
d_{\varphi}(\iota(x),\iota(y))=d(x,y)
, the map
\iota
is an isometric embedding. Hence it is a homeomorphism onto its image when the domain carries the
d
-metric topology, i.e. the fine topology.
∎

Corollary 4.5

(Fine topology and the type space)
.

The fine topology on
X
is completely regular, Hausdorff, and Baire. Moreover, a subset

is

at a point

if and only if
x
is not in the fine closure of
A\setminus\{x\}
.

5.
Examples

In this section, we introduce an analogue of rank for types in the harmonic setting. The guiding principle is that forking corresponds to a loss of harmonic capacity, measured by the definable sweeping operator. In this framework, low-rank types behave like rigid,
locally determined objects, whereas higher-rank types encode boundary pathologies and irregularity phenomena.

Let
T
be the Brelot theory and let
\mathfrak{M}\models T
be a
\kappa
-saturated model,
where
\kappa
is an uncountable regular cardinal. All parameter sets are assumed to have
cardinality strictly less than
\kappa
.

Definition 5.1

(Non-forking)
.

Let
A\subseteq B
be parameter sets and let
q\in S(B)
be a complete type extending

p\in S(A)
. The type

does not fork over
A
if for every positive superharmonic

definable over
B
with
\operatorname{Eval}(x,u)=0
in

, the sweeping of

onto the closure of
A
coincides with the sweeping onto the closure of
B
:

\operatorname{Bal}_{\operatorname{cl}(A)}(u)=\operatorname{Bal}_{\operatorname{cl}(B)}(u).

Otherwise,
q
forks over
A
.

Definition 5.2

(
U
-rank)
.

For a complete type
p\in S(A)
, the harmonic
U
-rank is defined by transfinite recursion.

(1)

U(p)\geq 0
.

(2)

U(p)\geq\alpha+1
if there exists a parameter set
B\supseteq A
and a forking
extension
q\in S(B)
of

with

.

(3)

U(p)=\alpha
if
\alpha
is the least ordinal such that

.

(4)

If
U(p)\geq\alpha
for every ordinal
\alpha
, then
U(p)=\infty
.

A type
p
has
U(p)=0
if and only if it is algebraic, that is, there exists a positive
superharmonic function
u
definable over the parameters of
p
such that the formula

\operatorname{Eval}(x,u)=0
belongs to

and the zero set of
\operatorname{Eval}(\cdot,u)

is polar.

Example 5.1

(Riemann surfaces)
.

Let
X
be a connected two-dimensional harmonic space, for instance a Riemann surface or a domain in the plane. For any point
x\in X
, let

p_{x}=\operatorname{tp}(x/\emptyset)

be the complete type of
x
over the empty set.

Proposition 5.1
.

U(p_{x})=1
,
x\in X
.

Proof.

In a two-dimensional harmonic space every positive harmonic function is locally the real part of a holomorphic function. Consequently, the unique continuation property holds globally. If two harmonic functions agree on an open neighbourhood of a point, then they
agree on the entire connected component. In model-theoretic terms this means that the algebraic closure of any parameter set equals its definable closure, and there are no non-trivial forking extensions of generic types. The only way to extend a type is by adding an independent generic point, which increases the rank by exactly one. Therefore,
U(p_{x})=1
.
∎

Lewy’s theorem (1936) states that a locally one-to-one harmonic map between plane domains has non-vanishing Jacobian and is therefore a diffeomorphism. In the present framework, this rigidity is consistent with the rank-one behaviour of point types. No non-trivial forking extension should encode a Jacobian degeneration.

Example 5.2

(Lebesgue thorn)
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
A\subseteq X
be a Lebesgue thorn, that is, a thin closed set obtained by rotation around an axis

The origin

is a limit point of the set which is also an irregular boundary point for the Dirichlet problem. Let

be the divergence type associated with

, as defined in Section 4.

Proposition 5.2
.

U(p_{A}^{\mathrm{Div}})\geq 2
.

Proof.

By Wiener’s criteria, the capacity of the complement of

near

is sufficiently small to force irregularity

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n}\,\operatorname{Cap}\big(B(x_{0},2^{-n})\setminus A\big)<\infty.

In the logical framework, this rapid decay produces a non-trivial forking chain for the
type
p_{A}^{\mathrm{Div}}
. Concretely, there exists a parameter set

and a positive superharmonic function

definable over
B

\operatorname{Eval}(x,u)=0
belongs to

differs from the sweeping onto the empty set. Moreover, the

∎

The type of the vertex

6.
Further Remarks

The present axiom scheme is not expected to be complete a priori. Rather, it should be viewed as a class of harmonic structures whose complete theories are obtained by fixing a concrete model or a canonical representation. A more refined first-order logic analysis may reveal whether the resulting theory is simple, or perhaps merely stable on the harmonic fragment, with balayage and Harnack control suggesting a weak form of independence. In low-dimensional settings, the rigidity of harmonic extension also suggests an analogy with o-minimal tameness. Definable sets may exhibit a strong geometric regularity, although a literal o-minimal interpretation is unlikely in full generality.

Moreover, the two isomorphism theorems above suggest a possible quantifier elimination phenomenon after a suitable expansion of the language, which remains to be investigated. It would also be natural to compare the present framework with Bauer spaces, where the existence of a regular basis provides a strong boundary regularity condition, and with non-local potential theory, in which balayage spaces connect harmonic analysis with Markov processes and jump phenomena. These perspectives point toward a broader classification of harmonic spaces in terms of their model complexity and boundary behaviour.
