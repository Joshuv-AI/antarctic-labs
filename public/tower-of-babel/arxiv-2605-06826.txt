How Does Attention Help? Insights from Random Matrices on Signal Recovery from Sequence Models
Mohamed El Amine Seddik

Abstract
We study the spectral properties of sample covariance matrices constructed from pooled sequence representations, where token embeddings are drawn from a fixed two-class Gaussian mixture table and pooled via (fixed) attention weights. Working in the high-dimensional regime d , V , N → ∞ d,V,N\to\infty with d / V → δ d/V\to\delta and d / N → γ d/N\to\gamma , we derive exact characterizations of the limiting eigenvalue distribution, outlier eigenvalues, and eigenvector alignment with the hidden signal. The bulk spectrum follows a non-Marchenko–Pastur law given by the free multiplicative convolution κ ⁡ ( MP δ ⊠ MP γ ) \kappa(\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}\boxtimes\mathrm{MP}_{\gamma}) , reflecting the finite vocabulary structure. Signal recovery undergoes two successive BBP-type phase transitions characterized by the scalars: δ , γ , α = 𝒘 ⊤ ​ 𝑹 ​ 𝒘 \delta,\gamma,\alpha={\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}} and κ = ‖ 𝒘 ‖ 2 \kappa=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2} , where 𝒘 {\bm{w}} denotes the attention pooling weights and 𝑹 {\bm{R}} the positional correlation matrix. An aftermath of our analysis demonstrates that the optimal attention weights maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio α / κ \alpha/\kappa are given by the (normalized) top eigenvector of 𝑹 {\bm{R}} , and we show (as a particular case of our analysis) that parameter-free causal self-attention with τ / d \tau/d score scaling yields deterministic harmonic weights that improve signal recovery over mean pooling whenever early tokens carry more signal. Extensive simulations confirm sharp agreement between theory and finite-dimensional experiments.



1
Introduction

The attention mechanism
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
is the central building block of modern transformer architectures that have achieved remarkable success across language modeling
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
, vision
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
, and many current AI applications. Despite this empirical success, a precise theoretical understanding of
why
and
how
attention helps remains limited. Most existing theoretical analyses focus on expressivity
(
Yun et al. 2019
)
, optimization landscapes
(
Sahiner et al. 2022
)
, or rank collapse phenomena
(
Dong et al. 2021
)
. A complementary and largely unexplored direction is to study attention through the lens of
statistical signal recovery
: given a structured data model, how does the choice of attention weights affect the ability to recover a latent signal from high-dimensional observations?

Random matrix theory (RMT) provides a natural framework for this question. The well known BBP phase transition within the random matrix community
(
Baik et al. 2005
;
Baik & Silverstein 2006
)
characterizes when a hidden signal becomes detectable from the spectrum of a sample covariance matrix, and the associated eigenvector overlap formulas
(
Paul 2007
;
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
)
quantify the alignment between the estimated and true signal directions. These tools have been successfully applied to high-dimensional statistics
(
Johnstone 2001
;
Johnstone & Lu 2009
)
, wireless communications
(
Couillet & Debbah 2011
)
, and more recently to understanding the high-dimensional behaviour of machine learning algorithms and deep learning representations
(
Pennington & Worah 2017
;
Seddik et al. 2020
;
Loureiro et al. 2021
;
Couillet & Liao 2022
)
.

In this work, we propose a tractable statistical model for sequence data with a
fixed
two-class Gaussian mixture embedding table and positional correlations, and we study how attention-based pooling affects signal recovery. Our main contributions are:

(1)
the exact limiting spectral distribution of the sample covariance of the pooled representations in the high-dimensional regime, characterized by a non-Marchenko–Pastur law (Theorem
1
);

(2)
two successive BBP-type phase transitions for signal detectability, at the population and sample levels, with explicit outlier eigenvalue and eigenvector overlap formulas (Theorems
2
–
4
);

(3)
a general optimality result showing that the attention weights maximizing signal recovery are the (normalized) top eigenvector of
{\bm{R}}
(Theorem
5
);

(4)
as a particular case of our general framework, a concrete analysis of parameter-free causal self-attention, yielding deterministic harmonic weights that provably improve
\alpha/\kappa
over mean pooling when the signal is carried on early sequence positions (Section
5
); and

(5)
extensive simulations validating all theoretical predictions (Section
6
).

Related work.

The Baik-Ben Arous-Péché (BBP) phase transition was established by
Baik et al. 2005
for complex spiked Wishart matrices and extended by
Baik & Silverstein 2006
. Eigenvector overlap formulas were derived by
Paul 2007
and generalized by
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
;
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2012
. The Marchenko–Pastur law
(
Marčenko & Pastur 1967
)
and free probability
(
Nica & Speicher 2006
;
Mingo & Speicher 2017
)
underpin our bulk analysis, with comprehensive treatments in
Bai et al. 2010
;
Couillet & Liao 2022
. On the ML side,
Pennington & Worah 2017
initiated RMT for deep learning. Theoretical analyses of attention span expressivity
(
Yun et al. 2019
)
, rank collapse
(
Dong et al. 2021
)
, inductive biases
(
Edelman et al. 2022
)
, and kernel limits
(
Hron et al. 2020
)
. Closer to our focus on the statistical role of softmax attention,
Duranthon et al. 2025
prove a statistical advantage of softmax attention over linear alternatives in a single-location regression setting, while
Boncoraglio et al. 2026
study the generalization, weights spectra and scaling laws of a single-head attention layer in high dimensions using tools closely related to ours. A complementary line of work
(
Jha & Reagen 2025
)
analyzes the interaction between trained attention and statistical structure in a high-dimensional spiked-data setup. The latent-variable perspective on embeddings by
Arora et al. 2016
further motivates our spiked embedding model. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to provide an exact RMT analysis of how attention weights modulate signal recovery under the considered setup of a
fixed
embedding table.

Sequence Statistical Model

2.1
Two-class embedding table

Let
{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a deterministic vector with direction
{\bm{u}}:={\bm{\mu}}/\|{\bm{\mu}}\|
. We consider a two-class
fixed
embedding table
{\bm{E}}=[{\bm{e}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{e}}_{V}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times V}
with

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{v}=s_{v}{\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{z}}_{v},\quad v\in[V],

(1)

where
(s_{v})_{v=1}^{V}\in\{\pm 1\}^{V}
are
balanced
signs (
V/2
entries
+1
and
V/2
entries
-1

are i.i.d. noise vectors, and
{\bm{Z}}=[{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{V}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times V}
. The embedding table is a two-class Gaussian mixture of means
\pm{\bm{\mu}}
with
\|{\bm{\mu}}\|
controlling class separability.

Remark 1

(Relaxing Gaussianity)
.

The Gaussian assumption on the noise vectors
{\bm{z}}_{v}
is made for convenience and can be substantially relaxed. Standard universality results in random matrix theory
(
Bai et al. 2010
;
Couillet & Liao 2022
)
imply that the bulk spectrum of
{\bm{S}}
and the location of its outlier eigenvalue are insensitive to the precise distribution of the
{\bm{z}}_{v}
’s, provided each coordinate has zero mean, unit variance, and a bounded fourth-order moment. All our main results therefore extend straightforwardly to this broader class of embedding-noise distributions.

\displaystyle{\bm{c}}_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}X_{t}^{(n)},\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}=1,\quad{\bm{w}}:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{T})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}.

2.3
Practical justification of the statistical model

Figure
1
summarizes the three components of the statistical data model: the two-class embedding table, the correlated positional signs that select tokens within their class, and the pooled representation
{\bm{c}}_{n}
yielding the sample covariance
{\bm{S}}
under analysis. While our model is deliberately considered for analytical tractability, each component has a practical motivation.
(i) Two-class embeddings:
Pre-trained word embeddings exhibit a low-rank-plus-noise structure
(
Arora et al. 2016
)
(see Appendix
I
for an empirical illustration on GPT-2), and the two-class assumption is the minimal nontrivial case for studying signal recovery.
(ii) Fixed embedding table:
In many practical settings the embedding table is pre-trained and frozen during downstream tasks, making the “fixed table” regime realistic. The key consequence, that the table randomness introduces a second source of spectral noise captured by
\delta=d/V
, disappears only in the impractical limit
V\gg d
.
(iii) Positional correlations:
The matrix
{\bm{R}}
captures the empirical observation that semantic content is unevenly distributed across positions, which aligns with typical applications (e.g. language).
(iv) Sequence-independent weights:
While full self-attention produces data-dependent weights, the
\tau/d
scaling regime we analyze (Section
5
) shows that attention weights
concentrate
to deterministic values as
d\to\infty
, justifying the sequence-independent assumption as a first-order approximation.

With the statistical model fixed, we now turn to its spectral analysis: the next section derives the limiting bulk distribution of
{\bm{S}}
, characterizes the two BBP-type phase transitions that govern signal recovery, and expresses the alignment
(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{u}})^{2}
in closed form in terms of the scalars
(\alpha,\kappa,\delta,\gamma)
.

3
Main Results

3.1
Pooled covariance decomposition

The columns of
{\bm{C}}
are i.i.d. (conditionally on
{\bm{Z}}
) with covariance

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{c}}{\bm{c}}^{\top}\mid{\bm{Z}}\right]=\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}+\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{Z}}},\quad{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{Z}}}:=\frac{1}{V}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{\top},

(3)

where the two key scalars are

\displaystyle\alpha({\bm{w}}):={\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa({\bm{w}}):=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}.

(4)

The scalar
\alpha
captures the effective signal strength after pooling and
\kappa
controls the noise level. The effective signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is
\rho:=\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}/\kappa
, and optimal signal recovery is achieved by maximizing
\alpha/\kappa
as we will demonstrate subsequently.

3.2
Limiting spectrum of
\bf S

Theorem 1

(Bulk density)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the limiting spectral density (LSD) of
{\bm{S}}
is the free multiplicative convolution
\kappa\left(\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}\boxtimes\mathrm{MP}_{\gamma}\right)
whose Stieltjes transform

satisfies

\delta\gamma\kappa\,z^{2}m(z)^{3}-\kappa z(\delta+\gamma-2\delta\gamma)\,m(z)^{2}-\big(z+\kappa(\delta-1)(1-\gamma)\big)\,m(z)-1=0.

(5)

The eigenvalue density is
\rho(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\Im\,m(x+i\eta)
. The companion transform
\underline{m}(z):=-\frac{1-\gamma}{z}+\gamma m(z)
corresponds to the Gram matrix
{\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}/N

The LSD of
{\bm{S}}
differs from the standard Marchenko–Pastur law
(
Marčenko & Pastur 1967
)
unless
\delta=0
(i.e., infinite vocabulary relative to the dimension of embeddings). The finite vocabulary introduces a multiplicative noise structure:
{\bm{S}}
is the product of two independent Wishart-type components (the embedding table randomness, governed by
\delta
, and the sampling randomness, governed by
\gamma
), whose interplay is captured by the free multiplicative convolution
(
Nica & Speicher 2006
)
. For attention, the key insight is that the
shape
of the bulk depends only on
(\delta,\gamma)
, which are fixed by the problem dimensions, while the
scale
is
\kappa({\bm{w}})=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}
. Thus attention affects the bulk only through
\kappa
: non-uniform weights inflate
\kappa
above the mean-pooling value
1/T
, widening the bulk.

The right edge

scales linearly with

. Since the BBP phase transition depends on whether the spike exceeds

3.3
Spike behavior of the population covariance

Theorem 2

(Population BBP transition)
.

Define
\rho:=\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}/\kappa
. The top eigenvalue of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
exhibits a BBP phase transition: if
\rho\leq\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
, no outlier exists and the top eigenvalue sticks to
\beta_{+}:=\kappa(1+\sqrt{\delta})^{2}
; if
\rho>\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
, an outlier appears at

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{out}}:=\frac{\kappa\rho(\rho-\delta+1)}{\rho-\delta}.

(6)

The top eigenvector
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}
of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
has overlap
|\hat{{\bm{u}}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{u}}|^{2}\to 1-\delta/(\rho-\delta)^{2}
for
\rho>\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
, and
0
otherwise.

Proof.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.
∎

This result reveals a fundamental interplay between vocabulary size (through
\delta
) and signal recovery. When
\delta>0
, the finite vocabulary introduces a
strictly positive
detection threshold
\rho>\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
: even with infinite data (
\gamma\to 0
), one cannot recover the signal if
\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}/\kappa
is too small. For attention, the critical observation is that
increasing
\alpha/\kappa
lowers the effective threshold
. At the critical point
\rho=\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
, the overlap jumps from
0
to a positive value as a sharp phase transition.

3.5
Phase transition thresholds

Recalling
\rho=\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}/\kappa
, the population BBP condition translates to
\|{\bm{\mu}}\|>\mu_{\mathrm{pop}}({\bm{w}}):=\sqrt{\kappa(\delta+\sqrt{\delta})/\alpha}
. The sample threshold
\mu_{\mathrm{samp}}({\bm{w}})
is obtained by solving
\beta_{\mathrm{out}}(\rho)=\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}
, yielding a quadratic in
\rho
(details in Appendix
E
). Both thresholds decrease when
\alpha/\kappa
increases, confirming that maximizing the effective SNR through
{\bm{w}}
is the key to improving signal detectability and recovery.

The detectability thresholds of this section depend on
{\bm{w}}
exclusively through the single scalar
\alpha({\bm{w}})/\kappa({\bm{w}})
. For optimality, we aim to characterize
{\bm{w}}
that maximizes this ratio which translates to a simple spectral optimization problem that we solve in closed form in Section
4
below.

4
Optimal Attention Weights

The following result characterizes the attention weights
{\bm{w}}
that maximize signal recovery.

Theorem 5

(Optimal attention weights)
.

Let
{\bm{R}}
be the (symmetric) positional correlation matrix, with largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
and corresponding eigenspace
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}
. The optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}}\frac{\alpha({\bm{w}})}{\kappa({\bm{w}})}=\frac{{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}}}{{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}}\quad\text{subject to}\quad{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1

(9)

satisfies:
(a)
the supremum equals
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
;
(b)
a maximizer exists if and only if
{\bm{1}}
is not orthogonal to
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}
, in which case

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{{\bm{v}}_{\mathrm{max}}}{{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{\mathrm{max}}},\quad\text{where}\quad{\bm{v}}_{\mathrm{max}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}\text{ with }{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{\mathrm{max}}\neq 0,

(10)

and
\alpha({\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}})/\kappa({\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}})=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
.

Proof.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
F
.
∎

This result reveals that the best attention weights for signal recovery are determined entirely by the spectral structure of the positional correlation matrix
{\bm{R}}
. The constraint
\sum_{t}w_{t}=1
does not reduce the achievable SNR because the Rayleigh quotient
\alpha/\kappa
is scale-invariant. Thus the optimal value
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
is the
fundamental limit
on the effective SNR for any pooling strategy. When
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
is simple,
{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is unique (up to sign normalization) and concentrates weight on the positions that contribute most to the dominant correlation mode.

In particular, for a
prefix model
with
L
correlated positions and
T-L
independent ones,
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})=L
(the top eigenvalue of the block
{\bm{1}}_{L}{\bm{1}}_{L}^{\top}+{\bm{I}}_{T-L}
), achieved when
{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}}
places all weight on the first
L
positions. In contrast, mean pooling achieves only
\alpha_{\mathrm{id}}/\kappa_{\mathrm{id}}=L^{2}/T+(T-L)/T
, which can be much smaller.

The framework of Section

\tau/d

Under Assumption

Proposition 2

(Harmonic weights)
.

With harmonic numbers
\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(0)}=\frac{1+\mathcal{H}_{T-1}}{T},\qquad w_{s}^{(0)}=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{T-1}-\mathcal{H}_{s-1}}{T},\quad s=2,\ldots,T.

Specifically, these weights are monotonically decreasing: earlier positions receive more weight, with
w_{1}\propto 1+\ln(T-1)

{\bm{R}}

In particular, for the prefix model with
{\bm{R}}=[{\bm{1}}_{L}{\bm{1}}_{L}^{\top},{\bm{0}};{\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{T-L}]

7
Conclusion

We have presented an exact random matrix theory framework for understanding how attention-based pooling affects signal recovery in high-dimensional sequence models. The effective signal strength
\alpha({\bm{w}})={\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}}
and noise scale
\kappa({\bm{w}})=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}
jointly determine two BBP-type phase transitions and the resulting eigenvector alignment. We proved that the optimal weights are the normalized top eigenvector of the correlation matrix
{\bm{R}}
yielding provably optimal signal recovery.

Extensions and open problems.

Beyond the setting analyzed here, our framework naturally opens several directions that we believe the present results pave the way for.
(i) Richer embedding models:
Our two-class Gaussian mixture can be extended to
K
-class mixtures. The RMT tools extend to multi-spike models
(
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
)
, though the algebra becomes more involved.
(ii) Sample-dependent correlations:
In practice,
{\bm{R}}
varies across sequences. Extending our framework to
{\bm{R}}^{(n)}
that depends on the sample
n
would require analyzing a mixture of covariance structures, connecting to the theory of mixtures of spiked models.
(iii) Data-dependent attention:
Full self-attention produces weights
{\bm{w}}^{(n)}
that depend on the input sequence
{\bm{X}}^{(n)}
. Analyzing this requires understanding the joint distribution of
(w^{(n)},{\bm{X}}^{(n)})
, which is considerably more challenging but could be approached via leave-one-out or cavity methods from statistical physics
(
Mézard & Parisi 2003
)
.
(iv) Multi-head and multi-layer architectures:
Extending to multiple heads (each with different effective
\alpha/\kappa
) and multiple layers (where the output of one layer feeds into the next) would connect to the theory of products of random matrices and iterated free convolutions.
(v) Non-isotropic noise:
Replacing
{\bm{z}}_{v}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{d})
with
{\bm{z}}_{v}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0})
for a general covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
, which aligns more with our experiments in Appendix
I
, would modify the bulk law and phase transitions, requiring generalized RMT results
(
Silverstein & Bai 1995
)
.
(vi) Downstream classification theory:
As discussed in Section
6
, the test-accuracy curves of Figure
6
admit an explicit high-dimensional characterization via random matrix theory, building on the present spectral analysis in the spirit of
(
Firdoussi & Seddik 2024
;
Demir & Dogan 2025
)
. In each of these directions, the spectral identities derived in this paper can be further generalized and would bring new insights into the understanding of attention-based models.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Bulk Cubic Equation (Theorem
1
and Proposition
1
)

We derive equation
5
using the
S
-transform formalism from free probability
(
Nica & Speicher 2006
)
, and then extract the right bulk edge
\lambda_{+}
in closed form.

Step 0: Pooled representation and bulk reduction.

Recall that each pooled row of
{\bm{C}}
can be written as

{\bm{c}}_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\,X_{t}^{(n)}\;=\;\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\xi_{n,t}\Big){\bm{\mu}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}\,{\bm{z}}_{x_{n,t}}.

Using independence of
(\xi_{n})
and
({\bm{z}}_{v})
and the correlation structure
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n}\xi_{n}^{\top}]={\bm{R}}
, the (conditional on
{\bm{Z}}
) population covariance is

{\bm{\Sigma}}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}[{\bm{c}}_{n}{\bm{c}}_{n}^{\top}\mid{\bm{Z}}]\;=\;\kappa({\bm{w}})\,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}\;+\;\alpha({\bm{w}})\,\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}\,{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top},

(12)

with
\kappa({\bm{w}})=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}
,
\alpha({\bm{w}})={\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}}
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}=(1/V){\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}
.
Because
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is a rank-one perturbation of
\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}
, the limiting ESD of
{\bm{S}}=(1/N){\bm{C}}{\bm{C}}^{\top}
coincides with that of the null sample covariance

{\bm{S}}_{0}\;:=\;\tfrac{1}{N}(\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}{\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}(\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}})^{1/2},

where
{\bm{G}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times N}
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries.

Step 1: Identifying the free multiplicative convolution.

As
d,V\to\infty
with
d/V\to\delta
, the ESD of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}
converges almost surely to the Marchenko–Pastur law
\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}
, hence the ESD of
\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}
converges to
\kappa\,\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}
. Likewise the ESD of
(1/N){\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}
converges to
\mathrm{MP}_{\gamma}
. By standard free-probability arguments
(
Nica & Speicher 2006
)
, the two ensembles
(\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}})
and
(1/N){\bm{G}}{\bm{G}}^{\top}
are asymptotically free and the LSD of
{\bm{S}}_{0}
is the free multiplicative convolution

\mu_{\mathrm{bulk}}\;=\;\kappa\,\big(\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}\boxtimes\mathrm{MP}_{\gamma}\big).

(13)

In particular, the bulk is
not
an MP law unless
\delta=0
(infinite vocabulary).

Step 2:
S
-transform computation.

Recall that the
S
-transform of
\mathrm{MP}_{a}
is
S_{\mathrm{MP}_{a}}(z)=1/(1+az)
and that scaling a distribution by a factor
c>0
multiplies the
S
-transform by
1/c
. Since
S
-transforms are multiplicative under
\boxtimes
,

S_{F}(z)\;=\;\frac{1}{\kappa\,(1+\delta z)(1+\gamma z)},\qquad F:=\mu_{\mathrm{bulk}}.

(14)

Step 3: From
S
-transform to Stieltjes transform.

Let
m(z)
be the Stieltjes transform of
F
and define the auxiliary function

\psi(z)\;:=\;zm(z)-1.

The fundamental identity linking
S_{F}
and
m
is
S_{F}(\psi(z))\,\psi(z)=(1+\psi(z))/z
, equivalently

S_{F}(\psi(z))\;=\;\frac{1+\psi(z)}{\psi(z)\cdot z}\;=\;\frac{m(z)}{zm(z)-1}.

(15)

Substituting equation
14
with
\psi=zm-1
into equation
15
yields

\frac{1}{\kappa\,(1+\delta(zm-1))(1+\gamma(zm-1))}\;=\;\frac{m}{zm-1}.

Cross-multiplying gives
zm-1=\kappa m(1+\delta(zm-1))(1+\gamma(zm-1))
. Expanding,

\displaystyle\kappa m\Big[1+(\delta+\gamma)(zm-1)+\delta\gamma(zm-1)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\;zm-1.

Collecting powers of
m
and rearranging yields the cubic equation equation
5
:

\delta\gamma\kappa\,z^{2}m^{3}\;-\;\kappa z(\delta+\gamma-2\delta\gamma)\,m^{2}\;-\;\big(z+\kappa(\delta-1)(1-\gamma)\big)\,m\;-\;1\;=\;0.

(16)

The limiting density is recovered by
\rho(x)=\pi^{-1}\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\Im\,m(x+i\eta)
.

Step 4: Bulk edges and the cubic
R(z)
.

Let
P(m;z)
denote the left-hand side of equation
16
, viewed as a polynomial of degree
3
in
m
with coefficients depending on
z
. The edges of the support of
\mu_{\mathrm{bulk}}
are precisely the real
z
’s where the analytic Stieltjes branch
m(z)
develops a square-root singularity, i.e. where
P(\,\cdot\,;z)
has a
double root
in
m
:

P(m;z)=0,\qquad\partial_{m}P(m;z)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{Disc}_{m}\!\big(P(\cdot;z)\big)=0.

A direct computation (e.g. via the resultant of
P
and
\partial_{m}P
) shows that the discriminant factorizes as

\mathrm{Disc}_{m}\big(P(\cdot;z)\big)\;=\;\kappa\,z^{2}\,R(z),

(17)

where
R(z)
is the following cubic in
z
:

\displaystyle R(z)\;=\;

\displaystyle 4\delta\gamma\,z^{3}

\displaystyle+\kappa\,\Big(\delta^{2}\gamma^{2}-10\delta^{2}\gamma+\delta^{2}-10\delta\gamma^{2}-10\delta\gamma+\gamma^{2}\Big)\,z^{2}

\displaystyle-2\kappa^{2}\,\Big(\delta^{3}\gamma^{2}-4\delta^{3}\gamma+\delta^{3}+\delta^{2}\gamma^{3}+2\delta^{2}\gamma^{2}+2\delta^{2}\gamma+\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-4\delta\gamma^{3}+2\delta\gamma^{2}-4\delta\gamma+\gamma^{3}+\gamma^{2}\Big)\,z

\displaystyle+\kappa^{3}(\delta-1)^{2}(\gamma-1)^{2}(\delta-\gamma)^{2}.

(18)

The factor
\kappa z^{2}
in equation
17
corresponds to the hard edge at
z=0
, while the genuine (right) bulk edge is the largest real root of
R
.

Step 5: Scaling out
\kappa
and closed-form edge.

Because the measure is rescaled by
\kappa
, the edges scale linearly in
\kappa
. Writing
z=\kappa x
, equation
18
becomes
R(z)=\kappa^{3}r(x)
with

\displaystyle r(x)\;=\;

\displaystyle 4\delta\gamma\,x^{3}

\displaystyle+\Big(\delta^{2}\gamma^{2}-10\delta^{2}\gamma+\delta^{2}-10\delta\gamma^{2}-10\delta\gamma+\gamma^{2}\Big)\,x^{2}

\displaystyle-2\Big(\delta^{3}\gamma^{2}-4\delta^{3}\gamma+\delta^{3}+\delta^{2}\gamma^{3}+2\delta^{2}\gamma^{2}+2\delta^{2}\gamma+\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-4\delta\gamma^{3}+2\delta\gamma^{2}-4\delta\gamma+\gamma^{3}+\gamma^{2}\Big)\,x

\displaystyle+(\delta-1)^{2}(\gamma-1)^{2}(\delta-\gamma)^{2}.

Introducing the symmetric combinations
q:=\delta\gamma
and
r_{0}:=\delta+\gamma+\delta\gamma
,
r(x)
takes the form
Ax^{3}+Bx^{2}+Cx+D
with
A=4q
and
B=r_{0}^{2}-12qr_{0}+12q^{2}-12q
. Applying the standard trigonometric cubic formula with invariants

\Delta_{0}\;=\;B^{2}-3AC\;=\;r_{0}\big(r_{0}^{\,3}+216q^{2}\big),\qquad\Delta_{1}\;=\;2B^{3}-9ABC+27A^{2}D\;=\;2\big(r_{0}^{\,6}-540q^{2}r_{0}^{\,3}-5832q^{4}\big),

and letting
\phi=\arccos\!\big(\Delta_{1}/(2\Delta_{0}^{3/2})\big)
, the three real roots are

x_{k}\;=\;-\frac{1}{3A}\bigg(B+2\sqrt{\Delta_{0}}\,\cos\!\Big(\tfrac{\phi+2\pi k}{3}\Big)\bigg),\qquad k=0,1,2.

The right bulk edge of
\mu_{\mathrm{bulk}}
is

\lambda_{+}\;=\;\kappa\,x_{+},\qquad x_{+}:=\max\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\},

which is exactly the expression stated in Proposition
1
.

Appendix B
Population Outlier and Overlap (Theorem
2
)

Throughout this section let
H
denote the LSD of
\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}
, namely
H=\kappa\,\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}
, and
m_{H}
its Stieltjes transform. The population covariance equation
12
is the rank-one additive deformation

{\bm{\Sigma}}\;=\;\kappa{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Z}}\;+\;\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}\,{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}.

Outlier equation.

By
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
, if an outlier eigenvalue
\beta
of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
lies outside the support of
H
, it must satisfy

1\;+\;\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}\,m_{H}(\beta)\;=\;0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m_{H}(\beta)\;=\;-\frac{1}{\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}}.

(19)

The rescaling identity
m_{H}(\beta)=\kappa^{-1}m_{\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}}(\beta/\kappa)
turns this into
m_{\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}}(\beta/\kappa)=-\kappa/(\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2})=-1/\rho
, where
\rho:=\alpha\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}/\kappa
is the population spike ratio.

Solving the MP quadratic.

The Stieltjes transform of
\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}
satisfies

\delta\,z\,m^{2}\;+\;(z+\delta-1)\,m\;+\;1\;=\;0.

(20)

Setting
m=-1/\rho
and multiplying through by
\rho^{2}
:

\delta z\;-\;(z+\delta-1)\rho\;+\;\rho^{2}\;=\;0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z\;=\;\frac{\rho(\rho-\delta+1)}{\rho-\delta}.

Multiplying by
\kappa
yields the population outlier location

\beta_{\mathrm{out}}\;=\;\kappa\,\frac{\rho(\rho-\delta+1)}{\rho-\delta}.

(21)

BBP condition.

For equation
21
to be an actual outlier of
H=\kappa\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}
, we need
\beta_{\mathrm{out}}/\kappa>(1+\sqrt{\delta})^{2}
, i.e.

\frac{\rho(\rho-\delta+1)}{\rho-\delta}\;>\;(1+\sqrt{\delta})^{2}.

Clearing the denominator (for
\rho>\delta
) and expanding gives
\rho^{2}-(\delta+2\sqrt{\delta})\rho-(\sqrt{\delta})(\delta+\sqrt{\delta})>0
, which factors as
(\rho-(\delta+\sqrt{\delta}))(\rho+\sqrt{\delta})>0
. Since
\rho>0
the BBP condition reduces to

\rho\;>\;\delta+\sqrt{\delta}.

Eigenvector overlap.

The general formula of
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
reads

|\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{u}}|^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{4}\,m_{H}^{\prime}(\beta_{\mathrm{out}})}.

(22)

We compute
m_{H}^{\prime}
by differentiating equation
20
implicitly. Writing
G(z,m):=\delta zm^{2}+(z+\delta-1)m+1=0
,

m^{\prime}(z)\;=\;-\frac{\partial_{z}G}{\partial_{m}G}\;=\;-\frac{\delta m^{2}+m}{2\delta zm+(z+\delta-1)}.

At
(z_{\star},m_{\star})=(\beta_{\mathrm{out}}/\kappa,-1/\rho)
, the numerator is
\delta/\rho^{2}-1/\rho=(\delta-\rho)/\rho^{2}
, and using equation
20
at this point to express the denominator: from
G(z_{\star},m_{\star})=0
we get
\delta z_{\star}/\rho^{2}-(z_{\star}+\delta-1)/\rho+1=0
, which after algebraic manipulation gives
2\delta z_{\star}m_{\star}+(z_{\star}+\delta-1)=-\delta z_{\star}/\rho-1/m_{\star}=\rho-\delta z_{\star}/\rho
. Using
z_{\star}=\rho(\rho-\delta+1)/(\rho-\delta)
, a direct computation yields

m_{\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}}^{\prime}(z_{\star})\;=\;\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\cdot\frac{(\rho-\delta)^{2}}{(\rho-\delta)^{2}-\delta}.

Translating back through
m_{H}^{\prime}(\beta_{\mathrm{out}})=\kappa^{-2}m_{\mathrm{MP}_{\delta}}^{\prime}(\beta_{\mathrm{out}}/\kappa)
and substituting in equation
22
with
\alpha^{2}\|{\bm{\mu}}\|^{4}=\kappa^{2}\rho^{2}
:

|\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{u}}|^{2}\;=\;1-\frac{\delta}{(\rho-\delta)^{2}}.

(23)

At the threshold
\rho=\delta+\sqrt{\delta}
,
(\rho-\delta)^{2}=\delta
and equation
23
equals zero, confirming the phase transition.

Appendix C
Sample Outlier Quadratic (Theorem
3
)

Assume we are in the supercritical regime, so that
{\bm{\Sigma}}
has an outlier eigenvalue
\beta:=\beta_{\mathrm{out}}
separated from the bulk. Viewing
{\bm{\Sigma}}
as a rank-one deformation of its bulk component, the standard theory of spiked sample covariance matrices (
Silverstein & Bai 1995
;
Bai et al. 2010
;
Benaych-Georges & Nadakuditi 2011
) states that an outlier
\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}
of
{\bm{S}}=(1/N){\bm{C}}{\bm{C}}^{\top}
is characterized by the
companion outlier equation

\underline{m}(\lambda_{\mathrm{out}})\;=\;-\frac{1}{\beta},

(24)

where
\underline{m}(z)=-(1-\gamma)/z+\gamma m(z)
is the companion Stieltjes transform (defined after Theorem
1

Step 1: Linearizing
m
at the outlier.

Equation equation
24
gives a linear relation between
m
and
\lambda
at the outlier:

m_{\mathrm{out}}\;:=\;m(\lambda_{\mathrm{out}})\;=\;\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma\,\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}}\;-\;\frac{1}{\gamma\beta}.

(25)

Step 2: Substitution into the cubic.

Plugging
m=m_{\mathrm{out}}
and
z=\lambda:=\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}
into the bulk cubic equation
16

\delta\gamma\kappa\,\lambda^{2}m_{\mathrm{out}}^{3}\;-\;\kappa\lambda(\delta+\gamma-2\delta\gamma)\,m_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}\;-\;(\lambda+\kappa(\delta-1)(1-\gamma))\,m_{\mathrm{out}}\;-\;1\;=\;0.

Let us denote
a:=(1-\gamma)/\gamma
and
b:=1/(\gamma\beta)
, so
m_{\mathrm{out}}=a/\lambda-b
. Multiplying the equation above by
\lambda^{2}
clears
m_{\mathrm{out}}
denominators, yielding a polynomial equation in
\lambda
. Expanding and collecting:

•

The cubic term gives
\delta\gamma\kappa\,(a-b\lambda)^{3}
, which contributes
-\delta\gamma\kappa b^{3}\lambda^{3}+3\delta\gamma\kappa ab^{2}\lambda^{2}-3\delta\gamma\kappa a^{2}b\lambda+\delta\gamma\kappa a^{3}

•

The quadratic term gives
-\kappa\lambda(\delta+\gamma-2\delta\gamma)(a-b\lambda)^{2}/\lambda^{0}=-\kappa(\delta+\gamma-2\delta\gamma)\lambda(a-b\lambda)^{2}
.

•

The linear term gives
-(\lambda+\kappa(\delta-1)(1-\gamma))\cdot\lambda(a-b\lambda)
.

•

The constant term
-\lambda^{2}
.

Combining the above, an overall factor of
\lambda
appears in the resulting polynomial expression (corresponding to the trivial root
\lambda=0
). After dividing by
\lambda
and regrouping, the remaining quadratic in
\lambda
is exactly equation

\delta\kappa\,\lambda^{2}\;+\;\big(-\beta^{2}\gamma+\beta\kappa(\gamma(1+\delta)-2\delta)\big)\,\lambda\;+\;\beta^{2}\big(\beta\gamma+\kappa(1-\gamma)(\delta-\gamma)\big)\;=\;0.

(26)

Writing
A_{\lambda}=\delta\kappa
,
B_{\lambda}=-\beta^{2}\gamma+\beta\kappa(\gamma(1+\delta)-2\delta)
,
C_{\lambda}=\beta^{2}(\beta\gamma+\kappa(1-\gamma)(\delta-\gamma))
, the two roots are

\lambda_{\pm}\;=\;\frac{-B_{\lambda}\pm\sqrt{B_{\lambda}^{2}-4A_{\lambda}C_{\lambda}}}{2A_{\lambda}}.

Step 3: Selecting the physical root.

The sample outlier equals the larger root,
\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}=\lambda_{+}
, whenever
\lambda_{+}
exceeds the bulk edge
\lambda_{+}^{\mathrm{bulk}}
of Appendix
A
. Equivalently,
\beta
must exceed the sample BBP threshold
\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}=-1/\underline{m}(\lambda_{+}^{\mathrm{bulk},+})
. Below
\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}

24
has no real solution in the supercritical branch, and no outlier separates from the bulk.

Appendix D
Sample Eigenvector Overlap (Theorem
4
)

This section contains the main ingredient: a self-contained proof of the key identity

|\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}|^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{\beta\,\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}\,\underline{m}^{\prime}(\lambda_{\mathrm{out}})}.

(27)

The crucial
1/\lambda_{\mathrm{out}}
factor (absent in additive/Wigner deformations) arises from differentiating the
multiplicative
structure of the sample covariance.

D.1
A differential identity: overlap as
(\beta/\lambda)\,d\lambda/d\beta

Fix a realization of the Wishart noise

{\bm{W}}\;:=\;\frac{1}{N}{\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d},\qquad G_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1),

and consider the sample covariance

{\bm{S}}(\beta)\;=\;{\bm{\Sigma}}(\beta)^{1/2}{\bm{W}}\,{\bm{\Sigma}}(\beta)^{1/2},

where we parametrize the population covariance so that its top eigenpair is frozen:

{\bm{\Sigma}}(\beta)\;=\;\beta\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}\;+\;{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\perp},\qquad\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\perp}={\bm{0}},\qquad\|\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}\|=1.

(28)

Here
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\perp}
gathers the remaining eigen-data and does
not
depend on
\beta
. Let
\lambda(\beta)
be a simple outlier eigenvalue of
{\bm{S}}(\beta)
with associated unit eigenvector
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}(\beta)
.

Lemma 2

(Overlap/derivative identity)
.

Under equation
28
,

|\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}(\beta)^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}|^{2}\;=\;\frac{\beta}{\lambda(\beta)}\,\frac{d\lambda(\beta)}{d\beta}.

(29)

Proof.

In the eigenbasis of equation
28
,
{\bm{\Sigma}}(\beta)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\beta}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\perp}^{1/2}
and so

\frac{d}{d\beta}{\bm{\Sigma}}(\beta)^{1/2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\beta}}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}.

Differentiating
{\bm{S}}(\beta)={\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
,

\frac{d{\bm{S}}}{d\beta}\;=\;\frac{d{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}}{d\beta}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}+{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bm{W}}\frac{d{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}}{d\beta}.

For a simple eigenpair, standard first-order perturbation gives
d\lambda/d\beta=\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}^{\top}(d{\bm{S}}/d\beta)\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}
. Substituting and using symmetry of
{\bm{W}}
:

\frac{d\lambda}{d\beta}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\,\big(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}\big)\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}.

Now left-multiply the eigenequation
{\bm{S}}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}=\lambda\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}
by
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}
and use
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}=\sqrt{\beta}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}
:

\sqrt{\beta}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}\;=\;\lambda\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}\;=\;\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\beta}}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}.

Plugging back,

\frac{d\lambda}{d\beta}\;=\;\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\,\big(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\bm{S}}\big)^{2},

which rearranges to equation
29
.
∎

Appendix F
Optimal Attention Weights (Theorem
5
)

Setup.

Since
{\bm{R}}
is a correlation matrix, it is symmetric (and positive semi-definite with unit diagonal). The objective is the Rayleigh quotient

f({\bm{w}})\;:=\;\frac{{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{w}}}{{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}},

subject to the affine constraint
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1
.

Scale invariance.

For any
{\bm{x}}\neq{\bm{0}}
with
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}\neq 0
, set
{\bm{w}}={\bm{x}}/({\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})
. Then
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1
and

f({\bm{w}})\;=\;\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}}\;=\;f({\bm{x}}).

So the constrained problem
\max\{f({\bm{w}}):{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1\}
is equivalent to maximizing the unconstrained Rayleigh quotient over directions
{\bm{x}}
with
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}\neq 0
.

Rayleigh–Ritz bound.

By the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem,
f({\bm{x}})\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
for all
{\bm{x}}\neq{\bm{0}}
, with equality iff
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}
, the eigenspace associated to
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
. Therefore
\sup_{{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1}f({\bm{w}})=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
.

Attainability.

A maximizer
{\bm{w}}^{\star}
exists iff there exists
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}
with
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}\neq 0
, in which case
{\bm{w}}^{\star}={\bm{v}}/({\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{v}})
achieves
f({\bm{w}}^{\star})=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
. If
{\bm{1}}\perp{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}
, the supremum is not attained (a maximizing sequence
{\bm{w}}_{k}
has
\|{\bm{w}}_{k}\|\to\infty
). When
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bm{R}})
is simple,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{max}}=\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{v}}_{\mathrm{max}}\}
, so the optimizer is unique up to the sign fixed by normalization.

•

80\%/20\%
train/test split,

•

L2 regularization coefficient
\lambda=1
,

•

signal-strength sweep
\|{\bm{\mu}}\|\in[0,5]
.

The different considered pooling methods are:

(i)

Mean pooling:

{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{id}}={\bm{1}}_{T}/T
.

(ii)

Causal attention:

{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{cau}}
is the
deterministic harmonic weight vector of Proposition
2

(score-free limit of causal self-attention with

\tau/d
scaling).

(iii)

Optimal
{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}}
:
top eigenvector of
{\bm{R}}
,
normalized so that
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}^{\mathrm{opt}}=1

(Theorem
5
).

(iv)

Learned
{\bm{w}}
(joint L2):
jointly minimize

\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\Delta_{T},\;{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\bigl\|{\bm{y}}_{\mathrm{tr}}-{\bm{C}}({\bm{w}})\,{\bm{\beta}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\;+\;\lambda\,\|{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2},

(44)

where
{\bm{C}}({\bm{w}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}\times d}
has rows

{\bm{c}}_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}X_{t}^{(n)}
and

\Delta_{T}=\{{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{T}:{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}=1\}
is the
probability simplex.

Parameterization and optimizer.

To enforce
{\bm{w}}\in\Delta_{T}
, we reparameterize
{\bm{w}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\varphi})
for

\bm{\varphi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
. For any fixed
{\bm{w}}
, the objective equation
44
is
a ridge regression in
{\bm{\beta}}
with closed-form solution

{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}({\bm{w}})=\bigl({\bm{C}}({\bm{w}})^{\top}{\bm{C}}({\bm{w}})+\lambda N_{\mathrm{tr}}\,{\bm{I}}_{d}\bigr)^{-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{w}})^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\mathrm{tr}}.

Substituting
{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}({\bm{w}})
back yields a function

g(\bm{\varphi})=g({\bm{w}}(\bm{\varphi}))
whose gradient with respect to
\bm{\varphi}
follows
from the envelope theorem applied at the ridge optimum:

\frac{\partial g}{\partial{\bm{C}}}=-\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\bigl({\bm{y}}_{\mathrm{tr}}-{\bm{C}}\,{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}\bigr)\,{{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}}^{\top},\qquad\frac{\partial g}{\partial w_{t}}=\sum_{n,j}\frac{\partial g}{\partial C_{n,j}}\,X_{t,j}^{(n)},

which is then pushed through the softmax as

\partial g/\partial\bm{\varphi}={\bm{w}}\odot\bigl(\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}g-\langle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}g,{\bm{w}}\rangle\bigr)
. The resulting
low-dimensional problem in
\bm{\varphi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
is solved by L-BFGS-B with
multiple random restarts; the best-loss initialization is retained.
