Non-Invertible Symmetries and Boundaries for Two-Dimensional Fermions
Guillermo Arias-Tamargo a, Philip Boyle Smith b,c, Rishi Mouland a, and Maxwell L. Velásquez Cotini Hutt a,d

Abstract
We study the relation between boundary conditions and categorical symmetries of two-dimensional fermionic conformal field theories. We determine all anomaly-free invertible global symmetries of two free complex Weyl fermions, which take the form ℤ k \mathbb{Z}_{k} for each primitive Pythagorean triple a 2 + b 2 = k 2 a^{2}+b^{2}=k^{2} . The theory is self-dual under gauging any of these symmetries, and so to each there is associated a non-invertible topological defect. We study the properties of these lines, and show that any conformal boundary condition of two Dirac fermions that preserves a U ​ ( 1 ) 2 U(1)^{2} symmetry can be found by dressing a trivial Dirichlet boundary with one of them. We discuss two microscopic descriptions of these defects: fermions coupled to a quantum-mechanical rotor degree of freedom; and an abelian gauge theory that realises symmetric mass generation in a half-space.

Introduction

Interesting features of quantum field theories (QFTs) can emerge when the theory is placed on a manifold with boundary. In understanding the fate of bulk excitations incident on such boundaries, and their interaction with boundary degrees of freedom that may live there, global symmetries provide a vital tool. In particular, the ’t Hooft anomalies of these symmetries provide robust insight beyond the reaches of perturbative methods. As a first port of call, the presence of such an anomaly is often effective in ruling out scenarios. More subtle is the question of what we can learn from the
absence
of an anomaly.

Consider for instance a Conformal Field Theory (CFT) with symmetry
G

G

simple
conformal boundaries, which for scattering means that the boundary cannot absorb
G

G
has a ’t Hooft anomaly

Thorngren_2021
. One is led to consider whether the converse could be true: that if
G
is anomaly-free, then a
G
-symmetric simple conformal boundary
does
exist. While a number of non-trivial such boundary conditions have been constructed (see e.g.
Han:2017hdv
,
Li:2022drc
,
BoyleSmith:2019jnh
,
BoyleSmith:2020nuf
,
Numasawa:2017crf
,
Thorngren_2021
), a few counterexamples in two dimensions have recently emerged

,
Wei:2025zyd
.

The non-triviality of this question is most simply seen in the theory of two Dirac fermions in two spacetime dimensions. Consider the

The purpose of this paper is to make manifest this relationship between chiral boundary conditions and non-invertible topological defects.

We will show that the theory of two Weyl fermions admits a non-invertible topological line defect associated with each primitive Pythagorean triple

\displaystyle a^{2}+b^{2}=k^{2}\,.

2
Discrete gauging and self-dualities

We begin simply, with the theory of two complex Weyl fermions in two dimensions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=i\psi_{1}^{\dagger}\partial_{+}\psi_{1}+i\psi_{2}^{\dagger}\partial_{+}\psi_{2}\,,

(2.3)

where we take our two single-component Weyl spinors
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
to be right-moving, and so
\partial_{+}=\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}
. We call this theory
\mathcal{T}
.

The aim of this section will be to gauge a symmetry
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{T}
. Such a symmetry must be anomaly-free; our first result will be a classification of all such symmetries. They are all finite symmetries, and indeed are all cyclic groups of odd order.

We will then gauge a generic such anomaly-free cyclic
\mathcal{G}
, which raises some subtleties. Coupling background fields for
\mathcal{G}
requires a choice of
\mathcal{G}
-action not just on the local fields
\psi_{1,2}
, corresponding to the states of the untwisted Neveu–Schwarz (NS) sector, but also on the
\mathcal{G}
-twisted NS and Ramond (R) sectors, as well as a choice of
(-1)^{F}
assignment on these new states. These choices reflect the freedom to add counter-terms for the background
\mathcal{G}
gauge field. We will see explicitly that most such couplings to
\mathcal{G}
will be inconsistent, in the sense that attempting to gauge
\mathcal{G}
will not give rise to a well-defined fermionic CFT. However, given that
\mathcal{G}
is anomaly-free, there must be at least one consistent coupling. On general grounds, different choices of consistent couplings are expected to differ by stacking SPT phases for the
\mathcal{G}
symmetry, which are classified by the reduced cobordism group
(\widetilde{I_{\mathds{Z}}\Omega^{\text{Spin}}})^{3}(B\mathcal{G})
. For an odd cyclic group
\mathcal{G}
, this group is trivial. Thus there should be a unique way of gauging
\mathcal{G}
. We determine this choice.

Finally we show that the resulting gauge theory
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
is precisely the theory we started with: two free Weyl fermions. This had to happen, since gauging a discrete symmetry does not change the central charges, and
\mathcal{T}
is the unique fermionic CFT with central charges
(c,\bar{c})=(0,2)
.
2
2

2

More precisely, it is the unique
single-vacuum
such theory. Since our symmetry
\mathcal{G}
is faithful, gauging
\mathcal{G}
preserves the fact the theory has a single vacuum, i.e. does not add a decoupled TQFT. Note we will also later encounter the theory
\mathcal{T}\otimes\text{Arf}
, but as we shall see, this is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}
.
However, in demonstrating this self-duality under gauging
\mathcal{G}
explicitly, we will establish how both local and extended operators are mapped by the gauging, along with the global symmetries that act on them. This mapping will be key to understanding scattering and boundary conditions later.

Before we look at Weyl fermions, however, we provide a brief interlude on an even simpler model—a single compact boson—where many of these same subtleties arise.

2.1
An interlude: the compact boson

As a warm-up, consider a compact boson in two dimensions at radius
R

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{R^{2}}{4\pi}(\partial\phi)^{2},\qquad\phi\sim\phi+2\pi\,.

(2.4)

This theory has a

\mathcal{G}={\mathds{Z}}_{2}\subset U(1)_{\text{shift}}

{\mathds{Z}}_{2}

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}(R;\tau,z_{\text{s}},z_{\text{w}})

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}\,\zeta^{h-1/24}\bar{\zeta}^{\bar{h}-1/24}z_{\text{s}}^{Q_{\text{s}}}z_{\text{w}}^{Q_{\text{w}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\eta(\tau)|^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in{\mathds{Z}}\\
m\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\end{subarray}}\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n}{R}-mR\right)^{2}}\bar{\zeta}^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n}{R}+mR\right)^{2}}z_{\text{s}}^{n}z_{\text{w}}^{m}\,.

(2.5)

Here,

The mixed anomaly between

Let us parameterise these two choices by

\displaystyle Z^{\text{gauged}}_{t}(R;\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}Z_{\alpha}(R;\tau,e^{\pi i\beta},e^{2\pi it\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{t\alpha\beta}Z_{\alpha}(R;\tau,e^{\pi i\beta},1)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} \,\,&Z_{0}(R/2;\tau,1,1)\,,\hskip 227.62204pt&t=0\\
&\frac{1}{|\eta(\tau)|^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in{\mathds{Z}}}\zeta^{\left((n+m)R+2(n-m)/R\right)^{2}/16}\bar{\zeta}^{\left((n+m)R-2(n-m)/R\right)^{2}/16}\,,&t=1\end{aligned}\right.

The

Indeed, we recognise

From a Lagrangian perspective, the additional phase
(-1)^{t\alpha\beta}
corresponds to our ability to add an additional counter-term when gauging the discrete symmetry
\mathcal{G}

(-1)^{\alpha\beta}=(-1)^{\text{Arf}[\rho+a]}

In some contexts one might refer to the phase

as a choice of

(\widetilde{I_{\mathds{Z}}\Omega^{\mathrm{SO}}})^{3}(B\mathcal{G})\cong H^{2}(\mathcal{G};U(1))

Below, we will see how an analogous phenomenon occurs in the theory of 2 Weyl fermions, whereby the presence of a mixed ’t Hooft anomaly allows us to introduce an additional phase when gauging a discrete group. Similarly to the compact boson CFT, we will find that there is a unique choice of phase which will lead to a well-defined fermionic theory after gauging, whereas other choices of phases would correspond to enlarging to the class of parafermionic theories

The set of all invertible 0-form symmetries of

\displaystyle SO(4)\cong(SU(2)_{L}\times SU(2)_{R})/{\mathds{Z}}_{2}\,,

, so we can exclude these cases. Next note that there is an eight-fold degeneracy in the parameterisation of the symmetry groups
\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}
in terms of coprime pairs
p,q\in{\mathds{Z}}
. Firstly within each of the two types the following groups are equivalent,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}\cong\mathcal{G}_{(-p,-q)}\cong\mathcal{G}_{(q,-p)}\cong\mathcal{G}_{(-q,p)}\,,\qquad\text{for all }p,q\text{ coprime}\,.

(2.19)

Secondly, every group of the first type is equivalent to one of the second, and vice versa. These equivalences are

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}\cong\mathcal{G}_{(p-q,p+q)}\,,\qquad\text{for all }p,q\text{ coprime with }pq\text{ even}\,.

(2.20)

These exhaust all equivalences between the maximal anomaly-free groups, as is proven in Appendix
A
. One can then take as the distinct maximal anomaly-free invertible symmetries the set
\{\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}\}
with
pq
even and
p,q>0
.

Finally, we need to consider subgroups of the
\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}
, where it suffices to consider
pq
even as we’ve just seen. These subgroups are automatically anomaly-free. It turns out that any such subgroup is already included in the classification. Precisely, if
k
has a prime factor
d
, then the subgroup
{\mathds{Z}}_{k/d}\subset\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}\cong{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{G}_{(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}
for some
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
coprime with
p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
even. A proof of this fact is also found in Appendix
A
.

We conclude that the set of
all
inequivalent anomaly-free invertible symmetries of two Weyl fermions is given precisely by

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}\cong{\mathds{Z}}_{k}\}_{(p,q)\in\mathcal{S}}\,,

(2.21)

where, as ever,
k=p^{2}+q^{2}
,
\mathcal{G}_{(p,q)}
is generated by

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\to e^{2\pi iq/k}\psi_{1}\,,\quad\psi_{2}\to e^{-2\pi ip/k}\psi_{2}\,,

(2.22)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{(p,q)\in{\mathds{Z}}_{>0}^{2}:\gcd(p,q)=1,\,\,pq\text{ even}\}\,.

(2.23)

Note that there is an equivalent parameterisation of this set which will prove useful later, in terms of primitive Pythagorean triples:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}=\{(a,b,k)\in{\mathds{Z}}_{\neq 0}^{2}\times{\mathds{Z}}_{>0}:{}&a^{2}+b^{2}=k^{2},\,\,\gcd(a,b,k)=1,\\
&(a,b,k)\sim(b,a,k)\sim(-a,-b,k)\}\,.\end{split}

(2.24)

The bijection between elements of the presentation (
2.23
) and representatives from each equivalence class in the presentation (
2.24
) was given by Euclid,

\displaystyle a=p^{2}-q^{2},\quad b=2pq,\quad k=p^{2}+q^{2}\,.

(2.25)

Gauging cyclic symmetries

labels the

It then remains to gauge
\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\pi i\left(Q_{1}+(2q-1)Q_{2}\right)\Big)\,.

(2.45)

We then find that the torus partition function of the gauge theory with spin structure

is given by

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}_{t}(\tau,\mu_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^{k-1}\hat{Z}_{t,(\alpha,\beta)}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{i})\,,

(2.46)

where
\hat{Z}_{t,(\alpha,\beta)}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{i})
is the partition function in a sector of

holonomies

. It is given explicitly by

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{t,(\alpha,\beta)}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{i})=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{u}{2}}

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}^{(n_{1}+\alpha q/k)^{2}/2+(n_{2}-\alpha p/k)^{2}/2}z_{1}^{pn_{1}+qn_{2}}z_{2}^{qn_{1}-pn_{2}+\alpha}

(2.47)

The first exponential is a result of introducing the twist by (

As a consistency check, one can verify that this expression is indeed independently invariant under all of

Note that, like in the ungauged theory, we can absorb the insertion of
(-1)^{F}

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{t}^{(u,1)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\hat{Z}_{t}^{(u,0)}(\tau,\mu_{1}+\pi i,\mu_{2}+\pi i(2q-1))\,.

(2.48)

2.4
Modular transformations

So far we know that
\mathcal{G}
has no ’t Hooft anomaly, and so can be consistently gauged. We now wish to understand the modular properties of the gauged theory. Recall that our aim is to find a
\mathcal{G}
such that the theory is mapped back to itself under gauging. For such a self-duality to be possible, the modular properties of the theory before and after gauging must be the same. Imposing this constraint will uniquely fix the value of
t
in the choice of
\mathcal{G}
-action (
2.43
).

One may intuitively think of this constraint as ensuring that there is no mixed
\mathcal{G}
-gravitational anomaly, but this is imprecise.
For a unitary two-dimensional fermionic theory with a
\mathcal{G}={\mathds{Z}}_{k}
symmetry, the anomaly is valued in
{\mathds{Z}}\times{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
when
k
is odd. The
{\mathds{Z}}
factor describes the standard gravitational anomaly, but the remaining
{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
factor cannot be said to be either a pure
\mathcal{G}
anomaly or a mixed
\mathcal{G}
-gravitational anomaly; in some sense it is both. The construction of the previous subsection ensures that this
{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
-valued anomaly is not present in our case. The correct way to interpret this is that there must be at least one value of
t
such that the resulting gauged theory is a unitary fermionic theory with the same gravitational anomaly as the original theory of two Weyl fermions. In fact, there is a
unique
choice of
t
with this property, which we will denote by
t_{*}
. This is because different such choices of
t
would be related by stacking a fermionic
\mathcal{G}
-SPT phase and for
k
odd there are no non-trivial SPTs to stack.
8
8

8

The only allowed one would be
(-1)^{\text{Arf}(\rho)}
, where
\rho
denotes the spin structure, but this does not become trivial when the
\mathcal{G}
background field is turned off. We will return to the role of
(-1)^{\text{Arf}(\rho)}
in the gauging in section
2.5
.
,
9
9

9

One may ask what becomes of the theory if we use other values of
t\neq t_{*}
. The results of section
2.5
will imply that for these values of

the gauged theory is not fermionic. Rather, its primary operators have spins

Thorngren:2021yso

In order to proceed, we must determine
t_{*}
. This can be done by studying modular transformations of the gauged partition function.

2.5
Demonstrating self-duality

By gauging
\mathcal{G}\cong{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
with the choice
t=t_{*}
fixing the action of
\mathcal{G}
on the twisted sectors, we have shown that we land on a fermionic CFT which we denote
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
. For notational convenience, we henceforth fix
t=t_{*}
and write
\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}\equiv\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}_{t}
for the partition function of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
with spin structure
\rho=(u,v)
. The hat distinguishes this from the partition function
Z^{(u,v)}
of the ungauged theory
\mathcal{T}
as given in (
2.31
).

As discussed above, the gauge theory
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
is necessarily the same as the theory
\mathcal{T}
we started with. This should be contrasted with the compact boson example, whereby gauging can (and generically does) send us to another point on the
c=1
bosonic conformal manifold. It will be important however to explicitly construct a map between
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{T}
, as we will do now.

The Neveu–Schwarz sector

Let us first consider the theory
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
with NS boundary conditions around the spatial circle. Since we have the relations (
2.32
) and (
2.48
), it suffices to study the NS-NS partition function with fugacities
z_{1},z_{2}
turned on for
U(1)_{1},U(1)_{2}

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(0,0)}(\tau,\mu_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}}\bar{\zeta}^{(n_{1}+\alpha q/k)^{2}/2+(n_{2}-\alpha p/k)^{2}/2}z_{1}^{pn_{1}+qn_{2}}z_{2}^{qn_{1}-pn_{2}+\alpha}

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\times\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\beta}{k}\left(qn_{1}-pn_{2}+t_{*}p\alpha\right)\right)\,.

(2.57)

The sum over
\beta
projects the other three sums onto those values of

satisfying

\displaystyle qn_{1}-pn_{2}+t_{*}p\alpha=0\quad\mod k\,,

(2.58)

which, since

\displaystyle\alpha-2(pn_{2}-qn_{1})=0\quad\mod k\,,

(2.59)

where we’ve used

k
.
The solutions
(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)
of this equation can be parameterised by a pair of integers
m_{1}(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha),m_{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)\in{\mathds{Z}}
defined by

\displaystyle m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{1}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha-2(pn_{2}-qn_{1})\big)\,,

\displaystyle m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{2}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha-2(pn_{2}-qn_{1})\big)\,.

(2.60)

This can be written equivalently in the compact form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\\
m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{k}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\\
n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.61)

with
a,b
defined in (

), although this obscures the fact that
m_{1},m_{2}
are indeed integers by virtue of the constraint (

). Crucially, this is a one-to-one parameterisation, as evidenced by the inverse map

\displaystyle\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\quad(\text{mod }k),\,\qquad\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\},

\displaystyle n_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{1}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\big)\,,

\displaystyle n_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{2}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\big)\,.

(2.62)

The virtue of this parameterisation is that the scaling dimension of the operator
(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)
appearing in (
2.57
) becomes simply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{2}^{2}\,,

(2.63)

recovering, as claimed, the spectrum of two Weyl fermions.

Furthermore, keeping track of chemical potentials, we find

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(0,0)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}}\bar{\zeta}^{m_{1}^{2}/2+m_{2}^{2}/2}z_{1}^{pm_{1}+qm_{2}}z_{2}^{-qm_{1}+pm_{2}}

\displaystyle=Z^{(0,0)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})\,.

(2.64)

Note in particular the non-trivial action on the chemical potentials, and thus on the action of
U(1)_{1}\times U(1)_{2}
. Indeed, we have landed on the NS-NS partition function of two Weyl fermions
\psi_{1}^{\prime},\psi_{2}^{\prime}
which carry the following charges under
U(1)_{1}\times U(1)_{2}
,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&\psi_{1}^{\prime}&\psi_{2}^{\prime}\\
\hline\cr U(1)_{1}&p&q\\
U(1)_{2}&-q&p\\
\end{array}

to be contrasted with (
2.3
). In particular, the
{\mathds{Z}}_{k}\subset U(1)_{1}\times U(1)_{2}
appearing in the quotient in (

) no longer acts trivially on
local
operators of the free fermion realisation of the gauged theory. However, there is a different
{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
that does act trivially, generated by
(e^{2\pi iq/k},e^{2\pi ip/k})
. In other words, local operators of the gauged theory fractionalise the charges carried by local operators of the ungauged theory. This is not a surprise, and is just a simple consequence of the fact that certain twist operators of the ungauged theory become local operators of the gauged theory, and vice versa.

We also need to check that the action of
(-1)^{F}
in the NS sector is preserved under the map (
2.61
) between the operators of
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
, which amounts to generalising (
2.64
) to the NS-R sector. This is straightforward using (
2.32
) and (
2.48
) which imply

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(0,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=Z^{(0,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})\,.

(2.68)

We have thus succeeded in mapping the NS Hilbert space of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
to that of
\mathcal{T}
.

This map is far from unique: for instance, after making the identification (
2.61
) we could furthermore conjugate one of the fermions, e.g.
\psi_{1}\to\psi_{1}^{\dagger}
, corresponding to
m_{1}\to-m_{1}
, or else swap them
\psi_{1}\leftrightarrow\psi_{2}
, corresponding to
m_{1}\leftrightarrow m_{2}
. More generally, we can always compose our map (
2.61
) with an invertible map from the NS sector of
\mathcal{T}
to itself. Such maps make up the
O(4)
acting on four Majorana–Weyl fermions; swaps
\psi_{1}\leftrightarrow\psi_{2}
and conjugations of each fermion generate a subgroup
D_{4}\subset O(4)
. Only elements of the subgroup
SO(4)\subset O(4)
extend to automorphisms also of the Ramond sector, and thus of the theory
\mathcal{T}
. This distinction will play an important role shortly.

The Ramond sector: Arf full or Arf empty?

We next consider the Ramond sector. The calculations here are entirely analogous to the Neveu–Schwarz sector, and so we relegate details to Appendix
B
. We find that the map (
2.61
) we have defined in the NS sector extends naturally to the Ramond sector. Using this map, we find

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(1,0)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=Z^{(1,0)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})\,.

(2.69)

This is not quite enough to conclude that the Ramond sectors of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{T}
are identified by this map, however. This is because in the Ramond sector of
\mathcal{T}
, there is an ambiguity in how we define
(-1)^{F}
. The two consistent choices of
(-1)^{F}
differ by
(-1)^{F}\to-(-1)^{F}
, and thus differ by a flip of sign of the R-R partition function. We have made a choice for
(-1)^{F}
in our definition (
2.31
) of the theory
\mathcal{T}
, and need to check if our map recovers this choice or not. We compute the R-R partition function of the gauge theory, apply the Ramond sector extension of the map (
2.61
), and indeed find that the answer is no:

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(1,1)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=-Z^{(1,1)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})\,.

(2.70)

Putting all four sectors together, we have

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=(-1)^{uv}Z^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})=(-1)^{\text{Arf}(\rho)}Z^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},-\mu_{2})\,,

(2.71)

where Arf
(\rho)
is the Arf invariant, defined on a general Riemann surface with spin structure
\rho
as

\text{Arf}(\rho)=\begin{cases}0&\rho\text{ even}\\
1&\rho\text{ odd}\end{cases}\,\,,

(2.72)

We learn that, using the map (
2.61
) to exchange
(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)
with
(m_{1},m_{2})
, we land not on the original theory
\mathcal{T}
but rather on
\mathcal{T}\otimes\text{Arf}
. This is a problem for our intended self-duality construction, for which we must land back on the original theory
\mathcal{T}
.

There is a quick fix available, however. Let us consider the slightly different map given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\\
m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{k}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\\
n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.73)

with inverse

\displaystyle\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\quad(\text{mod }k),\,\qquad\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\},

\displaystyle n_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=-m_{2}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,,

\displaystyle n_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{1}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,.

(2.74)

In other words, we use (
2.61
), composed with a map
S^{\prime}:(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\to(\psi_{2}^{\dagger},\psi_{1})
. The fact that this operation absorbs the extra sign in (
2.70
) can be seen in an elementary way. Turning off the fugacities for brevity, the torus partition function of
\mathcal{T}\otimes\text{Arf}
is

\displaystyle(-1)^{uv}\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{u}{2}}(-1)^{v(n_{1}+n_{2})}\bar{\zeta}^{n_{1}^{2}/2+n_{2}^{2}/2}\,,

(2.75)

where, as always, we parameterise spin structures by
u,v\in\{0,1\}
. Then
S^{\prime}
enacts the change of variables
(n_{1},n_{2})\to(-n_{2},n_{1})
under which this expression becomes

\displaystyle(-1)^{uv}\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{u}{2}}(-1)^{v(-n_{2}+n_{1})}\bar{\zeta}^{n_{1}^{2}/2+n_{2}^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{uv}\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{u}{2}}(-1)^{-2vn_{2}}(-1)^{v(n_{1}+n_{2})}\bar{\zeta}^{n_{1}^{2}/2+n_{2}^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}-\frac{u}{2}}(-1)^{v(n_{1}+n_{2})}\bar{\zeta}^{n_{1}^{2}/2+n_{2}^{2}/2}\,,

(2.76)

which yields the partition function of
\mathcal{T}
, as claimed.

Turning fugacities back on, we find that the map (
2.73
) yields

\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=Z^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{2},\mu_{1})\,,

(2.77)

and thus we recover the original theory
\mathcal{T}
, with charges under
U(1)_{1}
and
U(1)_{2}
swapped.

We say then that the map (
2.73
) defines an
isomorphism
between
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{T}
. In general, we define an isomorphism between two QFTs as a bijection between the operators, both local and extended, of one theory and another. QFTs related by such an isomorphism are usually said to be dual. For two-dimensional CFTs, this takes the form of an invertible linear map between the Virasoro primaries of the two theories that preserves conformal weights
(h,\bar{h})
and OPE coefficients.

Other isomorphisms

Our choice of map (
2.73
) is not unique, and could be composed with any invertible line of
\mathcal{T}
; that is, with any element of
SO(4)
. For example, we could consider

\displaystyle C\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\\
m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{k}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\\
n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.78)

for any of the matrices

\displaystyle C\in\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\,,

(2.79)

and similar manipulations to (
2.76
) would go through. Indeed, the first of these is simply the choice (
2.73
) used above. It is straightforward to check these four maps are indeed related by composition with a suitable
SO(4)
transformation. These four choices of map
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{T}
are singled out from all others by the fact that they map the highest-weight representations of a chosen
U(1)^{2}
current algebra of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
, labelled by
(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)
, to that of
\mathcal{T}
, labelled by
(m_{1},m_{2})
. In other words, they are an artefact of our choice to work with two complex fermions, rather than four real ones.

Different choices of
C
give different maps from
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{T}
in (

), mapping the
U(1)_{1}\times U(1)_{2}
fugacities differently. The generalisation of (
2.77
) for a more general choice of

is then (see Appendix
B
for the proof)

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}(\tau,\mu_{i})=Z^{(u,v)}(\tau,K_{ij}\mu_{j})\,,

(2.80)

where
K
is an orthogonal matrix given by

\displaystyle K\in\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\pm\frac{1}{k}\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\

(2.81)

for the choices of
C
in (
2.79
), with
a,b
given in (
2.25
). Indeed, for the first choice of
C
, we recover (
2.77
).

As a side remark, we note that these considerations make manifest that the invertible 0-form symmetry of
\mathcal{T}
is
SO(4)
, not
O(4)
. Indeed, if we consider the
O(4)
matrix acting on four Majorana–Weyl fermions which corresponds to the

that yields (
2.73
), we find (see Appendix
B
)

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\det\!{}_{O(4)}(C)=\det\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_{3}\\

so it is not an element of

. Those transformations which are elements of

are symmetries of
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. isomorphisms
\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
, while those which are elements of

but not

are
not

and give isomorphisms

to which this

.

3
Non-invertible symmetries

We have now established that the theory of two Weyl fermions is self-dual under gauging any anomaly-free symmetry. The aim of this section is to use these self-dualities to construct and study non-invertible topological defects of the theory.

We first review the half-space gauging construction of these defects in section
3.1

we will apply this to our setup, and study how extended operators are acted upon by the non-invertible lines. We then discuss in section
3.3

3.4

3.3
Scattering through self-duality defects

We can now consider what happens when excitations of

In this section we consider conformal boundary conditions for two Dirac fermions which preserve some
U(1)^{2}

4.1

Let us recall the unique parameterisation of all anomaly-free

Microscopic descriptions

In this final section, we explore some microscopic setups which flow at low energies to the defects

5.1
Non-invertible lines from rotors

The simplest of these comes in the form of fermions coupled to a quantum-mechanical rotor degree of freedom
Polchinski:1984uw

\mathcal{N}_{(p,q)}
corresponding to the
{\mathds{Z}}_{k}

One considers two Weyl fermions coupled to a periodic scalar degree of freedom
\sigma\sim\sigma+2\pi

\displaystyle S=\int d^{2}x\left(i\psi_{1}^{\dagger}\partial_{+}\psi_{1}+i\psi_{2}^{\dagger}\partial_{+}\psi_{2}\right)+\int dt\left(\frac{I}{2}\dot{\sigma}^{2}+\sigma\!\left(v_{1}\psi_{1}^{\dagger}\psi_{1}+v_{2}\psi_{2}^{\dagger}\psi_{2}\right)\right)\,.

For this construction to work, it is particularly important that the charge vector

Given then that the rotor flows to a genuine defect of

5.2
Boundaries from symmetric mass generation

We have seen that we can only construct a simple conformal boundary (that is, a topological interface to the trivial theory) preserving symmetries of Class
\mathcal{V}
. For symmetries of Class
\mathcal{A}

It is natural to ask whether this dichotomy between Classes

Discussion

We have studied the relation of categorical symmetries of two Weyl fermions and symmetric boundary conditions of two Dirac fermions in 1+1 dimensions. The non-invertible defects of relevance here are constructed by gauging discrete symmetries in a half space. All boundary conditions of two Dirac fermions preserving a
U(1)^{2}
symmetry can be constructed by dressing a Dirichlet boundary condition with one of these non-invertible topological lines and sometimes an additional invertible line. These come in two (SPT) classes, one of which (Class
\mathcal{V}
) are simple conformal boundary conditions while the other (Class
\mathcal{A}
) are interfaces to the Arf SPT. The latter can be dressed with a Majorana mode to yield a non-simple boundary condition.

A similar correspondence between topological lines and boundary conditions arise for the two-dimensional free compact boson (
2.4
). At rational values of the radius squared,
R^{2}=p/q
, a conformal boundary condition can be found which preserves
\mathcal{G}={\mathds{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathds{Z}}_{q}\subset U(1)_{\text{shift}}\times U(1)_{\text{winding}}

Gaberdiel:2001zq
. Correspondingly, at this value of
R
the theory is self-dual under gauging
\mathcal{G}
and this boundary condition can be constructed by stacking a trivial Dirichlet boundary with the corresponding non-invertible defect. Interestingly, while this
\mathcal{G}={\mathds{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathds{Z}}_{q}
is non-anomalous for all
R
, simple conformal boundary conditions preserving
\mathcal{G}
do not exist when
R^{2}\neq p/q

Wei:2025zyd
. This seems to hint that the relation between boundary conditions and non-invertible defects could be more general, which would be a very interesting point to study in the future; e.g. by generalising these ideas to interacting theories such as non-linear sigma models
Arias-Tamargo:2025xdd
,
Arias-Tamargo:2025fhv
.

We have also discussed how symmetric boundary conditions arise in the context of symmetric mass generation in section
5.2
, showing that a generalisation of the analysis of
Mouland:2025ilu
nicely manifests the difference between the Class
\mathcal{V}
and Class
\mathcal{A}
boundary conditions of two Dirac fermions. In particular, our construction of boundary conditions preserving symmetries of Class
\mathcal{A}
gives only
non-simple
boundaries. Indeed, these symmetries provide yet more examples of anomaly-free symmetries that cannot be preserved by any simple conformal boundary condition.

We finally comment on a few more open questions.

Generalising to
N>2
fermions

We have focused on the theory of two Weyl fermions, which is the simplest fermionic theory to exhibit the properties discussed above. Let us consider the generalisation of our analysis to
N>2
Weyl fermions.

Interestingly, there are several qualitative differences with respect to the
N=2
case. Firstly, for
N\geq 8
, this is not the unique CFT with
(c,\bar{c})=(0,N)
and it is possible that a generic discrete gauging will not result in a self-duality without further fine-tuning.
Even for
N<8
there are novelties: we have seen that for
N=2
there is always a choice of duality isomorphism
D
such that the corresponding topological line
\mathcal{N}
furnishes a Tambara–Yamagami fusion category. All other lines considered can be built by stacking invertible lines on
\mathcal{N}
. Already for
N=3
this is not necessarily the case. For example, half-space gauging an anomaly-free
{\mathds{Z}}_{39}
symmetry of three Weyl fermions with charges
(1,5,13)
and using a duality isomorphism
D:\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{T}
will lead to a topological self-duality defect. However there is no choice of
D
such that this defect has TY fusion rules.
20
20

20

This is guaranteed as there is no choice of
D
mapping the Wilson line
W
of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
to a generator of
\mathcal{G}
(see the discussion in section
3.2
).
We hope to be able to report on these matters soon.

Fermion-monopole scattering

One motivation for this work was the relationship between boundary conditions for two-dimensional fermions and the scattering of light fermions off of heavy monopoles in four-dimensional gauge theory
Callan:1982ah
,
Rubakov:1982fp
,
Affleck:1993np
,
vanBeest:2023dbu
,
vanBeest:2023mbs
. Let us recall the general story here. We begin with a non-abelian gauge theory that admits ’t Hooft--Polyakov monopoles. One is then interested in scattering massless fermions (that do not acquire a mass by the Higgs mechanism) at energies far below the Higgs scale.
21
21

21

In the context of the Standard Model, what we call here the Higgs scale is really the GUT scale, and we are interested in scattering far below this scale, but far above the electroweak Higgs scale, where quarks are effectively massless.
This is effectively captured by the scattering of massless fermion fields off of ’t Hooft lines in the Higgs phase low energy effective theory. If the monopole of interest is spherically symmetric, to first approximation we can solve this scattering mode-by-mode in an angular momentum decomposition. If the four-dimensional fermions were chiral, the scattering problem in the lowest angular momentum channel is precisely a boundary CFT problem of two-dimensional complex fermions with a boundary that must preserve an (anomaly-free) symmetry that is generically chiral, and often non-abelian.

In a number of examples it is precisely the kind of boundary conditions constructed in this paper which solve this scattering problem, where from a two-dimensional perspective local excitations become twist operators at the end of
invertible
lines. This is indeed the case for the charge 1 and 2 spherically-symmetric monopoles in the
SU(5)
GUT
vanBeest:2023mbs
, for instance.
As a final flourish, one lifts back to four dimensions, and seeks a codimension
1
topological surface corresponding to the invertible line generated in two dimensions. The idea is that the outgoing state lives on the boundary of such a surface, cut open. Often, the necessary surface in four dimensions is non-invertible, despite the fact it reduces to an invertible line in two dimensions.

A pressing open problem is that there are other perfectly consistent monopoles in perfectly consistent theories—such as the charge 3 monopole in the
SU(5)
GUT—where we still do not know the solution to the two-dimensional scattering problem of the lowest angular momentum modes. This is the case whenever the left-movers and right-movers carry different
N
-alities under some non-abelian symmetry that the boundary should preserve. Such scattering problems only begin once we have
N\geq 8
fermions in the two-dimensional effective theory. It would therefore be very interesting to see if a generalisation of the present work to
N>2
fermions could provide a route towards constructing these (even more) exotic boundaries.

Supposing the correct boundary condition in these cases still attaches a topological line, one is led to ask whether the topological lines in this mysterious case are invertible or not. This is closely related to the classification aspect of the present paper. In detail, we have classified all duality defects of two Weyl fermions that can be produced by gauging an
invertible
symmetry, which is equivalent to finding all boundaries for which the topological lines generated are invertible. We learn that all such defects are
\text{TY}({\mathds{Z}}_{k})
with
k
odd, up to fusion with an invertible line, and all have a UV description as an abelian rotor. The latter class of defects is known not to generate the required dynamics in those problematic four-dimensional set-ups. Thus if our analysis can be generalised to more than two Weyl fermions, we would have succeeded in showing that fermions scattering off such a monopole must become attached to non-invertible lines in the two-dimensional effective description, which should in turn lift to some non-invertible surface in four dimensions. We hope to report on this direction soon.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\\
m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{k}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\\
n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(B.185)

with
a,b
defined in (
2.25
). This is indeed a one-to-one parameterisation, as evidenced by the inverse map

\displaystyle\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\quad(\text{mod }k)\,,\,\qquad\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}\,,

\displaystyle n_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{1}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\big)\,,

\displaystyle n_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{2}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})-2(pm_{2}-qm_{1})\big)\,.

(B.186)

As for the NS-NS sector in (

), we can generalise this map by the replacement

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix}\to C\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(B.187)

with

one of the four matrices (

.

Plugging
n_{1}(m_{1},m_{2}),n_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})
into (

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{(1,0)}(\tau,\vec{\mu})

\displaystyle=Z^{(1,0)}(\tau,K\vec{\mu})\,.

(B.188)

Appendix C
Tambara–Yamagami bicharacter

The goal of this appendix is to elaborate on the structure of the two Tambara–Yamagami lines (defined via the first two matrices in (
2.79
)). In section
3.4
we have discussed the difference between the fusion rules of these lines and those of the lines corresponding to the final two matrices in (
2.79
). The full categorical data includes more information, namely the
F
-symbols that control the associativity properties of topological defects. In the case of a TY category, the
F
-symbols are completely determined by three pieces of data
Tambara:1998vmj
,
Thorngren:2021yso
: the order of the cyclic group
\mathcal{G}\cong{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
generated by the
\eta
line, the object known as the
symmetric bicharacter
(which we discuss in this appendix), and a sign
\epsilon=\pm 1
known as the Frobenius–Schur indicator. We will not discuss the latter here.

As stated in the main text, the defect
\mathcal{N}
obeys the fusion rules of a TY fusion category precisely when the isomorphism
D
between
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{T}
maps the dual symmetry
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}\cong{\mathds{Z}}_{k}
of
\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}
onto the symmetry
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{T}
. Let us assume this, so that
C
takes one of the first two values in (
2.79
). The way in which the elements of
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
are identified with those of
\mathcal{G}
is encoded in the integer
r
with
\gcd(r,k)=1
found in (
3.87
).
It is this integer which fixes the
symmetric bicharacter

\chi
of the TY fusion category. In general,
\chi
specifies how background gauge fields coupled to
\mathcal{G}
in
\mathcal{T}
are related to those coupled to
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
; that is, schematically we have

\displaystyle\sum_{A}Z[A]e^{i\chi[A,A^{\prime}]}=Z[A^{\prime}]\,.

(C.192)

Let us be more explicit. In our case, the bicharacter can be written
e^{i\chi(A,A^{\prime})}=\omega^{\int A\smile A^{\prime}}
where
\omega
is a primitive
k^{\text{th}}
root of unity. In terms of
r
, it is given by

\displaystyle\omega=e^{-2\pi ir/k}\,.

(C.193)

The isomorphism
D:\mathcal{T}/\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{T}
holds in the presence of an arbitrary background
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
gauge field, mapping it to a particular
\mathcal{G}
gauge field. Then
\omega
can be
defined
as the unique primitive
k^{\text{th}}
root of unity such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^{k-1}\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\omega^{(\alpha\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}\beta)}=\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}_{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}\,,

(C.194)

where, as in the text,
\hat{Z}^{(u,v)}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
is the twisted torus partition function (
2.47
) (with
t=t_{*}
). On the right-hand side we have coupled
\mathcal{T}
to a background gauge field
A^{\prime}
for
\mathcal{G}
which is specified by holonomies
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
. Meanwhile, on the left-hand-side
rA^{\prime}
is the background field for the dual symmetry
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
. This is achieved through a coupling to the dynamical gauge field
A
, specified by holonomies
(\alpha,\beta)
, given by

\displaystyle\omega^{\int A\smile A^{\prime}}=\omega^{(\alpha\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}\beta)}\,.

(C.195)

In section
3.2
, we found that (
C.194
) is satisfied with
r=\pm(2q)^{-1}p
, with sign depending on which
D
we’ve picked, for the special case
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})=(0,\pm 2q)
and
(u,v)=(0,0)
. For completeness, we now verify (
C.194
) for general
A^{\prime}
, still for
(u,v)=(0,0)
.

Let’s specialise to the isomorphism
D
corresponding to the first
C
matrix in (
2.79
). Picking the opposite sign for
C
follows similarly. On the left-hand side of (
C.194
) we then have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}&\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}}\bar{\zeta}^{(n_{1}+\alpha q/k)^{2}/2+(n_{2}-\alpha p/k)^{2}/2}\\
&\times\;\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\beta}{k}\left(qn_{1}-pn_{2}+t_{*}p\alpha+r\alpha^{\prime}\right)-\frac{2\pi ir\alpha\beta^{\prime}}{k}\right)\,.\end{split}

(C.196)

The sum over
\beta
projects onto states with

\displaystyle qn_{1}-pn_{2}+t_{*}p\alpha+r\alpha^{\prime}=0\mod k\,,

(C.197)

or, equivalently, those with

\displaystyle\alpha+2r\alpha^{\prime}-2(pn_{2}-qn_{1})=0\mod k\,.

(C.198)

We can parameterise solutions
(n_{1},n_{2},\alpha)
of this equation by
(m_{1},m_{2})\in{\mathds{Z}}^{2}
with

\displaystyle\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=-2r\alpha^{\prime}+2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\quad(\text{mod }k)\,,\,\qquad\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}\,,

\displaystyle n_{1}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=-m_{2}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})+2r\alpha^{\prime}-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,,

\displaystyle n_{2}(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=m_{1}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})+2r\alpha^{\prime}-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,.

(C.199)

This is a one-to-one parameterisation of solutions to (
C.198
), as evidenced by the inverse map

\displaystyle-m_{2}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{1}-\frac{q}{k}\big(\alpha+2r\alpha^{\prime}-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,,

\displaystyle m_{1}(\alpha,n_{1},n_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{2}+\frac{p}{k}\big(\alpha+2r\alpha^{\prime}-2(pm_{1}+qm_{2})\big)\,.

(C.200)

The dimension of the operator
(m_{1},m_{2})
is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{1}+\frac{\alpha q}{k}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{2}-\frac{\alpha p}{k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{1}-\frac{2pr\alpha^{\prime}}{k}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{2}-\frac{2qr\alpha^{\prime}}{k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{1}^{\prime}+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}q}{k}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}p}{k}\right)^{2}\,,

(C.201)

where we’ve defined shifted mode numbers

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=m_{1}+\frac{(-2pr-q)\alpha^{\prime}}{k}\,,

\displaystyle m_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=m_{2}^{\prime}+\frac{(-2qr+p)\alpha^{\prime}}{k}\,.

(C.202)

We require
m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}\in{\mathds{Z}}
for all
\alpha^{\prime}
, which is the case precisely if

\displaystyle r=(2q)^{-1}p\mod k\,.

(C.203)

Lastly, we need to evaluate the final term appearing in the exponential in (
C.196
). We find

\displaystyle-r\alpha(m_{1},m_{2})

\displaystyle=qm_{1}-pm_{2}+2r^{2}\alpha^{\prime}\mod k

\displaystyle=qm_{1}-pm_{2}+t_{*}p\alpha^{\prime}\mod k

\displaystyle=qm_{1}^{\prime}-pm_{2}^{\prime}+t_{*}p\alpha^{\prime}\mod k\,,

(C.204)

using
t_{*}p=2^{-1}
mod
k
. Hence

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi ir\alpha\beta^{\prime}}{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\beta^{\prime}}{k}\Big(qm_{1}^{\prime}-pm_{2}^{\prime}+t_{*}p\alpha^{\prime}\Big)\right)\,.

(C.205)

Putting the pieces together, with
r
given by (
C.203
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^{k-1}\hat{Z}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\omega^{(\alpha\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}\beta)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\overline{\eta(\tau)}^{2}}\sum_{m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}\in{\mathds{Z}}}\bar{\zeta}^{(m_{1}^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime}q/k)^{2}/2+(m_{2}^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}p/k)^{2}/2}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i\beta^{\prime}}{k}\left(qm_{1}^{\prime}-pm_{2}^{\prime}+t_{*}p\alpha^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\hat{Z}_{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}\,,

(C.206)

as claimed.
