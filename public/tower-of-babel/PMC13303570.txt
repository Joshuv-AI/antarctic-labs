Predicting SIRS after PCNL using machine learning: the joint impact of sarcopenia and staghorn stones
Authors: Song Wei, Boran Lv, Baiyu Liu, Qunxiong Huang, Cheng Hu, Hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate machine learning models for predicting systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) after percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), to establish a web-based prediction tool, and to investigate the association between sarcopenia and staghorn stones as well as their potential synergistic effect on postoperative SIRS.MethodsPatients undergoing PCNL between January 2021 and August 2025 at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University were retrospectively analyzed and randomly divided into training and validation sets (7:3). Feature selection was performed using elastic net and Boruta. Six machine learning models were developed and compared, with SHAP used for interpretability. The optimal model was used to build a web-based prediction tool. Associations and interaction effects between sarcopenia and staghorn stones were further assessed.ResultsA total of 755 patients were included, with a SIRS incidence of 17.62%. XGBoost achieved the best performance (validation set: AUC = 0.863, accuracy = 0.863, sensitivity = 0.763, specificity = 0.883, F1 score = 0.652). SHAP analysis identified staghorn stones and sarcopenia as the most important predictors. Sarcopenia was positively associated with staghorn stones. A significant synergistic effect on SIRS was observed, confirmed by both multiplicative interaction (OR = 4.229, 95% CI 1.354–14.432, P = 0.016) and additive interaction (RERI = 25.473, AP = 0.854, S = 8.600).ConclusionThe XGBoost model provides robust prediction of postoperative SIRS after PCNL, and the web-based tool may assist in risk stratification. Sarcopenia and staghorn stones are positively associated, and their coexistence is linked to a higher risk of SIRS with a potential positive interaction, highlighting the need for individualized perioperative management.

Introduction

Kidney stones are a common and recurrent urological disease, with a lifetime risk of up to 20%, and a higher burden in economically developed regions, influenced by climate, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle factors [1, 2]. Patients remain at substantial risk of recurrence after initial stone formation, with nearly half experiencing recurrence within 5 years and cumulative rates reaching up to 80% at 10 years [3]. By 2030, healthcare expenditures related to urolithiasis in the United States alone are projected to reach approximately USD 4.57 billion, underscoring its considerable economic burden and clinical impact due to complications such as renal colic and urinary tract infection [4, 5]. Based on clinical indications and surgeon experience, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), owing to its high stone-free rates and effectiveness across different stone compositions, remains the preferred treatment for large renal stones (> 2 cm) [6–8]. However, compared with other minimally invasive approaches such as retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), PCNL is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, particularly bleeding and infection [9, 10]. Among infection-related complications, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) is of particular concern, as patients who develop SIRS may further progress to sepsis, with reported rates of approximately 0.9–9.3% [11]. Sepsis remains a leading cause of perioperative mortality after PCNL, with mortality rates reaching up to 42% in the absence of timely intervention [12].

Sarcopenia, first described by Rosenberg in 1989, is a skeletal muscle disease characterized by progressive loss of muscle mass and function and is associated with adverse outcomes, including falls, frailty, and increased mortality [13, 14]. Compared with conventional nutrition-related laboratory markers such as serum albumin, imaging-derived evaluation of sarcopenia captures structural alterations in skeletal muscle and reflects long-term physiological reserve, thereby contributing to early perioperative risk assessment [15, 16]. A population-based analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) identified a positive association between sarcopenia and nephrolithiasis [17]. In line with these findings, He et al. further demonstrated that neutrophils partially mediated this association, implicating inflammation as a potential underlying mechanism [18]. In addition, An et al. reported that sarcopenia was associated with adverse outcomes among patients presenting to the emergency department with urinary tract infections [19]. To the best of our knowledge, the relationship between sarcopenia and postoperative SIRS following PCNL has not yet been explored. Moreover, staghorn calculi, a traditional predictor of post-PCNL SIRS, warrant further investigation regarding their potential interplay with sarcopenia.

Therefore, early identification and timely intervention for SIRS are essential to reduce sepsis-related morbidity and mortality after PCNL. Accordingly, this study sought to apply machine learning approaches to improve preoperative risk stratification for post-PCNL SIRS and to explore the association between sarcopenia and staghorn stones, as well as their potential combined effect on postoperative inflammatory risk.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted in four stages: patient inclusion and variable selection; development and performance evaluation of multiple predictive models; construction of a web-based prediction tool based on the optimal model; and evaluation of the association between sarcopenia and staghorn calculi and their combined effects on the risk of postoperative SIRS.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: SL-II2026-103-01). We retrospectively reviewed 755 eligible patients who underwent PCNL at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 2021 and August 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) renal calculi confirmed by preoperative computed tomography (CT); and (2) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of a preoperative indwelling nephrostomy tube; (2) presence of SIRS prior to surgery; (3) incomplete clinical data; and (4) absence of a preoperative CT scan performed within 1 month before surgery. Patients were randomly allocated to a training set (n = 529) and a validation set (n = 226) in a 7:3 ratio using R software. The study workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected from electronic medical records and categorized into clinical, laboratory, and imaging domains. Clinical variables included demographic characteristics and major comorbidities. Laboratory assessments comprised routine preoperative blood biochemistry and urine examinations, including urine culture. Imaging variables were extracted from preoperative non-contrast CT scans. Renal parenchymal thickness, stone density measured in Hounsfield units (HU) at the maximal cross-sectional area, and stone location were assessed using our previously described measurement method [20]. Stone burden was defined as the maximum diameter of the largest stone. Preoperative hydronephrosis was graded as none, mild, moderate, or severe based on the extent of pelvicalyceal dilation and renal parenchymal atrophy.

The skeletal muscle index at the third lumbar vertebra (L3-SMI) is widely used to assess skeletal muscle mass. Preoperative non-contrast axial CT images at the L3 level were opportunistically retrieved from the hospital Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. An open-source software developed by Paris et al. was then used for automated segmentation and quantitative analysis of body composition based on predefined HU thresholds, including skeletal muscle (psoas, erector spinae, and abdominal wall muscles; −29 to + 150 HU), visceral adipose tissue (− 150 to − 50 HU), and subcutaneous and intermuscular adipose tissue (− 190 to − 30 HU), with cross-sectional area (CSA, cm²) calculated for each tissue component (Fig. 2) [21].

Definitions

Staghorn calculi were defined as renal stones involving the renal pelvis with extension into at least two calyces [22]. Remnant cholesterol (RC) was calculated as total cholesterol minus high-density and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was diagnosed according to the criteria of the Chinese Diabetes Society [23], requiring the presence of at least three of four components related to obesity, glucose metabolism, blood pressure, and lipid abnormalities. L3-SMI was calculated as the skeletal muscle area (cm²) divided by height squared (m²). In this study, CT-defined sarcopenia was identified using L3-SMI cut-off values recommended for Asian populations, based on criteria proposed by the Japan Society of Hepatology: L3-SMI < 42 cm²/m² for men and < 38 cm²/m² for women [24]. SIRS was defined by the presence of two or more of the following criteria: (1) respiratory rate > 20 breaths per minute; (2) heart rate > 90 beats per minute; (3) abnormal white blood cell count (> 12 × 10⁹/L or < 4 × 10⁹/L); and (4) body temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C.

Perioperative management and surgical procedure

For patients with a positive preoperative midstream urine culture, antibiotics were selected based on susceptibility testing and administered for about three days before surgery. When culture results were negative, a single intravenous dose of cefuroxime was given approximately 30 min prior to the procedure for prophylaxis [25].

All patients underwent standardized single-tract PCNL. After induction of anesthesia, a 5 F ureteral catheter was retrogradely placed on the affected side in the lithotomy position to create artificial hydronephrosis, and the patient was then turned to the prone position. Under ultrasound guidance, percutaneous access was routinely established through the posterior middle calyx group. After urine efflux was confirmed, the tract was sequentially dilated to 18 F, and an 18 F peel-away sheath was inserted. Stones were fragmented using a holmium laser under nephroscopic visualization, and fragments were flushed out or retrieved with forceps. At the end of the procedure, a 6 F double-J stent and a 16 F nephrostomy tube were routinely placed. After surgery, the nephrostomy tube was typically removed on postoperative day 2 or 3 according to the patient’s recovery and drainage status. The double-J ureteral stent was subsequently removed 3–4 weeks later.

Feature selection

Forty-two candidate variables were extracted from preoperative clinical data, and all feature selection procedures were conducted exclusively within the training cohort. A dual-method strategy combining regression-based elastic net regularization and the random forest–based Boruta algorithm was applied. Only variables identified as important by both methods were retained to ensure consistency of feature selection. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were subsequently calculated to assess and control multicollinearity, resulting in the final set of variables included in model development.

Data preprocessing, model development, and evaluation

Given the low incidence of SIRS after PCNL, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to address class imbalance during model development and was implemented exclusively within the training cohort to prevent data leakage. Six machine learning models were subsequently developed and compared, including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), naïve Bayes (NB), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation conducted within the training set to mitigate overfitting.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for all six models, and the area under the curve (AUC) was compared to assess their discriminative ability for predicting postoperative SIRS. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score were additionally calculated to provide a comprehensive evaluation of model performance, and the model with the best overall performance was selected as the final prediction model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were subsequently used to evaluate the clinical net benefit and calibration performance of the final model. Model interpretability was further assessed using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), which quantified and visualized the relative contribution of each feature to the model predictions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression analyses were used to examine the association between sarcopenia and staghorn stones using three sequential models: an unadjusted model (Model 1), a model adjusted for age and sex (Model 2), and a fully adjusted model further controlling for MetS, serum creatinine, Uric acid (UA), urine culture, and SII (Model 3). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were incorporated into the multivariable logistic regression model (Model 3) to assess the dose–response relationship between L3-SMI and the risk of staghorn stone formation.

To evaluate the combined effects of sarcopenia and staghorn stones on postoperative SIRS, both multiplicative and additive interactions were assessed using logistic regression. Multiplicative interaction was examined by including an interaction term, whereas additive interaction was quantified using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (S). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 755 eligible patients were included, of whom 455 (60.26%) were male. The median age was 54 years (interquartile range [IQR], 44–61 years), and postoperative systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) occurred in 133 patients (17.62%). Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 529) and a validation set (n = 226) at a 7:3 ratio, with no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two cohorts (Table 1).

Feature selection

To identify predictors of postoperative SIRS, a dual-algorithm feature selection strategy was employed (Fig. 3). An elastic net model first selected nine predictors from 42 variables using the optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.0687, 1-SE criterion) (Fig. 3A–C), while the Boruta algorithm identified 23 important variables in parallel (Fig. 3D). Eight variables consistently selected by both methods were retained for further analysis: Serum creatinine, MetS, Neutrophil count (N), Urinary nitrite, Sarcopenia, SII, Staghorn stones, and Urine culture (Fig. 3E). All retained variables showed variance inflation factors (VIFs) < 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity (Fig. 3F).

Model development and evaluation

Before class balancing, the training cohort included 95 patients with postoperative SIRS and 434 without SIRS. SMOTE was applied to the training set to balance class distribution, while the validation set (n = 226) retained the original class proportions for unbiased evaluation. The SHAP summary plot visualizes the contribution of each feature at the individual level, with each point representing a single patient and its horizontal position reflecting the corresponding SHAP value. Interaction between sarcopenia and staghorn stones on postoperative SIRS

As shown in Table 4, the subgroups without either condition, with sarcopenia only, with staghorn stones only, and with both conditions included 417, 46, 224, and 68 patients, respectively; the corresponding SIRS events were 38 (9.1%), 7 (15.2%), 46 (20.5%), and 42 (61.8%). After multivariable adjustment (Model 3), patients with concomitant sarcopenia and staghorn stones had a substantially higher risk of postoperative SIRS than those without either condition (aOR = 29.825, 95% CI 14.141–65.402, P < 0.001). A significant multiplicative interaction between sarcopenia and staghorn stones on postoperative SIRS was observed (Model 3: aOR = 4.229, 95% CI 1.354–14.432, P = 0.016). Additive interaction analyses further suggested a positive interaction with respect to postoperative SIRS. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) was 25.473 (95% CI 10.636–74.699), suggesting that the joint effect exceeded the sum of individual effects. The attributable proportion (AP) was 0.854 (95% CI 0.698–0.944), indicating that approximately 85.4% of the SIRS risk among patients with both conditions was attributable to their interaction. Consistently, the synergy index (S) was 8.600 (95% CI 3.833–29.616), supporting a pronounced positive synergistic interaction.

Discussion

PCNL, first introduced by Fernström et al. [26], has become the first-line surgical treatment for large or complex renal calculi. Nevertheless, infection-related complications continue to pose substantial clinical challenges. In particular, postoperative SIRS represents an early but critical stage in the progression toward severe infectious outcomes [11, 27]. Previous studies have reported post-PCNL SIRS rates ranging from 10% to 35% [28, 29]. Consistent with these findings, the incidence of postoperative SIRS in our cohort was 17.62% (133/755). In this context, our study applied explainable machine learning approaches to develop a predictive framework for post-PCNL SIRS. Among the six evaluated models, XGBoost demonstrated superior and stable performance, and SHAP-based interpretation highlighted staghorn stones and sarcopenia as the most influential contributors to model predictions. These findings suggest that both anatomical stone complexity and host-related physiological vulnerability play central roles in shaping postoperative inflammatory risk. To enhance clinical applicability, the final model was translated into a web-based tool, enabling individualized risk estimation using routinely available preoperative variables. Beyond prediction, our study also demonstrated a robust positive association between sarcopenia and the presence of staghorn stones. Importantly, we identified a significant synergistic effect of sarcopenia and staghorn stones on the risk of postoperative SIRS following PCNL. This combined effect exceeded the expected impact of each condition alone and remained robust after adjustment for multiple potential confounding factors.

In this study, staghorn stones were identified as the factor most strongly associated with the occurrence of postoperative SIRS. This association may be attributable to two main aspects. First, infection-related stones represent the most common etiology of staghorn stone formation, and stone fragmentation during PCNL may facilitate the translocation of bacteria into the bloodstream, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative infection-related complications [30]. Second, previous studies have shown that patients with staghorn stones generally require longer operative times than those with non-staghorn stones, resulting in prolonged periods of elevated intrarenal pressure, which may further increase the risk of bacteremia [31]. In addition, we also observed that preoperative infection-related factors, including positive urine culture results, positive urinary nitrite, N, and SII, were associated with the occurrence of postoperative SIRS. These findings are consistent with previous reports and further support the role of preoperative infectious and inflammatory burden in the development of SIRS after PCNL [12, 32, 33].

Previous studies have suggested a potential link between sarcopenia and infection-related outcomes. A cohort study by Yu et al. reported that sarcopenia was associated with prolonged hospitalization due to infectious causes [34]. In addition, a study by Kino et al. focusing on patients with upper urinary tract stones (UUTS) showed that, in univariate analysis, patients with preoperative urosepsis had lower skeletal muscle mass compared with those without urosepsis; however, this association did not remain significant in multivariable analysis [35]. More recently, a study enrolling diabetic patients with UUTS found that sarcopenia was an independent risk factor for urosepsis among non-surgically treated patients [36]. Notably, the outcomes examined in these studies were limited to the occurrence of urosepsis in the preoperative period. In contrast, by incorporating opportunistic CT-based assessment, we investigated the impact of sarcopenia on postoperative outcomes following PCNL. Our results showed that sarcopenia was an important risk factor for post-PCNL SIRS, with a predictive contribution second only to staghorn stones in the model. Although no previous studies have directly established an association between sarcopenia and post-PCNL SIRS, this finding may be explained by several potential mechanisms. Beyond its role in locomotor function, skeletal muscle plays a crucial role in immune regulation by maintaining immune homeostasis through multiple molecular signaling pathways and interactions with immune cells. As muscle mass and function decline, this regulatory network becomes progressively impaired, rendering individuals with sarcopenia more likely to exhibit chronic low-grade inflammation and immune dysfunction [37, 38]. Consequently, under perioperative stress and potential bacterial burden, patients with sarcopenia may be more susceptible to an exaggerated inflammatory response, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative SIRS.

Interestingly, the prevalence of staghorn stones was significantly higher in patients with sarcopenia than in those without sarcopenia (59.65% vs. 34.95%, P < 0.001). Consistently, RCS analysis indicated an inverse, nonlinear association between L3-SMI and the likelihood of staghorn stone formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that MetS is positively associated with kidney stone formation and is also linked to an increased risk of post-PCNL SIRS [39, 40]. In light of these findings, and given that MetS ranked as the third most influential feature in our prediction model, MetS and other relevant covariates were included in the fully adjusted analysis (Model 3). After full adjustment, sarcopenia remained significantly associated with staghorn stone formation in the overall population (aOR = 2.321, P < 0.001). Age-stratified analyses showed a significant association among individuals aged ≥ 40 years, whereas the association did not reach statistical significance in those aged < 40 years. In the < 40-year subgroup, the estimated odds ratio remained above unity and was directionally consistent with that in the overall cohort; however, the limited number of patients with concurrent sarcopenia and staghorn stones (4/136, 2.94%) likely reduced the precision of the subgroup estimate and the power to detect an association. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance in younger patients should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect limited sample size, although a weaker or null association in this subgroup cannot be excluded. In addition, staghorn stone formation may reflect cumulative exposures such as long-standing or recurrent infections, and sarcopenia is more prevalent with advancing age; together, these factors may contribute to the more readily detectable association among older individuals.

In multivariable logistic regression analyses using patients without sarcopenia and staghorn stones as the reference group, those with concomitant sarcopenia and staghorn stones exhibited markedly increased odds of postoperative SIRS (aOR = 29.825, P < 0.001). In contrast, staghorn stones alone were associated with a 3.006-fold higher odds of postoperative SIRS (P < 0.001), whereas sarcopenia alone showed a non-significant trend toward increased odds (aOR = 2.346, P = 0.101). Compared with either exposure alone, the pronounced escalation in odds under joint exposure suggests a potential synergistic effect between sarcopenia and staghorn stones. Interaction analyses further supported this interpretation, demonstrating a significant multiplicative interaction between sarcopenia and staghorn stones with respect to postoperative SIRS (Model 1: cOR = 3.492, 95% CI 1.269–10.548, P = 0.020; Model 2: aOR = 3.451, 95% CI 1.247–10.480, P = 0.021; Model 3: aOR = 4.229, 95% CI 1.354–14.432, P = 0.016). Measures of additive interaction likewise supported the presence of a positive interaction. Sarcopenia is recognized as a systemic vulnerability state characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, immune dysregulation, and reduced physiological reserve. Elevated circulating inflammatory markers, including CRP and IL-6, have been reported in patients with sarcopenia [41], reflecting a persistent subclinical inflammatory milieu that may lower the threshold for exaggerated inflammatory responses following surgical stress. Experimental studies further highlight the close interplay between muscle metabolism and immune regulation; for example, Song et al. showed that γ-aminobutyric acid supplementation in female mice attenuated muscle protein degradation while suppressing macrophage-derived proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [42]. Collectively, these findings suggest that sarcopenia reflects impaired immunometabolic homeostasis, predisposing patients to excessive postoperative inflammatory responses. Conversely, staghorn stones are typically infection-related calculi, frequently associated with long-standing urinary obstruction, persistent bacterial colonization, and recurrent urinary tract infections, resulting in a high bacterial burden and sustained inflammatory stimulation [30, 31]. The coexistence of sarcopenia and staghorn stones may therefore represent a “second-hit” scenario, in which compromised host immune and metabolic reserves are superimposed on intense infectious and inflammatory insults, synergistically amplifying postoperative inflammatory cascades and substantially increasing the risk of SIRS.

Although this study is, to our knowledge, the first to apply machine learning approaches in a PCNL population to evaluate the association between sarcopenia and postoperative SIRS, and to demonstrate a positive correlation between sarcopenia and staghorn stones as well as a further increase in SIRS risk when these two conditions coexist, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because the model was developed and internally validated using data from a single-center retrospective cohort, its generalizability remains limited. Therefore, external validation in multicenter cohorts and, if necessary, model recalibration are required before routine clinical implementation. Second, owing to its retrospective design and the limited availability of clinical data, postoperative SIRS rather than sepsis was selected as the primary outcome, precluding further evaluation of associations with more severe infectious complications. Moreover, due to the lack of functional data in routine clinical records, sarcopenia in this study was primarily assessed based on the preoperative CT-derived L3-SMI, without evaluation of muscle strength or physical performance. Third, the absence of renal pelvic urine cultures or stone culture results limited a more detailed assessment of stone-related bacterial burden. Fourth, the proportion of patients with concomitant sarcopenia and staghorn stones was relatively low in the overall cohort (9.01%, 68/755), and particularly small among patients aged < 40 years (2.94%, 4/136). This imbalance may have reduced the statistical power of subgroup and interaction analyses, leading to wider confidence intervals and limiting the interpretability of subgroup-specific estimates in younger patients. Therefore, the observed interaction should be interpreted cautiously and requires further validation in larger prospective cohorts. Finally, owing to constraints in routine preoperative testing, certain inflammatory biomarkers, such as procalcitonin, were not available, potentially limiting a more comprehensive evaluation of inflammatory status.

Conclusion

This study compared the reliability of six machine learning algorithms for predicting the occurrence of postoperative SIRS following PCNL and, based on the optimal XGBoost model, developed a web-based prediction platform to facilitate clinical use. In addition, we identified a positive association between sarcopenia and staghorn stone formation and demonstrated that their coexistence was associated with a synergistically increased risk of postoperative SIRS. These findings suggest that sarcopenia and staghorn stones should be considered joint risk markers in clinical practice, and that intensified perioperative management may be warranted for patients presenting with both conditions to reduce the incidence of systemic inflammatory complications.
