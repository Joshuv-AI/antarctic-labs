Classifying electric service disruptions under deep uncertainty: A comparative analysis of algorithms and their policy outcomes
Authors: Akua Adeneke McLeod, Amritanshu Pandey, Destenie Nock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs machine learning finds increasing applications in energy policy, it is critical to evaluate model accuracy alongside interpretability and equity. We leverage classification of electricity disruptions within residential advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) data as a testbed for evaluating four models: logistic regression, a support vector machine (SVM), a histogram-based gradient boosting (HGB), and a neural network. We find that the HGB approach consistently outperforms logistic regression and marginally outperforms the SVM and the neural network across multiple evaluation metrics. Despite similar accuracy across models, derived duration and frequency metrics show that models prioritize different communities for investment, reflecting the challenges of decision-making under deep uncertainty. Depending on the reliability metric, up to 36 communities (over 100,000 households) are sensitive to the choice of classifier. These results highlight how the investment pathways for outage mitigation vary with algorithmic choice, emphasizing the need for both interpretable and equitable models.

Introduction

Machine learning increasingly finds applications in public policy, calling attention to the design of algorithms and their impact on stakeholders. Predictive algorithms and classifiers can offer distinct analytical advantages when used to reveal patterns and relationships within big data, ultimately serving to guide policy design, enhance decision making, and increase efficiency in resource allocation.1,2 As a result, these tools are employed in both decision-making and decision support roles with greater frequency,1 both for purposes of predicting the probability of constituent behavior, and tracking demographic features for targeted policy interventions.3 Application of machine learning now extends across policy domains, from predicting the likelihood of successful refugee integration,4 to detecting regions of poverty,5 to classifying regional food insecurity,6 and to assessing energy system reliability and access.

As machine learning becomes more prevalent in the public sector, these tools reach greater degrees of impact on communities and constituencies, and it becomes important to evaluate their algorithmic limitations. A growing body of research highlights the need for model validation to extend beyond assessments of predictive accuracy to further investigate model transparency, interpretability, and equity.1,7,8,9 Suresh and Guttag identify opportunities for harm during all three major stages of modeling, through data collection, design, and model application.10 Complex algorithms can characterize multi-faceted and nonlinear relationships within data, but perpetuate damaging outcomes with respect to equity,7,8 or offer limited resources to policy-makers who must explain the rules of their decision-making process, and the criteria that guide allocation of resources.2,3 Several studies reveal how algorithms can perpetuate bias and discriminate against the historically marginalized.7,8 Additionally, the “black-box” nature of some models used for decision-making directly contradicts “the right to an explanation” held by people subject to these decisions.11 However, much of this work has focused on prediction tasks, such as using algorithms to forecast behaviors or target interventions. In the current literature, less attention has been paid to event classification problems, where algorithms are used to measure and quantify energy hardships. In these cases, the choice of model affects not only accuracy but also how problems in the energy space are defined and understood.

There are growing efforts to address algorithmic harm and improve model interpretability, either during the model development process or through methods applied after the fact. Efforts to address model interpretability have primarily been theoretical, and Amarasinghe et al. (2023) identify a gap between the needs of policymakers and stakeholders, and the definitions and expectations for interpretability prioritized by modelers.9 Work to improve algorithmic fairness has mostly focused on prediction-based policies that seek to identify ideal populations for regulatory efforts or targeted interventions.12 For example, Battison et al. (2024) ranks models based on their social welfare outcomes, linking false positives directly to wasted regulatory efforts and sub-optimal allocation of resources.3 Other studies focus on the design of hybrid classifiers that are resilient in the face of real-world class imbalances,13 or combination classifiers that leverage learnings from several models within the same classifier formulation.14 Beyond metrics for comparing model accuracy, or methods for selecting the most “parsimonious” model within a given algorithm formulation,15 there is limited guidance for choosing models for event-classification tasks with policy applications, where algorithms are used not for forecasting behavior, but for measurement and statistical analysis of events. Electricity service disruptions provide a critical example of this challenge. If disconnections are misclassified as outages, policymakers may underestimate the prevalence of energy poverty, while if outages are mistaken for disconnections, utilities and regulators may misdirect resilience funding.

In this paper, we use the classification of electricity disruptions in advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) data as a testbed for the analysis of algorithmic choice and the policy outcomes that result. We build on our previous work, leveraging logistic regression to disaggregate power outages from utility disconnections within electricity consumption data,16 providing a comparative analysis with several other classifiers from varying model families. Specifically, we classify each zero-consumption event within a residential electricity dataset as either a power outage (due to system failure or maintenance) or a utility disconnection (due to non-payment). We study different classification algorithms to evaluate their performance in terms of accuracy as well as in the context of stakeholder preferences for model features such as interpretability and equity.

Classifying disruptions in AMI data reflects the challenges of decision-making under deep uncertainty. Deep uncertainty arises when stakeholders cannot reach consensus on how a system operates, how likely different future states may be, or which outcomes should be prioritized.17,18 In the case of energy access, this manifests in three ways. First, there are intrinsic limits to predictability: Multiple algorithms can classify the same zero-consumption event differently, with no single model universally correct. This is indicative of structural model uncertainty, and in the case of disruption classification, can manifest as materially different spatiotemporal distributions of burden. Second, stakeholder priorities diverge: Utilities may emphasize predictive accuracy, regulators may require interpretability and transparency, and community advocates may focus on equity in identifying vulnerable households. Third, the system itself is dynamic and evolving, as climate extremes, electricity prices, and regulatory frameworks shift over time. Within this framework, there is an absence of ground truth data for model verification, as publicly available disruption datasets rarely have the spatiotemporal granularity to validate the distribution of classified disruptions.

Ultimately, this paper evaluates and compares algorithms for the classification of disruptions in AMI residential electricity consumption data, with the goal of choosing the superior classifier and quantifying how policy outcomes vary with model choice. Our first research question asks: Which algorithm has superior classification performance and accuracy in distinguishing between outages and disconnections in AMI zero-consumption events? To answer this question, we compare classifiers from four families of algorithms: generalized linear models, kernel methods, deep learning approaches, and tree-based ensembles. For this analysis, we choose a logistic regression model, a neural network, a support vector machine (SVM), and a histogram-based gradient boosting (HGB) method for comparison.

Next, to understand how the choice of model may influence next steps in terms of investment, we ask: How does algorithmic choice impact resultant policy pathways? To this end, we evaluate the outage and disconnection burden metrics that result from each of the four models and evaluate their variability across the service territory. Rather than seeking a single optimal classifier, our analysis is motivated by the need to understand how different models perform and how their results shape policy decisions. Comparing across algorithmic paradigms, we evaluate both classification accuracy and the robustness of policy-relevant outcomes, including which communities are identified as most burdened. By highlighting when models agree and disagree, we assess how different classifiers could change policy conclusions about energy insecurity and equity.

Background

Residential electricity service disruptions are happening more frequently, just as extreme weather worsens, and residential heating and cooling needs rise in tandem with extreme weather. In the U.S., power outage frequency increased by roughly 27% in 2019–2023, relative to the five years prior, and the average power outage duration increased by roughly 20%.19 Nationally, the number of utility disconnections due to non-payment has also increased. A recent analysis of the 30 states with readily available utility disconnection data found a 29% increase in utility disconnections during the first 10 months of 2022, compared to the same period in 2021.20 Research continues to demonstrate that power loss is damaging to household wellbeing and health,21,22 and that the distribution of both power outages and utility disconnections is unequal across demographics.23,24,25 As global temperatures rise and electricity prices increase, these disparities are likely to widen, unless stakeholders pursue targeted interventions to ensure equitable electricity access.

Power outages and utility disconnections each require a different set of interventions, improving power system infrastructure and electricity affordability, respectively. Given their unique causes and solutions, tracking both types of service disruption separately is important. However, longitudinal analysis of the frequency and duration of these disruptions, and their distribution across demographics, is limited by a lack of data with sufficient spatiotemporal granularity. Many studies use county-level data from state regulatory or emergency management entities, or from the third-party data provider PowerOutage.US,26,27,28,29,30 which often relies on web-scrapings of utility customer-facing outage maps. These studies note distributional inequities but are often unable to evaluate the degree to which these patterns extend to smaller sub-geographies, such as ZIP codes or census tracts. With COVID-disconnection reporting requirements expiring,31 and federal support for outage data collection decreasing, analysis of both disruption types is becoming increasingly constrained.32 We can address this data gap, however, by leveraging machine learning classification algorithms to classify disruptions in electricity service in AMI data.

Existing literature on detecting electricity service disruptions has primarily focused on outage prediction and impact assessments for improved utility response and disaster management. Earlier efforts leveraged statistical modeling to forecast “post-storm restoration timelines” and facilitate advanced communication of weather event impact.33 More recent analysis has leveraged predictive machine learning, such as regression trees, in combination with historical outage data, utility infrastructure documentation, environmental data, and weather modeling results, to predict the spatial scope and distribution of outages,34 as well as the durations of outage events.35,36 These studies function in service of utility disaster management and thus typically benefit from confidential, utility-provided, historic outage data and proprietary electricity infrastructure data. Additionally, most studies focus on outage prediction during extreme weather events and do not further classify service disruptions that occur independent of large storms and atmospheric occurrences.

In this analysis, we focus on classifying disruptions that have already occurred, leveraging energy consumption data that is publicly available. This work supports third-party analysis of both electric service reliability and the distribution of utility disconnections across demographics.

Results

While the four models perform comparably in terms of outage F1 score and outage recall (all scores above 0.96 for both metrics), there is a notable difference in performance in the disconnection F1 score. The neural network, the SVM, and the HGB approach more accurately classify disconnections, with disconnection F1 scores of 0.99, 0.95, and 1.00, respectively. The logistic regression model’s lower disconnection F1 score is driven by its comparatively lower disconnection recall, which indicates that the logistic regression accurately identifies 78% of the true disconnections in the test set, compared to the neural network and HGB’s near 100%, and the SVM’s 97% disconnection detection rate. This suggests that relying on logistic regression alone risks systematically under-detecting disconnections. Further, the SVM (κ = 0.99), neural network (κ = 1.00) and HGB (κ = 1.00) have Cohen’s Kappa scores that are significantly higher than that of the logistic regression classifier (κ = 0.76), indicating the former models classify outages and disconnections more accurately, even adjusted for agreement between predicted and true labels by chance.

While the HGB has the best performance across all metrics, it performs only marginally better than the neural network. Results for this experimentation across all metrics are documented in supplementary info, Table S4. Overall, our findings show that the non-linear models form the Rashomon set, within which algorithmic choice matters less for overall accuracy than for which communities are prioritized for mitigation efforts. Because these three models are essentially equivalent in performance, non-technical factors (equity, interpretability, stakeholder trust) should guide which model policymakers adopt. With respect to interpretability, the SVM is slightly more interpretable because the support vectors shed light on feature importance and model complexity, while the neural network functions more as a black box. However, the HGB is the most interpretable of the three, due to its tree-based structure.

Model agreement

In this section, we compare model agreement, quantifying differences in regions prioritized by each classifier for targeted investment. We seek to investigate the importance of algorithmic choice to the ultimate policy outcome. In other words, we analyze the degree to which model choice influences which ZCTAs are categorized as having the highest outage or disconnection burden. Using the Jaccard coefficient to evaluate model agreement, we see that the logistic regression and neural network have the greatest agreement overall (87.5% of classifications), agreeing on 64.7% of the observations classified as disconnections, and 81.6% of the observations classified as outages (Figure 3). The SVM and the histogram-based boosting approach have the lowest overall agreement, agreeing on 71.5% of classifications, 46.5% of observations classified as disconnections, and 62.8% of observations classified as outages.Figure 3Jaccard similarity as a percentage of agreement between each model pair, by disruption type

Jaccard similarity as a percentage of agreement between each model pair, by disruption type

While the Jaccard coefficient is a useful tool for measuring model concurrence, it is necessary to have a more granular understanding of whether these models prioritize regions differently. This requires a comparison of model rankings. To do this, we derive outage duration and frequency metrics from each model’s classification outcomes, and evaluate the Spearman rank correlation and calculate Kendall’s tau for each pair of models (Figure 4). Figures S6 and S7 document the distributions of the derived reliability metrics for each classifier. The high Spearman correlation scores for both SAIDI and SAIFI indicate significant agreement in relative rankings across models (i.e., in the positions of each ZCTA in one model ranking relative to another), with the highest correlation occurring between the SVM and the neural network models (ρ = 0.98 for average SAIDI, ρ = 0.97 for average SAIFI). In other words, all four models primarily agree on the relative ordering of the ZCTAs both for outage duration and frequency scores. When we look at Kendall’s tau, there is more notable variation. Kendall’s tau is more sensitive to smaller variations in the ranking, and we see lower scores across both duration and frequency metrics, specifically for the logistic regression and HGB comparisons.Figure 4Spearman’s and Kendall coefficients between model pairs for the outage SAIDI and SAIFI scores

Looking at the intersection of ZCTAs that all four models agree belong in the top 20% most burdened by SAIDI, we find 56 ZCTAs. For 36 ZCTAs, the models diverge on whether the ZCTA would or would not be classified among the most-burdened by hours without power. A summation of the number of accounts for these 36 ZCTAs indicates that 108,117 households are classification sensitive with respect to outage duration. More broadly, this means that, despite the high level of agreement, as many as 108,117 could either gain or lose access to targeted resilience funding depending on the algorithm chosen.

There is significantly higher agreement about which ZCTAs are most and least burdened by frequent outages, as shown in Table 3. All four models agree on 62 ZCTAs. The models disagree on the membership of 23 ZCTAs in the category of most-burdened by high-frequency outages, corresponding to 36,532 households. However, it is important to note that the logistic regression and the SVM agree on 87.2% of the members of this most burdened group, across both outage metrics. Although our results indicate that all four models largely agree on ranking, we find that small shifts in top/bottom communities can lead to different policy outcomes and different distributions of benefits. Discussion

This paper has shown that the choice of algorithm for classifying zero-energy consumption events in AMI data has both technical and policy significance. Focusing first on model classification accuracy, our results suggest that relying on logistic regression alone risks less accurate classification, and more specifically, results in systematically under-detecting disconnections. While all four models we tested—logistic regression, neural networks, SVMs, and HGB—achieve strong performance and accuracy in detecting outages and disconnections (and distinguishing between the two), the gradient boosting approach consistently outperforms logistic regression and marginally outperforms the neural network and the SVM across multiple evaluation metrics, including F1 score and Cohen’s Kappa. However, all of the models have high accuracy scores (above 0.88). The similarity in performance between the neural network, SVM, and the HGB suggests the presence of a Rashomon set, where multiple models can achieve near-optimal accuracy. This indicates that other factors, such as interpretability, monotonicity, or transparency, can be used to guide model selection.

Model interpretability is an algorithmic property that is not quantified by traditional machine learning metrics.38 However, in policy contexts, interpretability becomes more than a desired feature, instead serving as a necessary component for equitable and transparent decision support.1,39 Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) define interpretability as a measure of whether a model can be presented in human-understandable terms, underscoring that interpretability provides the necessary foundation for the evaluation of other model features such as monotonicity, robustness, reliability, and fairness, and noting that in ML applications, tradeoffs often exist between these “desiderata.”38 Thus, interpretability is also deeply linked to the evaluation of model performance, as well as tradeoffs across several stakeholder pref.38

Our results demonstrate this tension. We identify a Rashomon set of nearly equivalent performing models, which underscores that model accuracy cannot be the only criterion informing algorithmic choice. In the presence of several well-performing models, decision makers should evaluate the differing impacts on downstream governance, and interpretability is a necessary feature for facilitating this analysis, as well as enabling stakeholder trust in ML-enabled decision support.37,39 Similarly, in cases of comparable model performance, Rudin (2019) urges a departure from black-box models such as neural networks, as well as associated post hoc explanations, in favor of inherently explainable models such as logistic regression classifiers.39

In addition to our accuracy analysis, comparing the models shows that algorithmic selection affects which regions are determined to be most affected by outages. Divergence appears when determining which ZCTAs are most impacted, despite the fact that rank correlations between models are typically strong. Up to 23 communities vary in their ranking as the most affected by high-frequency outages, corresponding to 36,532 households. Focusing on model rankings by average monthly SAIDI, we find that up to 36 communities vary in their ranking as the most affected by long-duration outages, corresponding to 108,117 classification-sensitive households.

These variations highlight the possible policy ramifications of methodological choices, since targeted energy insecurity relief efforts may prioritize different communities depending on the classifier used. This should be given particular consideration when thinking about the most underprivileged populations. Thus, to ensure that high-burdened areas are not overlooked or excluded from important energy aid and policy support, the most unbiased approach could be to identify target groups using several classifiers.

In order to extend this work, future research should determine whether a particular demographic group is disproportionately impacted by algorithmic divergence. More specifically, future research should connect model rankings of ZCTAs to sociodemographic characteristics such as age, race, and income.

Decision-making under deep uncertainty suggests that rather than seeking a single optimal model, policymakers should adopt robust approaches, for example, selecting classifiers from the Rashomon set based on stakeholder priorities, or applying ensemble methods to reduce regret across futures. Understanding trade-offs across different methodological approaches will be crucial for creating solutions that are both socially equitable and technically robust.

Limitations of the study

Data limitations form the primary constraint of this research. With respect to the labeling of AMI-derived disruption data, we are limited to labeling outages that occur in the absence of disconnections, and vice versa, to produce the labeled disruption dataset for supervised classification. This excludes from the training data outages and disconnections that happen concurrently. However, we assume the two classes of disruptions occur largely independently of one another, such that their concurrence is unlikely to materially alter the features used for classification. Additionally, the Bluefire Studios data may miss short-duration outages. However, because its temporal resolution (15-min intervals) is slightly more granular than that of the ComEd AMI data (30-min intervals), this is unlikely to result in noise within our labeled dataset.

As highlighted in McLeod et al. (2025), several features inherent to the ComEd AMI data constrain disruption measurement. By virtue of the 30-min temporal resolution, disruptions shorter than 30 min are not captured. Further, disruptions that begin or end within a 30-min measurement interval are truncated, as start and end times reflect the first and last full measurement intervals of zero electricity consumption, respectively.16 Finally, due to monthly account randomization, disruptions that straddle months are split into separate observations.16 In combination, these data features may result in slightly lower calculated SAIDI and SAIFI scores, and thus somewhat conservative reliability metrics.16 However, as we use the same training and testing data across models, we do not consider these limitations to be impactful for model comparison.

Finally, although we validate each model using out-of-sample data, the absence of ground truth data limits our ability to further validate the reliability metrics derived from classification outcomes. This data gap reflects a key source of deep uncertainty, and ultimately underscores the need for more granular reporting of outages and disconnections.

Method Details

In this section, we present our methods for data processing, classifier development, as well as comparative analysis of the classification algorithms and results. First, we outline our data-processing methodology, including data labeling, feature engineering, and development of the training, validation and test sets for model development and validation. Next, we discuss models from four different algorithmic paradigms that we use for classification of zero energy consumption events: a generalized linear model (logistic regression), a kernel-based method (support vector machine), a deep learning approach (neural network), and a tree-based ensemble method (histogram-based gradient boosting). After outlining the classification algorithms and model development process, we report the machine learning metrics we use to evaluate the accuracy of these models and validate their performance on out-of-sample data. Finally, we discuss our approach to measuring model ranking agreement to evaluate differences in which geographic regions are prioritized by each model.

AMI data processing

Data summary

For this analysis, we analyze Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) data for roughly 700,000 residential accounts in the Commonwealth Edison service territory in Northern Illinois in 2019. Each observation within this dataset is linked to a 9-digit ZIP code and records the daily electricity consumption registered by one electricity meter in 30-min intervals.49 The data is anonymized, and account IDs are reshuffled monthly to preserve consumer privacy.49 For further data privacy, the dataset includes only 9-digit ZIP codes that meet Illinois’ 15/15 data privacy rule, which requires both that 1 there are a minimum of 15 customer accounts within each ZIP code included in the data, and (2), that no one account represents 15% or more of the total electricity load within their respective ZIP code within the reporting time frame.49,50

We leverage the disruption dataset generated from this raw AMI data in McLeod et al. 2025. This dataset was produced by first isolating periods of zero electricity consumption through removal of all uninterrupted periods of electricity consumption within the time-series data for each account ID. Based on the AMI measurement interval, we define electricity disruptions as any period of 30 min or greater during which a household consumes no electricity.16 The start and stop intervals as well as the duration are calculated for each disruption by measuring individual and consecutive zeros, including sequences that span across daily observations.16 This produces a dataset of 15,649,746 individual disruptions.16 We provide a concise summary of this dataset in Supplementary Info (Table S1), including total disruption records, class-imbalance ratios, geographic coverage, and temporal range.

Data labeling

We next leverage auxiliary datasets to label a subset of this disruption dataset, using the AMI Data Labeling method outlined in McLeod et al. (2025). The AMI Data Labeling method employs two auxiliary datasets: (1) outage logs from Bluefire Studios PowerOutage.US database at the county-level, and (2) monthly disconnection records, at the five-digit ZIP code level, submitted by ComEd to the Illinois Commerce Commission (ICC) in response to a notice of inquiry. Using the ICC disconnection data, we isolate all disruptions that occur for month-ZIP pairs with no recorded disconnections. These we label as outages. Next, using the BlueFire studios outage data, we isolate all disruptions in the ComEd AMI disruption data that occur for county-date pairs with no recorded outages. Given that there are no recorded power outages for these county-date pairs, we label these AMI disruptions as disconnections. We see a small percentage of labeling overlap (1.18%); we consider these observations un-labelable and attribute this overlap to missing data or mismeasurement in the auxiliary datasets. This AMI Data Labeling method results in a labeled dataset of 251,819 outages and 48,587 disconnections that can be used for model development and validation.

To validate that the Bluefire studios data is an accurate summary of the outages in the service territory, we compared the timeseries of this data to that of the outage log submitted in ComEd’s 2019 Electric Reliability Report and Customer Satisfaction Survey.51 In Figure S1, we see the timeseries of outages recorded in the Bluefire Studios data for the ComEd service territory and outages reported by ComEd to the ICC in their 2019 Electric Reliability Report & Customer Satisfaction Survey.51 This figure indicates that the Bluefire Studios data is a useful proxy and sufficient for data labeling, with the sum of daily maximums (in green) closely resembling the ComEd ICC timeseries of maximum daily outaged customers (in black).

We calculated the total daily outages from the Bluefire Studios data in two ways: first by summing the total recorded customers affected for each day, and second by summing the maximum number of outaged customers recorded by county during that day. As indicated in this figure, the former approach inflates the number of outages – this is because ComEd uses staged restoration, and so individual timestamps in the Bluefire outage log may reflect duplicate customers, and thus simply summing double-counts customer accounts.

Feature engineering

Feature engineering follows the process outlined in McLeod et al. (2025) and facilitates comparative analysis across classification algorithms. Features were designed using domain knowledge to capture temporal, regional, meteorological, and socioeconomic elements associated with disruption events. Feature permutation importance is provided in Figures S3, S4, and S5, and feature rank across classification models is reported in Figure S4.

Energy consumption features

Energy consumption features capture the spatiotemporal distribution of disruption events, in consideration of differences in duration and frequency between outages and disconnections. We include features for the start and end dates of disruption features, as well their duration. Additional features were developed to capture the percentage of concurrent interruptions that occur on the same day and within the same ZIP code. These features were intended to facilitate model learning of patterns in disruption durations, as well as describe the greater spatial coverage of power outages, as compared to utility disconnections. However, as early experiments revealed that these features produced improbable classification ratios, features measuring disruption concurrence were removed from the final feature set.16

Regional features

Considering the frequent weather dependence of outages, as well as seasonal regulatory requirements governing utility disconnections, we also include regional meteorological and regulatory features. We add maximum and minimum daily temperature data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). Each ZIP code is paired with the closest 26 weather stations with recorded temperature data for 2019, and missing data is imputed using an average of temperature observations for the three days prior. Binary features for Illinois’ temperature-based and seasonal disconnection moratoria are developed from the Illinois Commerce Commission (83 Ill. Admin. Code § 280.130), leveraging day-ahead temperature from NOAA in the absence of historical forecasts.52 Finally, we include a feature that documents distance in days from the start of the seasonal moratorium, to capture utility disconnection behaviors in advance of the moratorium.

Energy insecurity features

Our energy insecurity features document monthly percentages of accounts in utility arrearage programs, accounts that have defaulted on those programs, and accounts that have been disconnected and remain disconnected through December 2019, for each five-digit ZIP code. These features are derived from data submitted by ComEd to the Illinois Commerce Commission, as part of a request for energy affordability data from investor-owned utilities.53

Multicollinearity and feature selection

We evaluate multicollinearity using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Evaluation of the Pearson coefficients highlighted four strongly correlated feature pairs (Pearson coefficients equal to or greater than 0.7). Figure S2 offers a heatmap of the Pearson coefficients across features. We also variance inflation factors across the feature space and considered features with VIF scores close to four for removal, that also belonged to a high-correlation feature pair.16 To select our final feature set, we leveraged these statistical diagnostics, alongside domain relevance (for example, prioritizing variables that better described operational mechanisms or policy context). This process enabled removal of redundant features and produced the final feature set (and corresponding VIF scores) included in Table S2.

Pre-processing and development

Next, we standardize our data such that each feature has unit variance, using Python’s SciKit-Learn StandardScaler. Datetime variables are converted to ordinal representations to facilitate use across classification algorithms. Finally, we split our labeled data into training (70%), validation (15%) and test (15%) sets, using SciKit-Learn StratifiedKFold to preserve class distribution. We train each of the classification models on the same feature set.

Classification algorithms

We evaluate classifiers from four algorithmic paradigms: generalized linear models, deep learning approaches, kernel-based methods, and tree-based ensembles. Each of these model families presents different strengths and limitations for binary classification and public policy applications. Specifically, we compare a logistic regression model, a support vector machine, a neural network, and a boosting-based ensemble tree learner. These four models span linear and non-linear algorithms, and facilitate comparison across single-model and ensemble-based learning, as well as parametric and nonparametric classification approaches. Together, these selected models provide a spectrum of algorithmic complexity, supporting evaluation of how both classification performance and downstream policy outcomes evolve when transitioning from linear, relatively interpretable models to more complex, nonlinear representations. In the following sections we document model formulation and development for each classifier. Hyperparameters and their selection methods for each classifier are provided in Table S3, and Brier score assessments of model calibration are reported in Table S5.

Logistic regression

We start with a generalized linear model due to its inherent explainability and interpretability. Miller et al. 2019 defines explainability in the context of artificial intelligence as modeling for which the “underlying causes” for decision-making are accessible to the human “explainee”.54 Linear models provide clear causes for decision making, by explicitly quantifying feature contributions to the classification outcome, and sparse linear models are thus often described as “intrinsically interpretable”.55 Thus, we choose logistic regression for its simplicity and explainability.56,57 Logistic regression is a probabilistic statistical method used for binary classification. Support vector machine

The support vector machine is especially useful for classification of datasets with non-linear decision boundaries.58 Support vector machines (SVMs) find the maximum margin hyperplane which maximizes separation between classes, defined by support vectors which are the observations with the shortest distance to the hyperplane.57,58 These models address nonlinearity by transforming the data with nonlinear kernels, such that the new representation of the data is linearly separable.58 To implement our classifier we use the support vector implementation in the Python Scikit-learn library and select a radial basic function kernel heuristically (Equation 4).47 Hsu et al. (2003) recommend the RBF as a strong first-choice kernel given its ability to learn nonlinear decision boundaries.59 We validate this choice using a simple, preliminary grid search cross validation to evaluate the performance of four kernels (linear, polynomial, RBF, and sigmoid) and find that the RBF kernel has the superior performance on our data.(Equation 4)K(xi,xj)=exp(−γ‖xi−x,‖2),γ=12σ2

We once again leverage grid search 5-fold cross validation for hyperparameter tuning on the model’s macro F1 score. This process selects the error penalty parameter (C = 1), as well as the gamma for the radial basis function (γ = 1). Class imbalance is addressed with balanced class weighting, which calculates class weights inversely proportional to the frequency of the respective class.60 While the use of a non-linear kernel reduces interpretability, the presence of support vectors and the geometry of the decision margin allow for a degree of model interpretation.57 Support vector machines can further be paired with post hoc explanatory methods to facilitate interpretability of feature importance.61

Neural network

From the category of deep learning algorithms, we select a neural network, specifically a multi-layer perceptron. Neural networks can capture nonlinear relationships within our disruption data that logistic regression models cannot. However, as with many deep learning approaches, neural networks function much like “black boxes,” offering accuracy gains and robust characterization of nonlinear boundaries in exchange for reduced model interpretability.39,55,62 Neural networks are characterized by layers of neurons, where each neuron applies a linear transformation to the input data, multiplying each observation in X by a vector of weights wi and adding bias b (Equation 4).(Equation 5)z=∑wixi+b

We implement a neural network using the PyTorch neural network module,63 with an input layer with our seven features, followed by two fully connected hidden layers containing 64 and 32 neurons, respectively. The number of neurons for each hidden layer, as well as the learning rate (lr = 0.001) are selected through grid search cross validation, and we use balanced class weighting (inversely proportional to the frequency of each class) to address class imbalance. Non-linearities are captured at each hidden layer through activation functions. Each hidden layer in our model uses a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, which outputs the maximum of the linear combination, z, and zero (Equation 5).64 We select the ReLU activation function for computational efficiency, and for its robustness against saturation for positive inputs.65(Equation 6)ReLU(z)=max⁡(0,z)

The cross entropy loss leverages a log-softmax transformation to calculate normalized class probabilities from the logits produced by the final layer. To calculate the difference between these probabilities and the ground truth training labels, we optimized the cross-entropy using the PyTorch implementation of the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−3, using mini-batches of size 32 for 100 epochs.

Histogram-based gradient boosting

We leverage a histogram-based gradient boosting approach with log-loss as an example of tree-based ensemble learning. This is aligned with the stage-wise optimization, additive logistic regression approach to boosting presented by Friedman et al. 1998.66 We select hyperparameters heuristically, opting for a conservative learning rate (lr = 0.05) to reduce overfitting.67 We select a moderate maximum tree depth of six, as ensemble learning with a maximum depth of five to eight trees supports nonlinear feature interactions without producing overfitting.67 Finally, we select a minimum leaf size of 20, corresponding to the literature which emphasizes the importance of large leaf sizes for variance reductions under log loss.67 As highlighted in our results section, default parameters yield exceptionally high classification performance. As such we do not pursue hyperparameter tuning during model development.

Quantification and statistical analyses

For feature engineering we calculate Pearson’s correlation coefficients for feature pairs, and the VIF score for each feature. Labeled data is split into training, validation, and testing sets using SciKit-Learn StratifiedKFold for class-wise stratification within splits.
