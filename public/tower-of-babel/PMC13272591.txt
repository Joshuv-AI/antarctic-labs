Europium-decorated carbon quantum dots for dual Cu2+/Fe3+ detection and live-cell bioimaging
Authors: Sonaimuthu Mohandoss, Lalitha Gnanasekaran, Rajkumar Sekar, J. Iyyappan, Mani Durai, Dharani Shanmugapriya, Kuppu Sakthi Velu, Naushad Ahmad, Prathap Somu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study demonstrated europium-decorated carbon quantum dots (Eu-CQDs), which were synthesized through a one-pot hydrothermal process using sucrose and melamine as the carbon and nitrogen sources and EuCl3 as the rare earth metal ion of Eu. The resulting blue-emissive Eu-CQDs were confirmed using spectroscopic and microscopic techniques, including UV-Vis absorption, fluorescence (FL), FT-IR, XRD, TEM, and XPS. The Eu-CQDs showed significant UV-Vis and FL emission at wavelengths of 320 and 424 nm, respectively, with a quantum yield (QY) of 33.6%. TEM analysis revealed well-dispersed spherical Eu-CQDs with an average size of 3.19 ± 0.21 nm. Remarkably, the Eu-CQDs served as an outstanding FL probe for the selective sensing of ferric ions (Fe3+) and copper ions (Cu2+) through FL dynamic quenching, especially photo-induced electron transfer (PET), while other metal ions produced minimal intensity changes. The detection limits were 65.6 nM for Cu2+ and 72.1 nM for Fe3+, with a linear relationship observed in the range of 0–20 µM. However, the dynamic quenched FL of Eu-CQDs/Cu2+ and Eu-CQDs/Fe3+ was swiftly enhanced by EDTA via a turn-on response. The practical use of the Eu-CQDs probe for quantifying the Cu2+ and Fe3+ ion concentrations was conducted with real water samples, and the findings indicated satisfactory recoveries (101.1% for Cu2+ and 104.8% for Fe3+) and good reproducibility, which aligned with the results obtained from UV-Vis spectroscopy. Additionally, the WST assay was assess the cytotoxic effects of Eu-CQDs, and their capability to image live HCT-116 (human colon cancer cell line) cells was assessed. These results indicate that the Eu-CQDs used for the nanoscale sensing of Fe3+ and Cu2+ metal ions are both selective and sensitive, and they can be successfully used for live cell imaging of cancer cell lines when used with this probe.

Introduction

The fluorescent carbon-based nanomaterials have garnered considerable attention in recent years because of their ability to address some of the limitations associated with traditional fluorescent dyes, including toxicity, hydrophobicity, and poor biocompatibility1. Moreover, carbon-based nanomaterials have unique advantages, such as nanosized dimensions (particularly less than 10 nm), high quantum yield (QY), flexibility, light weight, electrical conductivity, and thermal and photostability2. These properties are useful in various research areas and have been extensively applied in sensing and bioimaging3–5. Various studies have synthesized, characterized, and applied different types of carbon-based nanomaterials, including fullerenes, graphene, graphene oxide (GO), nanotubes, nanodiamonds, carbon fibers, and carbon quantum dots (CQDs)6–8. Among these, CQDs have attracted attention because of their specific chemical properties and versatility, particularly in the fields of drug delivery, bioimaging, catalysis, and electrochemical energy storage. Their highly tunable FL, which aids in advanced bioimaging and sensing as well as their biocompatibility, non-toxicity, hydrophilicity, and ease of surface modification, which are effective for drug delivery and wound dressing, have been highlighted. Xu et al. were the first to discover CQDs, particularly purifying single-walled carbon nanotubes9. Additionally, researchers have focused in various fields on developing and synthesizing different functional groups contained in CQDs using different precursors, and they have achieved different types of specific properties10. Top-down and bottom-up approaches, including refluxing, ultrasonication, carbonization, hydrothermal, solvothermal, thermal decomposition, electrochemical oxidation, arc discharge, and microwave treatments, are used to synthesize it11. Owing to its cost-effectiveness, low toxicity, and eco-friendliness, the hydrothermal method is a promising choice for researchers12. Moreover, the hydrothermal technique offers the additional advantages such as it allows for precise control over the reaction conditions, such as temperature, and the obtained CQDs have high quantum yields, reduced size (quantum effect), and less aggregation13.

Recently, CQDs synthesized and doped using the hydrothermal method have been applied in several research areas, including bioimaging, sensors, supercapacitors, and photocatalysis14. In the next section, studies have been developed on CQDs doped with different types of elements are discussed, which are essential for improving their core CQD properties and extending their applications15. This procedure entails the incorporation of specific components, such as heteroatoms like nitrogen (N), sulfur (S), and phosphorus (P), along with metal or metal-based nanoparticles like zinc oxide (ZnO), Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), and copper nanoparticles (CuNPs), into the core structure of CQDs16. The incorporation of dopants, such as heteroatoms and metals, modifies the electronic configurations, quantum yield, surface states, and optical characteristics, thereby enhancing the properties of CQDs in applications like catalysis, sensing, bioimaging, and electronic devices17,18. Recently, metal-ion-doped CQDs have gained significant interest owing to their great ability to tune electronic and optical properties and introduce new advanced functionalities compared with heteroatom doping19. This is because metal ions have several electrons and unoccupied orbitals compared to heteroatoms, which is beneficial for changing the charge density and reducing the energy level within the CQD core. These properties are used to improve the catalytic activity, electrical conductivity, and luminescence properties. Furthermore, co-doping with two or more metal ions or heteroatoms into the pristine CQDs core influences metal sensing, as it improves the modification of optical and electronic features, which can enhance the FL quenching for the detection of specific metal ions20,21. For example, Zhang et al. proposed that co-doing CQDs such as AgNPs with nitrogen into the CQDs core can lead to enhancing the surface area-to-volume ratio owing to the synergistic effects of both materials, providing more active sites suitable for sensor and photocatalyst applications22.

The CQDs doped with lanthanoid ions (Ln-CQDs) are a more precious area in recent 5 years of research because they offer more sensitive, selective detection of different ions (cations and anions) and biomolecules compared to metal-doped CQDs and heteroatom-doped CQDs23. Moreover, they exhibit the most intense sharp emission peaks because they are associated with several electrons, larger atomic radii, and partially filled f-orbitals24. In addition, they are less toxic than some transition metals, making them more suitable for biological and environmental applications25. Simultaneously, the co-doping of lanthanoids with the heteroatom incorporation of CQDs cores offers additional advantages because of the synergistic effect of nitrogen and lanthanoids, which can improve the sensing ability26,27. Furthermore, combining the heteroatoms of nitrogen and lanthanides provides more information through multimodal imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT) contrast27. Recent studies have highlighted various Ln-CQDs and their applications in sensing and bioimaging. In 2018, Yang et al. and Mondal et al. showcased lanthanum-doped CQDs and terbium-doped CQDs, which demonstrated high sensitivity and selectivity in detecting Fe3+ ions through a quenching mechanism28,29. Even though the majority of research based on the synthesis of Ln-CQDs has focused on the detection of Fe3+ and Cu2+ metal ions, bioimaging-based studies have been published in limited articles. Furthermore, as other metal ions such as Cu2+, Co2+, and Mn2+ are crucial for biological systems in the human body, their detection is essential. Therefore, we propose the use of Ln-CQDs as fluorescent probes.

Copper (Cu) and iron (Fe) ions play significant roles in biological systems because they are involved in many metabolic activities and physiological processes30. They can increase or decrease within the body, causing several health issues, including neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, anemia, and liver or kidney damage31. When maintained at the correct dosage level of both iron and copper, it prevents various health issues32. Moreover, the detection and quantification of metal ions are important for environmental observing, including pollution management and the maintenance of food safety33,34. Luminescent sensors are a remarkable technique owing to their excellent sensitivity and selectivity in detecting analytes35–40. Various researchers have developed materials based on CQDs to identify copper and iron by either diminishing or enhancing FL41–43. Despite these advances, most reports focus on a limited range of analytes, and studies on europium-doped CQDs (Eu-CQDs) for sensing applications remain scarce. In particular, the detection of environmentally and biologically relevant ions such as Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions using europium-decorated CQDs (Eu-CQDs) has not been well explored and the integration of sensing with real sample analysis and bioimaging is still limited. Europium was selected as the lanthanoid of choice because it offers numerous benefits, particularly its compatibility with biological systems and tunable optical characteristics, which make it an excellent candidate for bioimaging. Therefore, the objective of this work is to synthesize Eu-CQDs via a facile hydrothermal method and to evaluate their performance as a sensitive and selective fluorescent probe for Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions detection. The study further aims to assess their applicability in real water samples and their potential for bioimaging. This work provides a new approach for developing multifunctional lanthanide-doped CQD-based sensing platforms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the use of Eu-doped CQDs for the selective detection of both Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions, providing a new platform for multifunctional sensing and bioimaging applications.

This study involved the synthesis of europium-decorated carbon quantum dots (Eu-CQDs) using a single-step hydrothermal method with sucrose, melamine, and europium chloride as the starting materials. Europium decoration enhances the optical and chemical characteristics of core CQDs. The Er-CQDs were verified and examined using various analytical methods, including UV–Vis spectroscopy, PL analysis, FTIR, XRD, HRTEM, and XPS. The uniformly distributed quantum size of Eu-CQDs was confirmed by TEM studies, and the calculated size was 3.19 ± 0.21 nm. The UV results suggest that the dots emit blue light and the excitation/emission wavelengths are 320/424 nm, with a QY of 33.6%. Additionally, selectivity analysis demonstrated that the synthesized Eu-CQDs served as a fluorescent probe for detecting Copper (Cu2+) and Ferric (Fe3+) ions. (Scheme 1). The probe exhibited remarkable sensitivity and stability in detecting Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions in the 0–20 µM range, with detection limits of 65.6 nM for Fe3+ and 72.1 nM for Cu2+. From a practical application perspective, Eu-CQDs act as a probe for a range of real water samples, demonstrating exceptional stability and sensitivity in detecting both ions, with recovery rates of 101.1% for Cu2+ and 104.8% for Fe3+. The WST assay was used to assess the cytotoxic properties of Eu-CQDs and their ability to image live HCT-116 human colon cancer cells.

Experimental details

Chemicals and materials

Sucrose, melamine, and europium (III) chloride (EuCl3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Various metal salts in the nitrate and chloride forms, such as those of Ba2+, Cd2+, Ca2+, Al3+, Ag+, Cu2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Hg2+, Fe3+, Fe2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Pb2+, Sr2+, and Sn2+, were sourced from TCI Chemicals, South Korea. Stock solution (10− 5) of all metal ions and Eu-CQDs were prepared for selectivity studies. All reagents were obtained from TCL in analytical grade and used without any additional purification steps. Deionized (DI) water was used during the study.

Synthesis of Eu-CQDs

A simple hydrothermal method was used to synthesize Eu-CQDs44. Melamine (0.25 g) and sucrose (0.25 g) were first dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water. After complete dissolution, 10 mL of a 1 mM aqueous europium chloride (EuCl3) solution was introduced, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to ensure uniform mixing. The resulting solution was then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 160 °C for about 12 h. After cooling, the product was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to eliminate precipitated particles. The filtrate was subsequently purified using a dialysis membrane (1 kDa) to remove residual salt ions. Finally, the purified Eu-CQDs were collected by filtration, and the obtained solution was stored in the dark at 4 °C for subsequent characterization.

Characterization techniques

The absorption and emission characteristics of the Eu-CQDs were examined using UV-Vis spectroscopy within the 200–800 nm range (UV 3220 spectrometer (Optizen) and FL spectroscopy spanning 200–750 nm (F-2700 spectrophotometer; HITACHI). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to record the functional group spectra using a PerkinElmer FT-IR spectrophotometer (scan range: 400–4000 cm− 1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using an MRD model (PANalytic XPert Philips) with copper (Cu) Kα radiation), scanned from 10 ° to 80° at a speed of 5°. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; JEM-2100 instrument (JEOL) was conducted at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV to determine the morphology of the samples. The average particle size diameters were measured using ImageJ software (1.54j). Fifty particles were randomly selected for statistical analyses, and a bar graph was plotted using Origin software. The surface binding energies of the Eu-CQDs were identified using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) Multilab-2000 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an Al Kα monochromatized source using C 1s as the reference energy (EC1S = 284.6 eV).

Sample preparation

Before initiating the study on sensor selectivity, we adjusted the excitation and emission wavelengths of the Eu-CQDs and evaluated their stability by pH testing. Subsequently, the Eu-CQDs were dissolved in an aqueous solution at a 5 mg/mL, and different metal ions (Ba2+, Cd2+, Ca2+, Al3+, Ag+, Cu2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Hg2+, Fe3+, Fe2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Pb2+, Sr2+, and Sn2+) were added to the Eu-CQDs solution. The excitation wavelength was fixed at 320 nm, and the emission observed at 424 nm was analyzed using FL spectroscopy. The FL intensity decreased after the addition of Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions, confirming their presence of both metal ions.

Limit of detection (LOD)

A sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the detection ranges for both Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions. In this process, 1 mL of the Eu-CQD solution was placed in UV and FL cuvettes, followed by the addition of varying concentrations (0–20 µM) of Cu2+ and Fe3+ metal ions. Reversibility test

Eu-CQDs solution (1 mL; 5 mg/mL in PBS buffer, pH 7) was placed in a vial, followed by the addition of Fe3+ and Cu2+ solutions (20 µM) separately. This complex quenched FL. Subsequently, EDTA solution (50 µM) was introduced into the complex mixture, and the FL was successfully recorded at 424 nm upon excitation at 320 nm.

Real sample analysis

To evaluate the practicality of the Eu-CQDs probe, it was applied to the detection of Cu2+ and Fe3+ in real water sources. Water samples were collected from a tap and lake water (Yeungnam University campus, South Korea), filtered using a 0.22 µM filter paper, and centrifuged at 600 rpm for 20 min. Various spiked concentrations of Cu2+ and Fe3+ metal solutions (5, 10, and 20 µM) were added separately to each sample. Each solution was then mixed with a constant amount of Eu-CQDs. FL spectroscopy was used to measure the intensity and percentage recovery of metal ions from water sources.

In vitro cytotoxicity study

The WST assay was used to measure the cytotoxicity of the samples49. The HCT-116 human colorectal cell line was used to assess the cytotoxic properties of the synthesized Eu-CQDs. The cells were incubated with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) added with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 µL of penicillin-streptomycin and sustained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Various concentrations of Eu-CQDs (0–100 µg/mL) were added to the cell cultures. After the treatment, WST dye was added to each well containing cells, and the plates were kept in the dark for an incubation period of 2–4 h. Additionally, purple formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO. Bio-imaging studies

Bioimaging studies were conducted using HCT-116 cell lines. Three cell-wall plates containing HCT-116 cells cultured on coverslips coated with methanol were placed for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of Eu-CQDs, Eu-CQDs/Cu2+, and Eu-CQDs/Fe3+ were added and incubated for 24 h without disturbance. All cells were then completely washed with PBS (pH 7.4) to maintain a stable pH level. The cells were then permeabilized with Triton X-100. The cells were then stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 20 min at room temperature. Finally, images were captured using FL confocal microscopy.

Results and discussions

Optical properties of Eu-CQDs

The optical properties of the blue-emissive Eu-CQDs were evaluated through absorbance, FL emission, and quantum yield, which were studied using UV-Vis absorbance and FL spectroscopy. Figure 1(a) illustrates the absorption spectra of the synthesized Eu-CQDs, highlighting two absorption bands at 248 nm (sharp peak) and 323 nm (broad peak), which correspond to the π-π* and n-π* transitions due to the presence of C = C and C = O/C = N bonds in the CQDs in response to the melamine or sucrose moiety functional groups, respectively. The high-peak absorption intensity is responsible for the Eu decoration of CQDs, influencing surface modification. The broad peak at 320 nm was directly used as the excitation wavelength to analyze the emission wavelength with FL spectroscopy. Figure 1(b) shows the FL spectra of the Eu-CQDs, which were observed at 424 nm excitation at 320 nm. Moreover, the presence of Eu3+ ions contributes to the formation of numerous new energy levels leading to an increase in the number of surface-active sites, which enhances the FL properties. Furthermore, the energy level overlap between the Eu atoms and sp2 clusters in the CQDs was reduced by the electron transition from the 4f orbitals of the Eu atom to the CQDs cores50,51. The recombination rate and QY of Eu-CQDs can be enhanced based on this discovery. The quantum yield (QY) of the synthesized Eu-CQDs in this work (33.6%) is comparatively higher than most previously reported europium-based and related CQD systems51–56(Table 1). Earlier studies typically report significantly lower QY values, such as 2.4% for Eu-GQDs, 10.81% for conventional Eu-doped CDs51–53, while even advanced systems such as ligand-functionalized or co-doped CQDs exhibit QY values in the range of 24.8–31.4%54–56. In contrast, the present Eu-CQDs demonstrate superior fluorescence efficiency, representing a nearly threefold enhancement over pristine CQDs (11.8%). This improvement can be attributed to the synergistic effect of Eu3+ incorporation and nitrogen-rich surface passivation, which together promote efficient radiative recombination and suppress non-radiative pathways. Therefore, the obtained QY is among the highest reported for lanthanide-doped CQDs and confirms the effectiveness of the proposed synthesis strategy.

The FL properties of Eu-CQDs were investigated in detail using a range of excitation wavelengths from 250 to 400 nm at intervals of 10 nm. The emission peak experienced a slight shift when different excitation wavelengths were used, indicating that the FL of Eu-CQDs is excitation-dependent. This is due to a combination of factors, such as particle size distribution, surface states, and the presence of multiple emissive sites57. Additionally, CQDs of varying sizes and those with different surface functional groups (such as -OH and -NH2) absorb and emit light at distinct wavelengths. The maximum emission was found at 420–425 nm, specifically at 424 nm, when excited between 310 and 330 nm, and particularly at 320 nm (Fig. 1c). As shown in Fig. 1(d), the normalized FL spectra were better when the excitation wavelength was set between 250 and 400 nm, producing emission peaks in the 350–550 nm range. The emission colors are influenced by the presence of various emissive sites that change with the excitation wavelength.

Stability of Eu-CQDs

The stability of the Eu-CQDs probe was evaluated to ensure its suitability for sensing applications. To determine the stability of the Eu-CQDs, tests were conducted considering factors such as pH, NaCl concentration, irradiation time, and prolonged storage. In a previously reported study, most carbon-based nanomaterials, especially CQDs derived from amine, carboxylic acid, or hydroxy, presented molecules as a precursor; they were sensitive to pH detection58. In this context, melamine and sucrose serve as precursors for Eu-CQDs, featuring -NH2 and OH groups that play a role in protonation or deprotonation processes under acidic and basic pH conditions. The FL responses of the Eu-CQDs were examined in solutions with pH levels ranging from 2 to 12 (10 mM PBS). Figure 2(a) illustrates that there was no notable change in FL between the pH values of 6 and 8. This suggests that Eu-CQDs maintain higher stability in neutral to slightly basic environments within this pH spectrum. At a neutral pH, the surface charges on the Eu-CQDs were balanced, which reduced aggregation and ensured a stable dispersion in the solution. Conversely, FL was notably reduced under both acidic (pH 3–5) and more basic (pH 9–12) conditions. The surface charge of CQDs can change at very low or high pH values. Therefore, its FL property decreased due to the protonation and deprotonation of surface functional groups, which led to electrostatic interactions among the CQDs, thereby increasing aggregation. This pH analysis highlights that a neutral pH, particularly pH 7–8, is essential for sensor studies because of its enhanced FL properties and higher QY, and the stability was maintained throughout the studies. In addition, the NaCl salt solution with Eu-CQDs, as shown in Fig. 2(b). This helps in understanding the stability and maintenance of FL properties under saline conditions. Additionally, NaCl was used to prevent aggregation and precipitation, followed by an assessment of biological performance. The FL did not change even at high NaCl concentrations (0.2-1.0 mM). Figure 2(c) illustrates the time-irradiation graph, indicating the remarkable photostability of the Eu-CQDs. Even after the Eu-CQDs were continuously exposed to a UV lamp at 356 nm, the FL intensity remained constant for 30 min. Figure 2(d) presents the long-term storage graph, showing remarkable stability on day 90, as evidenced by the consistent FL intensity from day 0 to 90. The enhanced stability is attributed to effective surface passivation and the incorporation of Eu3+ ions, which improve structural integrity and reduce non-radiative degradation. These results confirm that the Eu-CQDs are stable and reliable for practical sensing applications.

Characterization of Eu-CQDs

Figure 3(a) shows the FT-IR spectra of the Eu-CQDs, which show two peaks at approximately 3400 cm− 1. These peaks were accredited to the stretching vibrations of O-H and N-H related with the presence of melamine or sucrose moieties. The C-H and C = O stretching vibrations were identified at 2946 cm−1and 1692 cm-1, respectively. The stretching vibration of melamine triazine (C = N) was detected at 1683 cm− 1. Additionally, the C-H stretching of the triazine ring appeared at 1034 cm− 159. The presence of OH and–NH2 groups enhances their dispersibility, hydrophilicity, and surface activity, making them crucial for sensor research. Moreover, the spectrum indicates the presence of carbonyl and carboxyl groups in the Eu-CQDs, which are beneficial for metal coordination with the probe. The overall FT-IR results indicate that the hydrophilic properties of the Eu-CQDs are primarily due to the C = O and O-H functional groups present on their surface. Figure 3(b) shows the XRD pattern of the Eu-CQDs, which is binominal for determining the crystalline/amorphous character of the material. A broad diffraction peak was observed at 22.34°, indicating the amorphous nature of the Eu-CQDs. The absence of different types of crystalline peaks in the CQDs may be attributed to the small size of the Eu-CQDs particles. The amorphous nature of Eu-CQDs is particularly beneficial for sensor research because it helps achieve a greater concentration of surface functional groups, which are crucial for metal binding and signal transduction in sensors60. Afterward, the surface morphology of the Eu-CQDs was observed using HR-TEM analysis, with the morphology TEM images and the average diameter histogram displayed in Fig. 3(c) and (d), respectively. The Eu-CQDs, which were uniformly distributed and spherical, exhibited a lattice spacing of 0.21 nm, which followed the (100) face of graphite carbon61. With an average size of 3.19 ± 0.21 nm, the particles had a constant size, as shown by the narrow size distribution histogram, which is advantageous for steady-state-based optical performance. Various synthesis techniques for CQDs result in different particle sizes. In particular, the hydrothermal synthesis method produces quantum-sized particles owing to its controlled reaction conditions and unique mechanism. Moreover, the use of high temperature and pressure in an autoclave promotes the extensive breakdown of sucrose and melamine, which facilitates the formation of smaller particles.

XPS analysis of the Eu-CQDs was employed to determine the chemical interactions involving Eu atoms on the surface of the CQDs core. The XPS spectra presented that the Eu-CQDs samples mainly included Eu, N, C, and O with atomic ratios of 9.46%, 16.39%, 54.28%, and 19.87%, respectively (Fig. 4)62. As shown in Fig. 4a, the full survey XPS spectrum of Eu-CQDs evidently shows four characteristic peaks at 1135.3, 401.2, 284.4, and 531.3 eV, which are attributed to the typical binding energy signals of Eu3d, N1s, C1s, and O1s, respectively. This indicates that Eu3+ ions were incorporated into the CQDs. The presence of nitrogen-containing surface states in Eu-CQDs was verified by the N1s XPS spectra (Fig. 4c), which display–NH2 and C-N binding energy peaks at 398.8 eV and 401.2 eV54. The presence of sucrose, melamine, and heteroatoms within the CQDs, which are responsible for the types of functional groups, suggests that the aromatic carbon, nitrogen, and europium groups contribute to the optical performance of the Eu-CQDs. Two different types of peaks linked to C = O and C-O/C-OH bonds were observed in the O1s spectrum of the Eu-CQDs at 530.8 and 533.6 eV, respectively (Fig. 4e). The solubility and surface reactivity of Eu-CQDs were enhanced by the presence of O-covering functional groups.

Sensing of metal ions using Eu-CQDs

FL spectroscopy is useful for the sensing of metal ions using the synthesized Eu-CQDs probe with a stable pH-7, as confirmed by our stability test. The detection of metal ions using Eu-CQDs acts as a fluorescent probe in aqueous solutions. Initially, the Eu-CQD (5 mg/mL) solution was added to various nitrate and chloride sources containing 20 µM concentrations of metal ions, such as Ba2+, Cd2+, Ca2+, Al3+, Ag+, Cu2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Hg2+, Fe3+, Fe2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Pb2+, Sr2+, and Sn2+. However, all solutions were exposed to FL spectroscopy, and their responses were examined using excitation (320 nm) and emission (440 nm) as shown in Fig. 5a. The addition of each metal ion did not lead to significant changes, except when Cu2+ and Fe3+ were added, which caused the quenching of FL emission at 420 nm63. Figure 5(b) shows the FL intensities of different metal ions, which decreased with the addition of Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions. These results established that the Eu-CQDs effectively detected Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions51,56,64. The sensing not only revealed the spectroscopy technique but also indicated a change in color under UV light (365 nm) (Fig. 5c).

Possible mechanism

The detection mechanism of Eu-CQDs toward Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions is mainly based on fluorescence quenching. The surface functional groups of Eu-CQDs (e.g-OH and -NH2) facilitate strong coordination with metal ions, leading to the formation of non-fluorescent complexes. Quenching mechanisms are generally categorized into two types: dynamic and static quenching. Our Stern-Volmer straight line curve with an excellent coefficient graph confirmed that a dynamic quenching mechanism occurred between Eu-CQDs and Cu2+/Fe3+76,77. Additionally, electron transfer from the excited Eu-CQDs to the vacant orbitals of Cu2+/Fe3+ ions promotes non-radiative recombination, resulting in decreased emission intensity. The dynamic quenching mechanism involves three types of transitions: photo-induced electron transfer (PET), Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and surface energy transfer (SET). In this study, the potential transition involved PET because we primarily focused on detecting Cu2+, which has an unfilled d-orbital (3d9)75. This property affects its ability to accept electrons, leading to electron transfer from Eu-CQDs (-OH and -NH2) to Cu2+, resulting in non-radiative FL quenching. Subsequently, we examined the detection of Fe3+. Typically, Fe3+ acts as a strong oxidizing agent because of its high electron affinity, allowing Eu-CQDs (-OH and -NH2) to transfer electrons from the excited state of Fe3+, resulting in non-radiative processes. These combined processes account for the sensitive detection of Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions. The possible reaction mechanism was presented in the equations below,

Reversibility study

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is extensively utilized in metal recovery sensing because of its potent chelating properties, which enable it to form stable complexes with metal ions, facilitating selective extraction and detection. The reversible capacity of the Eu-CQDs with the addition of Cu2+ and Fe3+ was confirmed by the EDTA test. When the Eu-CQDs/Cu2+ and Eu-CQDs/ Fe3+complexes (5 mg/mL, Cu2+ and Fe3+; 20 µM, EDTA; 50 µM, PBS = pH ~ 7.4) were treated with the EDTA solution (pH ~ 7.4). The FL intensity was enhanced as shown in Fig. 9 (a and b)78,79. Strong evidence of dynamic quenching through PET transfer was demonstrated as the FL was restored following complex formation, owing to the removal of Cu2+/Fe3+ from the Eu-CQDs surface, which prevented the PET transfer.

In vitro cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity and biocompatibility are crucial factors for biological and sensor applications, particularly in the fields of bioimaging and pharmaceutical research. In this study, the cytotoxic effects of Eu-CQDs on HCT-116 cancer cells were assessed using a traditional water-soluble tetrazolium (WST) assay. The HCT-116 cell line was selected because of its sensitivity to various cytotoxic agents affecting both normal and cancer cells; therefore, various researchers have utilized the HCT-116 cell model to evaluate the toxicity of potential bioimaging applications. Figure 10a shows a bar diagram of the viability of prostate cancer cells (HCT-116) exposed to Eu-CQDs at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL. Even at a high concentration of 100 µg/mL, no significant cytotoxicity was observed80, which may be due to the CQDs size, tunable surface features, and the absence of toxic components. The overall results suggest that the synthesized Eu-CQDs are biocompatible and a great choice for bioimaging applications.

Bio-imaging study

Eu-CQDs possess FL, photostability, and water solubility, making them ideal for bioimaging in live cells and tissues. These characteristics allow them to be conjugated with targeted moieties, such as peptides, antibodies, drugs, or imaging agents, for applications in cancer, such as HCT-116 cells. They evaluated bioimaging applications using the same HCT116 cancer cell line with DAPI staining. Figure 10b shows the bright-field, FL, and merged images of HCT-116 cells treated with or without Eu-CQDs, Eu-CQDs/Cu2+, and Eu-CQDs/Fe3+. The synthesized Eu-CQDs exhibited strong blue FL even at high concentrations (100 µg/mL), which was attributed to their high biocompatibility, as a similar result was obtained in our cytotoxicity analysis80,81. These findings confirmed that Eu-CQDs were nontoxic and safe for use in HCT-116 human colon cancer cells. However, in the presence of Eu-CQDs/Cu2+ and Eu-CQDs/Fe3+, the number of cells was reduced owing to FL quenching (turn off), which may be caused by metal-induced oxidative stress. This outcome indicates that Eu-CQDs can be used for bioimaging applications for the detection of both metal ions.

Conclusions

In summary, we synthesized Eu-CQDs using sucrose and melamine as sources of carbon and nitrogen, respectively, and EuCl3 as the lanthanoid source using a one-step hydrothermal process. The Eu-CQDs exhibited remarkable optical properties, with a quantum yield of 33.6%. The as-synthesized Eu-CQDs exhibited a spherical form with a diameter of 3.19 ± 0.21 nm and biocompatibility and cytotoxicity, as confirmed through UV-Vis, fluorescence (FL), FT-IR, TEM, XRD, and cytotoxicity assessments. The FL of Eu-CQDs was observed in stable high-ion-concentration NaCl salt solutions, as well as in neutral and alkaline aqueous environments. Additionally, the Eu-CQDs FL probe detected Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions with high sensitivity, and the calculated LOD values were 65.6 nM for Cu2+ and 72.1 nM for Fe3+ within a linear concentration range of 0–20 μm with an excellent coefficient. The interaction between Eu-CQDs and metal ions involved a dynamic quenching mechanism, which was confirmed by a linear Stern-Volmer plot. From a practical application point of view, the analyses with real water sources, including tap and lake water, Eu-CQDs successfully quantified Cu2+ and Fe3+ ions with maximum 101.1% and 104.8% accuracy, respectively, highlighting their promising application as FL detectors. Moreover, Eu-CQDs acted as a bioimaging agent in the HCT-116 human colon cancer cell line, as confirmed by FL imaging.
