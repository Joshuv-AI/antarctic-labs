Refining the role of extended resection in multidisciplinary treatment for cT4b esophageal cancer
Authors: Tomoki Makino, Makoto Yamasaki, Shigeru Miyagawa, Hidetoshi Eguchi, Yuichiro Doki
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Drs. Nakajima and Kojima (1), and Dr. Różańska-Walędziak and colleagues (2), for their thoughtful editorial commentaries on our recent article, “Surgical and long-term outcomes of combined organ resection for esophageal cancer invading adjacent organs: Experience of 90 consecutive cases” (3). We are grateful that both groups highlighted the clinical need to reconsider the role of surgery in selected patients with esophageal cancer that persists in invading adjacent vital organs after multidisciplinary treatment.

As emphasized in both commentaries, cT4b esophageal cancer has historically been regarded as unresectable, particularly when the trachea, bronchus, aorta, or major vessels are involved. Definitive chemoradiotherapy or systemic therapy is therefore often selected, but residual local disease after induction treatment remains a serious problem. Our intention was not to propose combined organ resection as a standard approach for all cT4b cases. Rather, we sought to evaluate whether this strategy can be performed safely and whether it can provide meaningful survival in a highly selected subgroup treated at an experienced center.

In our series of 90 consecutive patients, R0 resection was achieved in 89 patients, the 30-day mortality rate was 0%, and the in-hospital mortality rate was 2.2% (3). Although postoperative morbidity was not negligible, these findings suggest that combined organ resection can be feasible when the surgical indication, operative strategy, and perioperative management are carefully optimized. We agree with Nakajima and Kojima that these procedures require substantial institutional experience and should be undertaken only in centers with close collaboration among esophageal, thoracic, cardiovascular, anesthetic, intensive-care, radiology, oncology, and reconstructive teams.

The comments regarding airway and major vascular resection are particularly important. Airway resection requires precise assessment of the depth and extent of invasion. In our study, partial-layer tracheal or bronchial resection was associated with better survival than all-layer resection, indicating that the required extent of airway resection may reflect both surgical invasiveness and tumor biology (3). Similarly, for suspected aortic or major arterial invasion, the choice among adventitial resection after thoracic endovascular aortic repair, vascular graft replacement, or arterial reconstruction must be individualized. The operative goal should not be technical radicality alone, but an appropriate balance between the likelihood of R0 resection, procedure-related risk, and expected oncologic benefit.

We also fully agree with Różańska-Walędziak and colleagues that our study has important limitations. It was retrospective, single-institutional, and inevitably affected by selection bias. The patients who underwent surgery were fit enough for extended resection and were selected after detailed multidisciplinary assessment. In addition, the cohort covered a 20-year period during which induction therapy, imaging, perioperative care, and systemic treatment evolved. The absence of a contemporaneous non-surgical control group means that the survival benefit of surgery cannot be proven definitively from our data alone. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

Another key issue is response assessment after induction therapy. Post-treatment inflammation and fibrosis can obscure the boundary between tumor and adjacent organs, making persistent T4b invasion difficult to diagnose. Improved multimodal assessment using computed tomography, positron emission tomography/computed tomography, bronchoscopy, endoscopy, and magnetic resonance imaging may help identify patients most likely to benefit from surgery (4). In parallel, our recurrence findings indicate that systemic control remains essential. Lymphatic and hematogenous recurrences were frequent after surgery, suggesting that advances in perioperative systemic therapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, should be integrated with surgical strategies rather than considered separately (5,6).

Future progress will require multi-institutional collaboration. Because this population is uncommon and heterogeneous, randomized trials may be challenging; however, prospective registries capturing induction regimens, response assessment, operative details, complications, quality of life, recurrence patterns, and survival would be highly valuable. Such datasets may allow more objective patient selection and help define where combined organ resection fits within modern multidisciplinary treatment.

In conclusion, combined organ resection for esophageal cancer invading adjacent organs should be viewed as neither a universal standard nor an obsolete procedure. For carefully selected patients, and only within an experienced multidisciplinary framework, it may offer a meaningful chance of long-term survival. We appreciate the opportunity to continue this discussion and hope that our data will contribute to more refined, individualized treatment strategies for this challenging disease.
