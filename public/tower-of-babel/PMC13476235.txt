Projected heatwave hazards in an eastern Amazonian metropolis under contrasting CMIP6 scenarios
Authors: Everaldo B de Souza, Douglas B S Ferreira, Alan C da Cunha, Felipe G de Souza, Rodrigo H M Braga, Adriano M L Sousa, Carlos B B Gutierrez, Alexandre M C Carmo, Luiz R R Costa, Waléria P M Corrêa, Thaine S S Dias, Vania S F Sodré, Victor H M Paca, Daniel P Bitencourt, João de A Silva Junior, Pedro W M e Souza-Filho, Gilberto de M Rocha, Tercio Ambrizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeatwaves (HWs) are among the most impactful climate extremes affecting tropical urban environments, yet local‑scale assessments of their future characteristics remain scarce in the Amazon. Here, we quantify projected changes in key HW indicators for the Belém urban core, eastern Amazon, using a station‑referenced framework based on a carefully evaluated and bias‑corrected ensemble of CMIP6 global models. HWs are identified from daily maximum air temperature (Tmax) using a smoothed day‑of‑year 95th percentile threshold calculated over the 1994–2023 baseline period. Changes in annual frequency, event duration, and thermal intensity are assessed for near‑future (2025–2049) and far‑future (2050–2074) periods under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5). A multi‑metric performance evaluation is first applied to select the best‑performing CMIP6 models for Tmax over Belém, followed by bias correction using Quantile Delta Mapping calibrated against local observations. Results show a clear, scenario‑dependent intensification of HW hazards. In the current climate, 126 HW events were detected, with pronounced interannual variability and a marked increase during the last decade. Near‑future changes are modest and variable across scenarios. In contrast, far‑future projections under the high‑emission SSP5‑8.5 pathway reveal a substantial transformation of the HW regime: annual frequency more than doubles relative to the baseline, and the 99th percentile of event duration increases from approximately 19 to 65 days (Δ = +46 days), with a 14.5% probability that any given HW will exceed the current extreme threshold. Peak Tmax during HWs increases from 37.0 °C to 37.55 °C (Δ = +0.55 °C), thus indicating an upper-tail amplification. Under the strong mitigation pathway SSP1‑2.6, changes in HW duration and intensity remain statistically indistinguishable from present‑day conditions. While this study does not assess impacts or adaptation capacity, the results provide a hazard‑based, scenario‑dependent characterization of future heatwave behavior in an Amazonian urban context, offering a quantitative foundation for subsequent assessments of climate risk in tropical cities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57944-x.Keywords: Heat-related extremes, Amazon, Belém, Maximum air temperature, SSPs, QDM bias correctionSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The 30th edition of the United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP30), convened in Belém, Brazil, from 10 to 21 November 2025, placed delegates from around the world in the unique position to personally experience the phenomena that international climate policy seeks to mitigate or adapt to: the recurrence of extreme weather events and the warming of urban environments exacerbated by global climate change. During this period, the host city experienced two heatwave (HW) events, providing a tangible, in situ demonstration of these challenges. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a meteorological station recorded maximum air temperature (Tmax) exceeding the climatological 90th percentile (P90) for consecutive days during two episodes: 6–11 November and from 15 to 21 November, with the latter coinciding with the decisive second week of the summit. Spatial analysis of this second HW event reveals a pronounced thermal anomaly, with seven-day mean Tmax reaching 34.5 °C in the conurbation core (red areas on the map indicating dense urban coverage) formed by Belém and its three adjacent municipalities. These events highlight the phenomenon that will be the focus of this study: HW events, which are considered the most consequential climate extremes in tropical regions, particularly in large, overpopulated urban centers1. Here we initially use the static P90 threshold of 34.1 °C (corresponding to the period 1994–2023 in the dry regime, June to November) for simple and illustrative detection of the phenomenon; a more advanced and quantitative approach will be presented in this work.

Anthropogenic climate change has unequivocally intensified surface air temperature extremes globally, with HWs emerging as one of the most impactful extreme climate events. HWs are projected to become more intense, more frequent, and longer-lasting throughout the 21st century4–6. In the Amazon Basin, this global signal is amplified by regional drivers such as deforestation, rapid urban expansion, as well as large-scale climate variability, including strong El Niño events, thus contributing to even more intense and frequent HWs in recent decades7–12. An aggravating factor is that HWs have increasingly occurred alongside prolonged droughts, giving rise to extreme hot-dry compound events13–18.

Amazonian state capitals exemplify the convergence of these pressures. Cities such as Manaus and Belém have experienced rapid, unplanned urbanization over just a few decades, becoming densely populated metropolises where urban heat island (UHI) effects are now documented19–23. In the eastern Brazilian Amazon conurbation formed by Belém and its adjacent municipalities (Fig. 1, map), substantial forest loss and urban expansion since the mid-1980s have been linked to measurable local climate shifts, including dry-season warming, reduced relative humidity, and weakened near-surface winds24. These background changes provide a physical context in which HW events may be amplified, increasing the potential for socio-environmental impacts.

Unlike other extreme climate events, HWs have only recently become recognized as a significant threat to environment and society25. The effects of HW on human health in terms of mortality were investigated for Amazonian municipalities. The findings showed a higher risk for cardiovascular causes compared to non-external or respiratory diseases, with elderly individuals and women being more vulnerable to heat-related mortality26.

Using global models (GCMs) from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) with greenhouse gas scenarios, Li27 demonstrated that the likelihood of future extreme HW increases by 30% relative to the historical period, a finding consistent with previous work addressing similar projections5,6. These projections of escalating surface thermal extremes pose unprecedented challenges not only to forest ecosystems but also to the well-being of millions of human inhabitants across the Brazilian Legal Amazon28.

However, translating global‑scale climate projections into robust, locally relevant information remains challenging, particularly for rapidly expanding urban systems in the Amazon. The Belém metropolitan core, comprising Belém and its adjacent densely urbanized municipalities (Ananindeua, Marituba and Benevides), concentrates approximately 2.1 million inhabitants29 (2024 estimates) and represents a critical hotspot of exposure and vulnerability to extreme heat in eastern Amazonia. Despite this relevance, city‑scale assessments that explicitly characterize future HW hazards using carefully evaluated climate model ensembles remain scarce for the region. Here, we quantify observed patterns and projected changes in key HW characteristics for the Belém conurbation using a station‑referenced framework. HWs are identified from daily Tmax observations recorded at the only meteorological station with long‑term data available in the region, whose effective coverage radius (~ 10 km) encompasses the densely urbanized area of Belém and neighboring municipalities (Fig. 1, larger dashed blue circle on the map). Future projections are derived from the corresponding CMIP6 GCM grid point. We analyze changes in annual frequency, event duration, and mean and maximum thermal intensity under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5), considering near‑future (2025–2049) and far‑future (2050–2074) periods based on 25‑year statistics. To enhance the robustness of local‑scale projections, we first evaluate and select the best‑performing GCMs for Tmax over Belém using a multi‑metric statistical framework, followed by bias correction using a quantile‑based mapping approach calibrated against station observations. A key scientific advance of this work lies in the focused analysis of the upper tails of HW distributions, moving beyond mean changes to evaluate how the probability and magnitude of rare, extreme events shift under different emissions pathways. Ultimately, this research provides a hazard‑based, scenario‑dependent characterization of future heatwave behavior in an Amazonian context, offering a quantitative foundation for assessments of climate risk and adaptation strategies in rapidly urbanizing tropical cities.

Results

Selection of the best CMIP6 multi-model ensemble and bias-correction

We first evaluated the ability of CMIP6 GCMs to reproduce daily Tmax, the key variable defining HW events, by comparing historical simulations with in situ observations from the Belém meteorological station. Model data were extracted from the corresponding grid point nearest to Belém station (1.44° S, 48.44° W), to ensure direct consistency between observations and simulations. We used ten GCMs (details in Methods) under four scenarios: SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5. These scenarios span a wide range of radiative forcing, from strong mitigation to very high emissions, enabling scenario‑dependent characterization of warming trajectories and heat‑related extremes. Using a statistical multi‑metric evaluation framework applied to Tmax data in Belém from January 1985 to December 2014 (30‑year statistics, the period required for consistent results in model validation), we quantified the agreement between observations and the ten CMIP6 GCM historical simulations at both daily (n = 4,590) and monthly‑mean (n = 150) temporal scales. Model performance was assessed using complementary error, bias, association and agreement metrics (RMSE, MAE, PBIAS, Pearson’s r, R², and Willmott’s d; details in Methods), which were combined through objective rank aggregation based on the mean rank across all six metrics. Table 1 presents the quantitative values obtained from the statistical analysis at the daily Tmax scale. Selecting models with a composite mean rank below 5 (i.e., the almost top‑performing half of the ten models) provides a parsimonious yet skill‑informed ensemble that better balances systematic bias control, error minimization and temporal consistency with observations compared to the full model pool. This threshold is commonly adopted in model selection studies to retain models with above‑median performance while discarding those with consistently poor rankings, thereby reducing structural uncertainty without over‑pruning the ensemble. This ranked screening supports the use of a four‑GCM subset representing the best‑performing CMIP6 models: INM-CM5-0, MRI-ESM2-0, MIROC-ES2L and NorESM2-MM, therefore, these models are considered more reliable for analyses of Tmax behavior in Belém.

Using daily Tmax from the Belém station as reference for 1985–2014, we bias‑corrected the four selected CMIP6 GCMs across all scenarios using Quantile Delta Mapping (QDM; see Methods). In the raw simulations, all models exhibited a cold bias, ranging from − 0.67 (NorESM2-MM) to − 3.01 °C (MIROC-ES2L). QDM effectively removed the systematic bias and reduced errors: RMSE decreased from 3.11 to 1.74 °C, and MAE from 2.60 to 1.31 °C. Improvements varied across models, with the largest RMSE reduction for MIROC-ES2L (~ 54.0%) and the smallest for NorESM2-MM (~ 24.2%). Pearson correlation changed only marginally (multi‑model mean r from 0.294 to 0.297), as expected given that QDM primarily corrects distributional properties rather than day‑to‑day variability. For future projections, the QDM procedure was applied using an additive change factor approach, preserving the model‑projected warming signal while correcting historical biases. The resulting month-dependent correction factors are summarized for each scenario in Table S1 (Supplementary Material). Finally, after bias correction, we calculated the daily Tmax time series for the CMIP6 multi‑model ensemble (CMIP6_MME) as the simple mean of the four best‑performing GCMs for future projections (2015–2074) under all four SSP scenarios. These series were then used for HW event detection.

To assess the sensitivity of our model selection and address concerns about ensemble robustness, we compared the QDM‑corrected Tmax from our CMIP6_MME (top‑4 best models) with the complete set of all ten models (FULL_MME). Figure S1 (Supplementary Material) shows the time series and boxplots of observed Tmax (up to 2023) and the projections under SSP1‑2.6 and SSP5‑8.5. The FULL_MME systematically overestimates observed Tmax during the independent period (2015–2023), maintaining an elevated baseline that persists throughout the 21st century until 2074, as illustrated by the boxplot distributions. As shown in Table 1, models ranked 5th to 10th exhibit substantially larger errors and biases; when aggregated into a single ensemble, these deficiencies propagate and cause a persistent surface warming at the Belém grid point. This result demonstrates that including inferior‑performing models compromises the credibility of future heatwave projections by systematically raising the baseline from which changes are calculated. Therefore, our top‑4 ensemble is not only the best‑performing but also the most appropriate for anchoring future projections to the observed climate. Consequently, we retain the original CMIP6_MME (four models) as the basis for all subsequent HW analyses.

Baseline heatwave detection

Figure 2 shows the full daily Tmax (gray line) record at the Belém station from 1 January 1994 to 31 December 2023 (the series is split into two periods in the top and bottom panels), revealing pronounced day‑to‑day variability superimposed on a strong seasonal cycle. A HW event is defined as at least three consecutive days with Tmax exceeding a smoothed, day‑of‑year 95th percentile threshold (blue line) calculated over the 1994–2023 baseline period (30-year observational statistics). A total of 126 HW events were detected over the years, with the last decade showing markedly more intense activity (red line segments). The year 2023 exhibited the largest annual HW burden, with 23 events, followed by 2020 with 14 events. The longest individual event lasted 21 consecutive days (12 September to 2 October 2020), with a mean Tmax of 34.8 °C. The highest daily Tmax recorded within a detected event reached 37.2 °C on 4 December 2017. Overall, the event catalogue demonstrates not only an increase in the occurrence of P95 threshold exceedances, but also a transition towards more intense HW episodes in the most recent part of the record.

Projected changes in heatwave characteristics and statistical comparisons

The event detection procedure was applied to daily Tmax data from CMIP6_MME (after bias correction using QDM), yielding projections of HW characteristics under different future scenarios over Belém. The quantitative metrics to be analyzed are: event duration (days), mean Tmax for all days of the event (mean_Tmax), and the peak or maximum individual value of the hottest day of the event (peak_Tmax), the latter two denoting the intensity of the events in °C. The annual frequency (events per year) was also analyzed. To ensure statistical robustness and temporal consistency with future projections, we defined the current baseline used for change assessment as 1999–2023, and the near- and far-future projection windows as 2025–2049 and 2050–2074, respectively, thus corresponding to 25‑year statistics. This analysis is based on the Fig. 3 that shows the HW characteristics in current observations and CMIP6_MME projections for the near-future and far-future under all four SSP scenarios, with the time series (left panel) and a distribution‑focused view using combined violin‑boxplot graphs (right panel).

Vertical dashed black lines separate the current, near‑future, and far‑future event windows. Red dashed horizontal lines indicate the historical q95 and q99 thresholds. Outliers are shown as grey points.

HW frequency (Fig. 3a) shows pronounced interannual variability during the current climate, with a positive trend culminating in an exceptional peak of 23 events in 2023 during a strong El Niño. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2, which compares the observed annual frequency with the CMIP6_MME ensemble mean and the individual GCM range (1999–2014 for the historical period and 2015–2023 for the SSP5‑8.5 scenario), the increasing trend after ~ 2010 is reproduced within the envelope of the four models under the highest emission scenario (grey shaded area, minimum to maximum), including the simulation of the value above the climatological q95 in the extreme year 2023, thereby confirming that the selected CMIP6_MME adequately captures the magnitude of observed heatwave variability. In the near‑future, low‑forcing scenarios (SSP1‑2.6 and SSP2‑4.5) generally show lower annual HW counts than the current period, whereas SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5 approach or exceed the current distributional range in some years. By the far‑future, however, all scenarios except SSP1‑2.6 exhibit a marked upward shift in frequency. Median annual HW frequency rises to approximately 9 events yr−1 under SSP2‑4.5 and 10 events yr−1 under SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5, with SSP5‑8.5 reaching a mean of 11 events yr−1. The violin and boxplot distributions confirm this progressive displacement, showing broader and higher frequency distributions under stronger forcing, particularly in the far‑future. This indicates that HW occurrence becomes increasingly regular rather than episodic under intermediate‑ and high‑emission pathways.

HW duration (Fig. 3b) shows a clear scenario‑dependent amplification toward the far‑future period. The signal is more strongly expressed in the distributional tail than in the median alone. The current climate exhibits a median of 4 days, with relatively compact interquartile variability and only a small proportion of events exceeding the q95 and q99 thresholds. Near‑future projections retain a median duration close to 4 days across all SSPs, suggesting limited short‑term change in typical HW persistence. However, the upper tails expand under SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5, with maximum durations reaching 26–27 days. In the far‑future, the duration distributions broaden markedly, especially under SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5. Mean duration increases from 5.0 days in the current climate to 6.6 days under SSP2‑4.5, 8.9 days under SSP3‑7.0, and 11.2 days under SSP5‑8.5 in the far‑future. The exceedance of historical q95 and q99 thresholds also rises sharply, particularly for SSP5‑8.5, where approximately 19% of far‑future events exceed q95 and 15% exceed q99. This demonstrates that future HW persistence is characterized not only by longer average duration, but also by the emergence of exceptionally long events that are rare or absent under current conditions.

Changes in mean_Tmax (Fig. 3c) are comparatively more subtle. The current median is approximately 34.7 °C, and future medians remain within a narrow range, generally between 34.4 and 34.6 °C across periods and scenarios. This indicates that the central tendency does not undergo the same amplification observed for frequency and duration. Nevertheless, the violin distributions and threshold exceedances reveal important tail behaviour. In the far‑future, SSP5‑8.5 shows the clearest upward extension of the distribution, with maximum mean_Tmax reaching approximately 36.2 °C and q99 exceedance increasing to about 5% of events. Thus, while typical event‑mean HW intensity remains relatively stable, high‑emission forcing increases the probability of events with unusually high mean thermal intensity.

Peak_Tmax (Fig. 3d) exhibits a stronger and more consequential intensification than mean_Tmax. Under current conditions, median peak_Tmax is 35.1 °C, with relatively limited exceedance of the q95 and q99 thresholds. Near‑future changes remain moderate, although SSP5‑8.5 already shows a slight upward displacement of the distribution and a higher fraction of events exceeding q95. By the far‑future, the scenario dependence becomes more pronounced. Median peak_Tmax increases to approximately 35.6 °C under SSP5‑8.5, while the upper tail extends to 37.6 °C. Exceedance of the historical q95 threshold increases from approximately 5% in the current period to 14% under far‑future SSP5‑8.5, and q99 exceedance increases from less than 1% to more than 8%. SSP3‑7.0 also shows a clear intensification, with far‑future q95 exceedance approaching 9%. These results suggest that future HWs are not only becoming more frequent and longer‑lasting, but also increasingly capable of producing higher short‑duration thermal peaks, particularly under stronger radiative forcing.

Using the current baseline as the reference, projected changes in HW characteristics were evaluated across near‑future and far‑future time windows under SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5. Across all comparisons, Welch unequal‑variance t‑tests were used to assess changes in means, with 95% confidence intervals and Cohen’s d effect sizes, while Wilcoxon rank‑sum tests were applied to evaluate non‑parametric distributional shifts. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and adjusted p‑values are reported for both tests. The quantitative results are shown in Table 2.

For annual frequency, the near‑future period showed statistically significant mean reductions under SSP1‑2.6 and SSP2‑4.5 in the Welch tests, with mean differences of − 3.32 events yr−1 and − 3.08 events yr−1, respectively. However, these changes were not supported by BH‑significant Wilcoxon tests. In contrast, far‑future frequency showed a clear scenario‑dependent increase. Under SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5, frequency increased by 5.24 events yr−1 (+ 104.0%) and 5.96 events yr−1 (+ 118.3%), respectively. These changes were significant in both Welch and Wilcoxon tests after BH correction, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.11 for SSP3‑7.0 and d = 1.20 for SSP5‑8.5).

For HW event duration, near‑future changes were not statistically significant under any SSP scenario after BH correction. In the far‑future, however, duration increased significantly under SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5 in both Welch and Wilcoxon tests. Mean duration increased by 1.65 days under SSP2‑4.5, 3.93 days under SSP3‑7.0, and 6.28 days under SSP5‑8.5, corresponding to relative increases of + 33.3%, + 79.2%, and + 126.5%, respectively. The largest absolute increase occurred under SSP5‑8.5, although effect sizes were moderate because of the strong increase in variance and the emergence of long‑duration events.

For mean_Tmax, all future scenario‑period combinations showed statistically significant differences relative to the current period in both Welch and Wilcoxon tests after BH correction. However, the estimated mean differences were consistently small and negative, ranging approximately from − 0.14 to − 0.26 °C, with small‑to‑moderate effect sizes. This result indicates that changes in event‑mean maximum temperature were statistically detectable but not indicative of a systematic increase in the central tendency of HW mean thermal intensity in these event samples.

For peak_Tmax, the statistical response was more scenario‑dependent and more consistent with an upper‑tail intensification. Near‑future SSP5‑8.5 showed a significant increase of + 0.20 °C, while SSP2‑4.5 and SSP3‑7.0 did not show significant changes. In the far‑future, peak_Tmax increased significantly under SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5, with mean differences of + 0.24 °C and + 0.60 °C, respectively. The SSP5‑8.5 response was the strongest thermal‑intensity signal, with Cohen’s d = 0.87 and BH‑adjusted p‑values below 0.001 for both Welch and Wilcoxon tests.

It is worth noting that in the near‑future period, the projected changes in HW characteristics are relatively modest, largely due to the dominance of internal variability and the initial adjustment of the global models during the first two decades of the simulations.

Discussion and conclusion

This study provides a station‑referenced assessment of future HW hazards for the urban core of Belém, eastern Brazilian Amazon, based on a carefully selected and bias‑corrected CMIP6 multi‑model ensemble. Analytical credibility was first established by demonstrating that QDM effectively reconciles raw global climate models, which exhibit systematic cold biases in daily Tmax for Belém, with local observations, yielding a reliable ensemble for impact assessment.

In the current climate (1994–2023), we detected 126 HW events, with pronounced interannual variability and a clear increase in activity during the last decade. The year 2023 showed the largest annual HW burden, coinciding with a strong El Niño, underscoring the synergy between anthropogenic warming and natural climate variability in exacerbating extreme air temperatures. These observed characteristics align with recent regional analyses over Amazon region10–12,15,17 and provide a baseline against which future changes are evaluated.

Projections reveal a robust, scenario‑dependent intensification of HW hazards. Under the high‑emission SSP5‑8.5 pathway, far‑future projections (2050–2074) indicate a significant transformation of the HW regime. Annual HW frequency more than doubles relative to the current baseline, while event duration undergoes substantial lengthening: the 99th percentile of duration increases from approximately 20 days to 66 days, and the probability that any given HW exceeds the current extreme threshold rises from < 1% to nearly 15%. Peak daily air temperatures during HWs also increase, with the upper tail of peak_Tmax shifting significantly upward under high forcing. These changes are consistent with the emerging concept of a hypertropical climate, under which extreme hot conditions become pervasive, pushing the Amazon Basin beyond historical analogs30.

The divergence between the strong mitigation pathway (SSP1‑2.6) and high‑emission scenarios carries clear implications for risk assessment. Under SSP1‑2.6, the escalation of HW extremes is effectively curtailed: annual frequency in the far‑future (3.4 events yr−1) does not differ significantly from the current baseline, and duration remains statistically indistinguishable. Upper‑tail exceedance probabilities for duration under SSP1‑2.6 are near‑zero and not significant, and the ECDFs for both duration and peak_Tmax remain closely aligned with the current climate. Thus, under stringent mitigation, HW characteristics do not show the qualitative shift observed under higher‑emission pathway. Previous large‑scale studies have reported that following a low‑emissions scenario leads to 50% lower exposure to heatwaves for tropical South America31, whereas under a high‑emissions scenario, a threefold increase in population exposure to HWs is expected in most Brazilian states .

For metropolitan regions such as Belém, extended HW durations will exacerbate the urban heat island effect, with serious implications for public health systems and urban infrastructure. Recent assessments indicate that climate‑related disasters in the eastern Amazon have surged32, with HWs and droughts tripling in frequency and imposing disproportionate economic losses on highly vulnerable municipalities33. The long HW durations projected under high‑emission scenarios (mean durations of 9–11 days, with upper tails exceeding 50 days) imply that these effects could develop into chronic stressors that worsen existing social inequalities34, reduce labor productivity35, and increase heat‑related morbidity26,36.

We acknowledge an important limitation of this study: the use of a single station to characterize the Belém metropolitan environment. The city exhibits spatial heterogeneity typical of tropical urban areas, including intra‑urban heat island variability with diverse land‑cover gradients22,23. A single station cannot fully resolve this intra‑urban variability, and therefore our results primarily represent the climatological conditions at the station site. However, this station is the only long‑term, quality‑controlled observational record available for climatological studies in Belém, and it provides a robust reference for trend analysis.

Furthermore, it is essential to clarify what the CMIP6 projections used here represent. The nearest grid cell from each GCM was extracted without spatial interpolation, as stated in the methodology. Given that the native resolution of models is substantially coarser (ranging from 0.9 to 1.875° which corresponds to ~ 100 to 208 km) than the scale of the Belém urban area, these projections do not, and cannot, explicitly resolve urban‑scale processes such as localized convection, land‑use feedbacks, or the detailed spatial structure of the urban heat island. Instead, the projected changes in HW characteristics reported here should be interpreted as the large‑scale background warming signal that affects the Belém region as a whole, driven by global radiative forcing under different SSP scenarios. This signal represents the forcing to which the urban environment will be exposed, superimposed on any local‑scale modifications. Therefore, our results provide a robust estimate of how the regional climate context for Belém is expected to change, which is the primary objective of this study, namely, to report HW projections directly associated with large‑scale anthropogenic warming captured by GCMs under future emission pathways. Explicit urban‑microclimate simulations would require downscaling with urban canopy models and/or high‑resolution regional climate models, which is beyond the scope of the present work and is recommended as a priority for future investigations.

It is important to note that projected HW duration in Belém depends strongly on temporal persistence. While QDM effectively corrects distributional biases in Tmax, it does not explicitly constrain temporal sequencing. Accordingly, projections of very long‑duration HWs (e.g., 99th percentile under SSP5‑8.5) should be interpreted as upper‑bound hazard estimates rather than deterministic predictions of individual event persistence. Similarly, the muted near‑future responses (2025–2049), with weak or statistically non‑robust changes in annual event counts under low‑to‑moderate forcing, are consistent with the known behaviour of CMIP6 projections: the first two‑to‑three decades of scenario simulations are strongly influenced by internal variability and model adjustment from the historical baseline, whereas the forced anthropogenic signal only emerges clearly after mid‑century. Consequently, the pronounced intensification reported for 2050–2074 reflects the true scenario‑driven transformation of the HW regime, as a result of global warming.

In conclusion, the future of HW hazards in Belém, and by extension in similarly urbanized Amazonian contexts, is strongly CMIP6 scenario‑dependent. Under high‑emission pathways (SSP3‑7.0 and SSP5‑8.5), HWs become substantially more frequent (doubling to tripling of annual counts), persist much longer (mean durations increase by 80–125%, and 99th percentile durations reach 50–65 days), and exhibit higher peak surface air temperatures (upper‑tail increases of + 0.6 °C to + 1.0 °C). These changes represent a transition from episodic extremes to prolonged heat exposure with cascading consequences for human well‑being and urban sustainability. Conversely, under the stringent mitigation pathway (SSP1‑2.6), HW characteristics remain statistically indistinguishable from the current climate, with no significant upper‑tail amplification. These results provide a hazard‑focused, scenario‑dependent quantification of future HW behaviour in an Amazonian urban environment, offering a quantitative foundation for subsequent assessments of climate risk, vulnerability, and adaptation in tropical cities.

Methods

CMIP6 GCMs and selection metrics for the best multi-model ensemble

We utilized the Copernicus Climate Data Store37 (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu) to retrieve daily global climate projection data, derived from a comprehensive set of CMIP6 including historical experiments covering the period with extensive modern observations, as well as future climate projections based on four distinct SSPs scenarios, which describe contrasting global development trends and result in widely different climate forcing. In summary, SSP1-2.6 represents a sustainable pathway, assuming a shift toward a low-carbon economy leading to very low future greenhouse gas emissions and minimal additional radiative forcing. In contrast, SSP5-8.5 depicts a future driven by fossil-fueled development, with high levels of consumption and limited climate policy, resulting in the highest emission levels and a drastic rise in radiative forcing. In SSP2-4.5 the historical patterns of development are continued, leading to moderate emissions and stabilization of radiative forcing. SSP3-7.0 is characterized by nationalism and slow economic growth in developing regions, which results in high emissions from a heavy reliance on fossil fuels and limited environmental cooperation, producing a high-end, but not the maximum, forcing level. These contrasting pathways allows for a robust assessment of climate change impacts across a wide spectrum of plausible socioeconomic and climatic futures. Using multiple GCMs across multiple SSPs is particularly important for air temperature-extremes research because projected changes in the frequency and intensity of hot events depend not only on the forcing pathway but also on model-specific representations of land–atmosphere coupling, clouds, circulation, and regional feedbacks38–41.

Given that near-surface Tmax is the key variable for detecting the occurrence of HW, we evaluated the ability of ten CMIP6 GCMs (Table 5) to reproduce observed Tmax variability and magnitude at Belém over historical period 1984–2014 (30-year statistics).

This approach is consistent with state-of-the-art model screening42 in climate impacts and extremes applications, where it is important to retain models that jointly minimize biases (critical for threshold exceedances), maintain reasonable error magnitudes (critical for intensity-based indices), and represent variability and covariability (critical for timing and persistence of extreme conditions). Bias-correction using quantile delta mapping (QDM)

We evaluated and bias-corrected daily Tmax from set of selected CMIP6 GCMs using the observational meteorological station record as reference. The methodological workflow comprised data harmonization, historical evaluation, quantile-based bias correction, and application to future projections under different SSP scenarios. The bias correction was performed with QDM44, a distribution-based method designed to correct systematic errors while preserving the model-projected climate change signal in a quantile-consistent manner.

HW definition, threshold construction, and event metrics

The observational Tmax data were extracted from the conventional meteorological station belonging to the Instituto Nacional de Meteorologia (INMET), obtained from the database portal https://bdmep.inmet.gov.br. This station is the only one in the study area containing a complete historical series of daily data available for climatological analyses. Tmax was measured by thermometers inside the meteorological shelter following the international standards of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). Daily Tmax series from the last 30 years, from 1994 to 2023, are used (it was not possible to use the years 2024 and 2025 due to many gaps on consecutive days).

HW events were identified from daily Tmax by using a relative, seasonally varying percentile threshold and an event-based criterion, consistent with state-of-the-art practice in climate extremes research. This approach defines extreme heat relative to the local climatological distribution for each time of year, thereby accounting for the strong seasonal cycle and enabling robust comparisons across decades under a changing climate46,47.

Statistical analysis of projected changes in HW characteristics

Projected changes in HW characteristics were assessed by comparing each future scenario–period combination with the current baseline. The analyses cover 25 years–statistics, so the current period was defined as 1999–2023, while future projections were evaluated for the near-future, 2025–2049, and far-future, 2050–2074, under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathway scenarios: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. The analyzed HW characteristics were annual frequency, event duration, mean_Tmax and peak_Tmax. For each variable, future samples were compared against the corresponding current sample using two complementary parametric and non-parametric statistical tests. A Welch unequal-variance two-sample t-test was used to evaluate changes in the mean48, while a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess non-parametric distributional shifts49,50. We acknowledge that individual HW events may not be fully independent because they can cluster temporally, particularly during strong warm years. However, the statistical tests are applied here as descriptive measures of effect size and significance, and all results are interpreted with this limitation in mind. The robustness of key findings was additionally supported by bootstrap‑based quantile comparisons, which do not rely on independence assumptions. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false‑discovery‑rate procedure51.

Analysis of the upper-tail exceedance frequency and extreme quantile amplification

Because changes in HW risk under anthropogenic climate forcing may be expressed disproportionately in the upper tail of the distribution, we complemented the statistical analyses with an upper-tail framework specifically designed to quantify changes in rare, high-impact HW conditions. The two contrasting far-future pathways, SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5, were compared against current baseline. This contrast was designed to evaluate whether strong mitigation and high-emission futures produce different changes in the probability and magnitude of upper-tail heatwave events. This framework is similar with previous works, who used percentile-based thresholds and probability-ratio concepts to assess changes in global high-temperature extremes53, and the role of upper-tail distributional changes in the intensification of future heat extremes around the globe54.
