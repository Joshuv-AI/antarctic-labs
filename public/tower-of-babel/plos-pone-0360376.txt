Geospatial suitability of cacao in Amazonas (Peru): An ensemble predictive modeling approach (BIOMOD2) integrating multisource data, territorial management, and plant health

Jhon A Zabaleta-Santisteban, Angel J Medina-Medina, Katerin M Tuesta-Trauco, Abner S Rivera-Fernandez, Teodoro B Silva-Melendez, Marlen A Grandez-Alberca, Julio Puscan-Rojas, Rolando Salas López

ABSTRACT
Identifying climatically suitable areas for cacao cultivation (Theobroma cacao L.) is essential to guide sustainable intensification, territorial planning, and compliance with emerging environmental standards in tropical regions. This study assessed the current climatic suitability of cacao in the Amazonas department (northwestern Peru) using an ensemble modeling approach implemented in biomod2, integrating bioclimatic, topographic, and edaphic predictors with spatial biophysical exclusion criteria, legal conservation constraints, regulatory eligibility associated with the European Union Deforestation-Free Products Regulation (EUDR), and local phytosanitary evidence. A total of 403 quality-controlled presence records were used to calibrate multiple machine-learning algorithms under cross-validation, retaining only those with high predictive performance (AUC > 0.9; TSS > 0.7). Decision tree–based models, particularly Random Forest and XGBoost, showed the best individual performance, while the consensus model exhibited greater spatial stability and overall accuracy. Results indicate that approximately 14.8% of the regional territory presents high climatic suitability under current conditions, concentrated in inter-Andean valleys and low- to mid-elevation zones, with thermal seasonality identified as the main environmental predictor. However, the incorporation of territorial and regulatory constraints substantially reduced the effectively available area, especially under the EUDR scenario, demonstrating that cacao expansion potential is strongly conditioned by conservation and regulatory frameworks. High-priority degraded areas emerged as focal opportunities for productive restoration through agroforestry systems. Phytosanitary validation (2021–2024) revealed high spatial concordance between areas of greatest climatic suitability and the prevalence and incidence of major cacao pathogens and pests, particularly Moniliophthora roreri and M. perniciosa, confirming that optimal climatic niches for cultivation also favor biotic pressure. Overall, this study provides an integrative framework combining climatic modeling, territorial management, and phytosanitary risk, delivering robust scientific inputs to inform public policy, land-use planning, and sustainable cacao production strategies in the Amazon.

Introduction Cacao ( Theobroma cacao L. ) is a strategic perennial crop in the tropics due to its economic importance and its contribution to rural livelihoods [ 1 ]. Globally, its value chain supports millions of people, with approximately 5 million producing families, predominantly smallholders, and an estimated cultivated area of about 11 million hectares across the humid tropics [ 2 – 4 ]. Nevertheless, persistent constraints remain, including low yields, technological gaps, market vulnerability, and high biotic pressure from pests and diseases [ 5 ]. Global supply remains strongly territorially concentrated, increasing the sensitivity of the value chain to environmental and economic disturbances [ 6 ]. In 2022, global production reached approximately 5,047,000 t (metric tons), with Africa contributing about 73.6% and the Americas around 20% [ 6 ]. This spatial dependence, together with heightened socio-environmental scrutiny, reinforces the need for verifiable geospatial information to support sustainable production management [ 7 – 10 ]. In Peru, cacao is produced predominantly under smallholder Amazonian farming systems and has consolidated as a relevant export commodity [ 11 , 12 ]. The country is also recognized for fine-flavor or aroma cacao, with approximately 75% of its exports reported within this premium segment [ 6 ]. Between 2019 and 2023, cacao ranked ninth in terms of contribution to the gross value of agricultural production, with annual volumes estimated at 141.8–166.7 thousand tons (3.3% of agricultural GDP) [ 13 ]. In 2023, the average export price reached USD 3.17/kg (+34% year-on-year), consistent with growing demand for differentiated products [ 14 ]. Peruvian cacao expansion has occurred in landscapes where the agricultural frontier has historically interacted with tropical forests, generating tensions between production, deforestation, and biodiversity conservation [ 15 , 16 ]. In this context, cacao is crucial for rural livelihoods, including Indigenous territories and peasant communities, and its performance depends primarily on smallholders [ 17 ]. Consequently, delineating areas for sustainable intensification and conservation-compatible expansion constitutes a strategic priority [ 16 ]. At the national level, the use of GIS, remote sensing, and agricultural suitability approaches has enabled the identification of potentially favorable areas for cacao cultivation, highlighting the Amazonian region as a core area for sustainable cacao production [ 18 , 19 ]. In particular, Amazonas exhibits high ecological and topographic heterogeneity—from high Andean gradients to upper and lower rainforest—configuring mosaics of microclimates and edaphic conditions relevant to diverse production systems [ 19 ]. In 2024, Amazonas recorded 4,124 tons, ranking as the seventh cacao-producing department nationwide and consolidating its status as an emerging region [ 20 ]. Phytosanitary pressure constitutes a critical risk component, with frosty pod rot ( Moniliophthora roreri ) and witches’ broom ( M. perniciosa ) standing out as widely documented limiting factors in the region [ 21 , 22 ]. Available evidence indicates that their potential distribution is modulated by environmental conditions, justifying the integration of the sanitary component into spatially based territorial assessments [ 23 , 24 ]. Recent reviews on management and biocontrol in Latin America further emphasize the need for preventive approaches supported by geospatial evidence [ 25 ]. Species distribution models (SDMs) allow estimation of present-day environmental suitability for cacao by integrating occurrence records with bioclimatic, edaphic, and topographic predictors, generating probabilistic suitability surfaces for GIS analyses [ 26 , 27 ]. Ensemble modeling approaches have gained prominence for improving predictive robustness and reducing uncertainty associated with individual models [ 26 , 28 ]. Within this framework, biomod2 facilitates the calibration and validation of multiple algorithms under a reproducible workflow, suitable for spatial applications [ 29 – 31 ]. Despite these advances, regional analyses that simultaneously integrate ensemble-based SDM suitability, biophysical and conservation constraints, territorial accessibility, and local evidence of phytosanitary pressure remain scarce, particularly in emerging regions such as Amazonas [ 18 , 25 ]. These gaps become more critical under Regulation (EU) 2023/1115, which requires traceability and proof of absence of deforestation and forest degradation after 31 December 2020 [ 32 , 33 ]. Accordingly, consistent geospatial information is required to guide expansion and intensification without compromising forests and biodiversity, while ensuring verifiability for supply chains [ 16 , 32 ]. In this context, the objective of this study is to model the current environmental suitability of cacao in Amazonas using an ensemble approach implemented in biomod2, integrating bioclimatic, topographic, and edaphic predictors with layers of accessibility, conservation status, land degradation, and local evidence of phytosanitary pressure. The results are expected to provide spatial inputs for land-use planning, prioritization of areas for sustainable intensification, and preventive management of sanitary risk under present conditions, in alignment with sustainability and traceability standards. Materials and methods Study area The department of Amazonas, located in northwestern Peru ( Fig 1 ), lies within a transition zone between the Andean cordillera and the Amazonian lowlands. This ecotonal position and its physiographic configuration explain the high ecological and geomorphological complexity of the territory [ 34 , 35 ]. Amazonas covers approximately 42,050 km 2 and is administratively organized into seven provinces—Bagua, Bongará, Chachapoyas, Condorcanqui, Luya, Rodríguez de Mendoza, and Utcubamba—according to the national political-administrative division [ 36 ]. The altitudinal range extends approximately from 152 to 5,540 m a.s.l., generating an environmental gradient from lowland rainforest conditions to high Andean mountain environments [ 37 , 38 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0360376.g001 Fig 1 Study area and cacao occurrence records in Amazonas, northwestern Peru. (a) Location of the department of Amazonas, elevation, and provincial boundaries. (b) Spatial distribution of the final cacao (Theobroma cacao L.) occurrence records retained for modeling after spatial filtering (n = 403). World country boundaries were obtained from Esri; departmental and provincial boundaries from the National Institute of Statistics and Informatics of Peru (INEI; https://ide.inei.gob.pe/#capas ); and elevation data from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation model provided by the United States Geological Survey (USGS). Cacao occurrence records were compiled from GBIF, iNaturalist, field surveys, agricultural cooperatives, and published sources, as described in the Materials and methods. Maps were prepared by the authors in QGIS. From the perspective of cacao cultivation and climatic suitability, low- to mid-elevation belts (≈200–1,200 m a.s.l.) are particularly relevant. These areas are mainly associated with the valleys of the Marañón, Utcubamba, Chinchipe, Nieva, Imaza, and Cenepa rivers, where agricultural and agroforestry systems are concentrated [ 34 ]. The climate encompasses warm-humid, warm-dry, and warm-temperate domains; temperatures range from temperate values at higher elevations to records approaching 40 °C in northern rainforest sectors. Annual precipitation exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity, ranging from less than 1,000 mm to more than 3,000 mm, primarily modulated by altitude and topography [ 39 – 41 ]. The biophysical landscape includes lowland forests, tropical dry forest, Andean forests and grasslands, and upper rainforest or yunga ecosystems (Atalaya et al., 2022), developed on predominantly acidic soils and exhibiting susceptibility to erosive processes on steep slopes [ 39 ]. Within this context, cacao has consolidated as a strategic crop for the rural economy and for productive restoration initiatives [ 19 ]. Cacao presence data Information on the spatial distribution of cacao in the department of Amazonas was compiled from multiple sources. Georeferenced presence records were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; https://www.gbif.org/ ) using the GBIF Occurrences plugin in QGIS 3.28 [ 19 , 24 ], and were complemented with records available on the iNaturalist platform ( https://www.inaturalist.org ). When some records lacked exact coordinates, these were manually determined through inspection of high-resolution satellite imagery in Google Earth Pro. In addition, a proprietary database was developed that integrated presence records collected in the field using GPS, information provided by agricultural cooperatives involved in cacao production, and data derived from specialized scientific publications [ 42 – 44 ]. In total, 843 georeferenced records were compiled, including geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) and the scientific name of the species. To reduce spatial autocorrelation and prevent overestimation in environmental suitability models, a systematic data-cleaning procedure was applied [ 45 ]. This involved removing duplicates and retaining only one record per 250-m grid cell, corresponding to the spatial resolution of the environmental variables used. After this process, the final dataset comprised 403 unique cacao presence records. Collinearity assessment and predictor selection Multicollinearity among the candidate environmental predictors was assessed through a sequential procedure combining Pearson correlation analysis and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). First, pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for the complete set of climatic, topographic, and edaphic variables ( Fig 2a ). To minimize redundancy among predictors, variables showing strong pairwise correlations (|r| ≥ 0.80) were progressively excluded [ 29 , 50 ]. This procedure resulted in a final subset of seven relatively independent predictors: coarse fragments (cfvo), temperature seasonality (bio04), silt content, bulk density (bdod), sand content, slope, and aspect. 10.1371/journal.pone.0360376.g002 Fig 2 Collinearity assessment and predictor selection. (a) Pearson correlation matrix. (b) VIF values of the selected predictors. (c) PCA of the retained environmental variables . Multicollinearity within the retained subset was subsequently evaluated using the VIF [ 51 , 52 ]. All selected predictors showed VIF values below 2 ( Fig 2b ), substantially lower than the commonly applied thresholds of 5 and 10 for identifying moderate and severe multicollinearity, respectively [ 53 , 54 ]. Therefore, all seven predictors were retained for subsequent species distribution modeling. Finally, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to the retained predictors to characterize their multivariate relationships and visualize their association with the main environmental gradients [ 55 ]. The first two principal components explained 31.8% and 22.4% of the total variance, respectively, accounting for 54.2% of the combined variance ( Fig 2c ). The PCA was used as an exploratory visualization of the structure among the selected predictors rather than as a transformation of the variables used in the subsequent modeling. Construction and evaluation of SDMs To estimate the current and future potential distribution of cacao under the SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5 scenarios, an ensemble species distribution modeling (SDM) approach was applied using the biomod2 package (v. 4.2-6-2) [ 28 ] in R (v. 4.5.1) [ 56 ]. Biomod2 enables the standardized integration of multiple SDM algorithms, enhancing predictive robustness by reducing reliance on a single method and synthesizing consensus across models [ 57 ]. In this study, twelve algorithms implemented in biomod2 were evaluated [ 27 , 58 ]: artificial neural networks (ANN), flexible discriminant analysis (FDA), classification tree analysis (CTA), generalized additive models (GAM), generalized boosted models (GBM), generalized linear models (GLM), maximum entropy (MAXENT), an alternative maximum entropy implementation (MAXNET), multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS), extreme gradient boosting (XGBOOST), random forest (RF), and species range envelope (SRE). Because these algorithms require balanced presence–absence data [ 51 , 59 , 60 ], random pseudo-absences were generated in numbers equal to the available cacao presence records [ 31 , 61 ]. For calibration and evaluation, the dataset was partitioned into 80% for training and 20% for validation, following commonly applied SDM practices to minimize overfitting and ensure generalization [ 62 ]. Each algorithm was run for 10 repetitions, and k-fold cross-validation was applied to reduce variance associated with a single data split [ 50 , 63 – 65 ]. Model predictive performance was assessed using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the ROC curve and the True Skill Statistic (TSS). AUC values close to 1.0 indicate high discriminatory capacity and are operationally classified as excellent (0.9–1.0), good (0.8–0.9), poor (0.6–0.7), or failed (<0.6) [ 30 , 65 , 66 ]. TSS simultaneously accounts for omission and commission errors and corrects for chance agreement, being interpreted as excellent (>0.8), good (0.6–0.8), moderate (0.4–0.6), poor (0.2–0.4), or failed (<0.2), with TSS > 0.6 commonly accepted as an adequacy threshold [ 64 , 67 ]. Only individual models with AUC > 0.9 and TSS > 0.7 were retained for ensemble construction [ 30 , 68 ], SRE and MAXNET were excluded for not meeting performance criteria. Consequently, the final ensemble comprised 100 individual models (10 algorithms × 10 repetitions). Prior to ensemble consolidation, predictor variable contributions were estimated. In addition, the build.clamping.mask option was used to spatially identify areas where projections involved extrapolation beyond the environmental domain represented by the training data [ 69 ]. The ensemble prediction was obtained as the mean probability across the selected models, producing a continuous suitability raster ranging from 0 to 1000, where higher values indicate greater suitability [ 62 , 70 ]. This raster was subsequently rescaled to 0–1 and reclassified into four suitability levels: unsuitable (0–0.2), low (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), and high (>0.6) [ 64 ]. This classification facilitates spatial interpretation and subsequent overlay analyses with exclusion and inclusion scenarios. Los mapas fueron elaborados por los autores en QGIS. Exclusion scenarios. Three cumulative exclusion scenarios were established to progressively restrict areas considered suitable for cacao cultivation. Scenario 1. Biophysical and infrastructure restrictions. Areas incompatible with agricultural establishment were excluded, including rivers, wetlands (bofedal), lakes and lagoons, palm swamps, and urban areas, based on the National Ecosystems Map [ 71 ]. Infrastructure restrictions associated with the national and departmental road network were also incorporated ( Fig 3b ). Scenario 2. Conservation restrictions. Scenario 2 incorporated all restrictions from Scenario 1 and additionally excluded areas subject to conservation constraints, including Protected Natural Areas and their buffer zones, Regional Conservation Areas, Reserved Zones, and Private Conservation Areas, according to official SERNANP spatial information [ 72 ] ( Fig 3b ). Scenario 3. Forest-cover and EUDR-related restrictions. Scenario 3 retained the restrictions applied in Scenario 2 and incorporated an additional forest-cover filter to account for requirements associated with deforestation-free production under Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 [ 32 ]. Forest-cover information from the Global Forest Cover Map 2020, Version 2, produced by the European Commission Joint Research Centre, was used for this purpose [ 73 ] ( Fig 3c ). Prioritization scenario. Scenario 4. Prioritization of degraded lands. Among areas remaining eligible after Scenario 3, highly degraded lands were identified as priority areas for productive restoration and potential expansion of cacao-based agroforestry systems ( Fig 3d ). Degradation information was obtained from the National Degraded Lands Map [ 74 , 75 ], which classifies land degradation into four severity categories (non-degraded, low, medium, and high) and identifies degradation processes such as vegetation-cover change (CVC), fragmentation, negative net primary productivity (NPP), forest loss, and combinations of these processes. This dataset has also been applied in previous restoration and land-suitability assessments [ 76 , 77 ]. In this study, only areas classified as highly degraded were considered for prioritization because they represent locations with greater restoration needs and potential for productive recovery. Their inclusion as priority areas was intended to guide cacao-based agroforestry expansion toward previously degraded landscapes while avoiding expansion into forested or legally restricted areas. Phytosanitary characterization and field validation Modeling of cacao climatic suitability was complemented with field validation through active epidemiological surveillance, aimed at contrasting the spatial signal of climatic suitability with observational evidence of biotic pressure [ 78 ]. Under the assumption that bioclimatic conditions optimizing the physiological performance of Theobroma cacao—particularly relatively stable temperature regimes and high humidity—also favor key processes in the epidemiology, sporulation, and dispersal of its main pathogens, a partial overlap between climatically suitable areas and hotspots of frosty pod rot and witches’ broom was anticipated [ 79 , 80 ]. Similarly, warm–humid environments may increase the likelihood of co-occurrence with economically important phytophagous pests, including Monalonion dissimulatum and the Antiteuchus spp . complex [ 81 , 82 ]. Accordingly, a longitudinal database (2021–2024) was consolidated for the three provinces with the greatest relevance for cacao production in Amazonas: Utcubamba, Bagua, and Condorcanqui. Stratified sampling was applied to characterize phytosanitary pressure in sentinel units (n = 25 farms per district) and to estimate spatial prevalence (percentage of affected production units), following epidemiological criteria for sampling design and incidence estimation [ 83 ]. To capture damage intensity at higher resolution, 100 plants were evaluated per plot, recording incidence and severity at the plant level using standardized visual scales and best quantification practices to minimize assessment bias [ 84 ]. The biotic stress agents considered included the main fungal diseases of regional importance—frosty pod rot ( Moniliophthora roreri ) and witches’ broom ( M. perniciosa )—as well as economically impactful entomological pests ( Monalonion dissimulatum and Antiteuchus spp. ) [ 79 – 82 ]. No specific institutional or governmental permits were required for the fieldwork because the phytosanitary assessments were conducted on privately owned cacao farms. Prior to entering each farm, the purpose of the field assessment was explained to the landowner, and verbal permission was obtained to access the property and conduct the phytosanitary evaluations This approach enabled direct comparison between climatic suitability and empirical evidence of phytosanitary pressure, strengthening the ecological interpretation and territorial applicability of the models. Results Predictive performance of algorithms and base-model selection The ten algorithms showed differences in predictive performance based on ROC–AUC and TSS values ( Fig 4A – 4C ). ROC–AUC values were generally high across algorithms, although ANN showed the lowest values and the greatest difference relative to the highest-performing models ( Fig 4a ). A similar pattern was observed for TSS, with RF and XGBoost showing the highest values across model replicates ( Fig 4b ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0360376.g004 Fig 4 Predictive performance of algorithms used to model cacao suitability in Amazonas, Peru. (a) Distribution of ROC–AUC values across model replicates. (b) Distribution of TSS values across model replicates. (c) Mean ROC–AUC and TSS values for each algorithm; dashed lines indicate reference thresholds of AUC = 0.90 and TSS = 0.70. Based on mean performance, RF achieved the highest scores for both ROC–AUC (0.99) and TSS (0.99), followed by XGBoost (AUC = 0.98; TSS = 0.91) ( Fig 4c ). GBM reached an AUC of 0.93 and a TSS of 0.74, whereas CTA, MARS, GLM, and MaxEnt showed AUC values ranging from 0.91 to 0.92 and TSS values between 0.69 and 0.76. FDA and GAM obtained AUC values of 0.90 and TSS values of 0.65, while ANN showed the lowest mean performance (AUC = 0.81; TSS = 0.63). According to the combined ROC–AUC and TSS performance, RF ranked highest among the evaluated algorithms and was selected as the base model for subsequent spatial analyses. Environmental predictor importance, ensemble performance, and spatial suitability patterns The relative importance of environmental predictors varied among algorithms, although temperature seasonality (bio04) consistently showed the highest contribution across all evaluated models ( Fig 5a ). Its importance ranged from 30.3% in RF to 87.9% in ANN, with particularly high contributions in CTA (68.0%), GBM (62.3%), MARS (61.8%), and XGBoost (62.1%). Among the remaining predictors, bulk density (bdod) showed comparatively high contributions in XGBoost (29.5%) and CTA (25.5%). Discussion The use of ensemble-based species distribution models (SDMs) has become a robust methodological approach for assessing the environmental suitability of perennial crops in tropical regions characterized by high climatic and topographic heterogeneity [ 26 , 59 , 85 ]. In this study, applying an ensemble framework through biomod2 enabled the integration of multiple algorithms and reduced uncertainty associated with reliance on a single predictive method—an advantage widely documented in recent assessments of tropical crops and forest species [ 51 , 57 , 63 ]. The marked superiority of Random Forest and XGBoost over parametric and semi-parametric algorithms is consistent with studies showing that decision-tree–based methods have greater capacity to capture nonlinear relationships and complex interaction effects among environmental predictors [ 58 , 68 ]. In particular, Random Forest has consistently demonstrated high performance in agricultural suitability and ecological niche modeling under abrupt environmental gradients, owing to its tolerance to noise and residual collinearity among variables [ 31 , 65 ]. By contrast, models such as GLM and GAM, while useful for ecological inference, tend to underestimate suitability in complex landscapes by assuming simpler functional relationships among predictors [ 64 , 67 ]. The improvement observed in consensus model performance metrics relative to individual models reinforces evidence that ensembles not only increase overall accuracy but also stabilize the spatial signal of predictions, reducing local artifacts associated with specific algorithms [ 27 , 28 ]. This stability is particularly relevant in regions such as Amazonas, where small topographic variations generate significant microclimatic contrasts that can amplify uncertainty in individual models [ 29 , 37 ]. From an ecophysiological perspective, the dominance of temperature seasonality (Bio04) as the main predictor of climatic suitability aligns with studies highlighting cacao’s sensitivity to annual thermal variability rather than to mean temperature values [ 2 , 18 ]. Relative thermal stability favors key processes such as continuous flowering, fruit development, and reduced physiological stress—critical elements for sustained productivity [ 3 , 86 ]. Similar results have been reported in suitability assessments for cacao and coffee in Latin America, where thermal seasonality emerges as a structural limiting factor under current conditions [ 87 , 88 ]. The secondary contribution of edaphic variables, particularly soil bulk density (bdod), suggests that while climate defines the general suitability framework, soil physical properties modulate productive viability at local scales. This pattern is well documented in studies integrating SDMs with soil variables, indicating that compacted or low-porosity soils can constrain root development and water-use efficiency even in climatically favorable environments [ 38 , 48 ]. Conversely, the low relative contribution of topographic variables such as slope and aspect supports the view that, at the regional scale analyzed, these factors act mainly as secondary modulators of local climate [ 19 , 37 ]. The proportion of high climatic suitability identified for Amazonas (≈14.8%) is comparable to estimates from previous studies in Peru and the western Amazon, although slightly lower than those obtained using less restrictive approaches to variable selection and validation [ 18 , 19 ]. This difference suggests that the conservative approach adopted here prioritizes predictive robustness over maximizing potential area—an approach recommended for territorial planning and public policy applications [ 51 , 85 ]. Incorporating exclusion scenarios reveals that a substantial fraction of climatically suitable areas overlaps with territories subject to legal and regulatory constraints, particularly those related to conservation and the European Union Deforestation-Free Products Regulation (EUDR). The drastic reduction of class S4 under the EUDR scenario is consistent with recent assessments indicating that agricultural expansion potential in the Amazon is increasingly constrained by international regulatory frameworks rather than by biophysical limitations [ 7 , 32 , 73 ]. This result underscores the need to integrate regulatory eligibility criteria from the earliest stages of productive planning, as proposed in recent studies on sustainable cacao supply chains [ 8 , 16 ]. The inclusion scenario for degraded areas, although spatially limited, is strategically relevant as it identifies opportunities for productive restoration through cacao-based agroforestry systems. This approach is consistent with global frameworks promoting the reconversion of degraded lands as a pathway to reconcile agricultural production, ecological restoration, and deforestation mitigation [ 74 , 76 ]. Recent studies in the Peruvian Amazon suggest that, although scarce, these areas can generate disproportionately high environmental and socioeconomic benefits when properly prioritized [ 9 , 19 ]. Phytosanitary validation provides empirical evidence that areas of higher climatic suitability for cacao coincide with increased pressure from pathogens and pests, confirming that climatic niches optimal for the crop also favor the epidemiology of agents such as Moniliophthora roreri and M. perniciosa [ 23 , 79 , 80 ]. This overlap has been previously reported in phytosanitary risk studies under current climatic conditions, where host climatic suitability correlates positively with pathogen establishment and persistence [ 21 , 24 ]. The persistence of frosty pod rot as the main phytosanitary constraint, even under a declining regional trend, is consistent with recent reviews identifying it as the most limiting cacao disease in Latin America, particularly in warm–humid environments [ 22 , 25 ]. Complementarily, the focal behavior of pests such as Monalonion dissimulatum and Antiteuchus spp. aligns with studies highlighting their strong dependence on microclimatic conditions and local agronomic management [ 81 , 82 ]. Overall, the results confirm that high climatic suitability does not necessarily imply lower sanitary risk; rather, it may amplify biotic pressure if not accompanied by preventive integrated management strategies. This conclusion reinforces recent proposals advocating the integration of climatic, productive, and phytosanitary information within a unified agroecological planning framework, particularly in contexts of regulated expansion and increasing sustainability requirements [ 7 , 25 , 78 ]. Conclusions This study identified marked spatial differences in current cacao suitability across Amazonas, Peru. High-suitability areas represented 14.8% of the department (6,184.86 km 2 ) and were mainly concentrated in Utcubamba (1,635.36 km 2 ), Bagua (1,502.05 km 2 ), and Condorcanqui (1,473.34 km 2 ). Temperature seasonality was the most influential environmental predictor, while ensemble modeling produced consistently high predictive performance. The progressive incorporation of territorial restrictions substantially reduced the area available for cacao cultivation, particularly after applying forest-cover criteria associated with EUDR requirements, under which high-suitability areas decreased to 4.6% of the departmental territory (1,918.35 km 2 ). Within the areas remaining suitable after these restrictions, only 46.28 km 2 of highly degraded land were identified as priority zones for cacao-based productive restoration. Phytosanitary monitoring also showed persistent disease pressure across cacao-producing districts of Amazonas; frosty pod rot remained the most prevalent disease, although its regional mean decreased from 96.3% in 2021 to 65.4% in 2024, while witches’ broom declined from 42.4% to 28.5%. Overall, the integration of environmental suitability, territorial restrictions, restoration opportunities, and phytosanitary conditions provides a spatial basis for prioritizing cacao production in areas that combine productive potential with conservation and deforestation-free requirements.
