Improved Torn Paper Coding via Local Alignment
Junsheng Liu and Netanel Raviv

Abstract
In the torn paper channel, a transmitted codeword is broken at random locations into fragments that arrive at the decoder in an unordered manner. A central theoretical challenge within this model is global alignment—the task of determining each fragment’s original position—in order to faithfully reconstruct the entire codeword. Prior work by Shomorony and Vahid introduced an interleaved-pilot scheme that successfully achieved a vanishing error probability. However, their alignment strategy relies heavily on global statistics, requiring fragments to exceed a minimum length and effectively discarding many shorter ones as erasures, which results in rates significantly below capacity. To address this gap, we propose an improved coding scheme that achieves a provable rate increase through a novel approach we call local alignment . This approach identifies global alignment bits within each fragment using only local information, allowing the decoder to determine the positions of fragments that are shorter than those used in previous work. Consequently, the decoder can extract information from a much larger fraction of the channel output than in previous work, yielding significantly higher rates. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to torn paper coding with lost pieces (TPC-LP), a generalized model that accounts for length-dependent fragment deletion. For a class of TPC-LP channels that delete all fragments below a logarithmic length threshold while allowing arbitrary length-dependent deletion probabilities for longer fragments, we show that the proposed local alignment strategy achieves an arbitrarily small additive gap to capacity as the threshold increases.

Consider a simple game: you write an information sequence on a long strip of paper, but before the receiver can read it, a friend plays a trick by tearing the paper into random, shuffled pieces.
While the text on each individual fragment remains perfectly legible, all context regarding their original order is completely lost.
To win the game and recover the message, you must find a way to piece the fragments back together, based only on the information written on each fragment.
This intuitive scenario captures the core mechanics of torn paper coding, a fundamental reconstruction problem in information and coding theory.

This reconstruction problem arises naturally in emerging data storage and transmission systems that are vulnerable to physical fragmentation, with DNA storage and forensic 3D fingerprinting providing key examples.
DNA storage has emerged as an important alternative for large-scale archival systems
[
4
,
5
,
3
,
1
]
, but its biochemical nature makes DNA molecules susceptible to random strand breaks. This vulnerability motivates the torn-paper coding problem studied herein, where information must be reconstructed from unordered fragments.
Beyond synthetic biology, this fragmentation problem also appears in physical embedding of data within 3D-printed objects.
To enable secure forensic identification, fingerprinting information is encoded directly into the physical structure of a 3D-printed model
[
15
]
.
However, if the object is broken—whether accidentally or through malicious tampering—the embedded data string is shattered.
An adversary might even conceal certain pieces, leaving the decoder to recover the information from an incomplete, unordered set of fragments.

These physical vulnerabilities—from degrading DNA to shattered 3D prints—lead to a natural coding-theoretic abstraction: an encoder transmits a codeword through a channel that breaks it into pieces, shuffles them, and potentially discards some. The objective is to reliably recover the encoded information from the surviving, unordered fragments.

Torn paper coding was initially proposed in the seminal work by Shomorony and Vahid
[
12
]
, which established the channel capacity and proposed initial encoding strategies.
This framework was later generalized by Ravi, Vahid and Shomorony
[
11
]
to account for lost pieces, i.e., where each fragment is independently discarded with a length-dependent probability.
In parallel, practical designs in
[
10
,
7
]
utilize nested codes to achieve high empirical rates; however, these heuristic approaches currently lack rigorous guarantees that the error probability vanishes as the codeword length approaches infinity.

Beyond torn paper coding, several adjacent models address related fragmentation challenges under different constraints.
Motivated specifically by 3D printing forensics, break-resilient codes
[
14
]
are designed to tolerate an adversary introducing up to a fixed number of breaks, ensuring that every resulting fragment carries enough positional information to allow full recovery.
Similarly,
[
9
]
studied a model in which the decoder must reconstruct the data from only a single, sufficiently large fragment.
The adversarial torn paper model of
[
2
]
approaches the problem by restricting the lengths of possible fragments, while the sliced channel
[
13
]
examines a setting where the sequence is partitioned into fixed-length slices that may suffer internal substitution or deletion errors.
While these adversarial and structured models provide valuable insights, in this paper, we focus on torn paper coding, where fragments are generated by random cuts.

Under these random cuts, the fundamental challenge is global alignment, i.e., recovering the original sequence from an unordered multiset of fragments.
To tackle this, prior work introduced an interleaved-pilot scheme
[
12
]
that embeds a De Bruijn pilot sequence periodically among the symbols of a randomly shifted erasure code.
Since sufficiently long fragments contain a sufficiently long portion of the pilot sequence,
the decoder is able to determine the absolute global position of such fragments.
However, to reliably distinguish the pilot sequence from other data within the fragment, the decoder must rely on global statistical uniqueness, which imposes a relatively large restriction on minimum fragment length.
Consequently, the decoder is forced to establish a minimum fragment length threshold, discarding any fragment that falls below it.
By treating a substantial volume of these shorter fragments as erasures, this approach leaves a significant gap in the achievable transmission rate.

To close this gap, we propose an improved coding scheme that shifts the alignment paradigm from global statistical uniqueness to local structural patterns.
Specifically, we impose a Run-Length Limited (RLL) block constraint on the erasure-coded information to strictly cap the maximum number of consecutive zeros.
We then strategically embed a sequence violation—a deterministic marker of consecutive zeros exceeding this cap—exclusively within the De Bruijn pilot sequence.
Since this embedded marker is mathematically prohibited from appearing anywhere in the erasure-coded information, the decoder can distinguish the pilot-sequence bits from the erasure-coded bits within any fragment that contains the marker.
This local alignment guarantee effectively
halves
the minimum required fragment length.
By safely extracting positional information from much shorter fragments without the need for long statistical uniqueness, our scheme utilizes a significantly larger fraction of the channel output, yielding strictly higher achievable rates compared to existing provable schemes, while maintaining a vanishing decoding error probability.

Furthermore, the advantages of this local alignment strategy extend naturally to torn paper coding with lost pieces (TPC-LP)

In this generalized model, fragments are independently discarded (or “deleted”) with a length-dependent probability, mirroring physical scenarios where shorter pieces are entirely lost.
Since our proposed encoding treats short fragments as erasures, it is inherently robust to such deletions.
We analytically demonstrate that our method not only guarantees reliable recovery under such fragment deletions, but for a specific parameter regime it achieves an arbitrarily small gap to the theoretical capacity of the TPC-LP.

II
Problem Definition and Previous Results

We consider the problem of encoding
a message in
\{0,1\}^{k}
into a codeword
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
During storage or transmission, the codeword is subject to random physical breaks between consecutive bits.
For each index
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
, a break occurs between
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
with probability
p_{n}
and no break with probability
1-p_{n}
, where
p_{n}
is a channel parameter.
The fragmentation process does not delete or alter any bits; it only introduces random cuts between consecutive positions.
More specifically, let
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d.
\operatorname{Geometric}(p_{n})
random variables representing the lengths of the broken pieces, and let
K
be the smallest index such that

\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}\geq n.

The channel tears the string
\mathbf{x}
into fragments
\vec{X}_{1},\vec{X}_{2},\ldots,\vec{X}_{K}
, where

\vec{X}_{i}\triangleq\left(x_{1+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}N_{j}},\dots,x_{\sum_{j=1}^{i}N_{j}}\right),

and the final fragment is bounded by the total length
n
:

\vec{X}_{K}\triangleq\left(x_{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}N_{j}},\dots,x_{n}\right).

The decoder receives the unordered multiset
\{\{\vec{X}_{1},\vec{X}_{2},\dots,\vec{X}_{K}\}\}
of all resulting fragments, and the goal is to design a family of binary codewords
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
such that the probability of decoding error—defined as
P_{e}=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\text{Pr}(\psi(\{\{\vec{X}_{j}\}\}_{j=1}^{K})\neq c)
for a decoding function
\psi
that maps the multiset of fragments back to the original codeword—vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
Clearly, for any practical use the function
\psi
must be efficiently computable.
The channel is illustrated in Figure
2
, where raw information is encoded into a string, the string is torn into pieces, and the decoder must decode the correct information based on the unordered set of substrings.

We assume that the received fragments retain their orientation, i.e., when receiving a fragment
(x_{i},\ldots,x_{i+\ell})
, the decoder does not confuse this fragment with its reverse
(x_{i+\ell},\ldots,x_{i})
.
This assumption reflects actual DNA storage settings, where the underlying biochemical directionality of the DNA strand dictates the correct reading direction
[
16
]
and is similarly mirrored in 3D-printing forensics where asymmetric inner codes and orientation-specific markers are embedded into the model’s geometry to break rotational symmetry and ensure unique readability
[
15
]
.
Thus, the orientation of each fragment is assumed to be known.

Fig. 2:
A sketch of torn paper coding.

The capacity of the associated channel (called the
torn paper channel)
has been studied in
[
12
]
.
They defined
\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}\log n
and proved that the capacity of this channel is
e^{-\alpha}
.
In the case that
p_{n}=o(\frac{1}{\log n})
, we have that
e^{-\alpha}
approaches
1
when
n\xrightarrow{}\infty
, and similarly, if
p_{n}=\omega(\frac{1}{\log n})
then
e^{-\alpha}
approaches
0
when
n\xrightarrow{}\infty
.
Thus, the remaining regime of interest is
p_{n}=\Theta(\frac{1}{\log n})
, i.e., where
\alpha
is constant.

Ref.
[
12
]
also shows that purely random codes achieve this capacity.
As purely random codes are impractical for encoding and decoding, the authors introduce an interleaving framework based on combining a De Bruijn sequence with a shifted pseudorandom erasure code.
In this framework, the De Bruijn sequence serves as a global pilot structure for fragment localization; however, this requires fragments to be long enough to successfully distinguish pilot markers from information bits. We provide technical details in Section
II-A
, and extend this idea using a technique we call
local alignment
in Section
III-B
.
This technique uses local structures within a fragment to identify the locations of De Bruijn sequence bits, and thereby relaxes the fragment length requirement and leads to higher rates.

Ref.
[
11
]
studied torn paper coding with lost pieces (TPC-LP), which extends torn paper coding to accommodate lost fragments by introducing a length-dependent deletion probability.
In this model, an encoded binary sequence first passes through the standard torn paper channel.
Then, each resulting fragment
\vec{X}_{i}\in\{\{\vec{X}_{1},\vec{X}_{2},\ldots,\vec{X}_{K}\}\}
is independently discarded with some probability
d(\cdot)
that depends on fragment length.
The channel output is the unordered multiset of the surviving fragments, once again assuming no internal bit-level errors (the full details of TPC-LP are given in Section
IV
).
As demonstrated in Section
IV
, our proposed coding method achieves an arbitrarily small gap to the capacity of the TPC-LP in a specific fragment deletion regime.

A complementary line of work studies practical torn paper coding schemes through empirical evaluation, but does not provide rigorous analytical guarantees, i.e., does not establish that the decoding error probability vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
For instance,
[
10
,
7
]
propose nested designs that achieve higher rates than
[
12
]
in practice.
Thus, while these approaches offer useful algorithmic constructions, their fundamental limits and provable performance remain largely open.

II-A

Interleaved-pilot Scheme

Since our method is similar to the interleaved pilot scheme of
[
12
]
, we introduce it here as the basis for our refined construction in Section
III-B
.
We begin with the definition of a De Bruijn sequence.

Definition 1
.

A
De Bruijn sequence
of order
\log n
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a binary sequence of length
n
in which every possible binary string of length
\log n
appears exactly once as a (possibly cyclic) contiguous substring.

For a fixed positive integer
m
, consider a De Bruijn sequence
\mathbf{p}
of length
n/m
, which we refer to as the
pilot
sequence.
According to Definition
1
, every sequence of length
\log(n/m)
appears in
\mathbf{p}
exactly once.
To construct the codebook, the authors of
[
12
]
interleave the pilot sequence with codewords of an erasure code.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
be an erasure code of length
n/m
and rate
R_{\mathrm{er}}
. They form a
shifted
version of this code by drawing an i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)
random vector in
\{0,1\}^{n/m}
and XORing it with each codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
, thereby obtaining the pseudo-random code
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
A key property required for alignment is that the pilot sequence substrings should not coincide with any substring of a randomly shifted erasure codeword as follows.

Observation 1
.

For any
\mathbf{s}\in\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
, the probability that
\mathbf{s}
shares a length-
k
substring with the pilot sequence is

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}[i:i+k-1]=\mathbf{s}[j:j+k-1]\text{ for any distinct }i,j\in[\tfrac{n}{m}-k]\Big)\leq(\tfrac{n}{m})^{2}2^{-k},

where the probability is taken over the choices of the random vector used to construct
\tilde{C}_{\text{er}}
from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
, and where
\mathbf{p}[i:i+k-1]
refers to the
i
-th through
(i+k-1)
-th bits of
\mathbf{p}
.

Therefore, if
k=(2+\delta)\log n
for some
\delta>0
, this probability goes to
0
when
n\xrightarrow{}\infty
.
This means that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a large enough
n
so that it is possible to choose at least
1-\epsilon
fraction of the codewords in
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
such that any
(2+\delta)\log n
segment in any of its codewords is distinct from any
(2+\delta)\log n
segment of the pilot sequence
\mathbf{p}
.

Let
S=\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{|S|}\}\subset\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
be a set of
(1-\epsilon)2^{\frac{n}{m}R_{\mathrm{er}}}
such codewords, i.e., where none of them shares any
(2+\delta)\log n
contiguous subsequence with
\mathbf{p}
.
Ref.
[
12
]
builds a codeword
\mathbf{c}
by taking
m-1
codewords from
S
and interleaving their symbols with symbols from
\mathbf{p}
.
More precisely, for any function
u:[m-1]\to[|S|]
, they build the codeword
\mathbf{c}_{u}=(\mathbf{c}_{u}[0],\mathbf{c}_{u}[1],\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{u}[n-1])
where for every
t\in\{0,1,\dots,n/m-1\}
, we have

\mathbf{c}_{u}[mt+j]=\begin{cases}\mathbf{p}[t],&j=0,\\
\mathbf{s}_{u(j)}[t],&j=1,2,3,\dots,m-1.\end{cases}

(1)

The resulting codebook is of size
|S|^{m-1}=(1-\epsilon)^{m-1}2^{(1-\frac{1}{m})nR_{\mathrm{er}}}
.

Subsequently, decoding begins by recovering the global alignment of the fragments through the periodically embedded pilot symbols drawn from the De Bruijn sequence
\mathbf{p}
.
Since a window of length
(2+\delta)\log n
from the pilot sequence coincides with a window of the shifted erasure code with probability which tends to zero as
n\to\infty
, every fragment whose length exceeds
(2+\delta)m\log n
must contain at least one pilot window of length
(2+\delta)\log n
that is uniquely identifiable.
Moreover, because every
\log(n/m)
window of the De Bruijn sequence
\mathbf{p}
is distinct, the occurrence of such a window immediately reveals the fragment’s absolute position in the codeword, a process we call
global alignment
.
Once these long fragments are anchored, the short fragments—those too small to contain any identifiable pilot window—are discarded, and the resulting missing positions are treated as erasures.
The reconstruction of the full message is then completed by the outer shifted-erasure code
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
, which deterministically corrects the erased locations created by the random breaking process.

Notice that in the interleaved-pilot scheme, the decoder identifies the locations of the pilot sequence bits only in sufficiently long fragments.
The rationale is that the probability of a long window of the De Bruijn sequence coinciding with a window of a shifted erasure codeword is vanishingly small.
However, this approach has an inherent gap: the number of bits required to ensure that a De Bruijn window is
not
identical to a shifted erasure window is approximately
2\log n
(Observation
1
), whereas the number of bits needed to uniquely identify a De Bruijn window is only
\log(n/m)
.
Consequently, the decoder in
[
12
]
restricts itself to fragments of length at least
(2+\delta)m\log n
, discarding the large number of shorter fragments, which reduces the effective rate since all such fragments are treated as erasures.

This observation motivates the question of whether one can identify the locations of the pilot sequence bits by exploiting the local pattern surrounding each De Bruijn window, rather than relying solely on long fragments.
Specifically, we embed a small deterministic marker into the De Bruijn sequence and actively prevent this marker from appearing within the erasure code.
By detecting these distinct structural patterns within any given fragment, we can distinguish the De Bruijn bits from the erasure-coded bits—a technique we term
local alignment
.
Once this pattern is identified, the De Bruijn window can be reliably inferred.
This enables the decoder to determine the global position of fragments whose lengths are well below
(2+\delta)m\log n
.

III
Improved Torn Paper Coding via Local Alignment

To implement this local alignment, our proposed scheme constrains both the erasure code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
and the De Bruijn sequence using a Run-Length Limited (RLL)
(0,k)
block code. We then strategically embed a sequence violation—specifically,
k+1
consecutive
0
’s—exclusively within the De Bruijn sequence to serve as a local alignment anchor.
Since this violation is strictly prohibited from occurring within the RLL-constrained
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
-codeword (denoted
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
), as well as from the remainder of the De Bruijn sequence, the marker remains entirely distinguishable.
Consequently, the modified De Bruijn sequence functions as a dual-purpose pilot: it acts as a delimiter to identify local boundaries within a fragment while simultaneously establishing global alignment.
This dual functionality ensures alignment integrity, facilitating the recovery of short segments that would otherwise be discarded, ultimately achieving a measurable increase in the overall information rate.

III-A

Run Length Limited Code

Since our method uses an RLL technique for local alignment, we introduce it here as the basis for our construction in Section
III-B
.
The codewords of an RLL code are binary sequences of a fixed length
n
, characterized by parameters
d
and
k
.
These parameters indicate the minimum and maximum number of
0
’s between consecutive
1
’s in the sequence.
For example, an
\text{RLL}(1,3)
code, also known as Modified Frequency Modulation (MFM)
[
6
]
, requires that between any two
1
’s there are at least
d=1
and at most
k=3

0
’s.
To meet the requirements of Section
III-B
which follows,
we require an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
scheme and an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-2)
scheme, which are obtained by insertion of
1
’s into the information words, and are introduced in the following simple lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

There exists an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
fixed-length block code with rate
\frac{k-1}{k}
.

Proof.

Consider a binary input word
\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{l}
, and assume for simplicity that
k-1
divides
l
(otherwise, pad the last block with at most
k-2
dummy bits, which does not affect the asymptotic rate).
Partition
\mathbf{v}
into
\frac{l}{k-1}
consecutive blocks of length
k-1
, say
\mathbf{v}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbf{v}^{(l/(k-1))}
, and append a
1
to every block:

\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\triangleq\mathbf{v}^{(i)}1,\qquad i=1,\dots,\frac{l}{k-1}.

The final codeword is the concatenation
\mathbf{x}\triangleq\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\cdots\mathbf{x}^{(l/(k-1))}
, and therefore has length
\frac{lk}{k-1}
, yielding rate
\frac{l}{lk/(k-1)}=\frac{k-1}{k}
.
Since every length-
k
block ends with a
1
, any
k
consecutive bits contain a
1
and hence any zero run has length at most
k-1
; in particular,
\mathbf{x}
satisfies the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
constraint.
Decoding simply reverses this construction: split the received word into length-
k
blocks and delete the last bit of each block.
∎

While Lemma
1
established the existence of an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
fixed-length block code with rate
\frac{k-1}{k}
, the following lemma introduces an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-2)
fixed-length block code achieving a rate of
\frac{k-2}{k}
.

Lemma 2
.

There exists an
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-2)
fixed-length block code with rate
\frac{k-2}{k}
.

Proof.

Consider a binary input word
\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{l}
, and assume for simplicity that
k-2
divides
l
(otherwise, pad the last block with at most
k-3
dummy bits, which does not affect the asymptotic rate).
Partition
\mathbf{v}
into
\frac{l}{k-2}
consecutive blocks of length
k-2
, say
\mathbf{v}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbf{v}^{(l/(k-2))}
, and prepend and append a
1
to each block:

\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\triangleq 1\mathbf{v}^{(i)}1,\qquad i=1,\dots,\frac{l}{k-2}.

The final codeword is the concatenation
\mathbf{x}\triangleq\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\cdots\mathbf{x}^{(l/(k-2))}
, and therefore has length
\frac{lk}{k-2}
, yielding rate
\frac{l}{lk/(k-2)}=\frac{k-2}{k}
.
Moreover, since every length-
k
block begins and ends with a
1
, any
k
consecutive bits contain two
1
’s and hence any zero run has length at most
k-2
; in particular,
\mathbf{x}
satisfies the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-2)
constraint.
Decoding simply reverses this construction: split the received word into length-
k
blocks and delete the first bit and the last bit of each block to recover
\mathbf{v}
.
∎

Lemmas
1
and
2
demonstrate two distinct methods for constructing fixed-length block codes that achieve an asymptotic rate of
1
as
k\to\infty
.
Furthermore, these two constructions are tailored to serve different purposes within the overall encoding process in Section
III-B
.
To simplify our exposition and explicitly distinguish between these two schemes, we adopt the following terminology:

•

We refer to the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
fixed-length block code with rate
\frac{k-1}{k}
from Lemma
1
as the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
code
, and its corresponding operations as the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-1)
encoder and decoder
.

•

We refer to the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,k-2)
fixed-length block code with rate
\frac{k-2}{k}
from Lemma
2
as the
\widetilde{\mathrm{RLL}}(0,k-2)
code
, and its corresponding operations as the
\widetilde{\mathrm{RLL}}(0,k-2)
encoder and decoder
.

III-B

Encoding

The encoding process starts with an inner code designed to handle fragment losses.
Once fragments are aligned, their missing lengths and positions are known, making this fundamentally an erasure channel (see proofs in Section
III-D
).
By employing a random binary linear code, we can recover these erasures efficiently in polynomial time—essentially by using Gaussian elimination to solve a system of linear equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
The parameters for this code are defined below.

Definition 2
.

Fix sufficiently small constants
\delta,\eta>0
and a constant positive integer
m
(a construction parameter that will be optimized later), and let
\gamma\triangleq(1+\delta)m
.
Further, let
n_{\mathrm{er}}\triangleq\frac{(\beta-1)n}{m\beta}
for
\beta=\beta(n)
such that
\beta=o(\log(n))
and
\beta=\omega(1)
.
We let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
be a
random binary linear code
with parity-check matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\mathrm{er}}}
whose entries are chosen independently and uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
where
r=\left(1-(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}+3\eta\right)n_{\mathrm{er}}
.

The following lemma proves that the matrix
H
achieves full row rank with high probability, thereby establishing the rate of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\mathrm{er}}}
be a random binary matrix
whose entries are chosen independently and uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
where
r\leq n_{\mathrm{er}}
. Then,
\operatorname{rank}(H)=r
with high probability.
Consequently, the random binary linear code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
defined by the
parity-check matrix
H
has rate

R_{\mathrm{er}}=1-\frac{r}{n_{\mathrm{er}}}
.
In particular, if

r=\left(1-(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}+3\eta\right)n_{\mathrm{er}}
,
then

R_{\mathrm{er}}=(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}-3\eta

with high probability.

Proof.

The matrix
H
fails to have full row rank only if some row lies in the span of
the previous rows. Conditioned on the first
i-1
rows being linearly
independent, their span contains
2^{i-1}
vectors out of the total

2^{n_{\mathrm{er}}}
vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{\mathrm{er}}}
. Hence the
probability that the
i
-th row lies in this span is

2^{i-1-n_{\mathrm{er}}}
.
By the union bound,

\Pr(\operatorname{rank}(H)<r)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}2^{i-1-n_{\mathrm{er}}}\leq 2^{r-n_{\mathrm{er}}}
.
Since

r-n_{\mathrm{er}}=-\left((\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}-3\eta\right)n_{\mathrm{er}}
,
the probability above tends to zero exponentially fast in

n_{\mathrm{er}}
, provided
\eta
is sufficiently small. Therefore,

\operatorname{rank}(H)=r
with high probability.
Finally, when
\operatorname{rank}(H)=r
, the null space of
H
has dimension

n_{\mathrm{er}}-r
, so the corresponding binary linear code has rate

1-r/n_{\mathrm{er}}
. Substituting the value of
r
gives

R_{\mathrm{er}}=(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}-3\eta
.
∎

With the foundational erasure code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
established, the next critical step in the encoding process is constructing the pilot sequence.
Let
\mathbf{q}
be a De Bruijn sequence of length
\frac{n}{m}(1-\frac{\beta}{(1+\delta/2)\log n})\frac{\beta-2}{\beta}
, where
\delta
is a small constant that satisfies
\delta>\frac{2}{\beta-2}
.
This sequence
\mathbf{q}
has the property that every sequence of length
\log(\frac{n(\beta-2)}{m\beta}(1-\frac{\beta}{(1+\delta/2)\log n}))
appears in
\mathbf{q}
exactly once.
Subsequently, we apply the
\widetilde{\text{RLL}}(0,\beta-2)
encoder from Lemma
2
to
\mathbf{q}
, yielding a modified De Bruijn sequence
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
of length
\frac{n}{m}(1-\frac{\beta}{(1+\delta/2)\log n})
.
For simplicity, we further assume that
\beta|(1+\delta/2)\log n
and define the modified pilot sequence
\mathbf{p}
as follows.

Definition 3
.

Define the pilot sequence
\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{p}[0],\mathbf{p}[1],\dots,\mathbf{p}[n/m-1])
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}[t(1+\delta/2)\log n+j]=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq j\leq\beta-1,\\
\bar{\mathbf{q}}[t(1+\delta/2)\log n+(j-\beta)],&\beta\leq j\leq(1+\delta/2)\log n-1\end{cases},

where
t\in\{0,1,\dots,\frac{n}{(1+\delta/2)m\log n}-1\}
and
j\in\{0,1,\dots,(1+\delta/2)\log n-1\}
.

In Definition
3
we systematically embed runs of
\beta
consecutive zeros, which we term
markers
, at fixed intervals throughout the pilot sequence
\mathbf{p}
.
The process of constructing
\mathbf{p}
from the De Bruijn sequence
\mathbf{q}
is therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt\widetilde{\text{RLL}}(0,\beta-2)\hskip 8.19447pt}\bar{\mathbf{q}}\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt\text{embed~$\beta$ $0$-runs}\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathbf{p}

(2)

Building on this process, we establish the following key property of
\mathbf{p}
.

Lemma 4
.

A run of
\beta
consecutive zeros in
\mathbf{p}
occurs if and only if it corresponds to an embedded marker.

Proof.

The “if” direction is immediate: every embedded marker is a
0
-run of length
\beta
, and therefore gives a run of
\beta
consecutive zeros in
\mathbf{p}
.
To prove the converse, suppose for contradiction that there exists a run

(\mathbf{p}[j],\mathbf{p}[j+1],\ldots,\mathbf{p}[j+\beta-1])=\mathbf{0}_{\beta},

where
\mathbf{0}_{\beta}
is a vector with
\beta
zero, that does not correspond to an embedded marker for some
j
.
We split into cases according to the position
j
from which this run originates.

Recall that
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
is obtained by the

\widetilde{\mathrm{RLL}}(0,\beta-2)
encoder, which maps every block

\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{\beta-2}
in
\mathbf{q}
to
(1,\mathbf{v},1)
.
As
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
is a concatenation of length-
\beta
blocks and each block begins and ends with a
1
, no length-
\beta
run of zeros can lie completely inside
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
.

It remains to consider a run which intersects a marker but is not exactly that marker.
Since the marker itself has length
\beta
, such a run must cross one of the two boundaries between a marker and bits that originate in
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
.
By the assumption that
\beta\mid(1+\delta/2)\log n
, the markers are aligned with the length-
\beta
block structure of
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
.
Therefore the symbol of
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
immediately before a marker is the final
1
of some block of the form

(1,\mathbf{v},1)
, and the symbol of
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
immediately after a marker is the initial
1
of some block of the same form.
Consequently, any length-
\beta
window that overlaps a marker but is not exactly the marker contains one of these boundary
1
s, and hence it cannot be equal to
\mathbf{0}_{\beta}
.
∎

Next, we apply the
\text{RLL}(0,\beta-1)
fixed-length block encoding algorithm from Lemma
1
to all codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
, yielding a code
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
; the block length of
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
is
n/m
and its rate is
(1-\frac{1}{\beta})R_{\mathrm{er}}
and hence its size is
2^{\frac{n}{m}(1-\frac{1}{\beta})R_{\mathrm{er}}}
.

To construct the final code, we interleave the pilot sequence
\mathbf{p}
with the codewords of
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
in a manner that resembles the interleaving from
[
12
]
given in Section
II-A
.
Rather than interleaving
m-1
codewords from a subset of a random coset of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
(which has no substring intersection with their pilot sequence) with a De Bruijn sequence as done in
[
12
]
, we interleave
m-1
codewords of
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
with our pilot sequence.

Formally, we fix an arbitrary indexing
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}=\{\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{|\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}|}\}
, and write our code as

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{c}_{v}=(\mathbf{c}_{v}[0],\mathbf{c}_{v}[1],\dots,\mathbf{c}_{v}[n-1])~|~v:[m-1]\to[|\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}|]\},

where for each
t\in\{0,1,\dots,n/m-1\}
,

\mathbf{c}_{v}[mt+j]=\begin{cases}\mathbf{p}[t],&j=0,\\
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{v(j)}[t],&j=1,2,3,\dots,m-1\end{cases}.

(3)

Since the encoding process above is injective (i.e.,
\mathbf{c}_{v}\neq\mathbf{c}_{v^{\prime}}
whenever
v\neq v^{\prime}
), the resulting code size is
|\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}|^{m-1}=(2^{\frac{n}{m}(1-\frac{1}{\beta})R_{\mathrm{er}}})^{m-1}=2^{(1-\frac{1}{\beta})(1-\frac{1}{m})nR_{\mathrm{er}}}
.
Since
\beta=o(\log(n))
and
\beta=\omega(1)
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)=1,

and hence the resulting rate tends to
\left(1-1/m\right)R_{\mathrm{er}}
as
n
tends to infinity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)R_{\mathrm{er}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)R_{\mathrm{er}}.

(4)

III-C

Decoding

The decoding process essentially reduces the torn paper channel into a standard erasure channel by exploiting the interleaved structure of codewords.
At a high level, this is achieved through a three-step procedure:

A

Local Alignment:
The decoder identifies the positions of the
\mathbf{p}
bits (see (
2

(1+\delta)m\log n
and discards all other fragments.
For each such fragment, this is done by first de-interleaving it to obtain sequences
\mathbf{w}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m-1}
, and identifying the unique
\mathbf{w}_{i}
which contains
0
-runs of length
\beta
as the one which contains bits from
\mathbf{p}
.

B

Global Alignment:
In each sufficiently long fragment, we remove the
0
-runs of length
\beta
from the respective
\mathbf{w}_{i}
found in step
A
, obtaining a substring from
\bar{\mathbf{q}}
, which is then decoded to obtain a substring of
\mathbf{q}
(again see (
2
)).
This substring of
\mathbf{q}
—being a De Bruijn subsequence—is used to determine the unique global position of the fragment.

C

RLL and Erasure Decoding:
After global alignment, we obtain an estimate
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{v}
of
\mathbf{c}_{v}
, and extract substrings of the codewords in
\bar{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
from

(see (

i\in[m-1]
, the bits of
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{v(i)}
appear in positions
\{mt+i|t\in\{0,1,\ldots,n/m-1\}\}
.
Furthermore, we identify the positions of the
1
’s added by the
\mathrm{RLL}(0,\beta-1)
encoder, as follows.
By Lemma
1
this encoder partitions each codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
into blocks of length
\beta-1
and appends a
1
to each block, and hence
the added
1
’s occur at positions

\beta-1,\,2\beta-1,\,3\beta-1,\dots.

Under interleaving, these correspond to global positions

\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{n/(m\beta)}\{m\ell\beta-(m-1),\,m\ell\beta-(m-2),\,\dots,\,m\ell\beta-1\},

which are removed by Algorithm
3
.
Consequently, for each
i\in[m-1]
, the resulting sequence
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(i)}
is obtained by deleting all RLL-added
1
’s from
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{v(i)}
, leaving only the original symbols of the codeword in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
Therefore, each
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(i)}
is a partially observed version of a codeword in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
∎

It remains to prove the correctness of step
C3
in which the estimated codewords
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(1)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(m-1)}
are corrected to the true codewords
\mathbf{r}_{v(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{v(m-1)}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
, which requires showing that the number of erasures in each
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(i)}
does not exceed the erasure correction capability of
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
.
This is shown, with high probability as
n\to\infty
, in Theorem
1
which follows, and hence the decoding succeeds with vanishing error probability.

III-D

Rate Calculation

In this section we complete the analysis of step
C3
by proving that the random binary linear code

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
successfully corrects the erasures induced by the torn paper channel.
We then use this guarantee to calculate the overall achievable rate.

We first emphasize that the torn paper channel is
not
memoryless.
The torn paper channel cuts the transmitted codeword into pieces, and therefore the discarded fragments typically
induce bursts of consecutive erasures. Consequently, standard coding tools and concentration bounds for the
memoryless binary erasure channel (BEC) cannot be applied directly.

To handle this difficulty, we analyze the specific erasure pattern induced in each
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
-codeword.
For a given codeword, we represent the erased coordinates by a (random) subset
E\subseteq[n_{\mathrm{er}}]
, where
n_{\mathrm{er}}
is the codeword length.
Recall that our encoding scheme splits the data into
m-1
codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
For each
i\in[m-1]
, the de-interleaved sequence
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(i)}
is a partially observed codeword of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
, where the erasures are caused by discarded fragments.
We decode these erased symbols using the standard erasure-decoding procedure for linear codes. Specifically,
for a code defined by a parity-check matrix

H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\mathrm{er}}}

(see Definition
2
and
Lemma
3
), decoding reduces to solving a linear system over

\mathbb{F}_{2}
for the missing bits using Gaussian elimination.
This procedure succeeds if and
only if the columns of
H
indexed by the erased positions are linearly independent.
Equivalently, for
\mathcal{E}\subseteq[n_{\text{er}}]
let
H_{\mathcal{E}}
denote the submatrix of
H
formed by the columns in
\mathcal{E}
; decoding succeeds if and only if
\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}})=|\mathcal{E}|
.
The probability of satisfying this condition is analyzed in Lemma
10
.

To prove that our construction succeeds despite the non-memoryless nature of the erasure channel, we proceed as follows.
First, we define a collection of
good erasure patterns
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}
, whose sizes are strictly
below the erasure-correction capability of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
. We then show that the actual erasure pattern induced
by the torn paper channel belongs to
\mathcal{T}
with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
(see Lemma
15
in the Appendix
B
).
Then, in Lemma
10
we prove that a randomly chosen parity-check matrix achieves vanishing decoding error probability for each fixed erasure pattern in
\mathcal{T}
.

To show that the decoding error vanishes for all erasure patterns in
\mathcal{T}
, let
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)
denote the decoding error probability for the
i
-th
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
-codeword when the parity-check matrix is
H
.
Rather than analyzing one deterministic matrix directly, we evaluate

\mathbb{E}_{H}\!\left[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\right],

where the expectation is taken over uniformly random parity-check matrices
H
.
We show that this expectation
vanishes as
n\to\infty
(see Lemma
11
).
With the help of Markov’s inequality, we then prove that a uniformly random choice of
H
achieves vanishing
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)
with high probability (see Lemma
12
).

After proving the decoding error vanishes for each individual
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
-codeword, we extend the argument to the full torn paper code.
Since the overall decoder fails if at least one of the
m-1
individual
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
codewords fails to decode correctly, we apply a union bound over the
m-1
codewords in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
Corollary
1
shows that, with probability tending to

1
as
n\to\infty
, the proposed encoding and decoding scheme has vanishing total error probability.
Finally,
Theorem
1
combines this vanishing decoding error with the
construction parameters to establish the achievable rate.

In what follows, for a fixed
\eta>0
we define the
set of good erasure patterns
(good set, for short), denoted by
\mathcal{T}
, as the collection of all possible erasure configurations over
[n_{\mathrm{er}}]
whose size is at most
(1-R_{\mathrm{er}}-\eta)n_{\mathrm{er}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\binom{[n_{\text{er}}]}{\leq(1-R_{\mathrm{er}}-\eta)n_{\mathrm{er}}}.

(5)

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
be the random binary linear code from Definition
2
, with parity-check matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\mathrm{er}}}
, where
r=(1-R_{\mathrm{er}})n_{\mathrm{er}}
.
For every fixed erasure pattern
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\Pr_{H}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}})\neq|\mathcal{E}|)\leq 2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}.

A standard proof for Lemma
10
is given in Appendix
A
.
We proceed to compute the expected error probability induced by a random parity-check matrix.

Lemma 11
.

For
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\mathrm{er}}}
, where
r=(1-R_{\mathrm{er}})n_{\mathrm{er}}
, let
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)
be as explained above.
The expected probability of decoding error over all uniformly random parity-check matrices vanishes, that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]=0.

Proof.

Let
E_{i}
be a random variable representing the set of erased coordinates in the
i
-th codeword
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{v(i)}
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
As explained earlier, an error occurs if and only if the columns of
H
corresponding to the erased positions are linearly dependent, and therefore

\displaystyle P_{e}^{(i)}(H)=\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\mathbf{1}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|)+\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\notin\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\mathbf{1}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|).

Since the value of
E_{i}
depends on the channel and not on the code construction, taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{H}[\cdot]
on both sides yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\mathbb{E}_{H}[\mathbf{1}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|)]+\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\notin\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\mathbb{E}_{H}[\mathbf{1}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\Pr_{H}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|)+\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\notin\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\Pr_{H}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}_{i}})\neq|\mathcal{E}_{i}|)

\displaystyle\overset{Lemma~\ref{lemma:random linear code random erasures}}{\leq}\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\cdot 2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}+\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\notin\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})\cdot 1

\displaystyle=2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\Pr(E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{i})+\Pr(E_{i}\notin\mathcal{T})\leq 2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}+\Pr(E_{i}\notin\mathcal{T}).

As explained in the proof of Lemma
10
,

2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}
goes to
0
exponentially fast as
n\to\infty
.
Furthermore, it is shown in Lemma
15
in Appendix
B
that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{i}\notin\mathcal{T})=0
. Therefore, the expected error probability for the
i
-th sub-codeword over the random code ensemble vanishes:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]

\displaystyle=0\qed

The following lemma applies Markov’s inequality to demonstrate that a randomly chosen parity-check matrix successfully decodes with high probability.

Lemma 12
.

With probability which goes to
1
as
n\to\infty
, a randomly chosen parity-check matrix
H

achieves a vanishing error probability for the
i
-th
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
codeword.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}=\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]
denote the expected probability of decoding error; by Lemma
11
we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}=0
.
By Markov’s inequality:

\Pr_{H}\left(P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}}=\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}.

Hence, to establish convergence in probability, fix any arbitrarily small constant
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}=0
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{\varepsilon}
so that for all
n>n_{\varepsilon}
we have
\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}<\varepsilon
.
For any such
n
, the condition
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\varepsilon
implies that
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}
, and therefore we can bound the probability as follows:

\Pr_{H}\left(P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\Pr_{H}\left(P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}.

Taking the limit of both sides yields:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr_{H}\left(P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}^{(i)}}=0.

Thus, a randomly chosen
H
guarantees
P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\to 0
in probability when
n\to\infty
.
∎

Having established the decoding error for each of the
m-1

\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
-codewords, we must now account for all codewords simultaneously.
Since the encoding scheme splits the data into
m-1
codewords from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
, an overall decoding failure occurs if even one of these codewords is decoded incorrectly.
Thus, we formalize the decoding error of torn paper channel using a union bound.

Corollary 1
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{\text{er}}}
be the parity-check matrix of
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
, and let
P_{e}^{\text{total}}(H)
denote the resulting decoding error of the encoding/decoding methods in Sections
III-B
and
III-C
.
A parity-check matrix
H
chosen uniformly at random achieves a vanishing total error probability as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

By the union bound, the total decoding error is bounded by the sum of the individual decoding errors of the
m-1
codewords:

P_{e}^{\text{total}}(H)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}P_{e}^{(i)}(H),

and taking the expectation over
H
implies that

\mathbb{E}_{H}\left[P_{e}^{\text{total}}(H)\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{H}\left[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)\right].

Recall that
\mathbb{E}_{H}[P_{e}^{(i)}(H)]\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
for every
i\in[m-1]
by Lemma
11
, and since
m
is a constant, it follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{H}\left[P_{e}^{\text{total}}(H)\right]=0.

Applying Markov’s inequality to this total expected error, identical to the procedure in Lemma
12
, guarantees that a randomly chosen
H
will successfully decode all
m-1
codewords simultaneously with high probability.
∎

Corollary
1
shows that with probability that goes to
1
as
n
goes to infinity, our encoding/decoding methods guarantee vanishing error probability.
The following theorem establishes the resulting rate.

Theorem 1
.

The rate of the encoding method in Section
III-B
is arbitrarily close to
(1-\frac{1}{m})(m\alpha+1)e^{-m\alpha}
for any fixed integer
m\geq 2
.

Proof.

The total rate of the construction is given by:

\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)R_{\mathrm{er}},

as shown in (
4
).
Since
\beta=\omega(1)
, we have
1-\frac{1}{\beta}\to 1
.
Furthermore, the parameter
\delta
(Definition
2
) can be chosen arbitrarily small subject to the constraint
\delta>\frac{2}{\beta-2}
from Lemma
8
.
According to Definition
2
and Lemma
3
, as
\eta\to 0
,
R_{\mathrm{er}}
approaches
(m\alpha+1)e^{-m\alpha}
, and the overall achievable rate approaches

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)(m\alpha+1)e^{-m\alpha}.

IV
Improved Torn Paper Coding with Lost Pieces via Local Alignment

Building upon the fragmentation model of the TPC, the TPC-LP framework accounts for the practical reality that some fragments may be lost during transmission.
As mentioned in Section
II
, the TPC-LP
[
11
]
models a scenario where each fragment
\vec{X}_{i}
is independently lost with probability
d(\cdot)
(a function of fragment length).
Consequently, the decoder receives only the unordered multiset of the surviving (error-free) fragments.

In our analysis, we specialize this model to a “hard-threshold” regime in the following sense—we consider a scenario where the preservation of a fragment is determined by its length relative to the total sequence length
n
; fragments failing to meet a logarithmic length threshold of
m\log n
for some constant
m
are discarded, while those exceeding this threshold are retained for decoding with probability function
1-d(\cdot)
.
This thresholding approach is physically natural, as very short fragments are highly susceptible to being lost during transmission or filtering processes, while longer fragments are more likely to be retained.
Building on this framework, we establish the following definition.

Definition 4
.

For an integer
m\geq 2
, a deletion probability function

d:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]
is called an
(
m\log n
)-threshold deletion function
if

\displaystyle d(\ell)

\displaystyle=1\text{ if }\qquad\ell<m\log n,\text{ and }

\displaystyle d(\ell)

\displaystyle\in[0,1)\text{ if }\qquad\ell\geq m\log n.

Equivalently, all fragments shorter than
m\log n
are deleted, while fragments
of length at least
m\log n
may be retained with arbitrary length-dependent
probability
1-d(\ell)
.

In what follows, we show that the scheme presented in Section
III-B
can be used in the TPC-LP, and later compute the corresponding rates that enable vanishing error probability.
In the TPC-LP model, after the torn-paper channel produces fragments of lengths

N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{K}
, each fragment is independently deleted with probability

d(N_{i})
.
By Definition
4
, every fragment with
length smaller than
m\log n
is deleted, while fragments of length at least
m\log n
may be retained with arbitrary length-dependent probability
1-d(N_{i})
.

The decoding procedure in Section
III-C
only uses
fragments whose lengths are sufficiently large for local and global alignment, while all other fragments are treated as erasures (see Algorithm
1
).
Therefore, deleting fragments below the threshold
m\log n
does not interfere with the decoding process, since such fragments are not used whether or not they are present.

For fragments of length at least
m\log n
, the deletion function may retain
them with any probability depending on their length; this only changes the set
of fragments available to the decoder.
In our TPC-LP scheme, the retained fragments that are long enough are processed exactly as in Section
III-C
:
local alignment identifies the pilot subsequence, global alignment places the fragment in its correct position in the codeword, and the remaining unavailable positions are treated as erasures in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{er}}
codewords.

Thus, the coding scheme in Section
III-B
can be used without any modification for a TPC-LP channel with
(m\log n)
-threshold deletion function, after choosing
R_{\text{er}}
so that the induced erasures in
C_{\text{er}}
could be corrected.
In turn, this choice of
R_{\text{er}}
depends on the deletion probabilities
d(\ell)
for
\ell\geq m\log n
.
Consequently, the same encoding and decoding scheme is valid for any threshold
deletion function: all deleted fragments are treated as
erasures, while all retained (sufficiently long) fragments are aligned and used
for decoding.
The capacity of the TPC-LP is as follows.

Corollary 2
.

[
11
, Corollary 2]

Assuming all limits exist and are finite, the capacity of the TPC-LP is

C=\alpha\int_{1}^{\infty}(\kappa-1)\left(1-\hat{d}(\kappa)\right)h(\kappa)\,d\kappa,

(13)

where
\hat{d}(\kappa)\triangleq\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\kappa\log n)
and
h(\kappa)\triangleq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(N_{i}=\kappa\log n)\log n
.

Theorem 2
.

For
m\geq 2
, let
d(\cdot)
be an
(m\log n)
-threshold deletion function (Definition
4
).
Assume that the normalized deletion function

\hat{d}(\kappa)\triangleq\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\kappa\log n)

exists for all
\kappa\geq m
.
Then, for sufficiently large
m
, the encoding scheme in
Section
III-B
achieves an arbitrarily small
additive gap to the TPC-LP capacity (
13
).

Proof.

By Corollary
2
, the TPC-LP capacity is

C=\alpha\int_{1}^{\infty}(\kappa-1)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa.

Since
d(\cdot)
is a threshold deletion function, all fragments shorter than

m\log n
are deleted, hence
\hat{d}(\kappa)=1
for
\kappa<m
, and therefore,

C=\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-1)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa.

For a torn paper channel with break probability
p_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}\log n=\alpha
, the fragment lengths
N_{i}
are i.i.d. geometric random variables with parameter
p_{n}
, and hence
\mathbb{E}[N_{i}]=\ell_{n}=1/p_{n}
. Using the definition of
h(\kappa)
from Corollary
2
, we calculate the asymptotic density:

\displaystyle h(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(N_{i}=\kappa\log n)\log n

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-p_{n})^{\kappa\log n-1}p_{n}\log n

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\log n}\right)^{\kappa\log n}\left(p_{n}\log n\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha e^{-\alpha\kappa}.

(6)

Thus,

C=\alpha^{2}\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-1)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))e^{-\alpha\kappa}\,d\kappa.

The decoder uses the retained fragments of length at least
m\log n
.
As in
[
11
, Definition 1]
, the asymptotic fraction of codeword coordinates covered by such retained fragments is denoted by

\displaystyle F_{d}(m\log n)\triangleq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}\mathbf{1}(\vec{X}_{i}\in Y,\;N_{i}\geq m\log n)\right].

(7)

where
\vec{X}_{i}\in Y
means that the fragment is retained by the TPC-LP channel.

By the definition of the indicator function
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
, we have that
\mathbf{1}(\vec{X}_{i}\in Y,\;N_{i}\geq m\log n)=\mathbf{1}(\vec{X}_{i}\in Y)\mathbf{1}(N_{i}\geq m\log n)
.
Furthermore, by Lemma
13
in Appendix
B
, the total number of fragments
K
tightly concentrates around
n/\ell_{n}
, where
\ell_{n}=\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]=1/p_{n}
.
Since the fragment lengths
N_{1},N_{2},\dots,N_{K}
are identically distributed, the expected value inside the sum in (
7
) is identical for all
i
.
Thus, we can drop the index
i
and evaluate the expectation using the first fragment
N_{1}
as a representative, multiplied by
n/\ell_{n}
.
Namely, we replace the sum over
K
with
n/\ell_{n}
copies of the expected value for
N_{1}
; the factor of
n
cancels out, and we obtain

\displaystyle F_{d}(m\log n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{1}\geq m\log n\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\vec{X}_{1}\in Y\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}a\,\Pr\left(N_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{1}\geq m\log n\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\vec{X}_{1}\in Y\}}=a\right).

(8)

For any
a\geq 1
, the event

N_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{1}\geq m\log n\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\vec{X}_{1}\in Y\}}=a

occurs if and only if

N_{1}=a\qquad\wedge\qquad a\geq m\log n\qquad\wedge\qquad\vec{X}_{1}\in Y.

Moreover, conditioned on
N_{1}=a
, the fragment
\vec{X}_{1}
is retained with probability

1-d(a)
. Hence,

\Pr\left(N_{1}=a,\vec{X}_{1}\in Y\right)=\Pr(N_{1}=a)(1-d(a)).

Substituting this into (
IV
) gives

\displaystyle F_{d}(m\log n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{a=m\log n}^{\infty}a\Pr\left(N_{1}=a,\vec{X}_{1}\in Y\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{a=m\log n}^{\infty}a(1-d(a))\Pr(N_{1}=a).

Let
\kappa=\frac{a}{\log n}
, and denote increments
\Delta\kappa=\frac{1}{\log n}
.
By the definition in Corollary
2
,

h(\kappa)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(N_{1}=\kappa\log n)\log n.

Equivalently, for sufficiently large
n
,

\Pr(N_{1}=\kappa\log n)=\frac{h(\kappa)}{\log n}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{d}(m\log n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{a=m\log n}^{\infty}a(1-d(a))\Pr(N_{1}=a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{\kappa\geq m}\kappa\log n\left(1-d(\kappa\log n)\right)\frac{h(\kappa)}{\log n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n}{\ell_{n}}\sum_{\kappa\geq m}\kappa\left(1-d(\kappa\log n)\right)h(\kappa)\frac{1}{\log n}.

Since
\ell_{n}=\mathbb{E}[N_{1}]=1/p_{n}
and
p_{n}\log n\to\alpha
, we have

\frac{\log n}{\ell_{n}}=p_{n}\log n\to\alpha.

Moreover,
d(\kappa\log n)\to\hat{d}(\kappa)
by assumption, and the sum above is a Riemann sum with increment size

\Delta\kappa=1/\log n
. Hence,

\displaystyle F_{d}(m\log n)=\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}\kappa(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa.

(9)

By the same erasure-reduction argument as in
Theorem
1
, the retained fragments induce an effective erasure channel on the codewords of

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
The asymptotic fraction of un-erased coordinates is
precisely
F_{d}(m\log n)
, and therefore the erasure code

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
may be chosen with rate arbitrarily close to

F_{d}(m\log n)
while still guaranteeing vanishing decoding error
probability.
Furthermore, only
m-1
of the
m
interleaved subsequences
carry information symbols, since the remaining subsequence is occupied by
the pilot sequence.
Hence the overall rate is reduced by the multiplicative
factor
(1-1/m)
.
As in Appendix
B
, the discarded fragments produce erasure patterns whose asymptotic coverage remains below the
decoding threshold of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
,
and therefore the same decoding analysis continues to hold in the TPC-LP setting.
Consequently, the achievable rate is

R_{\mathrm{ach}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}\kappa(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa.

We now compare this achieved rate to the TPC-LP capacity:

\displaystyle C-R_{\mathrm{ach}}

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-1)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa

\displaystyle\quad-\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}\kappa(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{m}^{\infty}\left(\kappa-1-\kappa+\frac{\kappa}{m}\right)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{m}\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-m)(1-\hat{d}(\kappa))h(\kappa)\,d\kappa.

Since
0\leq 1-\hat{d}(\kappa)\leq 1
and
h(\kappa)=\alpha e^{-\alpha\kappa}
(
IV
), it follows that

C-R_{\mathrm{ach}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{m}\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-m)\alpha e^{-\alpha\kappa}\,d\kappa.

Evaluating the integral yields

\frac{\alpha^{2}}{m}\int_{m}^{\infty}(\kappa-m)e^{-\alpha\kappa}\,d\kappa=\frac{e^{-\alpha m}}{m}.

Therefore,

0\leq C-R_{\mathrm{ach}}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha m}}{m}.

Hence, the difference
C-R_{\text{ach}}
vanishes as
m\to\infty
.
Hence, for sufficiently large

m
, the encoding scheme in Section
III-B
achieves
an arbitrarily small additive gap to the TPC-LP capacity.
∎

Appendix A
Omitted proof

Proof of Lemma
10
.

Fix
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{T}
, let
t=|\mathcal{E}|
,
and let
H_{\mathcal{E}}
denote the submatrix of
H
formed by the columns indexed by
\mathcal{E}
.
A binary linear code successfully decodes the erasure pattern
\mathcal{E}
if and only if the columns of
H_{\mathcal{E}}
are linearly independent, which is equivalent to
\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}})=t
.
Since the entries of
H
are chosen independently and uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we can analyze the linear independence of
H_{\mathcal{E}}
by building the submatrix column by column.
Conditioned on the first
i
columns being linearly independent, their span contains exactly
2^{i}
vectors out of the
2^{r}
vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
.
The probability that the
(i+1)
-th randomly chosen column belongs to this span (an individual failure) is
2^{i}/2^{r}=2^{i-r}
.

Since rank deficiency occurs if any individual column belongs to the span of its predecessors, we can apply the union bound to sum these individual failure probabilities.
Thus, the probability that
H_{\mathcal{E}}
is rank deficient is at most:

\Pr_{H}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}})\neq t)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}2^{i-r}<2^{t-r}.

Since
r=(1-R_{\mathrm{er}})n_{\mathrm{er}}
and
t\leq(1-R_{\mathrm{er}}-\eta)n_{\mathrm{er}}
, it follows that
t-r\leq-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}
, and hence we obtain the bound
\Pr_{H}(\operatorname{rank}(H_{\mathcal{E}})\neq|\mathcal{E}|)\leq 2^{-\eta n_{\mathrm{er}}}
.
Since
n_{\mathrm{er}}=\frac{(\beta-1)n}{m\beta}
where
\beta=o(\log(n))
,
\beta=\omega(1)
, and
m=O(1)
, we have that
n_{\mathrm{er}}\to\infty
whenever
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, since
\eta>0
, this failure probability tends to zero exponentially fast when
n\to\infty
.
∎

Appendix B
Proofs for good erasure patterns

In this section, we define a collection of
good erasure patterns
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}
, whose sizes are strictly
below the erasure-correction capability of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{er}}
.
We then show that the actual erasure pattern induced
by the torn paper channel belongs to
\mathcal{T}
with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
.
The following Definition
5
, Lemma
13
and Lemma
14
from
[
12
]
are used to prove Lemma
15
.

Definition 5
.

[
12
, Definition 1]

For a constant
\gamma
, the coverage
V_{\gamma}
is the fraction of output bits which reside in a fragment of length at least
\gamma\log n
, i.e.,

V_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}\geq\gamma\log n},

where
\mathbf{1}_{N_{i}\geq\gamma\log n}
is the indicator function of the event
N_{i}\geq\gamma\log n
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
K
be the smallest index such that

\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}\geq n,

where
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d.
\operatorname{Geometric}(p_{n})
random variables representing the lengths of broken fragments. Then

K=O\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)

with high probability.

Proof.

For each
t\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, let
B_{t}
be the indicator of the event that a break occurred between
x_{t}
and
x_{t+1}
. Then

B_{t}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p_{n}),

and the random variables
B_{1},\dots,B_{n-1}
are independent. Since every break
increases the number of fragments by one, we have

K=1+\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}B_{t}.

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}\log n=\alpha
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that,
for all sufficiently large
n
,

p_{n}\leq\frac{c}{\log n},

and hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}B_{t}\right]=(n-1)p_{n}\leq\frac{c(n-1)}{\log n}.

By Chernoff’s bound, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\Pr\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}B_{t}>2\frac{c(n-1)}{\log n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c(n-1)}{3\log n}\right),

which tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, with high probability,

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}B_{t}\leq 1+2\frac{c(n-1)}{\log n}=O\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right).\qed

Lemma 14
.

[

For a constant

satisfies the following two properties

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(\left|V_{\gamma}-(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}\right|>\epsilon\right)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[V_{\gamma}]=(\alpha\gamma+1)e^{-\alpha\gamma}.

Lemma 15
.

Consider the decoding procedure after global alignment (Step
B3
) and de-interleaving (Step
C2
).
For each

Let

Then, for any fixed

Let
