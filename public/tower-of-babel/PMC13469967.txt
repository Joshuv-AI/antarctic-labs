ZMYM3 mutations modulate histone acetylation and cooperate with NOTCH1 mutations in chronic lymphocytic leukemia∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•ZMYM3 variants are associated with NOTCH1 mutations and shorter time to first treatment, stratifying clinical outcomes of early-stage CLL.•ZMYM3 mutations reduce histone acetylation, decrease chromatin accessibility, and lead to widespread transcriptional repression.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a B-cell malignancy characterized by a complex landscape of genetic alterations that contribute to its heterogeneous clinical course.1, 2, 3 Recent large-scale next-generation sequencing (NGS) studies on patients with CLL have identified >200 potential genetic drivers in CLL.4, 5, 6 Within this genetic landscape, a subset of genes (NOTCH1, SF3B1, ATM, and TP53) is frequently mutated in >10% of CLL cases, and their clinical impact has been extensively explored.7, 8, 9 Although comprehensive analyses have begun to unravel the significance of rare drivers,4 the functional and prognostic implications of most variants occurring in <5% of CLL cases remain incompletely elucidated. Investigating these molecular alterations is critical for understanding the biological mechanisms underlying CLL pathogenesis and refining patient management.

Zinc-finger MYM-type 3 (ZMYM3) has emerged as a candidate gene potentially involved in CLL pathogenesis, with mutation frequencies varying between 1.2% and 4% of CLL cases at diagnosis.4, 5, 6,10,11 Located on the X chromosome (Xq13.1), ZMYM3 comprises 9 tandem zinc-finger domains and an N-terminal region that may interact with chromatin, particularly at DNA damage sites. Previous evidence has suggested a putative role of ZMYM3 in facilitating DNA lesion repair via homologous recombination, thereby contributing to genomic integrity preservation.12 In addition, ZMYM3 has been postulated to function as a scaffold protein that coordinates interactions within a transcriptional repressor complex involving histone deacetylases, demethylases, and other transcriptional regulators.13,14 Despite these hypotheses, there are no functional studies exploring the role of ZMYM3 in histone acetylation and the implications of its mutations on epigenetic and transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, the clinical impact and mechanisms linking ZMYM3 mutations to CLL pathobiology remain undetermined.

In this study, we describe ZMYM3 variants identified in a cohort of 487 CLL cases analyzed by NGS, underscoring the distinct mutational profile of patients with ZMYM3-mutated (ZMYM3MUT) CLL, characterized by a high co-occurrence of mutations in the NOTCH signaling pathway. Moreover, we demonstrate the clinical relevance of ZMYM3 mutational status, particularly in the management of early-stage CLL patients. Using both cell line–based and primary cell–based experiments, we provide transcriptomic and epigenomic evidence showing that ZMYM3 mutations dysregulate gene expression, impact histone acetylation, and modulate chromatin accessibility. Finally, we elucidate the functional implications of ZMYM3 mutations and their co-occurrence with NOTCH1 mutations, unveiling potential therapeutic vulnerabilities for patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 487 patients with CLL diagnosed according to the International Workshop on CLL guidelines15 were included from 16 Spanish centers. All samples were analyzed before the initiation of first-line treatment. Samples and associated clinical data were centrally analyzed at the Molecular Cytogenetics Unit of the Cancer Research Center in Salamanca, Spain. Clinical information was available for 409 patients (supplemental Table 1). The median follow-up was 74 months. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the Comité Ético de Investigación Clínica of the Hospital Universitario de Salamanca.

NGS

Genomic DNA was isolated from CD19+ B lymphocytes obtained from peripheral blood or bone marrow using magnetically activated cell sorting. The Agilent SureSelectQXT Target Enrichment system (Agilent Technologies) was used to capture exonic regions of 54 genes associated with CLL pathogenesis, forming a custom-designed NGS panel that has been previously validated16, 17, 18 (supplemental Table 2). Libraries were prepared using the SureSelectQXT Library Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform. The median sequencing depth was 600 reads per base, with 97% of target regions achieving ≥100× coverage. Details of variant calling are described in the supplemental Methods.

CRISPR/Cas9-edited cellular model

Single-guide RNAs targeting exon 2 of ZMYM3 and exon 34 of NOTCH1 (supplemental Table 3) were designed using CRISPick, cloned into the pLKO5.sgRNA.EFS.GFP vector (Addgene 57822), and transfected into the HG3 Cas9-expressing CLL cell line.19 For NOTCH1 mutation (p.P2514fs∗4), a single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide was designed. GFP+ cells were single-cell sorted, expanded, and screened for the presence of mutations. For each genotype, at least 3 independently derived single-cell clones were established and used in subsequent experiments.

RNA-seq

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on both CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells and primary CLL samples using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol. mRNA was isolated from 200 ng of total RNA, fragmented, and converted into complementary DNA. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 platform. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human genome using spliced transcripts alignment to a reference, with gene counts extracted by the HTSeq package. DESeq2 was used to obtain normalized counts and differentially expressed genes (false discovery rate [FDR], <0.05) were identified via the Wald test. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the cancer hallmark and Reactome gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database. A heat map of significant differentially expressed genes was generated using the ComplexHeatmap package in R.

ATAC-seq

Chromatin accessibility in CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells was evaluated following an established protocol by Buenrostro et al.20 Briefly, 50 000 cells were lysed, and isolated cell nuclei were incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes in transposition reaction mix (Illumina, FC-121-1030). Transposed DNA was purified (MinElute Kit, Qiagen) and amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (supplemental Table 4). Tagged DNA fragments were purified using AMPure XP beads, and library quality was assessed using the 4200 TapeStation (Agilent). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (2 × 75 bp read length). Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4) and processed using SAMtools. Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1), setting an FDR cutoff of <0.01. Read counts across consensus peaks were generated using featureCounts (v2.0.1). Differential peak analysis was performed using edgeR (v4.0.16).

Primary CLL samples

Thawed cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 100 patients with CLL were isolated using Ficoll-Paque Plus (GE Healthcare) and immunophenotypically assessed by flow cytometry. To minimize potential treatment-related bias, only pretreatment samples with a CD19+/CD5+ fraction of >85% were included in the study. Patient characteristics are summarized in supplemental Table 5.

For drug sensitivity assays, 1000 HS-5 stromal cells per well were precultured for 24 hours before the experiment. Thawed primary CLL cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1.5 μg/mL CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (Sigma-Aldrich), and 50 ng/mL interleukin-2 (Peprotech). CLL cells were seeded onto the HS-5 stromal layer at a ratio of 100:1. Posttreatment viability of CLL cells was evaluated after 48 hours using CellTiter-Glo (Promega, G7571).

Statistical analysis

Student's t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or one-way analysis of variance were performed using GraphPad Prism v8. Clinical impact analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics v23.0 and R (v4.2.2). Continuous and categorical variables were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test and χ2 test, respectively. Time to first treatment (TTFT) and overall survival (OS) were calculated from the date of diagnosis to treatment initiation or death, respectively. Survival outcomes were compared using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test. Cox regression was applied for multivariable TTFT analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Supplemental methods

Additional details on NGS, cell lines, proteomic analyses, drugs, reagents, RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analysis, histone acetylation assays, digital droplet PCR, and functional experiments are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Results

ZMYM3 mutations are loss-of-function, associated with NOTCH1 mutations and shorter TTFT in patients with CLL

A total of 32 ZMYM3 mutations were identified in 30 of 487 patients with CLL (6.2%; Table 1). Variant allele frequencies (VAFs) ranged from 1.7% to 94.2%, with 75% of variants (24/32) exhibiting a VAF >10% (Figure 1A). Most alterations (75%, 24/32) were classified as loss-of-function mutations, predominantly frameshift (∼70%, 22/32) and nonsense variants (2/32). Frameshift variants resulted from 1- or 2-nucleotide insertions or deletions, potentially introducing premature stop codons and generating truncated proteins. In addition, 8 missense mutations, bioinformatically predicted to be pathogenic, were mainly located within known functional domains. Variants were distributed throughout the coding sequence (Figure 1B), with the exception of a recurrent frameshift mutation at proline 48 (p.P48fs) detected in 6 patients. RNA-seq data analysis of patients from the CLLmap cohort4 revealed no significant differences in ZMYM3 expression based on patient sex or the specific type of ZMYM3 mutation (supplemental Figure 1A).

Patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL exhibited a median of 4 co-occurring mutations (range, 2-9). The most frequently altered genes were NOTCH1 (60%, 18/30), MGA (16.7%, 5/30), ATM (16.7%, 5/30), BIRC3 (16.7%, 5/30), and MED12 (13.3%, 4/30; Figure 1C). Interestingly, ZMYM3 mutations were significantly associated with alterations in NOTCH1 (P < .0001), MED12, SPEN, and MGA (P < .05). In the CLLmap cohort,4 40% of ZMYM3MUT patients (10/25) carried NOTCH1 mutations (Figure 1D), supporting the co-occurrence observed in our series. Remarkably, 70% of ZMYM3MUT patients (21/30) harbored mutations within the NOTCH signaling pathway, either in NOTCH1 or in negative regulators including FBXW7, MED12, and SPEN (Figure 1E), suggesting potential cooperation between these genetic events during CLL pathogenesis. Of note, patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL displayed a significantly higher burden of driver mutations compared with ZMYM3 wild-type (ZMYM3WT) patients (median, 4 vs 2; P < .0001; supplemental Figure 1B), an increase that remained significant within the IGHV-unmutated subgroup (P < .001). This complexity is comparable to genomically unstable subtypes like del(11q)/ATM-mutated or del(17p)/TP53-mutated (median, 4 vs 3 ZMYM3MUT in each subgroup; supplemental Figure 1C).21, 22, 23

The clinical relevance of ZMYM3 mutations was evaluated in 409 patients with available clinical data. ZMYM3 variants were more prevalent in IGHV-UM patients (P < .05) and were correlated with a higher treatment requirement compared with the control group (79.3% vs 54.4%; P < .01). No significant association was observed between the presence of ZMYM3 mutations and other clinical/molecular CLL features (supplemental Table 1).

Patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL had a shorter TTFT than patients with ZMYM3WT (median, 35 vs 53 months; P = .010; Figure 2A), a finding validated in the CLLmap cohort (median, 38 vs 88 months; P = .017; supplemental Figure 1D). Although failure-free survival was reduced, OS remained similar in our cohort and in the CLLmap cohort patients (supplemental Figure 1E-G). VAF stratification (cutoff = 10%) revealed that both clonal and subclonal ZMYM3 mutations were associated with a shorter TTFT (Figure 2B). Similarly, truncating and missense variants exhibited overlapping, shortened TTFT curves (supplemental Figure 2A). Importantly, ZMYM3 mutations stratified early-stage CLL outcomes, including Binet A (median, 48 vs 108 months; P = .002; Figure 2C) and Rai 0/1 (median, 48 vs 91 months; P = .016; supplemental Figure 2B). No significant changes were observed within cytogenetic or IGHV-UM subgroups (median, 34 vs 25 months; P = .445; supplemental Figure 2C-F). Time to second treatment did not differ significantly, with 65% of ZMYM3MUT patients requiring retreatment compared with 54% of patients with ZMYM3WT (supplemental Figure 2G).

Among NOTCH1-mutated (NOTCH1MUT) patients, co-occurring ZMYM3 variants were associated with a trend toward shorter TTFT (median, 34 vs 44 months; P = .099; supplemental Figure 3A-B). To infer clonal hierarchy, paired ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 VAF analysis was performed, stratified by sex because of ZMYM3 X-chromosome location, which revealed substantial interpatient heterogeneity. We identified distinct patterns of ZMYM3 dominance, NOTCH1 dominance, and clonal codominance (supplemental Figure 3C).

Subsequently, multivariable Cox regression incorporating IGHV status, Binet stage, cytogenetic abnormalities, and recurrently mutated genes confirmed the independent prognostic significance of ZMYM3 mutations for TTFT (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.15; P = .009; Figure 2D-E). This independent association remained significant even when restricting the analysis to Binet A patients (HR = 2.09; P = .047) and to the IGHV-UM subgroup (HR = 1.78; P = .047; supplemental Figure 4). Collectively, our results suggest ZMYM3 mutational status as a potential biomarker for predicting early treatment requirement in patients with CLL.

Co-occurrence of ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 mutations leads to transcriptional dysregulation in CLL cells

To explore the functional consequences of ZMYM3 mutations and their co-occurrence with NOTCH1 mutations, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to introduce the recurrent frameshift p.P48fs ZMYM3 mutation, identified in 6 patients, in HG3 cells. We successfully generated ZMYM3MUT clones (n = 4), harboring a p.D69fs mutation that introduces the identical premature stop codon as the p.P48fs variant, from wild-type (WT) cells (n = 4; supplemental Figure 5A). In parallel, we engineered NOTCH1MUT cells (n = 3) by introducing the most prevalent NOTCH1 mutation reported in CLL (p.P2514fs∗4). Finally, ZMYM3 mutations were introduced into NOTCH1MUT cells, generating HG3 clones which concurrently harbored ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 mutations (ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT, n = 3; supplemental Figure 5B). Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed that frameshift ZMYM3 mutations induce a protein loss-of-function (supplemental Figure 5C-D), demonstrating nuclear localization of the WT protein. In addition, increased levels of C-terminal NOTCH1 fragments were observed in both NOTCH1MUT and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells (supplemental Figure 5E), consistent with higher protein stability because of the loss of PEST domain.24

To characterize the transcriptional impact, we first performed RNA-seq on HG3 WT (n = 3), ZMYM3MUT (n = 4), NOTCH1MUT (n = 3), and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells (n = 3; Figure 3A). Figure 3B represents a heat map illustrating the differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log2 fold change| > 1; FDR < 0.05) identified between WT cells and ZMYM3MUT or ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, respectively. Additional comparisons are shown in supplemental Figure 6A-D and supplemental Table 6. Compared with WT cells, 155 and 83 DEGs were identified in ZMYM3MUT cells and NOTCH1MUT cells, respectively, whereas ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells showed a marked increase with 690 DEGs, underscoring that the co-occurrence of ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 mutations leads to a profound transcriptional dysregulation.

To uncover biological pathways affected by ZMYM3 mutations, we conducted GSEA. ZMYM3MUT cells exhibited significant upregulation of pathways related to histone acetylation and DNA repair mechanisms, including nonhomologous end joining, base excision repair, or the G2/M checkpoint. In contrast, pathways involved in immune system regulation and apoptosis were downregulated (Figure 3C; supplemental Figure 6E-F, supplemental Table 6). This pattern persisted in ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, emphasizing a pronounced impact on programmed cell death and immunomodulation (Figure 3D). Comparisons between ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT and NOTCH1MUT cells further highlighted apoptosis evasion driven by ZMYM3 mutations (Figure 3E; supplemental Figure 6G-H).

To assess the impact of ZMYM3 variants in patients with CLL and validate findings from CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells, we analyzed a published transcriptomic data set comprising 274 RNA-seq profiles from the CLLmap cohort (ZMYM3MUT, n = 12)4 and performed RNA-seq on 10 CLL samples from our cohort (ZMYM3MUT, n = 5; supplemental Table 5). To minimize genetic background biases, all samples were matched for IGHV-UM status,25 and both control and mutated groups included samples with NOTCH1 mutations. Consistent with findings in CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells, we identified that histone acetylation-related processes and DNA damage repair mechanisms, including nonhomologous end joining, double strand-break response, and base excision repair, were among the enriched pathways in both CLLmap patients (Figure 3F) and the Salamanca cohort (Figure 3G; supplemental Figure 7). These transcriptomic patterns are consistent with the previously described roles of ZMYM3 in DNA damage repair mechanisms12 and indicate an impact of ZMYM3 mutations on histone acetylation-related pathways.

ZMYM3 mutations modulate chromatin accessibility by histone hypoacetylation

To investigate the ZMYM3 interaction network in CLL, we performed global proteomic profiling of primary CLL cells using data-independent acquisition coupled with parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation mass spectrometry.26,27 Rank-abundance analysis of the total proteome revealed high expression of chromatin-modifying proteins (Figure 4A). Specifically, the most abundant proteins comprised several components of the BRAF-HDAC complex,28,29 including the histone deacetylases HDAC1/2, the demethylase KDM1A, and the scaffolding protein RCOR1. Furthermore, paralogous zinc-finger proteins (ZMYM2/4), alongside additional deacetylases (HDAC3/6) were also detected. These findings expand the role of ZMYM3 within histone-deacetylase multiprotein complexes.13

Driven by this interaction, we investigated the transcriptional and epigenetic consequences of ZMYM3 mutations. A detailed examination of gene expression changes revealed a predominance of downregulated DEGs in both ZMYM3MUT (117 downregulated and 38 upregulated) and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells (458 downregulated and 232 upregulated) compared with WT cells. Notably, GSEA also identified a significant enrichment of histone-deacetylation processes in both ZMYM3MUT CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells and primary patient samples (Figure 4B; supplemental Table 7). Functionally, ZMYM3MUT and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT HG3 cells exhibited reduced global histone H4 acetylation levels (P < .05; Figure 4C), whereas histone H3 acetylation remained unaltered (supplemental Figure 8A). Histone H4 hypoacetylation was further validated in primary CLL ZMYM3MUT samples (WT, n = 7; ZMYM3MUT, n = 5; Figure 4D), linking the transcriptional downregulation to epigenetic alterations.

ATAC-seq was performed to link histone acetylation changes to chromatin conformation alterations. Differential accessibility analysis revealed 381 open chromatin regions (|log2 fold change| > 1; P < .01) that were exclusive to WT cells, whereas 1 and 9 peaks were detected solely in ZMYM3MUT or ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, respectively (Figure 4E; supplemental Table 8), suggesting that ZMYM3 mutations reduce chromatin accessibility. Interestingly, 83 regions that gained chromatin accessibility, shared between ZMYM3MUT and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, were enriched for IRF transcription factor binding sites (Figure 4F; supplemental Figure 8B). Subsequently, we integrated ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data to explore the interplay between chromatin accessibility and gene expression. Among the 381 regions with increased accessibility in WT cells, 125 genes were downregulated and 51 were upregulated in ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, whereas 205 peaks showed no significant gene expression changes. The 125 downregulated genes were predominantly involved in cell cycle regulation (CCNA1, CDC14A), MAPK signaling, or caspase pathway (LMNA, MGMT) (Figure 4G; supplemental Figure 8C; supplemental Table 9). These findings may underscore a ZMYM3-dependent intricate relationship between chromatin accessibility dynamics, histone acetylation, and transcriptional regulation.

Co-occurrence of ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 mutations promotes apoptosis evasion and potentiates growth capacity of CLL cells

Given that transcriptomic analyses identified apoptosis-related pathways among the most significantly dysregulated processes in ZMYM3MUT cells, we next assessed the impact of ZMYM3 mutations and their co-occurrence with NOTCH1 mutations on programmed cell death. ZMYM3MUT cells showed reduced annexin V+/PI+ events compared with WT cells (Figure 5A; supplemental Figure 9A), with an even more pronounced effect in ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells, suggesting cooperative apoptosis evasion. These cells also showed enhanced growth rates (Figure 5B), likely because of reduced apoptosis, as carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester staining indicated no effect on proliferation (supplemental Figure 9B).

Mechanistically, western blot analysis revealed decreased levels of extrinsic apoptotic proteins, including caspase-8, -3, and -7, and cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase in ZMYM3MUT cells (Figure 5C). Consistently, digital droplet PCR confirmed downregulation of caspase-7 and -8 in primary ZMYM3MUT CLL cells (Figure 5D), whereas RNA-seq showed downregulation of caspase-7 in ZMYM3MUT samples from the CLLmap cohort participants (Figure 5E). In addition, ZMYM3MUT cells exhibited moderately higher levels of the antiapoptotic proteins BCL2 and, to a lesser extent, BCL-XL and MCL1 (Figure 5F), whereas no significant changes were observed in proapoptotic proteins BIM, BAK, BAX, BAD, or NOXA (supplemental Figure 9C). Collectively, this imbalanced expression of apoptotic regulatory factors could potentially contribute to the antiapoptotic phenotype observed in ZMYM3MUT cells.

ZMYM3 mutations sensitize primary CLL cells to BCL-XL inhibition and ibrutinib

To define the drug response profile of patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL, we conducted a drug screen using clinically approved therapies on 100 untreated CLL primary samples (n = 91, control; n = 9, ZMYM3MUT; supplemental Table 5). The panel included the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax, BTK inhibitors ibrutinib and acalabrutinib, PI3K inhibitors idelalisib and duvelisib, and the chemotherapeutic agent fludarabine, each tested at 3 optimized doses. In addition, a subset of samples (n = 11) was evaluated with the BCL-XL inhibitor A-1331852. Drug responses were assessed after 48 hours in both monoculture and coculture settings with stromal stimulation, using HS-5 cells, CpG, and interleukin-219,30 (Figure 6A; supplemental Figure 10). In monoculture, primary ZMYM3MUT cells exhibited significant resistance to venetoclax in the entire cohort and within IGHV-UM subgroup (supplemental Figure 11). Moreover, ZMYM3MUT samples lacking NOTCH pathway alterations were more resistant to venetoclax. ZMYM3 mutations also increased venetoclax resistance among NOTCH1MUT samples, whereas ZMYM3 variants did not influence the response of TP53-mutated samples.

Although ZMYM3 mutations did not impact responses to acalabrutinib, idelalisib, duvelisib, and fludarabine, ZMYM3MUT CLL samples displayed increased sensitivity to ibrutinib in coculture (Figure 6A). Further exploration using CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells showed that ZMYM3MUT cells were more sensitive to the BCL-XL inhibitor A-1331852, a finding subsequently validated in primary ZMYM3MUT CLL cells under stromal stimulation (Figure 6B). These results suggest that ZMYM3MUT cells are more dependent on BCL-XL for survival, highlighting the potential clinical relevance of BCL-XL inhibition in the context of ZMYM3 mutations.

Discussion

ZMYM3 has emerged as a putative genetic driver of CLL in independent NGS studies.4, 5, 6 This work presents novel insights into the clinical significance of ZMYM3 variants, demonstrating their association with NOTCH1 mutations and their potential prognostic value in CLL. Using CRISPR/Cas9 models along with primary patient samples, this study highlights the function of ZMYM3 in regulating histone acetylation and gene expression. Moreover, ZMYM3 mutations compromise DNA damage response and promote apoptosis evasion, providing mechanistic insight into their role in CLL pathogenesis and offering potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

This study characterizes ZMYM3 variants in CLL, revealing that 75% of variants were truncating (frameshift and nonsense), whereas missense variants clustered within functional domains, thus proposing a tumor suppressor role for ZMYM3. These mutations do not affect transcript levels, indicating that protein dysfunction underlies their pathogenic effect. The recurrent frameshift mutation p.P48fs, previously reported in CLL4,31 and other cancer types,32, 33, 34, 35 suggests its potential as a mutational hot spot. In addition, patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL exhibit a distinct mutational profile enriched for NOTCH1 mutations, consistent with previous observations.11 Notably, paired VAF analyses identified ZMYM3 mutations within dominant or codominant clones, suggesting that ZMYM3 alterations are not merely late passenger events.

By integrating molecular insights with clinical outcomes, we observed that ZMYM3 variants were independently associated with shorter TTFT in multivariable analyses across the overall cohort, including Binet A and IGHV-UM patients. These results highlight ZMYM3 mutations as a biomarker to refine risk stratification by identifying patients with an earlier treatment requirement. Importantly, ZMYM3 mutations did not impact OS, suggesting that ZMYM3MUT patients require earlier treatment but respond comparably to nonmutated patients. This observation was consistent with our ex vivo data. Validation of these findings in independent cohorts is warranted.

Although a previous study had suggested that ZMYM3 may function in chromatin regulation,13 its precise role in CLL remains elusive. Functional studies using CRISPR/Cas9-edited cells, complemented with ex vivo assays and transcriptome analysis from 2 independent cohorts to overcome limitations of Epstein-Barr virus-transformed cell lines,36 revealed that ZMYM3 mutations dysregulate histone acetylation. To our knowledge, this study provides the first proteomic evidence in CLL supporting the association of ZMYM3 with histone-deacetylases complexes. Because histone acetylation is essential for gene expression through promoting open chromatin,37, 38, 39, 40 the involvement of ZMYM3 in this corepressor network provides mechanistic explanation for how ZMYM3 mutations drive a global histone H4 hypoacetylation and enforce a compacted chromatin conformation that results in transcriptional repression, with cooperative effects when ZMYM3 and NOTCH1 mutations co-occur.

Previous studies have reported that CLL cells display histone hypoacetylation and upregulation of histone-deacetylase–complexes compared with normal B cells, which are features associated with adverse clinical outcomes.41, 42, 43, 44, 45 Our results demonstrate how a CLL-driver mutation impacts histone acetylation, expanding our understanding of functional consequences resulting from chromatin remodeling alterations,4, 5, 6 and highlighting histone acetylation as particularly relevant in CLL. Moreover, ZMYM3 mutations may also display regulatory potential, as ZMYM3MUT and ZMYM3MUT NOTCH1MUT cells are enriched in IRF transcription factor motifs, a family associated with the IGHV-UM subtype.46

ZMYM3 was identified as a chromatin-interacting protein involved in maintaining genome integrity by binding to double-stranded DNA, H2A/H2AX, and BRCA1 complex recruitment during homologous recombination repair.12,47 In our study, this described role was reflected at the transcriptomic level in both cellular models and patients with CLL, establishing an impact of ZMYM3 disruption in DNA damage-related pathways. Our findings extend these observations by identifying apoptosis evasion as a biologically relevant consequence of ZMYM3 mutations in CLL, with caspase downregulation emerging as a potential molecular basis for the reduced basal apoptosis observed in ZMYM3MUT cells. Therapeutically, ex vivo experiments indicated that primary ZMYM3MUT CLL cells are more dependent on BCL-XL than BCL2 which, together with the co-occurrence with NOTCH1 mutations48,49 and overexpression of antiapoptotic proteins,50, 51, 52, 53, 54 may contribute to the observed venetoclax resistance. Mechanistically, this apoptotic shift suggests BCL-XL targeting as a strategy to overcome venetoclax resistance, positioning BCL-XL–targeting approaches and BTK inhibition as promising therapeutic strategies for patients with ZMYM3MUT CLL. Further studies are required to explore the broader implications of these results.

In conclusion, our work provides insights into the clinical and biological implications of ZMYM3 mutations in CLL through the integration of clinical data from patients with CLL and in vitro and ex vivo models. We show that ZMYM3 mutational status may refine outcomes of early-stage CLL patients. In addition, we uncovered that ZMYM3 mutations associate and cooperate with NOTCH1 alterations, leading to transcriptional dysregulation. Our study demonstrates that ZMYM3 variants reduce histone acetylation and modulate chromatin accessibility, resulting in the downregulation of key processes involved in CLL pathobiology such as apoptosis, underscoring the relevance of chromatin remodeling and histone acetylation in CLL development. Collectively, this study enhances our understanding of the heterogeneous genetic landscape of CLL and may contribute to the development of personalized medicine, facilitating the optimization of treatment strategies.
