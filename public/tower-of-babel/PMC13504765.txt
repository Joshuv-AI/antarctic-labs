Background

Urolithiasis is a prevalent urological disorder globally, imposing a significant socioeconomic and healthcare burden due to its high incidence and recurrence rates [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of kidney stones is complex and multifactorial, involving an interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors, particularly dietary habits [3, 4]. Clinical guidelines generally recommend dietary modifications, including increased fluid intake and sodium restriction, for the secondary prevention of urolithiasis [5, 6].

Observational studies have suggested a positive correlation between high dietary salt intake and nephrolithiasis [7, 8]. The physiological hypothesis for this association lies in the sodium-calcium coupled transport mechanism in the proximal renal tubule, where increased sodium excretion can lead to decreased calcium reabsorption, resulting in hypercalciuria—a primary driver of calcium oxalate stone formation [9, 10].

Previous observational and Mendelian randomization studies have suggested a potential relationship between sodium-related traits and kidney stone disease [11, 12]. However, evidence specifically focusing on urinary sodium excretion remains limited and requires further validation. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an analytical method that utilizes genetic variants, typically single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables to infer potential associations between an exposure and an outcome [13, 14]. Because genetic alleles are randomly allocated at conception, MR is less susceptible to environmental confounders and reverse causation [15]. In this study, we employed a two-sample MR approach using summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to evaluate whether genetically predicted urinary sodium excretion is associated with urolithiasis risk.

Methods

This study utilized a two-sample MR framework, which was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines. A valid MR analysis relies on three core assumptions: (1) the genetic instruments are strongly associated with the exposure (urinary sodium); (2) the instruments are independent of potential confounders; and (3) the instruments affect the outcome (urolithiasis) solely through the exposure pathway, without horizontal pleiotropy [16].

Data sources

Summary-level genetic data for urinary sodium excretion were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90013989, encompassing individuals of European descent, n = 396,020). For the replication analysis, a secondary dataset (GWAS ID: ukb-a-335, n = 326,831) derived from the UK Biobank resource [17] but representing a different data release within the OpenGWAS project was utilized. Genetic association data for urolithiasis were extracted from the FinnGen Consortium [18] (GWAS ID: finn-b-N14_UROLITHIASIS), which includes a total of 218,792 individuals (5,347 clinically diagnosed urolithiasis cases and 213,445 healthy controls).

Selection of instrumental variables

SNPs significantly associated with urinary sodium at the genome-wide significance threshold (P< 5×10−8) were selected as instrumental variables. To ensure the independence of the selected SNPs, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed (r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window) [19] utilizing European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 as the reference panel. Palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded during the harmonization process.

Statistical analysis

The primary causal estimate was calculated using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method [20]. To ensure robustness, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed, including MR-Egger regression and the weighted median method [21, 22]. Heterogeneity among SNPs was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic. Directional pleiotropy was evaluated via the MR-Egger intercept test. The MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) framework was applied to detect and correct for potential outlier SNPs [23]. Furthermore, MR Steiger filtering was applied to confirm the hypothesized causal direction. All analyses were conducted using the "TwoSampleMR" (version 0.7.0) and "MRPRESSO" packages in R software.

Results

Instrumental variables

After LD clumping and data harmonization, 38 independent SNPs were identified as valid instrumental variables for urinary sodium from the primary dataset. The secondary dataset retained 31 SNPs, with the difference attributed to the absence of certain genetic variants or their removal during data harmonization. For the primary dataset, the selected SNPs explained approximately 0.46% of the variance (Total R-squared = 0.0046) in urinary sodium excretion. The average F-statistic was 43.85, indicating sufficient instrument strength and reducing the likelihood of weak instrument bias. Furthermore, MR Steiger filtering confirmed that the hypothesized causal direction (from urinary sodium to urolithiasis) was valid for all selected instrumental variables(Supplementary Table 1).

Causal effect of urinary sodium on urolithiasis

As shown in Table 1, the IVW analysis revealed a significant positive association between genetically predicted higher urinary sodium and an increased risk of urolithiasis (OR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.45–4.29, P= 0.00099). The weighted median method yielded consistent results in terms of the direction of the effect (OR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.11–4.54, P= 0.023). This association was replicated using the secondary urinary sodium dataset (IVW OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.39–5.07, P= 0.0029).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the reliability of the main findings. Cochran's Q test indicated acceptable heterogeneity among the SNPs (P= 0.061). The MR-Egger intercept test showed no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (Intercept P= 0.523). Crucially, the MR-PRESSO global test did not identify any statistically significant outliers. Although visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested potential minor asymmetry, the rigorous statistical evidence from MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger ensures that our overall estimates are unlikely to be substantially biased by horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analysis further demonstrated that the significant association was not driven by any single genetic variant(Supplementary Figure 1).

Discussion

In this two-sample MR study, genetically predicted urinary sodium excretion was associated with an increased risk of urolithiasis across discovery and replication datasets. These findings provide supportive genetic evidence consistent with previous observational and MR studies. By focusing specifically on urinary sodium excretion, our study evaluates its genetic liability while minimizing confounding biases often encountered in traditional observational studies (Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

Our genetic findings are biologically plausible and align with established physiological mechanisms. In the proximal tubule of the kidney, sodium and calcium share common transport pathways. An increased sodium load in the renal tubules, reflecting high dietary salt intake, has been shown to inhibit the passive paracellular reabsorption of calcium [24, 25]. This sodium-induced hypercalciuria can increase the supersaturation of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate in the urine, promoting crystal nucleation and aggregation [26, 27]. Our results provide genetic-level support for this physiological cascade in the context of stone disease.

These findings hold certain clinical implications. While fluid therapy remains a cornerstone of stone prevention [28], our data highlight the valuable role of dietary sodium restriction. Urologists and nephrologists might consider emphasizing low-sodium diets as an essential intervention for patients with recurrent urolithiasis, particularly those presenting with idiopathic hypercalciuria [29, 30]. It is important to note that the relatively large MR effect estimates reflect lifelong genetically predicted exposure trajectories. Therefore, these results should not be directly interpreted as the magnitude of risk reduction achievable through short-term dietary sodium restriction.

A key strength of our study is the application of the MR design utilizing summary data from large GWAS cohorts (such as the UK Biobank [17] and FinnGen [18]), which provided substantial statistical power and allowed for comprehensive sensitivity testing. However, certain limitations must be carefully acknowledged. First, the exposure phenotype was based on estimated rather than directly measured 24-hour urinary sodium excretion, which may partially reflect dietary intake patterns and renal handling. Second, we were unable to perform multivariable MR analyses because adequately harmonized, sufficiently powered, and non-overlapping GWAS summary statistics for intermediate urinary phenotypes (e.g., urinary calcium, volume, and oxalate) remain limited. Third, the study population was restricted to individuals of European descent; thus, the findings may not be fully generalizable to non-European populations with different dietary sodium habits and stone compositions. Fourth, the use of summary-level data prevented subgroup analyses based on specific stone compositions (e.g., uric acid versus calcium stones). Future studies incorporating multivariable MR and individual-level urinary phenotyping are warranted to further clarify the underlying biological pathways.

Conclusions

This Mendelian randomization study provides supportive genetic evidence suggesting that higher urinary sodium excretion may contribute to urolithiasis risk. These findings are consistent with current physiological understanding and support existing guideline recommendations (such as AUA and EAU) regarding dietary sodium restriction for stone prevention.
